Two-Mode Janus States: non-Gaussian generalizations of thermofield double
Arash Azizi

Abstract
We introduce the Two-Mode Janus State (TMJS), a non-Gaussian quantum state defined as a coherent superposition of two distinct Two-Mode Squeezed States (TMSS). This construction serves as a direct, non-Gaussian generalization of the canonical thermofield double (TFD) state, which is itself a single, Gaussian TMSS. We develop a complete analytical framework for the TMJS’s arbitrary k k -th order photon statistics, identifying a new family of "squeezing polynomials" that govern all diagonal and off-diagonal moments. Our central result is that the state’s non-Gaussianity is dynamically steerable via an external "Janus phase." This phase acts as a switch, allowing the higher-order coherences ( g ( k ) g^{(k)} ) to be tuned from perfectly thermal (matching the TFD marginal) to deeply sub-Poissonian, a regime marked by strong Wigner negativity. We further establish a physical realization for the TMJS, proposing its generation via coherently superposed Dynamical Casimir Effect (DCE) trajectories, distinguishing it from the static, observer-dependent Unruh effect. The TMJS provides a versatile, interference-enhanced platform for engineering Unruh-DeWitt detector responses and probing non-Gaussian physics in relativistic settings.

Introduction

A central paradigm in quantum field theory and gravity is the thermofield double (TFD), a pure entangled state constructed on a doubled Hilbert space that, when traced over one-half, yields a perfect thermal state
Israel1976
. Its most celebrated physical realization is the Unruh effect, a cornerstone of quantum field theory in curved spacetime
Unruh1976
;
Hawking1975
;
Davies1975
;
Fulling1973
;
DeWitt1975PhysicsRep
;
Birrell_Davies1982
. This effect predicts that a uniformly accelerating observer will perceive the Minkowski vacuum—the state an inertial observer finds to be empty—as a thermal bath with a temperature
T_{U}=a/2\pi

a
.

The fundamental origin of this phenomenon is that the Minkowski vacuum, when described in the Rindler basis appropriate for the accelerating observer, is a Two-Mode Squeezed State (TMSS)
UnruhWald1984
;
Israel1976
. This state perfectly entangles the quantum field modes in, say, the right Rindler wedge, to which the observer is confined, with the modes in the causally disconnected left Rindler wedge
Rindler66
. The accelerating observer, by definition, can only access the right-wedge. Tracing out the inaccessible left-wedge modes leaves the observer’s local field in a precisely thermal state. This Unruh-TMSS is a canonical example of a Gaussian state, as its Wigner function is positive and Gaussian.

Gaussian states, such as the TMSS and coherent states, are foundational resources in quantum information
Weedbrook2012gaussian
;
Braunstein2005quantum
;
Adesso2014continuous
. They are readily produced, for example, via parametric down-conversion
Wu1986generation
or four-wave mixing
slusher1985observatio
, and are the basis for continuous-variable (CV) protocols like teleportation and cluster-state computation
Menicucci2006
. However, Gaussian states and operations are insufficient for universal quantum computation, and it is precisely the introduction of non-Gaussianity that provides a key resource for quantum advantage
Ohliger2010limitations
;
Mari2012Wigner
;
Albarelli2018Resource
. This establishes a strong motivation to develop controllable, non-Gaussian resources.

To this end, the “Janus program” was recently introduced as a framework for engineering such states from coherent superpositions of simple Gaussian components
Azizi2025Janus
;
Azizi2025Janus_higher
;
Azizi2025displacedJanus
. The foundational (single-mode) Janus state—a superposition of two squeezed vacua—demonstrates how quantum interference can be engineered to transform the strong bunching (
g^{(k)}\to\infty
) of its components into sub-Poissonian statistics, establishing a universal lower bound of
g^{(2)}(0)=1/2

Azizi2025Janus
. This analysis, which introduced “squeezing polynomials” to solve the
k
-th order statistics, was extended to show that a phase-controlled switch can drive all higher-order coherences (
g^{(k\geq 3)}
) to zero
Azizi2025Janus_higher
. The framework also includes displaced Janus (DJ) states, which are distinct from traditional "squeezed-cat" states and provide a complete toolkit for "dialing in" tunable nonclassicality

In this paper, we synthesize these two threads: we use the non-Gaussian Janus framework to generalize the Gaussian Unruh-TFD state. We introduce the

2
Formalism

2.1
The Two-Mode Squeezed State (TMSS)

This section fixes the notation and collects the elementary tools used throughout the paper. We begin with a system of two bosonic modes, described by operators
a
and
b
, which satisfy the canonical commutation relations
[a,a^{\dagger}]=[b,b^{\dagger}]=1
, with all other commutators vanishing. The fundamental operator for generating entanglement between these modes is the two–mode squeezing operator, defined as

\displaystyle S_{2}(\xi)\equiv\exp\{\xi\,a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}-\xi^{*}\,ab\big\},\qquad\xi=r\,e^{i\theta},\quad r\geq 0.

(1)

Here,
r\geq 0
is the squeezing parameter and
\theta
is the squeezing phase.

To analyze the properties of this operator, it is convenient to introduce the standard generators of the SU(1,1) algebra:

\displaystyle K_{+}\equiv a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger},\qquad K_{-}\equiv ab,\qquad K_{0}\equiv\tfrac{1}{2}\big(a^{\dagger}a+b^{\dagger}b+1\big).

(2)

These generators obey the commutation relations
[K_{0},K_{\pm}]=\pm K_{\pm}
and
[K_{+},K_{-}]=-2K_{0}
. Using these relations, the unitary squeezing operator can be re-written in a factorized or "disentangled" form. A standard operator identity for SU(1,1) (a proof of which is provided in Appendix
A
) gives:

\displaystyle S_{2}(\xi)=\exp\{\alpha K_{+}\big\}\,(1-|\alpha|^{2})^{K_{0}}\,\exp\{-\alpha^{*}K_{-}\big\},\qquad\alpha\equiv\tanh r\,e^{i\theta}.

(3)

A crucial application of this theorem is its action on the two-mode vacuum state
|0,0\rangle
. Since the vacuum is annihilated by the lowering operator
K_{-}
(i.e.,
K_{-}|0,0\rangle=0
) and is an eigenstate of
K_{0}
with eigenvalue
1/2
, the action of (
3
) simplifies significantly to the useful normal–ordered form:

\displaystyle S_{2}(\xi)\,|0,0\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\{\alpha K_{+}\big\}\,(1-|\alpha|^{2})^{K_{0}}\,\exp\{-\alpha^{*}K_{-}\big\}\,|0,0\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\{\alpha K_{+}\big\}\,(1-|\alpha|^{2})^{K_{0}}\,|0,0\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-|\alpha|^{2}}\;\exp\{\alpha\,a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\}|0,0\rangle,\qquad\alpha=\tanh r\,e^{i\theta}.

(4)

Expanding the exponential in this compact expression produces the familiar Fock–space expansion of the resulting state, known as the two–mode squeezed state (TMSS). We also define the shorthand parameters
x
(based on
\tanh^{2}r
) and
\bar{n}_{r}
(the mean photon number):

\displaystyle|\xi\rangle=\frac{1}{\cosh r}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big(\tanh r\,e^{i\theta}\big)^{n}|n,n\rangle,\qquad x\equiv\tanh^{2}r,\qquad\bar{n}_{r}\equiv\sinh^{2}r=\frac{x}{1-x}.

(5)

Alternatively, the key identity (
4
) can be derived from a more fundamental first principle: the uniqueness of states defined by annihilation conditions
Azizi2025Unitary_TFD
. That argument proceeds in two steps. First, one proves that the unique normalizable state
|\psi\rangle
satisfying the coupled annihilation conditions
(a-\alpha b^{\dagger})|\psi\rangle=0
and
(b-\alpha a^{\dagger})|\psi\rangle=0
must be the twin-Fock superposition
|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{1-|\alpha|^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{m}|m,m\rangle

Azizi2025Unitary_TFD
. Second, one shows (e.g., using the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula) that the unitary state
S_{2}(\xi)|0,0\rangle
also satisfies these exact annihilation conditions, with the identification
\alpha=\tanh re^{i\theta}

Azizi2025Unitary_TFD
. Because the solution is unique, the two states must be identical, thus rigorously proving Eq. (
4
) without recourse to the disentangling theorem.

2.2
The Two-Mode Janus State (TMJS)

In Refs.
Azizi2025Janus
;
Azizi2025Janus_higher
;
Azizi2025displacedJanus
we introduced the
Janus state
as a coherent superposition of two single-mode squeezed vacua. Let

\displaystyle S_{1}(\xi)\equiv\exp\!\Big[\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\xi\,a^{\dagger 2}-\xi^{\ast}a^{2}\big)\Big],\qquad|\xi\rangle_{1}=S_{1}(\xi)\,|0\rangle,\qquad\xi=r\,e^{i\theta},

(6)

and analogously
|\zeta\rangle_{1}=S_{1}(\zeta)|0\rangle
with
\zeta=s\,e^{i\phi}
.
The single-mode Janus state was defined as

\displaystyle|{\Psi}\rangle_{1}\;=\;\chi\,|\xi\rangle_{1}\;+\;\eta\,e^{\,i\delta}\,|\zeta\rangle_{1},

(7)

with real amplitudes
\chi=|\chi|\geq 0
,
\eta=|\eta|\geq 0
, and an external
Janus phase

\delta
that steers interference in moments and cumulants.

Now we introduce TMJS by extending the Janus philosophy from single-mode squeezing to the
two-mode
Gaussian resource generated by
S_{2}
across modes
a
and
b
. Introduce a second two-mode squeezed vacuum,

\displaystyle|\zeta\rangle=\frac{1}{\cosh s}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\big(\tanh s\,e^{i\phi}\big)^{m}|m,m\rangle,\qquad y\equiv\tanh^{2}s,\qquad\bar{n}_{s}\equiv\sinh^{2}s=\frac{y}{1-y}.

(8)

The
TMJS
is the coherent superposition

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\chi\,|\xi\rangle+\eta\,|\zeta\rangle,\qquad\chi=|\chi|\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\quad\eta=|\eta|\,e^{i\delta},

(9)

where
|\xi\rangle
and
|\zeta\rangle
are two distinct TMSSs (generally different
\{r,\theta\}
and
\{s,\phi\}
). For any normally ordered observable
\hat{O}
, expectation values obey the master bilinear rule

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\Psi}=|\chi|^{2}\langle\xi|\hat{O}|\xi\rangle+|\eta|^{2}\langle\zeta|\hat{O}|\zeta\rangle+2|\chi||\eta|\,\real\!\big[e^{-i\delta}\langle\zeta|\hat{O}|\xi\rangle\big].

(10)

Interference is governed by the complex overlap parameter

\displaystyle z\equiv e^{i(\theta-\phi)}\,\tanh r\,\tanh s,\qquad|z|<1,

(11)

which serves as the argument of all cross-state kernels. Closed-form expressions for

\langle\zeta|(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}|\xi\rangle
and

\langle\zeta|(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger})^{k}(ab)^{k}|\xi\rangle
,
together with the squeezing polynomials
P_{k}(u)
that control higher-order coherences, are derived in Sec.
3
.

3
Coherence, Statistics, and Results

3.1
Coherence of a Single TMSS (Diagonal Kernels)

We now derive the factorial moments for a single TMSS,
|\xi\rangle
, using the parameters defined in Eq. (
5
). These results will serve as the "diagonal" terms in the Janus state calculation.

3.1.1
Cross-mode coherence and Squeezing Polynomials

Our first goal is to compute the cross-mode
k
-th factorial moment,
\langle\xi|(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger})^{k}(ab)^{k}|\xi\rangle
, for any integer
k\geq 0
and
|x|<1
. This calculation is most directly performed in the Fock basis. The action of the lowering operator
(ab)^{k}
on a twin-Fock state
|n,n\rangle
produces the falling factorial:

\displaystyle(ab)^{k}|n,n\rangle=n(n{-}1)\cdots(n{-}k{+}1)\,|n{-}k,n{-}k\rangle.

(12)

By taking the adjoint and using the orthonormality of the Fock basis, the expectation value becomes a sum over
n
. Using the parameter
x=\tanh^{2}r
and
\cosh^{-2}r=1-x
, this sum is

\displaystyle\langle\xi|(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger})^{k}(ab)^{k}|\xi\rangle=\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}r}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Big[n(n{-}1)\cdots(n{-}k{+}1)\Big]^{2}\,(\tanh^{2}r)^{n}=(1-x)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big[n^{\underline{k}}\big]^{2}x^{n},

(13)

where
n^{\underline{k}}=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)
.

To evaluate this sum, we use a generating function identity based on the differential operator
x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}
, which acts on
x^{n}
to produce the falling factorial:

\displaystyle x^{k}\,\partial_{x}^{k}\,x^{n}=n^{\underline{k}}\,x^{n}\qquad(n,k\in\mathbb{N}).

(14)

Since our sum (
13
) involves the square of the falling factorial,
[n^{\underline{k}}]^{2}
, we can express it by applying this differential operator twice to the basic geometric series
\sum x^{n}=(1-x)^{-1}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big[n^{\underline{k}}\big]^{2}x^{n}=x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}\left[x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{n}\right)\right]=x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}\left[x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)\right].

(15)

We can now define the full, unnormalized moment
F_{k}(x)
by re-inserting the prefactor from (
13
):

\displaystyle F_{k}(x)\;\equiv\;\langle\xi|(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger})^{k}(ab)^{k}|\xi\rangle=(1-x)\,x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}\left[x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)\right].

(16)

We evaluate this expression from the inside out. The action of the inner operator is

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)=\frac{k!}{(1-x)^{k+1}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)=\frac{k!\,x^{k}}{(1-x)^{k+1}}.

(17)

Applying the outer differential operator,
x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}
, to this intermediate result requires the use of the Leibniz rule for the
k
-th derivative of a product:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{k}\big[x^{k}(1-x)^{-(k+1)}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\,\big[\partial_{x}^{k-j}x^{k}\big]\,\big[\partial_{x}^{j}(1-x)^{-(k+1)}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\,\left[\frac{k!}{j!}\,x^{\,j}\right]\,\left[\frac{(k+j)!}{k!}\,(1-x)^{-(k+1+j)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\,\frac{(k+j)!}{j!}\,x^{\,j}\,(1-x)^{-(k+1+j)}.

(18)

To complete the calculation of
F_{k}(x)
, we multiply this sum by the remaining factors from (
16
), which are the leading
x^{k}
and the prefactor
(1-x)
. This combination yields the final rational form:

\displaystyle F_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k!)^{2}\,x^{k}}{(1-x)^{2k}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\binom{k{+}j}{j}\,x^{\,j}(1-x)^{\,k-j}.

(19)

This finite sum can be written compactly using a hypergeometric representation:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\binom{k{+}j}{j}\,x^{\,j}(1-x)^{\,k-j}=(1-x)^{k}\;{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-k,\,k{+}1;\,1;\,\frac{-x}{1-x}\right)={}_{2}F_{1}(-k,-k;1;x),

(20)

where the last equality follows directly from Euler’s transformation,

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)=(1-z)^{-a}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\big(a,\,c-b;\,c;\,\tfrac{z}{z-1}\big)
,
by setting
a=-k
,
b=k+1
,
c=1
, and
z=-x/(1-x)
.

We can therefore express
F_{k}(x)
as a ratio involving a new polynomial family. It is conventional and highly useful to separate this expression into its polynomial part and its singular part (which diverges as
x\to 1
). We therefore define the
squeezing polynomial
family,
P_{k}(x)
, as the polynomial numerator:

\displaystyle F_{k}(x)=\frac{P_{k}(x)}{(1-x)^{2k}},\qquad P_{k}(x)=(k!)^{2}\,x^{k}\,{}_{2}F_{1}(-k,-k;1;x)=(k!)^{2}\,x^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}^{2}x^{\,j}.

(21)

Equivalently, this polynomial is related to the standard Legendre polynomial
\mathrm{P}_{k}
by

\displaystyle P_{k}(x)=(k!)^{2}\,x^{k}(1-x)^{k}\,\mathrm{P}_{k}\left(\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right).

(22)

Before deriving the cross-mode coherence, we first apply these results to the simpler case of the single-mode factorial moments.

3.1.2
Single-mode coherence: thermal statistics

The
k
-th order coherence function for a single mode is formally defined as the
k
-th factorial moment normalized by the
k
-th power of the mean photon number:

\displaystyle g^{(k)}_{a}(\xi)\equiv\frac{\langle(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}\rangle_{\xi}}{\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\xi}^{k}}.

(23)

To compute the numerator
\langle(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}\rangle_{\xi}
, we trace over the reduced density matrix of mode
a
. This state is thermal,
\rho_{a}=(1-x)\sum_{n}x^{n}|n\rangle\langle n|
, so the expectation value is

\displaystyle\langle(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}\rangle_{\xi}=\text{Tr}\left(\rho_{a}a^{\dagger k}a^{k}\right)=(1-x)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{n}\langle n|a^{\dagger k}a^{k}|n\rangle=(1-x)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{\underline{k}}x^{n}.

(24)

This sum is a standard generating function, which can be found by applying the differential operator from (
14
) to the geometric series:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{\underline{k}}\,x^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}(x^{n})=x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{n}\right)=x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)=\frac{k!\,x^{k}}{(1-x)^{k+1}}.

(25)

Substituting this result into our expression for the
k
-th moment gives:

\displaystyle\langle(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}\rangle_{\xi}=(1-x)\left[\frac{k!\,x^{k}}{(1-x)^{k+1}}\right]=\frac{k!\,x^{k}}{(1-x)^{k}}.

(26)

The mean photon number
\bar{n}_{r}=\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\xi}
is the
k=1
case,
\bar{n}_{r}=x/(1-x)
, which matches our definition in Eq. (
5
). We can therefore write the
k
-th factorial moment compactly in terms of the mean photon number:

\displaystyle\langle(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}\rangle_{\xi}=k!\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right)^{k}=k!\,\bar{n}_{r}^{k}.

(27)

Finally, we assemble the normalized coherence function
g^{(k)}_{a}(\xi)
:

\displaystyle g^{(k)}_{a}(\xi)=\frac{\langle(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}\rangle_{\xi}}{\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\xi}^{k}}=\frac{k!\,\bar{n}_{r}^{k}}{(\bar{n}_{r})^{k}}=k!.

(28)

This result,
g^{(k)}_{a}=k!
, confirms the well-known fact that the single-mode statistics of a pure two-mode squeezed vacuum, when one mode is traced out, are perfectly thermal.

3.1.3
Cross-mode coherence and final checks

We now return to the cross-mode coherence,
g^{(k)}_{ab}(\xi)
. This is obtained by normalizing
F_{k}(x)
from Eq. (
21
) by the
k
-th power of the product of the mean photon numbers,
\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\xi}\,\langle b^{\dagger}b\rangle_{\xi}=\bar{n}_{r}^{2}=(x/(1-x))^{2}
. This gives the final, compact form for the coherence:

\displaystyle g^{(k)}_{ab}(\xi)=\frac{F_{k}(x)}{\big(\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\xi}\,\langle b^{\dagger}b\rangle_{\xi}\big)^{k}}=\frac{P_{k}(x)/(1-x)^{2k}}{\left(x/(1-x)\right)^{2k}}=\frac{P_{k}(x)}{x^{2k}}=(k!)^{2}\,x^{-k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}^{2}x^{\,j}

(29)

As final checks, we can evaluate this for low values of
k
using the polynomial
P_{k}(x)
from Eq. (
21
).

For
k=1
, (
21
) gives
P_{1}(x)=x(1+x)
. Substituting into (
29
) yields:

\displaystyle g^{(1)}_{ab}(\xi)=\frac{P_{1}(x)}{x^{2}}=\frac{x(1+x)}{x^{2}}=\frac{1+x}{x}=1+\frac{1}{x}=1+\coth^{2}r=2+\sinh^{-2}r.

(30)

For
k=2
, (
21
) gives
P_{2}(x)=4x^{2}(1+4x+x^{2})
, which gives:

\displaystyle g^{(2)}_{ab}(\xi)=\frac{P_{2}(x)}{x^{4}}=\frac{4x^{2}(1+4x+x^{2})}{x^{4}}=\frac{4(1+4x+x^{2})}{x^{2}}.

(31)

For
k=3
, (
21
) gives
P_{3}(x)=36x^{3}(1+9x+9x^{2}+x^{3})
, which gives:

\displaystyle g^{(3)}_{ab}(\xi)=\frac{P_{3}(x)}{x^{6}}=\frac{36(1+9x+9x^{2}+x^{3})}{x^{3}}.

(32)

For
k=4
, (
21
) gives
P_{4}(x)=576x^{4}(1+16x+36x^{2}+16x^{3}+x^{4})
, which gives:

\displaystyle g^{(4)}_{ab}(\xi)=\frac{P_{4}(x)}{x^{8}}=\frac{576(1+16x+36x^{2}+16x^{3}+x^{4})}{x^{4}}.

(33)

3.2
Arbitrary
k
-th order coherences for TMJS

We now construct the Two–Mode Janus State (TMJS) by forming a coherent superposition of the two TMSSs,
|\xi\rangle
and
|\zeta\rangle
, using the parameters defined in Sec.
2
. The state is defined as

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\chi\,|\xi\rangle+\eta\,|\zeta\rangle,\qquad\chi=|\chi|\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\quad\eta=|\eta|\,e^{i\delta},

(34)

where
\xi=r\,e^{i\theta}
and
\zeta=s\,e^{i\phi}
are the complex squeeze parameters of the two constituent states, and
\delta
is the external "Janus" phase governing their interference. The expectation value of any normally ordered observable
\hat{O}
in the TMJS state is given by the fundamental bilinear expansion rule:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\Psi}=|\chi|^{2}\langle\xi|\hat{O}|\xi\rangle+|\eta|^{2}\langle\zeta|\hat{O}|\zeta\rangle+2|\chi||\eta|\,\real[e^{-i\delta}\,\langle\zeta|\hat{O}|\xi\rangle\big].

(35)

This expression cleanly separates the expectation value into a classical mixture of the two states (the first two terms) and a quantum interference term (the final term). Note that the interference phase
\delta
always appears with a minus sign inside the real part operation,
\real[\cdot]
.

To compute the
k
-th order moments, we need the diagonal kernels derived in the preceding subsection, and the off-diagonal (cross-state) kernels. We can derive these off-diagonal kernels using the exact same generating function method as in Sec.
3.1
. The mathematical structure of the calculation is identical, with the only change being the parameters that form the underlying geometric series. Let’s calculate the cross-mode kernel
\langle\zeta|(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger})^{k}(ab)^{k}|\xi\rangle
. We have:

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger})^{k}(ab)^{k}|\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\frac{(\tanh se^{-i\phi})^{m}}{\cosh s}\frac{(\tanh re^{i\theta})^{n}}{\cosh r}\langle m,m|(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger})^{k}(ab)^{k}|n,n\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cosh r\cosh s}\sum_{n}[n^{\underline{k}}]^{2}\big(\tanh r\tanh s\,e^{i(\theta-\phi)}\big)^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cosh r\cosh s}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[n^{\underline{k}}]^{2}z^{n}.

(36)

In the first step, we expanded both states in the Fock basis. In the second, we used the fact that the operator
\langle m,m|\dots|n,n\rangle
is number-preserving and thus non-zero only for
m=n
, collapsing the double sum. In the third, we used
\langle n,n|(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger})^{k}(ab)^{k}|n,n\rangle=[n^{\underline{k}}]^{2}
. In the final step, we substituted the definition of

from Eq. (

From our derivation of

evaluates to

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[n^{\underline{k}}]^{2}z^{n}=\frac{P_{k}(z)}{(1-z)^{2k+1}}.

(37)

Multiplying this result by its prefactor
\frac{1}{\cosh r\cosh s}
gives the final cross-mode kernel. A parallel argument for the single-mode kernel
\langle\zeta|(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}|\xi\rangle

\sum_{n}n^{\underline{k}}z^{n}=k!z^{k}/(1-z)^{k+1}
, yields its corresponding expression. This demonstrates that the off-diagonal kernels share the same functional form as their diagonal counterparts, evaluated at

instead of

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}|\xi\rangle=\frac{k!}{\cosh r\,\cosh s}\,\frac{z^{\,k}}{(1-z)^{k+1}},\qquad\langle\zeta|(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger})^{k}(ab)^{k}|\xi\rangle=\frac{1}{\cosh r\,\cosh s}\,\frac{P_{k}(z)}{(1-z)^{2k+1}},

(38)

where
P_{k}(u)
is the squeezing polynomial family defined in Eq. (

Before calculating the
k
-th order coherences, we must first find the mean photon number, which is required for normalization. By applying the bilinear rule (

) to the operator
\hat{O}=a^{\dagger}a
(using the

\displaystyle\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\Psi}=|\chi|^{2}\,\bar{n}_{r}+|\eta|^{2}\,\bar{n}_{s}+\frac{2|\chi||\eta|}{\cosh r\,\cosh s}\,\real\left[e^{-i\delta}\,\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}\right],\qquad\langle b^{\dagger}b\rangle_{\Psi}=\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\Psi}.

(39)

\langle(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}\rangle_{\Psi}

\displaystyle\langle(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}\rangle_{\Psi}=k!\,\Big(|\chi|^{2}\bar{n}_{r}^{\,k}+|\eta|^{2}\bar{n}_{s}^{\,k}\Big)+\frac{2|\chi||\eta|\,k!}{\cosh r\,\cosh s}\,\real\left[e^{-i\delta}\,\frac{z^{\,k}}{(1-z)^{k+1}}\right].

(40)

\displaystyle g^{(k)}_{a}(\Psi)=\frac{\langle(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k}\rangle_{\Psi}}{\big(\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\Psi}\big)^{k}},\quad\text{with }\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\Psi}\text{ from \eqref{eq:tmjs_mean_for_norm}}.

(41)

Similarly, we compute the cross–mode

\displaystyle\langle(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger})^{k}(ab)^{k}\rangle_{\Psi}=|\chi|^{2}\,\frac{P_{k}(x)}{(1-x)^{2k}}+|\eta|^{2}\,\frac{P_{k}(y)}{(1-y)^{2k}}+\frac{2|\chi||\eta|}{\cosh r\,\cosh s}\,\real\left[e^{-i\delta}\,\frac{P_{k}(z)}{(1-z)^{2k+1}}\right].

(42)

The normalized cross–coherence

(43)

\displaystyle 2|\chi||\eta|\,\real[e^{-i\delta}f_{k}(z)\big]=2|\chi||\eta|\,|f_{k}(z)|\,\cos\big(\varphi_k-\delta\big),

5
Physical Realization and QFT Analogues

This section clarifies what the Unruh/Rindler correspondence does—and does not—buy us for preparing a TMJS. The key point is that an observer’s acceleration, by itself, does not create different
field states
; it only changes the observer’s notion of particles and their physical response to the existing vacuum. By contrast,
time-dependent dynamics of the field
(e.g., accelerated boundaries, parametric driving, or cosmological pair creation) are true state-generating processes. These dynamics
do
produce the distinct TMSS resources that can be coherently superposed to realize a TMJS.

5.1
What Unruh/Rindler does and does not do

Let
\eta
denote the boost time in a Rindler wedge and
-i\partial_{\eta}
the corresponding generator. The Minkowski vacuum admits the exact continuous-mode TMSS factorization across the L/R wedges,

\displaystyle\ket{0_M}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{Z}}}\exp\!\Bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\omega\;e^{-\pi\omega}\,b_{L\omega}^{\dagger}b_{R\omega}^{\dagger}\Bigg]\ket{0_L}\otimes\ket{0_R},

(46)

where
\omega>0
is the
dimensionless
boost frequency (the eigenvalue of
-i\partial_{\eta}
). No “acceleration parameter” appears in (
46
); the state is fixed by Poincaré invariance. To use the discrete-mode formulas of Secs.
3.1
–
3.2
, one wavepacketizes the boost-frequency continuum into narrow, non-overlapping packets; within each packet the weight
e^{-\pi\omega}
is approximately constant, yielding a per-packet TMSS.

Uniformly accelerated worldlines have proper time
\tau
related to
\eta
by
\tau=\eta/a_{0}
(with
a_{0}
the proper acceleration). Detectors coupled along such worldlines physically observe a KMS thermal bath at temperature
T=a_{0}/2\pi
. However, this temperature characterizes the detector’s physical
response
to the static
\ket{0_M}
state; it is not a signal that a new global field state has been prepared.

This distinction is fundamental. As rigorously shown in
Azizi2023JHEP
, the Rindler vacuum is unique and independent of the observer’s specific acceleration. The Bogoliubov transformation between Rindler operators defined for two different accelerations,
a
and
a^{\prime}
, is “trivial”—it does not mix creation and annihilation operators. This proves that all Rindler observers, regardless of their acceleration scale, are probing the
same
vacuum.

Remark 1

The Rindler vacuum is unique. The Minkowski vacuum’s L/R TMSS decomposition (
46
) is a fixed, intrinsic property of the state. Superposing different observer accelerations cannot yield a superposition of different
field
TMSSs (a TMJS), precisely because all such observers share the identical vacuum.

Consequently, any physical construction of a TMJS must be based on
dynamics that change the field state itself
, not merely on a change of observer.

5.2
Operational routes that
do
prepare distinct TMSSs

Having established that a physical process—not merely an observer—is required to generate new field states, we now identify a family of dynamics that provides the necessary “bona fide” TMSS resources. These are true, multi-mode squeezed states created from the vacuum by a physical interaction. The central proposal is that by coherently superposing two distinct
histories
of such a process, one can generate a TMJS in the sense of Sec.
3.2
.

(i) Accelerated boundaries / dynamical Casimir effect

The most general and physically direct mechanism is the Dynamical Casimir Effect (DCE). In this scenario, a time-dependent, perfectly reflecting boundary (i.e., an accelerating mirror) with a worldline

actively injects energy into the field. This physical disturbance induces a non-trivial Bogoliubov transformation that

This Bogoliubov map is, in general, a complex, non-diagonal transformation that mixes all field modes. However, the Bloch–Messiah reduction (or Schmidt decomposition) theorem provides a powerful simplification. It guarantees that there exists a new, customized "Schmidt packet" basis (labeled by a discrete index

Thus, a single, complete history of mirror motion,

, generates a specific family of TMSSs, one for each Schmidt mode

\{\ket{\xi_k^{(1)}}=S_{2}(\xi_{k}^{(1)})\ket{0}_{k}\}

Now, consider two

and

5.3
) more complex "Ramsey-like" squeeze–dwell–unsqueeze sequences. Each history, when run independently, generates its own unique family of TMSSs:
\{\xi_{k}^{(1)}\}
and
\{\xi_{k}^{(2)}\}

To create the TMJS, we coherently superpose these histories. We can imagine coupling the boundary’s motion to an ancilla qubit. We first prepare the ancilla in a superposition

if the ancilla is in state

and history
\mathcal{T}_{2}

\ket{2}

), we "erase" the which-history information. This projection leaves the quantum field itself in the desired coherent superposition. Assuming an idealized case where the Schmidt bases of both histories are perfectly mode-matched, the final field state factorizes, preparing, mode by mode:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_k}\propto\chi\,\ket{\xi_k^{(1)}}+\eta\,e^{i\delta}\,\ket{\xi_k^{(2)}},

(47)

which is precisely the definition of a TMJS for each Schmidt mode

\displaystyle z_{k}=e^{i(\vartheta_{k}^{(1)}-\vartheta_{k}^{(2)})}\,\tanh r_{k}^{(1)}\,\tanh r_{k}^{(2)},\qquad|z_{k}|<1,

5.3
Physical Realization via Superposed Boundaries

The abstract formalism of the TMJS finds a direct and physically-grounded realization in the Dynamical Casimir Effect (DCE). The DCE describes the creation of real particles from the vacuum due to the motion of a boundary (e.g., an accelerating mirror). This section builds this model, starting with a crucial distinction between the observer-dependent Unruh effect and the state-generating DCE.

5.3.1
Unruh Effect vs. Dynamical Casimir Effect

It is crucial to separate the
quantum state of the field
from an
observer’s definition of particles
. The two canonical effects highlight this difference:

1.

Unruh Effect (Accelerated Observer, Static Vacuum):
The global field is prepared in the Minkowski vacuum,
\ket{0_M}
. An accelerated observer, by virtue of their motion, naturally uses Rindler modes to describe the field. In this Rindler basis, the
same
Minkowski vacuum state
\ket{0_M}
is mathematically equivalent to a two-mode squeezed state (TMSS) entangling the right and left Rindler wedges.

This observer will physically detect a thermal bath of particles. However, this describes the
response
of a localized, accelerated probe, which draws energy from the agent causing its acceleration to click. It is not a
state-generating process
that alters the global quantum field. An inertial observer in the same spacetime still sees the empty, unchanged Minkowski vacuum. As shown in
Azizi2023JHEP
, the Rindler vacuum is unique and independent of the acceleration scale, confirming that changing the observer’s acceleration does not create a new field state.

2.

Dynamical Casimir Effect (Inertial Observer, Dynamic Boundary):
Here, the observer remains inertial, but the boundary (the mirror) is accelerated. This time-dependent boundary injects real energy
into the field itself
. This motion induces a nontrivial Bogoliubov transformation on the
in
-modes, one with a non-zero
\beta
coefficient that mixes creation and annihilation operators. The resulting
out
-state is no longer the Minkowski vacuum. It is a new quantum state, one populated with real, correlated quanta that are detectable by
any
observer, including inertial ones. This is a true state-generating, physical process.

With this distinction, we can properly define the "vacuum" for the accelerating mirror scenario. For a perfect reflector with a worldline
v=p(u)
in
1{+}1
dimensions, the starting point is the
in-vacuum
: the state on the past null infinity,
\mathscr{I}^{-}
, which contains no incoming quanta. If the mirror was static in the far past, this is just
\ket{0_M}
. The subsequent acceleration of the mirror implements a dynamical
SU(1,1)
Bogoliubov transformation that
creates
correlated pairs, driving this well-defined in-vacuum to a nontrivial out-state populated with particles.

In the massless case, this physical creation of energy is explicit. The non-inertial boundary motion acts as a direct source of radiation, generating a detectable energy flux given by the Schwarzian derivative of the trajectory:

\displaystyle\langle T_{uu}\rangle_{\rm out}=-\frac{1}{24\pi}\,\{p(u),u\}.

(49)

This scattering process is a general Bogoliubov transformation. For a non-stationary trajectory, the map is not diagonal in the standard monochromatic frequency basis. Instead, it mixes
all
incoming frequencies (
\Omega^{\prime}
) to produce a single outgoing frequency (
\Omega
). Let
a_{\Omega}^{\rm in},b_{\Omega}^{\rm in}
be operators for incoming modes. The outgoing operators are given by complex integral transforms:

\displaystyle a_{\Omega}^{\rm out}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\Omega^{\prime}\,\Big[\alpha_{\Omega\Omega^{\prime}}\,a_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\rm in}+\beta_{\Omega\Omega^{\prime}}\,b_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\rm in\,\dagger}\Big],\qquad b_{\Omega}^{\rm out}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\Omega^{\prime}\,\Big[\tilde{\alpha}_{\Omega\Omega^{\prime}}\,b_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\rm in}+\tilde{\beta}_{\Omega\Omega^{\prime}}\,a_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\rm in\,\dagger}\Big],

(50)

which must satisfy constraints to preserve the canonical commutation relations. This general, "messy" linear transformation can always be diagonalized. By the Bloch–Messiah (or Schmidt-mode) decomposition, there always exists a new, discrete basis of orthonormal wave-packets—the "Schmidt modes"
k
—that are custom-built for this specific trajectory.

In this special Schmidt basis, denoted
(\tilde{a}_{k}^{\rm in},\tilde{b}_{k}^{\rm in})
and
(\tilde{a}_{k}^{\rm out},\tilde{b}_{k}^{\rm out})
, the complex integral transformation (
50

2\times 2

SU(1,1)
maps, one for each Schmidt mode
k

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{k}^{\rm out}=\cosh r_{k}\,\tilde{a}_{k}^{\rm in}+e^{i\vartheta_{k}}\sinh r_{k}\,\tilde{b}_{k}^{\rm in\,\dagger},\qquad\tilde{b}_{k}^{\rm out}=e^{-i\vartheta_{k}}\sinh r_{k}\,\tilde{a}_{k}^{\rm in\,\dagger}+\cosh r_{k}\,\tilde{b}_{k}^{\rm in}.

(51)

This provides the crucial link: the in-vacuum
|0\rangle_{\rm in}=\bigotimes_{k}|0\rangle_{{\rm in},k}
evolves to a tensor product of independent two-mode squeezed states, one for each Schmidt mode
k
:

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle_{\rm out}=\bigotimes_{k}|\xi_{k}\rangle=\bigotimes_{k}S_{2}(\xi_{k})|0\rangle_{{\rm in},k},\qquad\xi_{k}=r_{k}e^{i\vartheta_{k}}.

(52)

This establishes the rigorous connection: a single, generic accelerating mirror trajectory produces a set of discrete, independent TMSSs, not in the frequency basis, but in its own unique Schmidt-mode wave-packet basis.

This direct link provides a clear path to generating a TMJS. If a
single

superposition
of histories can produce a superposition of TMSSs. Consider two distinct mirror trajectories,
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, which generate two different sets of squeezing parameters,
\{\xi_{k}^{(1)}\}
and
\{\xi_{k}^{(2)}\}

\mathsf{G}

We start by preparing the ancilla in a superposition
\chi|1\rangle_{\mathsf{G}}+\eta\,e^{i\delta}|2\rangle_{\mathsf{G}}
. We then apply a controlled evolution
U_{\rm ctrl}
that couples the ancilla to the field:

\displaystyle U_{\rm ctrl}=|1\rangle\!\langle 1|_{\mathsf{G}}\otimes U_{1}+|2\rangle\!\langle 2|_{\mathsf{G}}\otimes U_{2},

(53)

where
U_{j}
is the unitary evolution corresponding to trajectory
\mathcal{T}_{j}

\ket{1}
, the mirror performs
\mathcal{T}_{1}
; if it is
\ket{2}
, it performs
\mathcal{T}_{2}
. This entangles the ancilla with the field, producing the state
\chi(U_{1}|0\rangle)\ket{1}+\eta e^{i\delta}(U_{2}|0\rangle)\ket{2}
.

Finally, we "erase" the which-history information by projecting the ancilla onto a superposition state, such as
|+\rangle_{\mathsf{G}}\propto|1\rangle_{\mathsf{G}}+|2\rangle_{\mathsf{G}}
. This post-selection disentangles the ancilla and projects the field into the desired coherent superposition:

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle_{\rm out}\ \propto\ \chi\,U_{1}|0\rangle+\eta\,e^{i\delta}\,U_{2}|0\rangle.

(54)

For this to result in a simple, per-mode TMJS, we must address the issue of the Schmidt basis. Each history
j
has its own Bloch–Messiah decomposition,
U_{j}=W^{(j)}\Big[\prod_{k}S_{2}(\xi^{(j)}_{k})\Big]V^{(j)}

that define its unique Schmidt basis. If the bases for
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
are different (i.e.,
V^{(1)}\!\neq\!V^{(2)}
), the resulting state (
54
) is a complex superposition that is not a simple product.

If, however, we assume an idealized case where mode-matching is enforced (i.e., the trajectories are chosen such that

and
W^{(1)}\!=\!W^{(2)}
), the state factorizes into a simple product. This process prepares,

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle_{\rm out}\ \propto\ \bigotimes_{k}\Big(\chi\,|\xi_{k}^{(1)}\rangle+\eta\,e^{i\delta}\,|\xi_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\Big).

Assuming this idealized mode-matched case, the parameters of this physical TMJS map directly to the abstract formalism of Sec.
3

from each history’s Schmidt decomposition and set:

\displaystyle x_{k}=\tanh^{2}r_{k}^{(1)},\qquad y_{k}=\tanh^{2}r_{k}^{(2)},\qquad z_{k}=e^{i(\vartheta_{k}^{(1)}-\vartheta_{k}^{(2)})}\tanh r_{k}^{(1)}\tanh r_{k}^{(2)}.

(56)

With this mapping, all our derived formulas for
g^{(k)}

A practical question is how to generate a non-trivial phase difference

\displaystyle M=Q(-r)\,R(\phi)\,Q(r)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\

The

This brings us to the final answer for our motivating question. The appropriate "vacuum" for an inertial observer is the

By contrast, the TMJS from superposed histories, Eq. (

Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced and analytically solved the TMJS, a non-Gaussian state defined as a coherent superposition of two distinct two-mode squeezed states. Our central goal was to synthesize the controllable, interference-based non-Gaussianity of the Janus program with the fundamental thermal structure of quantum field theory in curved spacetime, as exemplified by the Unruh effect.

We derived a complete analytical framework for the TMJS photon statistics, applicable to arbitrary

g_{ab}^{(k)}(\Psi)

Our visualization of the coherence landscapes in Sec.

Introducing a non-trivial relative squeezing phase (

as a master control knob. Near the antisymmetric point

strongly suppresses

Conceptually, the TMJS provides a non-Gaussian generalization of the thermofield double (TFD) state. While the standard Unruh effect supplies a

Appendix A
Proof of the SU(1,1) Disentangling Theorem

We provide a proof for the disentangling identity, Eq. (
3
).
The operator is
S_{2}(\xi)=\exp\{X\}
, where
X=\xi K_{+}-\xi^{*}K_{-}
. Let
\xi=re^{i\theta}
.
The generators satisfy
[K_{0},K_{\pm}]=\pm K_{\pm}
and
[K_{+},K_{-}]=-2K_{0}
.

We seek to find the functions
A,B,C
such that

\displaystyle S_{2}(\xi)=\exp\{AK_{+}\}\exp\{BK_{0}\}\exp\{CK_{-}\}.

(58)

This is a standard operator ordering problem. A common method is to solve the Wei-Norman differential equations. Let
S(\lambda)=\exp\{\lambda X\}
and assume a disentangled form
S(\lambda)=\exp\{A(\lambda)K_{+}\}\exp\{B(\lambda)K_{0}\}\exp\{C(\lambda)K_{-}\}
. The goal is to find
A(1),B(1),C(1)
.

We differentiate the ansatz (
58
) with respect to
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{d\lambda}=\left(\frac{dA}{d\lambda}K_{+}\right)S+e^{AK_{+}}\left(\frac{dB}{d\lambda}K_{0}\right)e^{-AK_{+}}S+e^{AK_{+}}e^{BK_{0}}\left(\frac{dC}{d\lambda}K_{-}\right)e^{-BK_{0}}e^{-AK_{+}}S.

(59)

We use the operator identities (Hadamard’s lemma):

e^{X}Ye^{-X}=Y+[X,Y]+\frac{1}{2!}[X,[X,Y]]+\dots

\displaystyle e^{AK_{+}}K_{0}e^{-AK_{+}}

\displaystyle=K_{0}-[AK_{+},K_{0}]=K_{0}-AK_{+}

(60)

\displaystyle e^{BK_{0}}K_{-}e^{-BK_{0}}

\displaystyle=K_{-}e^{-B}

(61)

\displaystyle e^{AK_{+}}K_{-}e^{-AK_{+}}

\displaystyle=K_{-}-2AK_{0}+A^{2}K_{+}

(62)

Substituting these into (
59
) gives:

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{d\lambda}S^{-1}=\frac{dA}{d\lambda}K_{+}+\frac{dB}{d\lambda}(K_{0}-AK_{+})+\frac{dC}{d\lambda}e^{-B}(K_{-}-2AK_{0}+A^{2}K_{+})

(63)

We also know that
\frac{dS}{d\lambda}S^{-1}=X=\xi K_{+}-\xi^{*}K_{-}
.
By equating the coefficients of
K_{+},K_{0},K_{-}
in (
63
), we get a system of coupled differential equations:

\displaystyle\text{coeff}(K_{+}):\quad\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{dA}{d\lambda}-A\frac{dB}{d\lambda}+A^{2}e^{-B}\frac{dC}{d\lambda}

(64)

\displaystyle\text{coeff}(K_{0}):\quad 0

\displaystyle=\frac{dB}{d\lambda}-2Ae^{-B}\frac{dC}{d\lambda}

(65)

\displaystyle\text{coeff}(K_{-}):\quad-\xi^{*}

\displaystyle=e^{-B}\frac{dC}{d\lambda}

(66)

We solve this system with initial conditions
A(0)=B(0)=C(0)=0
(since
S(0)=I
).
From the third equation:
\frac{dC}{d\lambda}=-\xi^{*}e^{B}
.
Substitute this into the second:
\frac{dB}{d\lambda}=2Ae^{-B}(-\xi^{*}e^{B})=-2A\xi^{*}
.
Substitute both into the first:
\xi=\frac{dA}{d\lambda}-A(-2A\xi^{*})+A^{2}e^{-B}(-\xi^{*}e^{B})=\frac{dA}{d\lambda}+2A^{2}\xi^{*}-A^{2}\xi^{*}=\frac{dA}{d\lambda}+A^{2}\xi^{*}
.
So we have the system:

\displaystyle\frac{dA}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=\xi-A^{2}\xi^{*}

(67)

\displaystyle\frac{dB}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=-2A\xi^{*}

(68)

\displaystyle\frac{dC}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\xi^{*}e^{B}

(69)

Equation (
67
) is a Riccati equation for
A
. We can verify the solution
A(\lambda)=\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\tanh(|\xi| \lambda)=\frac{\xi}{r}\tanh(r \lambda)
.
Let’s check:
\frac{dA}{d\lambda}=\frac{\xi}{r}(r\text{sech}^{2}(r\lambda))=\xi(1-\tanh^{2}(r\lambda))
.
RHS of (
67
) is:
\xi-(\frac{\xi}{r}\tanh(r \lambda))^{2}\xi^{*}=\xi-\frac{\xi^{2}\xi^{*}}{r^{2}}\tanh^{2}(r\lambda)=\xi-\frac{\xi(r^{2})}{r^{2}}\tanh^{2}(r\lambda)=\xi(1-\tanh^{2}(r\lambda))
.
The solution is correct.

Now we solve for
B(\lambda)
:

\frac{dB}{d\lambda}=-2\left(\frac{\xi}{r}\tanh(r \lambda)\right)\xi^{*}=-2\frac{r^{2}}{r}\tanh(r \lambda)=-2r\tanh(r \lambda)
.

B(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}-2r\tanh(r \lambda')d\lambda^{\prime}=-2\ln(\cosh(r \lambda'))\Big|_{0}^{\lambda}=-2\ln(\cosh(r \lambda))
.
So,
e^{B(\lambda)}=\text{sech}^{2}(r\lambda)=1-\tanh^{2}(r\lambda)
.

Finally, we solve for
C(\lambda)
:

\frac{dC}{d\lambda}=-\xi^{*}e^{B(\lambda)}=-\xi^{*}\text{sech}^{2}(r\lambda)
.

C(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}-\xi^{*}\text{sech}^{2}(r\lambda^{\prime})d\lambda^{\prime}=-\xi^{*}\left(\frac{1}{r}\tanh(r \lambda')\right)\Big|_{0}^{\lambda}=-\frac{\xi^{*}}{r}\tanh(r \lambda)
.

At the end of the evolution,
\lambda=1
:

\displaystyle A(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi}{r}\tanh(r)=e^{i\theta}\tanh(r)\equiv\alpha

(70)

\displaystyle B(1)

\displaystyle=-2\ln(\cosh r)=\ln(\text{sech}^2 r)=\ln(1 - \tanh^2 r)=\ln(1 - |\alpha|^2)

(71)

\displaystyle C(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\xi^{*}}{r}\tanh(r)=-e^{-i\theta}\tanh(r)=-\alpha^{*}

(72)

Substituting these
A(1),B(1),C(1)
back into the ansatz (
58
) gives:

\displaystyle S_{2}(\xi)=\exp\{\alpha K_{+}\big\}\,\exp\{\ln(1 - |\alpha|^2)K_{0}\big\}\,\exp\{-\alpha^{*}K_{-}\big\},

(73)

which exponentiates to the final form:

\displaystyle S_{2}(\xi)=\exp\{\alpha K_{+}\big\}\,(1-|\alpha|^{2})^{K_{0}}\,\exp\{-\alpha^{*}K_{-}\big\}.

(74)

This completes the proof.
