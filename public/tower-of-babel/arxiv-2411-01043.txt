Humidity-enhanced NO 2 \text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} gas sensing using atomically sharp edges in multilayer MoS 2 \text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
Abhay V. Agrawal, Alexander Yu. Polyakov, Jens Eriksson, Tomasz J. Antosiewicz, Timur O. Shegai

Abstract
Ambient humidity poses a significant challenge in the development of practical room temperature NO 2 \text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} gas sensors. Here, we employ atomically precise zigzag edges in multilayer MoS 2 \text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} , fabricated using electron beam lithography and anisotropic wet etching, to achieve highly sensitive and selective gas sensing performance that is humidity-tolerant at elevated temperatures and humidity-enhanced at room temperature under ultraviolet illumination. Notably, exposure to 2.5 parts per billion (ppb) NO 2 \text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} at 70% relative humidity under ultraviolet illumination and at room temperature resulted in a 33-fold increase in response and a 6-fold faster recovery compared to 0% relative humidity, leading to response values exceeding 1100%. The optimized samples demonstrated a theoretical detection limit ranging from 4 to 400 parts per trillion (ppt) NO 2 \text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} . The enhanced NO 2 \text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} sensing capabilities of MoS 2 \text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} edges have been further confirmed through first-principles calculations. Our study expands the applications of nanostructured MoS 2 \text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} and highlights its potential for detecting NO 2 \text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} at sub-ppb levels in complex scenarios, such as high humidity conditions.

Introduction

Detecting
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is crucial in modern society, as it is a common industrial byproduct of any high-temperature combustion
in nitrogen containing environments
Atkinson (2000)
;
Vineis et al. (2004)
.
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
interacts with water and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) to contribute to the formation of acid rain and photochemical smog. Prolonged exposure to even low concentrations, such as 1 part per million (ppm) of
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, can lead to the development of chronic bronchitis, respiratory-related diseases, and ocular discomfort
Vineis et al. (2004)
;
Schwela (2000)
. Furthermore,
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is extensively used in the production of nitric acid, a substance that finds a widespread use
Hodge (1994)
. A pulmonary condition, known as asthma, is often associated with elevated levels of nitric oxides (
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and NO) in the exhaled breath. While the exhaled nitric oxide (NO) concentration typically ranges from 20 to 25 parts per billion (ppb) in healthy individuals, those with asthma exhibit elevated levels ranging from 35 to 50 ppb, regardless of age
Korn et al. (2020)
. Recognizing these health risks, the United States Environmental Protection Agency (U.S. EPA) has set a regulated limit for exposure to
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
at 53 ppb
Esworthy and McCarthy (2013)
. Consequently, there is a pressing need for reliable sensors capable of detecting minute concentrations of
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
below the regulatory threshold
Esworthy and McCarthy (2013)
;
Guarnieri and Balmes (2014)
.

Various methods have been employed to detect
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
at ppb levels using
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
.
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is a semiconducting material exhibiting a thickness-dependent bandgap (1.2-1.8 eV), high surface-to-volume ratio, a wide spectrum of light absorption from ultraviolet (UV) to near-infrared (near-IR), as well as excellent catalytic and gas sensing properties
Splendiani et al. (2010)
;
Radisavljevic et al. (2011)
;
Jaramillo et al. (2007)
. Edges of
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
have attracted research interest as favorable sites for gas adsorption, surpassing their basal plane counterparts. This phenomenon stems from the existence of vacancies (and other defects) supporting high binding energies and charge transfer values for target gas molecules
Yue et al. (2013)
;
Cho et al. (2015)
;
Agrawal et al. (2018)
. It has been observed both theoretically and experimentally that the adsorption of
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
molecules is favorable at
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
edges in comparison to other gases such as
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, CO,
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}

Yue et al. (2013)
;
Cho et al. (2015)
;
Agrawal et al. (2018)
. Various advanced methods for improved gas sensing using
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
include decoration of the latter with metal nanoparticles (NPs), fabricating van der Waals heterostructures, and morphology engineering. For example, Kim
et al.
functionalized a solution-based
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
surface with different noble metal NPs
Kim et al. (2023)
. The selective character of
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensors may be modified by doping with metal NPs in order to target a specific gas. Long
et al.
proposed a
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/graphene heterostructure for ultrasensitive
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
detection. At room temperature (RT), this hybrid structure demonstrated a 50 ppb
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
detection
Long et al. (2016)
. A notable advantage of
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
heterostructures is their rapid charge separation, which has the potential to develop gas sensors with fast response and recovery times. Although these strategies could pave the way to developing ultra-sensitive and selective
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensors, both methods introduce fabrication complexity in device manufacturing. Furthermore, the use of noble metal NPs is potentially costly.

Recently, gas sensors based on edge-enriched
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
have emerged as a promising prospect. Edge-enriched
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
offers a higher surface area and more active edge sites. Real-world devices and applications such as transistors, catalysts, photodetectors, and gas sensors based on nanopatterned transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), especially
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, can experience significant improvements in their properties through edge enrichment
Shim et al. (2023)
;
Bala et al. (2022)
;
Sen et al. (2023)
;
Park et al. (2021)
;
Munkhbat et al. (2023)
;
Cho et al. (2015)
. Cho
et al.
compared gas adsorption on three distinct morphologies of
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(horizontally aligned (HA)
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, vertically aligned (VA)
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and a mixture of HA and VA
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) grown by the combination of radio frequency (RF) sputtering and rapid sulfurization
Cho et al. (2015)
. The authors showed theoretically that zigzag edges in
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
provide an 18 times higher binding energy for
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
molecules adsorption compared to the basal plane. The experimental and theoretical investigations indicated that edge-enriched
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
has a superior sensitivity for
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
gas molecules. Agrawal
et al.
proposed chemical vapor deposition (CVD) grown mixed in-plane and edge-enriched
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
flakes
Agrawal et al. (2018)
. The proposed structures showed a selective and sensitive nature for
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensing at room temperature. Li
et al.
used the hydrothermal method to prepare hollow, solid, and smooth
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanospheres. The sensing area for
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
was doubled, and the response was enhanced 3.1 times in hollow
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
in comparison to smooth nanospheres. Edges in
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
can be either zigzag or armchair, among which the zigzag edges are thermodynamically more stable and most promising for gas molecule adsorption
Xiao et al. (2016)
. However, in most morphology-driven
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensors, it is challenging to accurately control the precise edge orientation.

The top-down nanopatterning can substantially modify the structural, optical, and electrical characteristics TMDs materials. These properties are strongly influenced by changes in the atomic-scale structure and defects
Munkhbat et al. (2020)
;
Jessen et al. (2019)
;
Munkhbat et al. (2023)
. Numerous methods, such as atomic force lithography, plasma treatment, block copolymer lithography (BCP), thermal annealing, laser writing, and seed-assisted growth, have been used previously
Liu et al. (2023)
;
Cao et al. (2013)
;
Enrico et al. (2023)
;
Chang et al. (2023)
;
Way et al. (2018)
;
Yu et al. (2011)
. However, these approaches lead to reduced accuracy and sharpness, random orientation, uneven patterning, complex fabrication stages, and limited control over the size and shape of the nanostructures. In seed-assisted patterning, for instance, the resulting TMD materials may exhibit random orientation and face a continuous risk of contamination from seeds throughout the growth process
Way et al. (2018)
;
Li et al. (2018)
. The laser writing and thermal annealing methods potentially eliminate the necessity for masks and photoresists, simplifying the nanopattern design
Ryu Cho et al. (2017)
;
Liu et al. (2020)
. Similarly, BCP lithography has emerged as a promising strategy for nanopatterning. However, in all these processes, the patterns are typically randomly orientated, not precisely controlled, disordered, and contain structural defects
Park et al. (2021)
.

In addition to the aforementioned challenges, it is important to recognize that the relative humidity (RH) commonly present in the atmosphere poses a significant obstacle to the development of real-time gas sensors using semiconducting materials. The water concentration in ambient air (atmospheric pressure, 25
∘
C) is 6,280 ppm at 20% RH and 25,740 ppm at 80% RH levels, respectively
Yoon et al. (2016)
. Moreover, in real-life scenarios, the humidity levels are strongly dependent on environmental conditions, such as wind, rainfall, temperature, as well as day-night and seasonal oscillations. At room temperature, water adsorption leads to significant fluctuations in baseline resistance with variations in humidity
Reddeppa et al. (2020)
;
Wang et al. (2019)
;
Chen et al. (2021)
. Additionally, exhaled human breath contains substantial RH levels, which presents a challenge for reliable chemiresistive gas sensor operation. A commonly employed approach to overcome these challenges is elevated temperatures. Furthermore, various approaches to develop humidity-tolerant chemiresistive gas sensors, such as doping with metal NPs (Pd, Pt, Ag, Au), element doping (Ce, Pr, and Tb), integration with hydrophobic materials (polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), prepared polyaniline (PANI), graphite nanoflakes)
etc.
have been previously employed
Ma et al. (2015)
;
Wang et al. (2012)
;
Yang et al. (2020)
;
Qin et al. (2021)
;
Yoon et al. (2016)
;
Kim et al. (2019)
;
Li et al. (2020)
;
Maity and Kumar (2018)
. Although such methods improved the anti-humidity ability of the respective gas sensors, each mentioned approach added complexity, hindering their practical use.

Here, we report sub-ppb-level
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensors based on nanopatterned multilayer
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
exposing precise atomically sharp zigzag edges. The nanopatterned
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
resulted in honeycomb nanomesh structures fabricated by optimizing the process reported earlier
Munkhbat et al. (2020)
. The method provides a uniform, precise, and controllable mesh of
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanoribbons with widths ranging from a few hundred nanometers (nm) to sub ten nm (see Figure
1
b-d). We studied the concentrations of
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
gas in the range of 2.5 – 10 ppb, below the threshold limit set by the U.S. EPA. The density of exposed edges is proportional to the hexagonal holes’ density and their dimensions. We systematically studied the density-dependent
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensing in several honeycomb
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomesh samples. The
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
response was best at 200
∘
C in the optimized honeycomb
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomesh structures due to numerous exposed, uniform, precise zigzag edges, combined with optimal electrical performance. The studied samples demonstrated selective
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensing (against CO,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}
) and demonstrated an extraordinary limit of detection ranging from 4 to 400 ppt, depending on the exact mode of operation. In addition, we investigated the room temperature operation of our sensors under high RH levels (up to 70%) with and without UV illumination. Surprisingly, at RT the sensors showed a significantly enhanced response to
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
in a high-humidity environment. Under UV illumination, this humidity-enhanced response exceeds 1100% upon exposure to 2.5 ppb
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. Importantly, the humidity-enhanced sensing behavior is universal and was observed for other tested gases, including CO,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}
, albeit at much lower response levels than for
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. This universal humidity-enhanced behavior suggests that the mechanism of the enhanced sensor performance is also universal, and likely involves competition between
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
adsorbed on the
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
surface under the influence of UV illumination. Our density functional theory (DFT) calculations confirm that
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
adsorption is significantly influenced by the presence of edges and sulfur vacancies. Thus, the honeycomb
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomesh samples, studied here, offer an optimal configuration, featuring a high density of sulfur vacancy sites along the multilayer hexagon rims. This unique structure enhances the
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
adsorption capacity, making the honeycomb
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomesh particularly effective for chemiresistive gas sensing applications in high humidity conditions.

To understand the role of zigzag edges in honeycomb nanomeshes for

R(t)=R_{0}\exp\left(-k_{\text{des}}t\right),

(1)

R(t)=R_{\text{max}}\frac{C_{a}K}{1+C_{a}K}\left(1-\exp\left[-\left(\frac{1+C_{a}K}{K}\right)k_{\text{ads}}t\right]\right).

(2)

and
k_{\text{des}}

K

k_{\text{ads}}
and
k_{\text{des}}

C_{a}

\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

2.2.5
Discussion of sensing mechanism

In this study, we employed
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
as the active sensing material for the detection of
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
.
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomeshes were fabricated using flakes mechanically exfoliated from a commercial
n
-type 2H-
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
crystal
HQ ()
. Due to dry and wet etching, the resulting nanomeshes acquire a certain level of
p
-doping, which does not change the overall
n
-type nature of
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. The operational principle underlying the detection of
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
involves the perturbation of charge carriers (hence, resistance) in
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
due to the surface charge transfer interactions between the
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
molecules. The
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
concentration, flow rate, and the
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
surface stoichiometry influence the sensor’s resistance. The interaction between
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
varies under different operating conditions. Therefore, the sensing mechanism involves a complex interplay of physical and chemical processes, making it challenging to define precisely. Given this complexity, a comprehensive understanding of the sensing mechanism requires further investigation beyond the scope of this study. In this section, we briefly summarize the critical aspects of
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomeshes sensing behavior and suggest directions for future research. Detailed discussion is provided in the SI (section ”Sensing mechanism”).

The oxidative wet etching and environmental factors (gas atmosphere, humidity, temperature, light) significantly alter the
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
surface, potentially capping edges and sulfur vacancies with oxygen molecules or even forming O-defect sites. The nanomesh
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
devices (H1, H2, and H3) feature an abundance of edge sites, possibly capped with oxygen. Enhanced
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
adsorption and charge transfer at the
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
edges, particularly in the honeycomb H2, improve device response, affecting resistance even at single-digit ppb
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
levels.

In dry air and in the dark, molecular oxygen likely dominates the surfaces of all proposed devices, thereby diminishing the adsorption sites for
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. Despite this, the 2.5 ppb
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
response in the patterned device H2 reaches 8%, exceeding the 4% value for the unpatterned device H0. Additionally, the strong binding between
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
leads to incomplete recovery at room temperature in the dark, as illustrated in Figure S9.

To achieve efficient and reversible
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
detection, we first raise the temperature to 200
∘
C.
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
has higher binding energy to
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
than
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, so the elevated temperature facilitates the removal of oxygen adsorbed on the surface and creates more available sites for
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
adsorption. At 200
∘
C,
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomeshes show a significantly higher response than the unpatterned device H0, indicating that the edge sites are advantageous for
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensing. Moreover, the elevated temperature allows for full sensor recovery, which is not possible at room temperature.

In dry air, our second approach leverages UV illumination to both remove the adsorbed oxygen from the surface and enhance
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
adsorption. The photogenerated electron-hole pairs are crucial in modulating the adsorption and desorption thermodynamics and kinetics of both O
2
and
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
molecules. This effect is especially pronounced in the
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomesh H2, which exhibits higher
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensitivity than its H0 counterpart. Therefore, UV illumination is critical for amplifying
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
detection.

In dark and highly humid environments, the
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensing mechanism becomes complex, mainly due to the significant influence of water vapor, as elaborated in the SI. The presence of water vapor alters
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
detection through interactions with surface-bound hydroxyl ions (OH
-
) and protons (H
+
). These interactions may lead to the formation of surface-bound nitrate ions (NO
3
-
(ads)), which are characterized by their elevated binding energy and enhanced charge transfer on
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

Yao et al. (2011)
;
Han et al. (2019)
. This process enhances the ability to detect
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
in humidity-rich conditions, even in the dark.

The most compelling results of our investigation were achieved under high humidity and UV illumination at RT. Under these conditions, the
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomesh H2 device demonstrated a universal enhanced sensing response across all tested gases, including
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, CH
4
, H
2
, CO, and C
2
H
6
. In contrast, the unpatterned device H0 showed degraded performance when exposed to humidity, highlighting the exceptional role of zigzag edges in the H2 device’s enhanced gas sensing capabilities. This universal behavior suggests that the adsorption and charge transfer of all gases is higher in the presence of humidity for
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomesh device H2. Furthermore, it implies that the nanomesh surface has the most available sites for gas molecule adsorption.

While the precise mechanism behind the enhanced sensing performance remains elusive, we hypothesize that the synergy between humidity and UV light optimally cleans the surface. Specifically, UV illumination plays a dual role, removing adsorbed oxygen and preventing the accumulation of surface-bound hydroxyl ions (OH
-
) and protons (H
+
). This hypothesis is supported by the observed stable baseline resistance even at high RH levels at RT. Therefore, the exceptionally clean surface of our
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomesh H2 device significantly enhances its sensing response across all tested gases. Additionally, we can not rule out the possibility of enhanced adsorption and charge transfer between the exposed gas molecules and
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
nanomesh H2 surface in the presence of adsorbed water. The enhanced
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
adsorption and charge transfer in the presence of humidity has been previously reported using DFT
Azizi et al. (2020)
.

2.3
Conclusion

In conclusion, we have successfully demonstrated the fabrication of controlled, uniform, and sharp zigzag edge enriched hexagons
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. We developed room-temperature ultra-sensitive, ultra-selective, and humidity-enhanced
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
gas sensors. The sensor response to 2.5 ppb
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
reaches 99% at T=200
∘
C and 1120% at room temperature under UV illumination with 70% relative humidity. We showed that honeycomb
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
exhibits superior
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensing capability due to the high number of zigzag edges, as supported by DFT calculations. Our devices achieved an impressive parts-per-trillion detection limit even at high relative humidity. Our work lays the foundation for developing nanostructured
\text{MoS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
as real-life
\text{NO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sensors operable at room temperature, under UV light illumination, and in harsh humidity conditions.

2.4.1
Electrical Contacts Fabrication

The

The flakes with substrates were first covered with a thick layer of 300 nm of 950 PMMA A4 photoresist (MicroChem, USA) using the spin coating technique. The e-beam lithography was employed using the JEOL JBX 9300FS lithographer at 100 kV accelerating voltage and 30 nA current. The lithographically patterned design was developed using 1:3 MIBK:IPA mixture for 2 min and 20 s. The Cr/Au (5/200 nm)metals were evaporated by Lesker PVD 225 e-beam evaporation system (Kurt J. Lesker Company, Germany), followed by overnight liftoff in acetone.

6\times 4

added to the vicinity of the defect (or, respectively, where it would be) and relaxed until the forces were less than 0.02 eV/Å.
