Co-endemicity of Opisthorchis viverrini and Strongyloides stercoralis infections: evidence from stool examination and serology screening in Champasak Province, Southern Lao People’s Democratic Republic
Authors: Rutchanee Rodpai, Patcharaporn Boonroumkaew, Lakkhana Sadaow, Sakhone Laymanivong, Oranuch Sanpool, Chairat Tantrawatpan, Weerachai Saijuntha, Tongjit Thanchomnang, Vilasack Banouvong, Pewpan M. Intapan, Wanchai Maleewong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Helminth infections remain a major public health problem in the Lao People’s Democratic Republic (Lao PDR), particularly in rural communities where foodborne trematodes and soil-transmitted helminths are co-endemic. Opisthorchis viverrini is a well-established risk factor for cholangiocarcinoma, while Strongyloides stercoralis can cause severe and potentially fatal disease. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and patterns of helminth infections in southern Lao PDR using combined parasitological and serological diagnostic approaches. A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 242 adults residing in three rural villages in Champasak Province, southern Lao PDR. Stool samples were examined using the formalin–ethyl acetate concentration technique (FECT), and point-of-care immunochromatographic tests (ICTs) were applied to detect IgG antibodies against O. viverrini and S. stercoralis. Overall, 69.0% of participants were infected with at least one helminth species. O. viverrini was the most prevalent parasite, detected in 51.7% by FECT and 65.3% by ICT. Other common infections included minute intestinal flukes (20.2%), hookworms (14.9%), S. stercoralis (9.9% by FECT; 14.5% by ICT), Trichostrongylus spp. (9.1%), and Taenia spp. (6.2%). Polyparasitism was frequent, with 9.1% harboring three or more helminth species. The estimated true prevalences, based on combined results from stool examination and serological testing, were 66.6% for opisthorchiasis and 13.5% for strongyloidiasis. ICT identified substantially more infections than stool examination alone for both O. viverrini and S. stercoralis. Serological ICT screening offers a sensitive, practical complement to parasitological methods. Integrated diagnostics are essential for improved surveillance and targeted interventions in resource-limited endemic areas. Introduction

Opisthorchiasis and strongyloidiasis are the major contributors to neglected tropical diseases in low- and middle-income countries, disproportionately affecting rural populations in tropical and subtropical regions of Southeast Asia, especially in the Great Mekong Subregion (GMS) countries, i.e., Thailand, Cambodia, Myanmar, China, Vietnam, and the Lao People’s Democratic Republic (Lao PDR) (Sota et al. 2024; Gordon et al. 2024). Despite decades of control efforts, foodborne and soil-transmitted helminths continue to impose a substantial burden of chronic morbidity, disability, and premature mortality, reinforcing the cycle of poverty and health inequity (Utzinger et al. 2012; Hotez et al. 2014). In Lao PDR, helminthiases remain highly prevalent and are dominated by both foodborne trematodes (FBTs) and soil-transmitted helminths (STHs) (Forrer et al. 2012, 2015; Eom et al. 2014; Laymanivong et al. 2014). Among FBTs, Opisthorchis viverrini is of particular public health concern in the GMS, including Thailand, Lao PDR, Cambodia, and southern Vietnam, where transmission is strongly associated with the traditional consumption of raw or undercooked freshwater fish containing infective metacercariae (Sripa et al. 2010). In southern Lao PDR, especially in Champasak Province, O. viverrini coexists with hookworms (Ancylostoma/Necator spp.) and other intestinal nematodes, resulting in a high burden of helminth infections (Forrer et al. 2012, 2015). Chronic opisthorchiasis induces hepatobiliary inflammation and periductal fibrosis and is a well-established risk factor for cholangiocarcinoma, a highly lethal bile duct cancer with exceptionally high incidence in northeastern Thailand; accordingly, the parasite is classified by the World Health Organization as a Group 1 carcinogen (Sripa and Pairojkul 2008; Sithithaworn et al. 2012; IARC 2012; Brindley et al. 2021). Although national control programs have reduced infection intensity in some areas, O. viverrini infection remains endemic in many rural communities, emphasizing the need for improved surveillance and diagnostic strategies (Sripa et al. 2010; Saengsawang et al. 2016; Mationg et al. 2024).

Meanwhile, STHs remain prevalent in the region. For example, in Champasak Province, a survey found a hookworm prevalence of 48.8% using duplicate Kato–Katz smears, with most infections being of light intensity (Forrer et al. 2015). A survey using agar plate culture and Kato-Katz methods across three provinces in Lao PDR (Luang Prabang in the north, Khammouane in the center, and Champasak in the south) found an overall S. stercoralis prevalence of 41.0% (Laymanivong et al. 2016). Strongyloides stercoralis, an STH, represents another neglected but clinically important parasite in tropical regions. Unlike other helminths, S. stercoralis is capable of autoinfection, allowing infections to persist for decades if untreated (Nutman 2017). While many infections are asymptomatic, strongyloidiasis can lead to fatal hyperinfection or disseminated disease in immunocompromised individuals (Krolewiecki and Nutman 2019; Buonfrate et al. 2013). The true global prevalence of S. stercoralis is believed to be underestimated, largely due to diagnostic limitations and the low sensitivity of conventional stool-based methods (Schär et al. 2013; Bisoffi et al. 2013; Buonfrate et al. 2020). These methods are complicated, time-consuming, and require skilled microscopists. Furthermore, the intermittent shedding of larvae in feces necessitates multiple stool examinations (Krolewiecki and Nutman 2019).

Accurate diagnosis is central to effective control and elimination of helminth infections. Conventional parasitological techniques, such as the formalin–ethyl acetate concentration technique (FECT), remain commonly used because of their low cost and feasibility in field settings (Buonfrate et al. 2015). However, these methods are limited by relatively low sensitivity, particularly for light-intensity infections, and their accuracy depends heavily on the expertise of trained personnel for proper sample processing and microscopic identification. Therefore, serological assays such as rapid immunochromatographic tests (ICTs) have emerged as valuable complementary tools. These tests detect parasite-specific antibodies and may improve case detection at the population level (Sadaow et al. 2022; Boonroumkaew et al. 2025). In endemic settings, polyparasitism is common, with individuals frequently harboring multiple helminth species simultaneously. Mixed infections may exacerbate morbidity, complicate diagnosis, and undermine the effectiveness of parasite-specific control programs (Pullan and Brooker 2012; Hürlimann et al. 2014). Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and patterns of helminth infections in rural communities using both stool-based parasitological and point-of-care (POC) serological methods.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee for Human Research, Khon Kaen University, Thailand, (Human Ethics number: HE664044, approved November 30, 2023) and by the National Ethics Committee for Health Research, Ministry of Health, Lao People’s Democratic Republic (Human Ethics number: 95/NECHR). All procedures complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants prior to enrollment after a detailed explanation of the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. Participation was voluntary, and all personally identifiable information was removed from the dataset to ensure confidentiality. At the end of the study, all individuals who were found to be infected with any helminth species were treated with appropriate anthelminthic medications in accordance with the national treatment guidelines of the Ministry of Health, Vientiane, Lao PDR.

Study design, setting, and participants

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Champasak Province, southern Lao PDR, a helminth-endemic area along the Mekong River. Three rural villages, Samanh (15°10’53.5"N, 105°41’35.3"E), Nonghoy (15°03’29.4"N, 105°49’46.6"E), and Salao (15°04’19.0"N, 105°49’26.8"E), were purposively selected based on accessibility and prior epidemiological evidence of helminth transmission (Laymanivong et al. 2014; Vonghachack et al. 2015). All individuals aged ≥ 18 years residing in the study villages were invited to participate. A total of 242 participants were enrolled. Demographic information, including age and sex, was collected using a standardized questionnaire administered by trained field personnel (Table 1 and Supplementary File 1).

Collection and processing of stool and blood samples

Participants were provided with labeled stool containers and instructed to submit a fresh stool specimen on the same day as blood collection. Stool samples were preserved in 10% formalin and transported to the laboratory for parasitological analysis. Helminth infections were identified using the FECT (Elkins et al. 1986). Following FECT processing, the sediment was resuspended for microscopic examination. For each sample, two drops of the sediment were prepared on separate slides and mixed with either 1% iodine or normal saline solution (0.85% NaCl). Each slide was systematically examined under a light microscope using the 40× objective lens (400× total magnification), and the entire smear area was scanned. All helminth eggs and larvae were identified and counted, and results were recorded for each sample. Microscopic examination was performed by two experienced laboratory technicians, each with more than five years of microscopy experience, and all findings were confirmed by two trained parasitologists, also with more than five years of microscopy experience. Morphological criteria were applied to differentiate O. viverrini eggs from minute intestinal fluke eggs and to distinguish hookworm eggs from those of Trichostrongylus spp. Opisthorchis viverrini eggs were characterized by rough eggshells and prominent shoulders, whereas minute intestinal fluke eggs (heterophyid and lecithodendriid flukes) exhibited smooth eggshells (Tesana et al. 1991) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Trichostrongylus eggs are larger than those of hookworm, being slightly more elongated, with more pointed ends and thicker walls (Beaver et al. 1984) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Capillary blood was obtained by finger prick using sterile, single-use lancets. Whole blood samples were used immediately in the field for immunochromatographic testing without further processing.

Immunochromatographic assays

Serological screening for opisthorchiasis was conducted using a POC immunochromatographic test (named opisthorchiasis blood immunochromatographic test; Ov-ICT) designed to detect O. viverrini-specific IgG antibodies in whole blood samples (Sadaow et al. 2022). Detection of strongyloidiasis was performed using a whole-blood immunochromatographic test targeting S. stercoralis-specific IgG antibodies (named strongyloidiasis blood immunochromatographic test; Ss-ICT) (Boonroumkaew et al. 2025). The dilution factors used for Ov-ICT (1:10) and Ss-ICT (1:20) were based on the optimal conditions reported in previous studies (Sadaow et al. 2022; Boonroumkaew et al. 2025). For Ov-ICT, each capillary blood sample was diluted 1:10 (5 µL of blood sample plus 45 µL of running buffer), then 5 µL of the diluted blood sample was applied to the sample well, followed by 60 µL of running buffer added to the buffer well.

For Ss-ICT, each capillary blood sample was diluted 1:20 with buffer, after which 5 µL of the diluted sample was added to the sample well, followed by 60 µL of running buffer added to the buffer well.

For both assays, results were interpreted visually at 15 min after sample addition (Supplementary Fig. 2). All samples were tested in duplicate, yielding identical results. A positive result was defined by the presence of both a test line and a control line, whereas a negative result was indicated by the control line only. Tests lacking a visible control line were classified as invalid.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables, including sex, age groups, and diagnostic test results (e.g., ICT and FECT results), were reported as frequencies and percentages. Age was treated as a continuous variable and summarized as a mean, and its normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The prevalence of helminth infections was calculated for each parasite species. Seroprevalence was calculated as the proportion of ICT-positive individuals within each age group. Agreement between FECT and ICT results for the detection of O. viverrini and S. stercoralis was evaluated using McNemar’s test and Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata software version 18.1 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA), with statistical significance defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. A Bayesian latent class model was used to estimate the true prevalence and diagnostic accuracy of the two tests in the absence of a gold standard, assuming a multinomial likelihood based on cross-classified results (Gonçalves et al. 2012; Assefa et al. 2014; Easton et al. 2016). Beta prior distributions were specified for sensitivity and specificity based on prior knowledge, while a non-informative Beta (1,1) prior was used for prevalence. Posterior estimates were obtained using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling implemented in JAGS via the rjags package in R (version 4.5.3), with three chains, 5,000 burn-in iterations, and 10,000 iterations for inference.

Results

A total of 242 participants from three villages were enrolled in this study: Samanh (n = 134), Nonghoy (n = 36), and Salao (n = 72) (Table 1). Overall, males accounted for 44.2% of participants, while females represented 55.8%. The mean age of the study population was 53 ± 13 years (range: 18–93 years). At least one parasitic infection was detected in 69.0% (167/242) of participants. Opisthorchis viverrini was the most prevalent parasite, detected in 51.7% of participants, followed by minute intestinal flukes (20.2%), hookworm (14.9%), Strongyloides stercoralis (9.9%), Trichostrongylus spp. (9.1%), and Taenia spp. (6.2%). The prevalence of parasitic infections varied across villages. O. viverrini infection was most prevalent in Salao village (59.7%), followed by Nonghoy village (58.3%), and Samanh village (45.5%). In addition, parasite infection intensity was assessed based on parasite counts per gram of feces. Results of stool examinations using the FECT and serological screening using ICT are summarized in Table 2. Using FECT, 125 participants (51.7%) were positive for O. viverrini, while O. viverrini-specific IgG antibodies were detected in 158 participants (65.3%) by ICT. For S. stercoralis, FECT detected infection in 24 participants (9.9%), while ICT identified 35 participants (14.5%) as seropositive. Among participants with negative FECT results (n = 75), 36 (14.9%) were seropositive for O. viverrini IgG antibodies and 8 (3.3%) for S. stercoralis IgG antibodies by ICT, suggesting potential past or occult infections undetected by stool examination.

Mixed infections were common in this study population. Dual infections involving O. viverrini were frequently observed, particularly co-infection with minute intestinal flukes (14.9%) and hookworm (8.3%). Co-infection with O. viverrini and S. stercoralis was detected in 6.2% of participants. Additionally, 9.1% of participants harbored mixed infections with three or more parasite species (Table 2). Age-specific seroprevalence of ICT is presented in Table 3. Seropositivity for O. viverrini remained consistently high across all age groups, ranging from 60.0% to 67.6%. No clear increasing trend with age was observed. Similarly, S. stercoralis seroprevalence varied across age groups (0–20.6%) without a consistent age-dependent pattern.

Comparison between FECT and ICT for the detection of O. viverrini demonstrated a significant difference between the two methods (McNemar’s test, P < 0.001). The methods showed moderate concordance (κ-value = 0.424, P < 0.001) with 71.49% overall agreement (Table 4). ICT detected 65.3% more positive cases than FECT (158 vs. 125). Bayesian latent class analysis estimated the true prevalence of opisthorchiasis at 66.6% (95% credible interval (CrI): 49.7–77.4). The sensitivity of Ov-ICT (91.2%) was higher than that of FECT (73.6%), whereas the specificity of FECT (89.7%) was slightly higher than that of Ov-ICT (86.5%). For S. stercoralis, no statistically significant difference was observed between FECT and ICT (McNemar’s test, P = 0.093), though overall agreement was 82.23%. Cohen’s kappa was 0.174 (P = 0.003) (Table 5). However, ICT detected 14.5% more positive cases than FECT (35 vs. 24). Bayesian estimates indicated a true prevalence of strongyloidiasis of 13.5% (95% CrI: 3.3–28.5). The sensitivity of Ss-ICT (76.3%) was markedly higher than that of FECT (30.9%), while both methods showed high specificity (FECT: 93.1%; Ss-ICT: 94.5%).

Discussion

Lao PDR remains one of the countries with a high burden of helminth infections, where polyparasitism involving both FBTs and STHs is common, particularly in rural communities along the Mekong River (Sayasone et al. 2009; Kounnavong et al. 2011; Pullan and Brooker 2012). We conducted a cross-sectional investigation in rural villages of Champasak Province, southern Lao PDR, to determine the prevalence of O. viverrini and STH infections. Stool microscopy and POC serological screening were applied in parallel, allowing comparison of the diagnostic performance and agreement between FECT and ICT for the detection of O. viverrini and S. stercoralis, as well as characterization of mixed infections. Additionally, the prevalence of other intestinal helminths identified through stool examination was documented, providing evidence to support improved surveillance strategies and integrated control approaches in helminth-endemic settings. In the present study conducted in rural villages of Champasak Province, southern Lao PDR, 69% of participants had at least one helminth infection, confirming the persistent endemicity of multiple helminth species in this region. O. viverrini was the most prevalent parasite, followed by minute intestinal flukes, hookworms, S. stercoralis, and other intestinal nematodes, consistent with previous surveys from southern Lao PDR (Sripa et al. 2010; Sithithaworn et al. 2012; Laymanivong et al. 2014; Chan et al. 2023).

Using the FECT, we detected a high prevalence of O. viverrini (51.7%), together with substantial levels of other helminths, including minute intestinal flukes (20.2%), hookworms (14.9%), S. stercoralis (9.9%), and Trichostrongylus spp. (9.1%) (Table 1). These findings reflect continued exposure to multiple transmission pathways related to raw fish consumption, poor sanitation, and close contact with animals in rural settings (Sripa et al. 2010; Sithithaworn et al. 2012). The persistence of opisthorchiasis is particularly concerning given its established role in hepatobiliary inflammation, periductal fibrosis, and the development of cholangiocarcinoma (Sripa and Pairojkul 2008; IARC 2012).

Serological screening using ICT kits revealed higher prevalences of opisthorchiasis (65.3% vs. 51.7% by FECT) and strongyloidiasis (14.5% vs. 9.9% by FECT) compared with stool examination. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that serological methods offer higher sensitivity for detecting O. viverrini and S. stercoralis infections, particularly in endemic areas where light or chronic infections are common (Sultana et al. 2012; Bisoffi et al. 2013; Phupiewkham et al. 2021). Analysis of age-specific seroprevalence showed that O. viverrini seropositivity remained consistently high across all age groups, with no clear increasing trend with age. This pattern suggests sustained, lifelong exposure within endemic communities, rather than cumulative, age-dependent infection alone. The findings indicate that exposure likely begins early in life, driven by the persistent practice of consuming raw or undercooked freshwater fish that is deeply embedded in traditional food culture in Lao PDR (Saengsawang et al. 2016; Araki et al. 2018; Phupiewkham et al. 2021). Similarly, S. stercoralis seroprevalence varied across age groups without a consistent age-related pattern, which is in line with previous studies indicating persistent transmission and chronic infection in endemic areas (Schär et al. 2013; Buonfrate et al. 2020). These findings suggest ongoing transmission and support the need for community-wide control strategies rather than age-targeted interventions, consistent with the continued endemicity and public health importance of strongyloidiasis in the region (Laymanivong et al. 2016). The moderate agreement observed between FECT and ICT for O. viverrini detection suggests that reliance on stool microscopy alone may underestimate the true burden of infection (Table 4). This is further supported by the Bayesian latent class analysis, which estimated a higher true prevalence (66.6%) and demonstrated greater sensitivity of Ov-ICT compared with FECT. For S. stercoralis, no statistically significant difference was observed. The overall agreement (82.23%) likely reflects the low prevalence, with most participants testing negatively by both methods (Table 5). However, latent class estimates indicated substantially higher sensitivity of Ss-ICT than FECT, despite similar specificity.

For opisthorchiasis, the higher positivity rate by ICT than FECT may be explained by low O. viverrini egg output in the stool of lightly infected individuals or those with bile duct obstruction, which limits the sensitivity of stool-based methods (Sawangsoda et al. 2012). In addition, individuals living in highly endemic areas may develop partial immunological resistance to chronic infection, resulting in egg-negative but seropositive profiles (Akai et al. 1994). In such cases, detection of serum IgG antibodies may be particularly useful for identifying infected or previously exposed individuals at risk of long-term hepatobiliary complications. This is consistent with the higher sensitivity observed for Ov-ICT in the present study. Conversely, some FECT-positive but ICT-negative cases may represent early or acute infections, during which antibody responses have not yet fully developed (Sadaow et al. 2022; Rodpai et al. 2025).

Similarly, for strongyloidiasis, ICT detected substantially more infections than stool examination. This is consistent with the biology of S. stercoralis, which is characterized by low parasite burden and intermittent larval excretion, leading to frequent false-negative stool examinations when only a single specimen is analyzed (Siddiqui and Berk 2001). In contrast, serological assays can identify chronic carriers through detection of persistent IgG responses (Buonfrate et al. 2013; Bisoffi et al. 2013). This aligns with the markedly higher sensitivity of Ss-ICT observed in this study. The few stool-positive but seronegative cases may reflect early infections or impaired immune responses, such as in immunocompromised individuals (Uchikawa et al. 1991; Seet et al. 2005). The lack of strong concordance (κ-value = 0.174) between stool-based prevalence and seroprevalence suggests that the two methods identify different infection states (active larval shedding vs. antibody response), supporting their complementary use in epidemiological surveillance.

Polyparasitism was frequently observed in this study, with common co-infections involving O. viverrini and minute intestinal flukes, hookworms, and S. stercoralis. Additionally, 9.1% of participants harbored infections with three or more parasite species. Such polyparasitism reflects overlapping ecological and behavioral risk factors and may exacerbate morbidity, complicate diagnosis, and undermine parasite-specific control strategies (Pullan and Brooker 2012; Hürlimann et al. 2014). These findings underscore the importance of integrated, multispecies approaches to helminth control in endemic areas.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes assessment of reinfection dynamics, and ICT-based serology cannot distinguish between current and past infections. In addition, the study was conducted in only three villages, which may limit generalizability. Nevertheless, the combined application of stool microscopy and serological screening provides a more comprehensive assessment of helminth infection burden than either method alone.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that O. viverrini and S. stercoralis infections remain highly prevalent in rural communities of southern Lao PDR and that polyparasitism is common. ICT-based serological screening represents a practical and highly sensitive complement to stool microscopy for epidemiological surveillance. Integrated control strategies incorporating improved diagnostics, targeted treatment, health education, and behavioral interventions are essential to reduce transmission in these communities. Such approaches are critical for preventing severe complications such as cholangiocarcinoma and reducing the long-term burden of helminth infections in endemic settings (Akiyama and Brown 2018; Brindley et al. 2021; Carnino et al. 2021).
