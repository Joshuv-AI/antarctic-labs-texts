Intramedullary nailing compared with primary total hip arthroplasty for pertrochanteric fractures in autonomous elderly patients
Authors: Antoine Piercecchi, Alice Bordet, Ludovic Labattut, Alexandre Maczynski, Emmanuel Baulot, Pierre Martz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThe optimal surgical strategy for pertrochanteric fractures in elderly patients remains debated. While intramedullary nailing is the standard of care, primary total hip arthroplasty (THA) has been proposed to enhance early recovery. This study compared functional outcomes, complications, and survival between both techniques in autonomous elderly patients.MethodsThis monocentric randomized controlled trial was conducted between January 2015 and October 2017. Patients aged ≥ 70 years with pertrochanteric fractures and a pre-fracture Parker score ≥ 5 (indicating outdoor walking ability) were randomized to intramedullary nailing (n = 35) or cementless dual-mobility THA with trochanteric fixation (n = 34). Immediate full weightbearing was allowed in both groups. Functional outcomes were assessed at six weeks, six months, and 12 months using the Harris Hip Score (HHS), Postel-Merle d'Aubigné score, Parker mobility score, and visual analogue scale for pain. The primary endpoint was HHS at six months. Mortality, complications, and long-term survival were recorded.ResultsA total of 69 patients were analyzed. At six months, the mean HHS was 85.7 (SD 11.2) in the osteosynthesis group and 82.5 (SD 10.7) in the THA group (p = 0.291). Functional recovery, autonomy, and pain were comparable up to 12 months. Operating time was longer in the THA group (p < 0.001), while complication rates were similar. One-year mortality was 21.7% with no between-group difference. At a median follow-up of 4.1 years, 80% of patients had died. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed no difference in overall survival (p = 0.953). No secondary conversion to arthroplasty occurred.ConclusionNo clinically meaningful difference in functional outcomes or overall survival was detected. The observed HHS difference (3.2 points) fell below our a priori threshold and well below the closest available minimal clinically important difference estimate. In the absence of a detectable functional or survival benefit, routine primary THA does not appear justified in this population.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(9):1178–1186.

Introduction

Hip fractures represent a major public health burden, with a rapidly increasing incidence driven by population ageing and projections exceeding six million cases worldwide by 2050.1,2 Among them, pertrochanteric fractures account for more than half of all hip fractures and are traditionally managed with internal fixation, most commonly using cephalomedullary nails.3,4

Despite being considered the standard of care, osteosynthesis in elderly patients has been challenged by the risk of mechanical failure and the concern for delayed functional recovery. As a result, primary hip arthroplasty has been advocated by some authors as an alternative strategy, aiming to allow immediate full weightbearing and potentially faster functional recovery.5-9 However, this approach implies a more invasive procedure, longer operating time, and increased surgical complexity, raising the question of whether such an intervention provides a clinically meaningful benefit for patients.

The existing literature comparing internal fixation and primary arthroplasty for pertrochanteric fractures remains limited and inconsistent. Most available studies are retrospective or non-randomized; the few randomized trials conducted to date have reported conflicting results, with no clear evidence of superior functional outcomes associated with arthroplasty.10-12 Many of these studies included heterogeneous populations and focused on radiological or technical endpoints rather than patient-centered functional recovery.

The clinical relevance of primary arthroplasty is therefore particularly questionable in elderly patients who were functionally independent before injury and in whom the indication for arthroplasty is most often advocated. Whether the increased surgical burden of total hip arthroplasty (THA) translates into a clinically meaningful improvement in functional outcomes in this selected population remains uncertain.

The purpose of this study was to compare intramedullary nailing and primary THA with dual-mobility components in patients aged over 70 years with pertrochanteric fractures.

The primary objective was to determine, within the framework of a prospective randomized trial, whether primary arthroplasty provides a clinically relevant improvement in functional recovery compared with osteosynthesis during the first postoperative year. A secondary objective was to assess long-term survival and major hip-related events through an extended post-trial observational follow-up.

Methods

Study design

This study was a prospective, monocentric, randomized, controlled, open-label trial conducted between January 2015 and October 2017 and prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02171897). The randomized phase was designed to compare two surgical strategies for the treatment of pertrochanteric fractures in elderly patients: intramedullary nailing (control group) and primary THA (intervention group). The trial was approved by the institutional review board (CPP Est I; no. 2014-A00419-38), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

Participants

Consecutive patients aged 70 years or older presenting with a pertrochanteric femoral fracture (Ender types 1 to 6 13 or AO/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (OTA) types A1 to A2)14 were screened for eligibility in the emergency department. Inclusion criteria were a prefracture Parker mobility score ≥ 5 15 (scale 0 to 9, assessing indoor walking, outdoor walking, and shopping ability; a score ≥ 5 indicating the ability to walk outdoors with or without a walking aid) and the absence of cognitive impairment. Exclusion criteria included protected adults, lack of social security coverage, pathological fractures, and documented dementia or severe cognitive disorders.

Baseline demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, BMI, comorbidities, and Parker score, were recorded at inclusion.

Patient characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between groups, including age, sex, BMI, pre-fracture Parker score, and comorbidities (Table I). The mean age at inclusion was 84.7 years (71 to 98), and 82.6% of patients were female.

Randomization

Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to one of the two treatment groups using a secure web-based randomization system (Tenalea; Formsvision, Netherlands). Randomization was performed after confirmation of eligibility and allocation was generated by an independent statistician prior to study initiation. Treatment allocation was concealed until randomization was completed.

Surgical procedures

Surgery was performed either by ‘junior surgeons’, defined as residents or non-tenured assistant surgeons under supervision, or ‘senior surgeons’, defined as board-certified attending surgeons holding a permanent hospital position. Patients allocated to the osteosynthesis group (G1) underwent closed reduction and fixation using a cephalomedullary intramedullary nail (γ nail; Stryker, USA).

Patients allocated to the arthroplasty group (G2) underwent cementless THA using a dual-mobility acetabular component (Capitole; Evolutis, France) and a long cementless femoral comoponent (Stemsys; Evolutis). A standardized trochanteric fixation using a cable-plate system (GTR Cable-Ready; Zimmer Biomet, USA) was systematically performed. All arthroplasties were performed through an anterolateral (Hardinge) approach.

Postoperative management

Immediate full weightbearing was authorized in both groups. All patients followed the same standardized postoperative rehabilitation protocol and medical management.

Clinical and radiological assessments were scheduled at six weeks, six months, and 12 months postoperatively. Radiological evaluation included an anteroposterior pelvic radiograph and a lateral view of the operated hip. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Harris Hip Score (HHS),16 Postel-Merle d’Aubigné (PMA) score,17 Parker mobility score12, and pain using a visual analogue scale (VAS).18 Medical and mechanical complications were prospectively recorded at each follow-up visit.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the randomized trial was functional recovery assessed by the HHS at six months postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included PMA score, Parker mobility score, pain (VAS), mortality at six and 12 months, operating time, and postoperative complications.

Post-trial long-term follow-up

After completion of the randomized trial, all patients were traced to determine long-term vital status and major hip-related events. This extended follow-up was observational and retrospective in nature, with no additional clinical or functional assessments performed. Long-term outcomes included overall survival, any reoperation of the index hip, and major mechanical complications. Patients were analyzed according to their initial randomized group.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using means and SDs for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using an independent-samples t-test or Wilcoxon test for continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

All analyses were conducted on an intention-to-treat basis. The effect of treatment group on functional outcomes over time was evaluated using linear regression models with and without adjustment for age, sex, and baseline Parker score. Repeated-measures analyses of variance were used to assess changes in functional scores and pain over time.

Overall survival and reoperation-free survival were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier methods and compared using the log-rank test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software v. 9.4 (SAS Institute, USA).

The sample size was calculated prospectively. Based on a non-randomized pilot study conducted at our institution, a minimal relevant difference of 5 points in HHS at six months was assumed, with a mean HHS of 75 (SD 5) in the control group. Using a one-sided α of 5% and β of 10%, a minimum of 24 patients per group was required. Accounting for an estimated 30% attrition due to mortality and loss to follow-up, the final recruitment target was set at 35 patients per group (total n = 70).

Results

Study population

During the study period, 460 patients presented with a trochanteric fracture, 70 met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the trial (Figure 1). One patient was subsequently excluded due to a protocol violation related to duplicate inclusion following a contralateral fracture, leaving 69 patients for analysis. In total, 35 patients were allocated to the osteosynthesis group and 34 to the arthroplasty group.

Flowchart of the patient selection.

At six months, functional outcome data were available for 30 patients (85.7%) in the osteosynthesis group and 26 patients (76.5%) in the arthroplasty group. Missing data were primarily related to deaths occurring during follow-up. No patient was lost to follow-up at this timepoint. A higher proportion of procedures in the arthroplasty group were performed by senior surgeons (Table I).

Primary outcome

At six months postoperatively, the mean HHS was 85.7 (SD 11.2) in the osteosynthesis group and 82.5 (SD 10.7) in the arthroplasty group, with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.291; Table II).

Secondary outcomes

Functional scores: Postel-Merle d’Aubigné scores increased significantly over time in both groups, with no significant difference between groups at any follow-up timepoint (Table II).

Autonomy

There was no significant difference between groups in the evolution of the Parker mobility score at six or 12 months (Figure 2.). Pain

Pain evolution during the first six postoperative months was similar between groups (Figure 2). At six months, most patients in both groups reported no pain or only mild pain. At 12 months, no significant between-group difference in pain levels was observed.

Mortality

Overall mortality was 15.9% at six months, with four deaths (11.4%) in the osteosynthesis group and seven deaths (20.6%) in the arthroplasty group; this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.422). Most deaths (73.3%) occurred within the first six months after surgery. At 12 months, overall mortality reached 21.7% (15/69), with no significant difference between groups (osteosynthesis: 22.8%; arthroplasty: 20.6%; p = 0.683).

Operative data

The median operating time was significantly shorter in the osteosynthesis group (30 minutes (IQR 23 to 42)) compared with the arthroplasty group (94 minutes (IQR 78 to 120); p < 0.001). A significant imbalance in surgeon experience was observed between groups: 82.9% of osteosynthesis procedures were performed by junior surgeons compared with 35.3% in the arthroplasty group (p < 0.001).

Complications

One early mechanical failure occurred in the osteosynthesis group. In the arthroplasty group, one patient experienced both a prosthetic dislocation and a deep infection requiring reoperation. The number of postoperative blood transfusions was similar in both groups (n = 4). Medical complications occurred at similar rates between groups. Adjusted analyses accounting for age, sex, and baseline Parker score yielded similar results and did not alter the significance of the primary or secondary outcomes.

Post-trial long-term follow-up

At a median postoperative follow-up of 4.1 years (IQR 1.2 to 7.1), 56 of the 70 patients (80%) included in the randomized trial died during follow-up, including 29 patients in the osteosynthesis group and 27 patients in the arthroplasty group. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed no significant difference in overall survival between treatment groups (p = 0.953, log-rank test) (Figure 3). The median overall survival was 4.6 years (95% CI 3.2 to 6.2) in the intramedullary nail group and 3.9 years (95% CI 2.6 to 7.0) in the THA group. No additional hip-related surgical complications or reoperations were observed beyond the first postoperative year in either group. All mechanical complications and reinterventions occurred within the first postoperative year, with no late procedure-related adverse events observed thereafter.

Discussion

This prospective randomized study did not demonstrate any difference in functional outcomes between intramedullary nail osteosynthesis and THA for the treatment of pertrochanteric fractures in autonomous elderly patients. Functional recovery assessed by HHS, PMA score, and Parker score was comparable between both groups at all evaluated timepoints, without any trend in favour of primary arthroplasty. These findings do not support the initial hypothesis that THA provides superior functional outcomes compared with modern osteosynthesis in this specific population.

Those results are consistent with previously published randomized and prospective studies. Kim et al,10 in a randomized study including 54 patients, found no functional superiority of cementless arthroplasty compared with intramedullary nailing in per- and inter-trochanteric fractures. Similarly, Stappaerts et al,11 in a level I study comparing dynamic hip screw fixation with Vandeputte endoprosthesis, reported no difference in postoperative outcomes, despite including unstable fracture patterns. Although the authors suggested that cemented arthroplasty might represent an alternative option in frail osteoporotic patients, this tendency was not confirmed in the present study, despite the use of modern cementless THA with immediate full weightbearing and without an increase in complications.

Parker’s meta-analysis12 showed that literature was not in favour of any treatment, but it also pointed the lack of randomized and prospective studies about primary prosthesis in pertrochanteric fractures.

In contrast, some prospective and retrospective studies have reported early functional advantages in favour of arthroplasty. Shen et al6 observed better functional outcomes at six months in patients treated with hemiarthroplasty compared with osteosynthesis, although this difference disappeared and even reversed at 12 months. However, in that study, osteosynthesis outcomes were stratified based on radiological quality, which may have introduced a selection bias. Bonnevialle et al,5 in a large multicentre prospective cohort, reported superior PMA scores at six months in the prosthesis group. This difference may be partially explained by the multicentric design, heterogeneous surgical techniques, and non-standardized postoperative management across centres, in contrast to the strictly standardized monocentric protocol used in the present study.

Several studies with lower levels of evidence have suggested a superiority of prosthetic arthroplasty, particularly in osteoporotic patients.7-9 However, other authors did not identify any benefit of arthroplasty. De la Caffinière et al19 demonstrated that appropriate fixation using DHS allowed stable fracture healing even in unstable patterns, a finding supported by biomechanical studies.20 In the present series, functional scores at six months were slightly higher than those reported in the literature6,10 for both treatment groups, which may be explained by immediate full weightbearing and optimized perioperative management.

Postoperative autonomy, assessed using the Parker score, did not differ between treatment groups in this study. This contrasts with the findings of Bonnevialle et al,5 who reported higher Parker scores in the prosthesis group. The absence of difference in our cohort may be explained by the systematic allowance of immediate full weightbearing in both groups, without an increased rate of mechanical failure or complications in the osteosynthesis group.

Importantly, treatment strategy did not influence mortality. One-year mortality was 21.7%, consistent with previously reported rates ranging from 12% to 28%.7,10,21 While some studies reported increased mortality following prosthetic arthroplasty,10,21 others, including Bonnevialle et al5 and Rodop et al,7 found no difference between treatments. In our cohort, long-term follow-up revealed that approximately 80% of patients had died at nearly ten years, with no difference in overall survival between groups on Kaplan-Meier analysis. This high long-term mortality highlights the limited life expectancy of this population and raises questions regarding the relevance of choosing a more invasive primary arthroplasty strategy in the absence of demonstrable short- or long-term benefit.

A major finding of the present study is the absence of secondary conversion from osteosynthesis to THA. This challenges one of the main arguments often advanced in favour of primary arthroplasty — namely, the prevention of secondary failure requiring revision surgery. When modern intramedullary fixation is performed with strict adherence to biomechanical principles, particularly appropriate tip–apex distance,22,23 secondary failure appears to be uncommon. Only one mechanical complication (2.8%) was observed in the osteosynthesis group, a rate markedly lower than the 10% commonly reported in the literature.24-27

As expected, operating time was longer in the arthroplasty group, as reported by most authors,6,10 although some studies did not find significant differences.8,9,11,12 THA was chosen over hemiarthroplasty to limit acetabular wear-related pain, and cementless dual-mobility implants were used to reduce the risk of dislocation. This strategy proved effective, with only one dislocation (2.8%) and one deep infection, both occurring in the same patient. Importantly, no increase in periprosthetic fractures or mechanical complications was observed.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The study population was highly selected, excluding patients with dementia and low prefracture autonomy, which limits generalizability but ensured reliable functional assessment. No published minimal clinically important difference (MCID) exists for the HHS in the pertrochanteric fracture population. Our a priori threshold of 5 points, derived from an internal pilot study, falls below the closest available estimate (15.9 to 18 points).28 This study should therefore be interpreted as evidence against a large clinically meaningful difference, rather than formal equivalence. The EQ-5D questionnaire was not collected due to feasibility constraints in this elderly population, representing a deviation from the registered protocol that limits comparability with trials reporting this core outcome.29 The sample size limited sub-group analyses, and although follow-up was sufficient to capture early complications, longer-term functional assessment remains challenging given the high mortality rate. Additionally, postoperative evaluations were performed by the treating surgeons, which may introduce observer bias. Furthermore, although the absence of difference in complication rates was observed despite a significant imbalance in surgeon experience between groups this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that THA carries equivalent risk in all practice settings. Our results reflect a supervised academic centre, and extrapolation to institutions with less structured training programmes would be unwarranted.

Additionally, cementless femoral components were used systematically in this cohort, reflecting institutional practice at the time of the study (2015 to 2017). While current evidence increasingly supports the use of cemented femoral components in elderly patients, this choice does not alter the comparability of functional outcomes assessed at six and 12 months.30 Although no formal economic evaluation was conducted, the longer operating time and higher implant costs associated with THA raise questions regarding its cost-effectiveness in the absence of demonstrated functional benefit or secondary conversion.

Nevertheless, this study has several strengths, including its randomized, prospective, monocentric design, standardized surgical and rehabilitation protocols, and repeated functional assessments using validated scores. Despite this robust methodology, no functional or survival advantage of primary THA over intramedullary nail osteosynthesis was identified.

Taken together, these findings suggest that, in autonomous elderly patients with pertrochanteric fractures, modern intramedullary osteosynthesis remains a reliable and proportionate treatment option. Given the absence of functional superiority, the lack of secondary conversion, and the high long-term mortality, routine use of primary THA does not appear justified in this population.

In conclusion, no clinically meaningful difference in functional outcomes or overall survival was detected between intramedullary nailing and primary THA. The observed between-group difference in HHS at six months (3.2 points) fell below our a priori threshold and well below the closest available MCID estimate for this instrument. In the absence of a detectable functional or survival benefit, routine use of primary THA does not appear justified in this population.

Take home message

- In autonomous elderly patients with pertrochanteric fractures, primary total hip arthroplasty (THA) provides no clinically meaningful improvement in functional outcomes compared with modern intramedullary nailing.

- The absence of secondary conversion to arthroplasty challenges the main argument advanced in favour of primary arthroplasty, provided that fixation is performed with strict adherence to biomechanical principles.

- Given the high long-term mortality in this population, the increased surgical burden of THA does not appear justified in the absence of demonstrable functional or survival benefit.
