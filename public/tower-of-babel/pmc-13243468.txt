Fluorine-free fabrication of durable superhydrophobic membranes for efficient oil–water separation
Authors: Hongjie Zhang, Linjian Li, Meng Wang, Chan Guo, Fengguang Liu, Yipeng Zang, Cheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing occurrence of marine oil spills and industrial oily wastewater discharge demands efficient and sustainable oil–water separation technologies. Herein, we report a fluorine-free and scalable strategy to fabricate superhydrophobic nonwoven membranes via a simple two-step dip-coating process. By grafting hexadecyltrimethoxysilane (HDTMS) onto viscose/polyester substrates followed by polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) coating, the resulting membrane exhibits excellent superhydrophobicity, mechanical robustness, and chemical stability. The optimized membrane achieves separation efficiencies up to 99.98% with high oil fluxes exceeding 5800 L·m−2·h−1, reaching 7230 L·m−2·h−1 for carbon tetrachloride. Numerical simulations of velocity, concentration, and transport pathways further elucidate the mechanism of selective permeation. In addition, the fabrication strategy demonstrates strong versatility across multiple nonwoven substrates, all showing enhanced antifouling and self-cleaning performance. This work provides a scalable and cost-effective route for constructing durable superhydrophobic materials, offering practical potential for efficient oil–water separation in complex environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48607-y.

Introduction

Global environmental crises, including frequent marine oil spills and ubiquitous industrial and domestic oily wastewater, represent significant threats to both human health and animal life1–3. In response to these challenges, there is an urgent need for effective treatment methods that can address oily wastewater beyond the capacity of natural remediation processes4–6. Traditional remediation techniques, such as heating, centrifugation, flotation, and chemical treatments, are often hindered by high energy consumption, secondary pollution, and low separation efficiency, limiting their applicability to conventional oil-water mixtures7–9. As the treatment of oily wastewater continues to evolve, technological advancements are increasingly driven by a focus on sustainability and compliance with the regulatory standards. In this context, developing a highly promising solution that offers an efficient, environmentally friendly, and energy-saving method for oil-water separation is crucial.

Hydrophobization modification has gained significant attention as a promising method for oil–water treatment because of its unique selective wettability and high separation efficiency10,11. Various superhydrophobic materials with excellent oil–water separation performance have been developed, including surface-engineered sponges12–14, aerogels15–17, cotton-based fabrics18–20, and electrospun nanofibers21–23. Despite these advancements, most current fabrication approaches suffer from notable drawbacks, such as multi-step processing24, reliance on expensive or non-renewable materials, and use of environmentally persistent fluorinated compounds. Fluorine-based substances are recognized for their resistance to degradation, bioaccumulative potential, and associated ecotoxicological risks, raising serious concerns regarding long-term environmental and human health impacts25. In addition, the inadequate mechanical robustness and chemical resistance of many superhydrophobic customizations limit their practical deployment in harsh or dynamic environments26,27. Therefore, developing fluorine-free superhydrophobic materials with scalable fabrication, high durability, and robust performance remains a significant challenge.

Among various candidates, viscose/polyester spunlace nonwoven fabrics offer a promising platform for practical applications. Their intrinsic flexibility enables facile processing into devices with diverse geometries, while the combination of hydroxyl-rich viscose fibers and mechanically robust polyester fibers provides both chemical reactivity and structural stability28–30. Importantly, such nonwoven fabrics are industrially manufactured at large scale with low cost (~ 1.01–1.1 RMB m−2) and mature production processes, making them more attractive for real-world deployment than many laboratory-scale materials.

To functionalize these substrates, hexadecyltrimethoxysilane (HDTMS) can be employed to introduce hydrophobicity through covalent bonding with surface hydroxyl groups, offering a fluorine-free and efficient modification pathway31. Meanwhile, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a low-surface-energy silicon-based polymer, is widely used to enhance surface stability due to its strong adhesion, chemical resistance, and mechanical flexibility32–34. Despite these advantages, the synergistic integration of HDTMS and PDMS on industrial nonwoven substrates for achieving simultaneously scalable fabrication, long-term durability, and high separation performance remains insufficiently explored.

Herein, we report a simple and cost-effective strategy for preparing durable superhydrophobic nonwoven fabrics using a two-step modification process. Our approach combines the advantages of silane grafting and polymer coating, offering several notable improvements over existing methods: (1) the fluorine-free preparation process is environmentally friendly; (2) the simple dip-coating technique enables scalable production; (3) the resulting material exhibits excellent chemical resistance and mechanical durability; and (4) the method demonstrates good universality across different nonwoven substrates. This study provides new insights into the design of practical superhydrophobic materials for efficient oil-water separation.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Fabric: A 70 g/m2 viscose/polyester (50%/50%) spunlace nonwoven fabric was purchased from Sateri China Fibre Co., Ltd.

Chemicals: PDMS, Dow Corning with the corresponding curing agent, HDTMS, n-hexane (97%) (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China), and anhydrous ethanol (analytical grade, Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd., China).

Instruments: Vacuum drying oven (Shanghai Boxun Industrial & Commercial Co., Ltd., China), ultrasonic cleaner (Kunshan Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., China), magnetic stirrer (Quanan Laboratory Instrument Co., Ltd., China), contact angle goniometer (Shanghai Innuo Precision Instrument Co., Ltd., China), scanning electron microscope (ZEISS Sigma 300, Germany), X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA), and Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS20, USA).

Preparation of HDTMS and PDMS solutions

Solution A: HDTMS/ethanol solutions with concentrations of 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5% were prepared by dissolving the appropriate amount of HDTMS in anhydrous ethanol. The solutions were stirred at 400 rpm using a magnetic stirrer for 30 min under sealed conditions to ensure complete mixing and homogeneity.

Solution B: 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 g of PDMS were added to 20 mL of n-hexane. The mixtures were stirred at 400 rpm using a magnetic stirrer for 30 min under sealed conditions. Subsequently, the curing agent was added at a ratio of 10:1 (PDMS: curing agent) and stirring was continued for an additional 10 min to ensure thorough mixing and activation of the curing process.

Preparation of HDTMS/PDMS Co-modified superhydrophobic fabric

A 4 × 4 cm2 piece of viscose/polyester (50%/50%) nonwoven fabric was sequentially immersed in deionized water, ethanol, and deionized water, each for 30 min in an ultrasonic bath. The fabric was then dried in an oven at 60 °C to remove any residual liquids. Subsequently, the fabric was fully immersed in Solution A with a concentration of 4% for 10 min and dried in a vacuum oven at 100 °C to obtain Sample A. After cooling Sample A to room temperature, it was immersed in Solution B for 10 min, followed by curing in a vacuum oven at 100 °C for 2 h.

Characterization

The sample was directly adhered to a conductive tape and sputter-coated with gold to ensure conductivity. Subsequently, the surface morphology and elemental mapping of the samples were analyzed using a ZEISS Sigma 300 scanning electron microscope (SEM). Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS20, USA) was employed to characterize the sample within the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm− 1. Additionally, a sample smaller than 5 × 5 mm2 was prepared and analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA) to obtain both wide-scan and high-resolution spectra, enabling a detailed investigation of the chemical composition of the coating.

Hydrophobic performance evaluation

The static water contact angle on the sample surface was measured at room temperature using a goniometer. Three different locations on the sample surface were selected for testing, and the average value was determined.

When measuring the surface adhesion of rough substrates, such as fabrics, the sliding angle is no longer applicable because of interference from numerous adhesive fibers that hinder droplet sliding. To address this, a novel method called the shedding angle was proposed (the sliding angle formula is also applicable to the shedding angle). The shedding angle is defined as the critical angle at which a water droplet of a specified volume dropped from a predetermined height begins to rebound or roll off the tilted substrate. The shedding angle of the sample was tested at room temperature using a custom testing apparatus (Fig. S1). A 10 µL deionized water droplet was placed on the sample surface, and the angle between the sample surface and horizontal plane was adjusted. Mechanical stability performance evaluation

To evaluate the acid and alkali resistance of the samples, tests were conducted using hydrochloric acid solution (pH = 3) and sodium hydroxide solution (pH = 12). The samples were immersed in hydrochloric acid solution (pH = 3) and NaOH solution (pH = 12) for 1–5 h. After immersion, the samples were cleaned to remove residual solutions from the surfaces and dried at 60 °C for 30 min. Static water contact angle and shedding angle tests were performed, and the data were recorded and analyzed to evaluate the acid and alkali resistances of the samples.

Salt resistance tests were conducted by immersing the samples in natural seawater (Quanzhou, Fujian, China) for 1–5 h. After immersion, the samples were cleaned to remove residual seawater from the surfaces and dried at 60 °C for 30 min. Static water contact angle and shedding angle tests were conducted, and the data were recorded and analyzed to evaluate the salt resistance of the samples.

Abrasion resistance tests were performed by dragging the samples under a 100 g load over 1000-grit sandpaper for 15 cm. One back-and-forth motion was counted as one cycle, and the process was repeated for 1–5 cycles (Fig. S2). The static water contact angle of the side of the sample in contact with the sandpaper was measured, and the data were recorded and analyzed to evaluate the abrasion resistance of the samples.

Ultrasonic stability was tested at 40 kHz in deionized water for 1–5 cycles (20 min per cycle). After ultrasonic treatment, the residual deionized water on the sample surfaces was dried, and static water contact angle and shedding angle tests were conducted on the sides of the samples in contact with deionized water. The data were recorded and analyzed to evaluate the abrasion resistance of the samples.

Anti-fouling and self-cleaning performance evaluation

To systematically evaluate the antifouling performance of the samples, a series of experiments was designed in this study. First, common liquid contaminants, including milk, fruit juice, coffee, cola, black tea, and chlorella water, were selected and dropped separately onto the sample surfaces. After allowing them to stand for 60 min, the surfaces were observed to determine whether any stains remained. Second, to further investigate the antifouling capability of the samples under different pH conditions, deionized water solutions with pH values of 1, 3, 5, 9, 11, and 13 were prepared (Fig. S3) and similarly dropped onto the surface of the sample. After 60 min, the presence of residual stains was observed.

In the self-cleaning performance test, an appropriate amount of dirt was evenly spread onto the surfaces of the initial and oil-absorbed samples. The surfaces were then rinsed with deionized water, and dirt removal was observed. Through these experiments, the antifouling and self-cleaning performances of the samples in practical usage environments were comprehensively evaluated.

Results and discussion

Membrane fabrication and characterization

A two-step surface modification strategy was employed to impart superhydrophobicity to a viscose/polyester nonwoven fabric (Fig. 1). First, the fabric was immersed in an HDTMS solution at room temperature to initiate surface functionalization. Through hydrolysis and subsequent condensation reactions, the reactive groups of HDTMS covalently bonded with the hydroxyl groups present on the fiber surface, yielding the HDTMS @viscose/polyester composite. To further reduce the surface energy and enhance hydrophobicity, a secondary modification was performed using PDMS. The HDTMS-functionalized fabric was treated with PDMS via immersion, drying, and thermal curing, resulting in the formation of the PDMS@HDTMS@viscose/polyester composite. The PDMS layer further decreased the surface free energy and significantly improved the hydrophobicity of the material. The final composite exhibited excellent superhydrophobicity (Fig. 2G), along with a distinct silver mirror effect, indicating the presence of a stable air layer at the solid–liquid interface, a hallmark of the Cassie–Baxter wetting state (Fig. S4 and S5D)35. Overall, the experimental results demonstrate that the combined modification with HDTMS and PDMS effectively transformed the intrinsically hydrophilic viscose/polyester nonwoven fabric into a robust superhydrophobic material36.

The microstructural features of the viscose/polyester nonwoven fabric were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Fig. 2. The pristine sample exhibited a randomly entangled fiber network, where the polyester fibers displayed relatively smooth surfaces, whereas the viscose fibers possessed well-defined longitudinal grooves. Following the HDTMS modification, the overall morphology of the HDTMS@viscose/polyester composite remained largely unchanged. However, as shown in Fig. 2B, a noticeable decrease in the surface conductivity of the polyester fibers was observed, suggesting the successful chemical grafting of HDTMS, which introduced insulating long-chain alkyl groups onto the fiber surface37. Subsequent modification with polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) further altered the surface characteristics of the material. SEM images of the PDMS@HDTMS@viscose/polyester composite revealed a marked improvement in conductivity compared to that of the HDTMS-only modified sample. This enhancement indirectly verifies the successful deposition of PDMS, which formed a continuous and uniform coating on the fiber surfaces. High-magnification SEM images further confirmed that PDMS was evenly distributed across both the viscose and polyester fibers, forming a distinct micro/nano-scale hierarchical roughness. This multiscale surface architecture is critical for achieving the observed superhydrophobic performance because it facilitates the stable entrapment of air pockets and promotes the Cassie–Baxter wetting state38.

The surface chemical compositions of the samples were analyzed using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), as shown in Fig. 2D. In the original viscose/polyester fabric, absorption peaks at 3280.39 and 3310.12 cm−1 correspond to O–H stretching vibrations, indicative of hydroxyl groups in the viscose. The peaks at 2887.75 and 2820.87 cm−1 are attributed to C–H stretching, while the peak at 1711.02 cm−1 corresponds to the C = O bond in polyester. After HDTMS modification, new peaks at 2851.68 and 2921.55 cm−1 indicate the presence of long-chain alkyl groups in the FT-IR spectra. Additional peaks at 1486.40 and 1465.95 cm−1 (C–H bending), along with peaks at 1239.90 and 1288.82 cm−1 (Si–O–C/Si–CH₃), confirm the successful grafting of HDTMS39. Subsequent PDMS modification introduced new peaks at 1085.78 and 793.09 cm−1, corresponding to Si–O–Si stretching, while the peak at 504.94 cm−1 corresponds to Si–O–Si bending vibrations. These results confirm the effective coating of PDMS on the HDTMS-modified surface and the stepwise formation of the PDMS@HDTMS@viscose/polyester composites40.

To further verify the surface modification, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) were conducted. The XPS survey spectrum shows the presence of C and O in the pristine sample. After HDTMS modification, a Si 2p peak near 102.3 eV appeared, which was attributed to Si–C and Si–O–Si bonds (Fig. 2F). In the PDMS@HDTMS@viscose/polyester sample, the Si 2p peak at 102.4 eV confirmed the presence of PDMS. EDS analysis (Fig. S7) revealed a nearly twofold increase in silicon content after PDMS modification, further confirming the successful sequential grafting of HDTMS and PDMS onto the nonwoven fabric41.

The water contact angle measurements reveal distinct wettability evolution upon HDTMS (Fig. S8A) and PDMS (Fig. 2G) modification. For HDTMS-treated samples, the contact angle initially increased with concentration and then slightly decreased, reaching a maximum of 141.7°at a concentration of 4. Although this indicates pronounced hydrophobicity, it remains below the superhydrophobic threshold and shows sensitivity to concentration variations. In contrast, PDMS secondary-treated samples exhibit a more stable and pronounced hydrophobic enhancement, with a contact angle of 147.4° at a concentration of 1 and a plateau of 151.2–152.5° at concentrations ≥ 2, indicating the formation of a robust superhydrophobic surface. This behavior suggests that PDMS modification after HDTMS grafting results in more uniform and stable superhydrophobic surface modification.

Oil-water separation measurement

The oil–water separation performance of the superhydrophobic nonwoven fabric was evaluated by measuring the separation efficiency and permeation flux using dichloromethane and n-hexane as model oils42. Owing to its selective oleophilicity and superhydrophobicity, the fabric rapidly absorbed oil and efficiently separated it from water (Fig. 3A,B). A gravity-driven separation setup confirmed that oil selectively passed through the fabric, whereas water was blocked. As shown in Fig. 3C, the fabric achieved this selective oil permeation43.

Separation efficiencies above 99.97% for various oils and organic solvents, with fluxes exceeding 5800 L·m−2·h−1 were achieved. Specifically, dichloromethane achieved an efficiency of 99.98%, while carbon tetrachloride exhibited a maximum flux of 7230.45 L·m−2· After 30 separation cycles, the material maintained over 99.86% efficiency and stable flux above 6000 L·m−2·h−1 (Fig. 3D). These results demonstrate the fabric’s excellent oil–water separation efficiency, high throughput, and long-term stability, highlighting its potential for practical applications in wastewater treatment and environmental remediation44.

Optical microscopy of the as-prepared water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion reveals a large number of uniformly distributed and densely packed microdroplets, indicative of a stable dispersed system. After membrane separation, the filtrate became completely clear and transparent, with the initial turbidity entirely eliminated. Correspondingly, microscopic observation confirmed the absence of residual dispersed droplets, indicating effective removal of the encapsulated water phase and successful phase separation. The consistency between macroscopic observations and microscopic evidence demonstrates that the membrane can efficiently disrupt stable W/O emulsions, exhibiting strong demulsification capability and reliable oil–water separation performance.

To further evaluate practical applicability, separation tests were conducted using real oils, including olive oil and engine oil. The membrane exhibited excellent selective adsorption and separation performance for both systems. In the olive oil/water system, separation efficiencies reached up to 99.99% and remained above 97.22% across varying oil contents (0.3–0.8 g), accompanied by evident oil adsorption on the membrane surface. Similarly, for the engine oil/water system, efficient separation was achieved with a maximum efficiency of 99.28% and consistently above 90.84%, with the membrane surface fully wetted by oil after adsorption. These results demonstrate that the material maintains high separation efficiency and stability across different oil types, highlighting its strong potential for practical oily wastewater treatment.

Durability analysis of superhydrophobic surfaces

In practical oil-water separation applications, materials must not only exhibit high separation efficiency but also meet long-term stability requirements45. Particularly in complex environments, such as marine oil spill treatment, materials often need to withstand harsh conditions, including prolonged immersion and variations in the pH of contaminated seawater. Therefore, the development of superhydrophobic materials with excellent mechanical and chemical stability is of great importance46. In response to this demand, this study systematically evaluated the stability of the prepared PDMS@HDTMS@viscose/polyester composite material.

To assess the chemical stability of the material, PDMS@HDTMS@viscose/polyester samples were immersed in solutions with pH values of 3 and 12, as well as in seawater. After a certain period, the surface contact angles were measured. As shown in Fig. 4, the experimental results showed that even under the erosion of strong acids, strong bases, and seawater, the material maintained excellent hydrophobic properties, with water contact angles (WCA) consistently above 150.2° and sliding angles (SA) below 10°, fully demonstrating its good chemical stability.

The experimental results showed that after the mechanical treatments, the PDMS@HDTMS@viscose/polyester surface maintained high hydrophobicity (WCA > 150°) (Fig. S8B), with no significant decrease in the contact angles, indicating the excellent mechanical durability of the material47. To evaluate the mechanical stability of the material, ultrasonic treatment and sandpaper abrasion tests were conducted. The samples were subjected to ultrasonic treatment at 40 kHz and 200 W. After 100 min of ultrasonic exposure (Fig. S9), although the surface contact angles exhibited some fluctuations, the hydrophobic performance did not significantly degrade (WCA > 150.4°, SA < 8.9°). For the abrasion test, 1000-grit sandpaper was used for cyclic friction testing.

Anti-fouling and self-cleaning performance

Superhydrophobic materials exhibit excellent antifouling and self-cleaning capabilities owing to their unique surface properties, enabling them to maintain surface cleanliness over extended periods48. Leveraging this characteristic, the PDMS@HDTMS@viscose/polyester composite material prepared in this study also demonstrated remarkable anti-fouling performance. To validate the universality of this method, the research was further applied to modify various commercially available nonwoven fabrics and systematically evaluate their antifouling and self-cleaning properties49.

First, anti-fouling tests were conducted on the modified nonwoven fabrics using various common beverages (Fig. S11). Experimental results showed that when different beverages were dropped onto the material surface, no wetting or penetration occurred within 60 min owing to the material’s excellent hydrophobic properties, and the surface remained clean throughout50. Subsequently, the antifouling performance was further tested using dye solutions with different pH values (Fig. S12). After observation over different time intervals, the dye droplets did not spread or penetrate the material. Moreover, after rinsing with deionized water, the modified nonwoven fabric surface remained clean, fully demonstrating its outstanding antifouling performance51.

Additionally, Fig. Figure S13 illustrates the dust-repellent properties of the modified nonwoven fabric. When water droplets come into contact with the material surface in a rolling manner, they quickly form spherical shapes and roll off with ease. This phenomenon is attributed to the micro/nanostructured surface and low surface energy characteristics of the material52. As the water droplets roll off, they effectively carry away the dust particles attached to the surface, achieving a self-cleaning effect. This characteristic is highly similar to the self-cleaning mechanism observed on the surfaces of lotus leaves. Combining the experimental results mentioned above, the modified nonwoven fabric prepared in this study demonstrated a self-cleaning performance comparable to that of natural lotus leaves, providing strong support for its application in anti-fouling fields.

It is noteworthy that Young’s equation is only applicable to ideal surfaces that are chemically perfect, smooth, and homogeneous. Therefore, by introducing the surface roughness factor r, the contact angle in Young’s equation is modified.

The Wenzel model in Fig. 5B is based on the complete wetting of the recessed areas of a rough surface by a liquid and is applicable to situations in which there is no air layer between the liquid and the solid surface54. Its physical significance lies in the fact that if a smooth surface is hydrophobic, roughening further enhances hydrophobicity, resulting in a larger apparent contact angle. Superhydrophobic surfaces, through micro-nano-scale rough structures and low-surface energy chemical modifications, achieve water contact angles greater than 150°, creating the “lotus effect.” This high contact angle makes it difficult for water to wet the surface, whereas oil (typically with a lower surface tension) may exhibit a lower contact angle, enabling selective permeation. For superhydrophobic materials, the water contact angle θ > 150°, so cosθ < 0, and the permeation pressure is negative. This indicates that water cannot spontaneously penetrate the pores and is repelled from the surface. For oil, the permeation pressure was positive, indicating that oil could spontaneously penetrate the pores.

Based on the aforementioned theoretical models, oil-water separation efficiency exceeding 99.9%, and specific parameters, the software Comsol was used for modeling (Fig. S14). The simulations included the velocity field distribution of heavy oil (represented by dichloromethane) and light oil (represented by n-hexane), concentration distribution of the oil and water phases during the separation process, and motion streamline diagrams of the oil and water phases under concentration distribution conditions (Fig. 6, and Fig. S15)59. These simulations provide an intuitive understanding of the oil-water separation process and its effectiveness.

For heavy oil (dichloromethane), the simulations (Fig. 6a and c) reveal rapid downward permeation through the membrane driven by its higher density relative to water, forming a localized high-velocity region within the pores. The corresponding concentration and trajectory analyses confirm that dichloromethane preferentially penetrates the membrane while water is effectively retained above, demonstrating strong phase selectivity.

In contrast, for light oil (n-hexane; Fig. 6d and f), the lower density relative to water leads to a distinct transport behavior. The velocity field shows a slower and more uniformly distributed permeation process, as n-hexane must overcome buoyancy effects. The concentration profile indicates the formation of a continuous oil layer on the membrane surface, followed by gradual infiltration, while trajectory analysis reveals a more tortuous transport pathway through the porous structure.

These distinct permeation behaviors are consistent with the pressure difference predicted by the Laplace equation. The superhydrophobic surface (water contact angle > 150°) generates a negative capillary pressure for water, effectively blocking its, whereas the oil phase (near-zero contact angle) experiences a positive capillary pressure that promotes spontaneous permeation. This contrast provides the fundamental basis for selective oil–water separation. Overall, the simulations offer mechanistic insights into fluid transport at the pore scale and provide theoretical guidance for optimizing membrane structural parameters, including pore size, porosity, and thickness.

Conclusion

In this study, a durable superhydrophobic nonwoven fabric was fabricated via a simple two-step dip-coating strategy. The resulting PDMS@HDTMS@viscose/polyester composite exhibited outstanding oil–water separation performance, with efficiencies exceeding 99.97% and a maximum flux of 7230.45 L·m−2·h−1, while maintaining a separation efficiency above 99.86% after 30 cycles, demonstrating excellent durability and reusability. Numerical simulations of velocity, concentration, and transport pathways provided mechanistic insights into the selective permeation behavior, and stability tests under harsh chemical and mechanical conditions confirmed strong resistance to acids, alkalis, salts, abrasion, and ultrasound. In addition, the fabrication method proved to be scalable and versatile across multiple nonwoven substrates, all exhibiting effective self-cleaning and antifouling properties. Overall, this work presents a scalable and fluorine-free strategy for constructing robust superhydrophobic materials, offering a practical route for efficient oil–water separation. Future work will focus on evaluating long-term performance under more complex conditions, particularly flux decline and regeneration behavior in the presence of viscous oil fouling.
