Trainee growth vs. fixed mindset in clinical learning environments: enhancing, hindering and goldilocks factors
Authors: Samantha Rae Hopkins, Valerie Isobel Rae, Samantha E Smith, Victoria R Tallentire
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDoctors in training (trainees) have higher rates of burnout, anxiety and depression than other professionals. An important psychological tool to combat this crisis is promotion of a growth mindset, which increases resilience and improves mental health outcomes. How growth mindset might be promoted within the clinical learning environment is underexplored. This study aimed to explore the factors promoting a growth mindset versus fixed mindset in trainees from the perspective of the trainees.MethodsThis constructivist study employed a novel method of encouraging trainees’ reflections on mindset adoption in the clinical learning environment. Trainees played Mindset, a tabletop simulation board game that included challenges encountered within training. This was followed by a facilitated debriefing, focusing on factors that promoted growth or fixed mindset adoption in the clinical learning environment. Debriefing transcripts were analysed using template analysis.ResultsThree groups of oncology trainees and one group of medical education fellows participated (16 participants in total). Factors promoting growth mindset adoption included passion, collaboration, diverse career and role modelling. Factors promoting fixed mindset adoption included burnout and competition. Some factors, such as grit, previous success experience and singular training location promoted a fixed mindset when too much or too little were present, and promoted a growth mindset when they were in balance (the Goldilocks principle).ConclusionsThe importance of balance versus excess of certain factors, and the ubiquitous competitive culture in medicine promoting a fixed mindset, were compelling reflections. This study may aid invested parties to reflect on what factors they can upregulate and down regulate to optimise trainees’ adoption of a growth mindset.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06211-6.Keywords: Growth mindset, Mental health

Background

The experience of being a doctor in training (trainee) can feel like a constant struggle. At each stage, trainees encounter challenges they must overcome before progressing in their training pathway. Some trainees attempt to overcome obstacles with adaptable learning strategies, persistence, and incremental improvement, while others falter the moment they encounter a challenge. An inability to cope with the persistent challenges faced in the clinical learning environment can have detrimental psychological effects on trainees.

Doctors have higher rates of mental health problems such as burnout, anxiety, and depression, than other professional groups [1, 2]. The rising prevalence of mental health problems in trainees is particularly worrisome [3, 4] and can hinder professional development, negatively impact patient safety, and lead to substance misuse and suicide [5–9]. As a result, there have been increasingly impassioned pleas for more attention to be paid to measures that will improve trainees’ wellbeing and coping strategies when encountering challenging scenarios [10, 11]. Without diminishing the contribution of organisational factors, there are also some individual factors that might empower trainees to adopt more effective coping strategies when encountering challenging scenarios [12]. One possible individual contributor to trainees’ differing abilities to cope with such scenarios is the mindset that they adopt [13].

A mindset is a belief one has about their attributes, for example research acumen or procedural ability. In 2006, American Psychologist Carol Dweck published her pivotal book Mindset: The New Psychology of Success to a rapturous audience, primarily in the educational sphere [14]. In the book, she presents a social-cognitive learning theory [15–17] called the Implicit Theory of Mindset that describes two mindsets that sit at opposite ends of a spectrum: a fixed mindset and a growth mindset [14, 18, 19]. An individuals’mindset sits somewhere along this spectrum, and will fluctuate depending on the specific attribute in question, and a variety of individual and contextual factors.

A fixed mindset is the belief that one’s traits and attributes are pre-determined at birth and so potential is immutable. The primary focus is on performance-orientated goals as external validation of intelligence and natural abilities provide reassurance that innate and permanent attributes are adequate [14, 18]. Adoption of a fixed mindset increases avoidance of challenging scenarios for fear of failing, and consequently revealing one’s inescapable deficiencies [14].

A growth mindset is the belief that one’s attributes have the continual ability to improve with effort, challenge-seeking and adaptable learning strategies. The focus is on mastery and constant learning via incremental improvement, to achieve one’s goals [14]. Adoption of a growth mindset increases the belief that mistakes and failure are essential ingredients in the learning process, and confirm that an individual is challenging themselves adequately to grow [14]. Adoption of a growth mindset is associated with improved academic achievement, increased usage of evidence-based learning strategies, increased resilience, and improved psychological wellbeing [20–28]. To move trainees along the mindset continuum from a fixed to a growth mindset, we need to explore what factors promote its adoption in the clinical learning environment context.

An increase in trainees’ adoption of a growth mindset should improve the overall mental health of this cohort, allow them to fulfil their potential and support them to thrive. Our understanding of what factors influence trainee’ mindset in the clinical learning environment is a barren landscape in the medical education literature. Exploring these factors could help us to develop targeted interventions to upregulate factors that promote a growth mindset and down regulate factors that promote a fixed mindset. The first steps to better equip trainees to cope with the inevitable challenges encountered in the clinical learning environment is to explore the factors that influence their mindset.

Research aim

The aim of this study is to explore trainees’ perceptions of factors that promote growth mindset adoption in the clinical learning environment. The purpose is to provide information that may be used to help improve the mental health of trainees.

In our study, participants play a tabletop simulation board game (TSBG) called Mindset followed by a facilitated debriefing. We chose this study design because trainees needed an initial understanding of the Implicit Theory of Mindset prior to exploration of the research aim. We specifically chose a serious game, as opposed to other educational modalities, to enhance trainee engagement in the topic through constructive competition and strategic play.

Theoretical framework

An initial theoretical framework based on one existing theory by Zilka et al. in 2022 [29] was established to form the basis of our study. This theory was chosen because it explored the same topic but in a different context: teachers’ perceptions of factors that shape their growth mindset. To date, the Zilka theory has been referenced in thirty publications, all within the educational sphere. Please see Appendix 1 for further details of the adaptation to the theoretical framework made prior to commencement of data collection.

Methods

Ethics

We received ethical approval from the University of Edinburgh Ethics Committee (reference number: 2022/30). Participants were assured of anonymity prior to consent for the study. Informed consent was obtained from the participants.

Study design

In this constructivist study, participants played a TSBG called Mindset followed by a facilitated debriefing [30]. The TSBG was designed by our research team specifically for this study. In addition to our extensive research into board game design, the TSBG was created from the lead researcher’s (SRH) clinical experience to derive the scenario cards, and codes derived from the initial theoretical framework to create the factor playing cards. The scenarios encountered within the TSBG were used as the basis for the subsequent debriefing. The factor playing cards encouraged participants to reflect on the factors that could promote the adoption of a growth mindset versus a fixed mindset. For an explanation on how the initial theoretical framework was developed, please see Appendix 1.

Context

In the United Kingdom, specialty training is the final stage of becoming a qualified doctor, and usually lasts between five to eight years. Specialty trainees have worked as a doctor for at least two years prior to entering the training program. Medical education fellows are specialty trainees from a variety of clinical specialties, that have taken a one-to-two-year training break to pursue medical education endeavours.

Participant recruitment

All Edinburgh Cancer Centre oncology trainees in programme or taking a training-break, and all Lothian medical education fellows were emailed (26 in total). An information leaflet was provided, and participation was voluntary. All initial volunteers subsequently took part in the study.

Game design

At an in-person session conducted outside the clinical environment, participants received a 10-minute pre-brief by the facilitator (SRH) [31]. SRH included an introduction to the Implicit Theory of Mindset and summarised the vast amount of research identifying the benefits to learners [20–28].

After explaining the rules, participants played Mindset (please see Appendix 2: How to Play Mindset Guide). The purpose of the game is to provide an opportunity to apply a growth or fixed mindset in simulated scenarios mirroring common challenges encountered in medical training, such as failure to pass a membership (professional) exam or making a drug prescribing error [30].

We divided participants into growth and fixed mindset teams. The playing piece, trainee (Carol) started in the middle of the board with a neutral mindset. Teams competed to make Carol adopt their teams’ mindset to the challenging scenario by using factor playing cards. An example of a growth mindset factor card is structured and continuous feedback from supervisors and a fixed mindset factor card is lack of internal motivation. (Fig. 1)

Mindset game including scenario and factor playing cards

When playing a particular card, participants were asked to explain the application of the factors to the simulated scenario. This allowed participants to become acquainted with factors that might enhance their ability to adopt a growth or fixed mindset. It also stimulated reflection on whether they perceived the proposed factors to be relevant to their own experience, promoted deeper exploration of relevant factors, and provided a launchpad for participants to identify new factors to discuss in the subsequent debriefing. The TSBG aimed to make the abstract concept of growth and fixed mindset more tangible to participants and anchored the debriefing to clinical scenarios. In total, it lasted approximately 30 min and ended when all scenario cards were played, or when Carol reached the end of the game track at either the growth or fixed mindset head.

Debriefing

Immediately following cessation of the game, participants attended a debriefing using the frames-orientated debriefing model [32]. This debriefing model focuses on eliciting underlying assumptions and knowledge for an observed behaviour during the simulation, often using an advocacy and inquiry approach. Throughout the debriefing, participants were encouraged to use the TSBG as a launchpad to subsequently focus their reflection on the clinical learning environment [33]. SRH facilitated the debriefing which lasted approximately 30 min. The debriefing was audio recorded, transcribed verbatim, and anonymised.

Data analysis

We analysed transcripts using template analysis [34], using the amended version of the initial theoretical framework [29] detailed in Appendix 1 to pre-define the initial codes. Each transcript was analysed by the lead researcher (SRH) and then independently by a second researcher (one of VIR, SES, VRT). The purpose of double-coding was to ensure that all members of the research team were immersed in the data, and to enable all members to sense-check the presentation of the results. We applied the coding template to the data set and then modified it according to the data, with new codes added inductively, and some original codes modified or removed [34]. The new theory is SRH’s conceptualization of the factors promoting growth or fixed mindset, after discussions between SRH and her co-researchers.

Personal reflexivity

Our intention with this paragraph is to highlight how, as researchers, our preconceived beliefs and value systems impact our interpretation of the study results. [35] We are all clinicians with current or prior experience of being a trainee, which has influenced our personal beliefs about which factors influencing the adoption of a growth mindset are most pertinent in this context. SRH believes that fixed mindsets are maladaptive as they are associated with negative outcomes, and growth mindsets should be cultivated as they are associated with positive outcomes. She believes that internal factors, specifically passion for profession and grit, are more influential than external factors in the ability to adopt a growth or fixed mindset due to her personal experience. However, she believes that external factors, such as hospital learning climate and relationships, are interesting and important because strategies targeted at external factors are more likely to impact a larger number of trainees than strategies targeted at internal factors. SRH believes that in addition to targeting the systemic issues within medical training that contribute to the deteriorating mental health of trainees, trainees should be empowered and equipped to take personal responsibility for improving their wellbeing.

Internal factors

Motivation: passion, burnout, and grit

A strong inner motivation for pursuing a medical career enhanced trainees’ adoption of a growth mindset because it meant they were curious to learn and wanted to advance the field:

“I’ve been quite shaped in the direction of research by a genuine passion and interest… for driving progress.” [P11].

Burnout due to demanding clinical workloads promoted fixed mindset adoption as “most things that require change take a lot of time and effort” and that was difficult to do when you were “feeling busy in a clinical environment.” [P7] Chronic stress due to demanding clinical workloads restricted trainees’ cognitive and emotional capacity to tackle challenges and face possible failure:

“It’s the end of the shift, you’re so tired and you’re really intolerant of anything difficult…you’ve not finished anything, and you make a phone call for something that should just be easy and it’s not easy… My way to react to that is…. almost complete intolerance that something hasn’t worked the way that it should work.” [P10].

Burnout because of juggling too many things outside of the training programme or changes in life circumstances promoted a fixed mindset due to a reduction in capacity to take on challenges at work. In such circumstances, focus shifted towards outside responsibilities such as exams or caring responsibilities.

Chronic stress due to poor mental wellbeing promoted fixed mindset adoption because it limited trainees’ capacity to tolerate failure:

“Where you are on this depends on where your head is in general. If you’re having a tough time or your mood is low…then you might find yourself more intolerant to error.” [P15].

Grit could promote a growth mindset adoption, but too much grit sometimes encouraged fixed mindset adoption. A combination of passion and sustained persistence were useful to develop attributes and overcome challenge contributing to growth mindset adoption. However, these attributes could promote a fixed mindset if trainees continued to put effort into overcoming a challenge despite no perceived improvement, and if trainees’ felt unable to seek help:

“You sometimes continue and preserve things, when actually you perhaps should stop or escalate things…” [P11].

Personal experience: diverse career, singular training location, past failures, and past successes

Experiencing a diverse career path prior to becoming a trainee enhanced growth mindset adoption because it provided an appreciation that challenge is not unique to a career in medicine. Diverse career paths also provided trainees with unique skillsets that could lead to success.

Staying in the same hospital throughout training promoted growth mindset by helping to build relational support and trust with colleagues, and fixed mindset by limiting trainees’ ability to recognize their potential outside of that singular environment:

“Being able to see your potential outside of where we work, it probably limits that for you…I don’t think it allows you to see your opportunities or potential out with [your hospital]” [P3].

Previous experience of coping after failure promoted growth mindset adoption as it allowed trainees’ to “learn how to deal with failure” [P5] and develop resilience in the face of adversity:

“You start to realise that actually you were stressed but you got through it and that was fine…that things got better. I think you can fall back on those experiences as time goes on…I know, I’ve been through this before” [P3].

Past experiences of success could promote a growth mindset when it provided motivation that future success is possible. However, previous success experiences promoted a fixed mindset if they resulted in intolerance of failure and consequent avoidance of challenging scenarios:

“I think lots of us who are in medicine are often used to achieving and that can sometimes be a difficult and negative thing when you come up against things that you then struggle with.” [P11].

Previous success could also promote a fixed mindset if it caused trainees to become conditioned to only receive praise, and then struggle to hear negative constructive feedback.

External factors

Relationships: role modelling and feedback

Colleagues, particularly seniors and near peers, role modelling a growth mindset enhanced trainees’ adoption of a growth mindset due to social contagion, the spread of attitudes or beliefs from one member of a group to another. Witnessing colleagues overcoming challenges promoted growth mindset adoption by providing inspiration for what they were also capable of achieving:

“I’m not dissimilar from them. If they’re doing this, then, I guess, why can’t I? You see these inspiring people and then you want to be like them.” [P1].

Participants chronicled that an environment that “normalises giving constructive rather than just positive feedback” [P11] was essential for growth mindset adoption. However, many participants found it difficult to give and receive negative constructive feedback. Giving negative feedback was challenging because they feared upsetting a colleague and damaging their relationship. Receiving negative constructive feedback enhanced growth mindset if it was given with sensitivity and supporting evidence.

Permanent accessibility to feedback within the electronic portfolios promoted a fixed mindset as it encouraged trainees to be strategic when feedback seeking. They described preferentially asking for feedback from colleagues who they knew would provide positive responses as the electronic portfolio was permanent, easily accessible and could impact on future job opportunities.

Organization: insufficient resources, collaboration and autonomy, and competition

A department having insufficient resources (financial and staffing) promoted fixed mindset adoption because it limited trainees’ ability to implement improvement in challenging scenarios:

“You can do all these [projects] to try and improve things but if there is never the infrastructure available in a department how much could you actually achieve?” [P2].

Collaborative environments, particularly those with flattened hierarchies, encouraged growth mindset adoption because trainees were less afraid to “question things” [P9] and felt like valued team members. Autonomy also enhanced growth mindset adoption as it gave trainees control over their learning opportunities:

“You’re looking for…an acknowledgement of what you need out of your [training] for your skills and…where you can go to get it and have autonomy to be able to go and do that” [P3].

In contrast, competitive environments where trainees regularly heard colleagues compare them (or others) to peers, enhanced fixed mindset adoption because it moved the focus from how they, as individuals had improved, to how they measured up to someone else:

“I feel like in medicine you’re forever being ranked. You’re compared to the people at the same level that you work with. They [senior colleagues] will say, oh this junior… is really good or we miss you…we wish you were back on the team…it probably drives more of a fixed mindset…depending on how I’m comparing to another person.” [P10].

Competitive environments also promoted a fixed mindset because it encouraged trainees to apply for jobs where they would be most employable (i.e. they already had the necessary attributes) rather than jobs that would provide maximal learning opportunities:

“I think there’s something in the competition around…posts, in that you’re trying to figure out what post you want and understanding yourself and what skills you have, what attributes would suit you best and that isn’t necessary always a decision about what job you might want to do……it tries to force you back into have a fixed idea of what you are and what you’re good at.” [P4].

Time

Participants found it easier to adopt a fixed mindset in the initial period after they made a mistake, because they were more likely to feel emotive and irrational in the immediate aftermath:

“I think that initial reaction is oh I’ve done that wrong, that didn’t go well, I think I might leave medicine…The world is crumpling, I’ve failed, let’s leave.” [P3].

The passage of time enhanced growth mindset adoption because it allowed them to have a period of processing and time to re-establish confidence in their ability to improve:

“I think every time I’ve had a kick along the way, my mindset flips and then eventually my confidence gradually builds again, and it goes back to growth.” [P10].

Discussion

This study has explored trainees’ perceptions of factors that promote a growth versus fixed mindset, using data obtained from debriefings after a sensitising tabletop simulation board game. We identified five main themes and categorized them as internal (motivation and personal experience) and external (relationships, organization, and time) factors. In this discussion, we will explore two intriguing findings that present distinct practical challenges: the importance of balance versus excess of certain factors, and the competitive culture in medicine as detrimental to growth mindset adoption. We will close with examples of targeted interventions to down regulate factors that promote a fixed mindset and upregulate factors that promote a growth mindset.

The benefits of grit and previous experience of success include an association with lifetime educational fulfilment [36], professional success [37–40], and goal attainment [37, 41–43]. Similarly, a singular training location can be beneficial as it helps to develop trusting relationships. However, this study has shown that grit, previous experience of success and a singular training location could be both supportive and hindering. Trainees described a perception that too much or too little of these factors can be damaging to growth mindset promotion. We believe this is consistent with the Goldilocks principle, a metaphor that originates from a classic English fairy tale, where a young girl (Goldilocks) discovers that moderation of characteristics, rather than extremes of characteristics, provides the ideal experience of a pursuit [44].

Trainees explained that the right amount of grit is enhancing, but in excess can promote a fixed mindset if it causes a trainee to persist with a singular ineffective learning strategy despite lack of improvement, with associated avoidance of help-seeking. They also articulated that some previous experience of success promotes a growth mindset, but if the trainee has frequently experienced past success it could promote a fixed mindset if they become intolerant of failure and negative feedback, with consequent avoidance of challenging scenarios. Trainees revealed that a singular training location could also promote a fixed mindset by restricting their ability to see their potential in a range of environments. There is a paucity of evidence supporting the potential negative impact of traditionally celebrated factors like grit, previous experience of success, and consistent learning environments, although the U.S Department of Education report on grit has highlighted that inappropriate grit can have detrimental effects on psychological wellbeing [45]. The potential dark side of too much grit, previous success experience, and singular training location is compelling, and this interesting tension warrants further exploration.

The competitive culture of medicine is often heralded as a source of pride within the medical community. Potential benefits of competition include enhanced learner motivation, active learning, self-esteem, and effort [47–49]. However, trainees in this study perceived that working in a competitive environment promoted a fixed mindset. The mindset culture an organization adopts will shape employees’ beliefs [50–52] Fixed mindset organizations foster competitive working environments by rewarding employees who demonstrate their individual talent and ability. [50, 51] Consequently, employees understand that to succeed, they must prioritize being recognised for their individual accomplishments and compete with others for star status [50]. To the medical community, expunging the competitive nature of the learning environment may feel antithetical to its core tenets but it is essential to prioritize trainee mindset.

An intervention to down regulate the competitive culture in medicine is an overhaul of the admissions process at entry and transition points throughout the training pathway. [52, 53] Selection committees could focus on prioritizing candidates who view mistakes as learning opportunities and value collaboration rather than on exclusively performance measures. An adaption of the selection criteria would down regulate the competition factor that promotes fixed mindset adoption.

The trainees in this study described that supervisor role modelling of growth mindset enhanced their own growth mindset adoption. The contagion of educator mindset influencing learner mindset is supported in the literature [54]. To upregulate growth mindset role modelling, we recommend supervisors are selected and rewarded for demonstrating growth mindset values. Two examples of how this can be achieved is through supervisor disclosure of personal failures and associated learning with trainees, and provision of regular feedback on incremental improvement, resilience, and adaptable learning strategies. We hope our study findings and suggested interventions are the catalyst for further discussions about strategies to upregulate trainee growth mindset adoption.

Strengths and limitations

The use of a TSBG, a type of serious game [55], as the research tool in data collection is novel in the medical education sphere and may be of interest to other researchers looking for an innovative, cost-effective, and engaging research methodology. A further innovation is our use of a theoretical framework to create the TSBG which was then utilized for the template analysis. This theory-driven design of a serious game may benefit other researchers interested in providing a foundational understanding of a complex topic to participants before exploring their views in depth.

This study used existing theory as a conceptual lens [56] and while we did construct additional factors that were not present in the original theory, it significantly shaped our ideas about the topic and our interpretation of the data. We hope that the use of existing theory will improve the transferability of our findings to other fields. However, we acknowledge that when a conceptual lens is used, some concepts are illuminated while others are inevitably left “in the dark” [56].

A subset of the participants and the facilitator were from the same training program, therefore they had pre-existing relationships that may have made them either more or less comfortable to express their views. As the debriefings took place in a group setting, it is possible that some or all participants felt less comfortable to disclose and discuss more personal or controversial factors. Although the participants had diverse training experiences, all were working in the South East of Scotland at the time of the study. This conformity in training location could result in certain factors being perceived as more (or less) important to adoption of a growth mindset than if participants were in different locations, which may limit transferability to other contexts.

Further work

Further research avenues include exploring whether using serious games, like Mindset, can influence trainees’ mindsets in the clinical learning environment. If a serious game can encourage trainees to adopt a growth mindset, it could be used as an inexpensive and mobile method to influence mindset across different stages of training, and in different settings.

Conclusion

The adoption of a growth mindset is an important tool in trainees’ arsenals to increase their likelihood of long-term resilience and wellbeing. We have identified important internal and external factors that trainees perceive to promote growth versus fixed mindset adoption. We hope this exploration provides trainees, educators, and organizations with an opportunity to reflect on what factors they can up or down regulate to optimise trainees’ adoption of a growth mindset.
