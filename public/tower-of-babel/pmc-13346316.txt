Integrative bioinformatics and experimental validation identify IL1A as a novel therapeutic target for diabetic wound healing
Authors: Guowang Zhang, Tao An, Shuai Dai, Jinliang Huang, Pei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) represent a severe complication of diabetes mellitus, characterized by impaired wound healing and persistent inflammation. Despite advances in understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying DFU pathogenesis, effective therapeutic targets remain limited. This study aimed to identify novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets for diabetic wound healing through integrative bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation. We performed comprehensive transcriptomic analysis of DFU samples (n = 6) compared to normal skin controls (n = 6) from the GEO database (GSE80178). Differential expression analysis, functional enrichment, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), and protein-protein interaction network analysis were conducted to identify hub genes. Key findings were validated through in vitro experiments using keratinocyte cell lines cultured under normal glucose (NG), low glucose (LG), and high glucose (HG) conditions, with functional assays including colony formation and scratch wound healing assays. We identified 8,269 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 1,852 upregulated and 6,417 downregulated genes in DFU samples. Functional enrichment analysis revealed significant alterations in skin development, keratinocyte differentiation, inflammatory responses, and IL-17 signaling pathways. Hub gene analysis identified interleukin-1 alpha (IL1A) as a central hub gene and a key inflammatory mediator (log2FC = 5.18, adjusted p < 0.001), participating in seven distinct biological pathways. Experimental validation demonstrated that high glucose conditions impaired keratinocyte colony formation and wound closure capacity, accompanied by dysregulated inflammatory responses, consistent with the bioinformatics predictions. Through integrative computational and experimental approaches, we identified IL1A as a critical therapeutic target in diabetic wound healing. Our findings provide mechanistic insights into DFU pathogenesis and establish IL1A as a promising biomarker for developing targeted interventions to improve diabetic wound healing outcomes.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) represent one of the most devastating complications of diabetes mellitus, affecting approximately 15–25% of diabetic patients during their lifetime and serving as the leading cause of non-traumatic lower extremity amputations worldwide [1, 2]. The pathophysiology of DFUs is multifaceted, involving complex interactions between metabolic dysregulation, peripheral neuropathy, vascular insufficiency, and impaired immune responses [3]. Despite significant advances in diabetes management, the specific molecular drivers of impaired diabetic wound healing remain elusive, limiting the development of effective targeted therapies [4].

Normal wound healing proceeds through overlapping phases of hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling, orchestrated by precise temporal and spatial regulation of cellular activities and molecular signals [5]. However, in diabetic wounds, this finely tuned process becomes dysregulated, leading to prolonged inflammation, reduced keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation, impaired angiogenesis, and defective extracellular matrix remodeling [6, 7]. Chronic inflammation, characterized by sustained activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, has emerged as a central feature of DFU pathogenesis, creating a hostile microenvironment that prevents progression from the inflammatory to the proliferative phase of wound healing [8].

The advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies and bioinformatics approaches has revolutionized our ability to dissect complex disease mechanisms at the molecular level [9]. Transcriptomic profiling enables comprehensive identification of gene expression alterations, providing unprecedented insights into disease-specific molecular signatures [10]. Integration of advanced computational methods with traditional experimental validation represents a powerful strategy for discovering novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets, aligning with contemporary precision medicine initiatives [11, 12]. Several studies have applied transcriptomic analysis to diabetic wound healing, revealing dysregulation of inflammatory pathways, growth factors, and matrix remodeling processes [13, 14]. However, systematic identification and experimental validation of key hub genes that serve as central regulators in DFU pathogenesis remain limited.

The interleukin-1 (IL-1) family of cytokines plays pivotal roles in inflammatory responses and tissue repair processes [15]. IL-1 alpha (IL1A) and IL-1 beta (IL1B), the two main members of this family, act as potent pro-inflammatory mediators that initiate and amplify immune responses through activation of the IL-1 receptor (IL-1R) signaling cascade [16]. While IL-1B has been extensively studied in the context of diabetic complications and wound healing [17, 18], the specific role of IL1A in DFU pathogenesis has received comparatively less attention, despite its established function as an alarmin released upon cellular damage and its capacity to trigger inflammatory cascades [19, 20].

In this study, we employed an integrative approach combining comprehensive bioinformatics analysis with experimental validation to identify novel therapeutic targets for diabetic wound healing. We performed systematic transcriptomic analysis of DFU samples, including differential gene expression profiling, functional enrichment analysis, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), and protein-protein interaction network analysis to identify hub genes. Our computational predictions were subsequently validated through in vitro functional assays examining the effects of hyperglycemic conditions on keratinocyte behaviors. We hypothesized that this integrated methodology would reveal critical molecular mediators of DFU pathogenesis and identify actionable therapeutic targets for clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Data Acquisition and Preprocessing. Microarray gene expression data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE80178. This dataset comprised skin tissue samples from six diabetic foot ulcer patients and six healthy controls, profiled using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array platform. Raw CEL files were downloaded and processed using the R statistical environment (version 4.3.0) with Bioconductor packages. Quality control assessment was performed to evaluate data integrity, including examination of sample distribution, principal component analysis for batch effect detection, expression distribution analysis across all samples, density distribution curves, and sample-to-sample correlation analysis using Pearson correlation coefficients. Background correction and quantile normalization were applied using the robust multi-array average (RMA) algorithm implemented in the affy package to minimize technical variation and ensure comparability across samples. Probe sets were annotated to gene symbols using the manufacturer’s annotation files, and probes without gene symbol annotations or mapping to multiple genes were excluded from downstream analysis. When multiple probe sets mapped to the same gene, the probe set with the highest average expression was retained to represent that gene’s expression level.

Differential Gene Expression Analysis. Differential expression analysis between DFU and control samples was conducted using the limma package, which implements linear models and empirical Bayes methods for assessing differential expression in microarray data. The limma pipeline included fitting linear models to expression data, applying empirical Bayes moderation to standard errors, and computing moderated t-statistics for each gene. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they met the following criteria: absolute log2 fold change greater than 1.0 and adjusted p-value (false discovery rate) less than 0.05. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was employed for multiple testing correction to control the false discovery rate. Results were visualized using volcano plots displaying log2 fold change versus negative log10 adjusted p-values, MA plots showing log2 fold change versus average expression, and heatmaps depicting expression patterns of the top 50 most significantly differentially expressed genes across all samples with hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distance and complete linkage.

Functional Enrichment and Pathway Analysis. To elucidate the biological significance of differentially expressed genes, we performed comprehensive functional enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted for three categories: biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed to identify significantly altered signaling and metabolic pathways. Disease Ontology (DO) enrichment analysis was carried out to reveal associations with human diseases. For all enrichment analyses, we used the hypergeometric test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing, setting the significance threshold at adjusted p-value less than 0.05. Enrichment results were ranked by statistical significance and visualized as horizontal bar plots showing the top 15 most significantly enriched terms for each category. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the fgsea package to identify coordinated changes in pre-defined gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). GSEA was conducted using all genes ranked by their t-statistics from differential expression analysis, with 10,000 permutations to assess statistical significance. Gene sets with normalized enrichment scores (NES) greater than 2.0 or less than − 2.0 and adjusted p-values less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Hub Gene Identification and Network Analysis. Hub genes were identified through integration of multiple criteria including statistical significance of differential expression, degree centrality in protein-protein interaction networks, and participation in multiple enriched pathways. Protein-protein interaction data were retrieved from the STRING database (version 11.5) with a confidence score threshold of 0.7. The interaction network was constructed and visualized using the igraph package in R. Hub genes were defined as those meeting the following criteria: [1] significant differential expression with absolute log2 fold change greater than 2.0 and adjusted p-value less than 0.001 [2], high degree centrality (top 15 percentile) in the protein-protein interaction network, and [3] participation in at least one significantly enriched biological pathway. The expression patterns of hub genes were examined across all samples, and their involvement in multiple biological pathways was assessed. Functional categorization of hub genes was performed by manual curation based on GO annotations and literature review, classifying genes into inflammation-related, keratinocyte differentiation-related, antimicrobial defense-related, and extracellular matrix-related categories. Correlation analysis among hub genes was performed using Pearson correlation coefficients to reveal coordinated expression patterns.

Cell Culture and Glucose Treatment. Human immortalized keratinocyte cell line HaCaT was obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 units per milliliter penicillin, and 100 micrograms per milliliter streptomycin at 37 degrees Celsius in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide. To simulate diabetic hyperglycemic conditions, cells were treated with different glucose concentrations: normal glucose (NG, 5.5 millimolar), low glucose (LG, 10 millimolar), and high glucose (HG, 30 millimolar). Cells were cultured in glucose-containing medium for 48 h before subsequent functional assays. Media were changed every 24 h to maintain consistent glucose concentrations. Osmotic controls using mannitol were performed in preliminary experiments to exclude osmotic stress effects, confirming that the observed cellular responses were specifically due to glucose concentration rather than osmotic pressure changes.

Colony Formation Assay. To assess the long-term proliferative capacity of keratinocytes under different glucose conditions, colony formation assays were performed. HaCaT cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells per well in six-well plates and cultured in media containing NG, LG, or HG for 14 days with media changes every three days. At the end of the culture period, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 30 min. After washing with distilled water to remove excess stain, plates were air-dried and photographed using a digital camera. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted manually under a light microscope by three independent observers blinded to the treatment groups. Colony formation efficiency was calculated as the number of colonies divided by the number of seeded cells multiplied by 100%. Each experiment was performed in triplicate wells and repeated three independent times to ensure reproducibility.

Scratch Wound Healing Assay. Cell migration capacity was evaluated using scratch wound healing assays. HaCaT cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 5 times 10 to the fifth power cells per well and cultured until reaching 90–95% confluence. Cells were then pre-treated with NG, LG, or HG media for 24 h. A sterile 200 µl pipette tip was used to create a straight scratch wound across the cell monolayer. Detached cells were removed by washing twice with phosphate-buffered saline, and fresh medium containing the respective glucose concentrations was added. Wound closure was monitored by capturing phase-contrast microscopy images at 0 h and 48 h using an inverted microscope equipped with a digital camera. The wound area was measured using ImageJ software by three independent investigators blinded to the experimental conditions. Wound closure percentage was calculated using the formula: (area at 0 h minus area at 48 h) divided by area at 0 h multiplied by 100%. To minimize proliferation effects on migration measurements, cells were cultured in serum-reduced media (2% FBS) during the migration period. Each experimental condition was performed in triplicate wells and repeated three independent times.

Statistical Analysis. All bioinformatics analyses were performed using R statistical software version 4.3.0 with appropriate Bioconductor packages. For differential expression analysis, moderated t-statistics were computed using the limma package with empirical Bayes moderation. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control false discovery rate. For experimental data, statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Data from in vitro experiments are presented as mean plus or minus standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p-value less than 0.05. For all experiments, investigators were blinded to treatment groups during data acquisition and analysis to minimize potential bias.

Results

Quality Control and Data Characteristics of DFU Transcriptomic Profiles. To ensure data reliability for subsequent analyses, we first performed comprehensive quality control assessment of the microarray dataset comprising six diabetic foot ulcer samples and six normal skin control samples. Sample distribution analysis confirmed balanced group sizes with six samples in each condition (Fig. 1A). Principal component analysis revealed clear separation between DFU and control groups along the first principal component, which explained 57.2% of the total variance, while the second principal component accounted for 21.1% of the variance, indicating distinct transcriptomic signatures between diseased and healthy skin tissues (Fig. 1B). One DFU sample showed slight deviation from the main cluster but remained within acceptable range based on subsequent correlation analysis. This slight deviation likely reflects clinical heterogeneity among DFU patients, which is a common occurrence in human tissue transcriptomics due to variations in ulcer duration, severity, infection status, and individual patient characteristics. Expression distribution analysis demonstrated consistent median expression levels and interquartile ranges across all 12 samples, with DFU samples (GSM2114237-GSM2114242) showing slightly higher overall expression compared to control samples (GSM2114231-GSM2114236), suggesting global transcriptional activation in diabetic wounds (Fig. 1C). Density distribution curves of log2-transformed expression values showed similar distribution patterns between DFU and control groups, with DFU samples exhibiting a modest rightward shift consistent with increased gene expression (Fig. 1D). Sample correlation matrix analysis using Pearson correlation coefficients revealed high within-group correlations, with DFU samples showing correlations ranging from 0.93 to 0.96 and control samples showing correlations from 0.52 to 0.94, while between-group correlations ranged from 0.34 to 0.79, confirming biological consistency within groups and expected differences between conditions (Fig. 1E). These quality control metrics validated the dataset’s suitability for differential expression analysis and ensured that observed differences reflect genuine biological variation rather than technical artifacts.

Identification and Characterization of Differentially Expressed Genes in Diabetic Foot Ulcers. Differential expression analysis between DFU and control samples identified 8,269 significantly altered genes meeting the criteria of absolute log2 fold change greater than 1.0 and adjusted p-value less than 0.05. Among these differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 1,852 genes were upregulated and 6,417 genes were downregulated in DFU samples compared to controls, revealing a predominant pattern of gene repression in diabetic wounds. Volcano plot visualization displayed the distribution of all analyzed genes, with upregulated DEGs shown in red on the right side and downregulated DEGs in blue on the left side, while non-significant genes appeared in grey (Fig. 2A). The most significantly upregulated genes included S100A12 (log2FC = 6.69), SPINK6 (log2FC = 7.51), and several antimicrobial peptides, while the most dramatically downregulated genes included LOC102723517 (log2FC = -5.36), DCT (log2FC = -4.56), and GUSBP4 (log2FC = -4.10). MA plot analysis, plotting log2 fold change against average expression levels, revealed that highly expressed genes tended to show more moderate fold changes, while genes with extreme fold changes often had lower average expression, a pattern consistent with typical biological regulation (Fig. 2B). Hierarchical clustering analysis of the top 50 most significantly differentially expressed genes demonstrated clear separation between DFU and control samples, with DFU samples clustering together and control samples forming a distinct cluster (Fig. 2C). This heatmap revealed coordinated expression patterns, with a prominent cluster of genes highly expressed in DFU samples including inflammatory mediators such as IL1A, S100A12, and antimicrobial peptides RHCG and SPINK6, alongside a cluster of genes with reduced expression in DFU samples including pigmentation-related genes DCT and TYRP1, and various long non-coding RNAs. Among the top 10 most upregulated genes, SPINK6 showed the highest fold change (log2FC = 7.51), followed by S100A12 (log2FC = 6.69), RHCG (log2FC = 5.54), IL1A (log2FC = 5.18), and several uncharacterized transcripts (Fig. 2D). The top 10 downregulated genes were predominantly composed of melanocyte-related genes and long non-coding RNAs, with LOC102723517 showing the most severe downregulation (log2FC = -5.36), followed by DCT (log2FC = -4.56) and GUSBP4 (log2FC = -4.10) (Fig. 2E). The dramatic upregulation of inflammatory and antimicrobial genes coupled with downregulation of structural and pigmentation-related genes reflects the pathological tissue remodeling and chronic inflammatory state characteristic of diabetic foot ulcers.

Functional Enrichment Analysis Reveals Dysregulated Skin Development and Inflammatory Pathways. To gain biological insights into the functional consequences of the observed transcriptomic changes, we performed comprehensive functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes. Gene Ontology biological process analysis identified significant enrichment of terms related to keratinocyte biology and skin development, including regulation of keratinocyte proliferation (adjusted p-value = 1.2 × 10^-12), keratinocyte proliferation (adjusted p-value = 3.5 × 10^-11), keratinization (adjusted p-value = 2.8 × 10^-10), keratinocyte differentiation (adjusted p-value = 8.7 × 10^-9), skin development (adjusted p-value = 4.2 × 10^-7), and epidermis development (adjusted p-value = 6.5 × 10^-7), suggesting fundamental alterations in epidermal homeostasis and regenerative capacity (Fig. 3A). RNA processing and regulation were also prominently enriched, including mRNA processing, RNA catabolic process, and regulation of response to DNA damage stimulus, indicating widespread changes in post-transcriptional regulation and stress responses. These biological process alterations were supported by molecular function enrichment analysis, which revealed significant enrichment of catalytic activities acting on RNA and DNA, histone binding, chromatin remodeling activities, and various exonuclease activities, pointing to altered epigenetic regulation and RNA metabolism in diabetic wounds (Fig. 3B). KEGG pathway analysis identified multiple disease-relevant pathways significantly enriched among the DEGs, most notably cornified envelope formation (adjusted p-value = 3.2 × 10^-7), proteoglycans in cancer (adjusted p-value = 2.1 × 10^-6), cellular senescence (adjusted p-value = 1.8 × 10^-5), cell cycle regulation (adjusted p-value = 2.4 × 10^-5), and inflammation-related pathways including rheumatoid arthritis (adjusted p-value = 8.7 × 10^-6) and IL-17 signaling (Fig. 3C). The enrichment of cancer-related pathways likely reflects shared mechanisms of dysregulated proliferation, migration, and survival rather than malignant transformation per se. Cellular component analysis showed significant enrichment of genes localized to the collagen-containing extracellular matrix, nuclear speck, chromosomal regions, centrioles, and cornified envelope, consistent with the observed alterations in skin structure and nuclear regulation (Fig. 3D). Disease Ontology enrichment analysis revealed significant associations with various carcinomas, keratosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and notably breast carcinoma and renal cell carcinoma, reflecting the shared pathological features of tissue remodeling and chronic inflammation between diabetic wounds and these disease states (Fig. 3E). The convergence of these enrichment results highlights dysregulation of epidermal differentiation, chronic inflammation, and impaired tissue regeneration as core features of diabetic foot ulcer pathogenesis.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis Identifies Activated IL-17 Signaling and Suppressed RNA Processing. To complement the over-representation analysis and capture coordinated changes in functionally related gene sets, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using ranked gene lists based on differential expression statistics. GSEA of Gene Ontology biological processes revealed strong positive enrichment of sensory perception-related gene sets, particularly olfactory and chemosensory processes, including detection of chemical stimulus involved in sensory perception (NES = 2.97), sensory perception of smell (NES = 2.76), antimicrobial humoral response (NES = 2.58), and detection of chemical stimulus (NES = 2.46), while showing negative enrichment of RNA processing-related pathways including tRNA metabolic process (NES = -2.41), RNA export from nucleus (NES = -2.41), and RNA localization (NES = -2.40), indicating suppression of fundamental cellular processes in diabetic wounds (Fig. 4A). GSEA of KEGG pathways demonstrated marked positive enrichment of IL-17 signaling pathway (NES = 2.82), olfactory transduction (NES = 2.78), and cornified envelope formation (NES = 2.56), while showing negative enrichment of nucleocytoplasmic transport (NES = -2.31), lysine degradation (NES = -2.20), and spliceosome (NES = -2.00), further supporting widespread disruption of basic cellular functions alongside activation of inflammatory responses (Fig. 4B). The strong enrichment of IL-17 signaling is particularly noteworthy given the established role of this pathway in chronic inflammatory conditions and autoimmune diseases. Bubble plot visualization of GO biological process enrichment showed that the most significantly enriched terms related to keratinocyte biology and skin development, with mRNA processing showing the highest statistical significance (adjusted p-value = 3.8 × 10^-12) but modest gene ratio, while keratinocyte proliferation and regulation thereof showed strong enrichment with higher gene ratios (Fig. 4C). Similarly, KEGG pathway bubble plot analysis highlighted cornified envelope formation as the most significantly enriched pathway (adjusted p-value = 1.5 × 10^-7) with the highest gene ratio among cancer and disease-related pathways, followed by proteoglycans in cancer and cell cycle pathways (Fig. 4D). The concordance between over-representation analysis and GSEA results strengthens confidence in the identified biological processes and pathways, with IL-17 signaling emerging as a particularly robust finding that warrants further investigation as a potential therapeutic target.

Hub Gene Analysis Identifies IL1A as a Central Regulatory Node in DFU Pathogenesis. To identify key regulatory genes that serve as central nodes in the dysregulated biological networks of diabetic foot ulcers, we integrated differential expression data with protein-protein interaction network topology and pathway participation. Analysis of hub genes ranked by expression change magnitude identified 15 core genes with absolute log2 fold changes exceeding 4.0, comprising eight upregulated genes (SPINK6, S100A12, HEPHL1, RHCG, IL1A, PTHLH, DEFB4B, DEFB103A) and seven downregulated genes (KRT2, SCGB2A2, DCD, LOC102723517, DCT, TYRP1, GUSBP4) (Fig. 5A). Expression heatmap of these hub genes across all samples demonstrated remarkably consistent patterns within each group, with dramatic upregulation in DFU samples for inflammatory and antimicrobial genes and pronounced downregulation for structural and secretory genes (Fig. 5B). Network analysis examining participation of hub genes across multiple enriched pathways revealed that IL1A and KRT2 were the most highly connected hub genes, each participating in seven distinct biological pathways, followed by SPRR2F, SPRR2B, and KRT17 with six pathway connections, while other hub genes participated in one to two pathways (Fig. 5C). This multi-pathway participation positions IL1A as a critical regulatory hub capable of influencing multiple aspects of wound healing biology. Functional categorization of hub genes revealed that the majority (82%) fell into the “Other” category representing diverse functions, while inflammation-related genes comprised 6% (including IL1A, S100A12, S100A9), skin and keratinocyte-related genes comprised 10% (including KRT2, KRT17, SPRR2B, SPRR2F), and extracellular matrix-related genes comprised 2% of the hub gene set (Fig. 5D). Among the top upregulated hub genes, SPINK6 showed the highest fold change (log2FC = 7.51), but IL1A demonstrated a more moderate upregulation (log2FC = 5.18) coupled with extensive pathway connectivity, suggesting a central regulatory role rather than merely being a highly responsive gene (Fig. 5E). The top downregulated hub genes were enriched for genes involved in pigmentation (DCT, TYRP1) and epidermal barrier function (KRT2, DCD), reflecting the loss of normal skin architecture in diabetic wounds (Fig. 5F). The identification of IL1A as a hub gene with high pathway connectivity and significant upregulation, combined with its established role in inflammatory signaling, positions this cytokine as a priority candidate for therapeutic targeting.

Correlation Analysis Reveals Coordinated Expression Modules and Functional Gene Categories. To understand the regulatory relationships among hub genes and identify coordinated expression modules, we performed correlation analysis across all hub genes and examined the distribution of functionally related gene sets. The hub gene correlation matrix revealed distinct correlation patterns, with positive correlations among upregulated inflammatory genes (S100A12, IL1A) and among downregulated structural genes, while showing negative or weak correlations between genes from different functional categories (Fig. 6A). This pattern suggests the existence of distinct transcriptional modules that are coordinately regulated in diabetic wounds. Analysis of functional gene set distributions across four major categories (inflammation, keratinocyte, defense, matrix) showed that inflammatory genes were predominantly upregulated (34 upregulated versus 19 downregulated), as were keratinocyte-related genes (29 upregulated versus 8 downregulated) and defense-related genes (9 upregulated versus 0 downregulated), while matrix-related genes showed mixed regulation (7 upregulated versus 19 downregulated) (Fig. 6B). This functional categorization reveals selective activation of inflammatory and defense responses alongside suppression of matrix remodeling processes. Examination of top inflammation gene expression patterns demonstrated that IL1A was among the most highly and consistently upregulated inflammatory mediators across all DFU samples, with expression levels (mean log2 expression approximately 10.8 in DFU versus 5.6 in controls) exceeding those of other pro-inflammatory cytokines including S100A12, TNFRSF12A, IL7, S100A7A, and TNFRSF19 (Fig. 6C). This marked upregulation of IL1A in diabetic wounds, combined with its role as an alarmin and its capacity to initiate inflammatory cascades, underscores its potential significance in perpetuating chronic inflammation. Analysis of top keratinocyte gene expression showed mixed patterns, with some keratin genes such as KRT17 and KRT16P3 showing upregulation in DFU samples while others including KRT2 and SPRR2C showed downregulation, suggesting complex and potentially paradoxical alterations in keratinocyte differentiation programs (Fig. 6D). The coordinate regulation of functionally related gene sets and the central position of IL1A within the inflammatory gene module support the hypothesis that targeting IL1A might broadly affect the inflammatory microenvironment of diabetic wounds.

Pathway Network Analysis Highlights IL1A as a Priority Therapeutic Target. To systematically evaluate potential therapeutic targets among the identified hub genes, we examined their participation across multiple pathways and assessed their relevance to druggable biological processes. Analysis of genes participating in multiple enriched pathways identified AKT3 as the most highly connected gene involved in five distinct pathways, while ANAPC1 and ANAPC11 participated in two pathways each, and several metabolic enzymes including AADAT, AASS, ACAT1, and ALDH family members participated in single pathways related to amino acid metabolism (Fig. 7A). These findings highlight the complexity of pathway crosstalk in diabetic wound pathogenesis. Examination of differentially expressed gene distribution across the top eight enriched KEGG pathways revealed that cornified envelope formation showed the most dramatic imbalance with 45 upregulated genes and only 2 downregulated genes, while other pathways including cell cycle, proteoglycans in cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma showed more balanced distributions of upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 7B). The rheumatoid arthritis pathway showed enrichment of both upregulated and downregulated genes, reflecting the complex inflammatory responses in diabetic wounds that share features with autoimmune pathology. Prioritization of therapeutic target candidates based on fold change magnitude, pathway connectivity, and known druggability identified IL1A as the top candidate (log2FC = 5.18), followed by keratin genes KRT6B (log2FC = 3.92) and KRT17 (log2FC = 3.83), while downregulated candidates included KRT2 (log2FC = -6.12), CLDN10 (log2FC = -3.29), and cell cycle regulators CDC14A and KMT2A (Fig. 7C). The prominent position of IL1A among upregulated therapeutic candidates, combined with the availability of IL-1 receptor antagonists already approved for clinical use in other inflammatory conditions, makes this target particularly attractive for translational development. Overall DEG summary analysis confirmed that among 53,617 analyzed genes, 8,269 (15.4%) showed significant differential expression, with 1,852 (22.4%) upregulated and 6,417 (77.6%) downregulated, indicating widespread transcriptional repression as a hallmark of diabetic wounds (Fig. 7D). Functional gene category summary recapitulated the preferential upregulation of inflammatory genes (34 up, 19 down), keratinocyte genes (29 up, 8 down), and defense genes (9 up, 0 down), contrasted with mixed regulation of matrix genes (7 up, 19 down), providing a systematic overview of the molecular alterations underlying DFU pathogenesis (Fig. 7E). The convergence of multiple analytical approaches consistently highlighting IL1A as a central inflammatory mediator strongly supports its selection as a primary therapeutic target for experimental validation.

Experimental Validation Confirms Impaired Wound Healing Under Hyperglycemic Conditions. To validate the bioinformatics predictions, we performed in vitro experiments using human keratinocytes cultured under normal glucose (NG, 5.5 mM), high glucose (HG, 30 mM), and high glucose plus IL1A knockdown (HG + siIL1A) conditions. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis confirmed that high glucose dramatically induced IL1A mRNA expression to approximately 4.0-fold compared to normal glucose (p less than 0.01), while siRNA knockdown effectively reduced IL1A expression to approximately 1.4-fold, representing 65% reduction compared to high glucose alone (p less than 0.01) (Fig. 8A). This 4-fold induction is consistent with the log2 fold change of 5.18 observed in diabetic foot ulcer transcriptomic analysis. MTT cell viability assays revealed that high glucose significantly increased cell viability to approximately 1.5-fold compared to normal glucose (p less than 0.01), while IL1A knockdown reduced this to approximately 1.05-fold, nearly restoring baseline levels (p less than 0.01) (Fig. 8B). Flow cytometry analysis showed that high glucose dramatically increased apoptosis rate from approximately 5% to 18.5% (p less than 0.01), while IL1A knockdown significantly reduced apoptosis to approximately 8%, representing 57% reduction compared to high glucose alone (p less than 0.01) (Fig. 8C). To elucidate inflammatory mechanisms, ELISA measurements demonstrated that high glucose increased IL-6 secretion from approximately 125 pg/mL to 490 pg/mL (p less than 0.01), while IL1A knockdown reduced IL-6 to approximately 215 pg/mL, representing 56% reduction (p less than 0.01) (Fig. 8D). Similarly, high glucose increased TNF-α secretion from approximately 85 pg/mL to 330 pg/mL (p less than 0.01), while IL1A knockdown reduced TNF-α to approximately 145 pg/mL, representing 56% reduction (p less than 0.01) (Fig. 8E). Colony formation assays, which measure the long-term proliferative and self-renewal capacity of cells, demonstrated that low glucose (LG) treatment slightly increased colony formation compared to normal glucose (NG) controls, while high glucose (HG) treatment significantly reduced colony formation capacity, as evidenced by both representative colony images showing fewer and smaller colonies in HG-treated cells and quantitative analysis showing colony numbers of approximately 250 in NG, 280 in LG, and 200 in HG groups, with statistically significant differences between LG versus NG (indicated by single asterisk) and HG versus LG (indicated by double asterisks) (Fig. 8, upper panels). These results indicate that sustained hyperglycemia impairs the proliferative capacity of keratinocytes, which is essential for wound re-epithelialization. Scratch wound healing assays, which assess cell migration and wound closure capability, showed that at the initial timepoint (0 h), all three groups exhibited comparable wound widths with clear scratch boundaries. However, after 48 h of culture, the NG and LG groups demonstrated substantial wound closure with cells migrating into the scratch area and significantly reducing the gap, while the HG group showed markedly impaired wound closure with persistent wide gaps and reduced cell migration. Quantitative analysis of wound closure percentage revealed that LG treatment resulted in the highest wound closure (approximately 43%), NG treatment showed intermediate closure (approximately 28%), and HG treatment showed the lowest wound closure (approximately 17%), with statistically significant differences between LG versus NG (single asterisk) and HG versus LG (double asterisks) (Fig. 8, lower panels). The paradoxical observation that low glucose conditions enhanced both colony formation and migration compared to normal glucose may reflect metabolic adaptation mechanisms or reduced oxidative stress, while the clear impairment under high glucose conditions is consistent with the diabetic wound healing phenotype. These experimental findings validate the transcriptomic data showing dysregulation of keratinocyte proliferation and migration pathways in diabetic conditions, and provide functional evidence that hyperglycemia directly impairs critical cellular processes required for wound healing. The concordance between bioinformatics predictions of altered keratinocyte function and experimental demonstration of impaired proliferation and migration under high glucose conditions strengthens confidence in the overall analytical approach and supports the translational relevance of the identified molecular targets.

Discussion

In this study, we employed an integrative approach combining systematic bioinformatics analysis with experimental validation to identify novel therapeutic targets for diabetic wound healing. Through comprehensive transcriptomic profiling of diabetic foot ulcer tissues and normal skin controls, we identified 8,269 differentially expressed genes with predominant transcriptional repression in diabetic wounds. Functional enrichment and network analyses revealed dysregulation of keratinocyte biology, chronic inflammation, and impaired tissue remodeling as core features of DFU pathogenesis. Notably, we identified interleukin-1 alpha (IL1A) as a central hub gene with extensive pathway connectivity and marked upregulation in diabetic wounds, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target. Experimental validation demonstrated that hyperglycemic conditions impair keratinocyte proliferation and migration, consistent with the computational predictions and providing functional evidence for the role of metabolic dysregulation in wound healing failure.

The striking predominance of downregulated genes (6,417 downregulated versus 1,852 upregulated) in DFU tissues warrants particular attention. This global pattern of transcriptional repression likely reflects the metabolic exhaustion and cellular senescence characteristic of the chronic wound environment. Under sustained hyperglycemic conditions, cells in the wound bed undergo progressive metabolic stress, leading to widespread epigenetic silencing and reduced transcriptional activity. This transcriptional shutdown may represent a cellular response to chronic energy depletion and oxidative damage, resulting in the inability of wound-resident cells to mount adequate regenerative and reparative programs. The downregulation of genes involved in RNA processing, cell cycle progression, and extracellular matrix remodeling observed in our enrichment analyses further supports this interpretation of a metabolically exhausted wound microenvironment.

The identification of IL1A as a priority therapeutic target is supported by multiple lines of evidence from our analyses. First, IL1A showed robust upregulation with a log2 fold change of 5.18 in DFU samples, placing it among the top upregulated genes. Second, network analysis revealed that IL1A participates in seven distinct biological pathways, the highest connectivity among all analyzed hub genes, indicating its central position in the molecular network underlying DFU pathogenesis. Third, IL1A belongs to the inflammatory gene module that showed coordinated upregulation in diabetic wounds, suggesting that targeting this cytokine might have broad effects on the inflammatory microenvironment. Fourth, IL1A is an established alarmin released upon cellular damage and necrosis, and its elevation in diabetic wounds likely reflects ongoing tissue injury and inflammation [21]. Our findings align with previous studies demonstrating elevated IL-1 family cytokines in diabetic wounds and their role in perpetuating chronic inflammation [17, 18]. While most previous research has focused on IL-1 beta, our data highlight the potentially important role of IL-1 alpha, which signals through the same IL-1 receptor but has distinct cellular sources and release mechanisms.

It is important to clarify the different analytical frameworks used in Figs. 5C and 7A regarding gene connectivity. Figure 5C examines the connectivity of hub genes (selected based on differential expression magnitude with |log2FC| > 4.0 and PPI network centrality) across enriched biological pathways, where IL1A and KRT2 each participate in seven pathways. In contrast, Fig. 7A analyzes all differentially expressed genes participating in multiple KEGG pathways regardless of hub gene status, identifying AKT3 as the most connected gene across five KEGG pathways. Thus, IL1A is the most connected hub gene within our stringently defined hub gene set, while AKT3 represents the most broadly connected gene across KEGG pathways among all DEGs. This distinction reinforces that IL1A serves as a central inflammatory hub specifically within the most dysregulated gene network, while AKT3 functions as a general pathway crosstalk mediator.

The therapeutic potential of targeting IL-1 signaling in diabetic wound healing is supported by both preclinical and clinical evidence. Studies using IL-1 receptor knockout mice have demonstrated that the IL-1/IL-1R axis delays wound closure in diabetic conditions [22]. Administration of IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra) has been shown to improve wound healing in diabetic animal models by re-establishing a pro-healing microenvironment characterized by reduced inflammatory cells and cytokines, decreased senescent fibroblasts, and increased anti-inflammatory factors [23]. Importantly, IL-1Ra (anakinra) is already approved for clinical use in rheumatoid arthritis and other autoinflammatory conditions, providing a clear translational pathway for repurposing this agent for diabetic wound healing [24]. Our identification of IL1A as a central mediator in DFU pathogenesis provides strong rationale for clinical trials investigating IL-1R antagonism in diabetic foot ulcer patients.

Beyond IL1A, our analyses revealed several other notable findings that warrant discussion. The dramatic upregulation of antimicrobial peptides and defense-related genes, including SPINK6, S100A12, defensins (DEFB103A, DEFB4B), and RHCG, likely reflects both the chronic inflammatory state and the high risk of infection in diabetic wounds. The pronounced downregulation of pigmentation-related genes (DCT, TYRP1) and structural proteins (keratins, cornified envelope components) indicates loss of normal epidermal architecture and differentiation programs. Interestingly, we observed mixed patterns of keratin expression, with some keratins upregulated (KRT17, KRT16) and others downregulated (KRT2), suggesting complex alterations in keratinocyte differentiation states. KRT17 upregulation has been previously associated with wound healing and epithelial stress responses, while its persistent elevation in chronic wounds may indicate failure to complete the normal healing program [14].

An important question arising from our analyses is how IL1A-mediated inflammation specifically disrupts keratinocyte differentiation and cornified envelope formation, the most significantly enriched pathways in our bioinformatic analyses (Figs. 3A and 4C). IL1A, as a potent pro-inflammatory alarmin, can activate NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades that directly interfere with the ordered keratinocyte differentiation program. Sustained IL1A signaling may lock keratinocytes in a proliferative, undifferentiated state by suppressing the expression of terminal differentiation markers and cornified envelope precursors such as SPRRs and involucrin. Furthermore, IL1A-driven chronic inflammation promotes aberrant expression of wound-associated keratins (e.g., KRT17 upregulation) while repressing homeostatic keratins (e.g., KRT2 downregulation), consistent with the mixed keratin expression patterns observed in our data. This disruption of the normal differentiation cascade ultimately impairs the formation of an intact epidermal barrier, a prerequisite for successful wound closure.

The enrichment of cell cycle and cellular senescence pathways in our analysis aligns with accumulating evidence that cellular senescence contributes to impaired diabetic wound healing [6]. Senescent cells accumulate in diabetic wounds and secrete pro-inflammatory factors (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype or SASP) that can impair healing. The interaction between IL-1 signaling and cellular senescence is bidirectional, forming a positive feedback loop: IL-1 can induce senescence, while senescent cells produce IL-1 and other inflammatory mediators [8]. This self-reinforcing positive feedback loop perpetuates chronic inflammation and healing failure. Therapeutic strategies targeting both IL-1 signaling and senescent cells (senolytics) may have synergistic effects on diabetic wound healing.

Our experimental validation using hyperglycemic cell culture conditions demonstrated impaired keratinocyte proliferation and migration, providing functional evidence for the computational predictions. Interestingly, low glucose conditions showed enhanced performance compared to normal glucose in both assays, which may reflect the metabolic flexibility of keratinocytes or reduced oxidative stress under moderate glucose restriction. However, the clear impairment under high glucose conditions is consistent with the diabetic phenotype and validates the relevance of our transcriptomic findings. These results support the concept that hyperglycemia directly impairs keratinocyte function through mechanisms that may include increased oxidative stress, altered growth factor signaling, and metabolic dysregulation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our bioinformatics analysis was based on a relatively small sample size of six DFU patients and six controls, which may limit statistical power and generalizability. However, the robust separation in principal component analysis and high within-group correlations suggest that the dataset captures representative transcriptomic signatures. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the transcriptomic data provides a snapshot at a single timepoint and cannot capture the dynamic temporal changes that occur during wound healing. Longitudinal studies examining gene expression changes during wound healing progression and resolution would provide additional insights. Third, while we identified IL1A as a priority target, our IL1A knockdown experiments were performed in a single cell line under in vitro conditions, and the effects of IL1A inhibition on wound healing should be further validated in animal models and clinical settings. Fourth, our in vitro experiments used a transformed keratinocyte cell line (HaCaT) cultured in two-dimensional monolayers, which may not fully recapitulate the complex three-dimensional tissue architecture and cellular interactions present in actual wounds. Validation using primary keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and immune cells in three-dimensional culture systems or ex vivo wound models would strengthen the findings. Furthermore, given IL1A’s established role as an alarmin, future studies should investigate the paracrine effects of keratinocyte-derived IL1A on other wound-resident cell populations. Specifically, IL1A released from damaged or stressed keratinocytes may activate underlying dermal fibroblasts, promoting their transition to a pro-inflammatory, senescence-associated phenotype, and recruit and polarize macrophages toward a sustained pro-inflammatory (M1) state. Elucidating these paracrine signaling networks using co-culture systems with primary keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and macrophages would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how IL1A orchestrates the chronic inflammatory wound microenvironment and would strengthen the rationale for IL1A-targeted therapeutic interventions.

Despite these limitations, our study demonstrates the power of integrating computational and experimental approaches to identify therapeutic targets. The convergence of multiple analytical methods, including differential expression analysis, functional enrichment, GSEA, network analysis, and hub gene identification, provides robust evidence for the central role of IL1A in DFU pathogenesis. The identification of IL1A as a druggable target with existing approved inhibitors provides a clear path toward clinical translation. Future studies should focus on validating IL-1 inhibition in relevant preclinical models and designing clinical trials to test IL-1R antagonists in diabetic foot ulcer patients. Additionally, examining the interplay between IL-1 signaling, cellular senescence, and other pathways identified in our analyses may reveal combination therapeutic strategies with enhanced efficacy.

In conclusion, through integrative bioinformatics and experimental validation, we have identified IL1A as a central therapeutic target in diabetic wound healing. Our findings provide mechanistic insights into DFU pathogenesis, highlight the importance of chronic inflammation in healing failure, and establish a foundation for developing IL-1-targeted interventions to improve outcomes for diabetic foot ulcer patients. The systematic approach employed in this study, combining transcriptomics, network analysis, and functional validation, provides a roadmap for discovering therapeutic targets in other complex diseases and exemplifies the potential of precision medicine approaches in wound healing research.
