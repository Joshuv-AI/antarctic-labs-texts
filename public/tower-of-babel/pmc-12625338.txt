Socioeconomic and maternal mental health factors associated with the parenting and development of children in an early childhood promotion program: analysis via structural equation modeling
Authors: Laura M. T. Dal’Ava dos Santos, Vivian S. S. Gonçalves, Ariene Silva do Carmo, Gabriela Buccini, Nathalia Pizato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroudChildhood development is influenced by the quality of the environment in which children live. Social inequalities, non-responsive parental practices, and poor caregiver mental health can delay children’s development. Our objective was to evaluate the socioeconomic and maternal mental health factors associated with the parenting and development of children participating in Programa Criança Feliz (Happy Child Program; PCF), a Brazilian early childhood promotion program.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study with children aged up to 24 months who were participating in the PCF in the Federal District, Brazil. In 2022, sociodemographic data, maternal depression and anxiety, responsive and unresponsive parenting and the risk of child developmental delay were collected with validated instruments. The data were analyzed via a hybrid structural equation model.ResultsA total of 301 children whose data were reported by caregivers participated in the study. Mothers represented 92.4% of the caregivers. Among them, 61.8% reported having an income of up to US$200.00 per month, and half of the mothers and 66% of the fathers had incomplete secondary education or less. Half of the mothers were classified as having depression or anxiety (49.5%; 95% CI: 43.9–55.1). Nonresponsive parenting practices such as parental inconsistency (32.6%, 95% CI 27.5–38.0) and coercive practices (43%, 95% CI 37.4–48.5) were frequent. Among the children, 45.9% (95% CI: 40.2–51.7) were at risk for developmental delay, which was positively associated with the presence of maternal anxiety (β = 0.286; p = 0.001) and with lower education (β = 0.207; p = 0.006). Higher unresponsive parenting scores were associated with the presence of anxiety (β = 0.230, p = 0.036) and lower income (β = 0.392, p < 0.001). The use of coercive practices was positively associated with the presence of anxiety (β = 0.182, p = 0.019) and with mothers as heads of the family (β = 0.157, p = 0.012).ConclusionsSocioeconomic disparities and the presence of maternal anxiety were associated with unresponsive parenting and with the risk of developmental delay among children participating in the PCF in the Federal District.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24917-0.

Introduction

Healthy child development during the first years of life is dependent on the provision of nurturing care (NC); in such an environment, children have access to health services and adequate nutrition and to a safe and protected space where they have learning experiences and are stimulated and receive sensitive and responsive care that meets their needs [1]. Globally, 219 million children under 5 years of age are at risk of developmental delay due to vulnerability and socioeconomic disparities, such as food insecurity, toxic stress, lack of access to health services and education [2]. In this context, the WHO/UNICEF recommends investing in the NC framework as a guide for the implementation of multidimensional policies (education, nutrition, health, income transfer programs) with the objective of improving the care offered to children and providing tools that help caregivers meet the needs of their children [3, 4].

Positive parenting is described as the set of activities and actions performed affectively and responsively by reference adults toward children with the objective of ensuring their survival and full development and is one of the ways to achieve an NC environment [5–7]. Parents with higher education are associated with better parenting practices, greater knowledge about child development, and less use of physical punishment [8]. Unresponsive parenting practices are more common in situations of socioeconomic inequalities, such as low income and unemployment, and precarious situations of survival, such as a lack of access to basic sanitation and housing in unsafe places [9–14]. These factors can increase parental stress and the chance of practices such as physical punishment, which can impair the time dedicated by parents to the child’s activities and the verbal development of these children [15–17].

Another aspect of unresponsive parenting is its relationship with the mental health of caregivers, especially mothers, during the gestational and postnatal periods [18–20]. For example, children of parents with symptoms of anxiety are more likely to report fear and to present cognitive distortions such as victimization and blaming [18]. Depression is more common among women with up to 4 years of schooling, among women who do not have the support of the father of the child and family, and among women who live with two or more children under seven years of age; children of mothers with depression are more likely to have developmental delay [21].

One way to reduce child vulnerability is to invest in policies and programs, especially those aimed at early childhood development (ECD) [22, 23]. Through home visits, parental programs perform recreational activities with children and their families and support caregivers in promoting comprehensive and responsive care [24]. These programs are also mediators between families and health, providing educational and social assistance services aiming to promote healthier and more economically productive populations [22, 23]. Thus, knowing the factors associated with positive parenting can facilitate the focus of actions to promote child development (CD).

Currently, 31.6% of the population of Brazil is living in poverty, with black or mixed-race people living in single-parent households consisting of women with children up to 14 years of age being more vulnerable. In addition, 10% of the Brazilian population of children under 36 months of age have suspected developmental delay [25, 26]. To address disparities in child development, in 2016, Brazil created Programa Criança Feliz (Happy Child Program; PCF), a national program that aims to carry out weekly visits to reinforce positive parenting and encourage CD through the strengthening of the family relationships and the realization of stimulating activities, helping families to create an environment of nurturing care. Despite barriers in effectiveness [27] attributed to aspects of the implementation of the PCF [28, 29], currently, the program has been implemented on a large scale, reaching approximately 3,000 municipalities.

The Federal District (DF) is one of the Brazilian Federation Units that implemented the PCF. In the DF, 14.9% of the population lives in some degree of poverty, and 8% of children under 36 months of age are at risk of developmental delay [25, 30]. The first phase of implementation of the PCF in the DF took place in 2017. In 2021, after the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic, the second phase of the PCF was implemented, with expansion to other regions of the DF not included in the first phase.

A qualitative analysis of the experience of families participating in the PCF in the DF highlighted the importance of a multisectoral approach to reduce social disparities in the promotion of comprehensive care in early childhood [31]. This study highlighted the history of program implementation in the DF as an important case study to characterize the demographic, socioeconomic and mental health disparities associated with parenting and CD. However, to our knowledge, there have been no studies of the population assisted by the program in the DF that evaluated the main disparities and the magnitude of their association with aspects relevant to the promotion of healthy early childhood. In this context, our objective was to evaluate the socioeconomic and maternal mental health factors associated with the parenting and development of children participating in the PCF.

Methods

Study design and ethical aspects

This was a cross-sectional study conducted from the baseline of the Evaluation Project of the Criança Feliz Brasiliense Program (PIPA-DF), a longitudinal study that evaluated different aspects of the implementation of the PCF in the DF between the years 2022 and 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee for Research on Human Beings of the Faculty of Health Sciences, University of Brasília (Opinion No. 4,293,167). The research participants, caregivers of the children, signed a free and informed consent form (FICT). The study considered the ethical principles indicated by Resolution No. 466/12 of the National Health Council of December 12, 2012 [32] minimizing risks, maximizing benefits and ensuring the privacy and confidentiality of the data collected.

Study settings

The DF, which is divided into 35 administrative regions, encompasses Brasilia, which is the capital of Brazil, and is in the Central-Western region of the country. Between 2019 and 2021, the PCF was implemented in sixteen administrative regions, serving 3,200 beneficiaries [33]. The study was conducted at the 8 administrative regions of the DF that enrolled in the second phase of implementation in 2021, i.e., Gama, Varjão, Planaltina, Sobradinho, Itapoã, Paranoá, Fercal and Brazlândia. We chose to include only the administrative regions in which the PCF was implemented in 2021 to mitigate the influence of the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic on the activities developed by the program.

Calculation of sample size

To calculate the sample size of the PIPA-DF, we considered the proportion of 11.8% of children younger than 36 months (expected final age for follow-up) with child development scores (PIPAS Z scores) ≥ 1 reported in the study by PIPAS Ceará [34]. The following were also considered: a sampling error (d) of 5%, Z of 1.96, corresponding to the 95% confidence level; and a design effect (deff) of 1.5. The sample size of 300 children up to 24 months of age was estimated for the baseline follow-up and was used for the present study. But it is worth noting that this sample size is sufficient to carry out the structural equation model [35].

Participants

The register of PCF visitors, provided by the DF government, was used to select the sample by cluster for three sampling units. The primary sampling unit consisted of program visitors; the secondary sampling unit was the family; and the tertiary sampling unit was the child. The eight administrative regions included in the PCF in 2021 were considered. Within these regions, 26 visitors were randomly selected from the PCF’s visitors register of the Federal District government. All families with children up to 24 months of age served by these selected 26 visitors were considered eligible for the evaluation, and in families with more than one child between 0 and 24 months old, the younger one was selected. Children who received the Continuous Cash Benefit (BPC), a cash transfer benefit given to children with a disability, were not included.

Data collection

The interviewers trained by the PIPA-DF team visited the families with the support of PCF visitors and supervisors. In each of the interviews, age-specific research instruments were digitally applied via the KoboCollect® application with the aid of smartphones, this allowed greater control in the application of the questionnaires and that there was no missing data. Data collection took place between May and July 2022.

Measures

Child development

The Survey of Well-being of Young Children - Brazilian version (SWYC-BR), an instrument translated and validated for the Brazilian population, was used [36]. The psychometric properties of SWYC were considered adequate, convergent validity was measured by Average Variance Extracted (AVE = 0.73) and reliability was calculated by Cronbach’s Alpha (AC = 0.97) [31]. The instrument, composed of 12 age-specific forms, was developed to screen children younger than 65 months of age for cognitive, language and motor developmental and behavioral disorders. Parents or other caregivers complete the instrument. Each form has 10 items, and each item has three response options according to how well the child is able to perform a skill. The response categories and scores are as follows: “Not yet”, “0” points; “A little”, “1” point; and “A lot”, “2” points. The cutoff points for each age in months were those proposed by Alves [37]. For this study, when the score was below the proposed cutoff point, development was considered “below the expected age”.

Parenting

The Parenting and Family Adjustment Scales (PAFAS) assesses parenting practices and parental and family adjustment. It was adapted to Portuguese and validated for the Brazilian population [38, 39]. The instrument has 20 items divided into two subscales, the first of which refers to parenting covering four domains, i.e., (1) parental inconsistency, (2) coercive practices, (3) parental encouragement and (4) quality of the father‒child relationship, and the second of which refers to family adjustment and covers two domains, i.e., (1) parental adjustment and (2) family adjustment [38, 39]. The psychometric properties of PAFAS were considered adequate [38]. The parenting subscale presented CFI (Comparative Adjustment Index) = 0.95, SRMR (Residual Square Mean Pattern) = 0.047, RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation) = 0.046. The family adjustment subscale showed CFI = 0.95, SRMR = 0.047, RMSEA = 0.079. The items are answered via a Likert scale with 4 options (not at all; a little or some of the time; a lot or a good part of the time; a lot or most of the time), with scores ranging from 0 to 3 [38, 39]. The cutoff points used were obtained from the median value of each domain [40]. Scores above the median indicated dysfunctional parenting practices for the subscales parental inconsistency (1) and coercive practice (2). For the other subscales, scores above the median indicated better parenting practices. Thus, by default, all the variables were classified into responsive parenting and unresponsive parenting.

Maternal anxiety

The short form of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), which was adapted into Portuguese and validated in Brazil [41], was performed. In this version, the internal consistency coefficients proved to be adequate (Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.70). The trait anxiety scale measures the stable behavioral characteristics of individuals when reacting to threatening situations that elevate anxiety. State anxiety refers to a transient current emotional state that varies in intensity [42]. The scale can be used to identify individuals who vary in their tendency to react to psychological pressure with different degrees of intensity of trait anxiety and to determine current levels of intensity of anxiety states [43]. Each scale has 6 questions with 4-point Likert-type answers. The cutoff point adopted was the median, and scores above this value (positive) indicated signs of trait or state anxiety. The measures were considered maternal anxiety.

Maternal depression

Caregiver depression was assessed via the Beck-II Depression Inventory, created by Beck et al. [44] and translated and validated in Brazil by Gomes-Oliveira et al. [45]. The internal consistency coefficients proved to be adequate (Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.93), sensitivity 70%, specificity 87% [45]. It consists of 21 items related to attitudes and symptoms, and each item can be classified between 0 and 3 according to intensity. The results were calculated according to the score obtained, and a score greater than or equal to the median was classified as a symptom of depression.

Demographic and socioeconomic variables

The variables were categorized considering that the relationship between sociodemographic variables and health outcomes is often non-linear. Using categories allows researchers to model differences without assuming linearity or uniform incremental effects. Also, categorizing can help manage violations of linearity assumptions in regression models and outliers or sparse data in certain age ranges that could distort estimates in a continuous model. It helps detect heterogeneous effects across groups rather than assuming a uniform trend in health field research.

The demographic and socioeconomic variables of the family were collected through categories, except for the variable age of the mother, which was collected as a continuous variable. For this study, the variables were analyzed through 2 categories: age of the mother (adolescent < = 19 years; not adolescent > 19 years), maternal education (0–9 years of schooling; >9 years of schooling), paternal education (0–9 years of schooling; >9 years of schooling), maternal occupation (employed/retired; unemployed), head of household (mother; father; other), and monthly family income (lowest income < = U$200.00; higher income > U$200.00). The data for children included age (months), skin color (white; black/brown/indigenous) and sex (female; male).

Data analysis

Figure 1 shows the theoretical hybrid model initially proposed for the direct and indirect effects of factors associated with lower-than-expected child development for age and unresponsive parenting. In this theoretical model, socioeconomic factors (maternal and paternal education, occupation, maternal age, head of household and monthly family income) were considered more distal determinants (exogenous variables), exerting their effects on the variables of the model, namely, maternal anxiety and depression, parenting practices and child development [11, 15, 46–50]. Both child development and parenting practices are also influenced by maternal anxiety and depression [51–55]. The standard variable of parenting practices was chosen as the latent variable of the theoretical model while keeping the other variables overt (observed). In the descriptive analysis, the frequency distributions, measures of central tendency and dispersion were calculated, considering a 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Next, validation of the latent variable was performed, with exploratory and confirmatory analyses.

Exploratory analysis was performed via principal component analysis, with the Kaiser‒Meyer‒Olkin index (KMO) used as a measure of the adequacy of the factor analysis. Values between 0.5 and 1.0 were considered acceptable [56]. The number of retained factors was defined on the basis of the following criteria: components with eigenvalues greater than 1.0, a Cattell plot (scree plot) and the conceptual meaning of the identified patterns. In the analyses of the factor loadings for the construction of the latent variables, a factor loading ≥ 0.40 or ≤ −0.40 was considered. Confirmatory analyses were performed via standardized estimates for the construction of the latent variables, considering a factor loading greater than 0.4 with p < 0.05, indicating that the correlation between the observed variable and the latent variable is moderately high in magnitude [56, 57].

Next, structural equation models (SEMs) were used to analyze the simultaneous explanatory relationships between the many variables of the study, whether observed or latent (measures derived from observable indicators), according to the initial hypothesized theoretical model (Fig. 1) [35]. The performed analysis based on an estimator used for categorical variables, which presents robustness in the absence of normality and estimates the model parameters using all the information available in the data. The full information maximum likelihood (FIML) was used when missing values were identified in some variables to calculate likelihood for each individual, since FIML uses all available information and assumes that the pattern of missingness is random [35]. It is noteworthy that all study variables had valid data for all participants, except for paternal and maternal education, which was missing data for 2.3% and 5.6% of the sample, respectively, which were imputed. Theta parameterization controlled for differences in residual variances [58]. Additionally, was estimated bias-corrected confidence intervals (CIs) based on bootstrapping.

To evaluate the goodness of fit of the hybrid SEMs, the following criteria were considered: p value greater than 0.05 for the chi-square test; values lower than 0.05 for the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA); values close to or greater than 0.95 for the comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker Lewis index (TLI); and weighted root mean square residual (WRMR) values less than 1 [59]. A significance level of 5% was adopted for the analyses.

To obtain suggestions for changes to the initial hypotheses, the modindices command was used. When the proposed changes were considered plausible from a theoretical point of view, a new model was developed and analyzed if the value of the change index was greater than 10 thousand [57, 59]. In the final model, the direct and indirect effects of the latent and observed variables were evaluated, with a significant effect when p < 0.05.

Additionally, a model was also tested in which the sum of the presence of non-responsive parenting practices was considered in place of the latent variables formed through the observed variables of parenting practices. And in this model, the sum of the presence of non-responsive parenting practices was tested as one of the explanatory variables of the model in which the dependent variable is child development.

Descriptive and exploratory analyses were performed via Stata software, version 17.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, United States). Confirmatory analyses and hybrid SEMs were performed via Mplus software, version 8 [58]. The sampling weight was calculated based on a single-stage cluster sampling design, with the PCF home visitor as the primary sampling unit. The weight corresponds to the inverse of the sampling fraction and reflects the number of children in the program population that each interviewed child represents, given that the selection was conducted through clusters of home visitors [60].

Results

Sample characterization

A total of 301 caregivers of children participating in the PCF were interviewed in 8 administrative regions of the DF. Table 1 summarizes the main characteristics of the participants. The mean age of the caregivers was 29 years and mothers accounted for almost all the caregivers (92.4%). More than half of the parents presented incomplete high school degree or less. Approximately 86% of the caregivers are unemployed, and more than half of the mothers were declared to be the head of the family, 60,8%. With respect to family income, 61.8% of the caregivers earned a monthly family income equivalent to US$ 200.00 (value converted from reais, Brazilian currency, to US dollars in 2024). The children were 15 months old on average, and 69.4% were black, brown or indigenous; almost half of the caregivers were classified with symptoms of depression or anxiety (Table 1). Considering that the vast majority of respondent caregivers were mothers, we present measures such as maternal anxiety and depression.

Validation of the latent variables

In the exploratory analysis of the variables related to parenting practices, there were two main components The KMO index was 0.610, indicating a satisfactory value. Component 1 contributed to 30.1% of the variance in the total information, and component 2 contributed to 21.0%, totaling 51.1% of the variance. Thus, the exploratory analysis indicated the formation of two latent variables, instead of one as proposed in the initial theoretical model: pattern 1 –unresponsive parenting, composed of the observed variables parental encouragement (factorial load = 0.479), quality of the father‒child relationship (loading = 0.533), parental adjustment (loading = 0.474) and family adjustment (loading = 0.418), and pattern 2 – parental practices, composed of the variables parental inconsistency (factorial loading = 0.705) and coercive practices (factorial loading = 0.639). The highest score for both latent variables represented unresponsive parenting.

According to the standardized measure results in the confirmatory analysis, the standardized coefficients of the observed variables that composed the latent variable of pattern 1 of parenting practices were significant and above 0.4, indicating convergent validity. The correlation between the constructs was weak (0.149) and not significant (p > 0.05), suggesting discriminant validity (different measurements for each construct). However, the standardized coefficients of the observed variables that constitute the latent variable of pattern 2 of parenting practices were not significant; in addition, the coefficients were not within the reference values. Thus, only the latent variable of pattern 1 of unresponsive parenting was subsequently used in the hybrid structural equation model, and the observed variables “parental inconsistency” and “coercive practices” were treated separately in this model since the latent variable composed of both variables did not exhibit convergent validity (Table 3).

Hybrid structural equation models

Table 4 presents the standardized coefficients of the final hybrid SEM. According to the analysis, lower-than-expected development for age was positively associated with the presence of maternal anxiety (β = 0.286; 95% CI: 0.111; 0.460; p = 0.001) and with lower paternal education (β = 0.207; 95% CI: 0.046; 0.316; p = 0.006). Higher unresponsive parenting (latent variable) scores were associated with the presence of maternal anxiety (β = 0.230, 95% CI: 0.019; 0.500 p = 0.036) and lower income (β = 0.392, 95% CI: 0.113; 0.636; p < 0.001). The use of coercive practices was positively associated with the presence of anxiety (β = 0.182, 95% CI: 0.032; 0.334; p = 0.019) and with mothers as heads of household (β = 0.157, 95% CI: 0.029; 0.283; p = 0.012) (Table 4).

Discussion

This was the first study to identify that lower CD scores were associated with lower paternal education and with the presence of maternal anxiety for children in the PCF in DF. In addition, we found that the pattern of unresponsive parenting was associated with lower family income and the presence of maternal anxiety. Similarly, coercive parenting practices were associated with the mother being the head of the household and the presence of maternal anxiety. A lower level of paternal education, lower family income, and the mother’s status as head of the family were the socioeconomic factors associated with unresponsive parenting and the risk of developmental delay with respect to age. These data can guide important actions to organize care and promote healthy early childhood, with a focus on children in situations of social vulnerability, such as those assisted by the PCF.

The results of other Brazilian studies and studies in other countries corroborated our main findings. A recent systematic review analyzed data from 21 low- and middle-income countries, including Brazil, and showed that low maternal and paternal education were associated with lower development scores in children up to seven years of age [61]. The same study also revealed that parental education and child development scores are associated, exhibiting a dose‒response gradient [61]. Other studies conducted in South Africa, Bangladesh and Argentina with children and their caregivers revealed that the level of education of caregivers and married parents is a protective factor for CD [62–64]. The chance of children not having a risk of developmental delay is 11 times greater when the education level of the caregiver is high and 5 times greater when the parents are married [63].

Socioeconomic stressors, including low parental education, may delay the development of cognitive skills in children and adolescents [65, 66]. Different contextual and individual mechanisms explain the association between child development and parental education. Socially vulnerable groups have lower education and income and lower access to health and prenatal care services, consequently deteriorating the mother–infant relationship, with a lower frequency of good cognitive stimulation practices and responsive care, which can lead to impaired CD, especially in the neuronal and socioemotional areas [67–71]. Groups with lower access to formal education may have a lower degree of intersection with social and health policies and programs [72].

Socioeconomic status (SES), which is composed of variables such as income, education, occupation, and quality of the environment where individuals live, in childhood is associated with SES in adulthood [73]. Children who have a low SES exhibit compromised brain and behavioral development because they more often experience adversity (e.g., deprivation, threats, abuse and violence) and tend to maintain the cycle, continuing to belong to the population of people with less education, lower income, a higher prevalence of unemployment and a greater chance of receiving income transfer programs, which perpetuates the intergenerational cycle of poverty [73, 74].

Poverty causes increased levels of parental stress and interferes with the quality of relationships and CD [75, 76]. Family income can also influence the quality of parenting [77]. As in our study, in 2019, Bolsoni-Silva et al. reported greater difficulties in parenting activities for aspects of vulnerability that were traditionally identified in a study with families with lower income and education [78]. A lower income was also associated with a greater chance of using unresponsive practices among caregivers of children under 3 years of age in China [79]. In a study with middle-class parents and children with a mean age of 4 years old living in Beijing, China, parents with better socioeconomic conditions used negative parenting practices and more severe punishments less frequently than did parents with financial difficulties [80]. The environment in which children live can directly influence their development, making those who are disciplined nonviolently more likely to have higher CD scores than those exposed to violent discipline [66]. Situations experienced through poverty limit parental potential because, under these adverse conditions, there is often an increase in the psychological distress of parents, with a consequent reduction in the ability to engage in appropriate responses and interactions with their children, which is fundamental to stimulating the growth and development of their children [76, 81].

Mental disorders, especially anxiety and depression, are considered the third leading cause of disability worldwide, especially among women, with a multifactorial etiology and multiple combinations and interactions between risk factors [82]. In our study, maternal anxiety was another factor associated with unresponsive parenting and a higher risk of lower-than-expected development for age, corroborating the impairments resulting from the presence of anxiety in family, social and individual relationships and interactions. Similar data were reported in a systematic review of 14 studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries; the study presented that maternal anxiety is negatively associated with child development [83]. Maternal anxiety can compromise the creation of bonds between caregivers and their children and increase the chances of using dysfunctional parenting practices such as violent discipline and of not performing activities that can stimulate their children, which consequently does not support the adequate development of children in their first years of life [84–86].

The poor mental health of caregivers, especially mothers, may also be strongly associated with marital status. A recent study of single mothers living in extreme poverty in the DF reported that these mothers had a worse quality of life and greater chances of developing anxiety than did mothers with a partner [87]. Compared with those living with a partner, single mothers were 1.92 times more likely to present trait anxiety and 1.87 times more likely to present state anxiety [87].

The presence of anxiety in caregivers not only has consequences for themselves but also affects the development of children since pregnancy, as shown by other studies conducted in middle- and low-income countries, with results similar to those showed in this study [84, 88–91]. A study conducted in Indonesia with depressive caregivers of children up to 59 months of age revealed that the chance of children presenting some developmental risk is approximately 5 times greater than that of children of nondepressive parents [88]. In this study, poor child development was also associated with low maternal education [88]. In a longitudinal study with mothers and children participating in the PCF in 30 Brazilian municipalities, a negative influence of maternal depression on child development was observed [21].

Our results have important implications for improving the effectiveness of PCF actions in the DF. Although we did not find a significant association between parental practices and CD, the sociodemographic and mental health disparities present in the family context are among the main causes of the compromised provision of an NC environment, which in turn influences the quality of parenting practices and child development. PCF multisectoral actions should be strengthened between social assistance, a responsibility of the program, and other sectors, such as health and education, so that families can access necessary assistance programs [29]. In fact, the qualitative analysis of the NC offered by the PCF in the DF showed that the actions of the program have helped in the promotion of responsive parenting; however, topics such as disciplinary practices and mental health need to be present in the training offered to home visitors so that they can better support families that need help [31]. The lack of adequate continuous training and the poor multisectoral actions of the PCF probably contributed to the inadequate mental health and well-being of caregivers and helped to explain the associations of anxiety with CD and unresponsive parenting. Social policies that encourage school attendance and assist in the detection and treatment of mental disorders such as anxiety are actions that can improve the quality of life of caregivers and the prevalence of socially vulnerable children with adequate development.

This study has several limitations that need to be considered when interpreting the results. Because this study presents a cross-sectional design, the results refer to only one point in the study population, therefore, it is not possible to establish cause and effect relationships and it is suggested that future longitudinal studies also test the possible bidirectional relationship between child development and maternal anxiety and depression. This type of study it is necessary to consider possible biases such as response and memory by respondents. Despite the transformation of continuous variables into categorical variables can result in a loss of information, using sociodemographic variables as categorical variables reflects biological and social transitions in health field research. Categorization allows researchers to detect heterogeneous effects across groups rather than assuming a uniform trend. These categories represent qualitative shifts in health risk or behavior that aren’t well-modeled by a smooth, continuous function. Also, public health policies are often targeted based on discrete categories (e.g., age thresholds, education levels, income brackets), not continuous scores.

The sample of this study was restricted to the population of a single municipality in Brazil. On the one hand, this limitation may restrict the generalization of the results; it offers an analysis of social vulnerabilities that can support other municipalities or countries with part of the population living in situations of social vulnerability in contexts with a high rate of urbanization, such as the DF. It is also necessary to consider as a limitation the existence of other confounding variables in the tested relationships that could not be assessed in this study, such as the number of children (parity), presence or absence of a support network, and prematurity. We suggest that future studies include these variables in their analyses.

Some strengths of our study include the use of validated instruments for the Brazilian population to measure anxiety, CD, parenting practices and family adjustment, providing confidence and robustness in the results presented and a better understanding of the relationship between socioeconomic and mental health characteristics of mothers and CD. In addition, the data used are representative of the population assisted by the PCF in the investigated locations. The use of hybrid SEMs also stands out as a potentiality of the study, allowing the evaluation of direct and indirect interrelationships between the variables investigated [58]. Evaluating CD and identifying the determinants capable of influencing it are necessary actions to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals that compose the 2030 agenda of 193 countries. Investing in early childhood and an NC environment allows children to reach their full development by ensuring access to assistance, educational and health services and increasing their chances of receiving a high-quality education and obtaining better jobs and better wages [2].

Conclusion

The results of this study revealed that socioeconomic disparities and the presence of maternal anxiety in caregivers were associated with unresponsive parenting and with the risk of developmental delay among children participating in the PCF in the DF. The implementation of programs that encourage positive parenting practices and adequate child development, such as the PCF in Brazil, may be a way to identify and meet the needs of families, provide knowledge and improve their access to health, education and social assistance services.
