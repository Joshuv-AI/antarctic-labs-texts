Integrated multi-omics reveals the molecular basis of protein storage in developing oat grains
Authors: Mingxue Ji, Zhiyong Zhang, Zhuqing He, Jinghui Liu, Lijun Li, Bingjie Qi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOats (Avena sativa L.) are a nutritionally important cereal crop, but the molecular mechanisms regulating protein and amino acid accumulation during grain development remain poorly understood. Therefore, in this study, two oat varieties (HZ and MC) with significantly different grain protein contents were used as experimental materials. For the grains of these two varieties at different developmental stages, we conducted grain protein content determination, widely targeted metabolite detection, and transcriptome sequencing.ResultsIntegrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of two oat varieties (high-protein HZ and low-protein MC) revealed dynamic changes during grain filling. Key findings include: (1) The 10–20 days after flowering (DAF) period was critical for protein accumulation, with HZ showing higher expression of genes involved in carbon skeleton generation (e.g., isocitrate dehydrogenase, malate dehydrogenase) and amino acid biosynthesis (e.g., glutamate decarboxylase); (2) Weighted gene coexpression network analysis identified modules strongly correlated with protein content, including the blue module (r = 0.92, p < 0.01); (3) KEGG enrichment highlighted central roles for glycolysis, TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism pathways; (4) A regulatory network for protein and amino acid accumulation was constructed, identifying 157 candidate genes and 7 metabolites. Four key genes (GLA, PFK, PGK, TIM) and a transcription factor (AP2/ERF-ERF ) were identified as critical regulators; (5) qRT-PCR validated 10 candidate genes, with expression patterns consistent with RNA-Seq data (R² = 0.72–0.85).ConclusionsThis study elucidates the metabolic and transcriptional networks underlying protein accumulation in oat grains, providing targets for molecular breeding to enhance nutritional quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07624-1.

Background

Oat (Avena sativa L.) is a globally significant gramineous crop, originating from the Middle East (e.g., Iran, Iraq, Syria, and Turkey) and the Iberian Peninsula [1]. Oats are nutrient-dense, containing high levels of proteins (12–20%, three times that of rice), starches, and fats (over 5%, four times that of wheat) [2]. Unlike other cereals that primarily use prolamin as their storage protein, oats utilize globulin, which provides a balanced amino acid profile beneficial for human digestion. This makes oats less allergenic and more suitable for individuals with legume protein allergies [3, 4]. Consequently, oats hold potential as a functional ingredient in food processing. Protein quality is determined by amino acid composition, particularly the content and bioavailability of essential amino acids, which are vital for human growth, reproduction, and maintenance. Oats exhibit a superior amino acid profile compared to staple crops like rice and wheat, making them an excellent amino acid source [5]. Seed development, a complex process involving changes in seed size and nutrient accumulation, directly influences crop yield and quality. Previous studies have attempted to enhance protein and amino acid content in cereal seeds through increased nitrogen fertilization [6]. However, excessive nitrogen use can lead to soil acidification and biodiversity loss in aquatic ecosystems, posing environmental sustainability challenges [7]. Thus, understanding the intrinsic biosynthesis mechanisms of grain proteins and amino acids is essential. Research has shown that transcription factors such as MADS-box [8], MYC [9], and NAC [10] positively regulate grain storage protein content. In contrast, amino acid transporter family genes (CaAATs) may negatively regulate essential and non-essential amino acid levels in chickpeas [11]. The amino acid transporter gene TaATLa1 facilitates the transport of glutamine, glutamate, and aspartate in yeast. Overexpression of TaATLa1 in Arabidopsis thaliana significantly increases leaf amino acid content and seed protein content, while its knockout in wheat disrupts amino acid transport and inhibits growth, demonstrating its role in amino acid source-sink transport in wheat [12]. Similarly, overexpression of GmCGS2, a gene in the methionine synthesis pathway in soybeans, enhances methionine and free amino acid content in seeds [13]. Despite these advances, the regulatory mechanisms underlying protein and amino acid synthesis during oat grain development remain poorly understood. This study investigated two oat varieties with distinct grain protein contents. Protein and amino acid levels were compared across different grain development stages. Using transcriptomic and widely targeted metabolomic datasets, the regulatory network governing protein and amino acid accumulation during oat grain development was analyzed, focusing on gene expression and metabolite dynamics. The findings aim to provide a theoretical foundation for improving oat quality and advancing molecular breeding strategies.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sample preparation

The oat varieties used in this study, HZ (high protein) and MC (low protein), were provided by the Oat Genetic Breeding Research Group of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University. Both varieties were cultivated in 2023 at the Modern Agricultural Science and Technology Park of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University, located in Saihan District, Hohhot City, Inner Mongolia (111.87°E, 40.68°N). Plants with spikelets emerging on the same day were tagged after heading. Grain samples (approximately 10 g) were collected from tagged spikes at 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 days after flowering (DAF), labeled as MC5, MC10, MC15, MC20, MC25, MC30, HZ5, HZ10, HZ15, HZ20, HZ25, and HZ30. After treat-ment with liquid nitrogen for 2 h, the samples were stored at −80 °C. Three biological replicates were prepared for each sample, resulting in a total of 36 samples from 2 varieties across 6 developmental stages. Metabolic profiling and transcriptome sequencing were performed by Wuhan MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (www.metware.cn).

Determination of protein content

The grains used for the assay were dried seeds harvested at 30 days after flowering (30 DAF). These seeds were ground into a powder using a grinding mill and passed through an 80-mesh sieve to ensure sample homogeneity. An accurately weighed amount (0.2 g) of the well-mixed powder was transferred into a digestion tube, followed by the addition of 0.4 g of copper sulfate (CuSO₄), 6 g of potassium sulfate (K₂SO₄), and 20 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄). The mixture was digested in a digestion furnace at 420 °C for 1 h until the liquid in the tube turned into a clear, transparent blue-green color. After thorough cooling, the protein content of all 36 oat grain samples was determined using a fully automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (KN680, Jinan Alva Instrument Co., Ltd.) [14].

Metabolite extraction and UPLC-MS/MS analysis

Metabolite extraction was performed on 6 groups (18 samples in total) of oat grain samples from the HZ and MC varieties at three developmental stages (10, 20, and 30 DAF). After vacuum freeze-drying, the samples were ground into powder. Exactly 0.0500 g of powder was weighed and transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 1200µL of 70% methanol solution pre-cooled to −20 °C. The samples were vortexed every 30 min for a total of 6 times. After centrifugation at 1200 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, and stored in sample vials for UPLC-MS/MS analysis. Data acquisition was conducted using Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) (ExionLC™ AD, https://sciex.com.cn/) coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) [15]. Mass spectrometry data were processed using Analyst 1.6.3 to generate Total Ion Current (TIC) chromatograms for quality control (QC) samples and Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) extracted ion chromatograms (XIC). Metabolite content data were normalized using Z-score normalization in R software (www.r-project.org). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to assess sample separation trends. Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient (r) was used to evaluate biological reproducibility. Heatmaps were generated using the Complex-Heatmap package in R for hierarchical clustering analysis of metabolite accumulation patterns. Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) values were calculated using the Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) model. Significantly differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified based on VIP > 1 and fold change (FC) 2 (up-regulated) or 0.5 (down-regulated). The DAMs were assigned to the KEGG Pathway database. A P -value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

To account for the lag between gene expression and metabolite synthesis, transcriptome sequencing was performed on 12 sample groups (36 samples in total), including additional samples at 5, 15, and 25 DAF. Total RNA was extracted using the modified Trizol method [16]. mRNA with poly A tails was enriched using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Double-stranded cDNA was purified, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with sequencing adapters. Size selection and PCR amplification were performed to construct cDNA libraries. Library quality was assessed, and qualified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina platform. Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled by removing adapter sequences, reads with > 5% undetermined bases, and low quality reads (more than 50% of bases with quality score Q 20). Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (pepsico-oat-ot3098-v2-files-2021) using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million fragments mapped). Pearson correlation analysis and PCA were conducted to evaluate biological reproducibility and sample separation trends. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between developmental stages and varieties. DEGs were screened using log2 Fold Change 1 and FDR 0.05. Functional annotation and enrichment analysis were conducted using Gene On-tology and KEGG Pathway.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The varFilter function in the R genefilter package was used to filter low-expression genes (FPKM < 1) and genes with constant expression levels. Coexpression network modules were constructed using the WGCNA package in R. The correlation coefficient was weighted to ensure a scale-free network distribution. The optimal soft threshold (β-value) was determined using the pick-SoftThreshold function. Modules were clustered based on gene expression correlations, and highly correlated genes were grouped into modules. Modules were correlated with protein and amino acid content from metabolome data. The module with the highest correlation to protein content was selected for functional annotation and enrichment analysis.

Validation of candidate genes by real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

To validate transcriptome results, 10 DEGs with high expression levels and significant KEGG Pathway enrichment were selected for qRT-PCR. Primers were designed using Primer 3 Plus (https://www.primer3plus.com) based on CDS sequences of the selected DEGs and the reference gene (ACT). All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. (Table S1).

Total RNA was extracted from approximately 0.1 g of oat seeds using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Tianjin, China). RNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, while concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with the TIANGEN® FastKing cDNA First Strand Synthesis Kit. The reaction mixture consisted of 2 µL RNA, 2 µL 5× gDNA Buffer, 6 µL RNase-Free ddH₂O, 2 µL 10× King RT Buffer, 1 µL FastKing RT Enzyme Mix, and 2 µL FQ-RT Primer Mix. The reaction was incubated at 42 °C for 15 min, followed by heat inactivation at 95 °C for 3 min. qRT-PCR was carried out using the SGExcel FastSYBR qPCR Mix (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) with the following reaction composition: 10 µL 1× MonAmp™ SYBR® Green qPCR Mix, 0.4 µL each of 10 µM forward and reverse primers, 2 µL cDNA, and 7.2 µL RNase-Free Water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Each sample was run in three technical replicates. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCq method.

Results

Differences in protein content during the grain development process of two oat genotypes

We collected oat seeds at six developmental stages (Fig. 1A) and measured their protein content. Significant differences in protein content were observed between the two varieties across different developmental stages. Both varieties exhibited a gradual increase in protein content, with the high-protein variety HZ showing significantly higher protein levels than MC at all six stages (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1B). The protein accumulation rate followed a “slow-fast-slow” pattern (Fig. 1C). The period from 10 to 15 DAF marked the fastest protein accumulation rate, after which the accumulation rates for both varieties declined continuously (Table S2). Notably, the reduction in the protein accumulation rate from 15 to 20 DAF was less pronounced in HZ compared to MC, which may explain the differences in protein content between the two varieties. Approximately 86% and 88% of the total protein accumulation in HZ and MC, respectively, occurred during these stages. Quality control of oat grain transcriptome data and identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms regulating protein synthesis during oat grain development, RNA-Seq analysis was conducted on grains from both varieties at different developmental stages. The 36 samples yielded 2,372,542,166 raw sequencing reads with an error rate of approximately 0.03%. Quality metrics showed Q30 scores ranging from 93.32% to 94.62% and GC content between 51.36% and 53.24% (Table S3), confirming high sequencing quality suitable for downstream analyses. Pearson correlation analysis of the 36 samples’ sequencing data generated a correlation heatmap (Fig. S4A), demonstrating strong correlations (high coefficients) among biological replicates at each developmental stage. This indicated consistent gene expression patterns, meeting the prerequisites for differential gene analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed tight clustering of biological replicates (Fig. S4B), with Principal Components 1 and 2 explaining 31.9% and 14.4% of total variance, respectively. Notably, the two genotypes showed clear separation at each stage, reflecting significant expression differences throughout grain filling. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using thresholds of log2 Fold Change 1 and FDR 0.05 to examine expression variations between genotypes and developmental stages.

Screening and enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in oat grains

Comparative analysis of DEGs between varieties at identical growth stages revealed substantial transcriptional differences. In the six HZ vs. MC comparisons (HZ5 vs. MC5 to HZ30 vs. MC30), we identified 11,432 (5,580 up/5,852 down), 10,106 (4,692 up/5,451 down), 9,750 (4,516 up/5,234 down), 9,278 (4,011.up/5,267 down), 8,637 (3,836 up/4,801 down), and 9,511 (4,171 up/5,340 down) DEGs respectively (Fig. S5A). Notably, 3,777 DEGs were consistently differentially expressed across all six stages (Fig. S5B). Developmental stage comparisons within each variety (using HZ5/MC5 as controls) showed dynamic expression patterns. The ten comparison groups (HZ10-HZ5 to MC30-MC5) exhibited: 1,308 (768 up/540 down), 6,245 (3,318 up/2,927 down), 12,043 (6,082.up/5,961 down), 13,204 (6,726 up/6,478 down), 16,663 (8,410 up/8,253 down), 1,787 (1,357 up/430 down), 9,138 (5,111 up/4,027 down), 11,604 (6,165 up/5,439 down), 15,360 (7,638 up/7,722 down), and 18,424 (8,943 up/9,481 down) DEGs respectively (Fig. S6A, B). Core DEG sets comprised 764 (HZ) and 1,069 (MC) genes shared across all developmental comparisons (Fig. S6C, D).

To analyze the potential functions of these DEGs, we conducted KEGG Pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. S7−9). Among all the comparison groups, the most frequently annotated pathways were Starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500), Pentose phosphate pathway (ko00030), Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis (ko00010), Pyruvate metabolism (ko00620), Tyrosine metabolism (ko00350), Tryptophan metabolism (ko00380), Nitrogen metabolism (ko00910), Citrate cycle (ko00020), and Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (ko00520).

It is worth noting that the DEGs in the HZ group were more significantly enriched in pathways related to the synthesis and accumulation of proteins and amino acids, such as Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (ko00520), Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis (ko00010), Cyanoamino acid metabolism (ko00460), and Pyruvate metabolism (ko00620). This might be the potential reason why the protein content in HZ was higher than that in MC.

In addition, these DEGs were also significantly enriched in pathways related to the synthesis and metabolism of secondary metabolites and fatty acids, such as Flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941), Glycerolipid metabolism (ko00561), Fatty acid metabolism (ko01212), Fatty acid elongation (ko00062), Fatty acid degradation (ko00071), and Biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (ko01040). This might imply that there were potential associations among the metabolic pathways of proteins, flavonoids, and fatty acids.

Genes responsive to oat grain development

To characterize gene expression dynamics during oat grain development, we performed K-means clustering on differentially expressed genes (DEGs), yielding ten distinct expression clusters (Fig. 6). Classes 1 and 3 showed stable expression across developmental stages but significant inter-varietal differences - Class 1 was higher in MC while Class 3 predominated in HZ, suggesting potential roles in protein synthesis/accumulation. Classes 4 and 5 exhibited parallel downregulation during grain maturation in both varieties, possibly negatively regulating protein metabolism. Conversely, Classes 8 and 9 displayed progressive upregulation with development, likely associated with grain maturation processes. Class 7 DEGs remained stable in HZ but sharply declined in MC, potentially contributing to inter-varietal protein content differences. Class 2 DEGs peaked at 20 DAF in both varieties, mirroring protein accumulation kinetics and implicating direct involvement in protein biosynthesis. KEGG Pathway enrichment analysis was separately performed on the DEGs in these clusters (Fig. S11). Identification of the amino acid biosynthesis pathway in oat grains

Plant amino acid biosynthesis utilizes carbon skeletons derived from glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway, and citrate cycle intermediates. Integrated analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic data from developing oat grains identified 157 candidate genes encoding 64 pathway enzymes, along with seven differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs):N -α-Acetyl-L-ornithine, L-Arginine, L-Ornithine, L-Histidine, L-Tyrosine, L-Tryptophan, and S-(5’-Adenosyl)-L-homocysteine. The dataset included 27 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) encoding eight glycolytic enzymes, 10 DEGs for four pentose phosphate pathway enzymes, and 33 DEGs for eight citrate cycle enzymes, all potentially regulating amino acid carbon skeleton availability. An additional 87 DEGs encoding 44 enzymes directly participated in amino acid biosynthesis, influencing DAM accumulation.

In summary, The accumulation of protein and amino acids in oat grains is regulated through multiple metabolic pathways and genetic mechanisms. Enhanced glycolysis and TCA cycle activity in the high-protein variety HZ supply ample ATP and carbon skeletons (e.g., α-ketoglutarate, oxaloacetate) for amino acid synthesis. Key enzymes such as PDHA, NADP-IDH, and MDH2 are significantly upregulated in HZ, supporting efficient carbon flow. In contrast, the urea cycle is more active in MC, leading to nitrogen waste via amino acid degradation and reduced protein substrates. The shikimate pathway is strengthened in HZ, promoting the synthesis of aromatic amino acids. These interconnected mechanisms collectively contribute to the higher protein and amino acid content in HZ grains. Using oat Actin as an internal reference, we validated ten randomly selected candidate genes involved in amino acid biosynthesis via qRT-PCR.

AVESA.00001b.r3.7Ag0001223 encodes the E1 alpha subunit of pyruvate dehydrogenase, which catalyzes the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA—a key substrate entering the TCA cycle. This process ultimately leads to the production of α-ketoglutarate and oxaloacetate, playing an essential role in amino acid synthesis and protein accumulation. In HZ, the expression of AVESA.00001b.r3.7Ag0001223 increased during the early (5–15 DAF) and late (30 DAF) grain development stages, with an average expression level 1.6 times that of MC. However, its expression was lower in HZ than in MC at 20–30 DAF (Fig. 8C).

AVESA.00001b.r3.2Ag0002073 encodes γ-aminobutyric acid transaminase, which converts GABA to succinate semialdehyde. This metabolite is further oxidized to succinate by succinate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase encoded by AVESA.00001b.r3.2Dg0001025, thereby feeding into the TCA cycle. Both genes were up-regulated during grain development, with expression levels in HZ averaging 1.2–1.5 times those in MC (Fig. 8D, E).

Glutamate decarboxylase, encoded by AVESA.00001b.r3.7Dg0000915, catalyzes the conversion of L-glutamate to GABA. Up-regulation of this enzyme accelerates glutamate metabolism, promotes TCA cycle flux, supplies carbon skeletons for the synthesis of aspartate, lysine, arginine, and other amino acids, and simultaneously regenerates glutamate. The expression of AVESA.00001b.r3.7Dg0000915 was on average 1.1 times higher in HZ than in MC, exhibiting a fluctuating expression pattern (up-regulation followed by down-regulation and then up-regulation) in both varieties (Fig. 8F).

Alcohol dehydrogenase, encoded by AVESA.00001b.r3.1Ag0002942, catalyzes the oxidation of ethanol to acetaldehyde, which is subsequently converted to acetate by aldehyde dehydrogenase encoded by AVESA.00001b.r3.5Cg0003039. Acetate can then be converted to acetyl-CoA—a key TCA cycle substrate—catalyzed by acetyl-CoA synthetase. The expression levels of AVESA.00001b.r3.1Ag0002942 and AVESA.00001b.r3.5Cg0003039 were 2.2–2.4 times higher in HZ than in MC across all stages (Fig. 8G, H). AVESA.00001b.r3.1Ag0002942 showed an initial up-regulation followed by down-regulation during grain development, while AVESA.00001b.r3.5Cg0003039 exhibited a general up-regulation trend.

Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, encoded by AVESA.00001b.r3.4Cg0003498, uses oxaloacetate as a substrate to produce phosphoenolpyruvate—a precursor for aromatic amino acids. Phosphoenolpyruvate also participates in glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, yielding pyruvate (a precursor for alanine-family amino acids) and 3-phosphoglycerate (a precursor for serine-family amino acids). The expression of AVESA.00001b.r3.4Cg0003498 was 8.6 times higher on average in HZ than in MC. It was consistently up-regulated in HZ but down-regulated in MC (Fig. 8I), suggesting that its differential expression may be a key factor contributing to the differences in protein content between the two varieties.

Malate synthase, encoded by AVESA.00001b.r3.2Cg0002157, is involved in multiple metabolic pathways. It catalyzes the synthesis of malate from acetyl-CoA, which can directly enter the TCA cycle, while also competing with the cycle for substrates. In the transcriptomic dataset, the expression of AVESA.00001b.r3.2Cg0002157 was consistently higher in HZ than in MC. However, qRT-PCR results showed the opposite trend (Fig. 8J). The reason for this discrepancy requires further investigation.

Linear regression analysis of qRT-PCR and RNA-Seq data for these 10 genes yielded R² values of 0.85 (HZ) and 0.72 (MC) (Fig. 8K, L), confirming the reliability of transcriptome data for studying oat grain protein and amino acid synthesis-related genes.

Discussion

Differences in protein content during the grain development process of two oat varieties

Oat is a nutritionally rich whole grain, abundant in high-quality carbohydrates mainly composed of starch and high-quality proteins with a balanced amino acid profile. As people increasingly pursue dietary health, oats, a grain with high health benefits, have gradually found their way onto the tables of more families, leading to a rise in oat consumption. Currently, there are relatively few studies on improving the nutritional quality of oat grains and enhancing the quality of oat grains is a research direction that requires significant attention. It was found that the protein content of peony seeds was lower than that of soluble sugars and crude fat, and that the protein content consistently increased during seed development [17]. In this study, the protein contents of grains at six developmental stages of HZ and MC were measured, and it was observed that the protein contents increased with the development of grains. The grain protein accumulation rate was the fastest from 10 to 20 days after flowering, which is a critical period for oat grain protein accumulation.

Screening of differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) in oat grains and KEGG pathway enrichment

Metabolomics has been widely applied in the research on the nutritional quality of crops. Previous studies have shown that a large number of flavonoids, lipids, and amino acids and their derivatives were detected in developing soy-bean seeds [18]. Among the metabolites of wheat, maize, rice, millet, and sorghum grains, 201 flavonoid substances were identified [19]. Significantly different enriched metabolic pathways matter flavonoid biosynthesis, anthocyanin biosynthesis, and flavonoid and flavonol biosynthesis are significantly different enriched metabolic pathways between black rice, red rice, and glutinous rice [20]. To date, there have been no reported studies on metabolite changes during the development process of oat grains.

Based on broad-targeted metabolomics data, this study analyzed the metabolic profiles of developing oat grains with significant differences in protein content between the two varieties and identified a total of 1,178 metabolites belonging to different categories. Among them, 178 DAMs were common metabolites in the three comparison groups (HZ10 vs. MC10, HZ20 vs. MC20, and HZ30 vs. MC30) of different varieties at the same stage. These mainly included 6 amino acids and their derivatives, 17 lipids, and 97 flavonoids. Among the metabolites with differential accumulation only at 10 DAF, lipids (36 kinds) were the most abundant, while among the metabolites with differential accumulation at 30 DAF, the number of amino acids and their derivatives reached 17. The KEGG Pathway enrichment of DAMs in the three comparison groups indicated that the arginine biosynthesis pathway was significantly enriched at both 20 and 30 DAF, the amino acid biosynthesis pathway was significantly enriched at 30 DAF, and the linoleic acid metabolism pathway was significantly enriched at both 10 DAF and 20 DAF. Therefore, it is believed that the synthesis of fatty acids in oat grains occurs in the early stage of grain filling, while the accumulation of amino acids occurs in the later stage of grain filling. Combining with the accumulation pattern of oat grain proteins, it is considered that the amino acid accumulation in the later stage of grain development is not only related to amino acid biosynthesis but may also be associated with the slowdown of the protein accumulation rate.

Taking 10 DAF as a control, the changes in DAMs at different developmental stages of HZ and MC were analyzed respectively. The results showed that fewer DAMs were identified in the HZ group (HZ10 vs. HZ20 and HZ10 vs. HZ30) than in the MC group (MC10 vs. MC20 and MC10 vs. MC30), and the types of amino acids and their derivatives identified in the HZ group (6 kinds) were fewer than those in MC (12 kinds). This might be because the protein biosynthesis activity in HZ was relatively vigorous, and amino acids and their derivatives were consumed in large quantities. Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis uses some intermediate products in the shikimate pathway as raw materials and plays an important role in the biosynthesis of lignin, flavonoids, coumarins, and phenolic substances [21]. β-Alanine is a non-protein amino acid, and studies have shown that it can act as a stress response molecule and play a role in the alleviation of plant abiotic stress [22]. The metabolites in MC were more enriched in pathways unrelated to protein synthesis, such as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and β-alanine biosynthesis, which might explain why MC has a rich variety of amino acids but a low protein content. In contrast, the metabolites in HZ were more involved in the biosynthesis of valine, leucine, and isoleucine, as well as the biosynthesis of pantothenate and CoA.

Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in oat grains and KEGG pathway enrichment

In this study, considering that gene expression always precedes the production of metabolites, oat grain samples at 5 DAF, 15 DAF, and 25 DAF were added on the basis of the above three periods (10 DAF, 20 DAF, and 30 DAF) for transcriptome sequencing. In the six comparison groups (HZ5 vs. MC5, HZ10 vs. MC10, HZ15 vs. MC15, HZ20 vs. MC20, HZ25 vs. MC25, and HZ30 vs. MC30) for. pairwise comparisons between HZ and MC at the same period, it was found that the number of DEGs between the two varieties gradually decreased with grain development. KEGG Pathway enrichment analysis showed that pathways such as tyrosine metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, nitrogen metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, the citrate cycle, and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism were significantly enriched. The genes in these pathways might play a regulatory role in the synthesis and accumulation of oat grain proteins. In addition, the two pathways related to amino acid biosynthesis, namely tyrosine metabolism and pyruvate metabolism, were only enriched in the later stage of grain development, indicating that the genes in these two pathways were mainly expressed in the later stage of grain development. Combining with the quantitative data of multiple amino acids in the metabolome, it is believed that the accumulation of amino acids occurs in the later stage of grain development. In the pairwise comparisons of different periods of the same variety, the numbers of DEGs in both the HZ group (HZ10 vs. HZ5, HZ15 vs. HZ5, HZ20 vs. HZ5, HZ25 vs. HZ5, and HZ30 vs. HZ5) and the MC group (MC10 vs. MC5, MC15 vs. MC5, MC20 vs. MC5, MC25 vs. MC5, and MC30 vs. MC5) increased with grain development. The DEGs in the five comparison groups of the high-protein variety HZ were more enriched in pathways such as photosynthesis-antenna proteins, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, cyanoamino acid metabolism, and pyruvate metabolism. The changes in the expression levels of genes in these pathways might be one of the reasons for the higher protein content in HZ grains compared to MC.

In previous studies, the transcripts related to the biosynthesis of fatty acids, flavonoids, and phenylpropanoids had relatively high expression levels in the early stage of maturity [23]. In this study, it was also found that a large number of DEGs were enriched in pathways such as the biosynthesis of flavonoids, fatty acid elongation, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, and biosynthesis of fatty acids. Further research on the accumulation of flavonoids and lipids in oat grains will be considered in the future.

Co-expression modules and key metabolic pathways

The DEGs identified by transcriptomics were divided into 12 co-expression modules with correlations with 8 traits through WGCNA. KEGG Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted on the genes in the 5 modules (blue, turquoise, red, purple, and brown) that had significant or extremely significant correlations with the protein and amino acid contents of oat grains. It was found that most of the genes in these modules were significantly enriched in pathways directly or indirectly related to the citrate cycle (ko00020), pyruvate metabolism (ko00620), and alanine and amino acid biosynthesis. Notably, the ubiquitin mediated protein degradation pathway significantly enriched some DEGs in the red and brown modules. The ubiquitin proteolytic system plays an important role in basic cellular processes such as cell cycle regulation, signal transduction, and immune response by degrading protein [24]. Previous studies have shown that the N-degradation pathway related to the ubiquitin-proteasome system can be linked to the amino-terminal residues of proteins to regulate protein stability and thus regulate plant growth and development [25]. However, there is currently no direct evidence indicating that ubiquitin-mediated protein degra- dation has a regulatory effect on amino acid and protein accumulation. The hub genes AVESA.00001b.r3.5Ag0002754 and AVESA.00001b.r3.7Dg0002832 in the.

red and brown modules encode endoplasmic reticulum oxidoreductase-2 (ERO2) and Argonaute 2 (AGO2), respectively. In Arabidopsis, AGO2 is a core component of RNA interference (RNAi), plays a key role in the plant’s immune response to pathogens, and is regulated by methylated arginine [26–28]. Endoplasmic reticulum oxidoreductase (ERO) is a donor of disulfide bonds in the endoplasmic reticulum and can transfer disulfide bonds from its internal active center to the external active center, thereby participating in the oxidative folding of proteins [29]. In summary, it is speculated that AGO2 and ERO2 have a regulatory effect on the accumulation of oat proteins and amino acids.

Glycolysis and energy supply

Combining transcriptome and metabolome data, the amino acid synthesis pathway in oat grains was proposed, which included 157 DEGs encoding 64 enzymes and 7 amino acids and their derivatives. Three genes (AVESA.00001b.r3.7-Ag0001223, AVESA.00001b.r3.3Ag0001743, and AVESA.00001b.r3.7Cg0003088) were selected from the 157 candidate genes, along with 7 other randomly selected DEGs, for real-time quantitative PCR to verify the accuracy of gene expression levels and transcriptome sequencing data.

The glycolysis process is an important energy supply process for cells. Through glycolysis, organisms can utilize glucose to generate ATP required for various life activities. In addition, the intermediate products of the glycolysis process, such as 3-phosphoglycerate, phosphoenolpyruvate, and pyruvate, are precursor substances for the synthesis of various amino acids such as alanine, valine, tryptophan, serine, and phenylalanine, and play an important role in the synthesis of plant amino acids and proteins. Novel.11353 was annotated as 6-phosphofructokinase (PFK), which catalyzes the interconversion between 6-phosphofructose and 1,6-fructose diphosphate and participates in the glycolysis pathway to provide energy for the synthesis of grain storage substances [30].

The phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK) encoded by AVESA.00001b.r3.6Ag0000095 is the first enzyme to catalyze the production of ATP in the glycolysis process and is an important energy supply step. Silencing this gene can significantly reduce the protein content in rice plants [31]. The expression levels of these two genes in HZ grains at different developmental stages were higher than those in MC. This differential expression might be a key factor contributing to the differences in protein content between the two varieties. The 2-phosphoglycerate isomerase (TIM) encoded by AVESA.00001b.r3.1Cg0000331 had a much higher expression level in HZ than in MC and extremely high expression levels during the period of rapid grain protein accumulation (10–20 DAF). However, there is currently insufficient evidence to directly indicate that the expression of this gene is related to the accumulation of proteins and amino acids. It is speculated that TIM plays a positive regulatory role in protein synthesis and accumulation by promoting the glycolysis pathway.

TCA cycle and carbon skeleton supply

The citrate cycle is one of the most important energy metabolism pathways in plants. Numerous studies have demonstrated the role of the citrate cycle in plant photosynthesis, growth and development, and stress resistance [32]. AVESA.00001b.r3.7Ag0001223 encodes pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 component α–subunit (PDHA), which catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA to participate in the citrate cycle, representing a transition reaction between the glycolysis pathway and the citrate cycle and is closely related to grain yield [33]. AVESA.00001b.r3.3Ag0001743 was annotated as NADP-isocitrate dehydrogenase (NADP-IDH), which catalyzes the conversion of isocitrate to α-ketoglutarate required for glutamate synthesis while generating nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) [34]. Studies have shown that NADP-ICDH located in peroxisomes participates in stomatal movement [35]. AVESA.00001b.r3.7Cg0003088 was annotated as malate dehydrogenase 2 (MDH2), and the oxaloacetate anaplerotic reaction catalyzed by this enzyme is an important regulatory link in the citrate cycle [36]. In this study, the expression patterns of PDHA and MDH2 genes were consistent, that is, they were continuously upregulated with grain development and had higher expression levels in HZ than in MC. The accuracy of the expression levels was verified by real-time quantitative PCR. It is believed that the abundant expression of these enzymes in HZ provides a large number of carbon skeletons for amino acid biosynthesis in HZ, namely α-ketoglutarate, oxaloacetate, and acetyl-CoA, thereby providing sufficient substrates for protein synthesis.

Urea cycle and nitrogen metabolism

The urea cycle is an important regulatory link in plant nitrogen metabolism. Starting from the amino donor glutamine, arginine is generated through the catalysis of ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC), argininosuccinate synthase (ARGG), and argininosuccinate lyase (ARGH), and then arginase catalyzes the generation of ornithine and urea [37, 38]. Almost all amino acids in plants can be degraded through the urea cycle, and the urea produced by the urea cycle is a good nitrogen source for plants [39]. In addition, acetylornithine aminotransferase (ARGD) also participates in the process of generating ornithine from glutamate. Some scholars have found that ARGD is related to chlorophyll synthesis. In cells with elevated ornithine levels, ARGD forms a complex with the enzyme Gun4 in the chlorophyll synthesis pathway, hindering chlorophyll biosynthesis [40]. In this study, AVESA.00001b.r3.7Ag0000253 encodes acetylornithine aminotransferase (ARGD). The expression level of this gene gradually decreased with the development of HZ and MC grains, and its expression level in MC was always higher than that in HZ. The accumulation trends of its downstream products, N -α-acetyl-L-ornithine, ornithine, and arginine, were basically consistent. The contents of N -α-acetyl-L-ornithine, ornithine, and arginine in HZ were lower than those in MC at 10 DAF, and more accumulation occurred in HZ with grain development. The genes encoding ornithine carbamoyltransferase (OTC), AVESA.00001b.r3.6Ag0002064 and AVESA.00001b.r3.6Cg0001071, as well as the gene encoding argininosuccinate synthase (ARGG), AVESA.00001b.r3.1Dg0001225, in the urea cycle were downregulated with grain development, and their expression levels in MC were higher than those in HZ, indicating that the urea cycle in MC might be more vigorous than that in HZ. The period of 10 DAF is the fastest stage for oat grain protein accumulation. During this period, excessive accumulation of ornithine in MC might lead to the obstruction of chlorophyll synthesis, thereby having a certain negative impact on the accumulation of photosynthetic products. In the middle and late stages of grain development, although the accumulation amount of ornithine in MC decreased, the expression levels of the genes encoding argininosuccinate synthase (AVESA.00001b.r3.1Dg0001225) and ornithine carbamoyltransferase (AVESA.00001b.r3.6Ag0002064) in the downstream were still at relatively high levels and higher than those in HZ. The vigorous urea cycle degraded a large number of other types of amino acids, reducing the substrate level for protein synthesis. It is speculated that this is one of the reasons for the higher protein content in HZ than in MC. In addition, the gene encoding glutamine synthase (GLA), AVESA.00001b.r3.4Dg0001645, which is upstream of the urea cycle, was downregulated with grain development, and its expression level in HZ was much higher than that in MC. Glutamine is an amino donor in plants, and the glutamine in MC was largely consumed by the urea cycle, while the glutamine in HZ might supply more amino groups for the synthesis of other amino acids due to less consumption by the urea cycle.

Shikimate pathway and aromatic amino acids

The shikimate pathway is the synthesis route for aromatic amino acids among essential amino acids. The carbon skeletons of aromatic amino acids and phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) generate 3-deoxy-D -arabino-heptulosonate 7-phosphate (DAHP) through 3-deoxy-D -arabino-heptulosonate 7-phosphate synthase (AROG), and then flow into the shikimate pathway. AROG is the key rate-limiting enzyme in the shikimate pathway [41, 42]. In this study, the expression level of AVESA.00001b.r3.5Dg0000494 encoding DAHP synthase was higher in HZ than.in MC. In addition, AVESA.00001b.r3.7Dg0002212 encoding tryptophan synthase β-subunit (TRPB), novel.3229 encoding prephenate dehydrogenase (TYRATT), and AVESA.00001b.r3.1Dg0000759,AVESA.00001b.r3.2Dg0003613, and AVESA.00001b.r3.5Ag0003291 encoding prephenate dehydratase 6 (ADH6) in the shikimate pathway all had relatively high expression levels in HZ. The final accumulation amounts of their products, tryptophan and tyrosine, at 30 DAF were much higher in HZ than in MC. Phenylalanine was not detected. Tryptophan synthase is usually an β-subunit complex. Studies have shown that TRPB can effectively catalyze the synthesis of tryptophan under the joint action of the complex, while its catalytic activity will be greatly reduced when independent TRPB lacks the α-subunit [43]. In the transcriptome dataset constructed in this study, the α-subunit of tryptophan synthase was not annotated.

The content of S -(5′-adenosyl)-L-homocysteine decreased with grain development in MC, while it increased with grain development in HZ, and the content in HZ was higher than that in MC. Among its upstream related genes, the gene encoding S -adenosylmethionine synthase (MAT), novel.1197, had a higher expression level in each stage of HZ grain development than in MC. In addition, this gene was also annotated as an AP2/ERF-ERF transcription factor. Many studies have revealed the regulatory roles of AP2/ERF-ERF transcription factor family genes in aspects such as plant flowering stage [44], pigment metabolism [45], fruit development [46, 47], and stress defense [48–50]. Therefore, it is believed that AP2/ERF-ERF encoded by novel.1197 might play a regulatory role in the accumulation of proteins and amino acids during oat grain development.

Conclusions

This study elucidates the molecular mechanisms underlying protein and amino acid accumulation in oat grains through integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. The 10–20 DAF period was identified as critical for protein accumulation. We identified key metabolic pathways (glycolysis, TCA cycle, amino acid biosynthesis) and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that contribute to the superior protein content in high-protein variety HZ. Notably, genes involved in carbon skeleton generation (e.g., isocitrate dehydrogenase, malate dehydrogenase) and amino acid synthesis (e.g., glutamate decarboxylase) showed elevated expression in HZ.

In summary, we selected AGO2, ERO2, GLA, PFK, PGK, TIM as the final candidate genes, and novel.1197 (AP2/ERF-ERF) as the candidate transcription factor. These genes might regulate protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, energy metabolism in the glycolysis pathway, chlorophyll synthesis, and the nitrogen cycle in plants, thereby participating in the accumulation of amino acids and proteins in oat grains. Subsequently, attempts will be made to construct overexpression Arabidopsis lines of these genes, and those with significant regulatory effects on protein content will be selected for homologous transformation in oats. Our findings provide valuable insights for oat quality improvement through molecular breeding strategies.
