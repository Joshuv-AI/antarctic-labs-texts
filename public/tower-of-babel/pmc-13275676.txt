Laparoscopic transabdominal approach for radical excision of low-lying presacral cyst: a case report and narrative mini−review
Authors: Xirang Wang, Maoxing Liu, Fei Tan, Yuxiang Li, Xiaofeng Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Laparoscopic techniques offer minimally invasive advantages; however, the feasibility and safety of the transabdominal approach for low-lying cysts remain to be fully evaluated. Introduction

Presacral cysts represent a relatively rare clinical entity, originating from embryonic remnants or congenital anomalies within the presacral space. They are predominantly benign cystic lesions, including subtypes such as epidermoid cysts and tailgut cysts (1). Due to their deep-seated location and complex anatomical relationships, often adjacent to the sacral plexus, rectum, and vascular structures, preoperative imaging diagnosis poses significant challenges, leading to potential misdiagnosis or oversight. Clinical manifestations are mostly occult, with only a minority of patients presenting with pelvic pain or mass effect (2, 3). Currently, complete surgical excision is regarded as the only effective curative method, aiming to prevent recurrence and potential malignant transformation. Although traditional open surgery can achieve complete resection, it is associated with considerable surgical trauma, prolonged postoperative recovery, and increased risk of complications (4).

Minimally invasive laparoscopic techniques have been widely applied in the field of pelvic surgery in recent years, offering advantages including reduced intraoperative blood loss, less postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and accelerated recovery (5, 6). However, the feasibility and safety of the transabdominal approach for low-lying presacral cysts remain subjects of debate (4). Furthermore, research on laparoscopic approaches for presacral cysts has primarily focused on high or mid-level lesions. Data on the technical feasibility and long-term outcomes for low-lying cysts are scarce, owing to factors such as limited operative space and increased anatomical complexity, resulting in a lack of consensus in clinical guidelines (7).

This study, through a case report of laparoscopic transabdominal radical excision for a low-lying presacral epidermoid cyst combined with a literature review, aims to evaluate the technical characteristics, perioperative safety, and recurrence risk associated with this approach, thereby providing evidence-based medical support for the minimally invasive management of low-lying presacral cysts.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old female patient was admitted with a presacral mass discovered during a routine physical examination six months prior. The mass was incidentally found on a pelvic ultrasound during a gynecological check-up. The patient reported no specific discomfort such as abdominal distension or pain. Her bowel habits consisted of dry, yellow stools every 2–3 days, with no notable changes in urination. Her medical history included constipation for nearly 10 years and a previous cesarean section, with no other significant underlying conditions.

Physical examination revealed a flat abdomen with a lower abdominal surgical scar from the prior operation. The abdomen was soft throughout, with no palpable abnormal masses or hepatosplenomegaly. No shifting dullness was detected. Bowel sounds were normal at 4–5 per minute. Digital rectal examination identified a soft mass posterolateral to the rectum.

Laboratory tests, including complete blood count, biochemistry, tumor markers, coagulation profile, and infectious disease panel, showed no significant abnormalities.

Imaging studies: Contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT (Figure 1) revealed a cystic, low-density lesion in the presacral region, measuring approximately 8.3 cm × 6.2 cm with a pre-contrast CT value of about 28 HU, demonstrating no significant enhancement. Pelvic MRI (Figure 2) showed the cystic lesion exhibiting slightly low signal intensity on T1WI, slightly high signal on T2WI, markedly high signal on DWI, and low signal on the ADC map. Markedly high DWI signal and low ADC signal indicate restricted diffusion, consistent with a keratin−containing cyst (epidermoid cyst). Main differential diagnoses included tailgut cyst (usually multilocular, without restricted diffusion) and dermoid cyst (often containing fat). The superior pole of the cyst was at the level of the coccyx. The sagittal T2−weighted MRI (Figure 2) demonstrated that the superior pole of the cyst was at the level of the coccyx. According to the Chinese Expert Consensus on Standardized Diagnosis and Treatment of Presacral Cysts (2024 Edition), a low−lying presacral cyst is defined as one with its superior pole at or below the S4 level. Thus, the present case was classified as a low−lying presacral cyst. The most adherent point was at the coccygeal level. Multidisciplinary consultation (orthopedics, neurosurgery) concluded that connection to the spinal canal was unlikely. Colonoscopy revealed no significant abnormalities.

Diagnoses: presacral cyst, constipation, and status post cesarean section

Following multidisciplinary discussion and thorough communication with the patient and her family, who requested surgical removal of the presacral mass, a decision was made to proceed with laparoscopic exploration under general anesthesia. The patient was informed of potential risks of cyst enlargement, infection, or rare malignant transformation; she opted for surgical excision.

Surgical procedure: A five-port technique was employed. A 10-mm trocar was placed at the supraumbilical margin, a 12-mm trocar was placed 2 fingerbreadths medial to the right anterior superior iliac spine, 5-mm trocars were placed at the left and right midclavicular lines at the umbilical level, and an additional 5-mm trocar was placed at the left anti-McBurney’s point. Upon entering the abdominal cavity, no ascites was noted. The liver, spleen, gastrointestinal tract, omentum, and mesentery appeared smooth and free of nodules. Using an ultrasonic scalpel, the peritoneum at the junction of the sigmoid colon and the pelvic inlet was incised. Dissection proceeded along the mesorectal plane towards the anal side, revealing the presacral mass located posterolateral to the rectum. The mass was cystic, approximately 10 cm in diameter. Blunt and sharp dissection along its capsule was performed with careful preservation of the pelvic floor nerves, vasculature, and rectum. The bilateral hypogastric nerves and pelvic splanchnic nerves were identified and preserved. The mass was completely excised without rupture, and its capsule remained intact. The specimen was placed in a retrieval bag, shown to the family after extraction, and sent for pathological examination. The surgical field was inspected and showed no active bleeding. The total operative time was 176 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 10 mL. No intraoperative complications or cyst rupture occurred. After irrigation, the pelvic floor peritoneum was closed with absorbable sutures to prevent small bowel herniation or adhesion formation (as shown in Figure 3). To prevent localized fluid collection from oozing or exudation, one drain was placed in the presacral space (beneath the sutured peritoneum) and another drain was placed in the pelvic cavity (above the peritoneum). The drains were removed when the output decreased to less than 50 mL per day with clear serous fluid. The patient tolerated oral intake well on postoperative day 1. The pelvic drain was removed on postoperative day 2 and the presacral drain on postoperative day 3. She was discharged on postoperative day 4 without any complications (Clavien−Dindo grade 0).

At the 3-month follow-up, she recovered well. Imaging studies showed no evidence of fluid collection or recurrence, and her constipation had improved. She reported no new−onset voiding or sexual dysfunction.

Discussion and conclusions

A presacral epidermoid cyst is a rare benign neoplastic condition, primarily composed of epidermal tissue, which can cause local compressive symptoms such as pain, or difficulties with urination or defecation. Due to its deep-seated location and proximity to critical anatomical structures (e.g., the sacral nerve plexus and rectum), surgical excision is the primary treatment, necessitating meticulous technique to avoid complications such as nerve injury or cyst rupture (8). In the present case, the mass was low-lying, and a transabdominal laparoscopic resection was successfully performed without postoperative complications, supporting the feasibility of this approach in selected patients. Rationale for the anterior laparoscopic approach: The anterior (transabdominal) laparoscopic approach was chosen over posterior/perineal or combined approaches for the following reasons: (1) it provides superior visualization of the rectum and pelvic autonomic nerves (hypogastric nerves and pelvic splanchnic nerves), facilitating complete resection with nerve preservation; (2) it avoids a perineal incision, thereby reducing the risk of wound infection, sinus formation, and postoperative pain; (3) it allows en bloc resection while maintaining pelvic floor integrity; (4) in low−lying cysts (below S4), the transabdominal approach still enables adequate access by mobilizing the rectum and dissecting along the mesorectal plane. These considerations are consistent with the 2024 Chinese Expert Consensus (9). Notably, cyst rupture is a primary risk in laparoscopic surgery; however, literature indicates that even if rupture occurs, the incidence of severe complications such as chemical peritonitis is very low (10).

Robotic−assisted resection of presacral cysts has been reported in small series, offering better dexterity in the deep pelvis. However, laparoscopy remains more cost−effective and widely available. In this case, laparoscopy achieved complete resection without robotic assistance. We employed a laparoscopic transabdominal approach, emphasizing comprehensive preoperative evaluation (including imaging to localize the cyst and exclude malignancy) and meticulous intraoperative technique. The patient recovered uneventfully postoperatively, with no adverse events such as nerve injury or infection, highlighting the applicability of laparoscopic surgery in the low presacral region. In conjunction with the literature, the success of laparoscopic surgery relies on surgeon experience, appropriate instrument selection (e.g., ultrasonic scalpel, suction systems to prevent spillage of cyst contents), and multidisciplinary collaboration to address the anatomical complexity of the presacral space (11). This case also illustrates the importance of individualized decision-making: selecting laparoscopy over other approaches based on cyst size, location, and patient symptoms can maximize clinical benefit.

The follow−up period is only 3 months, which is insufficient to assess recurrence. Long−term imaging surveillance is necessary, and this remains a major limitation of this report. In conclusion, although presacral epidermoid cysts are rare, surgical resection is crucial for cure. The successful execution in this case confirms that, with strict preoperative assessment and precise intraoperative technique, laparoscopic transabdominal radical excision for low−lying presacral cysts is feasible in selected patients when performed by experienced surgeons. Long−term follow−up is required to confirm recurrence outcomes. Multicenter studies with longer follow−up are warranted to evaluate long−term efficacy and recurrence rates.
