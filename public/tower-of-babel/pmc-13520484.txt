Prediction and Controlling Factors of Macrolithotypes in Deep Coal Reservoirs
Authors: Yingying Zhao, Bo Jiu, Wenhui Huang, Zhenguang Shang, Li Long, Linghao Huang, Haonan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep coalbed methane (CBM) in the Ordos Basin is a critical resource for China’s unconventional gas supply. Accurate identification of coal macrolithotypes is essential for reservoir characterization and guiding efficient exploration and development. However, the deep coal seams in the Ordos Basin feature complex geological conditions and strong spatial heterogeneity. Traditional prediction models exhibit low accuracy at the basin-wide scale, thereby constraining the fine-scale identification of macrolithotypes. This study proposes a machine learning prediction workflow driven by dual geological constraints: roof lithology and burial depth. Based on core samples and well-logging data, multiple machine learning models were constructed to evaluate and compare the performance of global models versus geologically partitioned models. The findings reveal that (1) Geologically partitioned modeling significantly outperforms global modeling, and the optimal prediction models vary distinctly under different geological backgrounds. (2) Within the depth and regional scope of this study, the distribution of macrolithotypes is dominated by sedimentary microfacies. Bright coal is most extensively developed beneath limestone roofs (accounting for 53.3%), corresponding to a low-lying mire facies with stable hydrodynamics, whereas semibright coal dominates beneath sandstone roofs. (3) A “geology-engineering” dual sweet-spot evaluation paradigm is proposed: the “limestone roof + bright coal” combination, characterized by exceptionally high gas content, serves as the premier geological resource sweet spot; conversely, the “sandstone roof + semibright coal” combination, featuring a higher elastic modulus and stress stability, acts as an engineering stable-production sweet spot that ensures long-term fracture conductivity. This study provides a robust scientific basis for sweet-spot evaluation and tailored fracturing designs in deep CBM exploration.

Introduction

With the continuous growth of global clean energy demand, coalbed methane (CBM) exploration is transitioning from shallow to deep strata (>2000 m). Deep CBM resources possess immense potential. They have become a critical replacement domain for increasing unconventional natural gas reserves and production in China. − Currently, blocks achieving commercial development are predominantly concentrated in the Ordos Basin. However, deep coal seams are situated in unique geological environments characterized by “high temperature, high pressure, and high in situ stress.” The strong reservoir heterogeneity and complex geological features pose significant challenges to exploration and development, leading to high costs and risks. − As the fundamental compositional units of coal reservoirs, macrolithotypes (bright, semibright, semidull, and dull) not only govern geological attributes such as pore structure, gas content, and permeability, but also directly determine engineering properties including coal brittleness, fracability, and fracture propagation behavior. − Therefore, accurately identifying macrolithotypes under complex deep geological conditions is a core prerequisite for sweet spot evaluation and tailored fracturing design in deep CBM.

Traditionally, the identification of macrolithotypes has primarily relied on core drilling and visual observation. However, in deep, high-stress formations, coring operations are highly expensive, discontinuous, and often limited by low core recovery rates, making it difficult to meet the demands of fine-scale reservoir characterization. , In contrast, geophysical well logging technology offers the advantages of excellent data continuity, high vertical resolution, and relatively low acquisition costs, rendering it an essential auxiliary tool for modern coal reservoir evaluation. − Different macrolithotypes exhibit variations in maceral composition, thermal maturity, and ash content, , which manifest as distinct physical properties in log responses, thereby providing a basis for continuous identification using geophysical logging methods.

Considerable research has been conducted on the well-logging identification of macrolithotypes. Xu et al. proposed that different macrolithotypes correspond to specific log response intervals, while Tao et al. demonstrated that their distinct physical properties lead to quantifiable logging characteristics using density (DEN), γ ray (GR), and acoustic travel time (AC) logs. Zhao et al. utilized advanced logging curves, specifically Natural γ Ray Spectrometry (NGS) and Cross-multipole Array Acoustic (XMAC), to qualitatively identify macrolithotypes. However, these methods are mostly based on linear regression or crossplot techniques and are primarily applied to shallow and medium-depth coal seams. The unique geological environments of deep reservoirs pose severe challenges to conventional log interpretation. Under high effective stress in deep strata, intense compaction causes the closure of primary fractures and pores. This leads the petrophysical responses of different macrolithotypes “converge”. Consequently, the relationship between coal components and logging data often exhibits high nonlinearity, significantly reducing the predictive accuracy and applicability of traditional methods.

In recent years, with the rapid development of big data and artificial intelligence technologies, machine learning (ML) algorithms have become a research hotspot in geophysics due to their outstanding performance in handling multidimensional nonlinear geological problems. , Currently, several studies have attempted to utilize ML for predicting macrolithotypes. For instance, Cui et al. integrated multiple logging parameters, including acoustic travel time (AC), density (DEN), natural γ ray (GR), deep laterolog resistivity (RLLD), and deep induction resistivity (RT), using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to predict and quantitatively analyze macrolithotypes. Wang et al. demonstrated that Random Forest (RF) outperforms Back Propagation (BP) neural networks in characterizing coalbody structure and lithotype distribution. Duan et al. selected GR, DEN, AC, and RLLD as input variables to predict the planar and vertical distribution of macrolithotypes in deep coal seams using a BP neural network. Evidently, coupling ML with well-logging data for accurate stratigraphic prediction has become a crucial direction for fine-scale reservoir description. Nevertheless, most existing studies are based on small-scale local block samples for global modeling. Moreover, they often neglect the extreme heterogeneity of deep strata, resulting in models that lack refinement, exhibit weak generalization capability, and fail to capture the geological variations across different structural units within a basin.

To bridge this gap, this study targets the deep coal seams of the Ordos Basin. By fully considering the controlling effect of depositional environments on material composition and the dominant influence of burial depth on thermal evolution, we proposed a geologically partitioned modeling approach based on depth intervals and coal-roof combinations. By integrating core observations and high-quality logging data from 28 deep wells in the Ordos Basin, this study systematically evaluated the applicability of four algorithmsRandom Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)under different geological scenarios. Furthermore, this research quantitatively establishes the intrinsic relationships between macrolithotypes, in situ gas content, and triaxial geomechanical parameters, providing a robust theoretical basis and technical support for sweet spot evaluation and engineering optimization in deep CBM.

Geological Background

Located in the western part of the North China Craton, the Ordos Basin is the largest coal-bearing basin in China and a typical polycyclic cratonic basin, covering an area of approximately 370,000 km2. − Its tectonic framework is characterized by “peripheral uplifts and a stable interior,” comprising six secondary structural units: the Yimeng Uplift in the north, the Weibei Uplift in the south, the Western Thrust Belt, the Tianhuan Depression in the central-west, the vast Yishan Slope in the center, and the Jinxi Flexural Fold Belt in the east. At present, the overall strata exhibit the characteristics of a large, asymmetrical syncline or monocline dipping gently to the west ,,,− (Figure ).

Geological setting and sampling locations in the Ordos Basin. (a) Tectonic division and well locations; (b) Carboniferous–Permian stratigraphic column; (c) Late Carboniferous sedimentary microfacies.

The Ordos Basin possesses a complete stratigraphic sequence. In the Upper Paleozoic, the coal-bearing strata developed from bottom to top include the Upper Carboniferous Benxi Formation, the Lower Permian Taiyuan Formation, and the Shanxi Formation. Notably, the No. 8 coal seam, located at the top of the Benxi Formation, generally ranges from 2 to 25 m in thickness. It is stably and continuously distributed over a large area within the basin and is characterized by high gas content, making it the most favorable target horizon for deep CBM exploration. Recent breakthrough discoveries are primarily concentrated in the central Yishan Slope and the eastern Jinxi Flexural Fold Belt, at burial depths ranging from 2000 to 3500 m.

The basin has undergone a systematic sedimentary evolution from a shallow marine carbonate platform on a passive continental margin in the Early Paleozoic, to marine-continental transitional facies in the Late Paleozoic, and ultimately to a large inland lake basin in the Mesozoic. , Particularly during the Late Paleozoic, controlled by differential peripheral sediment supply and marine transgressions/regressions, the basin exhibited significant spatial depositional variations. The northern region, adjacent to the Yinshan Paleoland, was dominated by a fluvial-deltaic system, developing extensive delta plain and delta front facies belts. The central region was situated in a vast tidal flat-lagoon environment, where frequent seawater fluctuations led to the alternating development of brackish-water deposits and peat mires. The eastern region, strongly influenced by marine transgressions, developed a typical shallow marine shelf-barrier island-lagoon system, characterized by interbedded barrier sand bodies and shallow marine limestones. ,,,−

Sample and Methodology

Sample Collection and Experimental Testing

The core samples and corresponding well-logging data for this study were collected from the Ordos Basin, targeting the No. 8 coal seam at the top of the Upper Carboniferous Benxi Formation. A total of 255 coal samples were retrieved from 28 deep wells. The sampling locations are widely distributed across three major tectonic units within the basin (Figure ), encompassing diverse depositional environments such as fluvial-deltaic, tidal flat, lagoon, and shallow marine shelf facies. This spatial variation in the sedimentary background directly controls the strong basin-wide heterogeneity of macrolithotypes and material compositions. The burial depths of the samples mainly range from 2000 to 3500 m. The lithologies of the sample roof strata include sandstone, argillaceous sandstone, sandy mudstone, mudstone, and limestone.

To comprehensively characterize the geological and engineering attributes of the coal reservoirs and to provide high-precision ground truth labels for the subsequent ML models, systematic multiscale experimental tests were conducted on the samples as follows:(1)Macrolithotype classification and maceral analysis: According to the Chinese National Standard (GB/T 18023–2000) and macroscopic core observations, the samples were classified into bright coal (105 samples), semibright coal (81 samples), semidull coal (54 samples), and dull coal (15 samples) (Table ). Furthermore, representative cores from 12 wells were selected to prepare particulate coal pellets. Following the GB/T 8899–2013 standard, the percentage contents of macerals and minerals were quantitatively analyzed using the point-counting method (>500 points per sample) under an oil-immersion reflected-light polarizing microscope.(2)Proximate analysis: Following the GB/T 212–2008 standard, an automated proximate analyzer was employed to systematically determine the air-dried moisture (Mad), ash yield (Aad), volatile matter (Vad), and fixed carbon (FCad) contents of all samples, evaluating their basic geochemical properties.(3)In situ gas content and geomechanical testing: Addressing the high-temperature and high-pressure occurrence characteristics of deep coal seams, in situ gas contents were obtained using pressure-retained coring technology, strictly adhering to the GB/T 41041–2021 standard. Simultaneously, triaxial compressive tests were performed on 49 selected coal samples using an LFLab-I triaxial stress–strain testing system under simulated in situ reservoir stress conditions. Static Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio were precisely acquired to investigate the engineering responses of different macrolithotypes.

The modeling workflow encompasses two core phases: data preprocessing (depth calibration, outlier cleaning, and normalization) and model construction (feature selection and algorithm optimization) (Figure ).

Data Preprocessing

Data Cleaning

Since the traditional identification of macrolithotypes is highly dependent on visual observation, it exhibits strong subjectivity. To address ambiguous core boundary identifications, this study introduced proximate analysis data for quantitative correction. Based on the established physical principle that “ash content gradually increases from bright to dull coal”, ,, the proximate analysis results of all samples were cross-validated with their initial core labels. Through this procedure, 9 samples with obvious anomalous matching were excluded, ultimately establishing 246 highly reliable effective core samples.

Depth Matching

Establishing an accurate spatial correspondence between core sample depths and logging data is a prerequisite for constructing reliable predictive models. Due to factors such as cable stretching, measurement datum discrepancies, and drilling environments, systematic deviations often exist between measured core depths and logging depths. , To eliminate these deviations, the abrupt mutation interfaces of the GR curve were selected as key stratigraphic marker beds. Through feature matching, a global translation correction and precise depth alignment were performed on the cores, ensuring high spatial consistency between geological information and geophysical responses.

Data Standardization and Normalization

Given the massive differences in the dimensions and numerical ranges of various logging curves, feature scaling of the original data is essential to eliminate the influence of different dimensions on the model and to ensure fair and stable learning. ,,, To investigate the impact of feature scaling on different ML algorithms, this study compared Min-max normalization and Z-score standardization in preliminary experiments and contrasted them with the raw data to evaluate the algorithms’ sensitivity to data distribution characteristics. Initial experiments showed that the accuracy of SVM and KNN improved significantly after standardization (SVM improved by approximately 20%, KNN by approximately 15%), while the impact on tree-based models (RF, XGBoost) was minimal. The comparison revealed that Z-score standardization outperformed Min-Max normalization in preserving data distribution features, and the accuracy difference for KNN between standardization and normalization was only 3%. Model Construction

Well-Logging Parameter Selection

Based on the geophysical response characteristics of coal rocks and previous research, nine conventional logging curves were initially selected: acoustic transit time (AC), spontaneous potential (SP), natural γ ray (GR), density (DEN), caliper (CAL), compensated neutron (CNL), shallow lateral resistivity (RT), deep lateral resistivity (RLLD), and shallow focused resistivity (RLLS). Spearman correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the correlation between each logging curve and the macrolithotypes (Figure ). Due to severe collinearity within the resistivity parameter group (the correlation coefficient between RT and RLLD reached 1.00), this study retained only RLLD, which is most sensitive to the undisturbed formation response, while RT and RLLS were eliminated to reduce data redundancy. A log10 transformation was then applied to RLLD to improve its skewed distribution.

(a) Spearman correlation matrix of well-logging parameters; (b) Recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV) curve.

Simultaneously, to effectively capture the vertical heterogeneity of the coal seams and fully mine the morphological features of the logging curves, a sliding window method was introduced to construct a multiscale feature space. Based on the continuous statistical core data of the study area, the single-layer thickness of macrolithotypes is mainly concentrated in the 0.5–1.0 m range (mean thickness = 0.60 m, standard deviation = 0.55 m). The choice of a 5-point window (0.625 m) provides the optimal physical balance between logging tool resolution and geological boundaries. A smaller window (e.g., 3 points, 0.375 m) is below the vertical resolution of conventional logs and fails to eliminate high-frequency noise. Conversely, larger windows (e.g., 7 or 9 points) exceed the mean single-layer coal thickness. Such large windows would inevitably cross coal seam boundaries and incorporate roof/floor rock responses, leading to severe “boundary effects” that contaminate petrophysical features. Accordingly, the sliding window length was set to 5 sampling points. For the 7 selected logging curves, the window mean, standard deviation, and first-order gradient were calculated separately: the mean reflects the main physical characteristics of the formation at that depth; the standard deviation reflects the data dispersion, indicating formation heterogeneity; the first-order gradient reflects the vertical rate of change of the curve. By introducing the sliding window, 28 initial features were generated, forming a multiscale feature space. However, while increasing the information content, high-dimensional features constructed by the sliding window method may also introduce noise and increase the risk of model overfitting. To avoid such issues, this study employed Recursive Feature Elimination with Cross-Validation (RFECV) for feature selection. This process was based on a GroupKFold (5-fold) splitting strategy to ensure that the training and validation sets were completely independent spatially. The RFECV curve indicated that the model’s cross-validation accuracy reached its peak when the number of features was reduced to 13. Therefore, the finally selected optimal features include: the raw values of AC, SP, GR, and DEN; the window means of AC, GR, SP, DEN, CAL, CNL, and RLLD; and the window gradients of AC and SP.

Sample Input Data Selection

To maximize the utilization of high-resolution logging information, continuous data points corresponding to the 246 valid core intervals were extracted at a 0.125 m sampling rate, thereby expanding the data set into 523 basic logging samples (Table ). Subsequently, to determine the optimal data representation for ML modeling, three input formats were designed and comparatively evaluated: core interval logging means, raw logging data points, and multiscale sliding window features.

Model Selection

To comprehensively analyze the complex nonlinear relationships between well-logging responses and macrolithotypes, four supervised learning algorithms representing distinct learning paradigms were employed for comparative evaluation: RF, SVM, XGBoost, and KNN. Based on the Bagging strategy, RF introduces dual randomness through bootstrap sampling and feature subsets, demonstrating strong tolerance and antinoise capabilities against outliers in tabular logging data. , Representing the Boosting paradigm, XGBoost introduces a second-order Taylor expansion and a tree-structure regularization term Ω­(f). This approach effectively suppresses the overfitting issues commonly triggered by noisy logging curves while iteratively fitting residuals. − SVM employs the kernel trick to implicitly map nonlinear logging features into a high-dimensional Hilbert space, maximizing the geometric margin by solving a dual optimization problem, thereby ensuring robust classification and generalization capabilities across complex lithological boundaries. , As a representative of instance-based nonparametric learning, KNN discards complex global optimization and relies directly on distance metrics within the feature space, enabling it to intuitively and sensitively capture the local correlation features of similar strata in the logging data space.

Model Training and Hyperparameter Tuning

During the model training phase, considering the extremely strong spatial autocorrelation among adjacent logging data points within the same continuous core interval, traditional proportional random splitting of data sets would lead to severe data leakage. To address this, a rigorous GroupKFold cross-validation mechanism was introduced, binding all data points within the same core interval as an independent group. This ensures that spatial data from the same group are mutually exclusive in the training and test sets, thereby effectively avoiding evaluation bias caused by spatial proximity. Furthermore, to tackle the class imbalance caused by the scarcity of “dull coal” samples, Random Oversampling was strictly confined within the training sets of the cross-validation. This strategy increases the number of minority class samples to prevent the model from biasing toward the majority class, while completely eliminating the risk of synthetic samples leaking into the validation sets.

To ensure fairness and objectivity in evaluating different models, Nested Cross-Validation was applied to all four candidate algorithms. Cross-validation is a core tool for achieving and verifying optimal model performance. The outer loop assesses the model’s generalization performance, while the inner loop focuses on hyperparameter tuning on the training set. This method iterates through a predefined parameter space via Grid Search, generates all possible hyperparameter combinations, and evaluates the split data using K-Fold cross-validation to lock in the optimal configuration for each model. Specifically, the RF model primarily optimizes the number of decision trees and the maximum depth. , XGBoost enhances model performance by adjusting parameters such as learning rate, maximum tree depth, and minimum child weight. , For SVM, the core tuning parameters include the penalty coefficient C, the kernel type, and its corresponding kernel parameters. , For KNN, sensitivity analysis is conducted across different k values to determine the optimal number of nearest neighbors. ,

Considering the complexity of geological conditions at the basin scale and the nonlinear nature of logging responses, this study abandoned the traditional strategy of global uniform modeling. Instead, geologically partitioned modeling was implemented based on controlling factors: (1) categorized by compaction intensity into <2500 m, 2500–3000 m, and >3000 m intervals; and (2) categorized by depositional environments into five roof lithology combinations: sandstone, argillaceous sandstone, sandy mudstone, mudstone, and limestone. Burial depth represents the influence of diagenetic processes, including mechanical compaction, stress accumulation, and petrophysical property modification. Roof lithology, meanwhile, reflects sedimentary microfacies and depositional environments, which control peat accumulation conditions, mineral input, and maceral composition. To ensure a fair baseline comparison and isolate the effect of geological partitioning, the globally optimized feature subset (13 features) and hyperparameters were applied uniformly across all subdata sets. This approach prevents the severe overfitting that would inevitably occur if complex grid searches were performed on extremely small subsets. Finally, four evaluation metricsAccuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-scorewere employed for the multidimensional quantitative assessment of all models. All algorithms and data processing workflows were developed and executed in a Python environment.

In terms of proximate analysis, as the macrolithotype transitions from bright to dull, the ash yield shows a significant increasing trend: bright coal records the lowest ash yield (mean 11.45%), whereas dull coal reaches as high as 45.18%. Conversely, fixed carbon content exhibits a distinct decreasing trend as coal luster dims. Maceral analysis reveals that bright coal is absolutely dominated by vitrinite (mean 82.46%) with extremely low mineral content (mean 3.25%). As the luster dulls, vitrinite content gradually decreases while inertinite and mineral contents increase substantially. In dull coal, the mean inertinite content rises to 53.20%, and the mineral content reaches 32.56%. The in situ gas content also demonstrates a strong coupling relationship with coal lithotypes: bright coal exhibits excellent gas storage capacity, with an average gas content as high as 24.82 m3/t; however, a precipitous drop occurs as the macrolithotype darkens, plummeting to merely 5.64 m3/t in dull coal. Furthermore, triaxial geomechanical tests reveal strong heterogeneity in the elastic modulus across different lithotypes. Bright coal has the lowest elastic modulus, exhibiting lower rock stiffness, whereas the elastic modulus of dull coal increases significantly, displaying stronger stiffness and brittle characteristics. Concurrently, Poisson’s ratio also shows a slight upward trend as the coal lithotype becomes duller.

Comparative Evaluation of Data Representations

The comparative experiments involving three different input data sets (Table ) demonstrate that the multiscale feature set constructed using the sliding window strategy achieved the highest cross-validation accuracy (0.589), outperforming both the raw logging data points (0.575) and the core interval logging means (0.574). This indicates that the sliding window features effectively extract local morphological and textural characteristics of logging curves while preserving high-resolution geological information, thereby enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio. However, it must be emphasized that even with this optimal data representation, the prediction accuracy of the global model trained on the entire basin data set remains stagnated at 0.589. This relatively low accuracy profoundly exposes the extreme interwell heterogeneity and the complexity of logging responses across the entire basin. Consequently, it strongly corroborates the necessity and scientific validity of implementing “geologically partitioned fine-scale modeling” based on core geological controlling factors.

Machine Learning Performance Comparison

Predictive Performance Variations Based on Burial Depth

Experimental results indicate a systematic declining trend in the prediction accuracy of ML models for macrolithotypes as burial depth increases (Figure ). At depths shallower than 2500 m, all models perform excellently. Specifically, the RF model outperforms the others in this interval, achieving the highest accuracy (0.823), recall (0.823), and F1-score (0.844). Even the KNN model, which performs the weakest in this interval, achieves an accuracy of 0.725, demonstrating the broad applicability of the models and the high distinguishability of logging response features at this depth. At 2500–3000 m, performance declines significantly, with all accuracies falling below 0.7. RF remains the best performer at 0.678. When entering the ultradeep strata (>3000 m), the intense mechanical compaction from overlying strata severely compresses the petrophysical differences in logging curves. Consequently, all models largely fail, with accuracies generally below 0.50. It must be explicitly stated that for ultradeep strata (>3000 m), predictive models based solely on conventional logging suites possess extremely limited practical application value and should be marked as an ’unusable interval’. To achieve reliable predictions in actual wells within this extreme depth range, future field applications must integrate alternative high-resolution logging suites or supplement with continuous core calibration.

Predictive Performance Variations Based on Roof Lithology

The massive discrepancies in model performance under varying roof lithology combinations profoundly reveal the direct control of depositional environments on coal seam heterogeneity (Figure ). Under the coal-sandstone roof, all four algorithms achieve optimal results, with SVM reaching 0.846 accuracy and 0.848 F1-score. In the transitional coal-argillaceous sandstone and coal-sandy mudstone combinations, performance is weaker (accuracies 0.55–0.66) due to lithological heterogeneity. Under the coal-limestone roof, models show balanced performance (0.60–0.65), with RF leading at 0.649 accuracy and 0.640 F1-score. In the coal-mudstone combination, which has the smallest sample size, XGBoost achieves the highest accuracy of 0.753. However, due to the extremely small sample size (only supporting 3-fold cross-validation), this high score may be influenced by small-sample variance, raising concerns about its robustness. In future modeling, merging this group with other transitional lithologies or supplementing data from more wells is strongly recommended. By contrast, the other models’ performance (0.60–0.70) likely reflects a more realistic predictive pattern for this roof type.

Vertical Distribution Characteristics

Relying on the optimized geologically partitioned prediction models, depth and roof lithology matching were conducted for approximately 100 target wells, achieving continuous predictions of macrolithotypes. Discussion

Selection and Geological Applicability of Machine Learning Predictive Models

In this study, the significant variations in model predictive performance corresponding to changes in burial depth and roof lithology are fundamentally controlled by the diagenetic evolution dynamics of coal reservoirs and the characteristics of sedimentary petrophysical interfaces.

Physical Control of Thermal Evolution and Compaction on Logging Responses and Prediction Accuracy

Mechanical compaction is a critical geological factor constraining the accuracy of macrolithotype identification from well logs in deep coal seams. Experimentally, model prediction accuracy drops sharply once burial depth exceeds 3000 m. The physical mechanism underlying this phenomenon is that, low-effective-stress environments (<2500 m), bright coalrich in vitrinitecan well preserve its primary microfractures and pore structures, , showing significant petrophysical differences in DEN and AC from dense, high-ash dull coal (Figure a). Consequently, feature boundaries among macrolithotypes remain distinct and easily captured by ML models. However, beyond 3000 m, intense compaction under high in situ stress closes primary pores and microfractures in bright coal. Its petrophysical properties thus converge toward those of dense dull coal. ,, This “petrophysical convergence” effect induced by compaction (Figure b) severely blurs the classification boundaries established based on conventional logs, leading to highly overlapped feature spaces and fundamentally limiting ML model applicability in ultradeep strata.

Influence of Roof-Coal Geophysical Interface Characteristics on Boundary Identification

The controlling effect of roof lithology on prediction accuracy essentially reflects the differences in the physical interface properties of varying sedimentary sequences. Under the “coal-sandstone” roof combination, the models achieved exceptionally high prediction accuracy. This is attributed to the fact that thick channel sandstones typically possess high density, high resistivity, and low acoustic transit time, forming an extremely strong geophysical acoustic impedance interface with the underlying low-density coal seams. , The multiscale sliding window algorithm introduced in this study is highly sensitive to the gradient changes at this abrupt interface, thereby enabling precise lithological classification. Conversely, transitional facies lithologies, such as argillaceous sandstone and sandy mudstone, possess petrophysical properties intermediate between pure mudstone and coal, creating gradual and blurred geophysical boundaries. This greatly increases the difficulty for models to delineate hyperplanes within the feature space.

Model Optimization Strategies for Complex Geological Scenarios

Based on the aforementioned petrophysical mechanism analysis, it is evident that a single global model struggles to effectively handle the strong geological heterogeneity at the basin scale. Therefore, the “geologically constrained partitioned modeling strategy” proposed in this study proves particularly necessary, dynamically matching the optimal algorithm according to specific geological scenarios: Within the <2500 m depth interval, where logging response features are highly differentiated and boundaries are distinct, the RF model achieved over 82% prediction accuracy, leveraging its excellent antinoise capabilities and the representational efficiency of tree structures. This demonstrates the absolute dominance of the Bagging ensemble algorithm in scenarios with good feature separability. However, when the burial depth exceeds 3000 m, compaction dominated by high in situ stress leads to severe overlapping in the feature space. In this context, the KNN algorithm, which relies on local spatial distance metrics, paradoxically exhibited greater robustness than complex ensemble learning models when dealing with such boundary-blurred, highly nonlinear coupled samples. Furthermore, for scenarios like “coal-sandstone” roofs, where the sample size is relatively limited but the geophysical significance is clear, the SVM demonstrated exceptional antioverfitting and generalization capabilities in small-sample, high-dimensional feature spaces by seeking the maximum-margin hyperplane via kernel mapping.

In conclusion, integrating geological constraints with ML to dynamically optimize models for specific depositional and diagenetic scenarios effectively overcomes the limitations of global modeling, providing a robust pathway for deep CBM evaluation.

Controlling Factors of Macrolithotypes

Influence of Sedimentary Microfacies on the Distribution of Macrolithotypes

Quantitative statistical analysis across all predicted wells (Figure ) reveals the proportions of various macrolithotypes under different roof lithologies. This profoundly reveals the direct controlling effect of sedimentary microfacies (indicated by roof lithology) on macrolithotype distribution. The results demonstrate that under the “limestone roof” combination, the proportion of bright coal is the highest (53.3%). This is primarily attributed to the fact that a limestone roof typically represents a rapid marine transgression event postpeat accumulation. This event created a deep-water, stable, and highly reducing environment that effectively isolated the peat from oxygen. Such conditions greatly promoted the gelification of plant remains. Ultimately, this provided the optimal setting for the preservation and enrichment of vitrinite-rich bright coal. ,,

Relative proportions of the four macrolithotypes under different roof lithology.

In contrast, in channel or distributary channel microfacies represented by “sandstone roofs,” the prediction models show that semibright coal occupies absolute dominance. The geological mechanism for this phenomenon is 2-fold: first, the active hydrodynamic environment acted as a natural “hydraulic washing” process, sweeping away fine-grained clay minerals from the peat surface, thereby suppressing the development of high-ash dull coal; , second, dissolved oxygen carried by flowing water permeated the superficial peat layers, triggering weak oxidation or fusinization of organic matter. This hindered the development of extremely pure bright coal, ultimately facilitating a lithological combination with moderate luster, dominated by semibright coal. ,,

Furthermore, roofs composed of mudstone, argillaceous sandstone, and sandy mudstone generally represent floodplain, delta front, or shallow-depression mire environments. These low-lying areas were highly susceptible to receiving fine-grained clastic and clay inputs from rivers or overbank flows. , Frequent terrigenous clastic injections disrupted the pure accumulation of peat, leading to a significant increase in the mineral content of the coal seams. , Concurrently, due to the shallow water depth and intermittent weak oxidative conditions in these environments, the decomposition degree of plant remains was relatively high. This caused a decrease in the overall luster of the coal seam and a consequent significant increase in the development proportion of high-ash semidull and dull coals. ,,

Genetic Coupling between Coal Facies Evolution and Macrolithotype Distribution

The spatial distribution patterns of macrolithotypes are strongly corroborated by genetic insights derived from microscopic coal facies analysis. As previously noted, the paleoenvironmental characteristics of the lowland swamp facies include a deep and long-term stable water table under strongly reducing conditions. This is extremely conducive to the gelification of plant components, leading to massive vitrinite enrichment. ,, Macroscopically, this microscopic enrichment mechanism inevitably manifests as the extensive development of bright and semibright coals. Taking well T11H in the northeastern part of the study area as an example, its roof lithology is shallow-marine limestone, and the model predicted this area to be a dominant development zone for bright coal; correspondingly, the TPI-GI plot of its core samples clusters perfectly within the lowland swamp facies. This achieves a flawless closure of macroscopic and microscopic evidence. Conversely, well J32, located in the southwestern basin, falls into the wetland herbaceous swamp facies on the coal facies diagram. In this environment, characterized by shallower water depth and significant terrigenous clastic influence, intense oxidation and clastic input led to a sharp increase in inertinite and mineral contents, making it highly prone to developing high-ash dull coal. ,, This high consistency between microscopic evidence and predicted spatial distribution reveals that macrolithotype distribution is predominantly controlled by paleoenvironmental evolution, which simultaneously validates the high reliability of our ML predictions.

Implications of Macrolithotype Distribution for Deep CBM Exploration and Development in the Ordos Basin

Intrinsic Relationship between Macrolithotypes and Gas Content

This study further quantified the coupling relationship between macrolithotypes and in situ gas content in deep coal seams, confirming that coal composition is the material foundation for deep CBM enrichment. Based on the measured pressure-retained gas content data from cored wells in the study area (Figure a), the gas content exhibits a significant stepwise decreasing trend as the macrolithotype transitions from bright to dull: bright coal possesses the highest average gas content (24.82 m3/t), followed sequentially by semibright coal (16.95 m3/t) and semidull coal (12.86 m3/t), while the average gas content of dull coal plummets to 5.64 m3/t. This strong positive correlation fully indicates that the adsorption and gas storage capacities of deep coal reservoirs are fundamentally controlled by the absolute content of macerals (particularly vitrinite) rich in organic micropores.

Optimization of “Geology-Engineering” Dual Sweet Spots and Differentiated Fracturing Strategies

Based on the intelligent prediction models and geological distribution patterns established previously, this study proposes a differentiated exploration and development strategy for deep CBM in the Ordos Basin under “geology-engineering” dual constraints. The results indicate that bright coal is preferentially distributed beneath limestone roofs within the study area. This geological combination exhibits the highest gas content among the investigated roof–coal assemblages and benefits from the excellent sealing capacity of dense limestone strata, which favors the long-term preservation of both free and adsorbed methane. Therefore, in exploration target evaluation, the “limestone roof-bright coal” combination should be prioritized as the primary “geological resource sweet spot” for deep CBM exploration.

By comparison, the sandstone roof–semibright coal assemblage may represent a more favorable engineering sweet spot for stable production. Although the gas content of semibright coal is slightly lower than that of bright coal, its macroscopic structure is relatively uniform. Geomechanical tests (Figure b,c) demonstrate that the elastic modulus of semibright coal is higher than that of bright coal, implying greater resistance to deformation under compressive loading. Previous studies have demonstrated that rocks with relatively high Young’s modulus generally exhibit stronger resistance to fracture closure and proppant embedment under high closure stress, thereby contributing to the maintenance of fracture conductivity during long-term production. − Under deep high in situ stress and high closure pressure environments, the low-modulus bright coal matrix is softer and highly susceptible to proppant embedment, leading to premature fracture closure. Therefore, the relatively higher elastic modulus of semibright coal can effectively resist proppant embedment, thereby ensuring the long-term conductivity of the fracture network and the sustained stability of single-well production. − Nevertheless, it should be emphasized that the proposed geology–engineering dual sweet-spot concept remains an engineering inference derived from geological characteristics, ML-predicted macrolithotype distributions, and laboratory-derived geomechanical parameters. Future studies incorporating field hydraulic fracturing data, microseismic monitoring, and production performance are required to quantitatively validate and refine this conceptual framework.

At the engineering implementation level, it is recommended to execute targeted and refined hydraulic fracturing operations based on the continuous macrolithotype profiles predicted by ML: For intervals dominated by bright and semibright coals with low elastic modulus and low Poisson’s ratio, where primary cleats are well-developed but the matrix is soft, the fracturing design should implement a stimulated reservoir volume concept using “high-rate, low-viscosity slickwater + microproppants.” Low-viscosity slickwater facilitates fluid penetration into natural cleat systems and promotes the activation of pre-existing microfractures, while microproppants are more readily transported into secondary fractures than conventional proppants, thereby increasing fracture complexity and improving fracture connectivity. − In contrast, intervals dominated by semidull and dull coals generally exhibit relatively higher elastic modulus and high Poisson’s ratio, which are characterized by high ash content, a dense matrix, and poorly developed primary fractures. Intervals with relatively high Poisson’s ratio are generally considered less brittle and may exhibit a lower tendency to develop complex hydraulic fracture networks during stimulation, making it difficult for conventional techniques to form complex fracture networks. Accordingly, these intervals may benefit from hydraulic fracturing designs employing relatively high-viscosity fluids together with larger proppants to improve fracture width and fracture conductivity. Higher-viscosity fluids generally promote wider fracture apertures under comparable pumping conditions, while high-strength ceramic proppants help resist embedment under high closure stress and maintain fracture conductivity over long production periods, thereby improving the effectiveness of primary conductive flow channels. ,

Conclusions

(1)The geologically partitioned modeling strategy effectively overcomes prediction biases caused by deep coalbed heterogeneity. Constrained by burial depth and roof lithology, ensemble algorithms (RF, XGBoost) achieved exceptionally high accuracy in the <2500 m depth interval and moderate performance at 2500–3000 m. However, in ultradeep strata (>3000 m), intense compaction-induced “petrophysical convergence” significantly limits conventional logging predictions, necessitating the future integration of high-resolution imaging logs or advanced geophysical attributes.(2)Within the depth and regional scope of this study, the distribution of deep macrolithotypes is dominated by sedimentary microfacies. Spatially, a “bright-to-dull” transition occurs from northeast to southwest, with high-quality bright coals vertically concentrated in middle-upper seams. Genetically, deep-water reducing environments (limestone roofs) promote intense gelification and bright coal preservation, whereas active fluvial/deltaic environments (sandstone roofs) drive semibright coal development via hydraulic washing and weak oxidation.(3)A “geology-engineering” dual sweet-spot paradigm and tailored fracturing strategies are established based on the intrinsic coupling of macrolithotypes, gas capacity, and geomechanics. The “limestone roof–bright coal” combination serves as the primary geological resource sweet spot due to its exceptional gas capacity; however, its low elastic modulus requires high-rate/low-viscosity slickwater with microproppants to mitigate permeability damage under high stress. Conversely, the “sandstone roof–semibright coal” combination acts as the engineering stable-production sweet spot due to its superior compressive stiffness.
