Swimming exercise improves vascular function and mitigates cardiac apoptotic response via Bcl-2 and Caspase-3 pathway in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats: in vivo and in silico molecular docking study
Authors: Idara Asuquo Okon, Erdi Can Aytar, Nelson N Orie, Victor Otu Oka, Daniel Udofia Owu, Ngozi Glory Amadi, Etim Kingsley Bassey, Ogechukwu Grace Onuoha, Anietie Akpan Jacob, Gabriel Otu Ujong, David Chukwunyerem Nwachukwu, Favor Akpakpan Udoeyop, Bubaraye Robson Uko, Joy E Amadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus (DM) can result in impaired blood flow and apoptosis. This study investigated the in vivo and in silico effects of Streptozotocin-induced DM (STZ–DM) on vascular endothelial functions and cardiac apoptotic markers after swimming exercise.MethodsThis controlled experimental study was conducted using Wistar rats of both sexes. The rats were assigned to five groups (n = 7 each): control (C), diabetic (STZ-Dm), exercise (Ex), diabetic + exercise (STZ-Dm + Ex), and diabetic + insulin (STZ-Dm + In). DM was induced using streptozotocin (65 mg/kg IP), while 6 IU of insulin was administered (IP) to rats in the STZ-Dm + In group. The exercise groups were put through a structured swimming exercise for 27 days, after which blood samples and hearts were collected for biochemical and cardiac apoptotic marker determination, respectively. Molecular docking simulations of STZ on vascular and apoptotic markers were also conducted.ResultsRegular swimming exercise minimized hyperglycemia, reduced angiotensin converting enzyme activity, endothelin-1 levels, and raised nitric oxide level in STZ-Dm + Ex compared with STZ-Dm only group (p < 0.05). Hyperglycemia in STZ-Dm + Ex were comparable with control and STZ-Dm + In groups. Cardiac level of Bcl-2 was increased, while caspase-3, serum C-reactive protein, and myoglobin levels were reduced in Ex, STZ-Dm + Ex, and STZ-Dm + In groups when compared with the STZ-Dm group. STZ exhibited the strongest binding energy with ACE [1O86] (−6.9 kcal/mol], endothelin receptor A [5X93(−6.3 kcal/mol)], and less with iNOS, [3E7G, (−5.8 kcal/mol)], myoglobin [1A6M (−5.2 kcal/mol)], caspase-3 [1PAU (−4.6 kcal/mol)] and Bcl-2 [4LVT(−4.4 kcal/mol)].ConclusionsSwimming exercise mitigates the adverse effects of STZ–DM, probably by improving endothelial functions and reducing cell damage via the Bcl-2 and caspase-3 pathway.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Apoptosis, Cardiac biomarker, Diabetes mellitus, Exercise, Molecular docking, Swimming

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a metabolic disease characterized by persistent elevation of blood glucose due to insufficient production of insulin or inability of cells to utilize the insulin released from the pancreatic β cells; a condition known as insulin insensitivity [1]. The former is responsible for the rising rate of type 2 DM which poses an epidemic danger to about 500 million individuals globally, and this number is estimated to rise to 650 million by 2040 [2]. The risk factors include an imbalance between energy intake and energy expenditure, inactivity, and a sedentary lifestyle, especially in older adults [3].

Both type 1 and type 2 DM affect vascular function by altering some vascular biomarker activities, such as angiotensin converting enzymes (ACE), endothelin-1 (ET-1), and nitric oxide (NO) levels [4]. Endothelin-1 (ET-1) and ACE are proven potent vasoconstrictor peptides that play important roles in modulating vascular functions, especially by increasing arterial blood pressure [5], while improved activity of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOs) can lead to an increase in serum NO level, which promotes vasodilatation and improved blood flow [6]. Abnormal regulation of these vasoactive molecules has been associated with vascular complications of DM, such as retinopathy, neuropathy, nephropathy, and cardiovascular diseases [7]. Previous studies showed that both type 1 and type 2 DM can impede the activity of nitric oxide synthase and induce changes in ACE activity [8–10], which can indirectly impact both renal and cardiovascular health.

DM can also disrupt cellular activities, leading to apoptosis [11]. This can be determined via measurement of major apoptotic markers, such as B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) and caspases. Bcl-2 is a known protein that regulates apoptosis, with an increase in the serum level of Bcl-2 showing less apoptotic response [12]. Caspases, particularly caspase-3 and 9, are known for their role in causing apoptosis [13]. While caspase-9 has been shown to be an initiator caspase, caspase-3 can act as the main executioner of apoptosis [14, 15]. Exercise is one of the non-pharmacological interventions that influences cellular activities by modifying or modulating the activities of these apoptotic markers [16].

Lifestyle modification, including exercise, has been indicated in the management and treatment of diabetes [17, 18]. There is an increasing economic burden associated with the use of anti-diabetic medications, especially in developing countries, due to the high cost of living. However, a non-drug approach, such as structured aerobic exercise, can be employed to reduce this burden [19]. A recent investigation demonstrated that swimming exercise can be incorporated as one of the treatment plans for diabetic patients [20]. Way and colleagues showed that swimming is a good form of aerobic exercise for diabetic patients, since it increases insulin sensitivity [21] and also improves muscle strength and flexibility [22]. In addition, swimming intervention can raise the level of muscle glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) expression and stimulate insulin receptor signaling molecule-1 (IRS-1) and Akt phosphorylation required for insulin signaling [23]. At the vascular level, the pathophysiology of diabetic complications is highly similar, as endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis are the main manifestations of diabetic complications [24]. Curiously, regular movement of muscles of the trunk and limbs during swimming may play a significant role in reducing blood glucose levels [25] and mitigating cardiovascular risk factors [19]. Exercise indeed delays or prevents the development of vascular problems [26, 27]. Hence, the utility of structured regular exercise for intervention.

In recent years, in vivo scientific investigations have been validated with in silico molecular docking experiments [28]. This approach is essential, because it helps to identify possible drug candidates through interactions between drugs and their target molecules [29]. Different proteins (enzymes) with different binding energies are used to achieve this purpose. Although the benefits of regular exercise in diabetic models are well-documented, the use of in vivo and in silico molecular docking techniques to validate the vascular and apoptotic response to swimming exercise in diabetic models exposed has not been done.

In this study, streptozotocin (STZ) used as a diabetogenic agent was not investigated as a prospective therapeutic ligand but rather as a diabetogenic and cytotoxic compound used to model its potential molecular interactions with key vascular and apoptotic proteins. The molecular docking simulations were, therefore, conducted as an in silico predictive approach to elucidate the putative mechanisms through which STZ may exert endothelial and apoptotic toxicity observed in vivo. This computational assessment aimed to provide mechanistic insights into the toxicological pathways of STZ rather than to explore any pharmacodynamic or therapeutic implications. Thus, this study was undertaken to investigate the effect of structured swimming exercise on vascular function and apoptotic markers in a STZ diabetic model and to validate them using the in silico molecular docking technique.

Methods

Experimental animals and study design

The ethical approval committee of PAMO University of Medical Sciences granted permission for the animal study (Approval No: PUMS/REC/2023006). Every animal procedure complied with the guidelines established by the Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUC), the Animal Welfare Act, and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Thirty-five healthy Wistar rats of 12 weeks, weighing 150–250 g, were randomly assigned to 5 groups (n = 7 each). Group 1: control group that received normal rat chow and clean drinking water. Group 2: Exercise (Ex) only group. Group 3: STZ-Diabetic (Dm) only group, Group 4: STZ-Diabetic + Exercise group (Dm + Ex), and Group 5: STZ-Diabetic + Insulin (Dm + In) group. Diabetes mellitus was induced in groups 3, 4, and 5 by a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ) at a dose of 65 mg/kg body weight [30]. Diabetes was confirmed 72 h after induction in animals in 3, 4, and 5 with blood glucose above 12 mmol/L. Animals in groups 2 and 4 undertook swimming exercises, while Group 5 animals were diabetic rats treated with insulin (6 IU/kg). All experimental animals had access to normal rat chow and clean water.

Swimming exercise protocol

Animals in groups 2 and 4 participated in a swimming exercise that lasted 4 weeks (27 days) in a circular basin with a diameter of 90 cm and a height of 30 cm, filled with water at a temperature of 32.1 ± 1 °C. The swimming exercise regimen used in this study was previously reported by Bashiri et al. [31] and modified in our recent study [19]. Two phases of swimming exercise were adopted for this study: the adaptive phase and the swimming training phase. The adaptive phase was implemented to mitigate water-induced stress. The animals were acclimated to a 5 cm water depth for 10 min during this phase (week 1). The swimming training phase began in week 2 with two 20-min swimming sessions and a 10-min rest period in between. The depth of the water was raised from 5 to 15 cm. The duration of the exercise increased to 30 min from week 3. The swimming exercise was performed between 12:00 and 15:00 daily. Each day, after the swimming exercise regimen, animals were cleaned with dry towels and warmed by an electric heater for a duration of 5–10 min before they were returned to their cages. Animals in the non-exercised groups were exposed to a circular basin devoid of water, at the same time as the swimming exercise.

Determination of blood glucose level and body weight

Prior to inducing diabetes, the baseline body weight and blood glucose level for each animal were ascertained using a glucometer (Accu-check Active, Roche Diagnostics, and Mannheim, Germany) and a digital weighing balance (WANT Balance Instrument Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China. HS Code: 8423), respectively. 72 h after STZ treatment, diabetes was confirmed by blood glucose concentration greater than 7 mmol/L. The body weights and blood glucose concentrations were recorded before and after the structured exercise period.

Preparation of tissues

At the end of 27 days of swimming exercise regimen, animals were euthanized under ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (8 mg/kg) anesthesia, and a blood sample was obtained via cardiac puncture directly from the left ventricle. The blood was centrifuged, and the serum obtained was used for biochemical analysis. A section of the heart muscle was taken and prepared for assessment of cardiac apoptotic markers. The heart tissue was fixed in a phosphate buffer solution (PH 7.0) and homogenized using a high-speed dispersion homogenizer (model number: JRJ300-D-1, China). The homogenates were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min using a refrigerated centrifuge (Axygen, TM, China). The supernatant obtained was used for biochemical analysis.

Determination of indices of vascular functions

Indices of vascular functions, which included endothelin-1, angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE), and nitric oxide (NO), were determined following appropriate methods. Serum level of endothelin-1 was measured using enzyme immunoassay kits (Regenix Super Specialty Laboratories Pvt. Ltd. in Chennai, India. Catalogue number: DET-100) and serum concentration of ACE was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (SunLong Biotechnology, Shanghai, China. Catalogue number: SL0057Ra). Both kits were used in accordance with the respective manufacturers’ instructions. NO level was estimated as nitrite, a NO metabolite, in all experimental groups. In summary, 150 μL of cell culture media was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with an equal amount of Griess reagent [0.1% N-1-(naphthyl) ethylenediamine-dihydrochloride, 1% sulfanilamide in 2.5% phosphoric acid] and the absorbance was measured at 548 nm. The concentration of nitrite in the samples was estimated using the sodium nitrite (NaNO2) standard curve.

Assay for cardiac apoptotic markers

Cardiac Bcl-2 expression levels were assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay (Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd., China, catalogue number: ER0762). Briefly, 100μL standard or sample was added to each well containing a captured antibody for Bcl-2 and incubated for 90 min. The wells were then washed, and Biotinylated detection Ab working solution, HRP conjugate solution, Substrate Reagent, and Stop Solution were added in subsequent steps, and the plate was read at 450 nm. According to the instruction manual, cardiac caspase-3 activity was measured using a standard kit [Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. Santa Cruz, CA, USA. Catalogue number(31A1067): sc-56053]. Using an ELISA plate reader, the optical density for caspase-3 activity was determined at a wavelength of 405 nm [32]. Myoglobin was also determined in the serum using an ELISA kit (Millipore's MILLIPLEX® MAP Rat/Mouse, CRP Single Plex, USA. Catalogue number: RMYOMAG-88).

Assay for serum C-reactive protein

The assay for C-reactive protein was performed using an ELISA kit (ELISA, Bender MedSystems, Austria, with Catalogue number: CYT294). Materials and reagents used in this study were equilibrated to room temperature (26–28 °C) before use, and the assay of all standards, controls, and samples was in duplicate. Reagents, working standards, and samples used were prepared as indicated in the manufacturer's instructions, and the assay was carried out at room temperature.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained in this study were expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Data analysis was carried out with GraphPad Prism software version 9.02 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance was used to analyze the data, followed by a post hoc Tukey test, where F value was significant. A p value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Computational methodology

A molecular docking study was conducted to evaluate the binding propensity of streptozotocin (STZ) with selected protein targets implicated in the pathophysiology of diabetes. The selection of target proteins was based on their relevance to apoptotic regulation, oxidative stress, vascular contractility, and tissue oxygenation, all of which are significantly affected in diabetic conditions. The crystal structure of Caspase-3 (PDB ID: 1PAU) was chosen as a representative effector caspase responsible for executing programmed cell death. Caspase-3 is critically activated in response to β-cell damage induced by STZ and is considered a hallmark of apoptotic signaling pathways in diabetes [33]. BCL-2 (PDB ID: 4LVT) was selected for its fundamental role as an anti-apoptotic protein that regulates mitochondrial membrane integrity and cell survival. STZ-induced oxidative stress is known to disrupt BCL-2 expression, promoting pro-apoptotic responses in pancreatic and peripheral tissues. To explore the potential effects of STZ on vascular contractile function, Endothelin Receptor A (PDB ID: 5X93) was included, as it mediates vasoconstrictive responses and is implicated in diabetic endothelial dysfunction [34]. Endothelin signaling is often upregulated in diabetic models and contributes to altered vascular tone and hypertension. Myoglobin (PDB ID: 1A6M) was selected as a representative muscle oxygen carrier protein, with relevance to exercise physiology and muscle metabolism. In the context of diabetes and STZ toxicity, alterations in myoglobin expression and function may reflect impaired oxygen handling in skeletal muscle. Finally, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (PDB ID: 3E7G) was incorporated due to its role in producing high-output nitric oxide under inflammatory conditions. iNOS is known to be upregulated in STZ-induced diabetes and contributes to nitrosative stress, β-cell dysfunction, and systemic inflammation [35]. Together, these proteins provide a mechanistic framework to investigate how STZ may directly interact with apoptotic, inflammatory, contractile, and metabolic pathways via molecular docking simulations.

The three-dimensional crystal structures of the target proteins were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB). The selected structures included: Caspase-3 (PDB ID: 1PAU), resolved in complex with a tetrapeptide aldehyde inhibitor (AC–DEVD–CHO); BCL-2 (PDB ID: 4LVT), complexed with Navitoclax (ABT-263); Endothelin Receptor A (PDB ID: 5X93), co-crystallized with antagonist K-8794; Myoglobin (PDB ID: 1A6M) at atomic resolution, Crystal Structure of Human Angiotensin Converting Enzyme in complex with lisinopril (PDB ID: 1O86) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (PDB ID: 3E7G), bound to the selective inhibitor AR-C95791. Before docking, the 3D structure of streptozotocin (STZ) was obtained from the PubChem database and energy-minimized using the MMFF94 force field in Avogadro 1.2.0 software to ensure proper geometry optimization and conformational stability. The optimized ligand structure was then converted to PDBQT format and imported into AutoDock Tools for docking preparation. Protein structures were pre-processed using AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.7). This included the elimination of all crystallographic water molecules and non-standard residues, the addition of polar hydrogens to ensure proper hydrogen bonding capabilities, and the assignment of Gasteiger partial charges to optimize electrostatic interactions during docking. Molecular docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina, a widely accepted tool in structure-based drug design. For each target, a grid box was constructed to fully encompass the binding pocket, ensuring accurate spatial coverage of the active site. Ligands were docked in multiple conformations, and the binding pose with the lowest predicted free energy (kcal/mol) was selected for further interaction analysis. Subsequent post-docking analyses were performed using Molegro Virtual Docker (MVD), which enabled in-depth evaluation of ligand–receptor interactions, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic contacts, electrostatic interactions, and steric complementarity. The tool also facilitated the identification of active site cavities and assessment of ligand orientation within the binding pocket. Three-dimensional visualizations and interaction mapping were generated using Discovery Studio Visualizer (version 21.1.0, Dassault Systèmes). This platform enabled the depiction of key interacting residues and detailed interaction diagrams, providing insight into structure–activity relationships.

STZ exhibited a binding energy of −4.6 kcal/mol with Caspase-3 (1PAU), corresponding to a ligand efficiency (LE) of 0.256 and Fit Quality (FQ) of 0.442. The lipophilic ligand efficiency (LLE) was calculated as −1.840, and the estimated inhibition constant (Ki) was 422.798 µM, indicating a moderate binding affinity to this pro-apoptotic protein. Its interaction with the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 (4LVT) was slightly weaker, with a binding energy of −4.4 kcal/mol, LE of 0.244, and Ki of 592.683 µM, suggesting low binding potential in inhibiting apoptotic suppression pathways.

A stronger interaction was observed with the Endothelin Receptor A (5X93), a key modulator of vascular tone and blood pressure, where STZ demonstrated a binding energy of −6.3 kcal/mol, LE of 0.350, FQ of 0.635, LLE of −2.520, and Ki of 23.948 µM. This may point toward a possible role of STZ in interfering with vascular signalling, potentially contributing to vascular dysfunction observed in diabetes.

In addition, STZ showed moderate interaction with Myoglobin (1A6M), a muscle oxygen-storage protein, displaying a binding energy of −5.2 kcal/mol and Ki of 153.484 µM, while the interaction with inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS, 3E7G), involved in nitrosative stress, resulted in a binding energy of −5.8 kcal/mol and Ki of 55.718 µM. Interestingly, STZ showed the strongest binding affinity with angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE, 1O86), yielding a binding energy of –6.9 kcal/mol, LE of 0.383, FQ of 0.607, LLE of −3.484, and Ki of 8.693 µM, suggesting that STZ might also modulate renin–angiotensin signalling pathways.

These results collectively indicate that streptozotocin has weak to moderate direct binding potential toward various proteins associated with apoptosis, oxidative stress, vascular regulation, and inflammation, offering molecular insights into its multifactorial cytotoxic effects in diabetic models. In the case of BCL-2 (PDB ID: 4LVT), STZ established five hydrogen bonds involving residues, such as TYR105, ALA146, and SER102. In addition, alkyl interactions were identified between the [001:C6] atom and VAL130 and LEU134, reinforcing a hydrophobic interaction network. The lack of π-type interactions indicates a modest and selective binding orientation of STZ toward this anti-apoptotic protein (Fig. 6).

A more complex and diversified interaction network was detected for Endothelin Receptor A (PDB ID: 5X93). In addition, a π–lone pair interaction was observed between HIS314 and the [001:O7] atom of STZ. Unlike the previous complexes, no π or alkyl interactions were observed here, indicating that STZ binding is predominantly governed by polar interactions (Fig. 9).

Finally, the interaction with angiotensin-converting enzyme (PDB ID: 1O86) revealed hydrogen bonds with several catalytically and structurally relevant residues, including ALA354, ARG522, GLU384, HIS387, HIS353, HIS513, and TYR523. Discussion

The recent increase in the prevalence of DM requires urgent intervention not just via medical interpose, but with a well-planned lifestyle modification. Exercise is a structured, planned, and regular physical activity that aims at conditioning the body [37]. We investigated the effect of swimming exercise on vascular and apoptotic markers in DM using in vivo and in silico protocols. Our results showed that regular swimming exercise abates the increase in blood glucose level, reduces ET-1 and ACE activity, and improves NO level. The cardiac apoptotic markers of caspase-3 and serum myoglobin were reduced, while cardiac Bcl-2 improved after 27 days of swimming exercise. A known inflammatory marker (C-RP) also reduced after exercise and insulin treatment.

DM induction with STZ was confirmed by an increase in blood glucose level, supporting the diabetogenic effect of STZ. STZ treatment has been known to destroy pancreatic β-cells, reduce the available insulin for glucose uptake, and increase blood glucose level [38]. The mechanism of action of STZ on β-cells of the pancreas includes alkylation of DNA and induction of mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in cell death [39]. STZ penetrates β-cells via the GLUT-2 receptor and deregulates DNA, resulting in DNA damage [40] and ultimately to mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Interestingly, such changes can be mitigated by exercise. Studies have shown that aerobic exercise improves diabetic health by augmenting insulin sensitivity and regulating glucose and lipid metabolism [41]. This is accomplished by different mechanisms, such as enhancing mitochondrial function, upregulating glucose transporter proteins like glucose transporter-4 (GLUT-4), and improving muscle glucose uptake [42].

The present result showed that swimming exercise can reduce blood glucose level, an effect attributable to improved glucose uptake by the exercising muscles. This is consistent with previous reports of improved GLUT-4 activity and reduced blood glucose level in exercised diabetic models [43, 44]. In addition, aerobic training can improve insulin sensitivity by enhancing skeletal muscle's mitochondrial density to further reduce blood glucose [45]. The reduction in both blood glucose and body weights of the exercised diabetic rats probably represents the combined effects of exercise and DM on muscle and improved fat oxidation. The reduced body weight of the diabetic rats when compared with the healthy control group could also be due to muscle mass wasting, which is in line with a previous study [46]. This reinforces the need for adequate intake of a protein-rich diet by diabetic subjects undertaking regular exercise.

The data showed increased serum ET-1 and ACE levels but decreased NO levels in the STZ–DM group, which represents a potential risk for vascular dysfunction. ET-1 and ACE are important vasoconstrictors that regulate vascular functions and blood pressure [47] and high ACE activity has been linked to kidney damage and cardiovascular complications [48, 49]. 27 days of swimming exercise and insulin treatment reduced serum ACE level in the STZ–DM animals. Our result agrees with previous studies that reported a reduced level of ACE after exercise [50]. Increased ET-1 activity has also been associated with vascular dysfunction [5]. The increase in ET-1 in this model could be attributed to hyperglycemia, which is known to trigger increased ET-1 production in endothelium and smooth muscle cells [51]. A recent study has also shown that regular aerobic exercise can reduce cardiac ET-1 levels [52]. Interestingly, the increases in these parameters were mitigated by the structured swimming exercise adopted in this study, potentially confirming the modulatory effects of exercise on vascular functions. Our result is in agreement with an earlier study that reported reduced activity of ACE and ET-1 after aerobic exercise [53]. In healthy animals, swimming exercise can raise the expression of Ang II receptors (AT1R and AT2R), lower the levels of ACE and Ang II, and increase the levels of ACE2 and Ang (1–7) in the heart [54]. A decrease in ACE and Ang II can reduce vascular resistance and improve cardiac blood flow [55], while an increase in ACE2 and Ang 1–7 can cause the release of vasodilators, such as NO, bradykinin, and prostaglandins [56]. Consistent with this, the swimming exercise regimen adopted in this study was associated with an improved serum NO level in the diabetic rats. NO is a potent vasodilator that contributes to the regulation of blood pressure and blood flow [57]. DM impairs the activity of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), an enzyme responsible for NO production in blood vessels [58]. The improved NO level observed in this study can be due to the stimulation of eNOS [6] by the exercising muscles, as previously reported [59].

The markers of cardiac muscle apoptosis, which include B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) and caspase-3, as well as the muscle injury marker, myoglobin, were also measured. STZ–DM caused a significant increase in caspase-3 and myoglobin levels, while Bcl-2 was reduced significantly. Bcl-2 is a known anti-apoptotic marker with a high level of Bcl-2 revealing cellular integrity and anti-apoptotic response [60], while a low level of Bcl-2 is an indication of tissue damage and apoptosis [12]. STZ–DM induced apoptosis via an increased generation of ROS. Regular exercise reduced the level of ROS and protects the cell against apoptosis. The improved cardiac Bcl-2 level in this study is in line with the anti-apoptotic effects of regular exercise [61]. Conversely, caspase-3 is an apoptotic marker, with higher level indicating apoptosis, while a higher level of myoglobin is a sign of muscle injury [62, 63]. Previous studies have reported that DM can alter the levels of these apoptotic markers [64]. Swimming exercise or insulin treatment reversed these anomalies to near normal after 27 days. These results support previous findings of anti-apoptotic effects of exercise [65]. Chen and colleagues reported that regular aerobic exercise can cause regeneration of dead cells and improve cellular metabolism [66]. Moreover, improved insulin sensitivity during exercise can also protect the cell from damage [67]. This may be the case in this study. Interestingly, regular aerobic exercise can lower the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and improve Bcl-2 levels, resulting in anti-apoptotic effects through enhanced autophagy and decreased inflammatory molecules [68]. Although this study did not measure the expression of Bax, a pro-apoptotic protein, previous studies have reported that exercise inhibits the expression of these apoptotic proteins while enhancing the expression of Bcl-2 [69, 70]. A previous study also documented that aerobic training can induce autophagy, a process that removes damaged components of a cell, potentially enhancing the anti-apoptotic effects of exercise [71].

The C-reactive protein, a known inflammatory molecule, was also reduced following swimming exercise and insulin treatment. This supports the anti-inflammatory potential of aerobic exercise in diabetic conditions as previously reported [72]. In addition, exercise training can decrease C-reactive protein by reducing cytokine production in fat, muscle, and mononuclear cells and improving endothelial function, and reducing body weight [73].

In our molecular docking study, STZ demonstrated target-specific interaction diversity, engaging through hydrogen bonds, alkyl, and π-type interactions. The most complex and potentially impactful interaction networks were observed with endothelin receptor A and ACE, potentially suggesting an additional role for STZ beyond pancreatic β-cell toxicity. These computational observations are consistent with extensive in vivo evidence confirming that STZ can induce vascular oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction [74]. While the docking results provide valuable molecular insights into potential additional effects of STZ, they do not in themselves establish causality with the in vivo outcomes. However, the preferential binding of STZ to vascular proteins such as ACE and endothelin receptor A offers additional plausible mechanistic link to the endothelial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress observed experimentally in this model. ACE activation can promote angiotensin II formation and vasoconstriction, while endothelin receptor A engagement enhances endothelin-1 signaling, both leading to vascular tension and oxidative imbalance [47]. These interactions align with the reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, increased ROS levels, and vascular contractile abnormalities previously documented in STZ-treated animals [75]. Therefore, the in-silico interaction data provide a rational mechanistic framework that supports and complements the in vivo findings, even though direct causal inference would require targeted biochemical validation and receptor-level assays [76]. Ding and colleagues reported that STZ-induced apoE-deficient diabetic mice exhibit impaired endothelium-dependent relaxation and a reduction in EDHF-mediated vasodilation, indicating microvascular endothelial dysfunction [76]. STZ administration elevates vascular oxidative stress through NADPH oxidase activation and impairs endothelial relaxation, both of which are reversible by insulin therapy [77]. Similarly, Zhang et al. showed that STZ causes increased ROS generation and NOX2/NOX4 expression in diabetic mouse aortas, reinforcing oxidative stress as the central driver of STZ-induced vascular injury [78]. Moreover, a study has also shown that hyperglycemia-driven endothelial injury involves inflammatory signaling, VEGF pathways, and cell adhesion molecules, mechanisms that are consistently reproduced in STZ-based experimental models [79].

Together, these findings and our docking results support the interpretation that STZ exerts its diabetogenic and cytotoxic actions primarily by disrupting vascular and endothelial regulatory systems rather than through pharmacological receptor-mediated effects. The agreement between in silico interaction profiles and experimentally validated vascular damage underscores a mechanistic toxicity framework for STZ.

However, given that STZ is a small, highly polar molecule bearing multiple hydroxyl and amide functionalities, the interpretation of predicted hydrophobic or π-type interactions warrants caution. The presence of such interactions in docking analyses does not necessarily imply classical aromatic stacking but rather localized van der Waals contacts or weak dispersion interactions between the methyl or acetyl moieties of STZ and nonpolar residues, such as leucine, valine, or isoleucine. Similar observations have been reported for small polar ligands, where docking scoring functions tend to generalize weak nonpolar contacts under the hydrophobic term [80, 81]. These localized contacts may still contribute to transient ligand stabilization within mixed polar–nonpolar microenvironments of the binding pocket, particularly in receptor regions that exhibit amphiphilic character. Nonetheless, such interactions should be regarded as computational predictions and require experimental validation through site-directed mutagenesis or spectroscopic binding assays to confirm their physiological relevance.

Although this study reveals the adverse effects of STZ diabetes on vascular and inflammatory pathways beyond pancreatic β-cell toxicity, future studies should assess receptor-level assays to ascertain the direct causal inference between the in vivo and in silico studies. In addition, PCR of genes associated with these findings should be carried out to determine transcriptional changes caused by structured swimming exercise in this diabetic model. Further studies should be designed to include potential additive or synergistic effects between insulin therapy and swimming exercise on vascular function and apoptotic markers.

Conclusion

Overall, this study showed that regular swimming exercise can mitigate vascular dysfunction and apoptotic response in STZ diabetes by improving endothelial functions and reducing cell damage via the Bcl-2 and caspase-3 pathway. These findings reinforce the anti-hypertensive potential of aerobic exercise in DM conditions.
