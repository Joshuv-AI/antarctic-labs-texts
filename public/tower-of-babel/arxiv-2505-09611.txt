Exchange-driven giant magnetoelastic coupling in Sr 4 ​ Ru 3 ​ O 10 \text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{Ru}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
Carolina A. Marques, Luke C. Rhodes, Weronika Osmolska, Harry Lane, Izidor Benedičič, Masahiro Naritsuka, Rosalba Fittipaldi, Mariateresa Lettieri, Antonio Vecchione, Peter Wahl

Abstract
The interplay of electronic and structural degrees of freedom is at the heart of some of the most astonishing phenomena found in condensed matter physics, for example, the condensation of electron pairs into a macroscopically coherent ground state in superconductors. In magnetic materials, magnetic interactions couple to lattice degrees of freedom resulting in magnetoelastic coupling – an effect that is typically small and only detectable on macroscopic samples. Here, we demonstrate giant magnetoelastic coupling in the correlated itinerant ferromagnet Sr 4 ​ Ru 3 ​ O 10 \text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{Ru}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}} . We establish control of the magnetism in the surface layer and use this control to probe the impact of the magnetism on its electronic and structural properties. By using scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), we reveal subtle changes in the electronic structure dependent on ferromagnetic or antiferromagnetic alignment between the surface and subsurface layers. We determine the consequences of the exchange force on the relaxation of the surface layer, exhibiting a giant magnetostriction. Our results provide a direct measurement of the impact of exchange interactions and correlations on structural details in a quantum material, reveal how electronic correlations result in strong electron-lattice coupling and establish Sr 4 ​ Ru 3 ​ O 10 \text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{Ru}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}} as a system to study magnetism in 2D.



I
Introduction

The interplay between electronic, magnetic and lattice degrees of freedom leads to rich phase diagrams in strongly correlated electron materials. When an external parameter is used to modify one of these degrees of freedom, the others are inadvertently affected. Examples include the emergence of and relationship between charge density wave orders and superconductivity in underdoped high-temperature superconductors
Tranquada et al. 1995
;
Howald et al. 2003
;
Hanaguri et al. 2004
;
Kohsaka et al. 2008
;
Ghiringhelli et al. 2012
, colossal magnetoresistance in manganites
Ramirez 1997
and ruthenates
Ohmichi et al. 2004
;
Lin et al. 2005
and magnetostriction in itinerant metamagnetic materials.
Satoh and Ohkawa 2001
;
Gegenwart et al. 2006

Coulomb repulsion and the Pauli principle drive electron correlations. To understand the properties of these materials and enable designing them with specific ground states requires understanding the impact of correlations on the coupling between electronic, magnetic and lattice degrees of freedom and their impact on macroscopic properties. Here, we establish the impact of exchange interactions on structural properties from atomic-scale imaging and spectroscopy.

If spin-orbit coupling can be neglected, the coupling between magnetic and lattice degrees of freedom is dominated by exchange interactions. This can be illustrated by considering two electrons centred at neighbouring sites: the Pauli principle requires spatially antisymmetric wave function if the spins are parallel (Fig.
1
(a)), whereas the wave function has to be symmetric for antiparallel alignment of the spins (Fig.
1
(b)). The exchange interaction arises from the change in overlap of the wave functions between the two configurations, resulting in different amounts of Coulomb repulsion and hence different ground state energies. The exchange energy depends sensitively on the distance between the two atoms. For elemental solids, the distance dependence is described by the Bethe-Slater curve
Slater 1930
;
Sommerfeld and Bethe 1933
, with an antiferromagnetic ground state for small interatomic distances, and a ferromagnetic ground state at intermediate and larger distances where the interaction decays (Fig.
1
(c)). The Bethe-Slater curve explains the magnetic ground states of elemental metals based on their lattice constant and provides a simple description of the magnetostriction in materials where spin-orbit coupling is negligible, for example in nickel
Azumi and Goldman 1954
. Similar arguments apply for interactions in more complex materials, such as magnetic order in monatomic chains
Mokrousov et al. 2007
, exchange interaction in amorphous metallic alloys
Gallagher et al. 1999
, and the interlayer exchange interaction in 2D van-der-Waals ferromagnets
Wang et al. 2020
. The interaction also results in an exchange force: in the regime of decaying interaction, an antiparallel alignment results in an outward force compared to parallel alignment of the magnetization, where the exchange interaction pulls the atoms towards each other. Such exchange magnetostriction has recently been experimentally confirmed in mechanical resonators built from 2D van-der-Waals ferromagnets
Jiang et al. 2020
. In transition metal compounds, the magnetostriction typically remains well below
100\mathrm{ppm}
, unless strong spin-orbit coupling plays a role
Coey and Parkin 2021
; larger values can be considered as ’giant’.
Here, we demonstrate direct detection of the exchange magnetostriction for the strongly correlated itinerant ferromagnet
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{Ru}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
.

\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{Ru}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
is a metamagnetic material that undergoes a ferromagnetic transition at
T_{\mathrm{C}}\sim 100~\mathrm{K}

Cao et al. 2003
;
Lin et al. 2004
and a further change in the ordered moment below
T^{\star}\sim 50~\mathrm{K}
.
Crawford et al. 2002
The material shows significant magnetostructural coupling: the magnetic order is highly sensitive to pressure which results in canting of spins,
Zheng et al. 2018
and the bulk material exhibits significant magnetostriction
Schottenhamel et al. 2016
;
Benedičič et al. 2022
.
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{Ru}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
has a strongly anisotropic, quasi-two dimensional electronic structure with negligible out-of-plane coupling
Marques et al. 2024
, suggesting that also the magnetism behaves quasi-two dimensionally. Previous studies by low temperature scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) have provided significant constraints on the low-energy electronic structure
Benedičič et al. 2022
and show evidence for multiple Van-Hove singularities (VHss) in the vicinity of the Fermi energy

Our measurements reveal a giant exchange magnetostriction in

II.1
Detecting the interlayer coupling

Since the magnetic ground state of
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{Ru}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
is ferromagnetic, the magnetizations of the surface layer
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{S}}
and of the bulk
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{B}}
are expected to be aligned ferromagnetically (left panel Fig.
2
(a)) after zero-field cooling or after polarizing the sample magnetization in high positive magnetic fields and ramping back to 0 T (see suppl. Fig.S5). In this case, the tunneling spectrum, black curve in Fig.
2
(b), shows two peaks just below
E_{F}
, at
-2.6~\mathrm{mV}
(
P_{\mathrm{I}}
) and at
-0.8~\mathrm{mV}
(
P_{\mathrm{II}}
)
Marques et al. 2024
.

For magnetic fields larger than the coercive field of the bulk,
B_{\mathrm{B}}

\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{B}}
will flip direction. If
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{S}}
does not flip at the same field as the bulk, this results in a metastable antiferromagnetic alignment (right panel Fig.

We also expect small changes in the electronic structure due to finite interlayer coupling which can be detected in tunneling spectra (see suppl. section S2). Indeed, at magnetic fields slightly above
B_{\mathrm{B}}
in the opposite direction as the field with which the sample was polarized, we find that the energy of peak
P_{\mathrm{I}}
increases from
-2.6\mathrm{mV}
to
-1.85\mathrm{mV}
, while

remains at a constant energy of
-1.85\mathrm{mV}
(purple curve in Fig.
2

II.2
Pinning of surface magnetism

To establish the origin of the different magnetic behaviour of the surface compared to the bulk, we have studied the switching fields at which the surface and bulk magnetizations change as a function of temperature
T
and of the maximum of the field loop
B_{\mathrm{L}}
. Even though the two peaks in the tunneling spectrum are very close to each other, necessitating very low temperatures
T<500~\mathrm{mK}
to be able to clearly distinguish them, we can trace the changes in the spectra when the relative magnetizations change up to
T\approx 14.3~\mathrm{K}
(Fig.
3
(a, b, c), Suppl. Fig. S8). In Fig.
3
(d, e, f), we show representative field loops recorded up to a loop field
B_{L}=+1.3~\mathrm{T}
at
T=2\mathrm{K}
,
6\mathrm{K}
and
14.3\mathrm{K}
, respectively. The temperature dependence of the fields
B_{\mathrm{S}}
and
B_{\mathrm{B}}
at which surface and bulk switch are summarised in Fig.
3
(g). We find that: (1) the switching field
B_{\mathrm{B}}
of the bulk has only a weak temperature dependence – consistent with the bulk coercive field
B_{\mathrm{B}}
obtained from bulk magnetization measurements(circles in Fig.
3
(g) and suppl. Fig. S6); (2) the switching field
B_{\mathrm{S}}
of the surface has a strong temperature dependence, changing from
\sim 1~\mathrm{T}
at
T=80~\mathrm{mK}
to about
0.2~\mathrm{T}
at
T=14.3~\mathrm{K}
.

The switching field of the surface also exhibits a notable dependence on the field history, which we discuss here in terms of the loop field
B_{L}
to which the field is ramped during each full measurement loop. When changing the field direction immediately after the surface magnetization has switched, the switching field
B_{\mathrm{S}}
of the surface layer can be trained to lower magnetic fields. Fig.
3
(h) shows the fields at which the bulk,
B_{\mathrm{B}}
, and surface,
B_{\mathrm{S}}
, reverse their magnetizations for different loop fields
B_{\mathrm{L}}
. This behaviour is fully consistent with what is expected for a ferromagnet due to randomization of domains and suggests that the magnetic domain structure plays an important role in determining
B_{\mathrm{S}}
. Together with the dramatic temperature dependence of
B_{\mathrm{S}}
observed in Fig.
3
(g) and the large terrace sizes which are frequently larger than
100
nm and sometimes micrometers in lateral size (see suppl. Fig. S9), these findings suggest that the different magnetic behaviour between the surface layer and the bulk is governed by domain wall pinning. In the surface layer, the domain wall which triggers the flipping of the magnetization is pinned by step edges, a type of defect that does not exist in the bulk and that provides an extended and strong pinning centre. To verify this hypothesis, we have developed a model for the coercive field that accounts for strong pinning in a 2D magnet (see suppl. note S8). The behaviour expected for the switching field matches the experimentally observed behaviour very well (solid purple line in Fig.
3
(g)), confirming the importance of pinning for the distinct magnetic behaviour of the surface layer.

II.3
Magnetostriction of the surface layer

To establish the structural changes due to the exchange force associated with the flip of the magnetization direction, we record the change in the vertical tip position
\Delta z
while ramping the magnetic field. Such traces have been used previously to study magnetostriction
Trainer et al. 2021
;
Benedičič et al. 2022
. Here, we search for sudden changes when the relative magnetization of the surface layer and the bulk changes. Fig.
4
(a, b) show two traces of the vertical tip position
z(H)
while ramping the magnetic field
B_{z}
across the field
B_{\mathrm{S}}
at which the surface layer switches. The magnetostriction curves exhibit jumps of less than
500~\mathrm{fm}
around the field where the magnetization reversal of the surface layer from antiparallel to parallel to the bulk magnetisation occurs. To verify this assignment, we have recorded the tip height
z(t)
as a function of time
t
while ramping the field in steps of
10~\mathrm{mT}
and checking the orientation of the magnetization of the surface layer before and after the ramp from tunnelling spectra. Fig.
4
(c, d) show two such
z(t)
traces recorded when the surface layer had switched. Both traces reveal a jump of the same order. Since the only change between the tunneling spectra is the sudden shift of
P_{\mathrm{I}}
as shown in the insets (and the small change in field), we can assign the jump to the surface layer switching its magnetization direction. We note that the switching does not always occur during ramping the field, but sometimes before (Fig.
4
(c)) or after (Fig.
4
(d)). This can be ascribed to the activation of the depinning of the domain wall, which is a statistical process. Fig.
4
(e) shows a histogram of such jumps in magnetostriction curves, which show a relatively sharp peak close to
500~\mathrm{fm}
and which we attribute to the magnetostriction of the surface layer.

III
Discussion

The ability to control the magnetization of the surface layer independently of that of the bulk opens up an opportunity to study the interplay between crystal structure, low energy electronic states and magnetism in a strongly correlated electron material in unprecedented detail, providing a reference point for understanding and modelling the coupling between electronic and lattice degrees of freedom in these materials. The subtle changes in the tunneling spectra for parallel and anti-parallel magnetization of the surface and the bulk provide clues for the nature of the coupling between the surface and subsurface layer. The spectrum shows a peak shifting by about
750~\mathrm{\mu eV}
. In a minimal model that describes the
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{Ru}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
layer by just one of the three
\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
layers, this change can be rationalized by the hybridization between the spin-orbit-coupling induced VHss between adjacent layers: in the antiferromagnetic case this hybridization is zero (or close to zero if the spins are not perfectly collinear), while in the ferromagnetic case it is non-zero, resulting in a splitting (see suppl. Fig. S4). Using previously determined parameters of the band structure
Marques et al. 2024
, this coupling energy is close to the
750~\mathrm{\mu eV}
found experimentally. While spin-orbit coupling plays an important role in the electronic structure, the agreement found from our minimal model suggests that it, and hence magnetic anisotropy, affects the magnetism of the surface and bulk in the same way, and thus plays only a minor role in the difference of their magnetic properties - as is expected if the spins remain mostly aligned along the crystallographic
c
-direction. This is confirmed by the field-dependence of the energy of the VHs having a slope of constant magnitude independent of the field strength and direction, and the absence of Rashba spin-splitting in the electronic structure of the surfaces of ruthenates
Tamai et al. 2019
;
Marques et al. 2024
. Given the success of our model for the coercive field of a 2D magnet, differences in the pinning between surface and bulk are a much more likely explanation.

The most striking finding is that we can detect a clear change in the height of the surface, by about
500~\mathrm{fm}
, when the magnetization of the surface layer is switching. It suggests that the surface layer contracts slightly when its magnetization switches from being aligned antiparallel with the bulk to parallel, consistent with DFT calculations (suppl. fig. S1 and suppl. sect. S1). While the change recorded in STM could in principle be a consequence of the apparent height and changes in the electronic structure, the jump we observe is largely independent of the bias voltage, and the changes in the electronic structure are extremely subtle. A change in height of
\Delta z=500~\mathrm{fm}
corresponds to a relative height change of
\Delta l_{r}=\frac{\Delta z}{c/2}=3.5\cdot 10^{-4}=350~\mathrm{ppm}
. This value of the magnetostriction is giant for an exchange-driven mechanism in a transition metal compound. Similarly large magnetostrictions are only observed in rare earth compounds (see, e.g.,
Gratz et al. 1994
), due to metamagnetic transitions
Szymczak 2000
or with reorientation of magnetic moments and when spin-orbit coupling is important
He et al. 2023
;
Coey and Parkin 2021
. Our spectroscopic data shows that spin-orbit coupling plays only a minor role here, enabling identification of the exchange-driven mechanism.

The relaxation of the surface layer can be rationalized from the Bethe-Slater curve shown in Fig.
1
(c): switching between parallel and anti-parallel alignment of the surface and the bulk, the distance-dependence of the exchange interaction results in an exchange force which acts on the surface layer, and pushes it away from the bulk for antiparallel alignment. While the force and this effect are expected to be tiny, they provide a natural explanation for the relaxation we observe here. A strong magnetoelastic coupling in
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{Ru}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
, as suggested by these measurements, is also found in experiments under hydrostatic pressure
Gupta et al. 2006
;
Zheng et al. 2018
and in dilatometric measurements
Schottenhamel et al. 2016
. Our experiments provide microscopic insights into the mechanism and demonstrate the important and possibly dominant role of the exchange force for this giant magnetoelastic coupling. The spin-orbit-coupling mediated Van Hove singularities in the vicinity of the Fermi energy
Marques et al. 2024
provide a route to detection of the relative magnetizations, and may contribute in driving the dramatic response of the lattice to the change in magnetization. The magnitude of the structural change that we detect is significantly larger than what is obtained from DFT (suppl. section S1). DFT calculations for this system confirm two key findings: (1) for a ferromagnetic configuration, the interlayer distance is slightly smaller than for antiferromagnetic configuration; (2) a calculation of the exchange energy as a function of layer separation for two magnetic layers of
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
(with octahedral rotations) indeed confirms a Bethe-Slater like behaviour, as our magnetostriction measurements suggest (supply. Fig. S1). That the height change found in DFT is almost an order of magnitude smaller than the height change found experimentally is maybe not surprising given the short-comings of DFT to capture the electronic correlation effects in this material, which is also reflected in a significant band renormalisation between the band structure obtained from DFT and the one determined by ARPES and QPI
Ngabonziza et al. 2023
;
Marques et al. 2024
.

IV
Conclusion

Our results demonstrate a direct link between exchange interaction, electronic structure and crystal structure, providing microscopic insight into the structure-property relationships in a metamagnetic quantum material. The understanding that can be derived from this model system provides benchmark experimental results on which to test theoretical descriptions of strongly correlated electron materials and in particular modelling of the coupling between electronic and lattice degrees of freedom. We find significantly larger magnetostructural coupling than expected from DFT calculations, suggesting that electronic correlation effects play an important role. Materials modelling that can properly capture the correlation-enhanced coupling between electronic and structural degrees of freedom promises routes to tailor this coupling and exploit it, for example, in the stabilization of new correlated ground states and for unconventional superconductivity. The 2D nature of the magnetism in
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{Ru}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
makes this material an ideal system to study the influence of dimensionality on the magnetic properties and establish the implications of strong magnetostructural coupling.
Our experiments demonstrate novel routes to purely electronic or structural read out of magnetic information, using VHs’s as probes of the exchange interaction or by exploiting the giant magnetostructural coupling - demonstrated here at the level of a single domain wall between the surface and subsurface layers.
