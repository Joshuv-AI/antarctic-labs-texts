The great imitator: clinical, surgical, and molecular insights into diffuse midline gliomas, H3 K27–altered. Illustrative cases
Authors: Jan Hellmeyer, Lennart W Sannwald, Mats L Moskopp, David Capper, Dag Moskopp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDH3 K27–altered diffuse midline gliomas (DMGs) are a rare form of primary CNS tumors. In this retrospective single-center case study, DMGs were reviewed for clinical and imaging findings, surgical approaches and challenges, and molecular diagnosis.OBSERVATIONSFour cases of adult DMG, H3 K27–altered, located among midline structures of the thalamus, brainstem, and spinal cord are presented here. All tumors exhibited heterogeneous presentations on imaging. Symptoms ranged from unspecific back pain and vertigo to focal neurological deficits. Surgery was complicated by high vascularization, infiltrative growth, and proximity to eloquent areas. Diagnostic accuracy was increased by epigenetic DNA methylation–based classification. Three cases were rapidly progressive and resulted in death within 1 year of diagnosis. One case had an exceptionally long overall survival of > 5 years, which was associated with a FGFR1 p.N546K hotspot mutation.LESSONSDMGs are rare but imitate other pathologies due to variable clinical and radiological characteristics. Surgery is complicated by location and high vascularization. Although DMGs are rare, they should be considered as a differential diagnosis in intracranial and spinal masses in adults. The term “diffuse midline gliomas (DMGs), H3 K27–altered,” refers to a class of infiltrative gliomas arising from midline structures in the CNS, with a characteristic loss of the packaging protein H3 K27me3.1 Loss of this DNA packaging protein not only is caused by a mutation of histone 3 isoforms but also may occur as a result of EZHIP overexpression or EGFR amplification.2,3 H3 K27 mutation leads to increased H3 K27 acetylation with subsequent gene upregulation and oncogenesis thus promoting tumor growth.4

DMGs were first adopted into the WHO classification of CNS tumors as a distinct entity in 2016, initially under the name DMG, H3 K27M–mutated.5 Given the variable pathogenetic mechanisms of this tumor, the term was eventually changed to DMG, H3 K27–altered.6 All DMGs are designated as grade 4 tumors according to the WHO’s 2021 classification, irrespective of histopathology. This is especially relevant seeing as histopathological findings may be heterogeneous in DMGs, like other high-grade gliomas such as glioblastomas, not to mention their similarities in morphology, such as vascular proliferations and necrosis.1,7 However, these malignant findings are not essential for diagnosis according to the WHO criteria. It is noteworthy to mention that this entity encompasses tumors that primarily affect children, previously referred to as diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG).6

Epidemiological data on this newly defined entity are limited, especially for adult patients. The median age on diagnosis is 5 years.1 DIPGs account for 75% of pediatric brainstem tumors and about 15% of all pediatric tumors.8,9 Thalamic pediatric gliomas harboring the H3 K27M mutation are rarer, making up about 3% of all pediatric brain tumors.10 DMGs can occur anywhere along midline CNS structures. In adults, DMGs are predominantly located in the thalamus, whereas brainstem involvement is more commonly seen in children, with spinal cord DMGs being considered rare in both age groups.11,12

There is no curative treatment for DMGs to date. Surgery has proven to be challenging in many cases due to the tumor location, requiring a careful and intricate approach. However, achieving complete resection is often unattainable.13

Treatment options following surgery include chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy.4 However, only radiotherapy is considered to improve overall survival to a significant extent. Overall survival is very low, with about 50% of all DMG patients dying within the 1st year of diagnosis.13 However, survival rates may vary among age groups and between H3 isoforms.14 Overall survival tends to be higher among adult patients but may be influenced by the higher incidence of DMGs in the brainstem among pediatric patients.11,15

Illustrative Cases

Patients

This study retrospectively analyzed the medical records of 169 patients treated in the neurosurgical department of a Berlin-based tertiary care center. All of them had received the ICD-10 C71 diagnosis of a malignant neoplasm of the brain between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2021. The analysis focused on high-grade gliomas that underwent additional genome-wide epigenetic DNA methylation–based classification (EPIC) using the Illumina EPIC Array 850k (classifier version 11B4).

The inclusion criteria were limited to DMG with H3 K27 alterations confirmed by conventional pathology and additional EPIC. High-grade gliomas lacking the pathognomonic H3 K27 alteration were excluded from the analysis.

After admission to the neurosurgical department and diagnosis of the lesion through standard contrast-enhanced MRI, surgical biopsy or resection was performed (by D.M.), and pathological specimens were examined by the Institute of Neuropathology at Charité–Universitätsmedizin Berlin, including EPIC analysis (by D.C.). Positron emission tomography (PET) scans were incorporated into presurgical diagnostics and follow-up imaging to assess dissemination and metabolic activity of tumor lesions. Further treatment was determined individually through our interdisciplinary tumor board.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) using the Illumina TruSight Oncology 500 DNA/RNA panel was performed in 1 case. BRAF analysis was conducted with BRAF-V600 hotspot pyrosequencing. Four patients (2.4%) presented with a newly established diagnosis of DMG, H3 K27–altered, with conventional pathological and epigenetic features. All tumors exhibited significant variability in their location within midline structures, including thalamic, brain, and spinal involvement. As a result, symptom spectrum was broad, ranging from nonspecific back pain and vertigo to focal neurological deficits.

A detailed description of each case is presented below.

Case 1: Long-Term Survival After Thalamic DMG

The first patient was a 29-year-old Caucasian female who presented with a sudden onset of diplopia and headache. Diplopia was exacerbated by right downward eye movement. MRI revealed a right ring-enhancing thalamic mass with ventricular compression, as well as hydrocephalus (Fig. 1A and B). Stereotactic biopsy was performed, given the highly eloquent localization of the mass, and the probe was sent to neuropathology for further examination. Intraoperative histological analysis was compatible with a pilocytic astrocytoma. Subsequent ventriculoendoscopy revealed a hematocephalus caused by an acute intraventricular bleed from the tumor. The patient deteriorated within minutes in the operating room. Emergency craniotomy was carried out via a right parietal postcentral approach to evacuate the hematoma. Considering the intraoperative proximity to the lesion, we decided to resect the highly vascularized tumor and concluded the procedure with the placement of an external ventricular drain. Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit and extubated 5 days later. She exhibited symptoms of dysarthria and tetraparesis that was more pronounced on the left side. A ventriculoperitoneal shunt was placed to treat persisting hydrocephalus. Further neuropathological examinations of the resected tumor did not support the initial stereotactic biopsy findings. Rather, findings were consistent with a DMG or alternatively with an IDH-mutated glioblastoma (according to the 2021 WHO classification; now classified as astrocytoma, IDH-mutated, grade 4). EPIC diagnostics ultimately confirmed a DMG, thereby eliminating the possibilities of other entities.

Concomitant radio-chemotherapy with temozolomide resulted in disease regression. However, PET imaging still showed metabolically active lesions (Fig. 1C and D).

Three years postoperatively, the patient was alert, partially cooperative, showed mild overall motor improvement, and was able to ambulate with assistance. MRI showed a large right temporal defect (Fig. 1E and F). Five years later, the patient was still alive with no additional focal neurological deficits but then lost to follow-up. Given the positive overall survival outcome, NGS was performed. In addition to identifying the H3 K27M mutation, a FGFR1 p.N546K hotspot mutation was detected.

Case 2: Primary Spinal DMG With Cerebral Metastasis

The second patient, a 46-year-old Caucasian male, presented with lumbar pain and sensory deficits in the urogenital region and posterior left thigh. An intramedullary moderate contrast-enhancing mass at the level of T12–L1 was discovered on MRI (Fig. 2A–C). Imaging findings were concomitant with an ependymoma.

The vascularized tumor was partially resected via hemilaminectomy and durotomy under monitoring of motor function. Intraoperative histological analysis revealed findings of a high-grade glioma. Postoperatively, the sensory deficits worsened slightly, and voiding dysfunction occurred. Neuropathological diagnostics were able to detect the pathognomonic H3 K27M mutation and confirm diagnosis using EPIC screening.

At the 6-month follow-up, after further adjuvant radio-chemotherapy, the patient had developed significant motor dysfunction and a worsening general aspect. Amino acid–based PET revealed new metabolically active intracranial periventricular lesions (Fig. 2E and F). The patient died 9 months after the initial diagnosis was made while undergoing supportive care.

Case 3: Disseminated Spinal Tumors as a Primary DMG Manifestation

The third patient was a 34-year-old Asian male who presented with persistently worsening low back pain radiating to the legs, sensory deficits of the left ventromedial leg, vertigo, and B symptoms. MRI showed multiple contrast-enhancing intraspinal lesions throughout the spinal cord (Fig. 3A and B). To rule out leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, a lumbar puncture was performed in which only a slight lymphocytosis and no neoplastic cells were seen. Further diagnostic measures encompassed a glucose-based PET scan that demonstrated a highly metabolic lesion from T9 to T12 (Fig. 3C). Hemilaminectomy at the T12 level was performed for diagnostic purposes. Intraoperatively, the tumor was well vascularized and showed histological signs of an astrocytoma. Additional neuropathological analysis uncovered the presence of an H3 K27M mutation, leading to the diagnosis of a DMG. The sensory deficit improved after surgery and no new deficits were found. However, because of the severity of the tumor’s disseminated growth, the patient died 7 months after surgery, while on palliative care.

Case 4: Brainstem DMG With Delayed Diagnosis

The fourth patient was a 54-year-old Asian female who complained of acute vertigo and headache. CT imaging ruled out any acute bleeding or stroke. Further outpatient MRI showed an unspecific T2-weighted mildly hyperintense lesion in the brainstem (Fig. 3D), but the patient was not referred to the neurosurgery department for further evaluation. After 10 months, the patient returned with worsening symptoms, experiencing increased vertigo, vomiting, and headache. MRI now revealed a mild contrast-enhancing lesion within the brainstem consistent with a glioma (Fig. 3E and F). Suboccipital trepanation with resection of the posterior atlantic arch and removal of the highly vascularized tumor were performed. Intraoperative histological analysis was consistent with a high-grade glioma. Further pathological diagnostics successfully identified the characteristic H3 K27M mutation, and the diagnosis was confirmed through EPIC screening. The immediate postoperative condition was stable with no new neurological deficits. The patient’s condition worsened quickly with an exacerbation of her initial symptoms and severe gait ataxia. The patient died in hospice care 5 months after initial diagnosis and 15 months after the initial onset of symptoms.

Synopsis of Cases

We saw and treated 4 cases of DMG at our tertiary neurosurgical care center (Table 1). Intraoperatively all tumors could be well differentiated from seemingly healthy CNS tissue and were richly supplied with blood vessels. However, infiltrative growth with proximity to functional structures limited the extent of the resection. PET imaging further aided clinical diagnostics and follow-up imaging. Cases 1–3 underwent postoperative adjuvant radio-chemotherapy with temozolomide, while case 4 received only best supportive care.

Histological analysis showed pleomorphic glial, differentiated tumors in all 4 cases (Fig. 4A, C, E, and G), and immunohistochemical staining revealed characteristic H3 K27M mutations, supporting the diagnosis of DMG, H3 K27–altered. Further EPIC analysis (Illumina EPIC Array 850k, classifier version 11B4) was able to confirm the diagnosis by assigning all tumors to the DMG, H3 K27M–mutant, methylation class family and ruling out other tumor entities. Discussion

Observations

The pathological workup of these cases, including immunohistochemistry and EPIC diagnostics, clearly exemplified the underlying malignancy of this tumor entity in all 4 patients. However, these cases are also marked by their heterogeneity on clinical and radiological presentation. Three of the four DMGs mimicked other pathologies on MRI, such as leptomeningeal carcinomatosis or other CNS tumors, such as ependymomas or glioblastomas. Neurological symptoms were partially unspecific and varied.

In a recently published case report with some overlap in findings with our second case, the patient predominantly exhibited symptoms of urinary incontinence, further highlighting the broad spectrum of symptoms in DMGs.16 Leptomeningeal dissemination, as seen in the third patient, was recently reported in 2 of 18 and 32 of 304 DMG cases across 2 studies.17,18 Such an appearance may also lead to misdiagnosis as a CNS infection, considering its similarities to some meningitides.19

Giving the midline location of DMGs, especially in the case of intracranial thalamic gliomas, the extent of meaningful resection is limited.20 All the presented tumors exhibited qualities of high vascularization, which nearly resulted in fatal complications in our first case. In a recently published case, a DMG presented with cerebellopontine hemorrhage, further demonstrating the anticoagulant properties of these tumors.21 Overall survival in gliomas that affect midline structures favors H3 K27–mutated tumors over high-grade wildtype tumors; however, outcomes are poor in both groups.22 Thalamic DMGs, in particular, carry a prognosis comparable to that of brainstem lesions.23 In our cohort, overall survival was low in 3 of 4 patients, with limited treatment response and progressive tumor dissemination. Although the radical surgical approach in our first case may have contributed to a proportionately increased survival period, it resulted in severe neurological deficits following sudden clinical deterioration, raising ethical concerns about the justification of aggressive surgical interventions in this malignant tumor type with eloquent CNS involvement. Notably, our first case involved a thalamic DMG, yet demonstrated an unusually favorable response to radio-chemotherapy and subsequent tumor regression.

While the canonical driver of DMGs is the H3 K27M histone mutation, additional alterations in TP53, CDKN2A/B, TERT promoter, PDGFRA, NF1, and MYC are frequently observed in adult cases.24 Furthermore, aberrations in the MAPK pathway, including FGFR and BRAF, have been increasingly implicated in gliomagenesis. A recent study proposed that DMGs with concurrent BRAF or FGFR alterations may constitute a distinct molecular subtype; however, BRAF mutations were identified exclusively in pediatric patients, consistent with the absence of BRAF mutations in our cohort.25

The identification of the FGFR1 p.N546K hotspot mutation is noteworthy. While a FGFR1 mutation is typically associated with lower-grade gliomas, it can also be seen in DMGs. Two studies have highlighted a notable improvement in overall survival among DMG cases harboring this specific mutation.25,26 Although a third study did not statistically confirm this finding, it did lend support to the trend of a more favorable prognosis for DMG patients with the FGFR1 mutation.27 This specific mutation may consequently have contributed to the unusually long survival seen in the first case presented. In contrast, some studies have suggested that more radical surgery such as cordectomy might be the key to long-term survival.28 However, a recent study reported that resection did not significantly affect survival outcomes.29

In all, more research integrating molecular findings and surgical strategies is needed to guide neurosurgeons in deciding whether more radical surgical approaches might be justified in this subentity associated with eloquent nervous system structures.

Because of the limited number of successful treatment options for DMGs, research into new therapy regimens, including those based on immunotherapy, is of vital importance to patients diagnosed with DMGs. A recently generated vaccine targeting the H3 K27M driver mutation in combination with a PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor triggered an immunological response and showed safety among administration.30 Given the critical role of BRAF in MAPK signaling, these findings support the rationale for a future pharmacological target with MAPK pathway inhibitors in BRAF-mutant pediatric high-grade glioma, such as BRAF or MEK inhibitors in DMG patients.31

Because of the heterogeneous presentation of DMGs, as well as differences in adults versus children in incidence, survival outcome, and anatomical location, the question arises if the term can sufficiently encapsulate this amount of variety, or whether further differentiation within this entity may be necessary. Possible differentiation within this entity may follow a pattern more similar to the classification system of GBM methylation subtypes.32 A retrospective study of 164 cases of DMGs by Chinese researchers demonstrated that the incidence in adults may be similar to or even be higher than that of patients younger than 18 years of age.11 This study further underlines adult incidences, despite DMGs classically being known as pediatric tumors.

In this study, we reviewed all ICD-10 C71 cases from 2018 to 2021 and identified four DMGs. Given the low incidence of DMGs, especially in adults, physicians may not encounter these tumors regularly. In addition, DMGs, H3 K27–altered, can be considered a relatively new tumor entity by WHO standards. As diagnostic approaches have evolved, the WHO has shifted its focus toward advanced molecular and (epi-)genetic findings, while histological findings play a lesser role.

EPIC has become a substantial tool in diagnosing CNS tumors, allowing for a more exact classification of tumor entities as well as regrading of tumors diagnosed by conventional pathology.32 It also helps differentiate them from other malignant midline tumors, like GBM of the midline methylation subclass, which lack the H3 K27 alteration. DMGs represent a tumor entity defined solely by molecular characteristics and midline location.1 As EPIC screening incorporates molecular pathological characteristics, this tool is highly suitable in diagnosing DMGs.

According to the German philosopher Karl Jaspers, the origin of philosophy is to be sought in sensations of wonder, doubt, and a sense of forsakenness.33 As the cases above demonstrate, these experiences also form the foundation of the neurosurgeon’s experience: a sense of wonder and marvel at the intricacy of the CNS as well as the biology of this disease, uncertainty about how best to approach the individual’s suffering at the hands of this disease, and frustration and a sense of hopelessness regarding the grave consequences of both disease and therapeutic approach. Furthermore, the presented cases show the variability and elusiveness of the clinical manifestation of DMGs. Their potential to mimic a variety of lesions of the CNS from the cerebrum to the conus medullaris makes them especially insidious.

In his 1879 account of syphilis, Jonathan Hutchinson introduced the concept of a disease sharing a likeness to others, thus creating the illusion of mimicry.34 As a result of their heterogeneous nature and resemblance to other entities, this concept is readily applicable to DMGs, as great imitators of other CNS tumors or diseases.

Lessons

The cases presented showcase very distinct initial presentations of a thalamic, an infratentorial, and two primary-spinal DMGs. Symptom spectrums may vary significantly and may imitate other pathologies. Although DMGs, H3 K27–altered, remain rare, they must be considered as a differential diagnosis when evaluating lesions near midline structures in adults. A high bleeding propensity must be accounted for in the operative treatment of DMGs, as neglecting to consider this factor may have severe consequences for patient outcome. Because of the infiltrative growth and eloquent locations, a gross-total resection is difficult to achieve. Rapid disease progression of spinal DMGs with spreading to intracranial structures should be considered. While the heterogeneity of this tumor entity, in its clinical presentation, morphology, histology, and survival, may make it difficult to distinguish from other CNS tumors, the advancement of diagnostic measures such as EPIC analyses make it possible to diagnose DMGs more confidently.
