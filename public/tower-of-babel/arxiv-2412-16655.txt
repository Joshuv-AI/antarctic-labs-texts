Direct Inversion for Chi-Square Sampling, the Squared Bessel Process, and Applications
Simon J. A. Malham, Anke Wiese, Yifan Xu

Abstract
In this paper we derive a new direct inversion method to simulate squared Bessel processes. Since the transition probability of these processes can be represented by a non-central chi-square distribution, we construct an efficient and accurate algorithm to simulate non-central chi-square variables. In this method, the dimension of the squared Bessel process, equivalently the degrees of freedom of the chi-square distribution, is treated as a variable. We therefore use a two-dimensional Chebyshev expansion to approximate the inverse function of the central chi-square distribution with one variable being the degrees of freedom. The method is accurate and efficient for any value of degrees of freedom including the computationally challenging case of small values. One advantage of the method is that noncentral chi-square samples can be generated for a whole range of values of degrees of freedom using the same Chebyshev coefficients. This property is especially useful in model calibration. The squared Bessel process is a building block for the well-known Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) processes, which can be generated from squared Bessel processes through time change and linear transformation. Our direct inversion method thus allows the efficient and accurate simulation of these processes, which are used as models in a wide variety of applications.



1
Introduction

The squared Bessel process is a one-dimensional diffusion process that plays a fundamental role in modelling economic and financial
variables, see e.g., Göing-Jaeschke and Yor
[
8
]
. One important application is that it generates the well-known Cox-Ingersoll-Ross process,
a mean-reverting square-root process that serves as a model in a wide variety of applications (see Vere-Jones and Ozaki
[
31
]
in seismology,
Hu, Kessler, Rappel and Levine
[
9
]
in biology, and Cox, Ingersoll and Ross
[
4
]
in finance).
In this paper, we focus on its financial applications. The squared Bessel process also appears in describing key properties of Wiener processes, see for example Pitman and Yor
(
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
), and Pitman and Winkel
[
23
]
for an overview.
The correspondence between the squared Bessel process and square-root processes, or Cox-Ingersoll-Ross processes,
is given by linear scaling and time change (see Jeanblanc, Yor, and Chesney
[
10
]
, and Glasserman and Kim
[
7
]
).
This simple correspondence means that results for squared Bessel process can be extended to square-root processes.

The transition probability of the squared Bessel process
can be represented by a scaled non-central chi-square distribution,
where the degree of freedom is given by what is known as the dimension of the squared Bessel process—the drift coefficient in the stochastic differential equation describing the process
(see details in Sec 6.2 by Jeanblanc, Yor, and Chesney
[
10
]
, and Revuz and Yor
[
27
]
).
To simulate the squared Bessel process, we thus start with its transition probability density.

We propose a new direct inversion method to generate non-central chi-square samples, especially for the small degrees of freedom case, a computationally challenging situation
that is relevant for financial applications, for example foreign exchange markets. Direct inversion was applied in Malham and Wiese
[
17
]
, who derived a new direct inversion method
to sample Generalized Gaussian random variables and showed that sums of powers of these generate chi-square samples.
In our new direct inversion method, we use the direct inversion method to simulate the central chi-square samples directly.
Importantly, we treat the degrees of freedom as a variable.
Hence to simulate central chi-square variables, we establish a two-dimensional Chebyshev approximation with one of the variables being the degrees of freedom of the chi-square distribution.
One of the advantages of the two-dimensional Chebyshev approximation is that a series of coefficients from the Chebyshev approximation is therefore valid for a whole range of values of the degrees of freedom.
This flexibility in our method means that efficient and accurate pricing of options with different parameter values can be achieved.
Ga
\ss
, Glau, Mahlstedt and Mair
[
5
]
applied a multi-dimensional Chebyshev method directly to approximate option prices.
Acceptance-rejection methods to generate non-central chi-square samples were developed
by Ahrens and Dieter
[
1
]
, by Marsaglia and Tsang
[
20
]
, and the generalized Marsaglia method by Malham and Wiese
[
17
]
.
The reason we choose the direct inversion method instead of an acceptance-rejection method is that to generate the chi-square samples with small degrees of freedom,
acceptance-rejection methods may reject a large number of samples, and this may be inefficient.

An important advantage of the two-dimensional Chebyshev approximation is that it provides analytical derivatives.
Since the degree of freedom
\delta
is one of the variables in the Chebyshev expansion, knowledge of this derivative enables the sensitivity analysis of the squared Bessel process with respect to its dimension.
We then extend this analysis to the sensitivities of the mean-reverting square-root process with respect to its three parameters.
We apply these with our new direct inversion method to the calibration of the CIR process.

Section 2 provides the background to squared Bessel processes; we describe the relationship between
the squared Bessel process and the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross process and the transition probability density of the squared Bessel process.
In Section 3 we introduce our new direct inversion method to generate non-central chi-square samples, and we include a comparison of the relative errors
in the first ten sample moments generated by
our new method to other methods. In Section 4, we derive the analytical sensitivities of the squared Bessel process and CIR process.
In Section 5 we focus on the application of squared Bessel processes to the pricing of options.
We illustrate the accuracy and efficiency of our new method in pricing path-independent put options on interest rates,
path-dependent Asian options and basket-options, and in the robust calibration of the CIR process to European put, and Asian, options.
We conclude in Section 6 and provide an outlook. The appendix contains the Chebyshev coefficients for a range of values of degrees of freedom.

2
The squared Bessel process and the CIR process

This section provides the background for the development
of our direct inversion method and its application to the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) process.
We present the relationship between the squared Bessel process and the CIR process, and we derive the transition probability of the squared Bessel process through the transition probability of the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross process.

The squared Bessel process is the solution of the stochastic differential equation,

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\delta\mathrm{d}t+2\sqrt{Y_{t}}\mathrm{d}B_{t},

(1)

where
Y_{0}\geq 0
, and
where
\delta\geq 0
is a constant, and
B
is a Wiener process. For every initial value
Y_{0}\geq 0
, a unique
solution to the stochastic differential equation exists, see for example Jeanblanc, Yor and Chesney
[
10
]
. The parameter
\delta
is known as the dimension of the squared Bessel process.
Assuming
Y_{0}>0
, when
\delta\geq 2
, the squared Bessel process
Y
never reaches zero,
and when
0<\delta<2
, the squared Bessel process
Y
reaches zero in finite time, and the zero boundary is instantaneously reflecting.

As introduced in Sec 6.3 by Jeanblanc, Yor, and Chesney
[
10
]
, we define the function
C=C(t)
as follows:

C\coloneqq\frac{c^{2}}{4b}\left(1-e^{-bt}\right),

(2)

and set

X_{t}\coloneqq e^{bt}Y_{C(t)},

where
a
and
c
are positive constants satisfying
\delta=4a/c^{2}
, and
b
is constant.
Applying Itô’s Lemma to
X_{t}
, we have,

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\left(a+bX_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t+c\sqrt{X_{t}}\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(3)

where
W
is the Wiener process defined by,

W_{t}\coloneqq 2\int_{0}^{t}(\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{bs}}/c)\,\mathrm{d}B_{C(s)}.

We simulate the squared Bessel process by considering its transition probability density function following Malham and Wiese
[
17
]
,
who simulate the CIR process by using its transition probability density function.
The transition probability of the squared Bessel process is shown in Theorem
2.1
, quoted from Chapter XI in Revuz and Yor
[
27
]
,
who calculate this transition probability through the Laplace transform (for details see also Sec 6.2 in Jeanblanc, Yor, and Chesney
[
10
]
).

Definition 2.1
.

The distribution function
F_{\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\lambda)}
for
a non-central chi-square random variable is given for
y\in\mathbb{R}
by,

F_{\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\lambda)}(y)=\frac{e^{-\lambda/2}}{2^{\delta/2}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda/2)^{i}}{i!2^{i}\Gamma(\delta/2+i)}\int_{0}^{y}x^{\delta/2+i-1}e^{-x/2}\mathrm{d}x,

where
\delta
is the degrees of freedom,
\lambda
is the non-centrality parameter, and
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the standard gamma function.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
Y
be a squared Bessel process of dimension
\delta
.
Then for any
u_{n+1}>u_{n}
, given
Y_{u_{n}}
the conditional transition probability
P\left(Y_{u_{n+1}}<y|Y_{u_{n}}\right)

is given as
(u_{n+1}-u_{n})
times the non-central chi-square distribution with degrees of freedom
\delta
and non-centrality parameter

\lambda:={Y_{u_{n}}}/(u_{n+1}-u_{n})
, i.e., the conditional transition probability can be written as,

P\left(Y_{u_{n+1}}<y|Y_{u_{n}}\right)=F_{\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)}\left(\frac{y}{u_{n+1}-u_{n}}\right).

To simulate the squared Bessel process from timestep
u_{n}
to
u_{n+1}
,
we can generate a non-central chi-square sample with parameter
\lambda=Y_{u_{n}}/(u_{n+1}-u_{n})
, then we have
Y_{u_{n+1}}=(u_{n+1}-u_{n})\cdot\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\lambda)
.

3
Chi-square sampling

In order to design an efficient method to generate chi-square samples, we generate central chi-square samples first, and then use these
to generate the non-central chi-square samples; see Malham and Wiese
[
17
]
. We propose a new direct inversion method to generate the central chi-square samples in this paper.

3.1
Central chi-square sampling

Definition 3.1
.

We use
F_{\delta}
to denote the central chi-square
distribution function with
\delta
degrees of freedom.

In our direct inversion method, a tensored two-dimensional Chebyshev polynomial expansion is used to approximate the inverse central chi-square distribution function
F^{-1}_{\delta}(u)
.
To approximate the inverse function accurately, we divide the range of

F^{-1}_{\delta}(u)
into several regions based on the behaviour of the shape of its cumulative function.
This is motivated by Moro
[
22
]
and Malham and Wiese
[
17
]
who split the range of the inverse function of the generalized Gaussian distribution
into three regions based on the shape of the cumulative function to improve the approximation accuracy when using the direct inversion method to generate generalized Gaussian samples.
Malham and Wiese
[
17
]
use inflection points to determine how to divide the domain.
However, for the central chi-square distribution
function
F_{\delta}(w)
, there are no positive real roots of

F^{\prime\prime\prime}_{\delta}(w)=0
and
F^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}_{\delta}(w)=0
.
Hence, we choose the two points
w_{-}
and
w_{+}
to divide the domain of the cumulative function
F_{\delta}(w)
into three regions by observing the behaviour of the chi-square cumulative function.
In the first region, we have
w\in[0,w_{-}]
or
u\in[0,F_{\delta}(w_{-})]
, where the cumulative function shows a sharp increasing trend with a large slope.
In the middle region, we have
w\in[w_{-},w_{+}]
or
u\in[F_{\delta}(w_{-}),F_{\delta}(w_{+})]
, where the slope of the cumulative function decreases from a very large value to almost zero.
In the tail region, we have
w\in[w_{+},\infty)
or
u\in[F_{\delta}(w_{+}),1)
, where the cumulative function is flat and nearly equals one.
To approximate the inverse central chi-square function
F_{\delta}^{-1}(w)
in each region, we use a tensored two-dimensional Chebyshev polynomial expansion.
One potential issue is that since the parameter
\delta
is treated as one of the variables in the two-dimensional Chebyshev approximation, the boundary of each of the regions for the inverse variable
u

depends on the variable
\delta
.
This contrasts with the boundaries in a one-dimensional Chebyshev approximation. However, the regions for
u
are uniquely determined for each value of
\delta
.
We also divide the range of the variable
\delta
, the degrees of freedom, in several intervals to achieve the accuracy as required.
In what follows, we denote by
[\tilde{c},\tilde{d}]
such an interval for
\delta
, where
\tilde{c}
and
\tilde{d}
are constants.
We establish the two-dimensional Chebyshev approximation for each of the three regions of
u
.

Two-dimensional Chebyshev approximation.

The inverse chi-square function
F_{\delta}^{-1}(u)
can be approximated as follows:

F_{\delta}^{-1}(u)\approx\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{mn}T_{m}\left(\frac{2\delta-(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})}{\tilde{d}-\tilde{c}}\right)T_{n}(k_{1}\xi(u)+k_{2}),

(4)

where
T_{m}
and
T_{n}
are, respectively, the degree
m
and
n
Chebyshev polynomials.
Here
\xi
is a scaling function, specified below, that depends
in the region for
u
. The linear transformation

2\delta-(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})/(\tilde{d}-\tilde{c})
in
\delta
and
k_{1}
and

k_{2}
are chosen to map the regions for
\delta
and for
u

to the interval
[-1,+1]
, on which the Chebyshev polynomials are defined.
The values of
M
and
N
depend on the value of
\delta
.
A small value of
\delta
will lead to large values of
M
and
N
.
That means when the Chebyshev approximation is for chi-square samples with a small degree of freedom, more coefficients are needed to achieve the prescribed approximation accuracy.
Motivated by Moro
[
22
]
and Malham and Wiese
[
18
]
, we define the scaled and shifted variable
k_{1}\xi(u)+k_{2}
for each region as follows.
In the first region, the inverse function is smooth and slowly varying, so we set
\xi(u)=u
.
In the middle region, as the growth becomes steeper, we choose the logarithmic scaling variable to reduce curvature and improve approximation efficiency.
In the tail region, since the function grows almost singularly, we employ a
\log
-
\log
scaling variable to further compress the domain and regularize
the behavior for accurate Chebyshev approximation.
The function
\xi(u)
is defined as,

\xi(u)=\begin{cases}u,\hskip 14.22636pt&u\in[0,F_{\delta}(w_{-})),\\
\log((1-u)\Gamma(\delta/2)),&u\in[F_{\delta}(w_{-}),F_{\delta}(w_{+})),\\
\log(-\log((1-u)\Gamma(\delta/2))),&u\in[F_{\delta}(w_{+}),1-10^{-8}].\\
\end{cases}

(5)

We choose
1-10^{-8}
to be the upper boundary of the tail region.
That means we truncate the computational domain at
u=1-\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon=10^{-8}
.
This excludes a tail region with probability mass of order
O(10^{-8})
.
However, when we prescribe the accuracy of the Chebyshev approximation with or within order
O(10^{-8})
, the truncation does not introduce additional error beyond the target precision.
We label
\xi^{(1)}(u)
,
\xi^{(2)}(u)
, and
\xi^{(3)}(u)
for
\xi(u)
in the first, middle, and tail regions,
and similarly for each of the three regions, we also label
k_{j}^{(1)}
,
k_{j}^{(2)}
, and
k_{j}^{(3)}
for each
k_{j}
,
j=1,\,2
.
The values of
k_{1}^{(i)}
, and
k_{2}^{(i)}
for
i=1,2,3
are chosen such that:

(i)

k_{1}^{(1)}\xi^{(1)}(0)+k_{2}^{(1)}=-1
, and
k_{1}^{(1)}\xi^{(1)}(F_{\delta}(w_{-}))+k_{2}^{(1)}=1
;

(ii)

k_{1}^{(2)}\xi^{(2)}(F_{\delta}(w_{-}))+k_{2}^{(2)}=-1
, and
k_{1}^{(2)}\xi^{(2)}(F_{\delta}(w_{+}))+k_{2}^{(2)}=1
;

(iii)

k_{1}^{(3)}\xi^{(3)}(F_{\delta}(w_{+}))+k_{2}^{(3)}=-1
, and
k_{1}^{(3)}\xi^{(3)}(F_{\delta}(1-10^{-8}))+k_{2}^{(3)}=1
.

Coefficients of the two-dimensional Chebyshev expansion

As in Trefethen
[
30
]
and Scheiber
[
28
]
, the coefficients of the two-dimensional Chebyshev expansion can be calculated by the following formula:

c_{mn}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}I_{mn},\hskip 14.22636pt&m=0,n=0,\\
\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}I_{mn},&m=1,n=0,\\
\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}I_{mn},&m=0,n=1,\\
\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}I_{mn},&m\geq 1,n\geq 1,\end{cases}

with,

\begin{split}I_{mn}=\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}F^{-1}\left(\delta,u\right)\frac{T_{m}(\alpha)T_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\alpha,\end{split}

where the inverse chi-square function is written as a two-variable function
F^{-1}(\delta,u)\coloneqq F_{\delta}^{-1}(u)
.
However, the inverse function
F^{-1}(\delta,u)
is unknown. Therefore, to calculate the coefficients,
the inverse function
F^{-1}(\delta,u)
needs to be substituted when calculating the integral
I_{mn}
.

Let
x\coloneqq k_{1}\xi(u)+k_{2}
. Based on the function
\xi(u)
defined in equation (
5
), we have:

(i)

u=\bigl(x-k_{2}^{(1)}\bigr)/k_{1}^{(1)}
in the first region,

(ii)

u=1-\exp\bigl(\bigl(x-k_{2}^{(2)}\bigr)/k_{1}^{(2)}\bigr)/\Gamma(\delta/2)
in the middle region,

(iii)

u=1-\exp\bigl(-\exp\bigl(\bigl(x-k_{2}^{(3)}\bigr)/k_{1}^{(3)}\bigr)\bigr)/\Gamma(\delta/2)
in the tail region.

For
\delta\in[\tilde{c},\tilde{d}]
, let
\alpha\coloneqq\bigl(2\delta-(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})\bigr)/(\tilde{d}-\tilde{c})
, so
that
\delta=\bigl((\tilde{d}-\tilde{c})\alpha+(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})\bigr)/2
, and define
\eta^{(i)}(x,\delta)\coloneqq(x-k_{2}^{(i)})/k_{1}^{(i)}
for
i=1,2,3
.
Let
I_{mn}^{(1)}
,
I_{mn}^{(2)}
, and
I_{mn}^{(3)}

denote the integral
I_{mn}
of the coefficients
c_{mn}
in the three regions. We have the following expressions for the integral:

\begin{split}I_{mn}^{(1)}&=\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}F^{-1}\left(\frac{(\tilde{d}-\tilde{c})\cdot\alpha+(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})}{2},\eta^{(1)}(x,\delta)\right)\cdot\frac{T_{m}(\alpha)T_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\alpha,\\
I_{mn}^{(2)}&=\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}F^{-1}\left(\frac{(\tilde{d}-\tilde{c})\alpha+(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})}{2},1-\frac{\exp\left(\eta^{(2)}(x,\delta)\right)}{\Gamma(\delta/2)}\right)\cdot\frac{T_{m}(\alpha)T_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\alpha,\\
I_{mn}^{(3)}&=\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}F^{-1}\left(\frac{(\tilde{d}-\tilde{c})\alpha+(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})}{2},1-\frac{1}{\Gamma(\delta/2)\exp\left(\exp\left(\eta^{(3)}(x,\delta)\right)\right)}\right)\cdot\frac{T_{m}(\alpha)T_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\alpha.\end{split}

Substituting
u
and
\delta
for
x
and
\alpha
yields,

\begin{split}I_{mn}^{(i)}=\int_{c}^{d}G_{\delta}^{(i)}(u)\cdot\frac{T_{m}\left(\frac{2\delta-(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})}{\tilde{d}-\tilde{c}}\right)}{\sqrt{\delta(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})-\delta^{2}-dc}}\,\mathrm{d}\delta\end{split}

for
i=1,2,3
. The function
G_{\delta}^{(i)}(u)
is defined as follows:

\begin{split}G_{\delta}^{(1)}(u)&\coloneqq\int_{0}^{F_{\delta}(w_{-})}F^{-1}(\delta,u)\cdot\frac{T_{n}\bigl(k_{1}^{(1)}\xi(u)+k_{2}^{(1)}\bigr)}{\sqrt{1-\bigl(k_{1}^{(1)}\xi(u)+k_{2}^{(1)}\bigr)^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}u,\\
G_{\delta}^{(2)}(u)&\coloneqq\int_{F_{\delta}(w_{-})}^{F_{\delta}(w_{+})}F^{-1}(\delta,u)\cdot\frac{k_{1}^{(2)}\,T_{n}\bigl(k_{1}^{(2)}\xi(u)+k_{2}^{(2)}\bigr)}{(u-1)\,\xi(u)\,\sqrt{1-\bigl(k_{1}^{(2)}\xi(u)+k_{2}^{(2)}\bigr)^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}u,\\
G_{\delta}^{(3)}(u)&\coloneqq\int_{F_{\delta}(w_{+})}^{1-10^{-8}}F^{-1}(\delta,u)\cdot\frac{k_{1}^{(3)}\,T_{n}\bigl(k_{1}^{(3)}\xi(u)+k_{2}^{(3)}\bigr)}{(1-u)\exp(\xi(u))\sqrt{1-\bigl(k_{1}^{(3)}\xi(u)+k_{2}^{(3)}\bigr)^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}u,\end{split}

where
\xi(u)
is defined in equation (
5
).

Let
w\coloneqq F^{-1}_{\delta}(u)
, so that

F_{\delta}(w)=F_{\delta}\left(F^{-1}_{\delta}(u)\right)=u
, and

g_{\delta}^{(i)}(w)\coloneqq G_{\delta}^{(i)}(F_{\delta}(w))
. Then we have

\begin{split}g_{\delta}^{(1)}(w)&=\int_{0}^{w_{-}}w\cdot\frac{T_{n}\bigl(k_{1}^{(1)}\xi(F_{\delta}(w))+k_{2}^{(1)}\bigr)f_{\delta}(w)}{\sqrt{1-\bigl(k_{1}^{(1)}\xi(u)+k_{2}^{(1)}\bigr)^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}w,\\
g_{\delta}^{(2)}(w)&=\int_{w_{-}}^{w_{+}}w\cdot\frac{k_{1}^{(2)}\,T_{n}\bigl(k_{1}^{(2)}\xi(F_{\delta}(w))+k_{2}^{(2)}\bigr)f_{\delta}(w)}{(F_{\delta}(w)-1)\sqrt{1-\bigl(k_{1}^{(2)}\xi(F_{\delta}(w))+k_{2}^{(2)}\bigr)^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}w,\\
g_{\delta}^{(3)}(w)&=\int_{w_{+}}^{F_{\delta}^{-1}(1-10^{-8})}w\cdot\frac{k_{1}^{(3)}\,T_{n}\bigl(k_{1}^{(3)}\xi(F_{\delta}(w))+k_{2}^{(3)}\bigr)f_{\delta}(w)}{(1-F_{\delta}(w))\exp(\xi(F_{\delta}(w)))\sqrt{1-\bigl(k_{1}^{(3)}\xi(F_{\delta}(w))+k_{2}^{(3)}\bigr)^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}w.\end{split}

For the upper boundary
F_{\delta}^{-1}(1-10^{-8})
shown above, in practice we use a large value, here 20,
such that
F_{\delta}(20)>1-10^{-8}
, and use an indicator function to bound the inner function of the integral in the tail region as follows:

\begin{split}g_{\delta}^{(3)}(w)=\int_{w_{+}}^{20}\mathbb{I}_{(F_{\delta}(w)\leq 1-10^{-8})}\,w\cdot\frac{k_{1}^{(3)}\,T_{m}\bigl(k_{1}^{(3)}\xi(F_{\delta}(w))+k_{2}^{(3)}\bigr)f_{\delta}(w)}{(1-F_{\delta}(w))\exp(\xi(F_{\delta}(w)))\sqrt{1-\bigl(k_{1}^{(3)}\xi(F_{\delta}(w))+k_{2}^{(3)}\bigr)^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}w.\end{split}

The integrals are now transformed to a representation that is suitable for
the calculation of the coefficients of the two-dimensional Chebyshev expansion in the three regions.
These coefficients in the three regions are calculated using Gauss-Konrod quadrature approximation using MATLAB with double precision.
The calculation accuracy of the double integration of the coefficients is of order
10^{-8}
.

Remark 3.1
.

The accuracy is influenced by the presence of singularities.
When the chi-square variable
w
is close to the boundary
of each region, or when the variable
\delta
is close to the upper and lower boundaries
\tilde{c}
and
\tilde{d}
,
the integrand tends to infinity, which decreases the accuracy in the calculation of the double integrals.
The influence of
w
is greater than that of
\delta
in the calculation of the double integral.
To improve the accuracy, one potential way is to increase our double precision (16 digits) arithmetic to a larger digits arithmetic (for example
25
digits).
Another way to obtain a more accurate estimate would be to consider an asymptotic expansion in a small interval that includes the singular endpoint.

Remark 3.2
.

The sufficiently large truncation value (20 in our implementation) is chosen so that the remaining tail probability is below the prescribed tolerance
10^{-8}
.
The contribution beyond this point is negligible and does not affect the overall numerical accuracy. Since the inverse distribution function

F_{\delta}^{-1}(u)
depends on the parameter
\delta
, the exact value of the boundary for the tail region varies with
\delta
.
Therefore, the truncation value must be chosen uniformly for all parameter values considered. The value 20 is selected such that

F_{\delta}(20)\leq 1-10^{-8}
for all
\delta
in the parameter range of interest. This ensures that the truncation error remains within the prescribed approximation tolerance.

We have generated coefficients for different degrees of freedom by choosing different boundaries
\tilde{c}
and
\tilde{d}
for
\delta
.
The truncation error of the Chebyshev expansion is bounded by the magnitude of the first neglected coefficient.
The Chebyshev expansion is truncated once the absolute value of the Chebyshev coefficients falls below the prescribed tolerance level.
In Table
1
, we show the number of coefficients required in the three different regions to achieve
O(10^{-8})
accuracy.
Our direct inversion method achieves high accuracy approximation for any values of
\delta
, especially for
the challenging case of small values of
\delta
. However, higher orders of Chebyshew polynomials are required for small

\delta
. We also tested the region of
\delta\in[0.2,1]
,
and observe that a smaller number of Chebyshev coefficients is required to achieve the same order of accuracy compared with the small values of
\delta
scenario.
In the Appendix, we list the series of coefficients in the first region, middle region, and tail region for
\delta\in\left[0.1,0.2\right]
.
The CPU time to calculate the Chebyshev coefficients for the three regions ranges from 10 seconds to 50 seconds depending on the scaling of the shifted variables
k_{1}\xi(u)+k_{2}
.
The computation time for the coefficients in the tail region is the longest among all three regions because of the most complex log-log scaling function
\xi^{(3)}(u)
.

Two-dimensional Clenshaw’s recurrence

To generate central chi-square samples, we need to evaluate the sum of the two-dimensional Chebyshev series. We denote by,

S(x,y)\coloneqq\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{mn}T_{m}(y)T_{n}(x),

the Chebyshev polynomial in our approxmation for
F_{\delta}^{-1}
.
Recall
x
is the scaled variable of
\delta
with
x=\bigl(2\delta-(\tilde{d}-\tilde{c})\bigr)/(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})
,
and
y
is the scaled and shifted variable of
u
with different scaling as specified in equation (
5
) in the first region, the middle region and the tail region.
A one-dimensional Chebyshev approximation can be evaluated efficiently using Clenshaw’s recurrence formula (see Sec 5.8 in Press et al
[
26
]
).
As in Basu
[
3
]
, to evaluate the two-dimensional Chebyshev series efficiently,
we use Clenshaw’s recurrence for the variable
y
first and then again use the Clenshaw’s recurrence formula for the second variable
x
.
The sum of all the terms of the Chebyshev series can be written as follows:

S(x,y)=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{mn}T_{m}\left(y\right)T_{n}\left(x\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}T_{n}(x),

where
a_{n}=a_{n}(y)
is given by,

a_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{m=0}^{M}c_{mn}T_{m}(y).

The Chebyshev polynomials
T_{m}(y)
and
T_{n}(x)
have the recurrence relation as follows:

T_{m}(y)-2yT_{m-1}(y)+T_{m-2}(y)=0,

and

T_{n}(x)-2xT_{n-1}(x)+T_{n-2}(x)=0.

Define the quantities
d_{mn}
with
m=M,M-1,\cdots,1
and
n=N,N-1,\cdots,1
by the following recurrence:

\begin{split}&d_{M+2,n}=d_{M+1,n}=0,\\
&d_{mn}-2yd_{m+1,n}+d_{m+2,n}=c_{mn}.\end{split}

By solving the recurrence formula above we obtain the expression of
d_{1n}
and
d_{2n}
. Then we have,

a_{n}=yd_{1n}-d_{2n}+c_{0n}.

Similarly, define the quantities
g_{n}
with
n=N,N-1,\cdots,1
to solve the following recurrence:

\begin{split}&g_{N+2}=g_{N+1}=0,\\
&g_{n}-2xg_{n+1}+g_{n+2}=a_{n}.\end{split}

Solving this recurrence to obtain
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, we then have the expression,

S(x,y)=xg_{1}-g_{2}+a_{0}.

3.2
Non-central chi-square sampling

The method we use to generate the non-central chi-square samples using the central chi-square samples is taken from Malham and Wiese
[
17
]
. We describe this method briefly for completeness.

We distinguish two cases for the non-centrality parameter
\lambda
: the case
\lambda\leq 10
and the case
\lambda>10
.
When
\lambda\leq 10
, then the non-central chi-square random variable
\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\lambda)

is decomposed as
\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\lambda)\sim\chi_{\delta}^{2}+\chi_{2N}^{2}
(see Siegel
[
29
]
, and Johnson
[
11
]
).
Here
\chi_{\delta}^{2}
can be generated by our direct inversion method proposed in the last section, and
\chi_{2N}^{2}
is a central chi-square random variable with degrees of freedom
2N
,
where
N
is a Poisson distributed random variable with mean
\lambda/2
. We use the method in Glasserman
[
6
]
to generate
\chi_{2N}^{2}
.
First we generate the Poisson distributed random variable
N
,
and then we generate
N
independent uniform random variables
U_{1},\dots,U_{N}
.
Then
-2(\log(U_{1})+\dots+\log(U_{N}))
has a
\chi_{2N}^{2}
distribution, and,

\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\lambda)\sim-2\bigl(\log(U_{1})+\dots+\log(U_{N})\bigr)+Z,

where
Z
is the
\chi_{\delta}^{2}
random variable.

When
\lambda>10
, the non-central chi-square random variable
\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\lambda)
is decomposed as

\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\lambda)\sim\chi_{\delta+2N}^{2}\sim\chi_{\delta+2\bar{N}+2P}^{2}
,
using that
N
is a Poisson random variable, which can be expressed as a sum of two independent Poisson random variables
\bar{N}
and
P
with mean
10/2
and mean
\lambda/2-10/2
, respectively.
We generate a sample of the Poisson random variable
\bar{N}
, and consider the value of
\bar{N}
.
If
\bar{N}\neq 0
, then we generate
\bar{N}-1

independent uniform random variables
U_{1},\dots,U_{\bar{N}-1}
and two independent standard normal random variables
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
.
Then by the additivity property of non-central chi-square random variables, we have
\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\lambda)\sim-2(\log(U_{1})+\dots+\log(U_{\bar{N}-1}))+V_{1}^{2}+(V_{2}+\sqrt{\lambda-10})^{2}+Z
,
where
Z
is the
\chi_{\delta}^{2}
random variable. When
\bar{N}=0
, we set
\bar{\lambda}=\lambda-10
.
If
\bar{\lambda}>10
, we repeat the process above, otherwise for
\bar{\lambda}\leq 10
we use the algorithm of generating the non-central chi-square random variable
with the non-centrality parameter smaller than
10
to generate the
\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\bar{\lambda})
random variable.

Parameter sensitivities

Based on the new direct inversion method, we derive an analytical expression for the derivative of the squared Bessel process with respect to its dimension
\delta
,
in other words the parameter sensitivity of the squared Bessel process. We then derive explicit expressions for the parameter sensitivities of the CIR model.

4.1
Parameter sensitivity of squared Bessel processes

Recall that the squared Bessel process
Y
follows Equation (
1
).
As introduced in Section
2
, for the squared Bessel process at any time
t
, we have,

Y_{t}=t\cdot\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda),

where
\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)
is a non-central chi-square sample with the non-centrality parameter
\lambda=Y_{0}/t
.
For the decomposition of the non-central chi-square variable
\chi_{\delta}^{2}(\lambda)\sim\chi_{\delta}^{2}+\chi_{2N}^{2}
,
only the first central chi-square variable
\chi^{2}_{\delta}
depends on the parameter
\delta
.
The central chi-square variable
\chi^{2}_{\delta}
is generated by the central chi-square inverse function
F^{-1}_{\delta}(U)
,
which is defined in Equation (
4
), where
U\sim\text{Unif }[0,1]
.
Then we define the derivative of the squared Bessel process with respect to the degree of freedom parameter as follows:

\partial_{\delta}Y_{t}\coloneqq t\cdot\partial_{\delta}F^{-1}_{\delta},

where
\partial_{\delta}\coloneqq\partial/\partial\delta
.
The central chi-square inverse function
F^{-1}_{\delta}
is approximated by a two-dimensional Chebyshev expansion
with the degree of freedom parameter
\delta
as one of the variables, see Equation (
4
).
Hence the first derivative of the central chi-square variable with respect to the parameter
\delta
can be approximated as follows:

\displaystyle\partial_{\delta}\biggl(\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{mn}T_{m}

\displaystyle\Biggl(\frac{2\delta-(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})}{\tilde{d}-\tilde{c}}\biggr)T_{n}\left(k_{1}\xi(u)+k_{2}\right)\Biggr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{mn}\partial_{\delta}T_{m}\biggl(\frac{2\delta-(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})}{\tilde{d}-\tilde{c}}\biggr)\cdot T_{n}\left(k_{1}\xi(u)+k_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{mn}T_{m}\biggl(\frac{2\delta-(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})}{\tilde{d}-\tilde{c}}\biggr)\cdot\partial_{\delta}T_{n}\left(k_{1}\xi(u)+k_{2}\right).

(6)

We write the scaling functions inside of the Chebyshev polynomials
T_{m}
and
T_{n}
as functions of the target parameter
\delta
by setting,

x(\delta)\coloneqq\frac{2\delta-(\tilde{d}+\tilde{c})}{\tilde{d}-\tilde{c}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y(\delta)\coloneqq k_{1}\xi(u)+k_{2}.

For
n=0,1,\cdots,N
, we set for
x=x(\delta)
,

\begin{split}f_{1}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{m=0}^{M}c_{mn}T_{m}(x).\end{split}

The derivative of the Chebyshev expansion
f_{1}(x(\delta))
can be represented by a new series of coefficients generated
from the original Chebyshev coefficients as follows for
x=x(\delta)

(see details in Section 5.9 by Press et al.
[
26
]
):

\partial_{x}f_{1}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{M}c_{mn}\partial_{x}T_{m}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}b_{mn}T_{m}(x),

with

b_{mn}=b_{m+2,n}+2(m+1)c_{m+1,n},

where
n=0,1,\cdots,N
, and
m=0,1,\cdots,M-1
, and
b_{M+1,n}=b_{M,n}=0
.

Similarly, for
m=0,1,\cdots,M
, we set for
y=y(\delta)
,

f_{2}(y)\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{mn}T_{n}(y).

The expression of the derivative of the Chebyshev expansion
f_{2}(y)
for
y=y(\delta)
is,

\partial_{y}f_{2}(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{mn}\partial_{y}T_{n}(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\tilde{b}_{mn}T_{n}(y),

with new coefficients,

\tilde{b}_{m,n}=\tilde{b}_{m,n+2}+2(n+1)c_{m,n+1},

where
n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
,
m=0,1,\cdots,M
, and
\tilde{b}_{m,N+1}=\tilde{b}_{m,N}=0
.

Thus, with the new series of coefficients
b_{mn}
and
\tilde{b}_{mn}
,
the terms on the right in equation (
6
) have the following expression for
x=x(\delta)
and
y=y(\delta)
,

\begin{split}&\sum_{n=0}^{N}\partial_{x}f_{1}(x)\cdot\partial_{\delta}x\cdot T_{n}(y)+\sum_{m=0}^{M}T_{m}(x)\cdot\partial_{y}f_{2}(y)\cdot\partial_{\delta}y\\[8.0pt]
=&\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}b_{mn}T_{m}\left(x(\delta)\right)\right)\cdot T_{n}\left(y(\delta)\right)\right)\,\partial_{\delta}x+\left(\sum_{m=0}^{M}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\tilde{b}_{mn}\cdot T_{n}\left(y(\delta)\right)\right)T_{m}\left(x(\delta)\right)\right)\,\partial_{\delta}y,\end{split}

(7)

which is the approximate derivative of the central chi-square inverse function with respect to the parameter
\delta
.
The derivatives of the scaling functions are calculated as follows:

\partial_{\delta}x=\frac{2}{d-c},

and

\partial_{\delta}y=\partial_{\delta}\bigl(k_{1}\xi(u)+k_{2}\bigr)=\xi(u)\cdot\partial_{\delta}k_{1}+k_{1}\cdot\partial_{\delta}\xi(u)+\partial_{\delta}k_{2}.

We recall from (
5
) and the equations defining
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
immediately thereafter, that in general,
\xi=\xi(u)
,
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are
\delta
-dependent.
Hence hereafter, we explicitly denote
\xi=\xi(u,\delta)
,
k_{1}=k_{1}(\delta)
and
k_{2}=k_{2}(\delta)
.
The derivative of
\xi=\xi(u,\delta)
with respect to
\delta
is given by,

\partial_{\delta}\xi=\begin{cases}0,\hskip 14.22636pt&u\in[0,F_{\delta}(w_{-})),\\[8.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{2}\varphi^{(0)}(\delta/2),&u\in[F_{\delta}(w_{-}),F_{\delta}(w_{+})),\\[8.0pt]
\varphi^{(0)}(\delta/2)/2\log((1-u)\Gamma(\delta/2)),&u\in[F_{\delta}(w_{+}),1-10^{-8}],\\
\end{cases}

where
\varphi^{(0)}
is the polygamma function of order
0
.

Let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
denote the lower and upper boundary points of the variable
u
among the three regions.
The functions
k_{1}=k_{1}(\delta)
and
k_{2}=k_{2}(\delta)
are chosen such that,

k_{1}(\delta)\cdot\xi(u_{1},\delta)+k_{2}(\delta)=-1,\qquad\text{and}\qquad k_{1}(\delta)\cdot\xi(u_{2},\delta)+k_{2}(\delta)=1.

The derivatives of
k_{1}=k_{1}(\delta)
and
k_{2}=k_{2}(\delta)
with respect to
\delta
in each of the three regions are as follows:

\partial_{\delta}k_{1}(\delta)=\frac{2}{\bigl(\xi(u_{1},\delta)-\xi(u_{2},\delta)\bigr)^{2}}\cdot\bigl(\partial_{\delta}\xi(u_{1},\delta)-\partial_{\delta}\xi(u_{2},\delta)\bigr),

and

\partial_{\delta}k_{2}(\delta)=-\partial_{\delta}k_{1}(\delta)\cdot\xi(u_{2},\delta)-\partial_{\delta}\xi(u_{2},\delta)\cdot k_{1}(\delta).

Let
u_{j}
, for
j=1,2
, be the lower or upper boundary. We have,

\begin{split}\partial_{\delta}\xi(u_{j},\delta)=&\;\partial_{\delta}\xi(u_{j},\delta)+\partial_{u_{j}}\xi(u_{j},\delta)\cdot\partial_{\delta}u_{j}\\[8.0pt]
=&\;\begin{cases}\partial_{\delta}u_{j},\hskip 14.22636pt&u\in[0,F_{\delta}(w_{-})),\\[8.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{2}\varphi^{(0)}(\delta/2)-\frac{1}{1-u_{j}}\cdot\partial_{\delta}u_{j},&u\in[F_{\delta}(w_{-}),F_{\delta}(w_{+})),\\[8.0pt]
\frac{\varphi^{(0)}(\delta/2)}{2\log((1-u_{j})\Gamma(\delta/2))}+\frac{(1-u_{j})\cdot(\Gamma(\delta/2))^{2}}{\log((1-u_{j})\Gamma(\delta/2))}\cdot\partial_{\delta}u_{j},&u\in[F_{\delta}(w_{+}),1-10^{-8}].\end{cases}\end{split}

(8)

For the central chi-square cumulative function
F_{\delta}
, we have:

1.

\partial_{\delta}u_{1}=0
and
\partial_{\delta}u_{2}=\partial_{\delta}F_{\delta}(w_{-})
in the first region;

2.

\partial_{\delta}u_{1}=\partial_{\delta}F_{\delta}(w_{-})
and
\partial_{\delta}u_{2}=\partial_{\delta}F_{\delta}(w_{+})

3.

\partial_{\delta}u_{1}=\partial_{\delta}F_{\delta}(w_{+})

in the tail region.

4.2
Parameter sensitivities of the CIR model

In this section, we derive the analytical sensitivities of the CIR model, see Equation (
3
), with respect to its three parameters.
For a CIR process, parameter
a
is the mean-reversion level,
b
is the negative of the rate of convergence, and
c
is the volatility parameter.

Sensitivity with respect to the mean-reversion level.

We consider the sensitivity of the CIR process with respect to the mean-reversion level parameter
a
via the degrees of freedom parameter
\delta
in the squared Bessel process.
Since the CIR process is simulated through the squared Bessel process and we have the parameter relation
\delta=4a/c^{2}
,
the derivative of the CIR process with respect to the parameter
a
can be deduced via the derivative with respect to the parameter
\delta
by the chain rule as follows:

\partial_{a}X_{t}=\partial_{\delta}X_{t}\cdot\partial_{a}\delta=(4/c^{2})\cdot\partial_{\delta}X_{t}.

We interpret these derivatives of the CIR process in the same way as in Section
4.1
for the squared Bessel process.
For a squared Bessel process
Y
with the degree of freedom parameter
\delta
, since we have
X_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{bt}\cdot Y_{C(t)}
, the derivative can be expressed in the following form:

\partial_{\delta}X_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{bt}\cdot\partial_{\delta}Y_{C(t)}.

For the time changed squared Bessel process
Y_{C(t)}
, the time change function
C(t)
is independent of the parameter
\delta
.
Based on the analysis of the derivative of the squared Bessel process with respect to the parameter
\delta
in Section
4.1
, we have the following:

\partial_{\delta}Y_{C(t)}=C(t)\cdot\partial_{\delta}F^{-1}_{\delta},

where
F^{-1}_{\delta}
is the inverse function of the central chi-square distribution with degrees of freedom
\delta
.
The detailed expression of
\partial_{\delta}F^{-1}_{\delta}
is given in Section
4.1
.
Thus, we have established an expression of the derivative of the CIR process with respect to the parameter
a
.

Sensitivity with respect to the rate of convergence.

For a CIR process given by Equation (
3
), the rate of convergence is
-b
.
The parameter
b
occurs in both the time change function
C(t)
and the linear transformation from the squared Bessel process
Y
to the CIR process
X
.
Due to the complex positions of the parameter
b
, we cannot derive a direct expression for the derivative of the CIR process with respect to the parameter
b
.
Here, we use the score function to estimate the sensitivity with respect to the parameter
b

following the likelihood ratio framework described in Glasserman
[
6
, Section 7.3]
.
We illustrate this approach using examples of a European put option and an Asian option.

For a CIR process
X
, the price of a European put option with asset price
X_{T}
at expiration time
T
can be calculated using the following formula:

\mathrm{E}\left[(K-X_{T})^{+}\right]=\int_{0}^{K}(K-x)^{+}\cdot g_{b}(x)\mathrm{d}x,

where
K
is the strike price, and
g_{b}(x)
is the density function of
X_{T}
.
The derivative of the option price with respect to the parameter
b
is given by

\begin{split}\partial_{b}\mathrm{E}\left[(K-X_{T})^{+}\right]=\int_{0}^{K}(K-x)^{+}\cdot\partial_{b}g_{b}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathrm{E}\biggl[(K-X_{T})^{+}\cdot\frac{\partial_{b}g_{b}(X_{T})}{g_{b}(X_{T})}\biggr].\end{split}

(9)

To evaluate the expectation in Equation (
9
), we need to know the expression of
\partial_{b}g_{b}(X_{T})/g_{b}(X_{T})
.

As introduced in Section
2
, the simulation of the CIR process is closely related to the non-central chi-squared samples.
Recall the density function of the non-central chi-square distribution with the degrees of freedom
\delta

and the non-centrality parameter
\lambda
in Definition
2.1
is given as follows:

f_{\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)}\left(z\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot e^{-(z+\lambda)/2}\cdot(z/\lambda)^{\delta/2-1/2}\cdot I_{\delta/2-1}\bigl(\sqrt{\lambda z}\bigr),

where the modified Bessel function can be written as,

I_{\delta}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(x/2)^{2j+\delta}}{j!\,\Gamma(\delta+j+1)}.

The CIR process can be simulated using the method proposed by Malham and Wiese
[
17
]
,
who simulate the CIR process based on its known transition probability function.
In our scenario of the European put option, we only consider the time points at
t=0
and at
t=T
.
Recall the transition probability is given as follows, see for example Proposition 4.1 in Malham and Wiese
[
17
]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For a CIR process
X
, the conditional distribution of
X_{T}
given the initial value
X_{0}
is
c^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{bT}-1)/4b

times a non-central chi-square distribution with degrees of freedom
\delta
and a non-centrality parameter
\lambda
,
where,

\lambda=\frac{4bX_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}}{c^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{bT}-1)}.

(10)

Thus,
X_{T}\cdot 4b/\bigl(c^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{bT}-1)\bigr)
is a
\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)
distributed random variable,
with the non-centrality parameter
\lambda
given by (
10
).
Thus the density function of the CIR process is given by,

g_{b}(x)\coloneqq f_{\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)}\left(\frac{4bx}{c^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{bT}-1)}\right)\cdot\frac{4b}{c^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{bT}-1)}.

To simplify, we set:

z\coloneqq\frac{4bx}{c^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{bT}-1)},

and define the function
h=h(b)
as,

h\coloneqq\frac{4b}{c^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{bT}-1)}.

Then the density function of the CIR process is,

g_{b}(x)=f_{\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)}(z)\cdot h(b).

By the chain rule, we have,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial_{b}g_{b}(x)}{g_{b}(x)}=\frac{\partial_{b}f_{\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)}(z)}{f_{\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)}(z)}+\frac{\partial_{b}h(b)}{h(b)}.\end{split}

Recall that
\lambda
is given by the formula in (
10
).
Hence the density function of the non-central chi-square distribution has the following form:

\begin{split}f_{\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)}(z)=f_{1}(b)\cdot I_{\delta/2-1}\bigl(X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}/x\bigr)\end{split}

with

f_{1}(b)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot e^{\frac{2b(x+X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT})}{c^{2}(1-\mathrm{e}^{bT})}}\cdot\left(\frac{x}{X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}}\right)^{\frac{\delta-1}{2}}.

Again, by the chain rule, the derivative with respect to the parameter
b
is,

\partial_{b}f_{\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)}(z)=\partial_{b}f_{1}(b)\cdot I_{\delta/2-1}\bigl(X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}/x\bigr)+f_{1}(b)\cdot\partial_{b}I_{\delta/2-1}\bigl(X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}/x\bigr).

Then we have the following:

\frac{\partial_{b}f_{\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)}(z)}{f_{\chi^{2}_{\delta}(\lambda)}(z)}=\frac{\partial_{b}f_{1}(b)}{f_{1}(b)}+\frac{\partial_{b}I_{\delta/2-1}\bigl(X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}/x\bigr)}{I_{\delta/2-1}\bigl(X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}/x\bigr)},

with,

\frac{\partial_{b}f_{1}(b)}{f_{1}(b)}=\tfrac{1}{2}T(1-\delta)+\frac{2(x+X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT})+2bX_{0}T\mathrm{e}^{bT}}{c^{2}(1-\mathrm{e}^{bT})}+\frac{2(x+X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT})bT\mathrm{e}^{bT}}{c^{2}(1-\mathrm{e}^{bT})^{2}},

(11)

and

\frac{\partial_{b}I_{\delta/2-1}\bigl(X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}/x\bigr)}{I_{\delta/2-1}\bigl(X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}/x\bigr)}=\frac{I_{\delta/2}\bigl(X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}/x\bigr)}{I_{\delta/2-1}\bigl(X_{0}\mathrm{e}^{bT}/x\bigr)}\cdot\frac{X_{0}T\mathrm{e}^{bT}}{x}+\delta/2-1.

(12)

Here, equation (
12
) follows from the formula of the derivative of the modified Bessel function,

\partial_{x}I_{\delta}(x)=\frac{\delta}{x}I_{\delta}(x)+I_{\delta+1}(x).

Finally, we calculate the derivative of
h=h(b)
as follows,

\frac{\partial_{b}h(b)}{h(b)}=\frac{1}{b}-\frac{T\mathrm{e}^{bT}}{\mathrm{e}^{bT}-1}.

(13)

In summary, we observe that
\partial_{b}g_{b}(x)/g_{b}(x)
is given by the sum of the terms on the righthand side in (
11
), (
12
) and (
13
).
We can thus calculate the derivative in (
9
) via the righthand sides in (
11
), (
12
) and (
13
) by simulating the asset price
X_{T}
.

Next, we apply the score function to an Asian option price to estimate the sensitivity of the parameter
b
.
We assume that the payoff function of the Asian option is,

f(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{M}})\coloneqq\left(K-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{t_{m}}\right)^{+},

where
X_{t_{m}}
is the asset price at the monitoring times
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{M}=T
, and
M
is the number of monitoring times, and
K
is the strike price.
The derivative of the option price with respect to the parameter
b
can be written as follows:

\begin{split}\partial_{b}E\bigl[f(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{M}})\bigr]=E\bigl[f(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{M}})\cdot\partial_{b}\log(g(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{M}}))\bigr],\end{split}

where
g(X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{M}})
is the joint density function of the asset prices
X_{t_{0}},X_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{M}}
.
We have,

g(x_{t_{1}},\ldots,x_{t_{M}})=g(x_{1}|x_{0})\cdot g(x_{2}|x_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot g(x_{M}|x_{M-1}),

and thus,

\partial_{b}\log(g(x_{t_{1}},\cdots,x_{t_{M}}))=\partial_{b}\log(g(x_{t_{1}}|x_{t_{0}}))+\partial_{b}\log(g(x_{t_{2}}|x_{t_{1}}))+\cdots+\partial_{b}\log(g(x_{t_{M}}|x_{t_{M-1}})).

Each of the partial derivatives,

\partial_{b}\log(g(x_{t_{m}}|x_{t_{m-1}}))=\frac{\partial_{b}g(x_{t_{m}}|x_{t_{m-1}})}{g(x_{t_{m}}|x_{t_{m-1}})},

can be calculated using the same approach for the European put option case above via the righthand sides in (
11
), (
12
) and (
13
),
by substituting
X_{0}
with
x_{t_{m-1}}
, and substituting the asset price variable
x
with
x_{t_{m}}
.

Sensitivity with respect to the volatility parameter.

To consider the first derivative of the CIR process with respect to the volatility parameter
c
, let
Z_{t}\coloneqq\kappa X_{t}
for a constant
\kappa
.
The process
Z
is also a CIR process with SDE,

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=(\kappa a+bZ_{t})\mathrm{d}t+c\sqrt{\kappa}\sqrt{Z_{t}}\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(14)

We let
\hat{a}
,
\hat{b}
, and
\hat{c}
denote the parameters of the new CIR process by setting,

\hat{a}=\kappa a,\quad\quad\hat{b}=b,\quad\quad\text{and}\;\hat{c}=c\sqrt{\kappa}.

The degree of freedom
\hat{\delta}
of
Z
is given by,

\hat{\delta}=\frac{4\hat{a}}{\hat{c}^{2}}=\frac{4a}{c^{2}},

which is the same as the original degree of freedom parameter
\delta
for the CIR process
X
.
That means for the CIR processes
X
and
Z
, when considering the simulation based on the parameter sets

(\delta,b,c)
and
\bigl(\hat{\delta},\hat{b},\hat{c}\bigr)
,
the only difference is in the diffusion parameters
c
and
\hat{c}
. Since
\kappa=\hat{c}^{2}/c^{2}
, we have,

\frac{Z_{t}-X_{t}}{\hat{c}-c}=\frac{\kappa X_{t}-X_{t}}{\hat{c}-c}=\frac{X_{t}(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)}{c}.

For
\hat{c}\rightarrow c
, we have
\kappa=\frac{\hat{c}^{2}}{c^{2}}\rightarrow 1
, and thus the derivative of the CIR process with respect to parameter
c
is
2X_{t}/c
.

5
Applications

We apply our direct inversion method to the pricing of a standard European put option,
an Asian option, and a basket option to show the efficiency of our method for path-independent, path-dependent and multi-asset options.
Then we calibrate the European put option and the Asian option. We consider the model under the risk-neutral measure, and assume that the discount factor is equal to
1
.

5.1
Path-independent options: a put option on an interest rate

As an example of a path-independent option, we price a put option on an interest rate that is modelled as a CIR process.
First, we use our direct inversion method to simulate the squared Bessel process, which then generates the CIR process.
The payoff of the put option with one unit notional amount is,

\bigl(K-X_{T}\bigr)^{+},

where
K

The price

We investigate the efficiency of our direct inversion method by considering the relative error, the CPU times, and the length of the timestep in the path simulation.
As a comparison, we choose the full truncation Euler scheme by Lord, Koekkoek and Dijk

5.4
Calibration of the CIR Model

In this section we apply the direct inversion method to achieve a robust calibration of the CIR model.
The advantage of the direct inversion method is not only in the efficiency in asset path simulation but also in the flexibility with respect to the parameter values.
Specifically, the direct inversion method is based on a two-dimensional Chebyshev expansion, where one of the variables is the degree of freedom parameter of the squared Bessel process.
This means only one series of coefficients is required when simulating paths of the asset price process with different values of degree of freedom parameter.
This flexibility in parameter values is essential in model calibration.
The calibration is achieved by solving a nonlinear least-squares problem using the Levenberg-Marquardt method.
The analytical gradients that we use for the Levenberg-Marquardt method are deduced from the expressions of the sensitivities
of the CIR model with respect to its model parameters given in Section
4.2
. In this application section,
we calibrate synthetic data generated from the CIR model. Hence, in other words, the calibration is a parameter-recovery test.

Calibration problem and Levenberg-Marquardt method.

The calibration of stochastic models to observed market data is a fundamental problem in quantitative finance.
The objective of the model calibration is to determine the values of the model parameters to match the market prices.
Here we consider the calibration of option prices, and assume that the underlying asset price process is a CIR process.
The calibration problem can be formulated as an optimization problem, in which the errors between model prices and market prices are minimized.
The nonlinear least-squares method is a common approach to solving this optimization problem.
We calibrate the model to European put option prices and Asian option prices by finding the optimal values of the parameters using the Levenberg-Marquardt method
[
21
]
.

Let
P^{*}(K_{i},T_{i})
denote the market price of an option with strike price
K_{i}
and maturity time
T_{i}
, and
P(\theta;K_{i},T_{i})

be the option price calculated by the CIR model satisfying Equation (
3
) with the parameter vector
\theta\coloneqq(a,b,c)^{T}
.
To calibrate the CIR model, we minimize the difference between the option price calculated by the CIR model and the observed data in the real market.
The difference between the model data and the real market data is measured by the residual.
Here we set the residual vector for
n
options as follows:

r(\theta)\coloneqq\left(r_{1}(\theta),r_{2}(\theta),\cdots,r_{n}(\theta)\right)^{T},

where,

r_{i}(\theta)=P(\theta;K_{i},T_{i})-P^{*}(K_{i},T_{i})

with
i=1,2,\cdots,n
.
We define the function
f
of the residuals as follows:

f(\theta)\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}r^{T}(\theta)r(\theta),

Calibration of the CIR model can be treated as a nonlinear least-square problem; indeed
the
\theta
-value minimizing
f(\theta)
can be found via standard nonlinear optimization methods.

A widely used method to solve nonlinear least-squares problem is the Levenberg-Marquardt method
(see Levenberg
[
12
]
, Marquardt
[
19
]
and see details in More
[
21
]
).
This method can be viewed as a combination of two typical minimization techniques, the Gauss-Newton method and the gradient descent method, respectively.
For the search step of the Levenberg-Marquardt method, only the first derivatives of the target parameters are required.
We have derived the first derivatives of the CIR process with respect to its three parameters in Section
4.2
.
These results can be used to obtain analytical gradients for the Levenberg-Marquardt method. Analytical gradients are more accurate and efficient than numerical gradients.
The Levenberg-Marquardt method to find the optimal values of a parameter
\theta
is as follows:

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}+\Delta\theta_{k}
,
where
k=0,1,2,\ldots
.
The search step is defined as

\Delta\theta=(JJ^{T}+\mu I)^{-1}\nabla f
,
where
I
is the identity matrix, and
\mu
is a damping factor.
Here
J=\nabla r^{T}(\theta)
is the Jacobian matrix of the residual vector
r(\theta)
.
For an option with
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, and the specific parameter for
j=1,2,3
, we have the Jacobian matrix,

J_{ji}=\partial_{\theta_{j}}r_{i}(\theta)=\partial_{\theta_{j}}P(\theta;K_{i},T_{i}).

Then the gradient of the residual function is
\nabla f=Jr(\theta)
.

In the following sections, we perform the calibration of the CIR model to European put options and Asian options using the Levenberg-Marquardt method.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we designed a new direct inversion method to generate non-central chi-square samples and hence to simulate the squared Bessel process.
The method is based on a two-dimensional Chebyshev approximation of the inverse distribution function, where the degrees of freedom of the chi-square sample are treated as one of the variables.
Hence we can use the same series of Chebyshev coefficients to generate chi-square samples for a range of values for the degrees of freedom, making our method particularly efficient in such a setting.
The direct inversion method is efficient and of high accuracy in generating chi-square samples for all degrees of freedom,
particularly for the case of small degrees of freedom. High accuracy here means using a Chebyshev expansion with accuracy of order
10^{-8}
.
In principle, our method can be extended to a higher order, such as the
order
10^{-10}
accuracy Chebyshev expansion in Malham and Wiese
[
17
]
.
As a direct inversion method, the accuracy of the method
does not depend on the length of the timestep in path simulations of the squared Bessel process, an important factor for the efficiency of the method.
We derive expressions for the derivatives of the mean-reverting square-root process with respect to the three model parameters.
These enable us to employ a gradient-based method, the Levenberg-Marquardt method,
to develop a flexible calibration algorithm for the mean-reverting square-root process based on the new direct inversion method.
This algorithm can be used for the calibration of (discretely observed) path-dependent options, such as Asian options.

We list some directions for future research. Firstly, since we have
treated the degrees of freedom
\delta
, or equivalently the dimension of the squared Bessel process, as a variable in our Chebyshev expansion,
we can analyse the impact the model parameter
\delta
has on the model and on the method.
Secondly, we can use the simulated squared Bessel process to simulate other stochastic models, such as the CEV model.
We can extend our two-dimensional Chebyshev expansion to the simulation of the Heston model,
see also Malham, Shen, and Wiese
[
16
]
who established direct inversion algorithms for this model.
Also see Malham and Wiese
[
18
]
.
Thirdly, Makarov and Glew
[
15
]
develop an exact simulation method for squared Bessel and CIR processes
based on the representation of the transition probability as a randomized Gamma distribution.
Our direct inversion method provides an efficient and accurate method for generating chi-square samples and thus gamma samples,
particularly in the challenging small degrees of freedom scenario.
It would be interesting to investigate the combination of our direct inversion method with their exact simulation for computational efficiency and robustness.
Fourthly, we can consider extending our direct inversion method from the one-dimensional squared Bessel process to its matrix-valued extension, the Wishart process.
