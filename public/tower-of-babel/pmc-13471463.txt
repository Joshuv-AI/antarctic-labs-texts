Comparison of the Effects of Spinal Versus General Anesthesia on the Perioperative Intraocular Pressure: A prospective, randomized clinical trial
Authors: Muzaffer GENCER, Mehmet YALVAÇ
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:To evaluate the effects of spinal and general anesthesia on the intraocular pressure (IOP) in patients, who had lumbar spine and lower extremity orthopedic surgeriesMethods:Sixty adult were randomly classified into Group spinal anesthesia (SA) and Group general anesthesia (GA). The IOP, mean systemic arterial pressure, and heart rhythm were measured at 6 pre-determined timepoints (T0: Baseline, T1: Before spinal procedure or anesthesia induction, T2: After injection with intrathecal or anesthesia induction, T3: 5th minutes (min) after spinal procedure or tracheal intubation, T4: 30th min intraoperative, T5: At the end of surgery, T6: 5th min after surgery or tracheal extubation in the operation roomResults:The baseline IOP and IOP1 measurements during were not significantly different between the groups (SA = 14.28 ± 0.8 mmHg; GA = 14.62 ± 0.9 mmHg, p = 0.923; and IOP1: SA = 15.83 ± 0.9 mmHg; GA = 15.88 ± 0.9 mmHg, p = 0.929). The most significant decrease in IOP in both groups was recorded at T2 (SA: 12.37 ± 0.6. GA: 12.24 ± 0.4, p < 0.001). The decrease in IOP2 was more significant in the GA group. The IOP3 was significantly higher in GA group after intubation (GA: 19.82 ± 1.2, SA: 12.86 ± 1.0)(p < 0.05). The IOP4 and IOP5 in the SA group were lower than those in the GA group. The IOP6 in both groups was higher (SA: 15.20 ± 0.8, GA: 16.50 ± 1.0) than the baseline IOP values, albeit not significant (p > 0.05). In prone position, IOP at the T4 and T5 timepoints were significantly higher than the initial values in both the SA and the GA groups; however, the increase in the GA group was more higher than that of the SA group.Conclusion:In intraoperative period, intraocular pressure may increase in both management of anesthesia, but this increase is higher in the general anesthesia group.

Introduction

Perioperative visual loss (POVL) is a rare condition tha can develop during surgery under spinal anesthesia (SA) or general anesthesia (GA) and has no known treatment [1]. The factors that might affect intraocular pressure (IOP) during surgery include body and head positioning, methods of airway management, tonometry technique, and hemodynamic factors. Moreover, the method of anesthesia can lead to crucial IOP changes during the perioperative period. In this context, the aim of anesthesia is to lower the risk of ischemia by providing sufficient retinal perfusion, thus, preventing serious complications, such as the visual field defect and POV [2]. In this regard, one function of anesthetic management is to sustain the IOP within normal limits during surgery. The POVL during surgery is mainly associated withischemic optic neuropathy, whilediabetes, obesity, hypertension, excessive estimated blood loss, and male sex are other risk factors [3]. Other causes of POVL includecentral retinal artery–vein occlusion, cortical blindness, direct compression, and acute angle-closure glaucoma [4]. The frequency of POVL occuring during surgeries performed under GA is between 0.02% and 0.1%, while it is 0.03% in surgeries performed under SA in patients who are in the prone position. Normal IOP is 10–22 mmHg (mean 15 mmHg) under normal circumstances. Smith et al. [5] reported that dexmedetomidine used for premedication caused a significant decrease in IOP in dogs. Park et al. [6] compared the effects of rocuronium and vecuronium on IOP during orotracheal intubation, reporting no significant difference between both agents.

Although there have been studies on IOP changes in surgery performed under GA, especially in prone position, no similar studies have been conducted thatcompare the effects of SA and GA on IOP. The current literature on IOP has focused particularly on IOP changes in the prone position. Given that the number of studies comparing IOP changes during spinal and GA is limited, we conducted this study. As such, we aimed to evaluate the effects of SA and GA on the IOP in patients who had lumbar spine (such as lumbar discectomy surgery) and lower extremity orthopedic surgeries in both supine and prone positions.

Methods

In this prospective, randomized study, written informed consentwas obtained from all participants. Institutional ethics committee approval (Bozok University, Medical Faculty Ethics Committee) was also obtained. The study was conducted in the university hospital's operation theaterby the physicians of the department of anesthesiology between September 2015 and November 2025 in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 60 patients without any eye disease were included in the study. The study period was extended due to the lack of an ophthalmologist and suitable patients. However, the protocols, equipment, and anesthesia practices were consistent and standardized throughout the study period. The inclusion criteria for the study consisted of patients aged 20–45 years, categorized as I and II according to the American Society of Anesthesiology physical status classification, and scheduled for elective surgery for lumbar spine (such as lumbar disc surgery) or lower extremity orthopedic surgery under SA or GA, which did not last longer than 2 hours. Patients were excluded if they had glaucoma, an IOP between the eyes with a difference more than 8 mmHg, used drugs that might affect IOP, used β-blockers, had a cardiovascular disease, had morbid obesity, and if they refused to participate in the study. In the operation theater, the patients were randomly classified to either the GA group (n = 30) or the SA group (n = 30). Those who were administered SA were selected using a computer-generated randomization table with an allocation ratio of 1:1. Randomization was performed by an anesthesiologist who was not involved in anesthetic management or data collection. A flowchart of the study is shown in Fig. 1.

Consort flow diagram to illustrate the study design.

The patients were admitted to the operation ater 1 hour before surgery and allowed to rest in a supine position for 5 minutes (mins). Patients with normal physical examination findings were assessed for IOP and the results were recorded as the baseline. The IOP, mean systemic arterial pressure (MAP), and heart rhythm were recorded at 6 timepoints (T0–T6; Table 1). The IOP levels were compared both among the patients and between the groups (Table 1).

Patients were administered 1–2 drops of 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride as a local anesthetic 2–3 mins before the procedure, after which the IOP measurements were recorded by an ophthalmologistusing a Tono-Pen XL applanation tonometer (Reichert, Inc, Depew, NY, USA). After both measurements were taken, prophylactic ophthalmic drops (consisting of 0.03% oflaxosin) were applied. Measurements were taken from the right then the left eye before the mean IOP value was calculated. The IOP values between 10 and 20 mmHg were considered normal while an increase of more than 30% of the initial value was considered significant. In the GA group, all patients received 1 mcg/kg fentanyl intravenously and then inhaled 100% O2 for 5 mins. The mean dose of fentanyl was 75 mcg in the GA group. Before anesthesia, propofol (2–2.5 mg/kg) intravenously. After, 0.6 mg/kg of rocuronium was given intravenously. For adequate muscle relaxation, we manually ventilated the patients for longer than 2 mins. The rest of the anesthesia procedure was maintained with 2–3% sevoflurane. After the expired concentration of inhalation agent reached 1 minimum alveolar concentration, we performed endotracheal intubation. A 60% NO2 in 40% O2 was delivered. During anesthesia, fractionated doses of 0.5–1 mcg/kg fentanyl and muscle relaxants at maintenance doses were administered intravenously. While IOP measurements were performed by an ophthalmologist, an anesthesiologist performed endotracheal intubations.

In the SA group, the ocular procedure was performed in a sitting position using a 25G sharp-pointed needle that was positioned at either L4-5 or L5-S1 to alignthe point of the needle parallel to dural fibers. When clear cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was observed, 3 ml (15 mg) of 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine was injected into the intrathecal space by orienting the open-end of the needle towards the direction of the head. To prevent post-procedure hypotension, an adequate fluid replacement was provided before the procedure and arterial pressure was maintained within normal limits. Hypotension was treated with an intravenousbolus of 1 μg/kg phenylephrine. Bradycardia was treated with a 0.01 mg/kg intravenousbolus of atropine.

Statistical analyses

The analyses were performed using TURCOSA statistical software (Turcosa Analytics Ltd. Co., Turkey). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are presented as number (n) and percentage (%). The distribution characteristics of continuous variables were evaluated using histograms, Q-Q plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test; the homogeneity of variance was examined using the Levene test.

When comparing the baseline characteristics of the groups, the independent samples T-test was used for continuous variables where parametric assumptions were met, and the Chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. For continuous variables, in addition to statistical significance, Hedges’ g effect size was calculated to quantitatively assess the magnitude of the difference between groups. For Hedges’ g, approximately 0.20 was considered small, 0.50 medium, and 0.80 large effect size.

The IOP, mean arterial pressure (MAP), and heart rate (HR) were measured serially during the perioperative period and compared between the SA and GA groups at the predefined timepoints. Therefore, the findings from serial measurements should be interpreted within the framework of intergroup comparisons at defined timepoints and not as fully adjusted longitudinal estimates. Subgroup comparisons based on surgical position (supine and prone positions) were considered exploratory by nature.

The sample size was determined a priori based on data from a previously published study evaluating perioperative IOP under different anesthesia conditions [7]. In our study, an IOP difference of approximately 3.5 mmHg between the groups was considered clinically significant, as even moderate increases in IOP during the perioperative period can be considerable in terms of ocular perfusion and perioperative visual risk. Assuming a standard deviation of 4.0 mmHg, a bidirectional α level of 0.05, and a power of 80%, it was calculated that at least 23 patients were required for each group. To compensate for possible sample loss and missing perioperative measurements, 30 patients were included per group. Furthermore, descriptive post hoc power analyses were calculated for selected group comparisons, and high-power values (approximately 0.99) were obtained at significant timepoints. However, the primary assessment of sample size was based on a priori power analysis.

Due to the randomized design of the study, the initial aim was to balance potential confounding variables between groups. However, considering that multivariate adjustment could provide additional methodological contributions, we considered this as a limitation of the study. For all analyses, a 2-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For surgeries performed in the supine position, there was no significant difference in the baseline IOP (SA = 14.28 ± 0.8 mmHg vs. GA = 14.62 ± 0.9 mmHg; p = 0.923) and IOP-T1 (SA = 15.83 ± 0.9 mmHg vs GA = 15.88 ± 0.9 mmHg; p = 0.929) measurements in either group nor within the groups. The most prominent decline in IOP forboth groups was recorded during the T2 timepoint (after intrathecal drug injection or induction with the anesthetic agent). The IOP-T2 levels in the SA group after bupivacaine injection and anesthesia induction in the GA group were significantly lower than the baseline (IOP-T0) values (p < 0.001). The decline in the IOP-T2 value in the GA group was more prominent compared with the SA group. The IOP-T3 values in the GA group (after intubation or after 5 mins of the spinal procedure) were significantly higher than those of the SA group (GA = 19.82 ± 1.2 mmHg vs. SA = 12.86 ± 1.0 mmHg; p < 0.05). The IOP-T4 (intraoperative at 30 mins) and IOP-T5 (at the end of surgery) values in the SA group were lower than those in the GA group. The IOP-T6 values in both groups—5 mins after surgery in the SA group and 5 mins after tracheal extubation in the GA group) were more than IOP-T0 values; however, there was no significant difference between the groups (SA = 15.20 ± 0.8 and GA = 16.50 ± 10; p > 0.05; Table 4).

For surgeries performed in the prone position (lumbar discectomy surgery and Achilles tendon rupture), the baseline IOP-T0 and values at the T1, T2, and T3 timepoints in both the SA and the GA groups were similar to the surgeries performed in the supine position. In both groups, patients were changed to a prone position after the T3 timepoint and the operation was continued inthe neutral head position. The IOP-T4 and IOP-T5 values measured while patients at the prone position were significantly higher than the initial values in both the SA and the GA groups; however, the increase in the GA group was more prominent than the SA group. Group GA IOP-T4 (22.4 ± 2.8 mmHg) and IOP-T5 (32.8 ± 3.2 mmHg) values were significantly higher compared withthe SA group (IOP-T4 = 18.9 ± 2.4 mmHg, p = 0.016; IOP-T5 = 25.8 ± 2.6 mmHg, p < 0.001). The patients in both the SA and the GA groups were changed to thesupine position at the T6 timepoint, and the IOP values were higher than the initial values; however, they were not significant (SA = 16.24 ± 0.6 mmHg vs. GA = 18.16 ± 2.4 mmHg; p > 0.05; Table 5).

We compared the MAP, HR, and IOP levels between the groups. The change in MAP and HR trends at baseline (TO) and T1 points were similar between the groups, and there was no significant difference (p > 0.05). We observeda significant decrease at T2 in both groups; however, this was more prevalent in the GA group in which propofol was used for induction. When we compared the MAP and HR levels at T3, we found a prominent increase in the GA group due to intubation. The MAP and HR measurements in both groups at T3 and T4 showed similar trends while there was an increase compared with previous measurements in both groups at T6.

Two patients in the SA group developed hypotension (MAP < 50 mmHg) and bradycardia (HR < 50 bpm) after the SA procedure. Hypotension was treated with 10 mg intravenous ephedrine and additional colloid fluid replacement or 4–6 ml/kg/h Ringer lactate solution. Bradycardia was treated with 0.5 mg intravenous atropine. None of the patients in eithergroups suffered from any vision changes, congestion, eye inflammation, ordiscomfort in the eyes during the postoperative period.

Discussion

Elevated IOP leads to optic nerve damage. Rapid shifts or sustained low pressure (hypotony) in IOP can cause vascular issues and corneal damage. Monitoring these changes in IOP is crucial for patients during surgery. Therefore, IOP is carefully regulated and disturbances are often implicated in the development of pathologies, such as glaucoma, uveitis, and retinal detachment. Sudden increases in IOP can cause mechanical stress and ischemic effects on the retinal nerve fiber layer [8]. The IOP can be measured by tonometry and pressures between 11 and 21 mmHg are considered normal [9].

In our study, none of the patients in either group developed POVL; however, we assessed whether apatient developed visual loss according to their complaint of postoperative visual loss. A study by Chang et al. [10] revealed that increased IOP reduce ocular blood flow, leading to optic nerve edema and ischemia that can cause POVL, which is very rare (0.013 – 1% incidence) but catastrophic for the patient. Studies have reported a relationship between longer surgery duration and a critical increase in IOP that led to a risk of POVL. In a systematic study on spinal, general, and orthopedic surgeries, the incidence of POVL was 0.0235% [11]. In a study on patients who had robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomy in the Trendelenburg position and whose eye examinations were evaluated postoperatively, the rate of transient but significant visual field defects that did not cause any abnormal findings in the optic nerve or the retina was 20% [12]. In another study, it was emphasized that the wider use of robotic or laparoscopic surgeries in various fields would increase the prevalence of POVL [13]. On the other hand, Kondo et al. [14] showed that in robotic-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy cases, patients placed in the Trendelenburg position had a moderate increase of 7 mmHg in IOP 90 mins after positioning compared with the baseline value. Furthermore, they found that the baseline IOP was reached in 30 mins after the patient was turned to the supine position.

In our study, we found a significant decline in IOP in the SA group after the procedure and the GA group after induction. During surgery, aprominent increase in IOP was observed in the GA group, especially inthe prone position, while the increase in the SA group waslower. When we reviewed the literature, we found studies that showed an IOP decrease after SA, which was consistent with our findings [15]. In a recent study, the effects of SA versus GA with total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) on IOP during prone spinal surgery were compared, with an increase in IOP reportedly greater in surgeries performed under GA [16].

Another study revealed that during spinal surgery in cases performed under GA with Propofol, the increase in IOP after assuming the prone position was higher compared with cases performed with SA and desflurane anesthesia [17].

In a study on the relationship between theloss of CSF and IOP during neurosurgical procedures on 64 patients byCzorlich et al. [18], it was concluded that the loss of CSF as a result of dura opening would lead to a decrease in IOP. Sekeryapan et al. [3] studied the relationship between the IOP in patients receiving SA and blood pressure, and proposed that the decrease in blood pressure decreased IOP. The CSF pressure and cerebral perfusion pressure are closely related. A majordecline in MAP causes a decrease of cerebral perfusion pressure, thus, a decline in the CSF pressure. Asghar et al. [19] administered topical bupivacaine or lidocaine to the eyes of dogs and did not identify any significant relationship between these agents and IOP.

The increase in IOP in surgeries performed under GA and in the prone position has been suggested in some studies. In a study by Cheng et al. [20], the authors emphasized the importance of the duration spent in the prone position during GA and found a correlation between the duration and the increase in IOP. Other studies show a relationship between head orientation and IOP. In our study, follow-up of the patients was performed by using horseshoe-shaped headrest to provide a neutral head position. Emery et al. [21] showed that the elevation of the head at 10° prevented IOP increase related to the prone position comparedwiththe neutral head position in spinal surgeries performed in the prone position. Molloy et al. [22] changed the position of patients to supine temporarily for 5–7 mins when the duration of the surgery in the Trendelenburg position was extended and observed that the IOP increase of 35.7 mmHg could be decreased to 18.8 mmHg by this supine intervention method.

In our study, we observed a significant increase in IOP after orotracheal intubation of the patients in the GA group. Similarly, Tuzcu et al. [23] showed an increase in IOP after the orotracheal intubation in their studies. In laparoscopic surgeries, the end-tidal CO2 and central venous pressure (CVP) tend to increase due to rising intrathoracic pressure and postural changes, which could lead to an IOP increase. Other factors that contribute to the increase in IOP are pneumoperitoneum that candevelop due to an excess of CO2 insufflation and the Trendelenburg position, which cantrigger ocular hypertension [6]. In a recent study, it was emphasized that the Trendelenburg position poses more risk than pneumoperitoneum for IOP increase [24]. The ASA recommended that the head and the heart should be almost at the same level as much as possible because of the POVL risk during spinal surgery. The pneumoperitoneum pressure during laparoscopic surgeries should also be maintained at 10 mmHg [25]. In another study, a smaller Trendelenburg position tilt angle and shorter surgery duration decreased the ratio of postoperative complications by inhibiting the increase in IOP [26]. To prevent the potential increase in IOP during the surgery of patients who are at-risk, it is advisable to prevent direct pressure tothe eyes, to hold the head at neutral position at the same level or higher than the heart, have close follow-up of the blood pressure and CVP, and specifically, intravascular volume maintenance by colloidal fluid support [27].

In our study, intravenouspropofol for induction and inhaler agent sevoflurane for sustaining the anesthesia were used in the GA group. After induction with propofol, the IOP levels in patients decreasedbelow the basal levels. Otherstudies showed a decrease in IOP after induction by anesthetic agents during GA. Anesthetic agents—among them are propofol, opioids, and neuromuscular blockers commonly used in the management of anesthesia—cause a decrease in IOP while ketamine and succinylcholine cause an increase in IOP [28]. The cause of the decrease in IOP by propofol was shown to be the depression of central nervous system. Researchers have investigated the effects of fentanyl, sufentanil, alfentanil, and remifentanil on IOP after succinylcholine and endotracheal intubation during GA and found that remifentanil at 1.0 μg kg–1 best attenuated the increase in IOP measured after administration of succinylcholine and endotracheal intubation [29].

The depression of the ocular centers can lead to a decrease in IOP by facilitating the drainage ofthe humor aqueous and relaxing the extraocular muscles. In a study by Seo et al. [30], comparison of the anesthesia management by propofol versusdesflurane duringlaparoscopic colorectal surgeryrevealed that the IOP in most patients in the desflurane group increased to a level higher than 25 mmHg while no such increase was found in the propofol group. In some studies, the effect of propofol on IOP was safer than the effects of sevoflurane and isoflurane. In another study on surgeries performed inthe lateral position, the IOP in patients administered sevoflurane was higher than 28 mmHg [7]. In astudy ofthe pediatric age group, a significant decrease in IOP was reported to be associated with combined remifentanil with sevoflurane or propofol use [31]. In another study, the effect of inhaler agents, such assevoflurane and isoflurane, versus propofol-based TIVA in the management of perioperative anesthesia of laparoscopic surgeries on IOP was compared, and it was reported that propofol-based TIVA was more convenient in those types of surgeries due to its decreasing role on the risk of optic nerve damage as it did not cause IOP to increase [32].

We suggest that IOP changes-related to the anesthesia preference and surgery-related POVL would be more popular as robot-assisted laparoscopic surgery becomes widely available, and advocate that our IOP results from surgeries performed under both SA and GA could be beneficial.

Study limitation

In our study, the IOP measurements were performed in the operation theater by an ophthalmologist at various timepoints before and after the surgery. However, retinal, optic nerve, or field of vision changes had not been evaluated withadvanced ophthalmologic examinations during sudden increases in IOP.

Our study showed that both after GA induction and SA procedures, IOP reduction could be observed. Surgeries performed under both SA and GA can increase IOP during the intraoperative period, but the increase in IOP was higher in the GA group. The prone position also increased IOP, which was higher in the GA group compared with the SA group. The SA could be more helpful in patients who are at risk—especially forlumbar spine surgeries with short operation duration and performed in the prone position along with lower extremity orthopedic surgeries performed in the supine position—as it causes less of an increase in IOP.

One of the major methodological limitations of this study was that IOP measurements obtained serially during the perioperative period were evaluated as intergroup comparisons at predefined timepoints rather than within a fully adjusted longitudinal analysis framework. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly regarding inferences concerning the pattern of change with time and subgroup analyses based on surgical position. Additionally, subgroup analyses based on surgical position are exploratory in nature and require validation with larger samples and more comprehensive analytical models. Furthermore, while randomization is an approach aimed at initially balancing potential confounding variables between groups, the lack of multivariate adjustment does not mean that confounding factors are completely ruled out. Another limitation of the study was that it was conducted over a long period and with a small sample size due to the lack of ophthalmologists and suitable patients.

In this study, increases in IOP were observed to be more pronounced in the GA group compared with the SA group, particularly for procedures performed in the prone position. However, these findings need to be interpreted with consideration of the limitations of the analytical approach used and the exploratory nature of the subgroup studies. Therefore, while the current results reveal a clinically significant trend regarding perioperative IOP changes under SA and GA, they require further longitudinal and multivariate analyses.
