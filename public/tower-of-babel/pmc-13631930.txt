SCONE: a subset-contrastive method for multi-omics network embedding
Authors: Pedro Henrique da Costa Avelar, Jonathan Cardoso-Silva, Min Wu, Sophia Tsoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNetwork-based analyses of omics data are widely used and, while many of these methods have been adapted to single-cell scenarios, they often remain memory- and space-intensive. As a result, they are better suited to batch data or smaller datasets. Furthermore, the application of network-based methods in multi-omics often relies on similarity-based networks, which lack structurally discrete topologies. This limitation may reduce the effectiveness of graph-based methods that were initially designed for topologies with better defined structures. We propose Subset-Contrastive multi-Omics Network Embedding (SCONE), a method that employs contrastive learning techniques on large datasets through a scalable subgraph contrastive approach. By exploiting the pairwise similarity basis of many network-based omics methods, we transformed this characteristic into a strength, developing an approach that aims to achieve scalable and effective analysis. Our method demonstrates synergistic omics integration for cell type clustering in single-cell data. Additionally, we evaluate its performance in a bulk multi-omics integration scenario, where SCONE performs comparable with the state-of-the-art despite utilizing limited views of the original data. We anticipate that our findings will motivate further research into the use of subset contrastive methods for omics data.Keywords: graph contrastive learning, single-cell, multi-omics, autoencoder

Introduction

Precision medicine increasingly depends on the ability to integrate molecular information across multiple omic layers, capturing the complex interplay of genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic, and proteomic regulation that underlies disease phenotypes [1, 2]. Recent advances in single-cell and spatial multi-omics technologies now allow such measurements at unprecedented scale and resolution [3]. These developments have created both an opportunity and a challenge, i.e. the opportunity to map biological systems comprehensively, and the challenge of developing analytical frameworks that can extract meaningful structure from vast, heterogeneous datasets.

Network-based analyses have long been recognized as powerful means to uncover relationships between network topology and system behaviour [4–7], and have been widely applied to the integration of single- and multi-omics data across batch, single-cell, and spatial omics contexts [8]. Representing omics measurements as graphs enables the identification of modules, regulatory circuits, and functional associations that would otherwise be difficult to infer from isolated data types.

Recent advances in machine learning, particularly in deep learning, have significantly expanded the potential of network-based methods, with graph [9, 10] and knowledge graph embedding techniques [11–13] becoming integral to the standard toolkit for biological analyses [6, 7]. Building on these foundations, graph contrastive learning (GCL) methods have emerged as a powerful paradigm for exploiting structural information in biological networks, allowing models to learn representations that are both discriminative and topology-aware [14–16]. These methods learn low-dimensional representations that preserve topological and semantic information, providing a principled way to integrate multi-modal biological data.

Despite their promise, the application of network analysis in omics data often relies on similarity-derived graphs (k-nearest neighbours (KNN) or correlation networks) which lack well-defined discrete topologies. Such approximate structures limit the effectiveness of many GCL algorithms that were originally designed for explicit edge relationships. Moreover, existing graph contrastive models typically require pairwise comparisons across all nodes, resulting in quadratic memory growth that becomes prohibitive for large single-cell or spatial datasets.

To address these limitations, we propose SCONE (Subset-Contrastive multi-Omics Network Embedding) a scalable framework that adapts subset-contrastive learning to network-based multi-omics integration. SCONE transforms the pairwise similarity foundation of omics graphs into an advantage, learning robust joint embeddings from overlapping data subsets while maintaining linear-to-sublinear memory complexity. Through comparative analysis with state-of-the-art methods, we demonstrate that SCONE achieves competitive performance in both bulk and single-cell multi-omics scenarios while substantially reducing computational cost. More broadly, this work highlights the potential of subset-based contrastive learning as a general strategy for scalable biological network analysis.

Related work

Multi-omics representation learning has become a cornerstone of integrative biomedical analysis, providing a means to uncover latent biological relationships and enhance downstream predictive modelling. Early approaches such as MOFA and MOFA+ established probabilistic frameworks for multi-view data integration [17, 18], and subsequent studies extended these ideas using product-of-experts (PoE) and variational information bottleneck formulations to model complex cross-omic dependencies in bulk datasets [19].

Beyond probabilistic models, generative and contrastive learning methods have gained prominence for multi-modal integration. Subtype-GAN introduced a generative adversarial network (GAN) for joint omics representation [20], while methods such as Subtype-DCC, DMCL, and MOCSS adapted contrastive objectives to multi-view datasets to improve clustering and subtype discovery [21–23]. These methods highlighted the potential of contrastive and generative approaches for aligning heterogeneous omics modalities.

Recently, graph neural components have been incorporated into multi-omics integration frameworks, motivated by the view that biological systems can be naturally represented as networks. Subtype-MGTP employs graph convolutional modules to embed omic features within a multi-omics-to-protein translation framework, producing predicted protein expression profiles that are subsequently clustered using a contrastive deep subspace approach [24]. Subtype-MGTP jointly analyses multiple omics modalities for protein translation, with clustering performed on the inferred protein representation rather than a multi-omics latent representation. While this approach leverages graph neural modules to encode omic structure, it does not explicitly operate on sample-level similarity graphs or perform GCL.

The emergence of high-dimensional single-cell and spatial multi-omics technologies [3] has spurred the development of scalable methods such as TotalVI [25] and Multigrate [26] which combine probabilistic modelling with nonlinear data integration. Among recent graph-based approaches, GraphST [27] constructs KNN graphs from spatial transcriptomic profiles and applies Deep Graph Infomax (DGI) [28] to learn embeddings that preserve spatial structure and improve clustering performance. Similarly, scZAG [29] incorporated a zero-inflated negative binomial variational autoencoder [30] alongside GCL to improve single-cell clustering accuracy.

Recent graph neural network-based frameworks have also been proposed for spatial multi-omics integration, including SpatialGlue [31] and PRAGA [32], which leverage spatial neighbourhood information to align multiple molecular modalities across tissue sections. While these methods demonstrate strong performance in spatial transcriptomics settings, they are specifically designed for spatially resolved data and rely on deterministic, full-graph message passing. By contrast, non-spatial single-cell and bulk multi-omics settings require learning from similarity-derived graphs rather than physical neighbourhoods, motivating scalable objectives that can operate on sampled subgraphs and adapt to modality-specific biological signal.

Despite these advances, most GCL frameworks applied in omics data rely on similarity-derived graph structures (typically k-nearest-neighbour or correlation networks), which lack the discrete topologies that many GCL methods were designed to exploit. As a result, algorithms originally designed for discrete graphs may perform suboptimally, and their pairwise contrastive objectives often entail quadratic memory costs, limiting scalability to large single-cell datasets [15, 29].

To address these limitations, subset-contrastive learning has recently emerged in the machine learning literature as an effective strategy for scalable graph representation learning. Methods such as SubG-Con [33] and AdaGCL [34] achieve comparable embedding performance to full-graph approaches while reducing computational overhead by training on overlapping node subsets. However, these techniques have not yet been adapted to biological data. SCONE adapts these techniques to address this gap by adapting subset-contrastive principles to multi-omics integration. It leverages the similarity-based nature of omics graphs to enable efficient, subgraph-level contrastive training, achieving near-linear scalability while preserving biological structure across modalities.

Methodology

In order to outline the methodology employed in our experiments, first an overview of our framework is described, followed by detailed description of the graph attention network (GAT) layer, the framework of our proposed model, and finally the experimental pipelines and performance metrics utilized. Additional experimental details can be found in Supplementary Material for “SCONE: Subset-Contrastive Multi-Omics Network Embedding,” Experimental Setup section.

Overall framework

SCONE integrates multiple omic modalities through a unified graph-based embedding framework (Fig. 1). The model is implemented as a multi-modal graph autoencoder trained using a subset-contrastive learning objective, where modality-specific graph encoders are combined through a Product-of-Experts fusion mechanism. Each omic dataset is represented as a similarity graph in which nodes correspond to samples (cells or patients) and edges encode molecular similarity. Modality-specific graph encoders learn latent embeddings that capture local and global structure within each omic layer.

Subset-Contrastive multi-Omics Network Embedding framework

Building on the modality-specific graph embeddings produced by the encoders described in the previous section, SCONE was developed by adapting a Gaussian-prior Product of Experts (PoE) [41] Variational Autoencoder (VAE) [19] into a Multi-modal Deep Autoencoder [42]. Unlike [42], SCONE does not rely on pretraining using restricted Boltzmann machines. Instead, all pretraining is performed through contrastive learning inspired by DGI [28]. The encoder and decoder architectures employ graph-based modules, specifically using GATs [37, 38]. A detailed description of the framework is provided below and visualized in Fig. 1.

The SCONE framework handles Omics views, where each omics view contains a data table with samples (e.g. single cells or bulk samples) and features (e.g. gene or protein expression probes). The framework’s goal is to construct individual latent spaces for each omics view, which are then combined into a common latent space . From z, the model reconstructs each input table and then applies a reconstruction loss for each omics layer.

To achieve this, omics-specific encoders are constructed using GATs. As GATs require a graph as input and omics data do not inherently form a network, a KNN-graph is generated for each omic layer, connecting each sample to its nearest neighbours. Unless otherwise stated, we use k=15 for graph construction, following common practice in previous work [43] and supported by sensitivity analysis (Fig. S8). Many contrastive learning methods require either a linear number of comparisons (e.g. DGI [28]) or a quadratic number of comparisons, where each node is contrasted with every other node (e.g. [15, 24, 29]). Our analysis demonstrates that, under a sampling size smaller than , our method requires less memory for these linear-to-superlinear contrastive learning strategies. Although the reduction factor depends on the ratio , the improvement in memory usage is particularly crucial for datasets with large sample sizes, where the existing, full-graph algorithms become computationally prohibitive. For instance, the implementation of the SURER algorithm [15] exceeded available memory resource (≥ 64 GB) when applied to the dataset from [35], whereas SCONE completed training without issue.

These empirical observations are consistent with the theoretical results presented in Theorem 1, which show that the subset-contrastive formulation scales at most linearly with the number of samples and achieves sublinear memory growth relative to conventional full-GCL, which is highlighted in Fig. S9. We also provide results for the case where someone has a fixed memory budget, for an increasingly large dataset in Fig. S10, and for increasing subset sizes with a fixed dataset size in Fig. S11.

From a computational perspective, models such as TotalVI [25] operate directly on feature matrices using VAE architectures and, therefore, do not require graph construction or message-passing operations, typically resulting in lower per-epoch computational overhead. By contrast, SCONE introduces additional computation through graph-based encoders and subset-based contrastive learning. However, the subset-sampling strategy enables scalable graph-based representation learning while keeping memory usage and runtime practical for large datasets. Therefore, one of the main contributions of SCONE is the subset-contrastive objective which enables subset-sampled training to be used with graph-based methods, thereby facilitating scalable graph-based learning on large datasets.

SCONE’s single-omics models also performed remarkably, outperforming both its multi-omics counterpart and Subtype-MGTP in five datasets based on values. In BRCA, KIRC, and LUAD, the RNA-only SCONE model achieved significantly better results than all other models (P<.05). For SKCM, SCONE RNA-only outperformed Subtype-MGTP and was comparable to its multi-omics version. In UCEC, SCONE using only the methylation layer performed on par with both its multi-omics model and Subtype-MGTP, highlighting its robustness even in single-omics scenarios.

Although Subtype-MGTP achieved the highest average in BLCA, the differences were not statistically significant when compared to SCONE’s multi-omics or single-omics models using RNA or miRNA. In STAD, Subtype-MGTP significantly outperformed SCONE single-omics models (P<.05) but did not achieve a significant improvement over SCONE multi-omics model, further underscoring SCONE’s competitive performance and adaptability across diverse molecular profiles.

These results suggest that the benefit of multi-omics integration depends on whether different molecular layers provide complementary prognostic information, whereas datasets dominated by a single informative modality may not benefit substantially from integration. Together with the single-cell experiments in Section Single-cell multi-omics integration, these findings demonstrate that SCONE generalizes effectively across distinct cancer types and omics modalities. Consistency and integration behaviour across datasets

We now analyse the results presented in Sections Single-cell multi-omics integration and Bulk cancer subtyping across The Cancer Genome Atlas multi-omics datasets to identify consistent patterns in model behaviour across datasets. SCONE demonstrated robust performance across multiple datasets, and it was able to integrate multi-omics data when complementary signal was present, while maintaining stable performance in single-omics-dominant settings. On the Kotliarov2020 dataset, SCONE exhibited a narrower performance distribution compared with TotalVI multi-omics model, indicating a more consistent clustering performance. Notably, TotalVI achieved its best performance using only the CITE-seq data, which were also used to define the dataset’s “ground truth” labels [35]. As a result, clustering metrics may favour models trained on the protein modality used to derive the reference labels. In contrast, SCONE multi-omics integration strategy enabled a synergistic interaction between omics layers, effectively leveraging complementary information to outperform models utilizing only a single omics layer. For example, SCONE integration of RNA and CITE-seq data compensated for potential noise in individual layers, allowing it to achieve superior performance compared with models relying solely on the CITE-seq layer.

These clusters demonstrated clear separation at both broad and fine cell-type annotation levels, consistent with the reference cell-type definitions reported by Kotliarov et al. [35].

Cross-omic behaviour and dataset-specific patterns

Across TCGA datasets, SCONE exhibited consistent and biologically interpretable cross-omic behaviour. Single-omics RNA models consistently yielded higher values compared with protein and generalist models, indicating that transcriptomic features captured much of the relevant variation for patient survival. The main exception was the STAD dataset, where protein markers were particularly informative for patient survival and protein-based models achieved highest performance. This pattern aligns with Subtype-MGTP’s multi-omics-to-protein translation framework, which assumes that the protein omics layer provides the most informative signal [24].

Our experiments suggest that this assumption holds primarily in contexts where protein-level regulation is strongly associated with clinical outcomes, such as in STAD. In that dataset, both SCONE’s protein-only model and Subtype-MGTP achieved superior values relative to RNA- or methylation-based models, indicating a prominent role for protein-level signalling in gastric tumour biology. In contrast, in several other cancer types (BRCA, KIRC, and LUAD), SCONE’s RNA-only models achieved higher values, suggesting that transcriptomic signals were more predictive of disease subtypes and outcomes in these settings. Notably, UCEC exhibited a distinct pattern, with methylation-based models achieving performance comparable to the best-performing configurations, highlighting the context-dependent relevance of individual omics layers.

In terms of contextualizing the observed performance differences across cancer types, a noteworthy dataset-specific pattern is observed in KIRC, where the RNA-only SCONE model substantially outperforms the multi-omics configuration (Table 4). This behaviour is biologically well grounded, as KIRC is molecularly defined in most cases by biallelic inactivation of the VHL tumour suppressor gene [47], leading to constitutive activation of hypoxia-inducible factor transcriptional programmes [48]. These programmes generate a highly coherent transcriptomic signal governing hypoxia response, angiogenesis and metabolic reprogramming, which has been shown to be strongly predictive of clinical outcome [49]. Consequently, integrating additional omics layers may dilute this dominant prognostic axis rather than enhance it.

A complementary dataset-specific pattern is observed in UCEC, where the methylation-only SCONE model achieves performance comparable to the best-performing configurations (Table 4). This result is consistent with the established biology of endometrial cancer, which is characterized by pronounced epigenetic heterogeneity. DNA methylation plays a central role in defining key molecular subgroups in this disease, including hypermethylated phenotypes linked to microsatellite instability, as described by TCGA [50], and has also been identified as an independent prognostic factor in endometrial cancer [51]. As a result, methylation profiles capture prognostically relevant structure that may be less prominent at the transcriptomic or proteomic levels.

A further context-specific pattern is observed in STAD, where protein-based models achieve superior performance relative to RNA- and methylation-based approaches (Table 4). This observation aligns with the molecular biology of gastric cancer, which is characterized by substantial heterogeneity driven by signalling pathway dysregulation and post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, leading to distinct clinical outcomes across molecular subtypes [52]. In particular, aberrant activation of receptor tyrosine kinases and downstream signalling cascades is often more directly reflected at the protein, including phosphoprotein, level than in mRNA abundance alone, as demonstrated by recent proteogenomic analyses of gastric adenocarcinoma [53]. Accordingly, proteomic data capture clinically relevant variation that is particularly informative for survival stratification in this cancer type.

Taken together, the RNA-dominant behaviour observed in KIRC, the methylation-driven stratification in UCEC, and the protein-informative patterns in STAD illustrate that the optimal molecular modality for survival prediction is intrinsically linked to the dominant pathogenic mechanisms of each cancer type. These results highlight that no single omics layer is universally optimal; rather, predictive signal strength is context-dependent and shaped by disease-specific biology.

Importantly, SCONE maintained strong performance in both single-omics and integrated configurations, demonstrating that its subgraph-based learning strategy adapts to dataset-specific modality relevance rather than overfitting to a single omic layer. This flexibility enables SCONE to generalize effectively across diverse biological and clinical contexts, providing interpretable latent representations that reflect the dominant molecular mechanisms within each dataset.

Synergy and robustness of multi-omics integration

While multi-omics integration in SCONE did not always produce synergistic improvement, it often provided an advantage over analysing omics layers individually or averaging single-omics results. Specifically, we consider that a model provided synergistic omics integration if its downstream performance is better than if we trained individual single-omics models, and average their prediction: a single-omics ensemble. This differs from cases where an improvement over any single-omics layer is always required, but we believe that our definition is useful for cases such as unsupervised multi-omics integration, where the predictive endpoint is unknown and different omics layers may be more informative against different predictive endpoints.

Synergistic behaviour was most evident in three datasets, namely BLCA, STAD, and UCEC, where the integrated model achieved higher average performance than any single-omics variant. This pattern suggests that integration is most beneficial when distinct molecular modalities capture complementary aspects of tumour biology. In three other datasets, the average performance of single-omics models (RNA, Protein, and miRNA) was within 1 SD of the multi-omics model, suggesting that SCONE maintains stable integration even when synergy is modest.

Specifically, in the BRCA dataset the single-omics ensemble was 0.05σ below the multi-omics model, while in the KIRC and LUAD datasets, the single-omics ensemble outperformed the multi-omics model by 0.77σ and 0.79σ , respectively. These differences are consistent with dataset-specific modality dominance discussed earlier (Section Cross-omic behaviour and dataset-specific patterns). For example, transcriptomic programmes dominate prognostic variation in KIRC, whereas methylation-driven stratification in UCEC and protein-level signalling variation in STAD provide complementary information across modalities. Conversely, in SKCM, despite the RNA-seq-only model achieving the highest average value, the single-omics ensemble performed 1.58σ below the multi-omics model.

Taken together, these results indicate that the benefit of multi-omics integration depends on the relative strength, complementarity and noise characteristics of modality-specific signals. When multiple omics layers capture distinct but related biological processes, integration improves representation learning, while in cases when a single modality dominates the prognostic signal, single-omics models may perform comparably. Importantly, SCONE’s subset-contrastive learning framework allows the model to adapt to these conditions without enforcing integration when additional modalities provide limited complementary information.

In summary, SCONE achieved competitive or superior performance to existing state-of-the-art models while maintaining near-linear scalability and reduced memory consumption. Its subset-contrastive design enables efficient learning from large, heterogeneous datasets without sacrificing biological coherence. By jointly embedding heterogeneous omic layers, SCONE captures subtle cross-omic relationships while preserving dominant modality-specific structure when integration provides limited benefit. These properties establish its potential as a powerful analytical framework for integrative multi-omics and precision medicine applications.

Conclusions

We introduce SCONE, a scalable framework for multi-omics data integration that demonstrated robust and consistent performance across both single-cell and bulk datasets. By employing a subgraph-based contrastive learning strategy, SCONE effectively integrates multiple omic layers, capturing complementary biological signals while maintaining computational efficiency. Despite the stochasticity introduced by subset sampling, the model consistently matched or exceeded the performance of existing network-based and nonnetwork-based approaches.

Our findings highlight the promise of subgraph-contrastive learning for large-scale molecular data. SCONE integrates heterogeneous omics modalities efficiently with reasonable computational overhead while enabling scalable graph-based representation learning, features that are particularly important in multi-omics research. Results underscore the potential of subset-based methods for challenges inherent in emerging data modalities, such as spatial transcriptomics and single-cell multi-omics, where data sparsity and resolution constraints are particularly pronounced.

While SCONE demonstrates strong performance across diverse datasets, several practical considerations should be noted. Performance depends on graph construction choices and on the presence of complementary signal across omics modalities. Graph-based encoders also introduce modest computational overhead relative to matrix-based autoencoder approaches. SCONE provides a practical trade-off between computational cost and modelling flexibility, enabling scalable graph-based multi-omics integration for datasets where full-graph contrastive methods may become computationally prohibitive. Our results further indicate that the benefits of multi-omics integration are dataset dependent, while the contrastive objective primarily acts as a representation-alignment and regularization mechanism within the SCONE framework.

These considerations motivate several directions for future work. We anticipate that SCONE will motivate further research into advancing contrastive learning in systems biology, and enable efficient, biologically grounded representation learning. Future work may focus on refining subset selection and integration strategies to enhance cross-omic synergy, as well as extending SCONE’s framework to accommodate the specific demands of spatially resolved and temporally dynamic datasets. Together, these directions have the potential to expand the applicability of SCONE to new frontiers in precision medicine and data-driven biological discovery.
