Research on the adaptation path of corporate strategy based on the differences in AI governance visions between the Atlantic and China
Authors: Min Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Strategic adaptation to divergent AI governance regimes is increasingly central to multinational corporate strategy. Regulatory divergence across the European Union, United States, and China has moved AI governance from a compliance function to a strategy-shaping constraint, yet firm-level adaptation evidence remains disproportionately conceptual and fragmented by single-jurisdiction perspectives. Empirically specifying how multinational firms operationalise cross-regime alignment through changes in deployment architecture, compliance routines, and external signaling remains necessary to support theory-building on strategic adaptation under regulatory fragmentation. To address this empirical gap, the study identifies and quantifies the mechanisms by which multinational firms restructure AI deployment, compliance, and communication in response to fragmented AI governance regimes across the European Union, United States, and China. A comparative multi-case dataset of 12 multinational firms (4 tri-jurisdictional, 4 Atlantic, 4 China-primary) was analyzed, including 48 executive and technical informants and 500 coded adaptation events. This design enables cross-jurisdiction, cross-sector comparison of adaptation intensity and configuration, producing a replicable evidence base for theory-building on how governance exposure and organisational AI maturity jointly shape strategic adaptation pathways. Path-specific composite indices for bifurcation, modularity, ethical signaling, and compartmentalization were quantified using validated scales. Regression models and moderation analyses were performed in R (R Computing, Austria) to examine associations between governance exposure, AI maturity, and adaptation intensity. Tri-jurisdictional firms had larger workforces (5,380 ± 1,245) and higher annual revenues (2,310.4 ± 450.2 million USD) than other groups. Bifurcation scores were highest in the EU (0.84 ± 0.06), while modularity peaked in multinational corporations (0.86 ± 0.04). Ethical signaling intensity was greatest in tech firms (0.82 ± 0.04), and compartmentalization scores were highest for tri-jurisdictional organizations (0.82 ± 0.05). Regression showed governance exposure significantly predicted all adaptation indices (β = 0.35–0.47, R² = 0.29–0.41, all p ≤ 0.004), with AI maturity moderating these effects (p ≤ 0.035). These results clarify why divergent AI governance regimes generate systematically different adaptation profiles, and they provide an empirically grounded basis for aligning corporate strategy, internal controls, and external communication with evolving regulatory and standard-setting expectations across jurisdictions. Firms with higher governance exposure and AI maturity exhibit more advanced, multi-dimensional adaptation across regulatory environments.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become a strategic inflection point for multinational corporations because scaling AI systems expands capability while intensifying legal, ethical, and reputational exposure1,2. Corporate strategy is increasingly conditioned by governance environments that define acceptable AI conduct and shape the boundaries within which firms innovate, deploy, and communicate technological capabilities. In broader governance research, governance quality has been shown to affect institutional and economic outcomes, reinforcing the strategic importance of governance structures for organisational decision-making (Ali et al., 2024). Divergent AI governance visions, understood as region-specific institutional logics and normative orientations that structure how AI is classified, audited, and permitted, therefore influence innovation pipelines, internal controls, and market access decisions3–5.

Governance divergence is especially pronounced across the European Union (EU), the United States (US), and China. The EU has advanced a horizontal, risk-classified approach through the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act), emphasizing transparency, conformity assessment, and rights-based oversight6,7. The US landscape remains more fragmented and sectoral, combining state-level initiatives with technical risk governance through the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)2,4. China’s model places stronger emphasis on security, industrial coordination, and administratively structured control, producing durable differences in enforcement expectations, disclosure practices, and governance priorities8–10. Firms operating across these jurisdictions therefore confront not one global regulatory logic, but several governance systems that impose different operational, documentary, and legitimacy demands on AI deployment3–5.

Governance fragmentation places multinational corporations under simultaneous pressure to align comparable AI capabilities with different compliance thresholds, documentation rules, and legitimacy expectations across jurisdictions. Reported responses include modular AI architectures, jurisdiction-specific data pipelines, and differentiated compliance and communication teams11. Existing scholarship explains the breadth of this divergence, but mechanism-level evidence remains limited. Legal-policy and cross-country analyses document cost asymmetries and regulatory inconsistency without clearly specifying how firms redesign organisational structures, workflow segmentation, and audit ownership to sustain cross-jurisdictional deployment (Kang12; Trisnawati13. Related reviews also identify fragmentation pressures while leaving internal operating-model redesign insufficiently specified (Weerts14; Kulothungan15; Olayinka et al.16.

A similar limitation appears across the literature on innovation modularity, signaling, and organisational redesign. Technical studies link modular architectures to efficiency, flexibility, and system performance while giving less direct attention to governance pressure as a driver of architectural choice (Zhao et al.17. Qualitative studies describe modular human-machine configurations under admissibility and anonymisation constraints, but jurisdiction-specific adaptation pathways remain weakly articulated (Ivcevic and Grandinetti18. Audit-oriented studies identify modularisation of assurance systems, although modularity is often presented as a discrete compliance adjustment instead of an integrated compliance-innovation design response (Mustapha et al.19. Studies on ethical signaling show that voluntary and congruent AI signals can strengthen trust, although most evidence is drawn from single-country contexts and offers limited insight into how signaling is recalibrated under conflicting regime expectations (Smith et al.20; Singh et al.21. Research on ethical certifications, disclosures, corporate social responsibility (CSR), environmental, social, and governance (ESG) reporting, and internal ethics messaging points to layered communication effects, but a jurisdiction-sensitive framework for signal design and verification remains underdeveloped (Jain and Mitra22; Rajkumar23; Zafeirakopoulos and Stefaneas24; Kanta25; Yuling and Shan26. Organisational reconfiguration research documents artificial intelligence ethics boards, model-risk roles, maturity-linked internal controls, decentralised compliance workflows, cross-functional privacy and compliance hubs, and governance units responding to generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) disputes, yet a comparative typology linking governance exposure and organisational artificial intelligence maturity to recurring redesign patterns remains absent (Thoom27; Chen28; Vaneeva29; Sreeram Reddy30; Mu31; Hatz et al.32; Kyi et al.33.

The literature therefore establishes that governance divergence affects deployment, compliance, communication, and internal organisation, while leaving the mechanism of adaptation insufficiently integrated. The central gap lies in explaining how multinational firms convert external regulatory heterogeneity into coordinated internal adaptation across deployment architecture, innovation modularity, ethical signaling, and organisational restructuring. To examine this problem, the study applies a comparative multi-case design covering 12 multinational firms distributed across tri-jurisdictional, Atlantic, and China-primary exposure profiles. Evidence from executive and technical informant interviews, document audits, and coded adaptation events is used to examine four firm-internal pathways: deployment bifurcation, innovation modularity, strategic ethical signaling, and organisational compartmentalization. The Four-Path Strategic Adaptation Model (4P-SAM) is introduced as a mechanism framework that specifies how divergent governance environments are translated into observable and measurable forms of internal redesign. Within this framework, an adaptation path is defined as a patterned sequence of redesign decisions through which a firm realigns deployment architecture, compliance routines, governance accountabilities, and external communication under conflicting regulatory expectations8,34. 4P-SAM specifies four pathways—deployment bifurcation, modular innovation restructuring, strategic ethical signaling, and organisational compartmentalization—and provides an empirically testable explanation of how governance exposure and organisational artificial intelligence maturity shape strategic adaptation under regulatory fragmentation12–33.

Research questions

The study addresses three interlinked questions: (i) How do AI governance visions differ in operational and normative scope between the EU, US, and China? (ii) How do firms restructure corporate strategy in response to this divergence? (iii) What adaptation paths emerge across sectors, and what framework best captures these transformations?

Objectives

The study aims to: (i) examine how multinational corporations implement strategic bifurcation in AI deployment pipelines across divergent governance environments; (ii) analyse the influence of governance-induced regulatory variance on innovation modularity in AI system design and auditability; (iii) investigate how firms engage in strategic signaling to reconcile conflicting ethical expectations across jurisdictions; and (iv) identify patterns of internal organisational reconfiguration undertaken in response to regulatory fragmentation between the EU, US, and China.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a comparative multi-case design using the Four-Path Strategic Adaptation Model (4P-SAM) to examine how firms respond to AI governance divergence across the European Union, United States, and China. Each firm served as a bounded case, with departmental subunits enabling process tracing of internal adaptations. Strategic responses were analyzed at both firm and subunit levels. Standardized interviews and document audits ensured comparability, while stratified governance exposure tiers facilitated structured intra- and inter-group analysis (Fig. 1).

A structured case-identification and tiering protocol was applied to operationalise “comparative” and ensure jurisdictional comparability. Eligible firms were screened through international regulatory registries, industry compliance reports, and AI-specific company lists curated by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) AI Policy Observatory, the Stanford Global AI Index, and the AI Risk and Ethics Governance Database (REG-D), supplemented by targeted searches in LexisNexis and Factiva. Search filters included “AI ethics board,” “algorithmic transparency,” “jurisdictional compliance,” “cross-border AI,” and “regulatory divergence.” Inclusion required (i) deployment of AI-enabled services, systems, or platforms in at least two of the three jurisdictions (EU, US, China), (ii) documented internal AI governance structures (ethics board, legal oversight team, or risk committee), (iii) at least one AI governance statement, regulatory response, or algorithmic audit report released/ filed between January 2020 and May 2025, (iv) access to a minimum of three internal stakeholders involved in AI deployment, compliance, or governance design, and (v) a governance exposure index score of ≥ 2 on a 0–3 scale (0 = no formal exposure; 3 = high exposure across all jurisdictions). Exclusion criteria removed firms with AI limited to internal optimisation only, single-jurisdiction operations, active litigation that constrained disclosure, or insufficient organisational scale (< 250 employees and annual revenues < USD 50 million).

Data collection and analysis followed an embedded-case workflow aligned to 4P-SAM pathways and designed for reproducible cross-case inference. Within each firm, departmental subunits were treated as embedded units for process tracing, integrating standardised stakeholder interviews with document audits to reconstruct adaptation decisions and their timing. Evidence was converted into pathway-specific measurements by applying codified extraction protocols and scoring rules across the four adaptation paths of deployment bifurcation, innovation modularity, ethical signaling, and organisational compartmentalization using consistent thresholds and computation procedures across cases (Sect. 2.3–2.6). Quantitative association testing was then performed by modelling pathway indices as outcomes against governance exposure and organisational AI maturity predictors using regression and moderation analysis enabling both within-tier and cross-tier comparisons under a unified analytic specification.

Ethics

This study involved human participants through interviews with executive and technical informants. Ethical approval was granted by the University Research Ethics Board of Chifeng Industry Vocational Technology College (Protocol AD2025/0417). All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki, with participants fully informed of the study aims, data use, and withdrawal rights. Digitally signed informed consent was obtained, and recordings were made only with explicit permission. Corporate documents were pseudonymized, and identifiable details were redacted during transcription. All data were securely stored on encrypted institutional servers in compliance with GDPR and corresponding privacy laws in the United States and China.

Case strategy

Search sources and databases

Eligible firms were identified using a structured database search of international regulatory registries, industry compliance reports, and AI-specific company lists curated by three sources: (i) the OECD AI Policy Observatory, (ii) the Stanford Global AI Index, and (iii) the AI Risk and Ethics Governance Database (REG-D). These were supplemented by keyword-based searches in LexisNexis and Factiva to extract corporate disclosures referencing AI governance adaptation. Search filters included “AI ethics board,” “algorithmic transparency,” “jurisdictional compliance,” “cross-border AI,” and “regulatory divergence.” Inclusion was limited to firms with operational footprints in more than one major regulatory environment (EU, US, China) and verifiable engagement with AI governance structures post-2020.

Eligibility criteria

Cases were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (i) firms must operate AI-enabled services, systems, or platforms in at least two of the three targeted regulatory jurisdictions EU, US, and China; (ii) firms must maintain documented internal governance structures related to AI compliance, including either ethics boards, legal oversight teams, or risk committees; (iii) firms must have released or filed at least one public or internal AI governance statement, regulatory response, or algorithmic audit report between January 2020 and May 2025; (iv) firms must allow access to a minimum of three internal stakeholders involved in AI deployment, legal compliance, or governance design; and (v) firms must score ≥ 2 on a governance exposure index ranging from 0 (no formal exposure) to 3 (high exposure across all jurisdictions). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) firms that operate AI systems only for internal optimization (HR analytics) with no external deployment; (ii) firms active in a single jurisdiction only; (iii) firms undergoing ongoing litigation related to AI ethics that would preclude open disclosure; and (iv) firms with less than 250 employees and annual revenues under USD 50 million, as such firms lack the scale for relevant governance structures.

Power analysis

The sample size of 12 firms was determined to meet the saturation and comparative depth requirements of a multi-case embedded design. Each firm includes 3–5 stakeholders, generating sufficient variation across the four adaptation paths. This sample allows for balanced representation across exposure tiers and ensures inclusion of both typical and deviant cases. Power estimation was conducted using G*Power (Heinrich Heine University, Germany) to confirm adequacy for pattern detection and cross-case inference. No additional sample expansion was necessary based on model complexity or jurisdictional spread.

Path analysis

Each of the four adaptation paths defined in the Four-Path Strategic Adaptation Model (4P-SAM) was implemented through procedural triangulation across interview data, internal firm documents, and quantifiable structural indicators. Codified extraction protocols, scoring thresholds, and computation rules were used to ensure empirical consistency across cases.

Organisation structure

Organizational adaptation was assessed by analyzing governance structures using firm-submitted organograms, compliance reporting matrices, and ethics role-definition tables. A structural dispersion index (SDI) was calculated based on the distribution of key governance roles across the European Union, United States, and China. Firms with SDI values above 0.55 were classified as structurally compartmentalized. Interviews with governance leads confirmed whether roles were operational or symbolic. Supporting materials, including minutes and compliance workflows, were coded in NVivo, and Jira Enterprise logs (Atlassian Pty Ltd, Australia) were used to verify regional task decentralization and escalation protocols.

Instruments

Baseline governance exposure

Governance exposure was measured using a corporate-level adaptation of the OECD AI Governance Maturity Index (OECD, 2023), spanning the European Union, United States, and China. Firms received a score of 1 per jurisdiction if they had (i) deployments registered under the EU AI Act, (ii) participation in or self-attestation to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and (iii) licensing or filings under China’s MIIT algorithm registry. Scores (range: 0–3) were derived from public records, firm websites, and audit databases. Intercoder reliability on a pilot subsample yielded Cohen’s κ = 0.88. Triangulation used AlgorithmWatch regulatory trace archives.

Control variables

Firm-level covariates were included to account for structural heterogeneity in strategic adaptation models. AI maturity was assessed using the 20-item MITRE AI Maturity Model3, covering six dimensions and scored on a 5-point scale, with a composite mean score (α = 0.89). Firm size was defined by total full-time equivalent (FTE) employees; sector classification followed NAICS 2-digit codes; and ownership was categorized as public, private, or state-affiliated based on filings and registry data.

Semi-structured interviews

To assess pathway-specific adaptation, semi-structured interviews used two validated instruments aligned with the 4P-SAM model. The first, the Responsible AI Maturity Index3, comprised 24 items across ethics, transparency, engagement, and accountability, with subscales averaged by pathway and internal reliability above α = 0.85. The second, AI-CAM35, covered scalability, interoperability, risk, and adaptability. Each firm contributed responses from 3 to 5 senior informants. Scores were averaged per firm, and transcribed interviews supported both quantitative modeling and qualitative validation.

Case chronology

Each firm’s strategic adaptation was reconstructed using a retrospective process-mapping protocol spanning 24–36 months (see Fig. 2). First, dated adaptation events were extracted from firm documents and coded by unit, trigger, and jurisdiction. Second, interview transcripts were chronologically recoded and aligned to document events. Third, these events were cross-checked against external regulatory milestones from the Center for AI Policy Studies. Finally, firm timelines were encoded using four temporal variables: onset timing, sequence position, response frequency, and jurisdictional driver. Intercoder reliability reached κ = 0.91, enabling process tracing and pattern analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (R Computing, Austria). Main analyses involved stratified cross-case regression models linking adaptation intensity to governance exposure levels and firm controls. Subgroup and moderation analyses examined differential effects across sectors, ownership types, and jurisdictional combinations. Sensitivity analyses were performed using leave-one-case-out diagnostics and robustness checks on indicator weighting. All significance tests were two-tailed with an α threshold of 0.05.

Results

Baseline features

Across the three exposure tiers, firm size was largest among Tri-jurisdictional firms at 5,380 ± 1,245 employees, followed by Atlantic at 4,910 ± 1,083, and China-primary at 3,755 ± 1,472 (F(2,9) = 4.12, p = 0.049, Cohen’s d = 0.91; see Fig. 3A). Annual revenue was highest in Tri-jurisdictional firms at 2,310.4 ± 450.2 million USD, compared to 1,980.3 ± 510.1 and 1,435.6 ± 410.8 (F(2,9) = 5.27, p = 0.032, d = 1.07). Geographic diversification was 9.2 ± 1.3, 7.8 ± 2.1, and 4.1 ± 1.0 respectively (F(2,9) = 9.68, p = 0.006, d = 1.42). Ownership and sectoral variables showed non-significant chi-square values (all p > 0.09). Exposure Index Scores were 2.3 ± 0.5 in Finance, 1.7 ± 0.6 in Tech, and 1.2 ± 0.4 in Healthcare (F(2,9) = 7.88, p = 0.011, Cohen’s d = 1.26; see Fig. 3B. The number of applicable regulatory instruments was 4.8 ± 0.8, 3.7 ± 1.1, and 2.9 ± 0.9 respectively (F(2,9) = 6.14, p = 0.020, d = 1.13). External audit frequency over two years was 3.2 ± 0.7 for Finance, 2.5 ± 0.5 for Tech, and 2.1 ± 0.6 for Healthcare (F(2,9) = 5.91, p = 0.022, d = 1.08). EU AI Act registration and NIST compliance rates were highest in Finance (100%) but showed no significant difference (all p > 0.05). Overall AI Maturity Scores were 0.82 ± 0.05 for public firms, 0.77 ± 0.04 for private firms, and 0.68 ± 0.06 for state-linked firms (F(2,9) = 8.41, p = 0.009, Cohen’s d = 1.31; see Fig. 3C). Data infrastructure readiness averaged 0.85 ± 0.04, 0.81 ± 0.03, and 0.73 ± 0.05 respectively (F = 9.05, p = 0.007, d = 1.39). Model lifecycle integration scores were 0.79 ± 0.07, 0.76 ± 0.05, and 0.65 ± 0.06 (F = 6.90, p = 0.016, d = 1.19). Talent pool strength followed a similar trend (F = 7.12, p = 0.015, d = 1.21). Use-case breadth and monitoring capability were significantly lower in state-linked firms (all p < 0.05). Collectively, these baseline profiles indicate that governance exposure and organisational artificial intelligence maturity co-vary with firm scale and ownership, providing a contextual basis for interpreting cross-tier differences in subsequent strategic adaptation indices.

ESG disclosure volumes averaged 58.2 ± 8.5 pages for tri-jurisdictional firms, 41.6 ± 6.7 for Atlantic, and 24.5 ± 4.2 for China-primary firms (F(2,9) = 28.33, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.38; see Fig. 3D). Strategic adaptation outcomes

Bifurcation scores were highest in the EU at 0.84 ± 0.06, followed by China at 0.78 ± 0.07 and the US at 0.63 ± 0.09 (F(2,33) = 29.45, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.07; see Figure S1A). The average number of divergent components per jurisdiction was 4.1 ± 1.2 in the EU, 3.8 ± 1.4 in China, and 2.3 ± 1.1 in the US (F = 10.66, p < 0.001, d = 1.39). Localized dataset use reached 100% in both EU and China, but only 67% in the US (X²(2) = 9.23, p = 0.010). Overall modularity scores were highest among multinational corporations at 0.86 ± 0.04, followed by conglomerates at 0.71 ± 0.05 and startups at 0.63 ± 0.06 (F(2,15) = 32.10, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.34; see Figure S1B). Component decoupling scores were 0.88 ± 0.05 for multinationals, 0.73 ± 0.07 for conglomerates, and 0.65 ± 0.06 for startups (F = 26.72, p < 0.001, d = 2.16). The number of mean API-gated components was 6.1 ± 1.2, 4.2 ± 1.4, and 2.7 ± 1.1, respectively (F = 16.55, p < 0.001, d = 1.75). Taken together, higher bifurcation and localized dataset use in the European Union and China indicate greater reliance on jurisdiction-specific deployment separation, while higher modularity, component decoupling, and API-gating in multinational corporations indicate that governance-driven adaptation is operationalised through controllable architectural boundaries that reduce cross-regime coupling and enable differentiated compliance updates.

Discussion

The present study revealed that tri-jurisdictional firms experience the highest levels of deployment bifurcation, as reflected in a mean bifurcation score of 0.84 in the European Union compared to 0.63 in the United States and 0.78 in China. This aligns with Kang12, who reported that multinational corporations face 30–50% increased compliance costs as a result of regulatory fragmentation between the EU’s human-centric frameworks, U.S. innovation-centric priorities, and China’s balanced model. Trisnawati13 similarly demonstrated that European Union firms spend 40% more on compliance for clinical AI tools than their U.S. counterparts, underscoring the significant cost asymmetries induced by regional regulatory divergence. The higher bifurcation scores found in this study indicate that tri-jurisdictional adaptation is not merely a matter of duplicating compliance procedures but requires systematic architectural segregation of AI systems, deployment pipelines, and governance layers. This structural compartmentalization reflects an organizational response to persistent legal uncertainty, as firms segment technology stacks to ensure traceable, jurisdiction-specific conformity, thus absorbing the financial and operational burden of compliance at the system design level. The observed cross-jurisdiction bifurcation profile addresses the research questions by linking (RQ1) differences in governance visions and enforcement logics across the European Union, the United States, and China to (RQ2) concrete restructuring of corporate strategy in deployment architecture, compliance routines, and assurance workflows, and to (RQ3) the emergence of a recurrent adaptation pathway that generalises across sectors as a recognizable form of strategic response. Governance divergence is expressed not only in stated principles, but in implementability of how obligations translate into documentation, assurance, auditability, and enforcement routines that must be operationalised inside firms. Recent empirical work on Artificial Intelligence Act compliance shows that technical documentation requirements reorganise how development teams generate, maintain, and verify evidence, which helps explain why high-exposure firms in the present study moved toward separable deployment and assurance structures (Sovrano et al., 2025). Under European Union governance, compliance is increasingly channelled through harmonised standards and technical specification pathways, which can compress implementation timelines while raising documentation and assurance costs, increasing the strategic value of jurisdiction-separable deployment configurations and traceable audit trails36,37. In strategic terms, higher bifurcation intensity indicates a shift from uniform global rollout toward portfolio-like architecture management, where product scope, data pipelines, and evidence artefacts are reorganised to preserve market access under conflicting regimes.

The analysis indicates that modular adaptation extends beyond technical design, with public firms achieving a composite innovation score of 0.77 and state-linked firms at 0.71, indicating widespread integration of modular toolchains and governance-driven separation of critical functions. Mustapha, et al.19 reported that over 80% of firms, particularly those with dual-board structures, implemented modular AI audit systems, maintaining structural divergence between internal and external compliance oversight in response to differing EU and U.S. legal mandates. Sreeram Reddy30 found that the introduction of AI-driven workflows in the finance sector led to a 38% improvement in fraud detection and a 27% reduction in compliance processing time, driven by the establishment of AI ethics boards and adaptive governance units. Our study’s sectoral analysis demonstrates that modularization and governance redesign are mutually reinforcing processes, especially in heavily regulated industries. Firms systematically decouple auditing, risk management, and operational modules to ensure compliance is maintained under varying regulatory scrutiny, while cross-functional teams and differentiated roles enhance the agility and precision of strategic response. Recent responsible artificial intelligence research further indicates that governance-linked innovation is strengthened when technical separation is accompanied by joint planning, joint problem-solving, and an ethical climate across organisational actors, suggesting that modular adaptation becomes more durable when coordination mechanisms develop alongside architectural separation (Ye et al., 2025). This co-evolution of modularity and organizational redesign reflects a broader shift toward adaptive corporate architectures, where legal, technical, and ethical considerations are dynamically integrated into firm strategy. In mechanism terms, modular adaptation functions as “compliance engineering” when modular boundaries are aligned to evidence requirements—traceability, risk documentation, testing artefacts, and model governance records so that each module can be justified, audited, and updated under jurisdiction-specific expectations without destabilising the full system. Recent empirical software engineering work on Artificial Intelligence Act–aligned technical documentation underscores that documentation structure and requirements coverage are not administrative add-ons; they shape development workflows and the practical feasibility of modular releases under regulatory scrutiny38. Related legal analysis further highlights that compliance often turns on the manageability of data and bias constraints, making modular separation of data pipelines and evaluation routines a governance-driven design choice rather than a purely technical preference39. This interpretation strengthens RQ2 by clarifying why modularity co-varies with governance exposure: modular partitions reduce the coordination burden of reconciling conflicting documentation and assurance demands across regimes, even when the underlying model family remains comparable.

Structural compartmentalization was most advanced among tri-jurisdictional firms in our study, as indicated by a mean SDI of 0.82, with these organizations also reporting the greatest number of dedicated compliance units and the highest adoption of matrixed regional governance layers. Chen28 similarly found that 62% of Chinese firms implemented layered control modules in the wake of AGI-related reforms, with predictive governance dashboards reducing audit lag by 18% and the emergence of new strategic roles such as “Model Risk Manager” signaling a substantive shift in internal alignment. Vaneeva29 documented that Russian Railways achieved a 42% reduction in document processing times and a 21% drop in policy violations by deploying robotic process automation alongside modular governance structures and regionally adaptive AI audit offices. The observed pattern corroborates these findings, showing that organizational compartmentalization is not merely a response to regulatory complexity but a proactive strategy to ensure compliance agility and resilience. By embedding region-specific compliance functions, developing scenario-based monitoring, and institutionalizing specialized roles, firms are able to synchronize adaptation with both local and global governance demands. This layered approach not only enhances operational efficiency but also enables real-time recalibration as external legal frameworks evolve. Interpreted as a governance mechanism rather than an administrative expansion, compartmentalization is consistent with the institutionalisation of responsible artificial intelligence governance as a bundle of structural, relational, and procedural practices (RQ 3). Contemporary synthesis work differentiates these practices as (i) structural allocation of oversight and accountability, (ii) relational coordination across functions, and (iii) procedural monitoring, escalation, and evaluation routines, providing a direct interpretive scaffold for why higher compartmentalization accompanies higher governance exposure in the present cases40. Moreover, Papagiannidis et al., (2025) defines responsible artificial intelligence governance through structural, relational, and procedural practices, providing a clearer interpretive basis for the present finding that higher governance exposure is associated with stronger compartmentalization across roles, coordination routines, and monitoring processes. Complementary evidence further suggests that responsible governance practices can be strategically consequential, linking governance design to organisational outcomes and reinforcing why compartmentalization is plausibly an adaptive capability rather than a compliance overhead alone41. Xia et al. (2026) found that firm-level evidence also indicates that responsible artificial intelligence governance can be associated with stronger corporate performance, supporting the interpretation that compartmentalization operates as an adaptive organisational capability rather than as an administrative burden alone. This framing reduces reliance on repeated coefficients by translating the SDI pattern into a mechanism claim: compartmentalization is the organisational interface that allows bifurcation, modularity, and signaling to be coordinated without collapsing into inconsistent local practices across jurisdictions.

The analysis finds that ethical signaling intensity was highest in technology sector firms, with a composite score of 0.82 and a mean of 11.8 ethics-themed public statements per year, reflecting robust multi-channel communication and third-party verifiability. Yuling and Shan26 observed that human resources reforms, including public policy disclosures, explainability mechanisms, and systematic audit logs, led to a 30% increase in employee trust after the deployment of AI-driven protocols, highlighting the impact of internal-external signal symmetry and algorithmic fairness initiatives. Smith, et al.20 experimentally that “AI convergence” improved by 28% when employees were offered optional AI signals, and that congruence between human and AI ethical cues significantly boosted trust and decision alignment. The high signal intensity and diversity observed in our technology sector cases indicate that effective ethical signaling relies not only on external reputation management, but also on cultivating internal cultural and procedural alignment. Recent analysis of corporate artificial intelligence ethics statements shows that firms increasingly tailor ethics communication to primary and secondary stakeholder groups through strategic, responsive, and operational engagement, which is consistent with the present finding that signaling is more effective when message design is linked to differentiated audiences and governance expectations (Ye and Friginal, 2026). Firms strategically deploy explainability layers, fairness programs, and targeted communication platforms to synchronize workforce attitudes with regulatory expectations. This dynamic, multidimensional signaling mechanism enhances legitimacy and trust among both employees and external stakeholders, serving as a buffer against skepticism and resistance in the face of rapid algorithmic transformation. The technology-sector signal intensity also supports a refinement to RQ 2 by showing that signaling is effective only when calibrated to legitimacy conditions, not when treated as volume amplification. This interpretation is consistent with recent experimental evidence showing a non-linear transparency effect, in which moderate disclosure strengthens trust and certainty while excessive transparency can create cognitive overload and reduce adoption, underscoring the importance of calibrated rather than maximal signaling under cross-regime conditions (Ngo, 2025). Experimental evidence indicates that disclosure of artificial intelligence involvement can generate a trust penalty via legitimacy perceptions, implying that indiscriminate transparency may undermine rather than strengthen stakeholder confidence when signals are not paired with credible governance scaffolding42. At the same time, communication research on algorithmic transparency shows that transparency can strengthen organisation–public relationships when it is structured around understandable rationale and accountability cues rather than technical detail alone43–50. These findings help surface a counterintuitive implication of the present cases: stronger signaling is not automatically “better”; signaling must be verifiable and governance-linked to avoid triggering skepticism, particularly under cross-regime normative conflict.

Theoretical and practical implications

This study advances the theory of strategic adaptation to regulatory divergence by demonstrating that modularity, bifurcation, and ethical signaling operate as interdependent organizational mechanisms, shaped by both AI maturity and governance exposure. Practically, our findings equip multinational firms with empirical benchmarks for restructuring deployment, compliance, and communication architectures, guiding senior managers in designing agile, context-specific adaptation pathways that optimize compliance, innovation, and stakeholder trust in global AI environments.

For regulators and standard-setting bodies, the findings imply that cross-regime fragmentation is transmitted into firm behavior through implementability: obligations that are concretised through standards, documentation requirements, and auditability criteria encourage architectural separation, modular evidence generation, and formalised internal governance interfaces. Policy efforts that improve interoperability of assurance artefacts of risk documentation structures, testing expectations, and shared terminology for high-risk classification can reduce incentives for duplicative bifurcation while preserving jurisdiction-specific protections. This positions standard-setting not as a technical afterthought, but as a primary lever shaping the compliance costs and strategic adaptation forms observed across firms.

Limitations and future research

The comparative multi-case design is intentionally positioned for mechanism specification and theory-building under regulatory fragmentation rather than population-level estimation. The 12-firm sample supports analytic generalisation by identifying recurring adaptation mechanisms across exposure tiers and sectors, but it does not warrant broad cross-regional or cross-sector prevalence claims. Inference is therefore bounded to the conditions represented in the cases of multinational firms operating across the European Union, United States, and China with conclusions framed as testable propositions about how governance exposure and organisational artificial intelligence maturity shape adaptation intensity and configuration. One limitation of this study is the cross-sectional design, which may overlook longer-term evolution of adaptation strategies as governance landscapes shift. A second limitation is the reliance on self-reported and documentary data, potentially introducing response bias. In our future work, we will address this by implementing a longitudinal design to track adaptation trajectories over time and by integrating direct observational or ethnographic methods to validate organizational practices and outcomes.

Conclusion

This study provides comparative evidence that governance exposure and organisational artificial intelligence maturity are systematically associated with four observable strategic adaptation pathways of deployment bifurcation, modular innovation restructuring, strategic ethical signaling, and organisational compartmentalization across European Union, United States, and China operating environments. The principal contribution is therefore a mechanism account, formalised in the Four-Path Strategic Adaptation Model (4P-SAM), that explains how regulatory divergence becomes internal redesign of architecture, assurance, communication, and governance interfaces rather than remaining a policy-level description. These findings offer actionable guidance for organizations and policymakers to develop flexible, scalable governance models. Ultimately, integrating these insights can accelerate global harmonization and drive responsible, context-aware AI deployment in complex, evolving regulatory environments.
