Cervical cancer mortality trends and inequities, 1999 to 2023: Joinpoint and spatial analyses integrated with two-sample Mendelian randomization
Authors: Xueyi Lü, Zihao Feng, Xiaolu Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cervical cancer is preventable and screen-detectable, yet it continues to cause significant mortality, with inequities across populations and regions. This study aims to identify modifiable causal exposures contributing to cervical cancer and explore how they vary across different populations. The objective is to inform targeted prevention strategies and optimize resource allocation to address these disparities. We used a two-part complementary design. First, cervical cancer mortality data (1999–2023) were extracted from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research and stratified by age, sex, race/ethnicity, census region, urbanization level, and state. Death counts and age-adjusted mortality rates were calculated. Joinpoint regression estimated average annual percent change (AAPC) and segment-specific annual percent change, and spatial patterns were visualized. Second, we conducted two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) to evaluate genetically predicted trace element/nutrition-related exposures in relation to cervical cancer risk. Analyses included allele harmonization and instrument selection; inverse-variance weighted estimation was the primary approach (fixed vs random effects based on heterogeneity). Robustness was assessed using MR-Egger, weighted median, MR-Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier, and sensitivity analyses (leave-one-out, funnel, and scatter plots). Reverse MR was performed to examine the causal direction. Mortality declines differed markedly across strata. Age-adjusted mortality rate decreased significantly among those aged 65 to 74 and ≥85 years (AAPC = −1.81 and −2.81, respectively), whereas the decline in the 25 to 34-year group was smaller and not statistically significant (AAPC = −2.10, 95% confidence interval crossed 0). Heterogeneous declines were also observed by race/ethnicity, region, and urbanization level. In MR, genetically predicted vitamin C (7 single-nucleotide polymorphism instruments) showed a modest inverse association with cervical cancer risk in inverse-variance weighted fixed-effects analysis (odds ratio = 0.9962, 95% confidence interval = 0.9924–1.0000; P = .048). Weighted median and MR-Egger were directionally consistent but nonsignificant. No strong evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity was detected, and sensitivity analyses were generally robust. Reverse MR yielded no consistent evidence. Cervical cancer mortality has declined overall, but inequities persist, underscoring the need to pair vaccination and screening with more equitable access and continuity of care. MR provides suggestive genetic support for a small protective association of vitamin C, warranting validation with stronger instruments and larger datasets.

1. Introduction

Cervical cancer is among the malignant tumors that are preventable and amenable to screening worldwide, yet it continues to impose a substantial mortality burden in real-world settings. Despite ongoing advances in human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, screening strategies, and therapeutic approaches, mortality outcomes remain uneven across populations and regions.[1] Differences in socioeconomic conditions, access to healthcare, screening coverage, and adherence to follow-up and treatment may all contribute to pronounced stratified disparities in the magnitude of mortality improvement. Against this backdrop, a national-level, systematic characterization of long-term mortality trends and their population and geographic heterogeneity is essential for identifying high-risk groups, evaluating the effectiveness of prevention and control strategies, and informing resource allocation.[2,3]

Existing studies, on the one hand, often focus on specific local areas, relatively short time windows, or single stratification dimensions, leaving an incomplete understanding of long-term national changes and spatial inequities across the United States.[4] On the other hand, evidence linking potentially modifiable exposures (e.g., nutrition and trace elements) to cervical cancer risk largely comes from observational studies, which are susceptible to confounding (such as lifestyle factors, socioeconomic circumstances, and differences in health behaviors) and reverse causation, thereby limiting causal interpretation. In other words, trend descriptions can answer “how the burden changes” but cannot specify “which exposures may be causal,” whereas exposure–outcome associations alone may be unstable due to confounding. Therefore, an approach is needed that connects “real-world changes in mortality burden” with “potentially causal exposure signals.”[5,6]

Trace elements and nutrition-related exposures are of public health interest because they are modifiable and have been repeatedly implicated in biological pathways relevant to carcinogenesis and progression, including immune regulation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses. However, observational studies often struggle to adequately disentangle these exposures from correlated factors such as dietary patterns, health awareness, and participation in screening.[7] Mendelian randomization (MR) leverages genetic variants associated with exposures as instrumental variables (IVs) and, under certain assumptions, can reduce confounding and reverse causation at the genetic level, providing evidence closer to causal inference for exposure–outcome relationships. Accordingly, applying MR to evaluate the potential causal effects of trace element-related exposures on cervical cancer risk can serve as an important complement to conventional epidemiologic evidence.[8]

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This study adopted a two-part, mutually complementary design. Part 1 used Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) to conduct a national-level description of mortality attribution and temporal trends, characterizing the US cervical cancer mortality burden from 1999 to 2023 and its variation across populations and geographic areas. Part 2 applied two-sample MR for causal inference to evaluate potential causal relationships between trace element-related exposures and cervical cancer risk, with robustness assessed through multiple sensitivity analyses. Together, the 2 components address 2 tiers of questions – “how the mortality burden has changed” and “which exposures may be causally relevant”: the former provides macro-level epidemiologic evidence and clues to population disparities, whereas the latter, within a genetic instrumental-variable framework, seeks to minimize confounding and reverse causation and to offer mechanistic insight.

Part 1 was a repeated cross-sectional time-series/ecological trend study. The study population comprised aggregated cervical cancer mortality data from CDC WONDER for 1999 to 2023. Analyses were conducted for the overall population and stratified by sex, race/ethnicity, age group, US census region, urbanization level, and state. The primary outcomes were death counts and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR), and temporal trends were assessed and compared across strata. Joinpoint regression was used to identify inflection points and to estimate the average annual percent change (AAPC) and segment-specific annual percent change (APC), with corresponding confidence intervals and statistical significance. Spatial patterns were visualized at the state level for 2023 death counts and AAMR, as well as the magnitude of change from 1999 to 2023, to highlight regional differences and potential inequities.[6,9]

Part 2 was a two-sample MR study. Genetically predicted levels of trace element-related exposures were treated as exposures (instrumented by single-nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs] significantly associated with each exposure), and cervical cancer was the outcome. Genetic exposure–outcome associations were constructed with allele harmonization to ensure consistent effect directions. To ensure instrument validity, exposures with an insufficient number of instruments were excluded; exposure-associated SNPs were selected using stringent significance thresholds, harmonized with outcome data, and retained if suitable for MR. The primary estimator was inverse-variance weighted (IVW), with fixed- or random-effects models chosen based on heterogeneity tests; MR-Egger and the weighted median method were used as complementary approaches to enhance robustness. To address potential horizontal pleiotropy and outlier influence, MR-Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier was used to detect and correct outlier SNPs, and directional pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with statistical significance defined as P < .05. Sensitivity analyses – including leave-one-out analyses, funnel plots, and scatter plots – were conducted to examine susceptibility to single-SNP influence, potential biases, and model assumptions.[6,10]

Overall, by integrating “national mortality trend characterization” with “genetic instrumental-variable causal inference,” this combined framework links population-level patterns to plausible causal mechanisms: Part 1 helps identify high-burden/disparity groups and temporal turning points, whereas Part 2 tests the causal direction and robustness of trace element-related exposures in relation to cervical cancer risk, providing leads for subsequent mechanistic studies and public health interventions (STROBE checklist). The data used in this study are all publicly available, and therefore, no additional ethical review is required.

2.2. Data sources

All data used in this study were obtained from publicly available databases and publicly released genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics, and no individually identifiable information was involved.

For Part 1, data for mortality attribution and trend analyses were sourced from CDC WONDER, a public platform maintained by the CDC. We extracted US cervical cancer-related mortality data from 1999 to 2023, obtaining overall and stratified estimates (by sex, race/ethnicity, age group, US census region, urbanization level, and state) for death counts (deaths) and AAMR, along with their 95% CIs. These data were used to characterize temporal trends in mortality burden and spatial disparities.[11,12]

For Part 2, causal inference between trace element-related traits and cervical cancer was conducted using a two-sample MR design. GWAS summary statistics for both the exposure traits (trace element-related phenotypes [trace element ID: ieu-a-1073, ieu-a-1077, ieu-a-1079, ukb-b-6888, ukb-b-7372, ukb-b-7864, ukb-b-8951, ukb-b-9596, ukb-b-11349, ukb-b-16202, ukb-b-17881, ukb-b-18593, ukb-b-19390, ukb-b-19524, and ukb-b-20447[13]]) and the outcome (cervical cancer) were obtained from the MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit (MRC-IEU) OpenGWAS platform (commonly referred to as the IEU database). This platform curates, harmonizes, and standardizes a large collection of publicly available GWAS summary statistics, providing consistently formatted fields including SNP identifiers, effect alleles, effect estimates (β), standard errors (SEs), allele frequencies, and sample sizes, thereby facilitating cross-study two-sample MR analyses. We downloaded/queried the exposure and outcome summary statistics from the IEU database and performed instrument selection, exposure–outcome harmonization, and primary and sensitivity analyses locally. Because only aggregated, publicly available summary-level data were used, this study did not require access to individual-level genotype or phenotype data and therefore meets the general ethical requirements for secondary analyses.[14–16]

2.3. Criteria for selection of IVs

In MR analyses, the selection of IVs is critical. Based on recent literature, we adopted a P-value threshold of 1 × 10−5 to ensure that a sufficient number of SNPs were available as candidate instruments. We then calculated the F-statistic for each SNP using the formula (F = [beta/SE]2). Only SNPs with F > 10 were retained to exclude weak instruments and thereby improve the reliability of the causal estimates (Table S1).[17]

In addition, to minimize the influence of rare variants, SNPs were further filtered by minor allele frequency (MAF). MAF was derived from the effect allele frequency (EAF) as follows: if EAF < 0.5, MAF was set to EAF; otherwise, MAF was set to 1 − EAF. Only SNPs with MAF > 0.01 were retained, reducing potential bias driven by rare variants.[18,19]

Finally, the filtered data were formatted for MR analyses and subjected to linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping to ensure instrument independence and improve precision. LD clumping was performed using a window of 10,000 kb and an LD threshold of r2 = 0.001. Collectively, these steps strengthened instrument validity and provided a rigorous foundation for downstream MR analyses.[20]

2.4. Statistical analysis

2.4.1. CDC WONDER-based mortality attribution analysis

All data analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1 (https://www.r-project.org/). This component used cervical cancer mortality rate data from the US CDC for 1999 to 2023, covering strata by sex, race/ethnicity, age group, geographic region, and urbanization level. Data were imported using custom functions and formatted to extract key variables, including metric, measure, year, number of deaths, and AAMR. Records were restricted to the 1999 to 2023 period and merged into a unified analytic dataset for downstream analyses.

After data preparation, Joinpoint regression was applied to assess temporal trends in cervical cancer mortality. The Joinpoint approach identified inflection points in mortality trajectories and estimated the AAPC and segment-specific APC for each subgroup. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. Outputs – including AAPC/APC estimates, confidence intervals, and P values – were compiled into tables for further interpretation.

To more comprehensively summarize changes over time, we calculated the percent change in death counts between 1999 and 2023 and integrated these results with the AAPC estimates to generate a consolidated table, with statistical significance annotated.

For visualization, we used the usmap package to generate US state-level maps displaying 2023 cervical cancer deaths, AAMR, and the percent change in mortality over time. These maps highlight interstate disparities and provide intuitive evidence relevant to public health policy.

In addition, based on the annual change estimates from the Joinpoint analyses, we produced subgroup-specific trend plots depicting cervical cancer mortality from 1999 to 2023, with statistical significance indicated. These figures will be used in the manuscript’s results graphics.

2.4.2. Causal analysis of the relationship between trace elements and cervical cancer

The second part of this study aimed to evaluate potential causal relationships between multiple exposures and the outcome, and to assess the robustness of the findings. First, we loaded the exposure datasets and performed quality control for each exposure. Exposures with fewer than 3 IVs were excluded. Candidate instruments were then selected using a significance threshold (pval.exposure < 5 × 10−6) to ensure that each exposure retained a sufficient number of SNPs for downstream analyses.

Next, outcome data were read from a local file and restricted to SNPs overlapping with those in the exposure datasets. The outcome dataset was formatted to meet the requirements of two-sample MR, ensuring consistency with the exposure data in terms of alleles, effect estimates, SEs, allele frequencies, and sample sizes. During the harmonization step, the allele orientation between exposure and outcome was aligned, ineligible records were removed, and only SNPs suitable for MR were retained. We further required that each exposure–outcome pair had more than 3 valid SNP instruments.

To improve the accuracy of causal estimates, we applied MR-Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier to detect and address outliers. When pleiotropy diagnostics suggested potential issues, outlier testing and correction were performed: detected outlier SNPs were removed prior to the main analysis; if no outliers were identified, the dataset proceeded directly to the primary MR analyses.

For the formal two-sample MR analyses, heterogeneity was first assessed using Cochran Q test to evaluate consistency across instruments. If significant heterogeneity was present, a random-effects IVW model was used; otherwise, a fixed-effects IVW model was applied. To strengthen robustness, MR-Egger regression and the weighted median method were included as complementary estimators. For all methods, effect estimates were reported as ORs with 95% CIs, and statistical significance was evaluated at P < .05 (Table S2).

For result synthesis, IVW estimates were treated as the primary findings and significant associations (P < .05) were extracted. Multiple summary tables were compiled, including instrument characteristics and F-statistics (instrument strength), the MR-Egger intercept test (directional pleiotropy), heterogeneity statistics, and full MR estimates from IVW, MR-Egger, and weighted median methods (Tables S3 and S4).

Finally, sensitivity analyses and visualizations were conducted to assess robustness, including leave-one-out analyses, funnel plots, and scatter plots. All figures were combined and exported using a unified layout for presentation in the manuscript and supplementary materials.

3. Results

3.1. CDC WONDER-based mortality attribution analysis

State-level spatial patterns (Fig. 1) indicate marked geographic heterogeneity in the cervical cancer mortality burden across the United States in 2023. In terms of the number of deaths (Fig. 1A), deaths were concentrated in several more populous states, suggesting that absolute counts are partly driven by population size. In contrast, the AAMR standardized to population age structure (Fig. 1B) showed a clearer spatial clustering pattern, indicating substantive differences in underlying risk levels across states. Over the longer term, the percent change in AAMR from 1999 to 2023 varied considerably by state (Fig. 1C): some states, including those in the Northeast (e.g., Maine) and West (e.g., California), experienced more pronounced declines, whereas others, such as the South, showed limited improvement or even increases. The Joinpoint-estimated AAPC (Fig. 1D) further corroborated these interstate differences – although most states exhibited an overall downward trend, the pace of decline was highly uneven. This pattern aligns with the “uneven improvement” observed in analyses stratified by region and urbanization, underscoring that, alongside sustaining broad HPV vaccination and screening coverage, more targeted interventions and resource allocation are needed in states with limited declines or higher burdens.

3.1.1. Trends in mortality by age group

When examining changes in cervical cancer mortality from 1999 to 2023 across age groups, we observed pronounced differences in the magnitude of decline. As shown in Table 1, mortality decreased significantly among individuals aged 65 to 74 years and those aged ≥85 years. The ≥85-year group experienced the steepest decline, with an AAPC of −2.81 (95% CI = −3.38 to −2.24), while the 65 to 74-year group also showed a significant reduction (AAPC = −1.81, 95% CI = −2.65 to −0.96). In contrast, the change in mortality for the 25 to 34-year group was relatively small and did not reach statistical significance (AAPC = −2.10, 95% CI = −4.39 to 0.24; Fig. 2 and Table 1).

3.1.2. Racial/ethnic differences in mortality trends

Table 1 summarizes mortality changes across racial/ethnic groups. Notably, mortality declined significantly among Hispanic individuals (AAPC = −4.01, 95% CI = −5.17 to −2.84) and non-Hispanic Black individuals (AAPC = −4.85, 95% CI = −5.82 to −3.86). The magnitude of decline in these groups exceeded that observed among non-Hispanic White individuals (AAPC = −2.93, 95% CI = −3.66 to −2.18; Fig. 3).

Geographic variation in mortality declines was also evident. As shown in Table 1, the South exhibited the largest decrease (AAPC = −15.67, 95% CI = −19.66 to −11.49), and the West also demonstrated a significant decline (AAPC = −18.02, 95% CI = −24.19 to −11.34). By comparison, the Northeast showed a smaller – yet still significant – decline (AAPC = −13.67, 95% CI = −19.31 to −7.63; Fig. 4).

3.1.4. Urbanization and mortality trends

An urban–rural gradient was observed in mortality trends. According to Table 1, metropolitan areas experienced a relatively smaller decline (AAPC = −1.43, 95% CI = −1.67 to −1.19), whereas nonmetropolitan areas had relatively higher mortality with a comparable but slightly less favorable decline (AAPC = −1.28, 95% CI = −1.92 to −0.63). These findings suggest that urbanization – and the associated distribution of healthcare resources – may contribute to differences in mortality reductions (Fig. 5).

3.2. Causal analysis of the relationship between trace elements and cervical cancer

In the forward two-sample MR analysis (genetically predicted vitamin C levels as the exposure and cervical cancer as the outcome), 7 SNPs were retained as IVs after harmonization and instrument screening. The IVW fixed-effects model suggested a modest inverse association between vitamin C and cervical cancer risk (OR = 0.9962, 95% CI = 0.9924–1.0000; P = .048). Directionally consistent robustness estimators also indicated a protective direction but did not reach statistical significance: weighted median (OR = 0.9962, 95% CI = 0.9916–1.0008; P = .102) and MR-Egger (OR = 0.9918, 95% CI = 0.9400–1.0466; P = .777). There was no evidence of substantial horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept, P = .880) or heterogeneity (IVW Cochran Q, P = .951; MR-Egger Q, P = .902; Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content). Sensitivity plots further supported robustness: leave-one-out analyses showed no material change in the overall estimate after removal of any single SNP; the single-SNP forest plot and funnel plot did not suggest dominance by an individual variant or marked asymmetry; and scatter plots showed broadly consistent directions across methods (Fig. 6).

Two-sample Mendelian randomization of genetically predicted vitamin C and cervical cancer risk: main effect and sensitivity analyses. MR = Mendelian randomization, SE = standard error, SNP = single-nucleotide polymorphism.

Reverse MR analyses (genetic liability to cervical cancer as the exposure and vitamin C as the outcome) did not yield consistent causal evidence. The IVW estimate was nonsignificant with a very wide confidence interval (P = .154). Although MR-Egger reached nominal statistical significance (P = .024), the estimate was highly unstable with an extremely wide interval and was inconsistent with IVW and weighted median results, suggesting that this signal more likely reflects model instability and/or bias from pleiotropy or weak instruments. Overall, the evidence more strongly supports a modest protective association between genetically predicted vitamin C levels and cervical cancer risk, while providing no robust support for reverse causality (Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content).

4. Discussion

This study adopted a combined framework of “national mortality-burden trend characterization + two-sample MR causal inference” to address 2 complementary questions: how the cervical cancer mortality burden in the United States changed from 1999 to 2023, and which modifiable exposures might be associated with risk in a manner closer to causality. By integrating real-world population-level changes with causal signals derived from a genetic instrumental-variable framework, the 2 components provide complementary lines of evidence.

Regarding mortality trends, changes were not uniform across age groups. AAMRs declined significantly among individuals aged 65 to 74 years and ≥85 years, whereas the 25 to 34-year group showed a downward direction with a smaller magnitude that did not reach statistical significance. This pattern suggests that, although long-term improvements in prevention and treatment have contributed to overall mortality reductions, gains among younger adults may be comparatively limited.[1] Further investigation is warranted into potential contributors such as screening participation, continuity of follow-up care, stage at diagnosis, and access to timely treatment. Importantly, because the trend component is ecological and based on aggregated data, it cannot disentangle at the individual level whether this pattern reflects changes in incidence, stage distribution, or treatment benefits; thus, these findings should be interpreted as signals identifying priority groups for further validation and mechanistic decomposition.[9,21]

Stratified results further revealed clear inequities in mortality improvement. Declines differed by sex, race/ethnicity, census region, and urbanization level, indicating that progress has not been evenly distributed.[22] These disparities are consistent with an explanatory framework in which socioeconomic conditions, healthcare accessibility, screening coverage, and gaps along the follow-up and treatment continuum contribute to unequal outcomes.[23,24] From a public health perspective, while sustaining HPV vaccination and screening remains essential, an equally critical priority is to target resources and interventions toward populations and regions with smaller declines or higher burdens – by improving guideline-concordant screening coverage, diagnostic timeliness, and continuity of treatment and follow-up to narrow existing gaps.[25,26]

In the causal inference component, we applied two-sample MR to evaluate potential causal relationships between nutrition/trace element-related exposures and cervical cancer risk, aiming to reduce confounding and reverse causation that commonly affect observational studies. Forward MR suggested a modest inverse association between genetically predicted vitamin C levels and cervical cancer risk.[27,28] Robustness estimators were directionally consistent but showed limited statistical support; pleiotropy and heterogeneity assessments did not indicate major bias, and sensitivity analyses suggested that the results were unlikely to be driven by any single instrument. Nevertheless, the effect size was extremely close to the null and the statistical significance was borderline, implying limited clinical or public health impact and potential sensitivity to instrument strength, sample size, and modeling choices. Accordingly, a more appropriate interpretation is that vitamin C represents a hypothesis-generating protective signal rather than a definitive causal conclusion that can be directly translated into intervention recommendations. Reverse MR did not yield robust, consistent evidence, lending greater support to an “exposure-to-outcome” direction than to reverse causality.[29,30]

Key strengths of this study include the long observation window and systematic stratification by age, sex, race/ethnicity, region, and urbanization, allowing a comprehensive depiction of mortality changes and inequities. The integration of trend analyses with MR further enables complementary evidence spanning population patterns and plausible causal mechanisms. Limitations should also be acknowledged.[30] The trend analysis relies on summary-level data and cannot adjust for individual-level determinants such as screening history, HPV vaccination, stage, treatment, or socioeconomic factors; mortality estimates may also be influenced by variation in coding and reporting practices. Although multiple sensitivity tests were performed, MR remains dependent on instrumental-variable assumptions, and the limited number of instruments and borderline primary findings underscore the need for stronger instruments, larger samples, and additional approaches (e.g., multivariable MR and colocalization) to strengthen causal inference and interpretability.[31]

Moreover, the study’s reliance on CDC WONDER mortality data introduces an important external validity limitation. Although the data provide valuable national-level insights, they may not fully represent the cervical cancer population, as they are based on reported mortality rather than clinical or individual-level factors. This database may not capture important variables such as HPV vaccination coverage, screening history, access to healthcare, treatment regimens, or socioeconomic status – all of which can significantly impact cervical cancer outcomes and disparities. Additionally, the mortality data are subject to potential biases in coding, classification, and reporting practices across states, further limiting the generalizability of findings to the broader population. To improve the external validity of future studies, incorporating more granular, individual-level data from clinical or cohort-based studies would be beneficial.

Furthermore, as the genetic data largely represent specific ancestral backgrounds, generalizability across ancestries requires further validation.

Overall, US cervical cancer mortality declined over the long term, yet improvements were stratified across age, race/ethnicity, region, and urbanization, underscoring that equity in access and continuity across the screening–diagnosis–treatment–follow-up pathway is essential alongside sustained primary prevention and screening expansion. Meanwhile, the MR results provide a borderline-significant, potentially protective genetic signal for vitamin C, motivating future work using higher-quality genetic instruments, broader exposure profiles, and linked analyses incorporating incidence, stage, screening uptake, and vaccination coverage to clarify its public health relevance and translational value.

5. Conclusion

This study reveals that while cervical cancer mortality rates have generally decreased from 1999 to 2023, the reduction has been uneven across different age groups, gender, race/ethnicity, and regions. The most significant declines were seen in older age groups, while younger adults showed minimal improvement. These disparities highlight the importance of addressing healthcare access and follow-up care in underserved populations.

The MR analysis provided weak genetic evidence for a protective effect of vitamin C levels on cervical cancer risk, but the effect size was small and not statistically robust. Further research with stronger genetic tools and larger sample sizes is needed to confirm these findings.

In summary, while cervical cancer mortality has decreased, efforts should focus on improving healthcare equity and further investigating potential protective factors like vitamin C.
