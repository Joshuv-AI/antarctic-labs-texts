Extending Forrelation: Quantum Algorithms Related to Generalized Fourier-Correlation
Suman Dutta, Subhamoy Maitra, Pantelimon Stănică

Abstract
In this paper, we study different cryptographically significant spectra of Boolean functions, including the Walsh-Hadamard, cross-correlation, and autocorrelation. The 2 k 2^{k} -variation by Stănică [IEEE-IT 2016] is considered here with the formulation for any m ∈ ℕ m\in\mathbb{N} . Given this, we present the most generalized version of the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm, which extends the standard and previously extended versions, thereby encompassing them as special cases. Additionally, we generalize the Forrelation formulation by introducing the m m -Forrelation and propose various quantum algorithms towards its estimation. In this regard, we explore different strategies in sampling these newly defined spectra using the proposed m m -Forrelation algorithms and present a comparison of their corresponding success probabilities. Finally, we address the problem related to affine transformations of generalized bent functions and discuss quantum algorithms in identifying the shifts between two bent (or negabent) functions with certain modifications, and compare these with existing results.



I
Introduction

Quantum computing is a fundamental aspect of quantum mechanics with numerous exciting possibilities. However, demonstrating the separation between the classical and quantum paradigms remains a challenging and intriguing problem. In this context, the black-box model is widely employed to illustrate the distinction in their respective domains. Some of the most well-known quantum algorithms, such as Shor’s factoring
[
19
]
, Grover’s search
[
12
]
, and Simon’s hidden shift
[
20
]
, are all defined within this black-box paradigm. Naturally, this black-box model, also known as the query model, is one of the most studied areas in quantum computer science. In this domain, problems can be studied in the exact quantum and bounded error quantum models as well as the probabilistic and deterministic classical models.

One of the simple yet fundamental algorithm in this domain is the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm
[
6
]
. Informally speaking, given access to a Boolean function
f
, it produces a superposition of the normalized Walsh-Hadamard transform values of
f
as the amplitude of its pre-measurement state at the respective points. If
f
is promised to be either constant or balanced, the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm deterministically concludes which one it is with a single quantum query. In contrast, any deterministic classical algorithm requires exponentially many queries, which is asymptotically the maximum possible separation between these two models. However, obtaining the separation between the classical probabilistic model and the bounded error quantum model is not as straightforward and was recently resolved in
[
3
]
. In this direction, the ‘Forrelation’ algorithm is of great interest.

Given two Boolean functions
f
and
g
, the Forrelation (or Fourier-correlation) estimates the correlation between the truth table (output) of
f
and the Walsh-Hadamard transform (at the corresponding points) of
g

[
1
]
. In 2015, Aaronson et al.
[
2
]
demonstrated that, given any two Boolean functions, the absolute value of the Forrelation being less than
1/100
or greater than
3/5
can be resolved using a constant number of queries in the bounded error quantum model. In contrast, it takes an exponential number of queries in the probabilistic classical model, highlighting the classical-quantum separation.

In a recent study
[
8
]
, the Forrelation algorithm was exploited to enhance the sampling probability for different cryptographically significant spectra of Boolean functions, including the Walsh-Hadamard spectrum, cross-correlation, and auto-correlation spectra. In a subsequent work
[
7
]
, similar results were established involving the nega-Hadamard transforms. Additionally,
[
7
]
also explored various interesting properties related to the shift behavior of bent and negabent functions using Forrelation and nega-Forrelation. In a related work, Stănică
[
21
]
generalized the Walsh-Hadamard transform to the
2^{k}
-Hadamard transform by incorporating the weight factor of the
2^{k}
-th primitive root of unity. This work also extended the notions of cross-correlation and auto-correlation within this framework and generalized the bent criteria for Boolean functions to
2^{k}
-bent, introducing the concepts of strong and weak bent functions in this context. We point to also
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
for further descriptions of the
2^{k}
-bent concept and applications to relative difference set theory.

In our present endeavor, we first generalize these fundamental concepts from their
2^{k}
-variation to a more comprehensive formulation, by incorporating the weight factor of the
m
-th primitive root of unity, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, we generalize the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm beyond its extended variant
[
9
]
, identifying the standard Deutsch-Jozsa
[
6
]
and the extended Deutsch-Jozsa
[
9
]
as its specific instances. Furthermore, we extend the Forrelation formulation to
m
-Forrelation and introduce novel quantum algorithms for estimating these newly defined generalized spectra. The organization of the paper and its section-wise contributions are summarized bellow.

I-A

Organization and Contribution

In Section
II
, we present the preliminary results related to Boolean functions, along with the relevant quantum algorithms such as the Deutsch-Jozsa, Forrelation, nega-Forrelation, and their respective variations and applications.

Section
III
is our first contributory section, where we focus on generalizing the existing frameworks in terms of Boolean function properties. We begin with introducing new unitaries, exploring their implications, and establishing connections to existing ones.
Then, we extend the formulation of various fundamental concepts including the Walsh-Hadamard, cross-correlation, and autocorrelation spectra, to a generalized variation for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. In the process, we identify a previously unexamined class of real Hadamard transforms that lies between the Walsh-Hadamard and nega-Hadamard transformations, addressing a gap in the literature. Additionally, we introduce the most generalized version of the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm, which extends both the standard Deutsch-Jozsa and its prior extended version, incorporating them as special cases. Furthermore, we extend the Forrelation formulation to
m
-Forrelation and propose new quantum algorithms for estimating them for a given set of Boolean functions.

In Section
IV
, we present various sampling strategies of these newly defined spectra of Boolean functions using the generalized Forrelation algorithms, and present the comparison graph based on the corresponding sampling probabilities.

Section
V
presents our final contribution, where we study affine transformations of generalized bent functions. We refute a prior claim that two negabent functions cannot exhibit a hidden shift by providing a counterexample and extend hidden shift finding algorithms, originally developed for bent functions, to more general affine transformations and to negabent functions.

Section
VI
concludes the paper with a brief summary of our major contributions and outlines potential future research directions in this area.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we present the necessary definitions and results required to proceed with this paper. For more on Boolean functions and their cryptographic properties, the reader can consult
[
4
,
5
]
.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{2}=\{0,1\}
be the prime field of characteristic
2
and
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{2}\equiv\{\mathbf{x}=\left(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\right):x_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2},1\leq i\leq n\}
(usual operations) be the vector space of dimension
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Throughout the paper, elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
are written in bold to distinguish them from scalar elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. We define a
Boolean function

f
as a mapping
f:\mathbb{F}^{n}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}.

The set of all
n
-variable Boolean functions is denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
The
(Hamming) weight
of a binary string
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is given by the number of
1
’s present in the bit pattern of
\bm{\omega}
, that is,
wt(\bm{\omega})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}
.
The Hamming weight of a Boolean function is the Hamming weight of its output vector, that is,
truth table
(under some ordering of the input).

Given
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the
Walsh-Hadamard transform
(a discrete version of the Fourier transform) of
f
at a point
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a real valued function,
W_{f}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\rightarrow\left[-2^{n/2},2^{n/2}\right]\subset\mathbb{R}
, defined as

W_{f}(\bm{\omega})=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\bm{\omega}}
.
Parseval’s identity
constrains the Walsh transform values by
\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}W^{2}_{f}(\bm{\omega})=2^{n}
. If the Walsh transform values of a Boolean function
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
are equally distributed over all (
2^{n}
-many) points, i.e., if
W_{f}(\bm{\omega})=\pm 1
for all
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, then
f
is called a
bent function
. The bent functions are defined only for even
n\in\mathbb{N}
and have the highest nonlinearity. Let
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
are bent such that
(-1)^{g(\mathbf{x})}=W_{f}(\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, then
f
and
g
are called
dual
, denoted by
g=\widehat{f}
. Note that,
g=\widehat{f}
implies
f=\widehat{g}
, and therefore,
\widehat{\widehat{f}}=\widehat{g}=f
. Moreover, A bent function
f
is self dual if
f=\widehat{f}
.

Similarly, given
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the
nega-Hadamard transform

[
15
]
of
f
at
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a complex valued function,
N_{f}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, defined as
N_{f}(\bm{\omega})=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\bm{\omega}}i^{wt(\mathbf{x})}
, where the constraint,
\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}|N_{f}(\bm{\omega})|^{2}

=\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}N_{f}(\bm{\omega})\overline{N_{f}(\bm{\omega})}=2^{n}
is known as the
nega-Parseval identity

[
22
]
. For even
n
,
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
is called
negabent
if
|N_{f}(\bm{\omega})|=1
for all
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, where
|z|
denotes the modulus of a complex number
z
. Furthermore,
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
(for even
n
) is called bent-negabent if
f
is both bent and negabent.

In
[
21
]
, the discrete Fourier transforms of
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
have been generalized to
2^{k}
-Hadamard transform, a complex-valued function (
\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(2^{k})}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
), defined as

\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(2^{k})}\left(\bm{\omega}\right)=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\bm{\omega}}\zeta_{2^{k}}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}

at
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,
where
\zeta_{2^{k}}=e^{2\pi i/2^{k}}
is a primitive
2^{k}
-complex root of
1
. The
2^{k}
-Parseval identity
is given by
\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}|\mathcal{H}^{(2^{k})}_{f}(\bm{\omega})|^{2}=\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathcal{H}^{(2^{k})}_{f}(\bm{\omega})\overline{\mathcal{H}^{(2^{k})}_{f}(\bm{\omega})}=2^{n}
. A Boolean function
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
satisfying
|\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(2^{k})}(\bm{\omega})|=1
for all
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is called a
2^{k}
-bent function.

Remark II.1
.

It is important to note that for
k=0
,
\zeta_{2^{0}}=e^{2\pi i}=1
, resulting in
\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(2^{k})}=W_{f}
, the Walsh-Hadamard transform. Similarly, for
k=2
,
\zeta_{2^{k}}=i
, which leads to
\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(2^{k})}=N_{f}
, the nega-Hadamard transform.

Additionally, we identify the existence of a missing
2^{k}
-Hadamard transform for
k=1

(\zeta_{2^{1}}=-1)
, which is also a real-valued function that lies between the Walsh-Hadamard and nega-Hadamard transforms. For
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, it is described at a point
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
as follows:

\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(2^{1})}\left(\bm{\omega}\right)=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\bm{\omega}}(-1)^{wt(\mathbf{x})}.

Given
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the
crosscorrelation
of
f
and
g
at
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is defined as
C_{f,g}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}{(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus g(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})}}
, where taking
f=g
we obtain the
autocorrelation
of
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, represented by
C_{f}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}{(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})}}
. From
[
22
]
, it is known that a Boolean function
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
is bent if and only if
C_{f}(\mathbf{y})=0
for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0^{n}\}
.
In the same vein, the
nega-crosscorrelation
of
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
at
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is defined as
\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{f,g}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus g(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})}(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}
, and the
nega-autocorrelation
of
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
as
\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{f}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})}(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}
.
In
[
21
]
, the crosscorrelation and the autocorrelation have been generalized to
2^{k}
-crosscorrelation
and
2^{k}
-autocorrelation
, respectively, as follows.

C_{f,g}^{(2^{k})}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus g(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})}\left(\zeta_{2^{k}}^{2}\right)^{\mathbf{x}\odot\mathbf{y}},\text{ and }C_{f}^{(2^{k})}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})}\left(\zeta_{2^{k}}^{2}\right)^{\mathbf{x}\odot\mathbf{y}},

where
\mathbf{x}\odot\mathbf{y}
denotes the inner product in
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
.

Remark II.2
.

Note that for both
k=0
and
k=1
, the
2^{k}
-crosscorrelation and
2^{k}
-autocorrelation reduce to the standard crosscorrelation and autocorrelation, respectively. Similarly, for
k=2
, the
2^{k}
-crosscorrelation and
2^{k}
-autocorrelation correspond to the nega-crosscorrelation and nega-autocorrelation, respectively.

Since the expression for
2^{k}
-crosscorrelation (autocorrelation) includes the term
\zeta_{2^{k}}^{2}
, the variation in
\zeta_{2^{k}}
, where
\zeta_{2^{k}}=1
for
k=0
and
\zeta_{2^{k}}=-1
for
k=1
, does not have an overall impact. Consequently, unlike the
2^{k}
-Hadamard transform, there is no missing
2^{k}
-crosscorrelation (autocorrelation) value.

Moreover, the
2^{k}
-crosscorrelation is related to the product of
2^{k}
-Hadamard transform
[
21
, Theorem 3]
as follows.

C_{f,g}^{(2^{k})}(\mathbf{y})=\zeta_{2^{k}}^{wt(\mathbf{y})}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(2^{k})}(\mathbf{u})\overline{\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(2^{k})}(\mathbf{u})}(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{y}}.

Recall that there exist similar results for crosscorrelation
[
17
, Theorem 3.1]
as well as for nega-crosscorrelation
[
22
, Lemma 4]
.

C_{f,g}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}W_{f}(\mathbf{u})W_{g}(\mathbf{u})(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{y}},\quad\widehat{C}_{f,g}(\mathbf{y})=i^{wt(\mathbf{y})}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}N_{f}(\mathbf{u})\overline{N_{g}(\mathbf{u})}(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{y}}.

In Section
III
, we generalize the
2^{k}
-Hadamard transform,
2^{k}
-crosscorrelation,
2^{k}

m\in\mathbb{N}

In the
black-box model
of quantum algorithms, we are given the oracle access
\left(U_{f}\right)
of an unknown
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, and the goal is to determine certain specific properties of
f
with the minimum number of queries to
U_{f}
. Given
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
, the functioning of
U_{f}
on an
(n+1)
qubit state
\ket{\mathbf{x}}\ket{a}
is defined as
U_{f}\ket{\mathbf{x}}\ket{a}=\ket{\mathbf{x}}\ket{a\oplus f(\mathbf{x})}
.
For
\ket{a}=\ket{-}
, we have
U_{f}\ket{\mathbf{x}}\ket{-}=(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})}\ket{\mathbf{x}}\ket{-}
, which is also known as the
phase-kickback
.

At this point, let us briefly explain the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm
[
6
]
.
Given oracle access to an unknown
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, with the promise that
f
is either a constant or a balanced Boolean function, the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm can deterministically identify which one it is by producing a superposition state where the amplitude of an individual state is given by the normalized Walsh-Hadamard transform of the function at that point

2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}\ket{\mathbf{y}}=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}W_{f}(\mathbf{y})\ket{\mathbf{y}}.

Essentially, for a constant Boolean function, the complete Walsh spectrum is supported over the all-zero point, makes it certain to observe the state
\ket{0^{n}}
, upon measurement. However, for a balanced Boolean function, the Walsh transform at the all-zero point is
0
, thus the state
\ket{0^{n}}
never appears when measured. Therefore, the presence or absence of the all-zero state in the measurement results concludes with certainty whether
f
is constant or balanced, using only a single query to the oracle
U_{f}
. A schematic diagram of the corresponding quantum circuit is shown in Fig.
1
.

.

In 2017, a modified version of this algorithm, termed the extended Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm
[
9
]
was proposed in
[
9
]
. There, instead of using all Hadamard gates before measurement, as in
[
6
]
, a combination of Hadamard and nega-Hadamard gates subject to a bit pattern
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
was applied.
Observe that, for
\mathbf{c}=0^{n}
, it becomes the standard Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm, producing the superposition state with the normalized Walsh-Hadamard transform value of the respective states as the individual amplitudes. On the other extreme, when
\mathbf{c}=1^{n}
, only the nega-Hadamard gates are applied. As a result, the algorithm produces the superposition where the amplitude of the individual states are given by the normalized nega-Hadamard transform of the respective states,

2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}(i)^{wt(\mathbf{x})}\ket{\mathbf{y}}=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}N_{f}(\mathbf{y})\ket{\mathbf{y}}.

The Forrelation problem, introduced by Aaronson
[
1
]
, is one of the key results in the study of showing separation between the bounded error quantum model and the randomized classical model
[
2
]
. The Forrelation is defined as follows.

Definition II.3

(Forrelation
[
1
]
)
.

Given oracle access to
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the (
2
-fold) Forrelation measures the amount of correlation between
f_{1}
and the Walsh-Hadamard transform of
f_{2}
, by estimating the following:

\Phi_{f_{1},f_{2}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{x_{1}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f_{1}(\mathbf{x_{1}})}W_{f_{2}}(\mathbf{x_{1}})=\frac{1}{2^{3n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{x_{1}},\mathbf{x_{2}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f_{1}(\mathbf{x_{1}})}(-1)^{\mathbf{x_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{2}}}(-1)^{f_{2}(\mathbf{x_{2}})}.

The Forrelation formulation can be further extended for
k(>2)
Boolean functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
to

\Phi_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}}=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{(k+1)n}{2}}}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{x_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{x_{k}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f_{1}(\mathbf{x_{1}})}(-1)^{\mathbf{x_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{2}}}(-1)^{f_{2}(\mathbf{x_{2}})}\ldots(-1)^{\mathbf{x_{k-1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{k}}}(-1)^{f_{k}(\mathbf{x_{k}})},

known as the
k
-fold Forrelation.

[

, the schematic diagrams of the quantum algorithms for estimating the

:

Although the Forrelation algorithm was initially used to demonstrate theoretical separation between two complexity classes, recent work
[
8
]
has shown that it can also be leveraged for efficient sampling of different cryptographic spectra of Boolean functions, including the Walsh-Hadamard, crosscorrelation, and autocorrelation spectra. In this regard, we have the following results from
[

]

Theorem II.4

(
[
8
]
)
.

Given oracle access to
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
and a set of points
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, by defining
f_{1}=f_{3}=f
and
f_{2}=g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, where
g(\mathbf{x})=1
for
\mathbf{x}\in S
and
0
otherwise, the
3
-query
3
-fold Forrelation algorithm (Fig.
3(a)
) measures any state other than the all-zero state with probability of approximately
\frac{4}{2^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S}|W_{f}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}
.

Theorem II.5

(
[
8
]
)
.

Given oracle access to
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, and a point
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, the
3
-query
3
-fold Forrelation algorithm (Fig.
3(a)
) measures the all-zero state with probability
2^{-2n}\left(C_{f,g}(\mathbf{y})^{2}\right)
, by setting
f_{1}=f
,
f_{2}=\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{y}}
, and
f_{3}=g
, where
\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{x}
is a linear function in
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
With an exact same setup, the
2
-query
3
-fold Forrelation algorithm (Fig.

) measures
\ket{0}
with a probability
\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{C_{f,g}(\mathbf{y})}{2^{n}}\right)
.
Additionally, by setting
f=g
, the above results apply to the autocorrelation coefficient
C_{f}(\mathbf{y})
, at the point
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

Following the Forrelation formulation, the concept of (
3
-fold) nega-Forrelation was introduce in
[
7
]
, defined as follows.

Definition II.6

(nega-Forrelation
[
7
]
)
.

Given oracle access to
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the
3
-fold nega-Forrelation measures certain kind of correlation between the Boolean function
f_{1}
, the nega-Hadamard transform of
f_{2}
and the conjugate nega-Hadamard transform of
f_{3}
, mathematically formulated as

\displaystyle\eta_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}{(-1)^{f_{1}(\mathbf{x})}N_{f_{2}}(\mathbf{x})\overline{N}_{f_{3}}(\mathbf{x})}

III
Generalization of Boolean functions’ spectra

In this section, we generalize significant spectra of Boolean functions, including the Walsh-Hadamard, crosscorrelation, and autocorrelation spectra, and then present the most extended version of a Deutsch-Jozsa-like quantum algorithm, building upon the work of
[
6
,
9
]
. Finally, we introduce the most comprehensive form of Forrelation, that encompasses both Forrelation
[
1
]
and nega-Forrelation
[
7
]
as its special cases, and provide the necessary quantum algorithm for estimating it.

In this direction, let us introduce a single qubit unitary operator,
\Omega_{m}
, with parameter
m\in\mathbb{N}
, defined as
\Omega_{m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\zeta_{m}\\
1&-\zeta_{m}\end{pmatrix}
,
where
\zeta_{m}=e^{2\pi i/m}
denotes the primitive
m
-th complex root of unity, i.e.,
\left(\zeta_{m}\right)^{m}=1
.
This gate can be derived as a special case from the well known and generalized

U_{3}(\theta,\phi,\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\frac{\theta}{2}}&-e^{i\lambda}\sin{\frac{\theta}{2}}\\
e^{i\phi}\sin{\frac{\theta}{2}}&e^{i(\phi+\lambda)}\cos{\frac{\theta}{2}}\end{pmatrix}
structure.
The conjugate of
\zeta_{m}
is given by
\overline{\zeta_{m}}=e^{-2\pi i/m}=\zeta_{m}^{-1}
. Moreover, the inverse of the unitary operator
\Omega_{m}
is given by
\Omega_{m}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
\zeta_{m}^{-1}&-\zeta_{m}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
such that
\Omega_{m}\Omega_{m}^{\dagger}=\Omega_{m}^{\dagger}\Omega_{m}=I_{2}
, where
I_{2}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
.
The action of
\Omega_{m}
on the basis states is given by:

\ket{0}\xrightarrow{\Omega_{m}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0}+\ket{1}\right),\ket{1}\xrightarrow{\Omega_{m}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\zeta_{m}\ket{0}-\zeta_{m}\ket{1}\right).

In general, when
n
-many
\Omega_{m}
apply on an
n
-qubit state
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\alpha_{\mathbf{x}}\ket{\mathbf{x}}
with
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}|\alpha_{\mathbf{x}}|^{2}=1
, the resultant state is given by:

\Omega_{m}^{\otimes n}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\alpha_{\mathbf{x}}\ket{\mathbf{x}}\right)=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\alpha_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}\ket{\mathbf{y}}\right).

Remark III.1
.

Note that for
m=1
, the newly defined unitary operator reduces to the Hadamard gate,
\Omega_{1}=\mathrm{H}
. Similarly, for
m=4
, the unitary operator
\Omega_{m}
corresponds to the nega-Hadamard gate
\mathrm{N}
, as described in
[
7
]
. Moreover, for
m=2
, there exists a real unitary matrix, defined as
\Omega_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}
, such that

\Omega_{2}\left(\ket{0}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0}+\ket{1}\right)
and
\Omega_{2}\left(\ket{1}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(-\ket{0}+\ket{1}\right)
.

In a similar manner, we define another single-qubit unitary by conjugating the elements of
\Omega_{m}
, denoted as
\overline{\Omega}_{m}
, which is given by
\overline{\Omega}_{m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{\zeta_{m}}\\
1&-\overline{\zeta_{m}}\end{pmatrix}
. The inverse of
\overline{\Omega}_{m}
is given by
\overline{\Omega}_{m}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
\zeta_{m}&-\zeta_{m}\end{pmatrix}
, such that
\overline{\Omega}_{m}\overline{\Omega}_{m}^{\dagger}=\overline{\Omega}_{m}^{\dagger}\overline{\Omega}_{m}=\mathrm{I}_{2}
. The action of
n
-many
\overline{\Omega}_{m}
on an
n
-qubit state
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\alpha_{\mathbf{x}}\ket{\mathbf{x}}
is given by

\overline{\Omega}_{m}^{\otimes n}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\alpha_{\mathbf{x}}\ket{\mathbf{x}}\right)=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\alpha_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}(\overline{\zeta_{m}})^{wt(\mathbf{x})}\ket{\mathbf{y}}\right).

Furthermore, for
m=4
, the newly defined
\overline{\Omega}_{m}
coincides with the conjugate nega-Hadamard gate,
\overline{\mathrm{N}}
, largely utilized in
[
7
]
.

Additionally, we define another single-qubit gate,
\mathrm{S}_{m}
, which generalizes the phase gate (S), given by
\mathrm{S}_{m}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\zeta_{m}\end{pmatrix}
. The inverse of
\mathrm{S}_{m}
is given by
\mathrm{S}_{m}^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\zeta_{m}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
, and so
\mathrm{S}_{m}\mathrm{S}_{m}^{\dagger}=\mathrm{S}_{m}^{\dagger}\mathrm{S}_{m}=\mathrm{I}_{2}
. Notably, for
m=1
,
\mathrm{S}_{m}
reduces to the identity gate. When
m=2
, we have
\zeta_{2}=-1
, making
\mathrm{S}_{2}
equivalent to the Pauli Z gate. Similarly, for
m=4
,
\mathrm{S}_{m}
corresponds to the standard phase gate S, and for
m=8
,
\mathrm{S}_{m}
is equivalent to the well-known T gate. The existing special cases of this newly defined unitary
\mathrm{S}_{m}
are summarized as follows:

\mathrm{S}_{1}=\mathrm{I},\quad\mathrm{S}_{2}=\mathrm{Z},\quad\mathrm{S}_{4}=\mathrm{S},\quad\mathrm{S}_{8}=\mathrm{T}.

More generally, when
n
instances of
\mathrm{S}_{m}
are applied to an
n
-qubit state
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\alpha_{\mathbf{x}}\ket{\mathbf{x}}
, the resultant state acquires a multiplicative phase factor of
\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}
, that is,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{S}_{m}^{\otimes n}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}{\alpha_{\mathbf{x}}\ket{\mathbf{x}}}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}{\alpha_{\mathbf{x}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}\ket{\mathbf{x}}}.}

Next, we generalize the
2^{k}
-Hadamard transform,
2^{k}
-crosscorrelation,
2^{k}
-autocorrelation and the related results for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, establishing the existing results as specific cases within this generalized framework. We begin with extending the definition of
2^{k}
-Hadamard transform
[
21
]
to define the most generalized version of the Hadamard transform, and connect it with the newly defined unitary operator
\Omega_{m}
.

Definition III.2

(
m
-Hadamard transform)
.

Given
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the
m
-Hadamard transform (
m\in\mathbb{N}
) of
f
at
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a complex valued function,
\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined as

\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\bm{\omega})=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\bm{\omega}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}.

Remark III.3
.

Note that for
m=1
, the
m
-Hadamard transform reduces to the standard Walsh-Hadamard transform, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}^{(1)}_{f}=W_{f}
. Similarly, for
m=4
, it corresponds to the nega-Hadamard transform,
\mathcal{H}^{(4)}_{f}=N_{f}
. More generally, for
m=2^{k}
, the transform is expressed as
\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}=\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(2^{k})}
, representing the
2^{k}
-Hadamard transform.

Since the
m
-Hadamard transform is a complex valued function, we can define the conjugate
m
-Hadamard transform as follows:

\overline{\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\bm{\omega})}=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\bm{\omega}}\left(\overline{\zeta_{m}}\right)^{wt(\mathbf{x})}.

In this regard, the constraint
\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}|\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\bm{\omega})|^{2}=\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\bm{\omega})\overline{\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\bm{\omega})}=2^{n}
is known as the
m
-Parseval identity. The proof follows from Corollary
III.8
.

Similar to the bent-negabent classifications, a Boolean function
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
is called
m
-
bent
if its
m
-Hadamard transform is flat in complex modulus, i.e.,
\left|\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\bm{\omega})\right|=1
, for all
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Furthermore, any Boolean function
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
can be represented as a sum of its
m
-Hadamard transforms, as follows.

Lemma III.4
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Then the
m
-Hadamard transform is invertible, i.e.,

(-1)^{f(\mathbf{y})}=2^{-n/2}\zeta_{m}^{-wt(\mathbf{y})}\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\bm{\omega})(-1)^{\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{y}}.

Proof.

Let us begin with the right hand side (RHS)

\displaystyle 2^{-n/2}\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\bm{\omega})(-1)^{\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{y}}=

\displaystyle 2^{-n}\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\bm{\omega}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}(-1)^{\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\bm{\omega}\cdot(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}2^{n}\delta_{0}(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})=(-1)^{f(\mathbf{y})}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{y})},

and the claim is shown.
∎

Next, we compute the
m
-Hadamard transform for various combinations of Boolean functions (as in
[
22
,
21
]
).

Lemma III.5
.

Let
f,g,h\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
and
\zeta_{m}=e^{2\pi i/m}
. Then, the following holds.

(a)

If
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus d
with
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and
d\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
, then
\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}({\mathbf{u}})=(-1)^{d}\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}({\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{c}})
. Moreover, if
\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus d
, then
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{L}}^{(m)}({\mathbf{u}})=(-1)^{d}2^{n/2}\left(\cos(\pi/m)\right)^{n}\left(-i\tan(\pi/m)\right)^{wt(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{c})}\zeta^{n/2-wt(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{c})}
.

(b)

If
f(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})\oplus h(\mathbf{x})
, then
\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}({\mathbf{u}})=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{v}}\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{g}(\mathbf{v})W_{h}(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{v})=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{v}}W_{g}(\mathbf{v})\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{h}(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{v})
.

(c)

If
g(\mathbf{x})=f(A\mathbf{x})
, where
A
is an
n\times n
orthogonal matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, then
\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(A\mathbf{u})
.

(d)

If
h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=f(\mathbf{x})\oplus g(\mathbf{y})
with
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, then
\mathcal{H}_{h}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{v})
.

Proof.

The proof follows the same lines as in
[
21
, Theorem 2]
, with some tricks and tweaks.

(a)

From definition,
\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}({\mathbf{u}})=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\left(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{c}\right)\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus d}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}=(-1)^{d}\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}({\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{c}})
. Moreover,

\displaystyle 1+\zeta_{m}

\displaystyle=1+\cos(2\pi/m)+i\sin(2\pi/m)=2\cos^{2}(\pi/m)+2i\cos(\pi/m)sin(\pi/m)=2\cos(\pi/m)e^{i\pi/m},

\displaystyle 1-\zeta_{m}

\displaystyle=1-\cos(2\pi/m)-i\sin(2\pi/m)=2\sin^{2}(\pi/m)-2i\cos(\pi/m)sin(\pi/m)=-2i\sin(\pi/m)e^{-i\pi/m}.

For
\mathbf{u}=u_{1},\ldots u_{n}
and
\mathbf{c}=c_{1},\ldots c_{n}
, set
b_{i}=u_{i}\oplus c_{i}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{L}}^{(m)}({\mathbf{u}})=

\displaystyle 2^{-n/2}(-1)^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{c})}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-n/2}(-1)^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1+\zeta_{m}(-1)^{b_{k}}\right)=2^{-n/2}(-1)^{d}\prod_{b_{k}=0}(1+\zeta_{m})\prod_{b_{k}=1}(1-\zeta_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-n/2}(-1)^{d}\left(2\cos(\pi/m)e^{i\pi/m}\right)^{n-wt(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{c})}\left(-2i\sin(\pi/m)e^{-i\pi/m}\right)^{wt(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{c})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{d}2^{n/2}\left(\cos(\pi/m)\right)^{n}\left(-i\tan(\pi/m)\right)^{wt(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{c})}\left(e^{2\pi i/m}\right)^{n/2-wt(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{c})}.

(b)

We prove the first identity by starting with the RHS. The second identity follows in a similar manner.

\displaystyle 2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{g}(\mathbf{v})W_{h}(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{v})=

\displaystyle 2^{-3n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{g(\mathbf{x})\oplus h(\mathbf{y})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{v}\oplus\mathbf{y}\cdot(\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{v})}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-3n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{g(\mathbf{x})\oplus h(\mathbf{y})\oplus\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{y}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\mathbf{v}\cdot(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{g(\mathbf{x})\oplus h(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}=\mathcal{H}_{g\oplus h}^{(m)}({\mathbf{u}})=\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}({\mathbf{u}}).

(c)

From definition,
\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(A\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}
. Suppose,
A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
, then
\mathbf{x}=A^{T}\mathbf{y}
. Thus,

\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{y})\oplus(A^{T}\mathbf{y})\cdot\mathbf{u}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(A^{T}\mathbf{y})}=2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{y})\oplus\mathbf{y}\cdot A\mathbf{u}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{y})}=\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(A\mathbf{u}).

(d)

Starting with the RHS, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{v})=

\displaystyle 2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus g(\mathbf{y})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{v}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})+wt(\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\oplus(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\cdot(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}=\mathcal{H}_{h}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}).

The claims are shown.
∎

Next, we define the most generalized version of
crosscorrelation
and
autocorrelation
, as follows.

Definition III.6

(
m
-
crosscorrelation
)
.

Given
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the
m
-crosscorrelation of
f
and
g
at a point
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is described as

C_{f,g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus g(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})}\left(\zeta_{m}^{2}\right)^{\mathbf{x}\odot\mathbf{y}}.

Taking
f=g
in the above formulation, we obtain the
m
-autocorrelation, defined as

C_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y})}\left(\zeta_{m}^{2}\right)^{\mathbf{x}\odot\mathbf{y}},

where
\mathbf{x}\odot\mathbf{y}
denotes the inner product in
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
.

It is straightforward to observe that for
m=1
, the
m
-crosscorrelation (
m
-autocorrelation) reduces to the standard crosscorrelation (autocorrelation). Additionally, for
m=4
, where
\zeta_{m}=i
, the
m
-crosscorrelation and
m
-autocorrelation correspond to nega-crosscorrelation and nega-autocorrelation, respectively. The
m
-crosscorrelation of two Boolean functions is related with the product of their
m
-Hadamard transforms, as follows.

Theorem III.7
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Then

C_{f,g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{z})=\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{z})}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})\overline{\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})}(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}}.

Proof.

Let us begin with the RHS, namely,

\displaystyle\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{z})}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})\overline{\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})}(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-n}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{z})}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{g(\mathbf{y})\oplus\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{u}}\zeta_{m}^{-wt(\mathbf{y})}(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus g(\mathbf{y})}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})+wt(\mathbf{z})-wt(\mathbf{y})}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}}.

Now taking
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{z}
in the above expression:

\displaystyle 2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus g(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{z})}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x})+wt(\mathbf{z})-wt(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{z})}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{y}\oplus\mathbf{u}\cdot\left(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{z}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus g(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{z})}\left(\zeta^{2}_{m}\right)^{\mathbf{x}\odot\mathbf{z}}=C_{f,g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{z}).

∎

Corollary III.8
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Then
C_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{z})=\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{z})}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}|\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})|^{2}(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}}.

Assuming
\mathbf{z}=0^{n}
in the above corollary, we obtain
\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}|\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})|^{2}=C_{f}^{(m)}(0^{n})=2^{n}
, establishing the
m
-Parseval’s identity. Moreover, the
m
-bent criterion of a Boolean function is related to its
m
-autocorrelation as follows.

Theorem III.9
.

A Boolean function
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
is
m
-bent if and only if
C_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{z})=0
for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0^{n}\}
, and
C_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{z})=2^{n}
, for
\mathbf{z}=0^{n}
.

Proof.

If
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
is
m
-bent, i.e.,
|\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{u})|=1
for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, then
C_{f}^{(m)}(\mathbf{z})=\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{z})}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}}
. Now,
\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}}=0
for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0^{n}\}
, and
2^{n}
, if
\mathbf{z}=0^{n}
. Similarly, the converse follows.
∎

III-A

Generalized Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm

Suppose, we are given the oracle access to an unknown Boolean function
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Similar to the standard Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm, we start with an
(n+1)
-qubit quantum circuit initialized to
\ket{0^{n}}\ket{-}
. We then apply
n
-many Hadamard gates to the first
n
qubits, creating an equal superposition of all possible
n
-bit strings,
2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\ket{\mathbf{x}}
, which is fed into the quantum oracle
U_{f}
, thereby achieving quantum parallelism.

After the oracle query, instead of only applying the Hadamard gates to the first
n
qubits, as in the standard Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm, we apply a sequence of omega-gates determined by a sequence of natural numbers
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
where
d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
dictates the application of
\Omega_{d_{i}}
gate at the
i
-th qubit. Finally, we measure the first
n
qubits in the
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
basis, and the final state before measurement is given by

2^{-n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n},\,d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}\zeta_{d_{i}}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}\ket{\mathbf{y}}.

Therefore, the probability of observing any particular state
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
becomes

\mathcal{P}{(\mathbf{y})}=2^{-2n}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n},\,d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}\zeta_{d_{i}}^{wt(\mathbf{x})}\right|^{2}.

A schematic diagram of the generalized Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm is presented in Fig.
5
, where
U_{i}=\Omega_{d_{i}}
and
d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
. If
d_{i}=m
for all
1\leq i\leq n
where
m
is a fixed natural number, then we denote the generalized Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm by
\text{DJ}_{m}
.

Remark III.10

Depending on the exact sequence of natural numbers,
\mathbf{d}

1.

If
d_{i}=1
for all

\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

2^{-n}\left|N_{f}(\mathbf{y})\right|^{2}

3.

Similarly, if

4.

Finally, in algorithm
\mathrm{DJ}_{m}

Definition III.11

(
m
-Forrelation)
.

Given oracle access to
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the (
3
-fold)
m
-Forrelation measures a correlation between the Boolean function
f_{1}
, the
m
-Hadamard transform of
f_{2}

m
-Hadamard transform of
f_{3}

\Phi^{(m)}_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{x_{1}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}{(-1)^{f_{1}(\mathbf{x_{1}})}\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f_{2}}(\mathbf{x_{1}})\overline{\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f_{3}}(\mathbf{x_{1}})}},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{x_{1}},\mathbf{x_{2}},\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f_{1}(\mathbf{x_{1}})}\left((-1)^{f_{2}(\mathbf{x_{2}})\oplus\mathbf{x_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{2}}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x_{2}})}\right)\left((-1)^{f_{3}(\mathbf{x_{3}})\oplus\mathbf{x_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{3}}}(\overline{\zeta_{m}})^{wt(\mathbf{x_{3}})}\right).

Remark III.12
.

From the definition of (
3
-fold)
m
-Forrelation, we have the following remarks:

1.

The (
3
-fold)
m
-Forrelation,
\Phi^{(m)}_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}
is complex-valued and not symmetric, meaning its values depend on the order of the Boolean functions.

2.

When
f_{2}=f_{3}
, the product of the
m
-Hadamard transform and its conjugate equals the complex square of the
m
-Hadamard transform of
f
. Consequently,
\Phi^{(m)}_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{2}}
is always a real number.

3.

For
m=1
,
\Phi^{(m)}_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}=\Phi_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}
, the standard (
3
-fold) Forrelation as provided in
[
2
]
. For
m=4
,
\Phi^{(m)}_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}=\eta_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}
, the
3
-fold nega-Forrelation as introduced in
[
7
]
.

Additionally, note that, similar to the Forrelation, the
m

k

f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}

k
-fold

3

m
-Forrelation to refer to the
3
-fold
m
-Forrelation. Following the approach in
[
2

We begin with the

Ignoring the last qubit, the pre-measurement amplitude corresponding to the state
\ket{0^{n}}

\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x_{1}},\mathbf{x_{2}},\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f_{2}(\mathbf{x_{2}})}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x_{2}})}(-1)^{\mathbf{x_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{2}}}(-1)^{f_{1}(\mathbf{x_{1}})}(-1)^{\mathbf{x_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{3}}}(\overline{\zeta_{m}})^{wt(\mathbf{x_{3}})}(-1)^{f_{3}(\mathbf{x_{3}})},

which is equal to
\Phi^{(m)}_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}

Since,
\Phi^{(m)}_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}
is a complex number, the probability of observing the all-zero state upon measurement is given by the complex modulus square,
\left|\Phi^{(m)}_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}\right|^{2}

3

m

A^{(m)3,3}_{n}

Next, we present the
2

m

7

f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, we begin with an
(n+2)

\ket{+}\ket{0^{n}}\ket{-}
, where the first qubit is termed as the ‘driving qubit’ and the next
n

n

n
-query qubits, and distribute the state as follows:

\textstyle\ket{+}\ket{0^{n}}\ket{-}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{H}^{\otimes n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n+1}}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{x_{2}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\ket{0}\ket{\mathbf{x_{2}}}+\sum_{\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\ket{1}\ket{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right)\ket{-}.

Then controlled on the driving qubit being
\ket{0}

U_{f_{2}}\rightarrow\Omega_{m}^{\otimes n}\rightarrow U_{f_{1}}\rightarrow\mathrm{H}^{\otimes n}

\frac{\ket{0}}{\sqrt{2^{3n+1}}}\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x_{1}},\mathbf{x_{2}},\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f_{2}(\mathbf{x_{2}})}\zeta_{m}^{wt\left(\mathbf{x_{2}}\right)}(-1)^{\mathbf{x_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{2}}}(-1)^{f_{1}(\mathbf{x_{1}})}(-1)^{\mathbf{x_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{3}}}\ket{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\ket{-}.

Similarly, controlled on the driving qubit being
\ket{1}

\mathrm{S}_{m}^{\otimes n}\rightarrow U_{f_{3}}
and obtain

\frac{\ket{1}}{\sqrt{2^{n+1}}}\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f_{3}(\mathbf{x_{3}})}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{x_{3}})}\ket{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\ket{-}.

Combining both the scenario, we obtain
\textstyle\ket{\psi}=\sum_{\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left(\alpha_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\ket{0}+\beta_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\ket{1}\right)\ket{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\ket{-}

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{3n+1}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x_{1}},\mathbf{x_{2}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f_{2}(\mathbf{x_{2}})}\zeta_{m}^{wt\left(\mathbf{x_{2}}\right)}(-1)^{\mathbf{x_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{2}}}(-1)^{f_{1}(\mathbf{x_{1}})}(-1)^{\mathbf{x_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right)\text{ and }

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left(\alpha_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}+\beta_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right)\ket{0}+\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left(\alpha_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}-\beta_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right)\ket{1}\right)\ket{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\ket{-}.

\ket{0}

\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left|\alpha_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}+\beta_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left(\left|\alpha_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right|^{2}+\left|\beta_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right|^{2}\right)+2\Re{\left(\alpha_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\overline{\beta}_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right)}\right],

where
\Re{\left(z\right)}
denotes the real part of the complex number
z

\sum_{\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left|\alpha_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right|^{2}+\left|\beta_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right|^{2}

\ket{\psi}

1

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}2\Re{\left(\alpha_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\overline{\beta}_{\mathbf{x_{3}}}\right)}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Re{\left(\Phi^{(m)}_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}\right)}.

IV
Sampling of generalized spectra using
m
-Forrelation

Given
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, and a set of points
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, the goal is to estimate the
m
-Hadamard transform values of
f
at all the points in
S
. Recall that using algorithm
\text{DJ}_{m}
, one can sample the
m
-Hadamard transform of
f
at
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with probability

\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S}\left|\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}=:p
(say),
where
0\leq p\leq 1
. We now present the strategies for sampling the
m
-Hadamard transforms of
f
using the
m
-Forrelation algorithms,
A_{n}^{(m)3,3}
and
A_{n}^{(m)2,3}
.

Suppose,
g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
such that
g(\mathbf{x})=1
for all
\mathbf{x}\in S
,
and
g(\mathbf{x})=0
otherwise. From the definition of
\Phi^{(m)}_{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}}
, with
f_{2}=f_{3}=f
and
f_{1}=g
, we have

\Phi^{(m)}_{g,f,f}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}{(-1)^{g(\mathbf{x})}\left|\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{x}\not\in S}{\left|\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}}-\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S}{\left|\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}}\right).

Using the
m
-Parseval’s identity,
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}{\left|\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}}=2^{n}
we obtain,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(m)}_{g,f,f}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left(2^{n}-\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S}{\left|\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}}-\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S}{\left|\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}}\right)=1-\frac{2}{2^{n}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S}{\left|\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{f}(\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}}\right)=1-2p.

This implies,
p=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\Phi^{(m)}_{g,f,f}\right)
,
which is same as the probability of observing
\ket{1}
upon measuring the ‘driving qubit’ from running the algorithm
A_{n}^{(m)2,3}(g,f,f)
.

Proposition IV.1
.

Given
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
and a set of points
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that
g(\mathbf{x})=1
for all
\mathbf{x}\in S
and
g(\mathbf{x})=0
otherwise, the probability of observing
\ket{1}
upon measuring the driving qubit, from executing
A_{n}^{(m)2,3}(g,f,f)
is given by
p
.

Therefore, the
2
-query
m
-Forrelation algorithm
A_{n}^{(m)2,3}(g,f,f)
samples the
m
-Hadamard transform with a probability exactly equal to the sampling probability of Algorithm
\text{DJ}_{m}
.

Next, we compute the sampling probability of the
m
-Hadamard transform using the
3
-query algorithm,
A_{n}^{(m)3,3}
. Upon measurement, the probability of observing the all-zero state is given by
\left(\Phi^{(m)}_{g,f,f}\right)^{2}
. Consequently, the probability of observing a state with at least one
\ket{1}
in the output is
1-\left(\Phi^{(m)}_{g,f,f}\right)^{2}
. Substituting
\Phi^{(m)}_{g,f,f}=1-2p
, we obtain:

1-\left(\Phi^{(m)}_{g,f,f}\right)^{2}=1-\left(1-2p\right)^{2}=4p-4p^{2}\approx 4p.

Therefore, we have the following theorem.

Theorem IV.2
.

Given
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
and a set of points
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that
g(\mathbf{x})=1
for all
\mathbf{x}\in S
and
g(\mathbf{x})=0
otherwise, the probability of one of the states in the measurement outcomes being
\ket{1}
from executing the
3
-query quantum algorithm
A_{n}^{(m)3,3}(g,f,f)
is given by
4p-4p^{2}
.

From Theorem
IV.2
, it is evident that when
p<0.75
(i.e., when
p<4p-4p^{2}
), the sampling probability obtained from
A_{n}^{(m)3,3}
surpasses that of algorithm
\text{DJ}_{m}
, in terms of the required number of queries. More specifically, for any
f\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the algorithm
A_{n}^{(m)3,3}
samples the smaller values of the
m
-Hadamard transform more efficiently than the algorithm
\text{DJ}_{m}
. Conversely, for
p>0.75
, algorithm
\text{DJ}_{m}
alone is sufficient for estimating the
m
-Hadamard transform values.

Here, one might argue that the algorithm
\text{DJ}_{m}
requires only a single query to the oracle
U_{f}
, whereas the algorithm
A_{n}^{(m)3,3}
necessitates two queries to
U_{f}
. However, even after executing
\text{DJ}_{m}
twice (equivalent to two queries to
U_{f}
), the resulting sampling probability,
1-(1-p)^{2}=2p-p^{2}\approx 2p
, remains lower than that achieved by

V
On affine transformation and shift of (generalized) bent functions

In this section, we study the affine transformations of (generalized) bent functions, beginning with an interesting observation on the affine properties of
m
-bent ones.

Theorem V.1
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
be two
m
-bent function such that
g(\mathbf{x})=f(A\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{b})\oplus\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus d
, where
A
is an
n
-dimensional orthogonal matrix,
\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,
d\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Then, for
\mathbf{b}=0^{n}
, if
f
is
m
-bent, so is
g
.

Proof.

Combining Lemma
III.5
(a) and Lemma
III.5
(c), we obtain:

\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}(\bm{\omega})=(-1)^{d}2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{y})\oplus A\left(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega}\right)\cdot\mathbf{y}}\zeta_{m}^{wt(\mathbf{y})}=(-1)^{d}\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(A(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega})).

Therefore,
\left|\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(m)}(\bm{\omega})\right|=\left|(-1)^{d}\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(A(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega}))\right|=\left|\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(m)}(A(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega}))\right|
. Hence, if
f
is
m
-bent, then so is
g
.
∎

Notably, the similar results are already known for specific subcases such as standard bent functions, negabent functions, and
k
-bent functions. Consider two Boolean functions
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
related by the affine transformation
g(\mathbf{x})=f(A\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{b})\oplus\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus d
, where
A
is an
n\times n
orthogonal matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
(i.e.,
AA^{T}=A^{T}A=I_{n}
),
\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and
d\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Then, the Walsh-Hadamard transforms of
f
and
g
satisfy

\displaystyle W_{g}(\bm{\omega})=

\displaystyle 2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(A\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{b})\oplus\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus d}(-1)^{\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{d\oplus A(\bm{\omega}\oplus\mathbf{c})\cdot b}2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{y})\oplus A(\bm{\omega}\oplus\mathbf{c})\cdot\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{d\oplus A(\bm{\omega}\oplus\mathbf{c})\cdot b}W_{f}(A(\bm{\omega}\oplus\mathbf{c})),

which implies that if
g
is bent, then
f
is also bent
[
18
, Theorem 2]
. Similarly, for the nega-Hadamard transforms:

\displaystyle N_{g}(\bm{\omega})=

\displaystyle 2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(A\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{b})\oplus\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus d}(-1)^{\bm{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}}(i)^{wt(\mathbf{x})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{d\oplus A(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega})\cdot b}2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{y})\oplus A(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega})\cdot\mathbf{y}}(i)^{wt(\mathbf{y}\oplus\mathbf{b})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{d\oplus A(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega})\cdot b}(i)^{wt(\mathbf{b})}2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{y})\oplus A(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega})\cdot\mathbf{y}\oplus\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{b}}(i)^{wt(\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{d\oplus A(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega})\cdot b}(i)^{wt(\mathbf{b})}N_{f}(A(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega})\oplus\mathbf{b}),

which shows that if
g
is negabent, then
f
is also negabent
[
22
, Theorem 3(d)]
. A similar argument applies to the
2^{k}
-Hadamard transforms, yielding:

\mathcal{H}_{g}^{(2^{k})}(\bm{\omega})=(-1)^{d}\mathcal{H}_{f}^{(2^{k})}(A(\mathbf{c}\oplus\bm{\omega})).

Studying isomorphisms among Boolean functions is an interesting area of research from computational points of view (see
[
10
,
11
]
and the references therein). In this initiative, while we establish the theoretical associativity, it is not immediate how a quantum algorithm can efficiently recover the hidden parameters of the affine transformations given oracle access to the
m
-bent functions. Designing such algorithms remains an open and promising research direction for future work. On the other hand, for certain specific instances of bent and negabent functions, we discuss certain algorithms in the following subsections.

V-A

Quantum algorithm for finding the hidden shift of bent functions

In
[
7
]
, it was noted that the hidden shift finding algorithm for bent functions, proposed in
[
16

, is structurally similar to the
2
-fold Forrelation algorithm. Given two bent functions
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
satisfying
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{b})
, the algorithm recovers the hidden shift
\mathbf{b}
using a single query to
g
and the dual of
f
. Here, we extend this approach to analyze the affine properties of bent functions, in a more general setup.

Theorem V.2
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
be bent functions such that
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{b})\oplus\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus d
, where
\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
d\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Then, running the 2-fold Forrelation algorithm with
U_{g}
as the first oracle and the dual of
f
,
U_{\tilde{f}}
, as the second (see Fig.
10
), the state before measurement is given by
(-1)^{\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{c}\oplus d}2^{-3n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\tilde{f}(\mathbf{y}\oplus\mathbf{c})\oplus\tilde{f}(\mathbf{y})\oplus\mathbf{y}\cdot(\mathbf{z}\oplus\mathbf{b})}\ket{\mathbf{z}}
, and the probability of observing any state
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is

\mathcal{P}{\left(\mathbf{z}\right)}=2^{-3n}\big|\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\tilde{f}(\mathbf{y}\oplus\mathbf{c})\oplus\tilde{f}(\mathbf{y})\oplus\mathbf{y}\cdot(\mathbf{z}\oplus\mathbf{b})}\big|^{2}.

Proof.

We follow the 2-fold Forrelation circuit from
[
16
]
, illustrated in Fig.
10
.

Fig. 10
:

f

The starting state
\ket{0^{n}}

\displaystyle\ket{0^{n}}\xrightarrow{H^{n}}2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\ket{\mathbf{x}}\xrightarrow{U_{g}}

\displaystyle\,2^{-n/2}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{b})\oplus\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus d}\ket{x}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{H^{n}}

\displaystyle\,(-1)^{d}2^{-n}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{b})\oplus\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}}\ket{\mathbf{y}}

Clearly, from the above theorem, the value of

cannot be estimated. Furthermore, if

\mathbf{b}

1.

If the algorithm yields only the state
\ket{0^{n}}

\mathbf{b}\neq 0^{n}\neq\mathbf{c}

2.

If a fixed nonzero state
\ket{\mathbf{z}}
is observed with probability 1, then either
\mathbf{c}=0^{n}
and the observed state is
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

\mathbf{c}\neq 0^{n}
and
\tilde{f}(\mathbf{y}\oplus\mathbf{c})\oplus\tilde{f}(\mathbf{y})=(\mathbf{b}\oplus\mathbf{z})\cdot y

3.

If all the states are observed except for a fixed nonzero state, then

, and the missing state corresponds to the hidden shift
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

Remark V.3
.

Note that here we do not introduce any structural modifications to the algorithm; rather, we present an alternative analysis based on the affine transformations of the underlying Boolean functions.

V-B

Quantum algorithm for finding the hidden shift of negabent functions

In this subsection, we study the shift properties of negabent functions and show that the existing hidden shift algorithm for bent functions can be adapted to recover the hidden shift of negabent functions.

We begin with identifying an error in a prior work. In
[
7
, Theorem 5]
, it was wrongly claimed that if
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
are both negabent, then there exists no
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. An immediate counter example can be provided by any affine Boolean function
f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus c
, with
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,
c\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Defining
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})
, we obtain
g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}\oplus c^{\prime}
where
c^{\prime}=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{u}\oplus c
is a constant. Thus,
g
is also affine and therefore negabent, satisfying
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})
and contradicting the claim. We present another counterexample to
[
7
, Theorem 5]
by providing a non-affine, non-bent 6-variable negabent function,
f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{6})=x_{1}x_{3}\oplus x_{1}x_{4}
, where for a non-trivial shift,
\mathbf{u}=100001
, we obtain

f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})=f(x_{1}\oplus 1,x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{6}\oplus 1)=(x_{1}\oplus 1)x_{3}\oplus(x_{1}\oplus 1)x_{4}=x_{1}x_{3}\oplus x_{1}x_{4}\oplus x_{3}\oplus x_{4},

which is again a non-affine, non-bent, negabent function.

Additionally, let
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
be such that
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})
for some
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. If both
f
and
g
are bent as well as negabent, then the same
\mathbf{u}
serves as the shift for the negabent functions
f
and
g
. However,
\mathbf{u}
cannot be a period of the corresponding negabent functions
f\oplus s_{2}
and
g\oplus s_{2}
, as identified in
[
7
, Definition 6]
.
Specifically, if
(g\oplus s_{2})(\mathbf{x})=(f\oplus s_{2})(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})
, then it follows that
g(\mathbf{x})\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{u})
. Canceling common terms yields
\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{u})=0
. Thus, the linear function
\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\left(\oplus_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}u_{i}\right)
must be a constant, which implies
\mathbf{u}=0^{n}
.
We used above the known result of
[
14
]
: if
f
is bent,
f\oplus s_{2}
is negabent, and vice versa, where
s_{2}
denotes the quadratic symmetric Boolean function, which is itself a bent function.

On the other hand, since
\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})=\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})
, we obtain the following immediate results.

Lemma V.4
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
be two bent functions such that
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u)}
. Then
\mathbf{u}
is a period of the negabent functions
g\oplus s_{2}
and
\left(f\oplus s_{2}\oplus\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{u})\right)
, satisfying
(g\oplus s_{2})(\mathbf{x})=\left(f\oplus s_{2}\oplus\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{u})\right)(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u}).
Moreover, their duals
\tilde{f}
and
\tilde{g}
differ by the linear function
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}
, i.e.,
\tilde{g}(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{f}(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}
.

Proof.

We begin with
\left(f\oplus s_{2}\oplus\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{u})\right)
:

\displaystyle\left(f\oplus s_{2}\oplus\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{u})\right)(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})=

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})\oplus\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})\oplus s_{2}(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbb{L}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(g\oplus s_{2})(\mathbf{x}).

Furthermore,
(-1)^{\tilde{g}(\mathbf{x})}=2^{n/2}W_{g}(\mathbf{x})=2^{n/2}W_{f}(\mathbf{x})(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=(-1)^{\tilde{f}(\mathbf{x})}(-1)^{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=(-1)^{\tilde{f}(\mathbf{x})\oplus\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}}
. Hence,
\tilde{f}
and
\tilde{g}
differ by the linear function
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}
.
∎

Given two bent functions
f,g\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
satisfying
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{u})
,
[
16
, Theorem 4.2]
also proposed a quantum algorithm to compute the hidden shift
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with constant success probability, by making
\mathcal{O}(n)
many queries to
f
and
g
. Upon measurement, the algorithm yields the states
\ket{b,\mathbf{z}}
which are orthogonal to the hidden shift
1,\mathbf{u}
, i.e.,
(b,\mathbf{z})\cdot(1,\mathbf{u})=b\oplus\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}=0
. From the observed states,
\mathbf{u}
can be efficiently recovered via Gaussian elimination. In this context, we observe that the same algorithm can be adapted to recover the hidden shift when
f
and
g
are negabent functions.

To demonstrate this, we apply the hidden shift finding algorithm to the non-trivial 6-variable negabent function
f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{6})=x_{1}x_{3}\oplus x_{1}x_{4}
, which is not bent, along with its shifted version
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}\oplus 100001)=x_{1}x_{3}\oplus x_{1}x_{4}\oplus x_{3}\oplus x_{4}
(see Fig.

\ket{0000000}

\mathbf{u}=u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}u_{4}u_{5}u_{6}

Remark V.5
.

The hidden shift finding algorithm for negabent functions studied here is fundamentally and structurally different from the ‘nega-shift’ recovery approach proposed in
[
7
, Theorem 6]
. In
[
7
]
, the underlying negabent functions were of a specific form (as in
Lemma
V.4
), and the functions were first transformed into their corresponding bent counterparts. The hidden shift was then recovered using a single-query algorithm, analogous to the 2-fold Forrelation technique. In contrast, the present approach imposes no particular structure on the underlying Boolean functions. Instead, we construct a large class of quantum states orthogonal to the hidden shift and recover the desired shift via classical post-processing, such as Gaussian elimination.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we generalized various cryptographically significant spectra of Boolean functions, including the Walsh-Hadamard, cross-correlation, and autocorrelation spectra, extending previous formulations to any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We demonstrated that existing variants—such as the standard version, the nega variant, and the
2^{k}
-variant—are special cases of this more general framework. Additionally, we identified a previously unexamined class of real Hadamard transforms that lies between the Walsh-Hadamard and nega-Hadamard transformations, filling a gap in the existing literature. Furthermore, we introduced the most generalized version of the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm, which extends both the standard Deutsch-Jozsa and its prior extended version, thereby encompassing them as special cases. We established that the pre-measurement state in the generalized Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm corresponds to a superposition of the normalized
m
-Hadamard transforms evaluated at all points. In addition, we extended the Forrelation formulation to
m
-Forrelation, and presented new quantum algorithms for estimating these newly defined generalized spectra. We believe these techniques may be of use to understand the interactions among Boolean functions, various spectra and relevant quantum algorithms.
