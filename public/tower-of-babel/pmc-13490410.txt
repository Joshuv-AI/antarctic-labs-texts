A stacked multi-classifier for multi-modal data fusion in transcranial sonography-based Parkinson’s disease assessment
Authors: Hongyu Kang, Xinyi Wang, Yu Sun, Shuai Li, Zhen Song, Chao Hou, Xin Sun, Fangxian Li, Sai-kit Lam, Yong-ping Zheng, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a catastrophic neurodegenerative disorder and a major culprit of neurological disability worldwide. Accurate diagnosis of PD, especially in its early stages, is paramount for timely intervention and effective therapeutic management. However, contemporary clinical diagnostic methods are hindered by the complexities of procedures, inter-evaluator subjectivity, and challenges related to the accuracy and reproducibility of diagnoses. This study employs transcranial sonography (TCS)—a non-invasive, widely accessible, and highly affordable neuroimaging—and integrates radiomic analysis with deep learning via a stacked multimodal ensemble model for PD assessment. A retrospective cohort of 324 participants (103 with PD and 221 Non-PD controls) was recruited from Beijing Tiantan Hospital. The development of the proposed model involved systematically evaluating its performance across different combinations of clinical, radiomic, and deep learning features. The results demonstrated that: The integration of multimodal features—clinical, radiomic, and deep learning—consistently improved predictive performance across all ensemble methods (Bagging, Boosting, and Stacking classifiers). The Stacking ensemble (RF, XGB and HGBDT) model achieved the best performance using the combined feature set, with an AUC of 0.915 (0.002), PR-AUC of 0.868 (0.005), accuracy of 0.810 (0.019), and precision of 0.968 (0.040), with values reported as mean (standard deviation). Diagnostic efficacy including TCS grading significantly exceeded that without TCS, underscoring its clinical value in PD assessment. Furthermore, decision curve analyses showcased that the stacked multi-classifier (RF, XGB, and HGBDT) yielded the greatest net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities compared with other comparing methods. These findings suggest that the proposed Stacking model may serve as a promising TCS-based multimodal tool for PD assessment.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most common neurodegenerative disorder, affects over 6 million people worldwide, with prevalence increasing 2.5-fold over the past three decades, making it a leading cause of neurological disability1,2. PD is characterized by motor symptoms—tremor, rigidity, akinesia, and postural instability—and diverse non-motor manifestations, including autonomic, neuropsychiatric, sensory, and sleep disturbances3,4. Despite advances in understanding its pathogenesis, PD etiology remains incompletely understood, hindering accurate diagnosis5. Early detection and timely intervention are critical for slowing disease progression6, underscoring the clinical importance of accurate early-stage diagnosis.

In recent years, several organizations have issued guidelines for the clinical diagnosis of PD7,8, notably the diagnostic criteria proposed by the European Federation of Neurological Societies and the Movement Disorder Society–European Section, which have strongly influenced clinical practice worldwide7. However, PD diagnosis remains time-consuming, labor-intensive, and highly dependent on clinical expertise. Early-stage identification is particularly challenging due to symptomatic overlap with atypical parkinsonian syndromes9, including multiple system atrophy and progressive supranuclear palsy, as well as the lack of definitive biomarkers or imaging tools in early disease stages.

Radiomics is a non-invasive approach that enables high-throughput extraction of quantitative features from medical images, bridging image acquisition and clinical interpretation10,11. By characterizing intra- and perilesional tissue heterogeneity using texture and statistical features, radiomics has demonstrated strong predictive and prognostic value across multiple cancers, including breast12,13, lung14–16, gastric17,18, prostate19, and brain tumors20,21. Building on this foundation, radiomics has been extended to diverse clinical conditions, populations, and imaging modalities11, encompassing MRI, CT, ultrasonography, and nuclear imaging techniques such as SPECT and PET.

Moreover, radiomics has shown increasing utility in the diagnosis, differential diagnosis, severity stratification, and progression prediction of degenerative neurological disorders—notably PD, parkinsonian syndromes, and related neurodegenerative conditions—across multiple imaging modalities22–24. Sun et al. constructed a machine learning model integrating PET/MRI-derived radiomic features with clinical variables to discriminate between PD and multiple system atrophy (MSA) in a cohort comprising 81 PD patients and 38 healthy controls (HCs), attaining an AUC of 0.86025. In a separate study, Guan et al. developed a multiple-tissue-specific radiomics model based on MRI to differentiate PD patients from HCs using a sample of 123 PD patients and 121 HCs, reporting a mean accuracy of 78.5% ± 2.1% on the validation dataset26. Further contributing to this field, Sun et al. proposed a hybrid approach combining pre-trained deep learning features with radiomic analysis applied to [¹⁸F] FDG PET imaging. Their model, trained on data from 103 PD patients and 255 HCs, achieved a classification accuracy of 85.58%27. Similarly, Kang et al. investigated magnetic susceptibility values and radiomic features within the nigrostriatal system as potential neuroimaging biomarkers for PD. Their study, involving 104 PD patients and 45 HCs, demonstrated strong diagnostic performance with an AUC of 0.9028. Collectively, these studies underscore the increasing importance and applicability of radiomics and artificial intelligence in enabling automated, objective, and accurate diagnosis of PD.

Transcranial sonography (TCS) is a non-invasive neuroimaging technique that enables assessment of substantia nigra (SN) hyper-echogenicity and is endorsed by clinical guidelines as a valuable diagnostic tool for PD due to its high sensitivity and specificity29–31. Compared with other neuroimaging modalities, TCS is a reliable, non-invasive, and cost-efficient tool that is both commonly available and easily applicable, thereby rendering it particularly suitable for the early diagnosis and potential large-scale screening of PD. However, current evaluation of SN hyper-echogenicity largely relies on qualitative or semi-quantitative interpretation, which is subjective and operator-dependent, leading to inter-rater variability and limited reproducibility. Moreover, PD diagnosis is inherently multifactorial, requiring integrated analysis of clinical and imaging information to achieve optimal diagnostic accuracy32,33.

Current research increasingly employs multimodal integration—combining clinical data, radiomic features, and deep learning-based descriptors—to develop fusion models capable of addressing multifactorial diagnostic challenges, an approach that has gained considerable traction as a prominent research direction34–37. However, few studies to date have explored fusion models in the context of TCS-based PD assessment via integration of multimodal feature sets. To bridge this gap, this study aims to develop a multimodal fusion model that integrates TCS-based radiomic features of the SN in midbrain with a wide spectrum of clinical attributes, such as patient demographics, symptom-related surveys, biochemical tests, etc., and deep learning-generated features to enhance PD diagnosis. Using a cohort of 324 participants from Beijing Tiantan Hospital, we rigorously evaluated the complementary value of such integrated feature sets in improving diagnostic performance via comprehensive benchmarking across various modeling strategies. Further, decision curve analyses were carried out to assess the clinical utility of the developed models.

Comprehensive analysis of clinical, radiomics and deep learning features

First, a total of 24 clinical features were identified and documented by neurologists from the medical records. Significance assessments were performed for three categories of clinical features: Basic Clinical Information, Symptoms and Evaluation Metrics, and Biochemical tests. Among these, Weight, Symptom Duration, Constipation, UPSDA-III Score, Bilateral SN+ Area, and TCS grading demonstrated varying degrees of significant correlation as seen in Table 1. Furthermore, distributional analyses of continuous clinical variables were conducted as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1. The statistical significance of associations between these continuous features and PD status was evaluated and ranked in Supplementary Fig. 2. Additionally, the proportions of binary features across PD and non-PD groups were compared and presented in Supplementary Fig. 3. Besides, a total of 1316 radiomic features were initially extracted from midbrain region in the TCS images. Additionally, 4096 deep learning features were obtained from the TCS images using a pre-trained ResNet-50 model. Integration of the feature sets (24 clinical, 1316 radiomics, and 4096 deep) yielded a combined total of 5436 fusion features.

A PCA-guided feature pruning strategy followed by LASSO regression was applied independently within each of the three run-specific training sets to reduce feature redundancy while preserving interpretability. PCA was used solely as an unsupervised tool to identify clusters of highly correlated features among the initial 5436 variables. For each principal component, the feature with the highest absolute loading was retained as a representative, yielding 860 features. Subsequently, LASSO regression with cross-validation was applied to the training dataset to further eliminate redundant and weakly predictive variables, resulting in 312 features with non-zero coefficients. To enhance interpretability and exclude features with negligible contributions, features with absolute coefficients greater than 0.005 were retained, leading to a final set of 54 discriminative features (15 clinical, 31 radiomic, and 8 deep learning features). No information from the validation or test sets was used during PCA decomposition, representative-feature selection, LASSO fitting, or coefficient-threshold determination.

The correlation matrix of these 54 features is presented in Fig. 1A, the extracted 54 features exhibit low autocorrelation, achieving a favorable effect of feature redundancy reduction. Further, the relative importance of the top 20 features is depicted in Fig. 1B, among the features, there are 6 clinical features, 10 omics features, and 4 deep learning features that demonstrate high contributions. Bagging classifiers

In Table 2, among the Bagging classifiers, the best performance was achieved using Clinical features + Deep Features, which attained an averaged AUC of 0.779 (0.043) and a F1-score of 0.566 (0.015). This combination slightly outperformed other Bagging configurations in terms of AUC, while maintaining comparable accuracy and balanced accuracy, indicating a modest benefit from incorporating deep features into the Bagging framework. For Extra Trees, the optimal results were obtained with Clinical features + Radiomic Features + Deep Features, yielding the highest AUC of 0.802 (0.063) and PR-AUC of 0.726 (0.092), along with accuracy of 0.742 (0.026). This feature set enhanced overall discriminative ability compared with reduced feature combinations, although recall remained relatively limited. Random Forest performed the best with Clinical features + Radiomic Features + Deep Features, achieving superior results across most metrics, including an AUC of 0.837 (0.018), accuracy of 0.810 (0.039), precision of 0.983 (0.024), as seen in the underline in Table 2.

Across all classifiers and feature sets, the Random Forest model trained on Clinical + Radiomic + Deep Features delivered the best overall results. Specifically, this configuration achieved a high AUC of 0.837 (0.018), together with accuracy of 0.810 (0.039) and high precision of 0.983 (0.024), while also maintaining a favorable PR-AUC of 0.829 (0.030). These results indicate a well-balanced and high-performing model with excellent discriminative ability and low false positive rates, making it particularly suitable for the PD assessment result.

Boosting classifiers

In Table 3, based on the experimental results for Boosting classifiers, the performance of four algorithms—AdaBoost, XGBoost, Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT), and Histogram-Based Gradient Boosting (HGBDT)—was evaluated using different feature sets. The following is a summary of the key findings: For AdaBoost, the best performance was achieved using the combined Clinical features + Radiomic Features + Deep Features. This configuration yielded the highest precision of 0.974 (0.036) and PR-AUC of 0.799 (0.007) among its variants, while AUC of 0.782 (0.028) and accuracy of 0.769 (0.010). XGBoost demonstrated its optimal results with the same combined feature set: Clinical features + Radiomic Features + Deep Features. It achieved the highest PR-AUC of 0.827 (0.012), together with AUC of 0.805 (0.031) and accuracy of 0.803 (0.009), indicating strong discriminative capability and robust overall classification performance under the multimodal feature setting. Additional metrics, including precision, further supported its favorable classification behavior. The GBDT classifier performed best with Clinical features + Radiomic Features + Deep Features, attaining its highest AUC of 0.820 (0.007), along with accuracy of 0.756 (0.045) and F1-score of 0.690 (0.061) among its variants. While these secondary metrics indicate a favorable balance between precision and recall, the model’s overall performance was mainly driven by its improved AUC. HGBDT delivered its top performance with the full Clinical + Radiomic + Deep Features set, achieving the highest AUC of 0.857 (0.017) and PR-AUC of 0.851 (0.033) among all Boosting models tested, while maintaining competitive accuracy.

Discussion

The clinical evaluation of PD remains a complex and multifactorial challenge. Despite established diagnostic guidelines7,8, current practice still relies heavily on subjective clinical interpretation, limiting diagnostic accuracy and reproducibility. In this study, we leveraged a large TCS-based cohort of 324 participants to develop a multimodal framework integrating comprehensive clinical variables, radiomics features, and deep learning–derived representations, while explicitly evaluating the diagnostic contribution of TCS imaging. Multiple feature combinations were systematically assessed using ensemble learning strategies, including Bagging, Boosting, and Stacking architectures. Our results demonstrate that the Stacking ensemble combining RF, XGB, and HGBDT achieved optimal performance with the full multimodal feature set, and that TCS-derived imaging features and grading provided meaningful additional diagnostic value across both clinical-only and full multimodal feature sets, demonstrating consistent and robust discriminative potential. Moreover, following feature selection, several clinical variables—including symptom duration, hyperlipidemia, and constipation—were identified as potentially informative factors for PD diagnosis, suggesting that these features may capture clinically relevant aspects of disease manifestation.

Previous multimodal PD assessment studies have reported encouraging diagnostic performance, predominantly based on MRI or PET imaging. For example, Sun et al. integrated PET/MRI-derived radiomic features with clinical variables to discriminate PD from multiple system atrophy, achieving an AUC of 0.86 in a cohort of 81 PD patients and 38 healthy controls25. Similarly, Guan et al. developed an MRI-based multi-tissue radiomics model using 123 PD patients and 121 controls, reporting a mean accuracy of 78.5% ± 2.1%26. Other studies have combined radiomics with deep learning features derived from PET or susceptibility-based MRI, such as the FDG-PET framework proposed by Sun et al. (103 PD patients and 255 controls; accuracy 85.58%)27 and the susceptibility-based radiomics model reported by Kang et al. (104 PD patients and 45 controls; AUC 0.90)28. While these approaches show strong diagnostic potential, they rely on MRI/PET modalities that are costly, less accessible, or involve radiation exposure and are often evaluated in modest-sized cohorts. In contrast, TCS is a radiation-free, low-cost, and bedside-applicable modality. Using a comparatively large cohort of 324 participants, this study integrates clinical features with TCS-derived radiomics and deep learning representations within a Stacking ensemble framework, demonstrating the complementary value of TCS for scalable multimodal PD classification.

In this study, feature selection analysis demonstrated that all three feature types were retained in substantial numbers (clinical: 15, radiomic: 31, deep: 8) (Fig. 1), with each category contributing distinctly to PD assessment. PCA and LASSO were employed in this study to select features highly relevant to PD assessment, resulting in the selection of 15 clinical features, 31 radiomic features, and 8 deep features (Fig. 1). From a modeling standpoint, the Bagging-based RF model demonstrated robust performance on the integrated multimodal feature set—comprising clinical, radiomic, and deep features—achieving an AUC of 0.837 (0.018), a PR-AUC of 0.829 (0.030), and the highest accuracy of 0.810 (0.039) (Table 2). Similarly, the Boosting-based HGBDT model attained a superior AUC of 0.857 (0.017) using the same composite feature set (Table 3). A consistent trend across all ensemble methods—Bagging, Boosting, and Stacking—was that the inclusion of all three feature modalities yielded the optimal performance. Notably, the Stacking ensemble model, which integrated RF, XGBoost, and HGBDT, delivered exceptional diagnostic outcomes, with an AUC of 0.915 (0.002), a PR-AUC of 0.868 (0.005), an accuracy of 0.810 (0.019), a balanced accuracy of 0.741 (0.026), a precision of 0.968 (0.040) (Table 4). Furthermore, TCS grading consistently exhibited top-tier performance across all four Stacking configurations, reinforcing its diagnostic utility. These findings collectively underscore the clinical significance of multimodal feature integration and emphasize the specific value of TCS-based grading in advancing objective and reproducible PD assessment.

Among the 15 selected clinical features, 3 features were ranked as the most significant contributors: symptom duration, TCS grading, and hyperlipidemia. Regarding symptom duration, several research teams have established a strong correlation between extended symptom duration and the progression of degenerative diseases33,38–40. For instance, Melissa J. et al. demonstrated that the onset of dyskinesia is significantly associated with longer symptom duration33. Furthermore, the assessment of the SN in the midbrain via TCS serves as a key biomarker in diagnostic guidelines, and its strong association with PD has been well-established29,30,41. Furthermore, studies suggest a significant correlation between hyperlipidemia and PD42. Kim et al. found that prolonged statin use was associated with a higher incidence of PD in a Korean population with hyperlipidemia42. Furthermore, factors such as Constipatipation43–45, weight46,47, UPSDA-III Score48, fatty acid49, sleeping disorders50, homocysteine51, smoke52, depression53 have been established to exhibit significant associations with PD.

In this study, the target region for extracting radiomic and deep features from TCS images encompasses the entire midbrain, rather than being limited to the SN region alone. This approach is informed by research findings suggesting that not all hyperechoic signals identified as originating from the SN are confined to that area; such signals are also present in the dorsal band of the SN, the red nucleus, and the ventral tegmental area54,55. Further, radiomics analysis successfully identified a subset of highly discriminative features from TCS images that exhibit strong associations with PD, thereby offering novel insights into microstructural alterations within the SN. The significantly elevated median values (log-sigma-1-mm-3D_firstorder_Median) corroborate the presence of hyperechogenicity—a well-established hallmark of PD pathologically linked to iron deposition56. Concurrently, an increase in Robust Mean Absolute Deviation (log-sigma-3-mm-3D_firstorder_RobustMeanAbsoluteDeviation) reflects heightened signal heterogeneity, suggestive of non-uniform tissue remodeling. Texture analysis further elucidated underlying parenchymal changes: the prominence of features log-sigma-3-mm-3D_glszm_LowGrayLevelZoneEmphasis and log-sigma-3-mm3D_glszm_ SmallAreaLowGrayLevelEmphasis implies the existence of hypoechoic regions embedded within a predominantly hyperechoic background, possibly indicative of preserved neuronal clusters or incipient degenerative processes33,57. Moreover, the significance of log-sigma-1-mm-3D_glszm_SizeZoneNonUniformityNormalized underscores spatial disorganization in tissue architecture, consistent with patchy iron accumulation and regional nigrosome degeneration. Multi-scale filtering analysis revealed that pathological patterns manifest across varying spatial frequencies. The dominance of coarse-texture metrics (e.g., log-sigma-5-mm-3D_ngtdm_Strength) suggests pronounced large-scale interfacial discontinuities, likely resulting from structural disintegration. In parallel, wavelet-derived features such as wavelet-LL_glcm_ClusterShade captured fine-scale asymmetries in tonal distribution, indicative of subtle and diffuse microarchitectural disruption. These findings collectively may imply that both macroscopic and microscopic textural patterns contribute jointly to disease differentiation. Overall, the identified radiomic features provide a quantitative and nuanced characterization of nigral abnormalities in PD, surpassing the limitations of subjective visual interpretation. This study highlights the potential of radiomics as an adjunctive tool to enhance diagnostic precision and objectivity in PD evaluation.

As an easy-to-implement, non-invasive, and inexpensive technique, TCS shows promise in aiding the early differential diagnosis and assessment of PD29,30. In this study, TCS-based diagnostic outcomes were used as features to train the Stacking ensemble model. To further evaluate the contribution of TCS image-level diagnostic results to PD assessment, this study incorporated the TCS grading as an independent variable in the analysis. The inclusion of TCS grading consistently enhances model discriminative ability, as reflected by the elevated AUC values across all configurations. The most notable improvement was observed in the Stacking (RF + HGBDT) model using all features with TCS grading, achieving an AUC of 0.894 (0.006) compared to 0.801 (0.016) without TCS grading (Table 5). Similarly, the Stacking ensemble (RF + XGB + HGBDT) attains the highest AUC of 0.911 (0.007) with TCS grading, significantly outperforming its without TCS grading, AUC of 0.814 (0.016) (Table 5). The results enhance the referential value of TCS image-level assessment for neurologists in clinically diagnosing PD, thereby Boosting their diagnostic confidence.

The integration of clinical assessments, radiomic features, and deep learning-derived biomarkers via a Stacking ensemble model demonstrates a robust methodological framework for PD analysis58. From a feature perspective, each modality offers complementary information: clinical variables capture domain-specific manifestations, radiomic features quantify subvisual texture patterns from medical images, and deep learning features extracted via ResNet50 enable high-level representation learning from raw pixel data. Such multi-source multi-resolution feature fusion aligns with emerging trends in precision medicine, where heterogeneous data integration has been shown to improve diagnostic granularity. From a modeling standpoint, the Stacking architectures—combining Bagging and Boosting base learners—effectively mitigates individual model biases and variances while capitalizing on their distinct strengths. Bagging methods (e.g., RF) enhance stability through bootstrap aggregation, whereas Boosting algorithms (e.g., XGB) iteratively refine decision boundaries for hard-to-classify instances. By leveraging a meta-learner to synthesize these predictions, our approach capitalizes on the synergistic power of ensemble-based consensus, often achieving superior generalization compared to monolithic classifiers. This is particularly relevant in PD, where phenotypic heterogeneity necessitates flexible yet resilient modeling strategies.

From a public health perspective, the multimodal diagnostic approach based on TCS proposed in this study holds substantial practical value for clinical application. First, it offers a highly cost-effective alternative, presenting a clinically favorable option compared to MRI or MRI/PET-based methodologies. Second, given the limited availability and logistical constraints associated with MRI, as well as the radiation exposure inherent in PET imaging, the TCS-based fusion model stands out due to its low cost, absence of radiation, and procedural convenience. From a clinical perspective, these characteristics make it particularly suitable for rapid diagnosis, early detection, and large-scale screening initiatives. The proposed method is intended as a decision-support tool to assist initial assessment by providing an objective output from TCS-derived and clinical features before final specialist interpretation, while diagnosis remains based on comprehensive clinical evaluation and established criteria.

Although this study has yielded encouraging results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the inclusion of multi-center data is necessary, as the use of a single-center dataset may constrain assessments of model’s robustness and generalizability. Second, although the study included a considerable cohort of 324 participants—which is notable compared to related contemporary research—a larger sample size remains essential to enhance the clinical credibility of the proposed method. In the further work, firstly, although expert manual annotations were sufficient for the present study, automated segmentation methods—such as those validated in our prior work59,60— may be incorporated in future large-scale studies to improve scalability and reproducibility. Secondly, the current analysis was restricted to binary PD versus non-PD classification and did not address disease-stage characterization or severity stratification. Future work should extend the framework to stage-aware phenotyping using larger, well-annotated cohorts, ideally with longitudinal follow-up. Further, the dataset imbalance is a limitation, which is common to prior studies and reflects real-world disease prevalence; nevertheless, our findings remain informative and merit validation in larger and more balanced cohorts. Clinically, the proposed model is intended as an adjunctive decision-support tool to complement—rather than replace—specialist evaluation during the initial PD assessment workflow. Finally, as the dataset expands and more centers become involved, we intend to further refine the model by moving beyond pre-trained deep learning frameworks toward an integrated architecture where multiple feature types are input directly into a deep learning-based classifier. We anticipate addressing these aspects in future work to further facilitate widespread adoption of the developed model in clinical practice.

Methods

Study participants

A retrospective analysis was performed on 324 subjects (103 with PD and 221 Non-PD controls) who underwent TCS at Beijing Tiantan Hospital between January 2021 and December 2023. All PD diagnoses were confirmed by neurologists based on clinical evaluations and medical records. To evaluate robustness, the cohort was independently and randomly partitioned three times (using different random seeds) into training, validation, and testing sets, with each partition following an approximate ratio of 7:1.5:1.5. The overall workflow of the study is illustrated in Fig. 5.

The workflow diagram for establishing and evaluating the PD assessment prediction model.

A Perform TCS examinations on the left and right temporal windows and labels the target areas, followed by expert manual midbrain annotations. B Obtain the corresponding clinical report for the patient. C Use TCS images and segmented ROIs for radiomics feature extraction, apply a pre-trained ResNet50 to extract deep features, extract clinical features from medical reports, and combine these three types for feature selection. D Apply classifiers based on Bagging and Boosting, as well as Stacking classifiers that utilize both Bagging and Boosting. E Evaluate the fusion features and model performance separately.

Eligibility criteria included individuals, the availability of complete clinical data, and TCS examination results. Exclusion criteria comprised the following: secondary parkinsonism due to cerebrovascular disease, intracranial tumors, head trauma, or medication-induced parkinsonism; incomplete medical records; inconclusive SN hyper-echogenicity findings on TCS; and history of deep-brain stimulation (DBS) electrode implantation, stem cell therapy, or pallidotomy. The diagnosis of PD was established in accordance with the clinical diagnostic criteria defined by the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society7,8.

Ethics

This study involving human participants was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the approving ethics committees due to the retrospective nature of this study: Ethical approval was obtained from the respective ethics committees of Beijing Tiantan hospital, Capital Medical University (No. KT2022-015-04) and the Human Subject Ethics Sub-committee of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University (HSEARS20231102004).

TCS images acquisition

All subject TCS images were acquired using a Canon Aplio i900 i-series ultrasound system (Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Otawara, Japan) equipped with an i6CX1 convex array transducer (center frequency: 2.6 MHz) at Beijing Tiantan Hospital. All TCS images were acquired using the same ultrasound scanner (Canon Aplio i900) under a standardized protocol that remained unchanged throughout the 3-year study period. An experienced, uniformly trained physician performed the scans by positioning the probe over the bilateral temporal acoustic windows to obtain optimal images of the characteristic butterfly-shaped midbrain structures. Prior to feature extraction, all images underwent intensity normalization and spatial standardization to reduce acquisition-related variability and enhance feature robustness.

TCS grading

TCS grading refers to the assessment of a patient’s SN echogenicity based on TCS imaging, integrating both semi-quantitative and quantitative methods to evaluate patients. All TCS grading for the assessment process were conducted by two experienced ultrasound physicians with over 10 years of expertise.

The semi-quantitative method classifies SN echogenicity into grades Level I to Level V, with grade III or higher considered TCS-based positive (Supplementary Table 1)7,29,61. The quantitative method involves zooming in on the cross-sectional area with the largest hyperechoic region of the SN when hyperechogenicity is observed. If the absolute area of bilateral SN hyperechogenicity measures <0.2 cm², it is considered TCS-based negative; an area ≥0.2 cm² is classified as TCS-based positive7,62.

Clinical data collection

Demographic and clinical characteristics, mainly including three categories (Basic Clinical Information, Symptoms and Evaluation Metrics, and Biochemical tests) were systematically collected. Basic clinical information including age, sex, height, weight, smoking, drinking, hypertension, hyperlipidemia and diabetes. Symptoms and evaluation metrics, including symptoms duration, sleeping disorders, swallowing disorders, constipation, anxiety, depression, MMSE Score, UPSDA-III Score, TCS imaging diagnosis, bilateral SN+ area, the third ventricle width and TCS grading. Biochemical tests including Homocysteine, Fatty Acid, Vit B12, Ferritin and NSE. Fasting venous blood samples (5 mL) were obtained in the morning under standardized conditions. The serum was promptly separated via centrifugation, stored at –80 °C, and subsequently analyzed using an immunoturbidimetric method. Serum ferritin, folate, and vitamin B12 (VitB12) levels were quantified via chemiluminescent immunoassay (Cobas E601, Roche Diagnostics, USA), while homocysteine (Hcy) levels were assessed using a cyclase-based electrochemiluminescence immunoassay on an AU 5800 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics).

Cognitive and behavioral assessments

Prior to clinical assessments, patients underwent a minimum 12-hour withdrawal from antiparkinsonian medications. Neurological evaluations were performed by two neurologists, each with over 10 years of experience in movement disorders, who assessed all participants using the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS). The UPSDA-III score corresponds to the Part III motor examination score of the MDS-UPDRS. Cases without an initial diagnostic consensus were reviewed by a senior movement disorder specialist with more than 20 years of experience for final determination. Additionally, all patients completed neuropsychological evaluations using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE Score)63.

Data processing and analysis pipeline

The analysis pipeline was strictly defined as follows: (1) the cohort was partitioned at the subject level into training, validation, and test sets in three random partitions (ratio ~7:1.5:1.5 per partition), ensuring that all left/right TCS-derived data from the same subject remained within the same split; (2) expert manual midbrain ROIs were used for feature extraction; (3) all images underwent per-image intensity normalization and spatial standardization; (4) left/right radiomics and deep learning features were extracted and integrated with clinical variables at the subject level; (5) all features were combined, and numeric features were standardized from the training set; (6) PCA-guided pruning and two-stage LASSO-based feature selection were performed on the training set; and (7) model performance was evaluated on the independent test set, with bootstrap confidence intervals and paired DeLong tests.

TCS images segmentation

Two radiologists, each with 10 years of experience in radiology, independently manually contoured the midbrain region for midbrain segmentation. All TCS images and their corresponding ground-truth midbrain annotations were cropped from an original resolution of 1280 × 960 to 384 × 384. This cropping strategy effectively localizes the midbrain within the region of interest (ROI) while minimizing irrelevant information from other structures, thereby enhancing learning efficiency during subsequent model development.

Radiomics and deep features extraction

We extracted Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI)-based quantitative features using PyRadiomics v3.0.164. For detailed configuration of the radiomics extraction, refer to the Supplementary Table 2. For each patient, we have extracted radiomics features (1316 features: left: 658 features, right: 658 features) from both left and right midbrain TCS images. For the deep learning features, we utilized a pre-trained weights of the ResNet5065 model to extract the deep learning features from midbrain region, and total 4096 deep learning features (4096 features: left: 2048 features, right: 2048 features). In this process, if the radiomics features or deep features are extracted from the left side of the TCS images, the suffix “_L” is appended to the feature name; if they are extracted from the right side, the suffix “_R” is appended, Data partitioning was performed at the subject level, and all data derived from the same subject (including left/right TCS features) were kept within the same split in each partition.

Data fusion and features selection

We integrated the collected clinical data with radiomics and deep learning–derived features for data fusion. Upon integration of clinical, radiomics, and deep features, all feature selection procedures were performed exclusively and independently within the pre-defined training set. To reduce feature redundancy and dimensionality, PCA66 was applied to the scaled training data, retaining components that cumulatively explained ≥95% of the variance. To preserve the interpretability of the original clinical and radiomic variables, the original variables with the highest absolute loadings for each principal component were identified and selected as representative features67. LASSO regression with cross-validation was then employed for feature selection and coefficient shrinkage, followed by a second-stage LASSO to enhance interpretability by excluding features with negligible coefficient magnitudes. To control Type I error arising from multiple comparisons during feature selection, the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction was applied where appropriate.

Model development

Two categories of ensemble learning classifiers, namely Bagging classifiers and Boosting classifiers, were employed. The Bagging-based methods included Bagging, Extremely Randomized Trees (Extra Trees), and Random Forest (RF). The Boosting-based methods comprised Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT), and Histogram-based Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (HGBDT).

Bagging classifiers68

Bagging, through the training of multiple models on random subsets of data, effectively reduces variance and enhances stability. Extra Trees further optimize performance and speed by employing random splits and an increased number of decision trees to mitigate overfitting; while RF leverages the collective strength of multiple decision trees to deliver robust predictions and minimize overfitting risks through averaging.

Boosting classifiers69

AdaBoost improves accuracy by focusing on misclassified instances and adjusting sample weights iteratively. XGBoost enhances speed and performance through parallel processing and regularization, making it highly efficient and effective. GBDT builds models sequentially to optimize residuals, allowing it to capture complex relationships in the data. HGBDT accelerates training by binning features into histograms, improving computational efficiency without sacrificing accuracy.

Additionally, a Stacking model integrating both Bagging and Boosting approaches was implemented. Stacking, is an advanced ensemble learning technique that leverages a meta-learner to optimally combine the predictions of multiple base models. By integrating diverse algorithms through hierarchical learning, it captures complex patterns and interactions that individual models may overlook. Its key advantage lies in its ability to enhance predictive performance significantly, reducing bias and variance while improving generalization on unseen data. This makes Stacking particularly powerful for winning machine learning competitions and tackling challenging real-world problems58. In the study, we constructed Stacking models based on the top-performing models (RF, XGB, and HGBDT) from the Bagging and Boosting ensemble learning approaches evaluated by the PD assessment task. The specific combinations implemented were: Stacking (RF + XGB), Stacking (RF + HGBDT), Stacking (XGB + HGBDT), and Stacking (RF + XGB + HGBDT).

We constructed and evaluated models based on clinical features alone, as well as combinations of clinical features with radiomics features, clinical features with deep features, and clinical features with both radiomics features and deep features, employing Bagging, Boosting, and Stacking ensemble learning models for comparative analysis. Furthermore, we specifically compared models with versus without TCS grading within the Stacking ensemble (RF, XGB, and HGBDT) using both clinical features only and the combined of all features (clinical features, radiomics features, and deep features).

Model evaluation

The models established in this study were evaluated using parameters including Area Under the Curve (AUC), Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (PR-AUC), Accuracy, Balanced Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score. And all the metrics were evaluated by the three independent test sets.

AUC: measures the model’s ability to distinguish between classes by calculating the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. It represents the probability that the model ranks a random positive instance higher than a random negative instance. An AUC of 1 indicates perfect separability, while 0.5 suggests no discriminative power. PR-AUC summarizes the trade-off between precision and recall across different thresholds, which is particularly informative for imbalanced classification tasks. Accuracy: quantifies the proportion of correctly classified instances (both true positives and true negatives) out of the total number of instances. It is a general measure of model performance, but can be misleading in imbalanced datasets. Balanced Accuracy is defined as the average of the recall obtained on each class, providing a fair metric for imbalanced datasets. Precision: reflects the proportion of true positive predictions among all instances predicted as positive. It measures the model’s reliability in correctly identifying positive cases and is sensitive to false positives. Recall: indicates the proportion of true positive predictions among all actual positive instances. It evaluates the model’s ability to capture all relevant positive cases and is sensitive to false negatives. F1-score: is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a balanced measure of both metrics. It is particularly useful when dealing with imbalanced datasets, as it balances the trade-off between false positives and false negatives.

To evaluate robustness, the reported mean and standard deviation summarize model performance across the three independent test sets (Independent Tests 1–3). For each independent test set, 95% confidence intervals for all metrics were estimated using bootstrap resampling (1,000 resamples). ROC70 was employed to evaluate and visualize the performance of all classification models by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate across all classification thresholds. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)71 analysis was employed as an essential interpretability framework to rigorously evaluate and elucidate the contribution of all features throughout the model training process. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA)72 was employed to assess the clinical utility of the four ensemble models, both with and without TCS grading, by quantifying net benefit across various decision thresholds in this study.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Firstly, for the feature selection process, Individual p-values were estimated using auxiliary ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, with multiple testing controlled by applying the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. Secondly, to determine whether clinical features significantly distinguish between PD and non-PD groups, features with binary characteristics were analyzed using chi-square tests. Subsequently, the Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality distribution of the features. Features with normal distributions are evaluated using independent t-tests, while those violating normality are analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test. This approach identifies features that demonstrate statistically significant differences between the two patient groups, with a two-sided P value of less than 0.05 considered significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Python (v3.10.11).

A post-hoc power analysis was conducted to assess sample adequacy based on the observed AUC, using a one-sided test against the null hypothesis of AUC = 0.5, with effect size defined as Cohen’s h and α = 0.05. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was performed to determine the minimum detectable AUC corresponding to a statistical power of 0.80 given the available sample size.
