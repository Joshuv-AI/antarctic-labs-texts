Lost in Retraining: Roaming the Parameter Space of Exponential Families Under Closed-Loop Learning
Fariba Jangjoo, Matteo Marsili, Yasser Roudi

Abstract
Closed-loop learning is the process of repeatedly estimating a model from data generated from the model itself. It is receiving great attention due to the possibility that large neural network models may, in the future, be primarily trained with data generated by artificial neural networks themselves. We study this process for models that belong to exponential families, deriving equations of motions that govern the dynamics of the parameters. We show that maximum likelihood estimation of the parameters endows sufficient statistics with the martingale property and that as a result the process converges to absorbing states that amplify initial biases present in the data. However, we show that this outcome may be prevented by polluting the data with an infinitesimal fraction of data points generated from a fixed model, by relying on maximum a posteriori estimation or by introducing regularisation. Furthermore, we show that the asymptotic behavior of the dynamics is not reparametrisation invariant.



Introduction
–.
Most theories of learning start with a dataset from an observation or experiment. A model – like a parametric or non-parametric statistical model, or a neural network – is then trained on this data to make predictions about future observations. Powerful algorithms have been developed in the research community and a deep understanding of how this process works has been gained
bishop2023deep
. However, in many situations involving learning the situation somewhat differs from this standard setup because the data itself is either completely or partially generated from another model.
For example, in deep neural networks
bengio2006greedy
;
larochelle2009exploring
each layer is trained with output generated by the previous layer. Similarly in the cortex, learning in a region may take place based on receiving the activity of another region (e.g. the Hippocampus), after the latter has formed a representation of the data it has received
girardeau2011hippocampal
;
rothschild2017cortical
;
rolls2023brain
. In these cases, learning is a feed forward process leading to a hierarchy of representation.

The situation is different in
closed-loop
learning – the process by which models are repeatedly trained on data generated by models themselves – a subject that has raised considerable recent interest in the context of
Large Language models (LLM) and other state of the art neural networks
alemohammad2023self
;
bertrand2023stability
;
marchi2024heat
;
shumailov2024ai
;
guo2023curious
;
briesch2024largelanguagemodelssuffer
;
dohmatob2024modelcollapsedemystifiedcase
;
vu2025happens
. Indeed the success of these models relies on the availability of huge amounts of high-quality human-generated data. Yet the supply of such data has been argued to be running out
villalobos2024will
. At the same time, more and more AI-generated data is populating the Internet, thereby polluting the datasets with which models are trained. It has for example been argued that that this process of “data-autophagy” is pathologic with a severity that depends on the fraction of synthetic data used in training
alemohammad2023self
;
bertrand2023stability
;
briesch2024largelanguagemodelssuffer
. A possible outcome of this is
model collapse

shumailov2024ai
– a phenomenon observed also in Generative Adversarial Networks
thanh2020catastrophic
, diffusion models
biroli2024dynamical
and Variational Autoencoders
shumailov2024ai
and LLM
guo2023curious
– whereby the model generates only a limited variety of outputs, failing to capture the full diversity of the training data. Marchi
et al.

marchi2024heat
find that closed-loop learning can also lead to the opposite outcome, where models generate random outputs. As we shall argue in the conclusions, close-loop learning is also of relevance for a wide variety of other phenomena, ranging from the evolution of cultures to rational herding
witzel2012origins
;
bikhchandani1992theory
.

Here, we study closed-loop learning within a simple yet general setting, that of Exponential Family (EF).
Distributions in EF take the form (see e.g.
bishop2023deep
sec. 3.4)

f(s|\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{Z}e^{\sum_{a}\theta_{a}\phi_{a}(s)},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ Z=\sum_{s}e^{\sum_{a}\theta_{a}\phi_{a}(s)},

(1)

where the random variable
s
takes value in
\mathcal{S}
,
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector of
d
parameters and
\phi(s)
is a
d

s

As we show, this simplified setting allows for a transparent and fully controllable rationalization of a set of phenomena that occur when retraining models on model-generated data that has the potential to unearth robust, general mechanisms that apply to a broader domain. We derive the equations that govern the dynamics of the parameters of the inferred models. We show that the ultimate outcome of a process in which models are fully or partially trained on data they themselves produce depends on two factors: the specific inference criterium adopted, and the degree to which the data is fully self-generated by previously fitted models (closed-loop learning) or not. Specifically, in closed loop learning under maximum likelihood (ML) – as e.g. in maximum entropy models
jaynes1957information
;
tkavcik2016information
– the relevant variables (sufficient statistics) acquire a martingale property that lead to phenomena very similar to model collapse. Surprisingly, however, adding one single datapoint from the original data to the self-generated data and/or introducing a regularization – as in maximum
a posteriori
(MAP) inference – may be enough to avoid this outcome.
This occurs even in the limit where, at each retraining iteration, the amount of available data for the model approaches infinity. This is surprising because, in standard (non-closed-loop) learning, the differences between MAP estimation and maximum likelihood, as well as the effects of priors and regularization, become negligible.

Discussion
–. Here we studied a model of closed loop learning and analytically derived the equations governing stochastic learning dynamics. Despite its simplicity, the model predicts observations reported in more complex cases
alemohammad2023self
;
bertrand2023stability
;
marchi2024heat
but also allows us to study how including priors and additional data influences the dynamics. In classical statistical theory, the difference between MAP (
u=1
) and ML (
u=0
) estimates vanishes for
M\to\infty
. Likewise, polluting a sample with a finite number
m
of data points drawn from a different distribution has no effect on the asymptotic behavior of statistical estimates. Our results, however, show that these conclusions do not hold in closed-loop learning,
contributing to the literature on implicit biases arising from algorithms, architectures, and/or initial conditions in artificial neural networks
rahaman2019spectral
;
francazi2023initial
;
jain2024bias
. In particular, our results suggest that while ML amplifies biases in the initial conditions, MAP or the introduction of regularization emphasizes biases implicit in algorithms and/or architectures.
Our results also show that the Fisher Information matrix is what determines drift and diffusion in close loop learning. It thus suggest that studying the Fisher Information matrix in deep neural networks using techniques such as those of
hayase2021spectrum
;
cao2020nonparametric
may play fuitful in understanding the precise dynamics of closed-loop learning in such networks.

Dynamics similar to closed-loop learning is present in many domains including word-of-mouth communication, repeated model based imitation
baker2009action
;
najar2020actions
;
charpentier2020neuro
and rational herding
bikhchandani1992theory
, or cultural transmission across generations
witzel2012origins
.
Pre-literacy cultural evolution
has been based on one generation learning from the previous one, leading to a corpus of “data” of, e.g., mythological nature that shares remarkable similarities across distant cultures
witzel2012origins
. The debate remains open as to whether these similarities stem from shared ancestry and historical migrations
witzel2012origins
, from innate archetypal structures or from universal cognitive mechanisms and biases
Boyer2001
. In social contexts, individuals learn from the observed actions of others, and their resulting behavior is observed by others. This mechanism potentially leads to “informational cascades” that have been argued to determine many collective phenomena
bikhchandani1992theory
. In prenatal development, the brain develops to a large extent in the absence of external stimuli, driven by its own spontaneous activity
martini2021spontaneous
;
wong1999retinal
. What is common in these phenomena is that learning takes place using data generated by another learnt model that and an underlying structure that guides closed-loop dynamics in a direction that performs a specific function. Although the process discussed here is very simple and stylized, it offers a platform for studying these phonomena
and engineering models that implement specific functions or that feature pre-assigned structures of asymptotic states.

Appendix A
Absorption probabilities in martingales

In the dynamical process described in the main text,
\hat{\bm{s}}(t)
determines
\bar{\phi}_{a}(\hat{\bm{s}}(t))
and thus determines
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
and the distribution
p(\cdot|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
. Consequently, given
\hat{\bm{s}}(t)
and thus
\bar{\phi}_{a,t}\equiv\bar{\phi}_{a}(\hat{\bm{s}}(t))
, we have

\langle\bar{\phi}_{a,t+1}|\bar{\phi}_{a,t}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}=\bar{\phi}_{a,t}\,.

(24)

implying that
\bar{\phi}_{a,t}
is a martingale. Since the sequence of samples
\hat{\bm{s}}(0),\hat{\bm{s}}(1),\cdots
forms a Markov chain, so does
\bar{\phi}_{a,0},\bar{\phi}_{a,1},\cdots
.
Under the assumption that
\phi(s)
is bounded, the states

\phi_{a}^{+}=\max_{s}\phi_{a}(s),\qquad\phi_{a}^{-}=\min_{s}\phi_{a}(s)

(25)

are absorbing states, for any Markov chain that is also a martingale. In other words,
P\{\bar{\phi}_{a,t+1}|\bar{\phi}_{a,t}=\phi_{a}^{\pm}\}=1
if

\bar{\phi}_{a,t+1}=\phi_{a}^{\pm}
and
P\{\bar{\phi}_{a,t+1}|\bar{\phi}_{a,t}=\phi_{a}^{\pm}\}=0
otherwise. In addition, if there are no other absorbing states, the asymptotic probability to be absorbed at
\phi_{a}^{\pm}
starting from
\bar{\phi}_{a,0}
is given by

\lim_{t\to\infty}P\{\bar{\phi}_{a,t}=\phi_{a}^{+}|\bar{\phi}_{a,0}\}=\frac{\bar{\phi}_{a,0}-\phi_{a}^{-}}{\phi_{a}^{+}-\phi_{a}^{-}}

(26)

The proof of these statements is straightforward; we recall the argument in Feller
feller1968introduction
. For a single variable
\phi
taking discrete values in
[\phi_{-},\phi_{+}]
, let
p_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}=P(\phi_{t+1}=\phi^{\prime}|\phi_{t}=\phi)
be the transition probability matrix. The martingale property implies

\langle\phi_{t}|\phi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{\phi^{\prime}}p_{\phi_{0},\phi^{\prime}}\phi^{\prime}=\phi_{0}\,.

For
\phi_{0}=\phi_{-}
this necessarily implies that
p_{\phi_{-},\phi^{\prime}}=0
for all
\phi^{\prime}>\phi_{0}
and
p_{\phi_{-},\phi_{-}}=1
, so
\phi_{-}
is an absorbing state. The same is true for
\phi_{0}=\phi_{+}
.

Let
p_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{(t)}
be the
\phi,\phi^{\prime}
matrix element of the
t^{\rm th}
power of the transition matrix. If there is no other absorbing state, then
p_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{(t)}\to 0
for
t\to\infty
, for all
\phi^{\prime}\neq\phi_{\pm}
. The iteration of the martingale property infinite times then implies that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[p_{\phi_{0},\phi_{-}}^{(t)}\phi_{-}+p_{\phi_{0},\phi_{+}}^{(t)}\phi_{+}\right]=\phi_{0}

Eq. (
26
) follows from the fact that
p_{\phi_{0},\phi_{-}}^{(t)}=1-p_{\phi_{0},\phi_{+}}^{(t)}
.

Absorption probabilities in Ising model.
In the case of the Ising model,
\bar{\phi}_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})=M^{-1}\sum^{M,N}_{l,j=1}\hat{\sigma}^{l}_{j}
. From Eq. (
25
) above, we have
\phi^{\pm}_{1}=\pm N
and thus using Eq. (
26
) above, we retrieve Eq. (
8
) in the main text for the case of
\theta_{2}=0
. If, instead
\theta_{2}\neq 0
, we can write

f(s|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{1}{Z^{\prime}}\exp\left[\frac{\theta_{2}}{2N}\left(\phi_{1}(s)-\phi^{*}\right)^{2}\right],\qquad\phi^{*}=-N\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{2}}

(27)

with
Z^{\prime}
a normalization constant.
Consequently,
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are both coupled to the same order parameter
\phi_{1}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}
.
For a given
N
and
\phi^{*}=N-2k
with
k=0,1,2,\cdots N
,
f(s|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
in Eq. (
27
) the distribution of
\phi_{1}
becomes more and more concentrated around
\phi^{*}
, as
\theta_{2}\to-\infty
(with
\theta_{1}=-\theta_{2}\phi^{*}/N\to\infty
also diverging). This limit is realized as soon as, at some time step
t
,
\phi_{1}(\hat{s}^{l}(t))=\phi^{*}
for all data points
\hat{s}^{(}l)(t)
, so that
\bar{\phi}_{1,t}=\phi^{*}
. For all
t^{\prime}>t
we will also have
\bar{\phi}_{1,t^{\prime}}=\phi^{*}
and thus
\phi^{*}
is an absorbing state for
\bar{\phi}_{1,t}
. As a result, for
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
diverging with a finite ratio
\theta_{1}/\theta_{2}=-\phi^{*}/N
, the system thus has
N+1
absorbing states, corresponding to the
N+1
different values that
\phi^{*}
can take.

Absorption probabilities in REM.
Similar to the Ising model discussed in the previous section, the Random Energy model (REM) is also defined over configurations of
N
spins
s=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{N})
with
\sigma_{i}=\pm 1
. It assigns a different parameter – the energy
E(s)
– to each of the
2^{n}
spin configuration, and the probability of each state is proportional to
\exp[-E(s)]
. In the notation of Eq. (
1
) of the main text, for each configuration
s
, we have a corresponding
\theta_{s}
and
\phi_{s}
and

\phi_{s}(s^{\prime})=\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}

(28)

The model is an over-parametrized model in the sense that there is corresponding parameter for each spin configuration.

Closed loop learning in this case is straightforward. For
\hat{\bm{s}}
comprised of
M
data points, let us denote by
k_{s}
the number of times configuration
s
has been observed. Since
\sum_{s}k_{s}=M
, we have
\bar{\phi}_{s}(\hat{\bm{s}})=k_{s}
.
Starting from a sample where state
s
is observed
k_{s,0}
times, with
\sum_{s}k_{s,0}=M
, at iteration
t
, let
k_{s,t}
be the count of state
s
. Maximum likelihood returns the estimate
\exp[\hat{\theta}^{t}_{s}]/Z=\mu_{t}(s)=\frac{k_{s},t}{M}
. From this a new sample
\hat{s}^{(t+1)}
is drawn. It is easy to see that if a state
s
fails to be observed at time
t
, i.e. if
k_{s,t}=0
, then
\hat{\theta}_{s,t}\to-\infty
, which means that state
s
will never be observed forever after. Then the number of states with
k_{s,t}>0
is a non-increasing function of time. The asymptotic state will be one where
k_{s^{*},t}=M
for one state and
k_{s,t}=0
for all
s\neq s^{*}
. Alternatively, by noting that
for each
s
,
\bar{\phi}^{+}_{s}=M
and
\bar{\phi}^{+}_{s}=0
, and that
\bar{\phi}_{s,t}=k_{s,t}
, Eq. (
26
) can then be written as

\lim_{t\to\infty}P\{k_{s,t}=M|k_{s,0}\}=\frac{k_{s,0}}{M}\,.

(29)

Indeed Eq. (
26
) clearly applies to the reduced process
\tilde{s}=1
for state
s
and
\tilde{s}=0
for any other state
s^{\prime}\neq s
.

Appendix B
The continuum time limit

Let us begin by deriving an expression for the transition matrix

p(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}^{\prime}|\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\hat{\bm{s}}}f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\bm{\theta})\delta\left(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\hat{\bm{s}})\right).

(30)

In order to do so, we first write, assuming
M\gg 1
,

\displaystyle p(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}^{\prime}|\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{\bm{s}}}\frac{f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\bm{\theta})}{f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})}f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})\delta\left(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}\right)

(31)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{\bm{s}}}e^{-\frac{M}{2}(\bm{\theta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})\hat{J}(\bm{\theta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})}f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})\delta\left(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}\right)

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{M}{2}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime})J^{\prime}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime})}\sum_{\hat{\bm{s}}}f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})\delta\left(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}\right)

(33)

here
\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\hat{\bm{s}})
is a function of
\hat{\bm{s}}
. In the first line, the term
\frac{f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\bm{\theta})}{f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})}
is non-zero only for
\bm{\theta}\approx\hat{\bm{\theta}}
, so we expand the (log of the) numerator around
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
and retain only terms up to 2nd order (with an error which is of order
1/M
). In the second line,

J_{a,b}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{a}\partial\theta_{b}}\log Z=\langle\phi_{a}\phi_{b}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}}-\langle\phi_{a}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}}\langle\phi_{b}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}}

(34)

is the Fisher Information matrix and we use the shorthands
\hat{J}=J(\hat{\bm{\theta}})
and
J^{\prime}=J(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
. In Eq. (
33
) we substitute
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
with
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
, exploiting the presence of the
\delta
-function. For the last term, we use the identity

\int d\bm{\theta}g(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\hat{\bm{s}}}\int d\bm{\theta}g(\bm{\theta})f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\bm{\theta})\cong\left(\frac{2\pi}{M}\right)^{d/2}\sum_{\hat{\bm{s}}}\frac{f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})g(\hat{\bm{\theta}})}{\sqrt{{\rm det}J(\hat{\bm{\theta}})}}

(35)

where
g(\bm{\theta})
is a generic function. This identity follows by estimating the integral in the middle expression by the saddle point method for
M\gg 1
.
Using
g(\bm{\theta})=\left(\frac{2\pi}{M}\right)^{-d/2}\sqrt{{\rm det}J(\bm{\theta})}\,\delta(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
in Eq. (
33
), we find

p(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}^{\prime}|\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta})\cong\left(\frac{M}{2\pi}\right)^{-d/2}\sqrt{{\rm det}J^{\prime}}e^{-\frac{M}{2}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime})J^{\prime}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime})}\cong\left(\frac{M}{2\pi}\right)^{-d/2}\sqrt{{\rm det}J}e^{-\frac{M}{2}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta})J(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta})-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta})\bm{\alpha}}

(36)

where we expanded the term
\sqrt{{\rm det}J^{\prime}}\cong\sqrt{{\rm det}J}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\bm{\alpha}}
with

\alpha_{a}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\log{\rm det}J(\bm{\theta})\,,

and substituted
J^{\prime}
with
J
in the quadratic term at the exponent, neglecting sub-leading corrections. This is a Gaussian distribution for
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
with mean and variance

\langle\theta_{a}^{\prime}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}}=\theta_{a}-\frac{1}{2M}\sum_{b}J^{-1}_{a,b}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{b}}\log{\rm det}J(\bm{\theta})\,,\qquad\langle(\theta_{a}^{\prime}-\langle\theta_{a}^{\prime}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}})(\theta_{b}^{\prime}-\langle\theta_{b}^{\prime}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}})\rangle_{\bm{\theta}}=J^{-1}_{a,b}\,.

(37)

In the continuous time limit, this leads to the stochastic differential equation

d\theta_{a}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b}J^{-1}_{a,b}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{b}}\log{\rm det}J(\bm{\theta})d\tau+dW_{a}\,,\qquad\langle dW_{a,\tau}dW_{a,\tau^{\prime}}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}_{\tau}}=J^{-1}_{a,b}(\bm{\theta}_{\tau})\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})d\tau\,.

(38)

This corresponds to a stochastic differential equation for
\phi_{a}
that is readily obtained with Ito calculus

\displaystyle d\phi_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b}\frac{\partial\phi_{a}}{\partial\theta_{b}}d\theta_{b}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b,c}\langle dW_{a,\tau}dW_{a,\tau^{\prime}}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}_{\tau}}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{a}}{\partial\theta_{b}\partial\theta_{c}}d\tau

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dW_{a,\tau}^{\prime}\,,\qquad\langle dW_{a,\tau}^{\prime}dW_{a,\tau^{\prime}}^{\prime}\rangle_{\bm{\phi}_{\tau}}=J_{a,b}\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})d\tau

(40)

which is drift-less, as it should be given the martingale property. Here we used the identity

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\log{\rm det}J(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{b,c}J_{b,c}^{-1}\frac{\partial J_{b,c}}{\partial\theta_{a}}\,.

In the general case, the transition matrix is given by

p(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}^{\prime}|\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\hat{\bm{s}},\hat{\bm{t}}}p(\hat{\bm{s}}|\bm{\theta})q(\hat{\bm{t}})\delta\left(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}(\hat{\bm{s}},\hat{\bm{t}})\right)

(41)

where

\bar{\bm{\theta}}
is determined via

\bar{\bm{\theta}}(\hat{\bm{s}},\hat{\bm{t}})={\rm arg}\max_{\bm{\vartheta}}\mathcal{G}(\bm{\vartheta},\hat{\bm{s}},\hat{\bm{t}})\,,\qquad\mathcal{G}(\bm{\vartheta},\hat{\bm{s}},\hat{\bm{t}})=\log f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\bm{\vartheta})+\log f(\hat{\bm{t}}|\bm{\vartheta})+u\log p_{0}(\bm{\vartheta})

(42)

With
m=u=0
this problem reduces to the case where
\bar{\bm{\theta}}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\hat{\bm{s}})
is the maximum likelihood (ML) estimator and all samples are generated from the ML distribution at the previous step. With
m>0
this problem envisages the possibility that some samples
\hat{\bm{t}}
are generated from the “true” distribution
q(\cdot)
while the others are generated from
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
and with
u=1
we look at the case where
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
is the maximum
a posteriori
(MAP) estimator, with prior
p_{0}
.

With finite
m
and
M\gg 1
, the maximum is dominated by the ML estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\hat{\bm{s}})
, so we expand around it:

\mathcal{G}(\bm{\vartheta})\simeq\log\left[f(\hat{\bm{s}}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})f(\hat{\bm{t}}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})\right]+u\log p_{0}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})-\frac{M}{2}(\bm{\vartheta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})^{T}\hat{\bm{J}}(\bm{\vartheta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})+\bm{\alpha}^{T}(\bm{\vartheta}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})+\ldots\,,

(43)

where
\hat{\bm{J}}=\bm{J}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})
and
\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector with components

\tilde{\alpha}_{a}=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta_{a}}\log f(\hat{\bm{t}}|\bm{\vartheta})\right|_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}+u\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta_{a}}\log p_{0}(\bm{\vartheta})\right|_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}\,.

(44)

To leading order in
M
, the maximum is given by

\bar{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{s},\bm{t})=\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{s})+\frac{\hat{\bm{J}}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{M}\,.

(45)

Therefore, in the continuous time limit, the stochastic differential equation acquires an additional drift

{\bm{J}^{-1}\langle\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}}}=\bm{J}^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\left[u\log p_{0}(\bm{\theta})-mD(q|\bm{\theta})\right]

,
so that

d\theta_{a}=-\sum_{b}J^{-1}_{a,b}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{b}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\log{\rm det}J(\bm{\theta})-u\log p_{0}(\bm{\theta})+mD(q|\bm{\theta})\right]d\tau+dW_{a}\,,\qquad\langle dW_{a,\tau}dW_{a,\tau^{\prime}}\rangle_{\bm{\theta}}=J^{-1}_{a,b}(\bm{\theta})\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})d\tau

(46)

as in Eqs. (13-16) of the main text.

B.1
The stationary state

As discussed in Gardiner
gardiner2009stochastic
, the Fokker-Planck equation associated to the stochastic differential equation (
14
) in the Letter can be written as

\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=\sum_{a}\frac{\partial F_{a}}{\partial\theta_{a}}

(47)

where the probability current
\mathbf{F}
is given by

F_{a}=-A_{a}p+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{b}}J^{-1}_{a,b}p=-\left[A_{a}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b}\frac{\partial J^{-1}_{a,b}}{\partial\theta_{b}}\right]p+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b}J^{-1}_{a,b}\frac{\partial p}{\partial\theta_{b}}

(48)

The zero current condition
\mathbf{F}=0
reads

2A_{a}-\sum_{b}\frac{\partial J^{-1}_{a,b}}{\partial\theta_{b}}=\sum_{b}J^{-1}_{a,b}\frac{\partial\log p}{\partial\theta_{b}}

(49)

or

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log p}{\partial\theta_{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a}J_{b,a}\left[2A_{a}-\sum_{c}\frac{\partial J^{-1}_{a,c}}{\partial\theta_{c}}\right]

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\frac{\partial U}{\partial\theta_{b}}-\sum_{a,c}J_{b,a}\frac{\partial J^{-1}_{a,c}}{\partial\theta_{c}}

(51)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\frac{\partial U}{\partial\theta_{b}}+\sum_{a,c}J^{-1}_{a,c}\frac{\partial J_{a,b}}{\partial\theta_{c}}

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\frac{\partial U}{\partial\theta_{b}}+\sum_{a,c}\frac{\partial\log{\rm det}J}{\partial J_{a,c}}\frac{\partial J_{a,c}}{\partial\theta_{b}}

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{b}}\left[-2U+\log{\rm det}J\right]

(54)

where

U(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\log{\rm det}J-u\log p_{0}(\bm{\theta})+mD(q|\bm{\theta})

is the same as Eq. (14) in the Letter and we used the identities

\displaystyle\sum_{a,c}J_{b,a}\frac{\partial J^{-1}_{a,c}}{\partial\theta_{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{c}}\left[\sum_{a,c}J_{b,a}J^{-1}_{a,c}\right]-\sum_{a,c}\frac{\partial J_{a,b}}{\partial\theta_{c}}J^{-1}_{a,c}=-\sum_{a,c}J^{-1}_{a,c}\frac{\partial J_{a,b}}{\partial\theta_{c}}

(55)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J_{a,b}}{\partial\theta_{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{3}\log Z}{\partial\theta_{a}\partial\theta_{b}\partial\theta_{c}}=\frac{\partial J_{a,c}}{\partial\theta_{b}}

(56)

\displaystyle J^{-1}_{a,c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log{\rm det}J}{\partial J_{a,c}}

(57)

where
Z
is the normalization constant in Eq. (
1
) of the main text. In Eq. (
55
) we used the fact that the term in square brackets is the identity matrix and hence its derivative vanishes, and then we used identity (
56
) to swap indices
c\leftrightarrow b
.

Therefore, the stationary state distribution is

p_{s}(\bm{\theta})=c\,{\rm det}J\,e^{-2U(\bm{\theta})}

(58)

with
c
a normalization constant. The stationary state exists as long as
p_{s}
is normalizable, i.e. as long as
c
is finite. This yields Eq. (
21
) in the main text.

Appendix C
Non-canonical representations: the case of Poisson distribution

Let us consider the case of a Poisson distribution

f(s|\theta)=\frac{\vartheta^{s}}{s!}e^{-\vartheta}

with mean
1
1

1

Since the distribution of
s
depends on a single parameter, we use the notation
\bm{\theta}=\vartheta
.

\langle\bm{s}\rangle_{\vartheta}=\vartheta\geq 0
and prior
p_{0}(\vartheta)=\lambda e^{-\lambda\vartheta}
. Then the optimisation of
\mathcal{G}
yields

\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial\vartheta}=\frac{S+T}{\vartheta}-M-m-\lambda\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \bar{\vartheta}=\frac{S+T}{M+m+\lambda},\qquad S=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\hat{s}_{i},\penalty\ \penalty\ T=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\hat{t}_{i}\,.

The conditional distribution of
\vartheta^{\prime}
given
\vartheta
is given by

\displaystyle p(\vartheta_{t+1}=\vartheta^{\prime}|\vartheta_{t}=\vartheta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{S=0}^{\infty}\frac{(M\vartheta)^{S}}{S!}e^{-M\vartheta}\sum_{T=0}^{\infty}\frac{(m\vartheta_{0})^{S}}{S!}e^{-M\vartheta}\delta\left(\vartheta^{\prime}-\frac{S+T}{M+n+\lambda}\right)

(59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{2\pi}e^{ik\vartheta^{\prime}}\exp\left[M\vartheta\left(1-e^{-ik/(M+m+\lambda)}\right)\right]\exp\left[m\vartheta_{0}\left(1-e^{-ik/(M+m+\lambda)}\right)\right]

(60)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\exp\left[ik\left(\vartheta^{\prime}-\frac{M\vartheta+m\vartheta_{0}}{M+m+\lambda}\right)-\frac{M\vartheta+m\vartheta_{0}}{2(M+m+\lambda)^{2}}k^{2}+O(k^{3}/M^{2})\right]

(61)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\frac{M+m+\lambda}{\sqrt{2\pi(M\vartheta+m\vartheta_{0})}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\vartheta^{\prime}-\langle\vartheta^{\prime}\rangle_{\vartheta})^{2}}{\langle(\vartheta^{\prime}-\langle\vartheta^{\prime}\rangle_{\vartheta})^{2}\rangle}\right]

(62)

which is a Gaussian with mean and variance

\langle\vartheta^{\prime}\rangle_{\vartheta}=\frac{M\vartheta+m\vartheta_{0}}{M+m+\lambda}=\vartheta+\frac{m+\lambda}{M+m+\lambda}\left(\frac{m\vartheta_{0}}{m+\lambda}-\vartheta\right)\,,\qquad\langle(\vartheta^{\prime}-\langle\vartheta^{\prime}\rangle_{\vartheta})^{2}\rangle=\frac{M\vartheta+m\vartheta_{0}}{(M+m+\lambda)^{2}}

(63)

respectively. In the limit
M\to\infty
, in rescaled time, the parameter
\vartheta
follows the stochastic differential equation

d\vartheta=(m+\lambda)\left[\frac{m\vartheta_{0}}{m+\lambda}-\vartheta\right]d\tau+\sqrt{\vartheta}dW

(64)

which is a Cox-Ingersoll-Ross model
cox1985theory
. When
2m\vartheta_{0}\geq 1
the process admits a stationary distribution

p_{s}(\vartheta)=\frac{[2(m+\lambda)]^{2m\vartheta_{0}}}{\Gamma(2m\vartheta_{0})}\vartheta^{2m\vartheta_{0}-1}e^{-2(m+\lambda)\vartheta}

(65)

which is given by a Gamma distribution. This result agrees with numerical simulations. Note that with
m=0
the distribution is not normalizable, i.e.
\vartheta=0
is an absorbing state for all
\lambda>0
when
m=0
.

When the Poisson distribution is expressed in terms of the canonical parameter
\theta=\log\langle s\rangle_{\theta}
, Eq. (
58
) leads to a stationary state

p_{s}(\theta)=\frac{[2(m+\lambda)]^{2m\vartheta_{0}+2}}{\Gamma(2m\vartheta_{0}+2)}e^{2(m\vartheta_{0}+1)\theta-2(m+\lambda)e^{\theta}}

(66)

which, upon changing variables to
\vartheta=e^{\theta}
yields a stationary state in terms of the parameter
\theta

p_{s}(\vartheta)=\frac{[2(m+\lambda)]^{2m\vartheta_{0}+2}}{\Gamma(2m\vartheta_{0}+2)}\vartheta^{2m\vartheta_{0}+1}e^{-2(m+\lambda)\vartheta}

(67)

which is different with respect to Eq. (
23
) in the main text.
