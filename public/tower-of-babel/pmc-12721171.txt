The interplay between Caenorhabditis elegans larval development and Orsay virus infection
Authors: Izan Melero, Victoria G Castiglioni, María J Olmo-Uceda, Ana Villena-Giménez, María Olmedo, Rubén González, Santiago F Elena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe development of Caenorhabditis elegans is tightly regulated and highly sensitive to environmental cues, including pathogens. While the effects of bacterial and fungal infections on development have been studied, the influence of viral infections remains poorly understood. We investigate the bidirectional relationship between C. elegans larval development and infection by its natural enteric pathogen Orsay virus (OrV). We show that OrV can replicate efficiently during molting periods and that infection initiated in more developed larvae leads to significantly higher viral loads and broader intestinal cell infection. Conversely, OrV infection perturbs the expression of developmental regulators and alters the timing of larval transitions: it accelerates the first molt and delays the subsequent stages, ultimately resynchronizing overall developmental timing. These findings suggest that developmental transitions create windows of altered antiviral capacity. Our work highlights trade-offs between developmental progression and immune competence, offering new insights into how host-pathogen interactions are shaped by development.Subject areas: immunology, virology, developmental biology

Introduction

The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans is a key model organism for studying development. It is the only multicellular organism with a fully mapped cell lineage. Each of its somatic cells follows a predictable fate from fertilization to adulthood, and the organism has a fixed number of these cells.1,2,3 C. elegans develops through four larval stages (L1 – L4), each ending in a molt (Figure 1). At the start of each stage, before molting, stem-like divisions occur in the seam cells (lateral hypodermal cells).4 These divisions produce one new seam cell and one daughter cell that undergoes DNA endoreduplication (i.e., extra rounds of DNA synthesis without mitosis or cytokinesis, resulting in an endopolyploid cell) and fuses with the large syncytial hypodermal cell hyp7, which covers most of the adult body except the head and tail.5,6 Intestinal cells also undergo endoreduplication prior to each molting, resulting in a 32C DNA content in adult intestinal nuclei.7

Molting includes a lethargic phase in which animals stop feeding and moving and enter a sleep-like state.8,9 The process ends with ecdysis, where the nematode sheds its old cuticle and replaces it with a new one. These molting events are marked by rapid growth, while the periods between molts show more linear growth.10 Growth is supported by the elasticity of the collagen-based cuticle, which allows expansion both during and between molts.

The cuticle itself changes composition throughout life.11 It plays essential roles in locomotion and protection against pathogens and harsh environments. For instance, during the dauer stage —a stress-induced developmental arrest triggered by starvation, overcrowding, or high temperatures— the cuticle becomes thicker and more resilient.12,13 All internal epithelial surfaces are covered by a collagen-based cuticle except the pharynx, which contains chitin and other non-collagen proteins.14,15 The stiffness of chitin restricts pharyngeal growth to molting periods. Because of this constraint, it is thought that the pharyngeal cuticle must be renewed separately from the rest of the epithelial cuticle.15 The inability of the pharynx to grow outside molting periods may limit feeding and potentially trigger molting.16

Many genes show oscillating expression patterns synchronized with molting cycles.17,18,19 This includes key developmental regulators such as the heterochronic gene lin-42,20 the nuclear receptor nhr-23,21 or the microRNA let-7.22 Mutations in these genes can disrupt seam cell fate and alter molting timing and frequency, leading to early or delayed development.23,24,25 Hormonal signals may also influence molting, although the specific ligands involved are still unknown.15,25

C. elegans development is highly sensitive to external conditions such as temperature, food quality, and nutrient availability. In nature, these nematodes feed on diverse bacteria and are constantly exposed to potential pathogens. Different bacterial diets can affect development, lifespan, reproduction, and responses to infections.26,27,28,29,30,31,32 The nematode activates specific immune responses to various pathogens, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses. While some responses are general, others are tailored to pathogen type.33,34 For example, infections by viruses or microsporidia trigger the intracellular pathogen response (IPR).35 The main antiviral defense is RNA interference (RNAi), which can prevent reinfection in an age-specific manner36 and even be inherited by offspring.37 Additional strategies include viral RNA uridylation and expression of antiviral genes involved in ubiquitination and protein degradation.38,39,40 Collagens may also act as physical barriers in intestinal cells to prevent viral entry,41 although the mechanisms of antiviral defense are still not fully understood.

Orsay virus (OrV) is the first natural viral pathogen identified in C. elegans. It spreads via the oro-fecal route and targets intestinal cells.42 OrV is related to the Nodaviridae family and has a small, bipartite, nonenveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA genome. Its RNA1 encodes an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, while RNA2 encodes a capsid protein (CP) and the δ protein, which facilitate nonlytic viral release.43 A CP-δ fusion protein, generated by a ribosomal frameshift, plays a role in receptor binding and virus entry.44,45 OrV infections are generally mild, with limited effects on lifespan or reproduction in some C. elegans strains.42,46

However, it remains unknown whether OrV affects larval development or molting or whether molting influences viral infection dynamics. To explore these questions, we focused on (i) how molting affects OrV accumulation dynamics and (ii) how OrV infection impacts developmental timing. We used several complementary approaches, including RT-qPCR for measuring viral load, single-molecule inexpensive fluorescence in situ hybridization (smiFISH) for subcellular localization, and bioluminescence assays to measure the duration of each larval stage. Our findings enhance the understanding of virus-host interactions and provide new insights into how viral infections can influence development in C. elegans.

Results

Molting does not restrict OrV replication or drive viral-load decline

Recently, we have described the OrV dynamics throughout C. elegans larval development, showing a viral-load decay after reaching a maximum ∼12 h post-inoculation (hpi).47 To investigate whether this viral decline results from the onset of molting, which starts around 14 h post-hatching (hph) at 20°C, we inoculated L1 nematodes immediately after hatching (0 hph) or at 6 hph (Figure 1). Nematodes used were from the ERT54 strain (jyIs8[Ppals-5p::GFP; myo-2p::mCherry]X), which is derived from the N2 Bristol and carries a green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter for the activation of the IPR.38 Samples were collected every 2 h for 24 h, covering the first molting period, and OrV accumulation dynamics was measured by RT-qPCR. The inoculation at 6 hph was chosen to allow sufficient time for exponential viral replication before molt initiation (Figure 1), as this is the minimum time OrV requires to start exponentially replicating in intestinal cells under our conditions (see Figure 1B in Castiglioni et al.47).

We observed that the viral load of animals inoculated at 0 hph peaked around 12 hpi and subsequently declined, replicating previous observations.47 In contrast, for animals inoculated at 6 hph, the molting period aligned with the phase of exponential viral replication (Figure 2A). This demonstrates that OrV can replicate within intestinal cells even while C. elegans undergoes molting. Furthermore, molt initiation not necessarily led to a viral-load decline, and inoculating nematodes closer to the molting onset (6 hph) did not accelerate any decrease in viral titer. This suggests that the observed viral-load decay after ∼12 hpi likely results from factors other than the onset of molting.

Later L1 infection produces higher and more sustained viral loads than immediate post-hatching infection

Comparing the infection dynamics between nematodes inoculated immediately after hatching (0 hph) vs. those inoculated at 6 hph (Figure 2B) revealed that the first wave of viral infection was substantially higher and prolonged in time on nematodes inoculated at 6 hph. Specifically, the maximum viral load reached (2.6 ± 0.8)×107 RNA2 molecules/ng total RNA at ∼14 hpi for the 6 hph group, compared to (9 ± 3)×106 molecules after ∼12 hpi for the 0 hph group. Data in Figure 2B, fitted to a generalized linear model (GLM) (gamma distribution, log-link function; hph as fixed factor and hpi as covariable), confirmed a significant and large-magnitude difference between the two inoculation conditions (χ2 = 27.127, 1 degrees of freedon [d.f.], p < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.473).

This difference suggests that the host physiological state at the time of infection influences OrV replication dynamics, though whether this relates specifically to molting proximity or general developmental progression remains unclear. The enhanced viral replication in animals infected closer to a molt could indicate that the host’s capacity to restrict viral proliferation is transiently altered during this developmental transition. This observation aligns with the resource allocation principle, which states that trade-offs arise between competing functions due to limited resources.48,49 Consequently, resources invested in producing new cuticle might not be available or be diverted from defense, resulting in altered antiviral responses during developmental transitions.

Later L1 infection produces more widespread intestinal infection and enhanced viral egress than immediate post-hatching infection

To determine whether the larger wave of viral infection observed in nematodes infected at 6 vs. 0 hph corresponded to a greater number of infected intestinal cells, we performed smiFISH on animals collected at 12 hpi (the approximate peak of viral load for both conditions. We observed larvae with infected intestinal cells and the presence of viral RNA in the intestinal lumen (representative images shown in Figure 3A).

Data (Figure 3B), fitted to a GLM (binomial distribution, logit-link; hph as fixed factor), reveled that the frequency of nematodes with viral RNA signal was significantly higher on animals inoculated at 6 hph (0.84 ± 0.03) compared to nematodes inoculated at 0 hph (0.60 ± 0.05) (χ2 = 18.502, 1 d.f., p < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.251). Furthermore, the number of intestinal infected cells per nematode was also significantly higher on 6 hph-inoculated animals than in those inoculated at 0 hph (Figure 3C; Pearson homogeneity test, χ2 = 13.206, 2 d.f., p = 0.001; magnitude of the effect, Cramér’s V = 0.271). Specifically, 0.38 ± 0.09 (LaPlace estimator ± adjusted Wald margin of error) of the 6 hph-inoculated nematodes had two or more infected intestinal cells, compared to 0.15 ± 0.08 for 0 hph-inoculated nematodes.

We next assessed the spatial distribution of infection, specifically whether viral RNA localized exclusively within intestinal cells, in the lumen, or in both. The distribution of viral RNA differed significantly between animals inoculated at 0 and 6 hph (Figure 3D; χ2 = 15.404, 2 d.f., p = 0.001, V = 0.255). As reported in Castiglioni et al.,47 most animals (0.84 ± 0.08) inoculated at 0 hph retained the virus exclusively within intestinal cells, with only 0.11 ± 0.07 of the animals showing only smiFISH signal in the lumen (all viral particles already egressed). By contrast, when inoculation took place 6 hph, the frequency of animals with only intracellular location dropped to 0.61 ± 0.08. In this case, the frequency of animals with only luminal signal increased to 0.31 ± 0.07, suggesting a faster completion of the intracellular infection cycle and egression to the lumen.

Molting is a highly dynamic process involving extensive physiological remodeling. Because a new cuticle must be synthesized, cellular trafficking is a fundamental aspect of this period.15,50,51 This highly dynamic environment taking place during molting periods could explain the higher luminal viral signal found in animals infected near a molting stage and the higher number of infected intestinal cells found on these animals in comparison to the ones found on freshly hatched nematodes, as the virus could potentially have more opportunities to enter and exit the intestinal cells. As OrV exits intestinal cells in a nonlytic manner43 (likely through exocytosis; see Figure 2A47), and considering the highly vesicular transport happening throughout the nematode’s development,52 OrV might also sequester cellular resources, including vesicular components. Such sequestration could potentially disrupt developmental timing, adding another layer to the interplay between viral infection and host development.

Key antiviral pathways do not drive the observed viral-load decay

To investigate whether the viral-load decay observed at ∼12 hpi results from activation of known antiviral pathways, we examined OrV dynamics in mutants deficient in RNAi and viral RNA uridylation. For RNAi, we used drh-1(ok3495) mutants, which are defective in the RIG-I-like receptor essential for antiviral RNAi responses.46 For the uridylation pathway, we studied cid-1(tm936) mutants, which lack the viral uridyltransferase required for viral RNA uridylation.38

Both mutant strains showed virus replication dynamics similar to those of the reference N2 animals, with an initial peak in viral load followed by a rapid decline around 12 hpi (Figure 4). A GLM (gamma distribution and log-link function with genotype as fixed factor and hpi as covariable) was used to compare viral accumulation patterns on each mutant compared to their corresponding control N2. In the case of drh-1 (Figure 4A), significant and substantial differences in viral load among the two genotypes (χ2 = 70.910, 1 d.f., p < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.204). These differences varied depending on the time point (genotype by hpi interaction, χ2 = 54.120, 5 d.f., p < 0.001). In the case of cid-1 (Figure 4B), viral load also was significantly different from observed in N2 animals (χ2 = 43.218, 1 d.f., p < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.644), although the effect remained constant a long time (χ2 = 7.679, 5 d.f., p = 0.175).

In both mutants, especially in drh-1, the viral load peaked early and declined sharply at 12 hpi, mirroring the pattern observed in wild-type N2 animals (Figure 4). However, drh-1 animals maintained significantly higher viral loads throughout the 40-h period (mean ± SD, [7 ± 1]×107 RNA2 molecules/ng total RNA) compared to wild-type animals ([1.2 ± 0.2]×106, Bonferroni post hoc test, p < 0.001). A smaller yet significant difference was found between cid-1 ([8 ± 2]×107) and wild-type animals ([1.1 ± 0.3]×107, Bonferroni post hoc test, p = 0.001). These results suggest that viral clearance at 12 hpi is not solely driven by antiviral pathways associated with cid-1 and drh-1, indicating that other factors must be contributing to the observed viral dynamics.

Viral infection alters developmental gene expression patterns during larval development

Next, we investigated the potential impact of OrV infection on C. elegans transcriptional regulation during larval development. To this end, we re-analyzed transcriptional data from infected and control animals throughout larval development47 and identified a set of genes exhibiting an oscillatory expression profile synchronized with molting periods. Notably, these genes (many of which belonged to molting-related functions; Table S1) follow a periodic on/off pattern that is altered in infected animals (i.e., compare differences between L2 – L3 phases, Figure 5A), yet converged to a similar state by the end of larval development L4 (i.e., at the later time points of the infection, Figure 5A).

Furthermore, we found that many genes associated with development and molting were differentially expressed in response to viral infection. This includes the steroid dehydrogenase let-767, thought to play an important role on sterol uptake,53 an important process for extracellular matrix remodeling. Gene disruption in response to infection seemed to be especially significant during the late L1 – L2 larval stages (Figure 5B; Table S1). These findings indicate that OrV infection alters transcriptional programs linked to larval development, suggesting potential effects on molting timing.

Many C. elegans genes exhibit oscillatory expression patterns synchronized with the molting cycle.17,18,19 Among these genes, key developmental regulators such as the afore mentioned let-7,22 lin-42,20,47 or nhr-2321 are known to play important roles in the periodicity and control of molting timing. Mutants on these genes display disruptions in seam cell fate, causing defects on the time in which molting occurs and the number of molts happening during life, and resulting in precocious or retarded developmental phenotypes.16,23,24 When analyzing the expression profiles of these three developmental genes on both control and infected nematodes (Figure 5C), we found that their temporal expression was significantly altered in infected animals in comparison to control ones. In the three cases the effect was due to an overall difference among both temporal expression patterns, although of small magnitude (tests of infection status by hpi interaction, χ2 ≥ 31.740, 11 d.f., p ≤ 0.001, ηP2 ≤ 0.019). In the case of lin-42 and nhr-23, additional small yet significant effects of infection per se were also observed (tests of infection status, χ2 ≥ 6.711, 1 d.f., p < 0.001, ηP2 ≤ 0.003), with the average level of expression being higher in infected nematodes (grand mean 272 ± 20 for lin-42 and 496 ± 18 for nhr-23) than in the corresponding controls (207 ± 15 for lin-42 and 402 ± 15 for nhr-23). Expression profiles for the whole panel of developmentally related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Figure 5B) are shown in Figure S1. Altogether, these observations suggest that OrV infection indeed disrupts key aspects of nematode developmental programming.

Virus infection shortens the L1 intermolt and advances the first molt but extends the L2 intermolt, ultimately resynchronizing developmental timing

To investigate the specific effects of OrV infection on C. elegans developmental timing, animals with the reporter sevIs1[Psur-5::luc+::gfp] (constitutively expressing luciferase) were inoculated at 0 and 6 hph. Bioluminescence assays were then performed to monitor development and molting.54 Figure 6A shows an illustrative example of luminescence dynamics along larval development, where decreases in signal intensity correspond to molt phases. Focusing first on intermolt phases (Figure 6C), their duration is affected by the infection status (significant overall effect yet of small in magnitude; Table S2, p = 0.040, ηP2 = 0.015) and the inoculation time (significant effect of large magnitude; Table S2, p < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.591). Remarkably, 0 hph-inoculated nematodes exhibited a shortened L1 intermolt (I1) (15 ± 3 h) and initiated the first molt approximately 50 min earlier compared to control nematodes (16 ± 2 h). Conversely, these nematodes displayed a lengthened L2 intermolt (I2) (10 ± 1 h), extended by approximately 45 min compared to controls (9 ± 1 h). Later development continued at the same pace on both infected and control nematodes. In contrast, 6 hph-inoculated nematodes did not exhibit a shortened I1 (8 ± 1) when comparing with control animals (8 ± 1), but they did also lengthen the I2 (10 ± 1) compared to control nematodes (9 ± 1) for about 70.5 min. For nematodes inoculated on this specific time point, later developmental events did not readjust enough to resume normal developmental timing, making larval development last longer on inoculated animals in comparison with control ones (Figure 6B).

Regarding the molt phases (Figure 6D), their duration was not affected by either infection or inoculation time (Table S3, p ≥ 0.079, ηP2 ≤ 0.011). All molts lasted approximately the same time on both infected and control nematodes. However, the final molt phase was significantly larger (2.8 ± 0.2 h) than the previous three (average of 1.8 ± 0.4 h) regardless whether animals were infected or not.

Figure 6E shows the duration from the end of the last molt (M4) to reaching sexual maturity and production of the earliest progeny. A GLM analysis (Normal distribution, identity link-function; infection status and inoculation time as orthogonal fix factors) revealed a significant, yet minor, effect of infection (χ2 = 8.783, 1 d.f., p = 0.003, ηP2 = 0.003). Infected animals reached sexual maturity ∼36 ± 8 min sooner after M4 than non-infected animals (21.8 ± 0.1 h vs. 22.4 ± 0.1 h).

Finally, Figure 6F illustrates the duration from the first intermolt phase and the time of sexual maturity. These data were fitted to the GLM described in the previous paragraph. As shown above, the total duration of larval development does not depend on the infection status (χ2 = 0.233, 1 d.f., p = 0.629), as the initial delay observed in infected animals is later on compensated. However, a highly significant effect, of very large magnitude, of the inoculation time has been observed (χ2 = 246.324, 1 d.f., p < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.579), with animals infected at 0 hph reaching sexual maturity in 74.6 ± 0.3 h, while those infected at 6 hph take 67.1 ± 0.3 h.

Altogether, these results demonstrate that OrV infection transiently disrupts early larval timing but is later compensated for, resulting in synchronized overall development.

Discussion

In this work, we have explored the interplay between OrV infection and C. elegans larval development, focusing primarily on (i) the role of molting on OrV replicative dynamics and (ii) the impact of OrV infection on C. elegans developmental timing. Regarding the first aspect, earlier work demonstrated that OrV viral dynamics include a phase of exponential increase on viral load and a phase of clearance of the virus, which coincides at some extent with the initiation of the first molting period.47 Here, we have observed that OrV maintains the ability to replicate inside intestinal cells even when the nematode is going through a molting stage. This suggests that the observed viral dynamics, particularly the decay on viral load at ∼12 hpi, must be explained by other factors other than entering the molting periods, as entering molting is not impairing the virus to continue replicating, and the initiation of molting does not always lead to a decrease on viral load (as nematodes inoculated at 6 hph did not start a decay on viral titers until hours later of the end of the molting stage). However, this decay does not seem to be a direct consequence of the action of RNAi or viral uridylation. In addition, our results highlighted that infecting the animals near to the beginning of a molting phase increased viral load levels to a much higher extent and for a much longer period than infecting newly hatched nematodes. We obtained the same results after performing a smiFISH experiment, as the number of nematodes with fluorescent viral signal was higher on 6 hph inoculated nematodes in comparison to 0 hph inoculated ones, as were the number of infected intestinal cells. These results suggest that the animal’s capacity to control viral replication may be altered during certain developmental phases, as nematodes lose control in restricting virus takeover and need more time and effort to clear the infection. These observations highlight the importance of considering the resource allocation principle, which states that trade-offs arise between competing functions because limited resources must be devoted to one function or another.48,49 This could imply that many of the cell’s resources would not be available to be redirected toward immunity if the animal is expending efforts on exponentially growing, resulting in altered antiviral responses during developmental transitions.

Our study also points out that the lumen of 6 hph inoculated nematodes shows a much higher percentage of viral signal than the one found on the lumen of 0 hph inoculated ones. Molting is a highly dynamic process in which many physiological aspects of the animal are remodeled. Because a new cuticle must be synthetized, cellular trafficking is a fundamental aspect during molting, including the recycling of components like sterols.15 The intestine is thought to play an important role on sterol uptake, with LET-767 being an important intestinal steroid dehydrogenase in processes related with molting and development.53 let-767 is one of the DEGs in response to infection found in our study (Figure 4B). This highly dynamic cellular trafficking environment taking place during developmental transitions could explain the higher luminal viral signal found in animals infected near a molting stage, and the higher number of infected intestinal cells found on these animals in comparison to the one found on newly hatched nematodes, as the virus could potentially have more opportunities to enter and exit the intestinal cells. This idea also aligns with the fact that anterior intestinal cells (the ones that are mostly infected upon OrV infection) have a higher density of rough endoplasmic reticulum and secretory vesicles than posterior intestinal cells do.55,56 This could also enhance the chances of the virus to enter and exit the anterior intestinal cells in comparison with the posterior ones (which are mostly not infected). As OrV exits intestinal cells in a nonlytic manner43 (likely through exocytosis; see Figure 7 in Castiglioni et al.47), and considering the highly vesicular transport happening throughout the nematode’s development, it is possible that OrV could sequester cellular resources including vesicular components, thus disrupting developmental timing and adding another possible layer connecting viral infection and development on this animal system.

Turning to our second objective, we detected a desynchrony in developmental gene expression in infected vs. uninfected nematodes. Notably, the transcriptional profiles of critical regulators of molting lin-42 and let-7 diverged in infected nematodes, and various other developmental genes toggled on or off at different times in the presence of infection, especially early on. In newly hatched infected nematodes, this manifested as an accelerated start to the first molt. Interestingly, prior reports indicate that C. elegans may trigger a sleep-like state via antimicrobial peptides acting on the same neurons that govern molting-associated quiescence, likely to bolster survival.57 Antimicrobial effectors have also been shown to be differentially expressed in response to OrV infection.47,58 It has been also suggested that sleep can be induced from many, perhaps all tissues, that experience energetic stress,59 including the intestine (which is the site of infection of OrV). OrV infection is associated with a decrease in ATP levels,60 and the display of a quiescent behavior, which has been described to also increase survival.59,60 The nematode’s strategy to accelerate the beginning of molting could then be a way of inducing sleep to better defend itself from OrV infection. However, this hypothesis needs to be carefully tested. We observed that infected nematodes lengthened the L2 intermolt and resumed normal development to perfectly match the rest of developmental events relative to control non-infected nematodes. This phenomenon is reminiscent of “temporal scaling,” whereby the system recalculates individual developmental events to match the overall timeline despite environmental disruptions.61

Conclusions

We demonstrated that OrV maintains the ability to replicate inside intestinal cells even when the nematode is going through a molting stage and concluded that the decay on viral load at ∼12 hpi must be explained by other factors other than entering the molting periods. Our results suggest that developmental timing affects the nematode’s antiviral capacity, though the specific mechanisms remain to be determined.

We detected a desynchrony in developmental gene expression in infected nematodes. This disruption manifests phenotypically as an accelerated onset of the first molt and a compensatory extension of the subsequent larval stage, ultimately preserving overall developmental timing. This phenomenon resembles temporal scaling, where developmental systems recalibrate to maintain synchrony despite environmental or physiological perturbations.

In summary, this work advances our understanding of OrV dynamics in C. elegans and highlights how developmental processes and immune competence are intertwined.

Limitations of the study

Several aspects of our experimental design warrant further investigation. Firstly, we characterized viral dynamics at two strategically chosen inoculation times (0 and 6 hph) spanning the first molt. A finer temporal resolution could reveal additional windows of altered susceptibility. Secondly, while we demonstrate that OrV infection induces temporal scaling, the molecular mechanisms remain undefined. Genetic dissection using molting mutants would be valuable, though interpretation may be complicated by pleiotropic developmental defects in such strains. Third, we identified that the viral-load decline at ∼12 hpi occurs independently of drh-1 and cid-1, yet the actual driver of this clearance remains unknown. Fourth, our experiments used a single viral isolate (OrV JUv1580_vlc) in the N2 genetic background at 20°C. Natural variation in virus and host as well as temperature effects on both development and viral replication, which may reveal additional layers of complexity. Finally, we focused on larval development; whether similar virus-development interactions occur during embryogenesis or affect post-larval life history traits remain unexplored. These open questions provide clear directions for future mechanistic studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

C. elegans strains, maintenance and synchronization

All experiments regarding the characterization of OrV infection dynamics were done using the ERT54 (jyIs8[Ppals-5p::GFP; myo-2p::mCherry]X) strain, which is derived from the N2 Bristol and carries a fluorescent reporter for the activation of the IPR.39 Mutant strains are SX2635 (drh-1(ok3495)IV; mjIs228[myo-2::mcherry::unc54; lys-3p::eGFP::tbb-2]?) and tm936 (cid-1(tm936)III). Bioluminescence assays were done using the MRS387 (sevls1[Psur-5::luc+::gfp]X) strain.62 Strains were maintained at 20 °C on nematode growth medium (NGM) plates seeded with Escherichia coli OP50 as food source.63,64 For all experiments, animals were synchronized by carefully washing egg-containing plates with M9 buffer (0.22 M KH2PO4, 0.42 M Na2HPO4, 0.85 M NaCl, 1 mM MgSO4) to eliminate adult nematodes and larvae and retain only eggs on the plates. After a 1 h interval, plates were washed again with M9 buffer to gather the larvae that had hatched during that time. Successive 1 h interval washing rounds were done in case more animals were needed.

Method details

OrV stock, quantification, and inoculation procedure

SX2635 animals in N2 background, or JU2624 ([myo-2::mcherry::unc54; lys-3p::eGFP::tbb-2]IV) animals in JU1580 background were used for viral stock preparation. Nematodes from either strain were soaked into a 500 μL aliquot containing viral stocks for 1 h. The volume was then divided among ∼30, 9 cm NGM plates and nematodes were grown at 20 °C for around one week or less, until plates ran out of food. Animals were then washed with 4.5 mL of M9 buffer each 3 or 4 plates and the volume was collected in 15 mL tubes. Nematodes were vortexed and centrifuged at 1350 rpm for 2 min. Supernatant was recovered and centrifuged twice at 14,000 rpm for 5 min to eliminate any bacterial contamination. Liquid supernatant was then filtered through a 0.2 μm syringe filter and distributed in 500 μL aliquots for storage at −80 °C.

For viral stock quantification, viral RNA was extracted using the Viral RNA Isolation kit (NYZ Tech). Stock was quantified by RT-qPCR, using RNA2 as target. The primers used were oVG15_RNA2_qPCR_2_F and oVG16_RNA2_qPCR_2_R (Table S4). A standard curve method was used for quantification. Six 1/10 serial dilutions were used for the standard curve starting with a concentration of 1.25×109 molecules/μL. For the pristine RNA used to make the standard curve, cDNA of OrV was obtained using Accuscript High Fidelity Reverse Transcriptase (Agilent) and reverse primers at the 3′ end of the virus. Approximately 1000 bp of the 3′ end of RNA2 were amplified using forward primers containing 20 bp coding the T7 promoter and DreamTaq DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher). The PCR products were gel purified using MSB Spin PCRapace (Invitek Diagnostics) and an in vitro transcription was performed using T7 Polymerase (Merck). The remaining DNA was then degraded using DNAse I (Life Technologies). RNA concentration was determined by NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher) and the number of molecules per μL was determined using the online tool EndMemo RNA Copy Number Calculator (https://www.endmemo.com/bio/dnacopynum.php). The primers used were oVG7_OrV_RNA2_3′_F, and oVG8_OrV_RNA2_3′_R targeting RNA2 (Table S4).

For the experiments regarding the study of viral dynamics, 6 cm NGM plates were inoculated with 60 μL of viral stock of a concentration of 4.4×107 molecules/μL of OrV by pipetting the viral stock on top of the bacterial lawn containing the nematodes. For samples that were going to be collected up to 12 h post-hatching (hph), 700 nematodes per plate were inoculated. From 12 hph until 24 hph, 500 nematodes per plate were inoculated. A minimum of three replicates were done for each timepoint.

For the bioluminescence assay, after synchronization nematodes were washed and transferred to empty plates containing M9 buffer liquid. Nematodes were pipetted one by one (on a volume of ∼2 μL) and placed on 96-well plates. One nematode was placed on each well. Ten μL of viral stock were added into each well. Four replicates were done for these experiments, each replicate being considerate as an entire 96-well plate. On each plate, a maximum of 24 nematodes were transferred for each condition (virus-inoculated nematodes at 0 hph and 6 hph, and control non-inoculated nematodes placed at 0 hph and 6 hph).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Virus-inoculated and control nematodes were washed with 1600 μL of phosphate-buffered saline 0.05% Tween (PBST) at the designated times. Nematodes were centrifuged at 1350 rpm for 2 min and the supernatant was discarded. Two additional rounds of washing were performed for each sample using ∼620 μL of PBST. Samples were then quick-frozen in liquid N2 and stored at −80 °C until extraction procedure was initiated.

Five hundred μL of Trizol (Invitrogen) were added to the nematode pellet containing a volume of ∼100 μL of PBST. Samples were then vortexed, and five cycles of freeze-thawing were performed (from liquid N2 to 37 °C). Samples could be frozen again after this step until extraction protocol was continued. Five rounds of 30 s of vortex followed by 30 s of rest were then performed. One hundred μL of chloroform were added to the aliquots, tubes were shaken for 15 s, and then let rest for 2 min at room temperature. Samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 g at 4 °C. The top layer containing the RNA was recovered and mixed with the same volume of 100% ethanol. Samples were then loaded into RNA Clean & Concentrator columns (Zymo Research) and the rest of the protocol was followed according to manufacturer instructions.

RT-qPCRs were performed using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on an ABI StepOne Plus Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Ten ng of total RNA per sample were loaded, and a standard curve method was used for OrV quantifications (as described for viral stock quantification). The primers used were the same as the ones used for viral stock quantification (Table S4).

smiFISH

smiFISH protocol was adapted from previous studies.65,66 Briefly, virus-inoculated, and control nematodes were washed with 1600 μL of PBST and centrifuged for 2 min at 1350 rpm, and supernatant was discarded. Three additional rounds of washing were performed for each sample using ∼620 μL of PBST. Eight hundred μL of Bouins fixation mix (400 μL Bouins Fix, 400 μL methanol, 10 μL β-mercaptoethanol) were then added to the samples, which were later incubated at room temperature for 30 min in a rotatory shaker. Samples were then quick-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80 °C until continuation of the protocol.

To discard luminal viral signals were not an artifact of Bouins fixation, a parallel experiment was performed using paraformaldehyde (PFA) fixation. Briefly, virus-inoculated nematodes were washed with PBST as described above and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After removing the fixative solution, the pellet was washed thrice with 1× PBST, resuspended in 70% ethanol and stored at 4 °C overnight. The permeabilized worms were then washed thrice with 1× PBST to remove residual ethanol and once with 2× SSC buffer before hybridization with OrV probes, as described below. PFA fixation replicated the observation of luminal viral signal under the same infection conditions, confirming this is not a fixation artifact (Figure S2).

The day prior to the microscopy assay, samples were gently shaken at 4 °C in a rotatory shaker for 30 min and washed 4 times with borate Triton solution (20 mM H3BO3, 10 mM NaOH, 0.5% Triton), and 5 times with borate Triton β-mercaptoethanol solution (20 mM H3BO3, 10 mM NaOH, 0.5% Triton, 2% β-mercaptoethanol), leaving the borate Triton β-mercaptoethanol incubate for at least 1 h between each wash in the last three washes. Samples were then incubated for 5 min with wash buffer A (1 mL Stellaris RNA FISH Wash Buffer A, 1 mL deionized formamide and 3 mL H2O). Forty probes against OrV were designed using Oligostan65; 22 against RNA1, and 18 against RNA2 (Table S4). The probes were annealed as follows: 2 μL of 0.83 μM probe set, 1 μL of 50 μM fluorescent labeled amplification product (FLAP) label, 1 μL NEB3, and 6 μL of diethylpyrocarbonate-treated H2O were incubated at 85 °C for 3 min, 65 °C for 3 min, and 25 °C for 5 min. The FLAP labels contained CAL Fluor 610 or Quasar 670 modifications at both the 5′ and 3′ ends of the sequence 5′-AATGCATGTCGACGAGGTCCGAGTGT-3’ (Biosearch Technologies). One μL of annealed probe solution targeting RNA1 and 1 μL of annealed probe solution targeting RNA2 were then mixed with 98 μL of hybridization buffer (100 μL Stellaris smiFISH hybridization buffer, 25 μL deionized formamide). A volume of 100 μL of hybridization buffer containing the smiFISH probes were then added to the samples and the samples were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a rotary shaker. The following day, samples were washed with Wash Buffer A2 (1 mL of Stellaris RNA FISH Wash Buffer A, and 4 mL of H2O), incubated for 30 min in Wash Buffer A2 at 37 °C, and then incubated for 30 min in Wash Buffer A2 containing 25 ng of 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) at 37 °C. Samples were then incubated for 5 min in Stellaris RNA FISH Wash Buffer B, centrifuged, and resuspended in a small volume of Stellaris RNA FISH Wash Buffer B. A quantity of 0.1 ng of DAPI was added to each sample, and samples were mounted on slides using N-propyl gallate mounting medium.

Samples were imaged using Leica DMi8 microscope with Leica DFC9000 GTC scientific complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera and objective HC PL APO 40×/0.95 CORR PH2. Images were analyzed and processed using ImageJ (Fiji).67 smiFISH viral fluorescent signal was used to determine the percentage of infected nematodes, the number of infected intestinal cells on each nematode, and the spatial localization of the viral fluorescent signal. Samples were collected at 12 hpi after inoculating nematodes at 0 and 6 hph. Five replicates were done for each condition, 40 animals per replicate observed.

Bioluminescence assay and luminometry data analysis

C. elegans strain MRS387 constitutively and ubiquitously expresses the enzyme luciferase. Larvae form a plug of extracellular material in the buccal cavity during molting periods, ceasing pharyngeal pumping.58 This allows for a detailed quantification of molting time using the luciferase assay, since non-feeding individuals do not ingest luciferin, resulting in the cessation of light production and a decrease in the luminometry signal. Single synchronized L1 animals were transferred into a well of a white, flat bottom, 96-well plate by manual picking as previously described. Each well contained 160 μL of NGM mixed with 5 μL of E. coli OP50, and 10 μL of 1 mM D-Luciferin (plus 10 μL of OrV stock for the infected nematodes). Plates were sealed with a Breathe Easier gas-permeable cover (Diversified Biotech) prior to their introduction on the microplate luminometer. Luminescence was read for 1 s, at 5 min intervals, for a period of ∼100 h with a Berthold Centro LB960 XS3 (Berthold Technologies). Experiments were performed at 20 °C inside a Panasonic MIR-154 temperature-controlled incubators (PHC Europe BV). Temperature was monitored using HOBO Data Loggers (Onset).

The raw data from the luminometer was analyzed to detect the beginning and end of the molts as follows. The raw data was trend-corrected dividing by a centered moving average. The moving average was calculated for the duration of 10 h (approximated duration of each at 20 °C). To determine the timing of the molts, the trend-corrected data was converted to binary, using 75% of the value of the moving average as a threshold. The data was then evaluated for onset and offset of molting by detecting the transitions in the binarized data. Transitions from 1 to 0 corresponded to the onset of a molt, and transitions from 0 to 1 corresponded to the offset of a molt. To prevent the noise in the binarized data product of the variability introduced by the luminometry signal, the following rule was applied: onsets must be followed by “0” for at least 1 h, and offsets must be preceded by “0” for at least 1 h. The duration of each intermolt and molt stage, the time from the end of the last molt until the start in the production of progeny (characterized by an exponential increase in the luminometry signal), as well as the duration of development from the first intermolt until the production of progeny, were then calculated. Plots were done using ggplot268 in R version 4.2.069 under RStudio version 2022.7.1.554 (Posit Software).

Identification of genes with a switch-like expression pattern

DEGs previously identified (raw data from NCBI SRA PRJNA1037048) as genes with a differential gene expression profile (likelihood ratio test) between OrV infected and noninfected were used here.47 The gene expression counts of the uninfected animals were normalized to the maximum value per gene. Hierarchical clustering of transcriptional profiles along larval development was then applied using Euclidean distance. Visual exploration of all the clusters allowed us to identify one with a clear oscillatory expression pattern.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Number of animals included per technical replicate are indicated in the text when describing each experiment. Otherwise indicated, mean ± 1 SD will be reported.

Generalized linear models (GLM) were fitted to the different datasets by maximum likelihood using SPSS version 30.0.0.0 (IBM). Specific model designs are introduced in the text as necessary. Bonferroni post hoc tests were used to pairwise comparisons among factor values. The magnitude of the effects was evaluated using the ηP2 statistic (proportion of the total variability in the variable attributable to each factor in the model; conventionally, values of ηP2 ≥ 0.15 are considered as large effects). Additional statistical tests will be introduced as needed.
