Sonochemical intercalation and polymerization of PEDOT:PSS into ammonium vanadate nanofibers for high-power zinc-ion batteries
Authors: Se Hun Lee, Jeeseop Lee, Myung Sik Choi, Ok Sung Jeon, Jeeyoung Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zn-ion batteries(ZIBs) are promising candidates for next-generation energy storage systems due to their high stability, low cost and eco-friendliness. While vanadate is widely used as cathode materials in ZIBs, vanadate-based cathodes have been considered impractical for high-rate operation because of their low electrical conductivity, slow Zn ion diffusion kinetics, and the unsustainability of repetitive insertion and extraction at high current densities. In this study, PEDOT:PSS was successfully intercalated into the interlayers of ammonium vanadate nanofibers (EP-AVNF) using the sonochemical method, resulting in high performance for ZIBs with excellent rate capability and stability. Acoustic cavitation occurring under ultrasonic irradiation simultaneously induced oxolation and olation reactions leading to the growth of vanadate nanofibers, as well as in situ oxidative polymerization and interlayer intercalation of PEDOT:PSS, outcomes that were not obtained under silent (stirring-only) conditions. The presence of intercalated PEDOT:PSS not only efficiently expanded the structural interlayer of vanadate but also enhanced its electrical conductivity. Additionally, chemical bonding with vanadate accompanied the partial reduction of V5+ and the formation of vacancy-related defects. ZIBs utilizing the EP-AVNF cathode exhibited significantly improved electrochemical performance compared to those with pristine AVNF and PEDOT-intercalated AVNF. The EP-AVNF cathode demonstrated superior rate capability of 255mAh g−1 even at high current densities of 20 A g−1. Moreover, it achieved a capacity retention of 99.2 % after 1,000 cycles at 10 A g−1, along with high energy and power densities. This study indicates that the simultaneous intercalation and polymerization of PEDOT:PSS within the AVNF interlayer using the sonochemical method optimizes both structural and electrical characteristics with high efficiency, providing an efficient route for achieving high-rate and long-cycle ZIBs.

Introduction

Vanadate has garnered attention as a cathode material for Zn-ion batteries (ZIBs). [1], [2], [3], [4], [5] The one-dimensional morphology of vanadate nanofibers offers a structure favorable for electron hopping, which contributes to enhanced electrical conductivity. In addition, the pre-intercalated cationic species (H+, NH4+, K+, and Na+) that constitute the crystal framework play a decisive role in the intercalation and de-intercalation of Zn2+ ions, which serve as the charge carriers in ZIBs. Also, the interlayer distance of these vanadate nanofibers is primarily determined by the type of ionic species constituting the crystal lattice such as proton (H+), [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11] ammonium (NH4+), [12], [13], [14], [15], [16] potassium (K+) [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22] and sodium (Na+). [23], [24], [25], [26], [27] Additionally, organic molecules or conducting polymers can be intercalated into the vanadate crystal layers to further expand this interlayer distance. [28] Therefore, the simultaneous intercalation of ionic species and organic materials serves as a strategy to address two critical challenges: ensuring effective diffusion pathways for Zn2+ ions and enhancing structural stability.

Among the ionic species, ammonium vanadate nanofibers (AVNF) ((NH4)2V6O16·1.5H2O) demonstrate that the larger ionic radius of NH4+ (∼1.48 Å) compared to K+ (∼1.38 Å) and Na+ (∼1.02 Å) results in the largest interlayer distance. Strategies aimed at incorporating organic materials derived from AVNF are expected to be particularly effective. Our research group has previously synthesized AVNF in short duration using sonochemical method. [29], [30], [31] In the same sonochemical environment, we also intercalated and polymerized the conducting polymer PEDOT into the crystal layers to fabricate composite material (E-AVNF). This process successfully achieved both expansion of interlayer distance and enhancement of electrical conductivity. However, the PEDOT obtained through an oxidation polymerization mechanism that relies solely on the oxidative capacity of vanadates is limited in electrical conductivity. It constrains its rate capability at high current densities.

To address these limitations, this study emphasizes the concept of secondary doping of PEDOT with polystyrene sulfonate (PSS). As a strong anionic dopant, PSS induces conformational changes in the PEDOT chains and enhances charge transport between the chains. It results in a significant increase in electrical conductivity. Furthermore, PSS may enhance the interface compatibility with aqueous electrolytes due to the strong hydrophilicity of the sulfonic acid group (−SO3H), [32] further facilitating the diffusion of Zn2+ ions across the interlayer. Thus, the intercalation of PEDOT:PSS serves not only to enhance conductivity but also provides a multifaceted approach to enhance electrochemical performance by combining the expanded interlayer distance with improved electrolyte affinity.

In this study, we successfully synthesized the PEDOT:PSS-intercalated ammonium vanadate nanofibers (EP-AVNF) for the first time through a sonochemical synthesis method and evaluated their potential as cathode materials for Zn-ion batteries. The expanded interlayer distance and additional improved electrical conductivity from secondary doping with PSS achieved both the interlayer diffusion dynamics of Zn2+ and the rates of charge transfer. Consequently, EP-AVNF exhibited considerably enhanced electrochemical performance compared to AVNF and E-AVNF, achieving unprecedented rate capabilities that have not been reported for conventional vanadate nanofiber-based cathode materials. These findings validate the strategic alteration of interlayer spacing through ionic species and organic material intercalation as effective means for enhancing performance of Zn-ion battery cathodes. They also highlight the secondary doping strategy of conducting polymers as a promising direction for developing next-generation high-performance Zn-ion batteries.

Experimental

Materials

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Vanadium pentoxide (V2O5, 98 %), ammonium persulfate (APS, (NH4)2S2O8, 98 %), 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT, 97 %), poly(styrene sulfonate) (PSS), and zinc trifluoromethanesulfonate (Zn(CF3SO3)2, 98 %) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich and employed as received without additional purification. Conductive carbon (Super P, TIMCAL Graphite & Carbon), poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF, Mw = 534,000, Sigma-Aldrich), and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) were used in the electrode-fabrication step. Deionized (DI) water was adopted as the reaction medium throughout this work.

Sonochemical preparation of AVNF, E-AVNF, and EP-AVNF

The three cathode powders were obtained through a single-step probe-sonication route conducted at ambient pressure. For the pristine AVNF, V2O5 (0.4 g, 2.2 mmol) and APS (0.2 g, 0.88 mmol) were dispersed together in 100 mL of DI water, and the suspension was irradiated for 30 min, during which the initially yellow dispersion turned deep red, signalling the assembly of the ammonium-stabilized vanadate framework. To prepare E-AVNF, EDOT (94 µL, 0.88 mmol) was introduced into the as-formed AVNF suspension and the mixture was sonicated for a further 3 h; completion of the in-situ polymerization was accompanied by a colour change from red to dark green. EP-AVNF was synthesized by co-feeding an aqueous PSS (1.76 mmol) solution together with EDOT (0.88 mmol) into the AVNF suspension, followed by sonication for 3 h under conditions identical to those used for E-AVNF. The overall molar feed ratios were EDOT: V2O5 = 0.4: 1 and PSS: EDOT = 2: 1 on a monomer-unit basis (Table S1). No acid, base, or buffer was added at any stage; the reaction therefore proceeded in the intrinsically mildly acidic medium (pH ≈ 2–3) generated by the hydrolysis of APS and the proton-releasing condensation of the vanadate species. After the reaction, each product was recovered by repeated centrifugation/rinsing with ethanol and DI water and then dried at 40 °C overnight. For comparison, a pure PEDOT powder was also synthesized under identical sonication conditions using APS (0.2 g) and EDOT (94 µL, 0.88 mmol) in 100 mL of DI water without the V2O5 precursor, and was recovered by the same washing and drying procedure.

Sonication was delivered by a probe-type ultrasonic processor (Vibra-Cell, Sonics & Materials, USA) operating at a fixed frequency of 20 kHz with a maximum rated output of 750 W. The frequency (20 kHz) and probe geometry were fixed by the instrument. The processor was fitted with a 13 mm (0.5 in) Ti–6Al–4 V titanium-alloy probe (139 mm length, replaceable tip) coupled through a CV334 converter. The probe was immersed about 15 mm below the liquid surface in a 100 mL glass vessel (inner diameter ≈ 50 mm) and operated at 60 % amplitude in a pulsed mode of 2 s on / 2 s off (50 % duty cycle). These settings were selected during exploratory condition screening as mild conditions that reliably drive the reaction to completion within 3 h, the amplitude acting as a kinetic control factor in this system, while the pulsed operation was adopted for thermal management during the extended sonication. The pulsed operation was adopted to mitigate heat accumulation during the extended sonication; no external cooling bath was used. Under these conditions, the bulk temperature rose from room temperature and stabilized at approximately 70 °C within about 40 min, remaining at this self-limiting steady state for the remainder of the reaction, with only slight evaporative loss of the medium over 3 h. The acoustic power actually transferred to the medium was quantified by calorimetry from the initial slope of the temperature–time curve (P = m·c_p·(dT/dt)), giving a delivered power of approximately 25 W and a power density of about 0.25 W mL−1. A reference batch was also produced under silent conditions, in which an identical feed was magnetically stirred for the same period without ultrasonic irradiation (EP-AVNF-silent). A series of additional control batches was prepared under otherwise identical sonication conditions: (i) the as-formed AVNF suspension was sonicated for a further 3 h without EDOT/PSS, giving a total acoustic exposure of 4 h; (ii) an aqueous EDOT/PSS mixture was sonicated for 30 min without V2O5 or APS; (iii) EDOT (94 µL, 0.88 mmol) was sonicated with APS (0.2 g) for 1 h without V2O5; (iv) V2O5 (0.4 g) alone was sonicated for 1 h; and (v) for the sequential post-addition control, EDOT was introduced after the completion of AVNF formation and the mixture was sonicated for a further 3.5h.

Characterization

The fibre morphology was inspected by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, NOVA NANO SEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F); for the latter, powders were redispersed in acetone and drop-cast onto carbon-coated grids. Elemental distribution was mapped by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Phase and interlayer information was extracted from X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8-Discover) using Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) scanned from 5° to 40°. Functional groups were identified by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Nicolet iS10) over 4000 – 400 cm−1. The intercalated organic and ionic content was estimated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Perkin-Elmer TGA7) under flowing N2, ramping to 600 °C at 10 °C min−1. Surface oxidation states and bonding were resolved by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and unpaired-spin (V4+/polaron) signals were recorded at room temperature by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, JEOL FA-200). The electrical conductivities of V2O5, AVNF, PEDOT, E-AVNF, and EP-AVNF were measured by the four-point probe method (Keithley 2400 SourceMeter, Keithley Instruments, USA) at room temperature on dense pellets prepared by cold pressing the powders at 10 MPa; the pellet thicknesses (525, 622, and 640 µm for AVNF, E-AVNF, and EP-AVNF, respectively) were measured with a digital micrometer and used for the conductivity calculation.

Electrochemical measurement

The cathode slurries were formulated by blending the active material (AVNF, E-AVNF, or EP-AVNF), Super P, and PVDF in a 7:2:1 mass ratio with NMP, and the homogeneous paste was cast onto 20 µm stainless-steel foil. The coated foils were vacuum-dried at 110 °C for 12 h, giving an active loading of roughly 1–2 mg cm−2. CR2032 coin cells were built in an open-air environment with a glass-fibre membrane (Whatman GF/A) as the separator and Zn foil as the counter/reference electrode, using 2.58 M Zn(CF3SO3)2 as the aqueous electrolyte. Impedance spectra were acquired on a ZIVE SP1 analyzer (WonA Tech, Korea) across 106–10-1 Hz. Galvanostatic cycling and rate tests were run on a WBCS 3,000 cycler (WonA Tech, Korea) at 25 °C, with current densities and specific capacities normalized to the active-material mass alone. Cyclic voltammograms were collected on the ZIVE SP1 over 0.2–1.8 V (vs. Zn2+/Zn) at 25 °C at several sweep rates.

Results and discussion

Mechanism of growth, intercalation, and polymerization under acoustic cavitation

The simultaneous growth, polymerization, and intercalation realized in a single sonication step originate from the transient extremes created by acoustic cavitation. (Fig. 1) Under 20 kHz sonication, gas nuclei dissolved in an aqueous medium grow and collapse until they reach an unstable size, undergoing successive dilution-compression cycles. Each collapse instantaneously concentrates acoustic energy at a local high-temperature point, causing temperatures to skyrocket to thousands of Kelvin and pressures to hundreds of atmospheres, followed by rapid cooling at rates exceeding 109 K s−1. Three reaction zones coexist around the collapsing bubbles: the gas nucleus, where water is sonicated to decompose into highly reactive ·OH and ·H species; the superheated interfacial shell; and the surrounding liquid portion. The accompanying micro-jets, shock waves, and acoustic flow generate intense local mixing and shear forces that far exceed those attainable by conventional stirring, and this combination of chemical activation and mechanical stirring drives the synthesis. In the first step, these conditions rapidly ionize sparingly soluble V2O5 to generate vanadium (V) precursors. Hydrated vanadium species undergo spontaneous deprotonation and then condense via the classical polyoxovanadate pathway, which favors oleation and oxolation pathways that is, one-dimensional propagation to form a fibrous vanadate backbone. Ammonium cations released from APS occupy the interlayer gallery, stabilizing the layered V6O16 backbone and inducing preferential growth along a single axis to produce AVNF with a high aspect ratio as observed by SEM. In the presence of EDOT or EDOT/PSS pairs, the same cavitation field disperses the monomers and pushes them into the vanadate gallery. Repeated bubble collapse fragments and deaggregates the growing fibers, explaining the gradual shortening from AVNF to E-AVNF, and most notably to EP-AVNF.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of (a) the conventional sonication synthesis method based on acoustic cavitation and (b) the proposed one-step synthesis method for the preparation of EP-intercalated AVNF.

Schematic illustration of (a) the conventional sonication synthesis method based on acoustic cavitation and (b) the proposed one-step synthesis method for the preparation of EP-intercalated AVNF.

EDOT inserted within the confined interlayer space undergoes oxidative polymerization to become PEDOT. This reaction proceeds cooperatively through the vanadate host, persulfate initiator, and the oxidizing power of radical species generated by sonication; therefore, polymerization and insertion proceed simultaneously rather than sequentially. As the host provides electrons to the growing chain, some of the V5+ is reduced to V4+, which may be accompanied by the formation of vacancy-related defects, consistent with the increased V4+ content observed in the XPS spectra. The inserted chain simultaneously separates the interlayer space, which is consistent with the interlayer expansion observed in TEM and XRD.

The role of PSS is to act as a secondary dopant in this restricted polymerization process. As a strong anionic dopant, PSS modifies the shape of the PEDOT chain and converts polarons into delocalized bipolarons, thereby lowering the interchain charge transfer barrier. The broadened and attenuated EPR response of EP-AVNF compared to E-AVNF reflects this delocalization. The hydrophilic sulfonate groups also enhance the affinity of the complex for the aqueous electrolyte, allowing Zn2+ ions to easily access the interlayer channels. The attribution of these results to acoustic cavitation is further supported by the control experiment conducted under stirred conditions without sonication. When the same V2O5/APS mixture was simply stirred without sonication, no reaction was observed. The suspension retained its original color, and no vanadate fibers were formed, neither during the same duration used for sonication nor after continuous stirring for several days. This was further confirmed by the liquid-state FT-IR spectrum of the stirred mixture, (Fig. S1) which is a simple superposition of the bands of the individual unreacted starting materials and lacks any spectral signature of vanadate framework assembly or PEDOT formation. This indicates that the assembly, in situ polymerization, and co-insertion of the vanadate backbone do not proceed under ambient thermal and mechanical conditions on these timescales, and that the local extreme conditions caused by bubble collapse are responsible for initiating and completing the process within a few hours under the present unheated, ambient-pressure conditions. To further isolate the roles of the individual components, a series of control experiments was conducted. (Fig. S2) Prolonged sonication of the pre-formed AVNF without monomers, corresponding to a total acoustic exposure of 4 h that exceeds the total exposure of the composite syntheses, left the nanofiber morphology and the deep red suspension entirely unchanged, confirming that ultrasound alone does not alter the host structure. Sonication of an EDOT/PSS solution without the vanadate host or APS did not yield any recoverable PEDOT, producing only a slight darkening and turbidity attributable to marginal cavitation-initiated oligomerization and acoustic emulsification of the monomer. These results confirm that a chemical oxidant is required for the polymerization and that APS additionally serves as the ammonium source for the framework assembly. Furthermore, when EDOT was added after the completion of AVNF formation, the polymer was deposited on the fiber exterior and the (002) reflection shifted only marginally from 10.8° to 10.6°, corresponding to an interlayer gain of merely 0.015 nm, in sharp contrast to the pronounced expansion of approximately 0.23 nm achieved by the in-situ route. (Figs. S3 and S4) These controls demonstrate that the simultaneous growth, polymerization, and intercalation realized in the single-step sonochemical process is required to obtain the intercalated architecture.

Comparative characterization of AVNF, E-AVNF, and EP-AVNF

AVNFs were prepared by sonochemical method with APS. The ammonium ions within APS stabilize the layered V6O16 structure, where VO5 square pyramids share edges with VO6 octahedra. [33] The insertion of ammonium ions into the crystal offers pillar effect that mitigates potential structural collapse during Zn2+ insertion and extraction while promoting the preferential growth of continuous AVNF structures along a single axis during the sonochemical reaction. [34] Furthermore, in order to facilitate the movement of Zn2+ ions, the sonochemical process was conducted in the presence of EDOT to enable in-situ polymerization of PEDOT for obtaining E-AVNF. The EP-AVNF was ultimately synthesized by adding PSS as a secondary dopant to enhance the electrical conductivity and the affinity for the aqueous electrolyte.

The morphologies of those AVNF, E-AVNF, and EP-AVNF were analyzed via SEM. As confirmed in Fig. 2a, AVNF exhibited high aspect ratios with widths ranging from approximately 20 nm to 50 nm and lengths extending several micrometers, indicative of single-axis growth driven by the sonochemical process. In E-AVNF, while the fiber widths remained similar, noticeable decrease in length was observed while the length further decreased in EP-AVNF. (Fig. 2b) It indicates that the polymerized PEDOT could cover active sites such as V-O dangling bonds on the growing crystal surface, thus inhibiting axial growth. In the presence of PSS, stronger adsorption and steric hindrance effects with V-O species further reduce the aspect ratio. (Fig. 2c) [35] Such morphological changes imply that the polymer insertion expands the interlayer structure and enhances Zn2+ accessibility while resulting in shorter, more dispersed fibers, ultimately benefiting the dispersion of active materials and ion access upon electrode formation.Fig. 2Scanning electron microscopy(SEM) images of (a) AVNF, (b) E-AVNF and (c) EP-AVNF.

Scanning electron microscopy(SEM) images of (a) AVNF, (b) E-AVNF and (c) EP-AVNF.

TEM was conducted to closely examine the effects of fiber crystallinity on interlayer spacing and morphology. AVNF, E-AVNF, and EP-AVNF were confirmed to possess a well-defined fibrous. (Fig. S5) AVNF possessed a fiber width of approximately 25 nm with ammonium ions evenly distributed within the structure which established d-spacing of 0.91 nm. (Fig. 3a). Upon adding PEDOT, the fiber width decreased to 21 nm while the d-spacing increased to 1.03 nm. (Fig. 3b) Similar trends were noted in EP-AVNF, where the fiber width measured 17 nm and the d-spacing was confirmed to be 0.95 nm. (Fig. 3c) Notably, the d-spacing of EP-AVNF was slightly smaller than that of E-AVNF, although both remained substantially larger than that of AVNF. This is because the interlayer spacing is determined by the balance of electrostatic interactions within the gallery rather than by the molecular size of the guest alone. While the positively charged PEDOT chains act as spacers that prop the anionic vanadate layers apart, the densely anionic sulfonate groups of the co-intercalated PSS form ionic pairs and bridges with the PEDOT chains and the interlayer ammonium cations, serving as electrostatic cross-linkers that draw the adjacent layers toward each other. In addition, secondary doping by PSS converts the PEDOT chains from a coiled, benzoid-rich conformation into an extended, linear, quinoid-rich conformation, which packs flat and parallel to the layers with a smaller effective cross-section. These two effects account for the smaller net expansion of EP-AVNF, and the resulting electrostatically anchored PEDOT:PSS network functions as robust structural pillars within the retained Zn2+ diffusion pathway. XRD patterns further corroborated these observations which revealed the main peak of (002) plane in AVNF at 10.54°. (Fig. S6) [36] Upon the addition of PEDOT and PEDOT:PSS, the d-spacing of main peak widened with theta values downshifting to 8.09° and 8.25°, respectively. EDS mapping analysis of EP-AVNF provided insights into the uniform distribution of PEDOT and PSS throughout the V2O5 nanofibers. (Fig. 3d) Not only the core elements of V and O, but also the constituent elements of C, N and S for PEDOT and PSS exhibited homogenous dispersion. It indicated that PEDOT:PSS did not aggregate into bulk forms but was uniformly distributed within and around the V2O5 nanofibers. Such elemental distribution is particularly important when evaluating the role of secondary doping. TGA revealed the presence of ammonium cations, water molecules and conducting polymers within the vanadate nanofiber structure. In the TGA spectrum of AVNF, weight loss of 4.96 % occurred from around 50 °C to 200 °C due to the removal of the weakly bound water molecules. (Fig. 4b) [44] Further weight loss of 6.64 % until 350 °C was attributed to the removal of intercalated ammonium cations and coordinated water molecules. In contrast, E-AVNF exhibited weight loss of 9.33 % by 200 °C and additional 14.7 % from 200 °C to 450 °C, (Fig. 4c) attributed to the removal of weakly bound water, the combustion of ammonium ions within the crystalline structure as well as the combustion of PEDOT. [45] The elevated combustion temperature compared to conventional PEDOT may be due to the intercalated structure within the vanadate layers. EP-AVNF showed similar weight loss of 9.5 % for weakly bound water and significant reduction of 27.9 % from 200 °C to 450 °C compared to E-AVNF. This reflected the greater combustion of organic species contributed by the presence of PSS. (Fig. 4d).

XPS was employed to examine the impact of PEDOT and PEDOT:PSS intercalation on the chemical bonding within the vanadate structure. The S 2p spectrum confirmed the formation of peaks at 168.3 eV (S-O) and 164.8 eV, 163.5 eV (PEDOT peaks) indicating successful polymerization of PEDOT. (Fig. 5a) [46] The positively charged sulfur of PEDOT chemically bonds with the negatively charged oxygen of AVNF which induced intercalation of PEDOT within the vanadate structure. Additionally, PSS peaks at 169.5 eV and 167.7 eV confirmed successful polymerization during the secondary doping process. The V 2p spectrum revealed the presence of peaks at 516.2 eV, 517.2 eV, 523.1 eV, and 524.5 eV corresponding to V4+ 2p1/2, V5+ 2p1/2, V4+ 2p3/2 and V5+ 2p3/2, respectively. (Fig. 5b) [47] AVNF predominantly existed as V2O5 with the V5+ and V4+ as 93.9 % and 6.09 %. Upon intercalation of PEDOT and PEDOT:PSS, the V4+ content increased to 20.3 % and 17.4 %, respectively. The slightly lower V4+ fraction of EP-AVNF compared to E-AVNF reflects the polymerization chemistry: in the presence of PSS, the sulfonate groups stabilize the EDOT radical-cation intermediates and lower the effective oxidation barrier, so a larger share of the oxidation is carried by the persulfate-derived oxidant and a smaller electron burden falls on the vanadate lattice. Nevertheless, the V4+ fraction of both composites remains approximately three times that of pristine AVNF. To compare the chemical states of oxygen, the O 1 s spectrum was also observed. (Fig. 5c) lattice O, defect O and COO or SO bonds appeared at 530.1 eV, 531.2 eV and 532.1 eV, respectively. [48], [49] In the case of AVNF, lattice O constituted 71.7 % while defect O constituted 28.3 %. In contrast, with the addition of PEDOT:PSS, lattice O decreased to 57.9 % and defect O increased sharply to 42.1 %. In the N 1 s spectrum, the amounts of N in AVNF, E-AVNF, and EP-AVNF was observed at approximately 5.8 atom.%, 3.6 atom.%, and 2.6 atom.%, respectively, (Fig. S7) which confirmed the presence of ammonium within the crystal structure. These results suggest that, relative to pristine AVNF, polymer intercalation increases the fraction of reduced V4+ species together with vacancy-related defects. Ultimately, intercalation serves to both expand the interlayer distance and introduce mixed-valence character into the vanadate host.Fig. 5(a) S 2p, (b) V 2p and (c) O 1 s X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of AVNF, E-AVNF and EP-AVNF.

To confirm changes in electrical conductivity induced by intercalation, four-point probe measurements were conducted on cold-pressed pellets. (Table S1) The conductivity increased in the order of V2O5 (8.2 × 10-5 S cm−1) < AVNF (2.2 × 10-3 S cm−1) < PEDOT (4.0 × 10−3 S cm−1) < E-AVNF (3.5 × 10−2 S cm−1) < EP-AVNF (4.4 × 10−2 S cm−1), confirming that the secondarily doped EP-AVNF possesses the highest electrical conductivity among all samples. (Fig. 6a) Notably, both composites were approximately an order of magnitude more conductive than the pure PEDOT powder, indicating that the intercalated polymer forms an intimate, percolating conduction network with the vanadate host. Furthermore, the increase in conductivity from E-AVNF to EP-AVNF, despite the addition of intrinsically insulating PSS, indicates that PSS functions as a secondary dopant within the composite rather than as a passive insulating layer on the fiber surface. These enhanced electrical transport properties are further supported by the EPR results which provide insight into the variation of paramagnetic species and charge carriers associated with PEDOT incorporation. EPR measurements were conducted to further investigate the electronic structure evolution upon polymer intercalation. EPR allows identification of the magnetic field position at which unpaired electrons within vanadate materials undergo resonance (absorption) expressed as mT values. [50] For V2O5-based materials, the d-electrons present in V4+ are predominantly detected. [51] AVNF exhibited the highest intensity at 217 mT, (Fig. 6b) Upon addition of PEDOT, although the contribution from increased V4+ content is also included as evidenced by the XPS data, the intensity increased sharply by approximately 9-fold relative to AVNF, primarily due to the charge carriers (polarons) of PEDOT. [52] The simultaneous increase in V4+ species and PEDOT-derived polarons indicates the formation of additional electronic states within the composite structure which is consistent with the enhanced conductivity observed from four-point probe measurements. In contrast, EP-AVNF with PSS addition exhibited reduction in intensity accompanied by broadened linewidth. This reduction cannot be ascribed to a lower PEDOT content, since the two composites were synthesized with an identical EDOT feed and the TGA results show a greater organic combustion loss for EP-AVNF (27.9 %) than for E-AVNF (14.7 %), while the N 1 s analysis confirms a lower ammonium contribution in EP-AVNF. The intercalation of PSS led to further increase in polaron concentration which promoted greater overlap between polarons. [53] Consequently, the degree of conversion from polarons to bipolarons increased and PSS stabilized PEDOT that charge became delocalized across the entire chain and accelerated relaxation. This resulted in a significant decrease in EPR intensity and increase in linewidth. The normalized EPR integrated intensity and linewidth values were additionally calculated and summarized in Table S2 to quantitatively compare the changes in paramagnetic signals among the samples. Considering the XPS trends of the increase in V4+ content and the formation of PEDOT:PSS, these EPR results provide complementary evidence for the modification of electronic states and charge carrier behavior associated with the enhanced electrical conductivity.Fig. 6Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) curves of AVNF, E-AVNF and EP-AVNF.

Zn-ion batteries were constructed using AVNF, E-AVNF, and EP-AVNF as the cathodes with 2.58 M Zn(OTF)2 as the electrolyte and zinc metal as the anode to analyze the electrochemical changes induced by intercalation. Initially, the interfacial resistance of the cathodes was evaluated by EIS. (Fig. 7a) To quantitatively analyze the impedance response, the spectra were fitted using the equivalent circuit and corresponding fitting parameters summarized in Table S2. The fitted charge-transfer resistance decreased markedly from 259.9 Ω for AVNF to 122.1 Ω for E-AVNF and 39.54 Ω for EP-AVNF, confirming progressively improved interfacial charge-transfer kinetics after conducting-polymer intercalation. This trend is consistent with the directly measured electrical conductivities of the electrode materials summarized in Table S1, while the expanded vanadate interlayer facilitates Zn2+ transport. In addition, the Warburg coefficient decreased from 22.31 Ω s−1/2 for E-AVNF to 6.63 Ω s−1/2 for EP-AVNF. The lower Warburg impedance of EP-AVNF indicates reduced low-frequency ion-transport polarization and faster apparent Zn2+ transport kinetics. From a practical standpoint, the energy and time economy of the present route deserves note. On a delivered-energy basis, one batch consumes 25 W × 3.5 h ≈ 0.09 kWh per 100 mL at ambient pressure without external heating, and the bulk temperature remains at a self-limiting ∼ 70 °C throughout the reaction. By contrast, conventional hydrothermal syntheses of ammonium vanadate nanostructures typically require sealed autoclaves at 180–200 °C for 24–48 h, corresponding to several tens of kWh per batch including heating and cooling. The sonochemical route thus offers roughly two orders of magnitude lower energy input together with an order-of-magnitude shorter processing time. Nevertheless, the present probe-type configuration is subject to recognized limitations for scale-up, including the localization of the cavitation-active zone by acoustic attenuation, probe-tip erosion during extended operation, and the limited batch volume. Realistic pathways toward larger scales include flow-through sonoreactor cells operated in recirculation, multi-transducer reactor configurations, and continuous processing, for which the short intrinsic reaction time of this chemistry is well suited. [57].

In summary, EP-AVNF achieved superior rate capability, energy density, power density and long-term cycling stability through the simultaneous enhancement of electrical conductivity and expanded vanadate interlayer distance. As summarized in Table S3, current density of EP-AVNF delivers twice or higher than the highest rates reported in the compared studies, and the highest retention among the polymer-modified and cation-pillared vanadates compared, including PEDOT-based composites. [58], [59], [60], [61], [62], [63], [64] The straightforward sonochemical synthesis strategy presented in this study, which optimizes both the structural and electronic properties of vanadate, provides an effective route toward high-performance cathode materials for next-generation Zn-ion batteries.

Conclusions

In this study, cathode material for Zn-ion batteries consisting of PEDOT:PSS-intercalated ammonium vanadate nanofibers was successfully synthesized via a sonochemical method. The mechanism underlying the enhancement of rate capabilities and reversibility in Zn-ion batteries was elucidated. The interlayer spacing of the vanadate crystal lattice was expanded as validated by XRD and TEM analyses. Moreover, the intercalation of PEDOT:PSS into the vanadate lattice accompanied the partial reduction of V5+, increasing the proportion of V4+ ions together with vacancy-related defects relative to pristine AVNF. EPR analysis directly demonstrated that the secondary doping with PSS significantly reduced chain-to-chain charge transport resistance by inducing delocalization of polarons. The hydrophilicity of the sulfonic acid group also further improved compatibility with aqueous electrolytes. Benefiting from these synergistic effects, EP-AVNF exhibited significantly reduced charge transfer resistance and achieved impressive electrochemical performances containing maximum specific capacities 255 mAh g−1, energy densities of 140 Wh kg−1 and power densities 11,016 W kg−1. Additionally, the intercalated PEDOT:PSS chains served as robust structural pillars which enabled remarkable capacity retention of 99.2 % over 1000 cycles at high-speed charge/discharge conditions of 10 A g−1. In conclusion, the combined strategy of tuning of interlayer distance and secondary doping of conducting polymers proposed in this research represented an effective means to simultaneously overcome the inherent limitations of vanadium oxide cathodes which suffer from low electrical conductivity and structural instability. It paves the way for the developing direction of next-generation high-output and long-lifespan cathode materials for Zn- ion batteries.
