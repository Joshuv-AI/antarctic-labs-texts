Hair Loss in Patients on Glucagon-Like Peptide 1 Receptor Agonists: Understanding Risks and Managing Outcomes
Authors: Bianca Maria Piraccini, Sergio Vañó-Galván, Ulrike Blume-Peytavi, Virginie Ribet, Valérie Mengeaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and dual gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1RAs are used to improve glycaemic control in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus; some of these drugs are also used to help with weight loss. In recent years, safety signals concerning a possible link between GLP-1RA therapies and hair loss have been emerging in the USA. The volume of prescriptions for these drugs in Europe is now increasing, and it can be expected that the number of cases of GLP-1RA-associated hair loss will therefore also increase in Europe over the coming years. In this commentary, we consider the pharmacological context and market expansion for GLP-1RAs, examine the evidence for an association between GLP-1RAs and hair loss, explore potential pathophysiological mechanisms, and propose clinical recommendations. Most evidence suggesting a potential association between GLP-1RAs and hair loss comes from pharmacovigilance database analyses and retrospective cohort studies. No prospective, controlled studies have specifically evaluated this issue. The evidence suggesting an increased risk of hair loss is strongest for semaglutide and tirzepatide. However, although cases of hair loss have been noted across diverse settings and multiple analyses, causality has not been established. One potential underlying mechanism is the rapid weight loss seen with these drugs, which could induce telogen effluvium. Further research is needed to evaluate causality, underlying mechanisms, the role of dosing and route of administration, and patient factors that may increase the risk. Clinicians need to be aware of the possibility of hair loss occurring with GLP-1RAs and patients should be informed about the potential risk and monitored for hair loss.Keywords: Alopecia, GLP-1, Glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist, Hair loss, Weight loss, Telogen effluvium

Key Summary Points

Pharmacovigilance database analyses and retrospective studies suggest a potential risk of hair loss with glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), particularly semaglutide and tirzepatide.Similar findings reported by multiple independent analyses strengthen the possibility of an association. However, the evidence is not conclusive, causality has not been established, and further research is needed.One potential underlying mechanism is the rapid weight loss seen with these drugs, which could induce telogen effluvium.Clinicians should be vigilant for GLP-1RA-associated hair loss and should warn patients of the potential risk.

Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have been used for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and weight management for more than 10 years in the USA. They are effective at improving glycaemic control in patients with T2DM and aiding weight loss in patients with obesity [1–3]. Nonetheless, like all medications, GLP-1RAs can be associated with adverse effects, the most common of which are gastrointestinal in nature, such as diarrhoea and nausea [1, 2].

Recently, there has been considerable interest on social media about aesthetic changes after the use of GLP-1RAs for weight loss, particularly facial volume depletion and facial aging (“Ozempic face”) [4, 5]. In addition, safety signals concerning a possible link between GLP-1RAs and hair loss/alopecia have been emerging in the USA [6]. Hair loss can be distressing for affected individuals and have a negative effect on their quality of life [7, 8].

Among the GLP-1RAs that are available in Europe, several are now approved for use in the management of weight loss as well as the treatment of T2DM. The volume of prescriptions for these drugs is increasing each year [9]. It can therefore be expected that the number of cases of GLP-1RA-associated hair loss might increase in Europe over the coming years.

In this commentary, we consider the pharmacological context and market expansion for GLP-1RAs, examine the evidence for an association between GLP-1RAs and hair loss, explore potential pathophysiological mechanisms, and propose clinical recommendations.

Commentary

Pharmacological Context

Endogenous GLP-1, which is released from intestinal endocrine cells, is involved in glucose regulation. Its principal actions include enhancing insulin secretion, inhibiting glucagon secretion, delaying gastric emptying (which reduces the rate that dietary glucose enters to bloodstream), and promoting a feeling of satiety [10, 11].

GLP-1RAs were developed to mimic the effect of endogenous GLP-1. They bind to and activate the GLP-1 receptor in a similar manner to endogenous GLP-1 and provide the same beneficial effects on glucose metabolism and food intake. Therefore, treatment with GLP-1RAs leads to improved glucose-dependent insulin secretion, decreased glucagon release, delayed gastric emptying, and a sense of satiety with subsequent reduced food intake [10, 12, 13].

GLP-1RAs are used to improve glycaemic control in patients with T2DM, with all drugs in this class being effective at reducing HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose levels [1, 2]. Most GLP-1RAs act only on the GLP-1 receptor; however, tirzepatide is a dual gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1RA and therefore targets additional glucose regulatory pathways compared with other GLP-1RAs [10]. Meta-analysis indicates that tirzepatide is the most effective for the treatment for T2DM, followed by the GLP-1RAs semaglutide and liraglutide [2].

In addition to treating T2DM, some GLP-1RAs are also approved to help with weight loss in patients with obesity. This is achieved through their effects on gastric emptying, satiety and food intake [12, 13]. Evidence suggests that tirzepatide and semaglutide provided greater reductions in body weight than other GLP-1 agonists in patients who also have T2DM [2], and in patients who have overweight or obesity irrespective of the presence/absence of T2DM [3].

With respect to safety and tolerability, the most common adverse events seen with GLP-1 agonists are gastrointestinal, such as diarrhoea and nausea [1, 2]. A benefit–harm modelling study involving non-diabetic adults with overweight/obesity found that abdominal pain, cholelithiasis, constipation, diarrhoea, alopecia, hypoglycaemia, injection site reactions and vomiting were more frequent with GLP-1 agonists compared with placebo, but that achieving a 10% weight loss outweighed the cumulative harm, with a net benefit probability of 0.97 at 1 year [14].

Epidemiological Context in Europe

GLP-1RAs were first launched in key European markets in 2009–2011 [15]. GLP-1RAs currently available in Europe for the treatment of T2DM include dulaglutide, liraglutide and semaglutide, with liraglutide and semaglutide also approved for the management of obesity. The dual GIP/GLP-1RA tirzepatide is approved for the treatment of both T2DM and weight loss.

Studies from various European countries have demonstrated an increase in the use of GLP-1RAs over time [9, 15–18]. For example, in Denmark the annual number of initiators of GLP-1RAs increased sixfold between 2016 and 2023 [18]. Analysis of GLP-1RA annual sales data indicated that the volume of standard units sold across Europe increased by 56.6% between 2021 and 2025 [9]. This was most evident for semaglutide, for which there was an increase of 171.7%. This sales growth will be reflected in an increasing number of patients being exposed to GLP-1RAs in Europe.

Public Perceptions

An analysis of Google searches using the TRAJAAN platform found that searches related to GLP-1RAs increased by 45% between December 2024 and November 2025 (data on file), suggesting growing public interest. Most growth was seen in searches concerning price and side effects. Overall, searches mainly focused on weight loss benefits (96.8% of searches) and side effects, including fatigue, hair loss and tooth loosening.

Analysis of the Reddit social media platform over the same period, using Brandwatch, found a 19% increase in patients sharing their experiences with GLP-1RAs (data on file). Most users were being treated by healthcare professionals. Users reported that side effects affecting the skin and hair caused emotional stress, particularly skin dryness, cracks, facial ageing/sagging, loss of body skin tone, hair loss, hair thinning/loss of density, and hair breakage. Individuals receiving GLP-1RAs for medical reasons tended to regard side effects as more acceptable than individuals who had purchased a GLP-1RA without a prescription and were taking it for aesthetic reasons.

These findings indicate that possible hair loss associated with GLP-1RAs is of increasing concern for patients.

Association Between GLP-1RAs and Hair Loss

Most data on the risk of hair loss with GLP-1RAs or GIP/GLP-1RAs come from database analyses and other retrospective studies. In the clinic, dermatologists tend to use the term alopecia along with a descriptor to define the final pattern/type of hair loss (e.g. alopecia areata, scarring alopecia, whereas when the symptom is increased hair shedding it is described using the terms telogen effluvium; see Sect. “Clinical Forms of Drug-Induced Hair Loss”). However, in this section, which discusses safety data from observational studies assessing drugs, the terms used are those stated in the source papers.

Pharmacovigilance Data: Converging Signals of Alopecia in Multiple Databases

Analyses of pharmacovigilance databases, including the US FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) database and WHO global database (VigiBase), indicate that hair side effects are one of the most common dermatological adverse events reported for GLP-1RAs (Table 1) [19–22]. However, hair side effects are generally referred to with the generic terms “alopecia” or “hair loss”, without further explanation, making it difficult to understand which type of hair loss it is, and thus providing very generic prevalence data.

A US FAERS database analysis found that alopecia accounted for 10% of cutaneous adverse events reported for GLP-1RAs, and that it was more likely in patients being treated for T2DM than in those being treated for weight management [20]. Another FAERS analysis found that alopecia accounted for approximately 10% of cutaneous adverse events reported for liraglutide and dulaglutide, and approximately 15% of those reported for semaglutide [22] A disproportionality analysis using FAERS data on GLP-1RAs for 2022–2023 found increased reporting odds for alopecia with semaglutide (reporting odds ratio [ROR] 2.46, 95% confidence interval 2.14–2.83) and tirzepatide (ROR 1.73, 95% CI 1.42–2.09) [23]. Alopecia reports were also highest for these two drugs based on UK MHRA data [21]. However, a disproportionality analysis of GLP-1RA data from the WHO database VigiBase found that only semaglutide was significantly associated with hair loss (adjusted OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.30–1.55) [24]. Separately, a pooled analysis of data on diabetic therapies from four databases (FAERS, EudraVigilance, Australian Database of Adverse Event Notifications [DAEN] and VigiBase) found that GLP-1RAs were the most reported drug class for hair loss (ROR 0.61, 95% CI 0.60–0.64), but with no significant disproportionality signal [25].

Retrospective Studies: Increased Risk of Androgenetic Alopecia and Telogen Effluvium

Retrospective observational studies have reported mixed results. These studies tended to use more specific hair loss terms than the pharmacovigilance database studies. A small retrospective cohort study involving 283 recipients of GLP-1RAs seen at a dermatology clinic found no significant associations between specific drugs and development of either androgenetic alopecia or telogen effluvium, although non-significant trends were seen for semaglutide and tirzepatide [26]. However, analyses of large databases have found increased incidences of certain types of hair loss with some GLP-1RAs (Table 2) [27–31].

Among these database analyses, a retrospective analysis of a US nationally representative database found that any GLP-1RA use was associated with a significantly increased risk of any alopecia (OR 1.18, p ≤ 0.001) and androgenetic alopecia (OR 1.80, p ≤ 0.001) but not of alopecia areata or telogen effluvium [28]. Consistent with this, a retrospective analysis of the US TriNetX database found that among specifically evaluated patients with T2DM those treated with GLP-1RAs had an increased risk of androgenetic alopecia compared with recipients of metformin (hazard ratios, HR; tirzepatide 5.67, semaglutide 4.43, dulaglutide 2.75, liraglutide 2.34) (Table 2) [30]. The risk was greater with tirzepatide compared with semaglutide (HR 1.53), dulaglutide (HR 2.17) or liraglutide (HR 2.50) [30].

Looking at patients specifically being treated for weight management (Table 2), a retrospective analysis of Canadian health claims data found a higher incidence of hair loss with semaglutide compared with bupropion-naltrexone (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.52, 95% CI 0.86–2.69), with the risk being greater in women than in men [27]. Another analysis of the same database found that use of semaglutide or liraglutide for weight loss was associated with an increased risk of alopecia areata compared with bupropion-naltrexone (aHR 2.09, 95% CI 1.20–3.65), although a direct link between these drugs and the pathogenesis of alopecia areata has not been established [29]. A retrospective analysis of the US TriNetX database found an increased risk of telogen effluvium with GLP-1RA weight-loss treatment compared with other weight-loss interventions, but subgroup analyses showed this was due primarily to a significantly increased risk with tirzepatide compared with other weight-loss medications (not versus bariatric surgery), with no significant increase in risk seen for semaglutide or liraglutide [31].

Other Data Sources

As discussed above, real-world data indicate that, amongst GLP-1RAs, the risk of alopecia may be highest with tirzepatide and semaglutide [14, 21, 23, 24]. Alopecia was not generally reported as a notable adverse event in papers summarizing major phase 3 randomized controlled trials of these drugs (which usually reported events with an incidence of ≥ 5%) [32–36]. However, in a phase 3 trial in non-diabetic patients with obesity, alopecia occurred in approximately 5% of tirzepatide recipients versus 0.9% of placebo recipients [37].

Moreover, the summary of product characteristics (SmPC) for tirzepatide lists hair loss as a common adverse reaction (incidence ≥ 1/100 to < 1/10), noting that it mainly occurs in patients with overweight/obesity with or without T2DM [38]. The SmPC for semaglutide used for weight management also lists hair loss as a common adverse reaction, stating that it occurred in 2.5% of semaglutide recipients versus 1.0% placebo recipients, and that it was more common in patients with a greater weight loss (≥ 20%) [39]. Hair loss is not listed as an adverse reaction in the SmPC for semaglutide used for the treatment of T2DM [40].

No meta-analyses evaluating hair loss with GLP-1RAs have been published. Systematic reviews have not been able to draw firm conclusions, noting the limited data available, and highlighting that although cases of hair loss have been reported across diverse settings, causality has not been established [41–43]. For example, a systematic review of five studies involving 2905 patients treated with GLP-1RAs (predominantly tirzepatide) that reported hair loss outcomes yielded conflicting findings; some studies reported hair loss with semaglutide or tirzepatide, while others reported improvements in patients with pre-existing hair loss [41]. The authors concluded that further research was needed.

Factors that may affect the risk of hair loss with GLP-1RAs include the specific drug used; the dose, route and frequency of administration; and the indication for treatment (T2DM or weight loss) [20, 44].

Clinical Forms of Drug-Induced Hair Loss

In general, several types of hair loss/alopecia may be associated with different types of drugs, including telogen effluvium, anagen effluvium, alopecia areata and scarring alopecia [45, 46].

As discussed above, the available data suggest that the most common forms of hair loss reported with GLP-1RAs are telogen effluvium and worsening of androgenetic alopecia.

Acute telogen effluvium is the most common cause of reversible drug-induced hair loss, with diffuse hair shedding usually occurring approximately 3 months after drug initiation [47]. A wide range of drugs can cause telogen effluvium, including monoclonal antibodies, kinase inhibitors, and other targeted therapies, oral contraceptives, endocrine therapies, retinoids, antihypertensive agents, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, antipsychotics, anticoagulants, and antifungal and antiviral drugs [45, 47].

Anagen effluvium is caused by a sudden halt of anagen hair follicle growth, with hair loss occurring within days or weeks, and is commonly caused by chemotherapeutic agents [48].

Alopecia areata is an autoimmune disorder that causes non-scarring alopecia, which can range from discrete patches to total scalp or body hair loss. Although it is usually idiopathic, alopecia areata can occasionally be triggered by drugs that interfere with immune system activity, including monoclonal antibodies targeting cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor alpha and interleukin-4, checkpoint inhibitors, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors [45, 49].

In addition, although rare, some drugs (especially epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors) can cause scarring alopecia [45].

In most cases of drug-induced hair loss, i.e. telogen and anagen effluvium, hair regrowth occurs after cessation of the offending drug [45]. However, the course of alopecia areata triggered by drugs may not be influenced by drug withdrawal.

Androgenetic alopecia (male-pattern/female-pattern hair loss) is caused by a combination of genetic, hormonal and environmental factors, which can also include excess androgen intake (e.g. anabolic androgenic drug use) [50]. It is possible that androgenetic alopecia may become more visible if diffuse effluvium caused by GLP-1RA therapy occurs, although the androgenetic alopecia itself is not directly caused by the drugs.

Potential Pathophysiological Mechanism for GLP-1RA-Associated Hair Loss

The main hypothesis about potential mechanisms underpinning an association between GLP-1RA use and hair loss is stress from rapid weight loss leading to telogen effluvium.

Rapid and substantial weight loss due to low calorie intake can act as a stressor on the body, affecting the growth cycle of hair follicles and inducing telogen effluvium [51–53]. For example, hair loss is seen in some patients after bariatric surgery, a procedure that can lead to substantial weight loss and changes in nutritional status [54–56]. GLP-1RA drugs may also lead to micronutrient deficiencies associated with altered and decreased food intake; for example, levels of iron, calcium and some vitamins may be reduced [57–59]. In turn, micronutrient deficiency can be associated with hair loss [60–62].

The possibility of a direct effect of GLP1-RAs on the hair follicles has not been proven, although an in vivo and in vitro study showed expression of GLP-1 receptor in mouse skin hair follicles, suggesting the possibility of similar expression in human skin [63].

It should also be noted that it is theoretically possible that GLP-1RAs could have an indirect beneficial effect on hair loss, particularly in metabolically compromised patients (e.g. those with obesity, T2DM and peripheral insulin resistance) [64]. In T2DM, vascular damage from prolonged hyperglycaemia can lead to chronic hair loss [65]. In addition, androgenetic alopecia has been associated with insulin resistance [54], and GLP-1R expression is partly mediated by androgens [66], suggesting close interaction between the hormonal pathways. GLP-1RAs may improve insulin sensitivity, reduce hyperinsulinemia, improve microvascular function and enhance blood scalp flow, thereby targeting pathways involved in follicular miniaturization [54, 64]. It has been proposed that by improving metabolic regulation and vascular responsiveness, GLP-1RAs could potentially reduce both diabetes-related hair shedding and androgenetic alopecia [64, 65].

Discussion and Clinical Implications

Summary and Limitations of Data

Recent evidence suggests a possible association between some GLP-1RAs and GIP/GLP-1RAs, particularly semaglutide and tirzepatide, and hair loss. Limited data suggest telogen effluvium and worsening of androgenetic alopecia may be the most common types of hair loss seen with these drugs.

Most evidence on the potential association between GLP-1RAs and hair loss comes from pharmacovigilance analyses and retrospective cohort studies, which mainly mention alopecia or hair loss as a side effect without a specific diagnosis of the type of hair loss. No prospective, controlled studies have specifically evaluated this issue.

Similar findings reported by multiple independent analyses strengthen the possibility of an association, but causality has not been established.

In addition to the general lack of dermatological confirmation of an alopecia diagnosis, other limitations of the available data include heterogeneous definitions of hair loss/alopecia outcomes, possible under-reporting and reporting biases, and potential confounding due to factors such as T2DM, rapid weight loss and associated nutritional deficiencies.

Looking ahead, it is possible that reporting of hair loss associated with GLP-1RAs may increase as these drugs become more widely used to manage weight in addition to their use in diabetes, and this may provide additional information about the risk. In Europe, annual sales of semaglutide and dulaglutide have increased substantially since 2021 [9], particularly semaglutide, which is approved for both diabetes and weight loss.

Recommendations for Clinicians

To provide the best management for patients, it is important that clinicians are aware of the possibility of hair loss/alopecia occurring with GLP-1RAs.

Patients undergoing treatment with a GLP-1RA should be informed about the potential risk of hair loss. They should also be provided with advice on adequate nutrition to help mitigate the risk, such as maintaining adequate intake of protein and micronutrients [52, 54].

Rigorous clinical monitoring of patients for signs of hair loss is important, especially after rapid weight loss. Clinicians should implement proactive management of adverse effects. The specific type of hair loss should be identified, and patients should be referred to a dermatologist if appropriate. Dose reduction or withdrawal of the causative drug should be considered.

With respect to prognosis, further studies are needed to confirm whether, if hair loss is due to a GLP-1RA drug, the hair regrows if the drug is stopped. In cases of telogen effluvium, the hair loss will stop after the weight loss stops, aided by counselling on appropriate nutrition.

Research Perspectives

Further research is needed to evaluate causality, which should include prospective trials that control for weight loss and nutrition. This should include prospective controlled trials comparing weight loss achieved by different means, such as bariatric surgery and different pharmacotherapies (including different GLP-1RAs), and the effect on hair loss. Patients experiencing hair loss as a side effect should be dermatologically evaluated for a specific diagnosis and possible causes should be traced.

Research into underlying mechanisms is needed, which could involve studies on GLP-1 receptors in human hair follicles. Stronger signals have been seen for once-weekly drugs (semaglutide, tirzepatide), suggesting a potential role for dose and pharmacokinetics; therefore, additional research into the effect of different doses of GLP-1RA and route of administration on the risk of hair loss would be of interest.

Studies to identify patient factors that could affect the risk of GLP-1RA-associated hair loss (such as the amount and speed of weight loss, the type of nutrition and serum mineral levels), and to delineate appropriate dose adjustments for patients who experience hair loss would help guide clinical practice.

Conclusion

Recent evidence suggests a possible association between some GLP-1RAs and hair loss, mainly telogen effluvium. However, the evidence is not conclusive, causality has not been established, and further research is needed. An international observational study is underway to better understand the impact of these drugs on the skin and appendages. In the meantime, consideration should be given to the balance between benefits and risks when using these drugs. Clinicians should be vigilant for hair loss in patients receiving these drugs. The overall aim should be to ensure the best quality of life and dermatological care for patients using this therapeutic class, which is an essential treatment option for patients with T2DM and individuals with obesity.
