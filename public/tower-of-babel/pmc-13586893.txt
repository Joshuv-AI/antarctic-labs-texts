Dose-Response Overstatement and Vesicle-Attribution Uncertainty in Hypoxia-Conditioned MSC Exosome Therapy for Androgenetic Alopecia
Authors: Yujie CAI
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Suhendar et al.1, entitled “Evaluation of Hypoxia-Conditioned MSC Exosomes’ Effects on GM-CSF and IL-1α Expression in a Rat Model of Androgenic Alopecia”. The authors investigated whether hypoxia-conditioned mesenchymal stem cell exosomes modulate granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and interleukin-1α expression in a dihydrotestosterone-induced alopecia model1. Their work is timely because extracellular vesicle-based, cell-free regenerative approaches are increasingly explored in preclinical tissue-repair models. Nevertheless, several aspects of the statistical interpretation and vesicle-attribution strategy warrant caution in concluding that hypoxia-conditioned mesenchymal stem cell exosomes exert dose-dependent regenerative and anti-inflammatory effects.

The first issue concerns the repeated characterization of the findings as “dose-dependent.” Suhendar et al.1 state that hypoxia-conditioned mesenchymal stem cell exosomes increased granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and decreased interleukin-1α expression in a dose-dependent manner. However, the direct comparison between the 100 µg/kgBW and 200 µg/kgBW groups was non-significant for both granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and interleukin-1α, with p-values of 0.964 and 0.314, respectively1. Therefore, the data support a treatment-associated difference between exosome-treated groups and control groups, but they do not establish a statistically demonstrable dose-response gradient between the two active-dose groups1. This distinction is important because post-hoc group separation after an omnibus test is central to determining which specific treatment groups actually differ2. The small per-group experimental design also limits the ability to detect between-dose differences, and the absence of a clearly reported power assessment for the active-dose comparison weakens interpretation of these non-significant contrasts2.

A related statistical concern is the internal inconsistency in the reported post-hoc testing strategy. The methods state that one-way analysis of variance followed by least significant difference testing was used for variables with homogeneous variances, whereas Welch’s analysis of variance followed by Tamhane’s T2 testing was used for variables with heterogeneous variances1. However, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor showed heterogeneous variance by Levene’s test, yet Table 2 indicates use of least significant difference post-hoc testing for this outcome1. Conversely, interleukin-1α showed homogeneous variance, yet Table 2 indicates use of Tamhane’s T2 testing for this outcome1. Because post-hoc procedures influence pairwise inference and error control, mismatch between variance assumptions and multiple-comparison strategy may materially affect interpretation in small experimental groups2. Reanalysis using a variance-appropriate multiple-comparison procedure would clarify whether the reported between-group differences remain robust2.

A second major issue is the attribution of the observed cytokine changes specifically to exosomes. Suhendar et al.1 used tangential flow filtration to concentrate extracellular vesicle-enriched preparations, adjusted the final suspension according to total protein concentration, and confirmed CD63 and CD9 positivity. These steps support the presence of exosome-associated markers, but current minimal-information guidance for extracellular vesicle studies emphasizes that characterization should be tailored to the biological question and should transparently address the limitations of isolation and detection methods3. Therefore, CD63/CD9 positivity and total protein normalization alone do not fully establish particle number, particle-size distribution, particle-to-protein ratio, preparation purity, or exclusion of non-vesicular material3.

This point is particularly relevant because total protein concentration does not necessarily reflect extracellular vesicle dose. Webber and Clayton4 proposed comparing vesicle counts with protein concentration as a practical way to estimate extracellular vesicle preparation purity. In the present study, dosing by total protein could therefore reflect co-isolated soluble proteins, cytokines, protein aggregates, or other non-vesicular components rather than exosome particle number alone4. Without exosome-depleted conditioned medium, a protein-matched non-vesicular fraction, or particle-number-normalized dosing, the observed changes in granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and interleukin-1α cannot be confidently attributed to exosomal cargo alone3.

The absence of a normoxia-conditioned mesenchymal stem cell exosome comparator further limits the interpretation of the “hypoxia-conditioned” component. The central rationale of the study is that hypoxic conditioning may enhance mesenchymal stem cell exosome activity, yet the experimental design compared hypoxia-conditioned mesenchymal stem cell exosomes with saline and a standard topical comparator rather than with normoxia-conditioned mesenchymal stem cell exosomes1. This matters because studies of hypoxia-conditioned mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles have directly compared hypoxic extracellular vesicles with normoxic extracellular vesicles and corresponding supernatants to distinguish hypoxia-specific vesicular effects from broader conditioned-medium effects5. By contrast, the current design cannot determine whether the cytokine modulation reflects a general mesenchymal stem cell exosome effect or a hypoxia-specific enhancement1. Including normoxic mesenchymal stem cell exosomes, hypoxia-conditioned exosome-depleted medium, and particle-number-matched preparations would more directly test the mechanism implied by the title and rationale5.

Finally, the manuscript title refers to a “rat model,” whereas the abstract and methods describe male C57BL/6 mice and a dihydrotestosterone-induced murine model1. This discrepancy is not merely editorial; animal species designation is central to preclinical reproducibility and model interpretation. Correcting the title would improve indexing accuracy and reduce confusion for readers attempting to reproduce the model.

In summary, Suhendar et al.1 provide interesting preliminary evidence that hypoxia-conditioned mesenchymal stem cell-derived preparations can alter granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and interleukin-1α expression in a dihydrotestosterone-induced alopecia model. However, the current data do not substantiate a statistically supported dose-dependent effect1. The available vesicle characterization and control strategy also do not allow the cytokine changes to be attributed specifically to hypoxia-conditioned exosomes3. Variance-appropriate reanalysis, normoxic mesenchymal stem cell exosome controls, exosome-depleted conditioned-medium controls, particle-normalized dosing, and spatial- or protein-level validation would substantially strengthen the mechanistic and translational interpretation of this promising approach.
