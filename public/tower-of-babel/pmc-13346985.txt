Integrated mineralogical, microstructural, and thermal characterization of Azara barite Nasarawa State, Nigeria and its implications for radiation shielding materials
Authors: Nuraddeen Nasiru Garba, Muhammad Dahiru Audu, Rabiu Nasiru, Umar Ahmadu, Usman Musa Kankara, Jamilu Usman, Bashir Yusuf, Abdullahi Muhammad Vatsa, Musa Jibril
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a comprehensive physicochemical, structural, and thermal characterization of barite ore obtained from the Azara deposit in Nasarawa State, Nigeria, with the aim of evaluating its properties relevant to radiation shielding materials. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed barite (BaSO₄) as the dominant crystalline phase, with minor contributions from associated minerals including graphite, silicon oxide, and anhydrite. The estimated crystallite sizes ranged from 56.67 to 84.13 nm, indicating well-defined crystalline structures. Energy-dispersive X-ray fluorescence (EDXRF) analysis confirmed the elemental composition of the sample, with an empirical barite purity of approximately 74.42%, consistent with values reported for naturally occurring barite ores. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy identified characteristic sulphate functional groups associated with BaSO₄, along with minor silicate and organic-related bands. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed compact and irregular particle morphologies with relatively low visible porosity, which may contribute to increased packing density in composite materials. Thermogravimetric and differential thermogravimetric (TGA/DTG) analyses indicated good thermal stability of the dominant mineral phase within the investigated temperature range, with observed mass losses mainly attributed to surface-bound moisture and minor impurity phases. The measured specific gravity (4.34) and density (4340 kg m⁻³) are consistent with typical values reported for high-density barite minerals. Overall, the combined mineralogical, chemical, microstructural, and thermal properties suggest that the Azara barite deposit possesses characteristics favourable for potential use as a high-density component in radiation shielding materials. Further studies involving direct photon attenuation measurements are recommended to quantitatively evaluate its shielding performance.Keywords: Barite, BaSO₄, Radiation Shielding, XRD, FTIR, SEM, TGA, DensitySubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Radiation shielding materials are essential in medical diagnostics, nuclear facilities, industrial radiography, and other environments where exposure to ionizing radiation poses significant health risks. The growing global demand for effective radiation protection has stimulated research into cost-effective, environmentally friendly, and locally available shielding materials. In particular, naturally occurring high-density minerals have attracted increasing attention as potential alternatives or additives in radiation shielding systems due to their availability, stability, and relatively low environmental impact.

Barite (barytes), a naturally occurring barium sulphate mineral (BaSO₄) composed of approximately 66% BaO and 34% SO₃ (Bonel, 2005), is one of the most promising minerals for such applications. Owing to the presence of the heavy element barium, barite possesses a relatively high density (≈ 4.5 g/cm³), which enhances its ability to attenuate X-ray and gamma-ray photons. In addition, barite exhibits low solubility, chemical inertness, and relatively low toxicity compared with many other heavy-metal compounds, making it attractive for industrial and environmental applications1–3. Other advantageous properties include its relative softness, whiteness or colourlessness, and the possibility of colour variations arising from trace impurities or structural defects4.

Globally, barite is primarily consumed by the oil and gas industry, which accounts for approximately 85% of total demand, mainly as a weighting agent in drilling fluids. The remaining consumption is distributed among the chemical industry (about 10%) and various filler applications (about 5%)5. Beyond these conventional uses, barite has also attracted attention in the construction and medical sectors. In construction materials, it is sometimes incorporated into concrete to increase density and improve mechanical performance6. In medical imaging, barite suspensions are widely used as contrast agents in gastrointestinal radiography7,8. More recently, the high density and atomic number of bariums have made barite an attractive candidate for radiation shielding materials capable of attenuating X-ray and gamma radiation (Afolayan, 2021; Kinnunen et al., 2024).

Nigeria hosts substantial barite deposits distributed across several states, including Adamawa, Benue, Cross River, Nasarawa, Gombe, Plateau, Taraba, Ebonyi, and Zamfara1,9–15. Despite this abundance, many of these deposits remain under-characterized with respect to their physicochemical and structural properties relevant to advanced industrial applications, particularly radiation shielding materials.

Previous studies have attempted to characterize Nigerian barite deposits. For example, Otoijamun et al.5, investigated barite from Taraba, Nasarawa, and Rivers States using scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDX), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray diffraction (XRD). Their results indicated average Ba–S–O contents of about 66.5%, with variations between 59.36% and 98.86% among samples. FTIR spectra confirmed the presence of characteristic S–O, SO₄²⁻, Ba–S–O, and OH functional groups, while XRD analysis suggested that the barite samples met the American Petroleum Institute (API) standards for industrial use. However, the study did not investigate thermal characteristics, which are important for evaluating the stability of shielding materials under elevated temperature conditions.

Similarly, Utibe et al.16, examined barite deposits from Azara in Nasarawa State using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and X-ray diffraction techniques. Their analysis reported BaO and SO₃ contents of 8.595% and 7.45%, respectively, corresponding to approximately 15.885% BaSO₄. Mineralogical analysis revealed a complex composition consisting of quartz (45%), barite (29%), and other gangue minerals such as garnet, gypsum, orthoclase, davyne, illite, and muscovite. Although these findings provided useful insights into the mineral composition of the deposit, the microstructural features, density characteristics, and thermal behaviour relevant to radiation shielding performance were not comprehensively investigated.

Thermal stability is a critical parameter for radiation shielding materials, particularly in environments where temperature variations may affect structural integrity and shielding efficiency. Elevated temperatures can induce thermal expansion, dehydration, or microstructural changes in composite materials, which may influence density and consequently affect radiation attenuation properties17; Kontani et al., 2013). Therefore, a detailed understanding of the mineralogical composition, microstructure, density characteristics, and thermal behaviour of barite is essential for assessing its suitability in radiation shielding applications.

In this context, the present study provides a comprehensive physicochemical and structural characterization of barite ore from Nasarawa State, Nigeria, with the aim of evaluating its potential for radiation shielding applications. The investigation integrates mineralogical characterization using X-ray diffraction (XRD), chemical composition analysis using energy-dispersive X-ray fluorescence (EDXRF), morphological examination using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), functional group identification using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), specific gravity and density measurements, and thermal behaviour analysis using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA). By combining these complementary techniques, the study provides a detailed understanding of the properties of Azara barite and their implications for potential radiation shielding applications.

Materials and methods

Study area

Barite samples were collected from Azara (8.3661°N, 9.2491°E) in Awe Local Government Area of Nasarawa State, Nigeria (Fig. 1). Awe hosts an estimated barite ore reserve of approximately 731,000 tones, of which about 71,000 tones represent extractable barite18. The Azara barite deposit lies about 98 km south of Lafia, the state capital, and is part of the Benue Trough geological system (Fig. 1)16. Sample collection and preparation

Barite samples were collected from mining sites in Nasarawa State, located in the north-central region of Nigeria. In the laboratory, the samples were air-dried at room temperature (20–25 °C) for 24–48 h to remove residual moisture, after which stones, pebbles, and other extraneous materials were manually removed. The dried samples were then preliminarily crushed and sieved to obtain a particle size of < 100 μm (Fig. 3). The sieved fractions were thoroughly homogenized to ensure uniformity prior to analysis.

Sieved barite sample at particle size < 100 μm.

Energy-dispersive X-ray fluorescence (EDXRF) analysis

After preliminary preparation, the barite samples were further ground to a particle size of approximately 0.5 mm. Chemical composition analyses were performed at the Central Research Laboratory, Umaru Musa Yar’adua University, Katsina State, Nigeria, using an Energy-Dispersive X-ray Fluorescence spectrometer (ARL QUANT’X, 9952120, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Switzerland) equipped with Rh radiation. The instrument was operated at 50 kV with a maximum energy of 40 keV. Each sample was analysed for 10 min, and elemental concentrations were obtained from the resulting spectra.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

FTIR spectra were acquired using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two FTIR spectrophotometer at the Ahmadu Bello University Zaria Multi-User Laboratory. Prior to analysis, the samples were ground to a fine powder (~ 1–2 mg) using an agate mortar and pestle to ensure homogeneity. Infrared radiation in the range 4000–400 cm⁻¹ was transmitted through the sample, and the detector recorded the intensity of transmitted radiation at each frequency. The resulting spectrum represents the characteristic absorption patterns of the functional groups present in the barite sample.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

The morphology of the barite samples was examined at the Central Research Laboratory, Umaru Musa Yar’adua University, Katsina State, Nigeria, using a Phenom PRO: X SEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Switzerland) equipped with secondary electron (SE) imaging. Electrons were generated by heating a filament in the cathode ray tube and accelerated toward the sample at voltages ranging from 5 to 15 kV. The sample was mounted on a conductive sample holder prior to imaging. SEM provided detailed information on particle shape, surface structure, and microstructural features.

Thermogravimetric (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to investigate the thermal stability and decomposition behaviour of the barite samples. A PerkinElmer TGA 400 (Netherlands) was used, and instrument calibration including temperature, furnace, and weight was carried out according to the manufacturer’s specifications. Approximately 15 mg of the sample was loaded onto a platinum sample pan. Heating and cooling scans were conducted under a dry air flux from 30 °C to 660 °C at a constant rate of 10 °C/min.

Results and discussion

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

Figure 4shows the XRD pattern of the barite mineral sample. The characteristic barite peaks were observed in the 2θ range of 20.91°–44.36°. The most intense diffraction peak for barite (BaSO₄) appeared at a d-spacing of 3.273 Å, corresponding to the (102) plane at 2θ = 27.21°. Barite crystallizes in the orthorhombic system with space group Pnma (No. 62), consistent with PDF card number 04–007-7651 and the observations reported by Paulsen et al.23,. Its orthorhombic structure results in unique d-spacings that distinguish it from other minerals in the sample, such as anhydrite and strontium-bearing phases24.

XRD spectrum for a mineral sample.

Crystallite sizes and microstrain for the barite and associated minerals were determined using the Williamson–Hall (WH) method. Figure 5 presents the WH plot, which yielded an value of 0.0473. The microstrain for all reported minerals was 3.0 × 10⁻⁴, indicating relatively low lattice distortion and suggesting relaxation effects within the crystal structure25. This value is in good agreement with26, who reported a microstrain of 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ for silicon oxide, one of the minerals identified in the sample. The crystallite size of barite was calculated as 56.62 nm, which falls within the nanoparticle range reported by Patel et al.27.

Williamson -hall plot of XRD data.

Other minerals identified in the sample include graphite, silicon oxide, anhydrite, and strontium compounds (Table 1). Graphite coexists with barite and crystallizes in a hexagonal system (space group P6₃/mmc, No. 194, PDF card 00–001-0646) with a prominent peak at 2θ = 44.36° corresponding to the (101) plane and a d-spacing of 2.040 Å. The crystallite size of graphite was determined to be 84.13 nm. These minerals are readily distinguishable based on their distinct d-spacings and symmetry.

The XRD pattern also revealed the presence of silicon oxide (SiO₂) as a significant phase. Its most intense diffraction peak appeared at a d-spacing of 3.396 Å, corresponding to the (210) plane at 2θ = 26.21°. Silicon oxide crystallizes in the tetragonal system, belonging to space group P4/m (No. 83, PDF card 00–031-1234), with a crystallite size of 54.74 nm. This value is in good agreement with the 54.64 nm reported for SiO₂ by Hariyanto et al. (2021).

Anhydrite (Ca0.₇Sr0.₃SO₄) was also identified in the sample. Its strongest diffraction peak occurs at a d-spacing of 3.529 Å with Miller indices (002) at 2θ = 25.21°. Anhydrite crystallizes in the orthorhombic system, space group Pmm (No. 47, PDF card 00–045-0210), with a crystallite size of 60.02 nm. These results, together with those of barite, graphite, and silicon oxide (Table 1), confirm the multi-mineral composition of the sample and demonstrate distinct crystallographic characteristics for each phase.

Anhydrite is an orthorhombic mineral with distinct d-spacing, which differentiates it from barite in the sample. Overall, the minerals in the sample vary due to their unique d-spacings and crystallite sizes. The diffraction peaks for barite (BaSO₄) and associated compounds were observed over a 2θ range of 10°–70°, consistent with previous studies5,16.

The mineralogical properties of heavy minerals are critical for evaluating the suitability of barite as an effective radiation shielding material28. In particular, the lattice parameter the distance between atoms in a crystal lattice provides insights into structural changes caused by hydration, temperature, particle size, or irradiation29. Ahmad28, employed the Williamson–Hall method to determine barite crystallite sizes and reported that smaller crystallites (~ 31 nm) corresponded to slightly higher attenuation coefficients (µ/ρ) and improved mechanical strength due to denser packing. In comparison, the crystallite sizes observed in this study (Table 1) were all above 31 nm, indicating potential for effective radiation shielding.

Similarly, Zayed et al., (2024) reported a correlation between XRD crystallite size and the linear attenuation coefficient; a decrease in barite crystallite size from 60 nm to 40 nm increased linear attenuation by 3.5% at 0.662 MeV. In the present study, the observed crystallite sizes of all mineral phases ranged from 56.62 nm to 84.13 nm. Masoud30, also reported that barite minerals with finer crystallites and lower microstrain exhibit higher shielding efficiencies due to higher bulk densities and lower porosity. These findings are consistent with the present observations, where fine crystallite sizes and low microstrain suggest that the barite sample is suitable for gamma and X-ray shielding applications.

Interplanar spacing (d-spacing) is another key parameter that reflects the crystal structure of the material31,32. In this study, the unique d-spacings of barite, graphite, silicon oxide, and Sr-bearing anhydrite confirm the presence of distinct crystallite phases. Barite, having a higher atomic number, exhibits a smaller d-spacing (~ 3.27 Å), indicating densely packed atoms and excellent shielding potential30. In contrast, silicon oxide (~ 3.40 Å) and Sr-bearing anhydrite (~ 3.53 Å) have larger d-spacings and lower atomic numbers, suggesting comparatively reduced shielding performance. Graphite, with d-spacing smaller than silicon and anhydrite but lower atomic number, also contributes less to radiation attenuation. As observed by Cullity and Stock33, smaller lattice spacings correspond to more tightly packed atoms and higher bulk densities. Therefore, minerals with fine, dense crystal structures such as barite provide enhanced gamma radiation attenuation due to greater atomic packing density and smaller interplanar spacing30.

Quantitative XRD analysis (Fig. 6) revealed that barite constituted the highest fraction of the sample at 77(4) %, whereas silicon oxide was the least abundant at 2(2) %. Other minerals included graphite at 10(5) % and anhydrite at 10.9(8) %. This is comparable to Torkula barite analyzed by Afolayan et al.1, which contained BaSO₄ (87.79%), Fe₂O₃ (1.39%), and silicate salts (6.66%). Utibe et al.16, similarly reported that in Azara barite, the dominant phases were quartz (SiO₂) and BaSO₄ at 45% and 29%, respectively. The consistent association of barite with quartz suggests that the baryte veins of the studied area formed during the later stages of the Cretaceous34. Quartz, being a common sedimentary mineral, can also serve as an internal standard for accurate measurement of interplanar spacing35. Variations in sample phases are attributed to the geological diversity of the studied sites36.

Quantitative analysis of the sample.

Graphite, also present in the sample (Fig. 5), has a natural density of ρ = 2.26 g/cm³37 and is classified based on flake size, fixed carbon content, and crystallinity38. These properties make graphite suitable for electromagnetic radiation shielding39. Graphite coexists with barium oxide and silicon oxides in the deposits37,38. XRD studies of graphite from Ningi, northeastern Nigeria, revealed association with tritomite, pegmatite, knebetite, and pseudobrookite38, which differs from this study where graphite constituted ~ 10% in association with barite, silicon oxide, and anhydrite (Fig. 6). This variation is likely due to the distinct geology of the studied area36. Therefore, in the composite material, graphite may contribute approximately 10% toward radiation shielding performance.

Barium oxide (BaO) was the most abundant component at 52.577%, followed by Sulphur oxide (SO₃) at 21.846%. This indicates that the barite sample, with its high BaO and SO₃ content, is suitable for radiation shielding applications.

The sulphate (SO₄²⁻) group exhibited four characteristic vibrational modes. The absorption peak at 1180 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the sulphate group. The peak at 1130 cm⁻¹ is associated with the triply degenerate S–O stretching mode, while the band at 1090 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the symmetric stretching of the SO₄²⁻ group. Additionally, the peak at 990 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the symmetric stretching vibration of the SO₄²⁻ tetrahedron41.

These characteristic peaks confirm the presence of the sulphate group in the sample and validate the identification of barite (BaSO₄) as the dominant mineral phase.

The strongest FTIR absorption peak was observed at 1058.56 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the triple asymmetric S–O stretching vibration (ν₁) in barite, which also reflects the stretching of the SO₄²⁻ tetrahedron. This is in close agreement with Otoijamun et al.5, who reported a peak at 1059 cm⁻¹ for barite. Similarly, Bageri et al.42, observed diminished peaks at 1178.9 and 1083.8 cm⁻¹, which merged into a new peak at 1080.5 cm⁻¹, attributed to the ν₃ sulfate vibrational mode. The reduction in peak intensity was associated with partial separation of sulfate functional groups due to dissolution43.

Additional peaks were identified at 2109.67 cm⁻¹, corresponding to medium Ba–S–O stretching and bending vibrations, and at 1997.65 cm⁻¹, attributed to C ≡ C stretching from carbon impurities, indicating the presence of organic contaminants in the sample44. A further absorption at 3652.79 cm⁻¹ was observed, attributed to Si–OH stretching vibrations20,29.

These FTIR results are consistent with the XRD analysis, confirming the presence of barite (BaSO₄) and silicon oxide (SiO₂) in the sample (Table 1). For gamma and X-ray radiation shielding applications, the predominance of BaSO₄ bands indicates a material with favorable high-Z content for efficient photoelectric attenuation40. However, the presence of carbonate or silicate impurities and absorbed water may reduce the effective density and, consequently, the measured shielding efficiency28,45.

SEM analysis

Figure 9 presents the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the barite sample at various magnifications: (a) 500 μm, (b) 200 μm, (c) 50 μm, and (d) 40 μm. The micrographs reveal irregular grains with angular edges and platy to lamellar surfaces at finer particle sizes (40–50 μm), consistent with observations by20. At coarser fractions (200–500 μm), the grains appear blocky and well-defined, with dense particle centres, indicating low inter-particle porosity46,47.

FTIR Spectrum of the barite sample.

These observations align with reports by El-Sayed48and Adepoju et al.49, who described barite as tabular crystals with smooth to moderately irregular surfaces at fine particle sizes. As particle size increases, barite preserves angular and blocky morphologies50. Such morphologies are advantageous for X-ray and gamma radiation shielding because irregular and blocky particles enhance interlocking and packing efficiency, thereby increasing bulk density and the linear attenuation coefficient28,30.

Furthermore, the low porosity and dense particle cores reduce air-filled voids, which otherwise decrease effective shielding and promote scattered-photon transport, thus improving radiation attenuation (Barbhuiya et al., 2024; Piretes et al., 2014). Overall, the SEM analysis suggests that incorporation of barite into concrete or composite materials is likely to enhance mass attenuation and provide mechanically stable and reproducible shielding performance (28,30; Piretes et al., 2014).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA/DTG)

The thermal behaviour of the barite sample was investigated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and derivative thermogravimetry (DTG), as presented in Fig. 10. The sample was heated from approximately 30 °C to 660 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min⁻¹ under an air atmosphere in order to evaluate its thermal stability and the potential presence of volatile components and mineral impurities.

SEM images showing the microstructure of fine barite sample at different magnification (a) 500 μm (b) 200 μm (c) 50 μm (d) 40 μm.

The TGA curve shows a small mass loss (3.02%) from room temperature (~ 29.4 °C) to approximately 267 °C. This initial weight reduction is attributed primarily to the removal of physically adsorbed moisture and weakly bound water associated with mineral surfaces and minor hydrous phases present in the sample. Similar low-temperature mass losses have been reported in naturally occurring barite ores containing clay minerals and surface-bound water20.

Between approximately 267 °C and 497 °C, a more pronounced mass reduction is observed with a value of 83.7%. This weight change is likely associated with the combustion of trace organic matter and the thermal decomposition or structural modification of minor impurity phases present in the ore matrix. Natural barite deposits commonly contain accessory minerals such as clay minerals, carbonates, and silicate phases, which may undergo dehydration or decomposition within this temperature interval (51,52; Miltojevic et al., 2021). The presence of these associated mineral phases in the studied sample is supported by the XRD results (Table 1).

The DTG curve (Fig. 10) further highlights this process by showing a weak peak within the temperature range of approximately 250–350 °C, which is consistent with the oxidation of minor organic impurities and dehydration reactions within associated mineral components.

Above approximately 497 °C, the TGA curve becomes relatively stable, indicating that the major mineral phase remains thermally stable within the investigated temperature range. This behaviour is consistent with the well-known thermal stability of barite (BaSO₄), which typically remains structurally stable up to temperatures approaching 1400 °C53. The observed thermal behaviour therefore suggests that the dominant barite phase does not undergo significant decomposition under the experimental conditions employed in this study.

The thermal stability observed at elevated temperatures is an important characteristic for potential applications in radiation shielding materials. In composite shielding systems, thermal exposure can induce microstructural changes such as thermal expansion, micro-cracking, or increased porosity, which may influence density and mechanical integrity54–56. Nevertheless, the high thermal stability of the barite phase observed in this study suggests that the material can maintain its structural integrity under moderate thermal conditions relevant to many industrial applications.

Overall, the TGA/DTG results indicate that the Nasarawa barite sample exhibits good thermal stability with only limited mass loss associated primarily with moisture and minor impurities, while the principal BaSO₄ phase remains stable throughout the investigated temperature range.

Specific gravity and density of barite

The specific gravity of the barite sample was measured three times for accuracy, yielding a value of 4.34. This compares favourably with the American Petroleum Institute (API) standard, which specifies a minimum specific gravity of 4.10 for barite22. The observed 5.9% deviation above the standard may be attributed to the presence of mineral impurities such as quartz, graphite, and anhydrite, as confirmed by XRD analysis, as well as potential micro-porosity within the sample.

Using the measured specific gravity and Eq. 4, the density of the barite was calculated as 4340 kg·m⁻³. Specific gravity reflects the material’s density relative to water, and higher values generally correlate with improved radiation shielding performance25,26,40,57–84. The dense nature of the barite sample indicates its ability to effectively attenuate gamma radiation, confirming its suitability for gamma and X-ray shielding applications.

Conclusion

This study presented a comprehensive mineralogical, microstructural, and thermal characterization of barite ore from the Azara deposit in Nasarawa State, Nigeria, with the aim of assessing its potential relevance for radiation shielding materials. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed barite (BaSO₄) as the dominant crystalline phase, with minor contributions from associated minerals such as graphite, silicon oxide, and anhydrite. The crystallite sizes ranged from 56.62 to 84.13 nm, suggesting well-defined crystalline structures. Elemental analysis using EDXRF further supported the predominance of barium-bearing phases in the sample. FTIR spectroscopy verified the presence of characteristic sulphate functional groups associated with BaSO₄, while SEM observations revealed compact and irregular particle morphologies that may contribute to relatively high packing density. The measured specific gravity (4.43) and density (4430 kg m⁻³) are consistent with typical values reported for high-density barite minerals. Thermogravimetric analysis also demonstrated good thermal stability of the dominant BaSO₄ phase within the investigated temperature range. Collectively, these characteristics (physicochemical and structural) suggest that the Azara barite deposit possesses properties favourable for potential use as a high-density component in radiation shielding materials. However, further studies involving direct photon attenuation measurements, including the determination of linear and mass attenuation coefficients and half-value layer parameters, are recommended to quantitatively evaluate its shielding performance.
