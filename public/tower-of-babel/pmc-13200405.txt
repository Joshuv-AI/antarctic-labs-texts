NIR-II photothermal-amplified triple catalysis of a spherical mesoporous iron single-atom nanozyme for potentiating ferroptosis-based tumor therapy
Authors: Jingting Li, Sihan Wang, Fan Yang, Yang Guo, Xinyu Gao, Mingyu Nie, Zihan Zhou, Hao Xin, Fei Song, Changsong Dai, Zhaohui Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSingle-atom nanozymes (SAzymes) generally trigger catalytic reactions in situ within tumor cells, generating toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce cell death through oxidative stress, thereby achieving catalytic tumor therapy. However, therapeutic efficacy is often limited by insufficient ROS levels and intrinsic cellular antioxidant defenses.ResultsThis study developed a spherical mesoporous iron-based single-atom nanozyme (Fe–SAzyme) modified with polyethylene glycol (PEG) and (4-carboxybutyl)triphenylphosphonium bromide (TPP), achieving photothermal amplification of catalytic activities to deliver antitumor therapeutic effects. Fe–SAzyme exhibited triple enzyme-mimetic activities—peroxidase-like, glutathione peroxidase-like, and catalase-like activity. It simultaneously generated ROS and oxygen while oxidizing glutathione to oxidized glutathione. This elevation in ROS levels and weakening of antioxidant defenses disrupted intracellular redox homeostasis, ultimately triggering a process known as ferroptosis. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that Fe–SAzyme exhibited a low energy barrier for hydroxyl radical generation and possessed a viable cyclic catalytic pathway. Additionally, Fe–SAzyme–TPP exhibited exceptional photothermal conversion capabilities under second near-infrared (NIR-II) irradiation, enabling its use in photothermal therapy (PTT), while simultaneously enhancing catalytic activity and thereby significantly improving tumor treatment efficacy. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that PEG and TPP-modified Fe-SAzyme exhibited outstanding antitumor efficacy, along with enhanced biocompatibility and targeting capabilities. Under NIR-II irradiation, photothermal therapy synergized with catalytic activities, significantly amplifying tumor suppression effects.ConclusionThis study proposed a combined antitumor strategy based on iron-based SAzymes, utilizing photothermal amplification to enhance catalytic activities in conjunction with PTT, thereby achieving ferroptosis-based antitumor effects.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04389-z.

Introduction

Breast cancer continues to be one of the most aggressive malignant tumors [1]. Traditional treatment methods are often associated with low cure rates and a lack of targeted efficacy, making it challenging to eliminate tumors effectively [2, 3]. With the development of multiple treatment approaches, the concept of “nanocatalytic medicine” has been gradually applied in the field of oncology [4]. Nanocatalysts can catalyze the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the tumor microenvironment (TME), inducing oxidative stress damage in tumor cells, thereby enabling nanocatalytic therapy of tumors [5].

Among these, single-atom nanozymes (SAzymes) are considered a class of nanocatalysts with immense application potential due to their unique, atomic-level active sites and high catalytic efficiency [6]. SAzymes not only possess the excellent stability, safety, and targeting capabilities of conventional nanomaterials [7] but also mimic the catalytic activity of natural enzymes. They can replace traditional proteases and participate in various reactions across multiple physiological environments and metabolic pathways [8, 9]. Notably, SAzymes participate in reactions as catalysts without being consumed during the process, thereby endowing them with exceptional chemical stability and reusability [10]. The morphology and dimensions may influence the performance of SAzymes [11]. Spherical mesoporous nanomaterials, characterized by low surface energy, open mesopores, and short diffusion pathways, significantly enhance the accessible density of active sites and improve mass transport, demonstrating remarkable benefits in catalytic applications [12]. However, the catalytic activity of SAzymes is often limited by the low loading of metal active sites [13–15]. Spherical mesoporous structures can increase specific surface area and fully expose active sites, thereby promoting catalytic processes and enhancing catalytic activity. However, the controllable pore size depends on precise synthetic strategies [16]. Furthermore, spherical mesoporous framework structures are prone to collapse at high temperatures. Therefore, effective functionalization and the construction of stable structures are the current focus of research. SAzymes also exhibit lower substrate-binding capacity than native enzymes [17], whereas nanozyme modification can effectively enhance their bioavailability. FDA-approved hydrophilic polymer polyethylene glycol (PEG) suppresses bioadhesion via steric repulsion, thereby enhancing nanomaterial stability and prolonging their retention and circulation time in the bloodstream, thereby enabling sustained therapeutic effects [18].

The tumor-killing capacity of SAzymes primarily stems from its POD-like enzyme activity, which catalyzes the conversion of excess hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) within tumors into reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby inducing cell death [19]. To ensure ROS toxicity precisely targets tumor regions while avoiding damage to surrounding normal tissues, SAzymes design must integrate unique physiological characteristics within the TME—such as low pH and high H2O2 levels—alongside enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects and active targeting strategies to achieve safe, specific accumulation. Currently, multiple targeted antitumor strategies based on TME responsiveness have been developed. Zhang et al. used Mn2+-catalyzed CDT to kill tumor cells under microacidic conditions [20]. Wang et al. achieved targeted drug delivery via cell membrane encapsulation and responsive degradation within the TME [21]. Therefore, precise tumor recognition and localization are critical prerequisites for ensuring the safety of SAzymes therapy.

Photothermal therapy (PTT) holds significant application potential in the biomedical field. Photo-activated photothermal agents (PTAs) convert light energy into heat energy through PTT, enabling not only thermal ablation of tumors [22] but also targeted photothermal thrombolysis and inflammation suppression [23]. SAzymes are recognized as excellent PTA agents due to their unique carbon-skeleton structure and EPR effect [24]. Compared to the first near-infrared window (NIR-I, 650–950 nm), the second near-infrared window (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm) allows deeper tissue penetration due to reduced scattering and absorption in biological tissues [25–28]. This characteristic offers broad application prospects for precision medicine and safe therapeutic interventions [29]. In PTT, rapid cell death is primarily caused by protein denaturation and cell membrane damage [30]. Increased membrane permeability facilitates intracellular delivery of therapeutic drugs. Thus, under PTT, the amount of SAzymes penetrating tumor cells increases, allowing them to exert catalytic activity within the cells and trigger a series of catalytic reactions for the catalytic therapy of the tumor. Additionally, increased temperature accelerates enzyme reaction kinetics [31], thereby enhancing enzymatic reaction activity. PTT can also be combined with multiple therapeutic strategies, enhancing photodynamic therapy efficacy through dual mechanisms-synergistic enhancement and reduced toxicity [32]. In summary, PTT-based combination therapy strategies hold significant potential, offering new avenues for highly effective tumor treatment.

Nanozymes exhibiting both peroxidase (POD)-like and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px)-like activities are regarded as ideal ferroptosis inducers. Ferroptosis is a form of cell death distinct from apoptosis, dependent on intracellular iron and driven by lipid peroxidation within the cell [25]. Through the action of intracellular free iron or iron-containing enzymes, excessive oxidants, such as ROS, are generated, leading to lipid membrane disruption and lethal cellular damage [33]. Therefore, the substantial accumulation of ROS is considered a fundamental trigger for ferroptosis. SAzymes exhibit POD-like activity via stepwise catalysis of H2O2 dissociation at their active sites, forming an O = Fe=O intermediate. The O species in this intermediate adsorbs H+ from the environment to generate ·OH [34]. Simultaneously, the GSH-Px-like activity catalyzes the oxidation of glutathione (GSH) to oxidized glutathione (GSSG), thereby impairing ROS scavenging capacity to increase its intracellular accumulation [35]. Research had indicated that a decreased GSH/GSSG ratio downregulated the activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [36]. GPX4 is widely recognized as a key regulator of ferroptosis, and its inhibition activates the ferroptotic pathway [37-39]. Thus, through these catalytic reactions, SAzymes can regulate tumor metabolism and induce ferroptosis in cancer cells. Additionally, mitochondria and their metabolic processes play a crucial role in the induction and execution of ferroptosis [40]. Mitochondrial-localized GPX4 has been shown to participate in cellular defense mechanisms against ferroptosis [41]. Consequently, targeting mitochondria to induce ferroptosis has emerged as a ferroptosis-based cancer treatment strategy. SAzymes also exhibit catalase (CAT)-like activity, catalyzing the decomposition of H2O2 via the pathway “H2O2 → H2O2* → H2O* + O*” [42]. This property may also help regulate intracellular redox balance.

In this study, we reported a spherical mesoporous iron single-atom nanozyme with NIR-II photothermal conversion capability, whose surface was modified with PEG and triphenylphosphonium (TPP) to form Fe-SAzyme-TPP. This system exhibited photothermally enhanced triple enzyme catalytic activities, biocompatibility, and targeting property, enabling synergistic tumor thermal ablation and ferroptosis (Scheme 1). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations validated the pathway and capacity of Fe-SAzyme to catalyze the conversion of H2O2 into ·OH in a cyclic manner. A series of in vitro and in vivo experiments using 4T1 cells confirmed the exceptional therapeutic efficacy of Fe-SAzyme-TPP, presenting a promising strategy for the application of SAzymes in tumor treatment.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pluronic F127 and mPEG-NH2 (Mw≈2 kDa) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Dopamine hydrochloride (DA), 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene, N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N’-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), and (4-carboxybutyl)triphenylphosphonium bromide (TPP) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. Ethanol, ammonia solution (NH4OH), ferrocene, methoxypolyethylene glycol amine, 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO), 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 (RDPP), and DAPI were also supplied by Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. CCK-8 and trypsin were acquired from Seven Innovation (Beijing) Biological Technology Co., Ltd. The following reagents were procured from Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (Shanghai, China): mitochondrion-selective probe (Mito-Tracker Green), DCFH-DA, mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit (JC-1), Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis and necrosis detection kit, H&E staining kit, and One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit. Calcein AM/PI dual staining kit was sourced from Yeasen Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Antibodies and related reagents included: Ki-67 Recombinant Mouse Monoclonal Antibody (HuaBio, China); GPX4, HSP70, Bcl-2, and BAX antibodies (Abmart Shanghai Co., Ltd.); HIF-1α antibody (Chengdu Zen-Bioscience Co., Ltd.). PBS (pH 7.4, 10 mM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin (PS), and RPMI 1640 medium were provided by Gibco Life Technologies (CA, USA).

Synthesis of spherical mesoporous Fe-SAzyme

Synthesized according to the methods reported in the literature [43], 1.0 g of Pluronic F127 and 0.5 g of dopamine (DA) were dissolved in a mixed solution comprising 50 mL of ethanol and 50 mL of deionized water. The mixture was stirred thoroughly at room temperature. Subsequently, 0.7 mL of 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene was added dropwise to the solution, followed by stirring at 500 rpm for 30 min. Then, 2.0 mL of NH4OH was added dropwise, and stirring was continued for an additional 2 h. The resulting product was isolated via centrifugation and washed three times with distilled water. The collected material was dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 12 h to yield a black powder. The obtained black powder was then thoroughly ground with ferrocene at a mass ratio of 2:1. The ground mixture was subsequently calcined at 900 °C for 2 h under a nitrogen atmosphere, followed by a sulfuric acid (0.5 M) treatment for 12 h to produce spherical mesoporous Fe-SAzyme.

Synthesis of Fe-SAzyme-TPP

To conjugate TPP to Fe-SAzyme, mPEG-NH2 was first used to modify the Fe-SAzyme surface, imparting -NH2 groups and enhancing its aqueous stability. 5 mg of mPEG-NH2 were added to a methanol solution containing 5 mg of Fe-SAzyme and stirred for 12 h in the dark. Concurrently, 5 mg of TPP was dissolved in 5 mL of DMSO, to which 10 mg of 1-ethyl-(3-(dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide) hydrochloride (EDC) and 15 mg of N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were added. The mixture was activated in the dark for 4 h. The two solutions were combined and stirred for 24 h. The precipitate was then collected by centrifugation and washed. The final product was FeT.

Characterization

Sample morphology and atomic structure were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (SU8010, Hitachi, Japan), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (TALOS F200X, ThermoFisher, USA), and aberration-corrected transmission electron microscopy (AC-TEM) (ARM 200 F NEOARM, JEOL, Japan). Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR) (Nicolet is50, ThermoFisher, USA), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (ESCALAB 250Xi, ThermoFisher, USA), and inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES) (iCAP7400, ThermoFisher, USA) were employed to examine the chemical structure and composition of the samples. Enzyme activity was measured using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (LAMBDA950, PE, USA), an electron spin resonance (ESR) spectrometer (FLS1000, Edinburgh, UK), and a dissolved oxygen meter (JPBJ-607 A, INESA, China). Cellular morphology and structure were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM-1400FLASH, JEOL, Japan). Photothermal experiments were conducted using a 1064 nm near-infrared-II (NIR-II) laser (EL20602, CNI, Changchun, China) and a thermal imager (E5-XT, FLIR, USA). Fluorescence microscope (AF6000, Leica, GER), laser confocal microscope (LMS 800, ZEISS, Germany), flow cytometer (NovoCyte, Agilent, USA), microplate reader (ELX800, BioTek, USA), and chemiluminescence imager (ChemiDoc Touch, Bio-Rad, USA) for in vivo and in vitro experiments.

Study of photothermal properties

Aqueous Fe-SAzyme solutions (1 mL) at various concentrations (0, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 µg/mL) were irradiated with a 1064 nm laser (500 mW/cm2) for 5 min. Temperature changes were monitored every 30 s using an infrared thermal camera. Similarly, temperature changes were measured during a 5 min irradiation of Fe-SAzyme aqueous solution (1000 µg/mL) with 1064 nm lasers at different power densities (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 mW/cm2). For photothermal stability, the sample (1000 µg/mL) was repeated five times with laser on/off cycles (5 min irradiation followed by 5 min cooling). Finally, under identical conditions, a single irradiation was performed, followed by cooling to room temperature. Temperature was recorded every 30 s to plot a temperature curve and calculate the photothermal conversion efficiency.

The color changes were monitored using a UV-visible spectrophotometer to record the absorbance curve. Measurement of GSH-Px-like activity

The depletion of GSH was monitored using DTNB as an indicator of this process. It reacted with GSH to form a yellow product, exhibiting a characteristic absorption peak at 512 nm. Fe-SAzyme (10 µg/mL) and GSH (1 mM) were incubated in a pH 5.5 buffer at 37 °C or post-NIR-II temperatures. At specified time points (0–60 min), DTNB (2 mM in ethanol) was added, and absorbance at 512 nm was measured to determine residual GSH.

Computational methods

All spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) calculations using the projector augmented wave (PAW) method were carried out by the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) throughout the paper [45–47]. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerh (PBE) exchange-correlation functional of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) is used to describe the exchange-correlation potential between electrons [48]. The DFT-D3 method was employed to describe van der Waals (vdW) interactions [49]. The energy cutoff was set to 500 eV. The thresholds for electronic and force convergence were set at 10− 6 eV and 0.03 eV/Å. The 5 × 5 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack scheme k-point grid is used for sampling the Brillouin zone in all calculations [50]. In the z direction of the catalyst plane, a 20 Å vacuum layer was added to avoid the interactions between periodic images.

Cell culture

Breast cancer 4T1 cells were seeded in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin and 1% streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO2. HUVEC cells were cultured in MCM medium under identical conditions.

Cellular uptake

Fe-SAzyme-TPP was labeled with Rhodamine B. Cells were seeded in culture dishes and allowed to grow and confluence to 50%, and then treated with labeled Fe-SAzyme-TPP for 4 h. After staining with DAPI and Mito-Tracker Green, the fluorescence fusion status was observed by confocal microscopy. For quantitative analysis, cells were incubated with Fe-SAzyme-TPP for 0, 12, and 24 h, and then trypsinized and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Detection of intracellular iron levels

Seed 4T1 cells (1 × 103 cells/well) into a 6-well plate and culture until 50% confluence. Then, replace the old medium with medium containing 120 µg/mL Fe-SAzyme and Fe-SAzyme-TPP, and continue culturing for 4 h. Wash cells five times with PBS, digest with trypsin, and resuspend in PBS to form a cell suspension with a final concentration of 100,000 cells/mL. Quantify Fe content in both cell samples using ICP-OES analysis.

CCK-8 assays

4T1 and HUVEC cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 104 cells/well) and incubated for 24 h. Replace the fresh medium containing Fe-SAzyme or Fe-SAzyme-TPP at concentrations ranging from 0 to 120 µg/mL for 4 h in the 4T1 cell culture plate. Simultaneously, fresh medium containing 120 µg/mL Fe-SAzyme-TPP was added to the HUVEC cell culture plate. The Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II group was irradiated (1064 nm, 500 mW/cm2, 5 min) at 4 h post-treatment. After 20 h, cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay and measured with a microplate reader.

Hemolysis experiments

Collect 1 mL of blood from the mouse’s internal jugular vein—centrifuge for 15 min at 3000 rpm. Collect the lower layer of blood cells. Take 20 µL of this layer and mix it with 1 mL of physiological saline (negative control), 1 mL of ultrapure water (positive control), and 1 mL of Fe-SAzyme-TPP solutions at different concentrations (40, 60, 80, 100, 120 µg/ml) and incubate at 37 °C for 4 h. After incubation, centrifuge again, photograph and document the state of the mixture in the EP tube, record the hemolysis phenomena and calculate the hemolysis rate of the Fe-SAzyme-TPP.

Detection of ROS in cells

The ability to generate ROS within cells across different treatment groups was assessed using DCFH-DA as a probe. 4T1 cells were seeded in culture dishes for 24 h. The old medium was replaced with fresh medium containing different samples (120 µg/mL) and incubated for 4 h. The Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II group was irradiated (1064 nm, 500 mW/cm2, 10 min). Cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (10 µM) for 15 min. The intensity of green fluorescence observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope reflected intracellular ROS levels.

Oxidative stress assay

Maldodicarboxaldehyde (MDA) levels can be used to assess cellular damage. 4T1 cells were treated with Fe-SAzyme-TPP (120 µg/mL) for various durations. The MDA content within the cells at each time point was detected using an MDA assay kit.

Detection of O2 in cells

RDPP undergoes fluorescence quenching upon encountering O2, making it suitable as a fluorescent probe for detecting intracellular O2 production. Cells were treated as described in Sect. 2.14, incubated with RDPP (30 µM), and red fluorescence was observed to evaluate O2 generation.

Detection of GSH in cells

4T1 cells were treated with samples (120 µg/mL) for 12 h or with Fe-SAzyme-TPP for 0–12 h. GSH content was measured using a GSH assay kit to evaluate GSH depletion.

Western blot assay

Cells were treated as follows: GPX4, Bcl-2, BAX: 4T1 cells at ~50% confluence were treated with different groups of nanomaterials (120 µg/mL) for 4 h, with or without a 1064 nm laser (500 mW/cm2, 5 min) irradiation, and incubated for 20 h.

HIF-1α: Cells incubated in an anaerobic chamber (2% O2) served as the positive control, while cells incubated in a conventional incubator served as the negative control. Another group was incubated in an anaerobic chamber. When cells reached 50% confluence, Fe-SAzyme-TPP (120 µg/mL) was added, and incubation under anaerobic conditions continued for 24 h.

HSP70: Prepare four sets of cells. Select two sets and add Fe-SAzyme-TPP (120 µg/mL) when cell growth reaches 50%. After 4 h, subject one set to light exposure (1064 nm, 500 mW/cm2, 10 min) and incubated for 20 h. A separate group of cells was subjected to a 5-min incubation at 53 °C in a water bath without Fe-SAzyme-TPP treatment, followed by continued incubation for 20 h. This served to simulate HSP70 expression in tumor cells under high-temperature conditions. The last group of cells grew under normal conditions.

After undergoing the treatment as mentioned above, all cells were collected, and proteins were extracted. Western blot analysis was performed to assess the expression levels of the corresponding proteins.

Cell proliferation and migration

For colony formation, 4T1 cells (1 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in a 6-well plate. Once cells have expanded to approximately ten cells per well, treat them with different groups of nanomaterials (120 µg/mL). For the Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-Ⅱgroup, after 4 h of Fe-SAzyme-TPP treatment, expose cells to 1064 nm laser irradiation (500 mW/cm2, 5 min) and continue incubation. Incubation was terminated once distinct colony clusters became visible under microscopy. Cells were then washed with PBS and fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Finally, samples were stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution to visualize cell proliferation.

For wound healing, 4T1 cells (5 × 105/well) were seeded in a 6-well plate. When cells reach 100% confluence, draw a straight line through the center of the cell layer. Gently wash with PBS, then replace with low-serum medium (2% FBS) supplemented with different groups of nanomaterials (120 µg/mL). After 4 h, cells were either exposed or not exposed to light irradiation (1064 nm, 500 mW/cm2, 5 min). Photograph the scratch area under an inverted microscope at different time points post-scratching (0 h, 12 h, 24 h), analyze and measure the scratch width, and calculate the cell migration rate.

Cell living-dead/apoptosis staining

Seeded 4T1 cells in a 6-well plate and cultured for 24 h, and then were treated with different samples (120 µg/mL); the Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II group was then irradiated. After 20 h, cells were stained with Calcein-AM/PI or JC-1 and observed by fluorescence microscopy. Apoptosis was analyzed using flow cytometry with Annexin V-FITC/PI.

Intracellular Fe2+ staining

Seed cells at 5 × 104 cells/well in a 96-well plate. When cells reach approximately 50% confluence, replace the old medium with fresh medium from different groups and incubate for 12 h. Subsequently, stain the cells according to the protocol of the Cell Fe2+ Red Fluorescence Detection Kit and observe the intracellular red fluorescence using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Animal model

Female Balb/c mice (4–6 weeks) were housed under standard conditions. The Animal Care Committee approved all procedures, and the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Ethics No. 2024097) approved the use of these procedures. 4T1 cells (1 × 106 in 100 µL PBS) were injected into the dorsal region of the right flank of each mouse to establish a 4T1 subcutaneous tumor model. Initiate in vivo experiments when tumor volume reached approximately 80–100 mm3 (V = width× width × length ×1/2).

In vivo antitumor effect

Tumor-bearing mice (n = 6/group) received intravenous injections of: (I) PBS, (II) NC, (III) Fe-SAzyme, (IV) Fe-SAzyme-TPP, or (V) Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II (10 mg/kg). Group V was irradiated (1064 nm, 500 mW/cm2, 5 min) at 24 h post-injection. Tumor volumes and body weights were recorded every 3 days for a period of 15 days. Blood and tissues were collected for biochemical and histopathological analysis, including H&E, TUNEL, and Ki-67 staining, as well as immunohistochemical staining for GXP4 and HIF-1α. Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining for 4-HNE was performed.

In vivo photothermal properties

Using a portable thermal imaging camera, monitored temperature changes at the tumor sites of mice in the I and V groups during NIR-II irradiation, recording data every 30 s.

In vivo nanomaterial accumulation

A separate set of experiments was conducted by injecting Fe-SAzyme-TPP into the tail veins of tumor-bearing mice. After 24 h, the mice were euthanized, and their left hearts were perfused with physiological saline. The tumors and major organs were collected and weighed. ICP-OES quantitative analysis was employed to determine the Fe content in each tissue sample.

Statistical analysis

All calculated data in this experiment were presented as mean ± standard deviation. One-way ANOVA was used for multiple comparisons, while comparisons between two groups were conducted using t-tests. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), p < 0.0001 (****).

Results and discussion

Characterization of Fe-SAzyme-TPP

SEM and TEM images revealed that Fe-SAzyme possessed a uniform, spherical mesoporous structure with a diameter of approximately 200 nm (Figs. 1a, b). After modification with PEG and TPP, the spherical structure of Fe-SAzyme-TPP showed no significant changes (Figs. S1, S2), indicating that surface modifications did not affect the morphological structure of Fe-SAzyme. AC-HAADF-STEM images of Fe-SAzyme revealed isolated, high-density, bright spots (Fig. 1c), confirming the presence of individual metallic iron atoms. EDS elemental mapping further verified the distribution of Fe, C, and N elements within Fe-SAzyme (Fig. 1d).

XPS analysis of Fe-SAzyme revealed the presence of Fe, C, and N elements (Fig. S3a). The N 1 s spectrum exhibited four peaks corresponding to pyridinic N (397.03 eV), Fe–Nx (398.13 eV), graphitic N (399.88 eV), and oxidized N (402.73 eV) (Fig. 1e). In the formation of Fe-N bonds, the coordination sites for atomic Fe were primarily provided by pyridine-N and graphite-N, which helped stabilize the catalytic sites of individual Fe atoms and enhanced the catalytic activity of Fe-SAzyme. The Fe 2p spectrum showed two weak peaks corresponding to Fe 2p1/2 and Fe 2p3/2 (Fig. S3b), consistent with the low Fe loading of only 0.43% (Table S1). These XPS results indicated that individual Fe atoms had been successfully incorporated into the NC structure of Fe-SAzyme, forming Fe-N coordination bonds that served as effective catalytic sites for H2O2. ICP-OES measured the iron content of Fe-SAzyme at 0.51% ± 0.03 (Table S2), slightly higher than the XPS measurement. This discrepancy might be attributed to the surface-scanning nature of XPS, which could lead to underestimated results.

Aqueous stability is a prerequisite for the effective utilization of nanomaterials within biological organisms. The aqueous stability of Fe-SAzyme and Fe-SAzyme-TPP was evaluated in PBS and physiological saline (Fig. S4a). As the testing period extended, Fe-SAzyme exhibited noticeable sedimentation and stratification within 24 h, whereas Fe-SAzyme-TPP remained stably suspended in both PBS and physiological saline. Additionally, the stability of Fe-SAzyme-TPP was further validated by monitoring its polydispersity index (PDI) and particle size changes over seven consecutive days in physiological saline using dynamic DLS, indicating that the PDI of Fe-SAzyme-TPP consistently remained within the range of 0.24 ± 0.03, with particle size stabilized at 262.88 ± 8.98 nm (Fig. S4b). These results demonstrated that Fe-SAzyme-TPP maintained excellent monodispersity over seven days without significant aggregation, confirming that the incorporation of PEG and TPP effectively enhanced the stability of Fe-SAzyme.

Figure 1f characterized the crystal structure of the samples via XRD, revealing two peaks at 27° (002) and 44° (101) for the precursor carbon spheres (NC) and Fe-SAzyme, respectively, attributable to graphitic carbon [51]. No crystalline metal phases were observed, confirming the atomic dispersion of Fe within the carbon framework. The mesopore size of Fe-SAzyme was determined based on N2 isothermal adsorption-desorption isotherms (Fig. 1g). The Barrett-Joyner-Halenda model indicated an average pore diameter of 4.8 nm for Fe-SAzyme. Calculations based on the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) theory yield a specific surface area of 291.97 m2/g. This mesoporous structure and high specific surface area exposed more metal active sites, facilitating the proximity and adsorption of H2O2, thereby enhancing catalytic performance.

To verify the incorporation of TPP into Fe-SAzyme-TPP, FT-IR spectroscopy was employed to detect the characteristic absorption peaks of each structural component. As shown in Fig. S5, the FT-IR spectrum exhibited two new characteristic absorption peaks at 1437 cm− 1 and 1223 cm− 1, corresponding to the benzene ring skeletal vibration of TPP and the C-N stretching vibration of the amide bond, respectively. Zeta potential test results revealed that the surface charge shifted from negative to positive following PEG modification, and the zeta potential value increased after the TPP linkage (Fig. S6). Furthermore, thermogravimetric analysis revealed loading rates of 3.97% for PEG and 3.07% for TPP (Fig. S7). Together, these data indicated that PEG and TPP were successfully loaded onto the Fe-SAzyme surface to construct Fe-SAzyme-TPP.

The characterization results indicated that iron existed in atomic form on Fe-SAzyme, with its surface utilizing PEG to connect with TPP, thereby constructing a stable Fe-SAzyme-TPP nanoscale system featuring a spherical mesoporous structure.

Photothermal amplified enzyme-mimic catalytic performance of Fe-SAzyme

The carbon nitride framework served as a PTA capable of absorbing NIR-II light energy and converting it into thermal energy for tumor PTT [52, 53]. The photothermal and catalytic mechanisms of Fe-SAzyme were shown in Fig. 2a. To investigate the photothermal properties, Fe-SAzyme was irradiated with a 1064 nm laser, and temperature changes were recorded under varying laser power densities and nanozyme concentrations (Figs. S8, S9). The temperature increased with both increasing laser power and Fe-SAzyme concentration, reaching a maximum of 68.8 °C. Additionally, NC and Fe-SAzyme-TPP showed the same heating trend as Fe-SAzyme and reached similar temperatures. This confirmed that the photothermal effect of Fe-SAzyme primarily originated from the NC framework, and surface modifications had no significant impact on its photothermal performance (Fig. S10). Photothermal stability was assessed over five on–off cycles at 500 mW/cm2, showing a slight decrease in the maximum temperature (Fig. 2b). The photothermal conversion efficiency of Fe-SAzyme calculated from a single heating-cooling curve was 42.6% (Fig. 2c). These results confirmed that Fe-SAzyme exhibited highly efficient and stable photothermal performance under NIR-II irradiation. At a concentration of 1000 µg/mL and a power density of 500 mW/cm2, the temperature exceeded 50 °C within 5 min, reaching the typical threshold for effective photothermal ablation [54]. After five on-off cycles, the temperature remained within the therapeutically effective range, confirming that Fe-SAzyme is an excellent photothermal agent.

ESR spectroscopy revealed characteristic ·OH signal peaks (1:2:2:1) in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (1 mM), which disappeared upon addition of a ·OH scavenger (Fig. 2d). This confirmed that Fe-SAzyme exhibited POD-like catalytic activity, capable of generating ·OH from H2O2. The POD-like activity of Fe-SAzyme was further evaluated using 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) as a chromogenic agent in H2O2 solution. ·OH radicals can induce colorless TMB to form blue oxidized TMB (oxTMB) with a characteristic absorption peak at 652 nm. Fe-SAzyme produced characteristic absorption peaks in PBS buffer solution (H2O2 = 10 mM, pH = 5.5), and the absorbance increased with Fe-SAzyme concentration, indicating concentration-dependent ·OH generation (Fig. S11). The highest oxTMB signal was observed at pH 5.5, followed by pH 6.5 and pH 7.4, suggesting a pH-dependent behavior and demonstrating enhanced catalytic performance under acidic conditions (Fig. S12). Furthermore, ·OH production increased with rising H2O2 concentrations, confirming the sustained POD-like catalytic capability of Fe-SAzyme (Fig. S13).

Michaelis-Menten steady-state kinetics were employed to evaluate the catalytic efficiency of Fe-SAzyme. To simulate the temperature under NIR-II illumination, we selected the temperature after five on-off cycles (53 °C) for testing. Michaelis–Menten plots were constructed using different concentrations of H2O2 as the substrate under acidic conditions (pH 5.5) at room temperature and 53 °C (Figs. S14a, b). The Michaelis-Menten constant (Km) and the maximum initial velocity (Vmax) represent the enzyme-substrate affinity and the maximum reaction rate when the enzyme is fully saturated with substrate, respectively. A smaller Km value indicates a stronger affinity between the enzyme and substrate, while a larger Vmax value indicates higher efficiency of the enzymatic reaction. The Km and Vmax values of Fe-SAzyme at room temperature were 1.56 mM and 1.03 × 10− 7 M/s, respectively. Under photothermal conditions, Km decreased to 1.25 mM, and Vmax increased to 1.46 × 10− 7 M/s (Fig. 2e, f), indicating that elevated temperatures enhanced the substrate binding capacity and reaction rate of Fe-SAzyme, similar to a POD-like enzyme.

In addition, the CAT-like activity of Fe-SAzyme was evaluated by detecting oxygen (O2) production in H2O2 in the presence of Fe-SAzyme. As shown in Figs. S15 and S16, Fe-SAzyme also exhibited concentration-dependent and acidity-dependent enhancement of CAT-like activity. Increased H2O2 concentrations also generated more O2 (Figs. S17a, b). Michaelis-Menten steady-state kinetic analysis revealed a Km value of 21.93 mM and a Vmax value of 5.13 mg/L/min at room temperature. When heated to 53 °C, these values increased to 13.49 mM and 6.23 mg/L/min (Figs. 2g, h), demonstrating enhanced CAT-like catalytic activity of Fe-SAzyme at elevated temperatures.

To further investigate the effect of temperature elevation induced by NIR-II irradiation on enzyme activity, we calculated and plotted an Arrhenius plot based on temperature-dependent kinetic data. The activation energies for Fe-SAzyme as POD-like and CAT-like enzymes were computed to quantitatively assess Fe-SAzyme’s catalytic efficiency and the impact of thermal energy on overcoming reaction barriers. As shown in Fig. S18a and calculated using the Arrhenius equation, Fe-SAzyme exhibited an activation energy Ea = 10.07 kJ/mol as a POD-like nanozyme. Its rate constant, k (s− 1), increased with rising temperature, indicating that thermal energy could be efficiently converted into a driving force to overcome the reaction barrier, thereby enhancing the efficiency of POD-like catalytic reactions. Similarly, calculations based on Fig. S18b revealed that Fe-SAzyme exhibited an activation energy, Ea, of 5.67 kJ/mol as a CAT-like nanocatalyst, with its rate constant, k (min− 1), also increasing with temperature, confirming its highly efficient CAT-like catalytic activity. The above Arrhenius analysis clarified the mechanism by which NIR-II irradiation-induced temperature elevation enhanced Fe-SAzyme catalytic activity. Increased temperature effectively raised the energy of the reactant molecules in the system, making it easier for them to overcome the activation energy barrier of Fe-SAzyme, thereby significantly increasing the reaction rate constant k. Furthermore, Fe-SAzyme’s inherently low activation energy formed an ideal synergy with the temperature elevation in photothermal therapy. This enabled Fe-SAzyme to achieve high catalytic efficiency under NIR-II irradiation, providing crucial evidence for the realization of efficient, controllable catalytic therapy within the tumor microenvironment.

GSH-Px-like catalytic activity of Fe-SAzyme was evaluated by measuring GSH consumption. Dithiocarbamoylbenzene (DTNB), a catalytic product of GSH-Px, reacts with residual GSH to form trithiocarbamoylbenzene (TNB) with absorption at 412 nm. Fe-SAzyme and GSH solutions were incubated at different temperatures (37 °C and 53 °C), showing a gradual decrease in absorbance over time (Figs. S19a, b, 2i). It was confirmed that Fe-SAzyme possessed a sustained capacity to consume GSH, which increased with rising temperatures.

In summary, Fe-SAzyme exhibited triple enzyme-mimetic activity—POD-like, CAT-like, and GSH-Px-like activity—enabling the generation of ROS, O2 production, and GSH depletion. The POD-like and CAT-like activities were significantly higher than those of most reported nanozymes (Tables S3, S4). Its catalytic efficiency was closely correlated with reaction temperature, with elevated temperatures significantly enhancing its multifaceted catalytic performance. Additionally, Fe-SAzyme served as an effective photothermal agent capable of inducing temperature elevation under NIR-II irradiation. Therefore, Fe-SAzyme simultaneously possessed photothermal conversion capability and catalytic performance, enabling synergistic application of both properties and providing a foundation for its research in synergistic antitumor therapy.

DFT calculations

To investigate the catalytic mechanism by which Fe-SAzyme mimics the activity of the POD enzyme, this study employed density functional theory (DFT) to calculate the catalytic process of Fe-SAzyme at the atomic level. As shown in Fig. 3a, b, the H2O2 molecule was first adsorbed onto the Fe atom site of Fe-SAzyme*, forming activated H2O* with an adsorption energy of 0.38 eV. The low adsorption energy indicated that H2O2 readily bound to Fe-SAzyme. Simultaneously, computational results revealed that the O–O bond length of H2O2 elongated from 1.470 Å to 1.588 Å after adsorption (Table S5), which enhanced the susceptibility of the O–O bond to cleavage. Subsequently, activated H2O2 molecules undergo homolytic cleavage at single Fe atom sites, generating highly reactive ·OH and hydroxyl species (OH*) bound to the Fe atom sites. This process had an activation energy of 0.19 eV, which could be easily overcome at room temperature. This step was crucial for determining the ·OH-generating capacity of Fe-SAzyme. Therefore, elevated temperatures facilitated more efficient overcoming of the energy barrier, thereby enhancing ·OH production efficiency. Finally, OH* could revert to its initial state via two pathways. Pathway I involved ·OH directly desorbing from OH*. Pathway II involved reacting with H+ in a weakly acidic environment to form H2O molecules, which subsequently desorbed from the Fe-SAzyme surface. Calculations indicated that Path I possessed a higher energy barrier (2.87 eV), making it challenging to proceed spontaneously. In contrast, Path II benefited from the abundant H+ supply in the tumor microenvironment and a further reduction in system energy (−0.24 eV), rendering it more favorable for reaction occurrence. Consequently, Fe-SAzyme restored its initial state by forming and desorbing H2O, enabling cyclic activation of H2O2 to generate ·OH and achieve sustained catalysis.

Additionally, Fig. 3c revealed that the O2pz orbital in H2O2 exhibited identical hybridized electronic states with the 3dz2 orbital of the Fe atom at 1.234 eV, indicating strong interactions between Fe-SAzyme and H2O2. The charge evolution of H2O2 in Fig. 3d and Table S6 confirmed the charge transfer during the POD-like catalytic process of Fe-SAzyme, leading to ·OH generation. In summary, DFT calculations demonstrated that Fe-SAzyme possessed the capability for highly efficient cyclic catalysis of H2O2 to produce ·OH, with its POD-like activity being particularly prominent under acidic conditions, providing a reliable theoretical basis for its application in tumor therapy.

Cellular uptake and anti-tumor effect of the Fe-SAzyme in Vitro

The cellular uptake and mitochondrial localization of Fe-SAzyme-TPP were examined in 4T1 cells using Rhodamine B-labeled nanoparticles, DAPI nuclear stain, and MitoTracker Green mitochondrial fluorescent probe. After 4 h incubation, red fluorescence from Rhodamine B-labeled Fe-SAzyme-TPP was observed entering tumor cells and overlapping with green fluorescence from mitochondria (Figs. 4a, S20), confirming that Fe-SAzyme-TPP effectively targeted and accumulated in tumor cell mitochondria. Simultaneously, calculations revealed a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.86 ± 0.02 for mitochondrial targeting, indicating a strong positive correlation between the degree of nanoparticle aggregation and mitochondrial targeting efficiency. Flow cytometry further quantified the time-dependent cellular uptake (Fig. S21), showing an increase in Rhodamine B positivity with prolonged co-culture duration. Quantitative ICP-OES analysis confirmed that Fe-SAzyme-TPP was absorbed more readily by cells than Fe-SAzyme (Fig. S22). Biological transmission electron microscopy imaging visually demonstrated the intracellular internalization of Fe-SAzyme-TPP (Fig. 5e). These results indicated that Fe-SAzyme-TPP was efficiently internalized by tumor cells and localized to mitochondria, supporting its potential to facilitate the induction of mitochondrial dysfunction and promote tumor cell apoptosis.

The in vitro antitumor effect of Fe-SAzyme-TPP was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 4b, the viability of 4T1 cells decreased with increasing Fe-SAzyme-TPP concentration and was further attenuated under NIR-II laser irradiation. This enhanced cytotoxicity was attributed to the POD-like and GSH-Px-like catalytic activities of Fe-SAzyme-TPP, which led to the generation of ·OH radicals and the depletion of antioxidants, inducing oxidative stress damage in cells. Furthermore, NIR-II irradiation induced hyperthermic injury, and the elevated temperature further amplified its catalytic effects, synergistically promoting tumor cell death. In contrast, HUVEC cells maintained high viability even at 120 µg/mL Fe-SAzyme-TPP (Fig. S23), indicating that Fe-SAzyme-TPP exhibited no significant cytotoxic effects in normal cells and possessed excellent biosafety.

The intracellular level of ·OH was detected using the ROS-sensitive fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. As shown in Figs. 4c and S24a, green fluorescence was observed in cells co-incubated with Fe-SAzyme, confirming that POD-like activity for generating ·OH depended on the incorporation of the Fe atom. Cells treated with Fe-SAzyme-TPP exhibited stronger green fluorescence, indicating improved biocompatibility and enhanced cellular internalization, which amplified ·OH-mediated tumor cell damage. Furthermore, the addition of NIR-II further increased fluorescence intensity, suggesting the elevated intracellular ROS levels, which were attributed to the photothermally enhanced POD-like and GSH-Px-like activities, leading to increased intracellular ROS accumulation.

Elevated ROS levels disrupt cellular redox homeostasis, inducing oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, which in turn trigger ferroptosis. This process generates products such as malondialdehyde (MDA) [55]. Consequently, MDA levels are commonly used to assess the degree of cellular lipid oxidation. Figure 4d demonstrated that MDA levels increased with prolonged co-incubation time of Fe-SAzyme-TPP (120 µg/ml) with cells. However, the addition of Ferrostatin-1 significantly suppressed MDA levels (Fig. S25). This indicated that Fe-SAzyme-TPP, upon entering cells, persistently promoted ROS accumulation, inducing oxidative stress in 4T1 cells and thereby triggering ferroptosis.

GSH levels were monitored in 4T1 cells after different treatments using the DTNB assay kit (Fig. 4f). Fe-SAzyme-TPP induced a higher GSH depletion compared to Fe-SAzyme. At the same time, NIR-II radiation further reduced the levels of GSH. These results indicated that Fe-SAzyme-TPP significantly enhanced the ability to deplete intracellular GSH of Fe-SAzyme. This effect was amplified by photothermal conversion, thereby limiting ROS scavenging and promoting lipid peroxidation. GPX4, an intracellular lipid peroxidation inhibitor, exhibited reduced expression and impaired activity when intracellular GSH was depleted [56, 57]. GPX4 expression was markedly reduced in Fe-SAzyme-TPP-treated cells, particularly under NIR-II irradiation, whereas Ferrostatin-1 suppressed this reduction (Figs. 4g, S26), consistent with the above findings. This confirmed that Fe-SAzyme, acting as a GSH-Px-like nanocatalyst, depleted GSH and disrupted the GPX4-mediated antioxidant pathway, thereby diminishing cellular antioxidant defense levels. The combined use of Fe-SAzyme-TPP and PTT further reduced intracellular GSH levels, decreased the clearance of ROS, and exacerbated lipid peroxidation.

RDPP exhibits reduced red fluorescence upon oxygen exposure, serving as a probe to monitor intracellular oxygen production [37]. Gradual red fluorescence decay was observed in the Fe-SAzyme, Fe-SAzyme-TPP, and Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II groups (Figs. 4c and S24b), confirming that Fe-SAzyme exhibited CAT-like activity to generate oxygen intracellularly. Enhanced mitochondrial targeting and NIR-II irradiation significantly amplified this activity. Furthermore, hypoxia induces the upregulation of HIF-1α expression in tumor cells [58]. Intracellular HIF-1α expression was higher under hypoxic conditions and lower under normoxic conditions. After treatment with Fe-SAzyme-TPP, hypoxic cells exhibited significantly lower HIF-1α expression levels compared to untreated hypoxic cells, yet remained higher than those under normoxic conditions (Fig. 4e). This indicated that although Fe-SAzyme-TPP was unable to restore cellular oxygen levels to those found in a typical environment, it could alleviate tumor hypoxia to some extent.

The cell cloning assay provided a direct reflection of tumor cell proliferation capacity. Analysis of colony formation revealed reduced proliferation in cells treated with Fe-SAzyme-TPP. At the same time, the Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II group exhibited the fewest colony clusters (Fig. 4h). This indicated that Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II treatment strongly inhibited tumor cell proliferation. Concurrently, a cell scratch assay was used to evaluate migration capacity across treatment groups (Fig. S28). After 24 h, the scratch in the control group showed significant healing, confirming active migration in 4T1 cells. In contrast, the healing rate was 65% after Fe-SAzyme treatment and 49% after Fe-SAzyme-TPP treatment. The Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II group exhibited the lowest healing rate at 20%, demonstrating the most substantial migration inhibition effect. This confirmed that photothermal synergistic nanocatalytic therapy could significantly block tumor cell migration.

Collectively, the above results confirmed that Fe-SAzyme-TPP could fully leverage the enzymatic activity of nanocatalysts to inhibit and kill tumor cells. On one hand, Fe-SAzyme-TPP generated ROS through POD-like activity while simultaneously depleting GSH levels via GSH-Px-like activity. This disrupted the ROS production-clearance balance in tumor cells, causing a sharp increase in ROS levels. This induced oxidative stress damage in tumor cells, ultimately leading to ferroptosis. On the other hand, Fe-SAzyme-TPP also induced intracellular O2 production, improving the hypoxic tumor microenvironment and inhibiting tumor proliferation and metastasis. This demonstrated exceptional catalytic therapeutic potential against tumors of Fe-SAzyme-TPP.

Cell viability was assessed using a calcein-AM/PI double staining method. As shown in Figs. 5a and S24c, the Fe-SAzyme-TPP-treated group exhibited more red fluorescence than the Fe-SAzyme-treated group, indicating increased cell death. The Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II-treated group demonstrated the most severe cell death, where cells also progressively lost their typical morphology. Furthermore, apoptosis in tumor cells across different treatment groups was quantitatively assessed using flow cytometry (FCAS) with Annexin V-FITC/PI staining. As shown in Fig. 5b, compared to the Fe-SAzyme group, tumor cells co-cultured with Fe-SAzyme-TPP exhibited an apoptosis rate of 19.88%, indicating a certain degree of enhanced apoptosis. The Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II group demonstrated the highest apoptosis rate at 38.88%. These results were primarily attributed to the combined effects of enhanced cellular uptake, direct photothermal damage, and photothermally enhanced enzymatic activity. Apoptosis was validated by measuring changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) within tumor cells using the JC-1 probe. Under normal conditions, JC-1 aggregated as polymers within mitochondria, emitting red fluorescence. When mitochondria were damaged, membrane permeability altered, and membrane potential decreased, causing JC-1 to convert into monomers that emitted green fluorescence. Figure 5c showed green fluorescence was observed in Fe-SAzyme, confirming enzymatic activity-induced mitochondrial damage. The green/red fluorescence ratio was higher in the Fe-SAzyme-TPP group than in the Fe-SAzyme group, indicating enhanced targeting and uptake efficiency, which led to more severe mitochondrial damage. The Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II treatment group exhibited the highest green/red fluorescence ratio, signifying the most severe reduction in MMP and cellular damage within this group. Bcl-2 and BAX are key proteins associated with apoptosis. As apoptosis increases, Bcl-2 protein expression gradually decreases while BAX protein expression progressively increases. Figure 4g results indicated that Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II induced the highest level of apoptosis, followed by Fe-SAzyme-TPP. This finding was consistent with the aforementioned experimental results.

Biological TEM (bio-TEM) clearly demonstrated changes in cellular state and structure before and after Fe-SAzyme-TPP treatment. Mitochondrial morphology in control cells remained intact with distinct cristae. After 24 h of therapy with Fe-SAzyme-TPP, uptake of Fe-SAzyme-TPP into tumor cells was observed, accompanied by mitochondrial atrophy, reduced cristae, and increased membrane density (Fig. 5e). These changes align with the morphological hallmarks of ferroptosis [31]. This further validated the ability of Fe-SAzyme-TPP to induce cellular oxidative stress and trigger ferroptosis. Ferroptosis is a regulated cell death mechanism triggered by excessive intracellular iron accumulation, leading to lipid peroxidation [26]. To further validate the occurrence of ferroptosis, we assessed intracellular Fe2+ levels using a specific fluorescent staining. Results showed faint Fe2+-associated red fluorescence in Fe-SAzyme-treated cells. In contrast, fluorescence intensity progressively increased in Fe-SAzyme-TPP and Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II groups (Fig. 5f). Following co-culture with Ferrostatin-1 and subsequent treatments, intracellular Fe2+ levels were significantly reduced in the cells (Fig. S27). These findings confirmed the capacity of Fe-SAzyme-TPP to induce ferroptosis.

Subsequently, we investigated the mechanism of Fe-SAzyme-TPP in PTT. HSP70, a protective protein within cells, increases in expression when cells undergo thermal damage. Inhibiting HSP70 expression reduces cellular heat shock protection and enhances thermal sensitivity. Therefore, Western blotting was performed to detect HSP70 levels in cells and analyze the effect of Fe-SAzyme-TPP on HSP70 expression. Under simulated physiological conditions at 37 °C, HSP70 exhibited low expression. Raising the temperature to 53 °C significantly elevated HSP70 expression in the untreated cells, whereas Fe-SAzyme-TPP treatment markedly suppressed HSP70 expression (Fig. 5d). This demonstrated that Fe-SAzyme-TPP significantly inhibited HSP70 expression, thereby enhancing the high-temperature-induced destruction of tumor cells.

Based on the above experiments, we observed that Fe-SAzyme-TPP played distinct roles in tumor therapy by synergistically catalyzing multiple enzymatic activities, collectively driving ferroptosis in tumor cells. During the initial ROS amplification phase, POD-like activity predominated. In the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment, it catalyzed H2O2 to generate abundant ·OH radicals, triggering intense oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, thereby laying the foundation for ferroptosis. Subsequently, it leveraged GSH-Px-like activity to disrupt antioxidant defenses by depleting the key antioxidant GSH, thereby weakening cellular antioxidant capacity. This maintained and exacerbated ROS-induced oxidative damage, synergistically advancing ferroptosis. Subsequently, it synergized with hypoxia relief and treatment. Its CAT-like activity decomposed H2O2 within tumors to produce O2, alleviating tumor hypoxia while enhancing photothermal efficacy, forming sustained spatiotemporal synergistic therapy.

In summary, the triple enzyme-like catalytic activities of Fe-SAzyme-TPP enabled effective synergistic precision intervention in the tumor microenvironment: POD-like activity initiated ROS storms, GSH-Px-like activity dismantled cellular resistance, and CAT activity optimized therapeutic conditions. Furthermore, Fe-SAzyme-TPP’s outstanding photothermal performance demonstrated remarkable potential for tumor photothermal therapy, significantly enhancing thermal damage in tumor cells. The photothermal effect not only directly killed cells but also accelerated the reaction rates of enzyme-like catalysis through the generated heat, synergizing with the aforementioned catalytic pathways to induce ferroptosis. This confirmed that the combination of Fe-SAzyme-TPP with NIR-II irradiation constituted a highly efficient tumor treatment strategy, synergistically driven by catalytic and photothermal therapies. Through multi-mechanism, multi-stage synergistic actions, it collectively led to tumor cell death.

In vivo antitumor effect

Based on the remarkable antitumor efficacy demonstrated by Fe–SAzyme–TPP in vitro, we established 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse models to evaluate its synergistic antitumor effect with photothermal therapy in vivo (Fig. 6a). Before in vivo administration, its blood compatibility was assessed via a hemolysis assay. After incubating mouse red blood cells with different concentrations of Fe–SAzyme–TPP, the hemolysis rate in all tested groups remained below 3% (Fig. 6b), indicating negligible hemolytic activity and confirming its biological safety in vivo.

Subsequently, to investigate the antitumor efficacy of Fe-SAzyme-TPP in 4T1 tumor-bearing Balb/c mice, the mice were randomly divided into five groups: (I) PBS control group, (II) NC, (III) Fe-SAzyme, (IV) Fe-SAzyme-TPP, and (V) Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II treatment group. First, we investigated the photothermal effect of Fe-SAzyme-TPP in vivo. A thermal imaging camera monitored tumor temperature changes 24 h after tail vein injection of Fe-SAzyme-TPP and PBS under NIR-II laser irradiation. As shown in Fig. 6c and S29, after 5 min of irradiation, tumor temperatures in Fe-SAzyme-TPP-injected mice significantly increased to over 50 °C. In contrast, no noticeable temperature rise was observed in the PBS-injected group. This indirectly confirmed that Fe-SAzyme-TPP effectively circulated to tumor sites via tail vein injection, underwent photothermal conversion, and suppressed tumor growth by achieving effective therapeutic temperatures.

During a 15-day treatment period, tumor volume and body weight were measured every two days (Fig. 6d, e). Mice in the PBS and NC groups exhibited rapid tumor growth with no significant tumor suppression. The Fe-SAzyme group demonstrated a tumor growth inhibition rate of 48.7%, revealing the catalytic therapeutic effect of Fe-SAzyme. The Fe-SAzyme-TPP group exhibited more potent antitumor effects, indicating that enhanced tumor targeting and cellular uptake contribute to the enhancement of Fe-SAzyme’s antitumor efficacy. Impressively, the Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II treatment group demonstrated the most potent tumor suppression, achieving a growth inhibition rate of 88.4%. Throughout the entire treatment period, no significant changes in body weight were observed in any of the groups of mice, indicating that the aforementioned treatment method exhibited extremely low systemic toxicity. Finally, mice from all groups were euthanized, tumors were excised for imaging and weighing (Fig. 6f, g), clearly revealing the optimal antitumor efficacy of the Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II group. These results demonstrated that the combined application of Fe-SAzyme-TPP nanocatalytic therapy and PTT effectively inhibited tumor growth.

Histopathological analysis further elucidated the mechanism of tumor destruction (Fig. 6h). H&E-stained sections from the Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II group exhibited the most severe nuclear condensation and membrane disruption, with these morphological alterations clearly indicating tumor cell necrosis and apoptosis. Ki-67 staining revealed the lowest cellular proliferation activity in this group, while TUNEL assays confirmed the highest number of apoptotic cells.

The results above demonstrated that Fe-SAzyme-TPP could be safely administered intravenously and effectively circulated to tumor regions. Upon NIR-II irradiation, it induced localized temperature elevation in the tumor area, leading to tumor cell inhibition and death. This approach significantly minimized damage to healthy tissues, enabling precise tumor treatment.

Immunohistochemical staining revealed that Fe-SAzyme-TPP + NIR-II significantly suppressed HIF-1α expression, with reduced brown staining areas indicating effective alleviation of tumor hypoxia (Fig. 7a). Concurrently, a marked decrease in GPX4 expression was observed, reflecting enhanced lipid peroxidation and the occurrence of ferroptosis (Fig. 7b). These results confirmed that the triple enzyme-like activity of Fe-SAzyme-TPP could be realized in vivo and amplified under photothermal conditions. To further elucidate oxidative stress-induced damage in tumors, 4-HNE immunofluorescence staining revealed that cells emitted red fluorescence upon addition of Fe-SAzyme. The red fluorescence slightly intensified after adding Fe-SAzyme-TPP. Following the introduction of NIR-II, the red fluorescence reached its peak intensity, indicating that Fe-SAzyme induces oxidative stress (Fig. 7c). The severity of cellular damage increased upon TPP modification and NIR-II excitation. To investigate the in vivo expression of HSP70, we employed a carbon framework with NIR-II as a positive control. Figure 7d demonstrated that HSP70 expression was significantly reduced following the addition of Fe-SAzyme-TPP. This confirmed that Fe-SAzyme-TPP could inhibit heat shock protein activity in vivo and enhance tumor thermosensitivity, thereby improving the therapeutic efficacy of PTT.

Biodistribution of Fe–SAzyme–TPP was assessed by ICP-OES after 24 h of intravenous administration. As shown in Fig. 7g, Fe–SAzyme–TPP exhibited significant accumulation in tumor tissue (11.08% ID/g), with a level exceeded only by those in the liver and spleen. Fe-SAzyme and Fe-SAzyme-TPP were labeled with DiR and administered intravenously to tumor-bearing mice. An vivo animal fluorescence imaging system was used to observe the distribution of Fe-SAzyme and Fe-SAzyme-TPP 24 h post-injection. Samples were collected from tumors and major organs for in vitro imaging. Figure 7e demonstrated that Fe-SAzyme-TPP effectively accumulated in tumor tissues 24 h post-injection, whereas Fe-SAzyme exhibited lower accumulation levels. This distribution pattern indicated that Fe-SAzyme accumulated minimally at tumor sites via the EPR effect, whereas TPP modification enhanced tumor penetration of Fe-SAzyme-TPP, confirming its tumor-targeting capability. Additionally, significant aggregation of the nanomaterials was observed in the liver and spleen, suggesting that these organs served as primary clearance pathways for the nanoparticles. These findings highlighted the superior tumor-targeting capability of Fe–SAzyme–TPP, providing strong support for its potential for clinical translation. In addition, blood routine and biochemical measurements showed no significant changes (Fig. S30), indicating that Fe-SAzyme-TPP did not induce marked systemic toxicity in vivo. Major organs (liver, heart, lungs, spleen, and kidneys) were collected and stained with H&E. The results showed no significant damage or pathological abnormalities in any of the examined organs (Fig. 7f), indicating satisfactory biosafety of Fe-SAzyme-TPP. The results collectively demonstrated that Fe-SAzyme-TPP constituted a safe and reliable tumor treatment modality, exhibiting excellent catalytic therapeutic efficacy in vivo. Under photothermal activation, it leveraged thermal damage and heat-enhanced catalytic activity to achieve highly efficient antitumor effects.

Although this study had demonstrated the short-term therapeutic efficacy and preliminary biosafety of Fe-SAzyme-TPP, its long-term safety remained a critical issue to be resolved for successful clinical translation. Significant accumulation of Fe-SAzyme-TPP was observed in the liver and spleen, likely due to the capture of the nanomaterial by the mononuclear phagocytic system (MPS) following tail vein injection. However, it remained unknown whether these nanozymes persist in the long term or degrade via macrophage-mediated oxidative reactions. Therefore, subsequent studies must elucidate their in vivo biodegradation pathways through long-term, dynamic monitoring of metabolic products. Furthermore, prolonged retention of nanomaterials might induce delayed secondary toxicity, such as persistent inflammatory responses or immune activation. To advance the genuine clinical translation of carbon-based nanozymes, we must thoroughly investigate their long-term biosafety, establishing a robust theoretical foundation that encompasses degradation mechanisms and potential toxicity, to ensure the practical application of nanomaterials in the future.

Conclusions

In this study, a spherical mesoporous iron single-atom nanoenzyme (Fe-SAzyme-TPP) modified with PEG and pyridine triphosphate TPP was successfully constructed, exhibiting triple enzyme-like catalytic activity, outstanding photothermal conversion performance, and efficient cellular uptake. The atomic-level dispersed Fe–N sites served as catalytic centers, exhibiting POD-like activity in tumor microenvironments to generate ·OH. Concurrently, its GSH-Px-like activity depleted intracellular GSH, disrupting antioxidant defense systems and promoting ROS accumulation, ultimately inducing ferroptosis. The CAT-like activity alleviated tumor hypoxia by O2 production, thereby enhancing treatment sensitivity and inhibiting tumor metastasis. Furthermore, Fe-SAzyme-TPP exhibited an outstanding photothermal conversion efficiency under the NIR-II irradiation, inducing localized temperature elevation to achieve thermal damage. Most importantly, both in vitro and in vivo studies confirmed that NIR-II irradiation further enhanced the enzymatic activity of Fe-SAzyme-TPP, enabling synergistic nanocatalysis and PTT that significantly boosted antitumor efficacy. Surface modification with PEG and TPP simultaneously improved biocompatibility, promoted tumor-targeted accumulation, and intensified oxidative damage and ferroptosis. The mesoporous structure of this nanozyme offered potential for future applications as a nanocarrier. Collectively, this study demonstrated that the combined application of NIR-II photothermal therapy and Fe–SAzyme–TPP-catalyzed treatment represented a highly promising strategy for efficient tumor therapy.
