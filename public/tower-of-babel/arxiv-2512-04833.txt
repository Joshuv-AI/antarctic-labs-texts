Counterfactual Explanations for Power System Optimisation
Benjamin Fritz, Waqquas Bukhsh

Abstract
Enhanced computational capabilities of modern decision-making software have allowed us to solve increasingly sophisticated optimisation problems. But in complex socio-economic, technical environments such as electricity markets, transparent operation is key to ensure a fair treatment of all parties involved, particularly regarding dispatch decisions. We address this issue by building on the concept of counterfactual explanations, answering questions such as “ Why was this generator not dispatched? ” by identifying minimum changes in the input parameters that would have changed the optimal solution. Both DC Optimal Power Flow and Unit Commitment problems are considered, wherein the variable parameters are the spatial and temporal demand profiles, respectively. The thereby obtained explanations allow users to identify the most important differences between the real and expected market outcomes and observe which constraints have led to the solution. The framework uses a bilevel optimisation problem to find the counterfactual demand scenarios. State-of-the-art methods are compared with data-driven heuristics on the basis of computational efficiency and explanation accuracy. Results show that leveraging historical data from previously solved instances can provide significant speed benefits and allows us to derive explanations in cases where conventional methods would not be tractable.

I
Introduction

Optimisation software is at the core of solving many operational, planning and maintenance problems arising in modern power systems, where they introduce improved capabilities of enhanced decision-making and thereby facilitate significant economic savings.
But while computational advances have profoundly improved the speed of state-of-the-art solvers, and have expanded their applications in power systems, much less attention has been paid to
explaining
the outputs of such tools.

This becomes particularly crucial in the face of the ongoing democratisation and decentralisation of electricity systems, making them inherently more complex to interact with. In
Great Britain (GB), the system operator now accepts bids from assets with capacities below 1 MW in the
Balancing Mechanism
(BM)
[
1
]
, aiming to boost flexibility and cost-efficiency by leveraging the growing share of distributed energy resources (DER). From an optimisation perspective, this adds decision variables and expands the dimensionality of the decision space, an effect further magnified by the increasing coupling of power, heat and transport sectors.
Meanwhile, advances in solver efficiency have enabled harder problem formulations involving integer variables, non-linear constraints and multiple objectives, adding further complexity.

Despite offering improved modelling capabilities, such sophisticated models undermine a crucial aspect of multi-stakeholder decision-making: the explainability of black-box optimisation outputs. Explainability in machine learning (ML) has gained increasing attention with the rise of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI).
Most of the works in the field of
Explainable Optimisation
have emerged only very recently, mostly focusing on interpretability, i.e., deriving an easily comprehensible set of rules mapping instances to solutions. Tree-based methodologies (e.g.,
[
2
,
3
]
) build on the notion that decision-trees as inherently interpretable models. In this vein – but with direct application to power system optimisation – references
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
employ decision trees to introduce interpretability to the DC Optimal Power Flow and Unit Commitment problems, respectively.

While data-driven interpretability techniques can provide useful insights, they are unlikely to fully replace conventional optimisation tools in power systems. Instead,
explainability
methods aim to reveal the reasons for individual model outcomes, thereby allowing the justification of single optimal decisions. In GB, the debate on “
skip-rates
” has increased pressure on the operator to improve transparency in the BM
[
6
]
. A “
skip
” occurs when a seemingly uneconomical balancing action is chosen over an in-merit alternative. Stakeholders requesting reasons for such decisions would thus directly benefit from improved explainability.

Overall, we find that there is a gap of approaches aiming to explain individual solutions to power system optimisation problems.
We address this by proposing a framework based on counterfactual explanations (CEs) that builds on preliminary findings from a previous study
[
7
]
, but we extend it to the UC problem, generalise the methodology, and derive supporting theoretical principles. The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section
II
introduces the concept CEs, followed by a discussion of how CEs can be employed in a power system context in Section
III
. We present two illustrative case studies for DCOPF and UC instances respectively, exemplifying the functioning of our approach.
Section
IV
defines methods to efficiently compute CEs from bilevel problems. The experimental setup used to evaluate the performance of the proposed methodology and the accompanying results are presented in Section
V
. Section
VI
concludes the paper.

II
Counterfactual Explanations for Optimisation

Counterfactual explanations (CEs) fall into the realm of local, example-based explainability. Here, “
local
” refers to the idea that CEs explain a single instance of the model’s output with respect to (w.r.t.) a given input – as opposed to
global
explanations, which aim to characterise the overall decision behaviour across the entire input space.
In this way, CEs help users to identify cause-effect relations between the model inputs and outputs, allow exploring desired “what-if” scenarios, and are advocated for by studies in social and cognitive sciences

[
8
]
.

II-A

General Concept

While the concept of CEs can be applied analogously for ML models, this paper studies their application in the area of mathematical optimisation. We consider the following generic optimisation problem:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{*}\quad\in

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\quad

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})

(1)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle g_{i}(\mathbf{x},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})\geq{0}\quad,\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{C}\quad,

where the solution
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is the minimiser of an objective function
f
and
\MTversion
greekfont
\Omega
is a set of constant input parameters (e.g., for a linear optimisation problem this set may be
\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}=\{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\}
). The problem is subject to a set of constraints
{g}_{i}
collected in the set
\mathcal{C}
.
Now consider a situation in which an optimal solution
\mathbf{x}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}^{*}
deviates from the user’s intuitive expectation of what a typical solution for a given parameter set
\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}
should look like. A CE then answers the following question:
“
How can the input parameters
\MTversion
greekfont
\Omega
be minimally perturbed, such that the new solution falls into the region in which the user expected it to be?
”
In revealing this information, the CE helps the user to better understand the impact of the parameters on the solution and identifies the key parameter changes that characterise the difference between the expectation and the actual decision.

II-B

Bilevel Problem Formulation

The nearest counterfactual explanation can be found from a bilevel optimisation problem which is solved after the result of problem (
1
) is available.
Here, the goal is to find the parameter set
\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}
minimally perturbed from
\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}
, subject to the user’s definition of a
solution region

{\mathcal{X}}
in which they would have expected to find the solution (this may even be a single point).
Note that, generally,
{\mathcal{X}}\cap\mathbf{x}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}^{*}=\emptyset
, since otherwise the trivial solution

\displaystyle\left\lVert\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}-\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right\rVert_{1}

(2a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{y}\,:\,{g}_{i}(\mathbf{y},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})\geq 0,\forall i\in\mathcal{C}}\mkern 5.0muf(\mathbf{y},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})

(2b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}

(2c)

\displaystyle\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}}\mkern 5.0mu,

(2d)

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
is the
\ell_{1}
distance measurement (which, with slight abuse of notation, is here assumed to be taken element-wise for each entry of each subset within
\MTversion
greekfont
\Omega
). In theory, other distances measurements can be employed at this stage. We opt for the
\ell_{1}

Constraint (
2d
) is added to limit the range in which the parameters
\MTversion
greekfont
\Omega
are allowed to vary. By defining the
mutable parameter set

\mathcal{M}_{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}}
, we may limit the number or nature of mutable input parameters.

III
Explaining Generator Dispatch Decisions

Thus far only a generic optimisation of shape (
1
) was considered. The following sections introduce how to generate CEs for solutions to two well-studied power system optimisation problems – the DC Optimal Power Flow (DCOPF) and the Unit Commitment (UC) problem. Arguably, both of these problems lead to counterintuitive market outcomes (e.g., a more expensive generator was chosen over a cheaper unit), and thus motivate the usage of CEs.

III-A

DC Optimal Power Flow Formulation

We consider a transmission network where
\mathcal{N}
,
\mathcal{E}
,
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}
denote the sets for nodes, lines between nodes, generators and demands, respectively. The corresponding cost-minimising DCOPF problem is given as

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{p}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}}

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbf{c}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}

(3a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mkern-9.0mu\sum_{\{n,m\}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}}_{n,m}=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}_{g}-\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{d}\mkern 5.0mu,\mkern 5.0mu\forall n\in\mathcal{N}\quad(\bm{\lambda}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{KCL}}})

(3b)

\displaystyle p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}}_{n,m}=(\delta_{n}-\delta_{m})b_{n,m}\mkern 5.0mu,\mkern 5.0mu\forall\{n,m\}\in\mathcal{E}\quad(\bm{\lambda}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{KVL}}})

(3c)

\displaystyle-\overline{{\mathbf{p}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}}\leq{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}}\leq\overline{{\mathbf{p}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}}\quad(\underline{\bm{\lambda}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}},\overline{\bm{\lambda}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}})

(3d)

\displaystyle\underline{{\mathbf{p}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}\leq{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}\leq\overline{{\mathbf{p}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}\quad(\underline{\bm{\lambda}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}},\overline{\bm{\lambda}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}})\quad,

(3e)

where

is the incremental generation cost,
{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|\mathcal{G}|}

III-B

Unit Commitment Formulation

The UC formulation implemented in this paper implements a reduced version of
[
9
]
.
We aim to minimise the total operating cost over a time horizon
\mathcal{T}
:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{P}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}},\mathbf{U}}

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\Bigl[\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\underbrace{c_{g1}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{PR}}}p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}_{g,t}+c_{g0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{PR}}}u_{g,t}}_{\mathrm{(i)}}+\underbrace{c_{g}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SU}}}u_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SU}}}}_{\mathrm{(ii)}}+\underbrace{c_{g}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SD}}}u_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SD}}}}_{\mathrm{(iii)}}\Bigr].

(4a)

Here, (i) is the production cost, while (ii) and (iii) are the start-up and shut-down costs of unit
g
, respectively.
The continuous dispatch variables
p_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}\in\mathbf{P}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{G}|\times|\mathcal{T}|}
and the binary commitment variables
u_{g,t}\in\mathbf{U}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{G}|\times|\mathcal{T}|}
span the decision space. The start-up and shut-down variables
u_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SU}}},u_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SD}}}
are directly implied by the choice of
\mathbf{U}
, see constraint (
4e
). For later usage we define
{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}=\bigl[\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{d,1},\ldots,\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{d,|\mathcal{T}|}\bigr]
. Thus note that while
{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}
spanned along the nodal dimension for the DCOPF, it here stands for the net demand profile in every time step.

The following constraints are imposed onto the problem.

Power balance constraint
: Since transmission constraints are neglected in the UC formulation used in this work, individual nodal power balances do not need to be satisfied. Merely the overall system power needs to match.

\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}_{g,t}=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{d,t}\,\left(=P^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{t}\right)\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}

(4b)

Generator power limits
: Enforce on/off coupling between
u_{g,t}
and
p_{g,t}
, and on-state generator limits.

u_{g,t}\underline{p}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}_{g}\leq p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}_{g,t}\leq u_{g,t}\overline{p}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}_{g}\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T},\,\,\forall g\in\mathcal{G}

(4c)

Ramping limits
: The generator ramp rates are bounded by upper
R_{g}^{\,\uparrow}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and lower
R_{g}^{\,\downarrow}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
ramping limits.

-R_{g}^{\,\downarrow}\leq p_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}-p_{g,t-1}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}\leq R_{g}^{\,\uparrow}\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T},\,\,\forall g\in\mathcal{G}

(4d)

Logic constraint
: Ensures the assignment of the correct binary variables to
u_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SU}}}
and
u_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SD}}}
.

u_{g,t}-u_{g,t-1}=u_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SU}}}-u_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SD}}}\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T},\,\,\forall g\in\mathcal{G}

(4e)

Minimum up- and downtime
: The minimum number of periods that the unit must be online
T^{\mathrm{U}}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and offline
T^{\mathrm{D}}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
are guaranteed by the constraint below. The constraint further removes the possibility of a unit simultaneously starting up and shutting down as it dominates over the less strict constraint
u_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SU}}}+u_{g,t}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SD}}}\leq 1
.

\sum_{\tau=t-T^{\mathrm{U}}_{g}+1}^{t}u_{g,\tau}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SU}}}\leq u_{g,t}\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T},\,\,\forall g\in\mathcal{G}

(4f)

\sum_{\tau=t-T^{\mathrm{D}}_{g}+1}^{t}u_{g,\tau}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{SD}}}\leq 1-u_{g,t}\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T},\,\,\forall g\in\mathcal{G}

(4g)

III-C

Which question are we trying to find an explanation to?

The definition of the solution region
\mathcal{X}
depends on the nature of the question being asked. In power systems, such questions could be posed by the market regulator, asking the operator why a certain dispatch decision was made. In GB, this situation has led the operator to maintain a regularly updated
Dispatch Transparency
dataset, containing all actions taken in the BM for a given time interval, the reasons for them and alternative actions that were dismissed
[
10
]
.
Motivated by this insight, we make the two following assumptions:

Assumption 1

(Dispatch-restricting solution region)
.

The solution region for the DCOPF is merely restricted by the following condition:

\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{DCOPF}}:=\{p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}_{g^{\prime}}\geq P_{g^{\prime}}\}\,\cap\,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|\mathcal{G}|-1},

(5)

translating to the question of why generator
g^{\prime}
had not been dispatched above a specified minimum threshold
P_{g^{\prime}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, while all other dispatch values are free to vary.

For the UC problem, the solution region is defined as

\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{UC}}:=\{u_{g^{\prime}\mkern-1.0mut^{\prime}}=1\}\,\cap\,\{0,1\}^{(|\mathcal{G}|-1)\times|\mathcal{T}|},

(6)

i.e., asking why unit
g^{\prime}
was not committed at all during the time period
t^{\prime}
.

Assumption 2

(Solution Region Feasibility)
.

The solution region
\mathcal{X}
is defined such that it contains at least one point which is feasible w.r.t. the original problem (
1
), and, more restrictively, feasible w.r.t. problem (
2
), i.e., there exist a parameter variation for which at least one solution in
\mathcal{X}
becomes optimal.

If assumption
2
is dropped, one may still identify minimal parameter changes that would make problems (
1
) and (
2
)
feasible
by computing a Minimal Intractable System (MIS)
[
11
]
. Alternative ways of defining the solution region are theoretically possible. For instance, one could ask how parameters would need to be minimally modified to lower the flow across a congested line. Given that problem (
1
) is convex, one may even formulate
\mathcal{X}
as to impose constraints on dual variables, allowing for questions such as “Why is the
price
at this bus higher than
30\text{\,}\$\mathrm{/}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}\mathrm{h}
today?”.

III-D

What should the explanation look like?

The structure of the CE is also influenced by the user’s definition of the mutable parameter set
\mathcal{M}_{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}}
. It generally makes sense to ask oneself which problem parameters introduce the variability that ultimately leads to different solutions. The DCOPF and UC problems are both used in operational contexts, and thus require to be solved multiple times a day. If we would like to know why today we obtain a different optimal solution than yesterday, the main sources of variability are demand, renewable energy availability, and generator bids. This paper focuses on the former, and hence, makes the final following assumption:

Assumption 3

(Demand-based parameter variation)
.

The mutable parameter set is defined as

\mathcal{M}_{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}}=\left\{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}\,:\,{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\,\wedge\,\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}\backslash\{{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}\}=\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\backslash\{{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\}\right\}\,,

(7)

i.e., demand parameters can vary freely, while all other parameters are fixed to their factual values in
\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}
. The objective function (
2a
) thus reduces to
\left\lVert{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}-{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right\rVert
.

CEs defined by other parameter variations (such as those based on generator cost bids) are theoretically possible and would – at least for the DCOPF – not require a solution approach different from those presented in the following section. While the network structure itself is assumed to be fixed, users modelling long-term (stochastic) power system planning problems may also be interested in varying parameters such as line characteristics or perturbing the input scenarios.

Illustrative Examples

III-E
1
DC Optimal Power Flow

The DCOPF as described in (
3
), is a linear problem (LP). LPs are often perceived as the simplest among optimisation problems, yet we argue that the impact of DCOPF network constraints can yield unintuitive solutions – even for small instances. For this, we study the following example, which was already introduced in
[
7
]
.

Consider the network shown in Figure
1
, which is based on the
case5_pjm__api
found in
[
12
]
. For simplicity, assume that
(\underline{p}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}},\overline{p}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}})=(0,500)\,[$\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}$]
are the output bounds for all generators and all lines have a limit of
\overline{p}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}}=$240\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}$
. The susceptance and cost values are unchanged from the original data, where the latter is given as
\mathbf{c}=(14,15,30,40,10)\,[$\$\mathrm{/}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}$]
. Running the DCOPF problem (
3
) using
{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}=(300,480,140,600,20)\,[$\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}$]
as the “factual” demand scenario, we get the optimal dispatch
{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}*}=(500,306.2,485.9,14.1,233.8)\,[$\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}$]
. Evidently, this dispatch does not follow the merit-order. The dual variables indicate that four lines are congested (as shown in the left part of Figure
1
), with the maximum value
\underline{\lambda}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}}_{4,5}=$53.2\text{\,}\$\mathrm{/}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}$

Since
g_{5}
is the cheapest unit, however, not fully dispatched, the user asks why it was not dispatched at least, say 400 MW. The unique, minimal demand perturbation that satisfies this condition, is described by the variations shown in the right part of Figure
1
, and consists solely of an increase of

Although

is increased to the specified 400 MW threshold, the remaining generators need to adjust correspondingly. Interestingly, the demand variation alleviates congestion on line {1,4} rather than {4,5}, despite the latter exhibiting the highest dual value. Through this hypothetical scenario, the CE establishes a causal link between the demand at

and the optimal dispatch of
g_{5}
.

2
Unit Commitment

For the UC problem, factors such as ramping limits, minimum up- and downtime constraints, or a unit’s high minimum stable generation limit can lead to out-of-merit solutions
[

]
. Further, the combination of different cost factors (incremental, fixed, start-up, shut-down) may prohibit a straightforward economic comparison of the generators.

Consider the test case defined by the generator data shown in Table

g_{3}
is dispatched in the morning, while
g_{2}
is used in the evening. Overall,

has a higher operating cost than

by

IV
Generating CEs via Bilevel Programming

In the previous examples, we assumed that we can readily access the counterfactual demand scenario, without explicitly stating how the minimal perturbation was derived. We subsequently propose approaches to generate CEs for the DCOPF and UC problems. In ML, CEs are commonly derived via gradient descent methods due to the absence of constraints
[
14
]
. But, as presented in problem formulation (
2
), generating CEs for optimisation problems necessitates a bilevel formulation, where the optimality of the lower-level problem is explicitly enforced. The following sections present common strategies to solve bilevel problems with linear, or mixed-integer linear problems (MILP) at the lower level.

IV-A

Linear Problems

For cases where the lower-level problem is linear, solution techniques commonly resort to reformulating the problem into a single-level one, enforcing the optimality of the lower-level problem by adding optimality constraints
[
15
]
.
These solution techniques generally start from either of the two following reformulations: (i) via the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions or (ii) via the strong duality theorem. In this paper, we use KKT conditions, while the reader is referred to
[
16
]
for an approach employing strong duality. Assuming both
f
and
g
in (
2b
) to be continuous and convex in
x
, the KKT-based single-level reformulation of (
2
) reads

\displaystyle\mkern-10.0mu(\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}\,,\,\mathbf{x}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}^{*})\mkern 5.0mu\in\mkern 5.0mu

\displaystyle\argmin_{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}\,,\,\mathbf{x}}\mkern-20.0mu

\displaystyle\mkern 25.0mu\left\lVert\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}-\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right\rVert_{1}

(8a)

s.t.

\displaystyle g_{i}(\mathbf{x},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})\geq 0\mkern 5.0mu,\mkern 5.0mu\forall i\in\mathcal{C}

(8b)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x})-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda_{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})=\bm{0}

(8c)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\cdot g_{i}(\mathbf{x},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})=0\,\,,\forall i\in\mathcal{C}

(8d)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}

(8e)

\displaystyle\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}}

(8f)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda}\geq\bm{0}\quad,

(8g)

where
\bm{\lambda}
are the dual variables linked to the constraints in
\mathcal{C}
. (
8c
) and (

) enforce stationarity and complementarity respectively, and thereby replace the previous optimality constraint (
2b
).
A fundamental difficulty in this formulation is the complementarity constraint (
8d
) – containing bilinear terms, due to the multiplication of dual variables
\bm{\lambda}
with primal variables
\mathbf{x}
or mutable parameters within
\MTversion
greekfont
\Omega
. This turns the problem into a non-convex, non-linear optimisation problem, complicating its solution with off-the-shelf solvers. Note that this issue may be circumvented by specifying a fully fixed solution requirement, i.e., replacing (
8e
) with
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}
. Indeed, the resulting inverse optimisation problem would then also be linear
[
17
]
. This would, however, come with a profound loss of generality – even more for problems with many decision variables. The following paragraphs present commonly used strategies to address the non-linear nature of the above problem.

Special Ordered Sets

The complementarity conditions within (
8d
) tell us that for any constraint
i
, both
\lambda_{i}
and
g_{i}(\mathbf{x},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})
cannot simultaneously take non-zero values. This property can be enforced by leveraging so-called
Special Order Sets
of type 1, a special class of constraints that allows only one member of a set of variables to assume a non-zero value. Constraints of this type can directly be defined in off-the-shelf solvers such as
[
18
]
. This method is later referred to as
SOS1
.

Fortuny-Amat-McCarl Linearisation

Another standard approach is based on the Fortuny-Amat-McCarl linearisation, which relies on introducing binary variables, thereby transforming the problem into a Mixed Integer Problem (MIP).
It builds on the notion that complementarity constraints can be replaced by “
big-
M
” constraints. The complementarity condition (
8d
) is hereby replaced by two auxiliary constraints

0\leq\lambda_{i}\leq z_{i}M_{i}\,\,,\,\,\forall i\in\mathcal{C}

(9a)

0\leq g_{i}(\mathbf{x},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})\leq(1-z_{i})M_{i}\,\,,\,\,\forall i\in\mathcal{C}\quad,

(9b)

where
z_{i}
is a binary variable introduced for each inequality constraint. A drawback is that the choice of appropriate values for
M
is a challenging task in itself
[
19
]
. Chosen too small and the global optimum could be excluded from the feasible region. Conversely, when set too large, numerical instabilities may occur. This paper follows the procedure explained in
[
20
]
. For the primal variables, the
M
values are directly obtained from the given constraint bounds. Since the dual variables are not explicitly bounded, we assume that we can access a database of previous runs from where we can find the maximum
\lambda_{i}
ever recorded. These maximum values are then multiplied with a safety factor of 10 to obtain the respective
M
values. We henceforth refer to this method as
MIP
.

IV-B

Mixed-Integer Linear Problems

We now study the case where a vector of binary variables
\mathbf{u}
is included in both objective function
f
and constraints
g_{i}
. In contrast to the bilevel linear problem, the reformulation via KKT conditions or strong duality theorem is rendered invalid in the presence of integer variables. Due to the lack of easily implementable lower-level optimality constraints for MILPs, the range of exact solution algorithms is much sparser than for the linear case. Existing methods to globally solve bilevel MILP problems usually build on the following lower-level inequality formulation (here adapted to immediately fit the CE problem structure):

\displaystyle\min_{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}\,,\,\mathbf{x}\,,\mathbf{u}}\mkern 5.0mu

\displaystyle\left\lVert\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}-\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right\rVert_{1}

(10a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mkern 5.0mug_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})\geq 0\mkern 5.0mu,\mkern 5.0mu\forall i\in\mathcal{C}

(10b)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})\leq\min_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v}}\left.\begin{cases}f(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}):\\
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v},\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})\geq\bm{0}\end{cases}\mkern-20.0mu\right\}\mkern 2.0mu,\forall\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{U}(\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})

(10c)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\in{\mathcal{X}}

(10d)

\displaystyle\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}}\mkern 5.0mu.

(10e)

Here,
\mathcal{U}(\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})
is the set of
all possible
combinations of binary decision vectors yielding a feasible solution for a given set of parameters
\MTversion
greekfont
\Omega
. Hence, if constraint (
10c
) is satisfied for all vectors
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{U}(\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$})
, and the respective continuous variables
\mathbf{y}
are forced to assume minimising values (e.g., via KKT conditions), then the resulting triplet
(\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}\,,\,\mathbf{x}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}^{*},\mathbf{u}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}^{*})
is a global minimiser to (
10
).

Clearly, the upper formulation may not be implementable in practice, due to the exponentially large number of combinations within
\mathcal{U}
. To address this inherent computational difficulty, we implement the decomposition algorithm proposed in
[
21
]
, which iteratively adds new unique combinations of
\mathbf{u}
to
\mathcal{U}
. The decomposition algorithm splits the bilevel MILP into a master problem and a subproblem. Within each iteration, the master problem solves (
10
) for the currently minimal parameter variation of
\MTversion
greekfont
\Omega
, which are then fixed in the subproblem. The subproblem is then used find new combinations of
\mathbf{u}
. By doing so, the algorithm successively grows the lower bound of
\left\lVert\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}-\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right\rVert_{1}
until a parameter variation
\MTversion
greekfont
\Omega
is found that produces a MILP solution
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})
which inherently satisfies (
10d
) (at which point the constraint could as well be dropped). Due to space constraints, we do not contain a full outline of the algorithm, for which we refer the reader to the authors’ original work. This method is hereafter called
DECOMP
.

IV-C

Data-Driven Heuristic Cuts

Operators usually solve OPF or UC problems repeatedly, often as frequent as every 15 minutes
[
22
]
. Adding to a wealth of studies (
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
, to name a few) that have demonstrated performance improvements by using data for both DCOPF and UC problems, this paper aims to leverage historical data to speed up the solution of (
8
) and (
10
).

We consider the linear case first. Assume that the lower-level LP has been solved for
S
times, each time varying the input parameters. A dataset
\mathcal{S}=\{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$},\mathbf{x}^{*},\bm{\bm{\alpha}}\}_{j=1}^{S}
is thereby obtained, where
\bm{\bm{\alpha}}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{C}|}
indicates whether a constraint is binding or not. While in theory, the number of possible combinations of
\bm{\bm{\alpha}}
could be exponentially large, it has been shown that for many DCOPF problems, only a few combinations actually occur in practical settings
[
26
]
. This also manifests itself in the existence of subsets
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
at whose indices the constraints are always active or inactive, i.e.,
\alpha_{i}^{[j]}=1\,,\,\forall i\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\,,\,\forall j\in[S]
and
\alpha_{i}^{[j]}=0\,,\,\forall i\in\mathcal{C}_{0}\,,\,\forall j\in[S]
. We use this observation by adding cuts to the Fortuny-Amat-McCarl linearisation – fixing
z_{i}=1
if
i\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
or
z_{i}=0
if
i\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
. The goal of this method is to reduce the number of binary variable combinations and thereby improve the solution speed. This method is subsequently called
MIP+cut
.

For a lower-level MILP the access to dual variables is prohibited due to non-convexity.
But similarly as for the active constraint indicators, one may also find subsets of the binary variables that remain constant throughout all recorded samples, i.e.,
u_{i}^{[j]}=1\,,\,\forall j\in[S]
and
u_{i}^{[j]}=0\,,\,\forall j\in[S]
. In the master problem of the decomposition algorithm, these variables are fixed to either 0 or 1, resulting in cuts on the binary decision space.
An additional step to improve the performance is taken by bounding the objective function term
\left\lVert\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}-\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}\right\rVert_{1}
. This is done by finding the single sample within
\mathcal{S}
for which
\MTversion
greekfont
\Omega
is minimally distanced from the factual instance, and add a constraint to bound CE objective function from above. The method resulting from these data-driven cuts and bounds for the decomposition algorithm is referred to as
DECOMP+cut
.

Unlike other data-assisted optimisation methodologies, the above two methods avoid the sometimes non-trivial process of choosing the right ML model (and training it with a suitable set of hyperparameters). Instead, this approach purely relies on the information contained in the dataset.
Problems arise when the solution region
\mathcal{X}
is specified in a way that the optimal CE solution
\{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}},\mathbf{x}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}^{*}\}
to (
8
) (or
\{\text{\MTversion{greekfont}$\Omega$}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}},\mathbf{x}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}^{*},\mathbf{u}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}^{*}\}
to (
10
)) would require to produce a new, unseen
\widetilde{\bm{\bm{\alpha}}}\notin\mathcal{S}
that contradicts
\mathcal{C}_{1}
or
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, which would inhibit the recovery of the nearest CE parameters. In the worst case, one cannot find any feasible solution, despite its existence. Nevertheless, this problem would prevail for any ML surrogate aimed at approximating the optimal solution policy.

IV-D

Upper bound for CE Objective

Both for DCOPF and UC CE problems we rely on MILP formulations to implement the aforementioned solution methods. State-of-the-art MILP solvers generally employ branch-and-bound techniques, which can substantially benefit when provided additional objective function bounds. We thus make use of the following valid upper bound.

Proposition 1
.

For both DCOPF and UC CE problems, whose objective is to minimise the distance
\left\lVert{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}-{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right\rVert_{1}
, subject to fulfilling a solution requirement in line with assumption
1
, the following inequality holds at optimality

\left\lVert{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}-{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}*}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}-{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}*}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}\right\rVert_{1}.

(11)

Proof.

Provided in the appendix.
∎

In other words, the demand variation needed to satisfy a user-defined requirement on the optimal dispatch is at most equal to the resulting variation in the dispatch solution space. This inequality was thus added as an explicit constraint to all of the methods introduced so far.

V-B

Performance Metrics

Speed

The ability to quickly generate explanations would be highly desirable in real-life applications. If, for instance, a control room engineer is requested to justify their dispatch decision to affected stakeholders, a long delay could possibly add to the already existing time pressure in such environments. Furthermore, different generators may simultaneously ask for explanations, which could lead to a prohibitively long queue of CE generation problems.

Let
T_{\mathrm{run}}
denote the runtime. For CE problems addressing DCOPF instances,
T_{\mathrm{run}}
is measured for all of the proposed methods by recording the wallclock time of the solver. In the case of the UC CE problems,
T_{\mathrm{run}}
represents the total time required by the decomposition algorithm, and hence also includes the time of adding new cuts to the master problem. An upper time limit of 10 minutes is set on the solution time for both DCOPF and UC CE problems.

Minimality

Based on the definition in
II
, CEs are
minimal
changes to the factual parameters such that a user-defined solution region yields optimal solutions.
We define two indicators to measure the minimality of a CE. For a given counterfactual demand scenario
{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}
the first indicator, termed
peak-normalised distance
(PND), is computed as

\Delta_{\mathrm{PND}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}\left({\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}\right)=\frac{\left\lVert{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}-{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}\right\rVert_{1}}{P_{\max}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}}\times 100\%

(12)

where
P_{\max}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}
is the single maximum net system demand that occurred among all of the
S
samples.
To further obtain a meaningful estimate of how each method performs w.r.t. a primitive benchmark method, let

\Delta_{{k\mathrm{NN1}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}\left({\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}\right)=\frac{\left\lVert{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}-{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{CE}}}\right\rVert_{1}}{\left\lVert{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}-{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{k\mathrm{NN1}}}\right\rVert_{1}}\times 100\%

(13)

where
{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{k\mathrm{NN1}}}=\min_{i\in[S]}\bigl\{\bigl\lVert{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}-{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}[i]}\bigr\rVert_{1}\,\,:\,\,p_{g^{\prime}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}[i]}>P_{g^{\prime}}\bigr\}
is the counterfactual demand variation which one would obtain when simply choosing the demand sample nearest to
{\mathbf{p}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}
that satisfies the solution requirement on
p^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{G}}}_{g^{\prime}}
for the DCOPF and on
u_{g^{\prime}}
for the UC, i.e., carrying out a
k
-nearest neighbours look-up with
k=1
. After all, if the solutions to the algorithmic approaches lead to
\Delta_{{k\mathrm{NN1}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}
values mostly close to 100%, then their usage would be hardly justifiable, since the
k
NN1 method is much easier to implement. Evidently, the lower both
\Delta_{\mathrm{PND}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}
and
\Delta_{{k\mathrm{NN1}}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{D}}}
of a CE, the better the tested method performed on answering the respective question.

nodal

A second insight is that the speed improvements added by the data-informed cuts in
DECOMP+cut
are not as substantial as it was the case for
MIP+cut
. For instance, the 1.25x performance gain achieved via the data-driven method for
case89
is rather marginal. Further studies need to be carried to better interpret this behaviour, in particular with respect to the mathematical structure of the decomposition algorithm itself. A key factor could be the iterative nature of the decomposition algorithm, alternating between solving the master problem and the UC subproblem. While the cuts allow to solve both problems more efficiently, there is no reason to expect that the cuts will consistently reduce the number of iterations needed.
Nevertheless,

still manages to converge to a CE on average 3.77% more often than

and may thus be used as a fall-back option in case

VI
Conclusion

This paper develops an explainability framework based on counterfactual explanations (CEs), aimed at explaining dispatch decisions suggested by an optimisation solver for the DC Optimal Power Flow (DCOPF) and the Unit Commitment (UC) problem. We focus on answering why a certain generator was not dispatched as expected by revealing how the electric demand pattern would need to be changed such that a more usual dispatch configuration was restored. To derive CEs for optimisation problems we rely on bilevel problem formulations, for which we compare various solution techniques w.r.t. their computational speed and optimality. For the DCOPF, it is shown that obtaining exact CEs gets increasingly challenging for larger networks with many decision variables. In such cases, a proposed data-driven heuristic can yield significant overall speed improvements with little sacrifices on the minimality of the obtained explanations. Despite the presence of binary variables in the UC problem, CEs can be found within comparable timeframes as for the DCOPF, facilitated by an iterative decomposition algorithm.

Future work could study the impact of additional security constraints (e.g., N–1) on the explainability of solutions. For the UC problem one could investigate the inclusion of zonal transmission constraints, reserve requirements, or piece-wise production cost curves. Finally, an important step towards a more practical usage of this framework, would be to integrate it into recently emerging natural language processing tools such as
[
29
]
, where users can directly interact with the optimisation software through a dialogue-based interface.
