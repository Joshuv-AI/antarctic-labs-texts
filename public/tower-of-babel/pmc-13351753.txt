Exercise-induced β-hydroxybutyrate contributes to cognitive improvement in aging mice
Authors: Lian Wang, Liwei Mao, Danlin Zhu, Ke Li, Haoyang Gao, Muge Zhou, Jiabin Wu, Dan Yang, Ze Wang, Wenhong Wang, Yifan Guo, Yingying Xu, Peijie Chen, Weihua Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Aerobic exercise elevated circulating β-hydroxybutyrate (β-HB) levels and improved cognitive performance in aging mice.•Loss of 3-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase 1 (BDH1) impaired endogenous β-HB production and attenuated exercise-induced cognitive benefits.•Exogenous β-HB mimicked exercise effects in wild-type mice but showed limited efficacy in BDH1-deficient mice.•Activation of the β-HB/G protein-coupled receptor 109A–peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (GPR109A–PPARγ) axis promoted antioxidant and anti-inflammatory responses that support cognitive function in aging.Keywords: Exercise, β-hydroxybutyrate, BDH1, GPR109A, PPARγ

Introduction

Aging results in a gradual deterioration of physiological functions, elevating the risk of age-associated diseases. This process is characterized by heightened inflammation, weakened antioxidant defenses, impaired autophagy, increased apoptosis, and disrupted mitochondrial biogenesis, collectively escalating the risk of chronic conditions, particularly neurodegenerative disorders.1 Recent advances have expanded the hallmarks of aging to 12 interconnected processes spanning molecular, cellular, and systemic levels, including genomic instability, impaired proteostasis, defective autophagy, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, and chronic inflammation, which collectively drive organismal decline.2 Cognitive decline, motor impairment, and emotional disturbances are hallmark features of brain aging, arising from underlying structural and functional alterations. In aging animal models, significant alterations in dendritic morphology, including reduced proportions of mushroom-shaped and stubby spines, impaired neuronal plasticity, excessive glial cell activation, and decreased release of neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors, have been observed in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus.3 These pathological changes exacerbate brain dysfunction, contributing to cognitive and behavioral impairments. Aerobic exercise has been widely demonstrated to mitigate aging-related damage and provide extensive health benefits.4 It effectively enhances cognitive performance, alleviates mental health conditions like depression and insomnia, and slows the progression of neurodegenerative diseases (NDD).5 Beyond neurological outcomes, exercise also acts as a systemic regulator of immunometabolic homeostasis, reprogramming metabolic and immune pathways across multiple tissues to restore organismal balance and protect against disease progression.6 Recent advances further show that exercise-induced signaling molecules, termed exerkines, are secreted from multiple tissues (e.g., muscle, liver, adipose tissue, and neurons) and act via endocrine, paracrine, or autocrine pathways to regulate metabolic, immune, cardiovascular, and neurological health, thereby facilitating resilience and healthy aging.7 Thus, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying age-related neurodegenerative processes and exploring exercise-based interventions are critical for advancing both clinical and public health strategies.

During fasting or prolonged aerobic exercise, glycogen depletion in the liver and muscles promotes hepatic fat breakdown and ketogenesis, leading to increased circulating levels of β-hydroxybutyrate (β-HB).8 β-HB serves as both an energy substrate for extrahepatic tissues, including the brain, muscles, and heart, and a signaling molecule that modulates pathways involved in neural function and stress responses. These effects include the reduction of inflammation and oxidative stress, regulation of neurotransmitter systems, and modulation of epigenetic processes such as histone acetylation, β-hydroxybutyrylation, and ribonucleic acid (RNA) function, as well as contributing to the maintenance of mitochondrial regulation.9 Accumulating evidence further indicates that β-HB alleviates multiple age-associated pathologies, including neurodegenerative diseases, by modulating inflammatory, oxidative, and epigenetic pathways.10 Recent proteomic and in vivo studies also demonstrate that β-HB regulates proteostasis by insolubilizing misfolded proteins such as amyloid-β, thereby promoting their clearance and mitigating neurotoxicity in aging and Alzheimer’s disease models.11 Collectively, these actions support neural health and may help counteract aging-related pathologies. In liver mitochondria, 3-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase 1 (BDH1) catalyzes the final step of β-HB synthesis, whereas in extrahepatic tissues it facilitates β-HB degradation.12 BDH1 plays a central role in maintaining β-HB homeostasis during fasting and exercise, enabling the generation of β-HB, which is associated with neural benefits under specific physiological conditions.

As an endogenous ligand, β-HB activates hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2/G protein-coupled receptor 109A (HCAR2/GPR109A), a receptor implicated in regulating physiological and pathological processes in the brain, particularly in aging and NDD.13 Activation of GPR109A by β-HB induces anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, which may help alleviate NDD-related symptoms such as cognitive and memory impairments.14 Moreover, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) operates as a key regulator within nuclear receptor pathways, modulating inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial regulation, and autophagy.15 While previous studies suggest interactions between GPR109A and PPARγ, the precise molecular mechanisms linking these pathways remain unclear.16 Additionally, PPARγ binds to promoter regions of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) crucial for neuronal viability as well as cognitive function, highlighting its transcriptional role in supporting neuronal plasticity.17

This study aimed to elucidate how exercise-induced endogenous β-HB contributes to cognitive improvement and modulates pathways associated with cognitive function, with exogenously administered β-HB serving as a tool to simulate and validate the effects of exercise-induced ketogenesis in mitigating age-related cognitive decline. Specifically, we examined the roles of BDH1 knockout and GPR109A knockdown in disrupting β-HB-related cognitive and molecular effects using aged mice and HT22 mouse hippocampal neuronal cells. By investigating the interactions among BDH1, GPR109A, and PPARγ, this research provides mechanistic insights into β-HB’s signaling mechanisms and offers a foundation for potential therapeutic strategies targeting cognitive decline and neurodegenerative diseases in aging populations.

Materials and methods

Animals

Specific pathogen-free (SPF)-grade male C57BL/6J mice were obtained via Gempharmatech (Jiangsu Jicui Yaokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). These animals were separated into 2 age groups: 6-week-old mice, housed until 4 months of age, served as the young control (YC) group, while 6-month-old mice were maintained until 15 months of age and then randomly assigned into four subgroups: old control (OC), aged controls; old exercise (OE), subjected to aerobic exercise intervention; old intervention with placebo (OIPA), receiving physiological saline as the control for β-HB supplementation; and old intervention with β-HB (OIPB), receiving exogenous β-HB supplementation. Experimental interventions commenced at 15 months of age for all aged groups, while the YC group was analyzed in parallel to provide baseline comparisons. All experimental protocols adhered to institutional standards in addition to receiving approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (102772023DW022). The mice were maintained under a regulated environment with 40%–70% relative humidity, 25°C ± 1°C, and a 12-h day–night cycle. Clean water and standard laboratory chow were freely available to them. Experimental interventions commenced at 15 months of age for the aged groups, while the YC group underwent parallel analyses to provide baseline comparisons.

Exercise-induced experiment

Mice in the OE group first underwent a 5-day treadmill adaptation period (ZH-PT/5S; ZhengHua Biological, Changchun, China), running at 12 m/min for 15–20 min daily without incline. After a 2-day rest, they initiated a 16-week moderate-intensity aerobic exercise regimen, consisting of 1 h of continuous treadmill running at 15 m/min, 5 days per week, with no incline.18 The YC and OC groups remained sedentary under identical housing conditions to serve as baseline and aging controls, respectively.

Exogenous β-HB administration

The OIPB group received intraperitoneal injections of β-HB (DL-β-hydroxybutyric acid sodium salt (Cat# H6501, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA); 200 mg/kg body weight (BW)),19 whereas the OIPA group was given an equal volume of saline.20 Injections were administered 5 times per week for 16 weeks. β-HB (Cat# H6501; Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved and diluted in sterile saline before use.

Measurement of β-HB levels in blood

Blood β-HB levels were measured under different conditions to evaluate metabolic responses. Tail vein blood was collected after disinfection with alcohol, and a drop was applied to a ketone test strip. Measurements were performed using an Abbott ketone meter (Model YZB/UK 5991-2012, FreeStyle Optium; Abbott, Witney, UK), and the tail was disinfected with iodine solution after sampling to prevent infection.21 To assess exercise-induced β-HB changes, mice underwent a single 1-h treadmill running session at 15 m/min, with blood samples collected before exercise and at intervals from 15 min up to 24 h post-exercise. To examine β-HB responses to supplementation, mice were given a single injection of β-HB ((Cat# H6501; Sigma-Aldrich); 200 mg/kg BW) or saline, and blood was collected before injection and at intervals from 15 min up to 24 h post-injection. Additionally, β-HB dynamics during fasting were analyzed by subjecting mice to a 24-h fasting period, with blood samples collected before fasting and at intervals from 3 up to 24 h post-fasting.

Systemic BDH1 knockout mice

Systemic BDH1 knockout (KO) mice were generated on a C57BL/6J mice background using Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9) gene editing (GemPharmatech, Nanjing, China). The knockout strategy targeted the Bdh1-203 transcript (ENSMUST00000115227.9), the longest and most representative protein-coding isoform of the gene. Two single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed to delete exons 3 to 6, spanning a 479 bp coding region that includes conserved catalytic domains of the BDH1 protein. The sgRNA sequences were: guide RNA 1 (gRNA1) (5′-CAGTGCATAGCCGGCCGATC-3′, protospacer adjacent motif (PAM): TGG) and gRNA2 (5′-TAGGAAGGAACATGACGTCA-3′, PAM: TGG). The sgRNAs were transcribed in vitro and co-injected with Cas9 protein into fertilized zygotes of C57BL/6JGpt mice. The resulting F0 founders were screened by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and sequencing. Confirmed KO mice were backcrossed with wild-type (WT) mice to establish a stable F1 line. Male BDH1 KO mice at 15 months of age were obtained and housed under standard laboratory conditions. The KO mice were distributed randomly into 5 experimental groups: KC (KO controls); KE (KO with exercise); KEB (KO with exercise and post-exercise β-HB supplementation); KIPA (KO with saline); and KIPB (KO with β-HB supplementation).

Cell culture

HT22 mouse hippocampal neuronal cell line (Cat# CL-0697; Wuhan PriCells Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) with high glucose, 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin under standard conditions (37 °C, 95% air, 5% CO₂). A 1:3 ratio was used for cell passage, and cells in the exponential growth phase (Passages 3–10) were used for experiments.

GPR109A knockdown by small interfering RNA (siRNA) and transfection in HT22

GPR109A-targeting siRNAs were procured from Guangzhou RiboBio Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China), including sequences (5′ to 3′): si-GPR109A-1 (GCTTCAGCATCTGTTACAA), si-GPR109A-2 (GATAATAACCGAAGCACTA), and si-GPR109A-3 (GGCTTCTCTACAAATATAA). To achieve 60%–70% confluence, HT22 cells were prepared 24 h in advance of transfection. For transfection, 245 μL of serum-free DMEM (Gibco) was separately combined with 5 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Cat# 11668019; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) (Solution 1) or 5 μL of siRNA (Solution 2). Both solutions were individually incubated for 5 min, subsequently mixed, and incubated again for an extra 15–20 min. After washing in serum-free medium, cells were replaced with 2 mL of freshly prepared serum-free medium, followed by the dropwise addition of the transfection solution. Following a 5–6 h incubation, complete DMEM supplemented with serum was used to replace the medium, and cells were subsequently cultured for an additional 48–72 h. The efficiency of knockdown was subsequently validated through reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot analysis.

Cell treatment protocols

To establish a D-galactose (Dgal; Cat# G5388; Sigma-Aldrich)-induced cellular aging model, cells were assigned to Dgal at varying concentrations (12.5, 25.0, 50.0, 100.0, and 200.0 mM) for 24 h, while cytotoxicity was assessed to determine the optimal concentration. β-HB treatments (1.25, 2.50, 5.00, 10.00, and 20.00 mM) were tested to evaluate dose–response effects and identify the optimal protective concentration. For PPARγ signaling pathway evaluation, CON (control), β-HB (3 mM), Dgal (100 mM), Dgal + β-HB (100 mM + 3 mM), Dgal + β-HB + 2-Chloro-5-nitro-N-phenylbenzamide (GW9662) (10 μM, PPARγ antagonist; Cat# HY-16578; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), and Dgal + β-HB + Lanifibranor (IVA337) (3 μM, PPARγ agonist; Cat# HY-104049; MedChemExpress) were included in the experimental design. Cells were treated for 24 h before further analysis. For GPR109A knockdown, groups included CON, Dgal (100 mM), Dgal + β-HB (100 mM + 3 mM), Dgal +β-HB + si-GPR109A, Dgal + β-HB + si-GPR109A + IVA337 (3 μM), and Dgal + β-HB + si-GPR109A + GW9662 (10 μM). Cells were harvested following treatment for the analysis of gene and protein levels, as well as apoptosis evaluation.

Behavioral tests

Novel object recognition test

The novel object recognition test took place within an acrylic box (45 cm × 45 cm × 45 cm). In the training session, a pair of matching objects were positioned inside the box, allowing the mice to explore freely for a duration of 5 min. The exploration time for each object was documented. After a 2-h interval, a familiar object and a new object were introduced for testing. Mice were allowed another 5 min of free exploration, and the exploration time for each object was again measured. The discrimination index (DI) was determined using this formula: DI = (Tnovel − Tfamiliar​)/(Tnovel + Tfamiliar​) × 100%, where Tnovel and Tfamiliar​ represent the time spent investigating the novel and familiar objects, respectively.22 A higher DI indicates a greater capability to distinguish between novel and familiar objects. Periods of resting, grooming, or sitting on the objects were excluded from exploration time to ensure accurate measurements of active exploration behavior.

Y-maze spontaneous alternation test

Mice underwent testing in a 3-armed acrylic maze arranged in a Y-shape, each set 120° apart (50 cm × 10 cm × 30 cm; length × width × height). At the start of the test, mice were positioned at the terminal point of an arm and given 5 min to freely navigate the maze. The sequence of arm entries was documented to assess spatial working memory.23 Alternation refers to consecutive visits to each of the 3 arms in sequence (e.g., 1, 2, 3 or 1, 3, 2). The maximum possible alternations were determined by subtracting 2 from the total entries. The alternation percentage was calculated using the formula: Alternation rate = (Actual alternations/Maximum alternations) × 100%. A higher alternation rate and total alternations indicate improved spatial working memory performance.

Y-maze recognition memory test

The Y-maze test assessed spatial recognition memory by examining the mouse's preference for exploring a new arm.24 Mice were placed into a Y-shaped acrylic maze featuring 3 arms, arm “C” was designated as the novel arm, while arm “A” served as the starting arm. Throughout the training phase, arm “C” was restricted, while mice were allowed free access to arms “A” and “B” for 10 min to acclimate to the environment. After a 2-h interval, the test phase began, allowing each mouse to explore all 3 arms, including the newly opened arm “C”, for a duration of 5 min. Data on the frequency of entries and exploration duration for each arm were recorded. A preference for the novel arm, indicated by more frequent entries and longer exploration time, was used to assess recognition memory performance.

Morris water maze

The Morris water maze was utilized to evaluate hippocampus-dependent functions.25 The apparatus included a cylindrical tank, 120 cm wide and 50 cm tall, along with a circular platform of 10 cm diameter, serving as a resting area for the mice. The pool contained 30 cm deep water, kept at 23 °C ± 2 °C, and made non-transparent with white dye. The space was segmented into 4 quadrants of equal size, with the platform positioned in the 4th quadrant, which was designated as the target quadrant. Visible markers were placed on the tank walls to aid navigation, and these markers remained consistent throughout the experiments. The test included 2 phases: training and testing. During the training phase (Days 1–5), the platform was positioned 1.5 cm below the water level. Each mouse completed tasks in all 4 quadrants, with a maximum swimming time of 60 s per trial and a 15-min rest period between trials. On the testing phase (Day 6), the platform was taken out of the target quadrant. Mice were introduced into the 2nd quadrant (opposite the target quadrant) and given 60 s to locate the platform. Mouse movements and data were tracked and analyzed using the Any-maze behavioral tracking system (Stoelting Co., Wood Dale, IL, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy of hippocampal ultrastructure

The fresh hippocampus was trimmed into approximately 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm blocks and transferred to a new EP tube containing fresh electron microscopy fixative (Cat# G1102; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for continuous fixation at 4°C for at least 4 h. Post-fixation involved 1% osmium tetroxide and carried out for 2 h under dark conditions. This was followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series, infiltration, and resin embedding. After polymerization, ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and mounted on 150-mesh copper grids coated with Formvar film. The grids were dried overnight. The ultrathin sections were examined under a transmission electron microscope (HT7800/HT7700; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), and images were captured to analyze the hippocampal ultrastructure.

Genotyping of mice

Tail tissue samples were obtained from 2 to 3-week-old mice and placed into 1.5 mL EP tubes. The tissue was digested in lysis buffer (250 μL; Cat# PD101-01; Vazyme, Nanjing, China) containing Proteinase K (25 μL) at 56 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, the temperature was increased to 95 °C to inactivate enzymes. After digestion, the lysate was vortexed and centrifuged for a duration of 5 min. DNA amplification was carried out directly from the supernatant using a PCR kit (Cat# PD101-01; Vazyme). Electrophoresis was performed on a 1.5% agarose gel and separated at 120 V for 30 min. The resulting bands were visualized using a gel imaging system (GelDoc; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and images were captured for subsequent analysis.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Hippocampal tissue (20–50 mg) or cell samples were processed in 1 mL of Trizol (Cat# 15596018; Invitrogen) for homogenization. The aqueous phase was carefully separated and mixed with chloroform (200 μL); this was followed by a 5-min incubation and 15-min centrifuge at 12,000 revolutions per minute (rpm). RNA was isolated using 600 μL of isopropanol, rinsed three times with 75% ethanol, and reconstituted in diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated water. Reverse transcription utilized the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Cat# RR036A; Takara, Kyoto, Japan). RT-qPCR employed SYBR Green Master Mix (Cat# Q711-02; Vazyme) along with specific primers (Supplementary Table 1) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The PCR process involved an initial denaturation at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation and annealing, and concluded with a dissociation step. Gene expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Protein extraction and Western blot analysis

For hippocampal tissues, frozen tissue samples were weighed and homogenized in lysis buffer (100–300 μL per 10 mg tissue; Cat# P0013B; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) with steel beads at 65 Hz intervals, then centrifuged to separate the supernatant. Cells were lysed with lysis buffer (80 μL per well). The bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Cat# P0010; Beyotime) was used for protein quantification. Each sample (20 μL) was combined with 200 μL of working solution (50:1 ratio of reagents A and B), and the absorbance was recorded at 562 nm. Proteins were denatured by mixing with 5 × loading buffer. Western blotting was performed by separating proteins at 110 V for 1.5 h and transferring onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes at 90 V for 1.5 h. Incubation of primary antibodies and secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table 2) was performed on the membranes. Protein signals were visualized through chemiluminescence, with images analyzed via ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health (NIH), Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was conducted on brain sections and cells following standardized protocols.26 Brain tissues were sectioned using a cryostat at –25 °C. After dehydration in a sucrose gradient (15%–30%), tissues were trimmed, embedded within Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) (Cat# 4583; Sakura Finetek; Torrance, CA, USA), then flash-frozen for 15 min. Slices were prepared at 25 μm thickness, placed on glass slides, and stored in 0.01 M phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at –20 °C until use. Cells cultured on coverslips in 6-well plates were fixed and prepared for staining. Both tissue sections and cells were blocked using 10% normal donkey serum supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin for 60 min under ambient temperature. Primary antibodies were applied at 4 °C overnight. Following triple PBS rinses, secondary antibodies were applied at 20 °C–37 °C for 1 h. To protect fluorescence signals, all subsequent steps were performed in a dim environment. Samples were counterstained and mounted using an antifade medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Imaging was conducted using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), with fluorescence intensity analyzed through ImageJ software (NIH).

Cell viability assay

HT22 cells were distributed into 96-well plates at an estimated concentration of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well, and each well was supplemented with complete medium (100 μL). After incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 24 h, the growth medium was exchanged using either fresh complete medium or medium supplemented with test compounds at designated concentrations. After a further 24-h incubation, 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Cat# C0038; Beyotime) was added directly to each well containing 100 μL of culture medium (final volume 110 μL). Care was taken to avoid bubble formation. Subsequently, the plate was maintained for a 2-h incubation, and optical density at 450 nm was recorded with a spectrophotometer. Cell morphology was observed and documented using an inverted imaging system (Leica).

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining

After discarding the culture medium, HT22 cells in 6-well plates were rinsed with PBS. The cells were treated with 1 mL of β-galactosidase fixative solution (Cat# C0602; Beyotime) and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Next, the staining solution was prepared by combining solutions A (10 μL), B (10 μL), C (930 μL), and X-Gal (50 μL), ensuring thorough mixing. Subsequently, 1 mL of the newly prepared staining mixture was added to each well. Importantly, overnight incubation at 37 °C was conducted outside the CO₂ incubator to maintain optimal pH conditions for β-galactosidase activity. Senescent cells appeared as blue-stained structures, visualized using an optical microscope.

Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate/propidium iodide (V-FITC/PI) apoptosis assay

Following treatment, PBS-washed cells were supplemented with 195 μL of Annexin V-FITC binding buffer (Cat# C1062M; Beyotime) in each well. Subsequently, Annexin V-FITC (5 μL) and propidium iodide (PI) (10 μL) were sequentially added with gentle mixing after each addition. Cells were processed by incubating at 20 °C–25 °C in darkness for 10–20 min, protected from light with aluminum foil, and kept on ice throughout the staining process. Stained cells were immediately visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ti2; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) within 1 h to ensure signal stability. Annexin V-FITC, emitting green fluorescence, marked early apoptotic cells, whereas PI, displaying red fluorescence, stained necrotic or late apoptotic cells with compromised membranes. The proportions of Annexin V+/PI− and Annexin V+/PI+ were calculated using ImageJ software (NIH).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity assay

SOD activity in HT22 cells was measured using a colorimetric assay (Cat# A001-3; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). The resulting cell suspension was gently pipetted into EP tubes and subjected to centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 10 min. The cell pellet was rinsed before being centrifuged again under the same parameters. For ultrasonic disruption, sonication was performed at 300 W for 3–5 s per cycle, repeated four times with 30-s intervals, all conducted in an ice-water bath to prevent protein degradation. Using the BCA assay kit, protein concentrations were quantified by incubating samples, followed by optical density measurement at 450 nm with a microplate analyzer (Eon; BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA).

Database analysis

Publicly available datasets and bioinformatics tools were utilized to complement experimental findings. RNA expression data for BDH1, GPR109A, and PPARγ were accessed via The Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org). Tissue-specific expression patterns of BDH1 were analyzed to guide tissue selection for experimental design, while single-cell and single-nucleus RNA-seq datasets were examined to assess GPR109A and PPARγ expression profiles, focusing on neuronal cells and their relative distribution in glial, endothelial, and mesenchymal populations. To investigate transcriptional regulation, PPARγ-binding motifs in the BDNF promoter region were identified using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.genereg.net). Vertebrate-specific motifs were ranked by binding scores and genomic coordinates, providing insights into motif accessibility and transcriptional regulation. These analyses supported the evaluation of cell-type specificity and gene regulatory mechanisms underlying experimental findings.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out with SPSS Statistics 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and visualized with GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Independent sample t tests were employed for comparing two groups. To compare multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance was conducted, followed by Bonferroni corrections. Morris water maze behavioral data were subjected to χ2 analysis to calculate the percent of discovery platform. All data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was defined as a p value less than 0.05.

Results

Aerobic exercise improves cognitive function in aging mice via endogenous ketogenesis and β-HB-mediated signaling activation

To investigate the role of endogenous ketogenesis and β-HB synthesis, we examined its biochemical pathway in the liver (Fig. 1A). Free fatty acids are metabolized via β-oxidation to produce acetyl-CoA, which is further converted into β-HB by BDH1, enabling its release into circulation as an energy substrate. To assess whether exercise induces this process, aged WT mice underwent a single 1-h treadmill session (Fig. 1B). This acute exercise bout significantly elevated circulating β-HB levels in OE compared to OC, with levels peaking at 3 h post-exercise before gradually returning toward baseline at 24 h (Fig. 1C). A 16-week aerobic exercise intervention was further implemented to examine the sustained impact of exercise on cognitive outcomes (Fig. 1D). Behavioral tests demonstrated that exercise was associated with improved recognition memory, as indicated by higher exploration time and discrimination index in OE compared to OC (Fig. 1E and 1F). Y-maze tests confirmed better working memory and spatial recognition ability in OE (Fig. 1G–1L), while YC outperformed OC, confirming age-related deficits.

Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated higher levels of PPARγ and BDNF in OE mice relative to OC (Fig. 1M–1O), suggesting the upregulation of proteins associated with synaptic regulation and neuronal function. Reduced ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA1) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) intensities in OE (Fig. 1P and 1Q) indicated attenuated glial activation, consistent with a potential anti-inflammatory effect of exercise. Nissl staining (Supplementary Fig. 1B2) further revealed that exercise maintained more intact neuronal morphology across Cornu Ammonis 1 (CA1), Cornu Ammonis 3 (CA3), and dentate gyrus (DG) regions in OE, supporting structural maintenance. Transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 1R) showed better-preserved synaptic ultrastructure, including denser vesicles and intact cleft morphology in OE, while OC exhibited age-related degeneration.

Protein expression analysis (Fig. 1S–1C2) showed that exercise increased the expression of proteins related to ketogenesis (BDH1, GPR109A), neurotrophic regulation (PPARγ, BDNF, cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB)), and markers of mitochondrial dynamics and autophagy (sirtuin 3 (SIRT3), peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α), phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN)-induced kinase 1 (PINK1), Parkin, and Beclin-1) compared to OC. Age-related declines in these pathways were evident from comparisons between YC and OC (Supplementary Fig. 1F–1P), which revealed reductions in ketogenesis enzymes, neurotrophic proteins, and mitochondrial/autophagy markers, as well as decreased c-Fos expression, consistent with reduced synaptic activity and cellular homeostasis. RT-qPCR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1A–1E, 1Q–1A2) further indicated that exercise partially reversed age-related reductions at the gene level, including those associated with synaptic regulation, antioxidant defenses, and inflammatory markers.

Exogenous β-HB supplementation partially recapitulates exercise-associated cognitive benefits and modulates key signaling pathways in aging mice

To determine whether exogenous β-HB supplementation could reproduce some of the effects of exercise-induced ketogenesis, aged WT mice received intraperitoneal injections of β-HB sodium salt (200 mg/kg BW) (Fig. 2A and 2B). A single injection transiently elevated circulating β-HB levels, which peaked at 15 min and returned to baseline within 3 h (Fig. 2C), resembling physiological elevations observed during exercise. Long-term administration (16 weeks) was implemented to evaluate cognitive and molecular responses (Fig. 2D). Behavioral tests demonstrated that OIPB mice spent more time exploring novel objects and exhibited higher discrimination indices in the recognition test (Fig. 2E and 2F). Y-maze results further indicated improved working memory and spatial recognition as reflected by increased alternations and preferential exploration of the novel arm (Fig. 2G–2L). These findings suggest that β-HB supplementation can ameliorate age-related cognitive deficits, showing effects similar to but not identical with those of exercise.

Molecular and structural analyses further supported the association between β-HB supplementation and changes in pathways linked to neuronal function. Immunofluorescence staining showed elevated PPARγ and BDNF levels (Fig. 2M–2O) and reduced IBA1 and GFAP intensities (Fig. 2P and 2Q), findings consistent with attenuated glial activation and potential enhancement of synaptic plasticity. Electron microscopy (Fig. 2R) revealed more intact synaptic morphology with denser vesicles, whereas histological analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2K) showed better-preserved neuronal organization in the hippocampal DG region. Protein and gene expression analyses indicated upregulation of markers associated with mitochondrial regulation and antioxidant defense. Western blot analysis demonstrated increases in GPR109A, PPARγ, BDNF, PGC-1α, Parkin, and Beclin-1 (Fig. 2S–2Y), while RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 2A–2J) revealed elevated transcription of PPARγ, BDNF, CREB, c-FOS, superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), together with reduced expression of inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3). These findings suggest that β-HB supplementation reproduces molecular and behavioral features associated with exercise, engaging overlapping signaling pathways that may contribute to improved cognitive function in aging mice.

BDH1 KO disrupts exercise-induced ketogenesis and attenuates cognitive improvement from both exercise and post-exercise β-HB supplementation

To explore the role of BDH1 in ketogenesis and its contribution to cognitive regulation, systemic BDH1 KO mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing. Genotyping, RT-qPCR, and Western blot analyses confirmed significant reductions in BDH1 expression in both liver and hippocampal tissues of KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3A–3F). RNA expression profiling from The Human Protein Atlas showed high BDH1 enrichment in hepatic tissues and lower levels in the brain (Supplementary Fig. 3G), supporting its predominant metabolic role and the rationale for systemic knockout. BDH1 deficiency impaired ketogenesis as evidenced by significantly attenuated β-HB responses to 24-h fasting and a single 1-h aerobic exercise session in KO mice compared to WT (Fig. 3A–3C). Wild-type mice displayed expected increases in β-HB levels, peaking at 24 h under fasting and at 3 h post-exercise, whereas KO mice failed to achieve similar elevations, indicating reduced metabolic adaptation capacity.

Behavioral tests revealed significant cognitive impairments in KO mice, as evidenced by a diminished ability to differentiate objects during the novel recognition task, reflecting impaired recognition memory (Fig. 3E and 3F). Similarly, Y-maze tests showed reductions in working memory and spatial recognition (Fig. 3G–3L), while Morris water maze performance demonstrated longer escape latencies, reduced target quadrant exploration, and impaired probe trial metrics (Fig. 3M–3T). These behavioral alterations were accompanied by synaptic structural abnormalities, including reduced synaptic vesicles and disrupted cleft morphology as shown by electron microscopy (Fig. 3Z).

To evaluate whether aerobic exercise or post-exercise β-HB supplementation could mitigate cognitive impairments in KO mice, animals were subjected to 16 weeks of intervention (Fig. 3D). Exercise improved recognition memory, evidenced by increased exploration time in the novel arm of the Y-maze (Fig. 3K). For the Morris water maze, post-exercise β-HB supplementation increased target quadrant entries during probe trials (Fig. 3O), although other measures of spatial learning and memory showed no significant improvement.

Molecular and structural analyses highlighted the mechanisms underlying these effects. Immunofluorescence analyses of hippocampal DG regions revealed reductions in PPARγ and BDNF expression, coupled with increased IBA1 and GFAP intensities in KO mice, suggesting heightened neuroinflammation (Fig. 3U–3Y). Exercise was associated with partial restoration of PPARγ and BDNF expression and reductions in glial activation, whereas β-HB supplementation showed limited recovery and was accompanied by higher IBA1 and GFAP levels (Fig. 3V–3Y). Western blot data further supported these trends, showing significant reductions in GPR109A, PPARγ, BDNF, and mitochondrial marker (SIRT3) in KO mice compared to WT (Fig. 3A2–3D2). However, KE and KEB interventions failed to produce statistically significant increases despite trends toward partial restoration. Gene expression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3H–3Q) provided additional insights into molecular mechanisms. Exercise upregulated the expression of genes associated with neuroprotection (GPR109A, PPARγ), antioxidant defense (NRF2), and mitochondrial regulation (TFAM) while downregulating inflammatory markers (IL-1β, NF-κB). In contrast, β-HB supplementation was associated with reduced expression of neurotrophic genes (BDNF and NGF) and increased inflammatory markers (IL-1β, NF-κB). These transcriptional changes were consistent with the enhanced glial activation observed by immunofluorescence, highlighting the context-dependent role of β-HB in modulating protective vs. inflammatory signaling.

These findings indicate that BDH1 deficiency disrupted ketogenesis and enhanced neuroinflammatory responses, contributing to cognitive and structural impairments. Aerobic exercise partially alleviated these effects, whereas post-exercise β-HB supplementation provided limited benefit and appeared to exacerbate inflammatory activation. These results suggest a dual role for β-HB, whereby its effects on aging and neurodegeneration depend on the metabolic and signaling context.

Exogenous β-HB supplementation fails to improve cognition and exacerbates neuroinflammation in BDH1 KO mice

To evaluate whether chronic β-HB supplementation could mitigate cognitive impairments in BDH1 KO mice, we first confirmed β-HB delivery efficiency in the KIPB (β-HB-supplemented) and KIPA (saline) groups. A single intraperitoneal injection of β-HB significantly elevated circulating β-HB levels in KIPB mice compared to KIPA, peaking at 30 min (Fig. 4A and 4B). Unlike WT mice, in which β-HB returned close to baseline within 3 h (Fig. 2C), KO mice maintained significantly elevated levels at 3 h, only normalizing by 24 h. This delayed clearance indicates reduced metabolic utilization of β-HB in the absence of BDH1, consistent with impaired ketone body homeostasis. Following validation, a 16-week intervention with β-HB supplementation or saline was conducted to assess long-term cognitive and molecular outcomes (Fig. 4C).

Behavioral assessments indicated that β-HB supplementation failed to improve cognition. In the object recognition test, the discrimination index remained unchanged, indicating no detectable in recognition memory (Fig. 4D and 4E). Similarly, Y-maze spontaneous alternation tests showed no significant differences in alternations or correct entries (Fig. 4F–4H). Novel arm recognition tests revealed decreased duration in the novel arm without significant changes observed for entries or distance traveled (Fig. 4I–4K), reflecting persistent spatial memory deficits. Morris water maze tests further supported learning and memory impairments. KIPB mice exhibited slower platform acquisition rates during training (Fig. 4M) and fewer entries, shorter durations, and reduced distances traveled in the target quadrant during probe trials (Fig. 4N–4P). Lower platform crossing frequencies (Fig. 4R and 4S) further supported the presence of spatial memory impairments.

Hippocampal immunofluorescence analysis revealed no significant changes in PPARγ (Fig. 4U) but showed reduced BDNF (Fig. 4V) and elevated IBA1 and GFAP intensities (Fig. 4W and X) in KIPB mice, consistent with increased glial activation and neuroinflammatory responses. Electron microscopy (Fig. 4Y) revealed altered synaptic ultrastructure, including fewer vesicles and irregular presynaptic densities. Western blot analysis confirmed reductions in GPR109A, BDNF, CREB, and SIRT3 protein levels in KIPB mice compared to KIPA controls, whereas PPARγ levels remained unchanged (Fig. 4Z, 4A2–4E2), pointing to alterations in receptor-associated, neurotrophic, and mitochondrial proteins.

Gene expression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4) supported these observations. Inflammation-associated markers (IL-1β, TNF-α, NF-κB, NLRP3) were upregulated, while neurotrophic genes (PPARγ, BDNF) were downregulated in KIPB mice (Supplementary Fig. 4B–4G). No significant changes were detected in mitochondrial biogenesis or antioxidant defense genes (Supplementary Fig. 4I–4L), indicating that KIPB mice did not exhibit compensatory activation of these pathways.

These findings demonstrate that chronic β-HB supplementation did not improve cognition and was instead associated with enhanced neuroinflammatory responses in BDH1-deficient mice. Reduced PPARγ and BDNF expression, coupled with elevated IBA1 and GFAP, is consistent with insufficient activation of neurotrophic pathways alongside enhanced glial activation. These effects may reflect impaired GPR109A–PPARγ signaling, limiting metabolic and antioxidative benefits. Furthermore, the altered metabolic context caused by BDH1 deficiency may have constrained β-HB utilization, thereby shifting its impact from potential support of neuroprotective-related pathways toward an association with inflammation and synaptic alterations.

β-HB attenuates Dgal-induced senescence in HT22 cells through PPARγ-associated stress–response pathways

To examine the potential protective effects of β-HB in vitro, HT22 hippocampal neurons were exposed to varying β-HB concentrations (1.25–20.00 mM). Higher doses (10–20 mM) slightly reduced cell viability, while lower doses (2–5 mM) exhibited no cytotoxic effects (Fig. 5A and 5B). These findings established a safe concentration range for subsequent experiments. Oxidative stress and senescence were induced using Dgal at concentrations between 12.5 and 200.0 mM, revealing a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability with an estimated EC50 of approximately 100 mM (Fig. 5C). Accordingly, this concentration was used to generate the Dgal-induced senescence model for further evaluation (Fig. 5D).

β-HB supplementation (2–5 mM) restored cell viability following Dgal treatment, with 3 mM providing optimal protection (Fig. 5E). Senescence-associated activity, assessed by SA-β-gal staining, confirmed that β-HB reduced senescence levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5F and 5G). Morphological analysis revealed that Dgal-treated cells displayed irregular and shrunken shapes, whereas β-HB supplementation preserved healthier neuronal shapes, particularly at 3–4 mM concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 5K). These results indicate that β-HB attenuates oxidative stress-induced cellular senescence. Western blot analysis revealed dose-dependent increases in proteins associated with neurotrophic support (BDNF, CREB) and mitochondrial regulation (SIRT3), with 3 mM β-HB producing the most consistent effects (Fig. 5H–5K). RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that β-HB elevated mRNA levels of genes linked to neuroprotective signaling (GPR109A, BDNF, NGF), antioxidant defenses (SOD2, NRF2), and mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α, TFAM) (Supplementary Fig. 5A–5J). In parallel, β-HB also downregulated inflammatory markers (IL-1β, TNF-α, and NLRP3) (Supplementary Fig. 5D–5F), consistent with reduced inflammation.

To explore whether β-HB’s effects were mediated through PPARγ signaling, Dgal-induced senescent HT22 cells underwent treatment with 3 mM β-HB along with either a PPARγ agonist (IVA337) or antagonist (GW9662) (Fig. 5L). Western blot analysis confirmed that PPARγ activation (IVA337) further enhanced PPARγ, BDNF, CREB, and SIRT3 expression, paralleling the effects of β-HB, while PPARγ inhibition (GW9662) reversed these effects (Fig. 5M–5Q). RT-qPCR analysis similarly showed that β-HB elevated the transcription of neuroprotective-related and antioxidant genes (PPARγ, BDNF, NGF, SOD2, NRF2), while PPARγ inhibition elevated inflammatory markers (NF-κB, NLRP3) (Supplementary Fig. 5L–5R).

These results demonstrate that β-HB protects HT22 cells from Dgal-induced oxidative stress and senescence by engaging PPARγ-dependent pathways linked to neuronal support. The involvement of PPARγ signaling underscores its critical role in mediating β-HB’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions, further supporting PPARγ as a potential therapeutic target for maintaining cellular function under oxidative stress.

PPARγ activation enhances β-HB effects in GPR109A-silenced senescent HT22 cells

Gene expression profiles retrieved from The Human Protein Atlas confirmed high GPR109A levels in neuronal cells (Supplementary Fig. 6K), supporting the use of HT22 cells as a neuronal model. PPARγ expression showed lower cell-type specificity but remained relevant in neuronal regulation (Supplementary Fig. 6L), further justifying its investigation as a downstream mediator of β-HB–GPR109A signaling. Molecular docking analysis indicated that β-HB binds to GPR109A with a binding energy of –4.4 kcal/mol, stabilized by hydrophobic contacts (Val17, Trp88, Leu173, Phe274) and hydrogen bonds (Phe18, Ser175, Ser178) (Fig. 6A). To validate this approach, niacin—a canonical GPR109A ligand—was docked as a reference and showed stronger binding (–5.5 kcal/mol) while engaging largely overlapping residues (Trp88, Leu173, Phe274, Phe18, Ser175) (Supplementary Fig. 6A), reinforcing the reliability of our computational predictions. Protein–protein docking further suggested that GPR109A directly interfaces with PPARγ through a cluster of hydrogen bonds (Phe162, Leu169, Lys170 in PPARγ with His130, Arg215, Met217 in GPR109A) and surrounding hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 6B). These predicted interfaces support the plausibility of a receptor–transcriptional signaling cascade linking membrane GPR109A activation to nuclear PPARγ signaling.

Gene silencing experiments confirmed efficient knockdown of GPR109A at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6C–6E). Among the tested siRNA sequences, siRNA-3 (si-3) showed the strongest silencing efficiency and was selected for subsequent experiments. Experimental groups were designed as shown in Fig. 6F. In the presence of GPR109A knockdown, β-HB showed reduced efficacy: fluorescence staining (Fig. 6G) indicated higher proportions of preliminary stage apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI-, Fig. 6H) as well as terminal apoptotic/necrotic cells (Annexin V+/PI+, Fig. 6I) compared with β-HB treatment alone, and PPARγ and BDNF expression were attenuated (Fig. 6J and 6K). Furthermore, SOD activity was reduced following GPR109A knockdown (Fig. 6L), suggesting elevated oxidative stress in senescent cells. Nevertheless, β-HB supplementation retained partial activity, mitigating apoptosis and partially restoring PPARγ, BDNF, and SOD levels, consistent with its signaling effects through downstream PPARγ.

Western blot analysis showed that under GPR109A knockdown, β-HB’s ability to increase PPARγ, BDNF, CREB, and the mitochondrial marker OPA1 was attenuated. Pharmacological modulation confirmed PPARγ’s role: activation with IVA337 amplified β-HB’s effects on these proteins, whereas inhibition with GW9662 blunted them (Fig. 6M–6Q). RT-qPCR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6B–6J) supported protein-level findings, showing β-HB elevated the expression of genes linked to neurotrophic support (PPARγ, BDNF, NGF), antioxidant defense (SOD2, NRF2), and mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α, TFAM), with both GPR109A knockdown and PPARγ inhibition partially constraining these effects. Inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and NF-κB) were significantly elevated following GPR109A knockdown, but β-HB treatment and PPARγ agonists reduced their levels. Analysis using the JASPAR database identified high-scoring PPARγ-binding motifs in the BDNF promoter region (Supplementary Table 3), suggesting a direct transcriptional mechanism consistent with the observed increases in BDNF at both protein and mRNA levels.

These results suggest that β-HB engages GPR109A–PPARγ signaling to influence antioxidant defenses and apoptosis regulation. Pharmacological modulation further supports PPARγ as an important mediator, indicating that this nuclear receptor may amplify β-HB’s actions through coordinated transcriptional and translational control.

Discussion

This study identifies β-HB as a key mediator linking aerobic exercise to cognitive improvement, primarily via the GPR109A–PPARγ axis to modulate redox balance and inflammatory signaling. By integrating evidence from exercise interventions, exogenous β-HB supplementation, BDH1 knockout mice, and GPR109A-silenced neuronal cells, we show that the inability to generate endogenous β-HB during exercise compromises its downstream signaling and attenuates impaired cognitive benefits. These findings highlight β-HB’s role as a signaling molecule bridging exercise with cognitive improvement, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target to mitigate aging-related cognitive decline.

Aerobic exercise activates multiple metabolic and neuroprotective-related pathways, enhancing hippocampal neurogenesis, cerebrovascular growth, and cognitive function.27 A central mediator of these benefits is β-HB, a ketone body produced during exercise-induced ketosis.28 Elevated β-HB levels, generated through fatty acid oxidation and facilitated by monocarboxylate transporters, provide metabolic support in aging when glucose utilization is impaired.29,30 Beyond serving as an energy source, β-HB functions as a signaling molecule by inhibiting histone deacetylases (HDACs) and activating mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC1α, SIRT3) and antioxidant defenses (NRF2, SOD2), thereby reducing oxidative stress and preserving synaptic plasticity.31 In aging, β-HB levels decline, weakening these protective mechanisms; endurance exercise reverses this decline by elevating circulating β-HB and enhancing its transport across the blood-brain barrier, thereby reinforcing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant defenses.32 Together, these processes underscore β-HB as a molecular link between exercise and the maintenance of cognitive function during aging.

Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, driven by pathways such as NF-κB and NLRP3, are hallmarks of aging and neurodegeneration.33 In this study, exercise reduced NF-κB/NLRP3 activation and TNF-α levels in aged mice, consistent with elevated β-HB, which alleviates oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and synaptic dysfunction while supporting ATP production.34 Exercise also increased PGC1α to promote mitochondrial biogenesis and fusion, and enhanced PINK1-Parkin signaling for autophagy and quality control.35, 36, 37 These effects mirror prior evidence that β-HB and exercise synergistically suppress inflammation and oxidative stress. Beyond metabolism, β-HB and exercise also converge on synaptic regulation: β-HB, as an HDAC inhibitor, elevates hippocampal BDNF, thereby promoting neurogenesis, while exercise activates BDNF–CREB signaling to support synaptic integrity.38,39 These pathways improve neuronal structure and contribute to brain health in aging. Importantly, because endogenous β-HB synthesis is blunted in aging and sustained exercise is often difficult for elderly individuals, exogenous β-HB supplementation emerges as a potential strategy to replicate the neuroprotective benefits of exercise-induced ketosis.

Exogenous β-HB supplementation provides a controlled approach to inducing therapeutic ketosis (1–7 mM) without the restrictive demands of ketogenic diets.40 Ketone salts and esters elevate circulating β-HB, promote fatty acid oxidation, and lower respiratory exchange rates, thereby enhancing metabolic flexibility.41 Unlike ketogenic diets, supplementation increases β-HB levels without markedly altering glucose or insulin, offering a safer and more accessible alternative.42 Once transported into the brain, β-HB supports neuronal health by improving energy metabolism, activating GPR109A, inhibiting HDACs, and suppressing NLRP3-driven inflammation.43, 44, 45 These mechanisms have been linked to functional benefits across neurological disorders, including epilepsy, anxiety, and neurodegenerative diseases, where supplementation improves cognition, reduces neuronal loss, and slows disease progression.46, 47, 48 In this study, chronic β-HB administration alleviated age-related cognitive decline and enhanced mitochondrial (PGC1α, Parkin) and neurotrophic (BDNF) markers, underscoring its therapeutic relevance. Importantly, supplementation compensates for the limited capacity of aged individuals to sustain exercise-induced endogenous β-HB synthesis, highlighting its translational potential for promoting brain health.

Using BDH1 KO mice, we tested whether endogenous ketogenesis is required for exercise-linked benefits. We found that KO animals showed blunted exercise-evoked β-HB elevations and only partial behavioral and molecular gains, underscoring the importance of BDH1-dependent β-HB production in translating exercise signals to brain outcomes.49,50 Notably, whereas exogenous β-HB improved cognition in aged WT mice, the same regimen in KO mice provided limited benefit and was accompanied by higher NF-κB/IL-1β, suggesting that loss of BDH1 activity restricts effective ketone utilization and downstream signaling.51 Consistent with this, recent studies indicate that the anti-inflammatory effects of β-HB are highly time sensitive and context dependent, showing benefit in early metabolic dysfunction but diminished efficacy in advanced type 2 diabetes.52 Moreover, β-HB has been reported to exert pro-inflammatory actions under certain conditions, depending on cell type, concentration, duration, and the presence of co-modulators.53 Collectively, these findings indicate that exogenous β-HB can reproduce aspects of exercise-associated molecular and cognitive adaptations under physiological conditions, but its efficacy may be context dependent when endogenous ketogenesis is impaired. Future work should refine dosing strategies, integrate anti-inflammatory or PPARγ-targeted co-interventions, and apply cell-type-specific metabolic tracing to clarify the conditions under which β-HB acts as a beneficial signal–substrate pair vs. a maladaptive cue.

This study identifies the GPR109A–PPARγ axis as a key integrator of β-HB signaling, linking receptor activation to nuclear transcriptional regulation.54,55 GPR109A activation by β-HB suppresses NF-κB/NLRP3 pathways and pro-inflammatory cytokines, while its knockdown abolished these effects, underscoring its gatekeeper role.56,57 Downstream, PPARγ functions as a transcriptional hub in neurons and astrocytes, coordinating antioxidant defenses and BDNF expression to sustain neuronal integrity and cognitive function.58,59 Our findings show that β-HB’s ability to enhance antioxidant responses was diminished by GPR109A knockdown but partially restored by pharmacological PPARγ activation, indicating a receptor–nuclear signaling cascade. At optimal concentrations, β-HB also increased SOD activity and reduced apoptosis, consolidating its role as a signaling metabolite rather than a passive substrate.60 Collectively, the GPR109A–PPARγ axis provides a mechanistic framework for β-HB’s integration of metabolic, inflammatory, and transcriptional programs, and its disruption limits adaptive cellular responses.

This work advances a receptor–nuclear framework for β-HB signaling in brain aging but has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the evidence for the GPR109A–PPARγ pathway is largely based on marker expression and cell-level perturbations; more direct in vivo validation using pharmacologic blockade or conditional knockout models would help establish causal necessity. Second, analyses of synaptic changes and cellular senescence were mainly based on indirect markers or qualitative observations; incorporating complementary markers would strengthen mechanistic interpretation. Finally, molecular docking suggested receptor and transcriptional interactions but was not confirmed with binding assays so these predictions remain provisional. Future studies incorporating these approaches will be valuable to consolidate this signaling framework and enhance the translational potential of β-HB-based interventions in aging and neurodegeneration.

Conclusion

This study identifies β-HB as a central mediator of exercise-linked benefits, indicating that it acts through the GPR109A–PPARγ axis to coordinate antioxidant defenses and inflammatory balance and, thereby, sustain cognitive performance in aging. Using BDH1 knockout mice, we showed that loss of endogenous ketogenesis markedly weakened the cognitive and molecular adaptations normally elicited by exercise, establishing the necessity of BDH1-dependent β-HB production. Exogenous β-HB supplementation reproduced several exercise-associated signatures in WT mice but showed limited efficacy under BDH1 deficiency, underscoring the context dependence of its actions. Together, these findings position β-HB as a pivotal signaling metabolite that couples exercise to cognitive improvement, and suggest that combining exercise with targeted, physiology-aligned β-HB interventions may offer a translational avenue to mitigate age-related cognitive decline and neurodegenerative risk.
