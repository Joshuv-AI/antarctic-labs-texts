Correlation stabilized anomalous Hall crystal in bilayer graphene
Authors: Zhongqing Guo, Jianpeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAt low carrier densities, interacting electrons can form a Wigner crystal. Rhombohedral multilayer graphene hosts high-order Dirac fermions with nontrivial Berry phases. Using a beyond-mean-field theoretical framework, here we study the ground states of slightly charge-doped rhombohedral multilayer graphene under vertical displacement field. We find a Fermi liquid to trivial Wigner crystal transition at critical densities of ~1-2 × 1011 cm−2, which generally increase with displacement field and layer number. Notably, an anomalous Hall crystal with spontaneous quantized anomalous Hall conductivity emerges at lower densities ~2 × 1010 cm−2 and for dielectric constants ϵr⪅5. This topological anomalous Hall crystal is stabilized over the trivial Wigner crystal due to lower correlation energy gained from dynamical charge fluctuations. Our work identifies slightly carrier-doped bilayer graphene as a promising candidate for realizing the anomalous Hall crystal. Moreover, our method can be readily applied to other interacting 2D systems including moiré superlattices.Subject terms: Topological matter, Quantum fluids and solids

Introduction

When the carrier density is sufficiently low, interacting 2DEG system would undergo a transition from gapless Fermi liquid (FL) state to charge-gapped Wigner crystal (WC) state1–11. The latter spontaneously break spatial translational symmetry, forming a quantum electronic lattice with the lattice constant uniquely defined by the carrier density. Besides conventional 2DEG, various two-dimensional Dirac-fermion models may also be realized in condensed matter systems. It is well known that massless Dirac fermions with linear energy-band dispersion can emerge in monolayer graphene12,13. Moreover, even “n-order" Dirac fermions with energy-momentum dispersion Ek ~ kn may approximately describe the low-energy single-particle excitations in n-layer graphene with rhombohedral stacking14, the lattice structure (plotted using VESTA software15) of which is schematically shown in Fig. 1a. When these gapless “n-order" Dirac fermions are gapped out, e.g., by vertical displacement field, they would acquire non-vanishing Berry curvatures which further endow nontrivial topological properties to these Dirac fermions, as schematically shown in the right panel of Fig. 1b. It is then intriguing to ask what would be the interacting ground states and characteristic single-particle excitations when the density of these topological Dirac fermions is low, as can be realized in slightly charge-doped RMG.

Several fundamental questions need to be answered for such charge-doped RMG. First, is there any WC transition happening when the carrier density in the RMG is sufficiently low? Second, would there be any topological properties associated with the presumable WC state due to non-vanishing Berry curvatures of the system? Previous mean-field calculations suggest that a type of topological Wigner-crystal state with a nonzero Chern number, dubbed as “anomalous Hall crystal" (AHC) state, can be realized in RMG when the number of layers is greater than 316–19. However, it is well known that mean-field treatment drastically overestimates the tendency of Wigner crystallization4,5,20. It is thus crucial to study the fate of the AHC state under quantum fluctuation effects. Lastly, what would be the characteristics of the single-particle spectra of the RMG under slight charge doping, both in the FL state and the (presumable) Wigner-crystal state?

To answer these questions, we develop a “GW + RPA" framework to study both the ground-state properties and the single-particle excitation spectra of generic interacting 2D systems. To be specific, we first calculate the ground-state energy and single-particle spectra of the system under Hartree-Fock approximation, where two trial wavefunctions have been used: one is the WC state that spontaneously breaks translational symmetry, and the other is the FL state. Then, we calculate the frequency-dependent GW single-particle self energy21–24 (schematically shown in the upper panel of Fig. 1c), which gives more accurate descriptions to the single-particle excitation spectra for both WC and FL states. Since Hartree-Fock calculations neglect correlation energy, we further calculate correlation energy with random phase approximation (RPA)25,26 based on the GW single-particle spectra (see lower panel of Fig. 1c). We apply such “GW+RPA" method to charge-doped, n-layer RMG (with n = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6), and find that the critical density for the transition from FL to trivial WC is in the range 1–2 × 1011 cm−2. The critical density typically rises for larger D and increased layer thickness, unless van Hove singularities of the single-particle density of states under some proper displacement fields are reached. Remarkably, for slightly charge-doped bilayer graphene, an anomalous Hall crystal with Chern-number 1 emerges when the carrier density is below ~2 × 1010 cm−2 and when the relative dielectric constant ϵr ⪅ 5, thanks to the more concentrated Berry-curvature distribution in momentum space (see Fig. 1d for schematic illustration). However, such an AHC state becomes unstable when n≥3 due to the more spread momentum-space distribution of Berry curvatures. Counterintuitively, the topological AHC is favored over the trivial WC due to lower correlation energy gained from dynamical charge fluctuations. The AHC is characterized by a real-space charge-density distribution forming an emergent honeycomb lattice, exhibiting ground-state currents winding around the regions of charge-density peaks.

Results

GW+RPA method benchmarked using conventional 2DEG

We first apply the GW+RPA method to a conventional 2DEG system to study its Wigner crystallization problem, which yields significantly improved results compared with HF and HF+RPA calculations. The detailed formalism of the GW + RPA method can be found in the Methods section and in Supplementary Information. The 2DEG system, with its kinetic energy expressed as

serves as a fundamental platform for investigating many-body physics, including the Wigner crystallization and its transition to FL state. In our work, the e–e interaction in 2DEG is described by a dual-gate screened Coulomb interaction as expressed in Eq. (4). The key parameter controlling the WC transition is the dimensionless Wigner–Seitz radius rs=gνgsm*/(πnϵrm0aB), where gν and gs are the (possible) valley and spin degeneracies, respectively, n is the electron density, ϵr is the relative dielectric constant, m* is the effective mass, m0 is the electron mass, and aB is Bohr radius. The Wigner–Seitz radius measures the average inter-electron separation and characterizes the balance between interaction and kinetic energies.

In Fig. 2a, we show the condensation energy, Econd. = EWC − EFL as a function of rs, where EWC (EFL) denotes the total energy of the WC (FL) state. The results from HF calculations, as marked by the purple line, significantly overestimate the stability of the WC state by neglecting electron correlation effects. Under HF approximation, the WC transition occurs at a small critical value rs*~2.2, much smaller than the experimental value 35.1(9)3. The blue line in Fig. 2a shows the results after incorporating RPA correlation energy, which captures collective charge-fluctuation effects and partly corrects HF’s bias towards WC state. Including the RPA correlation energy shifts the WC transition to larger values of rs ~ 3.9, highlighting the critical role of correlations in stabilizing the FL state.

We further refined these calculations using the GW+RPA method, as shown in Fig. 2b. The inclusion of additional GW corrections to the HF bands (labeled by “GW(5-25)” with 5-25 denoting the number of HF bands corrected by GW self energies) progressively shifts the critical rs to larger values, ultimately converges around rs*~19.2 (data fitting is described in Supplementary Information5,20), a value that matches experiment (rs*~35.1) much closer than those without GW corrections. This demonstrates that the combination of GW corrections and RPA correlation energy better captures the delicate balance between exchange and correlation effects, providing a more accurate prediction of the WC transition. Fig. 2

c shows the QP band structure of a fully spin-polarized WC state at rs = 11.4. A gap opens at the Brillouin zone (of the Wigner lattice) boundary due to strong Coulomb interactions. When the GW correction is applied, charge fluctuations screen part of the Coulomb interaction, which reduces both the HF gap and the bandwidth of the WC state. Fig. 2d presents the band structure of the spin-degenerate FL state at the same rs = 11.4. After applying the GW correction, the bandwidth is significantly decreased, leading to a much larger effective mass due to correlation effects. The significant reduction in the bandwidth of the FL state leads to a much larger absolute value of RPA correlation energy, which counterbalances the HF’s bias towards the WC state. Additionally, from the GW calculation, we obtain the QP weight for the FL state, as shown in the inset of Fig. 2d. As rs increases, the QP weight gradually decreases, implying more significant correlation effects. These results suggest the GW + RPA method can faithfully describe the WC transition problem in conventional 2DEG, thus can be readily extended to other 2D systems with long-range Coulomb interactions such as RMG.

Rhombohedral multilayer graphene described by Dirac-fermion models

After being benchmarked in conventional 2DEG, we continue to apply the GW + RPA framework to slightly charge-doped n-layer RMG with n = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. We first start with an n-order Dirac-fermion model which approximately characterizes the low-energy non-interacting Hamiltonian of n-layer RMG14, expressed as:

where νμ = ℏvF(μkx + iky)/t⊥, ℏvF = 5.25 eV ⋅ Å is the in-plane Fermi velocity, and t⊥ = 0.34 eV is the nearest interlayer hopping amplitude, μ = ∓ denotes K/K′ valley of graphene. Δ denotes the Dirac-fermion mass, which physically originates from a vertical electric field applied to RMG. These parameters are all derived from a realistic Slater-Koster tight-binding model of graphene27. For all calculations based on such Dirac-fermion models, we set Δ = 0.2 eV. The above model results from a perturbative expansion of νμ14 in the subspace of the states from the A sublattice of the top layer and B sublattice of the bottom layer, which neglects further-neighbor interlayer hopping terms and other states higher in energy. Later, we will include the next-neighbor hopping terms using a full continuum model including states from all carbon sites, and we will see that the conclusions are qualitatively unchanged.

Before tackling multilayer graphene, we first study the single-particle spectrum of gapless Dirac fermions in monolayer graphene at 10% electron doping with respect to charge neutrality. Besides the main Dirac-cone-like bands, our GW calculations suggest that there are also surrounding satellite features. These satellites arise from electron-plasmon couplings, representing plasmaron (or plasmon-polaron) states, consistent with previous theoretical and experimental reports28–30. This further confirms that our theoretical framework can be safely applied to RMG. Then, we continue to study n-layer rhombohedral graphene (n = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) using the GW + RPA method. Here we assume that at low carrier densities (⪅1011 cm−2), the ground state is fully spin-valley polarized as observed in experiments31,32, so that flavor (spin/valley) fluctuations are expected to make less important contributions to self energy and correlation energy compared to charge fluctuations within a single flavor.

Fig. 3a shows the WC condensation energy as a function of WC lattice constant Ls for bilayer graphene calculated only in HF approximation. Two topologically distinct WC states are obtained: the topologically trivial Chern-number-0 WC state (blue line) emerges for Ls ≥ 160 Å, while the topologically nontrivial WC state with Chern-number 1 (red line) is stable within a finite range 180 Å⪅Ls⪅1000 Å. The Chern-number-0 WC state consistently has lower energy, which is energetically favorable under the HF approximation. The insets in Fig. 3(a) display the real-space electron distributions: the left inset shows that of the Chern-number-1 WC state, where electrons form a honeycomb lattice33, and the right inset shows the Chern-number-0 WC state, where electrons form a triangular lattice. The emergence of the sublattice structure of the honeycomb lattice is crucial for the nontrivial topological properties of AHC, which gives rise to pseudospin degrees of freedom defined in the space of the two emergent sublattices. The nontrivial winding of the pseudospin within the mini Brillouin zone of AHC leads to a nonzero Chern number33. Moreover, given the same Wigner lattice constant, the charge density of the trivial Wigner crystal is more localized compared to that of the AHC, which implies stronger charge fluctuations in the AHC state. Similarly, Fig. 3b presents the results for the pentalayer system (n = 5). The Chern-number-0 WC state (blue line) onsets for Ls ≥ 80 Å, while the Chern-number-1 AHC state (red line) emerges in a smaller range, 90 Å⪅Ls⪅340 Å. Again, the topologically trivial WC state always has lower HF energy. This is because the exchange energy of the trivial WC state is larger (in amplitude) than that of the topological AHC state. Since the results presented in Fig. 3a, b are calculated based on simplified Dirac-fermion models, we further test the validity of these results by performing HF calculations based on realistic continuum models of RMG in the basis of low-energy states of all carbon sites, including next-neighbor interlayer hoppings (see Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). The conclusions remain qualitatively unchanged. Especially, the AHC state becomes less stable for a larger number of layers. In Supplementary Information, we further provide the HF band structures of both topologically trivial and nontrivial WC states for n-layer Dirac-fermion models from n = 2 to 6. A general feature is that the single-particle charge gap of the topological AHC state is much smaller than that of the trivial WC state. Later, we will see that this feature remains unchanged even after including GW self-energy corrections (see Fig. 4d), which eventually makes the AHC state being more energetically stable than the trivial WC state after taking into account correlation effects due to dynamical charge fluctuations. This is reminiscent of the current-loop order in kagome metals, which is proposed to be mediated by dynamical bond-order fluctuations34.

It is worth noting that the Berry curvature of the non-interacting conduction band of the n-order Dirac-fermion model can be calculated analytically, Ω(k,n)=γ2n2Δk2n−22(γ2k2n+Δ2)3/2, where γ=(−1/t⊥)n−1vFn. The Berry curvature Ω(k, n) is peaked at the wavevector kpeak, kpeak=1vFn−1n+212nΔ1nt⊥n−1n. As the number of layers increases, kpeak shifts toward a larger value. HF calculations indicate that whenever the Fermi wavevector kF~4π/3Ls* coincides with kpeak, the system would undergo WC transition with the critical WC lattice constant Ls*. This means that, at the HF level, the WC state would emerge at larger carrier density with increasing number of layers n. Fig. 3c shows the variation of the critical lattice constant Ls* (at the WC transition onsets) as a function of the layer number n. It can be seen that the Ls* estimated using 4π/3kpeak closely matches the actual results extracted from numerical HF calculations. Moreover, the Chern-number-1 AHC states become unstable at large Ls for all of the n-layer systems, because at large Ls the Berry curvature would eventually be pushed out of the first Brillouin zone (of the corresponding WC) completely, thus it is impossible to yield a Chern-number-1 state. Fig. 3d presents the calculated lattice constant at which the Chern-number-1 WC state becomes unstable (denoted by Lsunst.) as a function of n, which is denoted by red filled circles. In the same figure, we also plot 4π divided by the wavevector, marking the half-peak-value of the Berry curvature (denoted as khalf) using solid blue circles. It can be seen that Lsunst. and 4π/khalf almost follow exactly the same trend as n increases. More detailed results of the HF calculations of WC transition in the n-layer systems are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Figure 3e, f illustrate the effects of including RPA correlation energy based on GW-corrected quasiparticle bands for the bilayer system and pentalayer system, respectively. The blue and red lines denote the WC condensation energies of the Chern-number-0 and the Chern-number-1 WC states calculated using the simplified Dirac-fermion models. In both systems, the inclusion of correlation effects through the GW + RPA approach would significantly mitigate the exchange energy bias towards the WC state, shifting the critical lattice constant of the topologically trivial WC phase (denoted by Ls*) from Ls*~200 Å to a much larger value Ls*~1100 Å, corresponding to a critical density ~1 × 1010 cm−2.

Notably, the correlation effects further lower the total energy of the Chern-number-1 WC (also known as AHC) state below that of the Chern-number-0 state, particularly for the bilayer system. As clearly shown in Fig. 3e, for the bilayer Dirac-fermion model, when Ls is greater than a critical value of 700 Å (corresponding to a density ~2.4 × 1010 cm−2), the AHC becomes the ground state over both FL and trivial WC. This is because the energy gap of the AHC state (C = 1 state) is much smaller than that of the trivial WC (C = 0 state), as clearly shown in Fig. 3h. This results in larger charge susceptibility and a lower RPA correlation energy (see Eq. 5) for the AHC state, which ultimately allows it to be the ground state over the topologically trivial WC for the bilayer system. In contrast, in the cases of n ≥ 3, the AHC state becomes less stable because the Berry curvature is peaked at a larger wavevector for larger n, and would be completely pushed out of the first Brillouin zone (of the presumable WC) for large Ls. As a result, our GW + RPA calculations suggest that AHC cannot be the actual ground state of the n-order Dirac-fermion models for n ≥ 3. For example, the WC condensation energy for a pentalayer system (n = 5 Dirac-fermion model) is shown in Fig. 3f. It is clearly seen that AHC is only a metastable state for Ls⪅340 Å, but always has higher energy than the FL state. Similar conclusion holds for n = 3, 4, 6. Figure 3

Trivial Wigner crystals in RMG based on realistic modeling

The Dirac-fermion model given by Eq. (2), though qualitatively captures the low-energy electronic and topological characters of RMG, it may not be able to provide a more detailed and quantitative description to the physical properties of RMG. For example, high-resistance regions developed from charge neutrality are observed in tetralayer, pentalayer and hexalayer rhombohedral graphene systems, which are suspected to be trivial Wigner crystal35,36. These high-resistance states persist up to 1–5 × 1011 cm−2, depending on the number of layers and displacement field, which are much larger than the theoretical critical densities (~1010 cm−2) calculated based on Dirac-fermion models.

To explain the experiment, we further perform GW+RPA calculations for the Wigner-crystal transitions in tetralayer, pentalayer and hexalayer systems based on a much more realistic continuum model including the low-energy states from all carbon sites, which is derived from an atomistic Slater-Koster tight-binding model27. More details about the realistic continuum model are given in Supplementary Information. In the upper panel of Fig. 4a, we present the GW+RPA condensation energies for the tetralayer system under different displacement fields (characterized by the potential drop between top and bottom layers Δ) as a function of the Wigner lattice constant Ls. The carrier density ne is related to Ls via ne = 1/Ωs with Ωs=Ls23/2. When Δ increases from 0.06 to 0.11 eV, the critical density for the WC transition (at which the condensation energy reaches zero) increases from 1.1 × 1011 cm−2 to 1.5 × 1011 cm−2. Similarly, for the pentalayer system, the critical density for the WC transition increases from 1.3 × 1011 cm−2 to 2.0 × 1011 cm−2 when Δ rises from 0.07 to 0.13 eV as shown in the upper panel of Fig. 4b; and for hexalayer system, the critical densities are 1.5 × 1011 cm−2, 1.8 × 1011 cm−2, and 2.1 × 1011 cm−2 for Δ = 0.08, 0.11, and 0.14 eV, respectively, as presented in Fig. 4c. HF calculations (see Supplementary Fig. 17) indicate that the electron density at which phase transitions occur for systems with n = 4–6 is greater than ~1012 cm−2, which is an order of magnitude higher than the experimental value of ~1011 cm−235,36. Our GW + RPA method overcomes the limitations of HF approximation by accounting for correlation effects, yielding results that are all consistent with the experimental observations35,36.

To see this, in the lower panel of Fig. 4a–c, we plot the single-particle density of states (DOS) of the lowest conduction bands as a function of carrier density ne and displacement field D for tetralayer, pentalayer and hexalayer RMG systems, respectively. First, we note that for each case there is always a peak of DOS around some “optimal” displacement fields that extend to relatively large carrier densities, i.e., D ~0.96 V/nm in (a), D ~0.90 V/nm in (b), and D ~0.86 V/nm in (c). These peaks of DOS may be attributed to the presence of van Hove singularities near conduction band minima under those “optimal” D fields. As a result, when the Fermi energy is around the peaks of DOS, various Fermi-surface instabilities, including correlated insulating states and even superconductivity, may be generated due to e–e interaction effects, which is beyond the scope of the present work. Indeed, large regions of high-resistance states that unexpectedly extend to 4–5 × 1011 cm−2 have been observed in experiments precisely around those “optimal” D fields corresponding to the peaks of single-particle DOS, as presented in Fig. 1c, d of ref. 35 for tetralayer and pentalayer, and in Fig. 1a of ref. 36 for hexalayer RMG systems. When the D is weaker than the “optimal” value, such that the peak of single-particle DOS is not approached yet, the narrow region of high-resistance state developed from charge neutrality observed in experiments may be attributed to Wigner crystallizations upon slight charge dopings of the conduction band minima. The blue shaded regions in Fig. 4a–c mark the Wigner-crystal states calculated using the GW + RPA method based on the realistic continuum model for tetralayer, pentalayer and hexalayer systems (see upper panels of Fig. 4), respectively, which exhibit excellent consistency with the high-resistance regions reported in refs. 35,36. Relative dielectric constants are set to 8, 9, and 10 for tetralayer, pentalayer, and hexalayer systems, which are chosen to match the calculated single-particle DOS peaks to the unexpectedly extended high-resistance states observed in experiments at large D fields. Interlayer potential drop Δ is converted to displacement field D (in unit of V/nm) via Δ = Dd/ϵr, where d is the thickness of the system. It is also worth noting that the dielectric constant of RMG is expected to increase with the number of layers since eventually it has to diverge in the bulk limit when the system becomes a metal.

Anomalous Hall crystal in bilayer graphene based on realistic modeling

To model bilayer graphene more realistically and accurately, we consider a full continuum model in the basis of low-energy states from all carbon sites as mentioned above, including the next-neighbor interlayer hopping terms. In particular, for the case of bilayer graphene, it is expressed as

where ℏv⊥ = 0.447 eV ⋅ Å is the leading-order coefficient of k in the next-neighbor interlayer hopping, usually interpreted as the interlayer Fermi velocity. Δ is the on-site electrostatic potential energy drop between the two layers due to the vertical displacement field. The full continuum Hamiltonian breaks the continuous rotational symmetry of the Dirac-fermion model, leading to trigonal warping of the band structure37. It also breaks the particle-hole symmetry of the n-order Dirac-fermion model described by Eq. (2). Correspondingly, the Berry curvature is no longer peaked along an isotropic circle at kpeak, rather becomes more anisotropic with discrete threefold symmetry as shown in the right panel of Fig. 1b. The Bloch wavefunctions of the full continuum model Hμ,2full are also considerably different from those of Dirac-fermion models. Due to the breaking of particle-hole symmetry, non-interacting Fermi surfaces on the electron-doped side and the hole-doped side become different. Indeed, previous transport measurements suggest distinct superconducting behaviors when BLG is in proximity with WSe238. As shown in the lower panel of Fig. 5a, on the hole-doped side, there is a single Fermi surface when the carrier density ρ = 2.89 × 1011 cm−2, which evolves to a trinity-knot shaped Fermi surface at ρ = 1.8 × 1010 cm−2, then eventually splits to three degenerate small Fermi pockets when ρ = 1.0 × 1010 cm−2. In contrast, as shown in the upper panel of Fig. 5a, there is always a single Fermi surface with threefold anisotropy on the electron-doped side. Previous works suggest the presence of diverse correlated states on the hole-doped side, including spin-valley-polarized metal39,40, and magnetic-field-stabilized superconductivity39, etc. On the electron-doped side, however, the system has a simpler Fermi-surface topology, which bears more resemblance to the Dirac-fermion model. Thus, we may expect the emergence of AHC on the electron-doped side. Therefore, we first study the interacting ground state of slightly electron-doped bilayer graphene using the GW + RPA approach based on the full continuum Hamiltonian Hμ,2full with Δ = 0.05 and 0.03 eV, corresponding to experimentally accessible vertical displacement fields D ≈0.6 V/nm and D ≈0.36 V/nm, respectively.

However, the AHC state becomes unstable at large Ls, such that the condensation energy of AHC never becomes negative.

We further study the ground-state properties of AHC. Different from the trivial WC, which spontaneously forms a triangular lattice in real space, the charge-density distribution of AHC forms an emergent honeycomb lattice in real space33. Moreover, since AHC spontaneously breaks time-reversal symmetry, the ground state carries spontaneous current density as shown by the black arrows in Fig. 5b. The ground-state currents wind around the charge-density peaks in a clockwise manner, while they wind around the central region of charge-density minima in a counter-clockwise way, with the maximal current density ~0.074 nA/AA2. Such ground-state current pattern corresponds to an orbital magnetization ~1 μB per electron.

To assess the stability of AHC under finite temperature, we perform temperature-dependent GW single-particle energy spectrum calculations. Specifically, we first compute the single-particle energy spectrum and wavefunctions within the finite-temperature HF approximation. These are then used to construct the finite-temperature charge polarizability and the GW self-energy, and to obtain the finite-temperature quasiparticle energy spectrum. Finally, we evaluate the temperature dependence of the single-particle gap and the leading-order Fourier component of the charge density ρ(Q) to characterize the thermal robustness of the AHC (Q is the primitive reciprocal vector of the Wigner lattice). More details about the finite-temperature GW calculations are given in Supplementary Information. In Fig. 5e we present temperature-dependent ρ(Q) for the AHC state of electron-doped BLG with Ls = 1000 Å, where the blue and red lines denote the cases of Δ = 0.05 eV and Δ = 0.03 eV, respectively. Clearly, ρ(Q) continuously drops to zero when the temperature T ~7 K. However, before the charge-density modulation vanishes completely, the single-particle gaps already close at lower temperatures ~2–3 K as marked by the vertical dashed lines in Fig. 5e. It is worth noting that here we only consider the melting of AHC due to single-particle thermal excitations. In reality, collective excitations, phonon effects, disorder effects and first-order transitions to other symmetry-breaking states should also be taken into account, which is beyond the scope of the present study.

Discussions

To summarize, we have developed a GW+RPA framework to study the WC transitions in generic interacting 2D systems at low carrier densities. After being benchmarked using a conventional 2DEG system, we adopt this framework to study the ground-state properties and single-particle excitations in slightly charge-doped RMG. Mean-field calculations within HF approximation indicate that both topologically nontrivial AHC and trivial WC states can emerge for all the n-layer RMG (n = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6), with the condensation energy of trivial WC being consistently lower than that of AHC. However, dynamical charge-fluctuation effects as faithfully captured by GW + RPA method, can significantly de-stabilize trivial WC state, shifting the WC transition point to lower critical densities compared with the Hartree-Fock results. Most saliently, using a realistic modeling of bilayer graphene including trigonal warping effects, we find that the topological AHC state emerges as the genuine ground state in electron-doped BLG under intermediate displacement field when the carrier density is below ~2 × 1010 cm−2 when ϵr ≲ 5. Such AHC state in bilayer graphene is stabilized due to a subtle interplay between exchange and correlation effects: on the one hand, the exchange energy of the charge-gapped, symmetry-breaking AHC state is much lower than that of the gapless, symmetry-preserving FL state, which makes the total energy of AHC being lower than that of FL state at sufficiently low carrier densities; on the other hand, the correlation energy of AHC turns out to be lower than that of the trivial WC state at low densities due to the much smaller single-particle charge gap (see Fig. 3h) thus stronger charge-fluctuation effects, which eventually helps AHC to be the genuine ground state over both FL and trivial WC. The AHC state forms an emergent honeycomb lattice, and carries an intriguing ground-state current pattern in real space. In contrast, when the number of layers n ≥ 3, we find that the Berry curvatures of non-interacting Bloch functions are distributed at wavevectors that are further away from the central Dirac points, thus can be easily pushed out of the first Brillouin zone (of the presumable WC) at low densities. As a result, it may be harder to stabilize the topological AHC state when the number of layers is greater than 2. Therefore, we propose that bilayer graphene under slight carrier doping may be one of the most promising candidates to realize the AHC state. Reducing the screening effect, e.g., by changing the thickness of the substrate, may favor the AHC state.

The understanding of the AHC state is still in a preliminary stage. A lot of its physical properties, such as the collective excitations, response to electromagnetic fields, and quantum critical behavior near phase transitions, are still open questions. Our work may stimulate further experimental and theoretical explorations of the AHC state, with more focus on the pristine bilayer graphene system. Moreover, the theoretical framework developed in this work can be readily applied to other interacting 2D systems, including moiré superlattices.

Methods

To study the WC transition in both conventional 2DEG and RMG systems, we adopt the GW+RPA framework, which accounts for electron correlation effects beyond-mean-field approximation. Compared with mean-field approach such as Hartree-Fock (HF) method or “Hartree-Fock + RPA” method (to be discussed below), the “GW + RPA” approach enables a more accurate calculation of single-particle excitation spectra and ground-state energy, offering deeper insights into the potential transitions between FL and WC states.

However, if one starts from a translation-symmetry-breaking σz order, the Hartree-Fock calculations would yield an anomalous Hall crystal.

The advantage of the MPA is that it can simultaneously describe both plasmon modes and the continuous spectrum, avoiding the need for complicated numerical frequency integrations, which greatly speeds up the calculation. Moreover, the accuracy of the approximation can be controlled by adjusting the number of poles in the model, allowing for a balance between computational efficiency and precision. This is especially useful for the spontaneous-symmetry-breaking states such as the Wigner crystal, where there are multiple collective modes such as acoustic and optical quantum phonons52.
