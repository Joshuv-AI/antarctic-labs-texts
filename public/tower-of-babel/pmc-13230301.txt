Is it a mixedwood? Spatially-explicit responses to gradients in forest structure and composition in three boreal bird species
Authors: Leonard Patterson, Brendan Casey, Erin Bayne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextMixedwood forests are typically defined using coarse measures of stand composition and do not account for spatial configuration or scale. Such definitions may oversimplify how birds perceive habitat, potentially biasing past attempts to identify mixedwood habitat associations.Objectives(1) Quantify how continuous gradients in tree species composition, spatial configuration, and forest age influence species’ abundance; (2) Identify the spatial extent and grain at which these relationships are strongest.MethodsWe analyzed ~ 9200 point counts across the forested region of Alberta, Canada. We used a multi-scale design to assess the influence of forest composition, configuration, and age within 150, 500, and 1000 m extents at 5 m and 30 m grains on species’ relative abundance.ResultsSpatial configuration was the strongest predictor for all three species. Black-throated green warbler and bay-breasted warbler responded most strongly to fine-grain, local (150 m) configuration, with abundance peaking in intimately–mixed stands. Tennessee warbler responded most to configuration at the broadest extent (1000 m), also favouring intimate mixtures. The proportion of conifer and forest age consistently emerged as the second and third most important predictors, respectively. Predicted abundance on synthetic landscapes showed consistently higher abundance (∼ 4–10 times higher) in intimate versus segregated mixedwoods.ConclusionsSpecies-specific and spatially-explicit mixedwood definitions that include spatial configuration better capture bird habitat use than composition alone. Preserving or restoring intimate mixtures of coniferous and deciduous species at fine spatial scales may be particularly beneficial for our focal species.Keywords: Mixedwood forests, Spatial configuration, Scale of effect, Multi-scale, Tree composition, Boreal birds

Introduction

North America’s southern mixedwood boreal forests comprise two broad categories of tree types: deciduous and coniferous. When deciduous and coniferous species are combined, they create mixedwood stands that have been defined at varying spatial grain and extents (Brandt et al. 2013). Mixedwood stands in this region support higher bird diversity than pure stands (Hobson and Bayne 2000). This increased diversity is likely the result of greater floristic and structural diversity at the canopy and sub-canopy levels (Hobson and Bayne 2000). However, increased bird diversity in mixedwood stands may not simply be the result of the blending of bird species that rely primarily on pure deciduous versus pure coniferous forests; rather, it has been hypothesized that some boreal bird species may occur more frequently or be more abundant in mixedwood forests relative to pure stands (Kirk et al. 1996; Hobson and Bayne 2000; Girard et al. 2004) because mixedwoods provide resources that can’t be obtained in pure deciduous or pure coniferous forests. We refer to these species as “mixedwood specialists”.

Mixedwood forests are typically defined as forests comprising deciduous and coniferous tree species at the canopy and sub-canopy levels, with no single tree species exceeding a basal area or canopy cover threshold (MacDonald 1995; Agriculture, Forestry and Rural Economic Development 2022). That is, most definitions focus solely on tree species composition, consider species composition at a relatively coarse grain, and do not explicitly address spatial scale. While this simplified abstraction is useful for forestry and land-use planning, it may not reflect how birds perceive or use mixedwood forests. Rather, they may respond to a gradient of compositional and structural forest attributes, and their response is likely scale-dependent (Girard et al. 2004). Therefore, there is a need to develop more nuanced, species-specific and spatially-explicit definitions of mixedwood forests to ensure appropriate forest types exist in the future for such species.

The few studies investigating how individual bird species respond to tree species composition have assessed community-level responses to coarse-grain forest classifications, such as mixedwood, deciduous-dominated, and coniferous-dominated stands (Welsh and Lougheed 1996; Kirk et al. 1996; Hobson and Bayne 2000). While these studies have identified broad associations with forest type, they provide little insight into how birds respond to a gradient in tree species composition and whether this varies with scale. In the boreal forests of Sweden, Felton et al. (2021) found distinct shifts in bird community composition along a deciduous-coniferous gradient. This suggests that birds respond to finer-scale variation in species composition than are represented by current definitions of mixedwood forests. Whether or not this relationship holds across broader geographic ranges or how individual species respond to a deciduous-coniferous gradient is not known.

In addition, the spatial configuration of the deciduous-coniferous mixture within mixedwood forests is not captured in most mixedwood studies, yet it may play a critical role in developing a more nuanced understanding of mixedwood habitat associations. The arrangement of coniferous trees in mixedwood forests exists along a continuum from random interspersion (i.e., intimate mixedwoods) to complete aggregation at a particular spatial extent (i.e., spatially-segregated mixedwoods). Despite limited research investigating how conifer spatial configuration influences bird habitat use, intimate mixedwoods have been suggested to be important for certain boreal bird species. In a study of black-throated green warbler (Setophaga virens) habitat use in northern Alberta, Robichaud and Villard (1999) demonstrate that black-throated green warbler preferentially establish territories around clusters of white spruce (Picea glauca) while also using deciduous trees within their territories for nesting and foraging. Therefore, the spatial configuration of the deciduous-coniferous mixture may be important for some species, highlighting the potential existence of intimate mixedwood specialist bird species and the need to consider spatial configuration as an important element of how we define mixedwood forests.

Forest age is another key stand attribute that may influence how birds perceive and use mixedwood forests. Distinct shifts in bird community composition along successional gradients have been well–documented (Schieck and Song 2006; Reinkensmeyer et al. 2007; Venier and Pearce 2007). However, most studies examining effects of forest age on boreal bird species have relied on coarse age classifications, with few specifically assessing this relationship within mixedwood forests. As a result, how individual species respond to a gradient of forest age within mixedwood forests remains largely unknown. Recent forestry policy changes have increased the prevalence of early-successional mixedwood forests in the boreal landscape, making direct comparisons between young and old mixedwood forests more feasible (Timoney 2003; Lieffers et al. 2008). Further research is needed to determine whether the concept of a mixedwood is meaningful to birds along a successional gradient.

Identifying the appropriate spatial scale at which birds respond to mixedwood forests is crucial for developing species-specific definitions of mixedwood forests. Our current conceptualization of the scale at which mixedwoods exist is influenced by concepts such as the minimum mapping unit used in developing forest inventories, which limit the size at which mixedwood forests are mapped (Agriculture, Forestry and Rural Economic Development 2022). However, habitat selection is a hierarchical process, with decisions occurring at varying spatial grains and extents (Wiens 1989; Orians and Wittenberger 1991). The scale at which habitat characteristics are measured and summarized significantly influences their relationship with species occurrence (Desrochers et al. 2010; Smith et al. 2011). Both the strength and direction of these relationships can shift dramatically across spatial scales (Richmond et al. 2012; Chase et al. 2018). Most studies examining scale-dependent habitat relationships of forest birds have assessed responses to habitat amount, patch size, or fragmentation (Desrochers et al. 2010; Betts et al. 2010; Richmond et al. 2012; Torrenta et al. 2018). In contrast, few studies have assessed how within-stand attributes—such as deciduous-coniferous composition, forest age, or the spatial configuration of tree species—vary as a function of spatial scale. A multi-scale approach is therefore necessary to determine the spatial scale at which birds respond to the defining attributes of mixedwood forests and improve our understanding of mixedwood habitat associations.

Current reforestation policy in Alberta requires that forestry companies replant harvested mixedwood forests with a similar proportion of conifer trees as pre-harvest conditions, but provides no explicit guidelines on their spatial arrangement (Robichaud and Villard 1999; Lieffers et al. 2008). As a result, reforestation practices often lead to structural changes in mixedwood stands, despite efforts to maintain species composition (Macdonald 2010). In addition, to comply with the free-to-grow standard, forestry companies must manage regenerating deciduous vegetation that competes with planted conifers, inadvertently contributing to the spatial segregation of tree species (Lieffers et al. 2008). Due to logistical and cost constraints, reforestation often results in an “unmixing of mixedwoods”, with spatially-segregated monocultures of deciduous and coniferous tree species at different spatial extents replacing intimately mixed stands (Cavard et al. 2011). Given the ongoing decline of many boreal bird species, understanding and defining mixedwood forests from a bird’s perspective is important for understanding the consequences of current harvesting and reforestation practices.

Our objectives were to:

i)assess the extent to which a continuous gradient of deciduous-coniferous composition, spatial configuration of conifers, and forest age of mixedwood forests influence the relative abundance of mixedwood-associated boreal bird species;

Methods

We examined how the relative abundance of three boreal bird species changed along a gradient of deciduous-coniferous composition, spatial configuration of conifer, and forest age measured at three spatial extents—a local (150 m), intermediate (500 m), and broad (1000 m) extent—and two grain sizes—a fine (5 m) and coarse (30 m) grain.

Study area

We used human and automated acoustic point counts from digital recordings conducted across forested areas in Alberta, Canada, including the Boreal, Foothills, and Rocky Mountain Natural Regions (Downing and Pettapiece 2006).

The Boreal Forest Natural Region spans most of northern Alberta and is composed of coniferous forests dominated by black spruce (Picea mariana), white spruce, and jack pine (Pinus banksiana), deciduous forests with aspen (Populus tremuloides) and balsam poplar (Populus balsamifera), and shrubby black spruce fens (for more detail, see Downing and Pettapiece 2006). Deciduous-coniferous mixes of all these species exist to some degree. In the Rocky Mountain region, in western Alberta along the continental divide, vegetation composition changes with elevation. Higher elevations consist mainly of alpine shrub and meadow, while lower elevations host coniferous and a greater proportion of mixedwood forests. Common tree species include lodgepole pine (Pinus contorta), white spruce, Engelmann spruce (Picea engelmannii), subalpine fir (Abies lasiocarpa), and aspen. The Foothills Natural Region, at the eastern edge of the Rocky Mountains, serves as a transition between the Rocky Mountain and Boreal Regions. At lower elevations, the Foothills are dominated by mixedwood forests of lodgepole pine, white spruce, aspen, and balsam poplar. Lodgepole pine forests are common at higher elevations.

Focal species

We modelled the count for three boreal bird species: black-throated green warbler, Tennessee warbler (Leiothlypis peregrina), and bay-breasted warbler (Setophaga castanea). We selected these three species on the basis that i) they are known to breed in mixedwood forests in our study area and ii) we anticipate these species will show varying responses to a gradient in forest structure and composition. In addition, black-throated green warbler and bay-breasted warbler are listed as Special Concern and Sensitive in Alberta, respectively (AESRD 2014). Therefore, understanding how these species respond to a gradient in forest composition and structure will help inform conservation efforts for mixedwood-associated songbirds.

In Alberta, bay-breasted warbler is primarily associated with old-aged, pure white spruce and mixedwood stands containing white spruce (Hobson and Bayne 2000; Norton 2001). Similarly, black-throated green warbler is most often found in older-aged stands, but are more strongly associated with white spruce-aspen mixedwoods than bay-breasted warbler (Hobson and Bayne 2000; Norton 1999). Tennessee warbler has been found to be more abundant in mixedwood forests (Hobson and Bayne 2000), but is also associated with pure white spruce and aspen stands and exhibits only a slight preference for older-aged forests (Alberta Biodiversity Monitoring Institute n.d.). Tennessee warbler and bay-breasted warbler are more likely to react to insect outbreaks, such as spruce budworm, than black-throated green warbler (Moisan Perrier et al. 2021).

Response variables

We obtained point count data from the Boreal Avian Modeling Centre (BAM) and the University of Alberta’s Bioacoustic Unit. Point count data were accessed through WildTrax, an open-source, cloud-based platform used to store and process bioacoustic data, using the wildRtrax package (version 1.3.2; MacPhail 2025). Point count data included both human and automated acoustic point counts collected via autonomous recording units (ARUs—Wildlife Acoustics SM2, SM3, and SM4), comprising 9202 point counts across 6841 locations conducted between 2009 and 2024 (Fig. 1).

Location of point counts used in this analysis. The study area comprised the Boreal, Foothills, and Rocky Mountain Natural Regions of Alberta

Point count data were filtered to only include point counts conducted: i) between 30 min before sunrise to 5 h after sunrise in June, ii) within ~ 10 years of the land cover data (i.e., in or after 2009), and iii) greater than 50 m from roads, pipelines, transmission lines, active and abandoned well pads, mines, industrial facilities, and cultivated land (Alberta Biodiversity Monitoring Institute 2022). Point count data were not filtered based on proximity to seismic lines, as seismic lines have been shown to have limited effect on species occurrence and are so extensive that each point is likely influenced to some degree (Bayne et al. 2005; Machtans 2006). To control for the influence of stand-replacing disturbances, we excluded point counts within 50 m of harvested or burned areas when the disturbance year fell between the point count year and the year represented by the land cover product. Since the spatial aggregation metric is a class-level metric and therefore requires at least two classes to compute, we only retained sites in which two classes (i.e., conifer present and conifer absent) were present across all buffer sizes. Lastly, we randomly selected one point count per year per site from the remaining point counts.

Predictor variables

We sourced predictor variables from two datasets that represent two grain sizes: the National Terrestrial Ecosystem Monitoring System for Canada (NTEMS) and the Land-use/Land-cover Classification of Alberta, derived from 2020 Sentinel-2 Multispectral Data (LULC) (Hermosilla et al. 2024; Chowdhury 2021)(Table 1). NTEMS is a collection of 30 m resolution maps of forest attributes for Canada that were developed using photo-interpreted high-resolution imagery, Landsat spectral imagery, and regional land cover models (Hermosilla et al. 2024). From NTEMS, we extracted 30 m resolution forest age and coniferous tree species rasters, which were created using satellite imagery from 2019 (Maltman et al. 2023; Hermosilla et al. 2024). The forest age raster contains continuous values ranging from 1 to 151 years within our study area. The LULC is a single 10 m resolution land cover map for Alberta derived from 2020 Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery (Chowdhury 2021).

Since a fine-grain (< 30 m resolution) coniferous tree species raster was not available for our study area, we chose to resample and reclassify a 10 m resolution land cover/land-use raster containing deciduous, coniferous, and mixedwood pixels. Because our objective was to quantify the spatial configuration of conifer trees within mixedwood forests, mixedwood pixels could not be used directly to quantify the spatial arrangement of conifer trees. Further, treating mixedwood pixels as either conifer or non-conifer would introduce systematic bias in estimates of both composition and configuration. To address this limitation, as well as to better approximate the fine-grain configuration at which mixedwood forests typically occur, we resampled the LULC raster from 10 to 5 m resolution and reclassified the raster using the following rules: conifer pixels were retained as conifer, all non-conifer and non-mixedwood pixels were assigned to non-conifer, and mixedwood pixels (which were divided into four 5 m sub-pixels after resampling) were assigned as two conifer and two non-conifer within each mixedwood parent pixel. The spatial configuration of the two conifer and two non-conifer sub-pixels was randomized within each parent pixel. While the configuration of coniferous and deciduous trees within a mixedwood patch is often influenced by the composition of the surrounding forest, assigning conifer and deciduous sub-pixels based on the tree composition of adjacent pixels would require finer-resolution data than were available. By randomizing the arrangement, we avoided introducing systematic bias and treated this uncertainty as random error.

We quantified forest composition by calculating the proportion of conifer pixels within 150, 500, and 1000 m radii around point count locations, measured at 5 m and 30 m grains. To quantify the spatial configuration of the coniferous-deciduous mixture, we calculated the Clumpiness Index (CLUMPY) using the landscapemetrics package (version 2.2; Hesselbarth et al. 2025). CLUMPY measures the degree to which pixels of a focal class are aggregated or dispersed relative to a random distribution and therefore captures the degree of interspersion between coniferous and deciduous trees. While CLUMPY is typically described in the landscape ecology literature in terms of aggregation and dispersion, these same conditions are commonly referred to in the forestry literature as “segregated” and “intimate” mixtures, respectively. We adopt the terms “intimate” (low CLUMPY) and “segregated” (high CLUMPY) throughout to maintain consistency with terminology widely used by forest managers. We selected this metric over other spatial configuration metrics (e.g., edge or isolation metrics) because CLUMPY measures the degree of coniferous-deciduous interspersion, and this is thought to be biologically relevant to our focal species. We calculated CLUMPY using the same extents and grains as forest composition. CLUMPY values range between -1 and 1, where the focal patch type is maximally disaggregated at values of -1 and maximally aggregated at values of 1. CLUMPY has been shown to be only weakly correlated with habitat amount (Wang et al. 2014), which allowed us to assess the influence of spatial configuration independent of the proportion of conifer. CLUMPY was calculated at the class level using the default 8-cell rule. Lastly, we measured forest age as the mean forest age at the 150, 500, and 1000 m extents and the 30 m grain.

Statistics

We used a boosted regression tree (BRT) model to examine the role of forest composition and structure on the relative abundance of three boreal bird species. BRTs are a machine-learning approach that combine regression trees with boosting to enhance model performance (Elith et al. 2008). We selected a BRT model for this analysis because they can model nonlinear relationships, automatically incorporate complex interactions between predictors, and are robust to correlated predictor variables (Elith et al. 2008).

BRT models were fitted for each focal species using the gbm (version 2.2.2; Ridgeway et al. 2024) and dismo packages (version 1.3.16; Hijmans et al. 2024). Models were trained with a Poisson error distribution, a tree complexity of 3, a learning rate of 0.005, and a bag fraction of 0.5, as recommended by Elith et al. (2008). To control overfitting, each model run included 10-fold cross-validation. In addition to forest composition and structure predictors, we included year, latitude, and longitude as predictors in the model. We modelled year as a categorical predictor to account for non-linear year-to-year variation in the count. We included latitude and longitude as predictors to account for changes in abundance across the range of each species (bay-breasted warbler and black-throated green warbler show distinct range edges in Alberta).

Points count locations were highly clustered, potentially leading to nonindependence of sampling units and causing bias in model parameters (Ploton et al. 2020). To address this, we used block bootstrapping to account for non-independent sampling units, whereby the study area was divided into 1 km2 grid cells and each point count location was assigned to a grid cell and given a corresponding block ID. We bootstrapped the model fitting process by fitting the model 100 times, sampling entire blocks with replacement. We assessed spatial autocorrelation by calculating Moran’s I on model residuals using the spdep package (version 1.3.10; Bivand et al. 2025). A spatial correlogram indicated that spatial autocorrelation declined to approximately zero beyond 30 km; therefore, Moran’s I was computed using a 30 km distance-based neighbourhood such that observations within 30 km were treated as spatial neighbours.

We used detection correction offsets derived from the QPAD v3 framework to account for variation in detectability across point counts (Sólymos et al. 2013). We included survey duration, time of day, day of year, and maximum survey distance band to calculate the offset. Offsets were calculated using the QPAD package and account for variation in point count methodology (version 0.0.3; Sólymos et al. 2013). The dataset we used included 3-, 5-, and 10-min duration point counts over an unlimited distance. After QPAD corrections, the predicted values can be interpreted as density estimates.

We identified the scale of effect for each predictor for each species using mean relative influence across 100 bootstrapped models. We define scale of effect here as the spatial scale at which an organism responds most strongly to their surrounding environment for a given predictor variable (Jackson and Fahrig 2012). We used partial dependence plots (PDP) to visualize marginal effects of predictors on species' relative abundance, after accounting for the influence of all other variables in the model. Because BRTs generate non-linear marginal functions that can be noisy across bootstraps, we smoothed PDP outputs using generalized additive models (GAMs) using the mgcv package (version 1.9.1; Wood 2025). Specifically, we fit Gaussian GAMs with penalized thin-plate regression splines and 95% confidence intervals to the bootstrapped PDP values for each scale of effect predictor, adding a random intercept for bootstrap iteration to account for non-independence among bootstraps. To quantify and compare the strength of interactions among predictors, we calculated the mean Friedman’s H-statistic (Friedman and Popescu 2008) across the 100 bootstrapped models for each combination of predictors within each extent and grain, excluding nuisance variables (i.e., year, latitude, and longitude).

To understand how spatial configuration per se influences focal species’ abundance, we generated predictions across synthetic landscapes representing intimate and segregated mixedwood forests at three age classes (30, 60, and 90 years). These landscapes were constructed to represent the extremes of spatial configuration observed in our dataset while holding other conditions constant. The intent of this analysis was to quantify population size of our focal species under contrasting forest management scenarios. We opted to make predictions on synthetic landscapes instead of across our study area as our analysis specifically focused on species’ responses within mixedwood forests; therefore, a full spatial prediction across our study area would extend beyond areas and conditions sampled. Synthetic landscapes were 100 km2 in size, corresponding to the approximate size of a township in Alberta, and were generated using 5 m pixels (Fig. 2). Each landscape was composed of 50% coniferous and 50% deciduous pixels. To more closely approximate real-world spatial patterns of mixedwood forests, we divided the landscape into 25 ha blocks—corresponding to the average harvest block size in Alberta (Alberta-Pacific Forest Products 2022)—and arranged conifer pixels within each block to achieve CLUMPY values of either 0 (intimate) or 1 (segregated). We overlaid synthetic landscapes with a grid of theoretical point count locations spaced 2 km apart and summarized the proportion and configuration of conifer pixels within 150, 500, and 1000 m buffers around point count locations. We then made predictions at the point count level before scaling abundance values up to the township scale to see how spatial pattern of the mixedwoods influenced total abundance of each species. We define abundance in this context as the number of territorial males per township.

We used scale of effect predictors for each environmental variable to create a final multi-scale model for predictions; that is, for each focal species, we used only the most influential scale (extent and grain) for each predictor to build the final model. The multi-scale model was created using the same spatial thinning, bootstrapping, and model fitting process as the global model. Nuisance variables included in the final model were set to representative values for predictions: we set coordinates to a location in the centre of our study area near Calling Lake, Alberta and used the modal year from our dataset (2014) for all predictions.

Results

Detections and model performance

We analyzed 9202 human and ARU point count surveys across 6841 sites, with black-throated green warbler detected at 339 point counts (4%), Tennessee warbler detected at 3791 point counts (41%), and bay-breasted warbler detected at 583 point counts (6%). Residual spatial autocorrelation calculated across 100 bootstraps was 0.04 for black-throated green warbler, 0.02 for Tennessee warbler, and 0.01 for bay-breasted warbler.

Relative importance and scale of effect of predictors

The most important predictor of black-throated green warbler relative abundance was spatial configuration. The scale of effect for spatial configuration was the 150 m extent and 5 m grain, with a mean variable importance of 9.9% across 100 bootstrapped models (Fig. 3). The scale of effect for spatial configuration was almost twice as important as the proportion of conifer scale of effect. When holding all other variables in the model at their mean values, the expected count peaked at a CLUMPY value of 0 and rapidly declined with increasing CLUMPY (Fig. 4). At the finer grain, the 500 m and 1000 m extents also had relatively high contributions to the model (7.1 and 4.7%, respectively). Across all spatial extents, spatial configuration measured at the fine grain had higher relative importance than the coarse grain. The proportion of conifer had the second highest relative importance, with the 500 m extent and 30 m grain contributing 5.5% to the model (Fig. 3). The expected count for the proportion of conifer scale of effect followed a quadratic relationship, reaching a maximum expected count around 0.20 (Fig. 4). Forest age scale of effect (150 m extent and 30 m grain) had the lowest contribution to the model among scale of effect predictors, contributing an average of 3.7% across bootstrapped models (Fig. 3). The expected count for this predictor exhibited a threshold response, with the expected count increasing significantly in forests older than 75 years (Fig. 4).

Spatial configuration was also the most important predictor for Tennessee warbler. The scale of effect for this predictor was the 1000 m extent and 5 m grain, which contributed 8.7% to the model, more than twice as much as the other scale of effect predictors (Fig. 3). The expected count followed a decreasing relationship with increasing CLUMPY (Fig. 4). Again, the 5 m grain for spatial configuration was more predictive than the 30 m grain at all extents with larger extents contributing more to the final model than smaller extents within each grain (Fig. 3). The proportion of conifer measured at the 1000 m extent and 5 m grain was the second most important scale of effect predictor, contributing 4.4% to the model (Fig. 3). The proportion of conifer followed a quadratic relationship with peaks in the expected count at intermediate amounts of conifer (Fig. 4). Forest age measured at the 150 m extent was the least important scale of effect predictor, contributing 3.6% to the model (Fig. 3). The expected count for forest age was highest in forests younger than 40 years old and rapidly declined in forests older than 40 years (Fig. 4).

Similar to black-throated green warbler and Tennessee warbler, spatial configuration was the most important scale of effect predictor for bay-breasted warbler. The scale of effect for all predictors was the 150 m extent and 5 m grain (except for forest age, which was only measured at the 30 m grain). The spatial configuration scale of effect contributed 21.1% to the model—more than twice as much as the proportion of conifer and more than six times as much as forest age (Fig. 3). The expected count for spatial configuration was highest at a CLUMPY value near 0 and decreased with increasing CLUMPY (Fig. 4). The proportion of conifer was the second most important scale of effect, contributing 8.2% to the model (Fig. 3). The expected count showed a left-skewed response, peaking at higher proportions of conifer (Fig. 4). There was a two-way interaction (mean H-value of 0.17 across 100 bootstrapped models) between the proportion of conifer and spatial configuration scale of effects. A three-dimensional pairwise interaction plot showed that the effect of the proportion of conifer on the expected count is mediated by spatial configuration, with the expected count increasing non-linearly with proportion conifer only at low CLUMPY values; that is, while bay-breasted warbler abundance was higher in conifer-dominated areas, this relationship only held when conifers were dispersed (Fig. 5). Forest age scale of effect contributed the least to the model among scale of effect predictors, with a mean variable importance value of 2.8% across bootstrapped models (Fig. 3). Similar to black-throated green warbler, the expected count exhibited a threshold response with forest age, peaking at 75 years and declining slightly in forests older than 75 years (Fig. 4).

Synthetic landscape predictions

Our three focal species consistently showed higher predicted abundance in intimate mixedwood configurations across all forest ages (Fig. 6), despite conifer and deciduous proportions being held constant. Mean predicted abundance ranged from four to ten times higher in intimate than segregated mixedwoods. Black-throated green warbler and bay-breasted warbler abundance increased with forest age in both configurations, with differences in abundance between configurations most pronounced in older forests. Black-throated green warbler had the lowest predicted abundance overall, with broad confidence intervals across age classes and configurations. This variability in predicted abundance is likely an artifact of this species’ restricted range in Alberta: although suitable mixedwood forest exists within the province, much of it lies beyond the species’ occupied range. In addition, black-throated green warbler had a small number of detections relative to the other two species. Together, these factors increased the likelihood that individual bootstrap models excluded occupied cells entirely, causing high uncertainty in black-throated green warbler predicted abundance relative to bay-breasted warbler and Tennessee warbler. Tennessee warbler also exhibited higher predicted abundance in intimate landscapes, but in contrast to black-throated green warbler and bay-breasted warbler, abundance was greatest in younger forests and declined with increasing age. Taken together, these results highlight that while these species differ in their responses to forest age, intimate mixtures of conifer and deciduous trees support higher predicted abundances regardless of stand age.

Discussion

We used a BRT model to assess how three boreal bird species respond to a gradient in forest composition, deciduous-coniferous spatial configuration, and forest age within mixedwood forests in Alberta, Canada. We used a multi-scale approach to understand the spatial extent and grain at which our focal species respond most strongly to their surrounding environment—also known as the “scale of effect” (Jackson and Fahrig 2012). We found scale-specific responses to a gradient in mixedwood forest composition and structure in our focal species, highlighting the need for scale and species-specific mixedwood definitions.

Role of spatial configuration

The spatial configuration of coniferous trees within mixedwood forests had the highest relative influence among scale of effect predictors for all three focal species. The abundance of all three species consistently declined with increasing aggregation, suggesting a strong preference for intimate mixedwood configurations. Further, all three species responded most strongly to fine-grain measurements of conifer configuration, suggesting that these species are perceiving and responding to fine-scale variation in forest structure. Black-throated green warbler’s strong response to dispersed conifers at local scales aligns with previous studies that note that this species prefers intimate deciduous-coniferous mixtures at the territory scale (Robichaud and Villard 1999). While previous studies have noted the influence of patch- and landscape-level attributes on this species (Schmiegelow et al. 1997; Hannah 2007; Hart et al. in review), our study is the first to quantify the importance of within-patch spatial configuration of tree species for this species. Bay-breasted warbler also responded most strongly to local-scale dispersed mixtures. While previous studies noted a high relative abundance of bay-breasted warbler in mature conifer-dominated mixedwood forests (Hobson and Bayne 2000; Norton 2001; Kirk and Hobson 2001), our findings suggest that spatial configuration may be a more important determinant of abundance than either tree species composition or forest age. In contrast, Tennessee warbler appears to make habitat selection decisions at broader scales, yet still prefers intimate deciduous-coniferous mixtures at this scale. To our knowledge, this study is the first to document the role of the spatial configuration of the deciduous-coniferous mixture on boreal bird habitat use. Consequently, our results highlight a significant limitation in existing mixedwood definitions that largely overlook spatial arrangement, emphasizing the need to broaden mixedwood definitions to include the spatial dimension of tree species mixtures. In part, this is due to how mixedwoods are defined in forest inventory data and highlights how more refined measurements of resources using remote sensing may provide new insights into bird-habitat associations (Gottschalk et al. 2011).

Mixedwood tree species composition

The proportion of conifer was the second most influential predictor for all three species; however, its effect on abundance varied among species and was often non-linear. Black-throated green warbler abundance peaked at low to intermediate proportions of conifer before declining at higher proportions, suggesting that while this species is associated with conifer presence, it avoids conifer-dominated stands. This result aligns with this species’ known association with mixed aspen-white spruce forests (Norton 1999; Robichaud and Villard 1999; Alberta Biodiversity Monitoring Institute n.d.). Our results contrast those of Hannah (2007), who reported that black-throated green warbler occupancy was highest in stands containing more than 5% white spruce in the overstory. However, Hannah (2007) quantified conifer composition using a coarse-grain, polygon-based digital forest inventory, and their study sites represent a relatively narrow gradient of conifer composition compared to the broader range examined here. This discrepancy highlights how high-resolution, continuous land cover data can provide unique insights into bird-habitat relationships that may be obscured when forest types are discretized into polygons constrained by minimum mapping units, which can mask fine-scale variation in tree species composition within stands. Bay-breasted warbler abundance increased positively with conifer abundance, peaking at mid to high amounts of conifer. While this suggests a preference for conifer-dominated stands, this relationship is strongly mediated by spatial configuration: abundance was highest in conifer-dominated areas only when conifers were spatially dispersed. This finding highlights the limitation of relying solely on tree species composition to identify habitat associations in mixed stands. Interestingly, Tennessee warbler was the only species that responded most strongly to forest composition at the broadest extent, preferring intermediate amounts of conifer at this scale. As such, Tennessee warbler appears to select for balanced deciduous-coniferous mixtures at a broad spatial extent.

Importantly, this study primarily included mesic upland forests where conifer species were predominantly white spruce and, to a lesser extent, balsam fir; that is, our study did not extensively sample pine or black spruce-dominated forests. Given the weak association of our focal species with these forest types, we likely would have observed relationships that differed greatly from those described in the literature (Norton 1999, 2001). Finally, our study only included mixedwood stands and thus did not include the full range of potential forest compositions (i.e., stands comprising only coniferous or deciduous trees). Further study using higher resolution satellite imagery or light detection and ranging (LiDAR) may reveal more precise estimates of habitat associations, and is something we are currently researching.

These findings highlight an important gap in our current understanding of mixedwoods and a potential bias in past attempts to identify mixedwood habitat associations. Our results show that mixedwood-associated bird species are responding to a gradient in tree species composition, rather than coarse classifications of mixed versus pure stands. Further, in contrast to studies that treat all mixedwood stands similarly, we show that the position of a mixedwood forest along the deciduous-coniferous continuum significantly influences its suitability for our focal species, revealing a range of responses that are masked by a simple "mixedwood" classification. Lastly, variation in the scale at which our focal species responded most strongly to the deciduous-coniferous continuum highlights the need for spatially-explicit definitions of mixedwoods.

Influence of forest age

Forest age was the least important predictor for all three focal species. Importantly, we observed strong, non-linear responses to forest age in all three species. Black-throated green warbler’s and bay-breasted warbler’s strong positive association with older forests is consistent with previous findings that these species prefer old-growth forests (Norton 2001; Hannah 2007). Black-throated green warbler’s threshold response to forest age can partially be explained by their reliance on large trees for song posts (Robichaud and Villard 1999) and preference for foraging in forest canopy gaps (Smith and Dallman 1996), features most common in old forests. Tennessee warbler, on the other hand, showed a strong negative association with forest age, with higher relative abundance observed in stands younger than 40 years old. This aligns with Tennessee warbler’s known use of early-seral forests following disturbance (Schieck and Song 2006). This finding underscores limitations of relying on coarse forest age categories (e.g., young, mature, and old) and highlights the importance of using continuous measurements of forest age when assessing habitat associations.

While all three focal species were most strongly associated with forest age measured at a local scale, their contrasting age preferences—with notable threshold responses observed in bay-breasted warbler and black-throated green warbler—suggest that young and old mixedwood forests are not functionally equivalent at the local scale for these species. Based on our findings, we expect that bay-breasted warbler and black-throated green warbler would not perceive mixedwoods as suitable habitat until they reach a mid-to late-successional stage. This highlights the importance of maintaining a diverse mosaic of age classes of mixedwood forests across the landscape to accommodate a diversity of responses to forest age classes, as demonstrated here. Further research is needed to explore whether the concept of a mixedwood is meaningful for species that are associated with early-seral vegetation, such as Tennessee warbler. Understanding how these species respond to tree species composition during early forest regeneration will clarify whether mixedwood characteristics are relevant outside of mature stand conditions.

Scale-dependence of habitat associations

Across all species and predictors, the strength and even direction of habitat associations varied with spatial extent and grain. Our findings add support to the idea that bird habitat selection is a hierarchical and scale-dependent process (Richmond et al. 2012; Knight et al. 2022; Hart and Bayne 2025). Interestingly, finer grain sizes were generally more informative for Tennessee warbler and bay-breasted warbler, whereas black-throated green warbler showed strong responses at both grain sizes, depending on the predictor. This finding broadly aligns with Gottschalk et al. (2011) who found that increasing the grain size of environmental predictors generally decreased the predictive accuracy of bird-habitat models. In terms of extent, there was much greater variability: while all three species responded most strongly to forest age at the local scale, the scale of effect for other predictors differed. Bay-breasted warbler responded most strongly to forest composition at the local extent, black-throated green warbler at the intermediate extent, and Tennessee warbler at the broad extent. This pattern is consistent with previous work showing that the spatial extent of habitat selection is not uniform across species or predictors, highlighting the value of multi-scale approaches (Mayor et al. 2009; McGarigal et al. 2016). More work is needed to assess the generality of using models like those from our 150 m extent to a greater array of species as this is the scale at which current provincial planning models for birds are generated and applied.

Implications for mixedwood forest management and restoration

Our results underscore that species-specific, spatially-explicit definitions of mixedwood forests may alter our conclusions about different forest management strategies. Relying solely on coarse classifications of stand composition may obscure important variation in how birds perceive and use forests. For example, while bay-breasted warbler and black-throated green warbler were negatively correlated with conifer aggregation at the local scale, their responses diverged with respect to tree species composition and the spatial extent and grain at which compositional effects were strongest.

Our findings have implications for forest management practices that seek to mitigate effects of forest harvesting on biodiversity, particularly retention harvesting. Previous studies have shown that old-growth forest associated species, such as black-throated green warbler, are unlikely to use aggregated retention patches within cut blocks between 2- and 60-years post-disturbance (Hobson and Schieck 1999; Venier et al. 2015). We show that suitability of such patches depend on their position along a gradient of tree species composition, spatial configuration, and forest age, and that these relationships are species-specific. Therefore, black-throated green warbler may not have been observed in retention patches in prior studies due to the patches not meeting the species’ specialized habitat requirements at that time, rather than residuals not having value now or in the future. Variation in habitat preferences among our focal species underscores the importance of considering not just tree species composition, but also forest configuration and age when managing for mixedwood-associated species. Our findings also emphasize the need to consider spatial scale in the context of retention harvesting: while all three species preferred forests with dispersed conifer, black-throated green warbler and bay-breasted warbler responded most strongly at the local scale, while Tennessee warbler responded most strongly at the broad scale. This suggests that while all three species may benefit from intimate deciduous-coniferous mixtures, Tennessee warbler may require larger forested patches than previously assumed. Collectively, these results highlight that retention harvesting strategies will be most effective when they account for species-specific habitat requirements across multiple scales.

Current reforestation policies in Alberta that mandate maintaining conifer proportions post-harvest, but that do not address spatial configuration, risk unintentionally degrading habitat for mixedwood-associated species. Practices such as the free-to-grow standard may exacerbate this issue by encouraging conifer-dominated patches that are spatially-segregated from deciduous components (Lieffers et al. 2008). Our findings suggest that preserving or restoring reasonably large areas of intimate deciduous-coniferous mixtures at fine spatial scales may be particularly beneficial for our focal species, which exhibit strong scale-dependent responses to spatial configuration.
