Nanobioengineered Al2O3 Core–Shell Nanoparticle Preparation Using Bauhinia Variegate Plant Extract for Efficient Photocatalysis and Electrochemical Sensing
Authors: Kshitij RB Singh, Pooja Singh, Jay Singh, Shyam S Pandey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCore–shell-based nanomaterials have garnered considerable attention in the recent past not only in catalytic applications but also in their potentiality in selective and efficient sensing. Present research reports the first and successful biosynthesis of the core (c-Al2O3)–shell nanoparticles (NPs) using Bauhinia variegate blossom extract as reducing and capping agents. The synthesized c-Al2O3 NPs were characterized and utilized to fabricate nanobioengineered electrodes on indium tin oxide (ITO) substrates via electrophoretic deposition. Electrochemical analysis, including cyclic voltammetry and differential pulse voltammetry, revealed quasi-reversible processes with high electron-transfer rates (Ks = 0.66 s–1) and a diffusion coefficient (D = 5.84 × 10–2 cm2 s–1). The electrode exhibited a very high sensitivity (23.44 μA μM–1 cm–2) and a low detection limit (0.463 μM) for sodium azide (NaN3) over two linear ranges of 1–6 and 8–20 μM. Additionally, c-Al2O3 NPs demonstrated the effective photocatalytic degradation of crystal violet dye under visible light, following pseudo-first-order kinetics. The fabricated electrode showed excellent selectivity, stability, and reproducibility, highlighting its potential for environmental monitoring and clinical diagnostics.Keywords: electrochemical sensors, core-aluminum oxide, sensing, nanobioengineered, core−shell nanoparticles

Introduction

Nanoengineered materials, particularly heterostructures, have garnered significant attention since Prof. Herbert Kroemer’s Nobel prize lecture, where he famously stated, “Often, the interface is a device”.1 Since then, the field has advanced considerably, showcasing its extensive applications in various domains. The advancements in this field are attributed to the distinct properties of materials at the nanoscale, including their unique size, high surface-to-volume ratio, and the ability to tune their surfaces, which allows for precise control over their chemical and physical properties.2−5 Still, nanoengineering of materials has many stumbling blocks, as it is not sustainable and is hazardous to mankind and the environment. To overcome this drawback, nanobioengineering of material has emerged as one of the best approaches, and this can be attained by utilizing plants or microorganisms,6−9 as this approach opens new possibilities for sustainable development.4 In these systems, the nanointerface is synthesized by utilizing biological reducing, capping, and stabilizing agents. This synthesis approach is sustainable, and it also retains the same properties as nanoengineered materials, such as high surface area and tunable surface chemistry, but it has more functionality, allowing stability and functional anchoring of biological molecules. This synergy increases the sensitivity and response time of the sensors while maintaining the biological activity.10,11 Additionally, their small size and high efficiency pave the way for miniaturization of sensing devices. Hence, combining nanotechnology and bionanotechnology holds significant promise for developing the next-generation sensing technologies through nanobioengineered materials.

For biosynthesis of the nanobioengineered nanoparticles (NPs), the most commonly explored source is plant extracts, as they have phytocompounds, which act as reducing agents. In this study, the Leguminosae family plant Bauhinia variegata, known as the orchid tree, Kachnar (Hindi), and Mountain Ebony (English), is used for NP synthesis. This plant grows 10–12 m tall with heart-shaped, lobed leaves and has diuretic, astringent, analgesic, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antidiabetic properties.12 It is also an endangered, blooming tree and is popular in subtropical and tropical climates for its fragrant flowers. It blooms between March and May, and its flowers contain active phytochemicals such as peonidin, malvidin, and kaempferol derivatives,13,14 which are very good reducing agents. The significance of sensing sodium azide (NaN3) and degrading crystal violet originates from their severe health and environmental risks. NaN3, a common preservative, poses health hazards like headaches and cytochrome oxidase inhibition, similar to cyanide salts, but the existing detection methods are costly and impractical for field use.15−18 Electrochemical methods offer a cost-effective and sensitive alternative, particularly with carbon, gold, and platinum electrodes.19−24 Similarly, crystal violet, a persistent dye pollutant from textile industries, poses cancer and respiratory risks, necessitating advanced degradation methods for pollution control.25−32 This study uniquely combines these two intriguing issues by using plant-based NPs as photocatalysts for crystal violet degradation and optimizing them for electrochemical NaN3 sensing. This dual approach addresses the need for efficient, eco-friendly solutions to mitigate dye and chemical contaminant impacts on health and the environment, enhancing water quality and safety.25,33−37 To achieve this, analytical and electroanalytical methods can be crucial in advancing the ability to detect and quantify chemical species in biological and environmental systems. These techniques offer enhanced sensitivity and specificity, which are essential for monitoring trace elements and pollutants. Recent research has emphasized the utility of these methods in environmental remediation and clinical diagnostics, further establishing their importance.38

Though core–shell-based nanomaterials have garnered significant interest in catalytic applications, their sustainability remains a concern for the current generation’s demands. Thus, in this study, core (c-Al2O3)–shell nanobioengineered NPs were synthesized, and uniqueness lies in the synthesis of core-based metal-oxide NPs surrounded by a shell of phytocompounds, and it also introduces, for the first time, the biosynthesis of core (c)-Al2O3 NPs using B. variegata blossom extract as a reducing and capping agent. This innovative approach ensures the successful synthesis of the c-Al2O3–shell (phytocompound) NPs and integrates biological engineering principles. By leveraging natural phytocompounds, this method addresses sustainability issues, offering an eco-friendly alternative for synthesizing core–shell materials and potentially transforming catalytic and other nanotechnology applications. Further, the biosynthesized NPs can help to develop highly efficient sensors for NaN3 detection and potential catalysts for the remediation of toxic dyes from wastewater. Initially, the characterization of the as-prepared materials was undertaken by utilizing X-ray diffraction (XRD), ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to determine its optical, structural, and morphological characteristics. After this, nanobioengineered electrodes were fabricated using electrophoretic deposition (EPD) to develop an electrochemical sensing device for NaN3 detection. In this scenario, the interfacial kinetics at the electrode’s surface play a crucial role in the sensing mechanism of NaN3 detection. These studies were conducted thoroughly using various parameters, and then, sensing experiments were performed via a three-electrode system. The photocatalytic degradation of crystal violet dye was also performed to study the optical behavior of the as-prepared nanobioengineered NPs. Hence, this study, as schematically summarized in Figure 1, reveals the importance of nanobioengineered NPs for sensing and environmental remediation applications and opens avenues for stable, rapid, and selective sensing based on advanced nanobioengineered sensing interfaces.

Experimental Section

The instruments and analytical chemicals employed in this investigation are listed in S1 and S2 of the Supporting Information (SI). In addition, all of the chemicals used in this study were of analytical reagent grade. The electrochemical analysis was performed in 18 Ω resistivity Milli-Q distilled water (Millipore).

Preparation of B. variegate Blossom Extract

For this work, B. variegate blossoms were collected from the IGNTU campus in March 2023 and fresh blossoms were explicitly selected for extract preparation. Milli-Q water was used to wash the B. variegate blossoms. The blossoms were then spread out on blotting paper to remove water and moisture content. The blossom was then weighed 1 g, chopped into fine pieces, dispersed in 10 mL of Milli-Q water, and heated at 70 °C for 30 min. After this, the extract was allowed to cool down at room temperature before filtering with a muslin cloth, followed by Whatman filter paper Number 1. Then, the collected filtrate was refrigerated at 4 °C for further experimentation. This method is an effective method to extract from the plant for NP synthesis, as previously reported by our group in previous works.8

Biosynthesis of c-Al2O3 NPs

For the synthesis of c-Al2O3 NPs, a freshly prepared precursor salt solution of aluminum nitrate nonahydrate (2M) was biologically reduced using the B. variegate blossom extract, and this was achieved by mixing the salt precursor with blossom extract in a 4:1 (v/w) ratio, and the reaction mixture was placed on a magnetic stirrer for 6 h with no interruptions. When mixed with salt solution, the plant extract turns pink from purple. When no changes were noticed after 6 h, 1 M NaOH was added dropwise to the reaction mixture to make the mixture pH basic. This resulted in a reddish pink precipitate at pH ∼ 10–12, finally changing into greenish. A magnetic stirrer was used to agitate the reaction mixture continuously for 2 h at 80 °C to complete the reaction. Then, the fine precipitate was collected and washed 3 times with Milli-Q water by centrifuging for 10 min at 8000 rpm; further, the pellets were gathered, and the supernatant was discarded. Then, the collected pellets were air-dried for 12 h in a hot air oven at 60 °C to remove the remaining water content; then, the dried pellets were collected and ground into a fine powder, utilizing a mortar and pestle before storing in an airtight container for further characterization and applications.

Fabrication of Nanobioengineered Platform for Sensing

A hydrolyzed indium tin oxide (ITO) glass substrate (1 × 2 cm2) was used to fabricate the c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO electrode. The ITO glass electrode was immersed in ammonia, hydrogen peroxide, and Milli-Q water solution of a 1:1:5 ratio for hydrolyzing and heated for 45 min in a hot air oven at 60 °C. Then, the ITO glass substrate was allowed to cool to ambient temperature. Further, to make colloidal suspension for EPD of c-Al2O3 NPs on the hydrolyzed ITO glass substrate, 2 mg of c-Al2O3 NPs was dispersed in 10 mL of 20% ethanol–water solution and then ultrasonicated for 30 min to form a homogeneous suspension. Before EPD, several parameters were optimized by adopting the hit-and-trial method. Then, c-Al2O3 NPs were deposited onto the ITO substrate by applying a DC potential of 25 V for 1 min to form a uniform thin film of c-Al2O3 NPs on the ITO glass substrate. Then, the fabricated modified electrode was utilized to perform electrochemical sensing. When not used, the modified nanobioengineered electrode was stored at 4 °C.

Results and Discussion

Plausible Mechanism of Synthesis

In this study, phytochemicals present in the B. variegate blossom extract are utilized to biologically reduce aluminum ions, as aluminum nitrate nonahydrate serves as the source of Al3+ ions (eq 1). As the blossom extract is used as a reducing and capping agent, owing to the presence of phytochemicals, such as tannins, flavonoids, and phenolics, they can reduce aluminum ions. Upon initial interaction of these phytochemicals with the salt precursor, the purple color changed to pink; this initial change suggests partial reduction and complex formation (eq 2). The reaction does not form entirely elemental aluminum but partially reduced aluminum ions or complex ions. Further, the addition of NaOH, for making the reaction mixture basic, initiates precipitation to form aluminum hydroxide (eq 3). Then, it further undergoes hydrolysis and condensation to obtain aluminum oxide (eq 4). Finally, the formed aluminum oxide interacts with the phytochemicals from the B. variegate extract (eq 5). The phytochemicals form a protective shell; in this way, the plant extract acts as a capping agent, which is evident from color change to greenish, indicating the formation of the core–shell structure

Characterization of the c-Al2O3 NPs

The c-Al2O3 NPs were initially studied using UV–visible spectroscopy, which revealed that the as-synthesized NPs are Al2O3, as the typical peak for these NPs appears between 200 and 300 nm (Figure 2A).39,40 The obtained value is 268 nm, corresponding to an intrinsic electronic transition in the NPs, and apart from this, it exhibits blue shifts owing to quantum confinement effects.41 Further, the vibronic shoulder and secondary peak broadening are observed in the 300–400 nm range. These are due to the presence of defect levels and surface states and due to adsorbed species on the NP’s surface. These adsorbed species are phytocompounds that formed shell structures on the c-Al2O3 NPs. The observed vibronic shoulder also indicates a strong electron–phonon coupling in NPs, as confirmed by the sharpness of the vibronic shoulder. It suggests that the synthesized NPs have highly ordered crystal lattices with well-defined phonon modes, possessing perfect crystalline structures with minimal structural defects.42,43 Further, the direct energy band gap of the c-Al2O3 NPs was calculated using eq 5 by the Tauc plot between energy and (αhν)2(eV cm–1)2 (Figure 2B) from the obtained UV–visible data.44 Further, the estimated band gap of 4.9 eV suggests the insulating properties of the as-synthesized material. Despite its insulating behavior, the material has semiconductor properties due to surface defects that introduce energy levels within the band gap, facilitating charge carrier generation and offering them potential application in the field of electronics and optoelectronics.45,46 In eq 6, “α” and “hν” are the absorption coefficient and energy of the photon, respectively. “A” and “Eg” are the constant and direct band-gap energy, respectively

Furthermore, it can be stated that the plant phytochemical utilized in this experiment and their polyphenols were retained on the material’s surface, providing persistent stability and forming a shell structure. Vibrational spectral analysis of the c-Al2O3 NPs is primarily utilized to determine the functional group used for the synthesis, capping, and stabilizing agents for the NPs between wavelengths 500 and 4000 cm–1 (Figure 2C). The FTIR spectra of Al2O3 NPs revealed a broad absorption between 3200 and 3500 cm–1, attributed to the O–H stretching vibration and suggesting a hydroxyl group. Compared to the published literature, the obtained vibrational frequency at 1065 cm–1 corresponds to metal–oxygen, attributed to the Al–O bonds. Further, comparing these findings to the prior literature may suggest that this material is synthesized and stabilized by bioactive phytochemicals.41

Photocatalytic Behavior of c-Al2O3 NPs

The photocatalytic degradation of crystal violet over nanobioengineered c-Al2O3 NPs catalyst was measured under visible irradiation at room temperature and ambient pressure. The catalyst-loaded dye solution was kept under dark conditions to attain an adsorption/desorption equilibrium for 60 min. The photocatalytic reaction of the nanobioengineered catalyst was performed by the simultaneous exposure of solution under visible irradiation for 60 min. The sampling was done at intervals of 10 min. The photodegradation of crystal violet dye was monitored by a change in the absorbance of crystal violet corresponding to Imax at 590 nm. The centrifuged solution was analyzed with a colorimeter. The photocatalytic stability and reusability test of the optimized (5 mg/5 mL) c-Al2O3 NP catalyst were performed under visible irradiance through a cycling experiment. Initially, 5 mg of NP catalyst was dissolved in 5 mL of crystal violet solution. After that, the catalyst-loaded solution was placed through four consecutive cycles, lasting 60 min. After each cycle, the reacted NP catalyst was filtered, and a new crystal violet (dye) solution was added to the filtered NP catalyst.

Kinetic Study and Mechanism

The photocatalytic degradation of crystal violet over the c-Al2O3 NP catalyst was monitored under visible irradiation at room temperature and ambient pressure. The photocatalytic degradation reaction was well fitted with the pseudo-first-order kinetics, ln(C/C0) = kt, where C0 and C are the initial and final concentrations of crystal violet dye at the time (t), respectively, and k is the degradation rate constant. The rate constant was determined by plotting ln C/C0 as a function of time as shown in Figure 5A. The photocatalytic behavior of the prepared NP catalyst was observed for different concentrations, keeping the concentration of the dye solution fixed. Initially, the NP’s catalyst-loaded dye solution was first kept under dark for adsorption/desorption equilibrium for 60 min. During this, the NP’s catalyst-loaded dye solution will likely be adsorbed onto the c-Al2O3 NP’s surface. However, it is observed that there is no significant decrease in the concentration of the crystal violet dye. Under dark conditions, only the freely accessible charge carriers on the surface of c-Al2O3 NPs participate in the degradation of crystal violet. The control experiment without a catalyst also underwent visible illumination but showed negligible degradation, as shown in Figure 5B. The photodegradation of crystal violet over the c-Al2O3 NPs catalyst under visible irradiance was measured, and the results are shown in Figure 5B. It is seen that with the NP catalyst with a 5 mg/5 mL concentration, the photodegradation of crystal violet dye is remarkably enhanced. Figure 5C shows the effect of contact time on the concentration of the crystal violet dye over the prepared catalyst. Thus, the photocatalytic activity is maximized at the optimal concentration of the c-Al2O3 NP catalyst. This is attributed to enhanced charge separation and transportation to the catalyst surface for photocatalytic reaction.

Nanobioengineered c-Al2O3 NP photocatalytic behavior: (A) ln C/C0 vs time plot for the degradation of crystal violet dye by utilizing different concentrations of c-Al2O3 NPs for rate constant determination; (B) degradation of dye at intervals of 10–60 min under visible light using three different concentrations of c-Al2O3 NPs (1 mg/5 mL, 5 mg/5 mL, and 10 mg/5 mL); (C) after being remaining in the dark for 60 min, concentration of the crystal violet dye solution; and (D) under visible irradiation, cycling occurs in the photodegradation of crystal violet dye for 5 mg/2 mL c-Al2O3 NPs.

However, at low concentrations (e.g., 1 mg/5 mL), photocatalytic activity decreases. This is because the number of photogenerated e–/h+ available at the surface site is minimal. Therefore, fewer charge species participate in the reaction to form active superoxide radicals and hydroxyl species to degrade crystal violet molecules. Also, at a very high concentration (10 mg/5 mL), the recombination rate of the photogenerated e–/h+ pairs gets accelerated, thereby decreasing its photoactivity.50 The cycling experiment further investigated the photocatalytic stability test of the optimized nanobioengineered NP catalyst (5 mg/5 mL) under visible irradiance (Figure 5D). The photodegradation of crystal violet dye was analyzed for four consecutive cycles, each for 60 min at intervals of 10 min. It is observed that there was no significant change in the reaction rate during each of the four consecutive cycles. This indicates that the optimized 5 mg/5 mL NP catalyst retains its stability during the photocatalytic degradation of crystal violet dye. The enhanced photocatalytic activity of the optimized NP catalyst is attributable to the effective suppression of e–/h+ and its migration to the catalyst surface. Thus, it can be concluded that the optimum concentration of nanobioengineered c-Al2O3 NP catalyst is a dominant factor that eventually decreases the recombination rate by promoting efficient charge separation and allowing their migration to the catalyst surface for the photocatalytic reaction.

Electrochemical Behavior

Fabricated nanobioengineered c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO electrodes were characterized and studied using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) in a three-electrode setup. Initially, it was subjected to pH optimization; for this, CV was recorded by exposing the fabricated electrode to different pH solutions ranging from 5.7 to 8, which was prepared by adding 5 mM ferri/ferrocyanide ([Fe(CN)6]3–/4–) and 0.9% sodium chloride (NaCl) in 50 mM phosphate buffer (Figure 6A). The optimized pH for performing the electrochemical studies was pH 5.7 for this investigation, as at this pH, the anodic/oxidation peak current (Ipa) response was at its maximum, as presented in Figure 6B. After this, the Ipa dropped at pH 6 and 7, and from that pH, the Ipa again started to increase but remained lower than the Ipa response at pH 5.7. This may be due to the highest interaction between the mediator and c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO-modified electrode and also owing to the optimal protonation state of phytocompounds on c-Al2O3 NPs, enhancing electron transfer with the [Fe(CN)6]3–/4– mediator. This pH ensures that functional groups are in a state that maximizes binding and electron transfer, unlike at other pH levels.8 After determining the pH, the nanobioengineered and bare ITO electrodes were analyzed using CV and DPV at pH 5.7 in PBS solution, with a scan rate of 50 mV/s, to justify that the fabricated electrode on the ITO substrate has a modified surface from nanobioengineered NPs for electrochemical NaN3 sensing. The redox behavior in the CV studies, attributed to the [Fe(CN)6]3–/4– mediator, was initially recorded for bare ITO and c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO electrodes yielding Ipa of 0.391 and 0.608 mA, respectively (Figure 6C). The bare ITO showed the lowest current due to its unmodified surface, which offers fewer active sites and a smaller surface area for electrochemical reactions.51 In contrast, the modified electrode displayed higher Ipa due to the increased surface area and active sites provided by the c-Al2O3 NPs. These modifications also enhance electron-transfer efficiency and catalytic properties, lowering the potential required for redox reactions and increasing Ipa response.52,53 Further, the DPV analysis confirmed these findings, with current responses for bare ITO and c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO at 0.094 and 0.608 mA, respectively, consistent with the CV results shown in Figure 6D.

Kinetics Studies

Before electrochemical sensing studies were conducted, interfacial kinetic studies of fabricated c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO electrodes were performed by varying the scan rate from 10 to 100 mV/s. This study reveals how the current response changes with scan rate variations, helping to determine reaction kinetics as either reversible/quasi-reversible (fast reaction) or irreversible (slow reaction). From this, it was evident that the Ipa value increases, while the cathodic/reduction current (Ipc) peak decreases with increasing scan rate (Figure 6E). Additionally, the potential of the anodic peak (Epa) shifts toward more positive values, and the cathodic peak potential (Epc) shifts toward zero values (negative end). This analysis provides insights into the electrochemical processes at the electrode surface by varying scan rates, as different mechanisms predominate at different potentials and scan rates, offering comprehensive details about system dynamics and behavior.54 Furthermore, the Ip (Ipa and Ipc) data at varying scan rates were plotted against the square root of the scan rate (Figure 6F) to establish ideal behavior and determine whether the plot follows eq 8. The results revealed that Ipa and Ipc values are completely linear with respect to the scan rate, suggesting that the reaction at the nanobioengineered electrode interface is a quasi-reversible process. For this type of reaction, the scan rate plot shows linearity with slight deviations, aligning with the obtained results

Additionally, the interfacial kinetics of the fabricated nanobioengineered c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO electrodes were analyzed using eqs 9, 11, and 12. For determination of the charge-transfer rate constant (Ks), eq 9 was used, yielding a Ks value of 0.657 s–1. This indicates a very fast electron-transfer rate, as Ks values near 1 s–1 are considered very fast, while values near 10–4 s–1 are considered very slow.55 In eq 9, the values are “m” (peak-to-peak separation, 0.5096 V), “ν” (scan rate, 50 mV/s), n (number of electrons transferred, 1), “T” (temperature, 27 °C), “R” (gas constant, 8.314 J mol–1K–1), and “F” (Faraday constant, 96,485 C mol–1). For calculating the diffusion coefficient (D), the modified Randles–Sevcik equation was used, as the system involves a one-electron-transfer process (n = 1) and a quasi-reversible electrochemical reaction. eq 11 was used to obtain the D value, with “α” (charge-transfer coefficient) calculated using eq 10. Ipc and Ipa values at 50 mV/s were substituted to obtain an α value of 0.90. The mediator concentration (C) was 0.000005 mol cm–3, with other values similar to eqs 9 and 12, yielding a D value of 5.841 × 10–2 cm2 s–1. This indicates relatively quick diffusion at the electrode surface, as typical D values range from 1 × 10–5 to 1 × 10–6 cm2 s–1. The obtained value in the order of 10–2 signifies faster reaction kinetics, demonstrating efficient sensing capabilities of the fabricated nanobioengineered electrode. The Brown–Anson model56 (eq 12) was used to determine the surface concentration (ϒ). In this equation, all variables were the same as in eqs 9 and 11, with “A” representing the electrode’s surface area (0.25 cm2). Substituting these values provided a low surface concentration value of 3.306 × 10–7 mol cm–2, indicating limited availability of electroactive sites, likely constrained by phytocompound shell coverage on the c-Al2O3 NP interface. Since the Ks value indicates a relatively fast electron transfer, the overall reaction at the fabricated electrode interface is diffusion-controlled

Nanobioengineered Electrode-Based Sensing of NaN3

After validating the fabricated nanobioengineered interface and further understanding the interfacial kinetics of nanobioengineered interface-based electrodes, sensing of NaN3 was performed using c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO (nanobioengineered) electrode. NaN3 is widely used in hospitals and laboratories; it is highly toxic, akin to cyanide, and can cause chronic diseases like Alzheimer’s. Its environmental discharge poses severe risks, necessitating an ultrasensitive sensor for detection in soil and food. Regulatory measures are essential to prevent health hazards and environmental contamination.57 Thus, proper monitoring of NaN3 in the environment is crucial for safety and human health purposes, so before selecting the standard NaN3 concentration for calibrating the fabricated sensor’s performance, a thorough literature survey of normal and abnormal ranges was conducted. It was evident from this that the normal range of NaN3 concentrations for human safety is below 10 μM, while concentrations above 10 μM are considered acutely toxic, and concentrations around 300 μM are potentially lethal. For environmental safety, NaN3 concentrations in aquatic environments should be kept below 1 μM to avoid toxic effects on aquatic life. Similarly, in soil, concentrations should be below 10 μM to prevent harmful effects on soil organisms.57−60 Thus, from this, it was concluded that the standard concentration range of NaN3 for calibrating and understanding the characteristics of the fabricated nanobioengineered electrode for sensing was chosen in two ranges: one for a lower NaN3 concentration of 0–6 μM and the other for a higher NaN3 concentration of 8–20 μM. The analysis of the fabricated c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO nanobioengineered electrode was performed under optimized conditions of pH 5.7 and 40 s of wait time after adding each concentration of NaN3 (Figure 7A), as the electrode takes this much time to respond properly toward the analyte (NaN3). From the obtained results of this analysis (Figure 7B), it was evident that, with increasing NaN3 concentration, Ipa increases linearly (Figure 7C) up to 0–6 mM concentration (lower NaN3 concentration range), and again, the Ipa is linear up to 8–20 mM concentration (higher NaN3 concentration range) with R2 of 0.981 and 0.965, respectively. These obtained R2 values indicate a strong fit for the sensing of NaN3, and it can also be concluded that 98.1 and 96.5% of the variance indicate a greater spread of data points to the mean, suggesting that the fabricated sensors are highly reliable and accurate. Further, from the obtained data (Figure 7B,C), the electrode’s limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantification (LOQ), and sensitivity were calculated using eqs 13–15, respectively. In these equations, “SD” stands for standard deviation (0.000905), “S” is slope (0.00586), and “A” (0.25 cm2) is the active area of the electrode. Thus, substituting all of the values in these equations, the obtained values of LOD, LOQ, and sensitivity are 0.463 1.544 μM, and 23.44 μA μM–1 cm–2, respectively. These obtained values indicate that the fabricated sensor is essential for environmental monitoring as it can detect and quantify NaN3 within and beyond the standard range used in this study (1–10 μM), and it can be inferred from the lower LOD and LOQ. In addition, the fabricated nanobioengineered electrode also demonstrates significant changes in signal per unit concentration of NaN3 as per the obtained sensitivity value, signifying a proper balance between sensitivity and LOD. In addition, the c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO electrode efficiently converts NaN3 into detectable signals faster, resulting in lower response time and higher sensitivity toward the target analyte, as obtained from the results of response time and sensitivity determination. These findings align with recent advancements in electroanalytical methods, which have shown significant promise in biological and environmental applications38

Furthermore, the stability (Figure 7D) of the c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO electrode was examined over 60 days at regular intervals of 5 days, and the findings revealed an RSD of 1.53%, indicating that the electrode is highly stable throughout the 60 day measurement window with measurements of 8 μM NaN3 concentration. Additionally, the reproducibility test (Figure 7E) was performed by fabricating 17 nanobioengineered electrodes and recording their CV in the presence of 8 μM NaN3. The relative standard deviation (RSD) obtained was 1.23%, indicating a high degree of reliability and suggesting that this electrode system is highly reproducible. The fabricated nanobioengineered c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO electrode was subjected to interference studies (Figure 7F). Initially, CV was recorded for 6 μM concentrations of NaN3 and then NaN3 with urea, hydrazine, and uric acid (UA) of 6 μM each. Then, a CV reading of NaN3 mixed with an interfering solvent mixture was recorded. The results indicated that the fabricated electrode is highly selective for NaN3, as the Ipa response is consistent with that of interferent samples. Comparing these findings with other pre-existing studies (Table 1) revealed that the fabricated sensor exhibits remarkable electroanalytical performance compared to other NaN3 sensors. In addition to this, there are very few NaN3 sensors that are explored in such a detailed manner and lack enormous studies owing to stability factors. This fabricated sensor has a dual linear range, offering a broader applicability in detecting NaN3 concentrations. Additionally, the sensitivity of the fabricated electrode is significantly higher than that of other sensors, ensuring precise detection of small NaN3 concentration changes. This response remained consistent upon the addition of all other interferents in the presence of NaN3. The LOD of the fabricated electrode allows for low-level NaN3 detection, and its response time of 40 s is also very less compared to existing NaN3 sensors. Thus, the overall performance of the fabricated sensor, with high sensitivity and a broad detection range, positions it as a robust and effective tool for NaN3 sensing in environmental and clinical diagnostics, making it superior in many respects to currently available NaN3 sensors.

Conclusions

In this work, a novel nanobioengineered core–shell NP was synthesized for the first time to the best of our understanding to fabricate a precise, selective, and sensitive electrochemical device for NaN3 sensing. Initially, B. variegate blossom extract was prepared, followed by the biosynthesis of c-Al2O3 NPs using the prepared blossom extract as reducing and capping agents for the successful formation of core (c-Al2O3)–shell (phytocompounds) NPs. This core–shell structure was achieved due to the interaction of phytochemicals from the blossom extract, and during the synthesis, the phytochemicals acted as both reducing and capping agents. Initially, aluminum ions (Al3+) were reduced to form aluminum hydroxide, which underwent hydrolysis and condensation to form c-Al2O3 NPs. The presence of phytochemicals on the surface of these NPs resulted in the formation of a protective shell around the c-Al2O3; this core–shell structure was crucial in enhancing the stability and functionality of the NPs. Further, the characterization of the c-Al2O3 NPs confirmed their composition, crystallinity, and morphology. These NPs were subsequently utilized to fabricate a modified electrode (c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO), which underwent extensive electrochemical and photocatalytic characterization. The interfacial kinetics studies of the fabricated nanobioengineered electrode revealed a quasi-reversible process with high electron-transfer rates (Ks) and peak currents (Ip) that demonstrated linearity with the square root of the scan rate, indicating efficient reaction dynamics. The electrode exhibited rapid response time, high sensitivity (23.44 μA μM–1 cm–2) toward NaN3, and a low detection limit of 0.463 μM within a linear range of 0–6 and 8–20 μM, along with high selectivity, stability, and reproducibility. The photocatalytic degradation of crystal violet dye by the nanobioengineered c-Al2O3 NPs exhibited significant photocatalytic activity with a well-fitted pseudo-first-order kinetic model, efficient charge separation, excellent stability, and reusability over multiple cycles.

Moreover, the enhanced sensing capabilities of the nanobioengineered c-Al2O3 NPs/ITO electrode, combined with its potential scalability for commercial applications, make it a valuable tool for environmental and health monitoring. Furthermore, the sustainable and green synthesis approach using plant extracts reduces the reliance on hazardous chemicals, promoting environmental sustainability. The mode of mechanism for the photocatalytic degradation of crystal violet dye (Figure 8A) and sensing of NaN3 (Figure 8B) using the c-Al2O3 NPs is deduced in this study. Thus, this work represents a significant step toward developing high-performance sensors through the innovative use of nanobioengineered NPs, and the findings pave the way for future advancements in point-of-care diagnostics, promoting healthy living and environmental sustainability.
