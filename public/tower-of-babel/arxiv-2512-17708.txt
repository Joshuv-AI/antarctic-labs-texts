Review of MEMS Speakers for Audio Applications Preprint: APS/123-QED
Nils Wittek, Anton Melnikov, Bert Kaiser, André Zimmermann

Abstract
Microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) speakers are compact, scalable alternatives to traditional voice coil speakers, promising improved sound quality through precise semiconductor manufacturing. This review provides an overview of the research landscape, including ultrasound pulse-based and thermoacoustic sound generation, classifying MEMS speakers by actuation principle: electrodynamic, piezoelectric, and electrostatic. A comparative analysis of performance indicators from 1990–2025 highlights the dominance of piezoelectric MEMS with direct air displacement, focusing on miniaturization and efficiency. The review outlines upcoming research challenges and identifies potential candidates for achieving full-spectrum audio performance. A focus on innovative approaches could lead to wideband adoption of MEMS-only speakers.



I
Introduction

Microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) are integral to modern technology. From inertial sensors in cars to accelerometers and microphones in smart consumer devices they have widely replaced parts manufactured by precision engineering. Their miniaturization, scalability through cost-effective mass production, and energy efficiency have enabled the success of many products essential to today’s life. However, MEMS speakers remain underrepresented, with smartphones and in-ear headphones still relying on century-old magnet-based technology
3_zentner_mems_2021
.

Since the 1960s, loudspeaker technology has seen limited innovation, continuing to use principles from the late 19th century
visse_microphones_2025
. Advances in semiconductor manufacturing since the early 2000s have sparked research on MEMS-based speaker designs
visse_microphones_2025
. This trend is reflected in the increasing number of publications on MEMS speakers, as shown in Fig.
1a
. From a market perspective, MEMS speakers are entering a phase of rapid innovation, while electrodynamic speakers have reached saturation
visse_microphones_2025
, indicating a potential disruptive shift in line with the innovation theory of Ref.
christensen_innovators_1997
.

According to Yole market research
visse_microphones_2025
, MEMS speakers are expected to enter the market through true wireless stereo (TWS) devices, where their compact size and energy efficiency are critical for battery life and form factor. However, TWS systems often include active noise control, which imposes additional performance requirements
kuo_active_1999
.

Listeners typically prefer specific frequency responses when listening to music, leading to standardized target curves such as the Harman curve shown in Fig.
1b
for in-ear and over-ear headphones
olive_preferred_2016
. Each speaker has a unique frequency response, requiring sufficient sound pressure levels (SPL) from the loudspeaker in amplified regions and attenuation from the entire system in others. Therefore, the speaker has to produce sounds with at least the same SPL as ambient noise, with the ability to reproduce audio playback on top of it. Especially at low frequencies, speakers must reach SPLs of up to
140\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
, accounting also for leakage losses
14_moore_speaker_2023
;
42_noauthor_sound_2024
.

To date, three review articles have addressed MEMS speakers for audio applications. Garud et al.
garud_mems_2024
discuss the fundamental drive mechanisms, research progress in India, and commercial developments. Gemelli et al.
gemelli_recent_2023
review MEMS speakers alongside microphones, covering drive and sensing principles as well as interface circuits. Wang et al.
52_wang_review_2021
focus on drive concepts and fabrication methods, highlighting potential performance improvements but excluding ultrasound pulse-based designs. Although not providing a review, Sun et al.
176_sun_figure_2025
introduce two performance indicators that simplify the design and optimization of piezoelectric MEMS cantilever speakers across the full frequency range.

This paper aims to provide an overview of international developments in MEMS speakers for audio applications, with emphasis on recent progress. It presents a detailed analysis of scientific research, including aggregated performance data for both qualitative and quantitative comparison. By analytically converting results from different measurement setups, the study enables cross-comparison of speakers with fundamentally different working principles. This supports a deeper understanding of sound generation concepts, including ultrasound pulse-based approaches, highlighting key design drivers, and aiding in identifying promising future concepts.

The article first introduces relevant acoustic parameters, sound generation concepts, and drive principles. Data collected from the literature are compared, followed by an overview of the different research approaches in the field. Modeling methods are assessed and insights from the literature survey are discussed.

III
Sound Generation Concepts

The following section gives an overview of the main sound generation concepts used in the literature on MEMS speakers.

III.1
Direct Displacement

Direct displacement is the most established method of sound generation in loudspeakers, originating with Alexander Graham Bell’s 1876 telephone patent. Sound generation in direct displaced speakers works through the displacement of a body, usually a diaphragm. In audio terminology, this movement is referred to as the excursion of the membrane.
As the diaphragm moves outward, it compresses air, increasing local density. When it retracts, regions of lower air density are created. These pressure variations propagate as longitudinal acoustic waves. Upon reaching the ear, they cause the eardrum to oscillate, enabling sound perception.
From Eq. (
6
), the pressure amplitude
\hat{p}
radiated by a directly displaced speaker can be expressed by Eq. (
7
), where the effective diaphragm area
A_{\mathrm{eff}}
and displacement amplitude

determine the radiated pressure. The frequency of diaphragm motion sets the frequency of the emitted sound.

\hat{p}\propto\hat{V}\propto\hat{x}\cdot A_{\mathrm{eff}}

(7)

III.2
Ultrasound pulse-based

Ultrasound pulse-based speakers operate using mechanically displaceable elements actuated at frequencies above the audible range (
>$20\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}$
)

. Variants of this principle include digital sound reconstruction (DSR)

;

In DSR, an array of micro-speakers, called speaklets, is driven at resonance for high displacement amplitudes. Each speaklet represents a digital bit, switching between displaced (high) and not displaced (low) states. The number of speaklets corresponds to the bitrate of a digital audio signal. Superposition of individual pulses creates an analog audio signal, also schematically shown in Fig.
1c

Pump-based speakers generate sound by pumping air with a diaphragm oscillating at an ultrasonic carrier frequency

The underlying modulation technique is known as double sideband modulation with suppressed carrier (DSB-SC). This method enhances SPL at low frequencies, as multiple pump cycles can occur within one period of the audio signal. However, this also leads to decreasing SPL with increasing frequency, following a

;

A variation of the pump-based concept uses a cavity with dimensions matched to a fraction of the carrier wavelength to form a resonance chamber. Amplitude-modulated diaphragms generate standing waves within the cavity. At points of maximum amplitude, additional diaphragms open the chamber to the opposite side, effectively demodulating the signal by releasing air at peak pressure locations.

Outside the scope of this review are parametric speakers, which employ arrays of ultrasonic transducers to emit a modulated carrier signal that is self-demodulated by the air, producing highly directional sound beams

III.3
Thermoacoustics

Thermoacoustic speakers, or thermophones, generate sound via the thermoacoustic effect. The sound signal passes as an alternating current through a conductive element, inducing temperature fluctuations in the surrounding gas. These thermal changes cause expansion and contraction of the gas, producing pressure waves

Several analytical models describe this phenomenon

IV.2
Piezoelectric

Applying a voltage across electrodes separated by a piezoelectric material leads to mechanical deformation through the reverse piezoelectric effect. V.2
Discussion of MEMS Speaker Performance

V.2.1
SPL and Normalized Pressure Trends over Years of Publication

Fig.
2a
presents the evolution of published absolute sound pressure levels (SPL) at
1\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
. Despite increasing publication activity, the average SPL has remained largely constant, as indicated by the linear fit. Piezoelectric speakers initially showed rising SPL values until around 2017, followed by stagnation. This trend could be reflected by a shift in research priorities toward miniaturization, reduced input voltage, and mitigation of low-frequency leakage. Factors not captured by a single SPL value. Electrostatic speakers exhibited early growth, then declined due to impractical initial designs, but have recently gained attention through novel concepts such as ultrasound pulse-based sound generation. Electrodynamic and thermoacoustic speakers show limited recent activity, likely due to poor compatibility with MEMS fabrication. While thermoacoustic devices can achieve high SPLs, this typically requires large form factors. Tab.
1
confirms the dominance of piezoelectric actuators with direct displacement, which account for more publications than all other principles combined. This is presumably due to the ability to reach high actuation forces at moderate voltages. Ultrasound pulse-based speakers remain underrepresented due to their recent emergence.

Fig.
2b
addresses limitations of the previous analysis by presenting pressure values normalized by active area and signal voltage amplitude, where data are available. The linear fit indicates a general increase in normalized sound pressure over the years of publication, possibly reflecting ongoing miniaturization trends. In contrast, the SPL data shown in Fig.
2a
do not exhibit a clear trend. Piezoelectric speakers show mixed performance in recent years, with a few high-performing designs but many near the average, suggesting that only specific implementations yield significant benefits, or their advancements are not captured by this metric of comparison. Electrostatic speakers initially performed above average, then declined, and have recently improved in normalized pressure, largely due to ultrasound pulse-based concepts using electrostatic actuation. While some piezoelectric devices still achieve higher normalized pressure, electrostatic approaches may surpass them with further development. It is important to note that this comparison is limited to a single frequency and does not consider the entire frequency response.

V.2.3
Correlations Among Key Performance Metrics

The correlations between selected parameters across MEMS speakers shown in Fig.
4a
mainly reflect the behavior of piezoelectric speakers with direct displacement, which dominate the dataset as shown in Tab.
1
.

SPL shows a positive correlation with capacitance for combined electrostatic and piezoelectric drive principles, also shown in Fig.
4b
. In these principles, quasistatic displacement
x
is proportional to the driving force according to Eq. (
11
), which is proportional to the geometric parameters influencing capacitance. Capacitance data are available only for these two drive principles, with piezoelectric capacitances showing high variability between specific designs due to material dependence. For instance, aluminum scandium nitride (
AlScN
) exhibits lower permittivity and thus lower capacitance compared to lead zirconate titanate (PZT).

Device area and active area are correlated, as larger devices accommodate larger diaphragms. Consequently, device area is not shown separately in the diagram.

SPL also correlates positively with active area. For directly displaced speakers, the pressure amplitude
\hat{p}
is proportional to the displaced volume
\hat{V}
, which increases with diaphragm size according to Eq. (
4
) for closed cavities and Eq. (
6
) for free-field conditions.

A positive correlation is observed between SPL and signal voltage amplitude. Displacement is proportional to the driving force (Eq. (
11
)), which is voltage-dependent for piezoelectric (Eq. (
16
)) and electrostatic (Eq. (
17
)) principles. In electrodynamic speakers, the Lorentz force is proportional to current
i
(Eq. (
12
)), which also relates to voltage via Ohm’s law. Thermoacoustic pressure generation is proportional to electrical power
P_{\mathrm{el}}
(Eq. (
10
)). Subsequently, the correlation is according to the expectations from device physics.

The correlation between SPL and first resonance frequency excludes thermoacoustic speakers, which lack resonances. For ultrasound pulse-based speakers, higher resonance frequencies allow more diaphragm cycles per audio period, increasing SPL. However, the behavior of directly displaced speakers dominates in the diagram, as displacement
x
is inversely proportional to stiffness, according to Eq. (
11
), which can be expressed by
k=m\cdot(2\pi f)^{2}
, leading to
x\propto\frac{1}{f^{2}}
. Thus, higher resonance frequencies reduce displacement and the volume amplitude
\Delta V
, lowering SPL.

Due to limited data, correlations involving capacitance are less conclusive. A positive relationship with active area is expected, as larger areas can yield higher capacitance. Higher capacitance should reduce required voltage, following
V=\frac{Q}{C}
. As speaker sizes decrease over the years of publication, capacitance may also decline. No clear correlation is observed between capacitance and resonance frequency.

Active area and signal voltage amplitude show a slight positive correlation, as larger diaphragms require stronger actuation forces. A reduction in active area over the year of publication is evident, contributing to increased normalized pressure per active area and signal voltage amplitude, as shown in Fig.
2b

2\text{\,}{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}20\text{\,}{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}
has been identified. The observed negative correlation between active area and resonance frequency is expected, since larger diaphragms typically imply lower stiffness and resonance frequency, as long as the speaker is not composed of multiple diaphragms. Signal voltage amplitude tends to decrease with the year of publication, reflecting miniaturization and efficiency improvements. Two preferred voltage levels are observed, approximately
2\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
and
30\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
. No clear correlation is found between signal voltage amplitude and resonance frequency. A slight decrease in resonance frequency is observed over the years of publication, with
69\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of data from

clustered between

Research Approaches on Speakers with Direct Displacement

VI.1
Electrodynamic Concepts

This section presents the progress of research on electrodynamic MEMS speakers with direct displacement. Key performance metrics are listed in Tab.
2
, and representative frequency responses are shown in Fig.

. A typical device structure is illustrated in Fig.

Shearwood et al.
167_shearwood_applications_1996
and Harradine et al.
58_harradine_micro-machined_1997
proposed electrodynamic MEMS speakers using circular polyimide diaphragms with SmCo magnets attached beneath, enabling pistonic out-of-plane motion. Cheng et al.
77_cheng_silicon_2004
later introduced a design featuring a polyimide diaphragm with a single-loop voice coil and a permanent magnet below, similar to the structure shown in Fig.
5a
.

Chen et al.
88_chen_optimized_2009
presented a speaker with an out-of-plane diaphragm actuated by a coil on its surface and a hard magnet beneath, similar to Fig.
5a
. Using a CuNi nanocomposite coil, they achieved higher SPLs than with copper at equal power input. A similar structure with a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) diaphragm was introduced in Ref.
87_chen_low-power_2011
, enabling larger displacements due to its low stiffness.

Neri et al.
145_neri_novel_2011
simulated a silicon diaphragm with a surrounding polymer suspension ring. Out-of-plane piston actuation was achieved via a coil on the diaphragm and a magnet below. The novelty lies in the adhesion method used to integrate the silicon diaphragm with the polymer springs.

Lemarquand et al.
90_lemarquand_mems_2012
and Shahosseini et al.
1_shahosseini_optimization_2013
published a speaker with a rigid silicon diaphragm clamped by silicon springs. The coil was placed on the diaphragm, and the magnet formed a ring around it. A structured backside increased rigidity while maintaining low mass. The use of an ironless magnet improved linearity. Sturtzer et al.
89_sturtzer_high_2013
later modified the spring design, as shown in Fig.
6a
.

Jin et al.
157_jin_stretchable_2015
introduced a flexible speaker that does not qualify as MEMS due to the absence of micromachining techniques, but is notable for its unique structure. The diaphragm is actuated out-of-plane by a coil formed from polymer microchannels filled with Galinstan, a liquid metal alloy at room temperature. A neodymium magnet provides the magnetic field. The coil is shown in Fig.
6b
. Due to its flexibility, the design is intended for wearable applications.

Ayatollahi et al.
96_ayatollahi_materials_2009
analyzed the material properties of a speaker design similar to Fig.
5a
, using a polyimide diaphragm. This structure was later fabricated and characterized by Majlis et al.
94_majlis_compact_2017
. Additional simulations with a PDMS diaphragm were conducted by Setiarini et al.
95_setiarini_novel_2018
.

Albach et al.
138_s_albach_sound_2011
designed a speaker based on magnetostriction. The device uses cantilevers with a magnetostrictive layer, inducing bending through unimorph actuation. This mechanism introduces nonlinear and hysteretic behavior. A large external magnet was required to generate the magnetic field.

Most electrodynamic MEMS speakers follow the structure shown in Fig.
5a
, typically using polymer diaphragms made of polyimide or PDMS. To achieve high magnetic field strengths, hard magnets composed of rare earth materials such as neodymium are commonly employed. While coil fabrication using micromachining has been demonstrated, magnet assembly remains manual.

Gao et al.

employs a diaphragm driven out-of-plane via piston-like motion, enabled by decoupling the drive unit from the diaphragm, allowing flexible material choices for the diaphragm, which is mounted on bending cantilevers

employs a silicon diaphragm actuated by piezoelectric cantilevers
34_noauthor_innovative_2023
. The resonance frequency lies above the audible range, minimizing phase shift in the output signal. Silicon as material for the diaphragm offers high stiffness, contributing to precise sound reproduction. These speakers are already integrated into commercial products as tweeters paired with dynamic drivers
43_noauthor_xmems_2024
.

The choice of piezoelectric material significantly affects speaker performance. Early designs used
ZnO
and
AlN
, while sol-gel PZT became common due to its high piezoelectric coefficients, further enhanced in ceramic form. Lead-containing PZT has prompted exploration of alternatives such as KNN and
AlScN
. Low driving voltages reduce hysteresis, and bias voltages help to prevent polarization shifts. Diaphragms are typically made from silicon (
Si
),
SiN
, Parylene-C, or polyimide, with stress management achieved through patterning, geometry, or cantilever bending. Array configurations and custom packages are used to improve the frequency response. Pre-stressing and bimorph cantilevers are applied for higher displacement, while varying cantilever sizes enable two-way designs. Spring geometry and out-of-phase electrode driving support piston-like motion for better linearity. Slits between cantilevers are minimized for acoustic sealing using viscous boundary layers or polymer coatings such as Parylene-C or PDMS. Research on MEMS speakers with piezoelectric direct displacement began in 1996, with notable growth since 2022.

summarizes key performance metrics, while Fig.

compiles frequency response data from selected studies.

In 1996, Rangsten et al.
59_rangsten_electrostatically_1996
introduced a CMOS-compatible speaker featuring a circular diaphragm with a sealed back cavity, actuated out-of-plane. The diaphragm is pre-bent by lower pressure in the cavity and operates in pull-mode. While audible, the device targets airborne ultrasound applications.

Neumann et al.
76_neumann_cmos-mems_2002
proposed a speaker with an out-of-plane polymer diaphragm clamped along all edges. Due to the polymer, fabrication requires a post-CMOS step. The silicon substrate serves as one electrode, while a stress-reducing mesh embedded in the diaphragm forms the second, enabling pull-mode actuation.

Kim et al.
85_hanseup_kim_bi-directional_2005
manufactured a speaker with two out-of-plane diaphragms, each with perforated electrodes above and below. The device comprises three bonded wafers with venting holes at both sides. The Parylene-C diaphragm limits CMOS compatibility and its deflection is constrained by the electrodes, enabling push-pull operation. The reason for higher SPL with a single diaphragm compared to actuation with both diaphragms remains unclear.

Roberts et al.
84_roberts_electrostatically_2007
adopted a similar design to Rangsten et al.
59_rangsten_electrostatically_1996
, also using a diaphragm clamped over a low-pressure cavity. The diaphragm, made of silicon carbide (
SiC

Researchers from
Infineon
and TU Graz adapted microphone fabrication processes to develop MEMS speakers. Glacer et al.
92_glacer_reversible_2013
created arrays of 255 out-of-plane actuated diaphragms using either a single perforated stator electrode in pull-mode or a push-pull setup with two perforated electrodes and a central diaphragm in Ref.
91_glacer_silicon_2014
. The push-pull configuration reduces nonlinearities at small deflections, although it is not optimized for sound generation. The CMOS-compatible process relies on stress-induced self-raising of stator electrodes. FEM simulations on a microphone used as a speaker by Tumpold et al.

, featuring a perforated stator electrode and fully clamped diaphragm in pull-mode, were used to analyze nonlinearities. A pre-distorted driving voltage with the inverse nonlinear behavior of the speaker was proposed to counteract its nonlinear response, enabling linear diaphragm motion. This approach reduced THD from
35.46\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
to
5.03\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
.

Kaiser et al.
68_kaiser_concept_2019
introduced an electrostatic speaker with in-plane diaphragm displacement in a CMOS-compatible process. The diaphragm consists of vertically oriented cantilevers clamped at both ends. Increasing cantilever height enhances displaced volume and therefore SPL without enlarging chip area. An image of the manufactured speaker with the measurement setup is shown in Fig.
6c
. The design was later refined
69_kaiser_push-pull_2022
by implementing a push-pull drive on cantilevers clamped on one end, to reduce THD caused by actuation asymmetry. Each cantilever integrates three electrodes. The actuation relies on the nanoscopic electrostatic drive concept published by Conrad et al.

Murarka et al.
142_murarka_printed_2016
presented a speaker with gold diaphragms suspended over an array of cavities, enabling out-of-plane pull-mode actuation between the conductive substrate and diaphragms. The diaphragms were fabricated using contact-transfer printing onto silicon dioxide (
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) cavities.

Sano et al.
160_sano_electret-augmented_2020
employed an electret to reduce the bias voltage required for electrostatic actuation. The structure is similar to Fig.
5f
, but with perforations in the diaphragm above the electric field and the electret placed beneath this gap. The manufacturing process is CMOS compatible, except for the electret formation step, which can be added post-process. The diaphragm operates out-of-plane in pull-mode. The speaker’s behavior, including the electret effect, was modeled analytically. Although a bias voltage is still needed, future designs may eliminate this requirement.

Godthi et al.
50_godthi_dynamics_2015
studied sound generation in field crickets and simulated their diaphragms using FEM. Inspired by this, Garud et al.
97_garud_novel_2020
proposed a speaker with a perimeter-actuated circular diaphragm similar to Fig.
5f

Khan et al.
158_khan_mutually_2020
proposed a mechanically robust, flexible, and transparent diaphragm composed of a graphene monolayer on a polymer for out-of-plane actuation.

Verreault et al.
103_verreault_surface-micromachined_2022
simulated a levitating diaphragm actuated in all degrees of freedom by surrounding electrodes. This floating design eliminates mounting structures, reducing damping and introducing a novel concept for electrostatic speakers. Experimental feasibility has still to be proven.

Bhuiyan et al.
35_bhuiyan_electrostatic_2024
presented a speaker with a square diaphragm actuated out-of-plane by vertically arranged actuator arrays on all four sides. The actuators bend during operation, inducing diaphragm deflection. Due to the absence of push-pull actuation, the system exhibits nonlinearities. A specialized high aspect ratio process was used for fabrication.

Ruiz et al.
181_ruiz_integrating_2025
proposed an electrostatic speaker based on a slot dipole antenna with cantilevers embedded in its slit. A radio signal with a carrier frequency of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
, amplitude-modulated by the audio signal, induces electrostatic displacement of the cantilevers, generating sound. The SPL depends on the radio signal power, enabling passive operation without an external power source.

Early concepts employed pre-bent diaphragms using cavity pressure and often repurposed electrostatic MEMS microphones for sound generation. Transitioning to structures optimized for acoustic radiation improved performance. The adoption of push-pull configurations enhanced sound quality by reducing THD. Most electrostatic microspeakers are CMOS compatible, except when polymer diaphragms are used.

Research Approaches on Ultrasound pulse-based Speakers

This section provides an overview of research on ultrasound pulse-based speakers. A tabular comparison of performance metrics is presented in Tab.
6
.

Diamond et al.
17_diamond_digital_2003
introduced the concept of digital sound reconstruction (DSR) using MEMS. They fabricated an array of 255 electrostatic diaphragms actuated out-of-plane via CMOS-compatible micromachining, enabling 8-bit signal reconstruction. The operating principle is explained in section
III.2
. Although individual diaphragm deflection is nonlinear with respect to voltage, the overall output remains linear. Sound pressure scales with the number of active speaklets, requiring minimal variation among them to avoid distortion. The speaker size must remain small relative to the acoustic wavelength to prevent interference between speaklets and preserve point-source behavior.

Monkronthong et al.
172_monkronthong_multiple-level_2014
proposed a DSR method using non-uniform pulses to reduce the number of speaklets required for signal reconstruction at a given bitrate. A corresponding speaklet design was published and simulated in
173_monkronthong_study_2016
, featuring an out-of-plane aluminum diaphragm actuated by a PZT layer. Simulations confirmed the feasibility of reducing the constraint of needing one speaklet per bit.

A related approach was presented by Santos et al.
170_santos_microelectromechanical_2014
, using a single diaphragm with individually addressable electrode regions for electrostatic out-of-plane actuation, validated through simulation.

Dejaeger et al.
25_dejaeger_development_2012
introduced a DSR array using PZT actuators based on the bimorph and inverse piezoelectric effects. Speaklets with varying radii were fabricated and tested, revealing hysteresis and nonlinearities. Building on this, Casset et al.
49_casset_256_2015
developed a 256-element PZT actuator array with circular actuators, producing out-of-plane displacement via bimorph actuation, with inner and outer electrodes enabling positive and negative deflections.

Arevalo et al.
28_29_arevalo_mems_2016
manufactured two DSR arrays, one based on piezoelectric transducers and the other on electrostatic actuation.

De Pasquale et al.
171_de_pasquale_modeling_2016
proposed a CMOS-compatible speaklet for DSR using electrostatic out-of-plane actuation in pull-mode, similar to Fig.
1g
, with a perforated diaphragm.

Mayrhofer et al. introduced the ADSR concept to enhance classical DSR, as described in Ref.
4_mayrhofer_new_2021
. This approach employs a novel speaklet within the array configuration. By incorporating side channels and shutter gates on both main and side channels, the diaphragm can be repositioned without inducing flow in the main channel. This enables the return of an actuated speaklet without generating reverse airflow. The principle was validated using a macroscopic pump.

Chen et al.
10_chen_modulated_2025
, affiliated with
SonicEdge
, published the modeling of their ultrasound pump-based speaker, detailing its operating principle and simulations using a lumped parameter model (LPM). The device comprises an array of electrostatically driven pumps, each with two movable diaphragms and one fixed diaphragm in between having a slit in the middle. The bottom diaphragm follows the carrier frequency of
400\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
, amplitude modulated with the audio signal, while the top diaphragm demodulates by modulating the acoustic impedance between the middle cavity and the ear volume. The frequency response, shown in Fig.
3a
, is represented by the red curve on top.

In addition to piezoelectric speakers with direct displacement,
xMEMS
has developed an ultrasound pulse-based design
liang_air-pulse_2024
, where piezoelectrically actuated cantilevers generate pressure waves in a chamber. Resonance forms standing waves, and audible sound is produced by periodically venting the chamber using secondary cantilevers driven by a modulated signal.

Tenorio et al.
146_tenorio_physics-based_2025
published an LPM for simulating ultrasound pulse-based speakers using amplitude modulation. The model includes an actuator, acoustic channel, and shutter, accounting for wave propagation and standing wave effects when channel dimensions exceed the acoustic wavelength. Validation was performed through comparison with FEM.

Ultrasound pulse-based speakers using DSR were first introduced in 2002 and further developed between 2014 and 2016. ADSR, an enhanced variant, was proposed in 2021 and remains at the proof-of-concept stage. A more recent approach involves modulation and demodulation, with only two publications to date. Both concepts can be implemented using piezoelectric or electrostatic actuation. Array configurations benefit from the precision of micromachining, enabling CMOS compatibility.

Research Approaches on Thermoacoustic Speakers

Although thermoacoustic speakers lack mechanical components and are typically fabricated using non-micromachining methods, they are included due to overlapping applications and advances in carbon nanotube materials. Tab.

Shinoda et al.
123_shinoda_thermally_1999
employed porous silicon tracks with electrodes on both sides to generate sound thermally. Kiuchi et al.

Xiao et al.

demonstrated a thermoacoustic sound source using four layers of carbon nanotube films on rigid or flexible insulating substrates. A device with an area of
900\text{\,}{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}

Niskanen et al.
141_niskanen_suspended_2009

Tian et al.
23_tian_graphene–paper_2011

Suk et al.

Fei et al.

D. D. Wang et al.
101_wang_study_2019

Debo Wang et al.
159_wang_research_2020

Thermoacoustic speakers have benefited from advances in graphene and carbon nanotube fabrication, which enable high SPLs due to their low heat capacity per unit area. Substrate materials and structures have also been explored to achieve a high heat capacity per unit area. Output sound pressure is proportional to input power, and frequency doubling occurs without a DC bias. The used materials support applications requiring flexibility, transparency, or large form factors. Research activity peaked between 2008 and 2012, with no new publications since 2020.

IX
Modeling Methods

Models are used to accelerate the design process of MEMS speakers by enabling simulation of transient and modal behavior, considering the coupling between electrical, mechanical, and acoustical domain, allowing data collection without the need for manufacturing and measurements.
kahrizi_modeling_2012

The design process of a microsystem like a MEMS speaker can be divided into three phases with three corresponding modeling methods mainly used in these phases
garcia_mechanics_2004
;
lenk_electromechanical_2011
. The concept phase is characterized by the use of high-level models based on simplified analytical equations, which are used to represent the core working principles. In the step of the individual component modeling, mainly distributed models in the form of field models are used, which are solved through numerical solvers. In this step the extraction of parameters for the system level modeling takes place
di_barba_mems_2020
, where combined models in the form of lumped parameter models (LPM) and their representation as equivalent circuit models (ECM) are used to simulate the entire system, which is necessary for developing the driving circuit of the MEMS speaker as well as the packaging
bombieri_mems_2016
. Extensive information about modeling of MEMS with distributed and lumped parameters can be found in Ref.
lenk_electromechanical_2011
.

Analytical models are fast to calculate
garcia_mechanics_2004
and help to understand the physics behind an effect, as they describe it fundamentally. But they can get too complex, therefore often simplifications are needed, or they can only be applied while certain conditions hold true
garcia_mechanics_2004
.

Distributed models like field models are based on partial differential equations and consider the physical layer of the device as well as the geometry of the components
di_barba_mems_2020
. They can be solved through numerical methods with varying degrees of accuracy and in different amount of dimensions
di_barba_mems_2020
. To code these models, understanding of the physical phenomenons is needed and computing is slow especially when solving in more dimensions and with high accuracy, but they are well suited to extract parameters for circuit models
di_barba_mems_2020
. A variety of different numerical methods exists to solve field models, with the most prominent today being the finite element method (FEM) because of its reliability and effectiveness
di_barba_mems_2020
. This method is often used through commercially available software like
ANSYS

117_becker_meander-shaped_2025
;
77_cheng_silicon_2004
and
COMSOL

11_stoppel_highly_2025
;
104_wang_high-spl_2020
;
72_liechti_high_2023
;
70_liechti_piezoelectric_2022
;
55_gazzola_design_2023
;
8_lin_bandwidth_2024
, but also open source software like
openCFS

161_guilvaiee_validated_2023
.

LPMs and their representation as ECMs are a combined approach for modeling. In these models space effects are not considered, as they are zero-dimensional and only based on ordinary differential or algebraic equations
di_barba_mems_2020
. They are based on circuit theory, necessitating the discretization of the structures into components like resistors, capacitors, conductors, and transforming elements between different physical domains
liechti_equivalent_2024
. These simplifications can reduce computation times and complexities for implementing them
di_barba_mems_2020
. The parameters for the models can be extracted by FEM or analytically
bombieri_mems_2016
. These fast dynamic models allow simulating complex systems with less computational resources than FEM, because the complexity is reduced to fewer degrees of freedom
garcia_mechanics_2004
with the used principle of modal superposition
bombieri_mems_2016
. Contrary to field models, ECM describe the devices’ behavior as black box while field models simulate the internal effects, which are often of multi-physical nature
di_barba_mems_2020
. The choice between them is a trade-off between cost and accuracy
di_barba_mems_2020
while they also have the restriction of only delivering accurate results when the dimensions of the loudspeaker are smaller than the used wavelengths, therefore they can describe the behavior in the range of the fundamental mode
169_bai_acoustic_2005
;
liechti_equivalent_2024
. A review focused on the application of ECM modeling for electroacoustic MEMS by Liechti
liechti_equivalent_2024
can be referred to for more details.

A more precise and faster development process is the result of these efforts. X
Discussion

X.1
Manufacturing and Materials

The choice of drive principle influences manufacturing complexity and material compatibility. Each approach presents distinct fabrication challenges and opportunities for integration with standard microfabrication processes.

Electrodynamic MEMS speakers face limitations due to manual assembly, particularly the need to glue permanent magnets to the substrate
86_je_compact_2009
. Micromachining techniques are not well suited for fabricating permanent magnets, which restricts scalability, increases device size as well as weight, and complicates integration due to the variety of required materials and components. Coil fabrication via molding or sputtering remains challenging but has been demonstrated
1_shahosseini_optimization_2013
. Achieving strong magnetic fields in compact speakers requires rare earth materials, which are costly and associated with environmental and ethical concerns.

In contrast, electrostatic MEMS speakers benefit from full CMOS compatibility, enabling automated mass-manufacturing and scalability. Exceptions include the use of polymers for diaphragms or sealing.

Piezoelectric speakers offer a middle ground in manufacturability, with materials well integrated into microfabrication and mostly CMOS compatible, though not fully manufacturable through CMOS processes
gemelli_recent_2023
. PZT remains widely used due to its superior piezoelectric properties, enabling high displacement at low voltages
73_kuentz_knn_2024
. It is typically deposited as sol-gel film or ceramic, with ceramics offering higher piezoelectric coefficients
104_wang_high-spl_2020
but requiring non-silicon substrates. The lead content of PZT poses environmental and health risks
ibn-mohammed_life_2018
. Alternatives such as
ZnO
and
AlN
offer CMOS compatibility
113_fawzy_design_2021
, lower thermal budgets during manufacturing, and improved linearity
114_lang_piezoelectric_2022
. Doping with scandium further enhances
AlN
performance
110_chen_monolithic_2025
. KNN is another lead-free option with comparable or superior properties
73_kuentz_knn_2024
;
148_gao_study_2014
, though its environmental footprint is higher than that of PZT
ibn-mohammed_life_2018
.

Thermoacoustic speakers perform best when materials with low heat capacity per unit area, leading to high thermal conductivity, are used. Carbon nanotubes and graphene are particularly suitable. The substrate should have high heat capacity per unit area to minimize thermal losses. Fabrication methods vary from simple processes
123_shinoda_thermally_1999
;
122_kiuchi_new_2005
;
141_niskanen_suspended_2009
to complex transfer techniques
23_tian_graphene–paper_2011
;
24_tian_flexible_2011
;
118_suk_thermoacoustic_2012
;
100_fei_lowvoltage_2014
;
159_wang_research_2020
, depending on the materials involved.

X.2
Sources and Mitigation of Nonlinearities

Nonlinear behavior is a critical aspect across all MEMS speaker drive principles, affecting their signal quality. Each drive principle introduces distinct sources of nonlinearity, often requiring specific design strategies or material choices to mitigate them.

Nonlinearities in electrodynamic speakers arise from magnetic field inhomogeneities
77_cheng_silicon_2004
;
127_lee_analysis_2010
, although ironless magnets can mitigate these effects
90_lemarquand_mems_2012
.

For piezoelectric speakers, ferroelectric materials used for higher piezoelectric coefficients introduce a hysteretic behavior. It can be accounted for with a bias voltage
72_liechti_high_2023
, preventing the polarization switch and nonlinear behavior for high voltages.

Electrostatic speakers need high actuation voltages, requiring push-pull configurations to reduce nonlinearities caused by electroelastic asymmetry. Electrets can lower voltage requirements
160_sano_electret-augmented_2020
, though this approach remains underexplored.

A characteristic of the thermoacoustic principle is frequency doubling of the output, which can be corrected using a DC bias
137_xiao_flexible_2008
;
141_niskanen_suspended_2009
.

X.3
Risks and Benefits of individual Concepts

Choice of sound generation concept and individual structure for a drive principle can come along with certain risks or benefits discussed in the following section.

Electrodynamic MEMS speakers in general benefit from compatibility with conventional voice coil designs, simplifying circuit integration.

For piezoelectric speakers, unimorph actuators are most common, but bimorphs can increase displacement at the cost of fabrication complexity
110_chen_monolithic_2025
. Another option is activating the
d_{33}
mode instead of the
d_{31}
mode, which offers higher piezoelectric coefficients and remains underutilized
155_kim_piezoelectric_2009
;
153_kim_effects_2015
.

In electrostatic devices, anodic corrosion under humidity and electric fields could be a concern. Although extensively studied
18_shea_effects_2004
;
22_liu_corrosion_2011
;
30_stolz_reliability_2021
, it is rarely addressed in MEMS speaker literature. Mitigation strategies include passivation coatings and corrosion-sensitive warning structures
18_shea_effects_2004
;
22_liu_corrosion_2011
;
30_stolz_reliability_2021
.

Regarding MEMS speakers with direct displacement overall, incomplete clamping of the diaphragm can lead to acoustic shortcuts between front and back volumes. This often occurs when multiple deflected elements are used to increase displacement at a given voltage. Sealing these slits with materials such as PDMS
109_wang_capillary_2024
or Parylene-C
82_hu_design_2025
;
105_ma_pzt_2023
;
119_wang_obtaining_2021
prevents air leakage but increases structural stiffness.

An alternative approach to address this, is the mechanically open, acoustically closed design
andreas_analysis_2017
;
56_stoppel_new_2018
;
andreas_design_2019
;
11_stoppel_highly_2025
;
55_gazzola_design_2023
, where slit dimensions are minimized to suppress airflow via viscous boundary layers. This method is frequency-dependent and may allow leakage at lower frequencies if slits are not sufficiently small.

Emerging concepts such as ultrasound pulse-based speakers present additional research opportunities considering ultrasound exposure. Chen et al.
10_chen_modulated_2025
report no health risks at hundreds of kilohertz. Ideally this should be confirmed with dedicated clinical studies, especially considering ultrasound emission directly in the ear canal at frequencies relevant for pulse-based speakers.

X.4
Performance Comparison

The performance of MEMS speakers varies significantly depending on the actuation principle, with each approach offering distinct advantages and limitations in terms of SPL, power efficiency, frequency response, and scalability.

Electrodynamic speakers are characterized by high power density, large mechanical displacement, low actuation voltage, and quasi-linear behavior
1_shahosseini_optimization_2013
;
89_sturtzer_high_2013
. They are well suited for the hearable frequency range, with resonances between
0.3\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}20\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
used to enhance displacement. Although early research highlighted magnetic actuation as promising, interest has declined in recent years. SPL values are moderate to high, with the highest average SPL per unit area among all principles. Power consumption ranges from
1\text{\,}\mathrm{mW}500\text{\,}\mathrm{mW}
, higher than piezoelectric devices but lower than thermoacoustic ones. Device areas typically fall in the range between
5\text{\,}{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}
and
180\text{\,}{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}
. However, the current-driven nature of these devices complicates direct comparison with voltage-driven alternatives, as their impedances are frequency-dependent.

Despite the dominance of electrostatic actuation in MEMS, only about
25\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of published speaker designs use this principle (Tab.
1
), likely due to the lower force output and higher voltage requirements compared to piezoelectric drives. Nevertheless, they achieve higher mean SPL and SPL per active area than piezoelectric and thermoacoustic alternatives. Their resonance frequencies are also the highest among all principles, allowing flat frequency responses in the lower audible range. Electrostatic speakers typically exhibit low capacitance in the range of
\mathrm{pF}
, resulting in lower current consumption compared to piezoelectric devices as depicted in Fig.
4b
.

Most publications focus on directly displaced MEMS speakers actuated by piezoelectric layers, with increasing research activity in recent years. They typically have one order of magnitude higher capacitances than electrostatic speakers, reducing required voltage, with the drawback of higher currents needed. SPL at
1\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
increased until 2015 and has declined since, as shown in Fig.
2a
. Many designs achieve flat frequency responses in a closed cavity between
0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}1\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
, with resonances above this range as depicted in Fig.
3a
. Normalized pressure has improved over the year of publication, but recent publications have moderate SPLs as shown in Fig.
2b
, suggesting diminishing returns. Current research often revisits established concepts or focuses on aspects not captured by SPL metrics, such as sealing strategies for improvement at low frequencies or lead-free materials.

Thermoacoustic speakers present a unique trade-off between structural flexibility and power efficiency. Power efficiency decreases for thermoacoustic speakers at lower frequencies due to heat dissipation in the audible frequency band
157_jin_stretchable_2015
. While high absolute SPLs have been achieved, efficiency remains low due to large device areas and the absence of resonant amplification. Drive voltages are relatively low, but power consumption is significantly higher than in other actuation principles.

Ultrasound pulse-based speakers offer promising performance for low-frequency sound generation due to the inverse frequency dependence of SPL, as for lower frequencies, more ultrasound pulses fit in a period of the audio signal. Simulating these systems is computationally demanding because of fine time resolution and multiphysics interactions, which has led to the use of LPMs for faster simulations
146_tenorio_physics-based_2025
;
10_chen_modulated_2025
. These speakers currently show the most potential for achieving SPLs around
140\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
at low frequencies, which are required for active noise cancellation
visse_microphones_2025
. As shown in Fig.
3a
, no directly displaced speaker reaches
120\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
at low frequencies, and performance drops further with leakage. Present designs rely on amplitude modulation, but alternative modulation schemes from signal theory remain unexplored and may offer future improvements.

XI
Outlook

Reviewing the literature on MEMS speakers for audio applications reveals several areas for future investigation.

For electrodynamic MEMS speakers, further research could be conducted on manufacturing methods for permanent magnets. However, electrodynamic speakers continue to suffer from assembly challenges. The observed decrease in research focus on this principle seems appropriate.

While piezoelectric MEMS speakers have been extensively studied, identifying lead-free materials with properties comparable to PZT offers potential for further investigations. Promising candidates include
AlScN
and KNN.

For electrostatic MEMS speakers, reducing the required drive voltage remains an area open for further research. Additionally, environmental robustness could be investigated, prioritizing mitigation strategies that avoid substances of concern for environmental and health safety. Research could focus on durability, impact on SPL, THD, and long-term performance.

One key challenge is improving low-frequency performance under leakage-prone ear coupler conditions. Ultrasound pulse-based speakers, which can achieve high SPLs at low frequencies, offer a promising solution. However, these concepts are still emerging, and many design aspects remain unexplored, including optimal speaker geometry and the choice between piezoelectric and electrostatic actuation. Additionally, systematic studies on the effects of ultrasound and novel applications present research opportunities. It is also unclear whether these speakers can function as full-range devices or if combining them with direct displacing units could improve the frequency response. Their performance under free-field conditions requires further evaluation. In terms of modeling, existing LPMs could be expanded to account for nonlinearities and wave phenomena in larger structures. For FEM, the feasibility of multi-domain modeling in the ultrasonic range can be further explored. Regarding ADSR, the next step is to translate the working principle into a functional MEMS speaker.

Thermoacoustic speakers, typically fabricated over large areas, present an opportunity for adaptation to in-ear formats, aligning them more closely with MEMS designs. However, they exhibit poor low-frequency performance and high power consumption. Their use may remain limited to applications requiring transparency or mechanical flexibility. A reduced research focus on this principle, as already observed in recent years, appears justified.

XII
Conclusion

The emergence of MEMS speakers as successors to traditional voice coil speakers in certain applications is driven by advantages in semiconductor manufacturing, including precise tolerances, scalability, and integration with drive electronics. This review provides a structured comparison of MEMS speaker technologies based on the sound generation concepts direct displacement, ultrasound pulse-based, and thermoacoustic, along with the drive principles such as electrodynamic, piezoelectric, and electrostatic. Current research is dominated by piezoelectric direct displaced speakers due to their favorable balance of performance and manufacturability.

Across these concepts, progress has been made in increasing sound pressure per area and voltage, reducing THD, and advancing miniaturization. However, meeting the SPL requirements of applications like TWS across the full audible range remains challenging, particularly at low frequencies under leakage-prone conditions. Ultrasound pulse-based speakers, though still in early development, show promise in addressing these limitations.

Modeling plays a critical role in design and optimization. Finite element models (FEM), lumped parameter models (LPM), and analytical approaches enable early performance prediction and accelerate development. Future work could focus on incorporating nonlinear effects and multi-physics interactions, especially relevant for ultrasound pulse-based designs.

Further research is also needed on lead-free piezoelectric materials such as
AlScN
and KNN, and on hybrid architectures that combine multiple sound generation principles, particularly direct displacement and ultrasound pulse-based approaches.

Despite substantial progress, many opportunities remain in this interdisciplinary field, which bridges materials science, microfabrication, acoustics, and circuit design. MEMS-only speakers have the potential to transform consumer electronics, medical devices, and industrial systems.

We believe that this review allows researchers and experts from MEMS and audio industries to get a condensed global overview over the state of the art of MEMS speakers. This comprehensive synthesis highlights the significant progress made with respect to miniaturization, performance, and integration, while also illuminating the persistent challenges and exciting opportunities for future innovation. Ultimately, continued collaborative efforts across these disciplines will be crucial in realizing the full potential of MEMS microspeakers for a new generation of compact, high-fidelity audio devices.
