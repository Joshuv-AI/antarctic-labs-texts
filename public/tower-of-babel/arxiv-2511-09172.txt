Introduction

Figure 1:
Examples of waveguides (source Wikipedia).

The aim of these lecture notes is to consider a concrete problem, namely the identification of situations of invisibility in waveguides, to present techniques and tools of applied mathematics that can be useful in other contexts. We will be interested in the propagation of scalar waves in guides which are unbounded in one direction. Such problems arise in many fields of physics. For example air ducts and horns in acoustics carry sound waves in musical instruments as well as in loudspeakers. Conductive metal pipes are exploited to propagate
high frequency radio waves while optical fibers serve as waveguides for light in electromagnetism. Waveguides problems also appear in water waves theory, in classical mechanics or in quantum mechanics. In general, the diffraction of an incident wave in such structures in presence of an obstacle generates a reflection and a transmission characterized by some scattering coefficients. Broadly speaking, our goal is to play with the geometry, the frequency and/or the index material to control these scattering coefficients.

This document is divided in four chapters. In the first one, we present classical results concerning waveguide theory. This is a rather long story and the aim here is not to be exhaustive but instead to present the main ideas and ingredients that will be useful to address the invisibility problematic. In Chapter
II
, we develop perturbative techniques based in particular on the use of shape derivatives to design invisible defects of the reference geometry. With these approaches, in principle we construct small amplitude invisible obstacles. In Chapter
III
, we exploit resonant phenomena to provide examples of larger invisible obstacles. There, we also propose a method to hid given objects by perturbing (in a singular way) the boundaries of the waveguide. Finally, in Chapter
IV
we change the point of view, assume that the obstacle is given, and construct a non self-adjoint operator whose eigenvalues coincide with frequencies such that there are incident fields which produce zero reflection.

Our approaches mainly rely on techniques of asymptotic analysis as well as spectral theory for self-adjoint and non self-adjoint operators. Wherever possible, we will illustrate the results by numerical experiments.

The first chapter contains classical material. In the next three, some more recent results are presented. They have been obtained with different colleagues, among them, Antoine Bera, Anne-Sophie Bonnet-Ben Dhia, Jérémy Heleine, Sergei Nazarov, Vincent Pagneux. I thank them warmly.

These lecture notes have been written as support material for a one-week course (5.5 hours in total) that I delivered at the Institut de Mathématiques de Toulouse in the period 23-27 June 2025 as part of the summer school Control, Inverse Problems and Spectral Theory. Assume that
f
in (

) belongs to
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)

\begin{array}[]{|l}\mbox{Find }u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\mbox{ such that }\\[3.0pt]
a(u,v)=\ell(v),\qquad\forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\end{array}

(I.6)

with

a(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v-k^{2}uv\,dxdy,\qquad\qquad\ell(v)=\int_{\Omega}fv\,dxdy.

The bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Therefore, with the Riesz representation theorem, we can introduce the linear bounded operator
A(k):\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

(A(k)u,v)_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}=a(u,v),\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(I.7)

In this section, we establish the following statement.

Theorem I.1
.

Pick
k\in(0;\pi)
. The operator
A(k)
decomposes as

A(k)=B+K

where
B:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is an isomorphism and
K:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is compact (
B
and
K
are allowed to depend on
k
).

Proof.

Define the bilinear form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
such that

b(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+((1+k^{2})\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{d}}-k^{2})uv\,dxdy,\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

where
\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{d}}
stands for the indicator function of the set
\Omega_{d}\coloneqq\{(x,y)\in\Omega\,|\,|x|<d\}
. Since
b(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we can define the bounded operator
B:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

(Bu,v)_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}=b(u,v),\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

From the Lax-Milgram theorem, to show that
B
is an isomorphism, it suffices to prove that
b(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Below we establish the 1D Poincaré inequality

\pi^{2}\int_{I}\varphi^{2}\,dt\leq\int_{I}(\partial_{t}\varphi)^{2}\,dt,\qquad\forall\varphi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)\coloneqq\{\psi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(I)\,|\,\psi(0)=\psi(1)=0\},

(I.8)

where we recall that
I=(0;1)
. Integrating this estimate with respect to
x\in(-\infty;-d)\cup(d;+\infty)
for
u\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
(the space of infinitely differentiable functions supported in
\Omega
) and using the density of
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we obtain

\pi^{2}\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{d}}}u^{2}\,dxdy\leq\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{d}}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dxdy,\qquad\forall u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

Therefore we can write, for all
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}b(u,u)&=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{d}}}|\nabla u|^{2}-k^{2}u^{2}\,dxdy+\|u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{d})}\\[10.0pt]
&\geq&\displaystyle\bigg(1-\cfrac{k^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\bigg)\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{d}}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dxdy+\|u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{d})}\\[10.0pt]
&\geq&(1+\pi^{2})^{-1}\bigg(1-\cfrac{k^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\bigg)\|u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega_{d}})}+\|u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{d})}\geq\alpha\,\|u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\end{array}

with
\alpha=(1+\pi^{2})^{-1}(1-k^{2}/\pi^{2})>0
. This shows that
b(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Now set
K=A(k)-B
. We have

(Ku,v)_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}=-(1+k^{2})\int_{\Omega_{d}}uv\,dxdy,\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(I.9)

To establish that
K:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is compact, we have to prove that from any bounded sequence
(u_{n})
of functions of
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we can extract a subsequence such that
(Ku_{n})
converges in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. By taking
v=Ku
in (
I.9
), we obtain, for all
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,

\|Ku\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq(1+k^{2})\,\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{d})}.

In particular, for
u_{mn}\coloneqq u_{m}-u_{n}
, we obtain

\|Ku_{mn}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq(1+k^{2})\,\|u_{mn}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{d})}.

(I.10)

Since
\Omega_{d}
is bounded, the Rellich theorem ensures that the embedding of
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{d})
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{d})
is compact. We deduce that we can extract from
(u_{n})
, which is bounded in
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
and so in
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{d})
, a subsequence, still denoted by
(u_{n})
, such that
(u_{n})
converges in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{d})
. Thus
(u_{n})
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{d})
. From (
I.10
), we infer that
(Ku_{n})
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Since this space is complete, we infer that
(Ku_{n})
indeed converges in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

We prove now the Poincaré inequality needed in (

Lemma I.2
.

We have

\inf_{\varphi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)\setminus\{0\}}\cfrac{\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}}{\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}}=\pi^{2}

(I.13)

so that there holds

\pi^{2}\,\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}\leq\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)},\qquad\forall\varphi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I).

(I.14)

Proof.

To obtain 1D Poincaré inequalities as (
I.14
), a classical approach consists in working with explicit representations. More precisely, for
\varphi\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(I)
, we can write, for
s\in(0;1)
,

\varphi(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\partial_{t}\varphi(t)\,dt.

According to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
, this implies, for all
s\in(0;1/2)
,

\varphi^{2}(s)\leq\int_{0}^{s}1\,dt\int_{0}^{s}(\partial_{t}\varphi(t))^{2}\,dt\leq s\,\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0;1/2)}.

Integrating this identity between
0
and
1/2
, we obtain

\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0;1/2)}\leq\cfrac{1}{8}\,\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0;1/2)}.

By establishing a similar estimate on
(1/2;1)
(note that
\varphi(1)=0
) and using the density of
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(I)
in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)
, we find

\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}\leq\cfrac{1}{8}\,\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad 8\,\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}\leq\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)},\qquad\forall\varphi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I).

This is a nice Poincaré inequality but it is not optimal (observe that (
I.14
) is better because
8<\pi^{2}
). Looking for the best Poincaré inequality leads us to consider the minimization problem

\inf_{\varphi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)\setminus\{0\}}\cfrac{\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}}{\|\varphi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}}\,.

(I.15)

Below we prove that this infimum, equal to some
\lambda>0
, is actually a minimum because it is attained at some functions
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)\setminus\{0\}
. Moreover we establish that these quantities satisfy

-\partial_{tt}^{2}u=\lambda u\qquad\mbox{ in }I.

(I.16)

In other words,
\lambda
is an eigenvalue (the smallest) of the Dirichlet Laplacian and
u
is a corresponding eigenfunction. Since
I=(0;1)
, a direct computation gives
\lambda=\pi^{2}
with
u(t)=\sin(\pi t)
(up to a multiplicative constant which does not change the ratio in (
I.15
)). Thus we obtain (
I.13
) and so (
I.14
).

To prove that the infimum in (
I.15
) is reached, let us first remark that solving (
I.15
) is equivalent to solve the constrained minimization problem

\inf_{\varphi\in\mathscr{B}}\left\{J(\varphi)=\int_{I}\partial_{t}\varphi^{2}\,dt\right\}

with
\mathscr{B}\coloneqq\{\varphi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)\,|\,\int_{I}\varphi^{2}\,dt=1\}
. The functional
J
is positive in
\mathscr{B}
, therefore we have
\inf_{\mathscr{B}}J\geq 0
. Consider
(u_{n})\in\mathscr{B}^{\mathbb{N}}
a minimizing sequence for
J
,
i.e.
a sequence of functions of
\mathscr{B}
such that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}J(u_{n})=\inf_{\mathscr{B}}J.

The sequence
(J(u_{n}))
is bounded and so
(u_{n})
is bounded in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)
. Then we know that we can extract a subsequence, still denoted by
(u_{n})
, such that there is some
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)
such that

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\text{ weakly in }\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I),\qquad u_{n}\rightarrow u\text{ strongly in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(I).

Now, by writing

0\leq(\partial_{t}(u-u_{n}),\partial_{t}(u-u_{n}))_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\|\partial_{t}u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}+\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}-2(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}u_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)},

we obtain
2(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}u_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}-\|\partial_{t}u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}\leq\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}
. By passing to the inferior limit, we deduce

\|\partial_{t}u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}\leq\liminf\limits_{n\to+\infty}\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}

and so

J(u)\leq\liminf\limits_{n\to+\infty}J(u_{n})=\inf_{\mathscr{B}}J.

But, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\|u_{n}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}=1
. Since
(u_{n})
converges strongly to
u
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)
, we deduce
\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}=1
, which shows that
u
belongs to
\mathscr{B}
. Thus
J
attains its infimum in
\mathscr{B}
at
u
.

Set
\lambda=J(u)
. For all
v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)\setminus\{0\}
, we have
v/\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)}\in\mathscr{B}
and so

J(v/\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)})\geq\lambda.

This gives, for all
v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)\setminus\{0\}
,

\int_{I}(\partial_{t}v)^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\,dt\geq 0.

Then we are led to consider the following minimization problem without constraint

\min_{v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)}\left\{\tilde{J}(v)=\int_{I}(\partial_{t}v)^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\,dt\right\}.

According to what precedes, the functional
\tilde{J}
is non negative in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)
. Moreover, we have
\tilde{J}(u)=0
and
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)
. Therefore
u
is a minimizer of
\tilde{J}
in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)
. Thus we must have, for all
v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I)
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

0=\tilde{J}(u)\leq\tilde{J}(u+sv).

This is equivalent to

0\leq s\left(\int_{I}\partial_{t}u\,\partial_{t}v-\lambda uv\,dt\right)+s^{2}\tilde{J}(v),\qquad\forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I),\,s\in\mathbb{R},

which holds if and only if

\int_{I}\partial_{t}u\,\partial_{t}v\,dt=\lambda\int_{I}uv\,dt,\qquad\forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(I).

We deduce that, in the sense of distributions, we must have the equation (
I.16
).

Remark I.4

In Figure

When

, if a function belongs to

. Therefore we have

\inf_{u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{S})\setminus\{0\}}\cfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dxdy}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dxdy}\leq\inf_{u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\cfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dxdy}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dxdy}\,.

Now integrating the 1D Poincaré inequality (

and using the density of

), this gives

\pi^{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dxdy\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dxdy,\qquad\forall u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

in
\Omega
. This shows that

is injective and Theorem

is an isomorphism of
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

In the previous paragraph, we studied Problem (

. Now we wish to understand what happens for

3.1
Computation of modes

The modes are defined as the solutions with separate variables,
i.e.
of the form

u(x,y)=\alpha(x)\varphi(y),

(I.18)

which solve Problem (
I.3
) in the reference strip
\mathcal{S}
for
f\equiv 0
. Inserting (
I.18
) in the equation
\Delta u+k^{2}u=0
in
\mathcal{S}
, this yields

\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x)\varphi(y)+\alpha(x)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(y)+k^{2}\alpha(x)\varphi(y)=0.

Dividing this identity by
\alpha(x)\varphi(y)
, we find that we must have

\begin{array}[]{|l}-\varphi^{\prime\prime}(y)=\lambda\,\varphi(y)\quad\mbox{ in }I\\[3.0pt]
\varphi(0)=\varphi(1)=0\end{array}

(I.19)

and

-\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x)=(k^{2}-\lambda)\,\alpha(x)\qquad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}

(I.20)

for some constant
\lambda
to be determined. Problem (
I.19
) is a spectral problem: we wish to find the values of
\lambda
such that (
I.19
) admits a non zero solution
\varphi
. A direct calculation shows that the eigenpairs of (
I.19
) are given by

\lambda_{n}=n\pi,\qquad\qquad\varphi_{n}(y)=\sqrt{2}\sin(n\pi y),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots\}.

Note that the

Here and below, the complex square root is chosen (this is a convention) such that if

Let us make a few observations concerning these modes. To set ideas, introduce

such that

.

\star
For

(ignore this case if

), we have

Since

for

, these modes do not decay at infinity. They are called propagating modes. For a fixed

, there is always a finite number of propagating modes. Moreover they do not exist when

(the situation studied in the previous paragraph). On the contrary, for all
k>\pi
, the modes

are propagating. Going back to time-domain, we observe that these modes lead to consider solutions of (

The waves

propagate to the right while the

propagate to the left. For this reason, we will say that the

are rightgoing modes while the
w_{n}^{-}
are leftgoing.

\star
For

, we have

Since

, these modes are exponentially decaying as

and exponentially growing as

. There are an infinite number of them.

Though these modes have been computed for the problem in the reference strip

, we will also use them in the analysis of Problem (

) in the perturbed domain

3.2
Ill-posedness in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

In this paragraph, our goal is to show that the existence of propagating modes for

Definition I.5
.

Let
\mathrm{X}
and
\mathrm{Y}
be two Banach spaces, and let

be a continuous linear map.
The operator

only if the following two conditions are fulfilled

and
\mathrm{range}\,T
is closed;

ii)

\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{coker}\,T)<+\infty
where
\mathrm{coker}\,T\;\displaystyle{\coloneqq\;\big(\mathrm{Y}/\mathrm{range}\,T\big)}
.

Besides, the index of a Fredholm operator
T

is defined by
\mathrm{ind}\,T=\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{ker}\,T)-\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{coker}\,T)
.

To prove that the range of

Let

,

,

be three reflexive Banach spaces, such that

is compactly embedded into

. Let

be a continuous linear map. Then the assertions below are equivalent:

i

i
i)

and
\mathrm{range}\,T
is closed in
\mathrm{Y}
;

i

,

.

Proposition I.7
.

For

, the operator

Remark I.8

We exclude the case

To proceed, we will apply some strategy which is classical in applied mathematics related to physics: we will add a bit of dissipation in the medium characterized by some parameter

3.3
Problem with dissipation

To model dissipation, let us work on the problem

\begin{array}[]{|rcll}-\Delta u_{\eta}-(k^{2}+ik\eta)u_{\eta}&=&f&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\[2.0pt]
u_{\eta}&=&0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}

(I.25)

with
\eta>0
. To get an idea of why this is a relevant way to model dissipation, let us come back to time domain. With the time harmonic convention
\mathrm{U}_{\eta}(x,y,t)=u_{\eta}(x,y)e^{-i\omega t}

I.25

\begin{array}[]{|rcll}\cfrac{\partial^{2}\mathrm{U}_{\eta}}{\partial t^{2}}+\eta\cfrac{\partial\mathrm{U}_{\eta}}{\partial t}-\cfrac{1}{c^{2}}\,\Delta\mathrm{U}_{\eta}&=&\mathrm{F}&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\[2.0pt]
\mathrm{U}_{\eta}&=&0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}

(I.26)

with some initial conditions. Assume that the forcing term
\mathrm{F}
is null. Then multiplying (
I.26

\partial_{t}\overline{\mathrm{U}_{\eta}}
and integrating in
\Omega

\cfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\,\cfrac{1}{2}\,\int_{\Omega}\bigg|\cfrac{\partial\mathrm{U}_{\eta}}{\partial t}\bigg|^{2}+\cfrac{1}{c^{2}}\,|\nabla\mathrm{U}_{\eta}|^{2}\,dxdy=-\eta\int_{\Omega}\bigg|\cfrac{\partial\mathrm{U}_{\eta}}{\partial t}\bigg|^{2}\,dxdy.

Therefore, the energy

E(t)=\cfrac{1}{2}\,\int_{\Omega}\bigg|\cfrac{\partial\mathrm{U}_{\eta}}{\partial t}\bigg|^{2}+\cfrac{1}{c^{2}}\,|\nabla\mathrm{U}_{\eta}|^{2}\,dxdy

indeed decreases, due to the term
\eta\partial_{t}\mathrm{U}_{\eta}
, when
\eta>0
.

The variational formulation associated with (
I.25
) writes

\begin{array}[]{|l}\mbox{Find }u_{\eta}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\mbox{ such that }\\[3.0pt]
a_{\eta}(u_{\eta},v)=\ell(v),\qquad\forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\end{array}

(I.27)

where the sesquilinear (resp. antilinear) forms
a_{\eta}(\cdot,\cdot)
(resp.

) are such that

a_{\eta}(w,v)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\overline{v}-(k^{2}+ik\eta)w\overline{v}\,dxdy,\qquad\qquad\ell(v)=\int_{\Omega}f\overline{v}\,dxdy.

Note that the functions are now assumed to be complex valued and the inner products introduced in (
I.4

I.5

Theorem I.9

For all
k>0
, for all
\eta>0

Proof.

For

\Re e\,a_{\eta}(v,v)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}-k^{2}|v|^{2}\,dxdy,\qquad\quad\Im m\,a_{\eta}(v,v)=-k\eta\int_{\Omega}|v|^{2}\,dxdy,

Therefore, we obtain, for

,

\Re e\,\big((1+i\gamma)a_{\eta}(v,v)\big)=\Re e\,a_{\eta}(v,v)-\gamma\,\Im m\,a_{\eta}(v,v)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}+(\gamma k\eta-k^{2})\,|v|^{2}\,dxdy.

Thus for any
\eta>0

large enough, the form

is coercive in

3.4
Problem without dissipation

Taking the limit
\eta
tends to zero in (
I.32
), we are led to consider the problem

\begin{array}[]{|rcll}\vrule\lx@intercol\mbox{Find }u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\mbox{ such that }\hfil\lx@intercol\\[2.0pt]

\cfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}&=&\Lambda_{\pm}(u)&\mbox{ on }\Sigma_{\pm L}\end{array}

(I.33)

where
\Lambda_{\pm}
are the operators such that

\begin{array}[]{lrcl}\Lambda_{\pm}:&\mathrm{H}^{1/2}_{00}(\Sigma_{\pm L})&\to&\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})\\[6.0pt]
&\varphi&\mapsto&\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}i\beta_{n}\,(\varphi,\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})}\,\varphi_{n}(y).\end{array}

(I.34)

The study of Problem (
I.33
) leads to consider the following variational problem

\begin{array}[]{|l}\mbox{Find }u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\mbox{ such that }\\[3.0pt]
a^{\mathrm{out}}(u,v)=\ell(v),\qquad\forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma),\end{array}

(I.35)

with

a^{\mathrm{out}}(u,v)=\int_{\Omega_{L}}\hskip-5.69046pt\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{v}-k^{2}u\overline{v}\,dxdy-\langle\Lambda_{+}(u),v\rangle_{\Sigma_{L}}-\langle\Lambda_{-}(u),v\rangle_{\Sigma_{-L}},\quad\ell(v)=\int_{\Omega_{L}}\hskip-5.69046ptf\overline{v}\,dxdy.

(I.36)

It is important to understand that the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators
\Lambda_{\pm}
in (
I.33
) on
\Sigma_{\pm L}
have a double action. First, they allow to look for a solution which decompose on propagating modes which are not in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. However we cannot allow for all propagating modes in the functional space because otherwise by combining them, we could create non zero functions satisfying the homogeneous problem,
i.e
we would obtain for all frequencies a non zero kernel. By working with the
\Lambda_{\pm}
, we also impose radiation conditions and select outgoing behaviors (this explains the choice of the index
out
for “outgoing”). To sum up, for
\pm x>L
the solution, which is the physical one, is searched as a superposition of outgoing propagating modes and evanescent modes.

In (
I.36
)
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Sigma_{\pm L}}
stands for the antilinear duality pairing between
\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})
and
\mathrm{H}^{1/2}_{00}(\Sigma_{\pm L})
. By exploiting that the operators
\Lambda_{\pm}:\mathrm{H}^{1/2}_{00}(\Sigma_{\pm L})\to\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})
are continuous and that the trace mappings from
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L})
to
\mathrm{H}^{1/2}_{00}(\Sigma_{\pm L})
are also continuous, we deduce that the sesquilinear form
a^{\mathrm{out}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L})
. Therefore, with the Riesz representation theorem, we can introduce the linear operator
A^{\mathrm{out}}(k):\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
such that

(A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)u,v)_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}=a^{\mathrm{out}}(u,v),\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma).

(I.37)

One has the following statement.

Theorem I.11
.

For
k\in(\pi;+\infty)\setminus\{\mathbb{N}\pi\}
, the operator
A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)
decomposes as

A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)=B^{\mathrm{out}}+K

where
B^{\mathrm{out}}:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
is an isomorphism and
K:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
is compact (
B^{\mathrm{out}}
and
K
are allowed to depend on
k
).

Proof.

Define the continuous operator
B^{\mathrm{out}}:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
such that

(B^{\mathrm{out}}u,v)_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}=b^{\mathrm{out}}(u,v),\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma),

with

b^{\mathrm{out}}(u,v)=\int_{\Omega_{L}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{v}+u\overline{v}\,dxdy-\langle\Lambda_{+}(u),v\rangle_{\Sigma_{L}}-\langle\Lambda_{-}(u),v\rangle_{\Sigma_{-L}}.

For
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
, we have

\Re e\,b^{\mathrm{out}}(u,u)=\|u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}-\Re e\,\langle\Lambda_{+}(u),u\rangle_{\Sigma_{L}}-\Re e\,\langle\Lambda_{-}(u),u\rangle_{\Sigma_{-L}}.

(I.38)

But (
I.34
) provides

\langle\Lambda_{\pm}(u),u\rangle_{\Sigma_{\pm L}}=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}i\beta_{n}\,|(u,\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})}|^{2}.

Introduce
N\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
such that
k\in(N\pi;(N+1)\pi)
. The
i\beta_{n}
for
n=1,\dots,N
are purely imaginary. On the other hand, the
i\beta_{n}
for
n>N
are real negative. Thus we have

\Re e\,\langle\Lambda_{\pm}(u),u\rangle_{\Sigma_{\pm L}}=\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}i\beta_{n}\,|(u,\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})}|^{2}=\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}-\sqrt{n^{2}\pi^{2}-k^{2}}\,|(u,\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})}|^{2}<0.

With (
I.38
), this gives

\Re e\,b^{\mathrm{out}}(u,u)\geq\|u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}.

From the complex version of the Lax-Milgram theorem, we deduce that
B^{\mathrm{out}}:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
is an isomorphism.

Now set
K=A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)-B^{\mathrm{out}}
. We have

(Ku,v)_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}=-(1+k^{2})\int_{\Omega_{L}}u\overline{v}\,dxdy,\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma).

Since
\Omega_{L}
is bounded, the embedding of
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{L})
is compact and we can show that
K:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
is compact by working as for the operator
K
appearing in the proof of Theorem
I.1
.
∎

This shows that
A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)
satisfies the Fredholm alternative. Either
A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)
is injective and in this case it is an isomorphism of
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
. Or
A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)
has a kernel of finite dimension
\mathrm{span}(u_{1},\dots,u_{P})
and in that case the equation

A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)u=F\quad\mbox{ in }\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)

has a solution (defined up to
\mathrm{span}(u_{1},\dots,u_{P})
) if and only if
F
satisfies the compatibility conditions

(F,u_{p})_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}=0,\qquad p=1,\dots,P.

(I.39)

For a given geometry, one can show that the set of
k\in(\pi;+\infty)\setminus\{\mathbb{N}\pi\}
such that
A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)
is not injective is discrete and accumulates only at
+\infty
.

If
u
solves (
I.33
), then by defining
\hat{u}
such that

\hat{u}(x,y)=\begin{array}[]{|ll}u(x,y)&\mbox{ in }\Omega_{L}\\[3.0pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(u,\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})}\,e^{\pm i\beta_{n}(x\mp L)}\,\varphi_{n}(y)&\mbox{ for }\pm x>L,\end{array}

(I.40)

we obtain a solution of (
I.3
). In general, this
\hat{u}
does not belong to
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
because it involves propagating modes.

In the (rare) cases where
A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)
is not injective, we show now that the element
u
of its kernel do not decompose on the propagating modes. As a consequence, the corresponding extensions
\hat{u}
are exponentially decaying as
x\to\pm\infty
and so are localized in a neighborhood of the perturbation in the geometry (see an example in Figure
I.5
). For this reason one usually call them trapped modes.

Proposition I.12
.

Pick
k\in(\pi;+\infty)\setminus\{\mathbb{N}\pi\}
and consider some
u
in
\mathrm{ker}\,A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)
. Then its corresponding extension
\hat{u}
defined via (
I.40
) decays as
O(e^{-\sqrt{(N+1)^{2}\pi^{2}-k^{2}}|x|})
as
x\to\pm\infty
and so belongs to
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Introduce again
N\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
such that
k\in(N\pi;(N+1)\pi)
. If
u
is an element of
\mathrm{ker}\,A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)
, we have
a^{\mathrm{out}}(u,u)=0
and so

0=\Im m\,a^{\mathrm{out}}(u,u)=-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}\,(|(u,\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{+L})}|^{2}+|(u,\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{-L})}|^{2}).

Since the
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{N}
are all positive, this proves that the corresponding
\hat{u}
in (
I.40
) decomposes only on the evanescent modes.
∎

3.5
Limiting absorption principle

In this paragraph, we prove that the dissipative solution converges to the solution of Problem (
I.33
) without dissipation as
\eta
tends to zero.

Theorem I.13
.

Fix
k\in(\pi;+\infty)\setminus\{\mathbb{N}\pi\}
and assume that the operator
A^{\mathrm{out}}(k)
defined in (
I.37
) is injective. Then there is
\eta_{0}
such that we have

\|u_{\eta}-u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}\leq C\eta\,\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{L})},\qquad\forall\eta\in(0;\eta_{0}],

with a constant
C>0
which is independent of
\eta
. Here
u
,
u_{\eta}
are the functions solving respectively (
I.33
), (
I.32
).

Proof.

Let
F
be the element of
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
such that

(F,v)_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}=\ell(v),\qquad\forall v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma).

Denote also by
A^{\eta}:\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
the operator such that

(A^{\eta}w,v)_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}=a_{\eta}(w,v),\qquad\forall w,v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma).

We have, for all
\eta>0
,

A^{\eta}u_{\eta}=F=A^{\mathrm{out}}u.

This gives
A^{\mathrm{out}}(u-u_{\eta})=(A^{\eta}-A^{\mathrm{out}})u_{\eta}
. Since
A^{\mathrm{out}}
is assumed to be injective, Theorem
I.11
together with the Fredholm alternative guarantee that
A^{\mathrm{out}}
is an isomorphism of
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
. Thus we can write

u-u_{\eta}=(A^{\mathrm{out}})^{-1}(A^{\eta}-A^{\mathrm{out}})u_{\eta}.

(I.41)

Now from the definition of
A^{\mathrm{out}}
,
A^{\eta}
, one establishes, for
\eta
small,

\|A^{\eta}-A^{\mathrm{out}}\|\leq C\eta,

(I.42)

where
C>0
is a constant which may change from one line to another below, but remains independent of
\eta
. Gathering (
I.41
) and (
I.42
), we find

\|u-u_{\eta}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}\leq C\eta\,\|u_{\eta}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}.

(I.43)

By using the inequality
\|u_{\eta}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}\leq\|u-u_{\eta}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}+\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}
in (
I.43
), we obtain, for
\eta
small enough,

\|u-u_{\eta}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}\leq C\eta\,\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}\leq C\eta\,\|(A^{\mathrm{out}})^{-1}f\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega_{L})}\leq C\eta\,\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{L})}.

∎

3.6
Scattering problem

In (
I.33
), we considered a problem with a source term
f
. In the following, we will be mostly interested in scattering problems for incident waves. To keep things as simple as possible, we assume all through this paragraph that
k\in(\pi;2\pi)
so that only the modes
w^{\pm}_{1}
can propagate. To make short, we denote them by
w_{\pm}
so that

w_{\pm}(x,y)=e^{\pm i\beta_{1}x}\varphi_{1}(y)=e^{\pm i\sqrt{k^{2}-\pi^{2}}x}\varphi_{1}(y).

(I.44)

The scattering of the rightgoing wave
w_{+}
coming from the left branch of the waveguide leads us to consider the problem

\begin{array}[]{|rcll}\vrule\lx@intercol\mbox{Find }u_{+}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0,\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(\Omega)\mbox{ such that }u_{+}-w_{+}\mbox{ is outgoing and }\hfil\lx@intercol\\[3.0pt]
\Delta u_{+}+k^{2}u_{+}&=&0&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\[2.0pt]
u_{+}&=&0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}

(I.45)

Here
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0,\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(\Omega)
denotes the set of measurable functions
v
such that
\zeta v
belongs to
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
for all
\zeta\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Moreover, the sentence
u_{+}-w_{+}
is outgoing means that we impose the decomposition

u_{+}-w_{+}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\alpha^{+}_{n}e^{i\beta_{n}x}\,\varphi_{n}(y)&\mbox{ for }x>L\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\alpha^{-}_{n}e^{-i\beta_{n}x}\,\varphi_{n}(y)&\mbox{ for }x<-L,\end{array}\right.

for some
\alpha^{\pm}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
. In this context,
u_{+}
is usually called the total field associated with the incident field
w_{+}
while the quantity
u^{s}_{+}\coloneqq u_{+}-w_{+}
is the scattered field.

Proposition I.14
.

For all
k\in(\pi;2\pi)
, (
I.45
) admits a solution. It is unique if trapped modes do not exist.

Proof.

Introduce some cut-off functions
\zeta\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\zeta(x)=\begin{array}[]{|ll}1&\mbox{ for }x\leq-L\\[2.0pt]
0&\mbox{ for }x\geq-d,\end{array}

If
u_{+}
satisfies (
I.45
), then
u^{s}_{+}=u_{+}-\zeta w_{+}
solves Problem (
I.33
) with

f=\Delta(\zeta w_{+})+k^{2}(\zeta w_{+})\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{L}).

(I.46)

Conversely, if
u^{s}_{+}
satisfies (
I.33
) with the above
f
, then
u_{+}\coloneqq u^{s}_{+}+\zeta w_{+}
is a solution of (
I.45
). Therefore it is sufficient to focus our attention on the study of (
I.33
) with
f
defined in (
I.46
).

If trapped modes do not exist, Theorem
I.11
together with the Fredholm alternative ensure that (
I.33
) admits a unique solution.

Now if trapped modes
\mathrm{span}(u_{1},\dots,u_{P})
,
P\geq 1
, exist, let us prove that for the particular
f
considered in (
I.46
) related to the incident mode, Problem (
I.33
) still have a solution. To proceed, we have to show that
f
satisfies the compatibility conditions appearing in (
I.39
). For
p=1,\dots,P
, integrating twice by parts in
\Omega_{L}
and using that
\Delta u_{p}+k^{2}u_{p}=0
in
\Omega
, we obtain

(f,u_{p})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{L})}=\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega_{L}}\left(\Delta(\zeta w_{+})+k^{2}(\zeta w_{+})\right)u_{p}\,dxdy=-\int_{\Sigma_{-L}}\cfrac{\partial w_{+}}{\partial\nu}\,u_{p}-w_{+}\cfrac{\partial u_{p}}{\partial\nu}\,dy.

Above we used the fact that
\zeta=1
on
\Sigma_{-L}
. Now we observe that
w_{+}
is propagating while Proposition
I.12
ensures that
u_{p}
decomposes only on the evanescent modes. From the orthogonality of the family
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)
, we conclude that
(f,u_{p})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{L})}=0
.
∎

Set
R_{+}\coloneqq\alpha_{n}^{-}
,
T_{+}\coloneqq 1+\alpha_{n}^{+}
so that for
u_{+}
we have the representation

u_{+}=\begin{array}[]{|ll}w_{+}+R_{+}w_{-}+\tilde{u}_{+}&\mbox{ for }x<-L\\[3.0pt]
\phantom{w_{+}mi}T_{+}w_{+}+\tilde{u}_{+}&\mbox{ for }x>L,\end{array}

(I.47)

with
\tilde{u}_{+}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. The quantities
R_{+}
,
T_{+}
are usually called the reflection and transmission coefficients. Let us emphasize that they are uniquely defined, even if trapped modes exist a certain
k
. Indeed since trapped modes decay as
O(e^{-\sqrt{4\pi^{2}-k^{2}}|x|})
as
x\to\pm\infty
according to Proposition
I.12
, the scattering coefficients
R_{+}
,
T_{+}
are insensitive to their existence.

In the same way, one shows that Problem (
I.3
) admits a solution
u_{-}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0,\mbox{\scriptsize loc}}(\Omega)
with the decomposition

u_{-}=\begin{array}[]{|ll}\phantom{w_{+}mi}T_{-}w_{-}+\tilde{u}_{-}&\mbox{ for }x<-L\\[3.0pt]
w_{-}+R_{-}w_{+}+\tilde{u}_{-}&\mbox{ for }x>L,\end{array}

(I.48)

where
R_{-}
,
T_{-}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\tilde{u}_{-}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. It corresponds to the scattering of the leftgoing wave
w_{-}
coming from the right branch of the waveguide.

With the coefficients
R_{\pm}
,
T_{\pm}
appearing in the decompositions (
I.47
), (
I.48
), we form the scattering matrix

\mathbb{S}\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}R_{+}&T_{+}\\[2.0pt]
T_{-}&R_{-}\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}.

Due to physics, the matrix
\mathbb{S}
has a very rigid structure. More precisely, we have the following statement:

Proposition I.15
.

The scattering matrix
\mathbb{S}
is symmetric (
T_{+}=T_{-}
) and unitary (
\mathbb{S}\,\overline{\mathbb{S}}^{\top}=\mathrm{Id}_{2\times 2}
).

Proof.

For
l>0
, set
\Omega_{l}\coloneqq\{(x,y)\in\Omega\,|\,|x|<l\}
,
\Sigma_{\pm l}\coloneqq\{\pm l\}\times I
. We have

0=\int_{\Omega_{l}}(\Delta u_{+}+k^{2}u_{+})u_{-}-u_{+}(\Delta u_{-}+k^{2}u_{-})\,dxdy=\int_{\Sigma_{l}\cup\Sigma_{-l}}\cfrac{\partial u_{+}}{\partial\nu}\,u_{-}-u_{+}\cfrac{\partial u_{-}}{\partial\nu}\,dy.

Using decompositions (
I.47
), (
I.48
) in the above identity and taking the limit
l\to+\infty
, we obtain
0=2i\beta_{1}(T_{+}-T_{-})
, which gives
T_{+}=T_{-}
. Then working similarly from the identities

0=\int_{\Omega_{l}}(\Delta u_{\pm}+k^{2}u_{\pm})\overline{u_{\pm}}-u_{\pm}(\Delta\overline{u_{\pm}}+k^{2}\overline{u_{\pm}})\,dxdy=\int_{\Sigma_{l}\cup\Sigma_{-l}}\cfrac{\partial u_{\pm}}{\partial\nu}\,\overline{u_{\pm}}-u_{\pm}\cfrac{\partial\overline{u_{\pm}}}{\partial\nu}\,dy,

one establishes the relations of conservation of energy

|R_{\pm}|^{2}+|T_{\pm}|^{2}=1.

(I.49)

Finally, by using that

0=\int_{\Omega_{l}}(\Delta u_{\pm}+k^{2}u_{\pm})\overline{u_{\mp}}-u_{\pm}(\Delta\overline{u_{\mp}}+k^{2}\overline{u_{\mp}})\,dxdy=\int_{\Sigma_{l}\cup\Sigma_{-l}}\cfrac{\partial u_{\pm}}{\partial\nu}\,\overline{u_{\mp}}-u_{\pm}\cfrac{\partial\overline{u_{\mp}}}{\partial\nu}\,dy,

one gets

R_{\pm}\overline{T_{\mp}}+T_{\pm}\overline{R_{\mp}}=0.

(I.50)

Identities (
I.49
) and (
I.50
) ensure that
\mathbb{S}\,\overline{\mathbb{S}}^{\top}=\mathrm{Id}_{2\times 2}
.

∎

In the following, we simply set
T\coloneqq T_{+}=T_{-}
so that we have

\mathbb{S}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}R_{+}&T\\[2.0pt]
T&R_{-}\end{array}\right).

In our study, we will need some formulas expressing the values of the scattering coefficients
R_{\pm}
,
T
with respect to
u_{\pm}
.

Proposition I.16
.

For
k\in(\pi;2\pi)
, the coefficients
R_{\pm}
,
T
appearing in (
I.47
), (
I.48
) in the decompositions of
u_{+}
,
u_{-}
satisfy

R_{\pm}=\cfrac{1}{2i\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{L}\cup\Sigma_{-L}}\cfrac{\partial u_{\pm}}{\partial\nu}\,\overline{w_{\mp}}-u_{\pm}\cfrac{\partial\overline{w_{\mp}}}{\partial\nu}\,dy\quad\mbox{ and }\quad T-1=\cfrac{1}{2i\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{L}\cup\Sigma_{-L}}\cfrac{\partial u_{\pm}}{\partial\nu}\,\overline{w_{\pm}}-u_{\pm}\cfrac{\partial\overline{w_{\pm}}}{\partial\nu}\,dy.

Proof.

From (
I.47
), we find on
\Sigma_{-L}

\cfrac{\partial u_{+}}{\partial\nu}=-\cfrac{\partial u_{+}}{\partial x}=-i\beta_{1}(w_{+}-R_{+}\,w_{-})-\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{x},\qquad\quad\cfrac{\partial\overline{w_{-}}}{\partial\nu}=-\cfrac{\partial w_{+}}{\partial x}=-i\beta_{1}w_{+},

and on
\Sigma_{L}

\cfrac{\partial u_{+}}{\partial\nu}=\cfrac{\partial u_{+}}{\partial x}=i\beta_{1}T\,w_{+}+\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{x},\qquad\quad\cfrac{\partial\overline{w_{-}}}{\partial\nu}=\cfrac{\partial w_{+}}{\partial x}=i\beta_{1}w_{+}.

By exploiting that the exponentially decaying modes appearing in the decomposition of
\tilde{u}
are orthogonal to
w_{\pm}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})
, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle\cfrac{1}{2i\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{L}\cup\Sigma_{-L}}\cfrac{\partial u_{+}}{\partial\nu}\,\overline{w_{-}}-u_{+}\cfrac{\partial\overline{w_{-}}}{\partial\nu}\,dy\\[10.0pt]
=&\displaystyle\cfrac{1}{2}\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{-L}}-(w_{+}-R_{+}\,w_{-})w_{+}+(w_{+}+R_{+}\,w_{-})w_{+}\,dy+\cfrac{1}{2}\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{L}}T\,(w_{+}^{2})-T\,(w_{+}^{2})\,dy=R_{+}.\end{array}

Formulas for
R_{-}
and
T
are obtained in a similar manner.
∎

3.7
Numerical approximation

Below we will have to compute numerical approximations of the quantities
u_{\pm}
, in particular to obtain
\mathbb{S}
. There are “three infinities” which are unpleasant to solve (
I.45
) with a computer. First, the domain
\Omega
is unbounded. To face this difficulty, we will exploit the above analysis and consider a formulation set in
\Omega_{L}
involving the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators similar to (
I.35
). Second,
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)
is a space of infinite dimension.
We will work with a finite element method and solve a variational formulation in a space of finite dimension. Third, the radiation condition involves an infinite number of terms. Quite naturally, we will truncate the series appearing in the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators at rank
M>N
where
N
is the number of propagating modes.

To set ideas, assume that
k\in(\pi;2\pi)
and consider the approximation of
u_{+}
. Since
u_{+}-w_{+}
is outgoing, we have the conditions

\cfrac{\partial(u_{+}-w_{+})}{\partial\nu}=\Lambda_{\pm}(u_{+}-w_{+})\quad\mbox{ on }\Sigma_{\pm L}.

This gives

\cfrac{\partial u_{+}}{\partial\nu}=\Lambda_{\pm}(u_{+})+\cfrac{\partial w_{+}}{\partial\nu}-\Lambda_{\pm}(w_{+})\quad\mbox{ on }\Sigma_{\pm L}.

Since
w_{+}
is rightgoing, using the definitions (
I.34
) of
\Lambda_{\pm}
, we obtain

\partial_{\nu}w_{+}-\Lambda_{+}(w_{+})=0\quad\mbox{ on }\Sigma_{L},\qquad\qquad\partial_{\nu}w_{+}-\Lambda_{-}(w_{+})=-2i\beta_{1}w_{+}\quad\mbox{ on }\Sigma_{-L}.

Thus
u_{+}
solves the problem

\begin{array}[]{|l}\mbox{Find }u_{+}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\mbox{ such that for all }v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma),\\[3.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{L}}\hskip-5.69046pt\nabla u_{+}\cdot\nabla\overline{v}-k^{2}u\overline{v}\,dxdy-\langle\Lambda_{+}(u_{+}),v\rangle_{\Sigma_{L}}-\langle\Lambda_{-}(u_{+}),v\rangle_{\Sigma_{-L}}=-2i\beta_{1}\int_{\Sigma_{-L}}\hskip-5.69046ptw_{+}\overline{v}\,dy.\end{array}

(I.51)

Note that (
I.34
) yields

\langle\Lambda_{\pm}(u),v\rangle_{\Sigma_{\pm L}}=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}i\beta_{n}\,(u,\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})}\,\overline{(v,\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{\pm L})}}.

Now introduce
(\mathcal{T}_{h})_{h}
a shape regular family of triangulations of
\overline{\Omega_{L}}
(in other words, we mesh the domain
\overline{\Omega_{L}}
with triangles). Define the family of Lagrange finite element spaces

\mathrm{V}_{h}\coloneqq\left\{v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{L};\Gamma)\mbox{ such that }v|_{\tau}\in\mathbb{P}_{q}(\tau)\mbox{ for all }\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},

(I.52)

where
\mathbb{P}_{q}(\tau)
is the space of polynomials of degree at most
q
on the triangle
\tau
. Finally, the problem we solve writes

\begin{array}[]{|l}\mbox{Find }u^{h}_{+}\in\mathrm{V}_{h}\mbox{ such that for all }v^{h}\in\mathrm{V}_{h},\\[3.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{L}}\hskip-5.69046pt\nabla u^{h}_{+}\cdot\nabla\overline{v^{h}}-k^{2}u^{h}_{+}\overline{v^{h}}\,dxdy-\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{M}i\beta_{n}\,(u^{h}_{+},\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{L})}\,\overline{(v^{h},\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{L})}}\\[10.0pt]
\hskip 140.84105pt-\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{M}i\beta_{n}\,(u^{h}_{+},\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{-L})}\,\overline{(v^{h},\varphi_{n})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Sigma_{-L})}}=-2i\beta_{1}\int_{\Sigma_{-L}}\hskip-8.5359ptw_{+}\overline{v^{h}}\,dy.\end{array}\hskip-22.76228pt

(I.53)

One can show that for
h
small enough,
L
large enough (actually one has exponential convergence with respect to
L
),
u^{h}_{+}
yields a good approximation of
u_{+}
. Then replacing
u_{+}
by
u^{h}_{+}
in the exact formulas

R_{+}=\int_{\Sigma_{-L}}(u_{+}-w_{+})\,w_{+}\,dy,\qquad T=\int_{\Sigma_{L}}u_{+}w_{-}\,dy,

we obtain good approximations of the scattering coefficients. Let us emphasize that numerically, it is more interesting to use expressions (

4
Neumann problem

In acoustics, we are led to study the same Helmholtz equation as in (

) but with homogeneous Neumann BCs which model sound hard walls. This yields the problem

\begin{array}[]{|rcll}\Delta u+k^{2}u&=&0&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\[2.0pt]

(I.55)

where

stands for the pressure of the fluid in
\Omega

. Reproducing what has been done in §

\sqrt{2}\cos(n\pi y)&\mbox{ for }n>0.\end{array}

3.4

5
Invisibility questions

In the following, our general goal will be to find situations, by playing with the geometry, the wavenumber
k
, … where we have some sort of invisibility, that is scattering features as if there were no obstacle in the waveguide.

The weakest invisibility that one can look for is
R_{\pm}=0
. In the sequel, we shall say that one has zero reflection or that the defect is non reflecting. In such a situation, an observer generating an incident plane wave, located a bit far from the defect in the geometry and measuring the resulting backscattering field will only measure the evanescent component. Due to noise, one will get something similar to the field in the reference strip and therefore will be unable to detect the presence of the obstacle. Note that due to conservation of energy ((
I.49
) or (
I.61
)), the fact that
R_{\pm}=0
implies
|T|=1
and so
T=e^{i\theta}
for a certain
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. As a consequence in general there is a phase shift in the transmitted field which can reveal the presence of the defect if one probes the field in that part of the guide.

A more demanding definition of invisibility is to have
T=1
. In that situation, we shall say that the defect is perfectly invisible or that we have perfect transmission without phase shift.

Figure I.6:
Cloaking by transformation optics.

The problem of cloaking an object has a large number of applications and has been the subject of intense studies over the last decade in the theory of waves propagation. Different notions of invisibility exist in literature. Our approach for example is different from the cloaking via transformation optics pictured in Figure
I.6
(see
[
35
,
29
]
). Let us describe this latter device. Imagine that one wish to hide a given obstacle. One technique consists in surrounding it by a well chosen penetrable material, localized in the annulus around the green region on the picture, so that an incoming wave leaves the whole device as if there were nothing. Said differently, on the picture, an observer probing the field outside of the larger disk marked by the white thin circle obtains the same measurements as in free space and so cannot detect the presence of the obstacle. Mathematically, this idea is quite simple to implement, it boils down to a change of variable. However this change of variable is singular and for this reason the physical parameters of the
ad hoc
material in the cloaking device should take infinite values. For this reason, for the moment designing such structures, even working with metamaterials, is still unreachable, in particular due to the presence of important losses.

What we propose is less ambitious because we only wish to control the scattering coefficients and not to act on the evanescent component of the field. In short, what we aim for is only cloaking at infinity. For this reason, it is more easily doable. Actually, we will not even need to play with penetrable materials. We will see that by working with a homogeneous material and acting only on the geometry is sufficient. On the other hand, though our setting is less ambitious, it is still relevant in numerous applications. Indeed, the evanescent part of the field that we neglect is exponentially decaying at infinity and therefore is really difficult to distinguish from noise a few wavelengths far from the obstacle. Finally, though we simply wish to control a finite number of complex coefficients, this problem is not trivial because the link between the variation of the parameters (geometry,
k
, …) and the variation of the scattering coefficients is non linear and not explicit. Additionally, let us emphasize that due to the fact that there is no coercivity in the problem, optimization methods fail due to the presence of local minima.

In the next three chapters, we present several ideas to reach invisibility.

In this chapter, we work with techniques of perturbations to construct invisible obstacles. The idea, proposed in the article

1
General scheme

Let us describe the method for the simplest problem, namely obtaining

. At this stage, the approach is the same whether one considers Problem (
I.45
) with Dirichlet BCs or Problem (
I.55
) with Neumann BCs (pick
k\in(\pi;2\pi)
in the first case,
k\in(0;\pi)
in the second situation).
Let us focus our attention on
R_{+}
and simply write
R
instead of
R_{+}
. Note that from conservation of energy (see relations (
I.49
)),
R+=0
implies
R_{-}=0
. Consider some real valued profile function
h\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
and let
\Omega(h)
be the waveguide whose upper boundary coincides with the graph of the function
1+h
(see Figure
II.1
right). Note that we make some assumption of smoothness here for simplicity but
\mathscr{C}^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
would be enough. Let
R(h)
be the reflection coefficient of the scattering solution
u_{+}
in the geometry
\Omega(h)
. Importantly, we have
R(0)=0
because when
h\equiv 0
,
\Omega(h)
is simply the reference strip
\mathcal{S}
. With this notation, the problem we consider writes

\begin{array}[]{|l}\mbox{Find }h\not\equiv 0\mbox{ such that }\\[2.0pt]
R(h)=0.\end{array}

Let us look for non reflecting geometries which are small perturbations of
\mathcal{S}
. To proceed, let us look for
h=\varepsilon\mu
with
\varepsilon>0
small and
\mu\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
to be determined. Since
\varepsilon
is small, we can write an asymptotic expansion of
R
with respect to
\varepsilon
. We obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}R(\varepsilon\mu)&=&R(0)+\varepsilon dR(0)(\mu)+\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{R}(\varepsilon\mu)\\[3.0pt]
&=&\varepsilon dR(0)(\mu)+\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{R}(\varepsilon\mu)\end{array}

(II.1)

where
dR(0)(\mu)
stands for the differential of
R
at zero in the direction
\mu
and
\tilde{R}(\varepsilon\mu)
is an abstract remainder. Since
dR(0):\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}
is a linear map from a space of infinite dimension to a space of dimension two (remember that we work with real valued functions
h
), we have
\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{ker}\,dR(0)=+\infty
. Therefore, we can pick
\mu_{0}\not\equiv 0
such that

dR(0)(\mu_{0})=0.

(II.2)

By choosing
h=\varepsilon\mu=\varepsilon\mu_{0}
, we obtain a perturbation of order
\varepsilon
which produces a reflection in
O(\varepsilon^{2})
. This is interesting because this is almost zero reflection but not completely satisfactory yet. To compensate for the remainder, we need to work a bit more. Below we will explain how to compute
dR(0)
and prove that
dR(0):\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}
is onto. This allows us to introduce
\mu_{1}\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mu_{2}\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
such that

dR(0)(\mu_{1})=1\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad dR(0)(\mu_{2})=i.

(II.3)

Finally, we look for
\mu
of the form

\mu=\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}\mu_{2}

where
\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
are parameters to tune. Inserting this
\mu
in the expansion (
II.1
), to get
R(\varepsilon\mu)=0
, we see that we must have

0=\varepsilon dR(0)(\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}\mu_{2})+\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{R}(\varepsilon(\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}\mu_{2})).

By exploiting (
II.3
), this yields

0=\tau_{1}+i\tau+\varepsilon\tilde{R}(\varepsilon(\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}\mu_{2})).

In other words, we find that the vector
\vec{\tau}\coloneqq(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
must satisfy the fixed point equation

\vec{\tau}=G^{\varepsilon}(\vec{\tau})\quad\mbox{ with }\quad G^{\varepsilon}(\vec{\tau})=-\varepsilon(\Re e\,\tilde{R}(\varepsilon(\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}\mu_{2})),\Im m\,\tilde{R}(\varepsilon(\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}\mu_{2})))^{\top}.

(II.4)

Now by proving uniform (with respect to
\vec{\tau}
) error estimates in the asymptotic expansions as
\varepsilon
tends to zero, one can show that for any
r>0
, there is
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that the map
\vec{\tau}\mapsto G^{\varepsilon}(\vec{\tau})
is a contraction from
\overline{B(O,r)}
to
\overline{B(O,r)}
for all
\varepsilon\in(0;\varepsilon_{0}]
. Here

denotes the open ball of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
centered at

of radius

. Therefore the Banach fixed point theorem guarantees that (

) admits a unique solution

. Then for

, we have
R(h^{\mathrm{sol}})=0
. This proves the existence of non reflecting geometries.

Let us comment a bit this method.

1) First, it is important to prove that the constructed
h^{\,\mathrm{sol}}
is not trivial. To proceed, let us work by contradiction and assume that
h^{\,\mathrm{sol}}\equiv 0
. Then we get

0=dR(0)(h^{\,\mathrm{sol}})=dR(0)(\varepsilon(\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}^{\mathrm{sol}}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}^{\mathrm{sol}}\mu_{2}))=\varepsilon(\tau_{1}^{\mathrm{sol}}+i\tau_{2}^{\mathrm{sol}}),

and so
\tau_{1}^{\mathrm{sol}}=\tau_{2}^{\mathrm{sol}}=0
. This implies
h^{\,\mathrm{sol}}=\varepsilon\mu_{0}\equiv 0
which is not true due to our choice for
\mu_{0}
.

2) Observe that this technique guarantees the existence of an infinite number of non reflecting perturbations. Indeed, first,
h^{\,\mathrm{sol}}
depends on
\varepsilon\in(0;\varepsilon_{0}]
(remark that it is not only a scaling because there are non linear terms involved). Additionally, for
\mu_{0}
we have some freedom because
\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{ker}\,dR(0)=+\infty
.

3) We can establish that there is a constant
C>0
independent of
\varepsilon
such that we have
|G^{\varepsilon}(\vec{\tau})|\leq C\varepsilon
in
\overline{B(O,r)}
. As a consequence, as
\varepsilon
tends to zero, we have
|\vec{\tau}^{\,\mathrm{sol}}|=O(\varepsilon)
and the shape of the perturbation
\varepsilon\mu=\varepsilon(\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}\mu_{2})
is mainly characterized by
\mu_{0}
.

4) Let us clarify the connection with the implicit function theorem. Introduce the functional

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathscr{F}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}&\to&\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
\qquad(\tau_{0},\vec{\tau})&\mapsto&(\Re e\,R(\tau_{0}\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}\mu_{2}),\Im m\,R(\tau_{0}\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}\mu_{2}))\end{array}

which is of class

in a neighborhood of
0_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
. From what will be shown below, we will be able to deduce that

is well-defined and bijective. On the other hand, we remark that

,

), we obtain the formula

\begin{array}[]{rcl}dR(0)(\mu)&=&\displaystyle\cfrac{i\pi^{2}}{\beta_{1}}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu(x)e^{2i\beta_{1}x}\,dx.\end{array}

Since the real part of

is even while its imaginary part is odd, we see that it is easy to find functions

,
\mu_{2}
satisfying the relations (

), which ensures that

is onto.

The formal calculus above can be rigorously justified by proving error estimates. To proceed, one method consists in rectifying the boundary of
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
using “almost identical” diffeomorphisms to transform the perturbed domain into the reference strip

(see
e.g.

[

\|u^{\varepsilon}-(u_{0}+\varepsilon u_{1})\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathcal{S}_{L}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{V}})}\leq C\varepsilon^{2},

where

is a constant independent of
\varepsilon
and
\mathcal{V}
is a neighborhood of
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\times\{1\}
.
∎

3
Numerical implementation

The theoretical approach presented above leads very naturally to an algorithm to construct numerically non reflecting perturbations. Let us describe the strategy.

The main idea consists in solving the fixed point equation

\vec{\tau}=G^{\varepsilon}(\vec{\tau})

(see (

,
\mu_{1}
,

. Denote
\mu^{p}\coloneqq\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}^{p}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}^{p}\mu_{2}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G^{\varepsilon}(\vec{\tau}^{\,p})&=&-\varepsilon(\Re e\,\tilde{R}(\varepsilon\mu^{p}),\Im m\,\tilde{R}(\varepsilon\mu^{p}))^{\top}\\[4.0pt]
&=&\vec{\tau}^{\,p}-\varepsilon^{-1}(\Re e\,R(\varepsilon\mu^{p}),\Im m\,R(\varepsilon\mu^{p})).\end{array}

Therefore, for

we obtain the recursive equation

\vec{\tau}^{\,p+1}=\vec{\tau}^{\,p}-\varepsilon^{-1}(\Re e\,R(\varepsilon\mu^{p}),\Im m\,R(\varepsilon\mu^{p})).

4
Perfect transmission for the Dirichlet problem

Can we hope for more and obtain
T=1
with the above approach? To proceed, a natural idea is to work with the quantity
T-1
. More precisely, in the reference strip we have
T-1=0
. Is it possible to perturb the geometry while keeping
T-1=0
? Let us compute the differential of
T
with respect to the geometry.

Proposition II.2
.

For
\mu\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}dT(0)(\mu)&=&\displaystyle\cfrac{i\pi^{2}}{\beta_{1}}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu(x)\,dx.\end{array}

As a consequence,
dT(0):\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}
is not onto.

Proof.

To show this result, one works as for
R
in Proposition
II.1
. Let us keep the same notation.
According to Proposition
I.16
, we have

T^{\varepsilon}-1=\cfrac{1}{2i\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{L}\cup\Sigma_{-L}}\cfrac{\partial u^{\varepsilon}}{\partial\nu}\,w_{-}-u^{\varepsilon}\cfrac{\partial w_{-}}{\partial\nu}\,dy.

Inserting the expansion (
II.6
) of
u^{\varepsilon}
in the above identity and using that
u_{0}=w_{+}
, this gives

\begin{array}[]{rcl}T^{\varepsilon}-1&=&\displaystyle\cfrac{1}{2i\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{L}\cup\Sigma_{-L}}\cfrac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial\nu}\,w_{-}-u_{0}\cfrac{\partial w_{-}}{\partial\nu}\,dy+\cfrac{\varepsilon}{2i\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{L}\cup\Sigma_{-L}}\cfrac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}\,w_{-}-u_{1}\cfrac{\partial w_{-}}{\partial\nu}\,dy+\dots\\[8.0pt]
&=&0+\cfrac{\varepsilon}{2i\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{L}\cup\Sigma_{-L}}\cfrac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}\,w_{-}-u_{1}\cfrac{\partial w_{-}}{\partial\nu}\,dy+\dots,\end{array}

where the dots correspond to higher order terms. We deduce that

dT(0)(\mu)=\cfrac{1}{2i\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{L}\cup\Sigma_{-L}}\cfrac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}\,w_{-}-u_{1}\cfrac{\partial w_{-}}{\partial\nu}\,dy.

Integrating by parts in
\mathcal{S}_{L}=(-L;L)\times I
, and using the third line of (
II.9
), this gives

dT(0)(\mu)=\displaystyle\cfrac{1}{2i\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{S}}u_{1}\cfrac{\partial w_{-}}{\partial y}\,dx=\displaystyle\cfrac{i}{2\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu(x)\cfrac{\partial w_{+}}{\partial y}(x,1)\cfrac{\partial w_{-}}{\partial y}(x,1)\,dx=\cfrac{i\pi^{2}}{\beta_{1}}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu(x)\,dx.

Since the real part of
dT(0)(\mu)
is null for all
\mu\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, this shows that
dT(0):\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}
is not onto.
∎

Remark II.3
.

The fact that the real part of
dT(0)
is necessarily null could have been guessed from conservation of conservation. Indeed, otherwise by linearity of
dT(0)
, we could find some
\mu\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
such that
dT(0)(\mu)>0
. Then for
\varepsilon>0
small enough,
T(\varepsilon\mu)
would have a real part larger than one. This is impossible due to conservation of energy.

Though
dT(0):\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}
is not onto, Proposition
II.2
proves that we can control the imaginary part of
T
. Let us exploit this property.

Define the map

\begin{array}[]{rccl}\mathscr{F}:&\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})&\to&\mathbb{R}^{3}\\[4.0pt]
&h&\mapsto&(\Re e\,R(h),\Im m\,R(h),\Im m\,T(h))^{\top}.\end{array}

(II.13)

We have
\mathscr{F}(0)=0
and we wish to find some
h\not\equiv 0
such that
\mathscr{F}(h)=0
. From Propositions
II.1
and
II.2
, we know that
d\mathscr{F}(0):\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is onto. Therefore there are
\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{3}\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
such that

d\mathscr{F}(0)(\mu_{0})=0,\qquad[d\mathscr{F}(0)(\mu_{1}),d\mathscr{F}(0)(\mu_{2}),d\mathscr{F}(0)(\mu_{3})]=\mathrm{Id}_{3\times 3}.

Set
\vec{\tau}\coloneqq(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and consider the new fixed point equation

\vec{\tau}=F^{\varepsilon}(\vec{\tau})

(II.14)

with

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F^{\varepsilon}(\vec{\tau})&\coloneqq&-\varepsilon(\Re e\,\tilde{R}(\varepsilon\mu(\vec{\tau})),\Im m\,\tilde{R}(\varepsilon\mu(\vec{\tau})),\Im m\,\tilde{T}(\varepsilon\mu(\vec{\tau}))^{\top}\\[4.0pt]
\mu(\vec{\tau})&\coloneqq&\mu_{0}+\tau_{1}\mu_{1}+\tau_{2}\mu_{2}+\tau_{3}\mu_{3}.\end{array}

Here
\tilde{R}
,
\tilde{T}
are the remainders in the expansions

\begin{array}[]{rcl}R(\varepsilon\mu)&=&\varepsilon dR(0)(\mu)+\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{R}(\varepsilon\mu)\\[2.0pt]
T(\varepsilon\mu)&=&1+\varepsilon dT(0)(\mu)+\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{T}(\varepsilon\mu).\end{array}

Then for any given
r>0
, one can show that (
II.14
) admits a unique solution
\vec{\tau}^{\,\mathrm{sol}}\in\overline{B(O,r)}
for
\varepsilon
small enough (here
B(O,r)
denotes the open ball of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
centered at
O
of radius
r
). Defining

h^{\,\mathrm{sol}}=\varepsilon\bigg(\mu_{0}+\sum_{p=1}^{3}\tau_{p}^{\mathrm{sol}}\mu_{p}\bigg),

we obtain
\mathscr{F}(h^{\,\mathrm{sol}})=0
.

Why does this imply
T^{\varepsilon}=1
? Conservation of energy imposes
|R^{\varepsilon}|^{2}+|T^{\varepsilon}|^{2}=1
. Therefore when
R^{\varepsilon}=0
and
\Im m\,T^{\varepsilon}=0
, the only possibility is to have either
T^{\varepsilon}=-1
or
T^{\varepsilon}=1
. Since we made a small perturbation of the reference strip, error estimates for the asymptotic expansion of
u^{\varepsilon}
imply that for
\varepsilon
small,
T^{\varepsilon}
is close too one. Therefore, for
\varepsilon
small enough, necessarily we must have
T^{\varepsilon}=1
exactly.

I.45

II.2
where we imposed only zero reflection, we observe that the scattered field is indeed exponentially decaying both as

and as

R^{\varepsilon}=0+\varepsilon\,4i\pi\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{O})w_{+}(M_{1})^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\qquad\quad T^{\varepsilon}=1+\varepsilon\,4i\pi\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{O})|w_{+}(M_{1})|^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Here
\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{O})
stands for the capacity of the domain
\mathcal{O}
, a constant which appears classically in asymptotic analysis. An important point for our study is that one has always
\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{O})>0
. Additionally, one finds that
w_{+}
does not vanish in
\Omega^{0}
. From these two properties, we infer that one single small obstacle cannot even be non reflecting: whatever the choice for
M_{1}
or for the shape
\mathcal{O}
,
\mathcal{O}^{\varepsilon}_{1}
will always generate a reflection whose amplitude is of order
\varepsilon
.

Let us add a second small obstacle

\mathcal{O}^{\varepsilon}_{2}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,|\,\varepsilon^{-1}(z-M_{2})\in\mathcal{O}\}

in the waveguide, centred at the point
M_{2}\in\Omega^{0}
with
M_{2}\neq M_{1}
. Still denoting by
R^{\varepsilon}
,
T^{\varepsilon}
the scattering coefficients in this new geometry, we obtain the expansions, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
,

R^{\varepsilon}=0+\varepsilon\,4i\pi\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{O})\sum_{j=1}^{2}w_{+}(M_{j})^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\quad T^{\varepsilon}=1+\varepsilon\,4i\pi\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{O})\sum_{j=1}^{2}|w_{+}(M_{j})|^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

(II.24)

Observe that the interactions between the small objects do not appear at order
\varepsilon
, only at higher orders. The interesting point is that now, using the known expression of
w_{+}
, we can find positions
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
of the obstacles such that

\sum_{j=1}^{2}w_{+}(M_{j})^{2}=0.

(II.25)

In that case, we have a perturbation of
\Omega^{0}
of order
\varepsilon
which produces a reflection in
O(\varepsilon^{2})
. As mentioned above, this is almost no reflection. But by working a bit harder, we can achieve more. Pick
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
such that relation (
II.25
) is satisfied. Then by slightly perturbing the position of one obstacle, we can get
R^{\varepsilon}=0
exactly. More precisely, for
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, define
M^{\varepsilon\tau}_{1}=M_{1}+\varepsilon\tau
and

\mathcal{O}^{\varepsilon\tau}_{1}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,|\,\varepsilon^{-1}(z-M^{\varepsilon\tau}_{1})\in\mathcal{O}\}.

One can show that there is
\tau
, which is defined as the solution of a fixed point problem similar to (
II.4
), such that the reflection coefficient in the corresponding waveguide is zero (for more details, see
[
18
]
). Note that since
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have enough degrees of freedom to cancel one single complex number.

Can we get perfect invisibility,
i.e.

T^{\varepsilon}=1
, with this approach? From (
II.24
) we see that the answer is no. Indeed, whatever the position of the small obstacles or their number, we always have a phase shift for the transmitted wave.

One can also study what happens at higher wavenumbers
k
. In that situation, as can be seen in the particular case (
I.22
), more modes can propagate. The reflection and transmission coefficients then become respectively reflection and transmission matrices
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
,
\mathcal{T}^{\varepsilon}
. One can prove that by working with a sufficiently large number of small obstacles, we can cancel exactly the whole reflection matrix. The idea is the same as above. First we compute an asymptotic expansion of
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Then we find positions
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
,… ,
M_{k}
of the obstacles to cancel the term of order
\varepsilon
in the expansion of
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}
. Then by slightly perturbing the position of one group of obstacles by solving a fixed point problem in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, for a certain

depending on the number of propagating modes, we get
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}=0
. The higher the number of propagating modes, the more obstacles we need.

To conclude this section, let us mention that in this work, the main difficulty mathematically consists in proving error estimates in the asymptotic expansions which are uniform with respect to the parameter
\tau
in a closed ball to justify that the map appearing in the fixed point problem is indeed a contraction for
\varepsilon
small enough. We will not elaborate more on that topic here and refer the interesting reader to
[
18
]
.

In this chapter, we constructed smooth and non-smooth perturbations of the reference geometry which are invisible, in a broad sense (non reflecting or perfectly invisible). We worked at a given wavenumber

Chapter III
Playing with resonances to reach invisibility

In the previous chapter, we showed how to construct small non reflecting or invisible defects of the reference strip by using variants of the implicit functions theorem. The goal of the present chapter is to create larger invisible obstacles. To proceed, we have to act strongly on scattering coefficients. We will do that by working with resonant phenomena. More precisely, in the first section we explain how to exploit the Fano resonance phenomenon together with symmetry considerations to construct large non reflecting obstacles in monomode regime. Then, we modify a bit the point of view and for a given waveguide in acoustics, we show how to perturb its boundary with resonant ligaments to get approximately
T=1
in the new geometry.

Classical complex scaling

As a first step, we remind briefly how to use a complex scaling to compute trapped modes. Define the unbounded operator

5
Concluding remarks

Determining the scattering coefficients for a range of frequencies to identify the
k
for which there are incident fields which produce zero reflection is a tedious work. This chapter shows that reflectionless frequencies can be directly computed as the eigenvalues of a non-selfadjoint operator
B_{\theta}
(see (
IV.14
)) with conjugated complex scalings enforcing ingoing behavior in the incident lead and outgoing behavior in the other lead. The reflectionless spectrum of this operator
B_{\theta}
provides a complementary information to the one contained in the classical complex resonance spectrum associated with quasi normal modes which decompose only on outgoing waves (see the operator
A_{\theta}
in (
IV.10
)). Note that eigenvalues corresponding to trapped modes belong to both the reflectionless spectrum and to the classical complex resonance spectrum because trapped modes do not excite propagating waves. Let us make a few additional comments and highlight future directions as well as open questions.

i)
We have seen that the non-selfadjoint operator
B_{\theta}
is
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric when the structure has mirror symmetry. Interestingly, a direct calculus shows that in the very simple case of a
\mathrm{1D}
transmission problem through a slab of constant index, reflectionless frequencies are all real. This gives an example of a non-selfadjoint
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric operator with only real eigenvalues.

ii)
In this work, we investigated scattering problems in waveguides with
N=2
leads for which two reflectionless spectra exist: one associated with incident waves propagating from the left and another corresponding to incident waves propagating from the right. The more general case with
N
(
N\geq 2
) leads can be considered as well. Among the total of
2^{N}
different spectra with an ingoing or an outgoing complex scaling in each lead, two spectra correspond to eigenmodes which decompose on waves which are all outgoing or all ingoing. As a consequence, there are
2^{N}-2
reflectionless spectra.

iii)
Above we computed reflectionless modes in waveguides containing penetrable obstacles. We can work completely similarly with perturbations of the geometry. In Figure
IV.11
, we give two examples of reflectionless modes in such structures. Note that in each case,
B_{\theta}
is
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric due to the symmetry of the geometry. Other kinds of BCs (Dirichlet, …) and higher dimension (
\mathrm{3D}
) can be dealt with similarly.

Figure IV.11:
Examples of reflectionless modes in waveguides with sound hard walls (Neumann).

There are many open questions with this work. Here we list just of few of them.

iv)
First, it would be nice to obtain criteria on the index material/geometry ensuring that the spectrum of
B_{\theta}
is discrete outside of
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(B_{\theta})
. In 1D, a rather general condition can be obtained. In higher dimension this is quite open.

v)
On the other hand, could we show that
B_{\theta}
has always real eigenvalues corresponding to reflectionless modes, at least for
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric problems?

vi)
The question of the approximation of the spectrum of
B_{\theta}
is a field of research in itself, the reason being that
B_{\theta}
is a non selfadjoint operator. Indeed, the theory of perturbations of non selfadjoint operators is not well developed and many phenomena can occur. Here it is easy to see in the situation where the waveguide contains only (Neumann) horizontal cracks. In that case we explained before Figure
IV.3
that
\sigma(B_{\theta})
fills a whole sector. However when we truncate the domain at some distance
L
, we can show that the corresponding spectrum is discrete. This seems particularly pathological. But even when
\sigma(B_{\theta})\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(B_{\theta})
is discrete, proving that the spectrum of the problem in the truncated waveguide converges to the one of
B_{\theta}
when
L
tends to
+\infty
is a challenging task. Additionally, it would be interesting to prove that spurious eigenvalues, which would converge to some non physical values, do not exist. In practice, we not only truncate the domain but also approximate the problem by working in finite dimension with finite elements. This is also an approximation which has to be studied. In this context, it seems that the notion of pseudo-spectrum, see
e.g.

[
41
]
, which has been developed to understand the properties of non normal operators, may provide useful information.
