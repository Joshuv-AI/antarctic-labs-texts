The role of dominant handgrip strength in fall occurrence possibility and frequency
Authors: Xuyang Du, Hualin Wei, Conghui Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Falls are a major cause of morbidity, disability, and healthcare burden among older adults in China, and handgrip strength is widely recognized as a physiological marker of fall risk. Existing studies have largely examined the impact of risk factors on the probability of falling, while overlooking fall frequency, which is critical because repeated falls account for most serious injuries and deaths. This study examines the association between handgrip strength and both fall occurrence and fall frequency using pooled cross-sectional data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, estimated with a two-part model and heterogeneity analyses by gender, age, and region. The main contribution lies in jointly quantifying the association between handgrip strength and both fall probability and fall frequency within one framework, addressing the literature’s narrow focus on fall occurrence and limited consideration of heterogeneity. Results indicate that higher handgrip strength is associated with reduced fall probability and fewer falls, particularly among men, adults under 85 years, and residents of eastern provinces. Based on these findings, policymakers should expand community-based strength training, improve access to rehabilitation, and integrate routine handgrip strength screening into primary care, with fiscal resources allocated to specific populations and regions to ensure efficient use.

Introduction

Falls are a major cause of injury-related death and disability among elderly. World Health Organization (WHO) indicates that approximately 35% of adults aged 60 and above experience at least one fall annually [32]. This risk escalates with age: among those over 70, the fall rate is reported between 32 and 42%, and it reaches nearly 50% for individuals aged over 80 [32]. In addition, Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 shows that 37.3 million falls each year require medical attention [22]. As a result, falls are the second primary cause of accidental injury deaths [9]. These figures emphasize the urgency of identifying fall risk and preventing future falls.

This issue is particularly relevant in China, a country experiencing one of the most rapid ageing speed around the world [12]. As the country with the largest elderly population in the world and relatively limited social welfare compared to developed nations, China faces particularly severe health and economic consequences [13]. Moreover, the prevalence and severity of falls differ widely across provinces in China. For instance, self-reported fall rates range from 16.4% in eastern regions to over 24% in central and western areas [19]. Injurious falls are also more common in the southwest than in the northeast [19]. These regional gaps are closely related to China’s east–west divide in population density and intensity of human activity [17]. Differences in how people live and move in daily life, together with unequal availability of local public services and health resources, shape exposure to fall frequency and the likelihood across regions [2]. These disparities reflect uneven regional development and healthcare access, underscoring the need to account for geographic variation when addressing fall-related risks.

In clinical practice, handgrip strength is widely used as a physiological markers to predict fall risk [5, 18, 23]. Individuals with handgrip strength below the commonly referenced thresholds, which range from 23.2 to 39.0 kg for men and from 15.9 to 22.0 kg for women, are generally considered to be at increased risk of falling [1, 5]. However, most studies focus solely on whether a fall occurs, ignoring the frequency of falls. Although a single fall can be clinically consequential, recurrent falls better capture persistent vulnerability and the accumulation of adverse outcomes. Evidence suggests that repeated falls are more strongly linked to subsequent disability and mortality than isolated events [3]. This highlights the importance of considering fall frequency as a more informative indicator of health deterioration in older adults.

Research on falls among older adults dates back to the late twentieth century, with early studies examining fall-related disabilities and their consequences [3]. Over time, researchers began to explore the multifactorial causes of falls, identifying factors such as physical deficits, demographic characteristics, and environmental influences [15, 18]. Handgrip strength emerged as a robust predictor of fall risk, with studies consistently finding that stronger handgrip strength is associated with lower fall risk [5, 24]. However, the focus of many studies has been on fall occurrence, often overlooking fall frequency, despite its greater relevance for assessing the severity of fall-related outcomes [3]. Furthermore, while the role of handgrip strength in fall prevention is well-established, limited research has considered regional differences in fall risk, particularly in China, where significant geographic disparities exist in healthcare access and infrastructure [19].

Based on this background, this study extends existing research by examining the association between handgrip strength and both fall occurrence and fall frequency, with regional heterogeneity analyses. Utilizing pooled cross-sectional data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) covering the years 2011, 2013, and 2015, a two-part model is employed to estimate the association between handgrip strength and both fall probability and fall frequency.

Data and methodology

Study population

Although the CHARLS has been updated to include data from 2020, handgrip strength measurements were not conducted after 2015 [7]. Therefore, this study utilizes pooled cross-sectional data from the 2011, 2013, and 2015 waves of CHARLS, comprising a total of 25,586 individuals in row dataset.

In the publicly released wave files, respondents who were interviewed in an earlier wave but not re-interviewed in a later wave (e.g., due to death or loss to follow-up) may still appear with wave-specific records flagged as non-participation, and interview-based variables are recorded as missing. So, as shown in Fig. 1, we first excluded 19,341 wave-specific observations for respondents who did not participate in that wave. These exclusions refer to non-interview records rather than individuals. Given that the target population of this study is older adults, we followed the United Nations' definition, which classifies individuals aged 60 and above as old individuals [29], and excluded 31,363 respondents under the age of 60. Finally, based on the variables used in this study, we removed an additional 13,913 individuals due to missing values. Variables descriptions

Covariates were selected based on prior literature. Health-related variables such as chronic disease (CD), medication use (MED), and functional status (ADL) reflect an individual's physical and mental condition and are closely associated with fall risk [9, 14, 28, 34]. Health behaviors including smoking (SMK) and drinking (DRK) capture adverse lifestyle patterns that may compromise physical functioning and elevate the risk of falling [26]. Additionally, family-related variables, such as household income and the number of surviving children, are used to represent the level of family support and care for older adults. Lastly, sex and age variables are employed for heterogeneity analysis and are excluded from the regression equation to avoid multicollinearity issues.

Method

Benchmark regression

Two-part models are employed to estimate the association between handgrip strength and both fall probability and fall frequency. Based on previous research, the first part applies a logit model to estimate the association of handgrip strength on the likelihood of experiencing a fall [9]. Building on this, since the distribution of the fall count data deviates from normality, the second part adopts zero-truncated Poisson estimation model to assess the association of handgrip strength on the number of fall incidents among individuals who have experienced at least one fall [13]. Heterogeneity analysis

To further investigate the subgroup patterns of the association of handgrip strength on falls, this study conducts heterogeneity analysis through based on gender, age group, and region. For the regional dimension, we classify observations using the Hu Huanyong Line, a canonical demarcation of China’s long-run east–west divide in population distribution and regional development [17]. We adopt the Hu Huanyong Line rather than conventional administrative regional groupings because, in this study, a population based boundary more directly reflects population density and the intensity of human activity, which shape daily mobility environments and exposure to fall related hazards. Broad administrative classifications can place provinces with very different population density into the same category, so the within group context becomes less comparable and the contrast in the handgrip strength and falls association across regions becomes less clear [2].

Baseline characteristics of participants by falls

Handgrip strength was measured in kilograms using the dominant-hand maximum value and categorized into quartiles. Specifically, Q1 corresponds to handgrip strength ≤ 24.0 kg, Q2 to 24.1–30.0 kg, Q3 to 30.1–38.0 kg, and Q4 to ≥ 38.1 kg. Individuals who experienced falls tended to have weaker handgrip strength (p < 0.001). Specifically, 45.1% of fallers were in the lowest quartile of handgrip strength (Q1), compared to 34.1% among non-fallers, while a higher proportion of non-fallers were in the highest quartile (Q4) (17.5% vs. 11.3%). This aligns with prior research showing that reduced handgrip strength is a strong predictor of future falls in older populations [9, 10], p. 50, [16].

The mean age of participants with falls was slightly higher than those without falls (68.09 vs. 67.42 years, p < 0.001). Females accounted for a significantly larger proportion in the fall group compared to the non-fall group (60.3% vs. 49.1%, p < 0.001), indicating potential gender differences in fall risk.

The proportion of individuals without any limitations in activities of daily living (ADL score = 0) was markedly lower among fallers (59.8%) compared to non-fallers (79.7%) (p < 0.001), suggesting that individuals without falls generally had better physical functioning. In contrast, participants with scores indicating moderate to severe disability (e.g., 4–6) were more concentrated in the fall group, demonstrating a clear relationship between poorer functional status and higher fall risk. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have identified impaired ADL capacity as a major predictor of falls in older adults [4, 36].

Drinking status did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.26), implying that alcohol consumption was not a distinguishing behavioral factor for fall incidence in this population. However, smoking status differed significantly: a smaller proportion of fallers were current smokers compared with non-fallers (23.7% vs. 27.6%, p < 0.001).

The prevalence of chronic diseases was higher among fallers (83.4% vs. 76.7%, p < 0.001), suggesting a strong association between multimorbidity and increased fall risk. This aligns with the broader literature, which indicates that chronic diseases contribute to reduced mobility, balance impairment, and frailty, thereby elevating the likelihood of falling [14, 28, 34].

Finally, the mean total household consumption was slightly lower among fallers than non-fallers (25,618.35 vs. 26,226.59 CNY), while the mean number of children was slightly higher in the fall group (3.38 vs. 3.23, p < 0.001).

In the logit models predicting fall probability, higher handgrip strength quartiles (Q2–Q4) were associated with progressively lower fall odds. Among the full sample, those in Q4 had a 52% lower fall risk (OR = 0.48, p < 0.001), with Q3 and Q2 also showing significant reductions (p < 0.01). Men showed a comparable trend: Q4 was linked to a 55% decrease in fall risk (OR = 0.45, p < 0.001). For women, only Q4 remained statistically significant (OR = 0.63, p < 0.01), while Q2 and Q3 effects were weaker or non-significant. These results align with evidence that improved handgrip strength is associated with a lower risk of falls in older adults [10, 20, 21, 24].

In the second part (zero-truncated Poisson), the estimates indicate that higher handgrip strength is associated with fewer falls. In the full sample, Q3 and Q4 are both statistically significant in column 1 and column 2. With controls variables, Q3 has an IRR of 0.757 (p < 0.01) and Q4 has an IRR of 0.638 (p < 0.01), implying roughly 24% and 36% fewer expected falls, respectively, compared with Q1. In men, the association is larger and more consistently significant across higher handgrip strength categories. With control variables, Q3 has an IRR of 0.714 (p < 0.05) and Q4 has an IRR of 0.490 (p < 0.01), implying about 29% and 51% fewer expected falls. In women, only Q4 remains statistically significant (IRR = 0.739, p < 0.05), while Q2 and Q3 are not significant, suggesting that the association between handgrip strength and fall frequency is weaker and less consistently detectable for women. This observed gender difference—where the association between handgrip strength and falls is stronger and more consistent among men—has also been reported in prior studies [16, 30].

These results suggest a clear inverse relationship between handgrip strength and both the likelihood and frequency of falls, particularly for males and individuals in the highest strength quartile. This pattern aligns with previous findings indicating muscular strength as a key protective factor against falls in older adults. This gender difference, in which women exhibit generally weaker and less consistent associations, has also been documented in previous studies.

Discussion

The study found a significant inverse relationship between handgrip strength and both the probability and frequency of falls, as estimated using a two-part modeling strategy [11]. This approach—first applying a logit model to estimate fall probability, followed by a zero-truncated Poisson estimation to quantify the number of falls among those who fell—provides a more comprehensive understanding of fall risk than studies focusing solely on fall occurrence [13]. By jointly capturing the likelihood of any fall and the intensity of fall events, this design allows the identification of factors that may influence not only whether an older adult falls, but also how often they fall once at risk, an important distinction that has been largely overlooked in previous literature [9, 19, 24, 28, 33].

Compared with individuals in the lowest quartile of handgrip strength (Q1), those in higher quartiles—particularly Q3 and Q4—had markedly lower fall risk, with the trend being most pronounced in the overall sample and among men, while associations in women were weaker and largely concentrated in the highest quartile. This finding is consistent with most previous research, which has reported a clear negative relationship between handgrip strength and fall risk in older adults [9, 19, 24, 30]. One possible explanation is that greater handgrip strength reflects overall muscular capacity, which enables older adults to maintain better lower-limb stability and mobility in daily activities [27]. Furthermore, higher strength may also facilitate faster and more effective responses to balance disturbances, reducing the likelihood of losing stability during walking or transferring [25].

The age heterogeneity analysis is consistent with previous research, which has reported that the negative association between handgrip strength and falls is stronger among relatively younger older adults and diminishes in the oldest-old [9, 14, 16, 35]. In this study, participants aged below 85 years exhibited a clear negative relationship between higher handgrip strength quartiles and both fall risk and fall frequency, with the magnitude of this relationship being particularly pronounced in men. This finding can be explained by the fact that in this age range, muscle strength remains sufficiently variable and functionally relevant to maintain balance, support mobility, and enable effective recovery from balance disturbances [6, 20, 21]. In contrast, among participants aged 85 years and above, the association between handgrip strength and fall outcomes was not statistically significant. Moreover, this age group commonly experiences a higher prevalence of sensory deficits, vestibular decline, and multimorbidity, factors that may outweigh the influence of muscle strength in fall mechanisms.

Regional heterogeneity analysis indicated that the negative association between handgrip strength and falls was more consistent and, in some cases, stronger in provinces east of the Hu Line, particularly among men. This is likely due to higher population density and better-developed healthcare systems in the east, which provide greater access to rehabilitation and preventive care, enabling older adults to translate muscle strength into improved balance and mobility [14, 19, 24]. In addition, safer built environments—such as well-maintained pavements and accessible public facilities—reduce external hazards, amplifying the protective effect of handgrip strength [33]. In contrast, western provinces face challenges including limited healthcare resources, poorer infrastructure, and greater exposure to environmental risks, which restrict the extent to which muscular strength can be converted into reduced fall risk [19].

Collectively, these findings suggest that handgrip strength is a robust predictor of falls in older adults, with significant heterogeneity by gender, age, and region, and particularly evident among men, those aged below 85 years, and residents of eastern China. These patterns highlight that the functional benefits of muscular strength are not uniform across populations but are shaped by physiological resilience, social environments, and access to supportive resources. By applying a two-part model that separates fall occurrence from fall frequency, this study adds to the literature by estimating handgrip strength associations on both fall occurrence and fall frequency within a single empirical framework.

In terms of practical contribution, this study highlights the utility of handgrip strength as a clinical screening tool, demonstrating its relevance not only to the probability of fall occurrence but also the number of fall incidents, thereby enhancing the precision of fall risk assessments among older adults. From an academic perspective, this study contributes to the literature by systematically reviewing prior research, identifying both content-related and methodological gaps—particularly the lack of focus on fall frequency and regional variation—and providing empirical evidence to help fill these gaps.

Conclusion

Using pooled cross sectional data from the CHARLS and a two part model, this study jointly examined the association between handgrip strength and both fall occurrence and fall frequency among older adults, with heterogeneity by gender, age, and region. Higher handgrip strength was consistently associated with a lower probability of falling, and with fewer falls. These associations are more significant in men, adults aged below 85, and regions east of the Hu Huanyong Line, while they are weaker or not evident in women, the oldest old, and regions west of Hu Huanyong Line.

Based on these results, several practical policy recommendations can be proposed. Policymakers should prioritize the design and funding of community-based strength training programs for older adults, with a particular focus on men under the age of eighty-five. These programs should emphasize progressive resistance training that helps maintain or improve handgrip strength. Governments should also adapt fall prevention strategies to the specific conditions of different regions. In the western provinces, this requires targeted investment in safer built environments, improvement of access to rehabilitation and preventive care services, and systematic reduction of environmental hazards, so that the benefits of muscular strength can be effectively translated into a lower risk of falls. Furthermore, health authorities should incorporate regular handgrip strength assessment into routine primary healthcare and geriatric check-ups. This would enable early identification of older adults at higher risk of falling and allow timely implementation of appropriate interventions.
