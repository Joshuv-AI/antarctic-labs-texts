How loneliness and social isolation are linked to cognitive decline among older adults? A systematic review of underlying physiological and psychobehavioral mechanisms
Authors: Kexin Yu, Karina Van Bogart, Jee Eun Kang, Harry O. Taylor, Karra D. Harrington, Lisa C. Silbert, Ihab Hajjar, Hiroko H. Dodge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite empirical evidence linking loneliness and social isolation to cognitive decline (CD) in later life, the underlying physiological and psychobehavioral mechanisms remain unclear. We conducted a systematic review to synthesize relevant evidence. Studies examining psychobehavioral pathways required mediation analyses, whereas studies on physiological pathways were included when mediation was examined or when outcomes are dementia‐related neuropathological changes. Thirty‐eight articles were included. Evidence pointed to multiple physiological mechanisms, including Alzheimer's disease pathology, brain structural differences, inflammation, cardiovascular risks, neurotrophic factors, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) ‐axis function, gene expression, and general health, although findings were mixed. Psychobehavioral pathways, including depressive symptoms, neuroticism, lower sense of control, sleep disturbance, and lower engagement in social or leisure activities, mediated the association. This review identified mechanistic targets that may be leveraged in non‐pharmacological interventions. Future intervention programs aimed at reducing social isolation and loneliness to delay CD could investigate whether they impact physiological and psychobehavioral pathways summarized here.

BACKGROUND

Health and social relationships are critical components that fundamentally influence how individuals age, and those two domains are interrelated. 1 , 2 For instance, aspects of social disconnection (e.g., social isolation and loneliness) have been increasingly recognized as modifiable risk factors for the development of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD), 3 which offers an opportunity for disease prevention given the prevalent experience of social disconnectedness in later life. About one‐third of community‐dwelling older adults report frequent loneliness, 3 and the estimated global prevalence of social isolation in this population is 25%. 4 A meta‐analysis with longitudinal data from 600,000+ older adults found loneliness is associated with a 31% higher risk of all‐cause dementia, independent of social isolation or depression. 5 Social connection, the opposite of social isolation, has been linked to reduced risk of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia. 6 , 7 Further, according to the 2024 Lancet Commission on Dementia Prevention report, about 5% of dementia cases could be attributed to social isolation and loneliness, which corresponds to about 2.75 million patients globally and 350,000 in the U.S. alone. 8 Despite extensive empirical evidence suggesting both social isolation and loneliness are separate risk factors for cognitive decline (CD) and possible subsequent dementia onset, the underlying mechanisms are less clear. 7 , 9 The current systematic review is set to address this gap by synthesizing evidence on the physiological and behavioral pathways linking loneliness and social isolation to CD.

Because social isolation and loneliness are related but distinctly different concepts, it is important to examine them together when considering pathways to CD. Social isolation refers to having few or no social relationships or interactions, 4 whereas loneliness refers to the subjective feeling that one's social connections are insufficient in quality or quantity. 10 In other words, social isolation captures a more objective lack of social contact, while loneliness represents the subjective experience of social disconnection. Social isolation and loneliness are positively associated with each other, but one can be isolated but not lonely (i.e., solitude) and vice versa (i.e., lonely in crowd). Further, social isolation and loneliness have been found to have an independent relationship with CD. 11 , 12 Synthesizing evidence related to both constructs in one review can contribute to identifying shared and different underlying pathways to CD, and informing mechanisms targeted intervention for older adults experiencing social disconnection and at higher risk of CD.

A biobehavioral framework is useful for understanding how social disconnection relates to cognitive outcomes, since it recognizes that cognition is shaped by biological, psychological, and behavioral processes. Biological pathways emphasize the impact that loneliness and social isolation can have on the physical body, which can increase risk for CD. Potential biological mechanisms include factors related to stress physiology, including dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. For example, loneliness is related to heightened cortisol, 13 which may contribute to CD due to the high concentration of glucocorticoid receptors in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex–brain regions critical for cognitive function. 14 Additionally, loneliness is associated with altered neural networks, which could accelerate neurodegenerative processes over time and contribute to CD. 15 Living alone, a proxy of social isolation, is related to a flattened diurnal cortisol pattern and higher C‐reactive protein (CRP) levels in adults. 16 Psychobehavioral pathways emphasize the negative impact that loneliness and social isolation can have on socioemotional wellbeing and behavior, which can increase risk for CD. Potential psychobehavioral pathways include poor mental health and maladaptive behaviors. For example, depressive symptoms and suboptimal sleep and physical activity patterns may all contribute to diminished cognitive reserve and reduced resilience to cognitive aging. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Framing this review within a biobehavioral perspective highlights the multiple pathways that could connect social disconnection to CD and sets the stage for future work examining their interplay.

Although previous reviews established the associations between social isolation, loneliness, and CD, 5 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 this systematic review aims to address a critical gap in understanding mechanisms through which social isolation and loneliness can be linked to CD among older adults. While previous evidence supports social isolation and loneliness as risk factors that precede and contribute to CD, 6 , 7 we acknowledge an opposing view that suggests that early cognitive changes manifest as social withdrawal, thereby increasing loneliness and social isolation, with loneliness being a prodromal symptoms of dementia. 25 , 26 By synthesizing mechanistic evidence, particularly from longitudinal studies incorporating ADRD biomarkers (e.g., amyloid‐β, tau, and brain atrophy), the current review evaluated empirical evidence that supports or opposes the plausibility of proposed pathways. Research areas with shared interest in this topic—such as Neurology, Psychology, Gerontology, and Public Health—have tended to operate in silos, limiting opportunities for cross‐disciplinary insights. This review integrates findings across physiological and psychobehavioral pathways, producing a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms and promoting future collaborative research agendas. METHODS

In the current review, we used the terms mechanisms and pathways interchangeably. CD was defined as a lower cognitive test score or an MCI/dementia incident. The systematic review protocol has been registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD42023404376). Throughout the review process, we amended the protocol three times to make changes to core team members and update review progress. This review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [ 27 ]. The completed PRISMA checklist for this review can be found in Table S1. We used Covidence for reference management, screening, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment.

For research examining psychobehavioral pathways, studies that investigated mediating pathways of the associations between social isolation, loneliness, and CD were included. To identify a broader range of potential mechanisms, studies that examined physiological pathways were not required to conduct a mediation analysis as long as the physiological outcomes directly addressed dementia pathology, for example, amyloid‐beta, tau, neuroinflammation, change in brain volume, white matter hyperintensity (WMH). We chose to include studies investigating brain physiological changes associated with social isolation and loneliness, recognizing that this is a rapidly developing research area where mediation analyses with cognitive outcomes are often lacking. The advantages and limitations of this decision are outlined in Section 4.6.

Selection criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) included middle‐ and older‐aged individuals (50+ years) from the general community‐based population; (2) examined loneliness or social isolation as the exposure variable; (3) examined CD as defined by a lower cognitive test scores and MCI/dementia incidence; (4) longitudinal and cross‐sectional mediation analysis for psychobehavioral pathways; (5) longitudinal and cross‐sectional studies examining the association between social isolation, loneliness, and dementia‐related neuropathological changes (including but not limited to amyloid, tau, neuroinflammation, and TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43), see the full list of neuropathological changes included in search term 4. Mechanisms). Studies that examined neuropathology did not have to include CD as an outcome to be included; (6) peer reviewed articles published in English.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) qualitative studies; (2) study conducted with specific clinical populations, such as older adults with severe depression.

Data collection and analysis

Four (K.Y., J.K., K.V.B., and H.T.) reviewers independently screened and selected studies for inclusion. At least two reviewers screened each article. We used Covidence to coordinate and record decisions. The search results were screened in two stages: title and abstract screening and full‐text screening. Disagreements were discussed in biweekly team meetings. A third reviewer was invited to reconcile different opinions between the primary reviewers when necessary.

For all included studies, four reviewers (K.Y., J.K., K.V.B., and H.T.) independently extracted publication information (author, year), setting, countries where the research was conducted, study design, sample size, participant demographics, recruitment methods, measurement instruments used for loneliness, social isolation, and cognition. For included longitudinal studies, we also extracted information regarding the follow‐up period, number of assessments, and attrition. If the included study examined ADRD biomarkers, we extracted the method used for assessing biomarkers (e.g., blood, positron emission tomography [PET] imaging, and cerebrospinal fluid [CSF]) and whether the biomarkers assessed were specific to various brain regions. Data extraction for each article was completed by two independent reviewers. KY cross‐checked all extracted data and any disagreements were resolved in regular team meetings. Each article reached consensus for data extraction.

Study quality and risk of bias

We used the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) risk of bias checklist for cross‐sectional studies and checklist for cohort studies to evaluate the quality of evidence for included articles. 28 Following PRISMA recommendations, we did not compute an overall quality rating for studies. Instead, we reviewed quality in separate areas, such as sample selection, study setting, variable measurement, confounding adjustment, and statistical analysis. Four reviewers (K.Y., J.K., K.V.B., and H.T.) independently assessed the quality of each included study. Each included study's risk of bias was assessed by two reviewers. Any disagreements were reconciled in regular team meetings.

RESULTS

Study selection

We identified 4314 unique articles, 4300 from the systematic search, and 14 from searching the reference lists. After title and abstract screening, the team assessed 104 full‐text articles for eligibility. Finally, 37 unique articles met the inclusion criteria. Two‐thirds of the studies included in this review were published within the past 5 years, reflecting the rapid growth of the research topic. The studies were conducted in a diverse array of countries spanning North America, Asia, and Europe. Among the 30 studies reporting education, approximately half included samples with relatively higher educational attainment, reflected in mean education levels of 14–16 years 30 , 32 , 33 or substantial proportions with college‐level education. 34 , 35 Geographic differences in educational attainment were observed among these studies. Studies conducted in Asian populations (n = 10) tended to report lower education (e.g., <10–12 years or majority ≤ secondary), 36 , 37 whereas studies conducted in North America (n = 12) and Europe (n = 8) more often reported higher average education (14–16 years) or substantial proportions with college‐level education, with some variability. 38 Eighteen out of the 37 included studies utilized data from larger aging cohort studies using population‐based systematic sampling. Some studies were conducted as part of a longitudinal observational study that recruited participants from a specific geographic region (e.g., Rush Memory Aging Project and Harvard Aging Brian Study). 30 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 38 , 39 , 40 Sample sizes for the included studies ranged from 26 to 55,340. The follow‐up period among longitudinal studies ranged from 2 to 24 years, with nine studies with follow‐up of less than 5 years, 33 , 34 , 39 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 and eight studies of more than 10‐year of follow‐up. 30 , 31 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52

The most commonly used loneliness measure was the University of California Los Angeles Loneliness Scale (UCLA‐LS, n = 16), followed by the modified DeJong Gierveld Loneliness Scale (DJG‐LS, n = 5). Studies varied on which items they used from the full UCLA‐LS, ranging from 3 to 20 items. Three studies used a single binary item to assess loneliness. 50 , 52 , 53 Of the 16 studies that examined social isolation, three measured social isolation with the 6‐item Lubben Social Network Scale (LSNS‐6), 41 , 48 , 54 two with Berkman–Syme Social Network Index, 55 , 56 two with the Steptoe Social Isolation Index, 45 , 57 and one with Cohen's social network index. 58 One study measured social isolation using a single‐item indicating frequency of contact with family and friends who do not live with them. 59 The remaining studies did not explicitly state a type of social network index and operationalized social isolation using time spent per week on social activities, 39 or a single or multiple latent variables constructed with various factors, such as frequency of contact with children, friends, and other family members, and frequency of social participation. 31 , 37

Cognitive function was assessed using a wide range of approaches across studies. More thorough information on measurements can be found in Tables S2–S5. Briefly, global cognitive function was most commonly measured using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE, n = 8), 60 the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA, n = 4), 61 and the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS, n = 3). 62 Six studies measured global cognition with a composite score from a number of domain specific cognition measures. 30 , 34 , 43 , 45 , 48 , 52 Common , , , domain‐specific cognitive functions assessed across studies included attention, working memory, visuospatial ability, executive function, processing speed, language (e.g., verbal fluency), and memory (e.g., episodic, semantic). Cognitive status (normal, MCI, or dementia) was categorized according to Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), 63 Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) ‐IV criteria, 64 one study (Duffner et al.) used self‐report of a physician diagnosis combined with the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly. 31

Loneliness and cognitive decline

Physiological pathways

The current review yielded several physiological pathways linking loneliness to CD, including (1) Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology, (2) brain structural changes, (3) cardiovascular risks, (4) neurotrophic factors, (5) AD‐related gene expression, (6) general health indicators, (7) inflammation, and (8) HPA‐axis functioning, with both significant and null findings reported. AD pathology (cross‐sectionally related to loneliness, not related to loneliness according to longitudinal evidence): Longitudinal evidence does not support a direct association between loneliness and AD neuropathological markers, 34 , 52 and while one cross‐sectional study reported associations with amyloid and tau burden, 43 the cross‐sectional design precludes conclusions about directionality or causality. One cross‐sectional study 40 found greater loneliness was related to accumulated tau pathology in the right entorhinal cortex and in the right fusiform gyrus. 38 On the contrary, Wilson et al. 34 analyzed autopsy data from older adults free from dementia at baseline with a subgroup developing AD during the follow‐up period. Although loneliness was related to dementia onset, no associations between baseline loneliness and the amount of beta‐amyloid plaque, tau tangle, or cerebral infarction were found. 34 The authors concluded that lonely individuals might be more vulnerable to age‐related neuropathology, but not as a reaction to incident dementia or an early sign of its pathology. 34 Van der Velpen et al. 52 reported loneliness was not associated with plasma neurodegeneration biomarkers (aβ40, aβ42, total tau, neurofilament light chain [NfL]) cross‐sectionally or longitudinally.Brain structural differences (related to loneliness in cross‐sectional studies, mixed longitudinal evidence): Loneliness has been linked to structural brain abnormalities that may underlie CD, mostly supported by cross‐sectional evidence while longitudinal studies are less common and the results were mixed. A cross‐sectional study found that those with higher levels of loneliness had gray matter atrophy in the thalamus, hippocampus, and entorhinal cortex. 65 Similarly, another cross‐sectional study reported higher levels of loneliness to be related to lower gray matter volume in the left amygdala/anterior hippocampus, the left posterior para‐hippocampus, and the left cerebellum. 66 . Furthermore, an cross‐sectional investigation stratified by participants’ cognitive status (e.g., subjective cognitive decline [SCD], MCI) showed that among individuals with SCD, loneliness was related to smaller regional grey matter volume in the bilateral thalamus; and among individuals with MCI, loneliness was related to decreased grey matter volume in the left middle occipital gyrus and the cerebellar vermal lobules I−V. 53 Structural brain changes have also been tested as mediators for the association between loneliness and CD. Park et al. 36 found the cross‐sectional association between loneliness and cognitive performance was mediated by the volume of WMHs among men only, yet the relationship was not mediated by hippocampal or gray matter volumes.

Psychobehavioral pathways

Psychological pathways (depressive symptoms supported by longitudinal evidence, sense of control, perceived constraint, neuroticism supported by one cross‐sectional study): Several psychobehavioral pathways emerged as potential mechanisms linking loneliness to CD. Depressive symptoms were the most frequently examined psychological pathway, and findings were consistent across the studies. All four longitudinal studies supported that depressive symptoms mediated the association between loneliness and CD in older adults. 29 , 30 , 31 , 43 Other psychological mediators included sense of control and neuroticism. 72 , 73 Lower sense of control and higher perceived constraint separately mediated the cross‐sectional association of loneliness and CD. 73 Additionally, loneliness was cross‐sectionally related to worse cognitive functioning through an indirect pathway of higher neuroticism. 72 Factors that were not supported as mediators were anxiety (longitudinal) 43 and a sense of personal mastery (cross‐sectional). 73 Behavioral pathways (leisure activities supported by one cross‐sectional study, social participation supported by one longitudinal study): In terms of behavioral pathways, the cross‐sectional association between loneliness and cognitive function was mediated by reduced leisure activities, including physical, social, and mental activities. 74 Social participation, measured at 5‐year follow‐up by frequency of attending organized meetings, attending religious services, volunteering, and socializing with family and friends, mediated the association between baseline loneliness and cognitive functioning in 10‐year follow‐up. 47

Social isolation and cognitive decline

Physiological pathways

Three cross‐sectional and five longitudinal studies investigated physiological pathways linking social isolation to CD. The pathways investigated included (1) AD pathology, (2) neurotrophic factors, (3) brain structural differences, (4) cardiovascular risks, (5) inflammation, and (6) oral health. Overall findings included a mix of positive and null associations. AD pathology (moderation supported by one longitudinal study): Social isolation was not directly related to Aβ deposition measured with PET scan, but baseline social isolation was associated with greater amyloid‐β‐related CD over 3 years. 39 Nonetheless, baseline Aβ was not related to a longitudinal change in social engagement. 39 Neurotrophic factors (cross‐sectionally related to social isolation according to one study): One mechanism through which social isolation could be related to accelerated CD is through lower levels of neurotrophic factors. Social isolation was cross‐sectionally related to lower serum BDNF levels. 56 Brain structural differences (association with social isolation supported by both cross‐sectional and longitudinal evidence, but longitudinal mediation was not supported by one study): Brain structural differences as physiological pathways for the association between social isolation and CD were examined in three studies (one cross‐sectional and two longitudinal). Cross‐sectionally, social isolation was associated to lower total brain volume and regional brain volumes in the temporal lobe, occipital lobe, cingulum, hippocampus, and amygdala, as well as higher white matter lesion volume, and depressive symptoms partially mediated these associations. 59 A longitudinal investigation over 7 years found both baseline social isolation and the longitudinal increase in social isolation were related to smaller volumes of the hippocampus at the end of the study and reduced cortical thickness compared to baseline. 48 However, hippocampal volume did not mediate the relationship between social isolation and global cognition. 48 Social isolation was associated with a greater rate of hippocampal atrophy over four years, and this effect remained significant after controlling for loneliness. 44 Cardiovascular risks (related to social isolation according to one cross‐sectional study): A cross‐sectional study provided evidence linking social isolation to cardiovascular risks for dementia. 58 Cerebral vasodilatory response (CVR) is the ability of blood vessels to dilate and constrict in response to carbon dioxide, which is critical for maintaining adequate blood supply to the brain. 58 Older adults with smaller social network size exhibited lower CVR in in the hippocampus, and less social network complexity was negatively associated with CVR in the regions previously implicated in social cognition, including hippocampus, right amygdala, right entorhinal cortex, and right para‐hippocampal gyrus. 58 Inflammation (one cross‐sectional study supported mediation among men only): One cross‐sectional study reported sex differences in the association between social isolation and inflammation in relation to cognitive functioning. Social isolation was related to higher levels of inflammation (CRP and fibrinogen) in older men but not older women. 55 Among older men, CRP and fibrinogen separately partially mediated the association between social isolation with cognition, yet albumin did not mediate the relationship. 55 Oral health (mediation supported by one longitudinal study): Poor oral health, measured by number of missing teeth, mediated the association between baseline social isolation and subsequent cognitive performance in 4 years. 45

Psychobehavioral pathways

Psychological pathways (depressive symptoms supported by cross‐sectional and longitudinal evidence, loneliness supported by cross‐sectional evidence, psychological well‐being supported by one longitudinal study): Depressive symptoms and loneliness are the most examined psychological mediators of the relationship between social isolation and CD with consistent evidence. 31 , 37 , 54 , 75 Loneliness was cross‐sectionally supported as a mediator between the association of social isolation and CD. 37 , 75 One longitudinal study found that depressive symptoms independently mediated the associations of older adults’ social isolation and incident dementia over a 15‐year period. 31 Psychological well‐being, encompassing both positive and negative affect as well as personality traits, has also been identified as a mediator in this longitudinal association. 41 Behavioral pathways (sleep disturbance and physical activity supported by longitudinal evidence): Increased sleep disturbance 57 mediated the longitudinal association between social isolation and CD over 8 years, and reduced physical activity 46 mediated the association over 3 years.

Risk of bias

Results of the risk of bias assessment are summarized in Table 5 for cross‐sectional studies and in Table 6 for longitudinal studies. Among cross‐sectional studies, methodological issues identified include inclusion criteria not clearly defined, and study subject and research setting not described in detail, which could introduce selection bias and limit the external validity of the research findings. Measurement concerns for the exposure variables (i.e., social isolation and loneliness) exist for both cross‐sectional and longitudinal studies. For example, three studies relied on single‐item measures of loneliness, which are generally less reliable than multi‐item scales. 76 Moreover, the operationalization and measurement of social isolation were even more heterogeneous than those of loneliness, which might make the findings less comparable across studies. Seven out of the total 19 longitudinal studies had incomplete follow‐up yet the reasons for loss to follow‐up were not explored and described. Additionally, nine out of 19 longitudinal studies did not describe strategies to address incomplete follow‐up.

DISCUSSION

Empirical evidence shows that higher levels of loneliness and social isolation are associated with CD among older adults; however, questions remain regarding the mechanisms through which loneliness and social isolation affect CD. This review synthesized evidence on how physiological and psychobehavioral mechanisms may explain the associations of loneliness and social isolation with CD in later life. Drawing on 37 empirical studies with a total of 185,794 participants, we identified multiple physiological and psychobehavioral pathways connecting loneliness and social isolation to CD. The literature has focused more extensively on mechanisms linking loneliness with CD, with comparatively fewer studies examining mechanisms linking social isolation to CD. Although there was considerable overlap in the mechanistic pathways identified for loneliness and social isolation, distinctions also emerged. Similar categories of physiological pathway domains (e.g., brain structural change) were identified for both loneliness and social isolation. However, their relationships with specific indicators (e.g., total gray matter volume) differ. Additionally, more behavioral pathways were examined for social isolation while more psychological pathways identified for loneliness. Below, we provide our interpretations of existing evidence on physiological pathways and psychobehavioral pathways, followed by evaluation of the strength and weakness of existing literature, future research directions, and strength and weakness of the current review.

Physiological pathways

Risk factor versus prodromal hypothesis

A debate in the literature concerns the nature of the association between loneliness, social isolation, and CD: are loneliness and social isolation risk factors that precede and contribute to cognitive deterioration, or are they early signs/symptoms that an individual may be developing dementia (i.e., the prodromal hypothesis)? The current review found evidence supporting both sides of this debate. Overall, more studies have examined loneliness and isolation as risk factors, with multiple longitudinal investigations supporting their role in predicting both CD and dementia onset. 30 , 31 , 52 , 57 The cognitive vulnerability hypothesis posits that loneliness and social isolation erode cognitive reserve—the brain's capacity to maintain function despite age‐related changes—making individuals more susceptible to neuropathological damage. 34 This systematic review identified several mechanisms that may underlie this vulnerability. Both loneliness and social isolation may activate the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system, which in turn may contribute to vascular dysregulation, immune dysfunction, and neurodegeneration. 71 Molecular evidence from two longitudinal studies suggests that loneliness is linked to altered expression of Alzheimer's‐related gene sets, 32 , 33 offering additional information for increased vulnerability among lonely individuals. Moreover, findings support that better social connection with others preserve cerebrovascular health and buffer against cognitive deterioration. 58

Additionally, some research has found important bidirectional relationships between social isolation, loneliness, and CD. As an example, a longitudinal study examining social engagement and CD found partial evidence that a bidirectional relationship may exist. 77 This study found a significant interaction effect between social engagement and Aβ; adults with greater baseline Aβ levels and lower baseline social engagement had greater CD three years later. 77 Moreover, this study found baseline CD scores predicted social engagement three years later, illustrating the potential for a bidirectional relationship between social engagement and CD. 77 More empirical investigations are necessary to disentangle the directionality of the association.

Psychobehavioral pathways

Psychobehavioral pathways linking loneliness and social isolation show commonalities but also important distinctions. For loneliness, depressive symptoms are the most consistently supported mediator across longitudinal studies, 31 , 37 , 54 , 75 with additional contributions from lower sense of control, 73 higher neuroticism, 72 and reduced engagement in leisure and social activities. 74 Although loneliness was associated with symptoms of anxiety, it was not supported as a mediating pathway and the researchers suggested that anxiety can have both positive and negative effects on cognition, as it can increase attention. 43 Core features of neuroticism—such as heightened fear of social rejection and low self‐esteem—may foster chronic stress and reduce opportunities for cognitively stimulating social interactions. 72 , 78 These findings suggest that loneliness may undermine cognitive resilience by intensifying negative psychological states and reducing participation in cognitively enriching activities, both of which have been linked to CD in prior research. In contrast, studies on social isolation highlight a wider range of behavioral mechanisms, including sleep disturbance 57 and reduced physical activity. 46 With the existing evidence, it remains unclear whether these differences reflect distinct underlying mechanisms or variations in what prior studies chose to test. Future studies are needed to explore whether behavioral pathways identified for social isolation, such as sleep disturbance and physical inactivity, might also play a role in the association between loneliness and CD.

Appraisal of current evidence and future directions

The breadth of recent research on physiological and psychobehavioral mechanisms is noteworthy; such a wide range of evidence on the pathways linking loneliness, social isolation, and CD did not exist just a few years ago. This growth reflects an exciting shift in the field, but it also reveals how much more remains to be understood. For example, the temporal dynamics of mediating pathways remain unclear: what level or duration of loneliness and social isolation triggers physiological or behavioral changes, and over what timeframe might it lead to cognitive change? Another important question is whether reversing loneliness or social isolation can also reverse the associated physiological and psychobehavioral changes, ultimately leading to preserved cognitive function. Some of these questions could be addressed through randomized controlled trials aimed at reducing loneliness or social isolation. For example, one clinical trial that reduced social isolation by providing frequent, engaging conversational interactions via the Internet showed that an increased amount of social interaction led to greater dorsal attention network connectivity, using resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). 79 The study also showed that the intervention led to increased social engagement, 79 , 80 suggesting that reducing social isolation even in later life could modify physiological and psychobehavioral factors. Animal model studies can also help posit hypotheses on biological sequences. For instance, in mice model studies, social isolation was linked to HPA‐axis dysregulation that decreased BDNF and further neurodegeneration. 25 Additionally, future human studies using time‐sensitive measures of social isolation and loneliness, 81 , 82 coupled with longitudinal biomarkers and behavioral assessments, are needed to investigate the temporal dynamics of mediating pathways. Furthermore, because most pathways have been examined in only two or three studies with different methods, conducting a meta‐analysis to estimate direct and indirect effect sizes is not yet feasible nor informative. More rigorous investigations are needed to enable meta‐analysis and establish pulled effect size of indirect pathways. Building evidence on the effect size of the pathways can contribute to a clearer understanding of their magnitude of influence on cognition, which can inform intervention targets.

An interesting pattern emerged in our review: more psychological pathways (e.g., stress and affective processes) were reported for loneliness, whereas more behavioral pathways (e.g., physical activity, social engagement, and health behaviors) were identified for social isolation. This distinction may reflect fundamental differences in the constructs themselves. Loneliness is a subjective, appraisal‐based experience that can arise even in the presence of social contact and may therefore operate primarily through emotional and stress‐related mechanisms. By contrast, social isolation reflects an objective lack of social ties or interaction, which may more directly constrain opportunities for cognitively and physically stimulating activities, aligning more with behavioral pathways. At the same time, these observed patterns could reflect differences in what prior studies have chosen to measure or emphasize. Future work is needed to jointly assess loneliness and social isolation, including their concordance and discordance (often conceptualized as social asymmetry) and to test multiple mechanistic pathways within the same study. Recent research has begun to examine how mismatches between subjective and objective social connectedness relate to health outcomes, 83 highlighting the importance of explicitly modeling these distinctions to determine whether observed differences in mechanisms reflect genuine underlying processes or methodological artifacts.

Several methodological concerns in the existing literature may increase the risk of bias, including heterogeneity in how loneliness and social isolation are defined and measured, and selection bias that might be introduced with convenience sampling. For loneliness, studies varied widely in their operationalization. Some adopted categorical cut‐offs to distinguish between “no loneliness” and “severe loneliness,” while others analyzed loneliness as a continuous construct. Such differences complicate direct comparisons across studies and may partly account for inconsistent findings. Moreover, few studies examined the duration of loneliness and social isolation, even though short‐term versus persistent experiences may have distinct implications for cognitive outcomes. Longitudinal analyses that adopt a measurement burst design to capture duration and intraindividual change within days, weeks, and months are needed to address this gap. 84 Figure 2 outlines the empirically examined pathways, yet it is worth noticing that not all pathways are examined with equal quality of evidence: a few studies had a small convenience sample of less than 70 participants, 33 , 68 , 73 and the results are more likely to be influenced by sampling bias. Racial/ethnic minority and individuals with lower socioeconomic status, although are at higher risks of social isolation, loneliness, and dementia, 85 , 86 , 87 are underrepresented in the current literature. A combined research approach on how social determinants (e.g., isolation due to socioeconomic status) may interact with biological vulnerabilities are needed, as such investigations might reveal more contextualized risks for CD and identify for which populations certain mechanisms have more explanatory capability and offer insight into appropriate intervention approaches for different groups.

Potential mechanisms beyond current evidence

In addition to the mechanisms empirically examined in the literature, several other pathways may be relevant but remain untested to our knowledge. One example is gut–brain axis dysregulation, which has been proposed as a therapeutic target for dementia prevention and treatment and may relate to social isolation and loneliness. 88 , 89 Microbiomes can be shared through social contact while socially isolated individuals have fewer opportunities for such exchange. 88 , 89 Gut microbiota dysbiosis is thought to promote CD in older adults by increasing intestinal permeability, allowing pro‐inflammatory microbial products (e.g., lipopolysaccharides and bacterial amyloids) to enter systemic circulation, disrupt the blood–brain barrier, and potentially leading to increased neuroinflammation. 90 , 91 Second, dietary patterns among older adults who experience social isolation and loneliness also warrant consideration. A recent scoping review suggests that loneliness and social isolation are associated with less healthy eating behaviors, including reduced fruit and vegetable intake and overall poorer diet quality. 92 These behaviors have been linked with lower intake of micronutrients such as magnesium, potassium, vitamin B6, folate, and vitamin C, 93 which in turn have been associated with CD and dementia onset. 94 Third, mindfulness, the practice of being fully present and aware of one's current experience, also represents a potentially relevant pathway. Evidence indicates that mindfulness training and practices can reduce loneliness and increase social contact among older adults. 95 , 96 However, we did not identify empirical studies that examine mindfulness as an underlying pathway for the relationships between loneliness, social isolation, and cognitive outcomes in older adults.

Across all three proposed pathways—gut–brain axis disruption, diet, and mindfulness—the inferential chains presented rely on evidence that differs in directness, population characteristics, and methodological design at each step. These mechanisms are best understood as theoretical hypotheses intended to motivate future research. Considering these additional pathways highlights the opportunities for future work to further clarify underlying mechanisms.

Suggestion for intervention programs

Findings from this review point to several mechanistic targets that may be leveraged in non‐pharmacological interventions (Figure 2). Future programs aimed at reducing social isolation and loneliness to delay age‐related CD could investigate whether they impact physiological and psychobehavioral factors summarized here. For example, the intervention behavioral mechanisms could be evaluated by assessing sense of control, sleep quality, enhanced social participation, and physical activities. The intervention outcomes can also be physiological pathways identified (e.g., neuroimaging, blood‐based AD biomarkers, inflammatory markers, and neurotrophic factors). These trials could generate valuable evidence on the modifiability of these mechanisms and accelerate clinical translation.

Limitations of the current review

This review comprehensively synthesized evidence on the physiological and psychobehavioral mechanisms and clarified how loneliness and social isolation can be linked to CD in later life. A few limitations of the current review need to be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. All included studies were observational and therefore limited in the ability to establish causality. The inclusion criteria required loneliness or social isolation to be treated as the exposure variable, which means studies examining CD as a precipitant of social withdrawal were not captured in this review. Given the possibility that loneliness may serve as a prodromal symptom of dementia, future reviews incorporating both directions of the relationship—ideally using studies with bidirectional designs such as cross‐lagged panel models—would provide a more complete picture of this complex association. Furthermore, conducting a meta‐analysis to quantify the strength of the indirect effect of the identified mechanisms was not feasible given the lack of comparable studies that examined similar mechanisms.

To capture a wide range of relevant evidence, we did not require the physiological pathways to have a mediating effect. However, having a physiological outcome with direct dementia implications does not guarantee mediating relationships. For instance, although loneliness is linked to upregulated AD‐related gene expression and such gene expression is associated with higher likelihood of CD due to AD risks 32 , 33 —it does not mean that loneliness can lead to CD through AD‐related gene expression as a pathway. That requires the indirect effect to be significant. Although we did identify a few empirical studies that analyzed physiological pathways as a mediator (see Figure 2), future studies are needed to further examine these potential physiological pathways identified in this current review are true mechanisms. More intentional examinations of physiological biomarkers (e.g., gray matter volume and microinfarcts) as mediators for the association between social isolation, loneliness, and cognition can help provide empirical support or falsify the hypothesis. In examining the behavioral pathways which examine how loneliness is associated with CD, some of these studies used social participation in activities as the key mediating variable. 29 , 74 Although social participation was operationalized behaviorally in these two studies, we acknowledge that social participation instruments vary across the literature and can capture subjective dimensions of felt connection that overlap conceptually with loneliness; future studies examining this psychobehavioral pathway should explicitly employ frequency‐based or activity‐based measures of social participation to minimize mediator‐exposure confounding.

CONCLUSION

This review is among the first to integrate evidence on physiological and psychobehavioral pathways linking loneliness and social isolation to CD. Physiological mechanisms identified include AD pathology, inflammation, cardiovascular risks, neurotrophic factors, AD‐related gene expression, HPA‐axis functioning, oral health, and general health, though findings remain mixed and the temporal sequence of these changes is not yet established. Psychobehavioral pathways, the most examined was depressive symptoms, along with neuroticism, sense of control, sleep disturbance, and reduced engagement in social and leisure activities, mediated the associations between social isolation and loneliness with CD. Future longitudinal and biomarker‐informed studies are needed to clarify directionality and guide intervention trials to prevent age‐related abnormal CD.
