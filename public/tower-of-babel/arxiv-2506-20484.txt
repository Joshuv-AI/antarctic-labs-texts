An adversary bound for quantum signal processing
Lorenzo Laneve

Abstract
Quantum signal processing (QSP) and quantum singular value transformation (QSVT), have emerged as unifying frameworks in the context of quantum algorithm design. These techniques allow to carry out efficient polynomial transformations of matrices block-encoded in unitaries, involving a single ancilla qubit. Recent efforts try to extend QSP to the multivariate setting (M-QSP), where multiple matrices are transformed simultaneously. However, this generalization faces problems not encountered in the univariate counterpart: in particular, the class of polynomials achievable by M-QSP seems hard to characterize. In this work we borrow tools from query complexity, namely the state conversion problem and the adversary bound: we first recast QSP as a state conversion problem over the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions. We then show that the adversary bound for a | 0 ⟩ ↦ ( P , Q ) \ket{0}\mapsto(P,Q) state conversion in this space precisely identifies all and only the QSP protocols over S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) in the univariate case. Motivated by this result, we extend the formalism to M-QSP: the computation of a M-QSP protocol of minimal space is thus reduced to a rank minimization problem involving the feasible solution space of the adversary bound.

Given vectors

\displaystyle v_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus v_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\ket{k}\ket{v_k}\ .

\displaystyle v(z)=\sum_{j}P_{j}(z)\ket{j}=\sum_{k}v_{k}z^{k}\ .

The vector space

Definition 2.1

2.1
Partial state conversion

We introduce a new version of state conversion, called
partial state conversion
. In this variant, the output condition in (
3
) becomes

\displaystyle\norm{\Pi\calA(O_x) \xi_x - \tau_x}\leq\epsilon

(4)

for some orthogonal projection
\Pi
independent of
x
. In other words, we only care about the behaviour of the final state in a fixed subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}
. This is motivated by our objective, since in the context of the block-encoding framework
[
13
]
or quantum signal processing
[
17
,
18
]
, we usually care about the behaviour of
\tau
only in a portion of the space, implying that the residual can be chosen freely to optimize the complexity measures. In other words, given
\xi,\tau
satisfying
\norm{\xi_x}\geq\norm{\tau_x}
, we want an algorithm that converts
\xi_{x}
into
\tau_{x}\oplus\sigma_{x}
, for some complementary state
\sigma_{x}
satisfying
\norm{\xi_x}^{2}=\norm{\tau_x}^{2}+\norm{\sigma_x}^{2}
, and the second Hilbert space will be projected out by
\Pi
.

Notice that partial state conversion is fundamentally different from the contraction oracle setting provided in
[
58
]
, since the contraction necessarily implies a permanent loss of probability mass (dependent on the input) at each call of the oracle.

Definition 4.2
.

The
adversary bound
for
\xi(\mathbf{z})\mapsto\tau(\mathbf{z})
state conversion is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathop{\gamma_{2}}\limits^{\leftarrow}}\quantity( \langle\xi(\vv{x}), \xi(\vv{y}) \rangle- \langle\tau(\vv{x}), \tau(\vv{y}) \rangle\,\bigg|\, \id- O(\vv{x})^* O(\vv{y}) )\ .

There are some subtleties, however. First of all, the objective function takes the supremum instead of the maximum (it should take the essential supremum, but we can assume they coincide, as otherwise the catalyst would be sub-optimal). Moreover, this is not a semidefinite program anymore in the traditional sense, and thus some results from classical optimization theory might not hold. In particular,
{\mathcal{W}}
is not even guaranteed to be finite-dimensional, and this might be a problem when we want to embed
v(\mathbf{z})
in finitely many qubits. More specifically, the Gram operators
G_{\xi},G_{\tau}
have finite rank (since they are Gram operators of functions in
\mathbb{C}^{K}
). On the other hand, the Gram operators
G_{v},G_{Ov}
can be proven to be trace-class (by the fact that
v\in L^{\infty}
and an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality).

Since we are working with trace-class operators, Theorems
2.6
and
2.10
, as well as the lower bound of Theorem
2.11
carry over to this case (the Hadamard product of two integral operators is given by the product of the kernels). For the argument of Theorem
2.6
, we need the adversary operator
\Gamma
in the optimization problem for the subrelative
\gamma_{2}
-bound to be restricted to the space of self-adjoint compact operators, but this does not really change the value of the
\gamma_{2}
-bound, since the trace-class operators are dense in the space of compact operators.

In other words, while it is always true that
G_{v}-G_{Ov}
has finite rank, this does not imply that
G_{v}
does (
G_{Ov}
, however, has finite rank if and only if
G_{v}
does). The rank of
G_{v}
will give the dimension of

, so we are interested in which cases we have finite-rank solutions.

Polynomial state conversion

We now restrict ourselves to the case where

4.3
Quantum signal processing as a state conversion over
L^{2}

The reader might have noticed that the QSP ansätze defined in Section

can be seen as quantum query algorithms. In particular, the signal operator
\operatorname{diag}(z,1)
used in GQSP is simply the oracle
O(z)=z
applied to half of the Hilbert space.

At this point, QSP as introduced in Section

coincides with the polynomial state conversion
\ket{0}\mapsto\tau(z)=P(z)\ket{0}+Q(z)\ket{1}
. The only difference is that QSP puts a constraint on the dimensionality of the protocol, as opposed to polynomial state conversion, but we’ll see that this does not make any difference, i.e., any protocol for polynomial state conversion will be embeddable in

Theorem 4.5
.

Every feasible catalyst

to the adversary bound for polynomial state conversion

-GQSP protocol which constructs

Another interpretation of this statement is that there is a correspondence between the space of catalysts and the set of GQSP protocols.

Proof Sketch.

Up to a unitary transformation (which always preserves the feasibility constraint) we can write

such that

has degree

(in other words, we carry out a

(P(z),Q(z))
can be reduced to
(v^{(k)}(z),\cdot)

will satisfy

\displaystyle\bra{0}A_{k-1}\tilde{w}\cdots\tilde{w}A_{0}\ket{0}=v^{(k)}(z)\ ,

i.e.,

is the left polynomial generated by the first
k
steps of the QSP protocol.
∎

The decomposition carried out by Theorem
4.5
uses the catalyst differently compared to Algorithm
2.12

T
times, and

is left unchanged in its half of the space (hence the name

-structure.

By this argument we can infer some structure for the catalysts.

Corollary 4.6
.

If

is in the form
v(z)=v^{(0)}\oplus\cdots\oplus v^{(n-1)}
as in the argument of Theorem

, then

\absolutevalue*{v^{(k)}(z)}\leq 1

for every

, and hence
\norm{v(z)}^{2}\leq n

The corollary comes from the fact that

is a component of the state in the GQSP protocol at step

Solving the semidefinite program for

is easy, as it simply requires to solve the linear system given by (

\displaystyle\delta_{k,0}\delta_{h,0}-\langle\tau_{k},\tau_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=X_{k,h}-X_{k-1,h-1}

where

\displaystyle X_{k,h}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k-j,0}\delta_{h-j,0}-\langle\tau_{k-j},\tau_{h-j}\rangle

where the infinite sum is only a simplifying notation, as the support of

(with

).

Corollary 4.7
.

The catalyst

for univariate QSP

This result can also be obtained from the bijectivity of the non-Linear Fourier transform

The partial version of the

We are thus interested in minimal-rank solutions to the adversary bound to identify minimal-space QSP protocols. We can infer something more on this solution space.

Lemma 4.10

Suppose

are two complementary states to

\alpha\sigma_{1}\oplus\beta\sigma_{2}

Proof.

Being complementary means that

\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}\norm{\sigma_1}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\beta}^{2}\norm{\sigma_2}^{2}

In other words, by convexity of the adversary bound, if we have two feasible solutions

\tau\oplus\alpha\sigma_{1}\oplus\beta\sigma_{2}
8
8

8

For a partial state conversion

Whether this convex hull actually contains

4.5
Polynomial state conversion over multiple variables

After building a theory over polynomials of a single variable, the results show that the feasible solution space of the adversary bound perfectly coincides with the space of all the possible QSP protocols. This makes the adversary bound a good candidate for filling the gap in the theory of M-QSP

While this ‘hint’ is not necessary in the univariate case, we’ll see that it is precious for M-QSP, as the existence of a valid catalyst constitutes a sufficient condition for the existence of a M-QSP protocol. We consider the oracle

By taking the Fourier coefficients

Lemma 4.12

5
Discussion and outlook

This work uses the adversary bound — a central tool in query complexity — to show the theory of quantum signal processing (QSP) from a new perspective. This new approach — which recasts QSP as a special instance of state conversion — allows one to better understand the structure of QSP, especially in the multivariate case. In particular, we found that the catalysts given by the adversary bound can always be used to construct an optimal-space protocol in the case of univariate oracle
O(z)=z
, unlike the algorithm of
[
58
]
, whose required space always depends on the dimensionality of the catalyst. Surprisingly, the constraint (
14
) of the adversary bound for the univariate case shows a displacement structure similar to the one in
[
30
,
31
]
.

The partial state conversion formulation is convenient, as the adversary bound encodes both the problem of finding a complementary polynomial and computing the corresponding QSP protocol. Dimensionality might still be a problem in the multivariate case, confirming that univariate QSP is a very special and well-behaved case of state conversion. We thus turn our attention to the following outlook:

(i)

Finding a minimal-space M-QSP protocol implementing some desired transformation
P(\mathbf{z})
requires finding minimal-rank elements of
{\mathcal{Q}}_{P}
. While rank minimization problems are usually NP-hard, might it be possible to at least approximate minimal rank with convex heuristics
[
70
]
?

(ii)

Theoretically any
P
with
|P|<1
can be achieved by the M-QSP over multiple dimensions we presented: one can think of a linear combination of unitaries
[
2
]
boosted with sufficiently many rounds of amplitude amplification. This naive approach, however, requires many qubits, and the adversary bound approach we presented would be able to give further optimization. In particular, can such
P
admit a solution whose rank depends only on the number
m
of variables, or the dimension
d
of the full, completed polynomial vector (as in Conjecture
4.15
)?

(iii)

What happens when the degree
n
of
\tau
goes to infinity? This is the setting of infinite quantum signal processing
[
71
,
28
]
, and the adversary bound should admit feasible catalysts of infinite rank. It is not clear, however, if the infinite-rank solution achieving a finite objective function would remain unique in the univariate case, as in Corollary
4.7
.

(iv)

Las Vegas complexity satisfies exactness and thriftiness properties, allowing to having complexity-preserving compositions. In light of this work, would there be a connection between the transducer formalism of
[
59
]
and the gadgets of
[
35
]
? This would require a translation of the latter work into the analytic QSP picture;

(v)

In this work we manage to find bounds for the space used by the protocols in the case of polynomial state conversion. Can we derive space bounds also for finite-labels state conversion
[
57
,
58
]
? The shape of the oracle
O(z)
in our case exhibits a particular structure, thus suggesting that this plays an important role. But there might be a way to split and consume the catalyst as we did in this work, so that embedding the full catalyst in the registers like in
[
58
]
might be unnecessary.

(vi)

Importantly, QSP is usually lifted to quantum eigenvalue transformation of unitaries (QET-U)
[
72
]
or quantum singular value transformation (QSVT)
[
13
]
for algorithmic applications. While a lift for the former is clear in the M-QSP setting over
SU(N)
(just replace the occurrences of
z_{j}
in the oracles with controlled versions of the unitaries
U_{j}
), it is unclear how one can obtain a multivariate QSVT from it. The authors in
[
37
]
provide a M-QSVT ansatz for their M-QSP over
SU(2)
, but the single-qubit structure is important for their construction. More recent work on obtaining a QSVT ansatz from GQSP might be relevant for this direction
[
63
]
.

(vii)

The adversary bound formalism here does not seem to have fundamental limitations if we remove the assumption that the
z_{j}
’s do not commute. This
non-abelian
version of M-QSP adds rigidity to the structure of the polynomials. We suspect that the non-uniqueness of the catalysts for M-QSP follows from the fact that the polynomials
z_{1}z_{2}
and
z_{2}z_{1}
are the same (which is the gist of the counterexample in (
17
)). This leads to the belief that non-abelian M-QSP might be easier to analyze than its abelian counterpart.

Appendix A
Proofs for polynomial state conversion

We give the detailed proofs of the results mentioned in Section
4
.

A.1
Univariate case

Lemma
4.3
.

If
v(z)
is a feasible catalyst for a
\xi(z)\mapsto\tau(z)
polynomial state conversion, where both
\xi,\tau
have degree bounded by
n
, then
v(z)
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
.

Proof.

The constraint of (
12
) can be rewritten in the Fourier basis as

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{k},\xi_{h}\rangle-\langle\tau_{k},\tau_{h}\rangle=\langle v_{k},v_{h}\rangle-\langle v_{k-1},v_{h-1}\rangle

for every
k,h\in\mathbb{Z}
. Assume for a contradiction that
v_{k^{\prime}}\neq 0
for some
k^{\prime}<0
, therefore by plugging
k=h=k^{\prime}
the constraint gives us

\displaystyle 0=\langle v_{k^{\prime}},v_{k^{\prime}}\rangle-\langle v_{k^{\prime}-1},v_{k^{\prime}-1}\rangle

since
\xi,\tau
do not have negative frequencies. By induction we thus have
\norm{v_k}^{2}=\norm{v_{k'}}^{2}\neq 0
for every
k<k^{\prime}
, which implies

\displaystyle\norm{v(z)}^{2}_{\infty}\geq\norm{v(z)}^{2}_{L^{2}}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\norm{v_{-k}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\norm{v_{k'}}^{2}\ .

The sum on the right-hand side diverges, and thus
v
has infinite objective function, contradicting its feasibility. The middle inequality is a standard application of Bessel’s inequality on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},{\mathcal{M}}\otimes{\mathcal{W}})

[
73
]
. Similarly, if
v_{k^{\prime}}\neq 0
for
{k^{\prime}}\geq n
, then the constraint gives us
\norm{v_k}^{2}=\norm{v_{k'}}^{2}
for every
k>k^{\prime}
, and the same argument on the
L^{2}
norm applies.
∎

Put simply, the proof looks for a subsequence whose sum of squares diverges, in order to conclude that the full sum (which is equal to the
L^{2}
norm, by Parseval’s identity
[
73
]
) diverges as well.

We now prove Theorems
4.5
,
4.8
. We will prove the latter, as the former is a special case.

Theorem
4.8
.

Let
v(z)
be a catalyst for
\ket{0}\mapsto\tau(z)=P(z)+\sum_{k=1}^{r}Q_{j}(z)\ket{k}
total state conversion, where
P,Q_{j}
have degree
\leq n
. Then
v(z)
identifies a QSP protocol over
SU(r+1)
.

Proof.

By (
12
),
v(z)
satisfies

\displaystyle 1-\langle\tau(x),\tau(y)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v(x),v(y)\rangle-\langle xv(x),yv(y)\rangle

(18)

We proceed by induction on
n
: if
n=0
then Lemma
4.3
shows that
v=0
and thus by (
18
)
\tau(z)
is equal to
\ket{0}
up to a unitary
A_{0}\in SU(r+1)
. When
n>0
, again by Lemma
4.3
we have that
v(z)
is a polynomial of degree
n-1
with values in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and is thus determined by its
n
coefficient vectors
v_{0},\ldots,v_{n-1}
. We carry out a
QL
-decomposition

\displaystyle V=\begin{bmatrix}v_{0}&v_{1}&\cdots&v_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}=Q\begin{bmatrix}v_{0,0}&&\\
*&v_{1,1}&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
*&*&\cdots&v_{n-1,n-1}\end{bmatrix}

(19)

and, by unitarity of
Q
, also
Q^{\dagger}v(z)
is a feasible catalyst. We can thus assume without loss of generality that
v
has this form, with
v_{k,k}\geq 0
. The
k
-th row of the lower triangular matrix above forms a scalar polynomial of degree
\leq k
, call it
v^{(k)}(z)
. Since
v=v^{(0)}\oplus\cdots\oplus v^{(n-1)}
, the constraint of (
18
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\tau(x),\tau(y)\rangle-\langle xv^{(n-1)}(x),yv^{(n-1)}(y)\rangle

(20)

\displaystyle+\langle v^{(n-1)}(x),v^{(n-1)}(y)\rangle-\langle xv^{(n-2)}(x),yv^{(n-2)}(y)\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle v^{(n-2)}(x),v^{(n-2)}(y)\rangle-\langle xv^{(n-3)}(x),yv^{(n-3)}(y)\rangle

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \vdots

\displaystyle+\langle v^{(1)}(x),v^{(1)}(y)\rangle-\langle xv^{(0)}(x),yv^{(0)}(y)\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle v^{(0)}(x),v^{(0)}(y)\rangle-1

By
\langle\tau(z),\tau(z)\rangle=1
, which must hold as a polynomial equation, we get
\langle\tau_{0},\tau_{n}\rangle=0
from the
n
-th degree term. Thus, up to a unitary transformation
A\in SU(r+1)
, we can split
A^{\dagger}\tau(z)=\tau_{+}(z)\oplus\tau_{-}(z)
, where
\tau_{+}(z)
has only degrees in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
\tau_{-}(z)
is supported on
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
. In particular, we can choose this split such that
\tau_{+}
has dimension
1
, as only one component — the one mapped from
\tau_{n}
— has a degree-
n
term.

We claim that
\langle\tau_{+}(x),\tau_{+}(y)\rangle=\langle xv^{(n-1)}(x),yv^{(n-1)}(y)\rangle
. This would complete the proof by induction because by (
20
) the remainder
v^{\prime}=v^{(0)}\oplus\cdots\oplus v^{(n-2)}
would be a catalyst for the state conversion
\ket{0}\mapsto v^{(n-1)}\oplus\tau_{-}
, a
(n-1)
-degree normalized polynomial.

By (
20
) we have that the two polynomial vectors

\displaystyle a(z)

\displaystyle:=\tau_{+}(z)\ket{0}_{A}\ket{0}_{B}+\tau_{-}(z)\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\ket{1}_{B}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}v^{(k)}(z)\ket{1}_{A}\ket{k}_{B}

\displaystyle b(z)

\displaystyle:=zv^{(n-1)}(z)\ket{0}_{A}\ket{0}_{B}+\ket{0}_{A}\ket{1}_{B}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}zv^{(k-1)}(z)\ket{1}_{A}\ket{k}_{B}

are equal up to a unitary transformation
U
. No polynomial, except for
\tau_{+},zv^{(n-1)}
, has a
n
-degree term, hence we can extract the Fourier coefficients

\displaystyle\tau_{+,n}\ket{0}_{A}\ket{0}_{B}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}a(e^{i\theta})e^{-in\theta}d\theta=U\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}b(e^{i\theta})e^{-in\theta}d\theta=U\quantity( v_{n-1,n-1} \ket{0}_A \ket{0}_B )\ .

This implies that
\ket{0}_{A}\ket{0}_{B}
is an eigenstate of
U
, and that
|v_{n-1,n-1}|=|\tau_{+,n}|\neq 0
by unitarity. Let
e^{i\phi}=\tau_{+,n}/v_{n-1,n-1}
be the associated eigenvalue. We can deduce that

\displaystyle\tau_{+}(z)=\bra{0}_{A}\bra{0}_{B}\ket{a(z)}=\bra{0}_{A}\bra{0}_{B}U\ket{b(z)}=e^{-i\phi}\bra{0}_{A}\bra{0}_{B}\ket{b(z)}=e^{-i\phi}zv^{(n-1)}(z)

which implies
\langle\tau_{+}(x),\tau_{+}(y)\rangle=\langle xv^{(n-1)}(x),yv^{(n-1)}(y)\rangle
as claimed.
∎

We remark once again that this can be seen as a layer stripping argument, as we are choosing
A_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\tau\overset{A^{\dagger}}{\mapsto}\tau_{+}\oplus\tau_{-}=e^{-i\phi}zv^{(n-1)}\oplus\tau_{-}\mapsto zv^{(n-1)}\oplus\tau_{-}\overset{\tilde{w}^{\dagger}}{\mapsto}v^{(n-1)}\oplus\tau_{-}

where the last processing operator
A_{n}^{\dagger}
is the composition of the first two transformations. From this proof we can deduce something on the structure of the catalysts
v
for this specific case of state conversion.

Corollary A.1
.

If
v(z)
is in the form of (
19
) with
v(z)=v^{(0)}\oplus\cdots\oplus v^{(n-1)}
, then
v_{k,k}>0
for every
k
. In other words,
v^{(k)}
has exactly degree
k
for every
k
.

Choosing a
QL
-decomposition with positive diagonal (which is unique) is equivalent to the constraint
a^{*}(0)>0
that guarantees uniqueness in the literature for the non-linear Fourier transform
[
74
,
69
,
28
]
.

A.2
Multivariate case

Lemma
4.12
.

Suppose the oracle is
O(\mathbf{z})=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{z})
and that
\xi(\mathbf{z}),\tau(\mathbf{z})
have degree
\leq n_{j}
in
z_{j}
for every
j
. Then any feasible solution
v(\mathbf{z})
to the total polynomial state conversion
\xi\mapsto\tau
must be a polynomial of degree
\leq n_{j}-1
in each variable
z_{j}
.

\mathbf{k}
\mathbf{k}+{\mathcal{L}}_{3}
{\mathcal{S}}

Figure 2
:
Visualization of the argument in two variables. The set
\mathbf{k}+{\mathcal{L}}_{3}
in red. The region on the top-right of the dashed red lines is the one constituting the divergent subsequence.

Proof.

The idea is to look for a divergent sum as in Theorem
4.3
. We claim that the hyper-rectangle

{\mathcal{S}}=\{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}:0\leq h_{j}<n_{j}\}

contains the support of
v
. For this purpose, suppose for a contradiction that
v_{\mathbf{k}}\neq 0
for some
\mathbf{k}\not\in{\mathcal{S}}
. For
s\geq 0
, we define the subset

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{s}

\displaystyle=\quantity{ \vv{h} \in\Z^m_{\ge 0} : \sum_j h_j = s }

One of the following must hold:

(i)

(\mathbf{k}+{\mathcal{L}}_{s})\cap{\mathcal{S}}=\emptyset
for every
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
or

(ii)

(\mathbf{k}-{\mathcal{L}}_{s})\cap{\mathcal{S}}=\emptyset
for every
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

Suppose for a contradiction that both claims are false: this implies that
\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{d}_{1},\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{d}_{2}\in{\mathcal{S}}
for some
\mathbf{d}_{1},\mathbf{d}_{2}\geq 0
. These two points identify a hyper-rectangle contained in
{\mathcal{S}}
, and since we have that, for every
j

\displaystyle 0\leq(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{d}_{2})_{j}\leq k_{j}\leq(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{d}_{1})_{j}<n_{j}

we would conclude
\mathbf{k}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, a contradiction (see Figure
2
for a visualization on the case
m=2
). If (i) holds then, for any
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbf{k}+{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
, (
16
) becomes

\displaystyle\langle v_{\mathbf{h}},v_{\mathbf{h}}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\langle v_{\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{e}_{j}},\Pi_{j}v_{\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{e}_{j}}\rangle\ .

By summing over all
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbf{k}+{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbf{k}+{\mathcal{L}}_{1}}\langle v_{\mathbf{h}},v_{\mathbf{h}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbf{k}+{\mathcal{L}}_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\langle v_{\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{e}_{j}},\Pi_{j}v_{\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{e}_{j}}\rangle\geq\langle v_{\mathbf{k}},v_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle

where the inequality comes from the fact that the sum in the middle contains all the pieces of
v_{\mathbf{k}}
. One can replace
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
with
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
, by an induction argument on
s
(more precisely, the sum over
\mathbf{k}+{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
contains all the pieces of the elements in
\mathbf{k}+{\mathcal{L}}_{s-1}
). Since
v_{\mathbf{k}}\neq 0
, the non-decreasing sequence

a_{s}:=\sum_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbf{k}+{\mathcal{L}}_{s}}\langle v_{\mathbf{h}},v_{\mathbf{h}}\rangle

does not converge to
0
, contradicting the fact that
v\in L^{2}
. Analogously if (ii) holds, on the other hand,

\displaystyle\langle v_{\mathbf{k}},v_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\langle v_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{e}_{j}},\Pi_{j}v_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{e}_{j}}\rangle\leq\sum_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbf{k}-{\mathcal{L}}_{1}}\langle v_{\mathbf{h}},v_{\mathbf{h}}\rangle\ .

By an induction on
s
, we obtain that the sequence

b_{s}:=\sum_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbf{k}-{\mathcal{L}}_{s}}\langle v_{\mathbf{h}},v_{\mathbf{h}}\rangle

does not converge to
0
, contradicting once again
v\in L^{2}
.
∎

Theorem
4.13
.

Consider the total state conversion

\ket{0}\mapsto\tau(\mathbf{z})=P(\mathbf{z})\ket{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{r}Q_{k}(\mathbf{z})\ket{k}\ .

where
P,Q_{k}
have degree
\leq n
. Any feasible solution
v
to the corresponding adversary bound can be turned into a M-QSP protocol of length
n
for
\tau(\mathbf{z})
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
, the case
n=0
being trivial. For
n>0
, let
\tau(\mathbf{z})=P(\mathbf{z})\oplus\sigma(\mathbf{z})
be the completion of
P(\mathbf{z})
according to the feasible solution. By choosing an appropriate unitary
A
, we can express
\tau
as a direct sum

A^{\dagger}\tau(\mathbf{z})=\tau_{+}(\mathbf{z})\oplus\tau_{-}(\mathbf{z})

where
\tau_{+}
is a component of degree
n
and
\tau_{-}
is a component of degree
\leq n-1
. Intuitively,
\tau_{-}
is the component multiplied by
1
by the inverse signal operator, just like in Theorem
4.5
.

We conclude the proof by showing that

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{+}(\mathbf{x}),\tau_{+}(\mathbf{y})\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\langle x_{j}\Pi_{j}v^{(n-1)}(\mathbf{x}),y_{j}\Pi_{j}v^{(n-1)}(\mathbf{y})\rangle=\langle Ov^{(n-1)}(\mathbf{x}),Ov^{(n-1)}(\mathbf{y})\rangle\ .

(21)

This would imply that there exists a unitary mapping
T
satisfying

\displaystyle TOv^{(n-1)}=T\sum_{j}z_{j}\Pi_{j}v^{(n-1)}(\mathbf{z})=\tau_{+}(\mathbf{z})\ .

Hence, the processing operator
A_{n}=A(T\oplus\mathbbm{1})
would then carry out the transformation

\displaystyle v^{(n-1)}\oplus\tau_{-}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle O}}{{\mapsto}}Ov^{(n-1)}\oplus\tau_{-}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\mapsto}}\tau_{+}\oplus\tau_{-}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\mapsto}}\tau

thus allowing us to reduce ourselves to the
(n-1)
-degree state conversion
\ket{0}\mapsto v^{(n-1)}\oplus\tau_{-}
, for which the remainder
v^{(0)}\oplus\cdots\oplus v^{(n-2)}
is a valid catalyst.

Similarly to Theorem
4.5
, we know by (
12
) that the Gram matrices of
\ket{0}\oplus Ov(\mathbf{z})
and
\tau_{+}(\mathbf{z})\oplus\tau_{-}(\mathbf{z})\oplus v(\mathbf{z})
are equal. Thus, by rearranging the terms, the two vectors

\displaystyle a(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle:=\tau_{+}(\mathbf{z})\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\ket{0}_{B}+\tau_{-}(\mathbf{z})\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\ket{1}_{B}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}v^{(k)}(\mathbf{z})\otimes\ket{1}_{A}\ket{k}_{B}

\displaystyle b(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle:=Ov^{(n-1)}(\mathbf{z})\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\ket{0}_{B}+\ket{0}_{A}\ket{1}_{B}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}Ov^{(k-1)}(\mathbf{z})\otimes\ket{1}_{A}\ket{k}_{B}\ .

are equal up to a unitary transformation
U
. The only difference with Theorem
4.5
is that
\tau_{+},\tau_{-},v^{(k)}
are not necessarily scalars. We introduce the following notation to extract degrees from a polynomial vector:

\displaystyle[p(\mathbf{z})]_{\mathbf{k}}:=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{m}}p(e^{i\theta})e^{-i\mathbf{k}\theta}d\theta\ .

The only terms of degree
n
are
\tau_{+},Ov^{(n-1)}
, hence for any
n
-degree term
\mathbf{z}^{\mathbf{k}}
, the following holds

\displaystyle[\tau_{+}]_{\mathbf{k}}\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\ket{0}_{B}=U[Ov^{(n-1)}]_{\mathbf{k}}\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\ket{0}_{B}\ .

Which means that
U
keeps
\ket{0}_{A}\otimes\ket{0}_{B}
fixed. In other words,
U
can be represented as

\displaystyle U=\begin{bmatrix}T&0\\
0&\cdot\end{bmatrix}

where the first row/column represents the subspace spanned by
\ket{0}_{A}\otimes\ket{0}_{B}
. By unitarity of
U
, also
T
must be unitary. This shows that
\tau_{+}=TOv^{(n-1)}
as claimed.
∎
