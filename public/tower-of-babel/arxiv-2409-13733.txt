RNR: Teaching Large Language Models to Follow Roles and Rules
Kuan Wang, Alexander Bukharin, Haoming Jiang, Qingyu Yin, Zhengyang Wang, Tuo Zhao, Jingbo Shang, Chao Zhang, Bing Yin, Xian Li, Jianshu Chen, Shiyang Li

Abstract
Instruction fine-tuning (IFT) elicits instruction following capabilities and steers the behavior of large language models (LLMs) via supervised learning. However, existing models trained on open-source IFT datasets only have the ability to follow instructions from users, and often fail to follow complex role and rules specified by developers, a.k.a. system prompts. The ability to follow these roles and rules is essential for deployment, as it ensures that the model safely interacts with users within developer defined guidelines. To improve such role and rule following ability, we propose RoleNRules, an automated data generation pipeline that generates diverse roles and rules from existing IFT instructions, along with corresponding responses. This data can then be used to train models that follow complex system prompts. The models are evaluated on our newly created benchmarks for role and rule following ability, as well as standard instruction-following benchmarks and general NLP tasks. Our pipeline significantly improves role and rule following capability in LLMs, as evidenced by over 25% increase in pass-rate on rule adherence, i.e. following all requirements, in our experiments with the Alpaca and Ultrachat datasets. Moreover, our models achieves this increase without any regression on popular instruction following benchmarks.

In contrast, closed-source models offer the ability to effectively adapt to various role and rule requirements. Therefore, there is a clear need for developing open-source IFT methods that support adaptable system prompts to enable developers’ fine-grained model controls.

To bridge this gap, we propose RoleNRules (RNR), a scalable and automated data generation pipeline designed to enhance the ability of LLMs to follow developer instructions specified in the system prompt. More specifically, we use powerful language models to generate diverse and high quality (system-prompt, instruction, response) triplets from existing open-source IFT datasets. These triplets are then used for training the model, resulting in superior adaptability to system prompts. As demonstrated in Figure

1
, the model trained with RNR can follow all the requirements defined inside the system prompts.

To generate diverse and high-quality (system prompt, instruction, response) triplets, the most challenging part is to create diverse and high-quality system prompts to avoid potential over-fitting of model training and hence improve model’s adaptability to new roles and rules. A system prompt often has two parts:
1)
Role
– which explicitly outlines the identity or persona that the LLM agent should assume during the interaction, e.g. “
You are a helpful coding assistant.
”.
2)
Rules
– which dictate the requirements, such as operational guidelines (e.g. “
Add brief comments after each line of code
”) and objectives, which follow the role description. To generate diverse and high-quality system prompts, we prompt a powerful LLM to generate a novel role with a set of rules for each instruction in an open source IFT dataset. After that, we use the generated system prompt and original instruction to generate a response that follow the instruction while satisfying the system prompt. Through these steps, RNR generates a diverse dataset demonstrating role and rule following abilities, which can then be used to train system prompt following LLMs.

In addition to the RNR data generation pipeline, we also provide a benchmark specifically designed to assess the ability of LLMs to adhere to system prompts. This benchmark provides a set of challenging (system-prompt, instruction) pairs written by both humans and LLMs, as well as a technique to evaluate system-prompt following ability using LLM-based judges.

To validate the effectiveness of our RNR, we conduct extensive experiments using the Alpaca

and Ultrachat

Ding et al. 2023

We find that RNR significantly boosts system-prompt following ability, improving the pass-rate by over 25% compared to conventional instruction fine-tuning. In addition, models trained with RNR maintain the ability to follow complex instructions even when there is no specific system prompt, validating RNR’s ability to train high performing and adaptive LLMs.

1.

2
Method

We propose RoleNRules, a data generation pipeline for the automatic generation of RNR system prompts and the corresponding responses for IFT datasets. As illustrated in Figure
3
, the RoleNRules pipeline augments an existing open-source IFT dataset into our RNR dataset with complex system-prompt data. Below, we describe the main processes in our pipeline.

2.1
RoleNRules System Prompt

Our method for improving role and rules following generates diverse and high quality system prompts from existing instruction tuning datasets. These system prompts are designed as a combination of a role description and a set of defined rules:

1.

Role
:
The role description explicitly specifies the identity or persona that the LLM should adopt during the interaction. For example, this role could be a technical assistant, customer service representative, data analyst, or any other persona with specific characteristics and knowledge.
By defining the role, we guide the model to adopt a particular set of behaviors and language styles appropriate for that persona.

2.

Rules
:
Accompanying the role description are specific rules that set the boundaries and objectives of the interaction.
These rules encompass operational guidelines, ethical considerations, data handling procedures, and specific goals to be achieved. For example, a rule might mandate prioritizing data privacy or adhering to industry-specific regulations.

By integrating these two elements, we can simulate a wide variety of possible developer requirements.
In particular, the role descriptions make the LLM’s response style customizable, while the rules ensure that the model operates within specific requirements. In addition, this approach enhances the LLM’s ability to understand and execute complex and specialized tasks. And it also provides a pipeline for developers to tailor the model’s responses for various professional scenarios.

2.2
System Prompt Generation

To generate unique system prompts comprised of different role and rules, we prompt powerful LLMs to generate role and rules based on an instruction from existing open-source datasets. We generate role and rules based on existing IFT datasets as existing IFT datasets contain instructions from a diverse range of subjects and tasks, which will in turn make our generated role and rules diverse.

To generate a role and rules based system prompt from an existing instruction, we sample a random variable
K
from
0-8
and ask Claude-2 to generate a role and
K
rules based on a set of guidelines. This can be seen in Figure
3
. These guidelines ensure that the role and rules are reasonable for a LLM to follow and that the role and rules are not overly specific. In addition, we provide three expert-generated demonstrations to help the LLM generate new roles and rules. Our complete prompt can be found in Appendix
G
. In our experiments we use Claude-2 to generate the roles and rules, but in practice other powerful LLMs can be used.

2.3
Response Generation

Although existing IFT datasets contain responses to the instructions, likely, the instructions do not follow the generated role and rules. Therefore, once we generate a system prompt for a given instruction from the IFT dataset, we combine it with the instruction to form a new prompt for response generation. This is demonstrated in Figure
2
.
Notably, we only use the instructions from the IFT dataset without incorporating the original responses.
This approach prevents overfitting to the original responses and preserves diversity in the generated requirements.

2.4
Fine-tuning

Once we have generated a dataset of (system prompt, instruction, response) triplets, we finetune a pre-trained LLM to maximize the probability of the response given the combined system prompt and instruction. To combine the system prompt and instruction, we follow the same template as in Figure
2
. This combined system prompt is used as the input for supervised fine tuning, where the target output is the response. Figure
3
is an overview of the RNR. In addition, we display examples of our training data in Appendix
F
.

Training Setup.
We present our main results on the Alpaca dataset
(
Taori et al. 2023b
)
and the Ultrachat dataset
(
Ding et al. 2023
)
. For Alpaca, we generate (system prompt, instruction, response) triplets on the whole dataset and for Ultrachat, we sample a subset of size 50K to generate the triplets. For our main experiments, we use Llama 2 7B
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
as our base model and fine-tune each model for two epochs with a batch size of 128 using a context length of 2048 and a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
.

Baselines.
To investigate the efficacy of RNR, we compare it with two IFT baselines. For both of these baselines we generate responses to the original training instructions with Claude-2, so that there is no discrepancy in the teacher model used for training. Once we generate these responses, we train models using (a) a fixed system prompt (“You are a helpful, harmless, and honest AI assistant”) and (b) with no system prompt. These baselines are denoted as IFT: fixed-system and IFT: no-system, respectively.

3.2
Benchmarks

We design three benchmarks using the RNR format (system prompt + instruction) to evaluate the effectiveness of RoleNRules:

1.

RNR-Expert benchmark
: This is a small benchmark comprised of 50 data points with RNR system prompts and instructions handcrafted by domain experts, who are LLM researchers and are familiar with prompting techniques. The roles and rules in these system prompts cover diverse domains including programming, engineering, medicine, law, finance, movies, book recommendations, travel advice, and cooking recipes, etc. We remark that this dataset contains challenging and high quality examples.

2.

RNR-Awesome benchmark
: This is benchmark has 495 data points based on the instruction collected by awesome-chatgpt-prompts
(
Akın 2023
)
. For each instruction in awesome-chatgpt-prompts, we randomly select 6 samples from the RNR-Expert benchmark as the few-shot prompt, then leverage GPT-4 to generate the RoleNRules system prompt for this instruction. Given this system-prompt, we also use GPT-4 to generate 2 more possible instructions, so the amount of data points in RNR/Awesome is 3 times of the number of prompts in the awesome-chatgpt-prompts dataset.

3.

RNR-AlpacaFarm benchmark
: This benchmark contain 805 datapoints, and the instructions are kept the same as the original AlpacaFarm
(
Dubois et al. 2023
)
. We also use 6 random samples from the RNR-Expert benchmark as the few-show prompt and leverage GPT-4 to generate the RoleNRules system prompt for each instruction in AlpacaFarm.

Evaluation.
To evaluate the system-prompt following ability of each model, we use large language models to calculate the number of rules and roles that are satisfied for each prompt. In particular, we develop a novel chain-of-thought
(
Wei et al. 2022b
)
like prompt for evaluating RoleNRules following ability. More concretely, we ask the judge LLM to examine each role and rule requirement individually, and then aggregate these score into a final judgement. In addition, we include some few-shot examples to further boost performance. The exact prompt can be found in Appendix
H
. In our human study, we find that this evaluation strategy has a high level of agreements with an ensemble of human experts.

In our experiments, we use Claude-2.1 to calculate the percentage of rules in the system prompt that each model passes over each RNR benchmark dataset. We remark that we use Claude-2.1 as the evaluator instead of Claude-2 to mitigate the effect of LLM judges favoring their own output. We calculate the rule-level pass percentage (defined as the percent of rules each model passes) and the prompt-level pass percentage (defined as the percent of prompts where all rules are satisfied).

Beyond measuring system prompt following ability, we also measure the ability of each model to follow user-inputted rules with IFEval
(
Zhou et al. 2023b
)
, role-playing ability with RoleBench
(
Wang et al. 2023c
)
, instruction following ability with Alpacafarm
(
Dubois et al. 2023
)
, and reasoning ability with benchmarks such as MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al. 2020
)
, BBH
(
Suzgun et al. 2022
)
, and DROP
(
Dua et al. 2019
)
. For IFEval, we can programmatically evaluate the percent of rules each model passes. For RoleBench and Alpacafarm we compare outputs from the RNR model to outputs from the baselines to evaluate which model better follows the defined role or better follows the instruction. To judge each pair of responses, we use Claude-2.1 to output its preferred response in a similar manner to
(
Dubois et al. 2023
)
. In order to mitigate the positional bias of the judge LLM, we show it each comparison in both possible orders (with RNR first and with RNR second). We only evaluate our Alpaca based models on RoleBench and Alpacafarm to reduce evaluation costs.

Human Evaluation

To validate the efficacy of RNR, we conducted a human evaluation experiment in addition to using public LLM APIs for response evaluation. In this experiment, expert human researchers evaluated the prompt-level pass rate of both the RNR model and the IFT: no-system model using an expert-curated set from the RNR benchmark. 4
Conclusion

In conclusion, our study presents a new approach to instruction tuning for Large Language Models (LLMs), focusing on both user-centric instructions and developer-defined roles and rules.
Our novel system prompt structure and automated data generation pipeline contribute to significant improvements in rule-following capabilities in LLMs, as evidenced by enhanced performance on our benchmarks using the Alpaca and Ultrachat datasets. Despite these advancements, our work has limitations, including the absence of a quality-based filtering mechanism in our data generation and the framework’s restriction to single-turn interactions. Future efforts will focus on addressing these issues, aiming to refine the model’s effectiveness in more complex, multi-turn conversation settings.

Analysis

Figure 3:
To construct (system prompt, instruction, response) triplets, RNR utilizes existing open-source IFT datasets and a powerful LLM, such as Claude2. First, we crafted a comprehensive guideline, accompanied by a few-shot demonstration. These serve as prompts for the LLM, guiding it to generate informative system prompts for the instructions in the given IFT dataset. Subsequently, we merge the generated system prompts with their corresponding instructions and feed them into the LLM to obtain the responses. Finally, we assemble these components into (system prompt, instruction, response) triplets to form the final dataset.

Human Evaluation.

In addition to using public LLM APIs to evaluate the responses generated by RNR models and baselines, we also design a human evaluation experiment to validate the efficacy of RNR. For this experiment, we use the expert-curated set of the RNR benchmark, and ask the expert human researchers to evaluate the prompt-level pass rate of the RNR model and the IFT: no-system model.

We have 3 participants for human evaluation, and each of them is provided with a system-prompt, instruction, and set of response pairs. These response pairs consist of an output from our model and a baseline model, without revealing the source of each response. We randomize the order to minimize the effect of evaluator bias. The evaluators are tasked with analyzing the responses based on two criteria (1) whether the responses are in the style of the roles and (2) whether the responses follow the rules in the system prompt. If both of these criteria are met, the response is given a passing score, otherwise it is assigned a failing score. We also tell the evaluators to ignore the minor factual or mathematical errors in the response, as we focus on the system-prompt following ability of RNR.

Varying the Amount of Requirements.
We investigate how the performance of RNR changes with varying amounts of requirements in Figure
6
. The evaluation dataset used here is RNR-Awesome, and we generated an additional 100 testing examples that contain from 10-15 examples. We observe that the performance of both RNR and conventional IFT drops as we use more requirements, even though RNR outperforms IFT in all settings. Interestingly, we find that there is a larger drop in performance when the number of requirements increases from 0-8 to more than 10. This is likely due to the fact that RNR was only trained on system prompts with 0-8 instructions, and therefore may fail to generalize well when we have a large amount of instructions. Nonetheless, we find that RNR still significantly outperforms IFT in the setting with many requirements, confirming its efficacy.

I am going to give you an instruction and your task is to generate an AI assistant role description followed with requirements for the role according to the guidelines in
<guideline>
xml tags:

<guideline>

(1) Make sure that a text language model without external tools, e.g. Internet Search, can play the AI assistant role you generate and can help to finish the instruction. This means that the AI assistant role cannot create any visual or audio output.

(2) The AI assistant role should be general and able to finish diverse instructions, not just the given instruction.

(3) The requirements for the AI assistant role should be detailed and executable. Remember that a text language model without external tools, e.g. Internet Search, can play the AI assistant role you generate. Hence, do not include not executable reqirements, e.g. finding the most up-to-date information.

(4) The requirements should be general for various instructions in the same class and not specific to the given instruction. Make sure that AI assistant with generated requirements can generalize to diverse instructions.

(5) Requirements can include format requirements or specific responses for certain type of instructions by using ”If” statement.

(6) Some requirements are more important than the others. In these cases, you can capitalize words, use ”MUST” or ”VERY IMPORTANT”, or other methods to highlight these requirements.

(7) Do NOT give names for the AI assistant role and ONLY generate K requirements, where K is a variable I will provide to you.

(8) If K is 0, describe the role and then put
\n

</output>

(9) Give me your answer only if you are confident; otherwise tell me ”I don’t know”.

</guideline>
