Drug-coated balloon treatment of in-stent restenosis in complex severe cardiac allograft vasculopathy
Authors: Tolulope Popoola, Madhan Shanmugasundaram, Elizabeth Juneman, Deepak Acharya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV) is a leading cause of late graft failure with limited revascularization options and high rates of in-stent restenosis (ISR). This report details a 33-year-old heart transplant recipient with progressive CAV that underwent multiple prior revascularizations, including drug-eluting stents and coronary artery bypass grafting. He developed ISR of the right coronary artery stent, treated with paclitaxel drug-coated balloon (DCB) without further stenting. Follow-up at 3 and 9 months showed patent vessels without recurrent restenosis and preserved graft function. DCB may be a viable option for ISR in advanced CAV when additional stenting is undesirable. Larger studies are needed to define its long-term role.

History of presentation

A 33-year-old man with a history of orthotopic heart transplantation complicated by severe cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV) presented for coronary angiography for post-cardiac transplant surveillance. On presentation, vital signs were stable and physical examination was unremarkable.

Past medical history

The patient underwent orthotopic heart transplantation at age 12 for dilated cardiomyopathy. His post-transplant course was complicated by atrial flutter status post ablation, sinus arrest requiring pacemaker, and a history of Mallory-Weiss tear. He had 2 episodes of cellular rejection treated with pulse dose steroids in the first 5 years after transplantation, and 1 episode of antibody-mediated rejection (pAMR1i+) treated with pulse steroids 14 years after transplant.

Management

Coronary angiography at age 20 (8 years post-transplant) demonstrated no angiographic stenosis but 1 to 1.5 mm intimal hyperplasia by intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) in the left main and left anterior descending. (LAD) arteries. After CAV diagnosis, his immunosuppression was changed from cyclosporine and azathioprine to cyclosporine and sirolimus. He remained on aspirin and statin. At age 24 (12 years post-transplant), during routine invasive surveillance, he was found to have progression of CAV with 70% mid right coronary artery (RCA) stenosis, which was stented with a 2.75 × 26 mm Resolute Integrity (zotarolimus) drug-eluting stent. At age 26, he had 1 episode of pAMR1i+ rejection treated with pulse steroids. At age 29, coronary angiography revealed patent RCA stent with mild late lumen loss without significant stenosis (Figure 1) but overall progression to International Society of Heart and Lung Transplant (ISHLT) CAV grade 3 with high-grade left main and mid-LAD bifurcation stenosis.1 He was not a candidate for re-transplantation at the time due to social reasons. Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) with left internal mammary artery-LAD and saphenous vein graft-ramus was performed with an uneventful recovery. The RCA was not grafted due to lack of important stenosis. Eighteen months later, he was found to have patent grafts but new 90% distal RCA stenosis, for which he underwent IVUS-guided percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) with a 2.5 × 26 mm Orsiro (sirolimus) stent. (Figure 2) Two years later, angiography revealed 80% stenosis in the mid RCA stent. IVUS revealed mild stent under expansion, with neo-atherosclerosis. After a discussion with his transplant cardiologist, he underwent intervention with a 3.0 x 15 mm Paclitaxel drug-coated balloon (DCB) without further stenting. (Figure 3). Surveillance angiography at 3 and 9 months post-DCB revealed patent RCA stents without additional angiographic late luminal loss, and patent left-sided bypass grafts. Echocardiogram showed left ventricular ejection fraction 68%. The antiplatelet regimen was aspirin and clopidogrel, and he was maintained on everolimus and mycophenolate mofetil for immunosuppression. The patient remains asymptomatic with good exercise tolerance at 13 months post-DCB, with plans for repeat angiogram in 3 months.

Discussion

CAV remains a major long-term complication following heart transplantation. The prevalence of CAV is approximately 30% at 5 years and 50% at 10 years post transplantation.2, 3 CAV is a major cause of late graft failure and death, with an estimated 10% mortality within 12 months of diagnosis.3

CAV pathophysiology can be distinct from atherosclerotic coronary artery disease (CAD) in non-transplant patients, with contribution from immune-related mechanisms, including T cell infiltration of vessel wall. Late CAV is also related to traditional CAD risk factors, including diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, steroid use, and renal dysfunction. These comorbidities result in diffuse, progressive luminal narrowing characterized by concentric intimal hyperplasia.4, 5, 6

CAV management is challenging. Lipid-lowering agents, antiplatelet therapies, and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors have modest efficacy.7 Coronary artery bypass grafting is often problematic due to high early mortality of over 30%, and often poor targets due to diffuse and distal disease.8 Re-transplantation is feasible, but is limited due to organ shortage, and is associated with inferior outcomes compared to primary transplantation.9

Given the limitations of these therapies, percutaneous coronary intervention has had a significant role in management of CAV. Balloon angioplasty was utilized for CAV soon after its inception.10 Early studies of coronary stenting for discrete CAV lesions showed high procedural success with low rates of complications.11 However, reports of in-stent-restenosis (ISR) soon followed.12 ISR rates for CAV PCI are higher than for standard PCI, with approximately 10% to 20% ISR reported for large vessels and up to 39% for smaller (<2.5 mm) vessels with modern drug-eluting stents.13 Biodegradable polymer stents may have lower ISR rates than durable polymer stents in CAV.13

Drug-coated balloon (DCB) has emerged as a therapy for small-vessel denovo lesions and ISR in the non-heart transplant population with robust clinical trial outcomes data and a Class I recommendation for DES ISR treatment in the European Society of Cardiology guidelines.14, 15 DCBs may be a good option for CAV given small caliber vessels, diffuse disease, high rates of ISR with stenting, and need for repeated angiograms. However, very scant evidence informs outcomes of DCB for native CAV or CAV ISR. A case report of an 11-year-old boy undergoing DCB of a proximal LAD lesion showed no restenosis at 7 months post intervention.4 Another case reported DCB of a mid-LAD denovo lesion with patent vessel 24 months later.16 In a case series, 2 patients underwent DCB for branch CAV with good results. One patient who had a Xience stent placed in the LAD 4 years post transplantation developed severe ISR 2 years later, which was treated with DCB, with patent vessel and 0.12 mm lumen loss at 6-month follow-up.17 We opted for DCB in our patient given his clinical trajectory of aggressive CAV despite stenting and CABG, and the risk of occluding a large, jailed RV marginal branch with further stenting and observed good results up to 9 months. Long-term durability is not known.

The role of DCB in CAV is evolving and yet to be clearly defined in the absence of large studies, The approval by the Food and Drug Administration of the Paclitaxel-Coated balloon catheter (AGENT) in 2024 may result in greater clinical utilization, thus making real-world DCB experience available for analysis in the United States. Although the current approval in US is for ISR lesions, patients with small vessel disease, in-stent restenosis, high bleeding risk, and those who have technical limitations to stenting, such as tortuosity or higher risk of stent fractures, may also be appropriate candidates for DCB. Prospective randomized studies are necessary to better understand short and long-term outcomes of DES vs DCB in this population.

Conclusion

DCBs may represent a viable therapeutic option for in-stent restenosis in advanced CAV, particularly when additional stent implantation is undesirable. Further investigation is warranted to better define the role of DCBs in this high-risk population both for ISR and denovo lesions.
