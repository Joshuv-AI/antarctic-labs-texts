The Association Between Self-Care Agency and Readiness for Hospital Discharge in Postoperative Patients with Diabetic Foot: The Chain Mediating Roles of Perceived Social Support and Quality of Discharge Teaching
Authors: Yu Zhang, Qian Peng, Rujun Liu, Cheng Peng, Pengfei Liang, Jingru Li, Yanxu Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to explore the chain mediating role of perceived social support and quality of discharge teaching in the association between self-care agency and readiness for hospital discharge.MethodsConvenience sampling was used to recruit 257 postoperative patients with diabetic foot who were admitted to four tertiary grade A hospitals in Hunan Province from March to December 2024.Data were collected using a self-designed General Information Questionnaire for postoperative patients with diabetic foot, the Readiness for Hospital Discharge Scale (RHDS),the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS),the Exercise of Self-Care Agency Scale (ESCA),and the Quality of Discharge Teaching Scale (QDTS).ResultsThe total scores for readiness for hospital discharge, perceived social support, self-care agency, and quality of discharge teaching among postoperative patients with diabetic foot were 89.18 ± 17.62,61.67 ± 12.37,104.61 ± 29.44,and 141.78 ± 27.66 points, respectively. The quality of discharge teaching (β = 0.336), self-care agency (β = 0.256), and perceived social support (β = 0.303) were all positively associated with readiness for hospital discharge in postoperative patients with diabetic foot. Perceived social support and quality of discharge teaching mediated the association between self-care agency and readiness for hospital discharge (β = 0.430,95% CI [0.260,0.638], 62.7% of total effect). The three indirect pathways were self-care agency → perceived social support → readiness for hospital discharge (β = 0.209,95% CI [0.018,0.539], 30.4%); self-care agency → quality of discharge teaching → readiness for hospital discharge (β = 0.090, 95% CI [0.005,0.230],13.1%); self-care agency → perceived social support → quality of discharge teaching → readiness for hospital discharge (β = 0.132,95% CI [0.043,0.304],19.2%).ConclusionPostoperative patients with diabetic foot exhibit moderate readiness for hospital discharge, with considerable potential for improvement. Significant associations were observed among self-care agency, perceived social support, quality of discharge teaching, and readiness for hospital discharge in postoperative patients with diabetic foot. Perceived social support and quality of discharge teaching may constitute indirect pathways linking self-care agency to readiness for hospital discharge.Practice ImplicationsClinical Healthcare Professionals should systematically strengthen self-care agency, perceived social support, and quality of discharge teaching in postoperative patients with diabetic foot. Based on the serial indirect pathway among these three factors, a hospital–community–family integrated discharge support system should be established to effectively improve patients’ readiness for hospital discharge and ensure their transitional safety.Keywords: diabetic foot, perceived social support, quality of discharge teaching, readiness for hospital discharge, self-care agency, structural equation model

Introduction

Diabetic Foot (DF) is a complication of diabetes caused by distal neuropathy and varying degrees of vascular lesions in the lower limbs, leading to foot infections, ulcers, and/or deep tissue damage.1 Statistics indicate that approximately 4 million patients with diabetes worldwide develop foot complications (ie, foot ulcers) annually. In addition to local adjuvant therapy, these patients often require surgical interventions to control infections, reduce recurrence rates, and prevent severe ulcer-related complications.2 Although surgical treatment for diabetic foot has demonstrated some efficacy, patients frequently remain in the rehabilitation phase post-discharge and may face a series of challenges, including slow wound healing, recurrent infections, risk of amputation, mobility impairment, high dependence on caregivers, glycemic fluctuations, difficulties with social adaptation and repeated hospitalisations.3 These challenges not only impede recovery and prognosis but also reduce quality of life.

Readiness for Hospital Discharge (RHD) refers to whether a person, following treatment, meets discharge criteria and possesses the ability to safely and effectively return to family and society in terms of physical condition, psychological state, and daily living skills.4 Inadequate readiness for hospital discharge may result in post-discharge difficulties,5 reduced self-management capabilities,6 increased adverse events,7 and even heightened risks of readmission8 and mortality.9 This not only exacerbates financial burdens but also severely threatens quality of life and safety. Therefore, as a crucial link between in-hospital treatment and post-discharge self-management, readiness for hospital discharge is of great significance in ensuring that postoperative patients with diabetic foot transition smoothly from hospital to home/community, helping them continue to manage their health effectively and achieve rapid recovery and reintegration into society.

Among the factors influencing readiness for hospital discharge, self-care agency, social support, and the quality of discharge teaching are particularly important. Self-care agency refers to an individual’s ability to proactively adopt various methods and integrate different resources to independently manage daily life and complete self-care tasks.10 This ability is not static but evolves with the individual’s health status. After discharge, changes in patients’ roles and access to health resources may lead to insufficient self-care capabilities, which can hinder recovery. Multiple studies have found that readiness for hospital discharge is positively correlated with self-care agency—the better a person’s self-care agency, the higher their readiness for hospital discharge.11–13 Furthermore, self-care agency has been identified as a key predictor of readiness for hospital discharge in patients with diabetic foot ulcers.3

Social support refers to the material and/or emotional assistance provided through various social connections centered around individuals.14 As an external protective factor, social support significantly promotes the maintenance and enhancement of physical and mental health. Research indicates that readiness for hospital discharge following surgery is positively influenced by social support. A robust social support system enables patients to access more healthcare assistance, making it a crucial strategy for improving readiness for hospital discharge.15,16 Wang Fang and Yuan Li17 demonstrated a positive correlation between readiness for hospital discharge and social support in patients with diabetes, suggesting that healthcare professionals should prioritize evaluating support systems. In addition to guiding patients to utilize existing resources, professionals should assist in identifying additional support channels.

The quality of discharge teaching refers to the effectiveness of information provided by medical staff through health education materials, lectures, and oral instruction. This process delivers essential care information to patients and primary caregivers throughout hospitalization, aiming to ensure a safe transition from hospital to home and better post-discharge care.18 Studies indicate that the quality of discharge teaching significantly predicts postoperative readiness for hospital discharge in patients with lumbar degenerative disease19 and is an independent influencing factor for postoperative readiness in patients with uterine fibroids.20 A survey of patients with type 2 diabetes revealed a positive correlation between readiness for hospital discharge and the quality of discharge teaching,21 consistent with the findings of Xu Lixian et al22 Therefore, effective discharge teaching can enhance patients’ readiness for hospital discharge.

Social cognitive theory posits that23 there is a dynamic, reciprocal interaction among the individual, behavior, and the environment—meaning that an individual’s behavioral capabilities actively shape their social environment, rather than being passively shaped by it. Patients with higher self-care agency are more proactive in seeking support from family members, friends, and the broader society, and they can also more effectively identify and perceive the support resources available in their surroundings. Conservation of resources (COR) theory suggests that24 the initial resources an individual possesses will further help them acquire additional resources. Self-care agency itself serves as a core personal resource for patients; it can stabilize their recovery status, reduce the caregiving burden, and facilitate access to higher-quality social support. Zhang Yanyan et al confirmed that25 self-management agency is positively correlated with social support, and that patients with stronger self-management agency are more likely to obtain social support, which enhances their discharge confidence and thereby enables a smoother transition from hospital to home. In clinical practice, discharge teaching is a two-way interactive process—not a one-way transmission from healthcare professionals—and its quality is highly dependent on the participation of both patients and their families. The higher the level of social support, the greater the patient’s engagement in discharge teaching. For example, patients who are accompanied by family members throughout the discharge teaching process engage in more thorough communication with healthcare professionals, directly improving the actual quality of the discharge teaching. Zhang Jing et al found that the higher the level of social support, the stronger the patient’s ability to absorb knowledge during discharge teaching.26 Zhao Liyan et al also maintained that social support is positively correlated with the quality of discharge teaching, and that social support can strengthen patients’ confidence in returning to life and society as early as possible, thereby enhancing their mastery of the content and skills delivered in discharge teaching and improving their physical and psychological well-being.27

Accordingly, perceived social support and the quality of discharge teaching may partially mediate the relationship between self-care agency and readiness for hospital discharge, and there is also an association between these factors. Existing research predominantly analyzes the impact of single factors separately, with limited exploration of their specific pathways and internal logic. In China, current intervention strategies targeting patient readiness for hospital discharge predominantly focus on a single dimension of health education, with a notable absence of chain-based intervention programs that systematically integrate patients’ intrinsic self-care agency, external perceived social support, and the quality of discharge teaching. This research gap urgently needs to be addressed.

Transition theory, introduced by Meleis et al28 into the nursing field, defines the process by which individuals move from one life stage, condition, or state to another. The transition from hospitalization to home care involves assessing personal capabilities and adapting to role changes. This theoretical framework comprises four core components: properties of the transition experience (the changes experienced by individuals), transition conditions (factors influencing the process and outcomes), nursing therapeutics (interventions provided by nurses), and patterns of response (indicators evaluating the transition process and outcomes). Based on this theory and a review of the literature, our research framework constructs the following relationships: properties of the transition experience involve changes in patients’ health status and care settings (hospital to home); transition conditions include demographic characteristics, disease-related factors, self-care abilities (personal transition conditions), and perceived social support (external transition conditions); nursing therapeutics refer to the quality of discharge teaching; and patterns of response measure readiness for hospital discharge.

In summary, based on transition theory and focusing on the perspectives of perceived social support and the quality of discharge teaching, this study explores factors associated with readiness for hospital discharge in postoperative patients with diabetic foot and investigates the potential pathways through which self-care agency affects readiness for hospital discharge. Meanwhile, the hypotheses of this study are proposed as follows: H1: Self-care agency, perceived social support, and the quality of discharge teaching are positively associated with readiness for hospital discharge among postoperative patients with diabetic foot. H2: Perceived social support mediates the association between self-care agency and readiness for hospital discharge. H3: The quality of discharge teaching mediates the association between self-care agency and readiness for hospital discharge. H4: Perceived social support and the quality of discharge teaching play a chain mediating role between self-care agency and readiness for hospital discharge. Our study aims to clarify the intrinsic relational logic among these four factors, thereby providing a theoretical reference for developing targeted strategies and interventions to enhance patients’ readiness for hospital discharge, and ultimately helping patients better transition from hospital to home, reduce adverse outcomes, and improve rehabilitation outcomes and quality of life.

Method

Design and Participants

Using convenience sampling, this study recruited postoperative patients with diabetic foot from four tertiary grade A hospitals in Hunan Province between March and December 2024. Inclusion criteria were: ① Age ≥ 18 years; ② Postoperative patients with diabetic foot; ③ Voluntary participation and no communication barriers with investigators. Exclusion criteria were: ① Patients with mental disorders or cognitive impairments preventing comprehension of the questionnaire; ② Those with severe visual or hearing impairments unable to complete the questionnaire; ③ Patients with severe infectious diseases, concurrent organ damage, or a history of malignant tumors.

In this study, the required sample size was first calculated using the formula for estimating a population mean. Based on a previous study, the standard deviation of the readiness for hospital discharge score among patients with diabetic foot ulcers was 18.39.3 With α = 0.05 and an allowable error of 15% of the standard deviation, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 171. Considering a 20% rate of invalid questionnaires or dropout, a minimum of 214 cases was needed. Given that the primary analysis involved a structural equation model with latent variables, a supplementary evaluation of the model’s sample size requirements was performed. The final model estimated 32 parameters, and 257 cases were actually included, yielding a sample size-to-parameter ratio of 8.03:1. A power analysis was conducted using the model’s degrees of freedom (59), α = 0.05, a null hypothesis RMSEA of 0.05, and an alternative hypothesis RMSEA of 0.08. The resulting statistical power was 0.920, indicating that the actual sample size was sufficient to meet the basic analytical needs of the model in this study.

Measures

1. The General Information Questionnaire collected demographic and disease-related data. (1) Demographic characteristics: sex, age, marital status, educational background, household per capita monthly income, method of medical insurance, housing arrangements, primary caregiver status, distance from residence to healthcare facility. (2) Disease-related information: progression of diabetic foot, number of diabetic complications, first hospitalization status, onset status, surgical procedure, length of hospital stay, preoperative Wagner grade.

2. Readiness for Hospital Discharge Scale (RHDS): Developed by Weiss and Piacentine,29 this scale has undergone global validation and extensive application.30–32 Lin Youhua et al33 translated and adapted the scale for use in China. It comprises 12 items across three dimensions: Personal Status (Items 1–3), Coping Ability (Items 4–8), and Expected Support (Items 9–12). Each item is scored 0–10, with a total score of 120. Readiness for hospital discharge is categorized as Low (≤72), Moderate (73–96), and High (≥97). The scale demonstrates a Cronbach’s α of 0.89, with dimension coefficients ranging from 0.73 to 0.90. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.938.

3. Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS): Developed by Zimet et al34 and adapted for China by Jiang Qianjin,35 this 12-item scale measures perceived social support across three dimensions: family support, friend support, and other support. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “totally disagree” to 7 = “totally agree”), with a total score of 84. Scores are categorized as Low (12–36), Moderate (37–60), and High (61–84). The scale demonstrates a Cronbach’s α of 0.921, with dimension coefficients ranging from 0.813 to 0.820. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.921.

4. Exercise of Self-Care Agency Scale (ESCA): Developed by Kearny and Fleischer36 and adapted into Chinese by Wang,37 this 43-item scale evaluates four dimensions: self-care skills, self-care responsibility, self-concept, and health knowledge. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = “completely dissimilar to me” to 4 = “completely similar to me”), with items 3, 6, 10, 16, 19, 22, 25, 28, 32, 34, and 39 reverse-scored. Total scores range 0 to 172, with self-care agency classified as Low (≤ 57), Moderate (58–115), and High (≥116). Cronbach’s α ranges from 0.86 to 0.92, and was 0.964 in this study.

5. Quality of Discharge Teaching Scale (QDTS): Developed by Weiss et al38 and adapted into Chinese by Wang Binghua,39 this 24-item scale evaluates three dimensions: required content, acquired content, and teaching techniques and effectiveness. Each item is scored 0–10, with the total score calculated as the sum of “acquired content” and “teaching techniques and effectiveness” dimensions (maximum 180 points). Scores are categorized as Low (<108), Moderate (108–144), and High (≥145). The Chinese version demonstrates strong reliability and validity, with an overall Cronbach’s α of 0.92 and dimension coefficients ranging from 0.88 to 0.94. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.976.

Procedure

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya School of Nursing, Central South University prior to data collection (Ethics Approval No.: E202421). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Consent and cooperation were obtained from the participating hospitals and departments. Following informed consent, participants were selected according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and data were collected face-to-face. Researchers explained the study objectives, assured participants of privacy protection, and confirmed that all personal information would remain strictly confidential and used solely for research purposes. Questionnaires were administered after obtaining consent. Participants who requested withdrawal were allowed to do so without any coercion. For those unable to complete the questionnaires independently due to physical or other limitations, researchers provided individual assistance, completing any missing sections as required. To ensure full comprehension, researchers promptly addressed any participant questions. Collected questionnaires were verified on-site, and incomplete entries were confirmed with participants. A total of 270 questionnaires were distributed, of which 13 were excluded due to invalid responses (eg, patterned or random answers), resulting in 257 valid questionnaires and a validity rate of 95.2%.

Data Analysis

Data were entered using Excel and analyzed with SPSS 26.0. Structural equation modeling (SEM) and path testing with mediation analysis were performed using Amos 24.0. A significance level of α = 0.05 was applied, and all reported p-values are two-tailed. Qualitative data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Quantitative data with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed data are presented as median and quartiles. Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between perceived social support, self-care agency, the quality of discharge teaching, and readiness for hospital discharge in postoperative patients with diabetic foot. Prior to constructing the structural model, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to test the measurement model. Readiness for Hospital Discharge, Perceived Social Support, Self-care Agency, and Quality of Discharge Teaching were specified as latent variables, with the dimension scores of each scale serving as the observed indicators for the corresponding latent variables. Maximum likelihood estimation was used for parameter estimation, and the following fit indices were reported: χ2/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA with its 90% confidence interval, and SRMR. Convergent validity was evaluated using standardized factor loadings, composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE); discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion and the heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT) of correlations. Before conducting structural equation model analysis, the distributional characteristics of the 13 observed variables in the model were examined. Univariate normality was assessed using skewness and excess kurtosis, with absolute skewness values less than 2 and absolute excess kurtosis values less than 7 adopted as the reference criteria indicating no severe univariate non-normality. The 257 participants included in the analysis had no missing values on any of the model variables; therefore, complete data were used for the analysis. Mediation effects were tested using 5,000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples, and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. An indirect effect was considered statistically significant when the 95% CI did not include zero. Using Amos 24.0, an SEM was established with self-care agency as the independent variable, perceived social support and the quality of discharge teaching as mediator variables, and readiness for hospital discharge as the dependent variable.

Most patients presented with Wagner grade 3 prior to surgery (66.5%). Detailed demographic and disease-related characteristics are presented in Table 1 and Table S1.

Model Modification

To evaluate the influence of demographic and clinical characteristics on the model results, a covariate-adjusted structural equation model was additionally constructed as a sensitivity analysis. Based on clinical relevance and data availability, age, primary caregiver status, distance from residence to healthcare facility, number of diabetic complications, first hospitalization status, surgical procedure, length of hospital stay, and preoperative Wagner grade were included in the model. Categorical variables with a natural order were coded in increasing ranks and standardized, while binary variables were coded as 0 and 1. The above covariates were simultaneously entered into the regression equations for self-care agency, perceived social support, quality of discharge teaching, and readiness for hospital discharge. The adjusted indirect effects were tested using 5,000 non-parametric bootstrap resamples, with statistical significance determined by whether the bias-corrected 95% confidence interval (CI) contained zero.

The results of the covariate-adjusted sensitivity analysis showed that the model yielded the following fit indices: χ2 = 272.332, df = 131, χ2/df = 2.079, CFI = 0.922, TLI = 0.892, RMSEA = 0.065 (90% CI: 0.054–0.076), SRMR = 0.044, indicating an overall acceptable model fit. After adjustment, the standardized path coefficients from self-care agency to perceived social support, quality of discharge teaching, and readiness for hospital discharge were 0.622, 0.293, and 0.203, respectively, all statistically significant (all P < 0.05). The path coefficient from perceived social support to quality of discharge teaching was 0.588 (P < 0.001), but its path coefficient to readiness for hospital discharge was 0.189 (P = 0.087), which did not reach statistical significance. The path coefficient from quality of discharge teaching to readiness for hospital discharge was 0.377 (P < 0.001). Bootstrap results showed that the specific indirect effect through perceived social support alone was not significant, with a bias-corrected 95% CI of −0.029 to 0.178. The specific indirect effect through quality of discharge teaching alone and the serial indirect effect remained significant, with bias-corrected 95% CIs of 0.001 to 0.118 and 0.021 to 0.145, respectively. The total indirect effect had a bias-corrected 95% CI of 0.086 to 0.244. These results indicated that after controlling for key demographic and clinical factors, the serial mediation pathway was still supported, whereas the specific indirect effect of perceived social support alone was attenuated (Table 4).

Alternative Model

To examine whether the directional specification of the theoretical model could be distinguished from other possible path sequences, two alternative structural equation models were additionally constructed. Alternative Model 1 was specified as “self-care agency → quality of discharge teaching → perceived social support → readiness for hospital discharge,” and Alternative Model 2 was specified as “quality of discharge teaching → self-care agency → perceived social support → readiness for hospital discharge.” The original model and the two alternative models used the same four-factor measurement model, the same sample, the same maximum likelihood estimation method, and the same approach to handling missing data. Model fit was compared using χ2/df, GFI, NFI, IFI, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR, AIC, and BIC, and the model-implied covariance matrices were further compared.

The results showed that the original model and both alternative models converged normally. The fit indices of the three models were identical: χ2 = 149.109, df = 59, χ2/df = 2.527, GFI = 0.916, NFI = 0.915, IFI = 0.947, CFI = 0.946, TLI = 0.929, RMSEA = 0.077 (90% CI: 0.062–0.093), SRMR = 0.054, AIC = 21,505.233, BIC = 21,618.803. The maximum absolute differences among the model-implied covariance matrices of the three models ranged from 1.28 × 10−4 to 1.78 × 10−4, indicating that their overall fit was indistinguishable in practical terms. Discussion

Readiness for Hospital Discharge in Postoperative Patients with Diabetic Foot

This study systematically examines the pathways through which self-care agency is associated with readiness for hospital discharge, while revealing the mediating roles of perceived social support and the quality of discharge teaching. The findings elucidate the intrinsic mechanisms linking self-care agency and readiness for hospital discharge and provide empirical evidence for developing targeted strategies and interventions to enhance discharge preparedness. Results indicate that postoperative patients with diabetic foot demonstrated moderate readiness for hospital discharge. This may reflect the study population’s characteristics, as 67.7% of participants experienced recurrent episodes and 80.2% were not first-time hospitalizations. These recurrent conditions and multiple hospital admissions allowed patients to become familiar with admission and discharge procedures, disease management knowledge, and self-care practices. Conducted at tertiary grade A hospitals—medical institutions providing superior healthcare services compared with primary care facilities—patients received comprehensive health education during hospitalization, equipping them to manage post-discharge challenges. Nonetheless, the study suggests that readiness for hospital discharge can still be further improved. Healthcare providers should conduct thorough discharge needs assessments, implement personalized health education, and ensure well‑coordinated post‑discharge transitional care to facilitate smoother transitions to home environments.

Relationships Between Self-Care Agency, Perceived Social Support, Quality of Discharge Teaching, and Readiness for Hospital Discharge

This study demonstrated a positive correlation between self-care agency and readiness for hospital discharge in postoperative patients with diabetic foot, consistent with previous research.12,40,41 Patients with stronger self-care agency not only master more effective self-care techniques but also experience enhanced confidence in rehabilitation, which promotes improved health behaviors and greater resilience in facing disease-related challenges during recovery.42 For postoperative patients with diabetic foot, these skills and methods are primarily embodied in behaviors such as inspecting their foot, keeping the wound clean, adhering to offloading or protective footwear, managing the diabetes diet, taking medications correctly, and seeking prompt help when signs of infection appear. The effective execution of these behaviors serves as an important foundation for improving readiness for hospital discharge. Regarding how to enhance patients’ self-care level, existing studies have confirmed the role of diversified intervention programs. Qian Dan et al43 report that empowerment education can enhance self-care agency by improving self-responsiveness and health literacy, thereby increasing readiness for hospital discharge through optimized personal condition and adaptability. In addition to in-hospital empowerment education interventions, long-term post-discharge follow-up management is equally indispensable. Evidence-based interventions, such as WeChat-based continuous care44 and community-based continuous care,45 have been shown to significantly improve patients’ out-of-hospital self-care capabilities, with the core mechanism being the continuous guidance and supervision of patients’ self-care behaviors. Therefore, healthcare providers can organically combine in-hospital health education guided by the empowerment education philosophy with a transitional care plan initiated prior to discharge. This integrated approach ensures that patients have thoroughly mastered the above core self‑care behaviors before discharge, so that they are fully prepared for home-based self-care after discharge.

Perceived social support is a critical factor associated with readiness for hospital discharge in postoperative patients with diabetic foot. Research indicates that family and social support can help patients maintain a positive attitude towards their condition, thereby enhancing their willingness and ability to engage in self-care.46 The recovery process of postoperative patients with diabetic foot is characterized by specific challenges, including slow wound healing, limited mobility, a high risk of reinfection, and the need for long-term adherence to foot care. These challenges create a more urgent need for practical assistance after discharge. Adequate family and social support can be manifested as various practical helping behaviors, such as assisting with wound dressing changes, accompanying patients to follow-up visits, providing timely medication reminders, offering mobility assistance, preparing appropriate diabetic meals, and delivering psychological counseling to alleviate negative emotions. Such multidimensional practical support provides an important safeguard for patients’ home-based recovery. Healthcare professionals should assess patients’ social support status postoperatively. For those with limited support, personalized interventions should guide the establishment of positive social interactions and encourage proactive seeking of emotional and practical assistance. Simultaneously, a family education mechanism should be implemented to inform relatives of the importance of family support in patients’ physical and psychological recovery. Developing a multi-level family support system—including financial assistance, emotional companionship, and daily care—can reduce pre-discharge anxiety and enhance patients’ adaptation abilities.

The quality of discharge teaching was also significantly positively correlated with readiness for hospital discharge, in line with previous findings.47–49 The quality of discharge teaching for postoperative patients with diabetic foot should be highly specific, with its core content covering standardized wound care education, glycaemic control, infection warning signs, activity and weight-bearing guidance, medication use, follow-up schedules, prevention of foot trauma, nutritional support plans, and caregiver involvement High-quality discharge teaching enables patients to acquire disease-related knowledge and skills effectively, enhancing their confidence and coping ability, facilitating a smooth transition to post-discharge life, and improving adaptability. This finding underscores the crucial role of discharge teaching in enhancing readiness for hospital discharge. Clinical practitioners should recognize this importance and, based on a comprehensive assessment of patients’ discharge needs, employ tailored teaching techniques to provide personalized health education. A feedback and evaluation mechanism can be established to verify the effectiveness of teaching, ultimately supporting a successful post-discharge transition.

The Mediating Role of Perceived Social Support in the Relationship Between Self-Care Agency and Readiness for Hospital Discharge

This study demonstrates that self-care agency is associated with readiness for hospital discharge through perceived social support in postoperative patients with diabetic foot. The mechanism likely reflects the psychological safety provided by family and friends, which reduces disease-related anxiety and discharge-related concerns, thereby enhancing patients’ confidence in managing post-discharge life and improving their readiness for hospital discharge. Family and friends not only help alleviate financial burdens but also effectively mitigate negative emotions, such as anxiety and depression.50 Strong social support enables patients to make better use of available resources for disease management. Consequently, healthcare professionals should prioritize assessing postoperative social support needs, assist patients in maximizing social support resources, establish effective support systems, reduce post-discharge concerns.

The Mediating Role of Quality of Discharge Teaching in the Relationship Between Self-Care Agency and Readiness for Hospital Discharge

The findings further indicate that the quality of discharge teaching significantly mediates the relationship between self-care agency and readiness for hospital discharge in postoperative patients with diabetic foot. High-quality discharge teaching systematically conveys essential knowledge and skills for post-discharge care, helping patients build confidence in self-management.51 It also provides patients with a sense of continuous medical support, effectively alleviating psychological burdens,52 and enables them to approach discharge with a proactive and positive mindset. Therefore, healthcare professionals should emphasize discharge teaching in clinical practice. By enriching content and optimizing teaching methods, the quality of discharge teaching can be continuously improved, providing a strong foundation for smooth postoperative recovery.

The Chain Mediating Role of Perceived Social Support and Quality of Discharge Teaching in the Relationship Between Self-Care Agency and Readiness for Hospital Discharge

The findings of this study support that perceived social support and quality of discharge teaching may constitute an indirect pathway between self-care agency and readiness for hospital discharge. However, given the cross‑sectional design, the temporal sequence and causal mechanisms of this pathway cannot be established. The underlying logic of this pathway may be as follows: patients with better self-care agency tend to be more adept at perceiving and accepting support from family and society; this positive perception of social support may help alleviate their fear of disease recovery and concerns about post-discharge life, thereby enabling them to face rehabilitation challenges with greater composure. With the positive mindset fostered by perceived social support, patients may engage more actively in the discharge teaching process and thus absorb the discharge teaching content more thoroughly; high-quality discharge teaching, in turn, may help patients systematically master key information such as self-care skills and rehabilitation precautions, equipping them with stronger coping abilities for post-discharge life, which ultimately manifests as a higher level of readiness for hospital discharge. Accordingly, clinical practice can translate the above chain mediating pathway into an intervention strategy of “ability enhancement—support reinforcement—teaching optimization” and integrate it into a hospital-community-family linked discharge support system. At the hospital level, on the one hand, systematic and stepwise training can be implemented to effectively enhance patients’ self-care agency; family members should be included as an essential part of health education, and through caregiving skill demonstrations and emotional support strategy guidance, they can become a stable source of social support for patients. Individualized high-quality discharge teaching should be provided based on patients’ self-care levels and family support characteristics. On the other hand, hospitals need to assume the coordinating function of transitional care, completing the handover with community health service centers before patient discharge, transferring standardized information on patients’ self-care status, disease risks, and home care needs, and simultaneously scheduling community home visit services to achieve a seamless transition from in-hospital rehabilitation to home-based care. At the community level, the community health service center serves as the core site for transitional care, with community healthcare providers acting as the crucial link between the hospital and the family. Community healthcare providers can conduct regular home visits to dynamically assess patients’ home care implementation, including wound healing status, signs of infection, blood glucose control, and medication adherence, to promptly correct improper practices, and to identify early warning signs of infection. Through family interviews, they can understand patients’ genuine experiences of family support and guide family members to adjust their caregiving approaches to improve patients’ rehabilitation mindset. Meanwhile, they should check the implementation of discharge teaching content item by item, including wound management, rehabilitation activities, and follow-up appointment adherence, and compile care difficulties, newly emerging patient needs, and abnormal physical signs into a feedback list, which is regularly synchronized with the hospital specialist team to construct a dynamic quality control closed loop of “assessment–feedback–optimization”. At the family level, under the guidance of professionals, family members can provide continuous family support from multiple dimensions such as emotional companionship and caregiving assistance, helping patients build self-coping confidence during home rehabilitation and genuinely translate the essence of discharge teaching into self-management behaviors, thereby enabling patients to attain a higher level of perceived social support and form a more positive perception of discharge teaching. The synergistic collaboration among hospitals, communities, and families facilitates a smoother and more confident transition for patients to post-discharge life.

Conclusion

Postoperative patients with diabetic foot demonstrate moderate readiness for hospital discharge, with substantial potential for improvement. Self-care agency is not only directly associated with readiness for hospital discharge but also may have an indirect association with it via the quality of discharge teaching and perceived social support. In clinical practice, healthcare professionals should prioritize assessing discharge readiness in these patients, with particular emphasis on enhancing self-care capabilities as a core intervention. Simultaneously, efforts should be coordinated to optimize the quality of discharge teaching and strengthen perceived social support. By establishing a multidimensional intervention system centered on self-care agency, Clinical Healthcare Professionals can better support patients in successfully completing their transition from hospital to home.

Practice Implications

The findings of this study indicate that self-care agency is a key factor associated with readiness for hospital discharge in postoperative patients with diabetic foot, and it may also have an indirect association with readiness for hospital discharge through perceived social support and the quality of discharge teaching. Clinically, this suggests that a multi-dimensional collaborative intervention model should be constructed with self-care agency at its core, and should simultaneously optimize discharge teaching and strengthen perceived social support. This approach can assist them in smoothly completing the transition from hospital to home, and also provide a replicable practical solution for the discharge management of postoperative patients with diabetic foot. Furthermore, it offers empirical evidence for improving the hospital-community-family linked rehabilitation support system for these patients.

Innovations and Limitations

This study constructed a structural equation model and utilized chain mediation analysis to quantitatively examine the complex relationships among multiple factors, clarifying both direct and indirect associations with readiness for hospital discharge. The findings not only elucidate potential mechanisms related to postoperative readiness in patients with diabetic foot but also provide evidence-based guidance for clinical nursing interventions. These insights support the optimization of patients’ transitions from hospital to home.

This study has several limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design, this study was unable to infer the temporal sequence of variables or establish definitive causal relationships. Although the original model was retained based on the theoretical framework, alternative directional relationships remain possible. Future research should employ longitudinal designs or multi-wave cross-lagged panel models to further examine the temporal sequence and potential causal relationships among the variables. Second, all key variables were measured at a single time point using patient self-report questionnaires, which may introduce common method bias. Future studies could incorporate objective clinical indicators, evaluations by healthcare professionals, and multi-timepoint measurements to reduce common method bias and further validate the findings. Third, the use of convenience sampling may have resulted in selection bias. Future research could adopt stratified random sampling or multi-stage sampling methods and expand sample recruitment to include diverse regions and different levels of healthcare institutions, thereby enhancing sample representativeness and the external validity of the conclusions. Finally, this study lacked objective post-discharge outcome indicators and thus could not examine the association between readiness for hospital discharge and long-term clinical outcomes. Future studies could incorporate follow-up assessments and include objective indicators such as readmission rates and complication rates to deepen the understanding of the clinical value of readiness for hospital discharge.
