Quantum algorithms based on quantum trajectories
Evan Borras, Milad Marvian

Abstract
Quantum simulation has emerged as a key application of quantum computing, with significant progress made in algorithms for simulating both closed and open quantum systems. The simulation of open quantum systems, particularly those governed by the Lindblad master equation, has received attention recently with the current state-of-the-art algorithms having an input model query complexity of O ⁡ ( T ​ polylog ⁡ ( T / ϵ ) ) O(T\polylog(T/\epsilon)) , where T T and ϵ \epsilon are the requested time and precision of the simulation respectively. For the Hamiltonian simulation problem it has been show that the optimal Hamiltonian query complexity is O ⁡ ( T + log ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) O(T+\log(1/\epsilon)) , additive in nature between the two parameter, but for Lindbladian simulation this question remains open. In this work we show that the additive complexity of O ⁡ ( T + log ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) O(T+\log(1/\epsilon)) is reachable for the simulation of a large class of Lindbladian by constructing a novel quantum algorithm based on quantum trajectories.

II
Prerequisites

We begin by defining some notation. We will be working with systems of
n
-qubits implying that general quantum states
\rho
are given by density operators, trace normalized positive linear operators acting on
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
. Pure quantum states will either be denoted as rank one density operators or normalized vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
. We will use the term superoperator to refer to a linear map acting on the set of bounded linear operators over
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
. Thus quantum channels are superoperators which are completely positive and trace preserving. The sets of completely positive trace preserving superoperators and unital completely positive superoperators will be denoted
\mathbf{CPTP}
and
\mathbf{UCP}
respectively. The identity superoperator will be denoted as
\mathcal{I}
.

The operator norm of a linear operator will be denoted as
\left\lVert A\right\rVert
. Similarly, the trace norm of a linear operator
A
is defined as
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A})
. Given a superoperator
\mathcal{L}
, the diamond norm of
\mathcal{L}
is given by
\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{\diamond}=\sup_{\rho}\left\lVert\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathcal{L}(\rho)\right\rVert_{1}
where the supremum is taken over all density operators
\rho
. The diamond norm of superoperators induces a diamond distance between superoperators given by
d_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{\diamond}
. We will also be using the total variational distance between probability distributions defined for continuous variables as
\delta(p,f)=\frac{1}{2}\int dx|p(x)-f(x)|
and discrete variables as
\delta(p,f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{N}|p_{N}-f_{N}|
.

Finally we denote the total simulation time requested to be
T
and the precision of the simulation to be
\epsilon
. We analyze the precision of the simulation with respect to the diamond distance given that it not only provides a measure of distance between quantum channels, ie the quantum channel our simulation implements versus the quantum channel we are trying to simulate, it also is used to upperbound the single shot probability of distinguishing between quantum channels
Watrous 2018
. Thus the diamond distance provides a relevant operational measure of similarity between quantum channels. In the sections to follow we introduce some tools and techniques we will use in building our algorithm as well as review the quantum jump unraveling of the Lindblad master equation
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
.

II.2
Quantum Jump Unraveling Of The Lindblad Master Equation

For convenience we re-state the full Lindblad master equation given below,

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(L_{\mu}\rho L_{\mu}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu},\rho\}\right)=\mathcal{L}(\rho)

(10)

where
\mathcal{L}
is defined as the Lindbladian superoperator for the system.
The standard way to interpret the Lindblad master equation is that the Lindbladian superoperator is a generator for a family of completely positive trace preserving maps acting on density matrices parameterized by time. Given that density operators can also be interpreted as a probability distribution over pure states in Hilbert space leads to another interpretation of the dynamics generated by the Lindblad master equation. Alternatively, we can interpret the density operator as some initial probability distribution over Hilbert space evolving under some stochastic process
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
. The Lindblad master equation is then “unraveled” as a stochastic Schrodinger equation describing how the random pure state vector evolves under the stochastic process defined by the Lindbladian. Averaging over many solutions of stochastic Schrodinger equation, called trajectories, reproduces the dynamics of a density operator evolving under the Lindblad master equation.

For a given Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
there exists multiple different unravelings, with the quantum jump unraveling being the one we will base our algorithm off of.
In this picture, the dynamics generated by the Lindblad master equation can be interpreted as a piece-wise deterministic stochastic process over Hilbert space and the quantum state in operator form can be reconstructed as the expectation value of the pure state operator
\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
with respect to this distribution at the given time. In this unraveling a pure state of the ensemble associated with the initial density operator evolves deterministically under a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian given by
\hat{H}=H-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}
for a random period of time before being interrupted by quantum jumps. The deterministic evolution is given by the map

G_{t}(\ket{\psi})=\frac{e^{-i\hat{H}t}\ket{\psi}}{\left\lVert e^{-i\hat{H}t}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert}

(11)

along with the
\mu
th quantum jump given by

J_{\mu}(\ket{\psi})=\frac{L_{\mu}\ket{\psi}}{\left\lVert L_{\mu}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert}\quad\text{ occuring with probability}\quad\mathrm{Pr}(\mu|\ket{\psi})=\frac{\left\lVert L_{\mu}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\left\lVert L_{\mu}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert^{2}}.

(12)

Given an initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}
evolving under a trajectory interrupted by a total of
N
quantum jumps, the resulting state at time
T
can be expressed as:

\ket{\psi(T)}=G_{(T-t^{(N)})}\circ J_{\mu^{(N)}}\circ G_{(t^{(N)}-t^{(N-1)})}\cdots J_{\mu^{(2)}}\circ G_{(t^{(2)}-t^{(1)})}\circ J_{\mu^{(1)}}\circ G_{t^{(1)}}(\ket{\psi(0)})

(13)

where each random
t^{(i)}\in[0,T]
indicates the time of the
i
-th quantum jump. Defining the
i
-th holding-time between quantum jumps to be
t_{h}^{(i)}=t^{(i)}-t^{(i-1)}
allows us to rewrite equation (
13
) as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(T)}

\displaystyle=G_{(T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)})}\circ J_{\mu^{(N)}}\circ G_{t_{h}^{(N)}}\cdots J_{\mu^{(2)}}\circ G_{t_{h}^{(2)}}\circ J_{\mu^{(1)}}\circ G_{t_{h}^{(1)}}(\ket{\psi(0)})

\displaystyle=P_{t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},\mu^{(N)}}(\ket{\psi(0)})

(14)

where we have defined the trajectory operator
P_{t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(n)},\mu^{(n)}}
to be the map which evolves the input state
\ket{\psi(0)}
along the sampled trajectory.
Since quantum jumps occur at random intervals of time, the holding times
t_{h}^{(i)}
are also defined as random variables, with the cumulative distribution function given by

\mathrm{Pr}\left(t_{h}\leq\tau|\ket{\psi(t)}\right)=1-\left\lVert e^{-i\tau\tilde{H}}\ket{\psi(t)}\right\rVert^{2}.

(15)

Evolving an initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}
under such sampled trajectory operators realizes a solution to the stochastic Schrodinger equation associated with the quantum jump unraveling. Thus, averaging over all trajectories associated with the initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}
reproduces the exact evolution of the initial density operator
\ket{\psi(0)}\bra{\psi(0)}
under the Lindblad master equation. Defining the superoperator form of the trajectory operator as

\mathcal{P}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},\mu^{(N)}}(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})=P_{t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},\mu^{(N)}}(\ket{\psi})P_{t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},\mu^{(N)}}(\ket{\psi})^{\dagger}

(16)

allows us to write

\displaystyle e^{T\mathcal{L}}\left(\ket{\psi(0)}\bra{\psi(0)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\ket{\psi(T)}\bra{\psi(T)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\sum_{\mu^{(1)}=1}^{m}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}\sum_{\mu^{(N)}=1}^{m}

\displaystyle p(t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},\mu^{(N)};T)\mathcal{P}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},\mu^{(N)}}(\ket{\psi(0)}\bra{\psi(0)}).

(17)

where
p(t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},\mu^{(N)};T)
denotes the probability density for sampling a specific trajectory with final evolution time
T
, holding times
\{t_{h}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and quantum jumps
\{\mu^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Averaging the initial pure state
\ket{\psi(0)}
over the distribution associated with the initial density operator
\rho(0)
gives us

\displaystyle e^{T\mathcal{L}}\left(\rho(0)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\sum_{\mu^{(1)}=1}^{m}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}\sum_{\mu^{(N)}=1}^{m}

\displaystyle p(t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},\mu^{(N)};T)\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\rho(0)}\left[\mathcal{P}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},\mu^{(N)}}(\ket{\psi(0)}\bra{\psi(0)})\right],

(18)

which relates the dynamics generated by Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
to an average over trajectories on the pure state space.

In the sections to follow we introduce a new quantum algorithm inspired by the framework described above restricted to Lindbladians which satisfy the constraint,

\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}\propto\mathbb{I}.

(19)

Although our algorithm is not fully general, many common Lindbladians satisfy the above constraint such as Pauli channel dissipation, Lindbladians used in thermal state preparation

Chen et al. 2023b

Characterization

To begin, let us define the set
\algL
as the set of all Lindbladians with a representation given by a Hamiltonian

\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}=\Gamma\mathbb{I},

(20)

where

\displaystyle L_{\mu}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=L_{\mu}+a_{\mu}\mathbb{I}

\displaystyle H^{\prime}

\displaystyle=H+\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{\mu}\left(a_{\mu}^{*}L_{\mu}-a_{\mu}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}\right)+b\mathbb{I},

9
found in Appendix A. Simplifying the above equation we find

\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-i\left(\Tr_{A}(H\omega_{A}\otimes\rho)-\Tr_{A}(\omega_{A}\otimes\rho H)\right)-\Gamma(\mathcal{R}_{A}(\rho)-\rho)

(30)

where the map
\mathcal{R}_{A}\in\mathbf{CPTP}
and is defined by
\mathcal{R}(\rho)=\Tr_{A}\left[\mathcal{R}(\omega_{A}\otimes\rho)\right]
. Using Lemma 2 from Appendix B of
vom Ende 2023
we find

\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-i[H_{A},\rho]+\Gamma(\mathcal{R}_{A}(\rho)-\rho)

(31)

where
H_{A}
is defined as the unique operator satisfying
\Tr(H_{A}\rho)=\Tr(H(\omega_{A}\otimes\rho))
, guaranteed by Lemma 2 from Appendix B of
vom Ende 2023
. Thus we find

\mathcal{L}=-i\mathrm{ad}_{H_{A}}+\Gamma(\mathcal{R}_{A}+\mathcal{I}),

(32)

which implies
\mathcal{L}\in\algL
by Lemma
9
from Appendix A.
∎

Finally, we may wonder if relaxing the embedding procedure described above can make
\algL
universal. At the bare minimum we only require that at the final time of simulation the quantum channel induced on the subsystem matches the quantum channel generated by the targeted Lindbladian dynamics. More formally we require, for all Lindbladians
\mathcal{L}
there exists a
\mathcal{K}\in\algL
such that for some
\omega_{A}

\Tr_{A}[e^{t\mathcal{K}}(\omega_{A}\otimes\rho)]=e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\rho).

(33)

Interestingly, the embedding procedure described above always exists due to the Stinespring dilation theorem, although this result is not as useful as suggested. The Stinespring dilation theorem always assures us there exists some unitary, generated by some Hamiltonian, which can implement an arbitrary quantum channel on a subsystem given the ancillary Hilbert space is large enough. Although this result tells us a
\mathcal{K}\in\algL
exists which can satisfy the embedding procedure described above, it does not tell us
how
to construct this
\mathcal{K}
from the final time
t
and target Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
. In addition, this result only informs us that the embedding ability of Hamiltonian dynamics, a restricted subset of the dynamics generated by
\algL
, is universal. Given that an arbitrary Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}\in\algL
may be expressed as a sum of a Hamiltonian portion and dissipator with jump operators satisfying equation (
20
), one may wonder whether pure dissipators with jump operators satisfying equation (
20
) can also embed arbitrary Lindbladian dynamics. Such a conjecture can be proven false as a Corollary to the following Lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\in\algL
where the Hamiltonian
H=0
when equation (
20
) is satisfied with
\Gamma\neq 0
. Then for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}

e^{t\mathcal{D}}=e^{-t\Gamma}\mathcal{I}+(1-e^{-t\Gamma})\Phi_{t}

(34)

where
\Phi_{t}\in\mathbf{CPTP}

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{D}\in\algL
where the Hamiltonian
H=0
when equation (
20
) is satisfied. Lemma
9
in Appendix B implies we can write
\mathcal{D}=\Gamma(\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{I})
where
\mathcal{R}\in\mathbf{CPTP}
. Thus we find

e^{t\mathcal{D}}=e^{t\Gamma(\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{I})}=e^{-t\Gamma}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(t\Gamma)^{n}}{n!}\mathcal{R}^{\circ n}=e^{-t\Gamma}\left(\mathcal{I}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(t\Gamma)^{n}}{n!}\mathcal{R}^{\circ n}\right)=e^{-t\Gamma}\mathcal{I}+(1-e^{-t\Gamma})\Phi_{t}

(35)

where we have defined the map
\Phi_{t}=(e^{t\Gamma}-1)^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(t\Gamma)^{n}}{n!}\mathcal{R}^{\circ n}
. Since
\mathcal{R}\in\mathbf{CPTP}
and
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
, then for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
,
\Phi_{t}\in\mathbf{CP}
. To prove trace preservation note,

\Phi_{t}^{\dagger}(\mathbb{I})=(e^{t\Gamma}-1)^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{t\Gamma}{n!}\mathcal{R}^{\dagger\circ n}(\mathbb{I})=(e^{t\Gamma}-1)^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(t\Gamma)^{n}}{n!}\mathbb{I}=\mathbb{I}

(36)

where the second equality follows from
\mathcal{R}\in\mathbf{CPTP}
. Thus
\Phi_{t}\in\mathbf{UCP}
implying
\Phi_{t}\in\mathbf{CPTP}
.
∎

Corollary 5.1
.

There exists a Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}\notin\algL
such that there does not exist a
\mathcal{D}\in\algL
where the Hamiltonian
H=0
when equation (
20
) is satisfied and

\Tr_{A}[e^{t\mathcal{D}}(\omega_{A}\otimes\rho)]=e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\rho).

(37)

Proof.

We proceed by proof by contradiction. Let
\mathcal{L}
be the Lindbladian which generates the amplitude damping channel, represented by the jump operators
L=\ket{0}\bra{1}
and Hamiltonian
H=0
. For all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the channel
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
has a Kraus representation given by

\begin{cases}K_{0}=\ket{0}\bra{0}+\sqrt{1-p(t)}\ket{1}\bra{1}\\
K_{1}=\sqrt{p(t)}\ket{0}\bra{1}\end{cases}

(38)

where
p(t)=1-e^{-t\Gamma}
. Note, the set of operators given by
\{K_{\mu}^{\dagger}K_{\nu}\}_{\mu,\nu=0}^{1}
is linearly independent for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
implying the channel
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
is an extreme point in the set of all quantum channels acting on a single qubit. Assuming there exists a
\mathcal{D}\in\algL
where the Hamiltonian
H=0
when equation (
20
) is satisfied and
\Tr_{A}[e^{t\mathcal{D}}(\omega_{A}\otimes\rho)]=e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\rho)
, implies we can apply Lemma
5
and write

e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\rho)=\Tr_{A}\left[e^{t\mathcal{D}}(\omega_{A}\otimes\rho)\right]=\Tr_{A}\left[(e^{-t\Gamma}\mathcal{I}+(1-e^{-t\Gamma})\Phi_{t})[\omega_{A}\otimes\rho]\right]=e^{-t\Gamma}\rho+(1-e^{-t\Gamma})\Tr_{A}\left[\Phi_{t}(\omega_{A}\otimes\rho)\right]

(39)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
Defining the map
\Phi_{t}^{(A)}(\rho)\in\mathbf{CPTP}
as
\Phi_{t}^{(A)}(\rho)=\Tr_{A}\left[\Phi_{t}(\omega_{A}\otimes\rho)\right]
we can write

e^{t\mathcal{L}}=e^{-t\Gamma}\mathcal{I}+(1-e^{-t\Gamma})\Phi_{t}^{(A)},

(40)

which is a convex combination of single qubit quantum channels. Thus we have arrived at a contradiction given that
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
has already been shown to be an extreme point in the set of quantum channels for
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
∎

Given the results discussed above, the set of Lindbladians for which our algorithm can be applied to is quite rigid. Lemma
3
confirms our intuition, that our algorithms restriction does limit its applicability to a subset of all Lindbladians. In addition, Lemma
4
and Corollary
5.1
imply the ability of this subset to embed arbitrary Lindbladian dynamics is restricted as well. Such results suggest, that extending the applicability of the algorithm will likely involve modifying its structure as opposed to using it as a black-box subroutine. In the sections to follow we introduce this new algorithm providing its description as well as its analysis.

IV
New Algorithm

At a high level our algorithm involves sampling the random times of deterministic evolution
t_{h}^{(i)}
during compilation, and building a quantum circuit that implements the trajectory operator on the initial state. Equation (
II.2
) assures the measurement statistics produced at the end of each run of a sampled circuit reproduces the measurement statistics of evolving the initial state under the map
e^{T\mathcal{L}}
. As inputs to our algorithm we will assume access to an
(\alpha,a,0)
-block-encoding
U(H)
of the Hamiltonian associated with
\mathcal{L}
, an
(\alpha_{\mu},a,0)
-block-encoding
U(L_{\mu})
for each of the
m
jump operators
L_{\mu}
associated with
\mathcal{L}
, as well as their respective inverses.

To see how our algorithm works, consider a pure state out of an ensemble which defines the density operator for a state at time
t
. Denote this pure state by
\ket{\psi(t)}
. Equation (
15
), which defines the cumulative distribution for the holding time between the current state and next jump
t_{h}
, gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(t_{h}\leq\tau|\ket{\psi(t)})

\displaystyle=1-\left\lVert e^{-i\tau\tilde{H}}\ket{\psi(t)}\right\rVert^{2}=1-\left\lVert e^{-i\tau H-\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}}\ket{\psi(t)}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\left\lVert e^{-i\tau H-\frac{\tau\Gamma}{2}\mathbb{I}}\ket{\psi(t)}\right\rVert^{2}=1-e^{-\tau\Gamma}\left\lVert e^{-i\tau H}\ket{\psi(t)}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=1-e^{-\Gamma\tau}

(41)

where the second line follows from applying the constraint of equation (
20
). The key point to note about equation (
IV
) is that the resulting cumulative distribution function is
independent
of
\ket{\psi(t)}
. This implies we can sample from both
\mathrm{Pr}(t_{h}\leq\tau|\ket{\psi(t)})
and
\mathrm{Pr}(t_{h}=\tau|\ket{\psi(t)})
without knowing the state
\rho(t)
or which pure state in the ensemble we are evolving. This condition is what allows us to sample the random times of deterministic evolution during the compilation of a circuit implementing a trajectory.

Next, we consider the types of trajectory operators associated with Lindbladians which statisfy equation (
20
). Consider the portion of a trajectory characterized by deterministic evolution given by equation (
11
). Applying our constraint of equation (
20
) allows us to write the map characterizing the deterministic evolution as:

\displaystyle G_{t}(\ket{\psi})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-it\hat{H}}\ket{\psi}}{\left\lVert e^{-it\hat{H}}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert}=\frac{e^{-itH-\frac{t}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}}\ket{\psi}}{\left\lVert e^{-itH-\frac{t}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert}=\frac{e^{-t\frac{\Gamma}{2}}e^{-itH}\ket{\psi}}{\left\lVert e^{-t\frac{\Gamma}{2}}e^{-itH}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert}=e^{-itH}\ket{\psi}.

(42)

Thus the deterministic evolution of equation (
11
) reduces to generic unitary evolution under the Hamiltonian
H
of the Lindbladian. Considering the jump dynamics next, and applying our constraint of equation (
20
), allows us to write down the jump dynamics for Lindblads that satisfy equation (
20
) as:

J_{\mu}(\ket{\psi})=\frac{L_{\mu}\ket{\psi}}{\left\lVert L_{\mu}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert}\quad\text{ occuring with probability}\quad\mathrm{Pr}(\mu|\ket{\psi})=\frac{\left\lVert L_{\mu}\ket{\psi}\right\rVert^{2}}{\Gamma}.

(43)

Defining
\mathcal{J}_{\mu}
to be the superoperator form of the jump maps defined above allows us to average over all possible jump maps, finding that that the average map

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\mathrm{Pr}(\mu|\ket{\psi})\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\mathrm{Pr}(\mu|\ket{\psi})J_{\mu}(\ket{\psi})J_{\mu}(\ket{\psi})^{\dagger}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\frac{L_{\mu}\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}}{\Gamma},

(44)

which is a valid quantum channel with Kraus operators
L_{\mu}/\sqrt{\Gamma}
. If we define the map
\mathcal{U}_{t}(\rho)=e^{-itH}\rho e^{itH}
, then we can express an arbitrary trajectory operator for a Lindbladian that satisfies equation (
20
) as

\mathcal{P}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\mu^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},\mu^{(N)}}(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})=\mathcal{U}_{(T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)})}\circ\mathcal{J}_{\mu^{(N)}}\circ\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(N)}}\cdots\mathcal{J}_{\mu^{(1)}}\circ\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(1)}}(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}).

(45)

Thus, sampled trajectories for Lindbladians which satisfy equation (
20
) are built from generic unitary evolution interrupted by quantum jumps. Pushing this analysis further, we can substitute equation (
45
) into equation (
II.2
) and perform the sums over
\{\mu^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Applying equation (
IV

\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle e^{T\mathcal{L}}\left(\rho(0)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)\mathcal{U}_{(T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)})}\circ\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(N)}}\cdots\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(1)}}(\rho(0))

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{1},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}(\rho(0)),

(46)

where

denotes the probability of sampling a trajectory that occurs over time
T
with holding times
\{t_{h}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}=\mathcal{U}_{(T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)})}\circ\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(N)}}\cdots\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(1)}}

\displaystyle p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\left(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)},S^{(N)}\leq T,S^{(N+1)}>T\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\left(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}\big|S^{(N)}\leq T,S^{(N+1)}>T\right)\mathrm{Pr}\left(S^{(N)}\leq T,S^{(N+1)}>T\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\left(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}\big|S^{(N)}\leq T,S^{(N+1)}>T\right)\left[\mathrm{Pr}\left(S^{(N)}\leq T\right)+\mathrm{Pr}\left(S^{(N+1)}>T\right)-1\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\left(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}\big|S^{(N)}\leq T,S^{(N+1)}>T\right)\left[\mathrm{Pr}\left(S^{(N)}\leq T\right)-\mathrm{Pr}\left(S^{(N+1)}\leq T\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\left(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}\big|S^{(N)}\leq T,S^{(N+1)}>T\right)\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)=N)

The equations (

U(L_{\mu})^{\dagger}
. Thus we find the total number of queries to each
U(L_{\mu})
being

O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\alpha_{\mu}^{2}}{\Gamma}}\right),

(56)

and similar for each
U(L_{\mu})^{\dagger}
.

The circuits
W
,
W^{\dagger}
, and
\mathbb{I}-2P_{1}

queries to the oracles

and

\ket{0}
to
\ket{\omega}=\sqrt{\Gamma/(\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\alpha_{\mu}^{2})}\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}(\alpha_{\mu}/\sqrt{\Gamma})\ket{\mu}
, each

and

can be implemented in
O(\log(m))
1- and 2-qubit gates. Thus the contribution to the 1- and 2-qubit gate count arising from queries to

is

O\left(\log(m)\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\alpha_{\mu}^{2}}{\Gamma}}\right).

(57)

Considering the reflections

\mathbb{I}-2P_{0}

O\left((\log(m)+a)\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\alpha_{\mu}^{2}}{\Gamma}}\right).

(58)

Equations (

) and (

) imply the total additional 1- and 2-qubit gate count to implement the circuit described in equation (

O\left((\log(m)+a)\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\alpha_{\mu}^{2}}{\Gamma}}\right)

(59)

With the randomized compilation procedure described above, along with constructions for components

and

IV.1
Analysis

To analyze the costs of the algorithm described above we must first bound the error between the quantum channel generated by the Lindbladian dynamics and the quantum channel our algorithm implements, denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{T,r}
. This bound on our simulation error, given in Lemma
7
, allows us to fix a value for
r
, the maximum number of jumps allowed to be compiled, which the assures the total simulation error is of
O(\epsilon)
. To compute the final resource estimates for implementing the algorithm, we analyze the worst-case circuit our randomized compilation procedures produces for the appropriate value of
r
and
T
.

Before proving Lemma
7
we must first characterize the map
\mathcal{E}_{T,r}
our algorithm implements. For a fixed simulation time
T
and maximum number of compilable jumps
r
, denote the probability density of compiling a circuit implementing the map
\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}
with holding times
\{t_{h}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
as
f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)
. Thus the map our algorithm implements is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{T,r}=\mathbb{E}_{f(T,r)}\left[\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right]=\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}.

(60)

All that is left is to characterize the probability density
f(T,r)
which our randomized compilation procedure implements.

Consider the randomized compilation procedure used in our algorithm. The protocol amounts to sampling a set of holding times
\{t_{h}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}\sim p(T)
and rejecting the sample if
N>r
. This procedure is repeated until a sample is accepted and the circuit implementing the map
\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}
is compiled. Given that we reject samples with
N>r
we can write down the probability density for
f(T,r)
as

f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)=\begin{cases}p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)\leq r)\text{ for }N\leq r\\
0\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}.

(61)

where the true probability density for sampling a trajectory
p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)
is defined in equation (
IV
). Thus with
f(T,r)
fully described we can proceed to proving Lemma
7
which bounds the error between the simulation and true dynamics in diamond distance.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{T,r}
denote the map our algorithm implements defined in equation (
60
). Then

d_{\diamond}(e^{T\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{T,r})\leq\left(\frac{e\Gamma T}{r}\right)^{r}e^{-\Gamma T}+(r+1)\epsilon_{H}

(62)

where
\epsilon_{H}=\sup\left(\left\{d_{\diamond}\left(\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(i)}},\mathcal{K}_{t_{h}^{(i)}}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}\cup\left\{d_{\diamond}\left(\mathcal{U}_{T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)}},\mathcal{K}_{T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)}}\right)\right\}\right)
.

Proof.

The goal is to upperbound
d_{\diamond}(e^{T\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{T,r})
, from equations (
IV
), and (
60
) it follows that

\displaystyle d_{\diamond}(e^{T\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{T,r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert e^{T\mathcal{L}}-\mathcal{E}_{T,r}\right\rVert_{\diamond}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{p(T)}\left[\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{f(T,r)}\left[\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right]\right\rVert_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}\left\lVert p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}-f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}\left(|p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)-f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)|\left\lVert\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)\left\lVert\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}-\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\right)

(63)

where the inequalities follow from the sub-additivity and sub-multiplicativity of the diamond norm. Given that
\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}
is a completely positive and trace preserving map,
\left\lVert\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}=1
, allowing us to write

\displaystyle d_{\diamond}(e^{T\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{T,r})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}\left(|p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)-f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)|\right.

\displaystyle+\left.f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)\left\lVert\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}-\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\right).

(64)

The first term in parenthesis is just the total variational distance between the probability densities
p(T)
and
f(T,r)
while the second term is just the error of a trajectory averaged over
f(T,r)
, thus we can bound the error of our simulation as:

d_{\diamond}(e^{T\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{T,r})\leq\delta(p(T),f(T,r))+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{f(T,r)}\left[\left\lVert\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}-\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\right].

(65)

Focusing first on the second term in equation (
65
) we find

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}-\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathcal{U}_{(T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)})}\circ\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(N)}}\cdots\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(1)}}-\mathcal{K}_{(T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(N)})}\circ\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{K}_{t_{h}^{(N)}}\cdots\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{K}_{t_{h}^{(1)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{U}_{(T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)})}-\mathcal{K}_{(T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)})}\right\rVert_{\diamond}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(i)}}-\mathcal{K}_{t_{h}^{(i)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}

(66)

which is just the sum over all the errors incurred by each segment of Hamiltonian simulation. Define

\epsilon_{H}=\sup\left(\left\{d_{\diamond}\left(\mathcal{U}_{t_{h}^{(i)}},\mathcal{K}_{t_{h}^{(i)}}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}\cup\left\{d_{\diamond}\left(\mathcal{U}_{T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)}},\mathcal{K}_{T-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{h}^{(i)}}\right)\right\}\right),

(67)

which represents the largest error incurred by a Hamiltonian simulation segment. Thus equation (
IV.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}-\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\leq(N+1)\epsilon_{H}

(68)

which implies

\mathbb{E}_{f(T,r)}\left[\left\lVert\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}-\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\right]\leq\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)(N+1)\epsilon_{H}.

(69)

Given that our randomized compilation procedure modeled by
f(T,r)
never compiles a circuit with more than the maximum of
r
quantum jumps, equation (
61
) implies
f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)=0
for
N>r
. Thus we find

\mathbb{E}_{f(T,r)}\left[\left\lVert\mathcal{T}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}-\mathcal{C}_{t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}}\right\rVert_{\diamond}\right]\leq(r+1)\epsilon_{H}.

(70)

Next, we focus on upperbounding the total variational distance between
p(T)
and
f(T,r)
. Using equations (
IV
) and (
61
) we calculate

\displaystyle\delta(p(T),f(T,r))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}|p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)-f(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T,r)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{N=0}^{r}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)|1-\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)\leq r)|\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{N=r+1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}p(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)};T)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{N=0}^{r}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}\mathrm{Pr}\left(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}\big|S^{(N)}\leq T,S^{(N+1)}>T\right)\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)=N)|1-\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)\leq r)|\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{N=r+1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(1)}\cdots\int_{0}^{T}dt_{h}^{(N)}\mathrm{Pr}\left(t_{h}^{(1)},\cdots,t_{h}^{(N)}\big|S^{(N)}\leq T,S^{(N+1)}>T\right)\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)=N)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{N=0}^{r}\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)=N)|1-\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)\leq r)|+\sum_{N=r+1}^{\infty}\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)=N)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)\leq r)|1-\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)\leq r)|+\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)>r)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)\leq r)+1\right)\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)>r)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(2-\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)>r)\right)\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)>r)\leq\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)>r).

(71)

All that is left is to upperbound the right-tail of the distribution for
N(T)
which is the random variable representing the number of quantum jumps occurring in an interval of time
T
. Since
N(T)\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\Gamma T)
we can apply the Chernoff bound for the upper tail of the Poisson distribution, Theorem 5.4 of
Mitzenmacher and Upfal 2005
, to find

\delta(p(T),f(T,r))\leq\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)>r)\leq\left(\frac{e\Gamma T}{r}\right)^{r}e^{-\Gamma T}.

(72)

Equations (
70
) and (
72
) allow us to write down the final upperbound on the error of our simulation as

d_{\diamond}(e^{T\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{E}_{T,r})\leq\left(\frac{e\Gamma T}{r}\right)^{r}e^{-\Gamma T}+\left(r+1\right)\epsilon_{H}.

(73)

V
Discussion

In this work we have presented a novel quantum algorithm for simulating time-independent Lindblad master equations inspired by the classical Monte-Carlo wavefunction algorithms for simulating Lindblad master equations. Interestingly, for the master equations it applies to, our algorithm achieves the optimal additive scaling between
T
and
\epsilon
with respect to queries to the Lindbladian’s jump operators. As far as we are aware, our algorithm is the first to achieve such a result. Heuristically, the key factor behind our algorithms improved jump operator query complexity comes from unraveling the Lindbladian dynamics as a stochastic process over Hilbert space. More specifically, because the unraveling chosen has quantum jump events driven by a Poisson process, the number of jump events tends to grow linearly with respect to the total simulation time, with the right-tail of the distribution decaying exponentially. Given that each jump event entails one query to the Lindbladian’s jump operators, implies that the total number of queries tends to grow linearly with respect to the time interval, with a small additive perturbation occurring from the exponentially decaying right-tail of the distribution. This allows us to significantly minimize the query complexity of our algorithm to be as close to linear as possible.

Although our query complexity to the Lindbladian’s jump operators is optimal, the algorithm we have presented above is still sub-optimal across several areas. For one, it requires the Lindbladian to satisfy the constraint of equation (
20
). This suggests lifting the constraint of equation (
20
) as a first avenue for improvement. Another possible future direction involves improving the algorithms query complexity to the Lindbladian’s Hamiltonian. Interestingly, there is a mismatch between our algorithms Hamiltonian query complexity and its jump operator query complexity. On one hand our jump operator query complexity is optimal but we sacrifice the optimal Hamiltonian query complexity that algorithms such as quantum signal processing achieve. It would be interesting to explore whether the Hamiltonian query complexity can be improved or whether there exists a trade-off between the two resources.

In this section we provide an alternative way to characterize the set of Lindbladians for which our algorithm can be applied to. As discussed in the main text our algorithm is applicable only to Lindbladians with jump operators

Lemma 9

\mathcal{L}\in\algL

\mathcal{R}\in\mathbf{CPTP}

\mathcal{L}=-i\mathrm{ad}_{H}+\Gamma(\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{I})

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}\in\algL

\mathcal{L}

\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\left((L_{\mu}\rho L_{\mu}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu},\rho\}\right)=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}\rho L_{\mu}^{\dagger}-\frac{\Gamma}{2}\{\mathbb{I},\rho\}=-i[H,\rho]+\Gamma\left(\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{I}\right),

(S2)

where we have defined the map
\mathcal{R}(\rho)=\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}K_{\mu}\rho K_{\mu}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\Gamma}\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}\rho L_{\mu}^{\dagger}
and
K_{\mu}=L_{\mu}/\sqrt{\Gamma}

\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}K_{\mu}^{\dagger}K_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\Gamma}\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}=\mathbb{I}.

(S3)

Thus
\mathcal{R}\in\mathbf{CPTP}
, implying the forward direction.

For the backwards direction let
\mathcal{L}=-i\mathrm{ad}_{H}+\Gamma\left(\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{I}\right)
with
\mathcal{R}\in\mathbf{CPTP}
. Choosing a Kraus representation for
\mathcal{R}
we find

\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-i[H,\rho]+\Gamma\left(\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}K_{\mu}\rho K_{\mu}^{\dagger}-\rho\right)=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}\Gamma K_{\mu}\rho K_{\mu}^{\dagger}-\frac{\Gamma}{2}\{\mathbb{I},\rho\}=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}\rho L_{\mu}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\Gamma\mathbb{I},\rho\},

(S4)

where we have defined
L_{\mu}=\sqrt{\Gamma}K_{\mu}
. The above equation is manifestly a Lindbladian dissipator with jump operators satisfying

\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}=\Gamma\sum_{\mu=1}^{m}K_{\mu}^{\dagger}K_{\mu}=\Gamma\mathbb{I},

(S5)

thus
\mathcal{L}\in\algL
.
∎

Lemma
9
allows a simple physical characterize Lindbladians in
\algL
as those which are a sum of a Hamiltonian portion and a dissipator which can be expressed as proportional to a difference between applying a quantum channel and not.

Appendix B

In this section we provide a proof of the well known result that events which occur with holding times
t_{h}^{(i)}
where
\mathrm{Pr}(t_{h}^{(i)}\leq\tau)=1-e^{-\Gamma\tau}
have a counting process which is Poissonian. To begin we define the random variables
N(T)
and
S^{(n)}
according to our holding time random variables. Define the total waiting time for
n
events to occur to be

S^{(n)}=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{h}^{(i)}\text{ for }n>0\\
0\text{ for }n=0\end{cases}.

(S6)

From
S^{(n)}
we can define the total number of events occurring with-in a time interval
T
to be

N(T)=\sup\{n:S^{(n)}\leq T\}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\bm{1}\{S_{n}\leq T\}

(S7)

where
\bm{1}
denotes the indicator function, returning one if event
S_{n}\leq T
occurs and zero otherwise.

Lemma 10
.

For any
T>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)=n)=\mathrm{Pr}(S^{(n)}\leq T)-\mathrm{Pr}(S^{(n+1)}\leq T)

(S8)

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)=n)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}(\{N(T)\geq n\}\cap\{N(T)<n+1\})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)\geq n)+\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)<n+1)-\mathrm{Pr}(\{N(T)\geq n\}\cup\{N(T)<n+1\})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)\geq n)-\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)\geq n+1)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}(S^{(n)}\leq T)-\mathrm{Pr}(S^{(n+1)}\leq T)

(S9)

where the last line follows from the fact that the event
\{N(T)\geq n\}
occurs only when the
n
th event occurs before some time
T
. Thus events
\{N(T)\geq n\}
and
\{S^{(n)}\leq T\}
occur together.
∎

Theorem 11
.

Let
N(T)
be defined according to equation (
S7
) and
\mathrm{Pr}(t_{h}^{(i)}\leq\tau)=1-e^{-\Gamma\tau}
for
\Gamma>0
. Then
N(T)\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\Gamma T)=e^{-\Gamma T}\frac{(\Gamma T)^{N}}{N!}
.

Proof.

Consider the probability of waiting longer than
T
for
n
events to occur, thus we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(S_{n}>T)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}(t_{h}^{(1)}>T)+\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{Pr}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}t_{h}^{(i)}>T-t\right)\mathrm{Pr}(t_{h}=t)dt

\displaystyle=e^{-\Gamma T}+\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma e^{-\Gamma t}\mathrm{Pr}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}t_{h}^{(i)}>T-t\right)dt.

(S10)

Defining
G_{n}(T)=\mathrm{Pr}(S_{n}>T)
, we can write the above equation as

\displaystyle G_{n}(T)

\displaystyle=e^{-\Gamma T}+\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma e^{-\Gamma t}G_{n-1}(T-t)dt.

(S11)

Proposing the ansatz
G_{n}(T)=\sum_{a=0}^{n-1}e^{-\Gamma T}\frac{(\Gamma T)^{a}}{a!}
, we can show it satisfies the recurrence relation above by plugging it into the right hand side of the above equation. We find

\displaystyle G_{n}(T)

\displaystyle=e^{-\Gamma T}+\int_{0}^{T}\Gamma e^{\Gamma t}\left(\sum_{a=0}^{n-2}e^{-\Gamma(T-t)}\frac{(\Gamma(T-t))^{a}}{a!}\right)dt=e^{-\Gamma T}+\Gamma e^{-\Gamma T}\sum_{a=0}^{n-2}\frac{\Gamma^{a}}{a!}\int_{0}^{T}(T-t)^{a}dt

\displaystyle=e^{-\Gamma T}+e^{-\Gamma T}\sum_{a=0}^{n-2}\frac{\Gamma^{a+1}}{(a+1)!}T^{a+1}=\sum_{a=0}^{n-1}e^{-\Gamma T}\frac{(\Gamma T)^{a}}{a!},

(S12)

thus
G_{n}(T)=\sum_{a=0}^{n-1}e^{-\Gamma T}\frac{(\Gamma T)^{a}}{a!}
is solution to equation (
S11
).

Applying Lemma
10
we find

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(N(T)=n)

\displaystyle=(1-\mathrm{Pr}(S_{n}>T))-(1-\mathrm{Pr}(S_{n+1}>T))=G_{n+1}(T)-G_{n}(T)

\displaystyle=e^{-\Gamma T}\frac{(\Gamma T)^{n}}{n!}=\mathrm{Pois}(\Gamma T)

(S13)

thus concluding the proof.
∎
