Brief intervention can change the trajectory of youth substance use
Authors: Shannon Charlebois, Shawn Kelly
Published: 2026

This issue of CMAJ features the updated Canadian guideline on screening for high-risk drinking and alcohol use disorder, which recommends screening everyone aged 12 years and older.1 Screening patients for the use of alcohol and other substances is important, but to be effective, a positive screen needs follow-up with intervention. This is particularly important for youth, because the earlier substance use starts, the higher the risk of future misuse and addiction.2

According to the 2024 Alcohol and Drug Use Among Students in Canada report, 22% of students in grades 7 to 12 reported alcohol use within the past 30 days, 2% had driven within an hour of drinking, and 10% had been driven by someone who had been drinking in the last hour.3 Although overall rates of teen drinking have declined, the use of other risky substances has increased. In a previous editorial, we discussed rising rates of youth opioid use; the fastest-growing cohort of opioid users are in grades 7 to 9.3–5 Nationally, the use of illegal drugs — including amphetamines, ecstasy, hallucinogens, salvia, heroin, cocaine, synthetic cannabinoids, benzylpiperazine, bath salts, and tryptamines — was reported at 9%.3 In the 2023 Ontario Student Drug Use and Health Survey, 21.8% of teenagers had used opioids for nonmedical purposes at least once.5

Both the landscape and the culture of youth substance use have changed. The social media platforms that form the fabric of adolescents’ social lives are rife with content that promotes substance use, and they even advertise local dealers. Novel substances, high-risk behaviours, and toxic drug supply are all factors that point to high risks for youth who use substances. A pervasive myth that teen substance abuse is simply “healthy experimentation” fuels negligence in care. The risks of substance use start with the first experiment.

We discuss why youth who screen positive for substance use should, at the very least, receive an immediate brief intervention. These focused and structured conversations, typically 5 to 10 minutes in length, use either motivational interviewing or cognitive behavioural therapy principles to increase a patient’s understanding of risks and benefits, and negotiate realistic and specific drug reduction or abstinence goals.6 The brief intervention has been endorsed by the World Health Organization since the 1980s and in current pediatric and addiction medicine guidelines.7,8 It can be delivered by anyone who cares for youth, is a cornerstone of management for substance abuse in adolescents, and is a fundamental part of the Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment model.9 When used as a practice standard in pediatric care, brief intervention can lead to marked benefits from reducing adolescent substance use and associated harms at the population level.9 The content of the intervention is tailored to the characteristics of the substance and of the patient in a moment of potential change. For example, after a positive screen, a physician might respond, “Thank you for opening up about the pills that you’ve been taking. I know that when a bottle from a pharmacy is involved, it might seem safe, but let’s talk about the realities of overdose of prescription pills and the dangers of mixing those with alcohol.”

A conversation about disclosed substance use can make a difference even if further treatment is not readily available. A 2018 Cochrane review of 69 randomized controlled trials on brief intervention alone in patients presenting to primary care and emergency departments showed a significant reduction in alcohol consumption (mean difference −20 g/wk, 95% confidence interval −28 to −12); even 1 intervention session was effective.10 A 2020 meta-analysis found that a brief intervention using motivational interviewing reduced heavy alcohol use, alcohol use days, and substance-related problems in adolescents.11 In a desert of available treatment programs, when a health care provider takes a few moments to talk with a teen about substance use, some of it seeps in.

Increasingly, addiction medicine experts agree that asking about features of substance withdrawal during a brief intervention can increase its efficacy, because doing so can identify teens for whom substance use is already a substantial problem and prompt appropriate intervention.12

Perceived barriers to implementing a brief intervention are worth overcoming, given the benefits. Clinicians may think they lack training, don’t have the time, or have more important competing clinical priorities.11 However, the brief intervention is low cost, with low potential for harm, and it helps to nurture a relationship with a teen patient that can pay off later. It offers a tool to counteract progression rather than leave clinicians feeling powerless. Brief interventions can be effective for youth no matter where they are on the substance use continuum.
