Targeting Melanoma‐Specific Tyrosinase Disrupts Cytoskeleton Dynamics for Precision Apoptosis Induction by a De Novo Designed Cyclic Peptide
Authors: Ruoyang Zhao, Xiaowei Wang, Jiajia Hu, Yonghan Zhang, Xinmin Zhao, Qianqian Hu, Yajin Wen, Yuxin Jiao, Huabin Zhu, Fan Yang, Qingqing Sun, Jun Guo, Feng Zhang, Min Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMelanoma remains highly aggressive and refractory to conventional therapies, with current treatments often limited by toxicity and acquired resistance, underscoring the urgent need for targeted strategies with improved safety and durable efficacy. Here, we report a tyrosinase‐guided cyclic peptide, c‐RGDKYQ, that exploits the overexpression of tyrosinase in melanoma cells to trigger intracellular oxidation and in situ assembly of supramolecular nanostructures. These enzyme‐instructed nanosystems selectively destabilize the actin cytoskeleton, thereby suppressing B16 melanoma cell migration, adhesion, and proliferation, and inducing apoptosis without requiring exogenous drug payloads or complex delivery carriers. This selective activity is strictly dependent on tyrosinase expression, as the linear control peptide lacking conformational constraint showed minimal cytotoxicity. In a murine B16 tumor model, c‐RGDKYQ exhibits potent antitumor activity with minimal off‐target effects and favorable tolerability. Transcriptomic profiling further confirms cytoskeletal collapse, evidenced by coordinated downregulation of actin/tubulin genes and compensatory upregulation of regulatory factors. This work establishes a proof‐of‐concept for a minimalist, enzyme‐responsive peptide platform that achieves targeted melanoma therapy through physical cytoskeletal disruption. Beyond melanoma, this paradigm of enzyme‐directed supramolecular assembly may offer a translatable framework for treating other malignancies characterized by dysregulated metabolic enzymes.

Introduction

Melanoma ranks among the most formidable and lethal malignancies, distinguished by its swift progression, potent metastatic capacity, and robust resistance to established therapies, including chemotherapy and conventional immunotherapy [1]. Despite groundbreaking strides in targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors, their clinical efficacy is frequently undermined by systemic toxicities, off‐target impacts, and the inevitable onset of drug resistance [2]. These profound hurdles emphasize the critical demand for revolutionary therapeutic paradigms. A distinctive feature of melanoma cells is the overexpression of tyrosinase, a melanocyte‐specific enzyme central to melanin biosynthesis [3]. Tyrosinase can catalyze the oxidation of tyrosine (Tyr) and its derivatives within melanoma cells, which makes the tyrosinase as a promising biomarker and activator for tumor‐specific therapeutic systems [4]. Recent advancements have harnessed tyrosinase activity to develop enzyme‐responsive systems, such as prodrugs, nanoparticles, and pigment‐mediated therapies [5, 6]. These strategies primarily rely on tyrosinase‐mediated oxidation to activate therapeutic agents or induce localized nanoparticle aggregation within melanoma cells, thereby exerting cytotoxic effects through the generation of reactive oxygen species or controlled drug release. However, the dependence on exogenous payloads or intricate delivery systems presents substantial challenges in terms of scalability and clinical translation, ultimately constraining their broader applicability.

Tyrosinase catalyzes the initial and rate‐limiting steps of melanin synthesis, converting Tyr to DOPA and subsequently to dopaquinoine. Tyrosinase, highly expressed in melanoma cells, plays a significant role in melanoma pathogenesis [3]. This overexpression provides a unique opportunity for targeted therapies, as normal cells exhibit low levels of tyrosinase activity, reducing off‐target effects. Previously, we designed a series of sequences involving Tyr and lysine (Lys), which are crucial for controlling self‐assembly and functionality [7, 8]. The possible mechanism of action is driven by tyrosinase‐catalyzed oxidation and sequence‐guided self‐assembly, enabling the formation of fluorescent or melanin‐like nanostructures. By leveraging the unique overexpression of tyrosinase in melanoma cells, an unprecedent opportunity arises to selectively disrupt cytoskeletal dynamics through enzyme‐triggered systems [9]. Such an approach should target key cellular processes, and meanwhile address the limitations of current enzyme‐responsive therapies by eliminating the need for external drug payloads and complex delivery mechanisms [10, 11]. This strategy is promising for significantly enhancing therapeutic selectivity while minimizing systemic toxicity, providing a novel framework for melanoma treatment.

Additionally, the actin cytoskeleton, a critical component of cellular architecture and function, has emerged as a compelling therapeutic target [12]. Actin dynamics regulates key processes, such as cell motility, proliferation, and adhesion, which are essential for tumor invasion and metastasis [13]. Disrupting cytoskeletal dynamics represents a promising approach to impair these functions in cancer cells [14]. However, direct targeting cytoskeletal structures with tumor specificity has proven challenging, due to existing strategies being often hindered by off‐target toxicity and insufficient therapeutic effects [15].

The discovery of the Arg–Gly–Asp (RGD) tripeptide sequence in the early 1980s marked a pivotal breakthrough in understanding cell–extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions. The RGD sequence was found to serve as a universal recognition site for integrin receptors—a family of transmembrane proteins critical for cellular processes including adhesion, migration, and signaling [16, 17]. Synthetic RGD peptides could competitively inhibit cell attachment to fibronectin, demonstrating the sequence's essential role in integrin‐mediated binding. Over the past four decades, RGD research has evolved from fundamental biochemical characterization to sophisticated biomedical applications [18]. Structural analyses elucidated that RGD‐containing peptides adopt distinct conformations (cyclic vs. linear) that modulate integrin binding affinity and specificity. Notably, cyclic RGD variants demonstrated enhanced receptor selectivity, driving their development as antiangiogenic agents in cancer therapy [19, 20]. Parallel advances in nanotechnology and biomaterials leveraged the bio‐adhesive property of RGD to engineer functionalized scaffolds for tissue regeneration and targeted drug delivery systems [19, 21].

In this study, we design a novel cyclic peptide system (c‐RGDKYQ) that leverages the overexpression of tyrosinase in melanoma cells to achieve in situ self‐assembly. The cyclic peptide is synthesized through an ortho‐phthalaldehyde (OPA)‐mediated cyclization reaction under mild aqueous conditions. Upon tyrosinase‐catalyzed oxidation, c‐RGDKYQ generates reactive quinone structures, which drives the formation of supramolecular nanostructures. These nanostructures selectively disrupt the actin cytoskeleton dynamics within melanoma cells, impairing cellular motility, proliferation, and adhesion, and ultimately inducing apoptosis. Notably, this approach simplifies the therapeutic design and facilitates its translational potential. Both in vitro and in vivo experimental results validate the high selectivity of c‐RGDKYQ for melanoma cells, demonstrating significant tumor growth suppression with minimal adverse effects. These findings, collectively, establish c‐RGDKYQ as a robust and efficient framework for enzyme‐responsive cancer therapies, specifically targeting tyrosinase‐expressing melanoma and other similar tumors.

Results

Characterization of the c‐RGDKYQ Peptide

The cyclic peptide (c‐RGDKYQ) was successfully synthesized using an OPA‐mediated cyclization reaction (Figure 1A). First, we tested whether this peptide can be selectively activated by tyrosinase via undergoing oxidation and further self‐assembly. After exposure to tyrosinase, the peptide solution color changed from transparent to deep yellow, indicating the formation of oxidative products and nanostructures (Figure 1B). UV–vis spectroscopy revealed characteristic absorption peaks between 330 and 450 nm, corresponding to the formation of aromatic and quinone structures through tyrosinase‐mediated oxidation of Tyr residues (Figure 1C). Fluorescence spectroscopy showed a strong emission peak at 450 nm when excited at 340 nm, confirming the structural transformation necessary for self‐assembly (Figure 1D).

Peptide solution incubated with tyrosinase self‐assembled into nanostructures with a narrow size distribution, which was much larger and more uniform compared with those of untreated peptide aggregates (Figure 1E). Zeta potential, a key parameter indicating the surface charge of nanoparticles, is critically related to the colloidal stability of a dispersion. This transition was accompanied by changes in zeta‐potential, which reflected the increased hydrophobicity and stability of the formed nanostructures (Figure 1F). High‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis further confirmed the enzyme‐specificity of the process, as oxidation and nanostructure formation did not occur in control samples lacking tyrosinase (Figure S1). Additionally, mass spectrograms are provided for the linear precursor RGDKYQ (Figure S2) and the cyclic peptide c‐RGDKYQ (Figure S3), verifying the molecular weights and structural integrity. FTIR indicates that the cyclization involving aromatic groups causes a change in the absorption of C─H out‐of‐plane bending vibrations in the benzene ring at 925 cm−1. Both linear and cyclic peptides, under the catalysis of tyrosinase, exhibit oxidation and polymerization of the phenolic hydroxyl group on the Tyr amine, as evidenced by the reduction in characteristic FTIR peaks between 1138 and 1208 cm−1 and an increase of 1400 cm−1 (Figure 1G). The tyrosinase‐mediated oxidation of Tyr residues to reactive quinones could promote intermolecular crosslinking and the formation of melanin‐like polymers that subsequently assemble into nanostructures (Figure 1H). This targeted transformation underscores the unique capability of the cyclic peptide to exploit the enzymatic environment of melanoma cells for selective activation and assembly.

Selective Cytotoxicity to Melanoma Cells

The cytotoxic effects of the linear peptide (RGDKYQ) and its oxidized form (c‐RGDKYQ) were systematically evaluated across melanoma cells (B16) and two other cell lines: mouse fibroblast cells (L929) and human osteosarcoma epithelial cells (U2OS). RGDKYQ exhibited negligible cytotoxicity in B16 melanoma cells at all tested concentrations. In contrast, c‐RGDKYQ demonstrated a concentration‐dependent cytotoxic effect, significantly reducing cell viability in B16 melanoma cells but sparing the L929 and U2OS cells (Figure 2A–D). This selective cytotoxicity underscores the tyrosinase‐mediated activation of the peptide within melanoma cells, which overexpress the enzyme. Morphological observations of B16 cells treated with c‐RGDKYQ revealed dose‐dependent cellular damage, including disruption of the cellular architecture and increased cell death (Figures 2E and S2). These findings align with the role of the oxidized cyclic peptide in impairing cytoskeletal integrity, a critical aspect of cellular survival and adhesion [22]. Apoptosis analysis demonstrated that with the increase of c‐RGDKYQ concentration, the number of apoptosis increased significantly (Figure 2F,G). Calcein AM/PI costaining assays (Figure S4) demonstrate the concentration‐dependent effects on B16 melanoma cells. Untreated controls show predominant green fluorescence (live cells), while increasing concentrations (50, 100, and 200 µg/mL) lead to a shift toward red fluorescence (dead cells via propidium iodide [PI] staining). To assess the impact of c‐RGDKYQ on cell motility, a wound‐healing assay was performed on B16 melanoma cells. Untreated cells and cells exposed to RGDKYQ showed progressive wound closure over 24 h. However, treatment with c‐RGDKYQ effectively inhibited cell migration, as evidenced by the delayed wound closure compared with control groups (Figure 2H,I). These results demonstrate that the oxidized cyclic peptide disrupts cellular motility, further supporting its antimelanoma activity. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the selective cytotoxicity and antimotility effects of c‐RGDKYQ in melanoma cells, highlighting its potential for targeted melanoma therapy, while minimizing off‐target effects in other cells.

Dynamic Disruption of Actin Cytoskeleton

The actin and microtubule cytoskeleton is a core structure governing cell morphology, motility, and division. Disrupting its dynamic polymerization can lead to cell cycle arrest and death. Treatment with c‐RGDKYQ induced rapid and progressive cytoskeletal disassembly in B16 melanoma cells, as visualized by time‐resolved immunofluorescence (Figure 3A). At baseline (0 h), control cells displayed well‐organized actin stress fibers (red phalloidin) radiating from the perinuclear region and an intact microtubule network (green β‐tubulin) arranged in an aster‐like order, consistent with the mesenchymal morphology typical of motile melanoma cells. Within 1 h, early disruption emerged: actin filaments shortened and fragmented, while microtubules showed initial bending and depolymerization at peripheral ends. By 2 h, cytoskeletal integrity deteriorated markedly, with actin condensing into punctate aggregates and microtubules exhibiting pronounced curvature and gaps (white arrows indicating pore‐like defects). At 4 h, near‐complete cytoskeletal collapse occurred, leading to cellular rounding accompanied by diffuse actin remnants and fragmented microtubules, ultimately culminating in vacuolization and fragmentation—hallmarks of apoptotic execution. Magnified insets (dashed boxes) accentuated these dynamic alterations, confirming the observed changes. A schematic model (Figure 3B) illustrates this temporal cascade: intact filamentous structures (0 h) give way to disorganized intermediates (1–2 h), progressing to degraded remnants (4 h), consistent with tyrosinase‐mediated peptide oxidation and self‐assembly.

Twenty highly expressed genes associated with cytoskeleton regulation (such as myosin, actin, etc.), including Myh10, Arhgef37, Kif5a, Myo5c, Ezr, Myo1e, Camsap2, Myo1g, Cav2, Fina, Tpm1, Actn1 (marked in red for actin‑related genes), Lcp1, Actg1, Tubb5 (marked in green for tubulin‑related genes), Tln2, Lima1, Mylk, Lasp1, and Lmod1, were selected from the results for hierarchical clustering analysis. The heatmap (Figure 4B) displays the Z‑score expression levels of cytoskeleton‑related DEGs. The color gradient represents standardized gene expression scores (Z‑score), with the scale ranging from orange (high expression, Z‑score = 3) to purple (low expression, Z‑score = −2). The dendrogram at the top illustrates sample clustering, indicating a clear distinction in expression patterns between the Ctrl and c‑RGDKYQ groups. These genes could be divided into two main clusters: the upper cluster (e.g., Myh10 to Cav2) appeared predominantly orange (upregulated) in the c‑RGDKYQ group and purple (downregulated) in the Ctrl group, whereas the lower cluster (e.g., Tpm1 to Lmod1) showed the opposite trend, being mostly purple (downregulated) in the c‑RGDKYQ group and orange (upregulated) in the Ctrl group. Compared with the control (Ctrl), c‑RGDKYQ treatment markedly altered the expression profiles of these cytoskeleton‑related genes, potentially implicating processes, such as actin regulation, cell migration, or adhesion. Myosin family genes including Myh10, Myo5c, and Myo1e were notably downregulated upon c‑RGDKYQ treatment, which may disturb actin polymerization and cell migration, thereby impairing melanoma cell motility and adhesion (Figure 4B). Expression changes in Rho GTPase activators and kinases such as Arhgef37, Lima1, and Mylk further suggest that c‑RGDKYQ disrupts cytoskeletal stability via modulation of Rho signaling, analogous to the reported critical role of myosin II in melanoma drug resistance and cell motility [23]. The reduced expression of microtubule‑associated genes, such as Kif5a, Camsap2, and Tubb5 in the treated group implies disruption of the microtubule network, which may cooperate with actin remodeling to enhance proapoptotic effects, consistent with the mechanism whereby cytoskeletal remodeling promotes invasion in melanoma [24]. Differential expression of actin‑binding protein genes including Ezr, Actn1, Tpm1, and Actg1 confirms that c‑RGDKYQ specifically interferes with actin filament assembly, leading to cell shape collapse and proliferation inhibition, supporting its role as a tyrosinase‑targeted enzyme‑responsive therapeutic strategy. Moreover, downregulation of focal adhesion and filament‑regulating genes such as Cav2, Lcp1, Tln2, Lasp1, and Lmod1 underscores the disruption of integrin‑mediated cell–matrix interactions, which amplifies the anti‑tumor efficacy; the involvement of these genes in melanoma pathogenesis has been corroborated by bioinformatic analyses [25]. Collectively, these transcriptomic differences reveal that c‑RGDKYQ achieves precise apoptosis induction through multidimensional cytoskeletal perturbation, offering a novel framework for overcoming drug resistance in melanoma.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of transcriptomic data (Figure 4D) revealed that the significantly enriched biological processes (BP) in the c‐RGDKYQ‐treated group included actin filament bundle assembly, actin filament bundle organization, negative regulation of cell motility, and negative regulation of cell migration, indicating that the peptide interferes with actin cytoskeleton dynamics to regulate cell adhesion and motility (Figure 4D). Cellular component terms were primarily enriched in cell–substrate junctions, while molecular function terms involved actin binding and cell adhesion molecule binding, further confirming the targeted disruption of cytoskeletal structures. Multigroup volcano plot analysis (Figure 4E) revealed significant differences in the expression of cytoskeleton‐related genes between the c‐RGDKYQ treatment group and the Ctrl. Specifically, genes such as Tubb2b and Myh10 were upregulated (orange dots, log2FC > 0), whereas genes including Tubb5, Actn1, Krt87, Actb, Tuba8, and Actg1 were downregulated (purple dots, log2FC < 0). Gray dots indicate no significant change (NS) (Figure 4E). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis combined with chord diagram visualization revealed that the DEGs in the c‐RGDKYQ‐treated group were significantly enriched in pathways such as Focal adhesion, Axon guidance, Rap1 signaling pathway, cytoskeleton in muscle cells, phospholipase D signaling pathway, and regulation of actin cytoskeleton. These pathways form an interactive network involving genes including Actb, Rac3, Vasp, Fyn, Braf, and Pdgfa to regulate cytoskeletal dynamics, adhesion, and migration (Figure 4F).

The Cyclic Peptide Reduced the Adhesion Ability of B16 Cells Relating to the Mechanical‐Related Signals

Traction force microscopy (TFM) results (Figure 5A,B) demonstrated that treatment with the cyclic peptide c‐RGDKYQ significantly reduced the traction forces exerted by B16 cells on the ECM. Control cells displayed intense red high‐traction regions (peak values exceeding 160 Pa), indicating strong mechanical forces generated through focal adhesions. In contrast, the heatmaps of the c‐RGDKYQ‐treated group showed markedly diminished red areas, predominantly replaced by blue low‐force zones. Statistical analysis revealed that the average traction force in the experimental group decreased to approximately one‐fourth of that in the control group, suggesting a substantial weakening of cell–matrix adhesion (Table S2). E‐cadherin immunofluorescence results showed that c‐RGDKYQ treatment led to significant downregulation of this intercellular adhesion molecule. In Figure 5C,D, control cells exhibited strong E‐cadherin signals (green) localized predominantly at cell–cell contact interfaces, forming a distinct membranous network. In the experimental group, E‐cadherin fluorescence intensity was drastically reduced, with nearly no continuous membrane localization observed. DAPI staining confirmed intact nuclei, and merged images verified the altered distribution of E‐cadherin. Quantification of relative fluorescence intensity showed that E‐cadherin expression in the experimental group was reduced by approximately 70% compared with the control (Table S3).

Ultrastructural Morphology of Apoptosis After Treatment With Cyclic Peptide

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis (Figure 7) revealed that treatment of B16 cells with the cyclic peptide c‐RGDKYQ induced typical and significant ultrastructural features of apoptosis in a time‐dependent manner. At 0 h, cells exhibited intact architecture. Mitochondria (blue arrows) appeared as oval shapes with well‐defined, regularly arranged cristae. The Golgi apparatus (red arrows) displayed normal morphology, and the cytoplasm was dense and uniform without evident vacuolization (white arrows) (Figure 7A). Following 2 h of treatment, cells began to exhibit early apoptotic characteristics. Numerous vacuoles appeared in the cytoplasm and the overall density of the cytoplasm was reduced (white arrows). Mitochondria were noticeably swollen with disrupted or blurred cristae. The Golgi apparatus (red arrows) showed dilation and fragmentation (Figure 7B). After 4 h of treatment, cells progressed to a late apoptotic stage with pronounced damage. The cytoplasm was highly vacuolated, containing numerous vacuoles of varying sizes (white arrows). Mitochondria (blue arrows) were severely swollen and vacuolated, with complete loss of internal cristae. The cytoplasm and organelles have been extensively damaged and is no longer distinguishable, representing the classic ultrastructural morphology of late apoptosis (Figure 7C).

Therapeutic Efficacy in a Murine Melanoma Model

The therapeutic efficacy of c‐RGDKYQ was assessed in a murine melanoma model using B16 melanoma cells grafted into the upper right side of the mice. The nude mice were treated according to the schedule shown in Figure 8A. Body weight measurements throughout the study showed no significant differences between treatment groups, suggesting minimal systemic toxicity of c‐RGDKYQ (Figure 8B). Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining of tissue sections (Figure S5) was used to assess biological safety, showing no significant adverse effects in the tested models. We next found that tumor growth was markedly inhibited in the c‐RGDKYQ‐treated group compared with both the Ctrl and RGDKYQ‐treated groups. Tumor volume measurements over time revealed pronounced suppression of tumor growth in the c‐RGDKYQ group (Figure 8C). The final tumor weights further confirmed the efficacy of c‐RGDKYQ, with treated mice exhibiting markedly smaller tumor masses than Ctrl (Figure 8D). Representative photographs of tumor‐bearing mice and excised tumors illustrate the substantial tumor reduction achieved by c‐RGDKYQ treatment (Figures 8E and S6). After performing HE staining on the tumor sections of the untreated group and the linear peptide group, it was observed that the tissues were loosely and diffusely distributed. In contrast, the tumor tissues of the mice treated with c‐RGDKYQ were more compact and surrounded by fibrous tissues, indicating that the tissues were undergoing an active remodeling process and that the drug effectively reduced the spread of the tumors (Figure 8F).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that the cyclic peptide c‐RGDKYQ exerts potent antitumor efficacy in the classical high‐tyrosinase‐expressing B16 mouse melanoma model by leveraging tyrosinase‐mediated self‐assembly to disrupt the actin cytoskeleton, thereby inducing apoptosis. The linear RGDKYQ peptide showed weak but detectable activity in some assays, likely due to residual tyrosinase responsiveness or nonspecific interactions. However, its markedly lower efficacy compared with c‑RGDKYQ underscores the importance of conformational constraint for efficient enzyme‑triggered self‑assembly and cytoskeletal disruption. Mechanistically, c‐RGDKYQ promotes tyrosinase‐catalyzed oxidation and subsequent intermolecular interactions, such as π–π stacking, facilitating in situ nanostructure formation [26]. These nanostructures physically dismantle the actin cytoskeleton, leading to significant reductions in cell adhesion forces, as quantified by TFM, and molecular disruptions evidenced by weakened E‐cadherin expression in adherens junctions and dissociation of focal adhesions. This cascade results in impaired cell morphology, motility, and intercellular/ECM communication, ultimately activating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. Ultrastructural analyses further corroborate these findings, revealing time‐dependent mitochondrial swelling and vacuolization by 2 h—indicative of mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and cytochrome c release—progressing to Golgi apparatus fragmentation and dilation by 4 h, signifying Golgi stress involvement. Concurrently, the peptide interferes with the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway: RhoA accumulates, potentially enhancing cellular stress responses, while ROCK downregulation inhibits myosin light chain phosphorylation, and Cofilin upregulation promotes actin depolymerization, collectively contributing to cytoskeletal instability and apoptosis [27]. The superior efficacy of the cyclic form over its linear counterpart, RGDKYQ, stems from conformational rigidity that enhances integrin binding affinity (e.g., to αvβ3 overexpressed on melanoma cells) [28], optimizes spatial orientation for tyrosinase catalysis, and directs efficient self‐assembly, transforming the peptide into an intelligent, modular therapeutic system integrating targeting, enzyme responsiveness, and cytoskeletal disruption [29].

This study demonstrates that the antitumor efficacy of the cyclic peptide c‐RGDKYQ is significantly superior to that of its linear counterpart RGDKYQ, primarily attributable to the conformational stability and functional synergy conferred by cyclization [30]. The conformational constraint likely immobilizes the key Tyr (Y) and Lys (K) residues in a spatial orientation more favorable for tyrosinase‐catalyzed oxidation, enabling more efficient initiation of the enzymatic reaction. Most importantly, the cyclic backbone provides a directional driving force for intermolecular interactions between the oxidized quinone groups, which is crucial for effective self‐assembly into in situ nanostructures. These nanostructures act as direct executors that physically disrupt the actin cytoskeleton [22]. In contrast, the linear peptide RGDKYQ possesses a flexible and variable conformation, resulting not only in poor in vivo stability but also in low efficiency of enzymatic reaction and weak self‐assembly capability, failing to initiate the aforementioned integrated “targeting–activation–assembly–disruption” therapeutic cascade. Therefore, cyclization is not merely a simple structural modification but a core design strategy that transforms the peptide from a mere recognition sequence into an efficient and orderly intelligent therapeutic system by integrating and optimizing the targeting, enzyme‐responsive, and self‐assembling modules.

A key innovation lies in the integration of the RGD motif within c‐RGDKYQ, which, beyond stabilizing conformation, may facilitate initial cell recognition and internalization via binding to integrins like αvβ3 [31]. However, the complete ineffectiveness of linear RGDKYQ underscores that tyrosinase‐triggered oxidation and self‐assembly are indispensable for cytotoxicity, with RGD serving as a nondecisive, auxiliary role. This high selectivity for tyrosinase‐overexpressing melanoma cells minimizes off‐target effects on normal cells, positioning c‐RGDKYQ as a promising enzyme‐responsive strategy for melanoma therapy [32]. Future studies will systematically deconstruct the precise contribution of the RGD motif to cellular uptake, subcellular localization, and ultimate efficacy by synthesizing RGE control peptides, conducting integrin blocking experiments, and comparing the effects of replacing RGD with different targeting peptides. This will aid in optimizing the design of next‐generation peptides for the precise assembly of modular functionalities.

Despite these advancements, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The study was confined to the B16 model [33], which may not fully capture the heterogeneity of human melanomas, where tyrosinase activity varies. Future investigations should evaluate the strategy's universality across diverse human melanoma cell lines, including high‐tyrosinase expressors like MNT‐1 and variable expressors such as A375 and SK‐MEL‐28. Although the design of this peptide capitalizes on the aberrantly high tyrosinase activity within the tumor microenvironment, due to the tumor heterogeneity in the secretion level of tyrosinase [34], future studies need systematically evaluate its potential impact on normal pigmented tissues. Subsequent investigations will include: assessing the cytotoxicity of c‐RGDKYQ in normal human epidermal melanocytes; evaluating its selectivity using 3D skin models or ocular tissue models containing melanocytes; conducting long‐term histopathological examination of skin and eye tissues in pigmented tumor‐bearing animal models to fully define its therapeutic window. Additionally, detailed pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies will be performed to quantify peptide concentrations in various tissues, thereby establishing the relationship between local drug exposure and potential off‐target effects. Compared with current melanoma therapeutics (e.g., BRAF/MEK inhibitors, immune checkpoint blockers), c‑RGDKYQ operates through a fundamentally distinct mechanism: it physically disrupts the actin cytoskeleton via tyrosinase‑triggered self‑assembly, leading to direct cell death. This mechanism is orthogonal to kinase inhibition or immune modulation, and therefore may circumvent common resistance pathways, such as MAPK reactivation or loss of antigen presentation potency. Importantly, the physical nature of cytoskeletal disruption, rather than targeting a single signaling node, makes it less likely for tumor cells to develop rapid resistance, as resistance would require complex changes in cytoskeletal organization or tyrosinase expression. Moreover, the high selectivity of c‑RGDKYQ for tyrosinase‑overexpressing melanoma cells suggests a favorable safety profile. While a direct head‑to‑head comparison with approved drugs is beyond the scope of this proof‑of‑concept study, our findings position c‑RGDKYQ as a promising candidate for combination therapy to overcome or delay resistance, and for the treatment of resistant melanomas. Future studies will need to systematically evaluate its efficacy in drug‑resistant melanoma models and in combination with standard‑of‑care agents.

Additionally, while peritumoral injection provided direct proof‐of‐concept for localized efficacy and safety, aligning with clinical needs for cutaneous melanoma, systemic delivery is essential for metastatic disease. Upcoming studies will assess intravenous pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, tumor targeting, and systemic toxicity, potentially via encapsulation in long‐circulating nanocarriers like liposomes or PEGylation to prolong circulation and enhance accumulation [35]. Potential impacts on normal pigmented tissues also require scrutiny, given the reliance on elevated tyrosinase activity in tumors [36]. Subsequent work will test c‐RGDKYQ's cytotoxicity in the normal human epidermal melanocytes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report c‐RGDKYQ, a de novo cyclic peptide that harnesses melanoma‐specific tyrosinase to trigger intracellular oxidation and supramolecular nanofiber assembly, physically dismantling the actin cytoskeleton, disrupting focal adhesions and adherens junctions, and inducing mitochondrial apoptosis via the RhoA/ROCK signaling axis—validated by TFM, ultrastructural TEM, and transcriptomic profiling. Its cyclic architecture confers conformational rigidity that enhances integrin binding, optimizes enzymatic oxidation, and drives efficient self‐assembly, accounting for its marked superiority over the linear counterpart and enabling a modular therapeutic system that integrates targeting, enzyme responsiveness, and cytoskeletal disruption without exogenous payloads. While current validation in murine B16 models and peritumoral delivery necessitates future extension to heterogeneous human melanomas, systemic administration, and safety assessment in pigmented tissues, c‐RGDKYQ establishes a compelling enzyme‐responsive paradigm for precision melanoma therapy and perhaps offers a translatable framework for other malignancies with dysregulated metabolic enzymes.

Materials and Methods

The peptide sequencing (RGDKYQ) was custom‐synthesized by Nanjing TGpeptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Tyrosinase (≥500 units/mg protein) and OPA (molecular biology grade) were supplied by Sigma–Aldrich and purchased through Merck China Co., Ltd. All materials were used as received without further purification. Water was purified using a Millipore system (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) with a minimum resistivity of 18.2 MΩ cm.

Preparation of c‐RGDKYQ

Unprotected peptide RGDKYQ were dissolved in phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) saline/EtOH (1:1) to give a clear solution. OPA (1.0 equiv, stock solution) was added to the solution at room temperature and stirred for 10 min. The freeze‐dried product is a light‐yellow powder.

Electron Spray Ionization—Mass Spectrum

Sample analysis was carried out using a Thermo Easy‐nano LC system interfaced with a Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were trapped on a Thermo Acclaim PepMap C18 precolumn (100 µm × 2 cm) and separated on a Thermo Acclaim PepMap C18 analytical column (50 µm × 15 cm). The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ionization mode with an electrospray voltage of 3.6 kV and a capillary temperature of 320°C, acquiring full‐scan mass spectrometry (MS) spectra over an m/z range of 300–1000.

Absorbance and Fluorescence Spectroscopy

Fluorescence spectroscopy was performed on a fluorescence spectrometer (Fluorolog‐MAX 4; Horiba) equipped with a 1.0 cm quartz cuvette with a fixed excitation wavelength at 350 nm. Both excitation and emission slit widths were set 5.0 nm. The absorbance spectroscopy was performed on a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1).

Infrared Spectrometry

Infrared spectra were recorded on Nicolet iS5 FTIR spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) with KBr pellets in the 4000–400 cm−1 regions.

Size and Zeta Potential

Diameter and zeta potential measurements were performed using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) instrument, specifically the Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Panalytical Ltd.), equipped with a quartz cuvette. The experiments were conducted at a controlled temperature of 25.0°C over 100 consecutive scans. The samples were prepared in a sodium borate buffer (10 mM, pH adjusted between 3 and 10) to ensure optimal conditions for accurate measurements.

HPLC and MS

HPLC analyses were conducted using an Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity system, fitted with an Inertsil ODS‐SP column (GL Sciences Inc.; 5 µm particle size, 4.6 mm × 250 mm dimensions). The mobile phase consisted of a sodium borate buffer (10 mM, pH 7). Detection was performed using a UV detector set at 280 nm and a fluorescence detector configured with an excitation wavelength of 350 nm and an emission wavelength of 500 nm.

Following HPLC separation, MS analysis was carried out to identify and quantify the compounds. The HPLC system was coupled online with a mass spectrometer (Agilent 6460 Triple Quadrupole LC/MS or equivalent), which operated in both positive and negative ion modes to maximize detection sensitivity. The electrospray ionization source parameters were optimized for each sample type, including capillary voltage, nebulizer gas pressure, drying gas flow rate, and temperature. Data acquisition and processing were managed using MassHunter Workstation Software (Agilent Technologies). The MS conditions were tailored to ensure accurate mass measurement and reliable compound identification, facilitating comprehensive characterization of the samples.

Cell Proliferation and Viability Assay

CCK‐8 assay kit (K1080; Shanghai WeiHuan Biotech CO., Ltd.) was utilized to assess cell proliferation and viability. Log‐phase B16, L929, and U2OS cells were dissociated using 0.25% trypsin to form single‐cell suspensions. Cells were counted using a hemocytometer and adjusted to a concentration of 5 × 104 cells/mL in complete medium. A volume of 100 µL of the cell suspension was seeded into each well of a 96‐well plate, with three replicates per condition. The plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 h to allow for cell attachment. Following incubation, different concentrations of the test drug (three concentration groups) were added to the wells. Each treatment group had three replicates. After 12 h, images were captured under a microscope to document initial cell viability. For B16 cells, the 96‐well plates were further incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK‐8 reagent was added to each well, gently mixed, and the plates were incubated for 2.5 h under the same condition. Absorbance values were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (U‐2900; Hitachi). Data were recorded, and the percentage of cell viability was calculated. Origin software was used for data analysis to generate cell viability curves and determine the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration.

Wound Healing Assay for Cell Migration

To evaluate the effects of Ctrl, RGDKYQ, and c‐RGDKYQ on cell migration, a classical wound healing assay was performed. B16 cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in six‐well plates and cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C with 5% CO2 until they reached 90–100% confluency. Cells were then starved in serum‐free medium for 24 h to synchronize the cell cycle and minimize nonspecific migration. Next, a straight line was gently scratched in the center of each well using a 200 µL pipette tip, creating a uniform “cell‐free zone,” followed by twice washes with PBS to remove unattached cell debris. For treatment, the control group received normal culture medium, the RGDKYQ group was treated with a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL, and the c‐RGDKYQ group received a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. Images of the scratch areas in the center of each well were captured at 0, 12, and 24 h using an inverted microscope (OLYMPUS DP74), ensuring consistent imaging positions. The changes in scratch width at different time points were measured using ImageJ software to calculate relative migration distances or percent closure of the scratch. Statistical analysis was performed using t‐tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare differences between the three groups, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Flow Cytometry Assay for Apoptosis Analysis

Flow cytometry was employed to analyze cell cycle progression and apoptosis. B16 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in six‐well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After reaching 70–80% confluency, cells were synchronized by serum starvation for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with Ctrl and c‐RGDKYQ (0, 50, 100, and 200 mg/mL) for 24 h. The apoptosis analysis was performed using Annexin V‐FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Catalog No. K2003; Weihuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) following the manufacturer's protocol. Stained cells were diluted with 500 µL buffer and analyzed immediately on a CytoFlex Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter). A minimum of 50,000 events per sample were acquired, and data were processed with CytExpert Software (v2.4). Statistical significance was determined by one‐way ANOVA with t‐test (p < 0.05; n = 3 independent experiments, triplicate wells).

Immunofluorescent Staining Observation and Analysis of Cytoskeletal Structure

B16 cells were seeded on glass coverslips in 24‐well plates at 2 × 104 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Following serum starvation for 24 h, cells were treated with 10 µM cRGDKYQ for 0, 1, 2, or 4 h. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h. Primary antibody against β‐tubulin, RhoA, ROCK2, and Cofilin (Abcam; 1:200) was incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen; 1:500) for 1 h. F‐actin was visualized with Alexa Fluor 568‐phalloidin (Invitrogen; 1:40) for 30 min, and nuclei counterstained with DAPI (Sigma; D9542; 1 µg/mL) for 5 min. Coverslips were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade (Invitrogen; P36930). Images were acquired on an OLYMPUS CKX53 microscope, with excitation/emission settings: 405/420–480 nm (DAPI), 488/500–550 nm (β‐tubulin), and 561/570–620 nm (phalloidin).

Effect of c‐RGDKYQ on the Viability of B16 Melanoma Cells

To examine the effect of c‐RGDKYQ on the viability of B16 melanoma cells, log‐phase B16 cells were utilized. B16 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well in 24‐well plates, with 500 µL of complete DMEM medium (containing 10% FBS) added to each well. The cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 h to allow for attachment and growth. c‐RGDKYQ was dissolved in sterile physiological saline to prepare solutions of varying concentrations (0, 40, 80, 200 µg/mL). After the initial 24‐h culture period, the cells in the 24‐well plates were treated with these different concentrations of c‐RGDKYQ solution for an additional 24 h. The control group received only sterile physiological saline without c‐RGDKYQ. Following the 24‐h treatment, cells were gently washed twice with PBS to remove residual c‐RGDKYQ solution. Calcein AM was diluted in serum‐free DMEM to a final concentration of 2 µM, and PI was diluted to a final concentration of 1 µg/mL. Each well received 200 µL of this staining solution and was incubated in the dark for 30 min. Cells were then washed twice with PBS. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon A1; Japan), with appropriate excitation and emission wavelengths: Calcein AM (excitation 488 nm, emission 515 nm) for live cells and PI (excitation 535 nm, emission 617 nm) for dead cells. Images were taken under identical conditions for all groups. Green fluorescence indicated live cells stained by Calcein AM, while red fluorescence represented dead cells stained by PI.

Transcriptome Sequencing Analysis of B16 Cells

Cell culture and peptide treatment. B16 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were seeded at 1 × 106/well in six‐well plates and treated with 10 µM linear peptide RGDKYQ (n = 6) or cyclic peptide cRGDKYQ (n = 6) for 48 h post 24 h serum starvation. Untreated cells served as Ctrl (n = 6). We extracted total RNA employing the TRIzol‐based RNA isolation kit (TIANGEN; Cat. No. DP424), obtaining over 2 µg per sample. RNA integrity was assessed via 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis combined with spectrophotometric analysis. Samples showing an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.2, indicating superior purity, were selected for subsequent library preparation and sequencing. Full transcriptome mRNA libraries were prepared by ApexBio Technology LLC (Shanghai, China) in accordance with the provided protocols. From 1 µg of total RNA, poly(A) mRNA was isolated using Dynabeads Oligo (dT). The enriched poly(A) mRNA underwent fragmentation and reverse transcription to produce cDNA. This cDNA was then amplified to synthesize the complementary second strand. The resulting cDNA was purified utilizing the AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). Following library assembly, fragments were amplified via PCR and size‐selected for 350–550 bp lengths. Quality control of the libraries was conducted with an Agilent 4200 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA). Sequencing occurred on the Illumina Xplus platform in paired‐end 150 bp mode to produce initial raw data.

Initial paired‐end FASTQ files were processed with Fastp to eliminate adapter sequences and substandard bases [37]. The resulting high‐quality reads were mapped to the murine reference genome via HISAT2 [38], after which StringTie [39] facilitated transcriptome reconstruction guided by the genome and quantification of gene expression levels. Differential gene expression analysis was carried out using DESeq2 (for replicated samples) [40], applying thresholds of log2FC > 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05 to define DEGs. Functional annotation and enrichment were executed with clusterProfiler [41] on the notable DEGs and module‐associated genes, focusing on GO terms and KEGG pathways. Enriched categories were deemed significant if their p value < 0.05.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

B16 cells treated with c‐RGDKYQ for 0, 2, and 4 h were initially fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) overnight at 4°C. After being washed three times with the same buffer, the samples were postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1.5 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30, 50, 70, 90, and 100%) followed by acetone, and then infiltrated and embedded in Epon 812 resin. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome, mounted on copper grids, and stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The sections were examined under a Hitachi HT‐7800 transmission electron microscope operated at 80 kV.

TFM for Measuring Cellular Mechanical Forces

The glass bottom of a 35‐mm confocal dish was pretreated with 3‐aminopropyltriethoxysilane (Sigma–Aldrich) to ensure the adhesion of PAA gel. PAA gel embedded with 0.2‐micron fluorescent particles (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a stiffness of 10 kPa was used to measure the traction force of B16 cells. After PAA gel polymerization, the gels were activated with sulfo‐SANPAH followed by coating with Type I collagen (Corning) at 4°C overnight. Then, cells were seeded on PAA gels for 6 h. Phase contrast of cells and fluorescence images of beads were taken. In situ fluorescence images of the fluorescent beads were taken again as reference images after cells were removed. Traction force generated by B16 cells was calculated using a custom‐developed MATLAB program (2021a) according to previous studies [42]. The data shown in the manuscripts represent the average traction force exerted by a cell, where T denotes the local traction stress exerted by the cell and A denotes the area of interaction between the cell and the substrate: Paverage=∫∫A|T|dAA

In Vivo Efficacy Validation Experiment

To evaluate the inhibitory effects of a cyclic peptide drug on B16 melanoma cells in nude mice, the following standardized protocol was employed: B16 melanoma cells were used. Cells were dissociated using trypsin and resuspended to form a single‐cell suspension. Cell concentration was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL using a hemocytometer for accurate counting. Cells were then resuspended in serum‐free DMEM medium (Gibco) to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 106 cells/200 µL. A volume of 200 µL cell suspension was inoculated subcutaneously into the right dorsal side of 7‐week‐old female nude mice. Each group consisted of five nude mice, including a control group and an experimental group. Postinoculation, the body weight and tumor formation of the nude mice were monitored daily, and tumor growth was recorded. Noticeable tumor formation was observed within 7–10 days postinoculation. Tumor dimensions were measured using calipers, recording the longest diameter (a) and shortest diameter (b), and calculating tumor volume using the formula V = ab 2/2. The cyclic peptide and its noncyclic counterpart were dissolved in sterile physiological saline and administered at a concentration of 7.5 mg/kg. The control group received an equivalent volume of sterile physiological saline. Starting from Day 7 after visible tumor formation, injections were administered every 3 days for a total of four injections. Before and after each injection, the body weight and tumor volume of the nude mice were recorded to assess the drug's effect on tumor growth inhibition. Survival times were also documented to evaluate the impact of the drug on survival rates. 7 days after the last injection, the nude mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and tumor tissues were excised, weighed, and prepared for further histological and molecular biological analysis. Data analysis was performed using Origin software. Comparisons between the control and experimental groups were made regarding tumor volume, changes in body weight, and survival rates. Statistical analyses were conducted using t‐tests and ANOVA to determine significant differences.

Morphological Observation of Tissues

To evaluate the inhibitory effects of the cyclic peptide drug on B16 melanoma cells in nude mice and its impact on tumor, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues, the following protocol was employed for morphological assessment: Upon euthanizing the nude mice, tissues were promptly excised and gently rinsed with PBS. All tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (Leica RM2255; Germany), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Finally, the HE‐stained sections were examined and imaged using an optical microscope (Pannoramic MIDI; Hungary).

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments unless otherwise specified. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was determined using unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's posthoc test was applied. For wound healing assay and tumor growth curves, two‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was used to assess differences over time between treatment groups.

For TFM analysis, due to the non‐normal distribution of the data, the Mann–Whitney U‐test was employed for pairwise comparisons [42]. For immunofluorescence quantification, each dot represents one microscopic field (average of three technical measurements). For each group, five independent biological replicates were analyzed, and 10 random fields were measured per replicate. Thus, a total of 50 fields is shown per group. For statistical comparison, the 10 fields from each biological replicate were first averaged to obtain one value per replicate (n = 5 per group), and statistical significance was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. For transcriptomic analysis, differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 algorithm with thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05. GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses were conducted with p value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Sample sizes for animal experiments were determined based on preliminary studies and power analysis to ensure adequate statistical power. Each experimental group consisted of five mice per group, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Tumor volume measurements were analyzed by two‐way ANOVA with repeated measures. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with significance levels indicated as follows: p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001. Nonsignificant differences are denoted as “n.s.” All statistical tests were two‐sided, and no data points were excluded as outliers unless explicitly stated.
