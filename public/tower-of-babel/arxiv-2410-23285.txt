Provable Acceleration for Diffusion Models under Minimal Assumptions
Gen Li, Changxiao Cai

Abstract
Score-based diffusion models, while achieving minimax optimality for sampling, are often hampered by slow sampling speeds due to the high computational burden of score function evaluations. Despite the recent remarkable empirical advances in speeding up the score-based samplers, theoretical understanding of acceleration techniques remains largely limited. To bridge this gap, we propose a novel training-free acceleration scheme for stochastic samplers. Under minimal assumptions—namely, L 2 L^{2} -accurate score estimates and a finite second-moment condition on the target distribution—our accelerated sampler provably achieves ε \varepsilon -accuracy in total variation within O ~ ​ ( d 5 / 4 / ε ) \widetilde{O}(d^{5/4}/\sqrt{\varepsilon}) iterations, thereby significantly improving upon the O ~ ​ ( d / ε ) \widetilde{O}(d/\varepsilon) iteration complexity of standard score-based samplers for ε ≤ 1 / d \varepsilon\leq 1/\sqrt{d} . Notably, our convergence theory does not rely on restrictive assumptions on the target distribution or higher-order score estimation guarantees.

diffusion model, training-free acceleration, iteration complexity, DDPM, probability flow ODE

Introduction

Score-based generative models (SGMs), also referred to as diffusion models
(
Song and Ermon,, 2019
;
Song et al., 2020c,
)
, have emerged as a powerful framework for sampling from high-dimensional probability distributions, achieving remarkable success across diverse domains. Notable examples include image generation
(
Dhariwal and Nichol,, 2021
;
Rombach et al.,, 2022
)
, natural language processing
(
Austin et al.,, 2021
;
Li et al.,, 2022
)
, medical imaging
(
Song et al.,, 2021
;
Chung and Ye,, 2022
)
, and computational biology
(
Trippe et al.,, 2022
;
Gruver et al.,, 2024
)
. Beyond these computer science and scientific applications, SGMs have also found important applications in the operations research domain by formulating sequential decision-making as generative sequence modeling. This novel perspective has led to impressive performance in planning
(
Janner et al.,, 2022
;
Chi et al.,, 2023
)
, policy learning
(
Wang et al.,, 2022
;
Chen et al., 2022a,
)
, and imitation learning
(
Pearce et al.,, 2023
;
Hansen-Estruch et al.,, 2023
)
. We refer interested readers to
Yang et al., (2023)
;
Chen et al., 2024b ()
;
Tang and Zhao, 2024b ()
for detailed surveys on recent advances in methods, applications, and theories of diffusion models.

Diffusion models involve two stochastic processes: a forward process and a reverse process.
The forward process progressively diffuses a sample from the target data distribution into pure noise (typically Gaussian noise). The reverse process, guided by the (Stein) score functions of the forward process, transforms pure noise into a sample from the data distribution, thereby achieving the goal of generative modeling. At the heart of constructing the reverse process lies score matching, the task of learning the score functions along the forward process, which is often accomplished via neural networks in practice
(
Hyvärinen and Dayan,, 2005
;
Vincent,, 2011
;
Song et al., 2020b,
)
.
The reverse process can be implemented through either stochastic differential equation (SDE)-based or ordinary differential equation (ODE)-based dynamics, exemplified by the Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM)
(
Ho et al.,, 2020
)
and the Denoising Diffusion Implicit Model (DDIM)
(
Song et al., 2020a,
)
, respectively.
Recent theoretical developments have shown that score-based samplers, when equipped with accurate score estimates, can achieve comparable or superior iteration complexity to classical methods such as Langevin dynamics
(
Bakry et al.,, 2014
)
—notably, without requiring structural assumptions such as log-concavity or even smoothness of the target distribution
(
Chen et al., 2022b,
;
Chen et al., 2023a,
;
Benton et al.,, 2023
;
Li and Yan, 2024b,
)
.

While diffusion models have demonstrated impressive empirical performance and provably attain the minimax convergence rate of sampling for smooth densities
(
Oko et al.,, 2023
;
Zhang et al.,, 2024
;
Dou et al.,, 2024
)
, they face a significant computational challenge—generating high-quality outputs typically requires a large number of iterative steps. Since each iteration involves a neural network evaluation for the score function, the iteration nature makes them substantially slower than single-step samplers such as variational auto-encoders (VAEs)
(
Kingma,, 2013
)
and generative adversarial networks (GANs)
(
Goodfellow et al.,, 2014
)
. This computational bottleneck highlights the pressing need to accelerate diffusion models without compromising their exceptional output quality.

To address this challenge, various acceleration strategies have been proposed, which can be broadly classified into two categories based on whether they require additional learning. Examples of “training-based” methods, such as distillation
(
Salimans and Ho,, 2022
)
and consistency models
(
Song et al.,, 2023
)
, aim to reduce the computational burdens by adapting pre-trained models into related architectures.
While these approaches demonstrate significant empirical improvements, the costs incurred by additional training processes remain prohibitively high for large-scale models. In contrast, “training-free” methods leverage the pre-trained score functions and directly modify the sampling procedure, avoiding resorting to additional training. Hence, this approach offers universal applicability to the off-the-shelf pre-trained diffusion models. Some notable examples in this category include DPM-Solver
(
Lu et al., 2022a,
)
, DPM-Solver++
(
Lu et al., 2022b,
)
, Unipc
(
Zhao et al.,, 2024
)
, which have achieved substantial empirical speedups.
However, theoretical understanding of training-free accelerated samplers remains inadequate.

Motivated by this, we investigate accelerating the convergence of the score-based samplers in a training-free manner.
Recent advances in both theory and practice have demonstrated that once
L^{2}
-accurate score estimates are available, score-based samplers can offer strong sampling guarantees, without requiring structural assumptions on the target distribution. Therefore, we focus on the general setting under minimal conditions—only assuming access to
L^{2}
-accurate score estimates and a finite second moment of the target distribution.
Given this theoretical focal point, we first present a brief overview of the state-of-the-art iteration complexity of the plain score-based samplers in this setting. Discussions about other sampling scenarios are deferred to Section
1.2
.
Here and throughout, the iteration complexity refers to the number of iteration steps required to yield a distribution that is
\varepsilon
close to the target in total variation.
In
Chen et al., 2022b ()
, the authors proved the DDPM sampler can achieve an iteration complexity of
\widetilde{O}(d^{5}/\varepsilon^{2})
, where
d
is the dimension of the data. This iteration complexity was improved to
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
by
Chen et al., 2023a ()
, then to
\widetilde{O}(d/\varepsilon^{2})
by
Benton et al., (2023)
, and recently to
\widetilde{O}(d/\varepsilon)
by
Li and Yan, 2024b ()
. In light of this remarkable theoretical progress, a fundamental question naturally raises: can we develop a score-based sampler that converges faster than
\widetilde{O}(d/\varepsilon)
?

While a variety of training-free accelerated sampling algorithms have been developed that achieve this goal, theoretical understanding of acceleration is far from mature. As we elaborate below, existing convergence theories often rely on additional assumptions on the target data distribution or estimation guarantees on the score function. 1.1
Contributions

Encouragingly, our results provide an affirmative answer to the above question. We develop a novel sampling scheme that achieves an iteration complexity of
\widetilde{O}(d^{5/4}/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, significantly improving upon the
\widetilde{O}(d/\varepsilon)
iteration complexity of standard score-based samplers for
\varepsilon\leq 1/\sqrt{d}
.
This acceleration substantially reduces the computational burden required to achieve minimax optimal sampling.

Our sampler incorporates a momentum term into the sampling update rule by leveraging higher-order approximations to the probability flow ODE.
Notably, our convergence theory replies
solely
on access to
L^{2}
-accurate score estimates and a finite second-order moment condition on the target data distribution. This illustrates that
L^{2}
-accurate first-order score estimates are sufficient to achieve provable sampling acceleration, without the need for higher-order score estimates or additional smoothness assumptions on the target distributions.

While our sampler shares similar intuitions with accelerated ODE-based samplers in prior works
(
Lu et al., 2022b,
;
Li et al., 2024a,
)
, in contrast to their fully deterministic strategies, it introduces additional randomness to mitigate potentially large worst-case errors in approximating the probability flow ODE. This randomization effectively averages out such errors, ultimately leading to provable acceleration under minimal assumptions.
Technically, we achieve this by developing a novel analysis framework that rigorously characterizes the smoothness property of the score functions induced by the probability flow ODE and quantifies the higher-order approximation errors up to low-probability events.
To the best of our knowledge, our result provides the first provable acceleration for score-based samplers that need minimal score estimation requirements and accommodates a broad class of target data distributions.

Other related work

Convergence theory for diffusion models.

Early convergence guarantees for SDE-based samplers were either qualitative
(
De Bortoli et al.,, 2021
;
Liu et al.,, 2022
;
Pidstrigach,, 2022
)
, replied on
L^{\infty}
-accurate score estimates
(
De Bortoli et al.,, 2021
;
Albergo et al.,, 2023
)
, or exhibited exponential dependence
(
De Bortoli,, 2022
;
Block et al.,, 2020
)
.

Lee et al., (2022)
established the first polynomial iteration complexity given
L^{2}
-accurate score estimates, albeit assuming a log-Sobelev inequality on the target distribution.
Chen et al., 2022b ()
;
Lee et al., (2023)
later relaxed this assumption by requiring Lipschitz scores and bounded support/moment conditions on the target distribution.
For ODE-based samplers,
Chen et al., 2023c ()
provided the first convergence guarantee, though without explicit polynomial dependencies and requiring exact score estimates.
Chen et al., 2024c ()
improved upon this by studying variants of the ODE that incorporate additional stochastic corrector steps.
Li et al., (2023)
established an iteration complexity of
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}/\varepsilon)
for ODE-based samplers assuming accurate Jacobian estimates of scores, which was later improved to
\widetilde{O}(d/\varepsilon)

(
Li et al., 2024b,
)
. Recent work has also explored convergence in 2-Wasserstein distance
(
Gao and Zhu,, 2024
;
Tang and Zhao, 2024a,
)
.

Training-free acceleration schemes.

Training-free accelerated samplers typically leverage efficient numerical methods for solving reverse SDE/ODEs. For ODE-based samplers, researchers have exploited semi-linear structures using higher-order ODE solvers
(
Lu et al., 2022a,
;
Lu et al., 2022b,
)
, exponential integrators
(
Zhang and Chen,, 2022
)
, and predictor-corrector frameworks
(
Zhao et al.,, 2024
)
. Acceleration for SDE-based samplers, though less explored due to the inherent complexity of solving SDEs, has progressed through stochastic Improved Euler’s method
(
Jolicoeur-Martineau et al.,, 2021
)
, stochastic Adams method
(
Xue et al.,, 2024
)
, and stochastic Runge-Kutta methods
(
Wu et al.,, 2024
)
.
In addition to resorting to efficient ODE/SDE solvers, alternative acceleration approaches include parallel sampling
(
Chen et al., 2024a,
;
Gupta et al.,, 2024
)
and exploitation of low-dimensional structures underlying the target distributions
(
Li and Yan, 2024a,
;
Huang et al., 2024c,
;
Azangulov et al.,, 2024
)
.

Other theory for diffusion models.

Beyond convergence analysis, another line of work focused on the sample complexity of score estimation.

Block et al., (2020)
provided a sample complexity bound in terms of Rademacher complexity, and
Oko et al., (2023)
;

established the sample complexity using neural networks to estimate scores.
From the perspective of nonparametric statistics,
Wibisono et al., (2024)
;
Zhang et al., (2024)

proposed kernel-based methods to achieve optimal score estimation.
The minimax optimality of diffusion models was then established for various target density classes, including Besov
(
Oko et al.,, 2023
)
, Sobolev
(

)
, and Hölder spaces
(
Dou et al.,, 2024
)
.
Sample complexity reduction through low-dimensional data structures has also been investigated in
Chen et al., 2023b ()
;
Wang et al., (2024)
.
In addition,
Han et al., (2024)
established optimization guarantees for score matching using two-neural networks trained by gradient descent.
In addition to SGMs, convergence of flow-based generative modeling has also been studied in
Cheng et al., (2024)
;
Xu et al., (2024)
.

2
Problem formulation

In this section, we provide a brief introduction to SGMs and introduce the assumptions for our algorithm and theory.

2.1
Preliminaries

Forward process.

Starting from the target data distribution
X_{0}\sim p_{\mathsf{data}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the forward process evolves as follows:

\displaystyle X_{t}=\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}X_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}W_{t},\quad t=1,2,\dots,T,

(1)

where
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{T}\in(0,1)
are the learning rates and
W_{1},\dots,W_{T}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
are standard Gaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For continence of notation, let us denote

\displaystyle\overline{\alpha}_{t}:=\prod_{k=1}^{t}\alpha_{k},\quad t=1,2,\dots,T.

(2)

This allows us to express

\displaystyle X_{t}=\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{t}}X_{0}+\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\,\overline{W}_{t},\quad t=1,2,\dots,T,

(3)

where
\overline{W}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is a standard Gaussian random vector independent of
X_{0}
.
In particular,
p_{X_{T}}
is approximately a standard multivariate normal distribution when
\overline{\alpha}_{T}
is sufficiently small.
The continuum limit of the forward process (
1
) can be modeled by the following SDE:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{t}X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad X_{0}\sim p_{\mathsf{data}};\quad t\in[0,T]

(4)

for some function
\beta_{t}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
denotes a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Reverse process.

The core of SGMs lies in constructing the time-reversal of the forward process.
Classical results in SDE
(
Anderson,, 1982
;
Haussmann and Pardoux,, 1986
)
establish that for a solution
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
to the forward SDE (
4
), the time reversal process
(Y^{\mathsf{SDE}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, defined by
Y^{\mathsf{SDE}}_{t}\coloneqq X_{T-t}
, satisfies the following reverse SDE:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}^{\mathsf{SDE}}=\frac{1}{2}\beta_{T-t}\Big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{SDE}}+2\nabla\log p_{X_{T-t}}\big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{SDE}}\big)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\beta_{T-t}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad Y_{0}^{\mathsf{SDE}}\sim p_{X_{T}};\quad t\in[0,T].

(5)

Here,
p_{X_{t}}
denotes the marginal distribution of
X_{t}
in (
4
), and the gradient of the logarithm of the probability density,
\nabla\log p_{X_{t}}(x)
, is known as the
score function
of
p_{X_{t}}(x)
, where the gradient is with respect to
x
.

Definition 1
.

The score function of
p_{X_{t}}
, denoted by
s^{\star}_{t}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is defined as

\displaystyle s_{t}^{\star}(x)

\displaystyle:=\nabla\log p_{X_{t}}(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(6)

Alternatively, there also exists a deterministic process
(Y^{\mathsf{ODE}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
that, when initialized at
Y^{\mathsf{ODE}}_{0}\sim p_{X_{T}}
, shares the same marginal distributions as (
5
), i.e.,
Y^{\mathsf{ODE}}_{t}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}Y^{\mathsf{SDE}}_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. This process, referred to as
probability flow ODE

(
Song et al., 2020c,
)
, is characterized by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}^{\mathsf{ODE}}=\frac{1}{2}\beta_{T-t}\Big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{ODE}}+\nabla\log p_{X_{T-t}}\big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{ODE}}\big)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad Y_{0}^{\mathsf{ODE}}\sim p_{X_{T}};\quad t\in[0,T].

(7)

We remark that the two processes
(Y^{\mathsf{SDE}}_{t})_{t}
and
(Y^{\mathsf{ODE}}_{t})_{t}
have identical marginal distributions but distinct joint or path-wise distributions.

Notably, the reverse processes—both the SDE (
5
) and ODE (
7
)—are fully characterized by the score functions of the forward process. This pivotal role makes them the cornerstone of successful generative modeling.

2.2
Assumptions

Score function estimation.

In practical applications, the true score functions
(s_{t}^{\star})_{t\in[T]}
are unknown and must be learned based on samples drawn from the target distribution. The estimation of the score functions, known as score matching
(
Hyvärinen and Dayan,, 2005
;
Vincent,, 2011
)
, is typically accomplished by minimizing the
L^{2}(p_{X_{t}})
loss via neural networks
(
Song et al., 2020c,
)
. Given these score estimates
(s_{t})_{t\in[T]}
, we can then start from
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and implement a discretized reverse process.

This approach motivates our analysis under the assumption of
L^{2}
-accurate score estimates across all time steps.

Assumption 1
.

The score estimates
(s_{t})_{t\in[T]}
for the score functions
(s_{t}^{\star})_{t\in[T]}
satisfy

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}^{2}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim p_{X_{t}}}\Big[\big\|s_{t}(X_{t})-s^{\star}_{t}(X_{t})\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big]=:\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\varepsilon_{t}^{2}.

(8)

Target data distribution.

Next, we impose a mild assumption on the target data distribution.

Assumption 2
.

The target data distribution has a bounded second moment in the sense that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|X_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\big]<T^{C_{R}}

(9)

for arbitrarily large constant
C_{R}>0
.

In short, this assumption requires the second moment of the target data distribution to be at most polynomially large in the iteration number
T
.
As
T
typically grows polynomially in the dimension
d
, this assumption allows the second moment to be exceedingly large, given that the exponent
C_{R}
can be arbitrarily large.
In particular, any distribution with constant-order second moments naturally falls under this requirement.
We express the condition in terms of
T
in order to express the convergence guarantees in cleaner and more concise manner.
This is among the weakest assumptions imposed in sampling analysis, one that is typically satisfied by empirical data in practical sampling applications.

3
Main results

In this section, we introduce an accelerated score-based sampler and present the theoretical guarantees on its convergence rate.

3.1
Accelerated sampler

Learning rate schedule.

Let us first introduce the learning rate schedule
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in[T]}
for the proposed sampler. Recall the definition of
\overline{\alpha}_{t}\coloneqq\prod_{1\leq k\leq t}\alpha_{k}
in (
2
) and the distribution characterization in (
3
) that
X_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\,x,(1-\overline{\alpha}_{t})I_{d}\big)
given
X_{0}=x
. We can specify the learning rates
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in[T]}
through
(\overline{\alpha}_{t})_{t\in[T]}
by the following recursive relationship:

\displaystyle\overline{\alpha}_{T}=\frac{1}{T^{C_{0}}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\alpha}_{t-1}=\overline{\alpha}_{t}+C_{1}\frac{\log T}{T}\overline{\alpha}_{t}(1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}),\quad t=T,\dots,2,

(10)

where
C_{0},C_{1}>0
are sufficiently large absolute constants with
C_{1}/C_{0}
large enough.
As demonstrated in Lemma
1
, these choices ensure

\displaystyle 1-\alpha_{1}\leq T^{-C_{1}/4}\qquad\text{and}\qquad 1-\alpha_{t}\lesssim\frac{\log T}{T},\quad t\geq 2.

Remark 1
.

Our results remain valid for a broader class of learning rate schedules satisfying

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=1-\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}(T)},\qquad\overline{\alpha}_{T}=\frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}(T)},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\asymp\frac{\log T}{T},\quad t=T,\dots,2.

These requirements ensure that
1-\overline{\alpha}_{1}
is sufficiently small (
p_{X_{1}}
is close to
p_{X_{0}}
) and that
\overline{\alpha}_{T}
is small (
p_{X_{T}}
is nearly Gaussian), which is realized by the chosen ratio
(1-\alpha_{t})/(1-\overline{\alpha}_{t})\asymp\log T/T
.
More generally, our analysis framework can accommodate arbitrary learning rate designs and the resulting convergence rate depends on how the learning rate
1-\alpha_{t}
compares to
1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}
.

Sampling procedure.

With the learning rate schedule in hand, we are now ready to present our acceleration procedure.

Initialized at
Y_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, the sampler employs the update rule as follows.
Working backward from
t=T,\dots,2
, we first compute an intermediate point
Y^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\equiv Y^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}(Y_{t})
based on the current iterate
Y_{t}
and noise
Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}
:

\displaystyle Y^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\Big(Y_{t}+\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{2\alpha_{t}}s_{t}(Y_{t})\Big)+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t},

(11a)

where
Z_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
,
t\geq 2
are standard Gaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Next, using the points
Y_{t},Y^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}
and noise
Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t},Z_{t}
, we generate the next iterate
Y_{t-1}\equiv Y_{t-1}(Y_{t},Y^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t})
by

\displaystyle Y_{t-1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\bigg(Y_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})\Big(s_{t}(Y_{t})+\mathsf{clip}_{t}\Big\{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}(Y^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t})-s_{t}\big(Y_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\Big\}\Big)+\sigma_{t}Z_{t}\bigg).

(11b)

Here,
Z_{t}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
,
t\geq 2
are standard Gaussian random vectors independent of
(Z_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}})_{t=2}^{T}
, the variance parameter
\sigma_{t}
is chosen as
\sigma_{t}^{2}\coloneqq\alpha_{t}-1/(3-2\alpha_{t})
, and
\mathsf{clip}_{t}\{\cdot\}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a thresholding function given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{clip}_{t}\{x\}:=x\,\ind\bigg\{\|x\|_{2}\leq C_{\mathsf{clip}}(1-\alpha_{t})\bigg(\frac{d\log T}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\bigg)^{3/2}\bigg\},

(12)

for some absolute constant
C_{\mathsf{clip}}>0
. The last iterate
Y_{1}
serves as the sample generated by our sampler.

In short, our proposed sampler first employs a DDPM-type update rule to generate a mid-point
Y^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}
. This intermediate step allows to incorporate an extra term

\mathsf{clip}_{t}\Big\{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}(Y^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t})-s_{t}\big(Y_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\Big\},

into the subsequent update for
Y_{t-1}
.
This additional term acts as a refined second-order approximation for the score function, effectively reducing the discretization error and leading to a faster convergence rate. To mitigate potentially large approximation errors in worst-case scenarios, we introduce a thresholding procedure—a technique also used in practical applications
(
Saharia et al.,, 2022
)
.

While this idea of leveraging a second-order approximation shares similarities with existing ODE-based accelerated samplers (e.g.
Lu et al., 2022a ()
), our method distinguishes itself by injecting additional random noise in each iteration. This stochastic component effectively reduces the second-order discretization error on average, thereby enabling faster sampling speeds without the second-order accuracy assumptions on the Jacobians of scores required in prior works
(
Li et al., 2024a,
)
.
Moreover, even though our sampler introduced random noise, it draws on insights from discretizing a probability flow ODE—a connection we will on in Section
4
when we discuss the rationale of the acceleration scheme.

Remark 2
.

Instead of running the sampling procedure all the way to
t=0
, we stop the update at
t=1
and take
p_{X_{1}}
as the new target distribution.
Since
X_{1}=\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}X_{0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{1}}Z_{1}
with
Z_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and the learning rate
\alpha_{1}
is chosen such that
1-\alpha_{1}=o(1)
, the distributions
p_{X_{1}}
and
p_{X_{0}}
are exceedingly close.
This is often referred to
early stopping
, as the score functions can blow up as
t\rightarrow 0
for non-smooth target data distributions, rendering non-trivial guarantees in total variation or KL divergence unachievable.
The early stopping technique is widely employed in both real-world applications
(
Song et al., 2020c,
)
and theoretical analysis
(
Chen et al., 2022b,
;
Benton et al.,, 2023
)
.

3.2
Convergence guarantee

We now present the convergence theory for our proposed sampler, with proof outline deferred to Section
4
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.
The output
Y_{1}
of the sampler (
11
) with the learning rate schedule (
10
) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathsf{TV}\left(p_{X_{1}},p_{Y_{1}}\right)\leq\sqrt{\mathsf{KL}\left(p_{X_{1}}\parallel p_{Y_{1}}\right)}\leq C\bigg(\frac{d^{5/2}\log^{5}T}{T^{2}}+\bigg(1+\frac{d^{3/2}\log^{3}T}{T}\bigg)\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}\sqrt{\log T}\bigg),

(13)

for some absolute constant
C>0
.

In a word, Theorem
1
demonstrates the sampling quality our proposed acceleration scheme, measured in the total variation distance, is determined by two principal factors. The first term in the error bound (
13
) reflects the discretization error incurred by approximating the continuous reverse process, while the second term arises from the estimation error of the score functions. When the number of iterations
T
is sufficiently large, the score matching error term dominates and the error bound in (
13
) becomes
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}})
.

We now elaborate on the important implications of Theorem
1
.

1.

Iteration complexity.

Given a score estimation error
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}
, for any desired accuracy
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}\sqrt{\log T}
, the proposed sampler provably achieves
\mathsf{TV}(p_{X_{1}},p_{Y_{1}})\lesssim\varepsilon
in

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}\bigg(\frac{d^{5/4}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}+d^{3/2}\bigg)

iterations, where the
\widetilde{O}(d^{3/2})
term can be interpreted as a burn-in cost.
By contrast, the state-of-the-art iteration complexity for plain score-based samplers scales as
\widetilde{O}(d/\varepsilon)

(
Li and Yan, 2024b,
;
Li et al., 2024b,
)
. Therefore, our algorithm achieves a significant acceleration in the high-accuracy regime where
\varepsilon\lesssim 1/\sqrt{d}
, specifically by a factor of

\widetilde{O}\bigg(\frac{d^{1/4}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\bigg).

It is worth noting that existing score matching error guarantees for neural networks
(
Oko et al.,, 2023
;
Cui et al.,, 2023
;
Cole and Lu,, 2024
)
and kernel-based estimators
(
Wibisono et al.,, 2024
;
Zhang et al.,, 2024
;
Dou et al.,, 2024
)
satisfy
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}=o(1)
as the training sample size grows. Hence, the high-accuracy regime
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}\sqrt{\log T}\leq\varepsilon\lesssim 1/\sqrt{d}
captures the most important and practically relevant setting.

2.

Mild target distribution assumption.

The established convergence theory only imposes a finite second moment condition on the target data distribution. It does not rely on structural assumptions commonly required for acceleration sampling analysis in the literature, such as Lipschitz/smooth score functions
(
Huang et al., 2024a,
;
Huang et al., 2024b,
;
Li and Jiao,, 2024
)
. We achieve this by rigorously analyzing the probability flow ODE and demonstrating that it implicitly enforces these desirable properties, except on events of low probability. This theoretical framework ensures the broad applicability of the proposed sampler across a diverse family of data distributions.

3.

Minimal score estimation requirement.

The developed method provably achieves a faster convergence rate using only first-order score function estimates, illustrating that acceleration is feasible without smooth score estimates or higher-order score estimation guarantees (e.g., accurate estimates of the Jacobian matrices of score functions).
In essence, we show that the additional random noise in our sampler implicitly “averages out” second-order discretization errors, thus circumventing the higher-order score estimation requirements imposed by prior works
(
Li et al., 2024a,
)
.
This reduced requirement for score matching confirms the practicality of the proposed acceleration scheme.

4.

Error metric.
Theorem
1
establishes sampling error guarantees of
p_{Y_{1}}
with respect to
p_{X_{1}}
rather than the target distribution
p_{X_{0}}
, a metric widely used in the literature
(
Benton et al.,, 2023
;
Li and Yan, 2024b,
)
. The key observation is that
{X_{1}}={\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}X_{0}}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{1}}Z_{1}
deviates only slightly from
{X_{0}}
, provided that the learning rate
1-\alpha_{1}
is vanishingly small. Consequently,
p_{X_{1}}
and
p_{X_{0}}
remain exceedingly close, making
\mathsf{TV}(p_{X_{1}},p_{Y_{1}})
a valid and practical measure of error. It is worth noting that since
p_{X_{1}}
is the convolution of
p_{X_{0}}
with a Gaussian distribution, it inherits a smoothness property that allows our analysis and theory to accommodate target distributions whose score functions may not be Lipschitz.

Finally, we remark that recent works show that score-based diffusion models, when equipped with
L^{2}
-minimax optimal score estimators (with respect to
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}
), achieve the minimax rate of sampling for smooth densities
(
Oko et al.,, 2023
;
Zhang et al.,, 2024
;
Dou et al.,, 2024
)
. Our acceleration framework thus improves the computational efficiency of theses minimax optimal samplers, reducing the computational cost of attaining minimax optimal sampling performance.

Analysis

4.1
Preliminaries

Before proceeding with the details, we first summarize several key properties that will be useful for the proof.

Properties of the learning rates.

To begin with, Lemma
1
collects several important properties of the learning rates
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in[T]}
chosen in (
10
).
The proof can be found in Appendix
C.1
.

Lemma 1
.

The learning rates
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in[T]}
specified in (
10
) satisfy that for all
t=2,\dots,T
:

\displaystyle 1-\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\frac{\log T}{T};

(14a)

\displaystyle\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\frac{\log T}{T};

(14b)

\displaystyle\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t-1}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+2C_{1}\frac{\log T}{T};

(14c)

where
C_{0},C_{1}
are defined in (
10
). In addition,
\alpha_{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle 1-\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T^{C_{1}/4}}.

(14d)

Distance between
p_{X_{T}}
and
p_{Y_{T}}
.

Recall that
X_{T}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{T}}X_{0}+\sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_{T}}Z
with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
independent of
X_{0}
and that
Y_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
.
It is well-known that the forward process mixes exponentially fast towards the standard normal distribution, as formalized by the following lemma.
The proof is deferred to Appendix
C.2
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds. Then the KL divergence between
p_{X_{T}}
and
p_{Y_{T}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{T}}\,\|\,p_{Y_{T}}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim T^{-10},

(15)

as long as
C_{0}
chosen in the learning rates schedule (
10
) is large enough.

Preparation.

Let
T_{0}\geq 2
be the largest integer such that
C_{1}(d\log^{5/2}T)/T\geq 1/2
where
C_{1}
is defined in (
10
). If
T\leq T_{0}
, we have
(d^{5/2}\log^{5}T)/T^{2}>\max\{\sqrt{d}/(2C_{1})^{2},d^{5/2}\log^{5}(2)/4\}
and hence the result of Theorem
1
naturally holds when the absolute constant
C
is chosen large enough.
Therefore, in the rest of the proof, we assume that
T>T_{0}
so that
(C_{1}d\log^{2}T)/T\leq 1/2
.

We are now prepared to present the proof strategy for Theorem
1
, organized into three main steps.

4.2
Step 1: constructing auxiliary processes

To begin with, we introduce several auxiliary processes which serve analytical purposes only and are not implemented in the practical sampling process. They allow us to compare our proposed sampler against idealized or modified processes in a controlled manner.

Sequences
(Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}})_{t=2}^{T}
and
(Y_{t}^{\star})_{t=1}^{T-1}
constructed using true scores.

We first define two reverse processes—
(Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}})_{t=2}^{T}
and
(Y^{\star}_{t})_{t=1}^{T-1}
—that evolve backward in time using the forward process
(X_{t})_{t=2}^{T}
along with the true score functions
\{s_{t}^{\star}\}_{t=2}^{T}
. These processes help us measure the purely second-order discretization error (up to some low probability event), independent of any score estimation error.
Concretely, for each
t=T,\cdots,2
, we define

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\Big(X_{t}+\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{2\alpha_{t}}s_{t}^{\star}(X_{t})\Big),

(16a)

\displaystyle Y_{t-1}^{\star}

\displaystyle\coloneqq X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})\Big(s_{t}^{\star}(X_{t})+\Big\{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}^{\star}\big(Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}}\big)-s_{t}^{\star}(X_{t})\Big\}\ind\{X_{t}\in\mathcal{E}_{t}\}\Big).

(16b)

Here
\mathcal{E}_{t}
is the set of “typical” points under the distribution
p_{X_{t}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:-\log p_{X_{t}}(x)\leq C_{2}d\log T\quad\text{and}\quad\|x\|_{2}\leq C_{3}\Big(\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}_{t}}T^{2C_{R}}+\sqrt{(1-\overline{\alpha}_{t})d\log T}\Big)\Big\},

(17)

for some sufficiently large absolute constants
C_{2},C_{3}>0
. In words, the set
\mathcal{E}_{t}
excludes points that are either extremely unlikely (having tiny density) or have exceedingly large norm.
Intuitively, the practical sampling process
\{(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}},Y_{t})\}_{t}
approximates these auxiliary processes
\{(Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}},Y_{t}^{\star})\}_{t}
, provided that the score estimation error is small.

Sequence
(X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
constructed using probability flow ODE.

Next, we introduce an auxiliary forward process
(X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
that evolves backward through a probability flow ODE and has the same marginal distributions as the original forward process
(X_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
.
This equivalence crucially allows us to replace
(X_{t})
with
(X_{t}^{\mathsf{aux}})
when bounding
\mathsf{TV}(p_{X_{1}},p_{Y_{1}})
, which is more tractable thanks to the special structure of
(X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t})
.

To motivate the construction, we first establish the following lemma, which demonstrates that for each
t>1
,
X_{t-1}
in the forward process is distributed identically to a deterministic function of
X_{t}
plus some independent Gaussian noise. The proof can be found in Appendix
B.1
.

Lemma 3
.

For each
t=2,\dots,T
, there exists a mapping
\Phi_{t}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\big(\Phi_{t}(X_{t})+\sigma_{t}Z_{t}\big)\overset{\,\mathrm{d}}{=}X_{t-1},

(18)

where we recall
Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is independent of
X_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}^{2}=\alpha_{t}-1/(3-2\alpha_{t})
.

Remark 3
.

As will become clear in the proof, the mapping
\Phi_{t}
is constructed by leveraging the probability flow ODE (
7
) associated with the forward process
(X_{t})
, effectively serving as a first-order discretization of the ODE. It is noteworthy that
\Phi_{t}
is designed to ensure
\Phi_{t}(X_{t})

plus
noise recovers the distribution of
X_{t-1}
. This is in contrast to the fully deterministic strategies where
\Phi_{t}(X_{t})
alone is to designed to match
X_{t-1}
in distribution, as used in prior acceleration work
(
Lu et al., 2022a,
;
Li et al., 2024a,
)
. This injection of additional random noise is key to reducing the worst-case second-order approximation errors, thereby yielding a faster convergence rate.

With these mappings
\{\Phi_{t}\}_{t=2}^{T}
, we now define the auxiliary process
(X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t})_{t\in[T]}
recursively:

\displaystyle X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{T}=X_{T},\quad\text{and}\quad X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\big(\Phi_{t}(X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t})+\sigma_{t}Z_{t}\big),\quad t=2,\dots,T.

(19)

By Lemma
3
, we have
X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}X_{t}
for all
t\in[T]
. This distributional equivalence underpins the subsequent decomposition of
\mathsf{TV}(p_{X_{1}},p_{Y_{1}})
.

Error decomposition.

Combining the auxiliary process
(X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t})_{t\in[T]}
with our proposed sampler
(Y_{t})_{t\in[T]}
allows us to bound the total variation distance between
p_{X_{1}}
and
p_{Y_{1}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{TV}^{2}\big(p_{X_{1}},p_{Y_{1}}\big)

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{=}\mathsf{TV}^{2}\big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{1}},p_{Y_{1}}\big)\overset{(\mathrm{ii})}{\leq}\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{1}}\,\|\,p_{Y_{1}}\big)\overset{(\mathrm{iii})}{\leq}\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{1},\ldots,X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{T}}\,\|\,p_{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{T}}\big)

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{iv})}{=}\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{T}}\,\|\,p_{Y_{T}}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\,\|\,p_{Y_{t-1}|Y_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{v})}{\leq}O(T^{-10})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p_{X_{t}}}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\,\|\,p_{Y_{t-1}|Y_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\big)\Big].

(20)

Here (i) holds as
X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{1}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}X_{1}
; (ii) applies Pinsker’s inequality; (iii) arises from the data-processing inequality; (iv) results from the chain rule of the KL divergence; (v) applies (
15
) in Lemma
2
and
X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}X_{t}
for all
t
.

Therefore, bounding
\mathsf{TV}(p_{X_{1}},p_{Y_{1}})
reduces to controlling

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p_{X_{t}}}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\,\|\,p_{Y_{t-1}\mid Y_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\big)\Big]

for each
t=2,\dots,T
.
As we will see in Step 2, the explicit dependence of
X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}
on
X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}
in the auxiliary process allows us to show that the above KL divergence—and thus
\mathsf{TV}(p_{X_{1}},p_{Y_{1}})
—is governed by the second-order approximation error of our sampler.

4.3
Step 2: controlling KL divergence between
p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}
and
p_{Y_{t-1}\mid Y_{t}}

In this section, let us fix an arbitrary
2\leq t\leq T
and focus on
\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p_{X_{t}}}\big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\,\|\,p_{Y_{t-1}\mid Y_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\big)\big]
.

Step 2.1: relating KL divergence to second-order approximation error.

Recall the definition of
\Phi(\cdot)
in (
18
), which can be interpreted as a first-order discretization of the probability ODE (
7
). We now define another mapping
\Psi_{t}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{t}}\big(\Phi_{t}(x)-x-(1-\alpha_{t})s^{\star}_{t}(x)\big).

(21)

To provide some intuition,
\Psi_{t}
represents a second-order correction term. Our proposed sampler is designed to construct an approximation of
\Psi_{t}
in order to to reduce the discretization error and, consequently, accelerate sampling.

In what follows, we will relate
\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\,\|\,p_{Y_{t-1}\mid Y_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\big)
to how well our sampler’s update matches this “ideal” update given by
\Psi_{t}
.
Specifically, for any
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by the data processing inequality and the independence between
Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}
and
(X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t},Y_{t})
, we can derive

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\Big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\,\|\,p_{Y_{t-1}\mid Y_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\Big)\leq\mathsf{KL}\Big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1},Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\,\|\,p_{Y_{t-1},Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\mid Y_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{z_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}}p_{Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}(z_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}})\int_{x_{t-1}}p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t},Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t},z_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}})\log\frac{p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t},Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t},z_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}})}{p_{Y_{t-1}\mid Y_{t},Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t},z_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}})}\,\mathrm{d}x_{t-1}\,\mathrm{d}z_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\sim p_{Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\Big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t},Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t},z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\,\|\,p_{Y_{t-1}|Y_{t},Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}\big(\,\cdot\mid x_{t},z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\Big)\Big].

Next, recall the definitions of
Y_{t-1}
and
X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}
in (
11
) and (
19
), respectively. By construction,
X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}
(resp.
Y_{t-1}
) is normally distributed conditioned on
X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}=x_{t}
and
Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}=z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}
(resp.
Y_{t}=x_{t}
and
Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}=z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}
), where the randomness arises from
Z_{t}
. Exploiting their expressions and invoking the KL divergence formula for normal distributions, we can continue the above derivation as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\sim p_{Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\Big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t},Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t},z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\,\|\,p_{Y_{t-1}|Y_{t},Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}\big(\,\cdot\mid x_{t},z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big\|\Phi_{t}(X_{t})-X_{t}-(1-\alpha_{t})s_{t}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-(1-\alpha_{t})\mathsf{clip}_{t}\Big\{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}\big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}(X_{t})\big)-s_{t}\big(X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\Big\}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big|\,X_{t}=x_{t}\bigg],

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{=}\frac{(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big\|\Psi_{t}(X_{t})-\mathsf{clip}_{t}\Big\{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}\big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}(X_{t})\big)-s_{t}\big(X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+s_{t}^{\star}(X_{t})-s_{t}(X_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big|\,X_{t}=x_{t}\bigg],

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(\mathrm{ii})}{\lesssim}(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big\|\mathsf{clip}_{t}\Big\{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}\big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}(X_{t})\big)-s_{t}\big(X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\Big\}-\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big|\,X_{t}=x_{t}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+(1-\alpha_{t})\big\|s_{t}^{\star}(x_{t})-s_{t}(x_{t})\big\|_{2}^{2},

where (i) plugs in the expression of
\Psi_{t}(\cdot)
in (
21
); (ii) holds because of
1-\alpha_{t}\lesssim\log T/T=o(1)
in (

) from Lemma

and thus
\sigma_{t}^{2}=(1-\alpha_{t})+{4(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}}/{(3-2\alpha_{t})}=\big(1+o(1)\big)(1-\alpha_{t})
.
As a remark, the notation
Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}(X_{t})
emphasizes that
Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}(X_{t})
is computed based on
X_{t}
according to (

) here.

Taking the expectation over
x_{t}\sim p_{X_{t}}
, we arrive at the key inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p_{X_{t}}}\big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\,\|\,p_{Y_{t-1}\mid Y_{t}}(\,\cdot\mid x_{t})\big)\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big\|\mathsf{clip}_{t}\Big\{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}\big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}(X_{t})\big)-s_{t}\big(X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\Big\}-\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|s_{t}^{\star}(X_{t})-s_{t}(X_{t})\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big\|\mathsf{clip}_{t}\Big\{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}\big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}(X_{t})\big)-s_{t}\big(X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\Big\}-\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]+\varepsilon_{t}^{2},

(22)

where the last step arises from Assumption
1
on score estimation.

This result reveals that the KL divergence between the ideal backward distribution
p_{X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t-1}\mid X^{\mathsf{aux}}_{t}}
and our sample’s backward distribution
p_{Y_{t-1}\mid Y_{t}}
is governed by two factors: the discrepancy between the true second-order term encoded by
\Psi_{t}
and those realized by our algorithm, and the error in score matching.
In what follows, we will focus on controlling the second-order approximation error term in (

).

Step 2.2: bounding second-order approximation error based on typical points.

Recalling the typical set
\mathcal{E}_{t}
defined in (

), we denote the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{t}\coloneqq\big\{X_{t}\in\mathcal{E}_{t}\big\}.

(23)

Let us first examine the behavior of the second-order correction term
\Psi_{t}(X_{t})
when
X_{t}
lies in this typical set
\mathcal{E}_{t}
, which is formalized by the following lemma.
Here, we remind the reader of the notation that for any event
\mathcal{E}
, we denote
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[\cdot]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\big[\cdot\ind\{\mathcal{E}\}\big]
. The proof is provided in Appendix

Lemma 4
.

The mapping
\Psi_{t}(\cdot)
defined in (

) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}_{t}}\bigg[\Big\|\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}^{\star}\big(Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}}\big)-s_{t}^{\star}(X_{t})-\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]\lesssim(1-\alpha_{t})^{4}\bigg(\frac{d}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\bigg)^{5}.

(24)

Moreover, one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{t}\subset\bigg\{\big\|\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\big\|_{2}\leq C_{4}(1-\alpha_{t})\bigg(\frac{d\log T}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\bigg)^{3/2}\bigg\},

for some absolute constant

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{c}}}\bigg[\big\|\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\big\|_{2}^{2}+(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}\bigg(\frac{d\log T}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\bigg)^{3}\bigg]\lesssim\frac{(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}}{T^{10}}\bigg(\frac{d}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\bigg)^{3}.

(26)

Remark 4
.

Through a careful analysis, we are able to characterize the intrinsic higher-order smoothness properties of true scores in establishing (
22
). This allows us to pin down the second-order approximation error of the proposed sampler without imposing additional higher-order smooth assumptions on the true scores or score estimators required by prior works
(
Li et al., 2024a,
;
Huang et al., 2024a,
;
Huang et al., 2024b,
;
Li and Jiao,, 2024
)
.

In a word, Lemma
4
tells us that on the event
\mathcal{A}_{t}
, the second-order correction term
\Psi_{t}(X_{t})
is close to the true score functions evaluated at the auxiliary process
Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}}
constructed in (
16
) in Step 1 and the forward process
X_{t}
. In addition, the
\ell_{2}
norm
\|\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\|_{2}
is bounded above on
\mathcal{A}_{t}
, which directly motivates our choice of the thresholding function
\mathsf{clip}_{t}(\cdot)
in (

\|\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\|_{2}
remains reasonably bounded.
By comparing the terms in (
22
) with the left-hand side of (
24
), one can expect that the right-hand side of (
24
) serves as the dominant term in the upper bound for the KL divergence (
22
).

To proceed, we must address a subtlety: the expression (
22
) involves the score estimators evaluated at random variables
Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}(X_{t})
and
X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}
. However, the assumption on score matching (Assumption

) only guarantees the estimation error of the score estimators when applied to the forward process
(X_{t})
. To address this issue, we can apply a change of measure. Specifically, we define the sets
\mathcal{F}_{t,1},\mathcal{F}_{t,2},\mathcal{F}_{t,3}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t,1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon p_{X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}(x)\leq 2\,p_{X_{t}}(x)\bigg\};

(27a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t,2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon p_{Y^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}(X_{t})}(x)\leq 4\,p_{X_{t-1}}(x)\bigg\};

(27b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t,3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon p_{Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}}(x)\leq 2\,p_{X_{t-1}}(x)\bigg\},

(27c)

and then define the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{t}\coloneqq\Big\{X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t,1},\,Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}(X_{t})\in\mathcal{F}_{t,2},\,Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t,3}\Big\}.

(28)

In other words, these sets contain points where the densities of the auxiliary random variables
X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}
,

, and
Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}
are upper bounded by a constant factor relative to those of the forward process
(X_{t})

\mathcal{B}_{t}

With the above preparation in place, we now decompose the first term of (

) versus the complementary set, using the bound of

on
\mathcal{A}_{t}
. Concretely, we derive:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big\|\mathsf{clip}_{t}\Big\{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}\big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}\big)-s_{t}\big(X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)\Big\}-\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{\lesssim}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}_{t}\cap\mathcal{B}_{t}}\bigg[\Big\|\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s^{\star}_{t-1}\big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}\big)-s^{\star}_{t}\big(X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)-\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}_{t}\cap\mathcal{B}_{t}}\bigg[\Big\|\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}\big(Y_{t}^{\mathsf{mid}}\big)-\alpha_{t}^{3/2}s_{t-1}^{\star}\big(Y_{t}^{\star,\mathsf{mid}}\big)-s_{t}\big(X_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})Z^{\mathsf{mid}}_{t}\big)+s_{t}^{\star}(X_{t})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{t}\cap\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{c}}\big)(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}\bigg(\frac{d\log T}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\bigg)^{3}+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{c}}}\bigg[\big\|\Psi_{t}(X_{t})\big\|_{2}^{2}+(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}\bigg(\frac{d\log T}{1-\overline{\alpha}_{t}}\bigg)^{3}\bigg]

5
Discussion

This work uncovers the feasibility of provable acceleration of score-based samplers under minimal assumptions: namely,
L^{2}
-accurate score estimates and a finite second moment of the target distribution. We have proposed a training-free accelerated sampler that attains an iteration complexity of
\widetilde{O}(d^{5/4}/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, establishing a theoretical foundation for efficient sampling speedups.

Several important directions remain for future investigation. One pressing issue is to sharpen our convergence theory with respect to the data dimension
d
through a more refined analysis.
Moving beyond the second-order approximation developed in this work, another promising direction is to explore whether higher-order ODE approximations can yield improved iteration complexity.
Finally, for target distributions exhibiting low-dimensional structures, developing specialized acceleration schemes that exploit these intrinsic properties presents another important avenue for sampling efficiency.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{\tau^{k}(1-\tau)^{k}}\frac{1}{\tau^{3k/2}}\bigg(\frac{d}{1-\tau}\bigg)^{5k/2}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2k}(1-\tau)^{2k}}\frac{1}{\tau^{k/2}}\bigg(\frac{d}{1-\tau}\bigg)^{3k/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{\tau^{3k}(1-\tau)^{3k}}\bigg(\frac{\tau d}{1-\tau}\bigg)^{k/2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\asymp C_{k}\frac{1}{(1-\tau)^{k}}\bigg(\frac{d}{\tau(1-\tau)}\bigg)^{5k/2},

as claimed in (
51b
).

Step 3: a high-probability bound for the
\ell_{2}
norm of the first derivative.

Fix an arbitrary
\tau\in[0,1)
such that
-\log p_{\widetilde{X}_{\tau}}(x_{\tau}^{\star})\lesssim d\log T
.
By Lemma
9
, we know that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\bigg\|\frac{x^{\star}_{\tau}}{\sqrt{1-\tau}}-\widetilde{X}_{0}\bigg\|_{2}^{k}\,\Big|\,\widetilde{X}_{\tau}=x_{\tau}^{\star}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\bigg(\frac{d\tau\log T}{1-\tau}\bigg)^{k/2}

for some constant
C_{k}>0
that only depends on
k
.
Further, recalling
\theta^{\star}(\tau)\coloneqq\tilde{s}^{\star}(x_{\tau}^{\star},\tau)
is the score function of
p_{\widetilde{X}_{\tau}}
, we know from (
36
) that

\displaystyle\big\|\theta^{\star}(\tau)\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|x^{\star}_{\tau}-\sqrt{1-\tau}\widetilde{X}_{0}\big\|_{2}\mid\widetilde{X}_{\tau}=x_{\tau}^{\star}\Big]\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d\log T}{\tau}}.

As a result, plugging these bounds into the expressions in (
112
) and (
113
) yields that

\displaystyle\big\|h_{0}(\tau)\big\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{1-\tau}}\sqrt{\frac{d\log T}{\tau}}=\sqrt{\frac{d\tau\log T}{1-\tau}};

\displaystyle\big|g_{1}(\tau)\big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\int_{x_{0}}p_{\widetilde{X}_{0}\mid\widetilde{X}_{\tau}}(x_{0}\mid x_{\tau}^{\star})\bigg\|\frac{x_{\tau}^{\star}}{\sqrt{1-\tau}}-x_{0}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{x_{0}}p_{\widetilde{X}_{0}\mid\widetilde{X}_{\tau}}(x_{0}\mid x_{\tau}^{\star})\bigg\|\frac{x_{\tau}^{\star}}{\sqrt{1-\tau}}-x_{0}\bigg\|_{2}\frac{\|\theta^{\star}(\tau)\|_{2}}{\sqrt{1-\tau}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\frac{d\tau\log T}{1-\tau}+\frac{1}{\tau}\sqrt{\frac{d\tau\log T}{1-\tau}}\sqrt{\frac{d\log T}{\tau(1-\tau)}}\asymp\frac{d\log T}{\tau(1-\tau)};

and

\displaystyle\big\|h_{1}(\tau)\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\int_{x_{0}}p_{\widetilde{X}_{0}\mid\widetilde{X}_{\tau}}(x_{0}\mid x_{\tau}^{\star})\bigg\|\frac{x_{\tau}^{\star}}{\sqrt{1-\tau}}-x_{0}\bigg\|_{2}^{3}\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{x_{0}}p_{\widetilde{X}_{0}\mid\widetilde{X}_{\tau}}(x_{0}\mid x_{\tau}^{\star})\bigg\|\frac{x_{\tau}^{\star}}{\sqrt{1-\tau}}-x_{0}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\frac{\|\theta^{\star}(\tau)\|_{2}}{\sqrt{1-\tau}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\bigg(\frac{d\tau\log T}{1-\tau}\bigg)^{3/2}+\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{d\tau\log T}{1-\tau}\sqrt{\frac{d\log T}{\tau(1-\tau)}}\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}\bigg(\frac{d\log T}{1-\tau}\bigg)^{3/2}.

Taking these bounds collectively with (
115a
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\big\|u_{1}(\tau)\big\|_{2}^{k}

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{k}\bigg\{\frac{\big\|\theta^{\star}(\tau)\big\|_{2}^{k}}{\tau^{k}(1-\tau)^{5k/2}}+\frac{\big\|h_{1}(\tau)\big\|_{2}^{k}+|g_{1}(\tau)|^{k}\big\|h_{0}(\tau)\big\|_{2}^{k}}{\tau^{k}(1-\tau)^{k}}+\frac{\big\|h_{0}(\tau)\big\|_{2}^{k}}{\tau^{2k}(1-\tau)^{2k}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{k}\bigg\{\frac{1}{\tau^{k}(1-\tau)^{5k/2}}\bigg(\frac{d\log T}{\tau}\bigg)^{k/2}+\frac{1}{\tau^{k}(1-\tau)^{k}}\frac{1}{\tau^{k/2}}\bigg(\frac{d\log T}{1-\tau}\bigg)^{3k/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{\tau^{2k}(1-\tau)^{2k}}\bigg(\frac{d\tau\log T}{1-\tau}\bigg)^{k/2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\asymp C_{k}\frac{1}{(1-\tau)^{k}}\bigg(\frac{d\log T}{\tau(1-\tau)}\bigg)^{3k/2},

as claimed in (
51c
).
This completes the proof of Lemma
7
.

C.4
Proof of Lemma
8

We begin with the first claim. Note that conditional on
\widetilde{X}_{0}
,
\widetilde{X}_{\tau}
satisfies
\widetilde{X}_{\tau}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{1-\tau}\widetilde{X}_{0},\tau I_{d}\big)
. For any integer
k\geq 2
, the standard Gaussian random vector
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|Z\|_{2}^{k}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}Z_{i}^{2}\Big)^{k/2}\bigg]\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{\leq}d^{k/2-1}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d}|Z_{i}|^{k}\bigg]=d^{k/2}\mathbb{E}\big[|Z_{1}|^{k}\big]\overset{(\mathrm{ii})}{\leq}\frac{2^{k/2}\Gamma\big(\frac{k+1}{2}\big)}{\sqrt{\pi}}d^{k/2},

where (i) uses Jensen’s equality that the convexity of
x\mapsto x^{k/2}
when
k\geq 2
; (ii) uses the moment property of the Gaussian random variable and
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function. As for
k=1
, we can also invoke Jensen’s inequality to find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|Z\|_{2}\big]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\big[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}\big]}=\sqrt{d}.

Consequently, it follows from the tower property that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\widetilde{X}_{\tau}-\sqrt{1-\tau}\widetilde{X}_{0}\big\|_{2}^{k}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\widetilde{X}_{\tau}-\sqrt{1-\tau}\widetilde{X}_{0}\big\|_{2}^{k}\,\big|\,\widetilde{X}_{0}\Big]\bigg]\leq C_{k}(\tau d)^{k/2}

for some constant
C_{k}
that only relies on
k
.

Turning to the second claim, note that
\tilde{s}^{\star}(x,{\tau})=-\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{X}_{\tau}-\sqrt{1-\tau}\widetilde{X}_{0}\,\big|\,\widetilde{X}_{\tau}=x\big]
. As a result, one can apply Jensen’s inequality and the tower property to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\tilde{s}^{\star}\big(\widetilde{X}_{\tau},\tau\big)\big\|_{2}^{k}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{k}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big\|\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{X}_{\tau}-\sqrt{1-\tau}\widetilde{X}_{0}\,\Big|\,\widetilde{X}_{\tau}\big]\Big\|_{2}^{k}\bigg]\leq\frac{1}{\tau^{k}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\widetilde{X}_{\tau}-\sqrt{1-\tau}\widetilde{X}_{0}\big\|_{2}^{k}\,\big|\,\widetilde{X}_{\tau}\Big]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{k}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\widetilde{X}_{\tau}-\sqrt{1-\tau}\widetilde{X}_{0}\big\|_{2}^{k}\Big]\leq C_{k}\bigg(\frac{d}{\tau}\bigg)^{k/2}.

C.5
Proof of Lemma
9

The claim (
65
) can be established using the same analysis as in
Li and Yan, 2024b (, Lemma 1)
. For brevity of presentation, we omit the detailed proof.
In what follows, we shall focus on proving (
67
).

Let us assume that there exists some
\widetilde{\tau}\in(0,1)
with
|\widetilde{\tau}-\tau|\leq c_{0}\tau(1-\tau)
such that

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}\coloneqq\sup_{\widetilde{\tau}\wedge\tau\leq\tau^{\prime}\leq\widetilde{\tau}\vee\tau}\frac{-\log p_{\widetilde{X}_{\tau^{\prime}}}(x_{\tau^{\prime}}^{\star})}{d\log T}>2\theta.

Otherwise, (
67
) holds directly.
In particular, by (
65
) and (
73
), this assumption implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{\widetilde{\tau}\wedge\tau\leq\tau^{\prime}\leq\widetilde{\tau}\vee\tau}\mathsf{tr}\big(\widetilde{J}(x^{\star}_{\tau^{\prime}},\tau^{\prime})\big)\leq\frac{C_{7}\theta^{\prime}d\log T}{\tau^{\prime}(1-\tau^{\prime})}-\frac{d}{\tau^{\prime}(1-\tau^{\prime})}\leq\frac{C_{7}}{2}\frac{\theta^{\prime}d\log T}{\tau^{\prime}(1-\tau^{\prime})},

for some sufficiently large absolute constant
C_{7}>0
, provided
T
is sufficiently large.

Now, by the probability flow ODE (
37
), it is straightforward to calculate that

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}}\log p_{\widetilde{X}_{\tau^{\prime}}/\sqrt{1-\tau^{\prime}}}\big(x_{\tau^{\prime}}^{\star}/\sqrt{1-\tau^{\prime}}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}}\log\frac{p_{\widetilde{X}_{\tau^{\prime}}/\sqrt{1-\tau^{\prime}}}\big(x_{\tau^{\prime}}^{\star}/\sqrt{1-\tau^{\prime}}\big)}{p_{\widetilde{X}_{\tau}/\sqrt{1-\tau}}\big(x_{\tau}^{\star}/\sqrt{1-\tau}\big)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}}\log\mathsf{det}^{-1}\bigg(\frac{\partial x_{\tau^{\prime}}^{\star}/\sqrt{1-\tau^{\prime}}}{\partial x_{\tau}^{\star}/\sqrt{1-\tau}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}}\log\mathsf{det}\bigg\{\exp\biggl(\int_{\tau}^{\tau^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{J}(x_{\tau^{\prime\prime}}^{\star},\tau^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime\prime}\bigg)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{tr}\big(\widetilde{J}(x_{\tau^{\prime}}^{\star},\tau^{\prime})\big),

where the penultimate line uses (
72
).

Also, for any
\tau^{\prime}\in(0,1)
such that
|\tau^{\prime}-\tau|\leq|\widetilde{\tau}-\tau|\leq\tau(1-\tau)/2
, one has

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{\tau^{\prime}(1-\tau^{\prime})}-\frac{1}{\tau(1-\tau)}\bigg|=\frac{|(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)(1-\tau^{\prime}-\tau)|}{\tau(1-\tau)}\frac{1}{\tau^{\prime}(1-\tau^{\prime})}\leq\frac{1}{2\tau^{\prime}(1-\tau^{\prime})}.

Therefore, combined with
p_{\widetilde{X}_{\tau}/\sqrt{1-\tau}}(x_{\tau}^{\star}/\sqrt{1-\tau})=(1-\tau)^{d/2}p_{X_{\tau}}(x_{\tau}^{\star})
,
this tells us that any
\tau^{\prime}\in[\widetilde{\tau}\wedge\tau,\widetilde{\tau}\vee\tau]
, the following holds for
c_{0}
sufficiently small:

\displaystyle-\log p_{\widetilde{X}_{\tau^{\prime}}}(x_{\tau^{\prime}}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq-\log p_{\widetilde{X}_{\tau}}(x_{\tau}^{\star})+\frac{d}{2}\big|\log(1-\tau^{\prime})-\log(1-\tau)\big|+|\tau^{\prime}-\tau|\frac{C_{7}}{2}\frac{\theta^{\prime}d\log T}{\tau(1-\tau)}

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{\leq}\theta d\log T+\frac{d}{2}\frac{|\tau^{\prime}-\tau|}{1-\tau}+|\tau^{\prime}-\tau|\frac{C_{7}}{2}\frac{\theta^{\prime}d\log T}{\tau(1-\tau)}

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{ii})}{\leq}\theta d\log T+\frac{c_{0}}{2}d+\frac{c_{0}C_{7}}{2}\theta^{\prime}d\log T\overset{(\mathrm{iii})}{\leq}\frac{3}{4}\theta^{\prime}d\log T,

where we use
\log(1+x)\leq|x|
for any
x
, (ii) is true as
|\tau^{\prime}-\tau|\leq|\widetilde{\tau}-\tau|\leq c_{0}\tau(1-\tau)\leq c_{0}(1-\tau)
, (iii) holds by the assumption
\theta^{\prime}>2\theta
.
This leads to a contradiction. Hence, the proof of (
67
) is complete.
