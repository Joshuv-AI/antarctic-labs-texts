Large serine recombinase-mediated gene insertion for high-throughput screens: advantages, design principles, and applications
Authors: Andrea Martella, Kenneth A Matreyek, David I Fisher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-throughput functional assays, such as multiplexed assays of variant effect (MAVE), increasingly demand stable, precise integration of large DNA libraries into mammalian genomes. While CRISPR-based technologies excel at localized, small-scale edits, they are constrained by payload size limits, heterogeneous editing outcomes, and, depending on the specific modality and repair pathway utilized, potential variability in junction fidelity during multikilobase insertions. In this review, we highlight large serine recombinases (LSRs) as highly efficient, single-enzyme alternatives for unidirectional, site-specific integration of large payloads with deterministic junctions. We survey targeted genomic integration strategies and detail best practices for implementing recombinase-based landing pad architectures. By enforcing single-copy, orientation-fixed integration at defined loci, landing pads decouple variant delivery from local chromatin effects to ensure the uniform, isogenic expression required for quantitative genotype–phenotype mapping. We further outline scalable applications of LSR-mediated integration across pooled and arrayed MAVE, CRISPR screens, and precise gene expression tuning. Finally, we assess current technological bottlenecks, particularly large donor delivery and the requisite pre-installation of canonical att recognition sites, while exploring emerging innovations in virus-like particle delivery, one-step CRISPR-recombinase systems, and computationally engineered programmable recombinases that promise to bypass these limitations and broaden mammalian genome engineering.

Introduction

Precision genome engineering is foundational to contemporary advances in biotechnology, medicine, and biological research. The advent of CRISPR-based technologies has greatly accelerated the field by enabling efficient generation of single-nucleotide variants and small sequence changes within endogenous genes, advancing both functional genomics and the modelling of human diseases [1–3]. Yet beyond editing endogenous genes, many applications require the efficient and accurate integration of large DNA molecules into specific loci within complex genomes. This need is especially evident in high-throughput functional assays, such as multiplexed assays of variant effect (MAVE), which often rely on the stable introduction of large, highly diverse libraries of gene variants into cell populations to systematically investigate genotype–phenotype relationships [4, 5]. For quantitative assays, it is essential to insert entire open reading frames, regulatory elements, or expanded coding regions in a controlled, isogenic manner, minimizing position effects and ensuring comparable expression across variants. However, while CRISPR technologies excel at precise, small-scale edits, they frequently show reduced efficiency and variable junction outcomes when integrating multikilobase payloads [6–8].

Large serine recombinases (LSRs), originally characterized in bacteriophages, offer a complementary solution. Unlike tyrosine recombinases (e.g. Cre and Flp) or programmable nuclease-driven integration, LSRs mediate unidirectional, sequence-specific recombination between short recognition sites—attP and attB—typically installed in the genome and donor DNA, respectively [9–13]. This specific orientation is generally preferred because the longer attP sequence is less likely to have close homology matches in the mammalian genome, reducing the risk of endogenous pseudo-sites and off-target recombination. This single-enzyme mechanism enables efficient insertion of large constructs with predictable junctions, orientation control, and minimal reliance on host repair pathways. Their modularity, broad host compatibility, and capacity to integrate long, complex donors have made LSRs valuable for building genetically defined cell lines, animal models, and screening platforms, establishing them at the forefront of the emerging generation of integration-based genome editors [14]. These phage-derived LSRs are also frequently referred to in the literature as serine integrases; hereafter, we will primarily use the term recombinases.

In this review, we first delineate the requirements and limitations of high-throughput variant functionalization, emphasizing why endogenous gene editing methods often fall short in efficiently introducing genetic variants at scale into endogenous loci. We then survey targeted genomic integration strategies for exogenous expression of variant libraries—from CRISPR-assisted approaches to recombinase-based methods—and introduce LSRs as a robust alternative. Next, we detail current best practices for genomic landing pad (LP) design and genome targeting and outline scalable applications in MAVE and CRISPR screens. Finally, we assess the current state of serine recombinase technology, highlighting existing limitations and emerging opportunities for further improvement and innovation.

High-throughput functional assays and limitations of endogenous gene editing

High-throughput functional assays such as MAVE aim to quantify genotype–phenotype relationships at scale [5, 15]. MAVEs introduce predefined variant libraries and measure effects via cell fitness, gene expression, protein stability, enzymatic activity, protein–protein interaction profiling, or reporter-based readouts. Across modalities, robust screen studies require high-fidelity variant libraries with sufficient coverage, one-perturbation-per-cell to preserve genotype–phenotype linkage, stable expression across the experimental timeline, and minimized context-dependent variability that can confound quantitative interpretation.

One approach is to assay variants at the endogenous locus using high-throughput CRISPR–Cas genome editing [5, 16–25]. Working in the native genomic context maintains physiological protein expression, endogenous regulation, and correct splicing/isoform usage. However, while practical for limited variant sets, approaching saturation mutagenesis is challenging. Several constraints are shared across CRISPR modalities, including guide targetability and protospacer adjacent motifs (PAM) availability, cell- and locus-specific chromatin states that affect editing efficiency, heterogeneous editing outcomes within individual cells that weaken genotype–phenotype linkage, and biallelic heterogeneity that complicates phenotypic attribution [6, 26–31]. These include the generation of intended edits alongside unintended byproducts within the same cell, as well as distinct edits across alleles or an edit on one allele and an indel on the other. On top of these, there are modality-specific limitations—whether using homology-directed repair (HDR), base editing (BE), or prime editing (PE) (Box 1). These issues compromise library completeness and uniformity across the target gene, elevate noise, and can bias selection readouts unless rigorous quality control and normalization are applied.

To circumvent the limitations of endogenous gene editing, transgene-based deep mutational scanning uses pooled or arrayed screening of plasmid-encoded variant libraries delivered into mammalian cells, linking each variant to a selectable or quantifiable phenotype [5, 6, 26–45]. This strategy avoids many genome-editing constraints but maintaining robust genotype–phenotype linkage requires several conditions. First, each cell should carry a single variant (one perturbation per cell) to unambiguously attribute effects. Second, expression must be stable over the experimental timeline and constrained to a defined range, with comparable levels across cells to support quantitative comparisons. Third, high delivery/integration efficiency is needed to achieve sufficient library coverage at practical cost. Finally, accurate variant assessment might require eliminating wild-type protein to avoid complementation or dominant-negative masking; when the gene is essential, this necessitates strategies that maintain viability while enabling variant discrimination.

For exogenous expression, lentiviral vectors and transposon systems are common delivery platforms but are suboptimal for quantitative variant assays. Lentiviral vectors provide efficient delivery but have limited payload capacity (typically capped around 8–10 kb) and positional effects from semi-random genomic integration, that generally favors integration into active transcription units [46]. Furthermore, lentiviral vectors undergo reverse transcriptase template switching through homologous sequences, which can scramble libraries and render distal DNA barcodes useless [47]. DNA transposon systems, such as piggyBac and Sleeping Beauty, accommodate larger payloads, but they complicate copy-number control. Whereas lentiviral copy numbers can be titrated by using a low multiplicity of infection (MOI), transposon-mediated integrations cannot be easily restricted to a single copy per cell. Additionally, their delivery efficiency inherently declines with increasing plasmid size, a constraint common to nonviral delivery methods. Furthermore, they exhibit quasi-random integration profiles dictated by local sequence preferences (e.g. TTAA target sites for piggyBac or TA dinucleotides for Sleeping Beauty). In both lentiviral vector and transposon-based systems, semi/quasi-random genomic integration drives context-dependent variability, increases the risk of silencing and long-term instability, and generates heterogeneous cell pools that disrupt expression uniformity and weaken genotype–phenotype consistency [48–52].

Targeted genomic integration offers a compelling alternative, helping to address context-dependent expression, copy-number heterogeneity, payload constraints, and long-term instability. By directing exogenous cassettes to predefined loci, targeted strategies can promote more uniform expression, support single-copy integration, and reduce variability arising from genomic context [53–58]. In mammalian systems, targeted architectures at safe-harbor or other locus-specific sites enable reproducible integration, facilitate standardized quality control, and can be adapted across cell types and payload sizes (Table 1). Targeted integration can be achieved either through programmable nuclease-mediated cleavage followed by repair-driven insertion or through site-specific recombination between short recognition sites, each with distinct trade-offs in efficiency, payload capacity, and workflow complexity. Targeted integration strategies for high-throughput screens: from CRISPR-assisted to recombinase-based approaches

Taken together, these considerations suggest that for high-throughput functional assays, exogenous gene expression is often preferable to endogenous gene editing, and targeted chromosomal integration a better alternative to uncontrolled integration. By promoting one variant per cell in an isogenic background with more uniform expression, this strategy can strengthen genotype–phenotype linkage. However, this approach is not without compromises: variants integrated at ectopic safe-harbor loci remain outside their native physiological context and are subject to local epigenetic regulation that may not fully recapitulate endogenous gene behavior.

We now have a look at the different targeted integration methods available. We examine CRISPR-assisted strategies for multikilobase inserts and their practical limitations in large-scale pooled or arrayed screens and then introduce LSRs and the LP strategy which mitigates many of these constraints and enables reproducible, quantitative assays at scale.

Over the past few years, multiple CRISPR-based strategies have emerged to place large DNA payloads precisely into the genome [14, 59, 60], yet their performance at scale remains challenging. Canonical HDR-mediated knock-in uses DSBs to stimulate homologous recombination for cassette insertion or repair with donor templates, offering precise, footprint-free, seamless junctions and native-locus placement (Fig. 1A). In practice, the limitations that affect the introduction of small edits in the endogenous gene, that we mentioned above, also apply to targeted integration of full expression cassettes: the efficiency is strongly influenced by cell cycle state, repair-pathway biases, local chromatin, and sequence context, especially when integration is directed downstream of a gene-of-interest promoter rather than into a well-characterized genetic safe harbor [61–64]. Importantly standard knock-in efficiencies fall steeply as payload size increases [65, 66] a limitation driven both by the inherent physical constraints of delivering large nonviral donor templates into the cell and the mechanistic constraints of homologous recombination itself. Recent optimized protocols have attempted to bypass this barrier; for instance, utilizing immune-evasive circular single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) or double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) templates combined with small “helper” plasmids has enabled ultra-large (∼10 kb) integrations at >20% efficiency in primary T cells and >60% in induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) [67]. Nevertheless, despite these optimizations, on-target edited products frequently include indels and complex rearrangements, necessitating extensive validation to confirm product purity and junction fidelity.

NHEJ-based methods including HITI [68] and Obligate Ligation-Gated Recombination (ObLiGaRe) [69] exploit end joining to insert donors without homology arms, can operate in nondividing cells, and may in some settings support larger inserts more efficiently than HDR (Fig. 1B). However, integration efficiencies are typically low. While HITI is rationally designed to enrich for directional insertion via a continuous re-cutting mechanism, achieving scarless, deterministic junctions is difficult due to the inherent error-prone nature of NHEJ. This variable junction fidelity, which is highly dependent on locus and cell type, complicates data interpretation in pooled screens. Furthermore, like all nuclease-based approaches, they introduce DSBs that can lead to undesirable consequences such as big deletions and chromosomal rearrangements [7, 70]. PE-assisted insertion strategies, such as twinPE [71] and GRAND [72] couple a Cas9 nickase (nCas9) to a reverse transcriptase and use pegRNAs to program small-to-moderate insertions without DSBs, broadening the accessible edit space while avoiding DSB-associated rearrangements (Fig. 1C). Recent iterations, such as QuadPE (utilizing four pegRNAs) [73] and Prime Assembly (combining twinPE flaps with in-cell assembly) [74], push these boundaries to accommodate multikilobase payloads without requiring recombinases. However, their performance is highly dependent on pegRNA and nicking-guide design, mismatch repair activity, and chromatin context, and, in the case of Prime Assembly, the requisite use of NHEJ inhibitors that may elevate genomic instability risks. Consequently, multikilobase insertions remain impractical at scale in mammalian systems.

CASTs utilize RNA-guided, nuclease-inactive complexes to recruit transposon machinery, expanding the toolkit for large insertions. Currently, two major classes, type I-F (which utilizes the multiprotein Cascade complex) and type V-K (such as Cas12k–Tn7 complexes), have been developed for genome engineering applications [75]. Both classes insert large DNA downstream of PAMs, combining programmable targeting with substantial payload capacity (Fig. 1D). Although V-K CASTs enable large DNA cargo integration in prokaryotes, their activity in mammalian cells remains low, with ∼1%–2% in HEK293T cells and <0.3% in hematopoietic lines [76, 77]. Recent directed evolution efforts by the Liu and Sternberg groups optimized the type I-F CAST machinery yielding evoCAST, which successfully achieved 10%–20% integration efficiency at genomic target sites in human cells [78]. Despite encouraging recent progress [79] current barriers include delivery complexity across multiple proteins, low and context-dependent targeting efficiencies, constraints on insertion position relative to PAMs, and ongoing engineering to curb off-target transposition.

Additional strategies further expand the genome engineering toolkit by coupling CRISPR-based targeting to recombination. These include nuclease-transposase fusion systems, such as Transposase-CRISPR Mediated Targeted Integration (TransCRISTI) [80] and Find and Cut-and-Transfer (FiCAT) technology [81, 82], as well as the recently discovered bridge RNA-guided recombinases (such as IS622 and ISCro4, which utilize a bispecific noncoding RNA to independently bind both target and donor DNA and have recently demonstrated up to ∼20% insertion efficiency in human cells) [83, 84], and the SSAP editors [85–87]. However, these approaches share several important limitations, including variable efficiency, pronounced cell-line dependence, sequence and context restrictions, off-target insertions and limited standardization for large cassettes in mammalian cells (Fig. 1E and F).

Taken together, CRISPR-targeted integration methods, while powerful, impose constraints that are particularly acute in high-throughput settings: intrinsic post-delivery efficiencies decline sharply for multikilobase payloads; outcomes depend on chromatin and repair pathways; guide/PAM coverage is uneven; editing outcomes are heterogeneous (e.g. mixed alleles and allele-specific edits); and orientation and junctions can be uncertain.

LSRs, such as Bxb1 and ΦC31 integrases, directly address these limitations. Unlike DSB-dependent CRISPR approaches, LSRs function as single enzymes with minimal reliance on host repair, delivering high, reproducible integration efficiency across loci and cell lines. They tolerate multikilobase donors without steep efficiency penalties and provide high specificity at matched att sites [88–90]. Orthogonality enabled by different LSRs or att site sequence determinants supports multiplexing of independent insertion events [9, 91, 92].

Targeted integration using LSRs is commonly implemented via a two-step genomic LP workflow—installing a genomic att site in the genome followed by LSR-mediated integration of att-bearing donors—which will be detailed below.

LSRs for genome engineering

Site-specific recombinases catalyze DNA rearrangements between short recognition sites, enabling insertions, deletions, and inversions with base-pair precision [93]. Two major families, tyrosine recombinases and serine recombinases, are named for the specific active-site amino acid residue that acts as the catalytic nucleophile during DNA cleavage. Despite their functional similarities, these two families share no structural or evolutionary homology and represent vast enzyme families, with thousands of members identified across bacterial and archaeal genomes via metagenomics. They differ in chemistry, directionality, and host-factor dependence [94]. Tyrosine recombinases (such as Cre, Flp) utilize a C-terminal catalytic domain and proceed via Holliday junction intermediates. While simpler members like Cre function autonomously, many tyrosine recombinases require complex accessory DNA-binding sites, host-encoded helper proteins (such as integration host factor), or specific DNA supercoiling to assemble an active recombination synapse. Furthermore, they frequently act reversibly. LSRs (such as Bxb1 and ΦC31 integrases) instead utilize an N-terminal catalytic domain and execute concerted double-strand cleavages and subunit rotation, yielding predictable, typically unidirectional outcomes with minimal host dependence, well suited for integrating multikilobase cassettes in mammalian cells [11] (Fig. 2A). It should be noted that LSRs constitute a subgroup of the broader serine recombinase family; the enzymes used for the targeted genomic integrations described here are phage serine integrases, which belong to the LSR subgroup, although not all LSRs are conventional integrases.

LSRs recombine asymmetric attP (phage genome) and attB (bacteria genome) sites to form attL/attR with fixed orientation. These sites contain recombinase-binding motifs flanking a central dinucleotide that governs compatibility and orthogonality (Fig. 2B). The recombinase assembles a synapse, cleaves, rotates, and ligates in an enzyme-intrinsic cycle; integration is favored, whereas excision generally requires a recombination directionality factor. Consequently, LSRs deliver deterministic orientation, sequence-defined orthogonality, and host-independent junction fidelity.

Two design features drive utility. First, att site polarity constrains recombination to a single insertion orientation, removing promoter/frame ambiguity and simplifying downstream readouts. Second, att sequence determinants, especially the central dinucleotide, enable mutually incompatible pairs, supporting multiplexing, recombinase-mediated cassette exchange (RMCE), and sequential installations without crosstalk [9, 10] (Fig. 2C). Together, short programmable att sites, enzyme-directed chemistry, unidirectional attP/attB to attL/attR conversion, fixed orientation, and orthogonality underpin scalable, high-fidelity integration of multikilobase payloads. However, unlike footprint-free HDR-mediated knock-ins, LSR-mediated integration inherently leaves an att sequence footprint (attL and attR) at the insert–genome junctions. When engineering specific endogenous loci, the potential impact of these footprints, such as altering the spacing of endogenous cis-acting regulatory elements or interfering with native splicing, must be carefully considered.

Among LSRs, Bxb1 integrase often delivers higher integration efficiencies and cleaner specificity in mammalian cells, with well-characterized att site variants that enable orthogonal multiplexing [95, 96]. Because Bxb1 integrase att sites are highly orthogonal to mammalian genomes, the enzyme exhibits negligible off-target activity. In contrast, ΦC31 integrase offers broad host range and long-standing utility but it possesses several natively occurring pseudo-att sites in the human genome. Recombination at these active pseudo-sites can lead to unintended off-target integrations or chromosomal aberrations, particularly if the integrase is constitutively expressed, warranting off-target surveillance [97]. The discovery of additional naturally occurring recombinases, such as Pa01 integrase [89], offer higher integration efficiencies (reportedly achieving up to 75% efficiency for payloads >7 kb in human cells), while engineered LSR variants (e.g. evoBxb1/eeBxb1 integrases) exhibit enhanced catalytic performance or modified specificity [98, 99]. These advances are promising but would benefit from standardized benchmarking across loci, cell types, and donor formats (Table 2).

LP construct designs and genome targeting strategies

LSRs are most deployed via a two-step LP workflow to achieve single-copy, uniform expression of variant libraries. First, a synthetic LP is installed at a defined genomic locus. This is typically achieved by using CRISPR-mediated HDR [88, 100–104] to knock in a full synthetic LP construct, which includes a constitutive promoter preceding an attP recombination sequence, followed by selection markers (SMs) and optional reporters (Fig. 3A). Alternatively, when selection/reporter cassettes are not required, PE [99, 105] can be utilized to knock in a minimal att site directly. The LP cassette typically includes a constitutive promoter, preceding an attP recombination sequence, followed by SMs, and optional reporters (Fig. 3A). Loci are chosen to promote stable, uniform expression. This is achieved by utilizing either validated safe harbors, such as AAVS1 and CCR5 [106], or recently discovered computationally validated human genomic safe harbors (GSHs) designed specifically for robust transgene expression without disrupting host networks [53], or regions near essential or constitutionally expressed genes in open chromatin to minimize silencing [107]. After installation, single-cell cloning and characterization establish LP lines with verified single-copy insertion, correct orientation, and baseline expression. Second, an attB-bearing donor library [e.g. complementary DNA (cDNA) variants, regulatory elements] is delivered to the LP cell line together with the appropriate serine recombinase. For simple integration workflows, recombination at the genomic attP site inserts the entire donor plasmid into the genome. After integration, the LP promoter is no longer linked to its original reporter or SM and instead begins driving expression of the newly integrated payload. If the donor plasmid includes its own selection or reporter marker, that marker is brought under control of the LP promoter. This creates a switch in selection or reporter signal, which can be used to enrich for true recombinants through a gene-trap strategy (Fig. 3A). Each cell undergoes this event independently, enabling pooled expression of large variant libraries while enforcing one variant per cell. Because recombination is att-directed, integration is orientation-fixed with deterministic junctions, key for quantitative assays and standardized quality control (QC).

Installing LP cassettes by CRISPR-based HDR or PE often requires weeks to months, encompassing transfection, decay of episomal copies, selection, single-cell cloning, and extensive genotyping to identify single-copy knock-ins (KIs). These timelines are especially protracted in poorly transfectable cell types or those where HDR is inefficient. As a pragmatic fallback in cell types where CRISPR-mediated HDR is highly inefficient, low MOI lentiviral delivery of LP cassettes enables semi-random integration across diverse lines. While this approach sacrifices the distinct advantages of integrating at a predefined, well-characterized GSH, it reliably achieves single-copy insertion of the LP cassette [108]. With this approach, extensive single-cell cloning and empirical characterization remain necessary to identify a clone with stable baseline expression and preserve isogenicity for downstream library integration. Therefore, LP cassettes should incorporate SMs and reporters to counteract silencing and enrich for active integrants.

Recombinase expression timing is a critical variable. Pre-expressing the serine recombinase prior to introducing the attB donor can boost recombination rates, yet sustained recombinase expression may be cytotoxic and may elevate the risk of recombination at genomic pseudo-sites [104, 109]. LP designs that encode an inducible recombinase, such as a doxycycline-inducible cassette, permit transient, on-demand expression to catalyze integration and then terminate recombinase production after integration, balancing integration efficiency with genomic stability [108] (Fig. 3A).

Despite high initial recombination rates, (such as 30%–80% in standard cell lines and ∼30% in primary cells), variant screening experiments targeting genomic LPs still require enriching for recombined cells. Fluorescence-based enrichment by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) is generally impractical at scale because it is time- and resource-intensive. Embedding complementary positive and negative drug SMs in the genomic LP cassette enables rapid selection that yields virtually 100% recombinants without the need for FACS [108].

LSRs enable genome operations beyond simple safe-harbor insertion. One strategy is to install an att site downstream of an endogenous promoter, so integrated variants are expressed under native regulatory control [71, 105]. Alternatively, a specific exon can be replaced using a LP cassette configured for splice-aware RMCE: att sites are placed in the flanking introns together with an intronic internal ribosome entry site (IRES)-selection cassette [110] (Fig. 3B). RMCE then swaps the placeholder exon for a variant-encoding exon, coupled to a SM switch to enrich correct exchanges. Both strategies are particularly valuable for essential genes where expression must be maintained. In safe-harbor implementations, endogenous gene function persists, so a separate knockout is required after variant integration. By contrast, installing the LP motifs at the endogenous locus enables direct replacement of the native gene (or exon) with the variant, eliminating the need for an additional knockout step. To ensure complete replacement, both alleles must be edited; allele-complete selection schemes, such as split-intein antibiotic resistance [111] that activates only when both alleles are modified, can enrich the desired genotype. This strategy relies on protein trans-splicing: the variant donors are designed to carry either the N-terminal or C-terminal half of an antibiotic resistance protein; each fused to a complementary split-intein domain. Only when both donors successfully integrate into the two alleles and are co-expressed will the split inteins associate and splice the two halves together, reconstituting a fully functional resistance enzyme. In practice, this is only appropriate for single gene variant analyses or arrayed library formats, since in a pooled library no mechanism exists to prevent insertion of different variants in each allele, confounding the ability to differentiate genotype–phenotype relationships.

It must be noted that variants placed in ectopic safe harbors are subject to local epigenetic regulation and chromosomal topologies that do not reflect the physiological regulation of the native locus of the GOI. Conversely, positioning LP motifs downstream of endogenous regulatory sequences preserves the native locus but introduces the risk that the synthetic cassette or the att footprints might subtly disrupt proper transcriptional or post-transcriptional regulation.

There are also instances where the expression of transgenes that negatively impact cell growth complicate isolation of recombinant cells [112, 113]. This rapid depletion of recombined cells would make MAVE experiments challenging because recombined cells would be difficult to enrich. In this scenario one approach is to unlink recombined cell selection from transgene expression using a constitutive promoter for selection and an inducible promoter for expression of the transgene [108]. Alternatively, the donor can be designed so that, upon integration, a SM downstream of the LP promoter is expressed, but the GOI is oriented opposite to that promoter and therefore silent. The SM and GOI are flanked with an inverted attP–attB pair recognized by an orthogonal LSR. After selection, delivering the orthogonal recombinase will induce inversion of the cassette, switching on GOI expression while turning off the SM [114] (Fig. 3C). This sequential, orthogonal inversion ensures robust recovery of recombinants and controlled activation of potentially toxic transgenes. In a similar approach, researchers reversibly silenced expression from TP53 variants using a LoxP-flanked transcriptional stop cassette containing SMs [19].

The two-step LSR paradigm based on prior LP installation described above is highly efficient and reliable; however, it is time- and resource-intensive because it first requires generating and validating a LP cell line. To reduce this upfront burden or expand genomic reach, several “one-step” and programmable strategies couple LSRs to CRISPR editing or engineered recombinase recognition. Broadly, these approaches fall into two strategies: att-dependent and att-relaxed. In the att-dependent approach, one-step systems preserve canonical att chemistry but install the genomic att site and integrate the GOI in a single step. In recently developed platforms, such as PASTE [115] and PASSIGE [99], the CRISPR moiety writes an att sequence directly via PE, after which the LSR (which is fused to the prime editor protein or co-delivered separately) mediates payload integration (Fig. 3D). ONE-STEP tagging [116], one-pot PASTA [117] and L-PGI [118] workflows co-deliver CRISPR reagents, recombinase, and att-armed donors to create and use the site contemporaneously, shortening timelines while leaving att installation as the principal bottleneck (Fig. 3D). The performance of these methods, indeed, hinges on the efficiency of the att-writing step, which in turns depends on local sequence and chromatin context and requires delivery of multiple components. Recent efforts have focused on minimizing the size of att sites to enhance the knock-in efficiency of the PASTE system, however significant off-target integrations have been observed [119]. Alternatively, att-relaxed or engineered recognition strategies aim to lessen dependence on strict att motifs [120, 121]; their rationale, current implementations, and limitations are discussed later.

While direct head-to-head comparisons are highly dependent on the target cell line and locus, exemplary studies demonstrate the high efficiency of these systems. Landing-pad serine recombinase systems display near size-independent efficiency for large payload, which is their most distinctive advantage. Single-entry attP/attB integration workflows frequently achieve high recombination rates. For example, using evolved Bxb1 integrase variants (e.g. evoBxb1/eeBxb1), unselected integration efficiencies of 30%–80% of transfected cells have been reported in cell lines like HEK293T and K562 for payloads up to 15.7 kb, and ∼30% in primary fibroblasts [99]. Wild-type Bxb1 integrase platforms like STRAIGHT-IN have successfully integrated payloads exceeding 50 kb in hiPSCs [122]. By contrast, dual-entry RMCE requires two simultaneous recombination events at orthogonal att sites. Consequently, RMCE often yields lower unselected baseline efficiencies—for instance, ∼12% in optimized cell line studies [123]—but remains highly practical because the precise “selection-swap” logic enables rapid enrichment of the recombinant population while strictly excluding the plasmid backbone. Applications at scale

Here, we outline how LP–based LSR systems can be applied broadly across high-throughput discovery and validation, spanning in vitro and in vivo use cases.

In recent years, numerous MAVE studies have implemented LP systems to screen pooled libraries, establishing LP-based integration as a practical, reproducible backbone for high-throughput discovery across diverse cell types and assay modalities (Fig. 4). For instance, in clinical genetics, this methodology has been heavily leveraged to evaluate tumor suppressors at scale, allowing researchers to precisely classify disease-associated PTEN mutations based on cellular abundance and activity [127]. Similarly, the technology is highly effective for screening complex membrane proteins, as it has been used to functionally adjudicate thousands of cardiac sodium channel (SCN5A) mutations to improve arrhythmia risk stratification [128]. In infectious disease research, LP-based stable integration was instrumental in allowing for the rapid, multiplexed assessment of SARS-CoV-2 viral fitness and receptor binding dynamics [129]. Beyond single-gene mutational scanning, LP platforms can also support massive library integration for a broader functional genomics. For example, uniform, proteome-wide gene overexpression has been used to systematically identify new synergistic cancer treatments [130].

In pooled screens, LP systems simplify logistics and improve quantitative fidelity compared with lentiviral workflows. Recombination-coupled gene-trap logic enforces single-variant expression per cell, making precise titration to one perturbation per cell unnecessary, even if cells transiently receive multiple donor plasmids. This bypasses the cumbersome MOI control inherent to lentiviral delivery. This is especially beneficial for large genes of interest, which reduce packaging efficiency and often preclude the inclusion of a reporter to track transduction [131]. Therefore, LPs remove payload-size constraints on cDNA donors. One caveat is that very large donors reduce transfection efficiency. This can be partially recovered by optimizing electroporation parameters, employing alternative delivery modalities, or using minicircle donors that improve uptake [132]. Additionally, retroviral particles package two RNA genomes but integrate only one DNA copy. When distinct variants are co-packaged, template switching during reverse transcription can decouple barcodes from their intended inserts, degrading library fidelity [133]. In contrast, LP systems enforce single-copy, orientation-fixed integration at a defined locus, preserving barcode–variant linkage.

For complex phenotypes that benefit from rich multiparametric measurements, arrayed screens are often preferred [134]. Arrayed screens with LPs are straightforward and cost-effective relative to lentiviral vectors approaches, while avoiding biosafety concerns. Donors are delivered by standard plate-based transfection, and post-recombination selection produces uniform integrant populations without FACS.

Library preparation for LP integration is conceptually simple and operationally scalable (Fig. 4). Variants are typically introduced by Golden Gate [135] or Gibson [136] assembly into an attB-bearing backbone, in pooled or arrayed formats. This avoids the complexity of lentiviral library packaging, titering, and integration-site quality control. Bacteria-free donor generation further streamlines the workflow: PCR-based assembly followed by circularization yields minicircle donors that retain the attB site but lack bacterial backbone. This improves delivery efficiency and reduces the risks of transgene silencing [137]. Minicircles are amenable to automation, facilitating large, arrayed screens and enhancing integration rates in pooled contexts.

LP approaches extend beyond MAVE to enable CRISPR screens with improved uniformity [138–143] (Fig. 5A and B). In pooled studies, integrating guide RNA (gRNA) cassettes into the LP yields single-copy, locus-stabilized expression that reduces variability and improves consistency compared to lentiviral delivery. Operationally, using inducible Cas9/dCas9 modules, or delivering nucleases separately after gRNA integrant selection, decouples library establishment from CRISPR activity. This minimizes off-target effects and enables time-resolved perturbation [143, 144]. One strategy to suppress early CRISPR–Cas9 activity is to co-transfect a plasmid that expresses an anti-CRISPR protein alongside the gRNA plasmids; as the transient gRNA plasmids diluted out, only the integrated guide remains active [142]. Alternatively, designs that gate gRNA transcription on successful recombination ensure that only true integrants express guides, tightening genotype–phenotype linkage [145, 146] (Fig. 5A and B). Arrayed LP systems are especially valuable for validating of CRISPRa/CRISPRi screens, where stable, uniform guide expression dictates activation magnitude and ensures reproducibility. When multiple guides are required, a single donor can encode multiplexed gRNAs; LP integration ensures all guides integrate in single copy, standardizing stoichiometry. Similar LP–based designs have coupled CRISPR perturbations with barcoded RNA readouts to map regulators of RNA metabolism, highlighting the platform’s flexibility to address diverse biological questions within a unified, scalable framework [140].

For investigators prioritizing native genomic context, LPs can enhance endogenous gene editing screens. Instead of integrating the variant directly, the LP is used to stably integrate and continuously express the necessary CRISPR components and donor templates required to edit the endogenous target gene (Fig. 5C). In HDR-mediated knock-in workflows, embedding programmable donor templates (such as RT modules that synthesize ssDNA intracellularly), alongside CRISPR reagents into a LP increase editing rates. This strategy is applicable to pooled formats, ensuring each cell produces a specific ssDNA-encoded variant [147, 148]. Continuous, co-localized ssDNA supply improves on-target product purity compared with exogenous donors, and gene-trap selection ensures cells carry the complete editing toolkit before induction, increasing the fraction of correctly edited cells.

Beyond variant identity, many screens interrogate phenotypes as a function of protein dosage or expression ratios. A recent MIT study used Cre recombinase to edit the promoter and vary the spacing between a synthetic gene and its promoter, setting expression to off, low, or high [149]. However, transcription can fluctuate, especially at low expression levels. Instead of relying on fluctuating transcriptional changes to modulate expression, LPs can enable expression tuning by holding transcription constant while modulating translation. For instance, libraries varying the Kozak sequence upstream of a fixed gene can be integrated downstream of a strong promoter in the LP cassette. Because the shared promoter establishes a stable transcriptional baseline, mRNA abundance remains uniform across the population. Consequently, protein output varies strictly according to Kozak sequence efficiency, as diverse Kozak sequence variants exploit differing ribosome recruitment and translation rates [150–152]. This generates defined expression gradients in an isogenic background, enabling parallel isolation of clones at desired levels (Fig. 5D). Bicistronic designs enable controlled ratio testing between two candidate genes: placing the second gene under an IRES makes its translation largely independent of Kozak variation applied to the first gene, allowing systematic evaluation of phenotypes across defined expression ratios between the two candidates. Applying the same logic to 5′/3′ UTR libraries allows regulatory element effects to be measured systematically without position or copy-number confounders [153, 154].

Finally, translational applications extend LP utility to in vivo studies and engineered cell therapies [155, 156]. Animal models bearing tissue-specific LPs allow nonintegrating viral particles to deliver recombinase and donor DNA, enabling in vivo variant screens. Induced pluripotent stem cell systems engineered with LPs and cell-type-specific promoters allow differentiation-stage mapping of variant effects and facilitate generation of isogenic primary-like cells for functional assays [157]. In adoptive cell therapy, LPs provide deterministic, single-copy integration of CAR constructs, supporting pooled or arrayed optimization in an isogenic background without the variability inherent to lentiviral or transposon-based approaches.

Current limitations and future directions

While LSR-based LP platforms offer substantial advantages, several bottlenecks currently constrain their scalability and generality. Addressing these limitations will broaden applicability across cell types, tissues, and use cases, and reduce the time and resources required to implement targeted integrations at scale.

Efficient delivery of large att-bearing donor DNA remains a primary challenge. As payload size increases, transfection and electroporation efficiencies decline sharply. LP cassette architectures mitigate this large payload size by placing the promoter in the LP (gene-trap strategy), allowing for promoterless donors that reduce vector length. Minicircle donors further trim size by removing bacterial backbone. Furthermore, conventional linear dsDNA donors can trigger intracellular innate immune responses, causing significant toxicity in sensitive cell types. Deploying immune-evasive circular ssDNA as the donor template can bypass these sensors, licensing highly efficient, kilobase-scale integration while preserving cell viability [158]. Nevertheless, some primary cells and cell lines remain refractory to transfection.

In hard-to-transfect settings, transduction of cells with integrase-deficient lentiviral vectors (IDLV) and virus-like particles (VLPs) offer alternatives [159]. A fraction of IDLV genomes circularize in cells, creating episomes that could serve as donors for LSR-mediated integration [160]. More robustly, RMCE designs that flank the GOI with att sites might allow linear forms of lentiviral vector to function as donors, increasing usage of delivered DNA and improving integration. VLPs could also be employed to co-deliver the donor DNA and the LSR protein, enabling more compact LP cassette designs that omit integrase expression cassettes. This approach would be ideal for att-only installations downstream of endogenous promoters.

A promising one-step concept is sequential or concurrent transduction with VLPs carrying CRISPR reagents to write the att site alongside VLPs bearing the LSR protein and the att-donor (Fig. 6A). This could achieve site-specific integration without prior generation of a LP cell line, substantially shortening timelines. However, such workflows would not ensure complete isogenicity of the resulting pool, nor would they bypass inherent cargo-size limits. Moreover, lentiviral template switching during reverse transcription can still recombine barcoded donors. Delivery platforms that do not rely on reverse transcription might bypass these issues. Baculoviral vectors can deliver both protein and very large DNA cargos to mammalian cells without strict size limits [161]. Similarly, T4-based artificial viral vectors (T4-AVV) [162], are inexpensive to produce in bacteria, highly stable at room temperature, and capable of efficient delivery of large DNA cargos and LSR proteins directly into mammalian cells. Continued engineering of tropism, endosomal escape, and nuclear import for these platforms, will be key to integrating them into routine LP workflows.

While LSRs recognize short att sequences with high fidelity, they can interact with naturally occurring pseudo-att sites in large mammalian genomes, leading to off-target integrations if the recombinase is overexpressed. The specificity profiles and pseudo-site risks of widely used LSRs such as ΦC31 integrase and Bxb1 integrase have been characterized [89], but comparable assessments are still needed for emerging serine recombinases. Inducible recombinase expression or transient delivery is the favored, as it minimizes off target recombination and potential cytotoxicity.

Furthermore, while LSR-mediated recombination is highly deterministic, it is not flawlessly precise; recombination between attP and attB sites can occasionally result in small sequence deletions or imprecise junctions depending on the specific integrase and cellular context [126].

Currently, the requirement for specific att motifs necessitates the pre-installation of LP sites, which is time-consuming and cell-type dependent. A long-term goal is to preserve LSR catalytic efficiency while making DNA targeting programmable, enabling direct integration at endogenous sequences without preinstalled att sites (Fig. 6B). Early attempts fused DNA-binding domains (e.g. zinc fingers) to LSRs to retarget specificity, but activities were generally low [163]. Recent efforts have increased throughput and computational guidance for designing zinc finger–recombinase fusions and narrowed sequence–specificity mapping [121]. For example, Sangamo Therapeutics’ Modular Integrase (MINT) platform redesigned Bxb1 integrase so it can insert DNA into natural places in the genome without needing pre-added target sequences. It uses structural modelling, directed evolution, and zinc finger fusions to guide this targeting, and has reported up to 29% at the AAVS1 locus and 35% at the TRAC locus in K562 cells [121]. Additionally, systems like S-SELeCT utilize serine recombinases evolved directly in human cells to target a native, symmetric safe-harbor locus (a naturally occurring pseudo-palindromic sequence situated in a transcriptionally permissive region). This enables highly efficient, large-cargo transgene integration without relying on host DNA-repair machinery or the need to pre-install canonical att sites [164].

These advances are encouraging but underscore the current path’s cost and complexity: directed evolution of recombinases relies on iterative library generation, selection against stepwise-altered target sites, and extensive screening. This process remains resource-intensive and slow when new targets are required.

A more rational design paradigm would be preferable, but recombinase activity arises from an intricate interplay among DNA binding, synapse assembly, DNA bending, cleavage, subunit rotation, and ligation, making de novo retargeting challenging. Emerging computational strategies aim to bridge this gap. Artificial intelligence (AI) and deep-learning foundation models, such as Evo2, are beginning to revolutionize synthetic biology by enabling the prediction and design of novel, functional proteins and regulatory elements at scale [165]. In the context of genome engineering, learning-based models trained on evolved recombinase libraries can capture complex sequence–affinity relationships and propose de novo-generated recombinase variants predicted to be active on novel targets [120, 166]. Integrating these models with structural constraints, high-throughput reporter assays for att compatibility, and multiplexed measurements of off-target activity could enable rapid development of programmable LSRs with acceptable efficiency and specificity for screening.

Addressing large cargo delivery limitations and advancing de novo LSR programmability will shift the LSR-based genome engineering from highly effective, preinstalled-att LP platforms to programmable LP-independent insertion systems. The combination of scalable donor handling, robust nonviral delivery, and recombinases with engineered specificity, will unlock comprehensive, quantitative studies across diverse cell types and in vivo contexts while maintaining the core advantages of single-copy, orientation-fixed integration and uniform expression.

Conclusions

Targeted integration of large, multikilobase DNA libraries remains a critical bottleneck in high-throughput functional genomics. While CRISPR-associated nucleases excel at small-scale editing, their intrinsic integration efficiencies and junction fidelities decline sharply as payload sizes increase. In this context, LSR-mediated integration provides a highly robust alternative.

The primary take-home message for practitioners is that utilizing LSRs with pre-installed LPs enables the deterministic, single-copy, and orientation-fixed integration of large DNA payloads. This standardized approach decouples variant delivery from local chromatin variability, guaranteeing the uniform, isogenic expression required for quantitative genotype–phenotype mapping in MAVEs, CRISPR screens, and translational cell models. Furthermore, the modular design of LP systems, combined with precise selection-swap logic, makes high-throughput workflows operationally scalable across diverse cell types.

Despite these distinct advantages, progress in the field is limited by two main challenges: the efficient delivery of large donor DNA (especially in primary cells) and the strict reliance on pre-installed canonical att recognition sites. Optimizing nonviral delivery platforms and utilizing one-step CRISPR–recombinase systems currently help mitigate these operational bottlenecks. Looking forward, the long-term trajectory of the field points toward the computational design and directed evolution of novel LSR specificities. As delivery modalities improve and LSRs become fully programmable, the field will transition from its current reliance on synthetic LP cell lines to true one-step, site-specific genome engineering, unlocking the next generation of mammalian functional genomics.
