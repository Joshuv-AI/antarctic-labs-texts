Mating triggers a physiological switch to reproduction in field crickets to mitigate the flight-oogenesis tradeoff
Authors: Meghan Laturney, Lisa A. Treidel, Sadia Khan, Caroline M. Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Mating triggers flight muscle histolysis and ovary growth in long-winged female field crickets.•Although long-winged females are predicted to have a delay in offspring production, mating eliminates the early-life reproductive cost associated with dispersal.•Mating induced histolysis coincides with activation of autophagy and iron mobilization.•Mated females show altered insulin-like peptide and juvenile hormone signaling.

Introduction

Organisms must continuously balance competing energetic demands to maximize fitness (Stearns, 1992; Roff, 1993). These competing demands can generate life-history trade-offs in which investment in one biological process constrains investment in another. As a result, individuals are required to transition between distinct physiological states that differentially prioritize growth, somatic maintenance, dispersal, or reproduction throughout their lives (Ng’oma et al., 2017; Pontzer and McGrosky, 2022). Such physiological state transitions are often initiated by environmental cues that elicit coordinated physiological and behavioral changes, thereby aligning an organism's phenotype with current ecological or reproductive conditions (Heyland and Moroz, 2006; Huijser and Schmid, 2011; Ng’oma et al., 2017). Key stimuli that induce state transitions can include food availability, seasonality, population density, or social interactions, including mating itself. Although transitions between physiological states are fundamental components of life-history strategies, the biologically relevant signals and the mechanisms through which they act remain poorly understood in many systems (Ng’oma et al., 2017).

The flight-oogenesis trade-off is a widely observed life-history trade-off that exemplifies the transition between alternative physiological states (Johnson, 1969; Zera and Harshman, 2001). The trade-off arises because energetic and metabolic demands of flight and egg production are distinct, leading to allocation trade-offs of limited resources to alternative biosynthetic pathways (Zera, 2005; Zera and Zhao, 2006). Metabolic trade-offs result in reduced investment in one process over the other, leading to a negative correlation between flight capability and fecundity at the organismal level (Mole and Zera, 1993; Williams, 2005; Iwamoto, 2011). Flight–oogenesis trade-offs have been documented across diverse taxa, including vertebrates, such as migratory birds (Price et al., 2011; Young et al., 2021) and several invertebrate clades (for reviews see Roff, 1986; Zera and Denno, 1997; Guerra, 2011). In wing-polymorphic insects, this trade-off is observed between conspecifics that develop as either flight-capable or flightless adult morphs, which prioritize either dispersal or reproduction in early adulthood, respectively. Importantly, investment in dispersal by flight-capable morphs is not maintained throughout adulthood. Instead, flight-capable individuals eventually transition from a dispersal-oriented state to a reproductive state, accompanied by the reallocation of resources from flight to egg production. Although this transition is critical for reproductive success, the signals that determine when flight-capable individuals initiate it and the mechanisms that mediate the transition remain poorly understood.

Wing polymorphic Gryllus field crickets are a well-established model for studying the physiological basis of life history trade-offs (Roff, 1984; Zera and Rankin, 1989; Zera and Harshman, 2001). Morph determination of wing polymorphic Gryllus species is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors and results in adults emerging as either a short-winged (SW) or long-winged (LW) adults (Treidel et al., 2026). Short-winged morphs with underdeveloped wings and nonfunctional flight muscles cannot fly but instead often show accelerated reproductive investment, including faster ovarian development and/or higher reproductive output (Roff, 1984; Zera and Rankin, 1989 (Guerra, 2011)). In contrast, long-winged, flight-capable morphs invest heavily in developing and maintaining large functional flight muscles and associated metabolic machinery in early adulthood (Zera et al., 1997; Zera and Larsen, 2001; Zhang et al., 2011; Treidel et al., 2023), which can delay and/or reduce egg production (Roff, 1984; Zera and Rankin, 1989). When shifting from the dispersive to reproductive portion of their life cycle, long-winged females undergo an irreversible physiological state change: a synchronized degradation of their flight muscles (histolysis) and concurrent growth/maturation of their ovaries, reallocating resources from one organ to another (Tanaka, 1993; Wheeler, 1996; Zera et al., 1997; Lorenz, 2007; Triedel et al., 2021).

The cues that impact the timing of flight muscle histolysis in female long-winged Gryllus crickets remain poorly understood. In migratory insects, prolonged flight can induce flight muscle degeneration (Kobayashi and Ishikawa, 1993). Although Gryllus crickets are not migratory, experimentally induced flight increases rates of histolysis in G. texensis (Guerra and Pollack, 2009), however, this does not appear to increase egg laying (Miyashita et al., 2020). Flight muscle histolysis has also been associated with wing removal in several insect species (Roff, 1989). While Gryllus do not naturally autotomize their wings, experimental wing removal similarly increases both histolysis and egg production in G. texensis (Miyashita et al., 2020) and other cricket species (Allonemobius fasciatus: Tanaka, 1986; Modicogryllus confirmatus: Tanaka, 1994).

Although mating has been implicated as a trigger of flight muscle histolysis in several insects, including the red cotton bug, Dysdercus intermedius (Edwards, 1969), the beet webworm, Loxostege sticticalis (Cheng et al., 2016), and fire ants, Solenopsis spp. (Davis et al., 1993), evidence within Gryllus is currently indirect. Shoemaker et al. (2006), while examining the trade-off between reproduction and the immune system, observed that mated G. texensis females were less likely to retain functional flight muscles and stored fewer mature eggs in their lateral oviducts than age-matched virgin females. This suggests that mating may accelerate the transition from a dispersal to a reproductive state. However, substantial variation exists in the timing of this transition. Miyashita et al. (2020) found that some mated long-winged G. texensis females retained functional flight muscles for much of their lifespan and exhibited delayed reproduction, documenting early-, intermediate-, and late-reproducing phenotypes. Together, these studies suggest that mating may influence the timing of flight muscle histolysis, but that considerable variation exists in the female response. To date, no study to our knowledge has directly examined the effects of mating on the coupled processes of flight muscle histolysis and ovarian development in a wing-polymorphic insect.

Beyond its potential role in flight muscle histolysis, there is extensive evidence that mating can elicit major physiological state transitions in female insects by inducing large-scale shifts in gene expression, physiology, and behavior. For example, in Drosophila melanogaster, mating causes decreased mating receptivity, increased egg laying, and changes in feeding, driven by female-specific neural circuits and modulation of juvenile hormone signaling (Laturney and Billeter, 2014; Wang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021; Laturney et al., 2023). More broadly, mating modifies endocrine pathways including juvenile hormone, ecdysone, and insulin-like peptides, all of which mediate physiological transitions (Laturney and Billeter, 2014; Kurogi et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). In G. firmus, levels of these hormones vary across wing morph and reproductive state (Zera and Bottsford, 2001; Zhao and Zera, 2004; Vellichirammal et al., 2014; Zera et al., 2018), implicating them in the regulation of the dispersal–reproduction trade-off. Together, these findings suggest that mating initiates endocrine and transcriptional changes, promoting the reallocation of resources from flight capability toward ovarian development and reproduction.

If mating acts as a cue initiating the transition from dispersal to a reproductive state, the resulting endocrine signaling and downstream transcriptional changes must ultimately be translated into coordinated physiological remodeling. A central component of this remodeling is the reallocation of resources away from flight capability and toward reproduction, requiring both the breakdown of flight muscles and the concurrent development of the ovaries. In the flight muscles, histolysis is mediated by autophagy, a conserved lysosomal-dependent pathway that degrades and recycles intracellular organelles (Lebenzon et al., 2022; Diaz et al., 2024). This process liberates amino acids and iron as these nutrients are abundant in protein- and mitochondria-dense tissues (Zera and Denno, 1997; Mizushima, 2007; Oliver et al., 2007; Lebenzon et al., 2022; Diaz et al., 2024). The released nutrients are hypothesized to be transported to the ovaries, where they can be utilized for oocyte maturation. Amino acids are essential for yolk protein synthesis (reviewed in Roy et al., 2018), while iron serves as a critical cofactor for enzymes and metabolic processes required for egg development (Cardoso-Jaime et al., 2022). Consistent with this model, deficiencies in either amino acids or iron are associated with reduced ovarian mass and fecundity (Treidel et al., 2021; Rani et al., 2022). However, how these two processes are coordinated in a state dependent manner and if mating can initiate their onset remains unknown.

We have three objectives of this research. First, we assess the effects of mating on timing of flight muscle histolysis and ovarian development in long-winged female Gryllus field crickets. We hypothesize that mating acts as a physiological cue that initiates the transition from a dispersal to a reproductive state by stimulating flight muscle histolysis and ovarian development. Accordingly, we predict that mated long-winged females will undergo flight muscle histolysis and accelerated ovarian development earlier than age-matched virgin controls, demonstrating a coordinated regulation of the transition. Second, we quantify the reproductive costs of flight by comparing the reproductive fitness of long-winged and short-winged crickets in both laboratory and field populations. We hypothesize that mating induced histolysis reduces the reproductive cost of flight in long-winged females. Consequently, we predict that mated long-winged females will exhibit similar egg and hatchling production over the first 14 days of adulthood compared to short-winged females. Finally, we investigate the mechanisms linking mating to flight muscle breakdown and resource reallocation to reproduction. We hypothesize that mating alters transcriptional programs in the flight muscles, ovaries, fat body, and brain, inducing endocrine changes and promoting autophagy-mediated muscle degradation and nutrient transfer to developing oocytes. We therefore predict that mated females will exhibit altered hormone signaling and increased expression of genes associated with autophagy, nutrient transport, and ovarian development.

We tested these hypotheses using G. lineaticeps Stål, 1858, the California variable field cricket, a model system with a stable flight polymorphism, well-characterized allocation trade-offs, and an increasingly powerful genetic toolkit (Treidel et al., 2021; Ylla et al., 2021; Treidel et al., 2023). By identifying mating as a potential trigger of flight muscle histolysis and reproductive development, this work addresses a longstanding question in life-history biology: how organisms determine when to transition from a dispersal-oriented to a reproductive physiological state. More generally, this study provides a mechanistic framework linking social cues, endocrine regulation, and resource reallocation, thereby advancing our understanding of how physiological state transitions shape organismal homeostasis and life-history evolution.

Materials and methods

Cricket stocks

All experiments used adult wing-polymorphic California variable field crickets (G. lineaticeps). The laboratory colony was founded in 2015 from adults (>200) collected from Sedgwick Reserve (34°41′34″N, 120°02′26″W, Santa Ynez, California). The colony is maintained at a constant size of 120 reproductively mature adults, with equal proportion of long-winged and short-winged crickets (50:50 wing morph ratio). To maintain genetic diversity, offspring from ∼100 field collected adults are added annually. Experiments used crickets from this lab colony unless explicitly stated.

All crickets used in experiments were reared under the same environmental conditions: 27 °C, 16:8 hour light:dark cycle; ad libitum access to water, and a standard laboratory diet (wheat germ, wheat bran, milk powder, and nutritional yeast in a 1:2:0.35:0.27 vol ratio, Zera and Larsen, 2001). Before adulthood, crickets were group reared in clear plastic bins (60 cm x 35 cm x 45 cm) with water, food, and three sheets of crumpled butcher paper (∼45 cm x 25 cm per sheet) for shelter and separated by sex at the last juvenile stage. To obtain virgin females of known age, bins were checked every 24 h, and new adult females transferred to individual housing in plastic pint containers with water, food, and two squares of butcher paper (10 cm x 10 cm). Bins with last instar males were checked weekly and new adult males were removed, housed collectively in clear bins (60 cm x 35 cm x 45 cm) with water, food, and three sheets of crumpled butcher paper (∼45 cm x 25 cm per sheet), and randomly chosen at the time of experiment once they were at least 7 days old.

Mating protocol

We generated age-matched mated and virgin females. During pairing, all females were transported to a separate behavior room (temperature range 22–24 °C). If a female was assigned to be mated, a random sexually mature male was placed in her plastic pint housing container between 9:00 and 12:00 h on day 2, 3, and 4 (post-adult emergence). Pairs were checked every 20 min, and copulation occurred if the male successfully transferred the spermatophore to the female. The male was removed after mating or at the end of the mating window (12:00). Females were mated exactly twice on different days to ensure that mating was successful and to eliminate any variation caused by differing mating rates. If females mated on day two and day three, they were not paired on day four. If females were paired and did not mate exactly twice, they were excluded from the experiment. Once males were removed from female’s container, regardless of if they mated, they were placed in separate bin until all mating trials for the day were complete and before being returned to group housing. Thus, while males were not allowed to mate multiple times within one day, because we randomly selected males from bins (n = 40 per bin) it was unlikely but not impossible that the same female was paired with the same male twice on different days. This process generated 4 groups of females: virgin long-winged (n = 32), mated long-winged (n = 36), virgin short-winged (n = 32), mated short-winged (n = 34).

Quantification of ovary mass and femur length

On day 7 (post-adult emergence) virgin and mated females were dissected, heads and legs were removed, and the thorax and abdomen were opened using fine-point micro-scissors. After removing the gut, ovaries were removed and weighed with an electronic balance (Sartorius RC 250S, Sartorius, NY). A randomly selected (left or right) hind femur length was measured using a Nikon SMZ18 stereomicroscope (Nikon Instruments, Melville, NY) and NIS-Elements software with the “Measure” tool. We measured from the joint of the trochanter to the femoro-tibial joint, taking the maximum end-to-end length (Bidau and Martinez, 2018).

Quantification of flight muscle color

Flight muscle color has traditionally been used as a qualitative proxy for muscle function, with red or pink muscle indicating functional tissue and white muscle indicating histolyzed, nonfunctional tissue. However, this assessment is inherently subjective, particularly when muscles are in the intermediate stages of breakdown. To reduce observer bias and improve reproducibility, we employed a previously developed digital image analysis approach to quantitatively measure muscle color (Laturney et al., 2026). Rather than assigning muscles to discrete categories (e.g. red, pink, or white), this method extracts red, green, and blue (RGB) pixel intensities from muscle images and converts them into a continuous color metric. This quantitative metric provides an objective measure of the degree of flight muscle histolysis and allows subtle changes in muscle condition to be incorporated into subsequent statistical analyses. To measure muscle color, we exposed the dorsal longitudinal muscles (DLMs) and imaged them using a Nikon SMZ18 stereomicroscope (Nikon Instruments, Melville, NY) and NIS-Elements software. To reduce glare and improve contrast, 50 μL of PBS was applied directly to the muscle. Images were saved as .tif files. Using Fiji (ImageJ version 1.53c; Schindelin et al., 2012), we separated the multi-channel image into its constituent color channels (red, green, and blue). Using the “Record” tool in the Macros plugin, we defined three non-overlapping regions of interest (ROIs) and recorded the mean pixel intensity for each ROI across all three channels. We calculated the color metric (color metric = 76.228 x R/G + 0.244 x R-G + 0.053 x R – 94.166).

Quantification of egg laying in mated females in a lab population

To quantify and compare the number of eggs laid by long-winged and short-winged females, twice mated females were generated as described in Mating protocol in Objective 1. After mating and until day 14 (post-adult emergence), mated females were provided a daily egg cup: a one-ounce plastic dish filled with wet sand. We restricted our assessment of reproductive output in G. lineaticeps to the first 14 days of adulthood, since reductions in reproductive output associated with flight capability are largely confined to the first 2 and 3 weeks of adulthood in other Gryllus species (Roff, 1984; Zera and Rankin, 1989). On day 15, when the last egg cup was removed, females were weighed with an electronic balance (Sartorius RC 250S, Sartorius, NY). When egg cups were removed, they were stored at −20 °C until the eggs could be counted. To separate eggs from the sand, we transferred the contents of the egg cup (sand and eggs) to a 6 fluid ounce plastic cup and mixed in 90 ml of 1.32 M sucrose solution, which caused the eggs to float. Using a stir stick, we pushed the eggs to the edges of the cup and marked a location on the cup to act as a starting position. Using a dissecting microscope and a tally counter, we counted the eggs, rotating the cup until we reached the starting position. The process of recovering and counting eggs from the oviposition substrate precluded subsequent assessment of egg viability and hatching success, as eggs were discarded following enumeration.

Quantify hatchling production in a lab population

Different mating protocols were used in the egg production and hatchling experiments because the two experiments were designed to address complementary aspects of reproductive investment. In the egg production experiment (described above), females were mated exactly twice to minimize variation in mating timing and frequency and isolate the effects of mating status on oviposition. By standardizing mating history, we were better able to attribute differences in egg production to wing morph and mating treatment rather than differences in the number or timing of matings. In contrast, the hatchling experiment was designed to quantify realized reproductive output under conditions that more closely resemble those experienced in nature. Because female Gryllus crickets likely mate multiple times when given the opportunity (Gershman, 2010), females were continuously housed with two males, allowing mating frequency and timing to vary naturally. This approach incorporates natural variation in mating behavior that may influence fertilization success and offspring production and is consistent with previous studies in Gryllus and other insects, which commonly co-house males and females rather than directly standardizing mating frequency (for a review, see Guerra, 2011).

To quantify and compare the hatchling output of long‐winged and short‐winged females, we co-housed a single 2-day old female that had not previously mated with two, randomly selected, sexually mature males for 72 h, recreating a more natural setting that allows for mate choice in plastic bins (15 cm x 30 cm x 15 cm). We also expanded this experiment to include males of both wing morph types to explore if females mated to long-winged or short-winged males have different reproductive capacity. We generated 4 types of crosses: a long-winged female with long-winged males (n = 15), a long-winged female with short-winged males (n = 13), a short-winged female with long-winged males (n = 17) and a short-winged female with short-winged males (n = 15). Females were provided an egg cup, a three fluid ounce cup of wet sand, every 3 days from day 2 until day 14. After removal, the egg cups were housed in a sealed plastic bin(15 cm x 30 cm x 15 cm) under standard rearing conditions (27 °C 16:8 light:dark cycle) and moistened with water to stay humid. From day 12 to 40 post-adult emergence, the egg cups were checked daily for presence of hatchlings and if found, the hatchlings were counted and removed.

Assessing mating receptivity of lab populations

Because unmated females lay very few or no eggs, virgin females were not included in the egg laying production assays. Consequently, those experiments assessed differences in reproductive output across morphs after mating. However, if mating receptivity differs between female wing morphs, then the less receptive morph may experience reduced realized reproductive success simply because fewer females successfully mate, even if mated females of both morphs produce similar numbers of offspring. To determine if morphs differ in mating receptivity, we separately quantified mating receptivity in long-winged and short-winged females during early adulthood. These trials tested whether long-winged females exhibit delayed mating receptivity that could contribute to differences in reproductive timing associated with the flight–reproduction trade-off. All mating receptivity trials were conducted under red light at an ambient temperature range of 22–24 °C between 11:30 and 14:00 h. A long-winged (n = 96) or short-winged (n = 104) virgin female of different ages (1, 3, 5, 7, or 9 days post-adult emergence) was placed in a clear plastic pint container with a randomly selected sexually mature long-winged or short-winged male. The pairs were observed for 45 min, and courtship occurred if the male produced at least one call bout during the trial duration. Copulation occurred if the male successfully transferred a spermatophore to the female. After the trial, all females were frozen and stored at −20 °C until they were dissected to measure ovarian mass using an electronic balance (Sartorius RC 250S, Sartorius, NY). The males were placed back into the colony to be used again on subsequent days.

Assessing mating receptivity and offspring output of field-caught crickets

To determine mating receptivity of non-lab reared female crickets, males and females (of unknown age and mating status) were collected directly in the field at Sedgwick Reserve at night using head lamps and housed separately with food and water ad libitum for at least 24 h before behavioral trials. Mating trials were conducted outdoors in ambient temperature under red light between 21:00–22:30 (August 4–10, 2022). Single females (long-winged n = 30, short-winged n = 30) were paired with two short-winged males and were observed for 1 hour. Females who copulated during the mating trial were individually housed with ad libitum food and water in plastic pint containers and transported back to the laboratory (University of California, Berkeley, California) and provided with egg cups (three fluid ounce containers of wet sand) for two weeks to quantify hatchling production. After egg laying, the egg cups were housed in a sealed container (20 cm x 10 cm x 8 cm) under standard rearing conditions (27 °C 16:8 hour light:dark cycle) and moistened with water to stay humid. Hatchlings were counted and removed weekly for five weeks starting 20 days after egg laying began.

Candidate transcript identification and primer design

We used semi-quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR going forward) to measure changes in transcript abundance in tissues from mated and age-matched virgin long-winged females. We designed primers using the PrimerQuest Tool by Integrated DNA Technologies (https://www.idtdna.com/PrimerQuest) using the mRNA sequences of genes from the publicly available G. bimaculatus genome database (https://gbimaculatusgenome.rc.fas.harvard.edu/; Ylla et al., 2021) and validated their efficiencies as described in Lebenzon et al., 2022.

Treatments and sample collection

To capture both transient and sustained gene expression changes, we collected tissues at multiple time points following copulation. We collected tissues from 2-day old once-mated females (dissected 6 h after copulation, n = 11), 3-day old twice-mated females (dissected 6 h after the second copulation, n = 11), 4-day old twice-mated females (dissected 24 h after the second copulation, n = 10), and age-matched virgin controls (2-day old virgin females, n = 8; 3-day old virgin females, n = 14; and 4-day old virgin females, n = 10). Once dissected, we found that some of the mated females had histolyzed flight muscles (appearing white) on day 3 and day 4 so we analyzed these samples separately. This produced 8 groups: day 2 mated, day 3 mated functional, day 3 mated histolyzed, day 4 mated functional, day 4 mated histolyzed, and 3 groups of age matched controls (day 2 virgins, day 3 virgins, and day 4 virgins). Brain, ovaries, fat body, and dorsal longitudinal flight muscles were dissected, immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until RNA extractions. We extracted RNA using Trizol according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ThermoFisher Scientific, Emeryville, CA, USA), removed residual DNA using DNAse (Quanta Biosciences, Beverly, MA, USA), and measured the absorbance of the preparation using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Mississauga, ON, Canada) to determine purity and concentration. We used the qScript cDNA synthesis kit (Quanta Biosciences, Beverly, MA, USA) to synthesize cDNA from 1000 ng of RNA, diluted to a constant concentration across samples, and stored samples at −20 °C until qPCR was performed.

Profile transcript abundance using qPCR

Each qPCR reaction contained forward and reverse primers (see Supplemental Table 15 for sequence and concentration), 1μg of cDNA, and Applied Biosciences PowerUp SYBR MasterMix (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). We ran each reaction in triplicate on a QuantStudio 3 thermocycler (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Data analysis

We completed statistical analysis with Python in a JupyterLab Notebook (version 3.6.3) using the pandas, numpy, scipy sklearn, and statsmodel packages or with R (version 2024.12.1 + 563) using tidyverse, lme4, mgcv, betareg, rstatix, emmeans, and multcomp packages. Data were visualized with Python in a JupyterLab Notebook the matplotlib and seaborn packages and diagnostic plots were generated in R using the DHARMa package as well as the plot function of the corresponding analysis package. All models were assessed for homoscedasticity by visualizing the residuals with a scatter plot, Bartlett's test of homogeneity (alpha = 0.05), and Levene’s test (alpha = 0.05). All model residuals were assessed for normality by visualizing the distribution with a Q-Q plot by comparing the quantiles of the observed data and the quantiles of the theoretical distribution, histogram plotting the frequency of the dependent variable, and performing a Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.05). To generate the models, we initially specified a fully saturated model including all main effects and interactions. We then simplified the model by sequentially removing non-significant higher-order interactions and assessing any loss of explanatory power. Model selection was based on the lowest AIC. When two or more models differed by <2 AIC points, we favored the model with fewer variables, while retaining terms that represented essential aspects of our experimental design, such as the random effect of Cricket ID.

Ovarian mass and muscle color

A generalized linear model was used to analyze ovarian mass and muscle color of 7-day old virgin and mated long-winged and short-winged females with wing morph, mating status, and body size (femur length) as factors (using the statsmodels package in Python). We did not assign and track male ID so it was not possible to use as a random effect. We also used a generalized linear model to analyze the ovarian mass of females across age groups (transformed with Yeo-Johnson method using the PowerTransformer in sklearn package). We used a Spearman correlation to show the monotonic relationship between age and ovarian mass. To further investigate the significant age × wing morph interaction for ovary mass, post hoc comparisons between long-winged and short-winged females were performed separately at each age. Because ovary mass data did not consistently meet the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, differences between morphs within each age were assessed using Mann–Whitney U tests. To account for multiple comparisons across ages, p-values were adjusted using the Holm method.

Egg laying dynamics and reproductive output

Daily egg laying and hatchling patterns were analyzed using generalized additive mixed model (using the mgcv package in R). The fixed effects were day, wing morph and body mass, and the random effect was an individual cricket identifier, Cricket ID, accounting for the repeated measures of individuals through time. Daily egg laying was modeled with a Tweedie distribution with a log link function for zero-inflated positive skewed count data. Daily hatchling production was modeled using a Negative Binomial distribution (due to dispersed data) with the log link function. Differences between wing morphs in total egg production, total hatchling production, field-caught hatchling production, and hatchling success were assessed with a Mann Whitney U test (p < 0.05, using the scipy package in Python). Proportional cumulative egg production and hatchling production was analyzed with a beta regression model (using the betareg package in R) with a cloglog or a loglog link function, respectively. As no females laid eggs on days 2–4, these days represented a common pre-reproductive period and were excluded from the GAMM analyses because they contained no variation in reproductive output. Similarly, because no hatchlings emerged on day 17, this day was excluded from the hatchling analyses.

Mating receptivity

We used a binomial linear regression to explore the relationship between wing morph, age, and body size with male courtship and successful copulation (using the statsmodels package in Python). As age and ovary mass were positively correlated (Spearman correlation, P = 0.68, p < 0.001) we created an age-corrected ovary mass variable by calculating the daily mean ovary mass and determined the residuals. We then used this variable along with age and wing morph type in the binomial regression to determine if mating receptivity was associated with ovarian development.

Genetic expression analysis

mRNA Transcript abundance was normalized to the expression of two tissue-specific reference genes (Supplemental Table 15). Relative normalized transcript abundance was calculated using the comparative CT (2-Δ ΔCT) method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001) with mated females normalized to the age-matched virgin controls. We compared differences in raw CT values in each tissue (brain, DLM, fat body, and ovary) for each gene with a two-way linear model with age and the mated status-muscle function status combination label (virgin, mated with functional flight muscles, mated with histolyzed flight muscles) as fixed effects. This was followed by post hoc treatment-vs-control contrasts using estimated marginal means, tested with Wald t-test with an adjusted p value for each gene (using the rstatix, emmeans, and multcomp packages in R). We also compared raw CT values across time in our controls (2, 3, and 4-day old post-adult emergence virgin females) to explore age related changes in gene expression with a one-way ANOVA and Dunnett’s post hoc test.

Results

Mating initiates flight muscle histolysis in female long-winged crickets

We hypothesized that mating triggers a physiological shift, initiating histolysis in long-winged females and inducing resource allocation to reproduction in both wing morphs. To test this, we generated twice-mated long-winged and short-winged females and age-matched virgin controls. On day 7, we quantified flight muscle color and ovary mass.

Flight muscle color (a proxy of muscle function) was best modeled by an interaction between wing morph and mating status, with mated long-winged females having whiter muscles than virgin long-winged females and muscle coloration similar to that of virgin and mated short-winged females (Fig. 1A and Table 1, Supplemental Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 1). We also note that the flight muscle color metric of virgin long-winged females appeared continuous reflecting substantial individual variation in the timing and pace of flight muscle histolysis. In contrast, the color metric of mated long-winged females appeared bimodal. The lack of intermediate muscle colors in this group may reflect a more rapid transition from red-functional muscle to white histolyzed muscle following mating. Nevertheless, the reduction in muscle color observed in mated long-winged females indicates that mating is associated with flight muscle breakdown in long-winged females. Ovary mass was best predicted by a generalized linear model that included wing morph and a mating status by body size interaction (using femur length as a proxy of body size) (Fig. 1B, Table 1, Supplemental Figure 1, Supplemental Table 2). This indicates that mating is associated with larger ovaries, regardless of wing morph, likely due to stimulated oogenesis and egg maturation, and that larger females have larger ovaries but only once they have mated. As predicted by the oogenesis-flight trade-off framework, we found a consistent morph effect on ovary mass with short-winged females producing larger ovaries than long-winged females, reflecting a fixed life-history strategy. Moreover, we found a significant negative correlation of ovary mass and muscle color (Spearman Correlation, P = −0.516, p < 0.001, Fig. 1C), which indicates that individuals with white, nonfunctional histolyzed muscles (low color value) had larger ovaries compared to individuals with red, functional muscles. Short-winged females lay more eggs initially, giving them a potential but transient reproductive advantage in early adulthood

Since mating may initiate histolysis in long-winged females and ovary growth in both wing morphs, we hypothesized that mating induces a state change from flight to reproduction in long-winged females, reducing the costs of flight. To test this, we explored if short-winged females exhibit an early-life reproductive advantage compared to long-winged females, predicted by the flight-oogenesis trade-off, by tracking the temporal dynamics of post-mating egg laying for the first 14 days of adulthood. Although all females were mated by day 3 and provided egg laying substrate from day 2 onwards, egg laying did not begin before day 5 for either wing morph (Fig. 2A). We found that egg laying initiation was highly variable. Of the total 64 females, 32 females laid eggs by day 5, 45 females laid eggs by day 6, and all females laid eggs by day 11. Additionally, age, wing morph, age by wing morph interaction, and body mass were all significant determinants of the daily cumulative proportion of eggs laid (Supplemental Table 4, Fig. 2C, Supplemental Figure 2E). Although the effect of age was expected (given that eggs accumulated over time for all females that laid repeatedly), we also found that greater body mass was associated with slower accumulation of cumulative egg production. The main effect of wing morph indicates that short-winged females had completed a larger proportion of their egg production at the midpoint of the observation period, confirming that short-winged females start out with a higher egg laying rate. Moreover, the age by wing morph interaction revealed that the effect of age was weaker in short-winged females, again suggesting a more consistent rate of egg laying through time. Together, this supports the conclusion that short-winged females produced eggs at a faster rate during early adulthood (i.e. the first ∼5 days of their reproductive window), but this initial difference was eliminated when long-winged females later exhibited a higher rate of egg production, likely after day 9. Therefore, at the level of egg laying, we see a potential reproductive advantage of short-winged females over long-winged females, but it is transient.

Short-winged female’s early life reproductive advantage is not observed in offspring production

To explore if short-winged females’ egg laying rate translates to greater offspring production, we recorded the daily hatchling counts of females of both wing morphs mated to short-winged males. Hatchling emergence began on day 18 post-adult emergence (Fig. 3A), which was expected since females start laying eggs on day 5 (Fig. 2A) and embryonic development can take a minimum of 13 days when reared at 27 °C (Donoughe and Extavour, 2016). The best-fit generalized additive mixed model indicated that long- and short-winged females show a nonlinear pattern of hatchling production over time (Table 2, Supplemental Table 5, Supplemental Figure 3A-C) with different temporal dynamics, since they are modeled by different smooth terms (long-winged EDF = 4.375, p < 00.1; short-winged EDF = 8.078, p < 0.001). Despite hatchling production being quite variable, as individual differences (represented in the random effect) contributed significantly to the overall variability, the model also found a significant fixed effect of female wing morph, suggesting that short-winged females produced less offspring than long-winged females (Table 2). However, a post hoc test did not support this (Fig. 3B, Mann Whitney U test, U = 110.0, p = 0.580). These overall patterns in daily hatchling production dynamics matched what we observed in daily egg laying numbers, with an age-specific pattern differing between morphs. However, we did not see the initial short-winged female reproductive advantage (Figs. 2A and Fig. 3A). A follow-up analysis of the cumulative proportion of daily hatchling emergence (Fig. 3C) by a beta regression model found a significant effect of age and wing morph, as well as an age by wing morph interaction (Supplemental Table 6). This revealed that short-winged females exhibited a different reproductive rate compared to long-winged females. Long-winged females produced a greater proportion of their offspring in the first 11 days of hatchling emergence compared to short-winged females. The age by wing morph interaction revealed that short-winged females displayed a steeper age-related increase in cumulative offspring production compared to long-winged females, suggesting that short-winged females have a more consistent rate of hatchling production. Finally, there were no differences in hatching success between morphs (Fig. 3D, Mann Whitney U test, U = 71.0, p = 1).

To extend our analysis on hatchling dynamics, we recorded the daily hatchling counts of females mated to long-winged males to determine if the female wing morph-specific patterns of offspring production are consistent across male wing morphs. When pooling all hatching production data (females mated to long-winged and short-winged males) we found that the best-fit generalized additive mixed model included a significant effect of male wing morph, revealing that females mated to long-winged males produced more offspring than females mated to short-winged males (Supplemental Table 7). Additionally, we analyzed the egg and hatching production dynamics of females mated to long-winged males and we found that offspring production by long- and short-winged females was extremely similar to each other (Supplemental Figure 3G-H) with the best-fit generalized additive mixed model for daily hatchling production indicating one non-linear curve for all females (EDF = 7.024, p < 0.001, Supplemental Table 8, Supplemental Figure 3D-F) instead of a separate curve for each morph, and the best-fit generalized additive mixed model for cumulative hatchling production revealing no significant age by wing morph interaction (Supplemental Table 9). Thus, we did not observe any evidence of an early-life reproductive advantage in short-winged females.

Female mating receptivity increases with age but is not associated with ovary mass

Previously, we found that mating is associated with a physiological state shift, inducing histolysis in long-winged females and reallocating resources to the ovaries in both wing morphs, thereby eliminating the early-life reproductive cost of flight to long-winged females. This highlights the importance of mating receptivity: long-winged females can avoid the reproductive delay associated with flight if they are equally receptive during the early days of adulthood. However, reproductive investment, as measured with virgin gonadal mass, is higher in SW morphs during this period in this species (Treidel et al., 2023). Therefore, to fully explore the potential of mating as a cue for physiological transition, we examined how gonadal mass and age relates to mating receptivity across the first 9 days of adulthood, quantifying mating behavior and subsequently dissecting females to measure ovary mass.

Ovaries from 1-day old adult females were extremely small and underdeveloped (long-winged: 17.77 ± 13.53 mg; short-winged: 20.27 ± 18.76 mg; Fig. 4A) and therefore were not included in the analysis. We fit ovary mass with a generalized linear model (Supplemental Table 10, Supplemental Figure 4A-B). Overall, variation in ovary mass was largely related to age with older females (day 9, long-winged: 196.05 ± 76.93 mg; short-winged: 255.09 ± 58.49 mg) having heavier ovaries than younger females (day 3, long-winged: 55.54 ± 45.89 mg; short-winged: 131.22 ± 64.97 mg), regardless of wing morph. Although there was no overall main effect of wing morph on ovary mass, we detected a significant age-by-morph interaction, indicating that the magnitude of ovarian growth through time differed between long-winged and short-winged females. Measuring mating receptivity in the same females (above) allowed us to also test if timing of mating receptivity is aligned with age and gonadal development. One-day old females exhibited a complete absence of mating receptivity and were thus not included in subsequent analysis (Fig. 4B). Logistic regression analysis revealed that age, and not age-corrected ovary mass or wing morph, was the best predictor of mating (Fig. 4C, Supplemental Table 11). Females increased mating receptivity over time with the lowest rate of mating on day 3 (4/18) and the highest rate of mating on day 9 (14/21) (Fig. 4B). The increase in copulation in older females could reflect an increase in courtship from males. However, our analysis revealed that female age was not a significant predictor of male courtship (Supplemental Table 12, Supplemental Figure 4D-E). Thus, the increase in receptivity with female age was unlikely due to differences in male behavior but instead reflects increased female mating receptivity itself. Together, these results suggest that receptivity increases with female age but is not associated with wing morph or differences in ovarian development. Therefore, receptivity is not a behavioral bottleneck, allowing mating to trigger resource allocation to the ovaries in females of both wing morphs, eliminating the cost of flight in long-winged females. Moreover, consistent with findings in the lab, field-caught long-winged and short-winged females did not differ in mating receptivity (X2 = 0.795, p = 0.374, Supplementary Figure 5A) and did not differ in total hatchling production (Mann Whitney U = 71.0, p = 1.0; Supplementary Figure 5B). However, because the age, prior mating history, and previous reproductive investment of field-caught females were unknown, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, the similar patterns observed in both laboratory and field populations suggest that the relationships between wing morph, mating behavior, and reproductive output identified in laboratory-reared crickets may extend to natural populations.

Mating triggers changes in hormone signaling and the upregulation of nutrient recycling in long-winged females

To investigate how mating initiates physiological resource reallocation, we compared tissue-specific transcript abundance of genes associated with flight muscle histolysis, iron transport, and hormone signaling among age-matched virgin females (controls), mated females retaining functional flight muscles, and mated females that had undergone flight muscle histolysis Table 3, Table 4). Comparisons were conducted in long-winged females across the three-day period during which histolysis can occur (day 2 – day 4). (Fig. 5, Table 3), Supplemental Table 13). Consistent with our phenotypic data, mated females activated molecular pathways that drive autophagy, the physiological process underlying flight muscle histolysis. Expression of beclin (bec), an autophagy-related gene, was significantly upregulated almost two-fold immediately following copulation on day 2 and remained upregulated (3.2-fold) in both groups of mated females (with functional and histolyzed flight muscles) on day 3 compared to age matched virgin female (Fig. 5A, Table 3). This increase demonstrates that bec expression preceded visual muscle breakdown (in the non-histolyzed groups) and indicates that a single mating event was sufficient to initiate tissue reprogramming in some individuals. We also saw a significant upregulation in bec expression (4-fold) in virgin controls across time (Supplemental Figure 6, Supplemental Table 14), which was expected as approximately half of all virgin females initiate histolysis of their flight muscles by day 5 (Treidel et al., 2023). In addition to autophagy, mated females activated molecular pathways underlying iron transfer. Mated females with functional and histolyzed flight muscles upregulated ferritin (fer, an iron transporter) expression in their flight muscles on day 3 (1.71-fold, Fig. 5C, Table 3) and mated females with histolyzed flight muscles upregulated ecotropic viral integration site 5 (evi5, involved in vesicular trafficking of iron-bound ferritin) in their ovaries on day 3 and day 4 (1.52-fold, Fig. 5D, Table 3). This suggests that mating can initiate a coordinated nutrient recycling process where iron is released from the flight muscles and absorbed by the developing oocytes. The upregulation of evi5 cannot be simply attributable to ovarian development as yolk-less (yl) expression, a yolk protein receptor, was unchanged across groups (Supplemental Figure 7, Supplemental Table 13). We also did not see any changes in expression of transferrin (tf, Supplemental Figure 7, Supplemental Table 13), a gene also associated with iron storage and transfer, suggesting that mating may selectively initiate pathways of iron mobilization.

Finally, mating also affected hormone signaling pathways. Day 3 mated females with histolyzed flight muscles transiently upregulated insulin-like peptide (ilp) 14-fold in the fat body, and day 2 mated females with functional flight muscles transiently upregulated its receptor (ilp receptor, inr) 2.3-fold in the flight muscle, suggesting that insulin synthesis and release from the fat body may be the endocrine signal that initiates histolysis and coordinates oogenesis (Fig. 5E-F and Table 3). Interestingly, we found that mated females with non-functional flight muscles significantly downregulated the expression of a juvenile hormone biosynthesis enzyme (juvenile hormone acid O-methyltransferase, jhamt) in the brain on day 2 but showed no changes in ecdysone-synthesis related gene expression in either the fat body or ovaries (Fig. 5G-H and Table 3). Together with the lack of changes in ecdysone-related gene expression, these findings suggest that mating-induced physiological transitions may not be maintained through sustained upregulation of classical endocrine pathways but instead may involve transient hormonal signals that initiate downstream transcriptional programs associated with resource reallocation. Overall, these gene expression patterns indicate that mating triggers a coordinated molecular program linking muscle degradation, iron redistribution, and hormonal signaling to redirect resources from dispersal toward reproduction.

Discussion

The flight-oogenesis trade-off demonstrates an incompatibility of dispersal and reproductive investment (Johnson, 1969). Since reproduction and flight are both resource heavy processes with distinct metabolic and nutrient demands, investing into one can come at a cost to the other (Roff, 1984; Zera and Rankin, 1989; Price et al., 2011; Young et al., 2021). Although the reproductive costs of dispersing females have been documented across taxa (Guerra, 2011; Tigreros and Davidowitz, 2019), how females transition from flight to a reproductive phase, potentially reducing the reproductive costs, remains unclear. Here, we address this gap in knowledge by testing the hypothesis that mating initiates a coordinated physiological shift in resource allocation to mediate the flight-oogenesis trade-off and the early-life reproductive cost of flight in G. lineaticeps, the California variable field cricket.

Consistent with previous literature and studies on this model (Treidel et al., 2021; Stahlschmidt et al., 2024), we found evidence of the trade-off between flight and reproduction: females with functional flight muscles have smaller ovaries than females with histolyzed muscles (Fig. 1C). However, unlike long-winged morphs from G. firmus and G. rubens that have significantly lower offspring production for the first 2 of 3 weeks of adulthood (Roff, 1984; Zera and Rankin, 1989), long-winged G. lineaticeps females do not show significant delays in early-life reproductive behaviors or output. Although long-winged females initially invested in flight, they showed no differences in the onset of mating receptivity (Fig. 4), egg laying (Fig. 2), or cumulative hatchling emergence rates within this period (Fig. 3). Lack of differences in mating receptivity and total reproductive output of wing morphs were similarly observed in field collected crickets (Supplementary Figure 5), further supporting our conclusion and suggesting that reduced costs are not a consequence of laboratory conditions. Instead, our data suggest that mated long-winged females avoid significant reproductive delays by initiating the transfer of resources from flight to reproduction earlier than would a virgin. Mated long-winged females displayed advanced histolysis of their flight muscles (Fig. 1) and upregulation of genes involved in autophagy, iron recycling, and insulin signaling (Fig. 5), Thus, we suggest that mating acts as a potent trigger to initiate the reallocation of resources from flight muscle maintenance to oogenesis.

Mating as a trigger for physiological shifts is well documented in some species, such as the short- and long-term mating response in D. melanogaster (for review see Laturney and Billeter, 2014). However, the role of mating as an ecologically relevant cue to transition from one physiological phase to another to reduce the cost of a trade-off is unclear. This gap has in our knowledge likely led to the overestimation of the cost of dispersal and represents a critical missing link in life-history theory. This is especially true when researchers have estimated fecundity using only the ovary mass of virgin females (e.g. Roff and Fairbairn, 2007a, 2007b; Mole and Zera, 1993; McCulloch et al., 2025; Guerra and Pollack, 2009; Treidel et al., 2021). Based on a metanalysis, when long-winged females are flight capable, wing morph influences gonadal investment more than reproductive timing or fecundity (Guerra, 2011). Consequently, using virgin female ovary mass to estimate reproductive output underestimates long-winged female fecundity and inflates the perceived cost of flight. Our findings are consistent with this and add further caution to using virgin ovary size as a proxy for fecundity, as it may cause overestimates of the evolutionary costs of flight. Furthermore, if mating is a tool for triggering physiological transitions associated with life history shifts across taxa, then mating receptivity may be a target of evolution to help time resource allocation to reduce the costs of non-reproductive states, a concept that has yet to be explored.

The extent to which flight-capable long-winged females incur reproductive costs varies considerably among wing-dimorphic species (Guerra, 2011), yet the mechanisms underlying this variation remain poorly understood. One possibility is that mating serves as the primary trigger for flight muscle histolysis across species, with interspecific differences in reproductive costs arising from variation in female mating receptivity. Under this hypothesis, species in which long-winged females exhibit little, or no reproductive cost would be expected to show similar mating receptivity across wing morphs (as seen in this study). Long-winged females would mate soon after adult emergence, initiate histolysis shortly thereafter, and begin reproduction at approximately the same time as short-winged females. In contrast, species with pronounced reproductive costs of the long-winged morph (e.g. G. firmus and G. rubens) would be expected to exhibit greater differences in mating receptivity between morphs, delaying mating, histolysis, and ultimately reproduction in long-winged females. However, mating is unlikely to be the sole determinant of histolysis timing, as considerable variation in the onset of flight muscle histolysis also occurs among mated females within species (Miyashita et al., 2020; this study). Whether interspecific variation in reproductive costs and intraspecific variation in histolysis timing are driven by the same underlying mechanisms remains an important question for future research.

Beyond mating receptivity, mating rate can substantially impact female reproductive output in the related cricket G. vocalis (Gershman, 2010). Therefore, differences in mating frequency across wing morphs could contribute to differences in fecundity. In the egg production experiment, mating history was standardized by restricting females to exactly two matings, whereas in the hatchling experiment females were continuously co-housed with males, allowing mating frequency and timing to vary naturally. This difference in the mating protocols used between experiments may have contributed to the differences in egg and hatchling patterns that we observed. Although the overall patterns in daily hatchling production dynamics matched what we observed in daily egg laying numbers, we did not see the initial short-winged female reproductive advantage that was observed in egg laying (Fig. 2A, Fig. 3A). The morph-specific differences in mating frequency or in the reproductive benefits gained from remating may have reduced or obscured the initial reproductive advantage of short-winged females observed under standardized mating conditions.

Despite these contrasting mating regimes, the morph-specific reproductive trajectories were qualitatively similar between experiments. Short-winged females exhibited relatively consistent offspring production throughout the observation period, whereas long-winged females displayed a dominant peak (Figs. 2A and 3A). Together, these findings suggest that the temporal differences in reproduction between morphs are likely driven primarily by differences in resource allocation and physiological state, rather than by differences in mating frequency alone.

Females mated to long-winged males produced more hatchlings than those mated to short-winged males. To our knowledge, no previous studies have reported or predicted this pattern. In contrast, studies of Gryllus have shown that females often prefer the calls of short-winged males, suggesting an advantage for short-winged males by achieving greater mate attraction through increased calling effort (Crnokrak and Roff, 1995). However, evidence from other cricket species indicates that investment in song production can trade off against post-mating traits such as sperm viability (Ng et al., 2018). It is therefore possible that long-winged males invest relatively more in post-mating reproductive traits, such as sperm or spermatophore quality, resulting in greater reproductive success despite reduced attractiveness before mating. Because our study was not designed to distinguish among the mechanisms underlying this pattern, alternative explanations, including differences in ejaculate allocation, sperm transfer, mating frequency, or female responses to males of different morphs, cannot be excluded. Consequently, we interpret this result not as evidence that long-winged males are intrinsically of higher quality, but rather as an indication that long-winged and short-winged males may differ in their reproductive strategies, with their relative fitness depending on the balance between pre- and post-mating sexual selection.

We found substantial individual variation in the timing of flight muscle histolysis across all females, such that by day 7 some females have already completed muscle breakdown whereas others have not yet initiated or completed this transition regardless of mating status, consistent with previous work (Lorenz, 2007; Zera et al., 1997). Unexpectedly, mated long-winged females exhibited an apparently bimodal distribution of flight muscle color, with individuals tending to have red, functional muscles or white, histolyzed muscles and relatively few females displaying intermediate (pink) coloration. One possible explanation is that flight muscle histolysis proceeds more rapidly following mating, resulting in a brief intermediate stage and consequently fewer females being sampled with partially histolyzed muscles. Because we quantified muscle color as a continuous variable using a recently developed method rather than assigning muscles to discrete color categories, direct comparisons with previous studies are not possible. Nevertheless, variation in reproductive timing associated with the flight-oogenesis trade-off has been documented in the wing-dimorphic cricket G. texensis, where long-winged females exhibit early-, intermediate-, and late-reproducing strategies (Miyashita et al., 2020). The apparent bimodality in muscle coloration among mated females observed here therefore raises the possibility that similar variation in life-history trajectories also exist in G. lineaticeps. For example, mated females with white muscles on day 7 may represent early-reproducers, whereas those with red functional muscles may represent individuals with later reproductive transitions. However, our data cannot distinguish whether these patterns reflect discrete alternative strategies or continuous variation in the timing of a common physiological transition. Regardless, the observed variation is consistent with previous evidence for substantial individual differences in the timing of resource reallocation from dispersal to reproduction within flight-capable morphs (Lorenz, 2007; Zera et al., 1997). However, the mechanism underlying this variation is currently unknown.

Our results underscore the strong influence of mating on nutrient-recycling physiology. Mated females upregulate beclin (bec) expression in their flight muscles (Fig. 5A), a gene required for autophagy dependent histolysis (Diaz et al., 2024), indicating that mating serves as a cue for flight muscle breakdown. However, bec upregulation alone is not sufficient as not all mated females immediately initiate muscle degradation. We hypothesize that the accompanied increased expression of target of rapamycin (tor), an inhibitor of histolysis (Kuleesha et al., 2016), observed only in the flight muscle of the youngest (day 2) mated females helps protect them from premature muscle breakdown (Fig. 5B). Expression of tor does return to similar levels of virgin females by days 3 and 4, suggesting an age-dependent release of reliance on this protective mechanism. Additionally, ferritin (fer), a protein involved in iron storage and transfer (Gorman, 2023), is upregulated in the flight muscles of mated long-winged females relative to virgins, likely reflecting the release of iron from mitochondrial degradation (Fig. 5C). At the same time, we observed elevated expression of ecotropic viral integration site 5 (evi5), a gene involved in intracellular iron transfer (Laflamme et al., 2012; Soltani et al., 2024), in the ovaries, consistent with enhanced iron import to support oocyte development (Fig. 5D). The role of iron in insect reproduction has been underexplored, with most of the research focusing either on blood-feeding species that commonly deal with a surplus of iron (Cardoso-Jaime et al., 2022; Rani et al., 2022), or in the biomedical field utilizing D. melanogaster to understand cellular mechanisms of iron transfer and storage (Gorman, 2023). However, these results suggest that iron could also be a key micronutrient regulating life history trade-offs between flight and oogenesis.

Based on these tissue-specific transcriptional changes, our results suggest a mechanistic hypothesis in which endocrine signaling upregulates bec and fer in the flight muscles of long-winged females after mating. We found evidence that insulin-like peptide (ILP) signaling may act as a central regulator linking histolysis to reproduction. Mated long-winged females exhibited increased ilp transcript abundance in the fat body and elevated insulin receptor (inr) expression in their flight muscles (Fig. 5E-F). This result suggests that a surge in insulin signaling could initiate muscle breakdown and help establish a new reproductive physiological state. The involvement of the nutrient-sensing ILP hormone provides an elegant mechanism for coupling oogenesis to environmental quality, creating a conditional system in which mating can trigger histolysis and ovarian development only when resources are sufficient. Substantial evidence supports ILP as a key reproductive hormone in insects: ILPs promote yolk protein synthesis (Parthasarathy and Palli, 2011), elevate juvenile hormone (JH) and ecdysteroid production (Brown et al., 2008; Abrisqueta et al., 2014), and mediate post-mating changes in feeding (Laturney et al., 2023). In G. firmus, ILP expression is chronically higher in short-winged females and long-winged females with histolyzed flight muscles (Vellichirammal et al., 2014; Zera et al., 2018), closely paralleling wing morph-specific patterns of ovarian growth. Insulin signaling has also been causally linked to controlling wing length development and wing morph determination of wing dimorphic plant hoppers and soapberry beetles (Xu et al., 2015; Fawcett et al., 2018). However, ILP’s role in muscle breakdown is unclear and not supported by current literature. Because ILP typically promotes tissue growth and maintenance, its reduction—rather than its increase—is usually associated with muscle atrophy or histolysis (Lin and Smagghe, 2019), a pattern that contrasts with our observations. Thus, if ILP does initiate histolysis, its action would require a reversal or repurposing of canonical pathways in flight muscle tissue. Eusocial insects provide examples of such pathway rewiring, where conserved endocrine signals—including juvenile hormone, insulin signaling, and vitellogenin—have been co-opted to regulate novel caste-specific traits such as division of labor, fecundity, and lifespan (Chandra et al., 2018; Shalem et al., 2025). Together, these findings suggest that insulin signaling may have undergone a similar functional shift in this species, enabling mating to coordinate both nutrient recycling and reproductive activation.

An alternative hypothesis is that ILP upregulation does not cause histolysis but instead occurs after muscle breakdown, in response to elevated circulating nutrients from muscle breakdown or increased feeding after mating. Work in Drosophila supports this alternate possibility: amino acid sensing in the fat body activates the TOR pathway, which in turn stimulates ILP synthesis and secretion by insulin-producing cells in the brain (Géminard et al., 2009). Although we detected an increase in ILP production in the fat body, not the brain, these findings demonstrate that ILP production can be driven by nutrient abundance. By extension, the elevated ILP transcripts we observe in the fat body of histolyzed females could represent a physiological response to amino acids released during muscle degradation rather than a hormonal trigger of histolysis itself. Similarly, after histolysis long-winged crickets display a shift in their dietary nutrient preferences and adjust their feeding behavior to increase protein intake (Treidel et al., 2021), which could also explain or contribute to increased insulin signaling. In both non-mutually exclusive scenarios ILP signaling would function downstream of muscle breakdown, acting to integrate nutrient release with reproductive activation rather than initiating flight muscle breakdown.

Interestingly, we found no significant increases in expression in juvenile hormone acid O-methyltransferase (jhamt), a JH synthesis enzyme, in mated long-winged females (Fig. 5G). This contrasts with Zera’s (2016) proposal that elevated and sustained JH signaling mediates the shift from dispersal to reproduction in wing dimorphic insects. JH induces flight-muscle breakdown in the closely related field cricket G. bimaculatus (Shiga et al., 2002), and methoprene (a JH analog) application reallocates resources toward reproduction in Pyrrhocoris apterus (Socha and Šula, 2008). However, since we observed a decline in JH production, our data do not support a model in which moderate, sustained JH levels drive the reproductive state in long-winged G. lineaticeps females. Instead, a decline in JH signaling aligns with long-standing interpretations of JH as a “status-quo” hormone that maintains existing developmental programs and inhibits state transitions (Wigglesworth, 1934; Hartfelder, 2000). Finally, the relationship between ILPs and JH further complicates this picture: although ILPs often stimulate JH biosynthesis (Smykal and Raikhe, 2015), Drosophila work shows that an insulin peptide can suppress JH production, with hypomorphic Drosophila insulin-like peptide 6 (dilp6) mutants exhibiting elevated JH levels (Rauschenbach et al., 2017). Future work incorporating, more temporally resolved sampling is needed to fully evaluate alterative models and better understand the role JH plays to support the transition between flight and reproduction. However, based on the findings here we hypothesize that reduced JH signaling—not elevated titers—may be a key hormonal feature enabling the shift from a dispersal phenotype to a reproductive one in the adult stage.

Together, our findings reveal that mating initiates a coordinated reorganization of physiology that links timing of flight muscle histolysis, nutrient mobilization, and reproductive activation. Rapid induction of early autophagy-related genes in the flight muscles is followed by increases in iron transport to the ovaries, supporting a model in which muscle breakdown directly fuels oogenesis. These tissue-specific changes coincide with shifts in endocrine signaling, including elevated ILP and InR transcription, suggesting that conserved hormonal pathways have been repurposed to regulate this mating-induced transition. Although ILP and JH signaling have well-established roles in reproduction and developmental state maintenance, our results indicate that their interactions during the dispersal-to-reproduction shift are more complex than previously appreciated. By integrating temporal dynamics of organismal level reproductive traits to tissue-specific changes in gene expression, this work provides a framework for understanding how mating triggers a switch in life-history strategy and highlights the potential for conserved endocrine pathways to be rewired in support of species-specific reproductive programs. By acting as a switch between dispersal and reproduction, mating may help organisms time resource allocation in ways that challenge long-held assumptions about the true costs of flight and fecundity.
