The relationship between aging anxiety, job stress and psychological resilience in oncology nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Nilüfer Eroğlu, Nilgün Kuru Alici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. Oncology nurses play an essential role in the care of oncology patients. Providing care for cancer patients and managing complex treatment processes can be challenging for oncology nurses. The psychological effects of caring for cancer patients and facing their illness and death are often overlooked. The relationships between aging anxiety, job stress, and psychological resilience remain unclear. The aim of this study was to determine the relationship between aging anxiety, job stress, and psychological resilience in oncology nurses.MethodsThis cross-sectional, correlational and descriptive study was conducted with 220 nurses working in an oncology hospital from December 2023 to February 2024. The Personal Information Form, Relational Aging Anxiety Scale (RASS), Nurse Stress Scale (NSS), and Resilience Scale for Nurses (RSN) were used to collect the data. Descriptive analyses, correlation analysis, and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) were performed.ResultsCorrelation analyses revealed a statistically significant low positive correlation between the relational aging anxiety subscale and NSS scores (r = 0.240, p < 0.05), and a low negative correlation with RSN scores (r = − 0.182, p < 0.05). SEM indicated that RAAS scores were a significant negative predictor of RSN scores (β = −0.214, p < 0.05) and a significant positive predictor of NSS scores (β = 0.608, p < 0.05). No significant direct effect between RSN and NSS scores (β = −0.007, p > 0.05).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that higher aging anxiety among nurses is associated with greater stress and lower psychological resilience, highlighting the need for comprehensive training programs to reduce aging anxiety and job stress, and to enhance psychological resilience among oncology nurses. Interventions focusing on stress management, emotional regulation, and self-care are recommended. Experimental studies to evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions are encouraged.Keywords: Aging anxiety, Neoplasms, Occupational stress, Oncology nursing, Resilience, Psychological

Introduction

Cancer is one of the leading causes of death worldwide [1]. According to Globocan 2022 data, it is projected that the number of new cancer cases, which is increasing each year, will reach 35 million by 2050 [2]. The increasing number of cancer patients, increased health care needs and the complexity of treatment protocols required to treat cancer can be challenging for oncology nurses providing care [3].

Oncology nursing in Türkiye

Oncology nursing constitutes a specialized area within the nursing profession and is recognized as a certified training program in Türkiye [4]. In Türkiye, the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Health and Provincial Health Directorates implement structured certification programs designed to enhance the quality of care provided to individuals diagnosed with cancer [5, 6]. The professional roles, responsibilities, and scope of practice of oncology nurses were formally defined by the national regulation enacted in 2011 [7].

Oncology nurses are responsible for delivering both direct and indirect care to cancer patients playing a critical role throughout the continuum of the cancer care trajectory. In addition to administering anticancer drugs, they provide support to patients and their families in pain and symptom management, provide education, and ensure care coordination [4, 8].

Background

Oncology nurses, while providing care to cancer patients, can develop a close relationship, empathy, and a sense of identification with the patient. Caring for patients with cancer can be emotionally exhausting for oncology nurses [9]. They may experience anxiety, especially when they witness the impact of the disease process on patients. Due to nature of their work, oncology nurses face illness and death more frequently, they may face concerns about health and death as they get older, and this may aggravate their feelings of anxiety [10]. Aging anxiety is the prediction and concern about the aging process [11]. Gendron et al. [12] defined aging anxiety, as multidimentional relational process with personal, social and collective aspects. Aging anxiety negatively affects the health of individuals as a source of stress [13]. The individual’s occupation, age, gender, and health status significantly influence their aging anxiety [14, 15]. Healthcare professionals who care for older adults during periods of illness tend to associate aging with illness and experience more aging anxiety [16].

Nurses are exposed to considerable job stress arising from the physical, psychological, and social conditions of the hospital environment, as well as from the demands of direct patient care [17]. Sustained exposure to such stress adversely affects nurses’ physical and mental health, contributing to burnout, reduced job satisfaction and concentration, increased error rates, and compromised quality of patient care [18]. In oncology settings, where emotional and clinical demands are particularly high, job stress has been shown to further impair work performance, increase stress-related health problems, and descrease job satisfaction among nurses [19].

Park et al. [20] defined psychological resilience in nursing as the " capability required of nurses to positively overcome numerous difficulties in practising fields, to exhibit professional competencies”. Psychological resilience is critical in enabling nurses to achieve professional success and effectively manage workplace stress [21]. Psychological resilience has a protective effect on depression, anxiety and stress in nurses. High psychological resilience reduces perceived stress, anxiety and psychological distress while increasing mental and general well-being, work engagement and coping with stress [22, 23].

There are studies showing the link between aging anxiety and psychological resilience [13, 24], and showing the relationship between job stress and psychological resilience in nurses [25, 26]. However, there is no study examining the correlation between aging anxiety, job stress and psychological resilience in oncology nurses as a whole. This study aimed to determine the relationship between aging anxiety, job stress, and psychological resilience in oncology nurses. Accordingly, this study aims to address the following research questions:

What are the levels of aging anxiety, job stress and psychological resilience in oncology nurses?

What are the levels of aging anxiety, job stress and psychological resilience in oncology nurses?

What is the direction and strength of the relationship between aging anxiety, job stress and psychological resilience in oncology nurses?

Methodology

Study design and participants

This study used a cross-sectional, correlational and descriptive design. It was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for the reporting of cross-sectional studies.

The study was conducted in the one of the largest cancer centers in Türkiye. A total of 495 nurses work in the hospital. The nurses’ working hours are 8-hours shift (8am-4pm), 16-hours shift (4pm-8am) and 24-hours shifts (8am-8am). Based on the sample size calculation, 217 or more participants were required to achieve a 95% confidence interval with a ± 5% margin of error.

The inclusion criteria for participation were voluntary consent and a minimum of six months of nursing experience. The exclusion criteria included having a diagnosed psychiatric or physical disorder that could impede participation in the study. The study sample was selected using convenience sampling. Nurses who met the inclusion criteria and volunteered to participate were included in the study.

Data collection

The data was collected from oncology nurses working at an oncology hospital in Ankara between December 2023 and February 2024. The principal investigator visited each department and consulted with the ward in-charges to determine a suitable time in accordance with the unit’s workflow. Participants were provided with information about the study, and those who met the inclusion criteria and consented to participate were given the data collection forms. When nurses were unavailable due to shift schedules, the forms were distributed to ward nurses. A specific date for data collection was established for each unit, and weekly reminders were issued to the respective wards. The timing of data collection and the process for obtaining informed consent were planned in advance according to the unit’s activity level. Participants provided both written and verbal consent to take part in the study. It took approximately 15–20 min for the nurses to complete the data collection forms. Only participants with complete datasets, with no missing data, were included in the analysis. The study was completed with 220 oncology nurses.

Measurements

The personal information form

The Personel Information Form was developed by utilizing the literature which included questions about gender, educational status, age, marital status, having children, family structure, health status and working conditions.

The RAAS

Gendron et al. developed the RAAS to measure aging anxiety [12]. The scale was adapted into Turkish by Çay et al. [27]. It consists of 16 items. The 5-point Likert-type scale, graded as “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” consists of three subscales: personal aging anxiety, a collective affinity for older people and relational aging anxiety. Items 8 and 13 are reverse-scored. The total scores for each subscale were as follows: personal aging anxiety (range: 5–22), collective affinity for older people (range: 5–25), and relational aging anxiety (range: 6–24) [27]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.80.

The NSS

The scale was developed by Gray-Toft and Anderson [17] and adapted into Turkish by Mert et al. [28] to to assess the job stress of nurses with more than six months of professional experience. The scale consists of 34 items and is a four-point Likert-type scale (1 = never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often, 4 = very often). The scale has seven subscales, including uncertainty about treatment, workload, death of a patient, conflict with a physician, conflict with other nurses, inadequate support, and suffering patients The higher the total score, the higher the level of job stress experienced by the nurse [28]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.92, indicating high internal consistency.

The RSN

This scale was developed by Park et al. [20] to measure psychological resilience in nurses. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Adıbelli et al. [29]. This 15-item scale is a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘’strongly disagree’’ to ‘’strongly agree’’. The scale consists of four subscales: philosophical construct, relational construct, situational construct and spiritual construct. The scale’s total score varies between 19 and 95, where higher scores reflect higher levels of psychological resilience in nurses [29]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated as 0.93.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 25 and AMOS software was used. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequency, mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to examine relationships between non-normally distributed continuous variables. The strength of correlations was interpreted according to Rowntree [30]. Correlation analysis and SEM were used to examine the relationships between the variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Hacettepe University Social and Human Sciences Research Ethics Committee (No: E-66777842-300-00003617187). Additionally, all necessary permissions for using the measurement scales were obtained. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. For confidentiality purposes, the datasets were anonymized by assigning participant codes (e.g., P1, P2, etc.). All study data were securely stored in cloud system and will be retained for 2 years.

Discussion

This study analyzed the relationships between aging anxiety, job stress, and psychological resilience among oncology nurses.

The findings of our study suggest that oncology nurses experience moderate levels of aging anxiety. Aging anxiety in oncology nurses is an under-researched topic [31]. A study by Abdollahi et al. [16] found that healthcare professionals had high levels of aging anxiety. In accordance with the limited existing literature, the moderate levels of aging anxiety observed among oncology nurses in this study may be related to their care for cancer patients, which can have both physical and emotional impacts. Most of the nurses in the study worked more than 40 h per week. The emotional burden of caring for oncology patients, combined with long working hours and shift work, may contribute to concerns about aging and personal health.

Oncology nurses’ job stress was found to be moderate in our study, consistent with previous research [32, 33]. They may experience higher job stress than other nurses due to patient death, workload, and emotional demands in their work environment [34]. Most participants were relatively young (aged 24–34) and had 6 months to 5 years of professional experience. Young nurses are reported to experience higher job stress in the literature [35]. In addition, exposure to emotionally challenging conditions—such as the workload associated with oncology care, fear of clinical errors, and loss of patients with whom they had formed emotional bonds—may contribute to increased job stress.

In our study, oncology nurses’ psychological resilience levels were found to be moderate, consistent with the findings of Uzar-Özçetin et al. [36]. Psychological resilience is essential for mitigating the emotional effects of challenges in oncology care, such as patient death and suffering. More than half of the nurses in our study were married and rated their health as good. According to the literature, individuals with good self-perceived health and those who are married tend to exhibit higher resilience [37]. Social support, including peer support and the presence of a spouse or partner, also enhances psychological resilience in oncology nurses [37]. Taken together, these findings suggest that perceived health status and social support may positively influence nurses’ resilience, helping to protect them from the psychological demands of caring for cancer patients.

Our study revealed a statistically significant but weak negative relationship between aging anxiety and psychological resilience. Similarly, Kim [24] reported that psychological resilience decreased as aging anxiety increased in middle-aged women. Psychological resilience can mitigate the adverse effects of aging anxiety, which acts as a stressor [13], and is influenced by individual characteristics such as social relationships, future perspective, and psychosocial factors [38]. In understanding aging anxiety, individuals’ relationships with older adults and stereotypes about aging play a role [12]. Among oncology nurses, both professional and personal relationships, as well as exposure to patients with cancer, may shape life perspectives and influence resilience. These findings suggest that psychological resilience may buffer the negative effects of aging anxiety in this population.

A modest positive relationship between aging anxiety and job stress was observed. Notably, no previous studies have examined aging anxiety in the context of job stress. Consistent with the limited literature, higher job stress may increase aging anxiety as a response to work-related stressors [39, 40]. Exposure to elderly patients and associated professional demands may reduce social support, which can further exacerbate aging anxiety and job stress, influencing the aging process [41]. Aging-related thoughts may also intensify job stress and contribute to psychological difficulties, leading to negative health outcomes [19]. Less experienced nurses may face additional challenges in coping with these combined stressors.

Several studies have reported that psychological resilience decreases as job stress increases [25, 42]. However, despite the findings of these studies, our study did not identify a significant relationship between psychological resilience and job stress among oncology nurses. Cooper et al. [21] identified social support, self-efficacy, work-life balance/self-care, sense of humour, optimism, and realism as key characteristics of psychological resilience in nurses. Yet, there is no consensus regarding the influence of personal characteristics on nurses’ resilience [23]. Furthermore, according to the Conservation of Resources Theory, stress arises independently of individuals’ appraisal of events and is primarily caused by people’s efforts to maintain a good life and protect themselves from threats, as well as by environmental, social, and cultural factors [43]. This may be attributed to the unique individual characteristics of the oncology nurses in our sample rather than the impact of job stressors on their psychological resilience.

Implications of the Study

Our study reveals that reducing the negative effects of aging, anxiety and job stress, and supporting psychological resilience, are essential for protecting and improving the physical and psychological health of oncology nurses. In this context, structured resilience training modules should be planned to address the aging process and health issues associated with aging and promote both physical and mental well-being, aiming to reduce oncology nurses’ levels of aging anxiety. To reduce job stress levels, targeted stress management training programs should be organized, social support resources should be strengthened, and working conditions should be reviewed. In particular, mentorship programs for early-career oncology nurses may support professional adaptation and reduce stress related to clinical workload and emotional burden. Training programs should be developed to provide oncology nurses with the skills needed to manage stress and trauma effectively. In accordance with advancing technology and the demanding workload, these training programs may incorporate virtual reality–based stress exposure training, as well as wearable technologies and mobile applications to support ongoing stress monitoring and self-regulation. Additionally, peer-support groups facilitated by oncology psychologists and regular aging-anxiety awareness workshops may help nurses share experiences, normalize emotional challenges, and enhance coping strategies. Additionally, online nurse-led peer support groups and psychological assessments every six months may be beneficial. Regular psychological assessments conducted every six months may be beneficial.

Strengths and limitations

This study focuses on an underrated topic of aging anxiety in oncology nurses. Also, examines relationship among aging anxiety, job stress and pscyhological resilience. The strengths of this study are to fill the gap in the literature and determine correlations.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, in our study examine single time-point in scope of cross-sectional design. Therefore, the results cannot be used to infer the direction of the relationship. Secondly, the findings are limited in terms of generalizability, as data were collected from public hospital in one province. The study did not include oncology nurses working in private or university hospitals. Therefore, the study’s findings cannot be generalised to these groups. Another area for improvement in the study is the impact of intensive working hours and shift work. Thirdly, the use of self-report measures may lead to bias, as participants may underreport or overreport aging anxiety, job stress, or psychological resilience participation levels based on their recollection. Also, potential social desirability should be considered while interpreting resilience and stress reports. Additionally, the study did not thoroughly examine the impact of intensive working hours and shift work, which could be significant factors influencing the psychological well-being of nurses. Lastly, as a methodological consideration, the NSS used in this study assesses the job stress of nurses with less than six months of professional experience. This may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly for newly graduated nurses. For assessing job stress among new graduate nurses, alternative validated scales may be more appropriate.

Future studies should include multiple centres and use probability sampling to increase generalisability, and should consider objective measures alongside self-reports. Also, to explore directionality, longitudinal study designs should be used.

Conclusions

Oncology nurses experience moderate levels of aging anxiety and job stress, while also demonstrating moderate levels of psychological resilience. In order to support the health and professional performance of oncology nurses, it is essential to organize in-service training programs aimed at reducing aging anxiety and job stress, as well as increasing social support resources. These training programs should focus on enhancing the psychological resilience of oncology nurses, helping them develop skills to effectively manage stress and trauma. Workshops and seminars covering stress management, emotional regulation, mindfulness, and self-care techniques are also recommended. By focusing on these areas, we can empower oncology nurses to provide more effective patient care. This study suggests that future research should focus on identifying additional personal, institutional, and relational factors that may reduce aging and job-related anxiety, and should include a broader range of settings with diverse working conditions.
