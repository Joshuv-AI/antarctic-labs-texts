Mechanistic insight into visible light photocatalytic removal of meropenem from wastewater utilizing nickel based LDH nanostructures with synergistic adsorption effects
Authors: Seyed Ali Hosseini, Mohammad Kazemeini, Alireza Mohammadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, two complementary synthesis routes were developed to prepare novel Ni-containing layered double hydroxide (LDH) catalysts. In Route 1, a trivalent-metal composite precursor was fabricated by compositing MIL-101(Cr) and MIL-88 A(Fe) MOFs and subsequently converted into Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH via Ni2+ incorporation. In Route 2, Ni-MCr LDH and Ni-MFe LDH were individually derived from MIL-101(Cr) and MIL-88 A(Fe) in the presence of Ni2+ and then combined to Ni-MCr LDH/Ni-MFe LDH composite. The synthesized materials were systematically characterized and applied to meropenem (MER) removal through a combined adsorption–photocatalysis process. Catalytic performance was evaluated by varying catalyst dosage, MER initial concentration, solution pH, and reaction temperature. Under optimized conditions (50 mg catalyst, 25 mg L−1 MER, visible-light irradiation), Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH achieved 98.1% MER degradation and maintained 93.4% efficiency after five cycles, while post-reaction XRD, FTIR, and ICP analyses confirmed structural stability, heterogeneity, and recyclability. Adsorption followed the pseudo-second-order kinetic model and Temkin isotherm, whereas photocatalytic degradation obeyed pseudo-first-order kinetics. A comprehensive and previously unreported MER degradation pathway was elucidated using HPLC–MS/MS, radical scavenger tests, intermediate identification, and literature comparison, demonstrating that Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH is a robust, reusable, and synergistic platform for visible-light-driven remediation of persistent pharmaceutical pollutants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52455-1.Keywords: Layered double hydroxides, Metal–organic frameworks, Antibiotic degradation, Advanced oxidation processes, Photocatalyst, AdsorptionSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Over the past two decades, global antibiotic consumption has risen by more than 65%, with projections indicating it could double by 20301,2. Many antibiotics are only partially metabolized in humans and animals, and a significant fraction is excreted in active forms into wastewater systems3. As a result, these compounds enter aquatic environments, promoting the dissemination of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and resistance genes that threaten ecosystems and human health2,4. Antimicrobial resistance already accounts for a substantial global burden of disease and mortality1.

Meropenem (MER) is a widely used β-lactam antibiotic applied as a last-line therapy for severe bacterial infections4. Reports of bacterial strains resistant to MER have reduced available clinical treatment options and heightened public-health concern1,4. Many pharmaceuticals, including MER, are not fully removed by conventional wastewater treatment, so they persist in sewage and surface waters2,4.

Conventional remediation methods (adsorption, coagulation, Fenton, biological treatments and classical oxidation) often suffer from high operating cost, narrow pH windows, sludge generation and limited efficiency toward highly persistent pharmaceuticals5,6.

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) feature ultrahigh surface areas, ordered porous networks, low cost, and easily tunable composition, which make them attractive for catalysis, separations, molecular recognition, and gas storage7.

Layered double hydroxides (LDHs) are anionic clays made of positively charged brucite-like layers separated by exchangeable interlayer anions. Their general formula allows flexible combinations of divalent and trivalent cations, which makes the composition highly tunable8.

Metal aggregation and leaching may occur under reaction conditions, which reduces the number of accessible active sites and leads to a gradual decline in photocatalytic performance9. Compared with conventional catalysts, LDHs offer large surface area, excellent thermal stability, adjustable particle size, intrinsic hydrophilicity, cost-effective synthesis approaches with enhanced charge density, and a unique layered structure. Through interlayer interactions, this structure achieves effective utilization of the interlayer space while maintaining low bulk density and favorable mechanical stability10. The positively charged layers strongly attract anions electrostatically, and the plentiful surface hydroxyl groups interact with adsorbates via hydrogen bonding. Both effects enhance removal of anionic contaminants11.

Although several MOF‑ and LDH‑based photocatalysts have been reported for removal of pollutants, each system still suffers from intrinsic limitations. For example, MIL‑88 A(Fe) shows good visible‑light absorption but achieved only about 42% degradation of Rhodamine B (RhB)12. In some composite systems, efficient pollutant removal also relies upon Fenton‑like processes that require the addition of H2O213. Similarly, MIL‑101(Cr) possesses a high surface area and strong adsorption capacity, yet it removed only about 38% of carbamazepine (CBZ)14. LDH derivatives also show several limitations. Ni‑MCr LDH demonstrates good adsorption and degradation performance, but its activity has mainly been reported under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, while its visible‑light response remains limited15. In contrast, Ni‑MFe LDH benefits from low electrical resistance and favorable band‑gap properties. Nevertheless, its individual catalytic performance is still insufficient for efficient pharmaceutical removal16. Moreover, several LDH-based systems still depend upon Fenton-like processes requiring H2O2 as an external oxidant17,18. Therefore, integrating these complementary properties into a single system may provide a synergistic strategy to enhance adsorption, visible‑light utilization, and overall catalytic efficiency.

In this study, two complementary synthesis routes were developed to produce novel Ni‑containing LDH catalysts which combined strong adsorption with visible‑light photocatalytic activity. In the first route, for the first time in LDH synthesis, a trivalent‑metal composite was assembled from MIL‑101(Cr) and MIL‑88 A(Fe), providing Cr3+ and Fe3+ species, and then converted into a ternary Ni‑[MCr/MFe] LDH by incorporation of Ni2+ into it. In the second route, MIL‑101(Cr) and MIL‑88 A(Fe) were prepared separately and served as trivalent‑metal precursors to form Ni‑MCr LDH and Ni‑MFe LDH, which were later combined to obtain a binary Ni‑MCr LDH/Ni‑MFe LDH composite. The main novelties of this work are as follows: (i) the first time design and synthesis of a novel ternary Ni‑[MCr/MFe] LDH from MOF precursors, together with the first time fabrication of a novel binary Ni‑MCr LDH/Ni‑MFe LDH composite constructed from two MOF‑derived LDHs; (ii) the systematic evaluation of these catalysts for the degradation of the antibiotic MER under different operating conditions; and (iii) the establishment of a more comprehensive adsorption–visible‑light photocatalytic degradation mechanism for MER species. In addition, the ternary Ni‑[MCr/MFe] LDH, the binary Ni‑MCr LDH/Ni‑MFe LDH composite, and MCr/MFe components are reported here for the first time for MER removal. Synthesis was achieved through a combination of hydrothermal treatment, coprecipitation, and urea hydrolysis to control phase formation and morphology. The ternary Ni‑[MCr/MFe] LDH inherited high adsorption capacity from the Ni‑MCr LDH component and enhanced visible‑light absorption together with low electrical resistance from the Ni‑MFe LDH component. These complementary properties produced a synergistic hybrid with improved thermal and chemical stability and significantly enhanced catalytic activity at a low catalyst loading. Furthermore, a dual adsorption–photocatalytic pathway was identified, in which MER is first adsorbed onto the catalyst surface and subsequently oxidized under visible light. This pathway was verified using High-Performance Liquid Chromatography–coupled with Tandem Mass Spectrometry (HPLC–MS/MS) analysis, radical scavenger experiments, monitoring of reaction intermediates, and comparison with literature data. The Ni‑[MCr/MFe] LDH also demonstrated high removal efficiency at low dosages and maintained its performance over several reuse cycles, confirming good heterogeneity and long‑term reproducibility. Finally, potential nickel release and catalyst recovery were examined to assess environmental sustainability and practical applicability in wastewater treatment. Overall, the presented synthesis strategies, newly developed Ni‑LDH composites, and the proposed mechanistic pathway established a promising materials platform for visible‑light‑driven removal of pharmaceutical contaminants.

Materials and methods

Materials and instruments

The chemical precursors and reagents used in this work are listed in detail in the Supporting Information File (SIF) (Section S-1.1) of this document.

The structural, morphological, and physicochemical properties of the prepared catalysts were characterized using several analytical techniques. Phase structure was analyzed by X‑ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical X’Pert PRO MPD). Surface morphology and elemental composition were examined using field‑emission scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy‑dispersive X‑ray spectroscopy (FESEM‑EDS, TESCAN MIRA 3 LMU). Functional groups were identified by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, PerkinElmer Spectrum RX I). Surface chemical states were investigated by X‑ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Specs EA10 Plus). Optical properties were evaluated using diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS, SHIMADZU UV‑2700). Thermal stability was examined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, METTLER TOLEDO). Surface area and pore characteristics were determined from nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements (BELSORP‑mini II). Electrochemical properties were studied using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, Corrtest CS350). Zeta potential was measured using a Malvern ZN Series instrument, and elemental composition was quantified by inductively coupled plasma spectroscopy (ICP, Vista‑Pro). In addition, photodegradation products and intermediates were analyzed using HPLC‑MS/MS (Shimadzu UFLC LC‑AD20 coupled with a 3200 QTRAP mass spectrometer). The total organic carbon content of the sample was measured using a TOC analyzer (Ana TOC, Australia). Concentration changes during photodegradation were monitored using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Detailed experimental conditions and instrument parameters, along with a comprehensive list of materials and their sources, are provided in the SIF (Sections S‑1.1 and S‑1.2).

Synthesis of the catalyst

The catalysts were synthesized through two main pathways, as shown in Fig. 1. In route 1, a bimetallic MCr/MFe precursor was first obtained by dispersing MIL‑101(Cr) and MIL‑88 A(Fe) in water and stirring them for an extended period. This composite precursor was then used to prepare Ni‑[MCr/MFe] LDH through a hydrothermal reaction in the presence of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and urea15,19. In route 2, two separate LDH materials—Ni‑MCr LDH and Ni‑MFe LDH—were synthesized using MIL‑101(Cr) and MIL‑88 A(Fe) as individual precursors. Each material was formed via hydrothermal growth of Ni‑based LDHs. The two LDH structures were then combined through a controlled aqueous assembly process to obtain the final Ni‑MCr LDH/Ni‑MFe LDH composite15,19,20. Adsorption and photocatalysis

MER adsorption and photocatalytic degradation experiments, showing a maximum absorption wavelength of 295 nm, were performed under irradiation conditions. At the beginning of each experiment, 100 mL of an aqueous MER solution containing the photocatalyst was magnetically stirred in the dark to allow the adsorption process to occur. During this dark period, samples were taken every 15 min to monitor the change in MER concentration until no significant variation was observed. The latter indicated that the system approached adsorption–desorption equilibrium. Based upon these preliminary observations, a dark adsorption time of 45 min was selected before starting the photocatalytic reaction. After this period, the suspension was exposed to light irradiation. The operational parameters were initially optimized, followed by a systematic evaluation of the effects of catalyst dosage, initial MER concentration, solution pH, and temperature on the removal performance. At regular time intervals, 3 mL samples were collected from the reaction system and promptly centrifuged to separate the photocatalyst. The clarified supernatant was determined by UV–Vis spectrophotometry at the maximum absorption wavelength to determine the residual MER concentration. Materials characterization

The morphology of the synthesized materials was examined using FESEM. Moreover, according to the bulk image, the size of these particles ranges from 2 to 6.5 . In Fig. 2c, d, Ni-MFe LDH shows that nickel encapsulates the MIL-88 A(Fe) rods, resulting in a hollow inner space, and creating needle-shaped Ni-MFe LDH structures as noted in earlier research20,22. Furthermore, according to the bulk image, the size of these particles ranges from 1.1 to 2.2 . Figure 2e, f represent the Ni-MCr LDH/Ni-MFe LDH composite. The newly formed particles are larger than those of Ni-MFe LDH and smaller than those of Ni-MCr LDH, and the characteristic structures of the individual precursors are not observed. Figure 2g, h illustrate the Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH. Here, nickel has encapsulated the MCr/MFe composite in such a way that both LDH types—Ni-MCr LDH and Ni-MFe LDH—are distinguishable. The spherical Ni-MCr LDH particles15 and the needle-like Ni-MFe LDH particles19 are well integrated, indicating a successful composite formation. In this synthesis approach, the LDH structure forms in such a way that both precursors, MIL-88 A(Fe) and MIL-101(Cr), become integrated during the process. The LDH layers grow on this combined framework, leading to a structure in which the distinctive features of both Ni-MCr LDH and Ni-MFe LDH are merged within a single architecture. Energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX) and elemental mapping results Fig. 2 (i) displays the EDX and elemental mapping results for the Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH composite, which confirm the morphological interpretation. A high Ni content is observed on the surface, followed by O and C, while the trivalent metals (Cr and Fe) are present in much lower amounts, indicating effective encapsulation by the nickel layer similar to earlier reports22. The crystalline structures of the synthesized materials were examined by XRD, and the patterns are presented in Fig. 3a. The diffraction peaks of MIL-88 A(Fe) and MIL-101(Cr) match well with previously reported data, confirming their successful formation14,23. The XRD pattern of the MCr/MFe composite shows reflections corresponding to both MIL-88 A(Fe) and MIL-101(Cr), indicating the coexistence of both crystalline phases. The presence of these two sets of characteristic peaks confirms the successful formation of a dual-phase structure14. In the synthesized LDHs, including Ni-MFe LDH, Ni-MCr LDH, and Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH, the diffraction patterns correspond to typical LDH phases reported in previous literature15,22,24,25. For Ni-MCr LDH/Ni-MFe LDH, five characteristic peaks were observed at 2θ ≈ 12.2°, 21°, 33.1°, 38.4°, 59.2°. These peaks are also observed in the precursors of this catalyst. For Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH, three characteristic peaks were observed at 2θ ≈ 12°, 34.7°, and 60°, which are attributed to the (001), (100), and (110) planes, respectively. The absence of diffraction peaks related to precursor species in Ni-MFe LDH and Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH suggests the complete transformation of the initial components during the LDH synthesis process22. Moreover, no significant peak broadening was observed in the XRD spectra, suggesting that the samples are likely free of major structural defects22,25. The FTIR spectra of the synthesized materials, as illustrated in Fig. 3b, exhibit characteristic peaks in the 500–800 cm−1 region, corresponding to the bending vibrations of M–O and M–OH bonds. Peaks located at 1388 and 1536 cm−1 are ascribed to the bending and stretching vibrations of carbonate groups (CO32−). Additionally, a peak at 1602 cm−1 corresponds to the bending vibration of molecular water. A broad absorption band around 3435 cm−1 is also observed, which is associated with hydroxyl groups (OH)27. Furthermore, the spectra of MIL-101(Cr), MIL-88 A(Fe), Ni-MCr LDH, and Ni-MFe LDH closely match those reported in the literature, confirming that these materials were successfully synthesized using comparable procedures12,15,28,29.

The XPS analysis of Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH illustrated in Fig. 4 reveals comprehensive insights into the material’s chemical states and bonding configurations. In the Ni 2p spectrum, two main peaks appear at 856.1 eV and 874.6 eV, corresponding to Ni 2p3/2 Ni 2p1/2, respectively. Deconvolution reveals the coexistence of Ni2+ (855.7/873.4 eV) and Ni3+ (857.3/875.4 eV), along with satellite peaks at 862.0 and 880.0 eV. These features indicate the mixed oxidation states of nickel, which is consistent with earlier studies24,30. The Cr 2p spectrum exhibits two broad peaks at 577.5 eV and 587.1 eV, corresponding to Cr 2p3/2 and Cr 2p1/2, respectively. The binding energy at 576.6 eV is attributed to Cr–O bonds, while the peak at 577.9 eV corresponds to Cr–OH groups. These assignments are in good agreement with previous findings30. The Fe 2p spectrum shows distinct peaks at 713.8 eV (Fe 2p3/2) and 725.4 eV (Fe 2p1/2), with an additional satellite feature at 715.6 eV. These signals confirm the presence of Fe3+ species, similar to those reported in the literature25. The O 1s spectrum contains multiple components. The peak at 530.6 eV corresponds to hydroxyl groups (–OH). The one at 531.5 eV is assigned to oxygen vacancies (VO), while the peak at 532.7 eV originates from adsorbed water. These features are in agreement with earlier studies27. Ni2+/Ni3+ species generate multiple redox-active sites, facilitating reversible charge transfer. This characteristic enhances the separation of photogenerated electron–hole pairs upon light irradiation, which is crucial for photocatalytic degradation31. Moreover, the oxygen vacancies detected at about 531.5 eV serve as localized defect states that trap charge carriers. These vacancies extend carrier lifetimes, reduce recombination rates, and promote the generation of reactive oxygen species. As a result, the overall photocatalytic efficiency of Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH is significantly improved32. Overall, the XPS results are in good agreement with earlier reports on LDH morphologies, indicating that Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH was successfully formed with preserved structural integrity.

The optical properties of the synthesized samples were studied using DRS (Fig. 5a). Band gap energies were estimated using Tauc plots (Fig. 5b)33. A wide band gap of ~ 3.7 eV was observed for MIL-101(Cr), consistent with previous reports. Similarly, Ni-MCr LDH showed a band gap around 3.0 eV with an indirect electronic transition, in agreement with earlier studies14,15. Both materials exhibited limited light absorption in the visible region. MIL-88 A(Fe) was also analyzed. It has a narrower band gap of approximately 2.5 eV, which aligns well with literature values12. In contrast, LDH materials containing iron showed significantly stronger absorption in the visible range. These include Ni-MFe LDH (2.0 eV), Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH (2.2 eV), and the hybrid Ni-MCr LDH/Ni-MFe LDH (1.9 eV). The band gap of Ni-MFe LDH is consistent with values reported for another Fe–Ni-based LDH16. This enhanced visible-light absorption allows these materials to be activated without UV irradiation. Therefore, they show strong potential for photocatalytic applications under visible light.

(a) UV–Vis DRS spectra; (b) Tauc plots; (c) Nyquist plots from EIS; (d) TGA/DTG analysis of the prepared catalysts.

The EIS results (Fig. 5c) show the Nyquist plots of all samples. Ni-MCr LDH has the largest semicircular arc radius, indicating the highest charge-transfer resistance, consistent with previous reports15. Both MIL-101(Cr) and MIL-88 A(Fe) exhibit smaller arc radii, with MIL-88 A(Fe) showing the smallest radius among them, confirming better charge transfer, in agreement with earlier findings12,14. The MCr/MFe composite shows an arc radius between those of MIL-101(Cr) and MIL-88 A(Fe)—lower than MIL-101(Cr) but higher than MIL-88 A(Fe)—indicating that MIL-88 A(Fe) alone did not significantly improve conductivity. However, the LDH form Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH presents the smallest semicircular arc radius overall, demonstrating a strong synergistic effect that substantially enhances conductivity and charge transfer efficiency compared to Ni-MCr LDH, which lacks MIL-88 A(Fe).

The TGA was performed to evaluate the thermal stability and decomposition behavior of the synthesized catalysts, as presented in Fig. 5d. All samples exhibited an initial weight loss below 100 °C, attributed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed water. A second weight loss up to 350 °C was due to the removal of interlayer and surface-bound water, with LDH-based samples showing higher moisture content34. For MIL-88 A(Fe), the early weight loss before 150 °C also reflected partial collapse of the organic skeleton. A sharp drop at ~ 300 °C resulted from combustion of fumaric acid, followed by framework collapse and Fe3O4–carbon formation near 500 °C35. MIL-101(Cr) showed terephthalic acid decomposition above 200 °C, and structural breakdown above 350 °C36. The MCr/MFe hybrid demonstrated improved thermal stability over the individual MILs, suggesting strong interfacial bonding. Nevertheless, distinct decomposition events from both MIL-101(Cr) and MIL-88 A(Fe) were still observed. Dehydroxylation of Ni-MCr LDH and Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH occurred in the temperature range of 210–500 °C, accompanied by the removal of interlayer anions and hydroxyl groups, as well as the crystallization of oxides. Decomposition of carbonates above 400 °C further facilitated the formation of metal oxides. These LDH-based samples exhibited the highest thermal stability, likely due to hydrogen bonds, the material’s layered architecture, and a robust structural framework15,34,37.

The pore size distribution and specific surface area of the Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH sample were determined from the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms, as presented in Fig. S2 of the SIF document. The total pore volume and average pore diameter were calculated using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model. The isotherm exhibits a steep increase at high relative pressure (P/P0 > 0.9), indicating the coexistence of mesopores and macropores in the Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH structure38. The specific surface area and pore volume were found to be 113.28 m2 g−1 and 0.2311 cm3 g−1, respectively, confirming the presence of abundant pores that contribute significantly to the adsorption capability of the prepared material.

Removal of pharmaceuticals

Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH was examined for its adsorption properties and photocatalytic activity in removing MER under visible-light irradiation. The experimental conditions, such as catalyst dosage, initial concentration of MER, temperature, and pH, were systematically optimized to maximize removal efficiency. Preliminary experiments identified the optimal conditions as 25 °C, pH 6.7, an initial concentration of MER of 25 mg L−1, and a catalyst dosage of 50 mg in 100 mL of solution. The selected parameter ranges were centered around these values. Adsorption–desorption equilibrium was approached within 45 min, resulting in a significant reduction in MER concentration and confirming the enhanced adsorption ability of the material. The slight slope observed between 30 and 45 min does not indicate that equilibrium had not been reached; rather, it reflects the ongoing adsorption occurring around 30 min, while preliminary experiments showed that the adsorption change near 45 min was negligible. Following equilibrium, the suspension was irradiated with visible light for 180 min to assess photocatalytic degradation. The results demonstrate that Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH possesses both strong adsorption capability and superior photocatalytic efficiency under optimized conditions.

To assess the impact on removal efficiency, the catalyst dosage was varied (30, 50, and 70 mg) while keeping the conditions constant (MER 25 mg L−1, 25 °C, pH 6.7) (Fig. 6a). At 30 mg, MER removal reached 86.2%. Increasing the loading to 50 mg significantly enhanced the efficiency to 98.1%, which can be attributed to the higher number of active sites and improved interaction between the catalyst and MER molecules39. A further increase to 70 mg resulted in only a slight improvement (98.6%). This marginal gain is explained by several well-known effects at high catalyst loadings. These include particle agglomeration that reduces accessible surface area, light scattering and shielding that limit photon penetration, and mass-transfer resistance in dense suspensions39,40.

Using Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH, MER was removed through photocatalytic and adsorption processes under visible light: (a) 30, 50, and 70 mg catalyst doses influenced the removal efficiency; (b) 10, 25, and 40 mg L-1 initial concentrations of MER impacted the removal efficiency; (c) 15, 25, and 40 °C temperatures affected the removal efficiency; (d) 5, 6.7, and 9 initial pH values influenced the removal efficiency; and (e) comparative removal efficiencies of MER for Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH versus other prepared catalysts.

The influence of initial MER concentration on removal efficiency was examined using the optimized catalyst loading of 50 mg under constant conditions (25 °C, pH 6.7) (Fig. 6b). At 10 mg L−1, the removal efficiency was 99.7%. Increasing the concentration to 25 mg L−1 slightly decreased the efficiency to 98.1%. A further increase to 40 mg L−1 caused a more significant decline, with 94.7% removal. The reduced efficiency at higher concentrations is mainly associated with surface saturation, electron–hole recombination, and competitive adsorption41,42. Therefore, 25 mg L−1 was selected as the optimum concentration for subsequent experiments, as it provides the clearest measure of the catalyst’s performance, free from desorption effects.

Temperature effect on MER removal was examined using the optimal catalyst dosage (50 mg) and MER concentration (25 mg L−1) under constant pH conditions (6.7) (Fig. 6c). The removal efficiency increased markedly from 67.9% at 15 °C to 98.1% at 25 °C. This improvement can be attributed to the higher kinetic energy of MER molecules at elevated temperatures. As temperature increases, the diffusion of MER molecules toward the catalyst surface becomes faster and the mass‑transfer resistance in the boundary layer decreases. In addition, the higher molecular mobility promotes more frequent interactions between MER molecules and the available active sites. As a result, the adsorption and subsequent degradation processes become more efficient43. When the temperature was further increased to 40 °C, no noticeable improvement in removal efficiency was observed. This suggests that most active sites were already occupied and the system was close to adsorption equilibrium. A slight desorption of MER was also observed at this temperature. This behavior may be related to the weakening of physicochemical interactions between MER and the catalyst surface at higher temperatures. Since 25 °C provides nearly the same performance as 40 °C, it was selected as the optimal temperature. In addition, operating at 25 °C avoids thermally induced desorption and does not require external heating, making the process more practical for subsequent photocatalytic experiments.

Influence of solution pH on the removal of MER was examined using the optimal catalyst dosage (50 mg), MER concentration (25 mg L−1), and temperature (25 °C) (Fig. 6d). MER is an amphoteric compound with two dissociation constants (pKa1 = 2.9 and pKa2 = 7.4). Therefore, MER exists predominantly in a positively charged form at pH values lower than 2.9, in a zwitterionic form between pH 2.9 and 7.4, and in a negatively charged form at pH values higher than 7.444. Removal efficiencies were high at all tested pH values: 96.9% (pH 5), 98.1% (pH 6.7), and 92.9% (pH 9). The highest performance was obtained at pH 6.7, which can be attributed to the strongly positive surface potential of the catalyst (+ 10.4 mV) that enhances electrostatic attraction with the dipolar form of MER. At pH 5, although the catalyst surface exhibits a slightly negative potential (− 6.6 mV), MER remains mainly in its zwitterionic form, which allows both electrostatic attraction (via the positively charged moieties of the zwitterionic MER) and non-electrostatic interactions, even though the catalyst surface exhibits a slightly negative potential. This explains the still very high removal efficiency observed at this pH. At pH 9, where MER carry a negative charge and the catalyst surface potential decreases to (+ 5.6 mV), electrostatic attraction becomes weaker, leading to a slight reduction in removal efficiency, although the catalyst still maintains considerable photocatalytic activity. Outside the range of pH 5–9, MER undergoes spontaneous degradation in highly acidic or alkaline environments, making it unsuitable for reliable evaluation of the catalyst’s performance45,46.

Kinetic studies

The removal kinetics of MER over Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH were analyzed under dark adsorption and light-driven photocatalytic conditions using pseudo-first-order (PFO) and pseudo-second-order (PSO) models. During the dark adsorption stage, the PSO model provided a superior fit with a higher coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.99125) compared to the PFO model (R2 = 0.94062) (Fig. 7a, b). The corresponding rate constants were k1 = 0.02361 min−1 and k2 = 0.00172 L mg−1 min−1 (Fig. 7a, b).

Removal kinetics of MER using Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH: (a) PFO adsorption model; (b) PSO adsorption model; (c) PFO photodegradation model; and (d) PSO photodegradation model.

Under light irradiation, MER degradation followed PFO kinetics more closely, yielding a higher R2 value (0.96942) than the PSO model (0.78611), consistent with a surface reaction governed by photogenerated reactive species at quasi-constant active site coverage (Fig. 7c, d). The corresponding rate constants were k1 = 0.01497 min−1 and k2 = 0.03346 L mg−1 min−1 (Fig. 7c, d). These results suggest a two-stage mechanism in which MER initially undergoes strong surface binding, followed by photocatalytic degradation obeying apparent first-order kinetics. Detailed kinetic models, equations, and fitting procedures are provided in the SIF document (Section S-3.3)7,43.

Adsorption study

Adsorption isotherms of MER on Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH were measured in darkness. Amongst the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin models, the Temkin isotherm provided the best fit (R2 = 0.97325) (Fig. 8c). This behavior suggests that the adsorption process is not governed by an ideal homogeneous surface. Instead, the layered structure of LDH provides closely spaced active sites where adsorbate–adsorbate interactions become significant. As the surface coverage increases, repulsive or competitive interactions between MER molecules lead to a gradual decrease in adsorption energy, which is consistent with the fundamental assumption of the Temkin model. The deviation from the Langmuir model indicates that the assumption of uniform adsorption energy and negligible intermolecular interactions is not valid in this system. Although the Freundlich model accounts for surface heterogeneity, it does not explicitly consider interaction‑induced variations in adsorption energy. This effect is more appropriately described by the Temkin model. Such behavior is consistent with the physicochemical characteristics of LDH materials, which typically exhibit a distribution of energetically interacting adsorption sites rather than perfectly uniform or highly heterogeneous surfaces. Therefore, the adsorption performance of the LDH is influenced by both the density of available active sites and the interaction‑induced variation in adsorption energy47–50. Degradation mechanism and pathway

MER was photocatalytically degraded using Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH under optimized conditions, and HPLC–MS/MS was used to characterize the intermediates. Samples were collected at particular time points. In this study, the complete analytical methodology is presented in the SIF (Section S-4). Three samples were examined: Sample 1 (light irradiation for 15 min), Sample 2 (light irradiation for 90 min), and Sample 3 (light irradiation for 180 min). HPLC–MS/MS data for these the findings indicate the pathway of MER degradation on Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH. Representative chromatograms and spectra appear in Fig. S3–S5 of the SIF. A summary of intermediate m/z values and proposed structures is presented in Table S2. Key results are illustrated in Fig. 9. It is reiterated that, the degradation pathway proposed in this study is more comprehensive than those previously reported. Earlier investigations identified only partial segments of the MER degradation route. For example, earlier mechanisms proposed51, detected several intermediates related to the pathway A, while another study52 reported additional fragments of the pathway B. However, these studies did not present a continuous or integrated degradation sequence. In contrast, the present work reveals a modified and more coherent pathway. The data show that hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH) play a more dominant role than in earlier mechanisms proposed by the authors15. Moreover, several additional intermediates were identified in this study. These species clarify the stepwise transformation of MER and provide a more complete description of the photocatalytic degradation mechanism.

Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH: illustration of the plausible photocatalytic degradation pathways of MER.

Interestingly, the diagnostic MER ion at m/z 384.1 is absent in the final spectrum (Fig. S5) in the present mechanism. Its disappearance by 180 min indicates an efficient photocatalytic transformation of MER into smaller intermediates and progressively converging into simpler and less toxic species. These observations support the mechanistic sequence proposed based on the HPLC–MS/MS identifications.

In pathway A, the carboxyl group is first attacked via hydroxyl radicals and photogenerated holes (h+)53. Fragments with m/z values of 340.3 and 357 are produced by these reactions, respectively. The breaking of C–N and C–C bonds can be attributed to various reactive oxygen species. Through an ene-type addition, singlet oxygen (1O2) interacts with C=C bonds, forming unstable peroxide intermediates which rapidly break down, leading to bond cleavage54,55. C–C bonds are cleaved by hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH), peptide C–N bonds are broken via α-hydrogen abstraction, and oxidative fragmentation proceeds through a dioxetane-mediated pathway. Superoxide radicals (⋅O2⁻), generated electrochemically, further aid in selectively cleaving C–C bonds in acyl groups, leaving amino functionalities untouched56,57. Subsequently, the β-lactam ring undergoes cleavage mediated by hydroxyl radicals, photogenerated holes, singlet oxygen, and superoxide, at m/z 254.1, an intermediate was observed58. Oxidation at the sulfur atom—potentially mediated by all four reactive species—together with radical attack at the pyrrolidine nitrogen yields fragments at m/z 207.2 and 17554. Further radical and oxygen-driven transformations at the carbon adjacent to sulfur and within the pyrrolidine ring generate fragments at m/z 157.6, 142.8, and 140.9.

In pathway B, the β-lactam C–N bond of MER is attacked by hydroxyl radicals, while photogenerated holes target the carboxyl group first, yielding an intermediate at m/z 374.5.

In parallel, the β-lactam ring is targeted via hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, and superoxide, while the C–S bond—similar to pathway A—is cleaved, producing a fragment at m/z 130.7.

Subsequently, the intermediate at m/z 374.5 undergoes further oxidation at the carboxyl group via hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, and superoxide, together with C–S bond cleavage, leading to a secondary intermediate at m/z 173.2. Subsequently, this intermediate proceeds through two parallel routes. In the first route, the C–C bond is attacked by singlet oxygen, hydroxyl radicals, and superoxide, leading to an intermediate at m/z 159.2. Further oxidation at the pyrroline nitrogen produces another intermediate at m/z 175. In the alternative route, the C–S bond is cleaved first, yielding an intermediate at m/z 140.1. This is followed by radical attack on the methyl group attached to the pyrroline ring, resulting in a fragment at m/z 126.7.

The observed reactions primarily target the bonds with the lowest bond strength within the MER framework and are in agreement with earlier studies15. In both degradation pathways, the subsequent transformations involve oxidation, dehydrogenation, and demethylation, ring cleavage, and conversion of C–OH groups to C=O functionalities, as well as fragmentation of methyl, carboxyl substituents, hydroxyl, possible amidation, and subsequent hydroxylation. Ultimately, progressive attacks by reactive oxygen species and photogenerated holes lead to complete conversion of intermediates into carbon dioxide and water, accompanied by transient generation and oxidation of low-molecular-weight carboxylic acids. The overall degradation route is depicted in Fig. 9a, highlighting the strong efficiency of Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH in achieving complete breakdown of pharmaceutical contaminants without leaving stable by-products43.

To clarify the photodegradation mechanism of MER using Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH, radical-trapping experiments were carried out. The scavengers used in the experiments included isopropanol, which acts as a hydroxyl radical (⋅OH) scavenger; ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), serving as a photogenerated hole (h⁺) scavenger; Sodium azide (NaN3), targeting singlet oxygen (1O2); using isopropanol (IPA) as a (⋅OH) scavenger, and 1,4-benzoquinone (BQ), which captures superoxide radicals (⋅O2−), as illustrated in Fig. 10, suppression of photocatalytic activity by EDTA was negligible, indicating that photogenerated holes do not play a major role. The addition of NaN3, BQ, and IPA, in contrast, substantially lowered the degradation efficiency; NaN3 produced the strongest inhibition, while BQ and IPA exhibited similarly high but slightly lower effects. These results indicate that singlet oxygen is the principal reactive species in the Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH-mediated photodegradation of MER, with hydroxyl and superoxide radicals contributing comparably but to a slightly lesser extent, and photogenerated holes contributing minimally. These findings are corroborated via HPLC–MS/MS analysis.

Evaluation of the inhibitory influence of various scavengers on MER degradation performance over Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH within the optimized conditions in the present work.

TOC removal analysis

To evaluate the mineralization efficiency of the photocatalytic process, the TOC removal was measured under optimal conditions. As shown in Fig. S6, a TOC removal efficiency of 62.9% was achieved after 180 min, indicating a substantial conversion of MER into smaller organic intermediates and CO2/H2O. Further details and in-depth discussions regarding the mineralization mechanism and intermediates are provided in the SIF document (Section S-5: TOC Removal Analysis).

Reusability studies

Over five successive adsorption–photocatalytic cycles, the reusability and structural stability of Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH in MER removal were examined. Removal efficiency decreased slightly from 98.1% in cycle 1 to 93.4% in cycle 5 (percent removal per cycle is shown in Fig. 11a). After each cycle, the catalyst was recovered by centrifugation, thoroughly washed, and dried prior to reuse. Each washing and drying step resulted in an approximate 8% loss of catalyst mass. To maintain a constant active loading without adding fresh material, an auxiliary parallel reactor—run under identical conditions—was used as a reservoir. Following each cycle, an equivalent portion of the recovered catalyst was introduced from a parallel reactor to replace the lost catalyst. This procedure preserved the target catalyst loading throughout the recycling tests. FTIR and XRD analyses indicated that the catalyst’s bulk structure and main functional groups remained unchanged after five cycles (spectra and diffractograms are shown in Fig. 11b and c, respectively). Optical emission spectroscopy using inductively coupled plasma (ICP-OES) measurements of acid-digested samples quantified metal leaching after the fifth cycle. Chromium decreased from 30,410 mg L−1 (fresh) to 27,340 mg L−1 (used). Iron decreased from 47,350 mg L−1 (fresh) to 36,740 mg L−1 (used). A hot filtration test performed under reaction conditions showed that the filtrate remained completely inactive after catalyst removal, confirming that the dissolved species do not contribute to catalysis. Moreover; consistent with ICP‑OES measurements, Fe exhibited a noticeably higher degree of leaching compared to Cr. Nonetheless, the absence of any catalytic activity in the filtrate indicates that neither the Fe‑ nor Cr‑containing species released into solution act as active homogeneous catalysts. Furthermore, the leaching of Ni- and Cr-containing species requires careful consideration due to their environmental relevance. While the solid catalyst remains the primary active phase, future efforts should focus upon enhancing the structural stability of the LDH to minimize metal loss and ensure compliance with green chemistry principles. Overall, the prepared Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH demonstrated a robust chemical and structural stability as well as; retained high catalytic activity over repeated cycles. However, it should be noted that, to maintain a constant catalyst loading and ensure consistent experimental conditions throughout the cycling experiments, the catalyst loss was compensated using material taken from parallel reactors operated simultaneously under identical conditions, whose catalysts had undergone the same treatment and reaction history. A stability comparison with other catalysts is provided in Table S1, where Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH shows superior durability. As an efficient and environmentally friendly adsorption–photocatalytic system for pharmaceutical wastewater treatment, the material’s practical applicability is confirmed by these results.

Conclusions

In this contribution a newly developed Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH composite for combined dark-phase adsorption and visible-light-driven photocatalytic degradation of MER was developed. This material exhibited a low bandgap, low electrical resistance, as well as strong light absorption capabilities. Moreover, under optimal conditions, this composite exhibited a removal efficiency of 98.1% for MER. Under identical conditions, Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH outperformed all other precursors of recently published materials it was compared with. Furthermore, the effects of catalyst amount, MER concentration, pH, and reaction temperature were systematically investigated. The composite showed good performances in both adsorption and degradation of the MER species. The adsorption process conformed to a PSO kinetic model and fitted the Temkin isotherm. Meanwhile, photocatalytic degradation followed PFO kinetics. HPLC–MS/MS analysis confirmed a feasible degradation pathway, and experiments involving radical trapping demonstrated that singlet oxygen, hydroxyl radicals, and superoxide radicals were the dominant reactive species with comparable contributions. In other words, singlet oxygen had the largest contribution, although differences with hydroxyl- and superoxide- radicals were rather small. In addition, the composite retained a rather high activity through five successive adsorption–photocatalytic cycles, with only slight activity decrease and insignificant metal leaching. Overall, the Ni-[MCr/MFe] LDH composite offers an effective route for antibiotic removal from wastewater by integrating adsorption with visible-light photocatalysis. This approach paves the way for further optimization and scale-up. Nevertheless, although the HF-assisted synthesis of MIL-101(Cr) enabled high-quality materials with desirable structural properties, future efforts should focus upon developing HF-free or greener synthetic routes to improve environmental compatibility and industrial scalability.
