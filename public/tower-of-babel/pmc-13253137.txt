Selenium-enriched strawberry–sea buckthorn–yam juice fermented by Weissella cibaria and Pediococcus acidilactici: antioxidant, flavor, and immunomodulatory effects
Authors: He Xu, Chunlin Ban, Dezhen Wang, Qingdong Wang, Zhenlong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A novel selenium-enriched fruit and vegetable juice (SFVJ) was produced through the co-fermentation of sea buckthorn, strawberry, and Chinese yam juices using Weissella cibaria DSE605 and Pediococcus acidilactici DSE72. Fermentation facilitated the conversion of inorganic selenium into organic forms, significantly decreasing selenite content, while enhancing antioxidant capacity, increasing organic acid levels, and generating 36 volatile compounds that contributed to improved flavor. In a cyclophosphamide-induced immunosuppressed mouse model, SFVJ significantly improved immune organ indices, hematological profiles, hepatic antioxidant enzyme activities, and cytokine and immunoglobulin levels, and mitigated hepatic and colonic damage. Moreover, 16S rRNA sequencing analysis demonstrated that SFVJ alleviated gut microbiota dysbiosis, partially restoring the microbial community structure toward that observed in the normal control group. These results suggest that SFVJ has considerable potential as a functional beverage with antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties.

Introduction

Selenium is an essential trace element that plays a critical role in maintaining redox balance, supporting immune function, and preserving overall physiological homeostasis (Kieliszek & Blazejak, 2013). Compared with inorganic selenium compounds, organically bound selenium and selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) show greater bioavailability and substantially lower toxicity, making them more appropriate for nutritional supplementation (Thiry, Ruttens, De Temmerman, Schneider, & Pussemier, 2012). Growing evidence suggests that certain lactic acid bacteria (LAB) can biotransform inorganic selenium salts into organic selenium species and SeNPs during fermentation. Martínez et al. demonstrated the conversion of Na2SeO3 into nano‑selenium using LAB, and both fruit and milk juices have been successfully fermented using selenium-enriched LAB cultures (Martínez, Cuencas Barrientos, Mozzi, & Pescuma, 2019). Yang et al. further reported the formation of nanoscale selenium particles on the surface of Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG following co-cultivation with Na2SeO3 (Yang et al., 2025). Comparable selenium biotransformation has also been observed in selenium-enriched Lactobacillus plantarum, accompanied by improved antioxidant properties and enhanced flavor characteristics in fermented products (Wang et al., 2021).

In recent years, the diversification of consumer preferences for beverages has stimulated the development of functional drinks enriched with specific bioactive compounds and probiotics. Fruit and vegetable juices represent excellent substrates for LAB fermentation because they are naturally rich in phenolics, vitamins, organic acids, and other bioactive constituents (de Godoy Alves Filho et al., 2017). Fermentation has been shown to improve the nutritional value, antioxidant capacity, and sensory qualities of various fruit-based beverages. For example, bergamot juice fermented with mixed LAB cultures showed greater nutritional value and antioxidant activity than fermentation with a single culture (Hashemi & Jafarpour, 2020). Similarly, co-fermentation of cherry silverberry juice with Lactobacillus plantarum and L. casei resulted in greater biological activity than fermentation with either strain alone (Lizardo, Cho, Won, & Seo, 2020). These findings indicate that mixed-strain fermentation may produce synergistic effects that further enhance the functional properties of fruit-based beverages. Sea buckthorn, strawberries, and Chinese yams are all rich in bioactive compounds and offer significant nutritional benefits. Sea buckthorn is particularly recognized for its strong antioxidant activity, and clinical studies have linked its consumption with improved mucosal health (Markkinen, Laaksonen, & Yang, 2021). Strawberries, valued for their sweetness and juiciness, contain amine compounds with potential therapeutic effects in leukemia and aplastic anemia (Giampieri et al., 2012). Chinese yam, meanwhile, is rich in polysaccharides that have been reported to enhance immune function and regulate intestinal motility (Cui et al., 2023). Despite their complementary nutritional and bioactive characteristics, these three plant materials have rarely been combined as fermentation substrates, and limited information is available regarding their application in selenium-enriched co-fermentation systems.

Beyond their nutritional attributes, selenium-enriched fermented foods have attracted increasing attention due to their potential biological functions, particularly antioxidant and immunomodulatory effects. Cyclophosphamide (Cy)-induced immunosuppression is widely used as an in vivo model to evaluate the immune-regulating activity of functional foods (Wen et al., 2020). However, studies that integrate LAB-mediated selenium biotransformation, multi-fruit and vegetable co-fermentation, and immunomodulatory evaluation in animal models remain limited. In particular, no previous investigation has examined selenium-enriched fruit and vegetable juice fermented with Weissella cibaria and Pediococcus acidilactici, two LAB strains known for their probiotic potential and versatile fermentation capabilities.

Therefore, this study aimed to:(i)develop a selenium-enriched fruit and vegetable juice through the co-fermentation of sea buckthorn, strawberry, and Chinese yam using W. cibaria DSE605 and P. acidilactici DSE72;(ii)systematically characterize the selenium content and speciation, along with the physicochemical properties, antioxidant activity, organic acid composition, and volatile compound profiles of the fermented juice;(iii)evaluate its protective effects against Cy-induced immunosuppression and related alterations in gut microbiota in mice.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh strawberries (Fragaria × ananassa) and Chinese yams (Dioscorea opposita Thunb., cv. Tiegun) were purchased from a local market in Zhengzhou, Henan Province, China. Sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides L.) was collected from wild sea buckthorn plants in Lvliang, Shanxi Province, China. The bacterial strains W. cibaria DSE605 and P. acidilactici DSE72 were provided by the Laboratory of Multi-functional Feed Research and Development, College of Life Sciences, Zhengzhou University.

In this study, MRS broth (de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe) served as the basal culture medium, and a solid medium was prepared by supplementing it with 2% (w/v) agar. Na2SeO3 was obtained from Shanghai Tairuire Bioscience Co., Ltd. The reagents 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), and 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Cyclophosphamide was supplied by Merck (Shanghai, China).

Strains and fermented SFVJ

Previously, fourteen bacterial strains were isolated from selenium-rich plant soil. Among them, two strains demonstrating strong selenium-enrichment capacity, W. cibaria DSE605 and P. acidilactici DSE72, were selected for further study (Fig.A1). Single colonies of each strain were inoculated into MRS broth and incubated at 37 °C until the cultures reached the post-logarithmic growth phase. The cell pellets of W. cibaria DSE605 and P. acidilactici DSE72 were then harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C (5000 ×g, 10 min) and washed twice with 0.9% sterile saline before inoculation.

To optimize the fermentation conditions, single-factor experiments combined with response surface methodology were conducted. The raw materials were weighed in a 1:1:1:2 ratio (strawberry: sea buckthorn: Chinese yam: water) and processed using a juicer (Table.A1). The resulting juice was filtered, its pH adjusted to 6.0 using saturated NaHCO3, and then pasteurized at 70 °C for 30 min. After cooling, 4% (v/v) of a mixed starter culture (W. cibaria DSE605 and P. acidilactici DSE72 = 1:2; Fig.A2) was inoculated into the juice supplemented with 4 μg/mL Na2SeO3. The fermentation was subsequently carried out for 10 h at 35 °C in an anaerobic incubator (Fig.A3,Table.A2, Table.A3, Table.A4).

Determination of total selenium content and se species

Total selenium content was measured using hydride generation atomic fluorescence spectrometry (HG-AFS). Briefly, 2.00 mL of the sample was transferred into a 50 mL digestion flask, followed by the addition of 20 mL HNO3 and 1 mL HClO₄. The flask was covered with a watch glass and heated at 150 °C for 2 h, after which the mixture was left to stand overnight. The sample was then further heated at 180 °C until white fumes were observed and the solution turned clear, indicating complete digestion. After cooling, 5 mL of HCl (1 + 1) was added to the sample to prepare it for HG-AFS determination. The solution was gently evaporated, allowed to cool, and finally diluted to 10 mL with deionized water before analysis.

Selenium speciation was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (HPLC–ICP–MS). Liquid samples were first filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter and then directly injected into the HPLC system without prior digestion. Chromatographic separation was carried out using a Hamilton PRP-X100 analytical column (250 mm × 4.1 mm, 10 μm) equipped with a PRP-X100 guard column (20 mm × 2.1 mm, 10 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 1.05 g citric acid monohydrate, 20 mL methanol, and water to 1000 mL, with the pH adjusted to 5.3 using 20% ammonia solution. The flow rate was maintained at 1.0 mL/min, and the injection volume was set at 100 μL.

The ICP–MS operating parameters were as follows: RF power of 1400 W, nebulizer gas flow rate of 1.05 L min−1, and helium gas flow rate of 1.0 L min−1. Quantification was performed by integrating peak areas while monitoring the 77Se isotope. The selenium species analyzed included selenocystine (SeCys₂), methylselenocysteine (MeSeCys), selenite (Se IV), selenomethionine (SeMet), and selenate (Se VI).

SFVJ physicochemical analysis

Viable cell counts and pH

The total viable cell count was determined using the standard serial decimal dilution method. The plates were incubated in an inverted position at 37 °C for 48 h in an anaerobic incubator. Only plates containing 30–300 colonies were selected for enumeration. The pH of the juice samples was measured using a digital pH meter (PHS-3E, Shanghai, China).

Determination of reducing sugar and ascorbic acid

The reducing sugar content was determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method (Li et al., 2018). The ascorbic acid content was measured according to a previously reported method (Silva & Sirasa, 2018), with minor modifications. Briefly, 5 mL of a standard ascorbic acid solution was transferred into a 25 mL conical flask, followed by the addition of 10 mL of 20 g/L oxalic acid solution. The mixture was thoroughly mixed and titrated with 2,6-dichloroindophenol sodium solution until a faint pink color appeared and remained stable for 15 s, which was taken as the titration endpoint. A blank determination was conducted using 10 mL of 20 g/L oxalic acid solution under the same experimental conditions.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC)

The TPC was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method with slight modifications (Kwaw et al., 2018). Briefly, 1 mL of sample was mixed with 1 mL of freshly prepared Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (1:1, v/v). After standing for 5 min, 2 mL of sodium carbonate solution (75 g/L) was added, and the mixture was thoroughly vortexed. The reaction mixture was then incubated at room temperature in the dark for 40 min. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. TPC was expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per milliliter of sample (mg GAE/mL).

The TFC was determined using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method with slight modifications (Kwaw et al., 2018). Briefly, 1 mL of sample solution was placed in a test tube and mixed with 0.15 mL of 5% NaNO2 solution. After standing for 6 min, 0.15 mL of 10% AlCl3 solution was added, and the mixture was allowed to react for 5 min. Subsequently, 0.4 mL of 1 mol/L NaOH solution was added, and the mixture was thoroughly mixed. After centrifugation and filtration, absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. TFC was expressed as milligrams of rutin equivalents per milliliter of sample (mg RE/mL).

Determination of antioxidant activity

The DPPH radical-scavenging activity of the samples was evaluated using a previously reported method (Yang et al., 2018) with slight modifications. Briefly, 2 mL of freshly prepared 0.2 mmol/L DPPH solution in anhydrous ethanol was mixed with 2 mL of the sample, and the mixture was thoroughly homogenized. The mixture was then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 20 min. After incubation, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The DPPH radical-scavenging activity was expressed as a percentage (%) and calculated using the following formula:DPPH clearance rate=1−A1−A2A0×100%where A0 is the absorbance of 2 mL of ultrapure water and 2 mL of DPPH solution, A1 is the absorbance of the 2 mL sample and 2 mL DPPH solution, and A2 is the absorbance of 2 mL of the sample and 2 mL anhydrous ethanol solution.

ABTS, Hydroxyl radical-scavenging activity, and Fe3 + −reducing power of the sample were determined according to the protocol previously described by Tang (Tang et al., 2020).

HPLC analysis of organic acids

The organic acid content was determined using an HPLC system equipped with a CAPECELL PAK MG S5 C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). Extraction and determination of organic acids were performed according to the method described by Li, with minor modifications (Li et al., 2014). Before analysis, both samples and standard working solutions were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter.

Chromatographic separation was performed using a mobile phase of 0.1 mg/mL phosphoric acid and methanol (97.5:2.5, v/v), with a total elution time of 10 min at 40 °C. The detection wavelength was set at 210 nm, and the injection volume was 20 μL. A series of standard working solutions was analyzed under the same conditions, and the corresponding peak areas were used to establish standard calibration curves.

HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis

Volatile compounds were analyzed using an HS-SPME/GC–MS system according to the method described by Wang (Wang et al., 2022), with minor modifications. Briefly, 4.5 mL of sample and 1.5 g of NaCl were added to a 10 mL headspace vial, which was then equilibrated at 40 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, the conditioned SPME fiber was inserted into the vial and exposed to the sample headspace for 30 min to allow adsorption of volatile compounds. The extraction fiber was then removed and immediately introduced into the injection port of a 7000E GC/TQ gas chromatograph for thermal desorption at 250 °C for 5 min before analysis. Quantification was carried out using 10 μL of cyclohexanone (4.75 μg/mL in anhydrous ethanol) as the internal standard.

Chromatographic separation was performed using a DB-5 capillary column (30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm; Agilent 7000E GC/TQ). High-purity helium (99.999%) served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.5 mL/min. The oven temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature of 40 °C held for 2 min, increased to 180 °C at 5 °C/min and maintained for 1 min, then further raised to 250 °C at 10 °C/min and held for 2 min. The injection port temperature was maintained at 250 °C, and splitless injection was used.

Animal experiment and its parameter analysis

Animal and experimental design

Six-week-old male specific pathogen-free (SPF) BALB/c mice (20.0 ± 2.0 g) were obtained from Henan Skobes Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanyang, China). All animal procedures were approved by the College of Life Sciences, Zhengzhou University (Approval No. 2026–08), and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The mice were housed in a rodent facility with controlled environmental conditions, including a temperature of 24–26 °C, relative humidity of 50%–70%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. Animals had free access to a standard diet and water. Following a 7-day acclimatization period, the formal experiment was initiated.

The mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 9 per group): normal control (NC), model (M), probiotic fruit and vegetable juice (LAB), low-dose selenium-enriched fruit and vegetable juice (L-SFVJ), and high-dose selenium-enriched fruit and vegetable juice (H-SFVJ). During the 17-day experimental period, all mice had unrestricted access to food and water. From day 1 to day 14, mice in the NC and M groups were gavaged daily with an equivalent volume of distilled water. The LAB group received the same volume of fruit and vegetable juice fermented with W. cibaria DSE605 and P. acidilactici DSE72. The L-SFVJ and H-SFVJ groups were orally administered selenium-enriched fruit and vegetable juice containing 3 μg/kg bw and 12 μg/kg bw, respectively. Based on body surface area–based dose conversion, these selenium doses correspond to approximately 17 and 68 μg/day for a 70-kg adult human, which falls within the recommended range of nutritional selenium intake.

From day 15 to day 17, mice in the NC group received intraperitoneal injections of normal saline. In comparison, mice in the remaining groups were injected with cyclophosphamide (Cy, 80 mg/kg) to induce immunosuppression. After a 12-h fasting period, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Blood, liver, thymus, spleen, colon, and cecal contents were then collected for subsequent analyses.

Determination of weight, thymus, and spleen indexes

The body weight, thymus, and spleen of the mice were accurately measured. The thymus (or spleen) index was calculated as the ratio of thymus (or spleen) weight to body weight of the mice (mg/g).

Analysis of blood index

Whole blood samples were collected from the mice using the orbital (eyeball) blood collection method. A 20 μL aliquot of blood was immediately diluted to 240 μL with an anticoagulant-containing buffer. The levels of white blood cells, red blood cells, hemoglobin, and platelets were then measured using an automated hematology analyzer.

Determination of liver antioxidant activity

The activities of MDA, SOD, and GSH-Px in the liver were determined using a kit from Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Bioengineering.

Determination of serum immunoglobulin and inflammatory factors

Retro-orbital blood samples were centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C to obtain the serum. The serum concentrations of IL-2, IL-6, IgG, and IgM were determined using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions (Shanghai Enzyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Determination of the intestinal IgA (SIgA)

The colon was quickly rinsed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 20 s and then centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. The level of SIgA secretion in the colon was determined using an ELISA kit (Shanghai Enzyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Histopathologic examination

The colon tissues were rinsed with cold PBS and fixed in 4% neutral paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The samples were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histopathological changes were subsequently examined using an Olympus confocal microscope.

16S rRNA analysis of gut microbiota

Fresh fecal samples were aseptically collected from the mice and immediately stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Total genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial DNA extraction kit in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and integrity of the extracted DNA were assessed before sequencing. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified by PCR using specific primers. The resulting PCR products were purified and quantified, then used to construct sequencing libraries. High-throughput sequencing was carried out on the Illumina MiSeq platform (PE300) by Shanghai BIOZERON Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Raw sequencing data were processed using QIIME2 for quality control, sequence denoising, and taxonomic annotation. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% sequence similarity, and taxonomic identification was carried out using the SILVA database. Alpha diversity indices were calculated to assess microbial diversity, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate differences in microbial community structure among groups. Differentially abundant taxa were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) with an LDA score threshold of 2.0.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0. Data normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. Differences among groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. A p-value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Total selenium content and speciation

The capacity of Weissella cibaria DSE605 and Pediococcus acidilactici DSE72 to accumulate and biotransform inorganic selenium during SFVJ fermentation was evaluated (Table 1). The total selenium content in SFVJ reached 1828.83 μg/L, which was comparable to that of the Na2SeO3 control group (1809.23 μg/L), indicating that selenium was largely preserved throughout the fermentation process. However, selenium speciation differed significantly between the two groups. In the Na2SeO3 control, selenium was predominantly present as selenite (Se4+, 341.50 μg/L), and no organic selenium species were detected. Although the detected concentration of SeCys₂ was relatively low, the high total selenium content suggests that a considerable fraction of selenium may exist in other forms that were not quantified in this analysis. In preliminary experiments, a characteristic red precipitate formed when the two strains were cultured in Na2SeO3-supplemented MRS medium, a widely recognized visual indicator of elemental selenium nanoparticle (SeNP) formation (Li, Wang, Dai, & Li, 2022). Previous studies have reported that lactic acid bacteria can convert inorganic selenium into multiple forms, including selenoamino acids and SeNPs (Alzate, Fernández-Fernández, Pérez-Conde, Gutiérrez, & Cámara, 2008; Spyridopoulou et al., 2021), some of which may not be fully detected by conventional speciation methods.

The disappearance of Se4+ and the emergence of organic selenium species indicate that inorganic selenium underwent biotransformation during co-fermentation. This conversion is likely associated with enhanced nutritional value, as organic and reduced selenium forms generally show greater bioavailability and lower toxicity compared with inorganic selenium (Cai, Su, Chen, & Zheng, 2022). The elimination of Se4+, considered the most toxic inorganic selenium species, further suggests improved safety of the final fermented product.

In SFVJ, the total viable cell count reached 8.79 log CFU/mL, significantly exceeding 8 log CFU/mL, a level commonly regarded as sufficient to provide probiotic benefits and support consumer health. Reducing sugar content ascorbic acid content

The reducing sugar content in the SFVJ group was significantly lower than that in the other groups (Fig. 1B, p < 0.05), suggesting enhanced carbohydrate utilization during fermentation. This decrease likely reflects increased microbial metabolic activity, resulting in the conversion of sugars into lactic acid and other fermentation products. Moreover, the presence of selenium during fermentation may influence microbial metabolism, potentially contributing to the observed differences in sugar consumption. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies (Liu et al., 2026).

Among all groups, the SFVJ group showed the highest ascorbic acid content (Fig. 1C). Selenium plays an important role in cellular redox regulation through selenoproteins, such as thioredoxin reductase, which has been reported to facilitate the reduction of ascorbate (Hondal, 2023). This mechanism may partly account for the elevated ascorbic acid levels observed in the selenium-enriched fermented juice.

TPC and TFC during SFVJ fermentation

Meanwhile, compared with the CK group, the TFC increased in both the LAB and SFVJ groups, while the TPC showed a significant increase in the Na2SeO3, LAB, and SFVJ groups following fermentation (p < 0.05; Fig. 1D–E). This enhancement may be attributed to the enzymatic activities and acid production of the fermenting strains, which promote the release of flavonoids and phenolic compounds from bound forms into more soluble free forms (Sharma, Sahota, & Kaur, 2021).

Antioxidant activity

Fruit and vegetable juices are not only abundant in vitamins, minerals, and dietary fiber, but also contain a variety of antioxidant compounds that play important roles in preventing chronic diseases and delaying aging. According to the results of the four antioxidant assays (Fig. 1F-I), both the LAB and SFVJ groups showed significantly higher in vitro antioxidant capacity compared with the control, with the SFVJ group showing the strongest activity. These findings suggest that fermentation plays a key role in enhancing the juice's antioxidant properties. Moreover, the greater antioxidant capacity observed in the SFVJ group may be related to the combined effects of selenium enrichment and fermentation-derived metabolites. Selenium is an essential component of antioxidant enzymes, such as GSH-Px, which may partly account for the enhanced antioxidant activity observed in SFVJ (Bardak et al., 2023).

Organic acids analysis in SFVJ

Flavor arises from the combined effects of volatile and non-volatile compounds and represents a key attribute of food that strongly influences consumer acceptance. Organic acids are among the most important bioactive components in fermented juices, contributing not only to sensory characteristics but also to product stability and preservation. For this reason, organic acids were selected as representative non-volatile compounds in this study. The results showed that, among all groups, the SFVJ group showed the highest lactic acid content and the lowest malic acid content (Table 2). Sugars are not the only carbon sources utilized by microorganisms; organic acids such as citric acid and malic acid can also serve as substrates for microbial metabolism (Xiong, Li, Guan, Peng, & Xie, 2014). Therefore, malic acid may have been converted into lactic acid during fermentation by W. cibaria DSE605 and P. acidilactici DSE72, which could reduce the sharp acidity of the juice and produce a smoother taste. Previous studies have reported that a higher proportion of lactic acid can improve the flavor characteristics of fermented vegetables (Trail, Fleming, Young, & McFeeters, 1996). Volatile compounds in SFVJ

A total of 36 volatile compounds were identified and quantified across all fermented juice samples, including 7 alcohols, 8 aldehydes and ketones, 7 esters, and 11 hydrocarbons. Approximately 25, 26, 29, and 32 volatile compounds were detected in the CK, Na2SeO3, LAB, and SFVJ groups, respectively. Alcohols were the predominant volatile compounds in the SFVJ group (Fig. 2 A1), which are generally associated with mild, sweet aromatic characteristics in fruit-based beverages (Yu et al., 2012). Linalool, known for its floral and citrus-like aroma, was present at the highest levels in the SFVJ and LAB groups. This naturally occurring compound also possesses anti-inflammatory properties (Lee, Wang, Li, & Liu, 2018). Esters are typically associated with sweet and fruity aromas in juices and are considered key contributors to flavor profiles (Petropulos et al., 2014). Compared with the LAB group, the ester content in the SFVJ group decreased slightly. The levels of ketones and aldehydes increased in both the LAB and SFVJ groups; however, their concentrations were significantly lower in the SFVJ group than in the LAB group (Fig. 2 A3). Previous studies have shown that selenium supplementation can influence cellular redox homeostasis, potentially affecting oxidative transformations, such as the conversion of alcohols into aldehydes or ketones, which contribute to the formation of volatile flavor compounds (Zhao et al., 2020). This mechanism may partly explain the relatively higher alcohol content observed in the SFVJ group.

Moreover, the SFVJ group showed the highest levels of hydrocarbons, with a particularly high concentration of d-limonene detected (Fig. 2B). In the other three groups, d-limonene was present only in trace amounts in the Na2SeO3 group and was absent in both the CK and LAB groups. The elevated d-limonene levels in the SFVJ group may be associated with alterations in microbial metabolic activity during selenium-enriched fermentation; however, the underlying biochemical mechanisms remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Effects of SFVJ on body weight, thymus, and spleen indexes of mice

Body weight is an important indicator for assessing the health status of mice. In this study, intraperitoneal administration of Cy significantly reduced body weight compared with the NC group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3A), confirming the successful establishment of the immunosuppression model. Administration of L-SFVJ and H-SFVJ effectively mitigated the Cy-induced weight loss. The primary toxic effect of Cy is immunosuppression, often manifested as atrophy of immune organs. The thymus and spleen are key immune organs that serve as important indicators of immune function (Zhu et al., 2019). In this study, Cy administration significantly reduced the thymus and spleen indices (Fig. 3B–C). However, compared with the M group, the thymus and spleen indices were significantly increased in the LAB, L-SFVJ, and H-SFVJ treatment groups (p < 0.05). Effects of SFVJ on the hematopoietic function of mice

Another adverse effect of Cy is the impairment of bone marrow hematopoietic function. Compared with the NC group, the M group showed significantly reduced levels of WBC, RBC, PLT, and HGB (Fig. 3D, p < 0.05). Treatment with H-SFVJ significantly alleviated the Cy-induced decline in peripheral blood cell counts (p < 0.05). These results suggest that H-SFVJ may help restore blood cell levels by reducing oxidative damage in the bone marrow and promoting the proliferation and differentiation of hematopoietic cells.

Effects of SFVJ on the liver oxidative stress markers

Cy-induced oxidative stress is known to impair liver function (Xu et al., 2023). To evaluate the protective effect of SFVJ on hepatic antioxidant capacity, the levels of MDA, SOD, and GSH-Px were measured. Compared with the NC group, the M group showed significantly increased MDA levels (Fig. 3E, p < 0.05), whereas treatment with LAB, L-SFVJ, and H-SFVJ significantly reduced MDA concentrations. In comparison, the activities of SOD and GSH-Px were significantly decreased in the M group but were significantly restored following intervention with LAB, L-SFVJ, and H-SFVJ (p < 0.05). These results indicate that SFVJ treatment effectively alleviates Cy-induced oxidative stress and enhances the liver's antioxidant defense capacity.

Effects of SFVJ on the levels of the immunoglobulins and cytokines of mice

Immunoglobulins and cytokines play critical roles in regulating immune responses. Therefore, the effects of SFVJ on the secretion of IgG, IgM, IL-2, and IL-6 were investigated. IgG and IgM are major immunoglobulins that contribute to humoral immunity and protect against a wide range of pathogens. IL-2 is an essential cytokine secreted by activated T cells and is required for the growth, proliferation, and differentiation of T cells into effector cells (Yu et al., 2014). IL-6, in comparison, is a pleiotropic cytokine that plays a central role in coordinating the immune defense network against infections (Rose-John, Winthrop, & Calabrese, 2017). Previous studies have reported that Cy treatment significantly reduces immunoglobulin and cytokine levels (Song et al., 2023), which is in good agreement with our observations. In this study, the IgM level in the M group was significantly lower than that in the NC group (p < 0.05). However, treatment with L-SFVJ significantly restored IgM levels (Fig. 4A, p < 0.05). Although Cy treatment significantly decreased serum IgM levels, it had no significant effect on IgG levels (Fig. 4B), suggesting that Cy-induced immunosuppression may differentially affect various immune cell populations and immunoglobulin types.Fig. 4Effects of SFVJ on (A) IgG, (B) IgM, (C) IL-2, and (D) IL-6 levels in mouse serum. Cytokines are key mediators of immune regulation. IL-2 levels were significantly reduced in the M group compared with the NC group (p < 0.05), whereas treatment with LAB, L-SFVJ, and H-SFVJ significantly reversed this decrease (Fig. 4C, p < 0.05). Unlike some previous reports, results of this study showed a significant increase in IL-6 levels in the M group following Cy treatment compared with the NC group (p < 0.05). Moreover, although IL-6 levels were further elevated after LAB, L-SFVJ, and H-SFVJ administration, no significant differences were observed compared with the M group (Fig. 4D). Several studies have also reported a significant increase in IL-6 levels following cyclophosphamide treatment (Wróbel et al., 2021). IL-6 is widely recognized as a pleiotropic cytokine that can exert either pro- or anti-inflammatory effects depending on the physiological context (Turano, Cammarota, Duraturo, Izzo, & De Rosa, 2021). Based on these observations, it is speculated that the elevated IL-6 levels in the LAB, L-SFVJ, and H-SFVJ groups may contribute to anti-inflammatory responses. IL-6 has been shown to play an important role in leukocyte trafficking and in the transition from neutrophil- to monocyte-dominated infiltration at sites of inflammation. When inflammation or tissue damage occurs due to infection or injury, IL-6 synthesis is rapidly induced, contributing to host defense by stimulating acute-phase immune responses and hematopoiesis (Tanaka & Kishimoto, 2014). These results provide further insight into the biochemical transformations occurring during selenium-enriched fermentation. They may help clarify the functional mechanisms underlying the immunomodulatory effects of selenium-enriched fruit and vegetable juices.

Effects of SFVJ on the secretion of SIgA in intestinal mucosa

Secretory immunoglobulin A (SIgA) is a first-line protective antibody on the surface of the intestinal mucosa. It often protects the vulnerable intestinal mucosa by neutralizing potential pathogens and harmful bacteria (Zuo et al., 2014). The level of mucosal SIgA was significantly reduced in Cy-treated mice compared to the NC group (p < 0.05). L-SFVJ and H-SFVJ interventions significantly increased mucosal SIgA levels in cyclophosphamide-treated mice (Fig. 5, p < 0.05). Hepatic histopathological changes were also examined using H&E staining. In the NC group, the liver showed a clear and intact hepatic lobular structure, with hepatocytes arranged radially around the central vein and displaying normal morphology. In comparison, the M group showed mild hepatic injury, including inflammatory cell infiltration, vacuolar hepatocyte degeneration, and occasional nuclear loss. At the phylum level, the dominant bacterial phyla included Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Thermodesulfobacteriota, and Campylobacterota (Fig. 7B). Cy administration significantly altered gut microbial structure, reducing the relative abundance of Bacillota (from 73% in NC to 57% in M) while increasing Bacteroidota (from 14% to 38%). SFVJ supplementation partially reversed these shifts, with Bacillota recovering to 66.2% and 66.5%. Bacteroidota decreased to 28.8% and 27% in the L-SFVJ and H-SFVJ groups, respectively. These changes indicate that SFVJ helped restore the Bacillota/Bacteroidota balance toward a profile more similar to that of healthy mice.

At the genus level, Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, Muribaculum, Blautia, CAG-873, Christensenellaceae_R_7_group, and Alistipes were predominant genera (Fig. 7C). Alistipes was significantly elevated in the M group, consistent with its reported association with inflammation and pathological conditions (Parker, Wearsch, Veloo, & Rodriguez-Palacios, 2020). Both L-SFVJ and H-SFVJ significantly reduced the abundance of Alistipes, suggesting a beneficial modulatory effect on Cy-induced dysbiosis.

β -Diversity was analyzed by PCA, a significant separation in gut microbiota composition between the NC and M groups (Fig. 7D), indicating substantial Cy-induced disruption of gut microbial composition. The microbiota profiles of L-SFVJ and H-SFVJ groups shifted closer to that of the NC group, demonstrating that SFVJ partially restored microbial community structure. Venn analysis showed 672 shared OTUs across all groups, with the M group having the fewest unique OTUs, further reflecting reduced microbial richness (Fig. 7E). SFVJ treatment increased the number of unique OTUs compared with the M group, supporting its role in enhancing microbial diversity.

LEfSe analysis was performed to identify differential gut microbiota among the experimental groups (Fig. 7F–G). The M group was characterized by significantly higher abundances of Eubacterium oxidoreductes_group, Butyricicoccaceae, and Clostridium methylpentosum_group (LDA > 2, p < 0.05). In comparison, L-SFVJ treatment significantly enriched Dehalobacterium, UCG_3, and Moraxellaceae, whereas H-SFVJ treatment increased the abundance of Butytibacter and Christensenellaceae_R_7_group (LDA > 2, p < 0.05). These differential taxa indicate that low- and high-dose SFVJ exert distinct modulatory effects on the gut microbiota. SFVJ alleviated Cy-induced dysbiosis by modulating key bacterial phyla, reducing the abundance of potentially harmful genera, and promoting a microbial community structure more similar to that observed in healthy mice.

Conclusion

This study developed a SFVJ through co-fermentation with Weissella cibaria DSE605 and Pediococcus acidilactici DSE72. The fermentation process preserved the overall selenium content while altering its speciation, as indicated by the disappearance of Se4+ and the formation of organic selenium and other reduced forms. In addition to selenium transformation, fermentation improved the antioxidant capacity and flavor characteristics of the juice through the generation of distinctive volatile compounds. Furthermore, in a cyclophosphamide-induced immunosuppressed mouse model, SFVJ supplementation significantly alleviated immune dysfunction, highlighting its potential as an effective functional matrix for selenium delivery. SFVJ offers a promising and palatable strategy for dietary selenium supplementation. Future research should aim to further characterize the various selenium species present, particularly nanoparticulate and protein-bound forms, and to clarify the mechanisms responsible for its immunomodulatory effects.
