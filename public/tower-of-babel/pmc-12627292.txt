Integration of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 methylation and AFP protein as plasma biomarker for hepatocellular carcinoma diagnosis
Authors: Haiyan Chen, Yingmin Luo, Linhong Li, Fei Xu, Xiaohuan Lai, Jinlong Li, Xiaomo Li, Wenjie Song, Yang Liu, Dengke Bao, Jiayun Liu, Guanlin Zhou, Shaogui Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly detection of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) represents a significant clinical challenge due to the lack of effective biomarkers. Previous studies have revealed abnormal hypermethylation of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 in HCC tissues, prompting further investigation into their utility as potential biomarkers for HCC diagnosis. In this study, we explored the clinical value of RASSF1A/TSPYL5 methylation alone and in combination with AFP protein as diagnostic biomarkers for early HCC detection.MethodsBioinformatics analysis using MethSurv, LinkedOmics, and Wanderer was conducted to assess the methylation levels of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 in HCC tissue samples from the public database. The methylation status of these two genes was validated in cell lines and plasma samples from HCC patients. Furthermore, the ARTHCC model, comprising the measurement of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 methylation and AFP protein, was developed and validated using plasma samples from HCC patients.ResultsOur comprehensive bioinformatic analysis revealed that TSPYL5 CpG sites were hypermethylated in HCC tissue samples, particularly among CpG island and shore regions. Approximately 40% of CpG sites of RASSF1A also exhibited hypermethylation. The hypermethylation status of TSPYL5 may be associated with portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT) in HCC patients. Combining analysis of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 methylation levels with AFP protein, the ARTHCC model demonstrates superior sensitivity and specificity for detecting HCC in both the discovery and validation cohorts. Notably, the ARTHCC model consistently exhibited robust performance across different subgroups.ConclusionsThis study provides compelling evidence that the combination of RASSF1A/TSPYL5 methylation and AFP protein is a promising biomarker panel for the early detection of HCC. The ARTHCC model demonstrates remarkable sensitivity and specificity, outperforming AFP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-025-06367-8.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Early detection, DNA methylation, Quantitative methylation-specific PCR, RASSF1A, TSPYL5, AFP

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022, Liver cancer ranks as the sixth most frequent cancer and is the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide (Bray et al. 2024). Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant form of primary liver cancer, accounting for 85–90% of cases (Ozakyol 2017). Despite recent advancements in HCC diagnosis and treatment, the overall 5-year survival rate for HCC patients in China remains dishearteningly low at 14.1% (Allemani et al. 2018). Clinical practice has shown that the overall 5-year survival rate of early-stage HCC patients receiving radical treatment is significantly improved, ranging from 69.0 to 86.2% (Tsilimigras et al. 2019). Currently, the primary reasons for the persistently low survival rates in HCC are the limited adoption of screening among high-risk populations and low rates of early diagnosis, with 70% to 80% of patients being diagnosed at intermediate to advanced stages (Wu and Qin 2013). Consequently, screening high-risk populations for HCC plays a pivotal role in facilitating early detection, diagnosis, and timely intervention, thereby improving survival rates for HCC patients.

The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) HCC diagnosis and treatment guidelines recommend semiannual screening with abdominal ultrasound (US) and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) testing, which exhibits a sensitivity of 63% for early-stage HCC detection (Singal et al. 2022). The overall diagnostic performance of US plus AFP is suboptimal in the clinic. Additionally, the efficacy of US is susceptible to the operator’s experience and the patient’s characteristics, such as obesity (Yu et al. 2011; Zocchi and Vindigni 2022; Srinivasa Babu et al. 2016). Compared to the US, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have superior diagnostic sensitivity in identifying HCC lesions, including early-stage tumors. However, intravenous contrast agents required by CT and MRI may cause allergic reactions and possible renal toxicity (Brunsing et al. 2020). In addition, transportation to medical centers and high cost also hinder their clinical application in HCC screening and diagnosis (Harris et al. 2019).

Tissue biopsies are constrained by sample accessibility and tumor heterogeneity, while liquid biopsies offer a non-invasive approach to detect tumor-related molecular changes (Gilson et al. 2022). Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-based liquid biopsies are among the most active research areas for early cancer screening and diagnosis. CtDNA not only harbors specific gene somatic mutations associated with tumors but also carries unique epigenetic marks, notably DNA methylation within certain GC-rich regions (Nishida and Kudo 2014). Methylation alterations often manifest in the very early stages of tumorigenesis, and HCC stands as a high-frequency, multi-gene CpG island-methylated malignancy (Baylin and Jones 2011, 2016; Calvisi et al. 2007). Epigenetic silencing of tumor suppressor genes mediated by promoter CpG island hypermethylation represents a pivotal molecular change during HCC development (Pogribny and Rusyn 2014; Herceg and Paliwal 2011).

Several cell-free DNA methylation profiling studies found that methylation biomarkers have the potential for HCC detection (Zhao et al. 2014; Luo et al. 2022; Phan et al. 2022). RASSF1A is one of the most frequently epigenetically inactivated tumor suppressor genes in human cancers (Grawenda and O’Neill 2015). Epigenetic silencing of RASSF1A has been implicated in the initiation and progression of HCC (Hu et al. 2010). Notably, the frequency of methylated RASSF1A in the peripheral blood of HCC patients (40%) significantly surpasses that in liver cirrhosis patients (14%) and the general population (0%) (p < 0.001) (Pasha et al. 2019). Thus, the promoter methylation level of RASSF1A in serum DNA is considered a valuable biomarker for early detection of HCC in high-risk populations.

TSPYL5 (testis-specific protein Y-encoded like 5) is a member of the TSPY-like protein family, which interacts with USP7, the deubiquitylase for the tumor suppressor p53 (Epping et al. 2011). By reducing the activity of USP7 toward p53, TSPYL5 increases p53 ubiquitination and decreases p53 protein levels. Recently, TSPYL5 was found to be an epigenetic gatekeeper of human hepatocarcinogenesis (Hernandez-Meza et al. 2021). During the transformation of HCC, TSPYL5 showed a stepwise promoter hypermethylation from cirrhotic tissue to dysplastic nodules and small HCC.

Recently, the combination of serum protein markers and methylation markers has shown great potential in HCC screening and diagnosis. A multi-target panel including AFP, sex, and three methylation biomarkers showed a higher sensitivity for early-stage HCC detection than AFP alone or GALAD (Chalasani et al. 2021, 2022). Similarly, the combination of serum RASSF1A methylation and AFP showed a higher AUC of 0.876 for discriminating HBV-related HCC from those with chronic hepatitis B than RASSF1A methylation (AUC:0.796) or AFP alone (AUC:0.756) (Dong et al. 2015a). This study aimed to develop a novel combination of serum methylation and protein biomarker panel for HCC screening and validate its performance for HCC diagnosis.

Results

We conducted a comprehensive analysis using Methsurv to explore the relationship between the clinical characteristics of the TCGA liver cancer cohort (LIHC) and the methylation profiles of RASSF1A and TSPYL5. Notably, we observed that most CpG sites in TSPYL5 exhibited hypermethylation, especially around the CpG island (CGI) and the North CpG Shore (Fig. 1A). However, only approximately 40% of CpG sites in RASSF1A displayed hypermethylation (Fig. 1B). The highly methylated sites in RASSF1A were primarily located in the gene body and 3’-UTR regions, whereas the methylation sites in TSPYL5 are predominantly found in the 1st exon. Among them, cg01568433 (South CpG Shore, 3’-UTR), cg05546296 (N CpG Shore, TSS1500; Body), cg20119308 (North CpG Shore, TSS1500; Body), cg13257331 (S-Shelf, 1st Exon), cg15426878 (South CpG Shelf, 1st Exon), and cg06174454 (North CpG Shelf, Body; 5’-UTR) in RASSF1A, as well as cg18458131 (North CpG Shore, 1st Exon; 3’-UTR), cg22328208 (CGI, 1st Exon), cg24512297 (North CpG Shore, 1st Exon; 3’-UTR), cg09503853 (South CpG Shore, 1st Exon; 5’-UTR), and cg23197859 (South CpG Shore, 1st Exon; 5’-UTR) in TSPYL5 exhibited the highest DNA methylation levels.

mRNA sequencing data analysis from the TCGA liver cancer cohort (n = 371) was performed using the LinkedOmics functional module. As illustrated in the volcano plot, a total of 1781 genes (depicted in deep red) exhibited a significant positive correlation with RASSF1A hypermethylation, while 2206 genes (depicted in deep green) displayed a significant negative correlation with RASSF1A hypermethylation (Fig. 2A). 1816 genes were significantly positively correlated with TSPYL5 hypermethylation, whereas 5890 genes showed a significant negative correlation with TSPYL5 hypermethylation (Fig. 2B) (p < 0.05, false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.01).

The volcano plot demonstrate differentially expressed genes in correlation with RASSF1A (A) and TSPYL5 (B) hypermethylation in the TCGA-LIHC cohort. The correlation between hypermethylation and differentially expressed genes was analyzed using the Pearson test

Subsequently, we conducted gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). The results of GO analysis indicated that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with RASSF1A hypermethylation were primarily involved in collagen trimer and extracellular matrix. These genes were responsible for functions such as cellular response to vascular endothelial growth factor stimulus, vasculogenesis, and artery development. GO Molecular Function analysis suggested their involvement in extracellular matrix structural constituent, transmembrane receptor protein kinase activity, and growth factor binding. Additionally, KEGG pathway enrichment indicated that differentially expressed genes associated with RASSF1A hypermethylation were predominantly concentrated in the calcium signaling pathway and regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1A–D). Similarly, differentially expressed genes associated with TSPYL5 hypermethylation were primarily located in the endoplasmic reticulum quality control compartment, with functions related to peroxisomal transport and rRNA binding. KEGG pathway enrichment suggested that TSPYL5 hypermethylation affected nucleotide excision repair and DNA replication (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D).

Based on the literature research (Zhang et al. 2007a; Kisiel et al. 2019), we selected two HCC-specific methylation regions of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 as candidate biomarkers for HCC detection. Wanderer was utilized to scrutinize the DNA methylation status within the specific regions of TCGA-LIHC and corresponding normal tissue samples. Significant differences in methylation levels were observed between tumor (n = 256) and non-tumor (n = 50) tissue samples within the selected regions (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A and B). We then designed primers (Supplementary Table 2) for quantitative methylation-specific PCR (qMSP) and further assessed the methylation levels of the two regions using HCC cell lines and clinical HCC plasma samples. The methylation levels of the two regions in HCC cell lines were significantly higher than those in normal leukocyte controls (Fig. 4A). In plasma samples from the methylation level validation cohort (88 cases), including 43 HCC and 45 CHB patients, we observed similar results (Fig. 4B).

The serum protein marker AFP has relatively high clinical utility in the diagnosis of HCC and was also incorporated into the biomarker analysis. Additionally, prior literature has established correlations between age and gender with HCC incidence (Zhang et al. 2020; Zhu et al. 2016). Thus, we selected patient age, gender, gene methylation (TSPYL5, RASSF1A), and protein AFP data to construct the ARTHCC HCC detection model based on a logistic regression algorithm. We divided our discovery cohort into 1:1-sized training and internal validation sets based on cancer stage, age, and gender to construct and validate the HCC detection model ARTHCC. The training set comprised 38 HCC cases and 69 liver disease controls. Males were more prevalent among HCC cases than controls, accounting for 89.5% and 81.2%, respectively. In contrast to previous studies in Western populations, the primary etiology of HCC in China is predominantly HBV (Alter 1995). Within the HCC cases, 32.4% represented early-stage lesions. For both overall and early HCC detection, ARTHCC exhibits AUC values of 0.917 (95% CI, 0.853–0.982) and 0.802 (95% CI, 0.636–0.967), exceeding the AUC values of individual markers (Fig. 5A and B). In both the training and internal validation sets, the predicted probability for HCC patients is significantly higher than that for patients with non-HCC liver diseases (Fig. 5C and F), and the predicted probability increases with tumor stage. In the training set, ARTHCC exhibited an overall sensitivity of 86.8% (95% CI, 73.7–97.4%) and early-stage sensitivity of 69.2% (95% CI, 46.2–92.3%), with a specificity of 85.5% (95% CI, 76.8–92.8%) (Table 2).

To determine whether AFP is the only diagnostic protein that correlates with the methylation status of TSPYL5 and RASSF1A hypermethylation, the methylation status of RASSF1A, TSPYL5, and liver function-related parameters from 72 HCC patients were subjected to normality testing using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The P-values for RASSF1A and albumin (ALB) were 0.3197 and 0.2072, respectively. At the significance level of α = 0.05 (P > 0.05), the null hypothesis could not be rejected, indicating that these datasets followed a normal distribution. The remaining parameters did not conform to a normal distribution. Since RASSF1A methylation status and ALB followed a normal distribution, whereas indicators did not meet normality assumptions, the Pearson correlation coefficient was employed to analyze the correlation between RASSF1A and ALB, while the Spearman correlation coefficient was used for the other parameters. RASSF1A methylation status demonstrated strong negative correlations with TBIL, AST, and AFP (P < 0.05), with the most pronounced correlation observed for AFP (ρ ≈ − 0.498, P < 0.0001) (Table 6). Since TSPYL5 methylation status did not follow a normal distribution, the Spearman correlation coefficient was employed. TSPYL5 showed a weak positive correlation with ALB, while demonstrating strong negative correlations with TBIL, AST, and AFP (P < 0.05). Among them, the strongest correlation was observed with AFP (ρ ≈ − 0.570, P < 0.0001) (Table 6).

Discussion

The screening of HCC remains a significant clinical challenge due to the suboptimal efficacy of traditional methods. Although ultrasound combined with AFP has alleviated this issue to some extent, it has a low detection rate for early-stage HCC. Patient adherence to semiannual screening with ultrasound/AFP is unsatisfactory. A meta-analysis by Wolf et al. (Wolf et al. 2021) showed that the average compliance rate for screening individuals at high risk for HCC is only 24%. Therefore, there is a need to develop high-performance and cost-effective alternative methods. In this study, through the analysis of public databases, we demonstrated significantly elevated methylation levels of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 in HCC tissue samples. Subsequently, we further validated these findings in liver cancer cell lines and clinical blood samples of HCC patients. Based on additional analyses, we found that a multi-target panel comprising RASSF1A, TSPYL5, and AFP exhibited excellent performance in detecting HCC in both discovery and validation cohorts. The combined detection of methylation and protein markers in blood holds promise as a non-invasive and efficient method for early HCC screening.

Previous studies have indicated hypermethylation of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 in liver cancer tissues (Hu et al. 2010; Qiu et al. 2016). Our bioinformatic analysis using public datasets confirmed the aforementioned results. Methylation analysis based on the Methsurv public database revealed that most CpG sites of TSPYL5 in liver cancer were hypermethylated, while around 40% of CpG sites in RASSF1A were hypermethylated. This difference may be attributed to the fact that the hypermethylated sites of TSPYL5 are mainly located in islands and shores, whereas those of RASSF1A are concentrated in shelves and shores, with some CpGs dispersed in islands. GO and KEGG analysis results suggest that the hypermethylation of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 might be involved in the initiation and progression of HCC by regulating tumor cell metabolism and DNA damage repair.

In addition to HCC pathogenesis, RASSF1A hypermethylation is also associated with the prognosis of HCC patients. Some studies found that higher RASSF1A methylation levels in HCC patients correlated with advanced tumor stages, increased risk of metastasis, and shorter survival (Pasha et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2017; Chan et al. 2008). A recent meta-analysis of 44 case–control studies involving 4,777 individuals showed that RASSF1A hypermethylation was significantly associated with HCC risk and worse outcomes (Xu et al. 2020). However, one single-center study in Thailand found that RASSF1A hypermethylation was significantly correlated with better prognosis in HCC patients (Saelee et al. 2010). The median survival period was 102.9 weeks in patients with RASSF1A hypermethylation and 14 weeks in those without. These conflicting findings may be due to differences in patient populations, etiologies, sample size, and statistical methods. RASSF1A methylation frequency varied across different populations, ranging from 59% in Bazilian HCC patients, 70% among Taiwanese HCC patients, to 86% in Thailand HCC patients (Zhang et al. 2007a; Saelee et al. 2010; Araújo et al. 2016). Of note, the Thailand HCC study had a small sample size (n = 29), and its multivariate analysis showed that patient survival was not associated with tumor size, metastasis, and treatment, which was inconsistent with the consensus of current HCC clinical guidelines (Saelee et al. 2010; Benson et al. 2021). Therefore, further research with large sample sizes is needed to clarify the role of RASSF1A hypermethylation as a prognostic biomarker for HCC.

TSPYL5, a putative tumor suppressor gene, was originally found to be silenced by hypermethylation in malignant glioma (Kim et al. 2006). Through targeted bisulfite sequencing of 24 paired HCC tumors and adjacent non-tumor tissues, Santella et al. identified that TSPYL5 was underexpressed and hypermethylated in HCC tumors in comparison to normal liver tissues (Shen et al. 2015). In another DNA methylation profiling study of 227 HCC samples, including 40 early-stage HCC samples, TSPYL5 was found to be one of the four epigenetic gatekeepers for HCC development (Hernandez-Meza et al. 2021). Furthermore, TSPYL5 was one of the two methylation biomarkers in a multitarget HCC blood test (Oncoguard Liver) developed by Exact Sciences, which also included AFP as a protein biomarker (Chalasani et al. 2022).

Studies found conflicting results about the correlation between RASSF1A methylation and serum AFP levels in HCC patients. There was no significant association between RASSF1A promoter methylation and elevated AFP levels in two Chinese HCC cohorts (n = 35/105) (Liu et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2007b). Similarly, no significant association between serum AFP and RASSF1A methylation was observed in an HCV-related HCC cohort (n = 45) when the AFP cut-off value was 20 ng/ml (Mansour et al. 2017). Consistently, a meta-analysis involving 4777 individuals did not find a correlation between RASSF1A methylation and serum AFP with the same cut-off (Xu et al. 2020). However, one study reported a positive correlation between hypermethylation of RASSF1A and serum AFP with a cut-off of 20 ng/mL (Pasha et al. 2019). Therefore, further large-scale, multicenter studies are needed to clarify the relationship between RASSF1A methylation and serum AFP levels.

Portal vein tumor thrombosis (PVTT) is caused by tumor invasion and metastasis through the intrahepatic portal vein, resulting in a poor prognosis for patients with HCC accompanied by PVTT (Bruix et al. 2014). The epigenetic methylation patterns of the apparent genome in PVTT have not been thoroughly explored. Fan et al.’s study (Fan et al. 2020) suggests that the methylation of genes such as TNFRSF10A, ZC3H3, and SLC9A3R2 plays a crucial role in PVTT formation by regulating metastasis-related pathways. In this study, we discussed the association between RASSF1A/TSPYL5 methylation and PVTT in HCC patients in the discovery cohort. Interestingly, we found a significant correlation between the methylation status of TSPYL5 and PVTT (P = 0.006), and the biological mechanisms underlying this association warrant further investigation （Table 7）.

In previous studies, the AUC of plasma RASSF1A methylation to distinguish between HCC and CHB patients was reported as 0.796 (95% CI, 0.721–0.864), with a sensitivity of 64.2% and specificity of 89.8% (Dong et al. 2015b). The AUC of plasma TSPYL5 methylation to differentiate between HCC and cirrhosis patients was 0.802 (95% CI, 0.657–0.948), with a sensitivity of 73% and specificity of 80% (Fu et al. 2023). In our study’s training dataset, the AUC of plasma RASSF1A and TSPYL5 methylation to distinguish between HCC and Non-HCC patients was 0.805 (95% CI, 0.708–0.903) and 0.846 (95% CI, 0.753–0.939), respectively, with a sensitivity of 63.2% and 76.3%, and a specificity of 98.6% and 92.8%. Both gene methylations showed higher specificity and sensitivity for HCC detection compared to previous studies. Moreover, we observed that RASSF1A had higher specificity for individual methylation markers, while TSPYL5 exhibited better sensitivity. Overall, the performance of individual methylation markers was inferior to the ARTHCC model. At a threshold with a predicted probability > 0.265, the ARTHCC model demonstrated optimal performance in distinguishing overall and early-stage HCC patients, with AUCs of 0.917 and 0.802, respectively.

In the internal validation dataset, the ARTHCC model also demonstrated excellent performance at the threshold probability > 0.265. At a specificity of 92.9%, the sensitivity of ARTHCC for discriminating overall and early-stage HCC patients was 85.3% and 81.8%, respectively. ARTHCC’s performance significantly surpasses AFP, particularly in early-stage HCC (81.8% vs. 36.4%). Subgroup analysis revealed the overall stability of ARTHCC model performance across different subgroups. Particularly noteworthy is its performance in the AFP-negative population (AFP < 20 ng/mL), with a sensitivity of 66.7% and specificity of 96.4%.

Finally, we conducted additional validation of the ARTHCC model performance in an independent external dataset, which had a high proportion of early-stage HCC patients (57.9%). The AUC for distinguishing between HCC and cirrhosis using ARTHCC reached 0.922, with a sensitivity of 81.8% for early-stage HCC detection at a specificity of 90.0%. This sensitivity was significantly higher than that of AFP in early-stage HCC (54.6%). These results strongly indicate the applicability of the ARTHCC model for HCC detection, highlighting its significant potential for early-stage HCC detection.

Our study has some limitations. Firstly, our approach was confined to literature mining and bioinformatics analysis using publicly available datasets. The biological functions of these two hypermethylated markers in HCC development lack imperative experimental validation. Therefore, our future plans involve a thorough exploration of their potential biological mechanisms in HCC carcinogenesis. Secondly, the relatively small sample size in our study may potentially impact the robustness of our findings. Future large-scale multicenter prospective randomized controlled clinical trials are required to validate the clinical performance of the ARTHCC model.

Conclusions

Through literature mining and bioinformatics network analysis, we confirmed the involvement of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 methylation in the occurrence of HCC. The integrated assessment of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 methylation, along with AFP protein, in plasma samples exhibits high efficacy for HCC detection. The utilization of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 methylation as novel non-invasive biomarkers for early screening of HCC is deemed feasible.

Methods

Marker identification

We utilized MethSurv (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/methsurv/) to retrieve DNA methylation data of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 from the TCGA-LIHC dataset and established heatmaps to determine the methylation level at different CpG sites (Modhukur et al. 2018). The LinkFinder module of the LinkedOmics platform (http://www.linkedomics.org/login.php) was used to screen differentially expressed genes related to hypermethylated RASSF1A and TSPYL5 (Vasaikar et al. 2018). The results assessed through Spearman’s correlation analysis were compiled in the volcano plot (correlation p-values and Spearman’s correlation coefficients). To further investigate the possible gene ontology terms and signaling pathways in which hypermethylated RASSF1A and TSPYL5 might be involved, the LinkInterpreter module of the LinkedOmics platform was used to carry out the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). The significance threshold for false discovery rate (FDR) was set at P < 0.05 to identify the most associated GO and KEGG pathways in each module of interest. The mean methylation level of RASSF1A and TSPYL5 in each CpG site was generated with Wanderer (http://maplab.imppc.org/wan derer/) (Díez-Villanueva et al. 2015).

Participants

A total of 398 participants from November 2020 to September 2021 were enrolled in this retrospective study. Clinical samples for the methylation level validation cohort (88 cases) and discovery cohort (211 cases) were recruited from the Liver Department of The Affiliated Fifth People’s Hospital of Ganzhou, while the independent external validation cohort (99 cases) was recruited from Xijing Hospital. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics review committees of both study units, and written informed consent was taken from all participants. Cases were recruited if they matched the following criteria: (1) > 18 years of age; (2) met the diagnostic criteria of the "Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Liver Cancer (2022 Edition)" (Zhou et al. 2023) and the initial HCC diagnosis confirmed by pathological evidence; (3) receiving no treatment during the six months prior to enrollment. The control group (CG) consisted of adults aged 18 years or older without an HCC diagnosis, who were willing to participate.

Cell line and plasma processing

The HuH-7, HEP3B, and HEP-AD-38 human liver cancer cell lines (Shanghai Gaining Biological Technology Co., Ltd.) were cultured in T25 flasks at a cell density exceeding 106 cells per flask. Subsequently, the cells were treated with trypsin digestion. The leukocyte controls from chronic hepatitis B and HCC patients were obtained from the Affiliated Fifth People’s Hospital of Ganzhou. Peripheral blood (10 mL) was collected using K2-EDTA anticoagulation tubes and processed within 4 h of an initial 10-min low-speed centrifugation at 1700 g, followed by a 10-min high-speed centrifugation at 20,000 g to separate plasma. Supernatants were then stored at -70°C for DNA extraction. AFP data were obtained from the hospital’s electronic records.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from frozen tissues following the standard operating procedure (SOP) of the blood/cell/tissue genomic DNA extraction kit (DP304-03, Tiangen). CfDNA was extracted from a total of 2 mL of plasma following the SOP of the magnetic bead-based large-volume cfDNA extraction kit (DP710-02, Tiangen). DNA quantification was performed using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit and Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen).

Methylation-specific PCR testing

The methylation-specific PCR (MSP) reaction system consisted of 10 μL of bisulfite-treated DNA, 5 μL of primer–probe mix, and 15 μL of AceQ® MSP Probe Master Mix (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). Testing was carried out on the ABI 7500 fluorescent PCR device (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) using TaqMan Reagents Standard mode, which involved an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 35 s. Fluorescent signals were collected at the end of each cycle’s annealing and extension phase. The type II procollagen gene (COL2A1) was selected as the internal reference gene.

Statistical analysis

Depending on the specific scenarios of analysis, group differences were assessed using Student’s t-test, Mann Whitney U test, or chi-squared test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to evaluate the clinical performance of each biomarker, and the cutoff points for each candidate biomarker were determined based on the Youden index (YI). Prior to data input, the Ct values of methylated CpG sites and the logarithmic-transformed values (base 10) of AFP were collected. For any undetected methylation markers, the Ct values were rounded to 45. The model developed by the ARTHCC (An integrated AFP Protein, RASSF1A, and TSPYL5 methylation biomarkers for early detection of hepatocellular carcinoma) was based on a logistic regression algorithm. All statistical analyses and graphs plotting were conducted with R software package (version 4.3.1), Statistical package for the social sciences (SPSS 26 for Windows, USA), and MedCalc Statistical Software version 20.014 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium).
