Understanding the drivers and barriers of elderly vaccination in post-pandemic China: a qualitative study in Shenzhen
Authors: Ziru Deng, Fang Huang, Rosetta Oi Ling Lee, Karen A. Grépin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVaccination coverage among older adults in China remains suboptimal despite increased awareness of vaccination during the COVID-19 pandemic. This study aimed to investigate the key drivers and barriers to elderly vaccination in China.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using the Critical Medical Anthropology (CMA) and the Integrative Model of Behavioral Prediction (IMBP) frameworks. Data were collected through in-depth interviews with 22 elderly individuals and 8 healthcare providers (HCPs) in Shenzhen between January and April 2024. Thematic analysis was employed to explore the significant factors influencing elderly vaccination across multiple levels.ResultsThe study identified individual perceptions of vulnerability and vaccination benefits as key motivators for uptake. Family influence, peer interactions, and trust in HCPs served as critical social norms shaping vaccination decisions. Challenges such as COVID-19 vaccine fatigue, limited community engagement beyond COVID-19 vaccination efforts, policy disparities in free vaccine eligibility, and misinformation on social media were significant barriers. While the pandemic heightened public awareness of life-course immunization, it also triggered new vaccine hesitancy among older adults.ConclusionsThe study highlights the need for targeted public health strategies, including policy adjustments to address free vaccination disparities, better involvement of family doctors, and enhanced communication to combat new emerging vaccine hesitancy. Promoting comprehensive life-course immunization is crucial for protecting China’s aging population in the post-pandemic era.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25437-7.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic greatly reshaped global health priorities, affecting public perceptions of vaccination. It disrupted routine immunization services worldwide, delaying vaccine uptake, particularly childhood vaccinations [1]. Traditionally, immunization programs focused on infants and children. However, the post-pandemic era has brought renewed attention to life-course vaccination, which advocates immunization across all stages of life, especially for vulnerable groups like older adults [2, 3]. While the global rollout of COVID-19 vaccines provided an opportunity to address vaccine hesitancy and boost elderly vaccination programs, it also introduced new challenges, notably vaccine fatigue [4, 5]. Studies found that 23.1% of adults across 23 countries were less likely to accept vaccines post-pandemic, with hesitancy extending beyond COVID-19 vaccines [1].

In China, pre-pandemic influenza and pneumococcal vaccination rates among older adults were persistently low [6, 7]. A 2020 study found only 4.8% of older adults with chronic conditions received the influenza vaccine, yet 92.7% expressed willingness for vaccination in the upcoming season, suggesting a pandemic effect on intentions [8]. However, limited research explored the pandemic’s impact on elderly vaccination for non-COVID-19 vaccines, with most studies focusing on National Immunization Program (NIP, free and mandatory) vaccines or non-NIP vaccines (optional and self-paid) for children. A national survey revealed that 42.2% of recovered COVID-19 patients in China were hesitant about future vaccine doses [9]. Common determinants such as perceived susceptibility, disease severity, vaccine effectiveness, side effects, trust in HCPs, and information channels are known to influence vaccination behaviors outside pandemic contexts [10–12]. However, the pandemic has intensified these concerns, adding new psychosocial dynamics.

Unlike practical barriers in other low- and middle-income settings (LMICs), where access limits vaccine uptake, COVID-19 vaccines in China are widely available. By 2022, influenza and pneumococcal vaccines were provided free of charge to elderly residents in 256 counties and districts [13, 14]. These programs are not nationally mandated and depend on local government financing arrangements. Eligibility is often tied to local household registration (Hukou) status or participation in local health insurance schemes, which exclude many migrants who live in cities but are not locally registered. Thus, low vaccine uptake among the Chinese elderly likely reflects not only psychosocial concerns but also structural and policy-related barriers that quantitative studies may insufficiently capture.

Qualitative methods, especially in-depth interviews, are particularly well-suited to this study as they allow for the exploration of deeper meanings, social dynamics, and structural influences that shape elderly vaccination behaviors—dimensions typically overlooked in standardized quantitative surveys. Previous qualitative studies on COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among the Chinese elderly have identified nuanced factors like perceived low threat, low vaccine confidence, inadequate decisional support, peer pressure, negative views on Western biomedicine, and distrust in the government [15–17]. However, qualitative research on the broader post-pandemic context’s impact on elderly attitudes toward other vaccines remains sparse, with only one study focusing on influenza vaccination barriers among HCPs rather than older adults [18].

Against this background, this study addresses this gap by integrating Critical Medical Anthropology (CMA) and the Integrative Model of Behavioral Prediction (IMBP), allowing for a comprehensive exploration of complex, multilayered decision-making processes within China’s sociopolitical and cultural context. Shenzhen was specifically selected as the study site due to its status as a rapidly aging migrant megacity with progressive local vaccination policies and notable policy variability linked to health insurance or Hukou. This unique setting enables in-depth examination of specific drivers and barriers to elderly vaccination, providing insights for future life-course immunization programs, including seasonal influenza and pneumococcal vaccinations.

Methodology

Theoretical framework

Various theories and models have been used to explore vaccine hesitancy and vaccination behaviors, including the Health Belief Model (HBM), Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), and the 3C and 5C models [19–22]. These models primarily focus on individual and interpersonal influences, including demographics, vaccination history, perceived risks and benefits, and subjective norms. However, they lack consideration for broader sociocultural and environmental contexts, which are crucial for understanding health behaviors in complex sociopolitical environments like China.

To address this gap, recent studies have incorporated integrative models that capture both proximal and societal factors [16, 23]. This study integrates the IMBP and CMA. IMBP expands on TPB and TRA by incorporating attitudes, perceived norms, and efficacy as central determinants of behavioral intentions, also considering upstream influences like media and culture [20, 24]. CMA emphasizes how individual health behaviors are embedded within and shaped by power dynamics, inequalities, and the wider sociocultural context across four levels: individual, micro-social, intermediate-social, and macro-social [25]. Grounding the study in IMBP and CMA provides a comprehensive approach to exploring the key drivers of elderly vaccination and the pandemic’s lasting impacts on their future vaccination patterns in China (Fig. 1). Both IMBP and CMA have been applied in vaccination studies [23, 26, 27]. Notably, CMA was applied in a qualitative study on pneumococcal and influenza vaccination among older adults in Hong Kong [27], providing a basis for comparison with our study.

Theoretical framework of this study building on IMBP and CMA

At the individual level, vaccination intentions are shaped by perceived disease risk, personal vulnerability, vaccine confidence, and past experiences, aligning with the IMBP construct of attitudes. These intentions are further shaped by micro-social level interactions, such as family influence, peer norms, and trust in HCPs. These factors correspond to perceived norms in IMBP and reflect interpersonal dynamics emphasized in CMA. The intermediate-social level refers to policy implementation, such as eligibility policies and service delivery models, which shaped perceived access and fairness. These factors influence individuals’ perceived behavioral control and are central to CMA’s emphasis on structures of power and inequality in health care systems. Importantly, the macro-social level, covering broader sociopolitical and cultural contexts such as trust in government, collective responsibility, culture and values, and media narratives, does not merely sit above the rest levels but actively filters down, shaping micro-level norms and institutional priorities. Additionally, the pandemic altered perceptions of vulnerability, reshaped institutional trust, and transformed social norms across all levels of the framework. Therefore, we conceptualize the pandemic as a cross-cutting sociopolitical and historical driver that influences post-pandemic vaccination behavior at the macro-social level.

Study design

Shenzhen, China’s youngest megacity, experienced a 72.9% increase in its elderly population from 2000 to 2020, reaching over 940,000 individuals aged over 60 [28]. We selected Luohu (8.57%), Futian (8.43%), Yantian (7.59%), and Nanshan (7.23%), the districts with the highest percentages of elderly residents, as study sites. Alongside free COVID-19 vaccines since April 2021, Shenzhen has implemented a policy since 2016 offering free influenza and pneumococcal vaccines to eligible residents aged 60 years and above who hold Shenzhen Hukou or are covered by Shenzhen medical insurance. Despite this, pre-pandemic vaccination coverage among the elderly was low: 4.8% for influenza vaccines and 4.3% for pneumococcal vaccines, lagging behind Western countries like the UK, where coverage rates reached 72.6% and 70.6%, respectively [7, 8, 29].

This study employed a qualitative research approach via semi-structured in-depth interviews. In-depth interviews reveal how older adults in China interpret vaccination based on their personal experiences and the sociocultural and policy contexts they live in, offering insights often missed by standardized surveys. Interview guides for this study were primarily developed from the WHO’s Behavioral and Social Drivers of Vaccination (BeSD) tools (Fig. 2), which provide structural templates for formulating questions to explore perspectives of both elderly participants and HCPs [30]. Additionally, we incorporated constructs from the aforementioned theoretical frameworks to enrich our inquiry.

The WHO’s behavioral and social drivers of vaccination framework

For elderly participants, sociodemographic details (age, sex, education, income, health insurance) and health status (self-rated health, chronic disease conditions) were collected initially. Key questions covered: (1) vaccination history and adverse effects (COVID-19, influenza, pneumococcal vaccines); (2) perceptions of disease and vaccines, including risk, benefits, and trust; (3) knowledge and willingness to receive future life-course vaccination; and (4) social and normative influences. For HCPs, we explored professional experience, insights into elderly vaccination, and suggestions for improvement.

The BeSD interview guides align closely with our theoretical frameworks. For example, questions exploring perceived risk and vaccine confidence (e.g., “How concerned are you about the disease infection?” “What are your attitudes towards this vaccine?”) in BeSD’s “Thinking and Feeling” domain map onto IMBP’s attitude construct and CMA’s individual level. Questions on family and peer influence (e.g., “Do you think people around you want you to get vaccinated?”), drawn from BeSD’s “Social Processes” and “Motivation” domains, reflect perceived norms and interpersonal dynamics emphasized in both IMBP and CMA. Similarly, questions about trust in HCPs and barriers to access (e.g., “What are the challenges that would affect your vaccination behaviors?” “How do you trust the vaccine-related information?”) address BeSD’s “Practical Issues” and capture influences at the intermediate- and macro-social levels in CMA. To ensure contextual relevance, certain adaptations were made for the Chinese setting, such as replacing religious leaders with neighborhood committee staff (see Supplementary file 1).

Data collection

Participants were recruited via purposive sampling between January and April 2024. Elderly participants aged over 60 with the ability to communicate cognitively were recruited from community healthcare centers and public parks. HCPs responsible for vaccination programs were identified through referrals from CDC officers or within healthcare centers. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached. Demographic diversity in gender, age, and location was also considered. Each interview, averaging 40 min, was audio-recorded with consent and transcribed.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using thematic analysis. Z.D. transcribed all interviews verbatim, and R.L. verified the transcriptions. The coding framework was guided by the theoretical frameworks. Thematic saturation was reached after approximately 20 elderly interviews and 5 HCP interviews, determined at the point where no new themes emerged across subgroups [31]. We also assessed saturation within key participant categories (e.g., Hukou vs. non-Hukou elderly; CDC officials vs. frontline community HCPs) to ensure thematic consistency across demographic and professional groups. Both deductive and inductive methods were employed using NVivo 14. Z.D. and R.L. processed all transcripts independently and discussed coding until reaching a consensus. Intercoder reliability was calculated using percentage agreement, which reached 80.82%, indicating strong consistency. Interviews were conducted in Chinese with selected quotes translated into English. Bilingual researchers Z.D. and R.L. ensured linguistic accuracy to preserve the original meaning and nuance of participants’ responses.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

The study included 22 elderly participants (aged 61-78 years) and 8 HCPs (4 from community health centers and 4 CDC officers at different levels) (Table 1). Among the elderly participants, most were male (59.1%), with educational backgrounds ranging from primary school to bachelor’s degrees. A majority lived with their children (72.7%), reflecting Shenzhen’s common family structure, while 54.5% lacked local Hukou, which limited their access to local healthcare services, including free vaccinations. All HCPs held at least bachelor’s degrees and had 5-35 years of professional experience.

All elderly participants were vaccinated against COVID-19, with 63.6% having also received the influenza vaccine and 36.4% receiving the pneumococcal vaccine. These rates marked an improvement compared to pre-pandemic levels, with many elderly participants only beginning their vaccination since the pandemic. While most elderly participants expressed willingness for future vaccinations, clear signs of vaccine fatigue emerged. Nearly 40.9% expressed reluctance or refusal to receive additional COVID-19 doses. Hesitancy also extended to future influenza and pneumococcal vaccinations, particularly among those without Shenzhen Hukou and those with lower educational attainment, highlighting potential disparities in vaccine access and education within this population.

Thematic analysis of drivers of elderly vaccination

Individual level

High perceived vulnerability and benefits of vaccination

The primary motivation for elderly vaccination was perceived vulnerability due to age and health conditions, particularly heightened post-pandemic. Many participants viewed vaccination as necessary. One shared, “Since COVID-19, many elderly people like me have passed away due to their underlying health conditions…Whenever we hear the ambulance sirens, we become sensitive and fearful…That’s why if there is any vaccine we need to take, we will definitely take it without hesitation” (SZE1). This perception reflects how attitudes toward perceived risks and expected outcomes influence vaccination intentions among the elderly. Although influenza and pneumonia were seen as less severe than COVID-19, participants acknowledged their importance, noting, “I will continue getting vaccinated for flu and pneumonia because as we get older, our immune system weakens. It becomes necessary to take remedial measures” (SZE3). Others emphasized, “Afraid of death [laugh]… fear of the flu. At my age, considering conditions like diabetes and hypertension, it’s easy for things to go downhill” (SZE6). The belief that vaccination could prevent health complications, despite some lingering concerns, was a strong motivator, indicating that vaccine behavior is grounded in health beliefs shaped by perceptions of aging-related risks and pandemic experiences.

Lower awareness of Pneumococcal vaccines than influenza vaccines

Awareness of pneumococcal vaccines was significantly lower than that of influenza vaccines. Some elderly participants even confused pneumococcal vaccines with COVID-19 vaccines due to similar Chinese abbreviations, reflecting the challenges in distinguishing the exact vaccines they had received. One participant admitted, “I’ve received several shots, but I’m not really sure what vaccines they were… When the doctor calls, I just go. That’s how it is” (SZE14). This reliance on HCPs for information led to a passive acceptance of vaccines rather than an active informed understanding. Additionally, prior positive experiences with influenza vaccines reinforced vaccine confidence, making them more open to future vaccinations. Participants noted, “Since I received the influenza vaccine, I have felt much less likely to get this illness” (SZE3, SZE11).

COVID-19 vaccine fatigue and its spillover effect on other vaccines

Vaccine fatigue, especially toward COVID-19 boosters, was prevalent. Some participants felt less inclined to receive additional doses after contracting COVID-19 despite having been vaccinated, questioning the vaccine’s efficacy and side effects: “I won’t get vaccinated. What’s the point of it? I found lung nodules during a check-up that weren’t there before. Maybe there’s a problem with the vaccine” (SZE9). Others felt no need to continue COVID-19 vaccination as widespread infections occurred after China’s reopening. One shared, “By the time everything is relaxed, maybe 80% to 90% of people caught covid, right? The government isn’t making it mandatory, so there isn’t much pressure to get an extra dose. I feel like I don’t need it” (SZE7), and one echoed, “We’ve all been vaccinated. Since the mandates were relaxed, I definitely won’t take it if you ask me to get vaccinated again” (SZE22). These responses illustrate the intersection of declining perceived susceptibility and distrust in vaccine efficacy, which contributes to hesitancy not only for COVID-19 but for other vaccines.

Micro-social level

Strong family influence on the elderly’s vaccination decision-making

Family members, particularly children and grandchildren, played a crucial role in shaping the elderly’s vaccination decisions. Many elderly participants expressed trust in their families’ judgments and followed their children’s advice on whether to vaccinate. One participant shared, “If my son or daughter-in-law believes I need the vaccine, I’ll get it. They understand what’s happening outside while we stay at home all day. They’ll ask us to get vaccinated and arrange it for us” (SZE20). Another participant added, “My daughter-in-law said, ‘Mom, you need to get vaccinated, go get the flu shot,’ and she even prepared the money for us. But she never mentioned the pneumonia vaccine, so we never got it” (SZE17). Family members, often more informed about health risks, act as the elderly’s key vaccination decision-makers. HCPs reinforced this observation, “Many seniors were brought in by their children. Changing older adults’ mindsets is not easy, but when their children intervene and bring them for vaccination, it becomes a vital factor” (SZH3), “Many elderly people’s knowledge and trust in vaccinations mainly come from their children. Whether their children support or endorse it is a key determinant” (SZH4). Family encouragement and logistics were often decisive.

Mutually, most elderly participants proactively chose vaccination to avoid burdening their children with the care of an ill parent. One explained, “Our children and grandchildren have jobs. Once we are not well, it affects them. They hope we stay healthy and manage ourselves through vaccination because if we fall ill, it impacts not only our health but also their work, making life difficult” (SZE3). This sentiment highlights the desire to minimize family burden and maintain harmony. Such reciprocity reflects how vaccination is embedded within cultural values like filial piety, intergenerational obligations, and familial trust. This family-driven encouragement not only bolsters the elderly’s determination to vaccinate but also offers the emotional support needed to address any doubts or hesitations.

Peer interactions as informal prompts

Peer influence also emerged as a strong motivator in shaping perceived norms to vaccinate, particularly through social networks and community interactions. Many participants reported discussing vaccines with friends and neighbors during the pandemic when strict lockdowns heightened vigilance. COVID-19 vaccination, in particular, became a form of peer pressure within communities, with participants sharing, “If you don’t get vaccinated, the people next door worry that if you get sick, the whole building will be locked down — every floor from the first to the top. Of course, everyone hopes you get vaccinated… If you’re fine, everyone is comfortable, and the whole building is at ease” (SZE16). This perceived norm and peer pressure extended to influenza and pneumococcal vaccinations. Elderly participants frequently shared their vaccination experiences and recommendations within their social circles, “People around me hope I get vaccinated. When we gather, we do remind each other about getting the COVID-19 vaccine and flu shots. Sometimes we forget, but others always remind us” (SZE2). These exchanges and peer support acted as behavioral cues and reinforcements, fostering a sense of communal responsibility, further motivating vaccine uptake.

Trust-based relationships with HCPs at community health centers

Elderly participants all expressed positive experiences with the vaccination guidance and services provided at the community health centers, demonstrating high trust in their providers. As many elderly participants shared experiences of being proactively contacted by their vaccination doctors: “If we need to get vaccinated tomorrow, the vaccination doctor would send us messages in advance, informing us about the vaccination schedule, the type of vaccine, what disease it prevents, and why it’s necessary. They explained clearly… They are quite good… We trust them, that is what matters to us” (SZE2). Despite limited knowledge about specific vaccines, the elderly trust the expertise and care provided by HCPs. Another participant stated, “We get vaccinations based on our family doctor’s advice, informing us about what should be done and what should not be done. Most of us don’t really know which vaccines to take. However, the doctors here are quite good to us — they care quite a bit” (SZE16). This trust, reinforced by attentive care and clear communication from HCPs, instills confidence among the elderly, making them willing to follow vaccination advice.

Intermediate-social level

Limited community engagement beyond COVID-19 vaccination

Participants expressed frustration with the inconsistent community outreach for vaccines beyond COVID-19, often viewing these efforts as politically assigned tasks rather than genuine public health initiatives. This gap was particularly evident in the promotion of influenza and pneumococcal vaccines, with the elderly stating, “Because the community currently does not actively advocate for vaccinations, I haven’t heard any such messages, but it seems like the community did notify us about the flu vaccine once” (SZE7). One elderly commented, “The community rarely organizes activities for us. There is none. We mainly rely on doctors for vaccination promotion” (SZE6), reflecting a lack of visible community engagement. This institutional inattention weakens perceived behavioral control by failing to provide stable cues to action or a supportive environment for vaccine decision-making.

Disparities in vaccination eligibility due to policy restrictions

Shenzhen policies restrict access to free influenza and pneumococcal vaccines for elderly individuals without a local Hukou or local health insurance. This exclusion fostered feelings of institutional unfairness and dissatisfaction among non-local elderly citizens. Many participants expressed frustration, stating, “If someone gets vaccinated for free and I have to pay for it, it definitely makes me unhappy” (SZE11). Another non-local resident who resided in Shenzhen for a long time voiced similar dissatisfaction and kept questioning, “Why? I’ve been working here for over 20 years, paying taxes every year. Don’t I deserve even this bit of consideration” (SZE14). HCPs acknowledged such constraints, noting that while non-local residents were not strictly excluded in practical implementations, the current limited vaccine supply necessitates prioritizing local residents: “In the past, when there were surplus vaccines, even non-local elderly managed to get vaccinated, but in recent years, due to vaccine shortages, it has changed” (SZH2). The lack of clarity in vaccination policies further complicates decision-making for frontline vaccination doctors. Another HCP commented, “There are significant discrepancies. The senior medical check-ups are offered free, irrespective of Hukou status, as part of a nationwide initiative. Some may question the rationale behind certain individuals qualifying for free vaccinations while others do not. This presents a dilemma for us” (SZH1). Additionally, “We hope for clearer policies. Should only local residents be vaccinated, or should non-local residents also be included? Sometimes, it’s challenging for doctors to decide” (SZH5). This policy disparity remains a delicate issue, lacking a clear-cut resolution that satisfies both sides.

Macro-social level

Trust in governments over vaccines

Participants exhibited high levels of trust in the government, often surpassing trust in vaccines, particularly concerning the newer and rapidly developed COVID-19 vaccines. For many, government endorsement was a definitive marker of vaccine safety and efficacy. As one participant remarked, “I think there won’t be any major issues with vaccinating as per the national call. If the government insists, then the vaccines definitely won’t have any problems” (SZE10). Another echoed, “I don’t have a deep understanding, but I will get vaccinated. I trust the country, and whatever the country promotes must be good” (SZE11). This faith in the government’s capacity to ensure public safety through rigorous oversight is further emphasized, “Believe in doctors and trust the products developed by the country will never be harmful. We should trust the government because they undergo multiple experiments before administering vaccines” (SZE13). These sentiments mirrored a broader cultural inclination to place trust in national initiatives, especially in healthcare, where the Chinese government has long played a protective role, a dynamic further reinforced since the COVID-19 pandemic.

Vaccination as a social responsibility

The deep trust in government is intertwined with the experiences of Shenzhen’s elderly, many of whom migrated to the city during its transformative years following China’s reform and opening in the 1980s. Several participants are veterans from the western regions of China who contributed to Shenzhen’s infrastructure development in the early 1980 s and now reside in communities established after the collective restructuring of the former Engineering Corps. Having witnessed the city’s rapid modernization, they hold a strong sense of collective pride and responsibility. For them, vaccination is not just a personal health choice, but also a societal obligation. One stated, “We get vaccinated not only for ourselves but also for our families and society. That’s how I see it… Even if it’s at my own expense, I will still get vaccinated” (SZE1). This sense of civic duty reinforced their commitment to vaccination, viewing it as a moral responsibility that protects both personal and societal well-being.

Hesitancy due to vaccine rumors on social media

The trust carefully cultivated between individuals and both the government and HCPs was being eroded by circulating misinformation on social media, particularly regarding the Sinovac vaccine. Many elderly raised concerns, “They are saying that the Sinovac is fake… After I received all three doses, I suddenly discovered issues with my breast within less than two months… I heard others talking about it, and I started wondering if there could be a possibility? We cannot discern and don’t have much knowledge in this area” (SZE7). Another echoed, “People are saying that if you receive the Sinovac, it might lead to the development of lung nodules” (SZE8). HCPs also noted the pervasive influence of social media, “I introduced some information about vaccines, they will have a different opinion. ‘Where did you get this opinion?’ ‘I saw it on TikTok.’ It’s true that what we say a hundred times is not as influential as one statement from social media…It is also strange, the authorities never come out to refute rumors. I don’t think I have ever seen it, maybe occasionally” (SZH3). The lack of timely official rebuttals allowed rumor-driven narratives to flourish, challenging state credibility and shifting risk perceptions. This also reflects how media narratives and regulatory vacuums destabilize public trust and vaccine confidence.

Public awareness of vaccination increased but trust in vaccines remains uncertain

Most elderly participants reported heightened awareness of influenza and pneumococcal vaccines post-pandemic. However, HCPs highlighted a complex situation: while the pandemic positively impacted public awareness, it also led to a new wave of vaccine hesitancy fuelled by negative discourse surrounding COVID-19 vaccines. As many commented, “The pandemic is the best health education… Over the past three years, public awareness of vaccines has undoubtedly increased. However, when it comes to ‘loyalty’ or the level of recognition and acceptance of vaccines, whether it has increased compared to the pre-pandemic, remains uncertain” (SZH1). The increased awareness did not consistently translate into trust or vaccine uptake. “There is a crisis of trust in vaccines, and it involves a backlash. After years of promoting influenza and pneumonia vaccines, a significant portion of elderly populations accepted vaccines. However, after COVID-19 vaccination policies, some family doctors reported a shift in sentiment and reluctance among the elderly” (SZH4). This emerging trust crisis could undermine the long-term efforts to promote other critical vaccines among this population.

Increased awareness of life-course vaccination but elderly vaccination remains a low priority

Although many elderly individuals did not fully grasp the professional concept of “life-course vaccination”, the pandemic has raised awareness of the importance of adult and elderly vaccination. One HCP observed, “They were unaware ‘do adults also get vaccinated?’, thinking that only children receive vaccines. This lack of awareness has been addressed to some extent” (SZH3). However, challenges remain in prioritizing elderly vaccination within public health efforts. CDC officers acknowledged institutional priorities, such as the emphasis on NIP vaccines and performance evaluations centered on childhood vaccination. Such persisting institutional inertia hindered elderly vaccination. One noted, “Apart from the free programs for the elderly, as a CDC or government agency, we haven’t specifically organized vaccination campaigns or education targeting the elderly… Our main focus is on NIP vaccination programs” (SZH7). This misalignment in priorities calls for institutional changes to better integrate elderly vaccination into public health strategies.

Insufficient supply of free vaccines due to mismatch between demand and procurement

A key barrier to elderly vaccination in Shenzhen is the mismatch between free vaccine demand and supply. HCPs expressed frustration over this imbalance: “Due to the limited doses, I can’t send out a mass notification to the elderly” (SZEH4). While previous years saw better alignment, a shortage was pronounced in the 2023/24 season. An HCP noted, “There have been more doses in previous years, but this year there seems to be a shortage. I wonder if procurement was higher back then” (SZH1). The shift towards allocating vaccines based on estimates rather than actual needs has further exacerbated this issue: “We used to report the doses we needed, and we would receive that amount. But now, they allocate the vaccines based on their estimation” (SZH3). The provision of free influenza and pneumococcal vaccines for residents over 60 in Shenzhen is a key public welfare policy and an important tool for community health centers to enhance elderly care management. However, the initiative’s effectiveness is hindered by the significant demand-supply mismatch, limiting its potential to improve health outcomes. One HCP pointed out, “For health centers, the free vaccine program is an important means to enhance services and manage elderly patients. They are actively promoting it. The main issue now is the insufficient vaccine supply” (SZH2).

Shortage of manpower and underutilization of family doctors

Shenzhen’s vaccination efforts are hindered by a shortage of dedicated staff in community health centers, where personnel are often stretched thin and tasked with multiple responsibilities, limiting their ability to focus on vaccination-related tasks. One HCP reported, “Currently, personnel are generally in short supply and often have multiple roles, not dedicated full-time to vaccination-related work” (SZH2). This strain affects service efficiency, resulting in delays in elderly vaccination. Some HCPs suggested involving family doctors in vaccination efforts. However, their role remains underutilized: “Family doctors often feel that vaccination work is not related to them, honestly speaking. They are not proactive in educating each elderly person about vaccination…It’s because they don’t have any work requirements directly related to it, and they simply lack that awareness” (SZH3). The lack of awareness and engagement of family doctors represents a missed opportunity to leverage their trusted relationships with elderly patients to boost vaccination rates.

Discussion

Reflections on the framework and tools

Our findings reveal that elderly vaccination attitudes in Shenzhen are shaped by interconnected factors at the individual, micro-social, intermediate-social, and macro-social levels, with the COVID-19 pandemic serving as a cross-cutting influence. Macro-social structures, particularly media and political compliance, reinforce micro-social and individual influences on elderly vaccination. In contrast to Hong Kong, where vaccine confidence is more directly tied to the vaccine product-specific attributes [16], vaccine confidence in Shenzhen is more strongly rooted in trust in government and compliance with its policies. This aligns well with the CMA framework, highlighting how multi-level factors interact to shape individual health behaviors. These levels are not discrete but dynamically interconnected. For example, macro-level discourses around national responsibility may inform family discussions (micro-level), which in turn shape the elderly’s individual intentions. Likewise, policy restrictions at the intermediate level (e.g., free vaccine access tied to Hukou) influence interpersonal conversations and affect individual perceptions of fairness and motivation.

Additionally, while the WHO’s BeSD tool effectively captures proximal factors, our study emphasizes the need to incorporate broader sociopolitical influences, especially in contexts like China, where trust in government and media narratives significantly shape health behaviors. The integrated use of IMBP and CMA serves as a heuristic framework that captures both individual intentions and social structural forces, offering a more comprehensive lens to examine vaccine decision-making in a post-pandemic context. Future research should build on this approach to better integrate both proximal and distal drivers of vaccine hesitancy and uptake.

Empirical lessons from Shenzhen

Individual level

The pandemic heightened elderly awareness of their vulnerability and risks, which drove vaccine uptake. This finding aligns with previous studies, which link perceived disease severity and high susceptibility to the elderly’s influenza and pneumococcal vaccine uptake [6, 32]. However, awareness of pneumococcal vaccines remains lower than for influenza, mirroring trends observed in Hong Kong, the US, and the UK [16, 32]. Research in the US has identified persistent underutilization of pneumococcal vaccines among adults over 50 as a major public health concern, given that invasive pneumococcal disease is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in this age group [33]. Local health authorities should intensify efforts to promote dual vaccination by advocating the “left influenza and right pneumococcal” approach (administering both vaccines in one visit to encourage uptake) [27].

Despite surveys indicating high willingness for COVID-19 booster shots [34, 35], we observed notable vaccine fatigue among elderly participants. Normally, contributing factors include adverse effects, perceived low vaccine efficacy, and overwhelming doses administered within a short period [4]. Our findings highlight an additional critical factor: the experience of post-infection skepticism about vaccine efficacy following China’s reopening in early 2023. Research similarly indicates that COVID-19 inpatients and recovered patients often exhibit lower vaccine uptake and intentions [36, 37]. Also, individuals who experienced a higher degree of pandemic fatigue exhibited greater reluctance to receive subsequent COVID-19 vaccine doses [9]. Similarly, a Canadian study analyzing editorial cartoons identified vaccine fatigue arising from both the perceived ineffectiveness of vaccines and the seemingly endless cycle of variant-driven booster doses [38]. Although COVID-19 vaccinations have significantly diminished in post-pandemic China, residual vaccine fatigue appears to negatively affect elderly individuals’ attitudes toward other vaccines, such as influenza and pneumococcal vaccines. To counter this spillover effect, public health strategies should address new vaccine hesitancy and leverage the pandemic experience to promote future vaccinations [2, 5].

Micro-social level

Family influence emerged as a strong injunctive norm in elderly vaccination decision-making, with children acting as health proxies. This reflects a broader trend in Chinese society where family structures are deeply intertwined with healthcare decisions, supported by studies identifying family recommendations as a key factor in vaccine uptake among older adults [39]. However, one study in Guangzhou found that closer adult child-parent relations paradoxically associated with greater vaccine hesitancy toward pneumococcal vaccination, highlighting that health education regarding elderly vaccination should be extended to adult children [40]. Additionally, peer interactions and trusted relationships with HCPs served as effective descriptive norms. These interactions acted as informal prompts for vaccine uptake. Research from Asia, the UK, and Canada has confirmed that injunctive norms, such as social expectations, had the strongest impact on vaccination intention. Interpersonal interactions and cues, such as peer chats and HCP reminders, also influenced vaccination decisions, highlighting the role of descriptive norms in promoting vaccine uptake [41, 42].

Intermediate-social level

Limited community engagement in promoting non-COVID-19 vaccinations and disparities in vaccine eligibility due to local policies posed major barriers. While community-based arrangements facilitated COVID-19 vaccination among older adults [39], similar efforts for influenza and pneumococcal vaccines were lacking, as COVID-19 vaccination was considered a political priority with strict uptake requirements. Thus, community efforts were less proactive in promoting other vaccines. Community health initiatives should be expanded beyond COVID-19 to ensure comprehensive vaccine promotion and access.

Shenzhen’s policy of providing free influenza and pneumococcal vaccines to eligible elderly since 2016, while intended to enhance public health and welfare, has exposed structural disparities, particularly for “snowbird seniors” who are restricted by the Hukou system. Although free vaccination policies have boosted vaccine coverage in cities like Beijing [43, 44], research in Ningbo revealed that migrant populations still face lower coverage than local residents, as migrants are often excluded from the target population and must bear vaccination costs themselves [45]. Current academic research primarily focuses on health economics and cost-benefit aspects of free vaccine policies [46], with insufficient attention to the broader issue of vaccine inequality. Addressing disparities created by such eligibility policies requires a shift in focus. Future research should examine health inequalities triggered by these policies and identify strategies to improve equitable access to vaccines, particularly for marginalized populations.

Macro-social level

Elderly individuals demonstrated high trust in government recommendations, perceiving vaccination as a collective social responsibility. This aligns with broader cultural and political patterns in China, where the government is often seen as a “third-party regulator” ensuring vaccine safety [47]. This sense of civic responsibility and community protection effectively promoted COVID-19 vaccine uptake in China, consistent with findings indicating that governments can achieve vaccination targets by fostering a sense of participation and civic duty [48]. From a CMA perspective, this dynamic highlights the powerful influence of state authority in shaping individual health behaviors through institutional legitimacy and public trust. In this context, vaccination transcends individual decision-making, becoming embedded within a collective social obligation, further reinforcing the potential effectiveness of government-led public health campaigns. However, this positive dynamic was significantly challenged by the proliferation of misinformation surrounding COVID-19 vaccines on social media, which generated new hesitancy. Studies have shown that vaccine rumors and safety concerns in China and Hong Kong substantially undermine public confidence [49, 50]. Reflecting CMA’s emphasis on the political economy of health, this phenomenon highlights how the commercialization of digital media, coupled with uneven governmental regulation of online spaces, can weaken institutional credibility and public trust. The widespread Sinovac rumors are particularly illustrative of this issue, underscoring a critical need for improved messaging and public health communication strategies. To effectively rebuild trust, local governments should provide transparent and easily accessible information regarding vaccination rates, safety data, and adverse events to the elderly, actively shaping public perceptions that are influenced by these broader political-economic forces.

The pandemic profoundly increased public awareness of the importance of vaccines among older adults. Quantitative studies have shown a positive shift in their vaccination behavior towards influenza, pneumococcal, tetanus, and shingles [32, 51, 52]. However, this impact appeared to be time-sensitive. Drawing on the HBM, prior research in Hong Kong has shown that perceptions of susceptibility and disease severity following health crises, such as SARS and H5N1, declined over time, leading to diminished preventive behaviors [53, 54]. Similar behavioral fatigue (e.g., social distancing, travel avoidance) was observed during later stages of COVID-19 due to reduced perceived severity [55], a trend echoed by the vaccine fatigue highlighted in our study. Existing studies also support our findings. For instance, while influenza vaccine uptake in China increased during the 2020/21 season, it declined in subsequent seasons [45]. Global interest in pneumococcal and influenza vaccines also waned after an initial peak as vaccine fatigue set in [35]. HCPs in our study warned that assuming a sustained positive impact on elderly vaccination beyond the pandemic is too early. Studies in the US, Canada, and Cambodia also revealed vaccine fatigue and mentioned the need to seek clear global guidance on how to sustain and modify the COVID-19 immunization program and address vaccine fatigue post-pandemic [37, 38, 56]. Despite this fatigue, the pandemic underscored the critical importance of life-course vaccinations, intensifying adult immunization in public health strategies. This presents a unique opportunity to reshape policies, attitudes, and healthcare systems to prioritize life-course vaccination for adults [57]. Moving forward, China should capitalize on this shift, particularly with its aging population. Strengthening evaluation systems and performance indicators for adult immunization programs is vital. A top-down policy approach can institutionalize a life-course immunization framework to ensure comprehensive vaccine protection across all age groups, thereby reducing the disease burden and improving public health.

Practical issues

Insufficient vaccine supply and manpower shortage in community health centers were the main practical challenges. In China, family doctors, who manage elderly patients under the National Basic Public Health Services Program, remain underutilized resources in vaccine education. Increasing the involvement of these trusted family doctors in elderly vaccination could enhance vaccine uptake [39]. Providing family doctors with enhanced training in vaccine education and certification in vaccine administration tailored to the need of the elderly is essential. A study found that HCPs’ reluctance to recommend vaccinations was linked to limited knowledge about influenza vaccines, with accuracy rates as low as 6.1% [18]. This indicates a substantial need for improvement, given that patients are more likely to trust recommendations from family doctors over immunization nurses [58]. Additionally, improving vaccine supply management through better allocation based on age and health status, along with adopting data-driven procurement and distribution, is crucial.

Policy recommendations

This study identifies several feasible strategies to improve vaccination uptake among older adults in post-pandemic China. First, to overcome institutional barriers such as Hukou-related eligibility exclusions, local governments should expand free vaccination programs to cover all older residents regardless of residency status. This can be achieved through the inclusion of adult vaccines in local health benefit packages and permitting the use of personal medical savings accounts for vaccine payments. Second, vaccination services should be systematically integrated into primary care. Family doctors and community HCPs should routinely assess vaccination status during consultations with older adult patients and provide timely recommendations. Linking vaccination uptake to provider incentives and adopting integrated care models that combine vaccination with chronic disease management and health checkups may improve vaccine uptake. Third, tailored health education efforts are needed to reach elderly populations and their families. Campaigns should leverage elderly-friendly channels, such as television, radio, and community bulletin boards, involving senior celebrities and community leaders. Clear and culturally appropriate messaging should focus on the benefits, timing, and safety of recommended vaccines, while also addressing common misinformation.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides valuable qualitative insights into the drivers of elderly vaccination in the post-pandemic era, as well as the broader impact of the pandemic on vaccination behaviors among older adults in China. Although data saturation was achieved, the relatively small sample size and recruitment from community health centers and public parks may have excluded homebound or mobility-impaired individuals, introducing potential selection bias. Also, many elderly participants in Shenzhen are migrants from other regions of China who moved here in the 1980 s with diverse backgrounds and exposure to technology, possibly reflecting higher vaccine uptake compared with the general elderly population in less urbanized regions, particularly central and western China. This unique demographic profile may limit the generalizability of our findings. Therefore, future research should seek to include underrepresented subgroups of the elderly population, such as homebound individuals or those in long-term care facilities, to ensure a more comprehensive understanding of vaccination behaviors across different elderly populations.

Conclusion

This study highlights the multi-level drivers of elderly vaccination in Shenzhen, China, with key motivators including individual perceptions of vulnerability, vaccination benefits, family-driven decisions, peer influence, and HCP recommendations. Notably, the elderly exhibit greater trust in the government than in vaccines, viewing vaccination as a social responsibility.

However, challenges persist, including vaccine fatigue, limited community engagement beyond COVID-19 vaccination, disparities in free vaccine eligibility, and vaccine misinformation on social media. While the pandemic has heightened public awareness of life-course vaccination, it has simultaneously sparked new waves of vaccine hesitancy. Policy adjustments, greater family doctor involvement, and targeted public health communication strategies are critical for advancing elderly vaccination in post-pandemic China, addressing the needs of an aging population, and fostering a life-course immunization approach.
