Reactive oxygen species in thoracic aortic dissection: Insights into mechanisms and disease progression
Authors: Mingzhou Jiang, Fandi Mo, Genmao Cao, Shiyi Li, Weiguo Fu, Lixin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThoracic aortic dissection (TAD) is a life-threatening vascular disorder defined by disruption of the aortic intima and progressive degeneration of the medial layer, accompanied with false lumen formation. Accumulating evidence supports a pivotal contribution of reactive oxygen species (ROS) to the initiation and progression of TAD, primarily by driving oxidative stress-associated cellular events. This review summarizes the ROS production mechanism in TAD pathogenesis and discusses how ROS contribute to disease progression through oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and structural degradation of the aortic wall. We further examine the multilayered regulatory networks governing ROS activity, including transcriptional and epigenetic regulation, metabolic reprogramming, that collectively shape vascular dysfunction in TAD. In addition, we discuss potential ROS-targeted therapeutic treatments, including inhibition of ROS-generating enzymes, enhancement of antioxidant systems, modulation of downstream signaling pathways, and correction of metabolic reprogramming. We also critically discuss the translational limitations of current redox-targeted approaches, emphasizing the lack of disease specificity, limited clinical validation, and the challenge of selectively suppressing pathological ROS. This review aims to synthesize current evidence linking ROS dysregulation to the onset and progression of TAD, while highlighting emerging therapeutic strategies and their potential clinical implications.Keywords: Thoracic aortic dissection, Reactive oxygen species (ROS), Oxidative stress, Vascular dysfunction, Endothelial dysfunction

Introduction

Thoracic aortic dissection (TAD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular disorder that is clinically categorized into Stanford type A and type B or DeBakey types I–III, classifications that bear important mechanistic and prognostic significance [1]. Stanford type A dissections affect the ascending aorta and are frequently complicated by rupture, pericardial tamponade, and cerebral ischemia, accounting for their high early mortality. By comparison, type B dissections, which spare the ascending aorta, tend to progress through more diverse pathological trajectories characterized by inflammation-associated false lumen expansion, thrombosis, and medial degeneration [2]. Beyond sporadic presentations, TAD also arises in the setting of inherited connective tissue disorders, most notably Marfan syndrome, which results from pathogenic variants in the fibrillin-1 (FBN1) gene, markedly increase susceptibility to medial degeneration and thoracic aortic dissection [3]. Despite these clinical distinctions, all TAD subtypes share core pathological features, including intimal tearing, medial degeneration, and formation of a false lumen, which can impair perfusion to visceral organs and lead to arterial occlusion [4]. Beyond visceral ischemia, TAD poses considerable risks, including spinal cord ischemia causing to paraplegia, cerebral ischemia leading to stroke, and catastrophic aortic rupture, which is often fatal [5,6].

Dysregulated production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) has emerged as a contributor to the initiation and progression of a broad spectrum of cardiovascular disorders. Under normal conditions, tightly regulated ROS signaling supports vascular development, endothelial integrity, and adaptive stress responses, while disease-associated effects emerge when ROS generation becomes excessive, mislocalized, or persistently sustained [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Building on this concept, oxidative stress mediated by ROS has emerged as a fundamental factor in the initiation and progression of TAD [[15], [16], [17]].

Within TAD, excessive ROS amplify inflammatory responses, disrupt vascular homeostasis and accelerate structural deterioration of the aortic wall. By activating redox-sensitive pathways, ROS promote inflammatory gene expression and immune cell recruitment, sustaining a localized inflammatory environment [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Excessive ROS production damages endothelial cells (ECs) and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), actively shape cellular fate by influencing stress-adaptive processes such as autophagy,apoptosis and ferroptosis [22,23]. Mitochondrial dysfunction further forms a feed-forward loop that amplifies ROS production [24,25]. In addition, ROS promote the activation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) such as MMP-2 and MMP-9, contributing to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. Together, these processes link oxidative stress to medial degeneration, false lumen expansion, and aortic wall destabilization [[26], [27], [28], [29], [30]].

Importantly, because direct mechanistic studies in human TAD tissue are inherently limited by the acute and catastrophic nature of the disease, and systematic analyses directly linking ROS signaling to human TAD pathology remain scarce, a substantial portion of mechanistic insight into ROS signaling is derived from experimental TAD models and from closely related aortic pathologies. Commonly used models include β-aminopropionitrile (BAPN) induced medial degeneration [31], angiotensin II (Ang II) infusion models that mimic hypertension associated aortic injury [32], and genetically engineered models such as FBN1 mutant mice that recapitulate MFS [33]. These models have been valuable for defining links between redox imbalance, inflammation, mitochondrial injury, and matrix remodeling. Notably, many of these experimental models exhibit overlapping features of aortic aneurysm and dissection, and do not fully recapitulate the distinct pathological progression observed in human TAD, which may limit the specificity of mechanistic interpretations [34,35].

Redox-targeted interventions are being explored as potential therapeutic approaches for TAD. Most evidence still comes from experimental models or broader vascular disease contexts. Some studies suggest that modulation of defined antioxidant pathways may exert protective effects in vascular disease contexts [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. In addition, some pharmacological agents could enhance endogenous antioxidant defenses and protect against oxidative injury and inflammation in aortic pathology [40,41]. However, direct clinical evidence in TAD remains limited. Studies of broad antioxidant supplementation in other populations have shown inconsistent results and may increase mortality in selected groups [42,43]. This highlights the context dependent role of ROS and the need for more precise redox modulation.

Despite growing recognition of ROS as a pivotal factor in TAD, several questions remain unresolved. It is still unclear how ROS activity changes across disease stages. The way redox signaling coordinates communication among ECs, VSMCs, macrophages, and neutrophils also remains incompletely defined. In addition, the impact of ROS on cell fate and matrix remodeling requires further clarification. Furthermore, the lack of TAD-specific oxidative biomarkers and the limited bioavailability of redox-targeting drugs hinder clinical translation.

This review synthesizes evidence from experimental models and limited human data. It aims to clarify the physiological and pathological roles of ROS and provides a unified framework to guide future mechanistic studies and more targeted redox-based therapeutic strategies in TAD.

Role of ROS in TAD pathogenesis and progression

ROS production in TAD predominantly originates from several interconnected cellular sources, including NOX enzymes, iNOS, uncoupled eNOS, and mitochondrial ETC leakage, which together establish and propagate oxidative imbalance within the aortic wall. As summarized in Fig. 1, these enzymatic and mitochondrial mechanisms form a hierarchical redox cascade in which NOX activation destabilizes nitric oxide signaling, promotes eNOS uncoupling, and ultimately amplifies mitochondrial ROS production. The following subsections outline how ROS are generated through these pathways and how their downstream effects initiate and accelerate TAD development.

ROS generation and signaling networks driving thoracic aortic dissection (TAD). ROS are generated in the aortic wall primarily through NOX, iNOS, eNOS uncoupling, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Excessive ROS accumulation promotes oxidative stress and activates multiple ROS-mediated pathways, including MAPK, inflammatory, metabolic, and redox-sensitive signaling. These pathways contribute to key pathological changes in the aortic wall, including ECM remodeling, VSMC apoptosis and phenotypic switching, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Collectively, these alterations lead to aortic wall destabilization in TAD. In addition, a positive feedback loop is formed, in which aortic wall injury further enhances ROS production through NOX activation and mitochondrial ROS generation, thereby amplifying oxidative stress.

ROS generation mechanisms in TAD

ROS encompass a group of chemically reactive intermediates produced as by-products of cellular metabolism, including superoxide anions (O2•-), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and peroxynitrite (ONOO−), increase markedly during TAD development [[44], [45], [46], [47]]. At disease onset, decreased SOD expression and elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) in the aortic media indicate impaired antioxidant defenses and increased oxidative injury [[48], [49], [50], [51]].

Within the aortic wall, resident ECs and VSMCs constitute major intrinsic sources of ROS, primarily through NADPH oxidase isoforms (NOX1, NOX2, and NOX4), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) leakage and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) uncoupling, each contributing to redox imbalance under pathological conditions [52,53]. Genetic deletion of ROS-generating enzymes (NOX-1, iNOS, and p47phox) alleviates hypertension-induced arterial wall damage in experimental models [38,[54], [55], [56], [57], [58]].

NOX enzymes represent a dominant upstream source of vascular ROS in aortic disease. In Ang II–infused mice, vascular O2•- production in the aorta increases by approximately 2–3-fold, and this response is virtually abolished in p47phox-deficient animals [59]. In human thoracic aortic aneurysm, circulating and tissue markers of NOX2 activity are significantly elevated compared with controls, supporting enhanced NOX-driven oxidative stress in diseased aortic walls [60]. Mechanistically, NOX1/2/4 activation has been shown to lie upstream of dihydrofolate reductase deficiency, and to associate with increased aortic hydrogen peroxide production and aneurysm progression [37]. Meanwhile, a previous study demonstrated ROS induced by cigarette smoke can inactivate the Nrf2/SIRT1 pathway, thereby activating NOX and iNOS and enhancing ROS production, suggesting that this pathway may also contribute to TAD pathogenesis [61,62]. NOX-derived superoxide establishes the initial oxidative microenvironment, which provides a mechanistic basis for sustained oxidative injury in the thoracic aorta.

eNOS normally protects the vasculature by generating nitric oxide (NO), but loss of tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) uncouples the enzyme and shifts it toward O2•− production. This promotes endothelial dysfunction and amplifies the oxidative burden within the aortic wall [[63], [64], [65]]. Folic acid supplementation has been reported to improve eNOS coupling and alleviates vascular damage [66]. Once eNOS becomes uncoupled, it reinforces the oxidative burden initiated by NOX activation. This interaction between NOX and eNOS represents a critical redox amplification step that propagates oxidative stress to mitochondria.

Persistent oxidative stress from NOX activation and uncoupled eNOS further increases mitochondrial ETC leakage, converting mitochondria from ROS targets into active amplifiers of oxidative injury [[67], [68], [69]]. In TAD models, VSMCs exhibit reduced ETC complex expression, impaired respiratory chain activity, decreased mitochondrial DNA levels, and enhanced ROS-driven mitochondrial autophagy, all contributing to VSMC dysfunction and medial degeneration [70,71].

Given the importance of mitochondrial redox balance in shaping cellular ROS levels, peroxisome proliferator activated receptor gamma coactivator 1 alpha (PGC-1α) functions as a central transcriptional regulator linking mitochondrial activity with cellular energy demand and antioxidant capacity [72]. Through activation of redox-responsive transcriptional programs, this pathway engages nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 1 and 2 (Nrf1 and Nrf2) and promotes the antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD and glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1), thereby strengthening cellular defenses against oxidative stress. Reduced PGC-1α expression has been observed in aneurysmal and dissected aortic tissue [73]. And Pluijm et al. demonstrated that loss of the ECM associated glycoprotein Fibulin-4 disrupts thoracic aortic architecture, resulting in the reactive upregulation of TGF-β, which suppresses PGC-1α transcription and further reinforces oxidative stress [74].

Thus, NOX activation, eNOS uncoupling, and mitochondrial dysfunction form an interdependent redox cascade rather than isolated ROS sources. NOX-initiated superoxide disrupts NO signaling, uncoupled eNOS intensifies oxidative stress, and mitochondria amplify and sustain ROS production, which collectively drive endothelial injury and medial degeneration in TAD.

ROS-mediated exacerbation of inflammation in TAD

In TAD, inflammatory activation is a central driver of aortic wall destabilization, false lumen expansion, and thrombus formation, and this process is tightly modulated by excessive ROS production. While resident vascular cells such as ECs and VSMCs constitute important intrinsic sources of ROS, infiltrating immune cells provide an additional and dynamically regulated source during disease progression [[75], [76], [77]]. Excessive ROS activate multiple redox-responsive transcriptional programs, involving nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)) signaling pathways. This activation disrupts endothelial tight junctions, upregulates adhesion molecules, and promote proinflammatory mediators, thereby facilitating immune cell recruitment and infiltration into the aortic wall [52,78,79]. In addition, oxidative stress promotes proteolytic remodeling of the aortic ECM through activation of MMP-2 and MMP-9. The resulting bioactive matrix fragments further reinforce inflammatory signaling within the dissected aortic wall.

Importantly, ROS-mediated inflammatory amplification in TAD arises from coordinated interactions among multiple immune cell populations within the aortic wall. Within dissected aortic tissue, macrophages contribute substantially to local oxidative burden through NOX-dependent ROS production and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby enhancing cytokine release and matrix degradation [80,81]. Neutrophils are rapidly recruited to the dissection site, where neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation aggravates medial injury and perpetuates inflammation [[82], [83], [84]]. I Adaptive immune cells, including T lymphocytes, are present in human and experimental TAD lesions and may further shape the inflammatory microenvironment through redox-sensitive cytokine signaling [85]. Together, these immune cell populations sustain a pro-oxidative and pro-inflammatory microenvironment that accelerates aortic wall degeneration.

ROS-driven inflammatory signaling in TAD can be broadly categorized into inflammatory cytokines, chemokine family, interferon (IFN) family, and colony-stimulating factor (CSF) family, which collectively contribute to vascular wall destabilization [77].

Various cytokines play central roles in ROS-amplified inflammation in TAD. Increased circulating levels of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) have been reported in both porcine models of TAD and patients with Stanford type A TAD, where higher concentrations are associated with more advanced disease states [[86], [87], [88]]. In addition, IL-1β drives ECM destabilization through activation of MMP-2 and MMP-9, thereby aggravating medial injury [87]. Moreover, IL-1β can further amplify ROS generation by enhancing NOX activity. Another notable cytokine is IL-33, which can also increase ROS production through NOX activation [89,90]. IL-33 has been recognized as a novel biomarker for TAD, as it is passively released into the extracellular space upon ROS-induced apoptosis, serving as an alarmin [90]. Oxidative modification may further alter IL-33 activity [91]. Conversely, IL-10 may counteract oxidative stress by suppressing NOX activation and NF-κB signaling [92].

Increased serum tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) levels have also been reported in patients with TAD, where they are associated with VSMC apoptosis and adverse prognosis [[93], [94], [95]]. TNF-α may induce mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in a redox imbalance [96]. TNF-α and IL-1β further reinforce redox inflammation through NF-κB related signaling [97].

Chemokines from both the CC and CXC subfamilies, including CCL-2, CCL-3, CXCL-1, and CXCL-5, promote inflammatory leukocyte recruitment into the dissected aortic wall, thereby enhancing oxidative injury, MMP expression, and medial destabilization [[98], [99], [100]].

Among interferons, IFN-γ has been implicated in TAD pathogenesis, given that its serum levels are positively correlated with disease progression [101]. IFN-γ can upregulate ROS synthesis by activating NOX in macrophages, enhancing macrophage activation and promoting matrix-degrading enzyme expression, thereby contributing to progressive medial injury [102] Antioxidant intervention could attenuate IFN-γ–driven NF-κB signaling, highlighting the redox sensitivity of this inflammatory pathway [103].

Within the CSF family, multi-colony-stimulating factor (multi-CSF) and granulocyte-macrophage CSF (GM-CSF) are the primary participants in TAD inflammation [104,105]. GM-CSF amplifies inflammatory signaling by stimulating the JAK/STAT pathway, which amplifies ROS signaling. By sustaining myeloid cell activation and ROS production, GM-CSF further amplifies ECM degradation and VSMCs injury in TAD [106].

Collectively, excessive ROS activate inflammatory signaling, amplify cytokine and chemokine networks, and recruit innate and adaptive immune cells within the dissected aortic wall. This coordinated inflammatory network accelerates ECM degradation, promotes VSMC injury, and destabilizes medial architecture, thereby directly facilitating false lumen expansion and aortic wall failure in TAD.

ROS-mediated structural deterioration of the aortic wall in TAD

In TAD pathogenesis and progression, inflammatory stimuli resulting from insufficient blood supply to the vasa vasorum contribute to excessive ROS accumulation, leading to oxidative stress. This ischemic and oxidative injury reduces medial integrity, increases susceptibility to shear stress, which further increases the risk of false lumen formation and vessel rupture [107,108].

In the initial phase of TAD, ROS driven ECM disorganization promotes collagen and elastin degradation, thereby compromising aortic wall stability [[109], [110], [111], [112]]. MMPs are key enzymes secreted by myofibroblasts that degrade ECM components [113]. In TAD, ROS induce the hyperactivation of myofibroblasts, resulting in the overexpression of MMPs and exacerbating ECM degradation [114]. Besides, ROS regulate MMP function through several regulatory layers. At the transcriptional level, H2O2 and superoxide activate redox-responsive signaling cascades, which enhance MMP-2 and MMP-9 gene transcription in vascular cells [27,28]. ROS also influence post-transcriptional regulation of MMP by altering microRNA networks [115,116]. Additionally, ROS like ONOO− increase the secretion of latent pro-MMPs through oxidative modification of tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) [29]. These ROS-driven alterations collectively accelerate the delamination of the aortic wall [117,118].

During TAD progression, oxidative stress promotes a phenotypic shift of VSMCs toward a synthetic phenotype that contributes to vascular remodeling, particularly under Ang II related hypertensive stress and enhanced NOX activity [[119], [120], [121], [122], [123]]. NOX1-derived ROS activate MAPK signaling in VSMCs, enhance VSMC proliferation and migration, and impair fibulin-4 related matrix stability [124]. Meanwhile, in vascular fibroblasts, NOX2 facilitates Ang II-induced hypertension and thickening of the tunica media, which is related to medial remodeling in vascular tissue [125]. NOX4 has also been linked to exacerbate vascular oxidative stress. For instance, in MFS mouse models, VSMC-specific NOX4 knockout reduced elastic fiber fragmentation and slowed the progression of aortic dilation [126]. However, whether NOX4 deficiency can directly prevent aortic dissection remains unclear. In the context of hypertension-induced VSMC dysfunction, NOX5 facilitates actin polymerization and migration in VSMCs, but its precise contribution to TAD pathogenesis has not yet been fully established [127].

Collectively, ROS mediated structural deterioration in TAD operates at multiple interconnected levels. Impairment of the vasa vasorum promotes ischemia and oxidative stress in the outer media, and ROS driven activation of MMPs accelerates collagen and elastin degradation. At the same time, NOX-dependent redox signaling reshapes VSMC phenotype and promotes vascular remodeling under hypertensive stress. Together, these processes link redox imbalance directly to progressive aortic wall destabilization and increased susceptibility to dissection and rupture. (Fig. 1).

ROS-mediated mechanisms linking risk factors to TAD

Numerous genetic, lifestyle, and metabolic risk factors contribute to TAD development, many of which converge on oxidative stress. Long-term smoking and hypertension, significantly increase the risk of TAD by inducing vascular injury and hemodynamic stress [[128], [129], [130]]. Moreover, metabolic disorders such as hyperlipidemia and hyperglycemia facilitate TAD progression through atherosclerosis, chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and oxidative stress [129,131,132].

MFS is a genetic disease related to FBN1 mutations. This genetic defect increases the susceptibility of the aortic wall to mechanical loading. Impaired Sirt3 and Nrf2 activity has been reported in patients with MFS,leading to compromised antioxidant defenses and excessive accumulation of ROS [[133], [134], [135], [136]]. Sirt3 deficiency impairs mitochondrial function and reduces ROS scavenging, whereas Nrf2 dysfunction downregulates antioxidant enzymes, including SOD2 and catalase, further exacerbating oxidative stress. Elevated ROS levels enhance VSMC apoptosis, ECM degradation via MMP activation, and inflammatory responses, all contributing to TAD progression among MFS patients [137,138].

Hypertension increases vascular shear stress and mechanical stress on VSMCs. Furthermore, in hypertension, Ang II facilitates NOX activation, which increases ROS generation [139]. ROS promotes VSMC proliferation and activates the JAK-STAT pathway, further amplifying the hypertensive effects of Ang II, driving the occurrence and progression of TAD [140,141].

Hyperlipidemia increases ROS production in blood vessels by facilitating lipid peroxidation, triggering inflammatory responses, and enhancing oxidative stress. Under these conditions, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) becomes more prone to oxidative modification, which activates NOX and amplifies ROS production. Lipid peroxidation byproducts further enhance oxidative stress by stimulating ROS generation. Hyperlipidemia-associated inflammatory mediators promote NADPH oxidase activation and intensify superoxide formation. Hyperlipidemia significantly aggravates mitochondrial dysfunction, which results in excessive ROS generation, thereby damaging endothelial function [142,143].

Hyperglycemia also enhances ROS production through advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) formation and mitochondrial overload. Excess reducing equivalents promote electron leakage from the ETC and increase mitochondrial ROS accumulation, thereby aggravating redox imbalance in ECs and VSMCs and contributing to medial degeneration [144].

These findings indicate that diverse genetic, hemodynamic, and metabolic risk factors converge on a common mechanism of redox imbalance in TAD. Through NOX activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired antioxidant defenses, these risk factors promote endothelial dysfunction, VSMC injury, inflammatory amplification, and matrix degradation, thereby linking heterogeneous risk factors to a unified redox driven mechanism of aortic wall destabilization.

Transcription regulation of ROS in TAD

ROS regulate vascular gene expression in TAD through both redox sensitive transcription factors and upstream signaling regulators. Among these, NF-κB represents one of the best characterized transcriptional mediators of ROS driven vascular inflammation [145]. In TAD, excessive ROS enhance TNF-α and the toll-like receptor family (TLR4) related inflammatory signaling, which subsequently stimulate NF-κB [146]. Upon activation, NF-κB signaling drives the expression of proinflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and TNF-α, amplifying the inflammatory cascade [147]. Increased NF-κB activation has been observed in dissected human thoracic aortic tissue and in experimental TAD models, where it is associated with inflammatory cell infiltration, medial degeneration, and disease severity [[148], [149], [150]].

The MAPK signaling pathway, particularly c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and ERK, acts as a ROS responsive upstream kinase pathway [151]. MAPK signaling contributes to ROS related stress responses, including apoptosis and inflammatory activation. ROS dependent activation of upstream kinases can promote JNK signaling and apoptotic responses [152]. In TAD, aberrant activation of JNK and ERK pathways has been associated with VSMC apoptosis, phenotypic switching, and ECM degradation in the dissected aortic media [[153], [154], [155]].

ROS regulate antioxidant gene expression primarily through the redox-sensitive transcription factor Nrf2. The Keap1-Nrf2-antioxidant response element (ARE) axis induces genes involved in NADPH production, glutathione metabolism, and mitochondrial redox homeostasis [[156], [157], [158]]. Under oxidative stress, Nrf2 dissociates from Keap1, translocates into the nucleus, and activates antioxidant genes such as HO-1, NQO1, and SOD [[159], [160], [161]]. In BAPN-treated mice, Nrf2 activation attenuates TAD development [41,162]. These findings provide experimental evidence that Nrf2-dependent transcriptional regulation modulates oxidative stress and disease susceptibility in TAD.

Redox balance is closely linked to AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK). Under oxidative stress, AMPK activation promotes metabolic adaptation to preserve NADPH availability [163]. AMPK also modulates fatty acid synthesis and oxidation, thereby influencing cellular redox balance [164]. However, the specific role of AMPK in TAD remains incompletely defined. Given its established role in redox sensing and metabolic homeostasis, AMPK may indirectly influence vascular oxidative stress and cell fate decisions relevant to TAD progression.

Together, these redox sensitive transcriptional and signaling regulators play complementary roles within the vascular redox network. NF-κB and Nrf2 directly regulate inflammatory and antioxidant gene expression, whereas MAPK and AMPK indirectly shape transcriptional responses by coupling stress and metabolic signals to downstream gene regulation. These pathways identify potential regulatory nodes within the inflammation and oxidative stress axis, although their therapeutic relevance in TAD requires further validation. However, transcriptional and signaling regulation alone cannot fully explain persistent redox imbalance, which is also shaped by epigenetic modifications.

Epigenetic regulation of ROS in TAD

Beyond transcriptional regulation, oxidative stress also engages epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modification, and noncoding RNA regulation, to modulate gene expression and cellular function in TAD. Epigenetic enzymes may also influence mitochondrial dynamics and ROS production. For example, Coactivator-associated arginine methyltransferase 1 (CARM1) mediated dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) modification has been linked to mitochondrial fission and ROS amplification [165]. In the thoracic aorta, histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) has been reported to enhance Keap1 dependent suppression of Nrf2, hence inhibiting antioxidant responses during oxidative stress [166]. Conversely, endothelial SIRT1 activity mitigates ROS induced cell injury and may support vascular protection [167,168].

ROS interact with microRNAs (miRNAs) to modulate oxidative stress damage and disrupt vascular homeostasis. For example, ROS induced miR-21 expression promotes SMC proliferation through NF-κB signaling pathway [169]. Simultaneously, miR-195 may aggravate oxidative stress by impairing mitochondrial regulation through MFN2 [170]. In ECs, ROS associated miR-200c upregulation promotes senescence and apoptosis [171]. Conversely, miR-145 may counteract oxidative injury by suppressing inflammation and apoptosis [172,173].

Beyond miRNAs, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are increasingly recognized as potential regulators of redox balance in vascular disease. Although direct evidence in TAD remains limited, oxidative stress–responsive lncRNAs such as MALAT1 and H19 may influence chromatin accessibility and transcriptional regulation in VSMCs and ECs [[174], [175], [176]].

Meanwhile, emerging studies indicate that ROS can reshape epigenetic landscapes through redox-sensitive chromatin modifications, including alterations in histone acetylation and methylation [177,178]. These chromatin changes are proposed to reinforce pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative transcriptional programs, but their specific roles in TAD pathogenesis remain to be fully elucidated.

Collectively, these epigenetic mechanisms represent an additional layer of ROS regulation that modulates transcriptional responses and vascular cell function. Beyond epigenetic regulation, metabolic reprogramming further connects redox signaling with cellular energy balance and functional adaptation.

ROS-mediated metabolic reprogramming in TAD

Beyond transcriptional and epigenetic regulation, oxidative stress also reshapes cellular metabolism in TAD. ECs and VSMCs undergo distinct metabolic adaptations under oxidative and inflammatory stress, facilitating cellular dysfunction and vascular instability. Elevated ROS levels promote mitochondrial membrane and DNA damage, impairing oxidative phosphorylation and promote further mitochondrial ROS accumulation [179]. During TAD progression, VSMCs tend to reduce fatty acid oxidation and increase glycolytic reliance, while ECs demonstrate enhanced glycolytic activity in the setting of mitochondrial impairment and lipid accumulation [[180], [181], [182]]. These metabolic changes promote lipid peroxidation, glutathione depletion, and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) inactivation, thereby increasing susceptibility to ferroptosis and contributing to medial degeneration [183,184].

During acute TAD, systemic stress responses may promote transient hyperglycemia and impaired insulin signaling. Persistent inflammation and ROS accumulation can further worsen insulin resistance and lipid metabolic dysfunction [185,186]. Metabolic reprogramming in ECs and VSMCs, specifically the disruption in branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism, has been associated with arterial structural instability and inflammation in TAD [187]. These findings suggest that systemic metabolic stress may reinforce local oxidative injury and aortic wall vulnerability.

Abnormal glucose metabolism promotes AGE accumulation, activating RAGE–MAPK signaling and disrupting endothelial homeostasis in TAD. This pathway impairs eNOS activity and amplifies oxidative injury [188,189]. Increased glycolytic flux also raises diacylglycerol (DAG) levels and activates protein kinase C (PKC) signaling, thereby contributing to endothelial dysfunction and MMP expression [190,191]. Although glycolysis may initially increase as a compensatory response to mitochondrial dysfunction, persistent oxidative stress ultimately disrupts key glycolytic enzymes and further worsening energy metabolic disturbances [192].

Although direct evidence in TAD remains limited, substantial evidence from cardiovascular disease demonstrates that ROS influence lipid metabolism and fatty acid oxidation [193,194]. During oxidative stress, suppressed fatty acid oxidation and lipid intermediate accumulation promote lipotoxicity, endothelial dysfunction, and mitochondrial ROS production [195]. In addition, glycated or oxidized LDL impairs mitochondrial function and activates lectin-like oxidized LDL receptor-1 (LOX-1) related endothelial signaling, further aggravating oxidative vascular injury [[196], [197], [198]]. These lipid related redox disturbances may increase endothelial vulnerability and contribute to medial degeneration in TAD.

ROS related alterations in amino acid metabolism and nucleotide synthesis in ECs and VSMCs may further affect vascular cell repair and inflammatory responses, although direct evidence in TAD remains limited. (Fig. 2) [199].

Multilevel regulation of ROS generation during the pathogenesis of thoracic aortic dissection (TAD). ROS production in TAD is regulated by integrated signaling, metabolic, and epigenetic mechanisms across ECs, VSMCs, and infiltrating immune cells. Pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-κB, MAPK (p38/ERK1/2), and PKC, promote NOX activation through p47phox phosphorylation and enhance mitochondrial ROS generation. Metabolic dysregulation further amplifies oxidative stress, as abnormal glucose metabolism activates the AGE–RAGE–MAPK axis, while lipid overload and free fatty acid oxidation contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction and ER stress.

Epigenetic regulators, including histone deacetylases, SIRT1, and ROS-responsive microRNAs, modulate redox homeostasis by influencing NOX activity, antioxidant capacity, and mitochondrial function. Crosstalk between metabolic stress and inflammation promotes macrophage M1 polarization, NET formation, and sustained ROS amplification. Together, these interconnected pathways establish a redox network that drives oxidative stress and vascular injury during TAD progression

Ultimately, the upstream transcriptional, epigenetic, and metabolic regulatory networks converge on cell fate decisions, determining whether vascular cells adapt, undergo phenotypic switching, survive, or progress toward programmed cell death during TAD progression.

ROS-mediated cell fate regulation in TAD

In addition to transcriptional, epigenetic, and metabolic regulation, ROS play a pivotal role in shaping cell fate and cell state programs, including apoptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, and mitophagy, and VSMC phenotypic modulation, all of which influence the initiation and progression of TAD.

Under transient oxidative stress, regulated apoptosis may remove irreversibly damaged vascular cells. In contrast, sustained ROS exposure promotes excessive apoptosis, leading to medial cell loss and structural weakening. In TAD, ROS driven lipid peroxidation generates reactive aldehydes that disrupt mitochondrial membrane integrity, promotes cytochrome c release, and activates caspase dependent apoptosis [200]. Furthermore, oxidative stress also induces ER stress, which further amplifies VSMC apoptosis and contributes to medial weakening [201,202]. Lipid peroxidation products such as 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE) further modulate apoptotic signaling [203,204]. Notably, studies have demonstrated that low or transient 4-HNE exposure may induce HO-1 expression and p38/MAPK activation as an adaptive stress response. However, persistent accumulation of lipid peroxidation products may shift this response toward sustained oxidative injury and apoptosis [205,206]. Besides, since multiple lipid peroxidation products converge on PKC-related pathways, PKC signaling may represents an additional regulatory node influencing apoptosis in TAD [207].

Autophagy is a lysosome mediated pathway that is generally protective but becomes dysregulated under excessive oxidative stress in TAD. Lipid peroxidation products, including 4-HNE, can induce autophagy in vascular ECs and VSMCs through several signaling pathways, including MAPK, phosphoinositide 3-kinase/AKT (PI3K/AKT), and PKC [206,208]. Under moderate hypoxic or oxidative conditions, ROS activate AMPK and suppress mTOR, thereby promoting adaptive autophagy [209,210]. However, when lipid peroxidation becomes excessive, 4-HNE forms inhibitory complexes with liver kinase B1 (LKB1), suppressing the LKB1–AMPK axis. This restores mTOR activity and shifts autophagy toward a maladaptive process that compromises cellular homeostasis and contributes to vascular cell dysfunction in TAD [211].

Ferroptosis is an iron dependent form of lipid oxidative cell death increasingly implicated in TAD pathogenesis [212]. Mitochondrial dysfunction, glutathione (GSH) depletion, GPX4 inactivation, and long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) mediated lipid remodeling increase susceptibility to ferroptosis [184]. Iron accumulation further accelerates ROS generation, promoting VSMC and endothelial ferroptosis and contributing to medial degeneration and false lumen expansion.

Beyond apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis, mitophagy represents another ROS-sensitive process relevant to TAD as the selective removal of damaged mitochondria. Under physiological conditions, fluctuations in mitochondrial ROS contribute to mitochondrial damage signaling, which in turn facilitates PINK1 stabilization and Parkin recruitment to initiate mitophagy [213]. However, excessive ROS disrupt this surveillance system, resulting in the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, sustained mtROS overproduction, and release of mitochondrial danger signals [214]. Although direct evidence in TAD remains limited, findings from cardiovascular studies suggest that impaired mitophagy may contribute to VSMC apoptosis, phenotypic modulation, and ECM degeneration, all of which weaken the aortic media [25,69].

ROS also drive VSMC phenotypic modulation, which is a key pathological event underlying medial weakening and TAD progression. Excessive ROS reduce contractile marker expression and promote a synthetic and proinflammatory phenotype associated with increased matrix degradation [120,215]. This transition is mediated by redox-sensitive pathways, including MAPK, Ang II–NOX1/NOX4 and NF-κB, which repress contractile gene transcription and enhance expression of synthetic and inflammatory genes [[216], [217], [218]].

Overall, these findings illustrate that ROS regulate multiple cell fate and cell state programs that converge to weaken the aortic media in TAD. Excessive ROS promote apoptosis through mitochondrial and ER-stress pathways, induce autophagy under stress while driving maladaptive autophagic responses, and triggers ferroptosis through lipid peroxidation and GPX4 inactivation. Dysregulated mitophagy further amplifies mitochondrial dysfunction and mtROS accumulation, exacerbating cellular injury. In parallel, ROS drive VSMC phenotypic modulation from a contractile to a synthetic, pro-inflammatory state, increasing ECM degradation and compromising medial integrity. Collectively, these ROS-dependent processes interact to promote structural instability and create a cellular environment highly susceptible to dissection (Fig. 3).

ROS-targeted therapeutic strategies in TAD

TAD is closely linked to oxidative stress and vascular destabilization, predominantly mediated by excessive ROS. Consequently, four primary ROS-targeted therapeutic strategies, including inhibition of ROS generation, enhancement of ROS clearance, modulation of ROS-related signaling pathways and metabolic modulation have garnered increasing interest. However, it is important to emphasize that ROS-targeted therapies are unlikely to replace established surgical repair or hemodynamic management, which remain the cornerstone of TAD treatment. Instead, redox modulation should be considered a potential adjunctive approach, with stage-specific application tailored to disease phase and clinical context, rather than a standalone therapeutic option.

The first focuses on inhibiting ROS-generating enzymes, especially NOXs. Selective NOX1/4 inhibitors, genetic NOX1 knockouts, and naturally derived compounds, including Setanaxib (GKT137831), lancemaside A (LMA) and senkyunolide I (SEI), are reported to attenuate NOX-mediated oxidative stress in experimental settings [219]. NOX1-deficient mice display reduced susceptibility to Ang II-induced dissection, with increased TIMP-1 expression and decreased ECM degradation [38,220,221]. NOX1/4 inhibitors appear to suppress ROS accumulation and improve eNOS coupling in vascular remodeling and Marfan animal models [[222], [223], [224]].

The second approach aims to enhance ROS detoxification and antioxidant capacity. SOD mimetics such as MnTBAP replicate the activity of the superoxide dismutase, facilitating superoxide dismutation, thus reducing intracellular oxidative stress [225]. Meanwhile, exogenous antioxidants, including polyphenolic compounds, such as resveratrol have exhibited protective effects in BAPN-induced TAD mouse models, associated with improved eNOS function, reduced NOX activity, lower MMP expression, and decreased aortic dilation and dissection incidence [[226], [227], [228], [229]]. In MFS models, resveratrol was also shown to improve vascular structural integrity and antioxidant enzyme activity [226,230]. Additionally, endogenous antioxidants, including vitamin C and NAD+ precursors (NMN, and NR), could restore eNOS activity, facilitate ROS clearance, and improve aortic endothelial function [[231], [232], [233]].

A third approach targets downstream ROS-related signaling pathways. Agents modulating MAPK, NF-κB, and JNK signaling, including calcium channel blockers, RAAS inhibitors, and natural compounds such as LMA and SEI have been reported to attenuate oxidative stress, which driven inflammation and vascular remodeling [[234], [235], [236], [237]]. Simultaneously, inhibitors of advanced glycation AGE-RAGE pathways and PKC–Egr1 signaling may mitigate oxidative amplification loops that contribute to endothelial activation and ECM degradation [[238], [239], [240]]. MMP inhibition is another relevant strategy, as excessive ROS activate matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2, and MMP-9), promoting structural disintegration of the aortic wall [241].

Given the metabolic disruptions in TAD, focusing on metabolic pathways are being actively investigated as potential adjunctive strategies. AMPK activation, including through metformin, has been associated with protective effects in experimental studies. Mendelian randomization studies suggest that metformin use is genetically associated with a diminished risk of aortic aneurysm and dissection [242,243]. Furthermore, targeting macrophage HIF-1α activation linked to fumarate accumulation represents another promising approach. In animal models, inhibition of the HIF-1α-ADAM17 pathway by acriflavine reduces the incidence and severity of TAD [80,244]. Emerging evidence suggests that ketone body metabolism may be relevant to TAD, as increased 3-hydroxybutyrate (3-HB) levels partially mediate the inverse association between type 1 diabetes and TAD risk [[245], [246], [247], [248]]. This supports ketone metabolism interventions, including 3-hydroxybutyrate supplementation or ketogenic diets, in regulating redox signaling and inflammation. Collectively, AMPK activation, HIF-1α inhibition, and ketone metabolism regulation may represent adjunctive approaches for modulating metabolic and redox imbalance in TAD.

Importantly, most ROS-targeted interventions discussed above are supported mainly by in vitro and animal model evidence, whereas direct clinical evidence in patients with TAD remains limited [249]. Their reproducibility, and translational relevance of these approaches may vary substantially depending on the experimental model, disease stage, and ROS source targeted.

ROS-related biomarkers have also been explored for risk stratification and disease monitoring in TAD. Clinical studies indicate that TAD is accompanied by heterogeneous systemic oxidative stress response. Patients with TAD exhibit significantly elevated levels of lipid peroxidation markers, particularly total hydroperoxides, together with increased inflammatory mediators such as C-reactive protein (CRP), IL-33 and IL-6, suggesting an association between redox imbalance and inflammation. Reduced circulating concentrations of vitamin C, β-carotene, and protein thiols, as well as an increased Cu/Zn ratio, indicate impaired antioxidant defenses. Notably, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) is not significantly elevated in TAD patients, which may be influenced by the widespread use of statin therapy in this patient population [[250], [251], [252]]. Meanwhile, most ROS-related biomarkers reported in TAD reflect systemic oxidative stress rather than aortic wall–specific redox dynamics, and their current utility is largely limited to risk stratification and disease monitoring rather than therapeutic decision-making.

Despite these preclinical findings, several barriers limit the clinical application of ROS-targeted treatment. It is important to distinguish strategies aimed at preventing TAD in high-risk populations from those intended to treat established dissection. Interventions targeting chronic oxidative stress may be more relevant for genetic susceptibility or early medial degeneration. In acute or established TAD, ROS-targeted interventions are unlikely to replace standard management, which remains centered on rapid hemodynamic stabilization and timely surgical or endovascular repair. Redox-modulating therapies may instead serve as adjunctive approaches to stabilize the aortic wall, suppress inflammation, and limit progressive medial degeneration at defined disease stages. Many antioxidants also have poor bioavailability, insufficient vascular targeting, or short plasma half-lives. Broad-spectrum antioxidants may also disrupt physiological redox signaling. The dynamic and cell-specific nature of ROS generation further complicates therapeutic window selection and dosing accuracy. Thus, there remains a need for advanced delivery systems, including ROS-responsive nanoparticles and enhanced strategies that distinguish between protective and pathological ROS in the context of TAD (Fig. 4).

Conclusions

TAD is a highly lethal vascular disorder in which dysregulated ROS contribute to endothelial injury, inflammatory amplification, ECM degradation, and VSMC dysfunction. Excessive ROS production arising from NOX activation, eNOS uncoupling, mitochondrial impairment, and metabolic disturbances disrupts vascular homeostasis and accelerates medial degeneration, thereby facilitating disease progression.

These insights indicate that ROS act not only as by-products of cellular stress but as integrative regulators linking inflammatory signaling, redox-sensitive transcriptional and epigenetic programs, metabolic reprogramming, and cell-fate pathways including apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis. This perspective provides a unifying framework for understanding how heterogeneous TAD risk factors such as genetic defects, metabolic disorders, hemodynamic stress, and environmental exposures, converge on redox imbalance to drive aortic wall failure. It also highlights multiple potentially druggable nodes within the ROS network, including NOX isoforms, Nrf2 dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ferroptosis susceptibility. This review outlines four potential therapeutic strategies: inhibition of ROS-generating enzymes, enhancement of antioxidant systems, modulation of ROS-related signaling pathways, and rectification of ROS-induced metabolic reprogramming. These approaches aim to restore redox homeostasis and maintain vascular stability.

Nonetheless, significant challenges remain. The clinical application of antioxidant and redox modulatory therapies in cardiovascular diseases remains limited. While integrative strategies such as dietary interventions may confer cardiovascular benefits, their lack of precision limits their therapeutic utility. In contrast, conventional antioxidant therapies remain insufficiently targeted, and emerging redox targeted approaches, although mechanistically promising, still require validation in rigorously designed clinical trials. Moreover, heterogeneity across disease stages and patient populations further contributes to variable and often inconsistent therapeutic outcomes.

This limitation likely reflects not only inadequate bioavailability and insufficient tissue specificity, but also a fundamental challenge in redox biology. ROS exert both physiological and pathological functions, influenced by cellular context, molecular source, and subcellular compartment. As a result, non-selective ROS scavenging may disrupt essential signaling while failing to effectively suppress pathogenic oxidative stress. Moreover, although many ROS-driven molecular pathways are shared between aortic aneurysm and TAD, including NOX activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and redox-sensitive inflammatory signaling, TAD is distinguished by its abrupt onset, extreme mechanical stress, and rapid inflammatory amplification, which together impose unique temporal and spatial demands on redox regulation. The lack of validated redox biomarkers and an incomplete understanding of the spatial and temporal dynamics of ROS within the aortic wall restrict early diagnosis and precision intervention in TAD.

Future research should focus on four priorities: establishing reliable redox related biomarkers with human relevant platforms such as iPSC derived vascular cells, microfluidic aortic wall models, and emerging imaging and biosensing tools; developing source specific and stage specific redox modulation strategies supported by targeted delivery and rigorous clinical validation; clarifying the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of ROS within the dissected aortic wall, especially across the media, adventitia, false lumen, and thrombotic microenvironment; and integrating human TAD tissue with experimental models to define dissection specific redox mechanisms and improve translational relevance. Clinically, the key message is that ROS are central mediators of aortic wall degeneration in TAD, but redox targeted strategies remain largely investigational. Their most realistic role is likely as adjuncts to established surgical and hemodynamic management, rather than as stand-alone alternatives.
