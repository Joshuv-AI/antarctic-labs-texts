Introduction

Effective data use in health management is pivotal to improving service delivery and health outcomes, particularly in low and middle-income countries where systems continue to struggle with resource constraints and fragmented reporting structures [1], [2]. Data use, defined as the systematic collection, processing, analysing, synthesis and review of information from diverse sources, enables evidence-based decision-making and resource allocation [3], [4]. Health Management Information Systems (HMIS), such as the District Health Information Software (DHIS2), are designed to equip district health managers with tools for planning, monitoring and evaluating service delivery [5], [6]. Developed and maintained by the Health Information Systems Programme (HISP) at the University of Oslo (UiO), DHIS2 was launched in the late 1990s to strengthen information systems in low-and middle-income countries by providing a flexible, scalable tool for collecting, analysing, visualising and reporting health data [7], [8]. Today, it is the world’s largest open-source health information platform, implemented in over seventy countries to support national health programmes, disease surveillance and routine service delivery [8]. Beyond serving as a reporting mechanism, DHIS2 consolidates data from multiple subsystems, including community and health facilities, that converge at the district level to meet both local and national needs. The system incorporates predefined datasets and indicators gathered during the delivery of primary healthcare services; which are aggregated at the community and facility levels before being forwarded to the district level for analysis and decision-making [9]. Implementation typically involves several stages, including, establishing a national server and database, mapping and integrating existing health data sources, piloting the system in selected districts and gradually scaling up to nationwide coverage. This process requires close collaboration among ministries of health, local stakeholders and technical partners to ensure sustainability, data quality and alignment, and iterative adaptation to local needs.

In Malawi, the digitalisation of the Malawi HMIS commenced in 1999, leading to the adoption of the DHIS2 in 2005 through HISP’s phased approach, with emphasis on capacity building, stakeholder engagement and iterative adaptation [10], [11]. The Central Monitoring and Evaluation Division (CMED) of the Ministry of Health (MoH) now serves as custodian of DHIS2, overseeing data coordination, processing, analysis and dissemination [12].

Despite the widespread adoption of DHIS2, low and middle-income countries such as Malawi continue to experience persistent challenges in data use at the district level [12], [13]. These challenges stem from fragmented reporting formats, parallel data systems driven by donor and government demands, and limited human capacity for data collection and analysis [14], [15], [16]. Fragmented and parallel systems to the HMIS undermine data integrity [17], [18], thereby compromising policy formulation, resource allocation, and ultimately, the achievement of improved health outcomes [19], [20].

This situation underscores the need for a coherent framework to strengthen data use in health management. While existing literature suggests that quality data requires knowledgeable people [3], highlighting the importance of building human capacity, improving data quality, and integrating reporting tools [21], less attention has been given to the institutional dynamics that shape data practices. Literature suggests that technology outcomes are moderated by institutional dynamics [[18], [22], [23]]. Institutional theory offers a valuable lens for this analysis, as it emphasises how external pressures, coercive, normative and mimetic, affect organisational behaviour and decision-making [[15], [24]]. It highlights three types of pressures that shape organisational behaviour and decision-making in organisations [25]. Coercive pressures arise from external forces such as government regulations and donor funding requirements, which often compel compliance even when such directives may limit innovation. These pressures ensure adherence to mandated standards but can constrain flexibility in adapting systems to local needs [26]. Normative pressures emerge from professional standards, training programs and capacity building initiatives that influence health workers’ behaviours and perception [27]. Such pressures foster a culture of professionalism and accountability, guiding data practices and reinforcing the importance of evidence-based decision-making. Mimetic pressures reflect the tendency of organisations to replicate practices perceived as successful in other contexts [28], [29]. While imitation can accelerate adoption of proven strategies, it may also result in the transfer of approaches that are not aligned to local realities, thereby limiting effectiveness. By examining these pressures, this study proposes a framework that integrates institutional pressures to strengthen data use in health management decision-making at the district level in Malawi. In doing so, it contributes to the ICT4D literature by offering actionable recommendations for policymakers, health managers, and practitioners, while advancing understanding of HMIS efficacy in low and middle-income settings. The central research question guiding this study is:

What institutional framework can be used by the Ministry of Health (MoH) to improve data use in management decision-making at the district level in Malawi?

Materials and methods

Institutional theory Isomorphism

The study employed a multi-faceted institutional theoretical framework [30] to analyse how institutional dynamics shape data use in health management, thereby informing the development of a framework for improved decision-making. While the concepts of coercive, normative and mimetic pressures were originally introduced to explain organisational isomorphism, why organisations within a field converge in structure and practice [25], subsequent scholarship has extended their application to information systems, governance and organisational change. For example, institutional theory was used to examine inter-organisational information system implementation [31], and to analyse how external funding pressures influenced the introduction of new health information systems [32]. Similarly, the institutional theoretical concepts were applied to understand leadership responses to external demands for quality improvement [33]. Building on this extended use, this current study applies these concepts not to explain convergence, but to illuminate how institutional dynamics shape data practices and HMIS design in Malawi. This reinterpretation is particularly beneficial for Malawi’s HMIS context, where data use challenges are not merely technical but deeply embedded in institutional arrangements. By framing data practices through institutional pressures, the study highlights how regulations, norms and imitation interact to enable or constrain effective data use.

Coercive pressures refer to mandates, regulations and policy enforcement imposed by government bodies and partner organisations. Scholars have demonstrated that the MoH regulations and donor requirements play a significant role in shaping data governance [34], [35]. Yet, compliance with these directives is often undermined by fragmented systems and limited resources [16], [36]. While these challenges are not institutional factors in themselves, they interact with institutional regulations to create barriers that hinder effective data use.

Normative pressures, on the other hand, arise from professionsl norms, training and expectations within organisations [30]. Although initially conceptualised as internal, they are reinforced externally through networks, capacity building initiatives, and professional standards. Weak health professional networks and insufficient training have been shown to impede the establishment of a strong data use culture [[20], [36], [37]]. Nevertheless, interventions such as mentorship programs and workshops – like those conducted during the implementation of the Integrated Community Health Information System (iCHIS) – illustrate the potential of targeted initiatives to strengthen normative pressures and foster improved data practices [38], [39].

Finally, mimetic pressures occur when organisations or individuals emulate practices percieved as successful elsewhere [24]. The widespread adoption of DHIS2 across more than seventy low- and middle-income countries [4], [13] exemplifies how imitation can drive system uptake. However, uncritical replication without adaptation to local contexts risks generating inefficiencies and missed opportunities for innovation. Thus, while mimetic pressures can influence adoption of practices, they also highlight the importance of contextual sensitivity in organisation learning and system design.

Importantly, this study distinguishes institutional factors − regulations, norms, and practices that shape behaviour – from resource constraints such as finances or staffing. While the latter influence data use, they are not institutional in themselves; rather, they interact with institutional arrangements to amplify challenges or opportunities.

By applying institutional pressures this way, the study provides a theoretically grounded, yet context-sensitive lens for analysing data use in Malawi’s HMIS. This approach motivates the development of a framework that accounts for regulatory demands, professional norms, and imitation dynamics, while recognising their interplay with technical and resource constraints. As depicted in Fig. 2, the framework positions institutional pressures as central to understanding and improving data use in health management decision-making at the district level.

Study context

Malawi is a landlocked country in southeastern Africa with a population of approximately twenty million people, with the majority leaving in the rural areas. The healthcare system is structured in three-tier service delivery model consisting primary, secondary and tertiary care, with most services delivered through the government facilities under the MoH. Primary care is provided at health posts and centres, while district hospitals serve as secondary referral points, and central hospitals deliver specialised tertiary services [40], [41].

Despite significant progress, Malawi continues to face persistent challenges, including shortages of healthcare workers, inadequate infrastructure and constrained financial resources. The system relies heavily on international donors and non-governmental organisations, which contribute substantially to financing and service delivery. To strengthen health system performance, the government has adopted successive Health Sector Strategic Plans, most recently the 2023–2030 plan, which emphasises Universal Health Coverage, improved data systems, and integration of digital platforms such as DHIS2 to enhance monitoring and evaluation.

This study was conducted in four purposefully selected District Health Offices (DHOs) in Malawi: Blantyre, Machinga, Kasungu, and Zomba. The DHOs were chosen for their central administrative roles in health service delivery and data management. All DHOs in Malawi interact with organisations operating across five administrative levels. At the national level, the MoH and national institutions oversee policy-making, strategic planning and regulation. Zonal offices provide technical support and supervision to districts, acting as intermediaries between national and district structures. At the district level, DHOs manage health services, coordinate facilities and oversee policy implementation. The facility level includes health centres, clinics and hospitals where services are delivered directly to patients. Finally, at the community level, village health committees, Community Health Workers (CHWs), known as Health Surveillance Assistants (HSAs) and outreach programs provide preventive and basic care at the grassroots [40], [41]. HSAs collect data using both paper-based and digital tools. These data are compiled into aggregated community reports and submitted to the facility level. Data Clerks or Focal personnel at facilities then integrate them into facility-level reports, which are forwarded to the district level. DHOs play a critical role in compiling these reports into integrated DHIS2 datasets, providing performance feedback to facilities, and sharing data with zonal offices and the MoH. At the national level, the MoH uses these data for policy formulation, planning and regulatory oversight of HMIS practices. Study design and population

This study adopted an interpretive qualitative approach, employing a case study design [42] to explore institutional factors influencing data use in Malawi’s HMIS. Data collection followed a multi-method strategy, combining semi-structured interviews, participant observations, and a review of relevant artifacts and documents to ensure triangulation and depth analysis.

Key informant interviews were conducted with personnel from the MoH and DHO, including policymakers, program managers, planners, and decision-makers. The interview population also encompassed HMIS Officers, Statistical Data Clerks, HSAs, and their supervisors.

In total, forty-two (42) individuals were purposively selected based on their direct involvement in data use processes. This purposive sampling strategy ensured that participants possessed relevant experience and insights, thereby enabling a rich understanding of the institutional dynamics shaping data practices. In addition, the first author temporarily joined the District Health Management Team (DHMT), attending scheduled meetings, participating in health facility supervision, and reviewing supervision data assessment reports, consistent with interpretive stance [43]. The author also attended partner organisation data assessment sessions. This insider role enabled deep immersion into the case and provided privileged access to institutional processes. However, it also carried a risk of bias in data interpretation. To mitigate this, the first author maintained a reflective field diary, systematically documenting observations, personal assumptions, and interactions. This practice of reflexivity ensured that positionality was critically examined and that interpretations were grounded in evidence rather than personal involvement.

The second author served as the technical lead for iCHIS, while the third author contributed to its design and review. Their roles’ initiatives were primarily technical and supportive, aimed at strengthening staff engagement in data use processes. Collectively, the authors’ insider positions provided rich contextual insights but were balanced by deliberate strategies of reflexivity, triangulation across multiple data sources, and critical self-awareness to minimise bias and enhance the trustworthiness of findings.

Data collection

Data was collected between December 2021 and October 2022. As detailed in Table 1, semi-structured interviews with key informants were conducted using a question guide tailored to participants, thematically structured around the institutional pressures of coercive, normative, and mimetic. Interviews were primarily conducted in English; however, at the community level, the local Chichewa language was used to ensure clarity and inclusivity. The interview guide itself was prepared in English, and verbal translation into Chichewa was provided during interviews where necessary. To ensure accuracy, translations were carried out by bilingual researchers familiar with both technical health terminology and local expressions. Where ambiguity arose, terms were cross-checked with participants to confirm meaning, thereby enhancing reliability and minimising misinterpretation.

Each one of the forty-two (42) interviews conducted lasted between 45 and 60 minutes and was audio-recorded with the informed consent using digital voice recorders. This number was determined by purposive sampling of individuals directly involved in data use processes and guided by the principle of data saturation. Saturation was reached when successive interviews yielded no new themes or insights, and responses began to converge across organisational levels. This ensured that the sample size was sufficient to capture the diversity of perspectives while avoiding redundancy.

In addition to interviews, the study reviewed policy and strategic documents, organisational charts, forms, registers, data reports, meeting minutes, and web pages. Other artifacts, such as participant use of the DHIS2 and CommCare applications, were also examined. These document and artifact reviews provided contextual depth and enabled triangulation of findings. Rigor was further enhanced through cross-verification of interview and observation data with systematic reviews of the DHIS2 database, ensuring consistency and credibility of the results.

Data analysis

The labelled audio-recorded interviews were transcribed, initially manually and later with the aid of Otter.ai. Transcripts were compiled in Microsoft Word, with edits made to ensure accuracy and fidelity to respondents' narratives.

Data familiarisation was the first step, followed by the generation of initial codes guided by the theoretical framework presented in Fig. 2. The study applied an abductive approach, moving iteratively between theory and data [[31], [44]]. This process resulted in three predefined thematic categories, coercive, normative and mimetic pressures, as outlined in Table 3.

Coding reliability and process

Coding was conducted solely by the first author, who systematically applied the predefined categories while remaining open to inductive codes. To enhance reliability, the drafted article containing the coding outputs was shared with the second and third authors for checking and verification. They peer reviewed coding decisions, assessed consistency, and provided feedback on category definitions and boundaries. Discrepancies emerging from the assessment were resolved through discussions, ensuring transparency and credibility.

Coding approach

Coding was primarily manual, supported by Microsoft Word for organising codes and categories. Manual coding was chosen to allow closer engagement with the data and iterative refinement of categories.

Inductive codes and integration

While the three institutional pressures provided the initial coding framework, inductive codes also emerged during analysis. These include themes such as “fragmentation of reporting formats”, “capacity gaps”, and “donor-driven priorities”. Emerging codes were mapped onto the three institutional pressures by examining their alignment with regulatory demands (coercive), professional norms and training (normative), or imitation of practices (mimetic). For example, “donor-driven priorities” was categorised under coercive pressures, while “capacity gaps” aligned with normative pressures. Results

The results were synthesised from data drawn from the forty-two (42) interviews purposively selected participants across organisational levels shown in Table 1. Observations, policy documents, registers, and digital artifacts (e.g. DHIS2 and iCHIS applications) complemented interview data. This diverse participant base provided insights into both enabling and constraining factors shaping HMIS data use at the district level in Malawi. These factors are organised under the thematic categories of coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures as depicted in Fig. 2. Importantly, while each category highlights distinct influences, our findings also reveal significant interactions among them, shaping the overall dynamics of data use in Malawi’s HMIS.

Coercive pressures

Coercive pressures reflect the influence of government mandates, donor requirements and international standards on HMIS data practices. Interviews with MoH officials highlighted the ministry’s supervisory role in ensuring data quality and compliance:

‘…our key role is to supervise and mentor the districts on data use. When the districts collect data and upload it into the DHIS2 … we… make periodic checks, determine issues of data quality, correctness, timeliness …’ [MoH Senior Officer].

Policy documents reinforce this authority. The National HMIS Policy (2015) stipulates CMED’s sole responsibility for approving new tools and ensuring interoperability. Yet, findings revealed fragmentation, as partner organisations introduced parallel applications without formal approval. An interviewee explained that:

‘… [partner organisations] gave us phones with an installed Application which we were using to enter Family Planning and Maternal Health and Child Care data.’ [HSA]

This proliferation undermines DHIS2′s role as the national repository. WHO guidance similarly stresses harmonisation of systems.

While coercive pressures set the regulatory framework, weak enforcement allows mimetic pressures (donor-driven tools) to proliferate; and limited normative capacity (training and supervision) undermines compliance.

Normative pressures

Normative pressures reflect the influence of professional standards, training, and shared practices that shape organisational behaviour. One Officer reiterated that:

‘… am able to analyse the data and see what can be done in contribution to what interventions can be done looking at the data… [I] use data … to assess how to help facilities that are having challenges e.g. data aggregation problems or failing to balance figures during compilation of reports … track performance of the facilities that needs assistance … and … try to mentor them until there is a positive outcome.’ [HMIS Officer]

However, resource constraints limit supervision, as noted by another Officer:

‘… we are supposed to visit health facilities at least once every quarter for supportive supervision in data processes… but, due to insufficient resources, we are not able to support them as expected.’ [HMIS Officer]

Normative processes also include formal training. While some HMIS staff receive certification at the National Statistics Office, most rely on on-the-job training. This uneven professionalisation weakens standardisation across facilities. For example, due to staff shortages, nurses and attendants often collect data without formal training:

‘… everyone has a role in collecting data … in our registers … one Data Clerk cannot do all the things. So, I am also responsible at times … and … the nurses … [and] we sometimes improvise… hospital attendants also do the data collection.’ [Facility-in-Charge].

This illustrates how professional norms, when consistently applied, can harmonise practices across diverse facilities. Conversely, reliance on untrained staff undermines data integrity.

Normative pressures are essential for sustaining coercive mandates and enabling mimetic adoption. Without consistent training and professionalisation, coercive policies remain unenforced and mimetic practices remain unevenly applied.

Our findings show that coercive, normative and mimetic pressures do not operate in isolation. Instead, they interact dynamically:

Where coercive enforcement is weak, mimetic pressures proliferate unchecked, leading to fragmentation. Where normative capacity is limited, coercive and mimetic initiatives fail to achieve intended outcomes. Conversely, when all three pressures align, policy enforcement, global adoption, and professional training, data use is strengthened, enabling improved decision-making in Malawi’s HMIS.

Mimetic pressures

Mimetic pressures arise when organisations adopt practices from peers or other countries, particularly under uncertainty. In Malawi, the adoption of DHIS2 reflects emulation of other countries’ perceived success with the platform. An interviewee explained that:

‘… iCHIS is one of our pathways to support strengthening … that system [DHIS2] … [iCHIS] is … linked to … DHIS2… [through] … an interoperability layer … the two systems … speak to each other.’ [Monitoring and Evaluation Coordinator - Partner Organisation]

Here, Malawi’s extension of DHIS2 through iCHIS illustrates how uncertainty about community-level data integration prompted imitation of global practices. Importantly, this emulation has enabled improved data aggregation and decision-making at district level. Discussion

This study demonstrates that data use within Malawi’s HMIS is not merely a technical output of software like DHIS2, but a socially embedded process shaped by the interplay of coercive, normative and mimetic pressures, as demonstrated in previous studies [[31], [33]]. While technology acts as a catalyst, its effectiveness at the district level is contingent upon whether institutional mechanisms, such as regulation, capacity building and coordination, are strategically aligned to foster a unified data culture in accordance with [3].

Applying the institutional isomorphism lens adds significant value by explaining how a fragmented organisational field can achieve the standardisation of data processes necessary for a national HMIS. By recognising the influence of coercive, normative and mimetic pressures [25], the MoH can move beyond purely technical solutions to address the both formal rules (policies) and informal constraints (norms) that shape how data is valued and used. This shift from a technical to an institutional focus aligns with recent ICT4D literature, which argues that technology outcomes are moderated by institutional dynamics [[18], [22], [23]].

The findings reveal vulnerabilities in coercive mechanisms, particularly a “legitimacy gap” created when partner organisations impose parallel reporting systems due to weak policy enforcement. Although CMED holds formal authority, fragmented donor mandates often bypass national standards, leading to duplication and inefficiencies. While Strengthening governance through active enforcement of national standards, supported by instruments such as Memoranda of Understanding, is essential to reduce the reporting burden and enhance data integrity [[2], [16]].

Normative pressures are also a source of challenges. The study identifies a significant “normative gap” where the lack of trained Data Clerks has led to informal practices of using untrained hospital attendants for data collection, thereby undermining professional standards and data integrity [[17], [18]]. Sustainable improvements require institutionalising continuous professional development and supportive supervision, moving beyond, on-the-job training. By embedding mentorship models, health workers can internalise the value of data for both clinical and managerial decision-making [21].

Mimetic pressures, while often associated with copying practices from other contexts, can provide opportunities for positive transformation. Emulating global best practices, such as promoted through DHIS2 implementation networks, provides a pathway for stable technology adoption in low- and middle-income settings [[3], [4]]. Institutionalising such networks within Malawi’s HMIS could transform isolated success stories into, persistent system-wide data practices.

In response to the research question: What institutional framework can be used by the Ministry of Health (MoH) to improve data use in management decision-making at the district level in Malawi, this study proposes the Institutional Dynamics and Data Use Framework (Fig. 7), which synthesises seven mechanisms derived from the results. These mechanisms − regulation of practice, human capacity development, data tools and platforms, continuity of efforts, coordinating of partnerships, sharing data practices and motivating contribution − illustrate how different institutional pressures interact to improve data use.

By applying this framework, the MoH can move Malawi’s health system from a culture of compliance to one of informed action, ensuring that technology serves as a true catalyst for change supported by institutional factors essential for long-term sustainability. While developed in the context of Malawi, the framework offers actionable insights for other low- and middle-income countries facing similar institutional challenges. In summary, this study shows that improving data use within Malawi’s HMIS requires more than technical solutions, it depends on aligning institutional pressures with supportive mechanisms. Coercive regulation, normative professional standards, and mimetic learning from global networks each play a role, but only when integrated into a coherent framework can they foster a sustainable data use culture. By actively enforcing policies, investing in human capacity, and leveraging shared practices, the MoH can move from fragmented compliance toward informed, system-wide action. This integrated approach not only strengthens Malawi’s health system but also offers lessons for other low- and middle-income countries seeking to embed technology within resilient institutional structures for improved data use in health management decision-making.

Contributions to knowledge

This study makes a significant contribution to both theory and practice by addressing the limitations of existing research on the use of HMIS data in low- and middle-income countries. It moves beyond traditional technology-centric perspectives to offer a more nuanced understanding of the institutional dynamics that shape data use.

Theoretical contribution

By extending institutional theory to the context of HMIS, the study demonstrates how coercive, normative and mimetic pressures interact to influence data practices. It shows that coercive pressures are not limited to government mandates but also stem from donor organisations, that normative pressures can embed professional standards through training and supervision, and that mimetic pressures – while offering opportunities for learning from global networks – must be carefully adapted to local realities. This synthesis provides a richer theoretical lens for understanding how institutional environments moderate the outcomes of digital health interventions.

Practical contribution

The study introduces an Institutional Dynamics and Data Use Framework that offers MoHs a practical roadmap for strengthening data use. It highlights the importance of enforcing national standards, institutionalising continuous professional development, ensuring national interoperability of digital platforms, and strategically coordinating partnerships. These recommendations provide actionable guidance, not only for Malawi, but also other low- middle-income countries with similar settings.

Way Forward: For Malawi – and other low- and middle-income countries − the path to resilient health information systems lies in coupling technological innovation with bold institutional reform. Ministries of Health must drive sustainable improvements through stronger governance mechanisms that enforce compliance, targeted investments in human capacity, and deliberate engagement with global networks to learn and adapt best practices. At the same time, donors and partners should align their support with national priorities to minimise duplication and enhance long-term sustainability. By embracing this integrated approach, countries can move beyond fragmented compliance toward evidence-driven health systems that advance universal health coverage and foster enduring improvements in data use.

Conclusion

The study proposes an Institutional Dynamics framework to strengthen data use at the district level in Malawi’s HMIS. The framework demonstrates how coordinated institutional leadership can enforce national policies (coercive), nurture standardised professional practices (normative), and strategically harness external networks for technology adoption and best practices (mimetic). It underscores the importance of a formalised regulatory environment, strategic collaboration with partners, targeted capacity building, and the sharing of best practices through global networks such as DHIS2 communities.

Taken together, the framework offers a practical and adaptable governance model for low-and-middle income settings. It provides a coherent pathway toward resilient, evidence-driven health systems and long-term improvements in data use. For practitioners beyond Malawi, the framework serves as a blueprint for embedding technology within institutional structures that ensure sustainability and impact.

Limitations of the study

Although the study provides valuable insights, it was limited to a sample of four sites out of the 28 districts in Malawi due to time and resource constraints. Focusing on Malawi also limits generalisability of the study. Nevertheless, this study broadens the application of institutional theory within public health domain by proposing a framework for future research in similar settings.

Future research should evaluate the applicability of this framework across diverse healthcare systems in other low- and middle-income countries, comparing institutional pressures and data use practices across diverse healthcare systems in other low and middle-income countries to identify common and context-specific dynamics. Expanding the sample to a wider set of districts would improve validity and reliability. Additionally, longitudinal studies could explore how institutional pressures evolve over time and influence the development of frameworks for improving data use in national HMIS.
