Pandemic research in a small island society–a narrative review of research contributions during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic in the Faroe Islands
Authors: Gunnhild Helmsdal, Maria Skaalum Petersen, Marnar Fríðheim Kristiansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall and geographically isolated societies face distinct challenges during a pandemic but may also possess advantages. This narrative literature review presents epidemiological, immunological and psychological studies conducted in the Faroe Islands during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, examining its effects on individuals in the Faroe Islands. The COVID-19 pandemic hit the Faroe Islands early in March 2020. With only around 55,000 inhabitants, the Faroe Islands' isolated, well-documented population enabled extensive epidemiological research and close monitoring of infection patterns. This resulted in studies on transmission, immunity, mental health and long COVID. Overall, the Faroese studies demonstrate how small, cohesive societies with robust infrastructure can conduct high-quality research during a crisis, contributing to both local and global knowledge.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic placed unprecedented strain on health systems and societies worldwide, and although the acute emergency phase has ended, SARS-CoV-2 continues to circulate and contribute to morbidity and mortality [1].

Small and geographically isolated societies represent a distinctive context for pandemic studies. Limited resources and dependency on external supply chains may increase vulnerability, yet cohesive governance structures and strong social trust may facilitate rapid, coordinated responses. The Faroe Islands - an archipelago in the North Atlantic consisting of 18 islands with approximately 55,000 inhabitants mainly living on 6 islands – provide such a setting. Owing to a well-documented, relatively homogeneous population [2], well-developed transport and digital/health infrastructure and a tradition of public engagement including high participation in research studies, the islands have historically contributed to epidemiological understanding, including Panum's foundational observations on measles immunity [3]. The Faroe Islands are therefore a society where the dynamics of infectious diseases can be studied in ways that are often difficult in larger or less isolated populations.

The aim of this narrative review is to present epidemiological, immunological and psychological studies conducted in the Faroe Islands during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic to demonstrate the importance of continuing research during a crisis period.

Methods

We searched the PubMed database for literature published from 1 December 2019 to 30 June 2026 using the search string “Faroe AND Islands AND COVID-19”. The search ended on 30 June 2026. From this, we found 39 papers, of which we used 18 papers, the remaining papers were either not based on Faroese data or not relevant to this review. We also used personal communication with fellow researchers in the Faroe Islands (one paper). All types of scientific papers were included, except preprints and papers without peer review, which were excluded. We categorised the papers into epidemiological, immunological, psychological and long COVID domains.

Results

The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic in the Faroe Islands

The first case of SARS-CoV-2 in the Faroe Islands was identified on 3 March 2020 [4]. Early implementation of public health measures, including lockdown, widespread PCR testing, intensive contact tracing, border screening and quarantine were implemented, and may have contributed to the interruption of transmission chains during the first phase of the pandemic [5–8]. As more transmissible variants, particularly Omicron, emerged, the national strategy gradually shifted from containment to mitigation. The combination of centralised healthcare, nationwide easy access to testing, comprehensive surveillance and high public participation provided a unique opportunity for prospective population-based research studies, allowing researchers to rapidly investigate transmission, immunity, mental health and long COVID while providing evidence to support public health decision-making.

Epidemiological studies of COVID-19 in the Faroe Islands

Through extensive testing and contact tracing, it was possible to identify a vast part of the cases of COVID-19 in the Faroe Islands, especially in the pre-Omicron period. This led to several periods without local transmission in the Faroe Islands, including an extended period of more than 100 days during the spring and summer of 2020 [8]. Given the extensive PCR testing, it was possible to recruit participants for several nationwide studies of SARS-CoV-2 in the Faroe Islands. Thorough mapping of the transmission chains identified individuals who each transmitted infection to more than 10 people each [9], which aligned with previous findings in many infectious disease outbreaks, including the SARS outbreak in 2002–2003.

This pattern changed with the emergence of the Delta variant in the autumn of 2021 and, more markedly, with the introduction of the Omicron variant. In January 2022, Omicron spread rapidly through the Faroese population, infecting almost one-third of inhabitants within a single month, highlighting the limits of elimination strategies once highly transmissible variants emerge [10]. This was also evident at a social gathering where even fully vaccinated people were infected with the Omicron variant, demonstrating substantial immune-escape properties and the potential for superspreading events [11].

During the two years of the pandemic, mortality due to COVID-19 was low – in the first year (March 2020 to February 2021), the overall mortality was only 2.0/100,000 and among the lowest worldwide [12]. Although case numbers increased substantially with first the Delta and subsequently the Omicron variant during the second winter of the pandemic, the number of deaths did not increase at the same rate (52.0/100,000 overall, similar to most other countries in the region) in the second year of the pandemic (March 2021 to February 2022) [12]. This suggests that containment, vaccination, previous infection, a lower virulence of the virus, combined with preserved access to care, may have resulted in less severe outcomes despite subsequent surges. This result should, however, be interpreted with caution, as mortality in a small population is highly sensitive to demographic structure, coding practices and chance.

Together, these studies demonstrated how comprehensive surveillance enabled detailed characterisation of transmission dynamics, variant-specific epidemiology and population outcomes.

Immunological studies on SARS-CoV-2 during the Faroese pandemic

In addition to characterising the epidemiology of COVID-19, several studies investigated the immune response to SARS-CoV-2 and evaluated the effectiveness of public health measures in the Faroe Islands [13–18]. Early serological studies demonstrated a low prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies and few undetected infections in the general population [13,14], providing evidence relevant to early containment strategies (personal communication). Seroprevalence was substantially higher among close contacts [15] – particularly household members – highlighting the importance of quarantine and contact tracing in limiting transmission. Longitudinal follow-up further showed that individuals infected during the first wave developed durable antibody responses that persisted for several months before vaccination became available [16,17]. Together, these studies provided valuable insights into population immunity, transmission dynamics and the effectiveness of public health interventions, contributing to both local pandemic management and the broader understanding of SARS-CoV-2 immunity.

Psychological effects of pandemic measures in the Faroe Islands

Mental health assessment during lockdown was conducted through three different surveys. Population surveys indicated that overall health, quality of life and activity levels were largely maintained, and stress did not rise [19]. Workplace surveys showed modest impacts on well-being but notable declines in job satisfaction and work–family balance, underscoring the need for targeted support for working parents and frontline sectors [20]. A survey among people aged 82-86 years demonstrated increased loneliness and poorer self-rated health and highlighted vulnerabilities that require sustained outreach and community-based support [21]. Qualitative diary data further suggested that resilience was strengthened by trust, remoteness and strong ties to nature - factors that can inform preparedness strategies in small societies [22].

The effects of isolation appeared to have a smaller impact on psychological and emotional well-being in the Faroe Islands than in other European populations [23], but were similar to those in an area in Iceland comparable to the Faroe Islands [24,25]. These comparisons indicate that small, cohesive societies may be relatively protected from severe psychological effects; however, the findings largely reflect the early pandemic period during lockdown, and psychological effects later in the pandemic may have differed as fatigue and social burden accumulated over time. Also, the findings are from study-specific populations (adults and the elderly) and not assessing the impact on children or adolescents.

Long-term effects of SARS-CoV-2 – long COVID studies in the Faroe Islands

Nationwide longitudinal studies of long COVID were conducted in the Faroe Islands during the pandemic, examining both early and late variants [26–29]. These studies align with international findings that early SARS-CoV-2 variants derived from the original Wuhan strain were associated with a higher risk of long COVID [30]. Additionally, the studies found long-term effects of COVID-19 with prevalence and symptom profiles comparable to those described in other studies [31] and underscored that most people recover from long COVID [26–28].

Whereas many studies relied primarily on self-reported questionnaires, the Faroese studies also incorporated nationwide prospective follow-up together with detailed clinical and paraclinical examinations, providing a comprehensive assessment of long COVID across different phases of the pandemic. These findings underscored the importance of long-term follow-up to assess the prevalence and severity of COVID-19 sequelae, even in settings with relatively low acute mortality.

Discussion

The experience of the Faroe Islands demonstrates that timely, high-quality research can be conducted during a pandemic. Although the Faroe Islands' small population and geographical isolation facilitated rapid implementation of nationwide studies, equally important were the centralised healthcare system, nationwide registries, local diagnostic capacity, close collaboration between healthcare providers and researchers, and high public participation. Together, these factors enabled rapid generation of data that added to the global understanding of SARS-CoV-2 and, to some extent, also informed the local health authorities during the first year of the pandemic.

An important lesson from the Faroese experience is that research capacity should be considered a core component of pandemic preparedness rather than an activity initiated after a crisis has begun. The ability to establish nationwide cohorts, perform longitudinal follow-up and rapidly analyse and disseminate findings depended on existing clinical infrastructure, laboratory capacity and collaboration across institutions. Maintaining these capacities between health emergencies may enable faster evidence generation during future outbreaks.

Collectively, the studies conducted during the pandemic provided complementary evidence across multiple domains, including transmission dynamics, immune responses, psychological well-being and long-term health consequences. Together, these findings illustrate the value of integrating research into routine public health responses, allowing evidence to be generated while a pandemic unfolds rather than after it ends.

Limitations of this review include its reliance on papers found through a PubMed search, which may omit regional or non-indexed literature, a predominance of studies involving overlapping Faroese research groups, and selective inclusion inherent in a narrative review. Statistical limitations include small-population statistical instability, but also a limited external generalisability to other than similar societies as the Faroe Islands.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the Faroese experience illustrates that a country's scientific contribution to health research is not determined by its population size but by its ability to generate high-quality data through coordinated healthcare systems and collaborative research environments. Therefore, although the findings originate from a small island population, they could be applicable to future infectious disease outbreaks. Continued investment in research infrastructure and preparedness will be essential for responding effectively to future pandemics.
