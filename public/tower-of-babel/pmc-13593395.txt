Emergence of Novel Highly Pathogenic Avian Influenza Virus A(H5N1) Clade 2.3.4.4b in Commercial Poultry, Bangladesh
Authors: Mohammed A Samad, Md Rezaul Karim, Amelia Coggon, Anowar Hossen, A S M Ashab Uddin, Md Golbar Hossain, Guillaume Fournie, Nicola S Lewis, Alexander M P Byrne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe high‐pathogenicity avian influenza (HPAI) viruses represent a main threat to animal and human health, poultry productivity, and ecosystems. The newly evolved A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b has become a worldwide concern for its expanding multihost ecology, including poultry and a wide range of mammalian species.MethodsAvian influenza virus (AIV) was isolated from an outbreak in commercial quail farms associated with severe respiratory signs and high mortality. Molecular characterization of the detected virus was performed through gene sequencing via whole‐genome sequencing technology and phylogenetic analysis.ResultsGenetic and phylogenetic analyses revealed that the hemagglutinin (HA) gene clustered closely with a recent HPAI A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b strain from live bird markets (LBMs) in Bangladesh, with a most recent common ancestor dated to July 2023. The NA gene and four internal genes (PB1, NP, MP, NS) were derived from contemporary A(H5N1) viruses of ducks in LBMs, while the PB2 and PA segments exhibited similarity to low pathogenicity avian influenza (LPAI) viruses, suggesting reassortment events within avian hosts. The presence of a multibasic cleavage site (PLREKRRKR↓GLF) with avian‐specific receptor‐binding residues (Q224 and G226) and five conserved glycosylation motifs, consistent with high‐pathogenicity phenotypes and strong avian receptor affinity. The PB2 gene harbored a rare I147T substitution; however, key mammalian adaptation markers such as E627K and D701N were absent.ConclusionThe reassortant A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b virus with high pathogenicity and genetic links to LBM‐origin strains is now circulating in the commercial poultry population in Bangladesh, increasing the risk of viral adaptation, the potential for cross‐species transmission, and threatening the poultry sector and public health.Keywords: Bangladesh, clade 2.3.4.4b, HPAI A(H5N1), quail

Introduction

Influenza A viruses are enveloped, negative‐sense, single‐stranded RNA viruses belonging to the Orthomyxoviridae family [1]. Among their defining features are the two major surface glycoproteins, hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), which serve as the basis for subtype classification. These subtypes are widespread in wild aquatic birds, which serve as the natural reservoirs of all known influenza A viruses [2]. Notably, the H5 and H7 subtypes are frequently associated with high‐pathogenicity avian influenza (HPAI) strains that exhibit increased infectivity and virulence in poultry and, occasionally, humans [3, 4]. Type A(H5N1) influenza viruses of the Goose/Guangdong/1/96 (Gs/GD) lineage first emerged in China in 1996, sparking a global poultry panzootic in 2003 [4].

Since its identification in China in 1996, many genotypes and subtypes of this Gs/GD lineage of A(H5Nx) viruses have evolved through the process of gene reassortment with other avian influenza viruses (AIVs), demonstrating remarkable genetic plasticity and global spread [5]. Over time, continuous mutation events have contributed to the evolution of more than 30 distinct clades and subclades, resulting in substantial antigenic diversity (https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/336259) [6]. In addition to this continuous mutation, reassortment has further enhanced this diversification and has enabled these viruses to adapt to a wide range of avian hosts and ecological niches [5]. Since 2016, the emergence and subsequent global spread of the H5Nx clade 2.3.4.4b viruses has led to extensive viral diversity, including numerous subtypes and a complex ecological landscape, leading to eventual endemicity in wild birds [7, 8]. HPAI A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b has caused over 11,400 outbreaks in Galliformes—including domestic chickens, turkeys, and quails—as well as wild birds across more than 100 countries, demonstrating its ability to infect a broad range of avian species. Moreover, spillover of this virus has been reported in mammalian species, such as wild and captive carnivores, marine mammals, and more recently livestock, including dairy cattle with sustained cow‐to‐cow transmission [9]. This lineage is of particular concern due to its enhanced capacity for genetic reassortment, efficient transmission in avian hosts, and increasing ability to cross species barriers, with documented infections in a range of mammalian species, including humans [10, 11, 12]. Reflecting its growing zoonotic risk, clade 2.3.4.4b A(H5N1) viruses have been included in the World Health Organization's list of zoonotic influenza candidate vaccine viruses (CVVs) (WHO 2021) [13].

Bangladesh reported its first HPAI A/H5N1 outbreaks in poultry in February 2007. According to the World Organization for Animal Health, as of June 2025, 586 outbreaks in farmed poultry have been reported, caused by viruses from H5 clades 2.2, 2.2.2, 2.2.3, 2.3.2, 2.3.4, 2.3.4.2 and 2.3.2.1a, but since 2012 only clade 2.3.2.1a has been detected in poultry [14]. However, since 2016, viruses from A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4h and A(H5N6) 2.3.4.4b have been detected in live bird markets (LBMs), particularly in ducks, and in wild birds in Bangladesh [15, 16]. These findings suggest ongoing viral introduction, reassortment, and silent circulation within the broader avian ecosystem.

Since 1990, quail farming has become a thriving industry in Bangladesh due to its economic importance as a commercially farmable poultry that produces both eggs and meat [17, 18]. It's considered that quails are intermediate hosts for duck viruses among terrestrial fowl and are more susceptible to aquatic bird–derived AIVs than other gallinaceous poultry species, without showing any clinical signs or mortality for certain subtypes [19]. Given their susceptibility to influenza viruses and frequent mixing with other poultry species in market chains, quail may act as intermediate hosts that facilitate viral adaptation and interspecies transmission [20]. Despite this potential role, reports of HPAI infection in intensively farmed quail in Bangladesh remain limited. In this study, we report the first detection and genetic characterization of an HPAI A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b virus associated with a severe outbreak in commercial quail farms in Bangladesh, and assess its relatedness with contemporary viruses circulating in other species.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection and Processing

Between October 2024 and February 2025, high mortality was reported from four different commercial quail farms in the Savar subdistrict of Dhaka division, located in central Bangladesh (Figure 1). These intensive commercial farms had flock sizes ranging from 1000 to 5000 birds, with ages between 40 and 180 days. None of these farms was vaccinated against H5 or H9 AIVs. On each farm, over 40% of quails had died within 48 h of clinical onset. A total of 32 oropharyngeal and cloacal swab samples were collected from moribund quail across the four farms, with eight samples (oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs from four quail) taken from each infected premise. All samples were collected and transported in universal transport media. All collected swab samples were promptly processed. Each swab was suspended in 500 μL of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing antibiotics to inhibit bacterial growth. The suspensions were then passed through 0.22‐μm syringe filters to eliminate bacterial contaminants, followed by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 10 min to clarify the samples. The resulting supernatants were retained for subsequent analysis, including specific PCR assays for Influenza A virus detection and virus isolation using 9‐ to 11‐day‐old embryonated chicken eggs (ECEs).

Molecular Detection of Avian Influenza Virus

Total RNA was extracted from the supernatant of the samples using the Quick‐RNA Viral Kit (Cat. No. R1035, Zymo Research), following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, viral particles were lysed, and RNA was bound to a silica column, washed, and eluted in RNase‐free water. The quantity and integrity of the extracted RNA were assessed using a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Extracted RNA was then screened for the influenza A matrix (M) gene using real‐time reverse transcriptase PCR (RT‐qPCR). Amplification reactions were carried out using the AgPath‐ID One‐Step RT‐PCR Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on a QuantStudio 5 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific), as previously established in standardized detection workflows [21]. Following initial screening, all samples that tested positive for the AIV matrix (M) gene by RT‐qPCR were subjected to subtype confirmation targeting H5 and N1 genes. Each RT‐qPCR reaction was carried out in a final volume of 12.5 μL, consisting of 2.5 μL of extracted RNA template, 6 μL of 2 × RT‐PCR master mix (AgPath‐ID One‐Step RT‐PCR Reagents, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.5 μL of enzyme mix, 1.5 μL of nuclease‐free water, and 2 μL of a preoptimized primer–probe mixture containing 10 pmol of each oligonucleotide component. Thermal cycling was performed under the following conditions: reverse transcription at 45°C for 10 min, followed by an initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min. Amplification was conducted over 40 cycles, each consisting of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 1 min, during which fluorescence data were acquired. Fluorescence was recorded at the HEX channel for H5 and the FAM channel for N1, based on probe‐specific dye labelling. A cycle threshold (Ct) value ≤ 35 was used to define positivity for the respective gene targets.

Virus Isolation

All samples that tested positive for the H5 gene by RT‐PCR were subjected to virus isolation using standardized virological methods, as described in previous studies [22, 23]. Briefly, 0.2 mL of each H5‐gene‐positive sample was inoculated into the allantoic cavity of five replicate 9‐ to 11‐day‐old ECEs, sourced from a specific pathogen‐free (SPF) flock. The eggs were incubated at 37°C and monitored daily by candling over 5 days to assess embryo viability. Eggs in which embryos died within the first 24 h postinoculation were considered nonspecific mortalities and were excluded from further analysis. Embryonated eggs that remained viable or exhibited death beyond 24 h were chilled at +4°C, and allantoic fluids were harvested aseptically. The harvested allantoic fluid was initially screened for hemagglutination activity using a standard hemagglutination assay (HA) [24]. A hemagglutination titer of ≥ 16 (i.e., 24) was considered indicative of successful viral replication. Samples with HA titers < 16 were repassaged into a fresh set of SPF ECEs and incubated for up to 6 days. Allantoic fluid from the second passage was then reevaluated for HA activity. Samples that remained HA‐negative following both primary and secondary passages were classified as negative for viable virus. For samples testing positive by HA assay, the allantoic fluid was clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min to remove cellular debris. Isolates demonstrating strong HA activity were selected and prioritized for complete genome sequencing to facilitate further molecular characterization.

Next‐Generation Sequencing

The cultured isolate with the highest HA titer was sequenced using next‐generation sequencing. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina Standard Total RNA Prep, Ligation with Ribo‐Zero Plus kit (Illumina Inc.), starting with approximately 100 ng of total RNA. Ribosomal RNA was depleted using biotinylated rRNA‐targeting probes included in the Ribo‐Zero Plus reagent set, followed by magnetic bead capture of rRNA–probe complexes. The remaining rRNA‐depleted RNA was fragmented and primed using random hexamers, with fragmentation performed at 94°C for 8 min to yield an average insert size of approximately 400 bp. First‐strand cDNA synthesis was carried out using reverse transcriptase and random primers, followed by second‐strand cDNA synthesis incorporating dUTP to maintain strand specificity. The resulting double‐stranded cDNA was end‐repaired, A‐tailed, and ligated to Illumina unique dual‐index adapters. Libraries were amplified by 12 cycles of PCR using a high‐fidelity polymerase, and the amplified products were purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). The final libraries were evaluated using the Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), confirming a mean fragment size of ~400 bp. Equimolar pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 system using a 2 × 150‐bp paired‐end read configuration with the NextSeq P1 300‐cycle kit. Raw sequencing reads in FASTQ format were processed as described previously [25].

Phylogenetic Analysis

The genome of A/quail/Bangladesh/3‐25H/2024 was analyzed alongside a curated dataset of avian influenza A genomes (January 1, 1996, to May 20, 2025) from GISAID, including all available full genome sequences from Bangladesh. This dataset was refined with SeqKit v2.7.0 (≥ 90% segment coverage) and CD‐HIT‐EST v4.8.1 (95% identity), and aligned with MAFFT v7.525 [26]. Maximum‐likelihood phylogenies were inferred using IQ‐Tree2 v2.3.6 with 1000 ultrafast bootstraps [27], for each segment independently. For the HA, time‐scaled phylogenies were then inferred using the maximum‐likelihood phylogenetic tree and TreeTime v 0.11.4 [28].

Mutation Analysis

Amino acid mutation analysis of the HA, NA, and internal genes of A/quail/Bangladesh/3‐25H/2024 was performed to identify substitutions associated with virulence, host specificity, and antigenic evolution of Influenza A virus. Full‐length sequences were translated and aligned with representative Bangladeshi strains and H5 clade 2.3.4.4b reference sequences, including CVVs, retrieved from the GISAID database. Multiple sequence alignment and manual curation were conducted using BioEdit. Mutation profiles were analyzed using FluMut and verified with FluSurver on the GISAID platform. Key functional regions of HA, including the cleavage site, receptor‐binding site (RBS), and potential N‐linked glycosylation motifs, were identified using Nextclade and confirmed by manual inspection. Internal gene mutations, particularly in PB2, were screened for known mammalian adaptation markers based on published literature.

Histopathology

Tissue samples, including pancreas, lungs, proventriculus, spleen, and duodenum, were collected from both HPAI A(H5N1)‐clinically infected quail and immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Following fixation, the tissues were dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax using standard histological techniques. Serial sections of approximately 5‐μm thickness were obtained using a microtome and subsequently stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) in accordance with established protocols [29]. The stained sections were examined under a light microscope to assess and characterize histopathological lesions associated with HPAI A(H5N1) infection.

Results

Detection and Isolation of HPAI Virus

Out of 32 collected samples, all were found positive for A(H5N1) AIV using real‐time RT‐qPCR. The cycle threshold (Ct) values ranged from 16 to 31, indicating varying viral loads across the samples, with lower Ct values suggesting higher concentrations of viral RNA. Following RT‐PCR confirmation, virus isolation was attempted by inoculating the positive samples into SPF ECEs. Out of the 32 PCR‐positive samples, viable virus was successfully isolated from 11 samples. Evidence of virus replication was confirmed by embryo mortality within 72 h postinoculation. Additionally, allantoic fluid harvested from the inoculated eggs tested positive in the HA, further validating the presence of replicating influenza A virus.

Histopathological Lesions

Histopathological examination of quail infected with HPAI revealed severe, multisystemic lesions consistent with systemic viral dissemination. The lungs showed marked congestion, hemorrhage, pulmonary edema, and diffuse inflammatory cell infiltration with thickening of the interstitium and partial obliteration of air spaces (Figure 2B). The pancreas exhibited extensive coagulative necrosis of acinar cells with loss of normal architecture and associated vascular congestion and mild inflammatory infiltration (Figure 2D). Histopathological examination of the brain revealed lesions consistent with encephalitis, characterized by glial cell proliferation and formation of microabscesses, occasionally associated with meningitis (Figure 2F). The trachea showed tracheitis with glandular proliferation and inflammatory changes (Figure 2H).

Phylogenetic Relatedness

Phylogenetic analysis of the HA gene generated from one of the viral isolates demonstrated similarity to other H5 clade 2.3.4.4b sequences from Asia, but particularly those from LBMs of Dhaka and Chattogram, Bangladesh (Figure 1). These samples were collected between 2023 and 2024 and demonstrated a time to the most recent common ancestor of July 2023 (90% maximum posterior region: April–September 2023) (Figure 3).

Analysis of the other seven viral gene segments found that the PB1, NP, NA, MP, and NS segments were similar to A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b viruses from ducks of LBMs from 2023 to 2024 (Figures 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8). Notably, the PB2 and PA segments demonstrated some similarity to other A/H5 clade 2.3.4.4b viruses from Bangladesh; they also showed similarity to non‐H5 LPAI viruses H4N6, H6N3, and H1N2 detected in Korea and Bangladesh (Figures 5 and 6), suggesting a reassortment event between preexisting H5 clade 2.3.4.4b HPAI and LPAI viruses circulating within the region. There was one A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b sequence collected from ducks in an LBM in Bangladesh (A/duck/Bangladesh/64227/2024), which clustered with A/quail/Bangladesh/3‐25H/2024 across all phylogenies (Figures 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8). Clustal Omega analysis showed 98.69%–99.80% nucleotide identity between the two strains across all gene segments and suggests that this particular combination of gene segments may be circulating within Bangladesh but is currently underdetected. It is also of note that in the NS, PB1, and NP phylogenies, there are several H5 clade 2.3.2.1a sequences that group with A/quail/Bangladesh/3‐25H/2024 (Figures 5, 6, and 8), suggesting there has been maintenance of these two groups of viruses in poultry populations and gene segment exchange between the two predominant H5 HPAI clades circulating in Bangladesh.

Genomic Analysis

Using the Nextclade web tool, genetic analysis of the HA revealed the multibasic cleavage site PLREKRRKR↓GLF, characteristic of HPAI viruses (Table 1). Analysis of the receptor‐binding site showed the presence of avian‐specific residues at key positions: Q226 and G228, along with E190 and G225 (H3 numbering) (Table 1), all of which confer a strong binding preference for avian α2,3‐linked sialic acid receptors in previous work [30]. In total, five potential glycosylation motifs were detected at positions 26 (NNS), 39 (NVT), 181 (NNT), 302 (NSS), and 499 (NGT) compared with A/Astrakhan/3212/2020 (H5N8) (EPI1846961). The glycosylation pattern is consistent with A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b strains, reflecting evolutionary adaptations to avian hosts and potential modulation of immune recognition.

The whole‐genome amino acid sequences of A/quail/Bangladesh/3‐25H/2024 and its three related sequences (based on the HA phylogeny; Figure 1), along with the HA and neuraminidase (NA) of H5 clade 2.3.4.4b CVVs, were analyzed using FluMut [31]. The HA and NA genes of A/quail/Bangladesh/3‐25H/2024 showed 99.47% similarity with the CVV A/Astrakhan/3212/2020 (EPI1846961) and 97.2% identity to the A(H5N1) CVV A/American Wigeon/South Carolina/22‐000345‐001/2021, respectively. Relative to the H5N8 CVV A/Astrakhan/3212/2020, the HA protein of A/quail/Bangladesh/3‐25H/2024 contained amino acid substitutions at positions 83 and 487. These substitutions were also observed in the related Bangladeshi sequences analyzed in this study. The NA protein contained 13 amino acid differences relative to the relevant CVV sequence. These substitutions were evaluated using FluMut and FluSurver to assess their potential association with antiviral resistance. No established antiviral resistance‐associated substitutions were identified based on the available annotation and current knowledge. The internal gene segments, including PA and M, were also examined for known antiviral resistance‐associated substitutions. No established resistance‐associated substitutions were identified. The polymerase complex 2 (PB2) gene of the quail virus sequence contained the I147T substitution. The functional and phenotypic relevance of these changes is unknown. Other PB2 substitutions observed in mammalian isolates of H5 clade 2.3.4.4b (T271A, E627K, M631L and D701N) were absent.

Discussion

HPAI A(H5N1) viruses pose a significant threat to both poultry industries and public health worldwide. Among the H5 clades, 2.3.4.4b has emerged as the predominant clade and has spread globally since 2020, causing widespread outbreaks in wild birds and poultry across Eurasia, North and South America, and Africa. Bangladesh has historically reported circulation of HPAI H5 clade 2.3.2.1a, primarily in LBMs, backyard flocks, and duck production systems. However, recent surveillance has identified a concerning shift in viral ecology. This study describes, for the first time, the detection and genomic characterization of an A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b virus from an outbreak in multiple commercial quail farms in Bangladesh with high mortality, similar to that reported in Eurasia and North America. Genomic analysis of the virus demonstrates similarity to other H5 clade 2.3.4.4b viruses, and evidence of sustained co‐circulation of clade 2.3.4.4b and clade 2.3.2.1a in Bangladesh, which have now been detected not only in LBMs but in poultry production systems.

Histopathological analysis in infected quail demonstrated severe multisystemic lesions, indicating systemic viral dissemination and high tissue tropism. The pulmonary lesions, characterized by congestion, hemorrhage, edema, and interstitial pneumonia with inflammatory infiltration, are consistent with previously described HPAIV‐induced diffuse respiratory damage in gallinaceous birds [21, 32]. Such lesions reflect both direct viral cytopathogenicity and secondary inflammatory injury due to dysregulated host immune responses. Of particular significance, the pancreas exhibited extensive coagulative necrosis of acinar cells with architectural collapse and vascular congestion. Similar pancreatic necrosis has been reported in experimental and natural A(H5N1) infections and is associated with systemic endothelial damage and viral replication in exocrine tissues [21, 33]. This finding reinforces the ability of clade 2.3.4.4b viruses to cause widespread visceral involvement beyond the classical respiratory tract. Neurological involvement was prominent, with the brain showing encephalitis characterized by glial proliferation, microabscess formation, and occasional meningitis. These lesions strongly correspond to the neurotropic behavior of HPAI viruses, which has been increasingly documented in clade 2.3.4.4b infections in both avian and mammalian hosts [33, 34]. Viral neuroinvasion is believed to occur via hematogenous dissemination or peripheral nerve spread, leading to neuronal degeneration and inflammatory gliosis. The presence of encephalitis in quail is particularly notable, as quail have been implicated as potential intermediate hosts facilitating adaptation of influenza viruses between aquatic birds and terrestrial poultry [35]. Tracheal lesions, including tracheitis with glandular proliferation and inflammation, further confirm active replication in the upper respiratory tract. This aligns with the established tropism of A(H5N1) viruses for both respiratory and digestive epithelia, contributing to efficient viral shedding and transmission [32].

The phylogenetic and genomic characterization of A/quail/Bangladesh/3‐25H/2024 provides compelling evidence of the continued evolution and reassortment of A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b viruses in Bangladesh, particularly within LBMs. The HA gene of the virus clustered closely with other H5 clade 2.3.4.4b sequences collected from Dhaka and Chattogram LBMs during 2023–2024, with a most recent common ancestor dated to mid‐2023. This finding is consistent with previous reports showing that LBMs in Bangladesh serve as critical hubs for viral persistence and evolution [36, 37]. The internal gene constellation of A/quail/Bangladesh/3‐25H/2024—particularly PB1, NP, NA, MP, and NS—was highly similar to other contemporary A(H5N1) strains from ducks, suggesting ongoing transmission chains among waterfowl within LBMs and spillover to the poultry production system. Importantly, the PB2 and PA segments displayed genetic similarity not only to A(H5N1) strains but also to LPAI viruses previously detected in Bangladesh and Korea. This strongly indicates a reassortment event between endemic H5 HPAI viruses and cocirculating LPAI strains, a process already described in other regional studies [15, 38].

From a molecular standpoint, the study virus retains hallmark features of HPAI, including the multibasic cleavage site (PLREKRRKR↓GLF), avian‐specific receptor‐binding residues (Q224 and G226), and conserved glycosylation motifs. These features of our study virus have also been described in other H5 clade 2.3.4.4b isolates across Asia and Africa [30], and are known to facilitate replication in avian species and modulate immune evasion [15]. The glycosylation motifs identified—particularly at positions 26, 39, 181, 302, and 499—mirror those found in CVVs such as A/Astrakhan/3212/2020 (H5N8), further confirming lineage conservation. The I147T mutation in PB2 is uncommon and of uncertain significance; mammalian‐adaptive PB2 mutations were absent, as also noted in recent Bangladeshi and European strains [15].

This study highlights the complex reassortment landscape of HPAI viruses in Bangladesh, underscoring the role of LBMs and minor poultry species in facilitating genetic exchange. Although the HA sequence exhibited high genetic similarity to the H5 clade 2.3.4.4b CVV A/Astrakhan/3212/2020 (H5N8), genetic relatedness alone does not necessarily indicate antigenic similarity or confirm an appropriate vaccine match. Therefore, antigenic characterization is essential to directly evaluate the antigenic relationship and assess the suitability of existing vaccines against the identified virus. Continuous genomic and antigenic surveillance is also crucial for monitoring emerging mutations that could potentially affect antigenicity and reduce vaccine effectiveness. These findings reinforce the urgent need for broader, multispecies, and longitudinal surveillance programs, coupled with functional virology studies, to detect emerging threats and guide control strategies.

Limitation: Of the 32 A(H5N1)‐positive samples collected from four commercial quail farms, 11 viable viruses were successfully isolated. Due to funding and resource limitations, whole‐genome sequencing was performed for one representative isolate, designated A/quail/Bangladesh/3‐25H/2024. Therefore, the genomic characteristics described in this study represent the sequenced isolate and cannot be extrapolated to all 11 isolates.

Conclusion

In Bangladesh, some poultry are vaccinated against H5. However, this vaccination probably offers suboptimal protection due to mismatches with currently circulating clade 2.3.4.4b viruses, which can result in virus shedding and maintenance without high mortality. This ongoing circulation increases the risk of viral adaptation, threatening food security, posing a risk for further potential reassortment, and raising the potential for the emergence of zoonotic strains with pandemic potential. As there is no direct causal association between vaccination and the emergence of the clade 2.3.4.4b virus, this work highlights the need for continual surveillance of AIVs in poultry to enable accurate assessments of the viruses that circulate in poultry and may have the potential to spill over into other susceptible hosts.
