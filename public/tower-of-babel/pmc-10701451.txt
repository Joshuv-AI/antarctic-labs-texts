Protocol for determining protein dynamics using FT-IR spectroscopy
Authors: Hao Shen, Cuiping Fu, Junting Zhang, Bin Feng, Shaoning Yu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn biological systems, protein function depends on spatial and temporal changes known as protein dynamics, which can be probed by amide hydrogen/deuterium (H/D) exchange. Here, we present a protocol for determining protein dynamics by Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. We describe steps for protein sample preparation and FT-IR spectra collection. We then detail procedures for spectra analysis. Applications include the effects of protein mutation or protein and metal ion or ligand interactions on the protein H/D exchange rate.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Yu et al. (2013).1

Before you begin

Innumerable biological processes ultimately rely on the transduction of information through fluctuations in protein conformations associated with folding/assembly, ligand binding, molecular recognition and catalysis, and these spatial and temporal changes in protein are termed protein dynamics.2,3,4,5,6,7,8 Protein dynamics can be probed by amide hydrogen/deuterium (H/D) exchange which characterizes transient conformations.4,5,6 The mechanism of H/D exchange is depicted in Figure 1.Figure 1Schematic illustration of H/D exchange in proteinLabile hydrogens, rendered here as black balls, can become deuterated when a protein is placed in D2O solution. Other hydrogens that exchange too slowly or too quickly to be measured are not shown.

Labile hydrogens, rendered here as black balls, can become deuterated when a protein is placed in D2O solution. Other hydrogens that exchange too slowly or too quickly to be measured are not shown.

A variety of detection techniques can be used to probe protein dynamics, including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR),9,10 mass spectrometry (MS),11,12,13 and FT-IR spectrometry14,15,16 (Figure 2). NMR has the advantage of providing dynamic data at the residue level ranging from sub-nanoseconds to days.17,18 Its disadvantages include molecular weight limitations, longer acquisition time, lack of paramagnetic cofactors and cumbersome labeling with 15N.19,20 MS has the advantage of providing dynamics data for extremely large protein assemblies and avoid the need for isotopic labeling of the protein.21,22,23,24 The protein concentration can be as low as 0.1 μM. However, MS measurements depend on the proteolytic digestion of the protein and analysis of the peptide fragments.2 FT-IR spectroscopy is a well-established technique for analysis of the secondary structure composition of proteins that is also a convenient and efficient technique for protein dynamics investigation and bacterial typing.25,26 The singular advantages of FT-IR are that spectra can be obtained for proteins of any molecular weight, requiring less time and sample.27,28,29 The sample does not require proteolytic digestion or isotopic labeling. FT-IR offers the advantage of monitoring the kinetics of different secondary structure components simultaneously and assessing the order of events, helping identify which regions of protein unfold or fold first. Expected outcomes

The dynamics of several proteins were analyzed by FT-IR spectroscopy. Applications include determining the effects of the interactions of proteins with ligands or metal ions and protein mutations on the protein H/D exchange rate.

Comparing the dynamics of PKA and PKA-cAMP complex

PKA, the major transducer of the second messenger cAMP in eukaryotic cells, is involved in a myriad of cellular functions, such as metabolism, cell growth, and differentiation.31,32 In the absence of cAMP, the catalytic (C) subunit of PKA is sequestered in an inactive state by interacting tightly with the inhibitory regulatory (R) subunit to form a tetrameric holoenzyme (R2C2). The binding of cAMP to the R subunit induces a conformational change that leads to dissociation of the holoenzyme into its constituent R and C subunits, which play critical roles in the cellular regulation of PKA.33,34

To explore the intrinsic protein dynamics of PKA in the presence and absence of cAMP, the H/D exchange rates of PKA and PKA-cAMP complex were monitored by FT-IR spectroscopy. The results are shown in Figure 8, which shows an overlay of the representative absorption spectra of PKA and PKA-cAMP complex recorded at different exchange times in D2O. The spectra of PKA in H2O (black) and fully deuterated PKA in D2O (black dot) were plotted as references. The overall FT-IR H/D exchange absorption profiles of PKA holoenzymes (Figure 8A) and PKA-cAMP complex were very similar (Figure 8B).Figure 8H/D exchange of PKA without cAMP and with 200 μL cAMP as monitored by FT-IR spectroscopy

H/D exchange of PKA without cAMP and with 200 μL cAMP as monitored by FT-IR spectroscopy

The H/D exchange rates of PKA and PKA-cAMP complex were estimated by plotting the fraction of unexchanged amide protons, calculated from the amide II band data using Equation 2, as a function of exchange time. The fraction of unexchanged amide protons at the first exchange time point (1 min) for PKA was ∼20% (Figure 9), suggesting that the majority of the amide protons exchanged so rapidly that their exchange was completed within the time interval for the acquisition of the first time point. Therefore, only the intermediate and slow exchange protons can be monitored semi-quantitatively over the time range employed in this study. The overall H/D exchange rate of the PKA-cAMP complex was faster than that of holo-PKA, indicating a more dynamic conformation. This finding is not surprising, as the formation on PKA-cAMP complexes involves a large intersubunit interface that should render a large number of exchangeable amide protons inaccessible to the solvent.13Figure 9Fraction of unexchanged PKA amide protons as a function of exposure time in D2O

Comparing the dynamics of CaM and CaM-Ca2+ complex

Calmodulin (CaM) is a ubiquitous Ca2+-binding protein that plays a key role in numerous Ca2+-dependent cellular signaling pathways.35 The crystal structures of Ca2+-saturated CaM reveal that CaM contains two similar globular domains with two calcium-binding sites in each.36 The cooperative binding of Ca2+ to the two Ca2+-binding domains of CaM induces large conformational changes that expose the solvent to a significant amount of non-polar surface area in each domain, resulting in a transition from a “closed” to an “open” domain conformation in which the exposed hydrophobic pocket serves as a target interaction site.25 While much information exists on the conformational changes in Ca2+-free CaM and Ca2+-saturated CaM, less is known about the intermediate structures of CaM-nCa2+ (n = 1–3).25

To explore the effect of Ca2+ binding on the intrinsic structural dynamics and conformational flexibility of CaM, we carried out the H/D exchange experiment and monitored the amide proton exchange of CaM using FT-IR spectroscopy. Figure 10 shows an overlay of the representative absorption spectra of CaM at different molar ratios of Ca2+/CaM recorded at different H/D exchange times in D2O with the spectra of the proteins in H2O plotted as references. The CaM in H2O exhibited characteristic amide I and II band maxima at 1656/54 and 1552 cm−1, respectively, and H/D exchange in D2O led to a time-dependent isotopic shift of the amide II band from 1552 to 1458 cm−1.Figure 10H/D exchange of CaM as monitored by FT-IR spectroscopyThe primary FT-IR spectra of CaM in the presence of 1 Ca2+, 2 Ca2+, 3 Ca2+, and 4 Ca2+ as a function of time at room temperature. Comparing the dynamics of WT CRP and CRP mutants

Catabolite gene activator protein (CAP), also referred as cAMP receptor protein (CRP), regulates the transcription of more than 100 genes in E. coli.37,38,39,40,41,42 CRP is a dimeric protein composed of two chemically identical subunits. Each subunit contains a larger N-terminal domain, which contains the characteristic cAMP binding structure, and a smaller C-terminal domain, which binds to DNA through a helix-turn-helix motif. The two functional domains are connected by a hinge region (residues 135–138).37,38,39,40,41,42 CRP undergoes a subtle allosteric conformational change upon binding of cAMP that enables the protein to recognize specific DNA sequences and interact with RNA polymerase (RNAP).43,44,45 Some CRP mutants can recognize specific DNA sequences without cAMP binding.46

To explore and compare the protein dynamics of WT-CRP and some Asp-138 mutants, the H/D exchange rates of these mutants were monitored by FT-IR spectroscopy. Figure 12 shows an overlay of the representative absorption spectra of different CRP mutants recorded at 1, 3, 5, 10, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min in D2O with the spectra of the proteins in H2O plotted as references. The overall H/D exchange rates were estimated by plotting the fraction of unexchanged amide protons as a function of exchange time (Figure 13). The data reveal that the dynamics of Asp-138 mutants can be grossly divided into three classes: D138L is the most dynamic mutant, followed by the group consisting of WT and D138K mutants. Limitations

In the experiment, the value of the fraction of unexchanged amide proton may be affected by many factors, such as the quality of the protein FT-IR spectrum in H2O, the experimental temperature, and protein or buffer lyophilization conditions. Thus, this method is only semi-quantitative. In addition, this protocol provides a concise framework for utilizing FT-IR measurement combined with H/D exchange to investigate protein dynamics occurring within minutes to hours, which means that its ability to monitor protein dynamics at time scales below minutes is insufficient.

This protocol focuses on an approach in which FT-IR with transmission windows is used to determine protein dynamics on a global scale in aqueous solution. In these instances, the scope of a given protocol is generally limited to: (i) proteins that are insoluble at high concentrations, (ii) proteins in buffer with cosolvents (cannot be lyophilized or affect IR absorbance), or (iii) proteins in buffer with high concentrations of salt (>200 mM; extremely hygroscopic dry form).

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

There are air bubbles in the constituted sample (Section “collecting FT-IR spectra for protein”, step 26).

Potential solution

The lyophilized protein must be suspended gently to avoid the generation of bubbles in the sample.

Problem 2

There are air bubbles in the cell (Section “collecting FT-IR spectra for protein”, step 26).

Potential solution

The CaF2 window is not clean or the volume of sample is not enough. Demount the cell and clean it very gently. More sample is needed for the experiment.

Problem 3

The operation is not completed in the initial delay time (Section “collecting FT-IR spectra for protein”, step 26).

Potential solution

Prepare another sample and repeat the experiment.

Problem 4

There is a very low IR absorbance signal or no signal (Section “collecting FT-IR spectra for protein”, step 27).

Potential solution

Check the cell spacer and ensure it is > 30 μm. Check the protein concentration and ensure it is high enough.

Problem 5

Sample in the cell leaks before completion of the experiment (Section “collecting FT-IR spectra for protein”, step 31).

Potential solution

The entrance hole and exit hole must be sealed well. Check the cell spacer and ensure it is < 80 μm.

Problem 6

ω is not nearly 2/3 or there is great variability between the values of ω obtained from the three independently acquired spectra (Section “data analysis”, step 42).

Potential solution

Check the cell spacer and ensure the spectrum for protein in H2O is obtained in very thin films (<10 μm). Alternatively, ω can be obtained from the spectra of very thin films of H2O.51 By such means, ω has been found to be very nearly constant for proteins. If ω is pH- and/or temperature-independent, the value of ω will shift under different pH or temperature. Control the experimental conditions and ensure they are consistent during the experiment.

Problem 7

F of the last point >5% (Section “data analysis”, step 44).

Potential solution

The spectra should be recorded for a longer time.

Problem 8

F versus exchange time plots cannot be fitted well with Equation 2 (Section “data analysis”, step 45).

Potential solution

Check if AII∞ is correct. Incorrect AII∞ values will affect the plots. Random errors arise through dissolution of Ca2+ from the windows into the solution, through the elevation of temperature when the thin film of solution resides in the intense infrared beam, and through baseline drift. This will affect the data. Try to increase the protein concentration and ensure that there are no cosolvents in the sample. All can be corrected by careful and ingenious experimental techniques.
