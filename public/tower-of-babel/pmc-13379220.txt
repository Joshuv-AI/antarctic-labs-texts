Smad2/3 Regulates IL-6R m6A Methylation through METTL3 to Influence Inflammatory Responses in Hemorrhoidal Disease
Authors: Chunling Li, Xu Han, Aili Chen, Jian Cheng, Yalong Li, Qiong Wang, Heng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study investigates METTL3’s function and mechanisms in hemorrhoidal disease-related inflammation.MethodsA rat model of hemorrhoidal disease was induced in vivo using croton oil. Human monocytic leukemia cells THP-1 were induced into macrophages using phorbol myristate acetate, and then lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was used to induce an inflammatory phenotype in the macrophages. The pathological changes in the rectal and anal tissues of rats were evaluated using the rectoanal coefficient and HE staining. Key gene and protein expression levels were detected using real-time quantitative PCR, Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and immunohistochemistry.ResultsMETTL3 expression was significantly upregulated in hemorrhoidal tissues and LPS-induced macrophages, and its knockdown alleviated rectal-anal lesions in hemorrhoidal rats. Silencing METTL3 suppressed the expression of M1 polarization markers (iNOS, CD80, CD86) while increasing interleukin-10 and reducing tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels in hemorrhoidal tissues and LPS-stimulated macrophages. Furthermore, LPS treatment enhanced Smad2/3 and IL-6R expression in macrophages, and positive correlations were observed between the expression of Smad2, Smad3, and METTL3, as well as between METTL3 and IL-6R. Mechanistically, nuclear-translocated Smad2/3 bound to METTL3 to promote its expression. The resulting elevated METTL3 then promoted IL-6R expression via m6A modification, driving macrophage activation and ultimately exacerbating the inflammatory response in hemorrhoidal disease.ConclusionThis work demonstrates that the Smad2/3-induced METTL3 drives hemorrhoidal inflammation via m6A modification of IL-6R, presenting a novel intervention target.

Introduction

Hemorrhoidal disease is an anorectal disorder caused by destructive changes in the supporting connective tissue, abnormal blood circulation within the anal cushion, and abnormal dilation and deformation of the hemorrhoidal plexus in sliding anal cushions, with a prevalence of nearly 40% in the general population [1]. Hemorrhoidal disease often manifests as chronic recurrent rectal bleeding, which not only reduces patients’ quality of life but also may lead to anemia [2]. Previous studies have shown that inflammatory responses in the vascular walls and surrounding connective tissues of hemorrhoids are key triggers for their malignant progression, as intense inflammatory reactions can cause mucosal ulcers, ischemia, and thrombosis [3], leading to unbearable pain [4]. Additionally, extensive macrophage infiltration in hemorrhoidal tissues is a common cytopathological feature [5], and macrophages also serve as critical regulatory mediators in perianal inflammation [6]. However, the specific mechanisms regulating macrophage inflammatory responses in hemorrhoidal disease remain unclear.

Interleukin-6 receptor (IL-6R) is the functional receptor for the pleiotropic cytokine IL-6 and plays a key role in regulating inflammatory responses in the body. Previous studies have shown that the binding of IL-6 to IL-6R can induce chronic intestinal inflammation [7]. In ulcerative colitis models, high levels of IL-6R are also significant drivers of inflammatory responses and apoptosis in mouse intestinal epithelial cells [8]. In Crohn’s disease, IL-6R is also a key regulator during Mycobacterium avium paratuberculosis infection in macrophages and intestinal epithelial cells, as well as in intestinal epithelial dysfunction [9]. Inhibiting IL-6R expression can effectively reduce macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses [10]. However, whether IL-6R is involved in regulating macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses in hemorrhoidal disease remains unknown.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is among the most prevalent and critical mRNA modifications and plays a crucial role in the posttranscriptional regulation of mRNA. Previous studies have indicated that m6A modification plays a role in the progression of hemorrhoidal disease [11]. Like other types of mRNA modifications, m6A modification is regulated by m6A reader proteins, m6A writer proteins, and m6A eraser proteins [12]. As a writer protein, methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) catalyzes the m6A methylation of target mRNAs. Importantly, METTL3-mediated m6A methylation plays a significant role in macrophage-regulated inflammatory responses in inflammatory bowel disease [13]. Additionally, the expression of IL-6R is regulated by m6A modification [14]. Therefore, we hypothesize that METTL3 may promote IL-6R expression through m6A methylation, thereby enhancing macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses in hemorrhoidal disease.

The transcription factor Smad2/3 is a member of the Smad family. When phosphorylated and activated, they translocate to the nucleus to control the expression of target genes [15], which are crucial for processes such as cell migration, differentiation, and proliferation [16]. In liver fibrosis, the molecular mechanisms through which Smad2/3 mediate hepatic inflammatory responses have been confirmed [17]. In inflammatory bowel disease, Smad2/3 is also involved in intestinal inflammatory processes [18]. Additionally, Smad2/3 serves as key regulatory mediators in macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses [19]. Notably, previous studies have shown that the nuclear translocation of Smad2/3 can promote METTL3 expression [20]. However, whether Smad2/3 can influence macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses in the hemorrhagic disease microenvironment by regulating METTL3 expression remains unclear. In summary, this study aimed to investigate the role of METTL3 in the inflammatory response to hemorrhoidal disease and elucidate the underlying mechanisms, providing new intervention targets for hemorrhoidal inflammation.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Sample Collection

Hemorrhoid tissue samples used in this study were collected from 24 patients who underwent hemorrhoid surgery at our hospital. Control tissue samples were obtained from 24 patients who underwent anal fistula surgery during the same period. To ensure the representativeness of the control samples, perianal tissues were collected from normal sites away from the anal fistula tract and inflammatory areas. The use of clinical samples in this study was approved by patients’ written informed consent, and all clinical-related experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Kunming Municipal Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (No.: [2021] Lunbei Zi (21)).

Animal Experiments

We purchased 15 SD rats aged 6–7 weeks (weighing 220 g–240 g) from Hunan Silaike Jingda Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. After a 1-week adaptive feeding period, the rats were randomly divided into 3 groups of 5 each and treated according to a preestablished experimental protocol: the croton oil (CO) group rats were induced with a hemorrhoid disease model as previously described [21]. Briefly, a sterile cotton swab with a diameter of 4 mm was soaked in 100 μL of CO and then inserted into the anus (20 mm from the anal verge) for 10 s to induce the hemorrhoid model. For the CO+sh-METTL3 group, rats were injected via the tail vein with 100 μL of lentivirus (Genepharma, China) at a titer of 1 × 107 TU/mL to knock down METTL3 expression 1 week before the hemorrhoid model was induced. The control group rats were treated with a sterile saline-moistened cotton swab using the same procedure as the CO group. Body weight was measured 5 days after CO induction, and all the rats were euthanized. The rectal-anal tissues (20 mm from the anal verge) were excised and weighed. Additionally, the rectoanal coefficient (RAC) was used to assess hemorrhoid severity, with lower RAC values indicating milder disease. In this study, the RAC was defined as the ratio of the weight (mg) of the rectal-anal portion in test animals to the body weight (g) of the same animal.

Cell Culture, Infection, and Treatment

Human monocytic leukemia THP-1 cells (CL-0233) were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (11875093; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (A5256701; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin dual antibiotics (C0222; Beyotime, China). Additionally, to induce the inflammatory phenotype of macrophages, the cells were first treated with 200 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; S7791; Shelleck, USA) for 24 h to induce differentiation into macrophages, followed by treatment with 5 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; HY-D1056; MCE, USA) for 24 h.

For cell infection, when the cell growth confluence reached 70%, the macrophages were seeded into medium supplemented with Smad2/3 knockdown (sh-Smad2/3), METTL3 knockdown (sh-METTL3), knockdown control (sh-NC), METTL3 overexpression (OE-METTL3), or overexpression control (OE-NC) lentiviruses (Genepharma, China) and incubated for 48 h, after which they were subjected to Western blotting to assess infection efficiency. Additionally, to detect the decay of IL-6R mRNA, macrophages were treated with 5 μm Actinomycin D (S8964; Shelleck, USA) for 0, 4, 8, or 12 h to inhibit mRNA synthesis, after which real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was used to measure the decay level of IL-6 mRNA.

Real-Time Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from clinical tissues and macrophages using TRIzol reagent (T9424; Sigma‒Aldrich, USA). The RNA was reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA using a cDNA first-strand synthesis kit (R312-01; Vazyme, China). RT-qPCR was subsequently performed using SYBR Green reagent (Q341-02; Vazyme, China). The results were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequence details are provided in Table 1.

Western Blot

Total protein from clinical tissues, rat anorectal tissues, and macrophages was extracted using RIPA buffer (89901; Thermo Fisher, USA) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor. The protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (PC0020; Solarbio, China). Equal amounts of total protein were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (IPFL00005; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The membranes were incubated with diluted primary antibodies at 4°C. The primary antibodies used in this study included METTL3 (1:1000; ab195352; Abcam, UK), iNOS (1:1000; ab178945; Abcam, UK), CD80 (1:1000; PA5-79001; Invitrogen, USA), CD86 (1:2000; MA5-32078; Invitrogen, USA), Smad2/3 (1:1000; ab202445; Abcam, UK), IL-6R (1:1000; MA5-29721; Invitrogen, USA), and β-catenin (1:1000; ab8226; Abcam, UK). The membranes were subsequently incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h and visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (WBULP, Millipore, USA). The protein bands were semiquantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry

Rat anorectal tissues were embedded in paraffin, and 5 μm thick sections were cut from the tissue blocks. The sections were then incubated with METTL3 primary antibody (1:200; ab195352; Abcam, UK) at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. After color development with DAB (P0202; Beyotime, China) and counterstaining with hematoxylin (C0107; Beyotime, China), the sections were observed and photographed under a microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Macrophages were seeded into 24-well plates precoated with cell climbing slides and treated according to experimental requirements. After treatment, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies against METTL3 (1:200; ab195352; Abcam, UK), iNOS (1:300; ab178945; Abcam, UK), and Smad2/3 (1:200; ab202445; Abcam, UK) at 4°C overnight. The cells were subsequently treated with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h, after which the nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (C0065; Solarbio, China) for 15 min. Finally, the cells were observed, and images were captured under a fluorescence microscope.

After rat anorectal tissue samples were obtained, tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against the macrophage surface marker CD68 (1:100; ab283654; Abcam, UK) and the M1 macrophage polarization marker iNOS (1:1000; ab178945; Abcam, UK). The corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies were subsequently incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Following 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (C0065; Solarbio, China) nuclear staining for 15 min, the sections were observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope.

HE Staining

An HE staining kit (G1076-500ML; Servicebio, China) was used to observe pathological changes in the rat anorectal tissues. Briefly, tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin. After dehydration with ethanol and permeabilization with xylene, the sections were observed under a microscope and photographed.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

The levels of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, SEKR-0009), interleukin-1β (IL-1β, SEKR-0002), and interleukin-6 (IL-6, SEKR-0005, SEKR-0002) in rat anorectal tissues were measured using ELISA kits from Solarbio (China). The level of interleukin-10 (IL-10) in rat anorectal tissues was determined using a rat IL-10 ELISA kit (CSB-E04595r) from CUSABIO (China). Briefly, the anorectal tissues were homogenized on ice and centrifuged at 5,000 g at 4°C for 5 min to obtain the supernatant. The absorbance of the supernatant at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader. Additionally, we used ELISA kits from Solarbio (China) to measure the levels of TNF-α (SEKH-0047), IL-1β (SEKH-0002), IL-6 (SEKH-0013), and IL-10 (SEKH-0018) in the supernatant of the macrophages.

Coimmunoprecipitation

The interactions between proteins were detected using a Pierce™ Co-IP Kit (26149; Thermo Fisher, USA). Macrophage lysates were incubated with Smad2/3 (1:100, ab202445, Abcam, UK) or IgG (1:100, ab172730, Abcam, UK) at 4°C overnight and then coupled with A/G magnetic beads. After precipitation and washing, detection was performed via Western blotting.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was conducted using the Pierce™ Magnetic ChIP Kit (26157; Thermo Fisher, USA). Smad2/3 (1:100; ab202445; abcam, UK) or IgG (1:100; ab172730; abcam, UK) antibodies were incubated with macrophage lysates at 4°C overnight. After precipitation and washing, DNA was extracted, and qPCR was used to detect enrichment of Smad2/3 in the METTL3 promoter region (F: 5′-GGG​GCA​GAT​CAC​GAG​GTC​A-3′, R: 5′-CCT​CAG​CCT​CCC​GAA​TAG​C-3′).

RNA Immunoprecipitation-qPCR

The m6A methylation level of IL-6R was detected using the PureBinding® RIP Kit (P0102; GENESEED, China). Macrophage lysates were incubated with m6A (1:100, ab208577, Abcam, UK) or IgG (1:100, ab172730, Abcam, UK) antibodies overnight at 4°C. After precipitation and washing, the RNA was collected, and the expression level of IL-6R mRNA (F: 5′-CGA​GCC​ACA​ACC​GAC​CAG​A-3′, R: 5′-ATC​CAT​CCC​ACC​TGC​CTG​A-3′) in the resulting complexes was detected by RT-qPCR. The relative expression level was used to evaluate the m6A modification level of IL-6R.

Statistical Analysis

All experimental data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.1 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A t test was used to compare data between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance was employed for comparisons among multiple groups, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. p values <0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

METTL3 Is Upregulated in Hemorrhoidal Disease and LPS-Induced Macrophages

Previous studies have shown that METTL3 is a key regulatory mediator of macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses in inflammatory bowel disease [13]. Therefore, to investigate the role of METTL3 in hemorrhoidal disease, we examined the expression of METTL3. We found that METTL3 mRNA (Fig. 1a) and protein (Fig. 1b) expression was significantly upregulated in clinical hemorrhoidal tissues. Second, METTL3 expression was markedly increased in the anorectal tissues of rats with hemorrhoidal disease (Fig. 1c, d). Additionally, LPS treatment promoted METTL3 expression in macrophages (Fig. 1e, f). These results indicate that METTL3 is upregulated in hemorrhoidal disease and LPS-induced macrophages.

METTL3 is upregulated in hemorrhoidal disease and LPS-induced macrophages. a RT-qPCR detection of METTL3 mRNA expression in clinical tissues. b Western blot detection of METTL3 expression in clinical tissues. c Western blot detection of METTL3 expression in rat anorectal tissues. d Immunohistochemical detection of METTL3 expression in rat anorectal tissues, scale bar, 50 μm. e Western blot detection of METTL3 expression in macrophages. f Immunofluorescence detection of METTL3 expression in macrophages, scale bar, 10 μm. ***p < 0.001.

METTL3 Knockdown Inhibits the Inflammatory Response and Macrophage Infiltration in Rat Anorectal Tissues

To investigate the effects of METTL3 on inflammation and macrophage infiltration in rat hemorrhoid disease, we knocked down METTL3 expression in rats. First, Western blot results showed that compared with the CO group, METTL3 expression was significantly downregulated in the CO sh-METTL3 group (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, RAC measurement revealed that CO treatment increased the value, whereas METTL3 knockdown reduced it (Fig. 2b). HE staining showed necrosis, epithelial edema, and marked inflammatory cell infiltration in the mucosal layer of rat anorectal tissue following CO treatment, all of which were ameliorated by METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 2c). Additionally, CO treatment upregulated the expression of the M1 macrophage polarization markers iNOS, CD80, and CD86 in anorectal tissues, while knocking down METTL3 downregulated their expression (Fig. 2d, e). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the coexpression levels of CD68 and iNOS in the anorectal tissues of CO-treated rats were increased, whereas knocking down METTL3 inhibited this coexpression (Fig. 2f). ELISA detection of the inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 revealed that CO treatment upregulated TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels but downregulated IL-10 levels in rat anorectal tissues, and these effects were partially reversed after METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 2g). These results demonstrate that METTL3 knockdown inhibits inflammatory responses and macrophage infiltration in rat hemorrhoid tissues.

METTL3 knockdown inhibits the inflammatory response and macrophage infiltration in rat anorectal tissues. a Western blotting was used to detect METTL3 expression in rat anorectal tissues. b The severity of hemorrhoidal disease was assessed using the RAC. c HE staining was used to detect pathological lesions in rat anorectal tissues, scale bar, 50 μm. d Western blotting was used to detect the expression of the M1 macrophage markers iNOS, CD80, and CD86 in rat anorectal tissues. e Histograms showing the protein expression levels of iNOS, CD80, and CD86 in rat anorectal tissues. f Immunofluorescence detection of co-expression of the macrophage marker CD68 and the M1 macrophage marker iNOS in rat anorectal tissues. g ELISA was used to measure the levels of the inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 in rat anorectal tissues. *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001.

METTL3 Knockdown Suppresses the LPS-Induced Macrophage Inflammatory Response

Next, to investigate the role of METTL3 in LPS-induced macrophage inflammation, we knocked down METTL3 in macrophages. Western blot results revealed that the expression level of METTL3 in the sh-METTL3 group was significantly lower than that in the sh-NC group (Fig. 3a), indicating the successful generation of METTL3-knockdown macrophages. Second, we found that LPS treatment upregulated the expression of iNOS, CD80, and CD86 in macrophages, whereas METTL3 knockdown suppressed the expression of iNOS, CD80, and CD86 (Fig. 3b–d). Additionally, LPS treatment increased the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 and decreased the level of IL-10 in macrophages, whereas METTL3 knockdown partially attenuated the effects of LPS (Fig. 3e). These results suggest that METTL3 knockdown inhibits LPS-induced inflammatory responses in macrophages.

METTL3 knockdown suppresses LPS-induced inflammatory responses in macrophages. a Western blot to detect the transfection efficiency of sh-METTL3. b Western blot to detect the expression of the M1 polarization markers iNOS, CD80, and CD86 in macrophages. c Histograms showing the protein expression levels of iNOS, CD80, and CD86 in macrophages. d Immunofluorescence detection of iNOS expression in macrophages, scale bar, 10 μm. e ELISA to measure the levels of the inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 in macrophages. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Nuclear Translocation of Smad2/3 Promotes METTL3 Expression

Previous studies have shown that upon translocation to the nucleus, Smad2/3 binds to METTL3 and subsequently promotes its expression [20]. Therefore, we also explored the effects of Smad2/3 on METTL3 expression in hemorrhoid disease. We found that the mRNA levels of Smad2 (Fig. 4a) and Smad3 (Fig. 4b) were significantly upregulated in clinical hemorrhoidal tissues and that the expression of Smad2 was positively correlated with that of METTL3 (Fig. 4c), whereas the expression of Smad3 was positively correlated with that of METTL3 (Fig. 4d). Additionally, we observed that LPS promoted the expression of Smad2/3 (Fig. 4e) and its nuclear translocation (Fig. 4f) in macrophages. Coimmunoprecipitation assay confirmed the interaction between Smad2/3 and METTL3 in macrophages (Fig. 4g). Further ChIP-qPCR results demonstrated that Smad2/3 binds to the promoter region of METTL3 in macrophages (Fig. 4h). Furthermore, to explore the effect of Smad2/3 on METTL3 expression, we knocked down Smad2/3 in macrophages. Western blot results revealed that the expression levels of Smad2/3 in the sh-Smad2/3 group were significantly lower than those in the sh-NC group (Fig. 4i), indicating the successful generation of Smad2/3-knockdown macrophages. Moreover, METTL3 expression was downregulated in LPS-exposed macrophages after Smad2/3 knockdown (Fig. 4j). These results demonstrate that Smad2/3 translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to METTL3 and subsequently upregulates METTL3 expression in macrophages.

Nuclear translocation of Smad2/3 promotes METTL3 expression. a RT-qPCR detection of Smad2 mRNA expression in clinical tissues. b RT-qPCR detection of Smad3 mRNA expression in clinical tissues. c Correlation analysis between METTL3 and Smad2 mRNA expression in clinical tissues. d Correlation analysis between METTL3 and Smad3 mRNA expression in clinical tissues. e Western blot detection of Smad2/3 expression in macrophages. f Immunofluorescence detection of Smad2/3 nuclear translocation in macrophages, scale bar, 10 μm. g Co-immunoprecipitation detection of the interaction between Smad2/3 and METTL3 in macrophages. h ChIP-qPCR determination of Smad2/3 binding to the METTL3 promoter region. i Western blot detection of sh-Smad2/3 infection efficiency. j Western blot detection of METTL3 expression in macrophages. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

METTL3 Promotes IL-6R Expression through m6A Methylation

Previous studies have indicated that IL-6R is involved in the inflammatory response of macrophages and intestinal epithelial cells during bacterial infection [9] and that the expression of IL-6R is also regulated by m6A modification [14]. Therefore, we investigated whether METTL3 regulates the expression of IL-6R in macrophages through m6A methylation. RT-qPCR results revealed that the expression of IL-6R mRNA was upregulated in clinical hemorrhoid tissues (Fig. 5a) and that the expression of IL-6R was positively correlated with that of METTL3 (Fig. 5b). Additionally, to explore the effect of METTL3 on IL-6R m6A modification and expression, we overexpressed METTL3 in macrophages. Western blot analysis of METTL3 expression revealed that the METTL3 expression level in the OE-METTL3 group was significantly greater than that in the OE-NC group (Fig. 5c), indicating the successful generation of METTL3-overexpressing macrophages. RT-qPCR results demonstrated that overexpression of METTL3 promoted IL-6R mRNA expression, whereas knockdown of METTL3 inhibited IL-6R mRNA expression (Fig. 5d). RIP-qPCR experiments to detect IL-6R m6A modification levels revealed that overexpression of METTL3 increased the m6A methylation of IL-6R, whereas knockdown of METTL3 decreased the m6A modification of IL-6R (Fig. 5e). Furthermore, overexpression of METTL3 in the presence of actinomycin D extended the half-life of IL-6R, whereas knockdown of METTL3 shortened the half-life of IL-6R mRNA (Fig. 5f). Furthermore, METTL3 overexpression promoted IL-6R protein expression, whereas METTL3 knockdown significantly downregulated IL-6R protein expression (Fig. 5g). These results indicate that METTL3 promotes IL-6R expression in macrophages through m6A methylation.

METTL3 promotes IL-6R expression through m6A methylation. a RT-qPCR detection of IL-6R mRNA expression in clinical tissues. b Correlation analysis of IL-6R and METTL3 mRNA expression in clinical tissues. c Western blot detection of METTL3 infection efficiency. d RT-qPCR detection of IL-6R mRNA expression in macrophages. e RIP-qPCR detection of m6A modification levels in IL-6R. f Actinomycin D experiment to assess IL-6R mRNA stability. g Western blot detection of IL-6R expression in macrophages. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Smad2/3 Promotes the LPS-Induced Macrophage Inflammatory Response by Enhancing METTL3/IL-6R Signaling

Finally, we explored the role of the Smad2/3-activated METTL3/IL-6R signaling axis in LPS-induced macrophage inflammation at the cellular level. We found that knocking down Smad2/3 in LPS-induced macrophages inhibited the expression of iNOS, CD80, and CD86, while overexpression of METTL3 increased the expression levels of iNOS, CD80, and CD86 (Fig. 6a–c). Second, knocking down Smad2/3 expression also decreased the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in macrophage culture supernatants and increased the level of IL-10, and these effects were partially reversed by METTL3 overexpression (Fig. 6d). Additionally, knocking down Smad2/3 expression in macrophages suppressed IL-6R expression, whereas METTL3 overexpression increased IL-6R expression (Fig. 6e). Collectively, these findings indicate that nuclear Smad2/3 binds METTL3 to drive its expression, which in turn upregulates IL-6R through m6A modification, ultimately exacerbating macrophage-mediated inflammation.

Discussion and Conclusion

Hemorrhoidal disease is a common benign anorectal condition that often leads to symptoms such as anal discomfort, pain, and bleeding and significantly impairs patients’ quality of life [22]. Notably, inflammatory responses play a critical role in multiple pathological processes of hemorrhoidal disease [23]. In this study, we revealed that high levels of METTL3 exacerbate inflammatory responses in hemorrhoidal disease by inducing inflammatory macrophage infiltration. Mechanistically, we discovered that after entering the nucleus, Smad2/3 interacts with METTL3 to promote its expression, which subsequently enhances macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses in hemorrhoidal disease by upregulating IL-6R expression through m6A modification.

Early histopathological studies have indicated that hemorrhoidal tissues are often accompanied by venous dilation, thrombosis, and damage to supporting tissues and mucosal suspensory ligaments [24]. Injury to the supporting connective tissues in hemorrhoids leads to insufficient fixation of the anal cushion, resulting in its prolapse into the anal canal [4]. When the prolapsed anal cushion is clamped by the sphincter muscles, it obstructs blood flow in the anal cushion [25], further triggering vascular wall damage and inflammatory responses in the surrounding tissues and anal cushion [26]. This induces the infiltration of large amounts of reactive oxygen species, inflammatory mediators, and inflammatory cells into local tissues, leading to extracellular matrix degradation and ulcer formation [27]. Additionally, as a common immune cell type in hemorrhoidal tissues [5], macrophages are rapidly activated under the induction of pro-inflammatory mediators, exacerbating the inflammatory response [28].

Notably, previous studies have highlighted the critical role of METTL3 in macrophage activation processes [29]. Furthermore, research by Yang et al. [30] demonstrated that overexpression of METTL3 exacerbates LPS-induced inflammation in intestinal epithelial cells and the progression of inflammatory bowel disease. Studies by Yin et al. [31] revealed that inhibiting METTL3 expression in macrophages alleviates intestinal inflammation. Therefore, we investigated whether METTL3 influences inflammatory responses in hemorrhoids by inducing macrophage polarization. Our findings revealed upregulated METTL3 expression in clinical hemorrhoidal tissues, anorectal tissues of hemorrhoid model rats, and LPS-induced macrophages. METTL3 knockdown reduced the RAC coefficient in hemorrhoid model rats, improved tissue damage, and decreased inflammatory cell infiltration. Concurrently, macrophage activation in the hemorrhoid tissues of rats and LPS-induced macrophages was reduced after METTL3 knockdown, with downregulation of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 and upregulation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Our study demonstrated that METTL3 exacerbates inflammatory responses in hemorrhoids by promoting macrophage activation. However, considering the functional complexity of METTL3, its inhibitory effects may be tissue- or cell-type specific. Studies have shown that inhibiting METTL3 in macrophages alleviates experimental colitis by upregulating IRAKM and promoting M2 polarization [32], which is consistent with our observation that METTL3 knockdown attenuates inflammatory responses in a hemorrhoid model, suggesting that the pro-inflammatory role of METTL3 in macrophages is conserved across disease models. On the other hand, research has demonstrated that METTL14, another member of the methyltransferase family, plays a critical role in maintaining colonic epithelial homeostasis: epithelial-specific deletion of METTL14 leads to colonic stem cell apoptosis, mucosal barrier dysfunction, and severe colitis [33]. Given that METTL3 and METTL14 are core catalytic components of the m6A methyltransferase complex and jointly catalyze m6A modification, whether METTL3 exerts a similar epithelial protective function in anorectal epithelial cells warrants attention. This indicates that the role of METTL3 in hemorrhoids requires more comprehensive investigation, and therapeutic strategies for hemorrhoids based on METTL3 inhibition should also take into account its potential multifaceted effects.

Smad proteins are a class of signal transduction and transcriptional regulators that mediate processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, and inflammation [34, 35]. Among them, Smad2/3, a member of the Smad family, has been confirmed to participate in hepatic inflammatory responses [17]. Research by Feng et al. [36] indicated that the overexpression of Smad2/3 promotes the progression of inflammation and fibrosis in patients with diabetic nephropathy. Studies by Yamashita et al. [18] demonstrated that high expression of Smad2/3 is a key factor in the development and progression of ulcerative colitis. Similarly, a study by Ghorbaninejad et al. [37] revealed that Smad2/3 is also an important regulatory factor in the inflammatory response of intestinal epithelial cells in inflammatory bowel disease. Notably, previous studies have shown that after nuclear translocation, Smad2/3 can promote METTL3 expression by binding to METTL3 [20]. Therefore, we investigated the effects of Smad2/3 on METTL3 expression and subsequent M1 macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses. We found that the expression of Smad2 and Smad3 is upregulated in hemorrhoidal tissues and that their expression is positively correlated with that of METTL3. Second, in macrophages, LPS promotes the expression and nuclear translocation of Smad2/3. Additionally, further coimmunoprecipitation and ChIP-qPCR experiments demonstrated that Smad2/3 and METTL3 interact and that Smad2/3 can bind to the promoter region of METTL3. Notably, knockdown of Smad2/3 expression inhibited LPS-induced METTL3 expression in macrophages and suppressed macrophage activation and inflammatory responses. Our study revealed that after nuclear translocation, Smad2/3 promotes METTL3 expression by binding to METTL3, thereby enhancing macrophage activation and exacerbating the inflammatory response in hemorrhoidal disease.

Furthermore, as a receptor for the inflammatory cytokine IL-6, IL-6R regulates inflammatory responses by binding to IL-6 [38]. A study by Luig et al. [39] demonstrated that IL-6R plays a role in macrophage activation during lupus nephritis. Research by Tu et al. [10] indicated that IL-6R is also involved in macrophage activation and choroidal neovascularization in retinal macular degeneration. In a study by Yao et al. [40], IL-6R was identified as a critical regulator of macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses and intestinal epithelial apoptosis under inflammatory infiltration in ulcerative colitis. Consistent with previous findings, we observed upregulated IL-6R expression in clinical hemorrhoid tissues. Notably, a study by Zeng et al. [14] revealed that IL-6R expression is also influenced by its m6A modification level. Additionally, in addition to mediating intestinal inflammation, METTL3 serves as a key m6A-modifying enzyme in macrophages [41]. Therefore, we further investigated the impact of METTL3 on IL-6R m6A modification and subsequent inflammatory responses in macrophages during hemorrhoidal disease. We found that METTL3 expression positively correlated with IL-6R expression in hemorrhoids and that overexpression of METTL3 enhanced IL-6R m6A modification and expression in macrophages, whereas METTL3 knockdown had the opposite effect. Moreover, Smad2/3 knockdown in LPS-induced macrophages suppressed IL-6R expression, which was reversed by METTL3 overexpression. Importantly, METTL3 overexpression in LPS-induced macrophages also mitigated the effects of Smad2/3 knockdown, leading to increased macrophage activation; elevated levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6; and reduced levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Our study demonstrated that Smad2/3 enhances macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses in hemorrhoidal disease by promoting METTL3/IL-6R signaling.

In summary, our study reveals a key mechanism through which the nuclear translocation of Smad2/3 promotes METTL3 expression through binding to METTL3 and by which upregulated METTL3 facilitates macrophage activation through m6A modification to increase IL-6R expression, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response in patients with hemorrhoidal disease. Our research provides a theoretical basis for targeting the Smad2/3-METTL3/IL-6R axis to treat hemorrhoidal inflammation.
