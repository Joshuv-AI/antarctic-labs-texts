Coefficient of variation of R-R intervals during deep breathing as a risk marker of orthostatic blood pressure decline in patients with Type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Qingbo Zhang, Mariko Sugiyama, Yu Liu, Naoko Matsuzawa, Mikiko Kojima, Keiko Furuta, Aya Kawaguchi, Yuko Shige, Ayano Osawa, Yaeko Terada, Ryoichi Banno, Akiko Yamamoto, Akiko Fujii, Hiroshi Arima, Etsuko Fujimoto, Yukari Takeno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic orthostatic hypotension, associated with cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy, can be assessed using the coefficient of variation of the R-R interval (CVRR). We investigated whether CVRR during deep breathing could serve as a risk indicator for orthostatic blood pressure decline in patients with type 2 diabetes. This study included 88 hospitalized patients aged 51.5–71.4 years with type 2 diabetes undergoing diabetes education or glycemic control. Blood pressure changes during postural transitions were measured using a non-invasive beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring device. Participants were divided into two groups based on CVRR values measured at rest and during deep breathing, using a cutoff of 2%. Blood pressure changes during postural transitions were compared. At rest, no significant differences in systolic or diastolic blood pressure changes were observed between the autonomic dysfunction group (CVRR ≤ 2%, n = 42) and the non-autonomic dysfunction group (CVRR >2%, n = 46; p > 0.05). However, during deep breathing, patients with CVRR ≤ 2% (n = 20) exhibited significantly greater decreases in systolic blood pressure at 0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–120, and 151–180 seconds after standing (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that a CVRR below 2% during deep breathing serves as a risk indicator for orthostatic systolic blood pressure decline in patients with type 2 diabetes.Key Words: autonomic dysfunction, coefficient of variation of R-R intervals during deep breathing, orthostatic hypotension, type 2 diabetes mellitus

INTRODUCTION

In type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), chronic poor glycemic control can lead to autonomic neuropathy and sensorimotor polyneuropathy,1-3 causing symptoms such as dizziness, lightheadedness, and orthostatic hypotension (OH).4 According to some studies, OH affects approximately 10–30% of patients with diabetes, a prevalence markedly higher than in healthy individuals.5,6 It is defined as inadequate blood pressure maintenance during the transition from seated or supine to standing, which increases the risk of falls.7 Raising awareness of these risks is crucial for T2DM management.

Upon standing, venous blood is redistributed to the lower extremities, requiring compensatory mechanisms to maintain blood pressure.8,9 The sympathetic nervous system plays a critical role by constricting peripheral vessels, preventing OH onset. This response is mediated by baroreceptor-mediated parasympathetic withdrawal and sympathetic activation, increasing heart rate and cardiac output.10,11 Therefore, contributions of both sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems should be considered when evaluating OH pathophysiology.

Various methods have been developed to assess autonomic nervous function related to OH. Among these, the orthostatic test is commonly used to evaluate sympathetic nervous activity. However, most previous studies utilizing this test have primarily focused on the incidence of OH,12,13 with limited attention given to the involvement of parasympathetic regulation during postural changes. To explore the potential involvement of parasympathetic function in this context, the coefficient of variation of the R-R interval (CVRR) has been introduced as a noninvasive and quantitative marker of parasympathetic nervous activity.2,14

Under physiological conditions, resting CVRR reflects baseline parasympathetic tone, whereas deep breathing CVRR indicates parasympathetic reactivity by capturing heart rate variability changes during controlled respiration.15-17 The disruption of autonomic balance can reduce heart rate variability, impairing blood pressure regulation and increasing fluctuations.18 In patients with diabetes, reduced CVRR at rest and during deep breathing has been associated with impaired supine blood pressure control,19,20 suggesting a broader role in blood pressure regulation. However, in patients with diabetes, the relative impact of reduced CVRR at rest versus during deep breathing on hemodynamic stability during orthostatic challenge remains unclear. Clarifying this may improve understanding of postural blood pressure decline and enhance clinical assessment of autonomic dysfunction in this population.

A previous study examined the effects of CVRR on postural blood pressure using age-adjusted thresholds derived from a regression model. By comparing raw CVRR values with individualized lower limits, the authors found an association between reduced resting CVRR and decreased diastolic blood pressure after standing, whereas they did not observe a statistically significant association for CVRR during deep breathing.21 That study included patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus and T2DM, and although no significant group differences were reported, type 1 diabetes mellitus is associated with greater severity of autonomic dysfunction than T2DM,22 which may have affected the evaluation of the relationship between CVRR and orthostatic blood pressure responses. To minimize this confounding and given the higher prevalence of T2DM, the present study focused specifically on patients with T2DM. Additionally, reliance on cuff-based blood pressure monitors may have limited the detection of rapid postural changes, as cuff inflation introduces time delays that could obscure transient drops.23 Continuous monitoring has greater sensitivity in detecting OH, particularly immediately after postural change.24

Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the roles of resting and deep breathing CVRR in orthostatic blood pressure regulation by employing noninvasive, beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring in patients with T2DM.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In this observational study, we compared orthostatic blood pressure changes between groups classified according to CVRR values.

Participants

This study included patients with T2DM aged 20–79 years who were hospitalized for preoperative glycemic control or diabetes education. Those using medications affecting the autonomic nervous system (eg, tranquilizers, stimulants, levodopa, antidepressants, beta-blockers, anticholinergics) were excluded. Patients with pacemakers or deemed unsuitable, such as those with difficulty standing, were also excluded. After screening for eligibility, study details were explained to patients on the day of admission, and those who provided written informed consent were enrolled. Data were collected at Department of Diabetes, Nagoya University Hospital, Japan, between October 2023 and July 2024. This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by Ethics Committee of Nagoya University, Department of Ethics Review (approval no. 2023-0213).

Continuous blood pressure monitoring, orthostatic test protocol, and data collection

Blood pressure was measured using a multi-item vital sign monitor (Radia Press μBP-mp, KANDS, Aichi, Japan), a noninvasive continuous blood pressure monitoring device. This device detects beat-to-beat changes in vascular blood flow using a reflective biosensor and calculates blood pressure values based on variations in the volume pulse wave.25 Prior to the orthostatic test, the sensor (RBP-100PWC-BS) probe was positioned on the skin overlying the left subclavian artery.

The orthostatic test was conducted between 10:15 and 12:00. On the test day, participants were instructed to refrain from coffee, smoking, alcohol, snacking, and strenuous physical activity. Testing was performed either fasting or at least 2 hours after a meal. Participants were also advised to avoid excessive water intake prior to the test.26

Before the orthostatic test, participants rested supine for approximately 10 minutes to stabilize blood pressure. The orthostatic test procedure (Fig. 1) was adapted from previous protocols.27,28 Participants independently transitioned from supine to seated, then standing, and remaining upright for 3 minutes, a period during which approximately 90% of OH cases can be detected.29 Blood pressure was continuously recorded throughout, and the procedure was video recorded to ensure accurate timing of transitions. The orthostatic test and CVRR measurements were performed on different days to minimize potential mutual influence.30 CVRR testing was conducted on the day of admission, whereas the orthostatic test was scheduled within three days after admission.

Orthostatic test method

Participants independently transitioned from supine to seated then finally to standing and remaining upright for 3 minutes. Baseline blood pressure was defined as the average during the last 2 minutes of supine rest, and subsequent blood pressure changes were calculated as absolute differences from baseline.

Phase A corresponds to the supine-to-sitting to standing, followed by Phase 1 (0–30 seconds), Phase 2 (31–60 seconds), Phase 3 (61–90 seconds), Phase 4 (91–120 seconds), Phase 5 (121–150 seconds), and Phase 6 (151–180 seconds).

CVRR data at rest and during deep breathing, including demographic variables (age, sex, body mass index, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and diabetic retinopathy), and clinical information—including hemoglobin A1c level, disease duration, estimated glomerular filtration rate, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, cardio-ankle vascular index, and the use of antihypertensive and glucose-lowering medications—were all extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records system.

CVRR measurement

CVRR at rest and during deep breathing was measured by a clinical technologist in the hospital’s clinical physiology laboratory, using an FCP-8700 electrocardiograph with analytical functions (Fukuda Denshi Co, Tokyo, Japan). As part of the routine procedure in the clinical physiology laboratory, participants remained supine for approximately five minutes to facilitate heart rate stabilization. Following this, 100 consecutive R-R intervals were recorded for CVRR calculation. During deep breathing, participants remained supine and breathed at a standardized rate of six breaths per minute, with R-R intervals recorded continuously and CVRR calculated in the same manner. The device automatically excluded ectopic beats or artifacts to ensure reliable measurements. Finally, CVRR was expressed as the standard deviation of R-R intervals divided by the mean R-R interval, as a percentage.

Participant classification

Although no universal cutoff value for CVRR has been established for the assessment of autonomic dysfunction, a threshold of 2% was adopted in this study based on previous evidence. Kageyama et al reported that patients with diabetes and autonomic symptoms such as OH exhibited markedly lower resting CVRR values, averaging 2.25%.31 Similarly, Asakawa et al found that patients with T2DM and peripheral neuropathy had a mean CVRR of 2.14 ± 1.20% during deep breathing,32 which was measured using the same method as in the present study for both resting and deep breathing conditions. Accordingly, participants were classified into two groups based on CVRR values at both rest and during deep breathing: CVRR > 2% (non-autonomic dysfunction group) and CVRR ≤ 2% (autonomic dysfunction group).

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.6 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf) for a repeated-measures analysis of variance with two groups and multiple measurement points (effect size f = 0.25,33 α = 0.05, power = 0.95, correlation among repeated measures = 0.3). Although the final analyses were performed using linear mixed-effects models, this calculation provided an approximate estimate because repeated-measures analysis of variance and linear mixed-effects model share a similar repeated-measures structure. The calculation indicated a total of 36 participants, with 18 per group.

Statistical analysis

The average blood pressure during the last 2 minutes in the supine position was defined as baseline. Blood pressure fluctuations were calculated by subtracting baseline values from subsequent measurements. The duration of postural transition from supine to sitting to standing (Phase A) varied among participants and was measured using video recordings. After Phase A, participants remained standing for three minutes, divided into six 30-second intervals: Phase 1 (0–30 seconds), Phase 2 (31–60 seconds), Phase 3 (61–90 seconds), Phase 4 (91–120 seconds), Phase 5 (121–150 seconds), and Phase 6 (151–180 seconds). Mean blood pressure was calculated for each phase, and changes from baseline were expressed as absolute differences. Thirty-second intervals balance temporal resolution with statistical stability and are broadly in line with previous studies segmenting the standing period into short intervals.34

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges, categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons of baseline characteristics used the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

To examine group differences in blood pressure fluctuations following postural change, linear mixed-effects models were applied using EZR version 1.61 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Japan). Each model included time (seven measurement points), presence or absence of autonomic dysfunction, and their interaction (time × group) as fixed effects. Participant ID was included as a random intercept. Age, sex, hemoglobin A1c, duration of diabetes, and estimated glomerular filtration rate were included as covariates. Post hoc comparisons of estimated marginal means were conducted at each time point, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance set at α = 0.05.

RESULTS

Basic characteristics of the study population

Eighty-eight patients with T2DM, 56 males and 32 females, were included in this study. The median age was 61.0 years (25th-75th percentile, 51.5–71.4 years), the median body mass index was 20.8 kg/m2 (25th-75th percentile, 17.6–23.3 kg/m2); regarding alcohol consumption, occasional drinkers were 18 (20.7%) and daily drinkers were 11 (12.6%), smokers were 11 (12.8%), diabetic retinopathy was present in 23 (28.4%), and the median hemoglobin A1c level was 8.5% (25th-75th percentile, 7.6%–9.3%). The median duration of T2DM was 10 years (25th-75th percentile, 3–20 years), the median estimated glomerular filtration rate was 75.5 mL/min/1.73 m² (25th-75th percentile, 62.6–89.4 mL/min/1.73 m²), the median urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio was 18.2 mg/g (25th-75th percentile, 6.9–58.4 mg/g), and the median left-side cardio-ankle vascular index was 8.2 (25th-75th percentile, 7.3–9.6). Antihypertensive medications were used by 43.2% of participants, including three patients (7.9%) who were on diuretics. Among glucose-lowering medications, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors were used by 15 patients (17%). OH, defined as a decrease in systolic blood pressure of ≥20 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of ≥10 mmHg upon standing, was observed in four patients (4.5%) based on systolic blood pressure but was not observed in patients based on the diastolic criteria. The median CVRR values were 1.91% (25th-75th percentile, 1.37%–2.44%) at rest and 3.40% (25th-75th percentile, 2.16%–4.91%) during deep breathing (Table 1).

Group characteristics based on CVRR at rest and during deep breathing

Participants were categorized into two groups based on CVRR at rest (non-autonomic dysfunction group, n = 42 and autonomic dysfunction group, n = 46) and CVRR during deep breathing (n = 68 and n = 20, respectively), using a cutoff value of 2%. In the grouping based on CVRR at rest, significant differences were observed in hemoglobin A1c levels (8.1% [25th-75th percentile, 7.4%–8.8%] versus 8.90% [25th-75th percentile, 8.2%–9.8%], U = 1259.5, p = 0.014) and CVRR at rest (2.50% [25th-75th percentile, 2.14%–3.22%] versus 1.39% [25th-75th percentile, 1.01%–1.67%], U = 0, p < 0.001) between the non-autonomic and autonomic dysfunction groups. In the grouping based on CVRR during deep breathing, significant differences were noted in estimated glomerular filtration rate (82.2 mL/min/1.73 m² [25th-75th percentile, 65.5–98.8 mL/min/1.73 m²] versus 66.9 mL/min/1.73 m² [25th-75th percentile, 38.4–71.6 mL/min/1.73 m²], U = 351.5, p < 0.001) and CVRR during deep breathing (3.91% [25th-75th percentile, 3.14%–5.52%] versus 1.21% [25th-75th percentile, 0.96%–1.59%], U = 0, p < 0.001). However, no other baseline characteristics differed significantly between the groups in either classification (all p > 0.05; Table 1).

Effect of CVRR at rest on blood pressure fluctuations

To evaluate the association between CVRR and orthostatic blood pressure variability, linear mixed-effects models were employed, with adjustment for potential confounders. Adjusted outcomes are presented as estimated marginal means with corresponding standard errors (SE). For reference, unadjusted raw values (mean ± standard deviation) are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

In the non-autonomic dysfunction group, the estimated marginal mean systolic blood pressure increased by 11.74 ± 1.08 mmHg from baseline during the postural transition phase (Phase A), followed by a progressive decline across the standing phases: −1.32 ± 1.08 mmHg in Phase 1 (0–30 seconds), −3.53 ± 1.08 mmHg in Phase 2 (31–60 seconds), −3.21 ± 1.08 mmHg in Phase 3 (61–90 seconds), −2.56 ± 1.08 mmHg in Phase 4 (91–120 seconds), −3.42 ± 1.09 mmHg in Phase 5 (121–150 seconds), and −3.34 ± 1.09 mmHg in Phase 6 (151–180 seconds) (Table 2).

Effect of CVRR during deep breathing on blood pressure fluctuations

In the non-autonomic dysfunction group, the estimated marginal mean systolic blood pressure increased by 11.75 ± 0.84 mmHg in Phase A, followed by a decline across the subsequent standing phases: −1.03 ± 0.84 mmHg in Phase 1, −3.19 ± 0.84 mmHg in Phase 2, −2.63 ± 0.84 mmHg in Phase 3, −2.30 ± 0.84 mmHg in Phase 4, −2.85 ± 0.84 mmHg in Phase 5, and −2.87 ± 0.84 mmHg in Phase 6.

In the autonomic dysfunction group, the estimated marginal mean systolic blood pressure increased by 11.17 ± 1.76 mmHg in Phase A, followed by a decline across the subsequent standing phases: −4.93 ± 1.76 mmHg in Phase 1, −8.46 ± 1.76 mmHg in Phase 2, −6.89 ± 1.76 mmHg in Phase 3, −6.21 ± 1.76 mmHg in Phase 4, −6.33 ± 1.76 mmHg in Phase 5, and −6.79 ± 1.76 mmHg in Phase 6.

The linear mixed-effects model showed no significant main effect of the deep-breathing CVRR group on systolic blood pressure (β = −0.95, SE = 1.96, t (136) = −0.49, p = 0.628). However, significant time × group interactions were observed in Phase 2 (β = −4.31, SE = 1.59, t (535) = −2.72, p = 0.0068) and Phase 3 (β = −3.30, SE = 1.58, t (535) = −2.09, p = 0.0375) upon standing. Random intercepts for subjects accounted for individual variability (SD = 5.08 mmHg), and the residual standard deviation was 4.00 mmHg. The model converged successfully (REML = 3696.3). Model fit was acceptable (AIC = 3746.2, BIC = 3852.9). Post hoc comparisons revealed significantly greater systolic blood pressure reductions in the autonomic dysfunction group in Phase 1 (β = −3.89, SE = 1.96, t (136) = 1.98, p = 0.0493), Phase 2 (β = −5.26, SE = 1.96, t (136) = 2.68, p = 0.0082), Phase 3 (β = −4.25, SE = 1.96, t (136) = 2.17, p = 0.0318), Phase 4 (β = −3.91, SE = 1.96, t (136) = 2.00, p = 0.0483), and Phase 6 (β = −3.92, SE = 1.96, t (136) = 2.00, p = 0.0479). DISCUSSION

The findings of our study indicate that the T2DM patients with reduced CVRR during deep breathing showed significant decline in systolic blood pressure immediately after standing.

Specifically, a CVRR value below 2% during deep breathing served as a risk indicator for a decrease in systolic blood pressure at 0–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–120 (total 0–120 seconds), and 151–180 seconds after rising from the supine position. In patients with diabetes, CVRR is a widely used and reliable indicator of autonomic dysfunction, with shortterm measurements showing intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.58–0.90, an outcome indicative of the reliability of the findings.15 Compared with other autonomic function tests, CVRR is reproducible and stable.35 A value below 2%, whether at rest or during deep breathing, may suggest the presence of autonomic dysfunction with clinical symptoms.31,32 Although the CVRR at rest primarily reflects basal parasympathetic tone during spontaneous respiration,36 CVRR during deep breathing serves as a dynamic indicator of parasympathetic responsiveness to controlled stimuli.37 Deep breathing enhances the high-frequency component of heart rate variability, a well-established marker of cardiac vagal activity.38 This increase may occur independently of changes in heart rate or blood pressure,39 indicating that CVRR during deep breathing enables a more precise assessment of parasympathetic function.

Under physiological conditions, transitioning from the supine to standing position elicits a rapid autonomic response to increase blood pressure, characterized by parasympathetic withdrawal and sympathetic activation. This compensatory mechanism increases heart rate and peripheral vasoconstriction.40 Baroreceptors located in the carotid sinus and aortic arch detect the initial blood pressure drop within seconds of standing, triggering autonomic adjustments via the afferent signaling pathways.37,41-43 However, in patients with diabetes mellitus, baroreflex sensitivity and parasympathetic responsiveness are often impaired.44 These impairments may delay the autonomic compensation required for maintaining blood pressure after postural changes, resulting in insufficient or mistimed hemodynamic regulation. The present findings—as illustrated in Fig. 3A—demonstrate that the group with autonomic dysfunction exhibited a more pronounced decline in systolic blood pressure after standing compared with the group without autonomic dysfunction.

Taken together, these findings suggest that parasympathetic dysfunction—particularly when CVRR during deep breathing falls below 2%—may serve as a risk indicator for impaired cardiovascular compensation during postural transitions, potentially contributing to the development of OH. The observation that reduced CVRR during deep breathing may serve as a risk indicator for a decline in systolic blood pressure contrasts with previous findings in hospitalized patients with diabetes, where no such relationship was reported.21 One possible explanation is variation in diagnostic criteria for CVRR, which may have influenced group classification. The present study used a uniform threshold of 2% for CVRR, offering a practical and clinically feasible criterion. In terms of blood pressure responsiveness, systolic blood pressure is generally more sensitive to postural changes than diastolic blood pressure.45 Methodological differences may also explain the discrepancy; earlier studies measured blood pressure only once—1 minute after standing—potentially missing transient hemodynamic fluctuations. By contrast, this study used beat-to-beat monitoring for 3 minutes, revealing the greatest systolic decline between 31–60 seconds (estimated marginal mean = −8.46 ± 1.76 mmHg, SE = 1.76). These findings highlight the value of CVRR ≤ 2% during deep breathing as an indicator of reduced parasympathetic responsiveness and the superiority of continuous monitoring for detecting hemodynamic instability.

In this study, a sustained decrease in systolic blood pressure was observed from 0 to 120 seconds after standing, followed by a slight rebound at 121–150 seconds and a decline at 151–180 seconds. These fluctuations may reflect confounding factors or the limited sample size in the autonomic dysfunction group under deep breathing. To better elucidate individual contributions to postural blood pressure changes over time, efforts are underway to expand the sample size, include a healthy control group, and perform multivariate analyses adjusted for medication use, diabetes duration, and renal function.

In summary, this study revealed that in patients with T2DM, a CVRR below 2% during deep breathing served as a risk indicator for a reduction in systolic blood pressure within 0–120 and 151–180 seconds after postural transition, with the most pronounced decline occurring during the 31–60 seconds interval. These findings highlight that CVRR during deep breathing may serve as a useful indicator for identifying patients at risk of orthostatic blood pressure decline. In this study, a 2% threshold was applied as a preliminary, clinically feasible reference; however, its utility as a single-measure marker of autonomic dysfunction requires further validation. Assessment of parasympathetic responsiveness may facilitate early detection of autonomic dysfunction and help identify patients at risk of OH, potentially contributing to preventive strategies for rehabilitation and fall risk management.
