Regulating oxygen activation and catalytic kinetics in ZnCo2O4 nanozymes via Mn substitution: insights into the oxidase-like activity mechanism
Authors: Duong Hoang Thuc, Vu Thi Huong Mai, Chu Quynh Mai, Pham-Cong De, Van Tan Tran, Vinh Ya, Quoc Huy Thi, Xuan-Dinh Ngo, Van-Tuan Hoang, Anh-Tuan Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mn-substituted ZnCo2O4 (Mn-ZnCo2O4) nanozymes were rationally engineered to regulate the intrinsic redox behavior and oxidase-like activity of pure ZnCo2O4. Partial substitution of Mn ions into the spinel lattice altered the local electronic structure and surface redox couples, increased the availability of mixed-valence states and enhanced surface reactivity. These changes promoted oxygen activation and accelerated interfacial electron-transfer processes during catalytic oxidation. Steady-state kinetic analysis experimentally confirmed that the optimized Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme exhibited a reduced Michaelis–Menten constant (Km = 0.860 mM) and an increased maximum reaction velocity (Vmax = 127 × 10−8 M s−1), indicating a marked improvement in catalytic efficiency relative to unsubstituted ZnCo2O4. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were further performed to provide mechanistic insights into the reaction mechanism of the Mn-ZnCo2O4/TMB/AA system. Building on this mechanistic understanding, a colorimetric sensing strategy for ascorbic acid (AA) was developed based on the Mn-ZnCo2O4/TMB system. The proposed method enabled AA quantification over a linear concentration range of 50–1000 µM with a detection limit of 26 µM. Its practical applicability was verified using real orange juice samples, and the obtained results agreed well with those from the classical iodine titration method. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Mn substitution provides an effective route for tailoring the catalytic properties of ZnCo2O4 nanozymes, while also enabling their practical use in colorimetric analysis of redox-active analytes in complex matrices.

Introduction

Zinc cobaltite (ZnCo2O4) is a mixed-metal oxide belonging to the spinel AB2O4 family and crystallizes in an isotropic cubic spinel structure, where a three-dimensional oxygen sublattice provides a robust and spatially uniform framework.1,2 In the normal spinel configuration, Zn2+ ions preferentially occupy the tetrahedral A-sites, whereas Co2+/Co3+ ions occupy the octahedral B-sites, constructing an interconnected Co–O–Co network.1 This cation arrangement not only ensures high structural stability but also facilitates efficient charge transport through Co3+/Co2+ electron hopping within the isotropic cubic lattice.3 Owing to these crystallographic and electronic features, ZnCo2O4 has attracted considerable attention as a multifunctional catalytic material and has been extensively investigated in electrocatalysis,2,4 photocatalysis,5 energy storage,6 gas sensing,7,8 and electrochemical sensing applications.9

Beyond its conventional catalytic and electrochemical applications, the intrinsic redox activity and oxygen-activation capability of ZnCo2O4 render it a promising platform for enzyme-mimicking catalysis. In this context, oxidase-like nanozymes are particularly attractive because they can directly activate molecular oxygen (O2) to oxidize chromogenic substrates such as 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), o-phenylenediamine (OPD), and ABTS without requiring external oxidants, thereby simplifying analytical procedures and enhancing operational safety.10–12 However, despite these favorable attributes, ZnCo2O4-based nanozymes reported to date have predominantly exhibited peroxidase-like (POD) activity,13–15 whereas their intrinsic oxidase-like (OXD) activity remains largely unexplored.16 This limitation mainly arises from the inherently weak O2 activation capability of pure ZnCo2O4 under ambient conditions. Unlike POD-like catalysis, which relies on externally supplied H2O2 as a strong oxidant, OXD-like reactions critically depend on efficient O2 adsorption, activation, and interfacial electron transfer.17 In pure ZnCo2O4, moderate electrical conductivity, a limited concentration of oxygen vacancies (OVs), and an insufficient density of surface-exposed redox-active sites collectively restrict O2 activation, resulting in a weak oxidase-like catalytic response.18,19

Given the pivotal role of oxygen vacancies in governing O2 adsorption, activation, and catalytic electron transfer, introducing OVs into metal oxides has been widely recognized as an effective strategy to enhance catalytic activity.20,21 Mechanistically, surface OVs serve as preferential adsorption sites for molecular oxygen, where they facilitate the weakening and cleavage of the O–O bond and lower the energy barrier for the formation of reactive oxygen species.22 Simultaneously, these vacancies act as electron-rich centres that donate charge to surface-adsorbed intermediates, thereby accelerating the redox cycle of multivalent metal cations that underpins oxidase- and peroxidase-like catalysis.23 Furthermore, OVs modulate the local electronic structure-narrowing the band gap, shifting the d-band centre, and enhancing electrical conductivity-which collectively promote interfacial charge transfer and improve catalytic turnover.24,25 These multifaceted mechanistic contributions have motivated extensive efforts to develop synthetic strategies capable of controllably generating and stabilising high concentrations of oxygen vacancies in metal oxide nanostructures.

While MOF-derived strategies can generate high defect densities, their reliance on complex precursors and high-temperature treatments restrict scalability and reproducibility.26,27 In contrast, metal-ion doping offers a synthetically simple and controllable approach to simultaneously enrich oxygen vacancies and increase the density of catalytically active sites, while suppressing nanoparticle aggregation that commonly leads to activity deterioration.28–30 Accordingly, metal-ion doping represents a practical and effective strategy for constructing defect-rich ZnCo2O4 nanozymes to overcome their intrinsically weak oxidase-like activity.

Among various transition-metal candidates, manganese (Mn) is particularly attractive as a substituent element for ZnCo2O4 owing to its multiple accessible oxidation states (Mn2+/Mn3+/Mn4+) and strong redox adaptability.31 These features endow Mn with a high propensity to induce charge imbalance when substituted into the ZnCo2O4 lattice, which in turn promotes the formation of oxygen vacancies for charge compensation. Unlike noble-metal or rare-earth modification, Mn substitution can be readily achieved through simple synthesis routes while maintaining the structural integrity of the spinel lattice. Moreover, the incorporation of Mn is expected to induce lattice distortion and effectively regulate the Co3+/Co2+ redox equilibrium, thereby enhancing electron-transfer kinetics.31,32 Such synergistic effects, including oxygen-vacancy enrichment and electronic-structure modulation, are highly favorable for molecular oxygen adsorption and activation, providing a rational basis for boosting the intrinsic oxidase-like activity of ZnCo2O4-based nanozymes.

In this work, Mn-substituted ZnCo2O4 (Mn-ZnCo2O4) nanozymes were systematically constructed to investigate the enhancement of oxidase-like activity induced by oxygen-vacancy engineering. Mn incorporation induces synergistic effects, including lattice distortion, oxygen-vacancy enrichment, electronic-structure modulation, and an increased density of surface-exposed active sites, collectively resulting in enhanced catalytic performance. Based on this property, a colorimetric sensing system was established using 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) as the chromogenic substrate and ascorbic acid (AA) as a representative reducing probe.

DFT calculations reveal that Mn substitution strengthens TMB binding on ZnCo2O4, consistent with the enhanced oxidase-like activity. Frontier orbital analysis shows that TMB possesses a higher HOMO energy level (−5.195 eV) compared to AA (−7.345 eV), confirming that TMB is thermodynamically more favorable for electron donation during the catalytic oxidation process. In the Mn-ZnCo2O4/TMB system, Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozymes catalyze the activation of dissolved oxygen to generate reactive oxygen species, which oxidize TMB into a blue-colored product with a characteristic absorbance at 652 nm. Upon the introduction of AA, competitive oxidation occurs due to its lower HOMO energy level, leading to the reduction of oxidized TMB and a decrease in color intensity. Based on this mechanism, a sensitive colorimetric platform for AA detection was developed. The method was successfully applied to real sample analysis, showing good agreement with conventional iodine titration, thereby confirming the reliability and practical applicability of Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozymes for cost-effective oxidase-like sensing.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

All chemicals were used as received without further purification. Manganese(ii) chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2·4H2O, 99%), zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, 99%), cobalt(ii) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 98.5%), and urea ((NH2)2CO, 99%) were purchased from Xilong Scientific Co. Ltd, China. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 35%) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 96%) were supplied by Duc Giang Chemical JSC, Vietnam. Double-distilled water used for all experiments was obtained from a Milli-Q® system (18.2 MΩ cm−1 at 25 °C). 3,3,5,5-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB, 98%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Sodium chloride (NaCl, 99.5%, Xilong), potassium chloride (KCl, 99%, Fuchen), and KH2PO4 (Merck KGaA, Germany, >99%). The phosphate buffer solution (PBS, pH 2.5) was prepared from a mixture of NaCl, KCl, HCl, and KH2PO4.

Experimental procedures

Preparation of Mn-ZnCo2O4 (Mn0.5Zn0.5Co2O4) nanozyme system

Mn-ZnCo2O4 was synthesized by a facile microwave-assisted hydrothermal method. Briefly, 2.5 mmol of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, 2.5 mmol of MnCl2·4H2O, and 10 mmol of Co(NO3)2·6H2O were dissolved in 250 mL of deionized (DI) water under stirring, followed by the addition of 60 mmol of urea dissolved in 50 mL of DI water. The mixture was transferred into a 500 mL Teflon-lined composite fiber vessel for microwave-assisted hydrothermal process using a SINEO UWave-2000 reactor at 140 °C for 20 min under 700 W microwave power. The product was cooled to room temperature, collected by centrifugation, washed with ethanol and deionized water, dried at 80 °C for 8 h and calcined at 400 °C for 2 h to yield Mn-ZnCo2O4 powder.

Oxidase-like activity and kinetic analysis of Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme

The oxidase-like catalytic activity of the Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme was evaluated using 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) as the chromogenic substrate. The reaction was initiated by mixing 1.2 mL of nanozyme solution (0.028 mg mL−1) with 1.2 mL of PBS buffer (pH 2.5) and incubating for 5 min, followed by the addition of 0.8 mL of TMB solution (2.25 mM). The reaction proceeded under shaking for 500 s. The effects of nanozyme concentration, TMB concentration, buffer pH, and reaction time were systematically investigated to determine the optimal catalytic conditions. The oxidase-like kinetics were studied by monitoring the time-dependent absorbance of the Mn-ZnCo2O4/TMB system at 652 nm, corresponding to the formation of oxidized TMB (ox-TMB).

To identify the reactive oxygen species (ROS) involved in the catalytic oxidation, selective quenching tests were performed under the optimized conditions. Each scavenger (p-benzoquinone for ˙O2−, tert-butanol for ˙OH, or KI for 1O2) was added at a final volume of 600 µL to the reaction mixture before initiating the reaction with TMB; an equal volume of deionized water was used in the control. After 500 s of reaction, the absorbance at 652 nm was recorded. A significant reduction in absorbance relative to the control was taken as evidence for the participation of the corresponding ROS. Each experiment was repeated at least three times, and the results are presented as mean ± SD.

The initial reaction rates were obtained from the linear region of the absorbance-time curves at different TMB concentrations. The kinetic parameters (Km and Vmax) were calculated according to the Lineweaver–Burk equation.

Analytical methods

Solid characterization

X-ray Diffraction (SIEMENS D5005, Bruker, Germany) was used to identify the phase of the solids. The morphology, elemental composition, and elemental mapping of solid samples were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray detector (SEM-EDX, JEOL JSM-7610F, JEOL Ltd, Japan). The X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, America, Al Kα radiation, 1486.6 eV) was employed to analyze the chemical state of materials. Raman shift of the solids was obtained using MacroRAM, Horiba Scientific, Japan.

Colorimetric detection of AA by Mn-ZnCo2O4-TMB system

Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme. In this system, Mn-ZnCo2O4 catalyzes the oxidation of TMB to the blue-colored ox-TMB with a characteristic absorption peak at ∼652 nm. In the presence of AA, ox-TMB is reduced back to its colorless form, resulting in a decrease in absorbance at 652 nm. Under optimized conditions (nanozyme concentration, TMB concentration, buffer pH, and reaction time), the absorbance change (ΔAbs652) showed a linear relationship with AA concentration, enabling quantitative detection.

For quantitative analysis, Mn-ZnCo2O4 was first incubated with AA solutions of different concentrations in acetate buffer, followed by the addition of TMB to initiate the reaction. After a fixed reaction time at room temperature, the absorbance at 652 nm was recorded, and ΔA (difference between the blank and AA-containing samples) was used to construct the calibration curve. The selectivity of the proposed method was evaluated using common potential interferents, including inorganic ions and small biomolecules. These species produced negligible changes in the colorimetric response compared to AA, demonstrating the high specificity of the Mn-ZnCo2O4 -based assay. Overall, the results confirm the applicability of this colorimetric platform for accurate AA detection in complex sample matrices.

Determination of AA in real samples

Sample preparation

Fresh orange juice was selected as a representative real sample to evaluate the practical applicability and detection performance of the proposed sensing method. The juice was first filtered to remove pulp and insoluble residues. To stabilize ascorbic acid (AA) and minimize matrix interferences, the clarified juice was extracted with a metaphosphoric acid solution following a procedure commonly employed for AA determination in food samples.33 The mixture was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was carefully collected. Finally, the obtained extract was diluted with distilled water to adjust the concentration range prior to colorimetric analysis.

Nanozyme/TMB colorimetric method

For ascorbic acid (AA) determination using the nanozyme/TMB system, an appropriate volume of the pretreated orange juice extract was introduced into the reaction mixture containing the nanozyme catalyst and TMB under optimized conditions. The nanozyme-catalyzed oxidation of TMB generated a blue-colored oxidized TMB (ox-TMB), while AA reduced ox-TMB, resulting in a decrease in absorbance at 652 nm. The absorbance changes at 652 nm (ΔAbs652) was recorded using a UV-vis spectrophotometer. The AA content in the samples was quantified by comparing the measured ΔAbs652 values with a previously established AA calibration curve, and the concentration was calculated from the corresponding linear regression equation with correction for the dilution factor.

Iodine titration method

For comparison, the ascorbic acid (AA) content in orange juice samples was determined using the classical iodine titration method. An appropriate volume of the diluted orange juice extract (100.0 mL) was transferred into an Erlenmeyer flask, followed by the addition of 2–3 drops of 1% (w/v) starch solution as an indicator. The sample was titrated with a standardized iodine solution (0.015 M I2) under continuous stirring until the appearance of a stable pale-blue coloration that persisted for at least 30 s, indicating the endpoint.34 The AA concentration was calculated based on the stoichiometric redox reaction between AA and iodine, as described by the following equation:1C6H8O6 (AA) + I2 → C6H6O6 (DHAA) + 2I− + 2H+

The results obtained from iodine titration were used as reference values to evaluate the accuracy of the proposed nanozyme/TMB method.

Characterization of Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme system

The crystal structures of ZnCo2O4 and Mn-ZnCo2O4 are displayed in Fig. 1, highlighting Mn partial substitution at tetrahedral sites. The phase composition and vibrational properties of the synthesized materials were examined via XRD and Raman spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 1A and B, respectively. The morphologies of the pure ZnCo2O4 and Mn-ZnCo2O4 samples are illustrated in Fig. 1C and D.

(A) XRD patterns of ZnCo2O4 and Mn-ZnCo2O4, (B) Raman spectrum of Mn-ZnCo2O4, (C and D) SEM image (photo inset: crystal structure and EDX elemental composition (wt%)) of ZnCo2O4 and Mn-ZnCo2O4.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the ZnCo2O4 (ZCO) and Mn-ZnCo2O4 (MZ5) samples were recorded to elucidate the crystalline structural changes driving the morphological evolution and to confirm phase purity. As shown in Fig. 1A, the diffraction patterns of both samples match well with the standard spinel ZnCo2O4 (JCPDS No. 23-1390).35 For pure ZCO, the peaks at 31.28°, 36.58°, 38.48°, 44.58°, 55.58°, 59.38° and 65.13° can be indexed to the (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511) and (440) planes, respectively. The MZ5 sample exhibits analogous reflections at 31.28°, 36.68°, 38.53°, 44.58°, 55.58°, 59.13° and 65.18° with no extra impurity peaks, confirming the high purity and crystallinity of both materials.

Upon Mn substitution, a slight shift of the (311) peak from 36.58 to 36.68° is observed, while the (511) peak shifts in the opposite direction (from 59.38° to 59.13°). Although an isotropic lattice contraction would cause all peaks to move to higher angles, the non-uniform shift observed here suggests that the structural change may involve local lattice distortions, partial cation redistribution, or measurement uncertainties rather than a simple uniform contraction. The shifts can be qualitatively associated with the isomorphous substitution of host cations by Mn ions with different ionic radii. More revealing is the evolution of relative peak intensities after normalizing to the (311) reflection: the intensities of the (400) and (440) peaks decrease markedly in MZ5 compared to ZCO. Such redistribution of diffraction intensity is often indicative of a textural or morphological change, plausibly involving a preferential exposure or development of (311) facets upon Mn substitution. Although powder XRD alone cannot definitively establish the dominant exposed facet, the trend is consistent with the notion that the (311) surface becomes more prominent in MZ5. This possible facet preference is noteworthy, as the (311) surface is expected to exhibit enhanced reactivity, which will be further examined by DFT calculations in the following section.18,36

The Raman spectrum of Mn-ZnCo2O4 sample is presented in Fig. 1B. The spectrum exhibits distinct characteristic vibrational peaks at 205 cm−1, 495 cm−1, 526 cm−1, 618 cm−1, and 695 cm−1, which can be assigned to the F2g, Eg, F2g, F2g, and A1g vibrational modes, respectively, confirming the formation of the spinel structure.37,38

The surface morphology and microstructural features of the synthesized ZCO and MZ5 samples were further investigated via SEM (Fig. 1C and D). The SEM image reveals a hierarchical architecture for pure ZnCo2O4 constructed by primary nanoparticle aggregates accompanied by distinct polyhedral microstructures, whereas that of Mn-ZnCo2O4 reveals a predominantly spherical morphology accompanied by a noticeable tendency for the particles to self-assemble into rod-like structures, as depicted in Fig. 1C and D, respectively. In addition, the sample displays a particle size distribution in the range of approximately 50–70 nm. The partial substitution of Zn/Co ions by Mn disrupts the lattice order and suppresses the growth of the characteristic facets of ZnCo2O4, thereby driving this morphological evolution. This transition, coupled with the diverse oxidation states and lattice defects introduced by Mn substitution, is expected to provide more active sites for catalytic processes.

The elemental compositions (wt%) derived from EDX analysis (inset tables) confirm the high chemical purity of the two samples, with the exclusive presence of Zn, Co, and O for ZnCo2O4, and Mn, Zn, Co, and O for Mn-ZnCo2O4, while indicating no detectable foreign impurities. Detailed information regarding the EDX survey spectra, elemental mapping images, and quantitative stoichiometric analysis is provided in the SI S1 (Fig. S(1 and 2)).

To further assess the spatial distribution of these elements, elemental mapping by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed. In the case of Mn-ZnCo2O4 (Fig. 2), the overlaid elemental map of O, Co, Zn, and Mn reveals a continuous and even dispersion of all four elements. The Co and Zn signals remain dense and uniform, whereas the Mn signal, although appearing sparser owing to its lower mass fraction (∼8.7 wt% of the total metal content as quantified by EDX), is homogeneously dispersed without forming Mn-rich aggregates. This observation corroborates the EDX finding that Mn cations are substitutionally incorporated into the spinel lattice rather than existing as a separate phase, and is consistent with the high phase purity later confirmed by XRD. Together with the homogeneous ZCO maps presented in the SI (Fig. S2), this consistent elemental homogeneity underscores the reliability of the synthesis protocol and the structural integrity of the prepared spinel catalysts.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed to analyze the elemental composition and oxidation states of the prepared Mn-ZnCo2O4 sample, as shown in Fig. 3. The survey spectrum (Fig. 3A) clearly displays the characteristic signals of Mn, Zn, Co, and O, confirming the successful incorporation of Mn into the ZnCo2O4 spinel oxide lattice. In the Mn 2p region, two peaks at 653.4 eV and 642.5 eV correspond to the spin–orbit components of the Mn 2p1/2 and Mn 2p3/2, respectively31,39. These binding energies are consistent with Mn3+ species, indicating that Mn primarily occupies the tetrahedral site within spinel structures (Fig. 3B).40 The Zn 2p spectrum exhibits two well-defined peaks at approximately 1044.4 eV (Zn 2p1/2) and 1021.3 eV (Zn 2p3/2), confirming the presence of Zn2+ species and its tetrahedral-site occupation in the spinel lattice (Fig. 3C).41 In the Co 2p spectrum (Fig. 3D), the characteristic spin–orbit doublets appear at approximately 780.3 eV (Co 2p3/2) and 795.5 eV (Co 2p1/2), accompanied by two well-resolved satellite peaks.42 The deconvoluted Co 2p3/2 peaks at approximately 780.2 eV and 782.1 eV indicate the coexistence of Co2+ and Co3+ species, with Co3+ being dominant. The O 1s spectrum (Fig. 3E) can be deconvoluted into two peaks located at 531.1 eV and 529.8 eV the peak at 529.8 eV is assigned to lattice oxygen (O2−) bonded to metal cations, while the higher binding energy peak at 531.1 eV, which collectively encompasses the overlapping signals of oxygen vacancies and surface-adsorbed –OH groups.43,44 This pronounced feature indicates the presence of abundant oxygen vacancies, which are expected to enhance the catalytic activity by providing preferential adsorption and activation sites for oxygen-containing species.

Proposed oxidase-like catalytic mechanism induced by Mn substitution

While pure ZnCo2O4 exhibits limited oxidase-like activity owing to inefficient O2 activation and restricted electron donation, Mn incorporation significantly enhances catalytic performance by introducing multivalent Mn redox centers (Mn2+/Mn3+/Mn4+) that promote interfacial electron transfer and facilitate the generation of reactive oxygen species, particularly superoxide radicals (˙O2−).31,32,45 A detailed mechanistic discussion and corresponding schematic are provided in the SI (Fig. S3).

The incorporation of Mn into the ZnCo2O4 structure significantly alters its oxidase-like catalytic behavior by regulating both the oxygen activation pathway and the interfacial electron-transfer process. In pure ZnCo2O4, oxidase-like activity is inherently limited by inefficient adsorption and activation of molecular oxygen, as well as restricted electron donation capability toward O2 (ref. 31) (Fig. 3). Upon Mn substitution, multivalent redox centers (Mn2+/Mn3+/Mn4+) are introduced into the spinel lattice, increasing the density of surface-accessible redox-active sites.31,32 These Mn redox couples function as electron mediators, facilitating electron transfer from the electron-donating substrate (TMB) to adsorbed O2 molecules. As a result, interfacial electron transfer is accelerated, promoting the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly superoxide radicals (˙O2−), which are responsible for the oxidation of TMB in oxidase-like catalysis.45

Meanwhile, the partial substitution of Zn2+ by Mn induces lattice distortion and local charge imbalance, which promotes the formation of oxygen vacancies within the spinel lattice. As revealed by the XPS analysis discussed above, the strong O 1s peak at 531.1 eV, which encompasses the overlapping signals of oxygen vacancies and surface-adsorbed –OH groups, points to an abundant concentration of oxygen vacancies and a defect-rich surface structure. These oxygen vacancies act as energetically favorable adsorption and activation sites for molecular oxygen and facilitate the stabilization of oxygen-derived intermediates during the catalytic process.46 Consequently, the abundant defect sites effectively lower the activation energy barrier for O2 reduction, thereby enhancing oxygen activation and promoting the oxidase-like catalytic performance of the catalyst.47 The defect sites promote a single-electron reduction of O2 to generate superoxide radicals (˙O2−), the key oxidants responsible for TMB oxidation. Such a pathway is consistent with the enhanced oxidase-like performance and is further confirmed by radical scavenging experiments (Fig. 4).

In addition, Mn incorporation modulates the local electronic environment of the Co–O framework, thereby facilitating charge transport within the catalyst lattice.48 According to the characterization results discussed above, the coexistence of multiple Co oxidation states indicates the presence of a mixed-valence electronic structure. Together with the multivalent Mn redox couples introduced by Mn substitution, this configuration establishes a cooperative Mn–Co redox network within the spinel structure,.31,49,50 Such electronic coupling promotes charge-carrier mobility and facilitates efficient electron transfer across the solid–liquid interface, thereby sustaining continuous catalytic turnover under ambient oxygen conditions.

Mn substitution does not merely enhance the catalytic activity of ZnCo2O4 but fundamentally transforms its oxidase-like catalytic pathway by enabling efficient O2 adsorption, activation, and electron transfer. The enhanced oxidase-like activity upon Mn incorporation can thus be attributed to the combined effects of enriched oxygen vacancies and cooperative Mn–Co redox chemistry.

Evaluation of oxidase-like catalytic activity and steady-state kinetics of Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozymes

Prior to sensing experiments, several experimental parameters, including reaction time, pH, nanozyme concentration, and TMB concentration, were systematically optimized to obtain the maximum catalytic response. The corresponding optimization results are presented in Fig. S3–S5 in the SI (S2). Under the optimized conditions, steady-state kinetic analysis was performed to further evaluate the oxidase-like catalytic activity of the Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme using TMB as the chromogenic substrate (Fig. 5). Initial reaction rates were determined from the time-dependent absorbance changes at 652 nm measured at different TMB concentrations (Fig. 5A). The resulting kinetic data were well fitted to the Michaelis–Menten model, and the corresponding Lineweaver–Burk plot was linear, confirming typical enzyme-like catalytic behavior (SI, S2).

Plot of absorbance at 652 nm peak versus time of the Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme systems (A) and Lineweaver–Burk plot (B), respectively.

The Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme exhibited a maximum reaction velocity (Vmax) of 127 × 10–8 M s−1, indicating effective catalytic turnover under the investigated conditions (Fig. 5B). These results demonstrate that Mn-ZnCo2O4 is capable of catalyzing the oxidation of TMB via an oxidase-like pathway. The presence of Mn in the ZnCo2O4 lattice is expected to modulate the surface electronic properties and redox characteristics of the nanozyme, thereby influencing its oxidase-like catalytic behavior.

The enhanced oxidase-like kinetics of the Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme can be further rationalized by considering the structural modulation revealed by the simulated crystal models. As illustrated in Fig. 1, pure ZnCo2O4 has a spinel lattice in which Zn2+ ions occupy tetrahedral sites and Co ions are located in octahedral coordination environments within a close-packed oxygen framework. This symmetric arrangement provides structural stability but limits the availability of electronically flexible redox centers and active sites for oxygen activation. Upon partial substitution of Zn2+ by Mn ions to form Mn0.5Zn0.5Co2O4, the local coordination environment of the spinel lattice becomes significantly perturbed (Fig. 1). Differences in ionic radius and preferred oxidation states between Mn and Zn introduce lattice distortion and modify the metal–oxygen bond environment.31 Such structural perturbation can redistribute the local electronic density within the Co–O framework and create additional redox-active sites associated with multivalent Mn species.41 These newly introduced centers provide alternative electron-transfer pathways within the lattice and facilitate electron shuttling between the catalytic surface and adsorbed oxygen molecules.51 Furthermore, the distortion of the oxygen sublattice induced by Mn substitution may increase the thermodynamic tendency for defect formation, particularly oxygen vacancies. These defects act as preferential adsorption and activation sites for molecular oxygen, stabilizing oxygen-derived intermediates during the catalytic cycle.21,52 As a consequence, the activation barrier for oxygen reduction and electron transfer is effectively lowered.

Building upon these structural and electronic modifications, the enhanced catalytic kinetics observed for the Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme directly reflect an improved catalytic turnover. Experimentally, this improvement is reflected by the increased Vmax value of Mn-ZnCo2O4, indicating that the catalytic process is governed primarily by accelerated intrinsic reaction kinetics, with no apparent dependence on altered substrate adsorption under the tested conditions. Such kinetic behavior suggests that the rate-determining steps of the oxidase-like reaction-particularly interfacial electron transfer and molecular oxygen activation-become more efficient after Mn incorporation into the ZnCo2O4 lattice.

This kinetic enhancement can be attributed to the electronic modulation introduced by Mn ions within the spinel framework. The coexistence of Mn2+/Mn3+/Mn4+ redox couples with the 48 native Co2+/Co3+ system forms a multivalent redox network that expands the accessible electronic states near the Fermi level.31 As a result, the energetic barrier for electron migration during the catalytic cycle is reduced, facilitating more rapid charge transfer and promoting faster redox cycling between active metal centers.48 In addition, Mn incorporation can influence the electronic band structure of ZnCo2O4 by introducing dopant-related energy states that increase the electronic density of states close to the Fermi level.53 This modification improves electrical conductivity and decreases charge-transfer resistance at the nanozyme–electrolyte interface. Consequently, electrons generated from the oxidation of TMB can be more efficiently transferred to surface-adsorbed O2, sustaining continuous redox turnover and accelerating the formation of oxidized TMB (ox-TMB).54–61

Steady-state kinetic parameters of the Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme system were further evaluated by comparison with representative metal-oxide-based nanozymes reported in the literature, as summarized in Table S1. The Km value of Mn0.5-Zn0.5Co2O4 (0.860 mM) indicates a moderate affinity for TMB. Within the oxidase-like group in Table S1, MZ5 tends to show a higher Vmax than the other oxidase-like systems examined. In the peroxidase-like group, MnCo2O4 NPs exhibit a notably high Vmax, which is consistent with their H2O2-dependent catalytic pathway.

Notably, both Mn-containing spinels show higher Vmax values than their Mn-free counterparts within their respective mechanistic groups. This trend appears consistent with a possible contribution of Mn to catalytic activity, which may operate through modulation of the electronic structure and defect-related activation mechanisms discussed above, although the available data alone cannot fully separate this effect from morphological contributions.62–64

Overall, the steady-state kinetic behavior of Mn-ZnCo2O4 confirms that Mn-induced structural and electronic modulation translates directly into enhanced oxidase-like turnover. The markedly increased Vmax, together with a moderate Km, demonstrates that Mn substitution accelerates the electron-transfer-controlled steps of the catalytic cycle without relying on improved substrate affinity. These findings establish Mn incorporation as an effective strategy to boost the oxidase-like activity of Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozymes.

In addition to its enhanced catalytic activity, the Mn-substituted nanozyme exhibited good storage stability, maintaining over 80% of its relative activity after 15 days (SI S4, Fig. S6).

Colorimetric detection of ascorbic acid (AA)

Principle of detection

DFT semicore pseudopotentials were employed to account for core-electron interactions, and the basis set was defined with a medium orbital cutoff quality. To ensure the reliability of the electronic structure calculations, both the smearing model and the Harris approximation were disabled.65

To gain molecular-level insights into the interfacial electronic interactions and clarify the facet-dependent catalytic behavior, DFT calculations were performed to evaluate the adsorption energetics of TMB on the (001) and (311) surfaces of pure ZCO and Mn-substituted MZ5 (Fig. 6). For pure ZCO, the computed adsorption energy Eads values are are −8.53 kcal mol−1 for (001) and −8.67 kcal mol−1 for (311), indicating a thermodynamically permissible but relatively weak physisorption interaction. Upon Mn incorporation, Eads becomes slightly more exothermic, reaching −9.71 kcal mol−1 on MZ5 (001) and −10.12 kcal mol−1 on MZ5 (311). Crucially, while these values reflect a more thermodynamically favorable substrate-surface contact on MZ5, the absolute adsorption energies remain within a moderate energy regime (−10.00 kcal mol−1). This mild energetic shift aligns remarkably well with the experimental steady-state kinetic results, where MZ5 exhibited a moderate Km value, confirming that Mn substitution does not cause excessive substrate pinning or over-binding-a phenomenon that would otherwise hinder product desorption and compromise catalytic turnover.

Optimized adsorption models of the organic molecule on ZnCo2O4 (ZCO) (A) and Mn0.5Zn0.5Co2O4 (MZ5) (B) (001)/(311) surfaces, constructed using a 5 × 8 supercell with a 30 Å vacuum layer.

Instead, the moderate increase in exothermicity, particularly on the MZ5 (311) surface, signifies an optimized interfacial electronic coupling between TMB and the catalyst. The multivalent Mn2+/Mn3+/Mn4+ redox couples, alongside under-coordinated defect sites on the (311) facet, establish a favorable interface that facilitates interfacial electron transfer from TMB without imposing a high energetic penalty, thereby lowering the kinetic barrier for the single-electron oxidation step.63 Furthermore, the facet-dependent analysis reveals that the high-energy (311) surface consistently yields more exothermic adsorption than the more stable (001) surface for both ZCO and MZ5. The MZ5 (311) configuration affords the most favorable interaction energy (−10.12 kcal mol−1), lending strong theoretical support to the XRD analysis regarding the preferential exposure of reactive (311) facets upon Mn substitution.

Overall, the DFT results harmonize well with the measured kinetic parameters: Mn substitution optimizes the substrate binding landscape and interfacial electronic network to promote rapid electron shuttling (Vmax acceleration) while maintaining balanced substrate binding dynamics (Km in a moderate range).

The electrostatic potential distributions and oxidized-state structures of ascorbic acid (AA) and 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) were then analyzed to compare their reactivity toward oxidizing species generated in the catalytic system.

As shown in Fig. 7A, TMB exhibits a relatively delocalized electron cloud across its conjugated aromatic framework, leading to a more uniform electrostatic potential distribution associated with its π-electron system. This delocalization suggests that the electron density of TMB is distributed over the molecular structure. In contrast, AA displays a more localized distribution of negative potential, with higher electron density concentrated around the hydroxyl-containing functional groups, making these moieties highly kinetically accessible sites for electrophilic attack by reactive oxygen species.

(A) Electrostatic reduction potential distributions of TMB and AA molecules, (B) DFT-calculated HOMO and LUMO energy levels of TMB and AA.

To further quantify their electronic properties, frontier molecular orbital energy levels were calculated.66 The results show that the HOMO level of AA is −7.345 eV, which is significantly lower than that of TMB (−5.195 eV) (Fig. 7B). Thermodynamically, the higher HOMO of TMB indicates that it is intrinsically more susceptible to one-electron oxidation than AA. However, the reaction outcome in a catalytic system cannot be judged solely by the HOMO energy, because the oxidation process involves not only electron transfer but also proton transfer and specific interactions with the oxidising species.67 The ESP analysis clearly shows that the hydroxyl groups of AA possess a high local electron density, making them kinetically accessible sites for the reactive oxygen species (˙O2−) generated by the nanozyme. In addition, the calculated energy gaps between the LUMO of the oxidant (˙O2−, −0.44 eV) and the HOMO of each substrate are 5.635 eV for TMB and 7.785 eV for AA; the smaller gap for TMB confirms its thermodynamic preference, yet the kinetic barrier for the oxidation of AA may be effectively lowered due to the favourable orientation and proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) pathway at its enediol moiety. Therefore, although TMB is thermodynamically easier to oxidise, the localised electron density around the hydroxyl groups of AA facilitates its rapid interaction with the oxidising intermediates, which explains the observed predominance of AA oxidation under the experimental conditions.

Consequently, when AA is introduced into the catalytic system, it preferentially reacts with the generated reactive oxygen species, including superoxide radicals, thereby competing with TMB for oxidation. This competitive radical-scavenging interaction, potentially combined with the direct chemical reduction of any formed ox-TMB back to its colorless state by AA, effectively suppresses the accumulation of the blue oxidized product. This dual pathway results in a continuous and concentration-dependent decrease in the characteristic absorbance at 652 nm. Several recent nanozyme-based colorimetric platforms have demonstrated sensitive AA detection through oxidase-like or radical-scavenging mechanisms, including metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), single-atom, multimetal-atom, and Mn-doped oxide systems.68–70 Therefore, a colorimetric sensing strategy for AA detection was established based on the Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme system.

Sensitivity test

Based on the theoretical analysis described above, AA was expected to preferentially interacts with the oxidizing species generated in the catalytic system. Accordingly, the concentration-dependent sensing performance of the nanozyme-TMB platform toward AA was investigated experimentally. Under the optimized reaction conditions, the absorbance at 652 nm decreased progressively with increasing AA concentration because AA inhibited TMB oxidation, and this response was quantified using the absorbance change (ΔAbs652). A linear relationship between ΔAbs652 and AA concentration was obtained within the tested range (R2 = 0.954), demonstrating reliable quantitative performance. The limit of detection (LOD), calculated using the 3.3σ/S method, was determined to be 26 µM, indicating adequate sensitivity for AA determination. The concentration-dependent inhibition behavior and linear response confirm that the nanozyme-TMB system provides an effective platform for colorimetric detection of AA, with the sensing performance primarily governed by the redox interaction between AA and Mn4+ species (Fig. 8).

Selectivity test

Selectivity is a critical parameter for evaluating the practical applicability of a sensing system. To assess the selectivity of the proposed Mn-ZnCo2O4-TMB colorimetric probe toward ascorbic acid (AA), its response was investigated in the presence of various potential interfering species. Typical inorganic ions (K+, Na+, Mg2+, H2PO4− and HPO42−) and representative organic compounds, including citric acid, d-glucose, benzoic acid, acetic acid and urea, were selected as interferents because they commonly coexist with AA in food and biological samples. All interferents and AA were introduced into the sensing system at the same concentration (1 mM), and the absorbance at 652 nm was recorded.

As shown in Fig. 9, the coexisting species produced only negligible variations in the absorbance signal, whereas the addition of AA resulted in a pronounced decrease in absorbance intensity, demonstrating the high selectivity of the Mn-ZnCo2O4-TMB system toward AA. This selective response originates from the distinct redox properties of AA.

Selectivity of the Mn-ZnCo2O4/TMB nanozyme system for ascorbic acid detection. (A) UV-vis absorption spectra illustrating the reduction of ox-TMB in the presence of AA and interfering species (inset: colorimetric response); (B) absorbance intensity at 652 nm for various analytes.

This selective response originates from the distinct redox properties of AA. On the one hand, as established by the ESP and HOMO–LUMO analyses, the localized electron density at the hydroxyl groups of AA, together with a favorable proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) pathway, enables it to rapidly scavenge the superoxide radicals (˙O2−) generated by the nanozyme, thereby interrupting the oxidase-like catalytic cycle and suppressing TMB oxidation. On the other hand, inspired by recent work on Mn-containing nanozymes,71 AA may also directly reduce surface-exposed Mn4+ species in the Mn-ZnCo2O4 lattice to lower oxidation states (Mn2+), further depleting the active redox centers responsible for O2 activation. Both pathways contribute to the effective inhibition of the catalytic oxidation of TMB, while the tested interferents lack either the appropriate radical-scavenging capability or the reducing potential toward Mn4+, and therefore cause negligible absorbance changes.

Detection of the AA in real samples

Based on the calibration curve established for the nanozyme/TMB colorimetric assay, the ascorbic acid (AA) contents in real orange juice samples were quantified, and the results are summarized in Table 1. Three independent orange juice samples were analyzed at different dilution factors to minimize matrix interferences, yielding AA concentrations of 21.18, 13.36, and 14.93 mg/100 mL for samples 1–3, respectively. The analytical sensitivity of the colorimetric method was characterized by a limit of detection of 26 µM, which is suitable for AA determination in beverage samples with complex matrices. In parallel, the AA contents of the same orange juice samples were determined using the classical iodine titration method as a reference procedure. The titration results gave mean AA concentrations of 22.02, 17.08, and 17.88 mg/100 mL for samples 1–3, respectively, with individual replicate measurements retained for subsequent evaluation.

With respect to analytical precision, the iodine titration method exhibited excellent repeatability, with RSD valuesranging from 0.69% to 2.08%, reflecting the inherent precision of classical volumetric analysis. Conclusion

In conclusion, Mn-substituted ZnCo2O4 (Mn-ZnCo2O4) nanozymes were successfully developed to enhance the intrinsic oxidase-like activity of ZnCo2O4. The substitution of Mn into the spinel framework played a pivotal role in regulating the surface redox couples and electronic structure, thereby facilitating oxygen activation and accelerating electron-transfer processes during catalysis. The Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme system exhibited favorable enzyme-like kinetic parameters, with a Km value of 0.860 mM and a Vmax of 127 × 10−8 M s−1, demonstrating the Mn substitution-induced enhancement of catalytic performance. Furthermore, density functional theory calculations revealed that while TMB is thermodynamically more susceptible to oxidation based on HOMO energies, the preferential oxidation of ascorbic acid is driven by kinetically favorable radical scavenging at its hydroxyl groups, providing theoretical support for the competitive colorimetric sensing strategy. Based on these material advantages, a robust colorimetric sensing platform for ascorbic acid was established, offering a broad linear detection range (50–1000 µM) and a low detection limit (26 µM). Quantitative analysis of AA in real orange juice samples showed good consistency between the Mn-ZnCo2O4/TMB colorimetric method and the conventional iodine titration, validating the accuracy and reliability of the proposed approach.

Importantly, the satisfactory analytical performance achieved in complex sample matrices indicates that the Mn-ZnCo2O4 nanozyme is not only suitable for AA determination but also holds significant potential for the colorimetric analysis of other redox-active analytes in real-world samples. This work provides clear materials-level insight into how Mn substitution modulates the catalytic properties of ZnCo2O4 and offers a viable strategy for designing high-performance nanozymes for analytical applications.
