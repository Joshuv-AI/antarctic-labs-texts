Development of a simplified three-dimensional viscoelastic constitutive model for thermally activated shape memory polymers and numerical analysis
Authors: Mingkun Li, Longbin Liu, Shaozhe Ding, Haosen Chang, Xingfu Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractShape memory polymers (SMPs) are a class of smart materials that exhibit remarkable shape memory effects, making them highly suitable for applications in aerospace, biomedical engineering, robotics, and textiles. Understanding their deformation mechanisms and developing accurate constitutive models to predict their thermomechanical behavior are crucial for advancing their practical applications. In this study, we propose a simplified three-dimensional viscoelastic constitutive model based on the Standard Linear Viscoelastic (SLV) framework to describe the shape memory behavior of SMPs under small strain conditions, which are typical in engineering applications such as torsion and bending. The model is implemented using a user-defined material subroutine (UMAT) in the finite element analysis software ABAQUS, enabling numerical simulations of SMPs’ shape memory behavior under uniaxial tension, torsion, and bending deformations. The simulation results demonstrate excellent agreement with experimental data, validating the model’s ability to accurately predict SMPs’ mechanical behavior. The proposed model is characterized by its simplicity, ease of parameter acquisition, and straightforward numerical implementation, offering valuable theoretical guidance for the design and application of SMP-based smart structures.Keywords: Shape memory polymers (SMPs), Viscoelastic constitutive model, Finite element analysis (FEA)Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Theory and computation

Introduction

Shape memory polymers (SMPs) are a novel class of smart materials with shape memory effects. At high temperatures, they exhibit low modulus and can deform under external forces. By maintaining the deformation and lowering the temperature, the shape of SMPs is stored; when reheated, the shape is recovered. Compared to other shape memory materials such as shape memory alloys and ceramics, SMPs offer advantages such as low density, high deformability, and tunable properties, making them widely applicable in aerospace, biomedical engineering, and robotics1–3.

To expand the applications of SMPs, it is essential to study their deformation mechanisms, establish macroscopic constitutive models to effectively describe their thermomechanical behavior, and predict their shape memory processes. Scholars from different fields tend to model their mechanical behavior from various perspectives, including macroscopic phenomenological mechanics based on viscoelastic theory and micromechanical constitutive models based on phase transition theory4.

According to the phase transition theory, SMPs undergo a transition between the activated phase and the frozen phase near the glass transition temperature Tg. In the activated phase, deformation is mainly caused by conformational changes such as twisting and stretching of molecular chains, while in the frozen phase, the relative motion between molecular chains is weakened, and deformation is primarily induced by changes in internal energy5,6. During the cooling process, the activated phase gradually transforms into the frozen phase, and the conformational changes in the activated phase are frozen, storing the deformation. Upon unloading and heating, the frozen phase gradually transforms back into the activated phase, releasing the stored strain. In 2006, Liu et al. established a rate-independent constitutive model under small strain conditions based on this theory. The model assumes equal stress in the activated and frozen phases, with the total strain being a linear superposition of the strains in both phases. The volume fraction of the frozen phase depends only on temperature. Subsequently, Chen et al. ,extended this model to a three-dimensional SMP constitutive model under finite strain conditions. Later models mainly focused on extending and modifying this framework. Pan et al10. and Rao et al11. considered the rate dependence of the phase transition process and modified the phase transition procedure, proposing constitutive models that describe the mechanical behavior of materials under different loading and heating rates. Bakhtiari et al12. took into account the dual-phase structure of SMPs (activated and frozen phases) and used equivalent inclusion theory and Eshelby tensors to capture the effects of inclusion morphology and volume fraction on the overall material behavior. They proposed an evolution equation based on temperature changes and inclusion volume fraction.

Phase transition models can accurately predict the thermomechanical behavior of materials, but most are based on specific experimental conditions, which do not align with the complex external stimuli encountered in practical applications of SMPs, limiting their applicability. In contrast, viscoelastic models consider the shape memory effect as a manifestation of the material’s viscoelastic behavior, independent of strain history, making viscoelastic models advantageous for expressing material deformation under complex stress and temperature conditions. In 1997, Tobushi et al13,14. introduced an irreversible slip element into the classical Standard Linear Viscoelastic (SLV) model to describe material rheological behavior, attributing the shape storage effect of SMPs to the slip element and establishing a one-dimensional linear viscoelastic constitutive model to describe the thermomechanical behavior of SMPs during shape memory processes. This model effectively describes the mechanical behavior of shape memory polymers under small deformations. Subsequently, Zhou Bo et al.15, Shi Guanghui et al.16, and Huang Tianpeng et al17. developed three-dimensional constitutive models for SMPs based on this model and conducted numerical analyses using finite element models. In 2014, Balogun et al18,19. adopted a similar strategy, adding a reversible element in series with the standard linear viscoelastic model and attributing the shape storage and release to this reversible element. Later, Balogun et al20. extended this model to three dimensions.

In addition to improving strain storage by introducing slip elements, some scholars have proposed that the mechanism of shape storage in SMPs after cooling is the reduction in molecular chain segment motion, increasing the time required to return to equilibrium. Therefore, shape storage and release can be achieved by adjusting the relaxation time or viscosity coefficient at different temperatures21,22. These models typically use the time-temperature superposition principle to adjust the time scale, correlating viscosity data at different temperatures. In these models, the relationship between relaxation time and temperature is described using the WLF equation or the Arrhenius equation. For example, Arrieta et al.23, Diani et al.24, and Fan Peng et al25. used the generalized Maxwell model combined with the WLF equation to model the shape memory process of SMPs. Fan Peng et al25. also pointed out in reference that the viscoelastic mechanism of SMP shape memory is the tendency of relaxation time to infinity after cooling. In recent years, Gu et al26,27. proposed a multi-branch thermoviscoelastic finite deformation constitutive model combining structural and stress relaxation, using the nonlinear Adam-Gibbs model to describe the relationship between structural relaxation time and temperature and the Eyring viscous flow model to capture the multiple relaxation processes of SMPs. Ostadrahimi et al28. proposed a comprehensive three-dimensional finite deformation constitutive model considering material nonlinear viscoelasticity, hyperelasticity, and stress softening mechanisms, with the nonlinear viscoelastic branch also using the WLF equation to describe the time-temperature dependence of SMPs.

Although current research on the shape memory behavior of SMPs under finite and even large deformations has reached considerable maturity, such models typically involve numerous parameters with cumbersome solution processes. The mathematical modeling and numerical analysis require substantial effort. In practical applications, SMPs often achieve desirable large deformations through small-strain conditions such as torsion and bending. The complexity of existing models limits their engineering applicability. Therefore, this study proposes a simplified small-strain viscoelastic constitutive model based on the Standard Linear Viscoelastic (SLV) framework and implements it using a user-defined material subroutine (UMAT) in the finite element analysis software ABAQUS. Finally, finite element analysis was conducted to validate the model’s effectiveness and accuracy.

Finite element model setup

The simplified constitutive model proposed in this study requires a relatively small number of material parameters, making it highly practical for engineering implementation. Only three temperature-dependent parameters need to be determined: the elastic modulus E(T), viscosity coefficient µ(T), and relaxation time λ(T). Under known strain conditions ε(t), these parameters can be easily obtained by measuring the corresponding stress response σ(t) and applying Eq. (5). For example, stress relaxation experiments at different temperatures can be effectively utilized to acquire these material parameters.

The material parameters used in this study were directly adopted from Tobushi et al.14, including the glass transition temperature Tg = 328 K, transition temperature range Tw = 15 K, and the temperature-dependent relationships of elastic modulus E(T), viscosity coefficient µ(T), and relaxation time λ(T) calculated using Eqs. (11)-(13). To validate the accuracy of the model and the rationality of the simplified approach, this study replicated the experimental conditions reported by Tobushi et al14.. Using the finite element model integrated with the developed UMAT, uniaxial deformation shape memory cycle tests were performed on SMP specimens. The simulation results were systematically compared with the experimental data from Tobushi et al., where stress, strain, and temperature values for comparison were extracted using image data processing tools.

Furthermore, to verify the model’s applicability under complex stress states, additional shape memory cycle tests were conducted on the finite element model, including bending deformation experiments and torsional deformation experiments.

These comprehensive validations ensure the robustness and versatility of the proposed model across varying loading conditions and deformation modes.

Using the developed UMAT subroutine, shape memory cycle experiments were numerically simulated on the finite element model, encompassing the high-temperature deformation process, cooling and shape-setting process, low-temperature unloading process, and shape recovery process. The upper temperature limit selected for the numerical simulation was Th=348 K, and the lower temperature limit was Tl=308 K.

The model dimensions were set to 1.6 mm × 10 mm × 90 mm. A structured hexahedral mesh was employed for discretization, utilizing 20-node quadratic hexahedral elements (C3D20) with an approximate element size of 0.4 mm. The final mesh consisted of 22,600 elements and 110,873 nodes.Compared to linear elements, quadratic elements employ quadratic shape functions to describe the displacement field within each element, resulting in linear variation of strain/stress across the element (as opposed to constant values in linear elements). This formulation provides superior capability for capturing stress/strain gradients and effectively avoids shear locking issues, offering an optimal balance between accuracy and computational efficiency that makes it the mainstream choice for industrial simulations. The boundary conditions were configured with one end fixed while applying time-varying displacement/rotation boundary conditions at the opposite end. Temperature was applied to the model through predefined fields, with heat generation from thermal conduction and creep dissipation being neglected. The finite element model is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Simulation steps

First, a uniaxial deformation experiment was conducted using the finite element model. The numerical simulation conditions were consistent with the experimental conditions of Tobushi et al.14, employing four viscous analysis steps to simulate the uniaxial tensile shape memory cycle process. The specific steps are as follows:

Step 1: High-temperature loading process (load). Under a uniform temperature field of Th=348 K, the model is subjected to constant-rate stretching until reaching the maximum strains of εmax = 2.4%, εmax = 4%, and εmax = 10%, respectively. The loading rate is set at 5%/min.

Step 2: Cooling and setting process (cool). During this process, the strain is held constant, and the temperature is reduced from Th=348 K to Tl=308 K at a rate of 4 K/min.

Step 3: Unloading process (unload). In this process, the temperature is maintained at Tl=308 K, and the stress is unloaded at a constant rate until σ11 = 0. The unloading time is set to 20s.

Step 4: Shape recovery process (recover). The displacement constraint on the right end of the model is removed, and the temperature is increased from Tl=308 K to Th=348 K at a constant heating rate of 4 K/min.

The implementation methods for torsional and bending deformations are similar to those for uniaxial deformation. By replacing the displacement boundary conditions in Step 1 with rotational boundary conditions about the x-axis and y-axis, respectively, these deformations can be achieved. The rotation amplitude is set to 90°, with a rotational rate of 45°/min. Other conditions remain consistent: a cooling rate of 4 K/min, an unloading time of 20 s, and a heating rate of 4 K/min.

As shown in Fig. 6, during the loading and unloading process, due to the short duration, stress relaxation is not significant, and the stress-strain relationship is approximately linear. In the cooling process, initially, due to the high temperature of the material, the viscosity coefficient is small, and significant stress relaxation occurs, with a slight decrease in stress over time. As the temperature further decreases, the material begins to undergo a glass transition, the viscosity coefficient increases, and stress relaxation becomes less noticeable. At this point, the stress further increases due to the material’s cooling contraction, which aligns with the experimental observations of Tobushi et al14.. During the unloading process, a small portion of the elastic strain is released, while the majority of the strain is retained. The strain after unloading, εfix, is essentially equal to the maximum strain εmax, demonstrating a pronounced shape storage effect. In the shape recovery phase, as the temperature rises, the strain gradually decreases from εfix to zero, and the shape is restored.

Effect of heating rate on shape recovery

To investigate the influence of different heating rates on the shape recovery process and to analyze the shape memory mechanism of the material, numerical simulations of the shape recovery process under various heating rates were conducted in this study. The results are shown in Fig. 10.

Strain-temperature curves during shape recovery at different heating rates.

Figure 10 illustrates the shape recovery process of SMPs at εmax = 10% under different heating rates. It can be observed that as the heating rate decreases, the shape recovery capability increases. This indicates that the mechanism of shape storage in SMPs after cooling is due to the reduced mobility of the molecular chains, which prolongs the time required to return to the equilibrium state. As the heating rate decreases, SMPs have more time to recover their equilibrium state, resulting in a greater shape recovery capability and larger recovered strain at the same temperature.

As previously mentioned, the temperature-dependent relationship of material parameters is key to the material’s ability to exhibit the shape memory effect. In Fig. 11, the black dashed line represents the strain history curve throughout the shape memory process. During the shape memory process, after the constraints are released in Step3, the material is at a low temperature, and the mobility of the molecular chains is reduced, causing the strain to remain essentially unchanged. Upon heating in Step4, the mobility of the material’s molecular chains increases, reducing the time required to return to the equilibrium state, and the strain gradually recovers. Calculations were also performed for the isothermal process, and the red solid line in Fig. 11 represents the strain history curve at the constant temperature Th. It can be seen that after unloading at high temperature, most of the material’s strain is recovered, and the shape memory effect is diminished.

Conclusion and discussion

Conclusion

This study proposes a simplified small-strain three-dimensional viscoelastic constitutive model based on the Standard Linear Viscoelastic (SLV) model to describe the shape memory behavior of thermally activated shape memory polymers (SMPs). Using the user-defined material subroutine (UMAT) in the finite element analysis software ABAQUS, the numerical simulation of this constitutive model was implemented. Numerical simulations of shape memory cycle experiments were conducted for uniaxial tensile, torsional, and bending deformations of SMPs. The main conclusions of the study are as follows:

1) The proposed simplified three-dimensional viscoelastic constitutive model effectively describes the shape memory behavior of SMPs under complex stress states such as uniaxial tension, torsion, and bending. The numerical simulation results align well with experimental data, particularly under small strain conditions, where the model exhibits high predictive accuracy.

2) Through numerical simulation, the four typical stages of SMPs in the shape memory cycle were successfully reproduced: high-temperature loading, cooling and fixing, low-temperature unloading, and shape recovery. The model is able to accurately capture the material’s stress relaxation, thermal expansion, and shape recovery behaviors at different temperatures.

3) The model is not only suitable for uniaxial tensile deformation but also effectively simulates shape memory behavior under complex stress states such as torsion and bending. The numerical simulation results demonstrate that the model’s predictive capability under complex stress states is comparable to that under uniaxial tension, further validating its broad applicability.

4) Through shape recovery experiments at different heating rates, it was found that lower heating rates facilitate the shape recovery of SMPs. This phenomenon is closely related to the temperature-dependent mobility of SMPs’ molecular chains, providing further insight into the microscopic mechanisms of SMPs’ shape memory behavior.

Discussion

This study employs the Standard Linear Viscoelastic (SLV) model, consisting of a linear spring and a linear dashpot in combination with temperature-dependent material parameters, to model the shape memory behavior of SMPs. The viscoelastic mechanism for shape storage and recovery is attributed to the accumulation and release of strain in the dashpot component of the SLV model. At low temperatures, the motion of the dashpot is constrained, causing the time required for strain release to approach infinity, thereby effectively storing the material’s strain. As temperature increases, the mobility of the dashpot is restored, leading to the release of the stored strain.

Currently, all material parameters in the model are directly adopted from Tobushi et al14. based on their study of a specific polyurethane SMP, which imposes certain limitations. However, the proposed model features a simple formulation with relatively few parameters and relies entirely on experimental data for modeling. It avoids excessive incorporation of internal molecular mechanisms or internal state variables associated with the shape memory effect of SMPs, ensuring generality and high robustness. In principle, high simulation accuracy can be achieved by accurately measuring temperature-dependent material parameters such as the elastic modulus E(T), viscosity coefficient µ(T), and relaxation time λ(T).

By modifying the viscosity coefficient and elasticity, the model effectively captures the variation in molecular chain mobility at different temperatures, a key characteristic of SMPs. This approach exhibits broad applicability, making it valid for most amorphous or semi-crystalline SMPs. For practical applications, material parameters at different temperatures can be obtained through creep tests or stress relaxation experiments, enabling the fitting of functional relationships between material parameters and temperature.

Additionally, the model is established under small-strain conditions and does not account for nonlinear issues under large deformations, plastic deformation problems, fatigue, or potential irrecoverable strain issues. Under large-strain conditions, the predictive accuracy of the model may decrease. However, considering that in practical engineering applications, SMPs mostly achieve significant deformations through bending, torsion, and similar methods, in such cases the overall stress and strain levels of the model are not excessively high. For example, in the torsional and bending deformation cases presented in this paper, the model still demonstrates high applicability. Moreover, the proposed model has a simple form, with material parameters that are easy to obtain and numerical simulations that are straightforward to implement, indicating that it retains high application value in engineering contexts.

Although the model proposed in this study demonstrates high predictive accuracy under small strain conditions, there are some deviations between the model’s predictions and experimental data under large strain conditions. Future research could focus on refining the model to improve its predictive accuracy under large strain conditions. Additionally, while the model successfully simulates the shape memory effect under torsional and bending deformations, further validation of its accuracy under complex stress states is needed. Future work will also aim to apply the model to predict SMPs’ behavior under more complex stress states or multi-field coupling conditions.
