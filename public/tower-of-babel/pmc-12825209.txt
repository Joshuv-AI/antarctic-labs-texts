Effectiveness of transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation for lower limb rehabilitation in spinal cord injury: a systematic review
Authors: Ravi Shankar, Kevin Sim Wei Wen, Ziyu Goh, Brandon Tan, Gobinathan Chandran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI) results in devastating motor and sensory deficits below the level of injury. Transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS) has emerged as a promising non-invasive neuromodulation technique for improving motor function in individuals with SCI. However, the effectiveness of tSCS for lower limb rehabilitation remains unclear.ObjectiveTo systematically review the effectiveness of tSCS for improving lower limb motor function, walking ability, spasticity, and functional independence in individuals with SCI.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, CINAHL, MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, and Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) from inception to March 2025. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), non-randomized studies, and case series examining tSCS for lower limb rehabilitation in adults with SCI. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias using appropriate tools (Cochrane RoB 2, ROBINS-I, JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist). We used GRADE to assess certainty of evidence.ResultsFrom 2042 identified records, 14 studies (n = 183 participants) met inclusion criteria, including 5 RCTs, 4 non-randomized studies, and 5 case series. Due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, we conducted a narrative synthesis. The single high-quality RCT (Comino-Suárez 2025) showed significant improvements in Lower Extremity Motor Score (LEMS) at follow-up (MD: 3.37, 95% CI: 0.29–6.46) and 10-meter walk test (10MWT) speed (MD: − 37.51 s, 95% CI: − 72.78 to − 2.23), but no significant effects on 6-minute walk test (6MWT) or spasticity. Most studies reported only mild adverse events (skin irritation, tingling). The certainty of evidence was very low for clinical outcomes and moderate for safety.ConclusionsCurrent evidence suggests tSCS may improve lower limb motor function and walking speed in SCI, with a favorable safety profile. However, the evidence base has significant limitations including small sample sizes, heterogeneous protocols, and methodological concerns. Well-designed, adequately powered RCTs with standardized protocols are urgently needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-025-01775-w.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) represents one of the most devastating neurological conditions, affecting approximately 27 million people globally with an incidence of 0.9 million new cases annually [1]. While traumatic causes account for the majority of cases, non-traumatic etiologies including tumors, infections, vascular malformations, and degenerative diseases contribute significantly to the SCI population, particularly in older adults [2]. The etiology of SCI may result in differing incidence of medical complications and influence prognosis, but both benefit from a comprehensive management approach. Both traumatic and non-traumatic SCI result in similar functional impairments and rehabilitation challenges, making comprehensive treatment approaches equally relevant across etiologies. The consequences of SCI extend far beyond the initial trauma, resulting in profound motor and sensory deficits below the level of injury, autonomic dysfunction, and significant psychosocial impacts. The economic burden is substantial, with lifetime costs ranging from US$0.7 million to US$2.5 million per individual depending on injury severity and age at onset [3].

The pathophysiology of SCI involves both primary mechanical damage and secondary injury cascades that disrupt neural circuits controlling voluntary movement [4]. While the spinal cord below the injury retains intrinsic neural networks capable of generating motor patterns, these circuits remain dormant due to interrupted supraspinal input. This understanding has led to a paradigm shift in SCI rehabilitation, moving from compensatory strategies to restorative approaches aimed at reactivating these preserved neural circuits.

Over the past two decades, electrical neuromodulation has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for SCI rehabilitation. Epidural spinal cord stimulation (eSCS) has demonstrated remarkable results, enabling voluntary movement and even independent standing and stepping in individuals with clinically complete SCI [5, 6]. However, eSCS requires invasive surgical implantation, carries surgical risks, and involves substantial costs, limiting its widespread clinical implementation.

Transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS) has emerged as a non-invasive alternative that applies electrical currents through surface electrodes placed over the spine. This technique aims to activate similar neural structures as eSCS, including proprioceptive afferents, interneuronal networks, and motor neurons, without the need for surgical intervention. The postulated mechanism of action of transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS) to effect functional recovery is suggested to be due to the activation of the proprioceptive fibres during stimulation, contributing to spinal cord neuromodulation [7–9] and motor recovery following SCI [10–12]. tSCS has been shown to increase voluntary motor response, improve trunk stability and function and better quality of life [13].

Despite growing interest in tSCS for SCI rehabilitation, critical knowledge gaps persist. Wide variation exists in stimulation parameters across studies, including frequencies (15–50 Hz), intensities (10–190 mA), electrode configurations, and treatment durations (single sessions to months). The characteristics of individuals most likely to benefit remain unclear, with limited comparative data between tSCS and standard rehabilitation or other neuromodulation approaches. Most studies report only immediate or short-term effects, and the precise neurophysiological mechanisms underlying tSCS effects require further elucidation.

Current evidence is fragmented due to methodological variability, diverse patient populations, inconsistent parameters, and heterogeneous outcomes. Prior reviews have explored tSCS broadly but lack systematic synthesis specifically targeting lower limb rehabilitation. Megía García et al. [13] examined tSCS and motor rehabilitation generally across both upper and lower limbs. Alashram et al. [14] specifically investigated tSCS effects on spasticity in SCI patients but included only six studies (five pilot studies and one case series) with heterogeneous evidence and limited methodological quality (PEDro scores 2–4). Shi et al. [15] conducted a meta-analysis on motor function but included only six small-scale trials with mixed upper and lower extremity outcomes. Hassan et al. [16] focused specifically on transcutaneous spinal direct current stimulation effects on spasticity across various upper motor neuron conditions. Tajali et al. [17] systematically reviewed neural pathway excitability modulations but emphasized neurophysiological mechanisms rather than functional outcomes. Other reviews either combined upper and lower limb data without differentiation (García et al. [13]), focused exclusively on upper limb function (García-Alén et al. [18]), or provided general mappings without specific tSCS outcomes analysis (Barss et al. [19]). Hernandez-Navarro et al. [20] highlighted this gap, including only one tSCS-focused trial.

To address these limitations, this systematic review integrates diverse study designs, incorporates recent evidence through March 2025, examines broader outcome measures including spasticity and functional independence, employs rigorous database search methodology, and applies GRADE assessment for robust clinical guidance and comprehensive safety evaluation.

The primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to evaluate the effectiveness of tSCS for improving lower limb motor function in individuals with SCI. Specifically, we aim to: (1) synthesize evidence on the effects of tSCS on motor scores, walking ability, and spasticity; (2) assess the safety profile of tSCS interventions; (3) evaluate the quality of evidence using standardized tools; and (4) identify factors that may influence treatment outcomes. Our secondary objectives include examining the effects on functional independence and quality of life, exploring optimal stimulation parameters, and identifying knowledge gaps to guide future research.

This review addresses several critical questions facing clinicians and researchers in the field of SCI rehabilitation: Does tSCS produce clinically meaningful improvements in the lower limb motor function? What is the magnitude of effect compared to control interventions? Which patient subgroups are most likely to benefit? What are the optimal stimulation parameters and treatment protocols? By providing evidence-based answers to these questions, this review aims to inform clinical practice and guide the development of future trials in this rapidly evolving field.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [21]. The protocol was registered prospectively in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420250652270). We used the PICOS framework to define eligibility criteria. The population included adults aged 18 years or older with traumatic or non-traumatic SCI of any level and severity (American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale [AIS] A-D), at any time post-injury. The intervention of interest was transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation applied for lower limb neuromodulation, either alone or in combination with rehabilitation interventions. Comparators included no intervention, sham stimulation, standard rehabilitation, or active comparators. Primary outcomes were lower limb motor function and walking ability, while secondary outcomes included spasticity, functional independence, quality of life, and adverse events. Motor function was specifically assessed using standardized measures including the Lower Extremity Motor Score (LEMS) from the International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI), manual muscle testing scores, and validated motor assessment scales. Walking ability was evaluated through objective measures including the 10-meter walk test, 6-minute walk test, and Walking Index for Spinal Cord Injury II. We included randomized controlled trials, non-randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, case-control studies, and case series with five or more participants.

We excluded conference abstracts without full-text publication, single case reports (n < 5), studies mixing SCI with other neurological populations without separate SCI data, studies focusing exclusively on upper limb or non-motor outcomes, animal studies, and review articles, though these were screened for references.

We extracted data on whether outcomes were assessed during active stimulation, immediately post-stimulation, or at follow-up periods to understand the temporal relationship between intervention and measured effects.

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, Scopus, CINAHL, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Physiotherapy Evidence Database from inception to March 31, 2025. While our search strategy focused on ‘transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation’ and related terms, we acknowledge that alternative terminology such as ‘transspinal stimulation’ is used in the literature. Future reviews may benefit from incorporating broader search terms to capture additional studies examining physiological outcomes such as H-reflex modulation and neurotransmission parameters. The search strategy combined terms related to SCI (e.g., “spinal cord injury,” “paraplegia,” “tetraplegia”) and tSCS (e.g., “transcutaneous spinal stimulation,” “non-invasive spinal stimulation,” “surface spinal stimulation”). The full PubMed search strategy was:

((spinal cord injury[MeSH] OR spinal cord injuries[MeSH] OR “spinal cord injury“[tiab] OR “spinal cord injuries“[tiab] OR “spinal cord injur*“[tiab] OR “traumatic spinal cord injur*“[tiab] OR SCI[tiab] OR paraplegia[MeSH] OR paraplegia[tiab] OR tetraplegia[tiab] OR quadriplegia[tiab]) AND (transcutaneous[tiab] OR “transcutaneous spinal“[tiab] OR “transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation“[tiab] OR “transcutaneous electrical spinal cord stimulation“[tiab] OR tSCS[tiab] OR TSS[tiab] OR “non-invasive“[tiab] OR “non invasive“[tiab] OR noninvasive[tiab] OR surface[tiab] OR “surface electrical“[tiab]) AND (“spinal stimulation“[tiab] OR “spinal cord stimulation“[tiab] OR “spinal cord stimulation“[MeSH] OR “Electric Stimulation Therapy“[MeSH] OR “electric stimulation“[MeSH] OR neuromodulation[tiab] OR neurostimulation[tiab] OR “electrical stimulation“[tiab] OR scES[tiab] OR ESCS[tiab])).

We also searched grey literature sources, screened reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews, and performed forward citation searching using Google Scholar.

Search results were imported into Covidence systematic review software. After removing duplicates, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently assessed by the same reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form piloted on three studies. Extracted data encompassed study characteristics, participant demographics, intervention details, comparator information, outcome measures and results, adverse events, study limitations, and conflicts of interest.

Risk of bias was assessed using design-specific tools. For RCTs, we used the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2) [22], while non-randomized studies were assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [23]. Case series were evaluated using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Series Studies [24]. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and results were presented using traffic light plots.

We initially planned to conduct meta-analyses for outcomes reported by three or more comparable studies. However, due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, and the limited number of comparable studies for each outcome, we conducted a narrative synthesis following Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) reporting guidelines [25]. The synthesis was organized by outcome, grouping studies by design and summarizing findings with attention to direction and magnitude of effects, consistency across studies, methodological quality, and clinical significance. We used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to assess certainty of evidence for each outcome.

Results

Study selection

The search yielded 2042 records from databases and 13 from other sources. After removing 926 duplicates, we screened 1129 titles and abstracts, excluding 1037 as irrelevant. Of 92 full texts assessed, 78 were excluded for various reasons. These included wrong outcomes in 15 studies, insufficient sample size in 14 studies, duplicate publications in 13 studies, wrong study design in 10 studies, conference abstracts only in 9 studies, wrong intervention in 6 studies, not primary research in 6 studies, and wrong population in 5 studies. Ultimately, 14 studies met inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The 14 included studies comprised 5 RCTs, 4 non-randomized controlled studies, and 5 case series, collectively including 183 participants. Studies were conducted in 6 countries: USA (n = 7), UK (n = 2), Spain (n = 2), Switzerland (n = 1), Austria (n = 1), and Russia (n = 1). Participants ranged from 18 to 69 years old, with most studies including predominantly male participants, representing approximately 75% of the total sample. Injury levels varied considerably, ranging from C1 to T12. Eight studies included only participants with cervical injuries, two focused exclusively on thoracic injuries, and four included mixed levels. Regarding injury severity, three studies included only participants with motor-incomplete injuries (AIS C-D), three included only motor-complete injuries (AIS A-B), and eight included mixed severities. Time since injury showed wide variation, ranging from 36 days to 57 years. Five studies focused on chronic SCI of more than one year duration, four examined subacute SCI between one and twelve months, and five included participants with mixed chronicity. Baseline ambulatory status also varied considerably across studies, with six including only ambulatory participants, two including only non-ambulatory participants, and six including mixed ambulatory status.

Intervention characteristics

The tSCS protocols demonstrated substantial heterogeneity across studies. Stimulation frequency ranged from 15 to 50 Hz, with 30 Hz being most common, used in seven studies. Intensity varied widely from 10 to 190 mA, typically set to individual tolerance or below motor threshold. Pulse width ranged from 0.5 to 1.0 ms, with 1 ms being most common. The waveform consisted of biphasic pulses in all studies, with five studies incorporating a 10 kHz carrier frequency. Electrode placement was relatively consistent for the cathode, positioned most commonly at T11-T12 in nine studies, though some used cervical or multi-site placement. The anode was typically placed over the abdomen or iliac crests.

Treatment duration showed considerable variation across studies. Session length ranged from as brief as 10 min to as long as 3 h. The total number of sessions varied dramatically from single session experiments to protocols involving 120 sessions. Treatment periods ranged from a single day to 40 weeks. Eight studies combined tSCS with rehabilitation interventions including gait training, functional task practice, or robotic-assisted therapy, while six examined tSCS in isolation or with minimal co-intervention.

The timing of stimulation relative to therapeutic exercises varied across studies: most delivered stimulation during training sessions to enhance neural activation (Lin et al. [26]—during treadmill walking; Hope et al. [27]; Estes et al. [28]; McKenzie et al. [29]; McHugh et al. [30]; Suggitt et al. [35]—during locomotor training), while others applied brief stimulation before training to prime neural networks (Kumru et al. [33] − 2 min before each assessment). No studies in this review used post-training stimulation for consolidation effects.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias varied considerably by study design. Among the five RCTs, all showed some concerns for overall bias. The primary sources of bias included blinding challenges inherent to electrical stimulation interventions, unclear allocation concealment in three studies, baseline imbalances between groups in two studies, and high dropout rates in one study. All four non-randomized studies demonstrated high risk of bias, mainly attributable to confounding due to lack of randomization, absence of appropriate control groups, and small sample sizes limiting statistical power. The five-case series showed generally moderate quality, with limitations primarily in consecutive case selection, comparability of participants, and short follow-up periods.

Effects on lower limb motor function

Eleven studies assessed lower limb motor function, with most using the LEMS from the International Standards for Neurological Classification of SCI. The highest quality evidence emerged from Comino-Suárez et al. [38], who performed a double-blind RCT involving 27 participants. This study compared tSCS plus robotic gait training to sham stimulation plus robotic training. At four-week follow-up, the tSCS group demonstrated significantly greater LEMS improvement with a mean difference of 3.37 points (95% CI: 0.29–6.46, p < 0.05).

Two smaller RCTs yielded mixed results. Hope et al. [40] study of 10 participants found no significant between-group differences, though effect sizes favored tSCS with Cohen’s d of 0.08. Similarly, Estes et al. [28] study of 16 participants found no significant between-group differences in LEMS change.

Non-randomized studies and case series generally reported improvements, though with notable variability. Savenkova et al. [34] study found that six of seven participants in the tSCS group improved muscle strength compared to only one of eight controls. Tefertiller et al. [37] case series reported that four of seven participants improved LEMS by zero to two points. Al’joboori et al. [36] pilot study found three of five participants in the tSCS group improved ISNCSCI motor scores. However, improvements were consistently modest, typically ranging from one to seven points, and the clinical significance remains unclear as the minimal clinically important difference for LEMS in SCI has not been established. The available data suggest that improvements occurred across various baseline strength levels, though detailed muscle strength analyses were limited by inconsistent reporting methods. Most studies used composite motor scores (LEMS, UEMS) rather than individual muscle strength grading, and none stratified results by specific muscle strength levels or reported improvements relative to clinically relevant strength thresholds.

Effects on walking ability

Walking outcomes were assessed in eleven studies using various standardized measures. For the 10-Meter Walk Test, seven studies assessed walking speed with variable results. Most studies assessed self-selected walking speed (McKenzie et al. [29] specified both “self-selected velocity” and “fast velocity”; other studies using 10MWT did not specify speed instruction), which may have introduced variability due to motivational factors rather than purely reflecting intervention effects. The RCT by Comino-Suárez et al. [38] demonstrated significant improvement in the tSCS group with a mean difference of − 37.51 s (95% CI: − 72.78 to − 2.23, p < 0.05). The case series by McHugh et al. [30] reported a significant mean improvement of 0.16 m/s. The study by Tefertiller et al. [37] found both ambulatory participants which met the MCID improved speed by 0.10 to 0.17 m/s. Other studies showed mixed results with generally small effect sizes.

Five studies assessed walking endurance using the 6-Minute Walk Test. Comino-Suárez et al. [38] found no significant between-group difference, while McHugh et al. [30] reported a significant mean improvement of 44.65 m. Individual studies reported improvements ranging from 9 to 69 m, showing considerable variability.

Three studies evaluated walking function using the Walking Index for Spinal Cord Injury II scale. Comino-Suárez et al. [38] found significant improvement in the tSCS group at follow-up with a mean difference of 3.43 (95% CI: 0.45–6.41, p < 0.05). McHugh et al. [30] reported a mean improvement of 2.6 levels, suggesting functional gains in walking independence.

Effects on spasticity

Six studies assessed spasticity, primarily using the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS). Evidence from RCTs showed mixed results. Comino-Suárez et al. [38] found no significant between-group differences in MAS scores. The crossover study by Lin et al. [26] reported a trend toward reduced minimal margin of stability during adaptation (p = 0.05), suggesting possible effects on dynamic balance related to spasticity.

Observational studies provided some evidence for temporary spasticity reduction. single-The session study by Hofstoetter et al. [39] found significant immediate reduction in MAS sum scores (p = 0.003), which was maintained at two hours post-intervention (p = 0.010). Hope et al. [40] reported an overall reduction of 2.2 ankle clonus oscillations with a small effect size (d = − 0.2). Other studies showed mixed results with generally small, transient effects. The collective evidence suggests tSCS may provide temporary spasticity reduction, but effects appear short-lived and clinically modest.

Effects on functional independence and balance

Only three studies assessed functional independence using the Spinal Cord Independence Measure III. Comino-Suárez et al. [38] found no significant between-group differences. Al’joboori et al. [36] reported no changes exceeding the minimal detectable change threshold. Tefertiller et al. [37] noted improvements in two of seven participants who changed AIS grade during the intervention period. The limited data preclude definitive conclusions about tSCS effects on functional independence.

Two studies assessed balance using the Berg Balance Scale. Suggitt et al. [35] reported significant improvement from a median score of 2.0 to 4.0 (p = 0.013). However, the limited data from single-arm studies prevent definitive conclusions about balance improvements.

Adverse events

All 14 studies reported on adverse events, with tSCS demonstrating a consistently favorable safety profile. Common mild adverse events included skin irritation or redness at electrode sites, which was the most frequently reported issue, tingling or paresthesia during stimulation, and mild discomfort during stimulation that typically resolved with intensity adjustment.

No serious adverse events were directly attributed to tSCS across all studies. One case of autonomic dysreflexia occurred in the study conducted by Al’joboori et al. [36] but was later determined to be related to a leg injury rather than the intervention. One participant in McKenzie et al. [29] study withdrew due to transportation issues unrelated to safety concerns. One participant in Suggitt et al. [35] study transitioned from AIS A to AIS B. No studies reported burns, seizures, or other serious complications. The evidence for safety demonstrated moderate certainty per GRADE assessment, representing the highest quality evidence among all assessed outcomes.

Subgroup analyses and patterns

While limited data prevented formal subgroup analyses, observational patterns emerged from the narrative synthesis. Regarding injury severity, participants with motor-incomplete injuries (AIS C-D) showed more consistent improvements than those with motor-complete injuries (AIS A-B). For chronicity, both subacute and chronic participants demonstrated responses to tSCS, with no clear superiority of either group. Concerning stimulation parameters, while 30 Hz frequency was used most commonly, optimal parameters remain unclear due to the lack of systematic dose-finding studies.

Certainty of evidence

GRADE assessment revealed very low certainty for all clinical outcomes except safety. Key factors reducing certainty included risk of bias from blinding difficulties and baseline imbalances, inconsistency from heterogeneous protocols and populations, imprecision from small sample sizes and wide confidence intervals, and suspected publication bias given the small number of studies and potential for unreported negative trials (Table 2).

tSCS may improve lower extremity motor scores (LEMS), with mean improvements up to 7 points, although the evidence supporting this is very uncertain. Similarly, tSCS may enhance walking speed and endurance, but findings vary widely across studies, and the overall certainty of the evidence remains low. In terms of spasticity, tSCS may offer temporary relief, though the effects are short-lived and the supporting evidence is also very uncertain. Regarding safety, tSCS appears to be generally safe, with only minor adverse events such as skin irritation and tingling reported, and this conclusion is supported by evidence of moderate certainty.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 14 studies examining tSCS for lower limb rehabilitation in 183 individuals with SCI. The main findings indicate that tSCS may improve lower limb motor function and walking speed, with the highest quality evidence from Comino-Suárez et al. [38] showing statistically significant improvements in LEMS and 10MWT. However, effects on walking endurance, spasticity, and functional independence remain uncertain. tSCS demonstrated a favorable safety profile with only mild adverse events reported across all studies. Despite these promising signals, the overall certainty of evidence remains very low for clinical outcomes due to significant methodological limitations inherent in the current evidence base.

Our findings align with and extend previous systematic reviews while highlighting persistent evidence gaps. The review by Megía García et al. [13] included only five studies and concluded that evidence was insufficient to support tSCS effectiveness. Our review, incorporating nine additional studies published since 2020, provides a more comprehensive assessment but reaches similar conclusions regarding evidence limitations. This consistency across reviews, despite doubling the available evidence, underscores the fundamental challenges in establishing tSCS effectiveness. This pattern reflects a familiar challenge in rehabilitation research, but also a chance for progress. As small and diverse studies continue to emerge, collaborative efforts to enhance coordination and standardization could help the field move toward more definitive and impactful conclusions.

The modest motor improvements observed, typically ranging from one to seven LEMS points, raise important questions about clinical significance. While statistical significance was achieved in some studies, the functional impact of these changes remains unclear. The minimal clinically important difference for LEMS in SCI has not been established which limits our ability to interpret whether statistically significant changes translate to meaningful functional improvements for patients based on LEMS improvement alone.

Walking improvements showed greater promise, particularly the 37.5-second improvement in 10MWT from Comino-Suárez et al. [38]. This magnitude of change exceeds the suggested minimal clinically important difference of 0.13 m/s for walking speed in incomplete SCI. In patients with SCI, walking speed is reduced likely primarily due to effect on reduced cadence from reduced maximal stride frequency [42]. The motor improvements seen could possibly be a contributing factor improving maximal stride frequency and subsequently walking speed, particularly with return in power of propulsive muscles.

Less promising were results on endurance and exercise capacity assessed through 6MWT.The limited effects on 6MWT performance may indicate that while tSCS can acutely enhance neuromuscular function, it may not address the cardiovascular and metabolic limitations that constrain endurance performance in SCI. This differential effect pattern provides important insights into tSCS mechanisms and suggests that combined interventions addressing both neuromuscular and cardiovascular components may be necessary for comprehensive walking recovery.

The optimal stimulation parameters remain elusive despite the growing number of studies. Frequency parameters showed relatively equal distribution between 30 Hz (5 studies: McKenzie et al. [29]; Al’joboori et al. [36]; Tefertiller et al. [37]; Comino-Suárez et al. [38]; Kumru et al. [33]) and 50 Hz (4 studies: Hope et al. [27]; Estes et al. [28]; McHugh et al. [30]; Hofstoetter et al. [39]), with some studies exploring variable frequencies (15–50 Hz range). This distribution may reflect different theoretical approaches rather than evidence-based optimization, highlighting the need for dedicated dose-finding studies to establish evidence-based parameter selection. The wide intensity range from 15 to 190 mA across studies confirms that individualization is necessary, with most studies setting intensity based on individual tolerance thresholds or motor activation levels. Several studies incorporated 10 kHz carrier frequencies (McKenzie et al. [29]; Tefertiller et al. [37]; Kumru et al. [33]; Savenkova et al. [34]), though the specific rationale and comparative effectiveness of this approach were not systematically evaluated within this body of literature. The consistent approach of individualizing stimulation intensity based on tolerance or motor threshold across multiple studies suggests this may be a more evidence-based practice than frequency selection, though systematic dose-finding studies remain notably absent.

Current evidence provides qualified support for considering tSCS as an adjunct to rehabilitation in motivated individuals with SCI, particularly those with motor-incomplete injuries showing walking potential. The favorable safety profile and non-invasive nature make it an attractive option compared to surgical alternatives. However, several factors should guide clinical implementation. Patient selection appears critical, with motor-incomplete SCI showing more consistent responses. Realistic expectations must be set, as improvements are likely modest and may require sustained intervention over weeks to months. The evidence suggests tSCS is most effective when combined with active rehabilitation rather than as a standalone intervention. While adverse events are mild, regular monitoring of skin integrity and autonomic responses remains important.

While optimal standardized protocols remain undefined, current evidence suggests clinicians should adopt an individualized approach to tSCS implementation. The successful study by Comino-Suárez et al. [38] used 20 sessions over 4 weeks, providing a reasonable starting framework. However, the wide variation across studies indicates that treatment parameters may need individualization based on patient response. For stimulation intensity, a patient-specific approach involving gradual increases to achieve desired motor effects or maximum tolerable intensity appears most appropriate. Some studies showing benefits after as few as 6 sessions while others extended to 120 sessions suggest treatment duration should be tailored to individual progress. This need for individualization, while allowing flexibility in clinical practice, highlights the urgent need for dose-response studies and comparative effectiveness research examining modulated versus unmodulated tSCS approaches to guide evidence-based clinical decision-making.

This review has several notable strengths. The comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases captured the most current evidence in this rapidly evolving field. Inclusion of recent studies effectively doubled the available evidence compared to previous reviews. The rigorous methodology following PRISMA guidelines, design-specific risk of bias assessment, and GRADE certainty evaluation provide transparent and systematic evidence synthesis. However, important limitations must be acknowledged. The substantial heterogeneity prevented meta-analysis, limiting our ability to provide pooled effect estimates. Small sample sizes across studies limit generalizability, and variable follow-up periods prevent assessment of long-term effectiveness. The potential for publication bias cannot be excluded given the relatively small number of studies.

This review identifies critical research priorities for advancing tSCS in SCI rehabilitation. Standardization of protocols through development of consensus guidelines for stimulation parameters, electrode placement, and treatment duration would enable meaningful comparisons across studies. Mechanistic studies combining tSCS with neuroimaging could elucidate mechanisms and identify biomarkers predicting response. Systematic dose-response investigations are essential for optimizing treatment parameters. Larger, multicenter RCTs with standardized protocols, appropriate sham controls, and long-term follow-up are needed to establish effectiveness definitively. Machine learning approaches could help identify patient characteristics predicting tSCS response, enabling personalized treatment selection. Systematic evaluation of tSCS combined with pharmacological agents, other neuromodulation techniques, or specific rehabilitation approaches could identify optimal combination therapies. Development and validation of user-friendly devices for home use could improve accessibility and enable longer treatment courses. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses comparing tSCS to standard care and invasive alternatives would inform healthcare policy decisions.

Several implications emerge for clinical practice and policy despite the current evidence limitations. For clinicians, tSCS may be considered for motivated patients with incomplete SCI as an adjunct to rehabilitation, with clear communication about modest expected benefits and standardized outcome assessment to facilitate clinical monitoring. For healthcare systems, major investment in training and equipment should be planned for, while participation in research studies should be encouraged and development of clinical registries could provide valuable real-world effectiveness data. For regulatory bodies, current evidence supports tSCS as generally safe with evolving evidence on its effectiveness, and post-market surveillance systems could monitor long-term efficacy and sustained benefits as clinical use expands.

Conclusions

This systematic review provides the most comprehensive synthesis to date of tSCS effectiveness for lower limb rehabilitation in SCI. While promising signals exist for motor function and walking improvements, the evidence base remains limited by small samples, heterogeneous protocols, and methodological concerns. The single high-quality RCT showing significant benefits provides proof-of-concept but requires replication in larger, diverse populations.

tSCS represents a potentially valuable addition to SCI rehabilitation, offering a non-invasive alternative to surgical neuromodulation. However, current evidence provides low certainty for clinical effectiveness. The favorable safety profile supports continued investigation, but clinical implementation should proceed cautiously with realistic expectations about modest potential benefits.

Future research must address fundamental questions about optimal protocols, patient selection, and long-term outcomes through well-designed, adequately powered trials. Standardization of methods and outcomes would accelerate evidence synthesis and clinical translation.

The rapid growth in tSCS research, with over half the studies published in the last three years, suggests the evidence base will continue expanding. Regular updating of this systematic review will be essential as new trials report results. The potential for non-invasive neuromodulation to improve function after SCI remains compelling, but realizing this potential requires continued rigorous investigation guided by the research priorities identified in this review. As the field matures, we anticipate that optimized protocols, better patient selection criteria, and combination approaches will enhance the therapeutic potential of this promising technology.
