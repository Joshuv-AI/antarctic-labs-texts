Initial treatment of COPD with LABA-ICS or LABA-LAMA: real-world comparative effectiveness
Authors: Samy Suissa, Mathew Cherian, Sophie Dell’Aniello, Pierre Ernst
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe 2023 Global initiative for chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) recommendations advocate long-acting beta2 agonist (LABA) and long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) combinations (LABA-LAMA) for the initial pharmacological treatment of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) with multiple exacerbations. However, this choice, rather than combinations of LABA and inhaled corticosteroids (LABA-ICS), no longer recommended in GOLD, was based on randomised trials that excluded patients with multiple prior exacerbations.Research questionWhat is the comparative effectiveness of initiating COPD treatment with LABA-ICS versus LABA-LAMA inhalers, particularly in patients with multiple COPD exacerbations, in a real-world clinical practice setting?Study design and methodsWe identified a cohort of patients with COPD, 40 years of age or older, from the United Kingdom’s Clinical Practice Research Datalink. Treatment-naïve initiators of single-inhaler LABA-ICS or LABA-LAMA, with no prior asthma, LABA, LAMA or ICS use, were compared on the incidence of moderate or severe COPD exacerbation over 1 year, after adjustment by propensity score weighting.ResultsThe study cohort included 20 750 initiators of LABA-ICS inhalers and 16 594 of LABA-LAMA. The overall adjusted HR of a first moderate or severe exacerbation with LABA-ICS relative to LABA-LAMA was 1.03 (95% CI 0.98 to 1.08). Among patients with two or more prior exacerbations, the HR of exacerbation with LABA-ICS versus LABA-LAMA was 0.89 (95% CI 0.81 to 0.97), while it was 1.07 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.15) among patients with no prior exacerbations. The HR was 0.92 (95% CI 0.86 to 0.99) among those with forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)≥50% predicted.InterpretationIn a real-world clinical practice setting of COPD treatment, initiating therapy with LABA-ICS inhalers may be more effective than LABA-LAMA inhalers among patients with multiple exacerbations, particularly those with FEV1≥50% predicted, but less effective among those with no prior exacerbations and FEV1<50% predicted. This study supports a targeted approach to initial therapy for COPD.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

The recent Global initiative for chronic Obstructive Lung Disease treatment recommendations for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) advocate a long-acting beta2 agonist (LABA) and long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) (LABA-LAMA) inhaler, rather than a LABA and inhaled corticosteroids (LABA-ICS), as initial treatment for patients with COPD with multiple exacerbations, based on evidence from randomised trials that excluded patients with multiple prior exacerbations.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Using a large cohort of over 37 000 patients with COPD, this observational study found that, overall, the two combinations provided similar results but that initiating treatment with LABA-ICS inhalers may be more effective than with LABA-LAMA inhalers among patients with multiple exacerbations, and particularly so for those with forced expiratory volume in 1 s≥50% predicted, while LABA-LAMA inhalers appeared more effective among those with no prior exacerbations.

Introduction

The pharmacological management of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) revolves mainly around long-acting bronchodilators, including long-acting beta2 agonists (LABAs) and muscarinic antagonists (LAMAs), and inhaled corticosteroids (ICS).1 Several combinations of these drugs are now formulated into single inhalers.

The 2023 Global initiative for chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) recommendations for the initial pharmacological treatment of COPD advocate a LABA-LAMA combination as initial maintenance therapy for patients with a history of multiple exacerbations and to consider adding an ICS for those with elevated eosinophil counts.2 The evidence that GOLD used for this recommendation is a Cochrane systematic review and network meta-analysis that ‘showed that the LABA+LAMA combination was the highest ranked treatment group to reduce COPD exacerbations’.3 However, the head-to-head randomised trials that dominated the finding that LABA-LAMA combinations resulted in fewer exacerbations than LABA-ICS combinations excluded patients with multiple, or even with any, prior exacerbations.4 5

A recent meta-analysis of 15 randomised trials found no difference in the risk of exacerbation, but lower incidence of pneumonia and increased all-cause mortality with a LABA-LAMA compared with a LABA-ICS.6 These trials were based on subjects with varying history of COPD exacerbation. Observational database studies on this question also reported dissimilar results.79 One found no difference in the risk of exacerbation between LABA-LAMA and LABA-ICS.7 A second found LABA-LAMA associated with lower exacerbation rates than one but not the other LABA-ICS combinations.8 The third found LABA-LAMA associated with lower rates of both exacerbation and pneumonia.9 These studies, however, included patients who had previously been treated with maintenance therapy, thus were unable to provide evidence for initial treatment and did not have data on lung function measurements.

In view of these discrepancies, we assessed the effectiveness of initial maintenance pharmacological therapy with a combination inhaler of LABA-ICS compared with a LABA-LAMA on the incidence of COPD exacerbation and the risk of severe pneumonia using a large population-based cohort formed from real-world clinical practice data, specifically designed to address the limitations of previous studies. We focused particularly on subjects with multiple exacerbations, the profile of interest for this GOLD recommendation.

Methods

Data source

This study was conducted using the clinical practice research datalink (CPRD), a primary care database from the UK that contains primary care medical records for over 50 million people enrolled from over 1800 practices. General practitioners record medical information, including demographic data, lifestyle factors and medical diagnoses, using the Systematised Medical Nomenclature for Medicine–Clinical Terminology classification. Prescriptions are automatically transcribed using the UK Prescription Pricing Authority Dictionary. For over 85% of the practices, the CPRD can be linked to the Hospital Episodes Statistics (HES) database that provides hospitalisation data. The recorded information on medications and diagnoses has been validated and shown to be of high quality.1012

Study design

We first identified all patients from the CPRD, linkable to HES databases, with a clinical diagnosis of COPD and who subsequently received a prescription for a LABA-ICS or LABA-LAMA from September 2014 to March 2021. We used an incident new-user design to form the study cohort, with cohort entry taken as the date of the first prescription for the LABA-ICS or LABA-LAMA single inhaler. To increase the likelihood of a COPD diagnosis, we only included patients 40 years of age or older on the date of first COPD diagnosis and subjects had at least 1 year of medical history prior to cohort entry to allow a baseline period and to identify new users of the study inhalers. Subjects with a clinical diagnosis of asthma and those who received LABA, LAMA or ICS during the baseline year were excluded. Subjects were followed for up to 1 year from the date of study cohort entry or until the addition of ICS for the LABA-LAMA arm or of LAMA for the LABA-ICS arm, the date of death, 31 March 2021, or the end of coverage in the practice, whichever occurred first.

Outcome events

The primary outcome was the first moderate or severe COPD exacerbation to occur after cohort entry. A severe exacerbation was defined as a hospitalisation for COPD and a moderate exacerbation by a new prescription for prednisolone. Safety outcomes were the occurrence of the first hospitalisation for community-acquired pneumonia (severe pneumonia) and all-cause mortality. The diagnostic and medication codes to identify these outcomes have been shown to be valid for these outcomes and used in several studies using the CPRD.1317

Covariates

Balance in covariates between the two treatment arms was based on the various measures of lifestyle, clinical diagnoses and prescriptions, from CPRD and HES data. Age, sex, body mass index, smoking status and alcohol abuse were measured at or prior to cohort entry. The severity of COPD at treatment initiation was assessed by the number of prior moderate and severe COPD exacerbations, and the use of other respiratory drugs, including short-acting inhaled beta agonists and anticholinergics, methylxanthines and antibiotics used for respiratory conditions, all measured during the 1-year baseline period. The most recent measures of dyspnoea, forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and blood eosinophil count prior to cohort entry were identified. Dyspnoea was measured by modified Medical Research Council, COPD assessment test score or clinically noted dyspnoea symptoms.18 The percent predicted FEV1 measurement, generally post-bronchodilator,19 was calculated from the absolute FEV1 value using age, sex and height, with ethnicity imputed as ‘Caucasian’ for all.20 Baseline comorbidity, including cardiovascular conditions, renal disease, cancer, diabetes as well as the use of antihypertensives, oral anticoagulants, proton-pump inhibitors, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and opioids in the year prior to cohort entry, was measured using clinical diagnoses, hospitalisations and prescriptions (table 1).

Data analysis

The propensity score of treatment initiation was estimated by logistic regression using all covariates. Fine stratification weights were computed by dividing the propensity score into its 100 percentile strata and calculating separate weights for the LABA-ICS and LABA-LAMA within each stratum, according to the proportions of subjects in each stratum, resulting in comparable treatment arms with the same covariate distribution as the study cohort.21 Standardised mean differences of covariates were computed to assess the comparability of the two treatment groups.

A weighted Cox proportional hazards regression model with a robust variance estimator was used in an as-treated analysis to estimate the HR of COPD exacerbation, pneumonia and death, comparing the initiators of LABA-ICS with initiators of LABA-LAMA single inhalers, during the first year after initiation. The as-treated analysis was based on continuous treatment, defined by the durations of successive prescriptions of the initial single-inhaler treatment, with a maximal 30-day grace period between the end and start of successive prescription dates.

The data analysis for the primary outcome was stratified by the number of moderate or severe exacerbations during the baseline year, by baseline blood eosinophil counts as <150, 150–300 and >300 cells/µL, and by percent predicted FEV1, using the weighted regression model with an interaction term between these factors and the treatment. Cubic splines were used to estimate the HR of an exacerbation comparing LABA-ICS with LABA-LAMA, as a continuous function of blood eosinophil count and percent predicted FEV1. Cubic splines used four knots located at 5%, 35%, 65% and 95% of the distribution for each of the two factors, corresponding to 80, 150, 250 and 600 (cells/µL) for blood eosinophils and 31%, 53%, 67% and 92% for percent predicted FEV1.

Sensitivity analyses included an intention-to-treat analysis, namely irrespective of the treatment duration, over the 1-year follow-up. Second, continuous use for the as-treated analyses was assessed by varying the grace period between prescriptions at 15 and 45 days. We also compared LABA-ICS to LABA-LAMA, restricting the contrasts to inhalers containing the same LABA, as well as stratifying the LABA-ICS by the ICS formulation, namely fluticasone-based, budesonide-based and beclomethasone-based inhalers. Finally, we restricted the analysis to the subgroup of the subjects with no history of asthma diagnosis ever. All analyses were conducted using SAS V.9.4. The study protocol was approved by the Independent Scientific Advisory Committee of the CPRD (Protocol 23-002846) and the Ethics Committee of the Jewish General Hospital (JGH Protocol # 2024-3847), Montreal, Quebec, Canada.

Patient involvement

No patients were involved in setting the research question or the outcome measure nor were they involved in developing plans for design or implementation of the study. No patients were asked to advise on interpretation or writing up of results. There are no plans to disseminate the results of the research to study participants or the relevant patient community.

Results

The study cohort included 20 750 new users of LABA-ICS inhalers and 16 594 new users of LABA-LAMA dual bronchodilators, after excluding patients with an asthma diagnosis and users of LABA, LAMA or ICS prior to treatment initiation (online supplemental figure S.1). The baseline characteristics, comparing the initiators of LABA-ICS with the initiators of LABA-LAMA, show that the two groups are well balanced after weighing by fine stratification of propensity scores (table 1). Of note, 11.6% had two or more COPD exacerbations in the year prior to initiating treatment, including 6.5% who had been hospitalised for their exacerbation, while 67.7% had no exacerbation. Around 76% were previously treated with short-acting bronchodilators. The unweighted baseline characteristics of the two treatment groups are provided in online supplemental table S.1.

The LABA-ICS single inhalers included formoterol–beclomethasone dipropionate (41%), salmeterol–fluticasone propionate (26%), formoterol–budesonide (21%), vilanterol–fluticasone furoate (10%) and formoterol–fluticasone propionate (2%), while the LABA-LAMA single inhalers included vilanterol-umeclidinium (58%), olodaterol–tiotropium (13%), indacaterol–glycopyrronium (14%) and formoterol–aclidinium (14%). The mean duration of continuous treatment was 5.0 months for LABA-ICS and 6.3 months for LABA-LAMA. Among the LABA-ICS group, 51.9% stopped treatment and 11.3% switched or added another inhaler during follow-up, while among the LABA-LAMA subjects, 41.7% stopped treatment and 6.3% switched or added another inhaler.

The cumulative incidence of a first moderate or severe exacerbation over 1 year was approximately 34% for the two treatment groups (figure 1). The corresponding HR of the first moderate or severe exacerbation for LABA-ICS compared with LABA-LAMA was 1.03 (95% CI 0.98 to 1.08) (table 2). For severe exacerbation, the HR was 1.07 (95% CI 0.92 to 1.24), for all-cause mortality, the HR was 1.00 (95% CI 0.88 to 1.14) and, for severe pneumonia, HR was 1.09 (95% CI 0.95 to 1.26) when comparing patients treated with LABA-ICS relative to patients treated with LABA-LAMA (table 2).

Stratifying by patient phenotypes, table 3 shows that LABA-ICS was associated with an 11% lower incidence of moderate or severe exacerbation among patients with two or more exacerbations in the year prior to cohort entry (HR 0.89; 95% CI 0.81 to 0.97), but a 7% higher incidence with LABA-ICS among patients with no prior exacerbation (HR 1.07 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.15). The incidence of an exacerbation was higher with LABA-ICS in patients with FEV1<50% predicted (HR 1.15; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.27), but lower among those with FEV1 ≥50% predicted (HR 0.92; 95% CI 0.86 to 0.99). Figure 2 displays the corresponding HR of an exacerbation as a function of continuous FEV1 % predicted values. There was no variation in the HR of exacerbation according to blood eosinophil count, with an HR of 1.01 (95% CI 0.91 to 1.13) comparing LABA-ICS with LABA-LAMA among subjects with a blood eosinophil count>300 cells/µL (table 3). Online supplemental figure S.2 displays this HR according to the continuous measure of blood eosinophil counts. The logistic regression analysis used for the computation of propensity scores was not affected by multicollinearity (correlations<0.8 and variance inflation factor<10). The assumption of proportional hazards for the Cox model was also satisfied for all outcomes in table 2 (p>0.05).

Exploratory stratification by both baseline percent predicted FEV1 and prior exacerbations, displayed in online supplemental table S.2, suggests that the higher incidence of an exacerbation with LABA-ICS in patients with no prior exacerbations is particularly notable among those with FEV1<50% (HR 1.31; 95% CI 1.15 to 1.48), but not those with FEV1 ≥50% predicted (HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.84 to 1.03). For those with two or more prior exacerbations, the lower incidence of an exacerbation with LABA-ICS is particularly reduced among those with FEV1 ≥50% (HR 0.82; 95% CI 0.73 to 0.93), but not significantly so for those with FEV1<50% predicted (HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.77 to 1.06), although the number of subjects in these stratified analyses is lessened. Exploratory stratified analyses by the frequency of moderate and severe exacerbations defining GOLD group E are displayed in online supplemental table S.3. It suggests that the lower incidence of moderate or severe exacerbation with LABA-ICS among patients with two or more prior exacerbations is not observed among those with a single prior severe exacerbation, a part of the GOLD group E definition.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our primary results (online supplemental table S.4). The HR of an exacerbation with LABA-ICS was somewhat higher in the intention-to-treat analysis over the 1-year follow-up (HR 1.06; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.11). Varying the grace period between prescriptions to define continuous treatment did not significantly alter the main result. The analysis restricted to the subgroup with no history of asthma diagnosis ever prior to treatment initiation, involving 17 666 initiators of LABA-ICS and 16 392 of LABA-LAMA, found similar results, namely an overall HR of a first moderate or severe exacerbation with LABA-ICS versus LABA-LAMA of 1.02 (95% CI 0.98 to 1.08). Among those with two or more prior exacerbations, the HR was 0.85 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.93) and it was 1.11 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.19) with no prior exacerbations, while it was 0.94 (95% CI 0.88 to 1.00) among those with FEV1 ≥50% predicted.

The comparisons between LABA-ICS and LABA-LAMA, restricted to the same LABA in both combination inhalers, found that, for formoterol, the HR was 0.86 (95% CI 0.78 to 0.95), while, for vilanterol, it was 1.12 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.24) (online supplemental table S.4). The comparison between LABA-ICS and LABA-LAMA, according to the specific ICS, finds some variations among fluticasone, budesonide and beclomethasone-based inhalers on the risk of moderate or severe exacerbation and pneumonia hospitalisation (online supplemental table S.5).

Discussion

In this observational study conducted using real-world data on patients with COPD, we found that the incidence of exacerbation, mortality and pneumonia was no different if the initial treatment was with a LABA-ICS or with a LABA-LAMA single inhaler over the first year of use. However, this study population, reflecting real-world practice, included two-thirds of subjects with no COPD exacerbations in the year before initiating either of these inhalers. Restricting our analysis to the patients with two or more prior COPD exacerbations, as well as among those with FEV1 ≥50% predicted, we found that a LABA-ICS single inhaler was more effective than a LABA-LAMA single inhaler at reducing the incidence of a moderate or severe exacerbation. In contrast, a LABA-LAMA inhaler was more effective among patients with no previous exacerbations and those with FEV1<50%. This is the first study, randomised or not, to compare these two combinations as the very first maintenance treatment of COPD, thus addressing the recent GOLD recommendations.

We can hypothesise that the finding that LABA-ICS is more effective in patients with two or more exacerbations, but LABA-LAMA is more effective in those with no prior exacerbations, may be due to the varying types of underlying inflammation. Indeed, LABA-ICS may benefit recurrent exacerbators due to a higher prevalence of type 2 inflammation, which is a known risk factor for recurrent exacerbations,22 and responsive to ICS.23

On the other hand, the specific finding that LABA-ICS is more effective than LABA-LAMA in patients with both two or more exacerbations and an FEV1>50%, namely patients with ‘milder’ disease but more exacerbations, may seem somewhat paradoxical. However, this patient profile has been the specific target of ICS-based treatment studies, as suggested by the randomised trials of LAMA-LABA-ICS triple therapy. The IMPACT trial of patients with confirmed COPD diagnosis included subjects with a predicted FEV1 of 50%–80% and at least two moderate exacerbations or one severe in the previous year, which formed 36% of the study population.24 Likewise, the ETHOS trial used a similar patient group, although with a predicted FEV1 of 50%–65%, which accounted for 28% of the study population.25 This phenotype would merit particular attention in future studies involving ICS-based therapies.

An interesting finding from exploratory analyses of the subgroup of GOLD group E patients suggests that the comparative effectiveness of the two treatments may be affected by the severity of the prior exacerbations. Indeed, the GOLD group E defines ‘frequent exacerbations’ as at least two moderate exacerbations or one hospitalised exacerbation in the past year. Our analyses of this group suggest that patients with multiple exacerbations may benefit from LABA-ICS, while those with only a single severe exacerbation that also defines group E would not, perhaps benefiting from LABA-LAMA. This observation in our data suggests research into the possible need to distinguish histories of moderate and of severe exacerbations, particularly those with a single severe exacerbation. It would not be surprising if patients admitted to hospital with COPD have more severe disease, less responsive to corticosteroids, than patients with moderate exacerbations treated with ambulatory oral corticosteroids.

Our study results differ from the head-to-head randomised trials used as evidence for the initial treatment of patients with multiple exacerbations.2 4 5 Indeed, both the FlowTriever for Acute Massive PE (FLAME) and Lung Cancer Multiomics Digital Human Avatars for Integrating Precision Medicine Into Clinical Practice (LANTERN) trials reported a lower incidence of moderate or severe exacerbation with the LABA-LAMA combination compared with LABA-ICS, over a 1-year follow-up.4 5 Our overall findings are, on the other hand, concordant with most trials that did not find such differences between these combinations.6

There could be several reasons for these discrepancies. Foremost, the LANTERN and FLAME trials excluded patients with multiple exacerbations. Indeed, 74% of patients in LANTERN had no exacerbation in the previous year, while 81% of the patients in FLAME had a single exacerbation in the prior year but none in the prior 6 weeks.

Another explanation is that the trials used combinations that involved different LABAs, such as indacaterol, formoterol or vilanterol, in the LABA-LAMA combination group and another LABA, salmeterol, in the comparator LABA-ICS group. Such a design strategy does not permit to distinguish whether the difference in outcomes between the groups is due to a more effective LABA in the LABA-LAMA group, a less effective one in the LABA-ICS group or some combination of the two effects. Our study also had different LABAs. Our analyses restricted to the same LABA in both combination inhalers found different HRs of a first exacerbation by LABA, namely 0.86 (95% CI 0.78 to 0.95) with formoterol and 1.12 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.24) with vilanterol. These results suggest possible differences in effectiveness among the LABAs that suggest consideration for further research. Nonetheless, residual confounding by indication is also possible if vilanterol is used preferentially in those with more severe disease.

An important limitation of all randomised trials is that most patients were already using these and other combinations before randomisation, which they had to discontinue. Thus, the trials do not provide evidence regarding the choice of the initial maintenance treatment. Yet, the recommendation is to use a LABA-LAMA as initial treatment for patients with multiple exacerbations and to consider a triple LABA-LAMA-ICS single inhaler for those with elevated eosinophil counts. In contrast, our study, based on the very first maintenance treatment, finds that a LABA-ICS inhaler is a more effective initial therapy than a LABA-LAMA among patients with multiple COPD exacerbations in the year before initiating treatment. On the other hand, it finds that LABA-LAMA is more effective than LABA-ICS in patients with no prior exacerbations.

The three observational studies of these treatment combinations also included patients who had previously been treated with maintenance therapy, thus were unable to provide evidence for initial treatment.79 Moreover, the studies did not have lung function measurements. One study provided a subgroup analysis among patients with multiple exacerbations, finding no significant difference in the incidence of moderate or severe exacerbation between the two inhalers in this exacerbator subgroup, while the results including all patients favoured the LABA-LAMA inhaler.9

All trials and two observational studies showed a higher incidence of pneumonia with the LABA-ICS combination compared with LABA-LAMA.6 8 9 Our study, however, did not find a significant increase in severe pneumonia with the LABA-ICS inhaler (HR 1.09; 95% CI 0.95 to 1.26). One possible explanation is the short duration of exposure to LABA-ICS, which could be insufficient to detect increases in severe pneumonia requiring hospitalisation. Other possible explanations are the low incidence of pneumonia (6 per 100 per year) and random variation.

Our study has several strengths. First, the new-user design identified study subjects at the time of initiation of the two study inhalers, a design that emulates the randomised trial approach in an observational setting. Second, our design excluded patients previously treated with any maintenance therapy, addressing the relative value of the very initial treatment with LABA-LAMA or LABA-ICS, data which trials used as evidence for guidelines do not provide. Third, our observational study was performed in a setting of general clinical practice, which provides real-world evidence on these treatments, particularly on the way these medications are used in clinical practice. As such, we found that the study population included a wide range of patients, particularly in terms of exacerbation history and lung function. As such, unlike the trials, our real-world study can provide comparative effectiveness of the two combination inhalers in specific subgroups, such as patients with multiple previous exacerbations or with no previous exacerbations.

Our study has limitations. First, the exposure is based on written and not dispensed prescriptions, resulting in potential exposure misclassification. Second, the two inhalers under study included several different combinations of LABAs, LAMAs and ICS, which have been shown to result in outcome variations.26 Analyses based on the same LABA suggest differences in effects, though imprecise due to the small numbers of subjects, that should be investigated further. Third, unlike the trials, the duration of continuous treatment was relatively short (mean 5–6 months), a duration that cannot be controlled, reflecting the real-world clinical setting of this study. Fourth, unlike the trials, we could not confirm COPD on spirometry in nearly 35% of the subjects, but the definition of COPD that we used has been validated in the CPRD, though it is possible that some undiagnosed asthma may remain.15 16 However, the subgroup analysis among those with no history of asthma diagnosis ever produced similar results. Moreover, the propensity score on which the groups are balanced includes the blood eosinophil count in the year prior to treatment initiation, available in more than 80% of subjects, and which is closely associated with asthma and ICS response. Fifth, we recalculated the percent predicted FEV1 measurement using race-neutral equations27 and found minimal effect with less than 3% of subjects reclassified as more than 50% predicted FEV1, namely 2.5% and 2.8% in the LABA-ICS and LABA-LAMA arms, respectively. Finally, as for any observational study, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, despite the use of weighted methods and propensity scores that included key covariates, which resulted in very similar distributions for the LABA-ICS and LABA-LAMA arms on all available patient characteristics.

Future research could also investigate the use of triple inhalers as the initial therapy for COPD, currently recommended only for patients with multiple exacerbations and blood eosinophil count>300 cells/µL.2 However, real-world data suggest that their use in clinical practice diverges significantly from these recommendations. Indeed, our previous studies using this UK database found that 42% of initiators of single-inhaler triple therapy had no exacerbations in the prior year, with close to 80% having blood eosinophil count<300 cells/µL.28 29 Research is needed on the effectiveness and safety of initiating triple therapy in patients with these profiles.

Conclusion

Our study based on data from a real-world clinical setting found no overall difference in reducing the exacerbations of COPD but suggests that initial maintenance treatment with a LABA-ICS inhaler is more effective than with LABA-LAMA for patients with two or more COPD exacerbations. This finding contrasts with the recent GOLD recommendation to use a LABA-LAMA for these patients. On the other hand, our results support the GOLD recommendation to initiate treatment with a LABA-LAMA for Group B patients with no prior exacerbations. Our study also suggests that lung function could be an important determinant of effectiveness, with LABA-ICS appearing to be more effective among patients with FEV1 ≥50% predicted, while a LABA-LAMA inhaler was more effective among those with FEV1<50%, though these effects vary by the number of prior exacerbations. As this is the first study to compare these two combinations as the very first maintenance treatment of COPD, further research involving larger cohorts that allow multiple stratification with sufficient power is needed to corroborate these findings that suggest a more targeted approach to initial treatments for specific COPD phenotypes, including the possible distinction between histories of severe and moderate exacerbations.
