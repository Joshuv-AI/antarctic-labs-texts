A clustering-stratified cross-validation framework for validating omics survival models: application to head and neck cancer
Authors: Antoine Dubray-Vautrin, Olivier Choussy, Constance Lamy, Grégoire Marret, Joey Martin, Jerzy Klijanienko, Sophie Vacher, Ladidi Ahmanache, Ivan Bieche, Célia Dupain, Christophe Le Tourneau, Jimmy Mullaert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study tackles the challenge of developing reliable prognostic models for time-to-event (TTE) outcomes using high-dimensional omics data in head and neck cancers. Resampling methods, particularly nested cross-validation, are considered as standard for model hyperparameter selection and performance evaluation. When handling clustered data, balancing the random partition of the cross-validation folds to minimize optimism bias and instability could be tested. This work compares the performance of three nested cross-validation implementations, including random assignment of the folds, clustering-based resampling, and internal-external validation using an hold out approach.MethodWe analyzed two head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cohorts: The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and SCANDARE (NCT03017573), with clinical data and transcriptomic data normalized as log-transcripts per million. Three model selection methods LASSO, IPF-Lasso, and Priority-LASSO were evaluated within five nested cross-validation frameworks: Standard nested cross-validation, Clustering-based nested-cross validation, nested-cross validation with Combat correction, Nested cross-validation for optimization combined with hold-out for validation, Nested cross-validation for optimization combined with hold-out and ComBat correction for validation. Predictive performance was assessed using 3-year AUC and Integrated Brier Score (IBS).ResultsWe analyzed data from 581 patients (mean age 61.0 years, 33.6% female) across TCGA-HNSC (n = 505) and SCANDARE (n = 76). Clustering analyses, using UMAP and k-means, identified three transcriptomic clusters. Validation strategies demonstrated reduced instability for Lasso (p < 0.001), IPF-Lasso (p < 0.001) and Priority-lasso (p < 0.001) without apparent optimism in discrimination and calibration metrics with stratified nested cross-validation (SNCV), supporting its utility. As an application using IPF-Lasso Cox models with SNCV, we integrated clinical and transcriptomic data, selecting 35 prognosis variables of head and neck carcinomas. This model achieved a 3-year AUC of 0.71 and IBS of 0.08.ConclusionClustering-based nested cross-validation combined with stratified cross-validation offers a robust compromise for developing high-dimensional survival models and evaluating their predictive performance. This approach leverages clustering-derived stratification to balance heterogeneity in the dataset within cross-validation folds, although the training and test sets remain derived from the pooled dataset rather than fully independent cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-025-02709-9.Keywords: Cross-validation, Machine learning, Cox penalization, Survival, Internal validation, ROC

Introduction

Prognosis models using time-to-event (TTE) are particularly developed in medical research and epidemiology for prediction [1]. These models are essential for analyzing survival data and estimating the risk of specific events occurring over time. However, the increasing availability of high-dimensional data as genomic, transcriptomic, DNA methylation, proteomic or metabolomics sets presents both opportunities and challenges for developing robust and generalizable time-to-event models for personalized predictions and therapeutic [2].

Integrating omics data into a coherent model is still challenging because of the multicollinearity between the layers, the heterogeneity of the methods between the centers [3] when disposing of different sources of data. There are various approaches in the literature for omics data integration, encompassing supervised, semi-supervised, and unsupervised integrative analyses. These methods often employ advanced statistical techniques such as penalization approaches (e.g., LASSO, elastic net, hierarchical regression [4–6]) to handle the high dimensionality and complex relationships within omics datasets [7]. External validation is hindered by the limited availability of independent datasets that include all relevant omics layers. Addressing these challenges requires the development of advanced integration methods, standardization efforts and omics-specific validation strategies. In the context of model validation, internal and external validation strategies have to assess model performance across different settings and populations. The internal validation evaluates model performance within the development data set, and external validation have to assess generalizability to new populations [8]. Internal-external validation strategy are described in the literature validating the model in a distinct dataset [9].

The cross-validation approach for assessing predictive performance in high-dimensional settings [10], can be affected by varying levels of correlation when integrating multi-platform data, especially if the data are clustered. The challenges posed by both intra-platform and inter-platform correlations in multi-omic datasets can be addressed through model selection using sparse methods such as Lasso, and/or hierarchical integration approaches like Priority Lasso or IPF Lasso. To account for clustered data structures, stratification of cross-validation folds is a promising strategy, particularly valuable when handling time-to-event data in high-dimensional contexts. The problematic of source or cluster correlation between the samples could be deal by modifying the structure of the random cross-validation adding a clustering stratification of the folds. The Stratified cross-validation for time-to-event models addresses several key challenges in model development and validation [11]. It helps maintain the balance of event rates across folds, ensures proper handling of censored observations, and accounts for potential temporal dependencies in the data. Stratification in the cross-validation process ensures that each fold maintains a balanced representation of the internal and external datasets, reflecting their proportions within the entire dataset. This approach is particularly relevant in high-dimensional settings, where the risk of overfitting is heightened, and the need for robust validation strategies is paramount when the effect of the center have not to be measured in the statistical process. The impact of clustering based-stratification of the nested cross-validation is not described in the literature and need further consideration to improve the assessment of omics models to avoid overfitting [12].

The objective the study is to assess the performance of stratification on cross validation strategy for omics model assessment using penalized regression with application on head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC).

Methods

Implementation and analysis

All statistical analyses and figure generation were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; R Core Team, 2024, Austria). Data manipulation, survival analysis, and visualization were conducted within the R environment to ensure reproducibility and transparency. We summarized the process of the study on Fig. 1.

Data acquisition and processing

The data sources of the study were obtained from the samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/, accessed 12 october 2024) and SCANDARE head and neck cohort (NCT03017573||https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/, registration date: January 11, 2017).

From the TCGA cohort

We downloaded samples from bulk tumor tissue with the following data: RNAseq and count data expression in FPKM normalization, clinical characteristics, and survival data. The FPKM value from TCGA was converted to transcripts per kilobase and per million reads (TPM) and combined as a data matrix.

After merging the matrix of clinical data, Count data, we included a total of n = 505 complete samples in the analysis. We represented in the statistical analysis the count data in TPM excluding transcripts below 1 TPM for > = 50% of the samples and we used a logarithmic transformation of TPM with continuous values in the models.

From the SCANDARE cohort

We included a total of n = 76 complete samples in the analysis using clinical data, transcriptomic data with TPM normalization. As processed for the TCGA HNSC database we normalized the count data in TPM excluding the transcript variables when < 1 TPM for > = 50% of the samples and we used a logarithmic transformation of TPM with continuous values in the models.

Data merging

After merging the clinical and transcriptomic datasets, we retained 581 complete cases with available survival data, time to event measured in years, a non-specific event of death, and demographic variables including age (continuous, per year), biological sex (male = 1, female = 2),, tumor staging according to the 7th edition of TNM (stages 1–4) and Sublocation (Oral Cavity, Oropharynx, Pharyngo-larynx, Others). Gene expression values were integrated as log-transformed transcript per million (logTPM), providing a continuous measure of transcript abundance for each case. All samples underwent identical normalization and log-transformation protocols to minimize technical variability. We assessed two approaches to account for batch effects. The first involved a clustering approach without applying count transformation based on the negative binomial distribution. The second combined clustering with data transformation using the ComBat R package to limit batch effects between the two cohorts; both approaches were included in the validation strategies assessed. To further evaluate cohort comparability and control for batch effects, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the combined expression matrix. The top four principal components (PCs) were inspected to ensure that the major sources of transcriptomic variation did not simply reflect cohort origin (efigure 1).

Outcome

In this study, we selected overall survival (OS) as the outcome of interest for our statistical analysis. We defined OS as the time from the start of treatment until death from any cause, which was coded as event 1 when it occurred.

Clustering methods

We determined clusters of samples using dimensional reduction approaches of matrix of predictors merging the TCGA and the SCANDARE cohorts with two crossmatch approaches: (i) UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) [14] and (ii) K-means clustering [15] approaches. We determined the K-clustering trade-off using Elbow method [16] and gap statistic method [17] of factoextra R package (efigure 2). We fixed K = 3 after K-optimization to ensure a sufficient event rate within each fold. The interaction variable “cluster”/“outcome” was used to balance the folds through stratification into six levels.

Validation strategies

We proponed as an internal validation strategy a cross-validation approach [18] splitting the available sample into K subsamples of equal size. K − 1 subsamples are used as the training sample, while the remaining subsample is used as the assessment of the metrics. We introduced the nested optimization with a second partition of the sample in J subsamples of equal size in the inner loop to tune the hyper parameters of the evaluated models.

To evaluate the discriminative and calibration performance of the best model we performed a J nested k-folds-cross validation approach with and without stratification with the fixed parameters K and J:

We calculated the predictive performance of the best model using the test data at each iteration. The final discriminative and calibration performance corresponded to the mean of the performance of the best model over the outer-loop iteration. We considered as stratification factor the interaction variable between the clusters of the samples and the outcome of the study and we applied a J nested k-folds cross validation. Estimands and performance metrics

We measured the rate of non-convergence according to the cox penalized regression and the stratified and non-stratified approaches. We evaluated the quality of model optimization and sparsity by calculating the mean and standard deviation of the selected non-zero coefficients for each selection method (IPF Lasso, PL, and Lasso) under both stratified and non-stratified NCV. Pairwise comparisons were performed using a non-parametric Wilcoxon test, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

We determined a discriminative approach of the predictive performance using the Area Under the Curve receiving operating Characteristics (AUC) at a 3 years horizon using timeROC R package [19]. We considered for each dynamic analysis of AUC, a cumulative/dynamic method for calculation of AUC-time dependent [20, 21]. We determined the predictive calibration approach calculating the Brier Score at 3 years and the Integrated Brier Score using pec R package estimated with inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) [22, 23] calculating the individual survival probabilities using pec package from time 0 to 3 years for all the tested internal validation methods. We determined AUC and IBS dispersion assessment by using 100 replications of the experiments with different seed.

Where corresponded to AUC or IBS estimates obtained by outer folds estimation.

We obtained the density of probability of the outer fold AUC distribution using the gaussian function of the ggplot2 R package.

To compare the optimism of the performance assessment we used an ANOVA comparison with a p-value < 0.05 considered as statistically significant. If the ANOVA comparison was significant a post hoc pairwise Tukey comparison was conducted. To compare the stability of the performance assessment we performed homogeneity test using Bartlett test for variance comparison between the validation strategies performance according to the model selection, a p-value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant rejecting the null hypothesis of equal variances.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to enhance different implementations of nested cross-validation approach for penalized regression applied to correlated data from multiple sources, with a time-to-event endpoint in a high-dimensional setting. We demonstrated that incorporating clustering-based stratification within the nested cross-validation framework is a feasible and effective strategy. The stability of the tested nested cross-validation implementations appeared similar in this study. These implementations reduce the instability of the validation process compared to hold-out methods and limit optimism bias in both discrimination and calibration metrics.

In high-dimensional settings, a compromise must be made between partitioning the data for model development and evaluating predictive performance. Cross-validation methods have been implemented to allow the use of the data both for building the model and for assessing its predictive accuracy. When developing a model using multiple data sources, especially under conditions of limited data availability, an inadequate modeling strategy and performance evaluation approach can lead to unstable and non-reproducible results. The regularization pathway could be a source of instability in model development [24]. Internal validation of high-dimensional models remains a significant challenge and cross-validation for model prediction error assessment reduce bias error estimation [25] and particularly in high dimensional setting using time to event outcome [26, 27]. Assessing the true performance of a developed model in settings with limited sample sizes often leads to overly optimistic estimates and complicates the application of the model to external datasets. When multiple sources of correlated high-dimensional data are available, a critical trade-off arises between using a subset of the data for model development where predictor selection and coefficient estimation are inherently difficult and utilizing the entire dataset for both training and performance evaluation. This compromise requires careful consideration to ensure robust model development and reliable assessment of predictive performance. Various internal and combined internal/external validation approaches have been developed to assess model performance, including nested cross-validation [28] and hold-out validation. In the context of multi-platform integration for time-to-event outcomes, penalized Cox regression methods are particularly appropriate, as they maintain interpretability. Examples include Lasso Cox regression, IPF-Lasso, and Priority Lasso [4, 5, 29]. Additionally, other methods such as elastic net, adaptive regularization, and Bayesian approaches can be integrated to enhance performance and account for the complex correlations among features, which is especially relevant for high-dimensional data types like genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and DNA methylation datasets [30]. Bayesian methods offer a principled framework for quantifying uncertainty in parameter estimates through posterior distributions [31]. These features are particularly valuable for omics data, where complex correlations among features are prevalent and hierarchical or network-based Bayesian models can be leveraged to capture these dependencies. However, these methods often entail substantial computational complexity and require careful prior specification and tuning of hyperparameters. Despite these challenges, their ability to simultaneously perform feature selection, model prediction, and missing data handling presents a promising direction for future research in integrative multi-omics analysis. In our current study, we focused on benchmarking established regularized frequentist methods for survival prediction to provide a clear comparative framework, reserving the exploration of Bayesian hierarchical models for subsequent investigations where computational resources and data completeness allow for their effective application.The inner loop optimization of the nested-cross validation could be implemented by different model selection approach using the both of nested-cross validation. The stratification of the cross-validation folds is described to address unbalanced class covariable linked to outcome and optimistic bias in model development and assessment from electronic health records due to data leakage and duplication records [32].

When using genomic or transcriptomic data, batch effects must be taken into account, as they influence the correlation structure inherent to the original data and the variation of the experimental conditions [33, 34]. It could involve transforming the transcriptomic data using appropriate algorithms such as ComBat, or clustering the transcriptomic data matrix to use as a stratification factor for development and validation. Developing a model based on data from a single source of data with a limited sample size, without incorporating information from other sources, poses significant challenges for model validation. Our proposed approach combines nested cross-validation with penalized regression for survival prediction, enabling both model development and performance assessment. This strategy improves the quality of predictor selection and coefficient estimation while reducing instability and mitigating optimism in discrimination and calibration metrics. When disposing different replicates sample of omics platform analysis other methods as the Ratio-based scaling [35] could be performed to limit this batch effect. Additionally, clustering genomic or transcriptomic data or more straightforwardly, labelling samples by their sources/clusters provides a means to incorporate proportional contributions from all data sources during model development and to limit the inconsistency for estimation of coefficients associated to each variables.

Using this approach, we selected 35 variables in the prognostic model that demonstrated promising predictive performance in both discrimination and calibration. A recent narrative review identified 34 studies reporting prognostic gene signatures for head and neck cancer survival, primarily derived using Lasso-Cox regression [36]. These studies classified the gene signatures into key cancer-related pathways such as programmed cell death, epigenetic regulation, immune response modulation, metabolism, and resistance to cell death. Consistent with these classifications, our model included genes associated with tumor migration (ARPC4 [37]), epigenetic regulation (RBP4 [38], EIF4A3 [39], ZNF134 [40]), tumor suppressor (C5ORF66-AS1 [41]), tumor immune invasion and immune response modulation (CELSR3 [42], SLC25A39 [43], CD79A [44], MASP1 [45], CTLA4 [46], IGLV9-49 [47]), metabolism regulation (xxx), angiogenesis (EFNB2 [48]), and resistance to cell death and drug resistance (FAM83 [49], ATP6V0D1 [50], STIP1 [51], S100A10 [52]). Some genes as SLC25A51, EMC3 are described as important mitochondrial metabolic regulator with possible links to tumor biology, but their use as a prognostic biomarker in head and neck cancer remains to be confirmed. The COX4I2 (colorectal cancer), TCP11 (cervical cancer) [53], VILL, NKX2-8, RGS5, NDUFV3, TSPAN5, IGHG3 genes have been linked to anti-tumor effects in various cancers, but have not been described in head and neck cancers. Additionally, our statistical approach identified genes (PFKF, SPATA5L1, ZNF266, TMEM22, ARL9, AP1G2, KANSL1) not previously associated with head and neck cancer prognosis, which may represent either correlated pathways or potential false-positive findings.

Limits

We conducted a benchmark study utilizing three implementations of nested cross-validation, integrated with three penalized regression methods. The study design, however, does not permit the quantification of optimism bias through external validation or simulation-based conditions. Additionally, data clustering in this study was performed using UMAP and K-means approaches. While effective, alternative methods such as principal component analysis or simpler strategies such as incorporating a center variable directly into the modeling could also be considered for further exploration.

Conclusion

In high-dimensional settings with time-to-event endpoints, incorporating clustering-based stratification within nested cross-validation provides an effective compromise between robust model development and reliable assessment of predictive performance reducing the instability of the process. This approach uses clustering-informed stratified cross-validation as a validation strategy for predictive models and requires testing across diverse patient populations and data settings.
