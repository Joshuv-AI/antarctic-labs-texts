Emergency superficial temporal artery–superior cerebellar artery bypass for hyperacute basilar artery occlusion following elective middle cerebral artery aneurysm clipping: illustrative case
Authors: José Luis Andrade Valencia, Wojciech Czyżewski, Kohei Yoshikawa, Rokuya Tanikawa, Nakao Ota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDBasilar artery occlusion (BAO) is a threatening condition requiring rapid reperfusion to preserve brainstem function. Although endovascular thrombectomy is considered standard treatment, underlying pathology or limited resources may require surgical intervention. The authors performed an emergency superficial temporal artery–superior cerebellar artery (STA-SCA) bypass in the hyperacute setting of BAO following elective aneurysm surgery. The bypass prevented stroke progression and dramatically improved consciousness.LESSONSThis case demonstrates that with early recognition, protocolized monitoring, and microsurgical capability, STA-SCA bypass is a viable effective strategy for revascularization in acute posterior circulation stroke and can prevent progression of the ischemic core.OBSERVATIONSA 69-year-old man with a recent history of bilateral cerebellar infarction underwent elective microsurgical clipping of a left M1 middle cerebral artery aneurysm. The authors confirmed complete aneurysm exclusion with postoperative CTA. Eight hours later, the patient developed aphasia and deep state coma. CTA perfusion revealed BAO, with extensive cerebellar penumbra and a small core. Due to severe preexisting right vertebral and basilar intracranial atherosclerotic disease, urgent STA-SCA bypass was performed with a right subtemporal approach. Intraoperative studies confirmed graft patency reperfusion. The patient recovered consciousness and was discharged with a modified Rankin Scale score of 3. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25710

BAO accounts for less than 1% of ischemic strokes but carries mortality rates approaching 80% without timely reperfusion.1 Endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) has revolutionized BAO management; nevertheless, it may be inaccessible in resource-limited areas or rendered ineffective by unfavorable vascular anatomy, intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAS)–related stenosis, complex tandem lesions, or clot characteristics.2,3 Microsurgical bypass, once a mainstay of cerebrovascular surgery, has reemerged as a salvage option when modern endovascular techniques fail.4 We report a unique case of acute BAO with severe underlying ICAS occurring shortly after uneventful clipping of a middle cerebral aneurysm, successfully treated with an emergency right superficial temporal artery–superior cerebellar artery (STA-SCA) bypass. After research in the PubMed, Cochrane, and Google Scholar databases, we concluded that this represents the first report of such a strategy applied in the hyperacute setting after elective middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysm surgery, successfully preventing progression of ischemia in the posterior circulation.

Illustrative Case

A 69-year-old right-handed man with a history of bilateral cerebellar and right occipital infarctions secondary to ICAS and a 40-year smoking history (10 cigarettes/day), with cessation 2 years prior, underwent routine surveillance MRA, which demonstrated a 2.9 × 4.0–mm left M1 MCA aneurysm with a dome-to-neck ratio of < 2, not found in previous studies in a 2-year follow-up in consultation (Fig. 1). Digital subtraction angiography confirmed the morphology, and elective microsurgical clipping was elected to avoid coil instability and possible migration. Intraoperative monitoring and Doppler ultrasound verified complete aneurysm obliteration without parent artery compromise. Immediate postoperative CTA demonstrated intact distal flow (Fig. 2). Eight hours later, the patient abruptly developed global aphasia and a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 6. Pupils were equal and reactive; brain CT ruled out hemorrhage. CTA revealed complete thrombotic occlusion of the basilar artery extending from the midsegment to just proximal to both posterior cerebral artery (PCA) origins (Fig. 3). As diagnostic assessment tools we used CT perfusion that showed marked bilateral cerebellar hypoperfusion (Tmax > 8 seconds) with a core volume of < 10 ml and large penumbra (Fig. 4). Routine laboratory checkup and chest radiographic imaging were normal. Given the severe stenotic right vertebral artery and the absence of flow in the left vertebral artery on preoperative CTA, the decision was made for immediate surgical revascularization. The patient was placed in a classic supine position and lateral left 45° angle and fixated with a static Mayfield clamp. A right preauricular incision in posterior horseshoe fashion allowed harvest of the parietal branch of the STA (2.0-mm caliber, 10-cm length). A right classic subtemporal craniotomy was made, followed by exposition of the SCA (Fig. 4B). After temporary clipping of the recipient artery and adequate preparation of the vessel graft, an end-side anastomosis was completed with 10-0 nylon under high-magnification microscopy (Fig. 5). Intraoperative indocyanine green (ICG) video angiography, Doppler ultrasound, and postoperative CTA confirmed graft patency reperfusion (Fig. 5). The total temporary occlusion time was 30 minutes with intermittent occlusion and no changes in motor evoked potentials or somatosensory evoked potentials. Additional surgical information is included in Video 1. The CTA performed within 30 minutes of skin closure demonstrated robust flow through the bypass. The patient remained intubated under light propofol sedation. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 1; neurological examination was normal (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score 0). MRI performed on day 3 showed small infarcts in the cerebellar hemispheres with a small preoperative pontine infarct, without additional infarcts or progression of ischemia. The patient was discharged on day 12 with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 3. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of all clinical details and images. Discussion

A few hours after an uneventful anterior circulation aneurysm clipping, our patient developed aphasia and a severe decline in consciousness. Imaging confirmed BAO with underlying severe ICAS. While EVT is usually the preferred recanalization method,5,6 in cases with ICAS-related large-vessel occlusion, lower recanalization rates and higher complication risks after EVT have been reported.7 The patient’s preexisting atherosclerotic right vertebral and occluded basilar artery led us to consider a microsurgical bypass. We proceeded with an emergency right (STA-SCA) bypass to prevent progression of the ischemic core. In the literature, reports of STA-SCA bypass in a hyperacute setting of basilar occlusion are limited, and only one specific article was found in searching the PubMed, Cochrane, and Google Scholar databases, on preventing the progression of stroke.8 Interestingly enough, there is sufficient evidence on the benefits of bypass in the setting of chronic vertebrobasilar stenosis; of 50 patients who received STA-SCA bypass, 35 patients had complete resolution of symptoms, 5 deaths were reported, 3 patients were severely unstable hemodynamically, 1 case was complicated by leakage of the anastomosis, and 1 case was complicated by acute basilar thrombrosis.9 The current technology allows us to have great results with advanced microsurgery and high-resolution imaging. Other reports have described double-barrel STA-PCA and STA-SCA bypasses for large thrombosed basilar apex aneurysms, particularly in high-flow demands. These cases have demonstrated that such constructs can provide sufficient and durable flow to the posterior circulation.10 In our case, preoperative CTA clarified the relationship of arachnoid planes and veins, helping us minimize temporal lobe retraction. Although it adds operative time, this strategy can reduce retraction on the temporal lobe. A recent Operative Neurosurgery series on flow replacement strategies recommended selecting the donor vessel—STA versus occipital artery—based on the perfusion demands of the target territory.10 In our patient, CT perfusion and experience confirmed that a single STA graft could satisfy the SCA territory. For larger penumbral areas, a dual graft or larger donor vessel might be preferable. These techniques and descriptions combine flow considerations, complex anatomy, and ultra-microsurgery and are redefining cerebrovascular surgery. In 2018, 5 emergency STA-SCA bypasses in patients with refractory vertebrobasilar insufficiency were realized, achieving good 6-month outcomes in 60% of cases.11 Their findings reinforce the principle that rapid restoration of flow—when EVT is not an option—can be lifesaving. Still, in hyperacute occlusion settings this has not been reported sufficiently. A recent article reported the prevention of additional ischemic strokes in posterior circulation bypass.12 Our total bypass time—from skin incision to completed anastomosis—was 2 hours 5 minutes per video records, surpassing conventional times.11 Although EVT remains the first choice for BAO, direct comparisons to emergency bypass are lacking. Prospective trials or retrospective studies will help clarify which patients benefit most from each modality. Mortality remains high even in the scenario of BAO with ETV available, ranging from 30% to 40%, even with successful reperfusion.13–15 This case serves as an excellent example that emergency STA-SCA bypass can help salvage the ischemic penumbra and prevent stroke progression. When comparing the pre- and postoperative diffusion-weighted images, no new infarctions appeared after surgery, although the preoperative hyperintensity eventually evolved into infarction. The patient’s consciousness improved dramatically after revascularization with this technique. Similar to large-vessel occlusion stroke treated with ETV, emergency bypass revascularization is effective, and the extent of infarction depends on various factors including, but not limited to, the time of onset of ischemia, the patient’s pre-mRS status, and, largely, the patient’s collateral circulation. Looking ahead, we aim to build a collaborative registry to track emergency bypass. As mentioned, one such descriptive article has been found, which strengthens the concept of preventing further strokes and effective revascularization. Microneurosurgery is in constant evolution, such as advanced optical microscopes and vessel couplers for faster anastomosis, currently used in vein anastomosis in neck surgery and optical coherence tomography for real-time graft assessment to refine technical precision; currently, only a few articles mention this.16 We need to maintain a high index of suspicion for ICAS-related stroke complications after aneurysm clipping, especially in high-risk populations. A multidisciplinary team is recommended to remain agile in choosing between endovascular and microsurgical solutions, to interpret and utilize perfusion studies correctly, allowing guidance in critical decision-making. Most importantly, ongoing hands-on workshops and peer video reviews allow us to maintain sharp bypass skills, ensuring we can respond decisively when every minute counts. Hybrid models are the standard of care and should be implemented when possible.

Observations

Although many technologies have been developed in the endovascular route, there is an important observation to be made: not all cases are amenable to this method, be it by underlying disease or by availability of endovascular procedures. It is always important to have an arsenal of tools at our disposal to resolve the current issue in the patient. These observations have been previously reported, yet randomized large trials are difficult to develop. More information is needed, and we emphasize the need to optimize bypass protocols and sharpen microsurgical skills.

Lessons

In patients with acute posterior circulation ischemia who are not ideal candidates for EVT, an emergency STA-SCA bypass can provide rapid revascularization and prevent stroke progression. Our case demonstrates that with careful perfusion studies, strict patient care, and rigorous microsurgical technique, bypass can transform a potentially devastating stroke into an opportunity for meaningful recovery. Even as endovascular tools evolve, microsurgical bypass must remain a core skill in the cerebrovascular surgeon’s repertoire. STA-SCA bypass should be embraced as a vital reconstructive tool in the armamentarium for acute posterior circulation stroke when mechanical thrombectomy cannot be promptly realized.
