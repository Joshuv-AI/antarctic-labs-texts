Early post-traumatic avascular necrosis with secondary osteoarthritis following an acetabular fracture in a 19-year-old patient treated with total hip arthroplasty: a case report
Authors: Hamza Khaiata, Osman Desho, Ahmad Abdul Hakim Alhamid, Mostafa Sagheer, Yaman Jamal Qoudra Danial
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Acetabular fractures are complex orthopedic injuries that can lead to severe long-term complications, including avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head and secondary osteoarthritis. These sequelae may result in progressive joint destruction and may ultimately require total hip arthroplasty (THA), particularly in cases where joint preservation is no longer feasible.Case presentation:We report a case of a 19-year-old male presenting with progressive left hip pain and a restricted range of motion 1 year after sustaining a posterior column acetabular fracture associated with posterior hip dislocation. The initial injury had been treated with open reduction and internal fixation through a Kocher–Langenbeck approach. Radiographic and computed tomography imaging revealed the collapse of the femoral head with degenerative joint changes consistent with AVN and secondary osteoarthritis. The patient underwent cementless ceramic-on-ceramic THA using a direct lateral (Hardinge) approach. The postoperative course was uneventful. Even after adequate fracture reduction and fixation, disruption of femoral head vascularity may lead to progressive joint degeneration, requiring conversion to THA.Conclusion:This case highlights the potential for the early development of AVN following acetabular fractures. Careful long-term follow-up is essential, and THA remains an effective treatment for advanced post-traumatic hip degeneration, especially in young patients when modern ceramic bearing surfaces are used.

Introduction

Acetabular fractures represent some of the most challenging injuries encountered in orthopedic trauma, exhibiting a bimodal epidemiological distribution. These injuries commonly occur in younger individuals following high-energy trauma, such as motor vehicle collisions, and elderly patients sustaining low-energy mechanisms, typically in the context of osteoporotic bone[1,2]. Postoperative complications following open reduction and internal fixation of acetabular fractures remain relatively frequent. These complications include post-traumatic arthritis (PTA), cartilage degeneration (chondrolysis), and osteonecrosis of the femoral head, which may develop as delayed sequelae after osteosynthesis[3,4]. In cases associated with posterior hip dislocation, prompt reduction of the dislocated joint is essential, as early intervention significantly reduces the risk of femoral head avascular necrosis (AVN). AVN may subsequently lead to structural compromise, including femoral head fragmentation, collapse, and eventual development of secondary osteoarthritis (OA)[3].

The reported incidence of AVN following acetabular fractures ranges between 4% and 29%[5]. Several pathological conditions may necessitate conversion to total hip arthroplasty (THA), including malunion, nonunion, implant-related complications such as fixation failure or hardware cutout, osteonecrosis, and progressive degenerative joint disease[6]. Giannoudis et al[7] reported that approximately 9% of patients with acetabular fractures ultimately required conversion to THA at a mean interval of 2 years following the index procedure. However, THA in young patients remains a clinical concern due to inferior long-term implant survivorship compared with older populations, primarily attributable to increased wear rates and a higher risk of aseptic loosening. The introduction of ceramic-on-ceramic bearing surfaces has demonstrated promising outcomes, with reduced wear and lower osteolysis rates, particularly in younger and more active individuals[8].

Although AVN following acetabular fractures has been widely reported, early progression to symptomatic secondary OA requiring THA in young patients remains uncommon. This case highlights the rapid progression of femoral head osteonecrosis within 1 year after acetabular fracture fixation in a 19-year-old patient, emphasizing the challenges of managing post-traumatic hip degeneration in young individuals in accordance with the SCARE guidelines for surgical case reporting[9].

Case presentation

A 19-year-old male presented to the orthopedic outpatient clinic with complaints of left hip pain and a restricted range of motion. His medical history was significant for a posterior column acetabular fracture associated with posterior hip dislocation. The injury occurred following a high-energy road traffic accident. The dislocation had been previously reduced, and surgical fixation was performed through a Kocher–Langenbeck approach using reconstruction plates. The injury occurred approximately 1 year before presentation. The patient had no comorbidities, no prior medical conditions, and a negative family history. On clinical examination, there was marked limitation of hip flexion and internal rotation, in addition to restricted motion in other planes. A limb length discrepancy of approximately 2.2 cm was noted. Plain radiography of the pelvis demonstrated joint space narrowing with degenerative changes and collapse of the femoral head (as shown in Fig. 1). A computed tomography scan confirmed significant joint space narrowing and well-demarcated erosive changes at the superolateral aspect of the femoral head (as shown in Fig. 2). A diagnosis of secondary OA of the femoral head due to AVN was established. Surgical management with left THA was planned. The patient was prepared for surgery and received prophylactic vancomycin 1 hour preoperatively. A direct lateral (Hardinge) approach was performed. The gluteus medius muscle was identified and retracted, and the joint capsule was incised, and the remaining femoral head was excised. A cementless ceramic-on-ceramic total hip prosthesis was implanted. Intraoperative assessment confirmed excellent stability and complete restoration of range of motion. Postoperatively, the patient received vancomycin and amikacin, in addition to thromboprophylaxis with enoxaparin, later transitioned to oral rivaroxaban. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 3 in excellent condition with a normal neurovascular examination. Postoperative rehabilitation included supervised physiotherapy focusing on range of motion and progressive weight-bearing. Full weight-bearing was permitted 1 month after surgery. The patient was followed clinically at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months postoperatively. At the 90-day follow-up, clinical examination revealed no postoperative complications. The patient demonstrated favorable functional recovery with a Harris Hip Score of 92, indicating good postoperative hip function. Follow-up radiographs showed a well-positioned prosthesis without evidence of complications (as shown in Fig. 3). Following THA, the patient experienced marked pain relief and substantial improvement in hip function. At follow-up, he reported satisfaction with the surgical outcome and was able to resume normal daily activities without discomfort or limping (Table 1). Discussion

Acetabular fractures are severe injuries that frequently result from high-energy trauma in young individuals and may lead to significant long-term complications affecting hip joint function. Despite advances in surgical fixation techniques, post-traumatic sequelae such as PTA, chondrolysis, and AVN of the femoral head remain major concerns after acetabular fracture management[3,4]. These complications are often related to the initial trauma severity, vascular disruption of the femoral head, associated hip dislocation, and the quality of fracture reduction. Posterior hip dislocation associated with acetabular fractures is considered a significant risk factor for the development of femoral head osteonecrosis. Early reduction of the dislocated hip is crucial to restore blood supply to the femoral head and reduce the likelihood of AVN. Nevertheless, even with timely reduction and appropriate surgical fixation, disruption of the vascular supply may occur, ultimately leading to femoral head collapse and progressive secondary OA[3].

The incidence of AVN following acetabular fractures has been reported to range between 4% and 29%, highlighting the variability of this complication depending on injury severity and management strategies[5]. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies. Morison et al[2] demonstrated increased complication rates and lower implant survivorship following THA after acetabular fractures compared with primary arthroplasty. In contrast, our patient demonstrated favorable short-term outcomes despite prior fixation and young age. Secondary OA following acetabular fractures represents one of the most frequent indications for conversion to THA. THA remains one of the most successful surgical procedures for the treatment of hip joint disease, consistently achieving substantial improvements in pain, functional mobility, and patient quality of life[10]. Previous studies have demonstrated that post-traumatic degenerative changes may develop months to years after the initial injury due to altered hip biomechanics, cartilage damage, and residual joint incongruity[1].

In a systematic review evaluating THA following acetabular fractures, patients commonly required arthroplasty due to complications, including osteonecrosis, PTA, implant failure, or malunion of the acetabular fracture[4]. Similarly, conversion to THA has been reported in approximately 9% of patients after operative treatment of acetabular fractures, typically occurring within the first few years following the index procedure[7]. Performing THA after acetabular fracture fixation presents several surgical challenges. Previous surgical scars, retained hardware, distorted anatomy, and potential bone defects can complicate the procedure and increase the risk of complications compared with primary THA performed for degenerative OA. Morison et al. reported that THA performed after acetabular fractures is associated with higher complication rates and lower implant survivorship when compared with primary arthroplasty procedures[2]. Therefore, careful preoperative planning and appropriate implant selection are essential to achieve optimal outcomes. The choice of bearing surfaces is particularly important in young patients requiring hip arthroplasty, as their higher activity levels may accelerate implant wear and loosening. Ceramic-on-ceramic bearing surfaces have been introduced to address these concerns due to their excellent wear resistance and reduced osteolysis rates. THA in patients under 50 years provides favorable functional outcomes and reliable implant survival with low complication and revision rates. The use of cementless fixation and modern bearing surfaces, particularly ceramic-on-ceramic articulations, improves durability in young and active patients, supporting THA as an effective option for hip reconstruction in this population[11].

In the present case, a cementless ceramic-on-ceramic prosthesis was selected in order to maximize implant longevity and reduce wear-related complications. Our patient developed symptomatic AVN with secondary OA 1 year after sustaining a posterior column acetabular fracture with an associated posterior hip dislocation. This relatively early progression highlights the severity of vascular compromise that may occur despite initial surgical management. The patient underwent cementless ceramic-on-ceramic THA with satisfactory short-term clinical and radiological outcomes. At the 90-day follow-up, the patient demonstrated excellent functional recovery with a Harris Hip Score of 92, indicating good postoperative hip function. From a clinical perspective, this case emphasizes the importance of long-term surveillance following acetabular fracture fixation, particularly in patients with associated hip dislocation. Early identification of femoral head osteonecrosis may facilitate timely intervention before severe joint destruction occurs. In cases of advanced degeneration, THA remains a reliable salvage procedure capable of restoring pain-free function even in young patients. This case report has several strengths. It describes early post-traumatic AVN in a very young patient and provides short-term functional outcomes using the Harris Hip Score. However, this report also has limitations. As a single case report, long-term implant survivorship cannot be evaluated, and the findings may not be generalizable. Further studies with longer follow-up are required.

Conclusion

Post-traumatic AVN of the femoral head remains a serious complication following acetabular fractures, particularly when associated with posterior hip dislocation. Even when appropriate fracture reduction and fixation are achieved, vascular compromise may still lead to progressive femoral head collapse and secondary OA. This case illustrates that symptomatic joint degeneration may develop relatively early after the initial injury, especially in young patients with severe trauma. THA represents an effective treatment option for advanced post-traumatic hip joint destruction when joint-preserving strategies are no longer feasible. In young and active individuals, the use of cementless ceramic-on-ceramic bearing surfaces may provide improved durability and reduced wear, thereby enhancing long-term implant performance. Careful patient selection, meticulous surgical planning, and appropriate implant choice are essential to achieve favorable outcomes. This report demonstrates the importance of long-term surveillance after acetabular fracture fixation, particularly in young patients at risk of early femoral head osteonecrosis.
