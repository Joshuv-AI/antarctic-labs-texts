Multiobjective optimization of natural convection and entropy generation in a porous wavy cavity with a heated star-shaped obstacle using a hybrid nanofluid
Authors: Khalid B Saleem, Muneer A Ismael, Kothuru Venkatadri, Ahmed Mir, Walid Hassen, Lioua Kolsi, Kaouther Ghachem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConvectional heat transfer continues to receive extensive research attention due to its importance in vital applications such as solar energy collectors and cooling of nuclear reactors. This research investigates natural convection and associated entropy generation within a porous wavy enclosure with a star-shaped hot cylinder. The fluid saturated in the porous medium is assumed to be a water-Cu-Al2O3 hybrid nanofluid. The energy balance equation accounts for thermal non-equilibrium between the hybrid nanofluid and the local structure of the porous medium (LTNE). The effects of the number of lobes of the star-shaped cylinder (N), the Rayliegh number (Ra), the Darcy number (Da), the porosity of the medium (ε), and the volume fraction of the different nanoparticles (φAl2O3 and φCu) are inspected using numerical FEM analysis and optimized by the aid of artificial neural network (ANN). The results show that it is not possible to increase the Nusselt number without increasing entropy, and it is not possible to decrease entropy without decreasing heat transfer. The objective function (OBF), defined as the ratio of the Nusselt number to the total entropy generation, indicates that the best design is achieved with low obstacle waviness and low nanoparticle loading. The number of lobes and the nanoparticle volume fraction often increase entropy faster than they improve heat transfer. The maximum OBF = 3.877 (Nuavg = 38.705, ST = 9.9824) occurs at N = 1, φAl2O3 = 0, φCu = 0, Ra = 103, Da = 10−4, ε = 0.1. This study demonstrates the advantages of using artificial neural networks (ANN) to optimize the design of heat exchangers filled with nanofluids. This approach minimizes losses caused by thermal and flow irreversibility, thereby contributing to energy savings.Keywords: Optimum natural convection, ANN, Entropy generation, LTNE, Star obstacle, Wavy cavity

Introduction

Natural convective heat transport in enclosures is an interesting area in computational fluid mechanics and has numerous applications in science and engineering, including electronics cooling, nuclear reactors, solar energy collectors, and geothermal reservoirs. Moreover, the porous medium is a primary means of improving convective heat transfer, given its high surface area and its efficiency in storing and transporting heat. The buoyancy-induced flows generated when a porous medium saturated with a fluid, exposed to a temperature gradient can be described by the Darcy, Brinkman, and Forchheimer equations, which are based on permeability [1–3].

In low-permeability media, Darcy’s law can describe the pressure drops. However, when the flow velocity increases or the permeability rises, the viscous shear effects (Brinkman term) and inertial resistance (Forchheimer term) become essential and require more complex formulations [2, 3]. The phenomena of such flows are dominated by dimensionless numbers such as the Rayleigh number (Ra), which is a measure that relates to buoyancy forces; the Darcy number (Da), which is a measure of permeability; and the porosity (ε), which is indicative of the structure. The transport phenomenon is also marked by the Prandtl number (Pr), and for nanoparticle suspensions, the solid volume fraction (ϕ) also affects it. Geometric complexities completely change the nature of convective flow structures. Wavy walls, or undulated walls, compared with flat walls, cause greater separation and thus increase recirculation and mass transfer due to enhanced mixing [4–7]. Such configurations are, in fact, more representative of real-life systems, such as microchannels, rock layers encountered in geothermal systems, and wavy insulation panels. In a porous setting, waviness influences not only the velocity profile but also the effective thermal conductivity, as both the interface area increases and local temperature gradients change [8, 9].

Furthermore, the presence of an internal obstacle alters the temperature field, flow patterns, and entropy generation. Most existing studies have been conducted on rectangular obstacles, such as circular, square, and elliptical shapes. The main purpose of these studies is to create symmetry and simplified boundary conditions [10–12]. However, biological implants, or embedded devices often impose obstacles in practical scenarios and are usually irregularly shaped. These irregularities result in non-uniform flow separation, complex vortex shedding, and localized thermal hotspots, and thus require detailed investigation [13–15]. Further applications to improve the thermal conductivity include the use of nanofluids. Nanofluids are materials in which nanoparticles are added to the base fluid. Adding nanoparticles such as Al2O3, CuO, and TiO2 can significantly enhance energy transport properties due to Brownian motion, thermophoresis, and microconvection [16–18]. Hybrid nanofluids comprising two or more types of nanoparticles have demonstrated synergistic effects, thereby improving the performance of conventional nanofluids in terms of heat transfer [19–21]. Experiments have demonstrated the dominance of hybrid nanofluids in complex enclosures, whether in combination with porous media or magnetic fields [22–25].

Some recent studies considered various combinations of porous media, nanofluids, magnetic and/or entropy analysis, and wavy enclosures. These include studies by Kolsi et al. [26] and Rashad et al. [27], which investigated convective transport in porous enclosures filled with hybrid nanofluids under magnetic fields. Also considered were double-diffusive convection and inner rotating blocks within irregular enclosures by Armaghani et al. [28], and by Hamid et al. [29]. Afifi et al. [30] conducted an entropy generation analysis of nanofluid-saturated porous enclosures around cylinders and found that entropy generation due to heat transfer increases near the corners of the obstacles. Despite these important findings, there exists little understanding of natural convection in wavy enclosures containing porous media and irregular body obstructions within the cavity. The complex interactions among wall undulations, resistance due to the porous nature, and the body configuration make it a quasi-nonlinear, multi-scale heat transfer problem. Unsteady mixed convection in an inclined porous media was considered by Munir and Turabi [31] to study the effects of inclination and interaction between nanofluid, while the effects of the undulated walls were considered by Majdi et al. [32].

Artificial neural networks (ANN) has been applied to analyzed natural convection heat transfer in numerous enclosures utilizing various hybrid nanofluids by several studies. Aktary et al. [33] investigated the natural convection heat transfer in a partially heated trapezoidal containing Cu-MgO-H2O hybrid nanofluid with utilizing finite element method (FEM), response surface methodology (RSM), and ANN. Through the assistance of a neural network, Islam et al. [34] carried out a study of magneto-hydrodynamic Sisko nanofluid flow which is a blood-based magnetic suspension over an inclined stretching surface under the effects of non-uniform heat generation and thermophoretic effects. Ali et al. [35] simulated electroosmotic flow of a ternary hybrid Sutterby nanofluid with microorganisms, that includes porous media, nonlinear radiation and heat effects. Numerically and with the help of ANN, they obtained the heat transfer performance and the effect of parameters in advanced microfluidic and thermal systems. Rana et al. [36] applied an AI-driven ANN to study chaotic transport in periodic electroosmotic flow of Sutterby ternary nanofluids with oxytactic microbes. More recent papers related to ANN and hybrid nanofluids can be found in [37–44].

In general, recent studies have assumed constant obstacle geometry and failed to investigate the number of lobes in non-circular obstacles. Furthermore, these studies lacked a clear optimization algorithm for their suggested designs.

Despite the extensive literature on buoyancy-driven flow in enclosures, the coupled influence of wavy sidewalls, porous-medium resistance, hybrid nanofluid, and an irregular heated internal obstacle remains insufficiently understood. To address this gap, the present work numerically examines the steady laminar natural convection and entropy generation in a two-dimensional wavy-walled cavity saturated with a homogeneous porous matrix and filled with a Cu–Al2O3/water hybrid nanofluid, while incorporating an irregular star-shaped wavy heated obstacle. The best design is to be attained by using the artificial neural network (ANN) algorithm, enabling less computational time in future design [45]. The flow and thermal fields are modeled using the Darcy-Brinkman formulation under the Boussinesq approximation, with local thermal non-equilibrium (LTNE) considered between the fluid and the porous solid matrix. The main objectives are to quantify the individual and combined effects of the Rayleigh number (Ra), Darcy number (Da), porosity (ε), obstacle shape factor (N), and hybrid nanoparticle volume fraction (φ) on flow structure, heat transfer (local/average Nusselt number), and thermodynamic irreversibility (entropy generation and Bejan number), and to provide practical guidance for designing compact porous thermal systems with complex boundaries and embedded heat sources. The star-shaped obstacle with multiple lobes and the wavy complex wall act as protruding mixing generators, increasing the heat exchange area. Furthermore, hybrid nanofluids allow for the integration of low-cost and high-cost nanoparticles in small weight ratios, achieving superior thermodynamic properties unattainable with a single type of nanofluid.

Physical description

The two-dimensional natural convection porous enclosure with height (2L) and horizontal walls (L). The vertical walls are wavy with an amplitude A following and . The center of the enclosure contains a star-shaped obstacle, with several lobes N, as shown in Fig. 1. The star-shaped obstacle is centered at (L/2,L) with a formula , where . The hybrid nanofluid Cu-Al2O3 is occupying the enclosure with water as base fluid. Cu nanoparticles possess superior thermal conductivity but are costly. Alumina (Al2O3) is necessary to prevent oxidation when these particles are suspended in water. Consequently, Cu nanoparticles can be used in smaller quantities with a reduced risk of oxidation. The inner baffle has a hot temperature (Th), which is placed inside the cavity as the heat source. The top and bottom horizontal walls are insulated, while the wavy walls are maintained at the cold temperature (Tc). Since the working fluid is liquid inside a closed enclosure, hence the incompressibility assumption can safely be applied. This liquid circulates by with the Boussinesq approximation, where the maximal Rayleigh number is limited to 106; therefore, the flow is within the laminar steady-state free convection regime. Free convection within a closed space, characterized by stationary walls, no fluid pumping, and constant thermal conditions, supports the steady-state assumption. A moderate Rayleigh number limits the temperature difference, allowing radiation to be safely neglected. Given the significant length of the cavity, variations along this dimension are negligible, justifying a 2D approximation. The LTNE is a heat transfer process that occurs when the thermal conductivities of the solid matrix and the fluid within a porous medium differ significantly. Given that the thermal conductivity of a nanofluid can reach high levels, the LTNE assumption becomes plausible, even when the solid matrix temperature is not represented separately. The energy balance equation accounts for thermal non-equilibrium between the hybrid nanofluid and the local structure of the porous medium (LTNE).

Results and discussion

Figure 7 presents the isotherms for various Ra at N = 5, Da = 0.1, ε = 0.9, and φAl2O3 = φCu = 2.5%. It illustrates a clear progression from a conduction-dominated regime at low Ra to a convection-dominated regime at high Ra. At Ra = 103, heat transfer is primarily by conduction, so the isotherms remain largely stratified and nearly parallel to the heated and cooled boundaries. The distortion of the thermal field is minimal; this indicates a weak buoyancy. For 104, a slight bend in the isotherm is caused by the convection. By Ra = 105, the isotherms become deformed due to the buoyancy-driven circulation; in fact, the convective transport becomes significant. The thermal boundary layers close to the hot surfaces become thinner with the increase of (due to stronger buoyant pull), and the originally smooth temperature gradients give way to more distorted thermal layers. At Ra = 106, the flow becomes fully dominated by convection with highly distorted isotherms that are clustered near the hot surfaces, leading to the formation of plume-like structures rising upward from the top of the heated obstacle and along the undulating hot wall.

Isotherms contours for various Ra numbers with N = 5, Da = 0.1, ε = 0.9, φAl2O3 = 2.5%, φCu = 2.5%

Figure 8 presents the stream function contours for Ra = 103, 104, 105, and 106 in the wavy-walled porous cavity with the star-shaped heated obstacle (N = 5 lobes, Da = 0.1, ε = 0.9) filled with the 2.5% Al2O3 + 2.5% Cu hybrid nanofluid. The increase of Ra makes the flow to evolve from a near-stagnant conduction-dominated state at Ra = 103 to a vigorous convection-driven regime at Ra = 106. At Ra = 103, the buoyancy is weak and cannot overcome the viscous and Darcy resistance, so fluid motion is minimal and characterized by very weak symmetric recirculation around the heated star. In this case, the heat transfer is dominated by the conduction regime, characterized by almost undisturbed, concentric isotherm contours as presented in Fig. 7a. By Ra = 104, convection begins to manifest with the apparition of a stronger symmetric circulation cell, and the stream function magnitude also rises. However, the overall circulation at Ra = 104 remains moderate, and the flow is largely confined to the vicinity of the obstacle. At Ra = 105, natural convection becomes the dominant transport mechanism. The buoyant forces now generate robust, large-scale circulation loops that fill the cavity, forming two major counter-rotating vortices on either side of the star-shaped obstacle. These vortices are much stronger and extend closer to the wavy walls, with fluid rising along the hot star and descending along the cooled undulating walls. The streamlines at Ra = 105 cling to the obstacle surface and the wavy boundaries, signaling the development of thin boundary layers and high-velocity flow near those surfaces. The flow structure is well-organized and symmetric, and the strengthening of circulation at this stage coincides with the distortion of isotherms into a plume-like pattern in Fig. 7c (i.e., isotherms are no longer concentric but pulled upward above the hot star). At the highest Ra = 106, the convective motion becomes very vigorous, with drastically increased vortex strength and flow intensity. The primary vortex pair now occupies almost the entire cavity, and the streamlines are tightly packed near the star and along the wavy walls, indicating extremely thin thermal and momentum boundary layers. The flow remains symmetric, but small secondary eddies appear in the flow field. For example, recirculation zones form in the troughs of the wavy walls and near the sharp tips of the star obstacle, caused by flow separation around these geometric irregularities under strong convection. This fully developed convective regime corresponds to the highly distorted isotherms in Fig. 7d, where the thermal field is marked by steep vertical temperature gradients and plume-like heat transport.

Stream function contours for various Ra numbers with N = 5, Da = 0.1, ε = 0.9, φAl2O3 = 2.5%, φCu = 2.5%

Figure 9 depicts the isotherms in the wavy-walled porous cavity containing a heated star-shaped obstacle, for different Darcy numbers. All cases are at a high Rayleigh number (Ra = 106) with ε = 0.9 and a 5% hybrid nanofluid (Al2O3 2.5% + Cu 2.5%). At the lowest Darcy number (Da = 10−4, the isotherms remain mostly undistorted and symmetric around the heated star-shaped obstacle. The temperature contours are relatively smooth and evenly spaced, indicating that heat transfer is conduction-driven. In fact, despite the large buoyancy force (Ra = 106), the high flow resistance of the porous matrix suppresses fluid motion, so the thermal plume cannot develop freely. As a result, the hot obstacle’s influence is confined to a local region: isotherm lines wrap concentrically around the star with minimal tilt, and the thermal gradient is distributed broadly around the obstacle instead of forming sharp layers. At Da = 10−4, the porous medium acts like an insulator, so buoyant convection plays a negligible role. At Da = 10−3, noticeable convective effects are introduced. As the medium becomes slightly more permeable, buoyant fluid can start to circulate, and, accordingly, the isotherm contours begin to deviate from the purely conductive pattern. The once-uniform, symmetric thermal field now shows asymmetry and bending of isotherms, especially above the heated star: warmer isotherm lines bow upward, hinting at the onset of a rising thermal plume. However, at Da = 10−3 the convective flow is still relatively weak, so the heat transport remains partly diffusion-dominant. The isotherms are no longer parallel or concentric, and they are stretched and drawn upward over the obstacle without exhibiting extreme distortion. This intermediate regime corresponds to a mixed conduction–convection mode, where the fluid’s ability to carry heat has improved but not to the point of completely overtaking conduction. In other words, some thermal stratification persists, but buoyancy-driven circulation is beginning to reorganize the temperature field. At Da = 10−2, a clear transition to convection-dominated heat transfer is observed. The higher permeability allows vigorous fluid motion, so the buoyant hot fluid from the star-shaped heater can rise substantially before being cooled at the cavity boundaries. The isotherm contours are now highly distorted, no longer horizontal or evenly spaced, but drawn into an upwelling shape. We see the formation of a pronounced thermal plume above the heated obstacle: tightly packed isotherm lines lift upward from the star, indicating a column of warmer fluid rising toward the top of the cavity. Compared to low Da values, the temperature gradients in this case are steeper and more localized. The nearly parallel isotherms seen at low Da become strongly non-parallel and curved at higher permeability, due to the dominance of convective transport. In the highest-permeability case (Da = 10−1), convection becomes fully dominant, with only minor resistance, enabling intense circulation loops. The isotherm contours are highly irregular and non-uniform, with significant bending and clustering in certain regions. A hot plume rises directly above the star-shaped obstacle. Near the plume core, the temperature is relatively uniform, but along the plume’s edges and near solid surfaces, the isotherms bunch tightly together. This indicates sharp temperature gradients localized in the boundary layers.

Isotherms contours for various Darcy numbers with N = 5, Ra = 106, ε = 0.9, φAl2O3 = 2.5%, φCu = 2.5%

Figure 10 illustrates the flow structure for different Da values. At Da = 10−4, the porous drag heavily damps the circulation; only a weak, localized vortex forms around the heated star-shaped obstacle, and the rest of the cavity remains almost stagnant with heat spreading mainly by conduction. For Da = 10−3, the flow begins to penetrate further into the cavity, and the vortical region grows slightly. For Da = 10−2, the reduction in resistance leads to a stronger convective flow, and the stream function magnitude increases. At the highest permeability (Da = 10−1), the porous medium offers minimal resistance, and the flow resembles that in a clear fluid: vigorous, symmetric circulation cells fill the enclosure and closely hug the wavy walls and the heated star, creating pronounced shear near both the obstacle and the boundaries. Throughout this progression, the flow maintains symmetry about the vertical mid-plane (due to the centered heating and geometry), but the vortices expand from small obstacle-bound cells at low Da into large, cavity-filling loops at high Da, moving closer to the cold boundaries as permeability increases. These flow developments directly influence the thermal patterns observed in Fig. 9. In the low-permeability case (Da = 10 − 4), the isotherms remain relatively undisturbed and nearly concentric around the star (conduction-dominated heat transfer) with a thick thermal boundary layer around the obstacle. In contrast, at Da = 10−1, the isotherm contours are strongly distorted by convection and cluster tightly near the hot star surface and along the wavy walls, indicating the formation of thin thermal boundary layers and steep temperature gradients due to vigorous convective cooling. The transition to the convection-dominated regime enhances the heat transfer from the internal heater.

Figure 11 illustrates the isotherm contours in the wavy-walled porous cavity for porosity values ε = 0.1, 0.4, 0.7, and 0.9 (with Ra = 106, Da = 0.1, N = 5, and 2.5% Al2O3 + 2.5% Cu hybrid nanofluid). At the lowest porosity (ε = 0.1), the porous matrix greatly restricts fluid motion, so heat transfer is largely conduction-dominated. The temperature field remains relatively stratified, with only mild isotherm distortion, and a very weak buoyant plume above the heated star-shaped obstacle. As porosity increases to 0.4 and 0.7, the reduced resistance to flow allows stronger natural convection: the isotherms become increasingly distorted as buoyant hot fluid rises, and thermal stratification grows more pronounced. A discernible thermal plume begins to form above the star, carrying heat upward. At the highest porosity (ε = 0.9), the cavity approaches near-clear-fluid behavior, with vigorous convection dominating heat transport, resulting in highly deformed isotherm contours and a well-developed hot plume reaching the top wavy wall. The hottest fluid concentrates beneath the top boundary (indicating a stable horizontal thermal layer there), and strong temperature gradients localize both around the obstacle and along the upper wall where the plume impinges, highlighting those regions as the primary sites of heat transfer.

Isotherms contours for various porosity numbers with N = 5, Ra = 106, Da = 0.1, φAl2O3 = 2.5%, φCu = 2.5%

Figure 12 illustrates the stream function contours for four porosity values: ε = 0.1, 0.4, 0.7, and 0.9 (with Ra = 106, Da = 0.1, N = 5, and 2.5% Al2O3–Cu each). At the lowest porosity (ε = 0.1, Fig. 12a), the fluid motion is highly damped by the porous matrix, with only a weak circulation forming around the heated star. At the highest porosity (ε = 0.9, Fig. 12d), the cavity behaves almost like a clear fluid. The porous resistance is negligible, and strong buoyant convection develops: the streamlines form large, vigorous vortex cells filling most of the enclosure. In this ε = 0.9 case, a robust twin-vortex structure appears, with two counter-rotating cells symmetrically wrapping around the hot star and circulating fluid from the hot core to the cooler periphery. The vortex intensity, as reflected by the tighter, denser streamlines and higher stream function values, is much greater at high porosity, confirming that the flow strength grows as the medium becomes more permeable. Intermediate porosities (ε = 0.4 and 0.7, Fig. 12b–c) show transitional behavior: as ε increases, the previously weak cells expand and strengthen. For ε = 0.4, a small central vortex is present but still limited in reach; by ε = 0.7, the flow has formed a much larger circulation that begins to span the cavity. Throughout this progression, the flow-field symmetry about the vertical mid-plane becomes more pronounced, and the left and right recirculation patterns equalize in size and strength at higher ε. At ε = 0.9, the stream function contours on either side of the star are nearly mirror images, indicating a fully developed, symmetric convection roll pair. By contrast, at ε = 0.1, the flow is so weak that the notion of large-scale symmetry is almost moot; the contour lines there suggest only a very slight upward buoyant motion, and any asymmetry is suppressed by dominant thermal diffusion. Overall, increasing the porosity clearly enhances circulation and restores the symmetric, two-cell convective structure characteristic of stronger natural convection in this symmetric cavity.

Figure 17 (at N = 3, Ra = 106, ε = 0.7, and φCu = 2.5%) shows that increasing the Al2O3 nanoparticle volume fraction (φAl2O3) leads to a steady decrease in the average Nusselt number (Nuavg) across all Darcy numbers (Da = 10−4 to 10−1). About 2.3% and 1.7 reduction of Nuavg when raising φAl2O3 from 0 to 2.5% at Darcy’s numbers 10−3 and 10−2, respectively. For higher concentrations of Al2O3 nanoparticles, the convective heat transfer efficiency reduces. As a result, even though the nanofluid becomes more thermally conductive, the buoyant flow circulation is weakened at higher φAl2O3, and thus the overall convective heat transfer drops with increasing nanoparticle loading for every permeability level considered. This observation was also reported by Chamkha and Ismael [2] and Redouane et al. [51], where this observation can be attributed to that at high Rayleigh numbers, where buoyancy forces are significant, an increase in viscous forces, represented by increasing the nanoparticles fraction, can counteract the strong buoyancy-driven flow. This increased viscous resistance can overcome the positive effect of enhanced thermal conductivity, leading to a reduction in the Nusselt number. Meanwhile, the total entropy generation STOT increases as φAl2O3 rises, and the Bejan number (Be) remains high (close to 1). The rise in ST with nanoparticle fraction indicates that although heat transfer is enhanced, the system incurs greater thermodynamic irreversibilities at higher φAl2O3. This is because the strengthened conductive heat transfer allows more thermal energy to flow for a given temperature difference, producing more entropy via heat transfer (thermal irreversibility). At the same time, the nanoparticle-induced suppression of flow strength tends to reduce entropy production from fluid friction, so heat transfer effects overwhelmingly dominate the entropy generation. About 4.6% and 5.3 increase of STOT when raising φAl2O3 from 0 to 2.5% at Darcy’s numbers 10−3 and 10−2 Accordingly, the Bejan number stays very high and even slightly increases with φAl2O3, confirming that the contribution of viscous dissipation is negligible when compared to thermal entropy generation.

Figure 18 presents the influence of the Cu nanoparticle volume fraction (φCu) on Nuavg, STOT, and Be for Da ranging from 10−4 to 10−1 (with N = 3, Ra = 106, φAl2O3 = 0.025, and ε = 0.7). Across all Darcy numbers, increasing φCu produces a clear decrease in Nuavg (Fig. 18a). In other words, a higher loading of Cu nanoparticles consistently weakens the convective heat transfer effectiveness. As a result, the heat transfer shifts toward a conduction-dominated regime, and the convective enhancement is reduced. Consequently, Nuavg declines with increasing φCu at every Darcy number. Even at the highest Darcy (Da = 10−1), where the porous medium offers the least resistance to flow, augmenting φCu dampens the buoyancy-driven circulation enough to lower Nuavg significantly because the domination of the viscous force. At the lowest Darcy (Da = 10−4, nearly conduction-dominated already), Nuavg is inherently small and becomes only marginally smaller with added Cu, reflecting that convection is minimal in this limit.

Effect of φCu and Da on a Nuavg; b STOT; c Be for N = 3, Ra = 106, φAl2O3 = 0.025 and ε = 0.7

STOT increases with φCu for all Da values (Fig. 18b), indicating that higher nanoparticle fractions boost the irreversibility. It is worth noting that even as convective heat transfer diminishes at high φCu, the system still transports a substantial amount of thermal energy (now more via conduction), and the irreversibility associated with that heat transport manifests as higher STOT. Despite these changes, the Bejan number (Be) remains very high across the entire range of φCu and Da (Fig. 18c). The Bejan number near 1.0 indicates that thermal entropy generation dominates fluid friction entropy generation in this system. In other words, almost all entropy production results from heat transfer, with negligible contributions from viscous dissipation. This is expected, as the porous medium and moderate Rayleigh number limit flow intensity.

Figure 19 shows that Nuavg decreases with N for all porosity values from ε = 0.1 to 0.9. Lower N allows buoyant flow to develop more freely and carry heat effectively. The increased wetted surface from extra lobes comes with higher frictional resistance and flow separation, which trap fluid in eddies and stagnant pockets instead of promoting efficient heat transport. Consequently, Nuavg is highest at the lowest N and decreases as N increases (where at N = 5, the Nuavg is 19.8% and 21% lower than that at N = 1 for ε = 0.1 and 0.7, respectively). This behavior is observed across all porosities (ε = 0.1–0.9): even though a more porous medium (higher ε) generally permits stronger convection, any gains are eroded by increasing the obstacle’s lobe count. At the highest porosity (ε = 0.9), the base convective flow is vigorous, but adding lobes significantly curtails it; at the lowest porosity (ε = 0.1), the flow is already weak (dominated by conduction through the porous matrix), and additional obstacle roughness further stifles what little convection remains. For example, at N = 5, the Nuavg decreases about 20.3% when ε is reduced from 0.7 to 0.1. Thus, greater obstacle waviness consistently undermines heat transfer by making the flow path more tortuous and dissipative. For entropy generation, at fixed porosity, increasing N adds more surface-induced viscous dissipation, thereby boosting STOT through additional fluid friction irreversibility. But the figure shows a critical number of lobes, indicated at N = 3, where the entropy is maximum. Likewise, at a given obstacle geometry, a higher porosity allows more fluid motion; this stronger flow produces greater entropy generation overall, since both thermal mixing and viscous losses grow with convective vigor. For example, the STOT of a medium having ε = 0.1 is lower than that of ε = 0.7 by 7.1%. In contrast, in a very low-porosity medium, flow is severely damped, and hence the convective carrying fluid contributes little, and heat transfer approaches pure conduction, so STOT remains relatively low and almost insensitive to N in that limit. As the medium becomes more permeable, however, obstacle geometry and porous resistance interplay to govern irreversibilities: the highest STOT is encountered in cases combining a high porosity with a high N, where an abundance of fluid flow (due to low resistance) is simultaneously subjected to intense frictional disruptions (due to obstacle waviness). Meanwhile, Be decreases with increasing N and ε.

Added value of the ANN surrogate compared with CFD-only analysis

Although the CFD/FEM model provides the high-fidelity numerical data and resolves the detailed flow, temperature, and entropy-generation fields, a CFD-only approach becomes computationally expensive when large parametric sweeps or optimization studies are required. Therefore, the ANN was introduced as a fast surrogate model trained on the CFD-generated database. Once trained and validated, the ANN provides almost instantaneous predictions of , , and Be for any combination of input parameters within the investigated domain. This enables rapid response-curve generation, sensitivity analysis, and optimization without repeating the full numerical simulation for every new design point. Hence, the ANN complements the CFD analysis by transforming the discrete numerical database into a reusable predictive correlation suitable for design screening and multi-objective optimization. It should be emphasized that the ANN is used only for interpolation within the trained parameter ranges, while CFD remains the reference model for physical field resolution and final validation of selected optimal cases.

Conclusions

Free convective heat transfer is investigated using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and artificial neural networks (ANN) in a wavy porous cavity containing a star-shaped heated cylinder saturated with a water-Cu-Al2O3 hybrid nanofluid. The associated entropy generation is also assessed, and local thermal non-equilibrium (LTNE) is taken into account. The parametric investigation focuses on the geometrical and structural properties of the porous medium, along with the strength of natural convection governed by the Rayleigh number. The results lead to the following conclusions, which are useful for heat exchanger design applications.

At high Rayleigh numbers, adding nanoparticles in such a natural convection problem is not recommended because the increased viscous forces overcome the elevated thermal conductivity, weakening natural convection and increasing irreversibility losses. This means that it is not possible to increase the Nusselt number without increasing entropy, and it is not possible to decrease entropy without decreasing heat transfer.

Limitations and future work

This study is limited to 2D laminar flow with a Rayleigh number up to 10⁶, assuming an isotropic porous medium and neglecting radiative heat transfer. The nanofluid is treated as a single-phase fluid. Increasing the Rayleigh number to 10⁸ would induce a transition to turbulent convection. Future research should investigate 3D turbulent natural convection, incorporate radiative heat transfer, account for tortuous paths within the porous medium, and utilize a two-phase hybrid nanofluid model.
