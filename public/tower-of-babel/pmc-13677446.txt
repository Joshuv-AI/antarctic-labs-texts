Valid Inference for Two Sample Summary Statistics Mendelian Randomization
Authors: Kai Wang, Grace Z. Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMendelian randomization (MR) studies increasingly rely on summary statistics from genome‐wide association studies (GWASs). However, current summary‐data methods treat marginal GWAS effect estimates as independent across instrumental single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), which holds only when the SNPs have no effect. Without imposing this independence assumption, we derive expressions for the two‐stage least squares (TSLS), two‐sample TSLS (TSTSLS), and generalized method‐of‐moments estimators together with their variance estimators using only GWAS summary statistics, assuming the instrumental SNPs are in linkage equilibrium. We show that the widely used inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) estimator is generally not equivalent to either TSLS or TSTSLS, contrary to a common claim in the MR literature. We further demonstrate that the conventional IVW standard error is generally inconsistent and provide a consistent alternative. Extensive simulations confirm the theoretical results and reveal that Wald confidence intervals based on summary statistics can be anti‐conservative. An application to publicly available GWAS summary statistics on body mass index and systolic blood pressure illustrates the practical implications of the proposed methodology.

Introduction

Mendelian randomization (MR) is an instrumental variable (IV) method that employs single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as IVs. A widely used approach in MR analysis leverages summary statistics from genome‐wide association studies (GWASs), facilitated by the growing number of available GWASs. It can be applied in either a one‐sample or a two‐sample setting [1]. One key advantage of summary data MR is that it does not require individual‐level data and thus avoids privacy concerns related to individual‐level data.

In a one‐sample setting, the instrumental SNPs, the exposure, and the outcome are measured in the same group of individuals. Conversely, in a two‐sample setting, SNP‐exposure associations and SNP‐outcome associations are estimated from two different samples. Traditionally, these samples are assumed to come from the same population, ensuring homogeneous genetic structures [2, 3]. Methods have been developed to assess such heterogeneity between two GWAS studies using only summary statistics [4].

Two‐stage least squares (TSLS) regression is a powerful tool in handling IV variables [5, 6]. It has some nice asymptotic properties like consistency and asymptotic normality. TSLS is originally developed for one‐sample individual‐level data and has been generalized to two‐sample data [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Recently, the two‐sample TSLS (TSTSLS) and other two‐sample IV methods have been applied to two‐sample MR [10, 12]. These methods use moment statistics computed from individual‐level data rather than summary statistics from GWASs.

A popular method for two‐sample summary statistics MR is based on the inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) average of Wald ratios [3, 13]. Using SNPs in linkage equilibrium as instruments, this method adopts techniques developed for meta‐analysis, including point estimation and Cochran's Q test for homogeneity, by treating GWAS summary statistics at each SNP as if they were from “independent studies.” Under the NO Measurement Error (NOME) assumption, the variance of a Wald ratio is estimated using “first‐order weights” [3]. To avoid making the NOME assumption, another weighting scheme named “second‐order weights” is proposed [3]. However, the “second‐order weights” approach is still within the “meta‐analysis” framework.

This meta‐analysis approach, although convenient and straightforward, may not be appropriate for summary statistics MR. The effect estimates at two SNPs are dependent even when those SNPs are in linkage equilibrium. Although the dependence among these effect estimates is acknowledged [14], there are still some studies that develop their theory and conduct simulation studies under the critical assumption that these effect estimates are mutually independent [14, 15, 16]. This practice seems to be continuing [17].

Zhao et al. [12] applied the generalized method of moments (GMM) to the IV analysis using data from two heterogeneous samples. This method encompasses several common two‐sample IV methods, including TSTSLS, as special cases. It relies on moment statistics computed from the individual‐level data and is not designed for summary statistics from GWASs. The variance of their estimator depends on two unknown residual variances [12], Equation ((8) and it is recommended to replace them by the variances of the exposure and the outcome, respectively [12]). Remark 5 Such a solution does not work with GWAS summary statistics as the variances of the exposure and the outcome are not available from a GWAS.

Under the assumption that the instrumental SNPs are in linkage equilibrium, we derive expressions for the TSLS, the TSTSLS, and the GMM estimators as well as their respective asymptotic variances in terms of GWAS summary statistics. Furthermore, we study the property of the IVW method in comparison with the TSTSLS method.

We have several interesting findings. There is a long‐standing claim in the MR literature that the IVW estimate is (asymptotically) equivalent to the TSLS method [18, 19]. However, our results indicate that this claim is misleading. In one‐sample setting, the standard error (SE) of the TSLS estimator even cannot be computed from GWAS summary statistics. In two‐sample setting, the TSLS estimator doesn't apply, and one has to use a two‐sample MR method such as TSTSLS. We show that the current estimate of the SE of an IVW estimator is inconsistent when the effect size is non‐zero, and provide a consistent estimate of the SE of the IVW.

The issue with the IVW method arises from two fallacies: The NOME assumption ignores part of the uncertainties in the summary statistics. The second fallacy comes from not distinguishing the marginal residual variances, there is one for each instrumental SNP, from the residual variance of a linear regression that jointly uses all instrumental SNPs. The second fallacy appears in other methods based on GWAS summary statistics as well, for instance, dIVW [15].

In what follows, we begin with a brief introduction to TSLS method in one‐sample setting. We then address a two‐sample setting, allowing for heterogeneity between the two samples. We present expressions for the TSLS estimator, the TSTSLS estimator, and the optimal GMM method in terms of GWAS summary statistics. In the simulation study, we compare our summary‐data‐based estimates with the IVW estimate and those directly computed from individual‐level data. These estimates are then applied to publicly available GWAS summary statistics on the causal effect of one's body mass index on systolic blood pressure. We conclude with some final remarks.

Method

We are interested in estimating the causal effect b of an exposure x on an outcome y. The model may be represented as follows: (1) y=bx+ϵ, where ϵ represents unobserved factors, some of which may be correlated with x. Without loss of generality, all variables are centered so that E(y)=E(x)=E(ϵ)=0. The variance of ϵ is denoted by σϵ2. It is well‐known that the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimate of b is biased because Cov(x,ϵ)≠0.

Instrumental variable (IV) method is an effective way to produce a consistent estimate of b. An IV, denoted by z, is exogenous and satisfies the following three assumptions [20, 21].

Assumption 1 (Relevance) It is associated with exposure x (i.e., Cov(z,x)≠0).

(Relevance) It is associated with exposure x (i.e., Cov(z,x)≠0).

Assumption 2 (Exclusion Restriction) It affects outcome y only through its association with exposure x.

(Exclusion Restriction) It affects outcome y only through its association with exposure x.

Assumption 3 (Exchangeability) It is not associated with any confounders of the exposure‐outcome association (i.e., Cov(z,ϵ)=0).

(Exchangeability) It is not associated with any confounders of the exposure‐outcome association (i.e., Cov(z,ϵ)=0).

In MR, an IV is typically a SNP. There are often multiple instrumental SNPs. The number of instrumental SNPs is denoted by p and the p IVs are denoted by zj,j=1,…,p.

Two‐sample Setting

The two‐sample IV framework has been extensively investigated in the econometrics literature. Methods developed include the two‐sample IV [7, 8] and the two‐sample TSLS [9, 10, 11]. The two‐sample IV is less efficient than the two‐sample TSLS [10]. These studies, except one [11], assume that the two samples are homogeneous, i.e., the covariance matrix of the IVs in one sample is the same as that in the other. To the best of our knowledge, Zhao et al. [12] is the only publication on MR that addresses heterogeneity between the two samples.

We use subscripts 1 and 2 to distinguish notations for sample 1 and sample 2. In sample 1, we have summary data Γ^1 and se(Γ^1), and in sample 2, we have summary data γ^2 and se(γ^2). That is, summary data regarding Γ2 and γ1 are unavailable.

To make the causal effect identifiable, the following assumption is made.

Assumption 5 (Structural Invariance) There are Γ and γ so that Γ1=Γ2=Γ,γ1=γ2=γ.

(Structural Invariance) There are Γ and γ so that Γ1=Γ2=Γ,γ1=γ2=γ.

This is an assumption made by Zhao et al. [12] as well. It implies that these two samples share the same causal effect: b=Γj/γj,j=1,…,p, where Γj and γj are the j th elements of Γ and γ, respectively. Therefore, any weighted average of {Γ^j/γ^j,j=1,…,p} would be a consistent estimator of b as Γ^j and γ^j are consistent for Γj and γj, respectively, for all j=1,…,p. However, an estimate using an ad hoc set of weights may not be efficient.

IVW Estimator

The inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) method is arguably the most widely used approach for MR based on summary data. The IVW estimator is defined as [13, 22, 23]: b^IVW=∑jwjΓ^jγ^j,wj=γ^j2/Var^(Γ^j)∑jγ^j2/Var^(Γ^j). As before, Γ^j denotes the marginal regression coefficient for the association between SNP j and the outcome, whereas γ^j denotes the marginal regression coefficient for the association between SNP j and the exposure. In addition, Var^(Γ^j)=(n1−2)−1y1′(I−Pj)y1·(zj′zj)−1. Under the NOME assumption the estimated variance of b^IVW is given by [13, 19] (10) Var^naïve(bˆIVW)=∑jwj2γ^j2Var(Γ^j)=∑jγ^j2Var^(Γ^j)−1. The quantity Var^naïve(b^IVW) is referred to as the naïve variance estimator of IVW.

It is often asserted that b^IVW and b^TSLS are asymptotically equivalent [18, 19]. However, it is unclear whether this assertion refers to the one‐sample setting, the two‐sample setting, or both. In fact, the claim is misleading in either context.

In one‐sample setting, the variance of b^TSLS cannot be computed from GWAS summary statistics alone (Remark 4). By contrast, the variance estimator for b^IVW in Equation (10), depends only on GWAS summary statistics. Therefore, the two variance estimators cannot coincide, implying that b^TSLS and b^IVW are not equivalent, even asymptotically. In the two‐sample setting, b^TSLS is not defined, so the question of its equivalence to b^IVW does not arise.

Because b^TSTSLS extends b^TSLS to the two‐sample setting, one might suspect that the claimed equivalence instead concerns b^IVW and b^TSTSLS. However, no such equivalence holds. First, b^TSTSLS=∑jγ^jΓ^jΓ^j2+(n1−2)Var^(Γ^j)−1∑jγ^j2Γ^j2+(n1−2)Var^(Γ^j)−1, under Assumption 6. Thus, although b^TSTSLS, like b^IVW, can be expressed as a weighted average of Wald ratios Γ^j/γ^j,j=1,…,p, the two estimators employ different weighting schemes and are therefore generally distinct. Indeed, b^TSTSLS=b^IVW only under highly restrictive conditions, such as Γ^j=0,j=1,…,p.

The variance estimators are also different. The variance formula commonly reported for b^IVW under the NOME condition, given in Equation (10), is generally inconsistent. To demonstrate this, we derive a consistent variance estimator below.

Analogous to the derivation of Var(b^TSTSLS) in the previous subsection, the variance of b^IVW is given by (11) Var(b^IVW)=Var(b^IVW−b)=∑jwj2γ^j2Var(Γ˜j−bγ˜j)+op(n1−1)=∑jwj2γ^j2Var(Γ˜j)+b2Var(γ˜j)+op(n1−1), where the second equality follows from the delta method and the identities Γ˜j=Γ^j and γ˜j=γ^j, which hold under Assumption 4.

A natural question is why Var(Γ˜j−bγ˜j) appears in Equation (11) rather than Var(Γ^j−bγ^j). The reason is that the variance calculation must be conditioned on the complete set of instruments, namely Z1 and Z2. By the law of total variance, Var(Γ˜j−bγ˜j)=EVar(Γ˜j−bγ˜j|Z1,Z2)+VarE(Γ˜j−bγ˜j|Z1,Z2). Since E(Γ˜j−bγ˜j|Z1,Z2)=0, the second term vanishes, yielding Var(Γ˜j−bγ˜j)=EVar(Γ˜j|Z1,Z2)+b2EVar(γ˜j|Z1,Z2)=EVar(Γ˜j|Z1)+b2EVar(γ˜j|Z2). Consequently, a consistent estimator of Var(Γ˜j−bγ˜j) is Var^(Γ˜j−bγ˜j)=Var^(Γ˜j|Z1)+b2Var^(γ˜j|Z2). Since Var^(Γ˜j|Z1)=Var^(Γ˜j),Var^(γ˜j|Z2)=Var^(γ˜j), by definition, Equation (11) leads to the consistent variance estimator Var^(b^IVW)=∑jwj2γ^j2Var^(Γ˜j)+b^2Var^(γ˜j), where b^ denotes any consistent estimator of b such as b^TSTSLS or b^IVW.

The naïve variance estimator in Equation (10) is consistent only when b=0. In that case, Var^(Γ˜j) and Var^(Γ^j) converge to the same value for all j=1,…,p. Therefore, the commonly stated asymptotic equivalence between IVW and TSLS lacks theoretical support in general.

How do the naïve and consistent variance estimators compare? Comparing Equations (10) and (11), we see that the key distinction lies between Var(Γ^j) and Var(Γ˜j)+b2Var(γ˜j). When b=0, Varnaïve(b^IVW)>Var(b^IVW), because Var(Γ^j)>Var(Γ˜j). By continuity, the inequality may continue to hold when |b| is sufficiently small. In such cases, the naïve variance estimator overestimates the true variance and therefore leads to conservative inference.

However, when |b| becomes large, it is possible that Var(Γ^j)<Var(Γ˜j)+b2Var(γ˜j),j=1,…,p, even though Var(Γ^j)>Var(Γ˜j), in which case Varnaïve(b^IVW)<Var(b^IVW). The naïve variance estimator then understates the true uncertainty, leading to anti‐conservative inference and an inflated false‐positive rate. Numeric evidence of this phenomenon is provided in the Simulation Study section.

The “second‐order weights” [3] uses Var(Γ^j)+b2Var(γ^j) rather than Var(Γ˜j)+b2Var(γ˜j) in Eq. (11). Such a weighting scheme apparently results in a larger variance estimator.

The IVW estimator is not unique in failing to condition on the full set of instruments when estimating variance. Several summary‐data MR methods, including IVW, dIVW [15], and the “exact” weight IVW [3], rely on analogous variance calculations and therefore suffer from the same issue. To the best of our knowledge, this problem has not been explicitly recognized in the Mendelian randomization literature.

Covariates

So far, we have assumed that neither the exposure GWAS nor the outcome GWAS includes covariates. When covariates are included, however, b^TSTSLS, b^TSIV, and their corresponding standard errors can still be expressed in terms of GWAS summary statistics, provided that the assumptions are modified appropriately. We illustrate this extension using b^TSTSLS.

Let C1 be the n1×k1 matrix of k1 covariates in the outcome GWAS and C2 be the n2×k2 matrix of k2 covariates in the exposure GWAS. Accordingly, the k1 covariates enter the outcome model in Equation (1). Define M1=I−C1(C1′C1)−1C1′. After partialling out the effects of these covariates, Equation (3) for sample 1 becomes M1y1=M1Z1Γ+uy1, as M1uy1=uy1 under uncorrelatedness between C1 and uy1. Similarly, Equation (2) for sample 2 becomes M2x2=M2Z2γ+ux2, where M2=I−C2(C2′C2)−1C2′. It follows that γ˜=(Z2′M2Z2)−1Z2′M2x2,Γ˜=(Z1′M1Z1)−1Z1′M1y1. The cross‐sample predictor for M1x1 is M1Z1γ˜. The TSTSLS estimate of the causal effect b becomes b˜TSTSLS=γ˜′Z1′M1y1γ˜′Z1′M1Z1γ˜=γ˜′Z1′M1Z1Γ˜γ˜′Z1′M1Z1γ˜. The GWAS estimate of Γ controlling for C1 is Γ^=D1−1Z1′M1x1,D1=diag(Z1M1Z1). Likewise, the GWAS estimate of γ adjusted for C2 is γ^=D2−1Z2′M2x2,D2=diag(Z2M2Z2). If the off‐diagonal elements of Z1′M1Z1 and Z2′M2Z2 are assumed to be zero, which is a covariate‐adjusted version of Assumption 6, then Γ˜=Γ^ and γ˜=γ^. Consequently, b˜TSTSLS reduces to b^TSTSLS=γ˜′D1Γ˜γ˜′D1γ˜=∑jγ^jΓ^jv1,y,j∑jγ^j2v1,y,j, where v1,y,j=z1,j′M1z1,jy1′M1y1=Γ^j2+(n1−2−k1)Var(Γ^j)−1 is the j th element of D1(y1′M1y1)−1. The quantity (n1−2−k1) appears because, in addition to the intercept and the j th SNP, the regression includes k1 covariates. With these modifications, the expression for Var^(Γ˜) remains unchanged and the same is true for Var^(γ˜). That is, the variance formulas given in Equations (6) and (7) continue to apply. In particular, the variance estimator in Equation (9) remains consistent.

Simulation Study

A simulation study was conducted to evaluate the theoretical findings developed in this paper. Some previous studies generated γ^j and Γ^j independently from normal distributions [14, 15, 16, 17]. However, data generated in this manner do not represent genuine GWAS summary statistics. As emphasized throughout this paper, summary statistics obtained from a GWAS are generally correlated rather than independent. Therefore, valid GWAS summary statistics should be generated from an underlying data‐generating model rather than simulated independently.

The generating model we use is similar to the model considered elsewhere [24] and is given by (12) γj=j/10,j=1,…,10,zj=Binomial(2,0.3)independently acrossj=1,…,10,u=Normal(0,2),ϵ=Normal(0,2),x=∑j=110γjzj+u+ϵ,y=bx+ϵ,. where u and ϵ are independent. GWAS summary statistics are calculated from the generated data.

Under this model, Var(x)=5.617. Collectively, the ten SNPs z1,…,z10 explain 28.79% of the variance in x, while the proportion explained by an individual SNP ranges from 0.07% to 7.48%. Furthermore, Var(y)=b2Var(x)+2b·Cov(x,ϵ)+Var(ϵ)=5.617b2+4b+2. For b=0.4, Var(y)=4.49872. In this case, the ten SNPs jointly explain 5.75% of the variance in y, while the proportion explained by an individual SNP ranges from 0.01% to 1.49%.

One reviewer expressed interest in the magnitude of Cor(γ^j,γ^k) and Cor(Γ^j,Γ^k). To investigate this, we estimated these correlations from 1000 datasets generated under model (12), each with a sample size of 10,000. We obtain Cor^(γ^1,γ^5)=0.015 and Cor^(Γ^1,Γ^5)=0.033 for b=0.4. These values are of the same order of magnitude as the theoretical correlations implied by Equation (8): Cor(γ^1,γ^5)=0.004 and Cor(Γ^1,Γ^5)=0.004, considering the observed substantial variability in the simulated correlations. For example, Cor^(γ^1,γ^6)=0.003, which is smaller than Cor^(γ^1,γ^6), even though the corresponding theoretical correlation is larger according to Equation (8).

Final Remarks

This study tackles the challenge of drawing valid inferences in summary statistics MR without relying on assumptions like the NOME condition and the independence of summary statistics across instrumental SNPs that are known to be violated. Although these issues may be subtle, they have significant implications and have been largely overlooked or inadequately addressed in the existing literature. These issues are closely interrelated. To disentangle them, we examined several widely used MR methods, beginning with the well‐established TSLS method. We derived expressions for TSLS and TSTSLS, including their standard errors, in terms of GWAS summary statistics.

We identify two fallacies in the standard calculation of the IVW standard error. The first concerns the variance of the Wald ratio estimator, Γ^j/γ^j, where the NOME assumption neglects the variability in γ^j. The second relates to the variance of the IVW estimator, b^IVW, which should be computed conditional on all instrumental SNPs jointly. In contrast, the naïve variance, calculated as ∑jwj2/γ^j2Var(Γ^j), assumes Cov(Γ^j,Γ^k)=0 for j≠k and calculates Var(Γ^j) conditional only on SNP j. This assumption is not only inconsistent with the underlying model but also unnecessary. When conditioning on the full set of instrumental SNPs, one naturally obtains Cov(Γ˜j,Γ˜k)=0 for j≠k. Failure to make this distinction leads to an inconsistent variance estimator.

Another way to understand why Cov(Γ^j,Γ^k)=0 for j≠k is inconsistent with the underlying model is to notice that, in those marginal regressions, the exposure is the same even though the SNPs are in linkage equilibrium. This term may be small but does not vanish, even asymptotically.

Our simulation results highlight a limitation of using summary statistics in MR: the resulting confidence intervals tend to be anti‐conservative. This anti‐conservatism arises from the common practice of ignoring the off‐diagonal elements of Z′Z, as specified in Assumption 4. This assumption influences both the point estimate and its SE. Our finding suggests that the primary driver of the reduced coverage probability is the estimate, not the SE.

Our results are based on summary statistics from GWASs that use linear models. For GWASs based on other models, such as logistic regression, further investigation is warranted.

As in many other methods, the common assumption that the instrumental SNPs are in linkage equilibrium is made. One reviewer pointed out that there exists a version of IVW that does not require this assumption [27].

The two‐sample summary statistics TSTSLS, TSIV, and IVW (with consistent SE) have been implemented in an R package iGasso via function tssmr.
