Ivan Arzhantsev, Kirill Shakhmatov

Abstract
. We give a criterion of factoriality of a suspension. This allows to construct many examples of flexible affine factorial varieties. In particular, we find a homogeneous affine factorial 3-fold that is not a homogeneous space of an algebraic group.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 14M05, 14M17; Secondary 13A05, 13F15, 14R20

Introduction

We work over an algebraically closed field
{\mathbb{K}}
of characteristic zero. Let us say that an algebraic variety
X
is homogeneous if the automorphism group
\Aut(X)
acts on
X
transitively. Examples of homogeneous varieties are homogeneous spaces of algebraic groups. Namely, if
G
is an algebraic group and
H
is a closed subgroup of
G
, then the variety
X=G/H
of left cosets is obviously a homogeneous variety.

Since the group
\Aut(X)
need not be algebraic, one may expect that the class of homogeneous varieties is wider than the class of homogeneous spaces. This is indeed the case. For example, any smooth quasi-affine toric variety is a homogeneous variety; see
[
4
, Theorem 0.2(2)]
and
[
6
, Theorem 4.3(a)]
. An explicit example of a smooth quasi-affine toric surface that is not a homogeneous space can be found in
[
4
, Example 2.2]
.

In
[
5
]
, we study homogeneous affine varieties. The study is based on the concept of a flexible variety and on the construction of a suspension. Let
{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathrm{a}}
be the additive group of the ground field
{\mathbb{K}}
and
{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathrm{a}}\times X\to X
be a regular action on an algebraic variety
X
. The corresponding subgroup of the automorphism group
\Aut(X)
is called a
{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathrm{a}}
-subgroup. The special automorphism group
\SAut(X)
is the subgroup of
\Aut(X)
generated by all
{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathrm{a}}
-subgroups.

Denote by
\Xreg
the set of smooth points in
X
. A point
x\in\Xreg
is called flexible if the tangent space
T_{x}X
is spanned by tangents to orbits of
{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathrm{a}}
-subgroups passing through the point
x
. A variety
X
is called flexible if any point
x\in\Xreg
is flexible.

In
[
3
, Theorem 0.1]
it is proved that an irreducible affine variety
X
is flexible if and only if the group
\SAut(X)
acts on
\Xreg
transitively. Moreover, if
X
has dimension at least
2
, then these conditions are equivalent to the condition that
\SAut(X)
acts on
\Xreg
infinitely transitively. Recall that an action of a group
G
on a set
X
is infinitely transitive if it is
m
-transitive for any positive integer
m
, i.e., for any two tuples
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
and
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
of pairwise distinct points of
X
there exists an element
g\in G
such that
gx_{i}=y_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
.

Now we recall the definition of a suspension. Let
Y
be an affine variety and
f\in{\mathbb{K}}[Y]
be a non-constant regular function. The hypersurface
\Susp(Y,f)
given in the direct product
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}\times Y
by the equation
uv=f
, where
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}=\Spec{\mathbb{K}}[u,v]
, is called a suspension over
Y
. In the context of automorphism groups suspensions were considered for the first time in
[
8
]
.
In particular, it is proved in
[
8
]
that any suspension over an affine space is flexible. In
[
4
, Theorem 0.2(3)]
we show that
\Susp(Y,f)
is flexible provided the variety
Y
is flexible.

In
[
5
, Corollary 1]
it is proved that
\Susp(Y,f)
is smooth if and only if both the variety
Y
and the scheme

\Spec{\mathbb{K}}[Y]/(f)
are smooth. Since any smooth flexible variety is homogeneous, we obtain many examples of homogeneous affine varieties. Moreover, it is shown in
[
5
, Lemma 2]
that the Picard number of an affine homogeneous space
X
is at most
\dim X
. Since we can construct smooth suspensions over an affine space with the Picard number bigger than the dimension of the suspension, we obtain homogeneous affine varieties that are not homogeneous spaces; see
[
5
, Theorem 4]
.

In this paper we concentrate on homogeneous affine factorial varieties. Again examples of such varieties can be found among homogeneous spaces. Recall that any connected linear algebraic group
G
is a semidirect product
G^{\red}\leftthreetimes R_{u}(G)
of a reductive group
G^{\red}
and the unipotent radical
R_{u}(G)
, see e.g.
[
12
, Theorem 6.4]
. If the group
G^{\red}
is a direct product of a torus
Z
and a simply connected semisimple group
G^{\ssss}
, then the group
G
is factorial
[
13
, Theorem 6]
. Moreover, if
H
is a connected semisimple subgroup of such a group
G
, then the homogeneous space
G/H
is affine and by
[
13
, Theorem 4]
the variety
G/H
is factorial.

Our aim is to construct many flexible affine factorial varieties via suspensions. The paper is organized as follows. We begin with some preparatory lemmas in Section
1
. They are used in Section
2
to prove that the suspension
X=\Susp(Y,f)
is a factorial variety if and only if
Y
is factorial and
f
is a prime element in
{\mathbb{K}}[Y]
. This result (Theorem
1
) is obtained in the broadest possible context of arbitrary commutative domains. In these settings the passage from
Y
to
X=\Susp(Y,f)
may be considered as a generalization of replacing a ring
R
with the Laurent polynomial ring
R[T,T^{-1}]
.

Combining this with the criterion of smoothness of suspensions from
[
5
, Corollary 1]
, we observe in Section
3
that for a smooth flexible affine factorial variety
Y
the suspension
\Susp(Y,f)
is smooth factorial if and only if the function
f
is prime in
{\mathbb{K}}[Y]
and
\{f=0\}
is a smooth subvariety in
Y
. It provides many examples of homogeneous affine factorial varieties. We proceed with some results on topology of suspensions over the field of complex numbers. We prove that if the variety
Y
is simply connected, then any suspension
\Susp(Y,f)
is simply connected as well (Proposition
3
). It follows from
[
14
, Theorem 2 and Proposition 19]
that there are precisely three pairwise non-isomorphic affine factorial 3-folds without non-constant invertible functions that are homogeneous spaces of algebraic groups: they are
{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
,
\SL(2)
and
\SL(2)/2I
, where
2I
is the binary icosahedral group. We show that the hypersurface
\{x^{2}y-xy-uv+1=0\}
, which is the suspension
X=\Susp({\mathbb{C}}^{2},(x-1)xy+1)
, has
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
as the third homotopy group
\pi_{3}(X)
. We conclude that
X
is a homogeneous affine factorial 3-fold that is not a homogeneous space of an algebraic group (Theorem
3
).

It is an important problem to find algebraic invariants that allow us to distinguish between non-isomorphic smooth flexible affine factorial varieties of the same dimension. For example, in order to prove that a suspension
\Susp({\mathbb{A}}^{2},f)
is not isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}^{3}
one may use the positive solution of the Linearization Problem, see
[
11
]
. It implies that any action of an algebraic torus on
{\mathbb{A}}^{3}
has a fixed point. So it suffices to find a free action of a one-dimensional torus on
\Susp({\mathbb{A}}^{2},f)
. It seems to be a more difficult task to distinguish between
some
\Susp({\mathbb{A}}^{2},f)
and
\SL(2)
.

The divisor class group of a suspension
\Susp(Y,f)
is computed in Section
4
. This gives an alternative proof of Theorem
1
in geometric settings. Finally, in Section
5
we show that the natural properties of the projection from
\Susp(Y,f)
to
Y
are not sufficient to characterize suspensions.

1.
Basic lemmas

Let
R
be a commutative associative ring with unit. Consider the polynomial ring
R[u,v]
and fix an element
f\in R
. The main object studied in this section is the factor ring

S:=R[u,v]/(uv-f).

This ring carries a natural
{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading
S=\oplus_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}S_{i}
given by

S_{0}=R,\quad S_{i}=Ru^{i}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{-i}=Rv^{i}\quad\text{for}\ i>0.

Indeed, the relation
uv=f
implies that
S
is the sum of subspaces
S_{i}
. The inclusion
{S_{i}S_{j}\subseteq S_{i+j}}
is clear. If we have
r_{0}+\sum_{i>0}r_{i}u^{i}+\sum_{j>0}r_{j}^{\prime}v_{j}=0
in
S
, then

r_{0}+\sum_{i>0}r_{i}u^{i}+\sum_{j>0}r_{j}^{\prime}v^{j}=h(u,v)(uv-f)
in
R[u,v]
for some polynomial
h(u,v)
. Consider the lexicographic order
\succ
on terms in
u
and
v
with
u\succ v
.
If
h
is nonzero, then the leading term of
h(u,v)(uv-f)
is divisible by
uv
, while there is no such term on the left hand side.

The following lemma generalizes
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 1
.

For a nonzero
f
, the ring
S
is a domain if and only if
R
is a domain.

Proof.

The direct implication follows from the inclusion
R=S_{0}\subseteq S
. Let us prove the converse.

Assume that
g_{1}g_{2}=0
for some nonzero
g_{1},g_{2}\in S
. Denote by
L(g)
the nonzero homogeneous component of maximal degree of a nonzero element
g\in S
. Then
L(g_{1})L(g_{2})=0
. On the other hand, we have
L(g_{1})L(g_{2})=abf^{d}u^{p}v^{q},
where
a,b\in R\setminus\{0\}
and
p,q,d
are non-negative integers with
pq=0
. This leads to a contradiction.
∎

If the element
f
is invertible in
R
, then replacing
v
by
fv
we come to the relation
uv=1
. This shows that
S
is the ring of Laurent polynomials over
R
.

From now on we assume that
R
is a domain and
f
is nonzero non-invertible in
R
. Denote by
A^{\times}
the set of invertible elements of a domain
A
.

Lemma 2
.

We have
R^{\times}=S^{\times}
. In particular, the elements
u
and
v
are not invertible.

Proof.

Let
g\in S
and
gh=1
for some
h\in S
. Since
1
is homogeneous, the elements
g
and
h
are homogeneous as well. So we may assume that
g=u^{d}a
and

h=v^{d}b
, where
a,b\in R
. If
d=0
then
g\in R^{\times}
. If
d>0
then
gh=f^{d}ab=1
. This contradicts the condition that
f
is not invertible.
∎

Let us recall that a nonzero element
r
in a domain
R
is irreducible if
r
is not invertible and if whenever
r=r_{1}r_{2}
with
r_{1},r_{2}\in R
, then one of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
is invertible.

Lemma 3
.

The elements
u
and
v
are irreducible in
S
.

Proof.

If
u=gh
then
g
and
h
are homogeneous. We may assume that
g=u^{d+1}a
and
h=v^{d}b
for
a,b\in R
. Then
u=gh=uf^{d}ab
. This implies
f^{d}ab=1
.
Since
f
is not invertible, we have
d=0
and
b
is invertible. So the element
h
is invertible. We conclude that
u
is irreducible. The same arguments work for
v
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
a\in R
. Then

(i)

if
a
is irreducible in
S
, then
a
is irreducible in
R
;

(ii)

if
a
is irreducible in
R
and not associated with
f
, then
a
is irreducible in
S
.

Proof.

We start with (i). If
a=cd
with
c,d\in R
, then we may assume that
c
is invertible in
S
. By Lemma
2
, the element
c
is invertible in
R
, and so
a
is irreducible in
R
.

Now we come to (ii). If
a=gh
and
g,h\in S
, then
g=u^{d}b
,
h=v^{d}c
for some
b,c\in R
. If
d=0
then either
g
or
h
is invertible by assumption. Assume that
d>0
. Then
a=gh=f^{d}bc
. Since
a
is irreducible in
R
, we have
d=1
and
bc
is invertible. Then
a
is associated with
f
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 5
.

An element
g=b_{l}v^{l}+\ldots+b_{1}v+b_{0}
,
b_{i}\in R
is divisible by
u
if and only if all the
b_{i}
are divisible by
f
.

Proof.

If every
b_{i}
is divisible by
f=uv
, then
g
is divisible by
u
. Conversely, if
g=uh
, then
h=c_{l+1}v^{l+1}+\ldots+c_{2}v^{2}+c_{1}v
, where
c_{i}\in R
, and so

g=uh=c_{l+1}fv^{l}+\ldots+c_{2}fv+c_{1}f.

∎

Recall that a nonzero non-invertible element
r
of a domain
R
is prime if whenever
r
divides a product
ab
, then
r
divides either
a
or
b
. In other words, the principal ideal
(r)
is prime in
R
. Note that any prime element is irreducible.

Proposition 1
.

The element
u
is prime if and only if the element
v
is prime if and only if the element
f
is prime in
R
.

Proof.

By symmetry, it suffices to prove that
u
is prime if and only if
f
is prime in
R
. Assume that
f
is prime in
R
. Take two elements
g,h\in S
not divisible by

u
. We may assume that

g=b_{l}v^{l}+\ldots+b_{1}v+b_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad h=c_{m}v^{m}+\ldots+c_{1}v+c_{0},

where all
b_{i},c_{j}\in R
are not divisible by
f
. The lowest homogeneous component of the product
gh
equals
b_{l}c_{m}v^{l+m}
. By assumptions, the element

b_{l}c_{m}
is not divisible by
f
. By Lemma
5
, the element
gh
is not divisible by
u
. We conclude that
u
is prime.

Now assume that
u
is prime. If
f
divides
ab
for some
a,b\in R
, then
uv
divides
ab
in
S
. Since
u
is prime, we may assume that
u
divides
a
.
Then
a=uc
with
c=vd
and
d\in R
. This implies
a=uvd=fd
, so
f
divides
a
in
R
, and the element
f
is prime in
R
.
∎

The following result shows that the construction of suspension preserves normality.

Proposition 2
.

The ring
S
is integrally closed if and only if the ring
R
is integrally closed.

Proof.

Assume that
S
is integrally closed. Consider
a/b\in\Frac(R)
integral over
R
, where
a,b\in R
and
b\neq 0
. Since
S
is integrally closed, we have
a/b=g
for some
g\in S
. From
a=bg
it follows that
g\in R
. Therefore,
a/b\in R
and
R
is integrally closed.

Conversely, assume that
R
is integrally closed. Consider
\varphi=p/q\in\Frac(S)
integral over
S
, where
p,q\in S
and
q\neq 0
. For all

j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
we have

\varphi=\frac{p}{q}=\frac{u^{j}p}{u^{j}q},

hence we may assume that
p,q\in R[u]
. Consider an integral equation

\varphi^{m}+s_{1}\varphi^{m-1}+\ldots+s_{m}=0,

where
s_{i}\in S
for all
i=1,\dots,m
. Multiplying this equation by
u^{mk}
for large enough
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, we see that
u^{k}\varphi\in\Frac(R[u])
is integral over
R[u]
. But
R[u]
is integrally closed, hence
u^{k}\varphi=g\in R[u]
. By a symmetric argument for
v
there exists
l\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
such that
v^{l}\varphi=h\in R[v]
. We obtain

\varphi=\frac{g}{u^{k}}=\frac{h}{v^{l}}.

For
d=\max(k,l)
we can rewrite these equations as

\varphi=\frac{u^{d-k}g}{u^{d}}=\frac{v^{d-k}h}{v^{d}}.

Clearly,
u^{d-k}g\in R[u]
and
v^{d-k}h\in R[v]
. So it remains to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Consider an element
\varphi\in\Frac(S)
. Assume that there exist
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
,
g\in R[u]
, and
h\in R[v]
such that

\varphi=\frac{g}{u^{d}}=\frac{h}{v^{d}}.

Then
\varphi\in S
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
d
. The case
d=0
is trivial. Assume that the assertion is true for some
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
. Consider
\varphi\in\Frac(S)
,
g\in R[u]
, and
h\in R[v]
such that

\varphi=\frac{g}{u^{d+1}}=\frac{h}{v^{d+1}}.

Write

g=a+ug^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad h=b+vh^{\prime},

where
a,b\in R
,
g^{\prime}\in R[u]
, and
h^{\prime}\in R[v]
. Denote

g^{\prime}=\sum_{i}a_{i}u^{i}\quad\text{and}\quad h^{\prime}=\sum_{i}b_{i}v^{i},

where all
a_{i},b_{i}
are elements of
R
. Comparing coefficients of
v^{d+1}
and
u^{d+1}
in the equality
v^{d+1}g=u^{d+1}h
we conclude that

a=b_{2d+1}f^{d+1}\quad\text{and}\quad b=a_{2d+1}f^{d+1}.

Therefore,

\frac{g}{u^{d+1}}=\frac{a}{u^{d+1}}+\frac{g^{\prime}}{u^{d}}=b_{2d+1}v^{d+1}+\frac{g^{\prime}}{u^{d}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{h}{v^{d+1}}=\frac{b}{v^{d+1}}+\frac{h^{\prime}}{v^{d}}=a_{2d+1}u^{d+1}+\frac{h^{\prime}}{v^{d}}.

Denote
\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi-b_{2d+1}v^{d+1}-a_{2d+1}u^{d+1}
. Then

\varphi^{\prime}=\frac{g^{\prime}}{u^{d}}-a_{2d+1}u^{d+1}=\frac{h^{\prime}}{v^{d}}-b_{2d+1}v^{d+1}.

Clearly, the elements

\frac{g^{\prime}}{u^{d}}-a_{2d+1}u^{d+1}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{h^{\prime}}{v^{d}}-b_{2d+1}v^{d+1}

can be written in the form
\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{u^{d}}
and
\frac{h^{\prime\prime}}{v^{d}}
respectively, where
g^{\prime\prime}\in R[u]
and
h^{\prime\prime}\in R[v]
. By the induction hypothesis
\varphi^{\prime}\in S
. Since
-b_{2d+1}v^{d+1}-a_{2d+1}u^{d+1}\in S
, we conclude that
\varphi\in S
.
∎

This completes the proof of Proposition
2
.
∎

2.
A criterion of factoriality

Now we come to factoriality. A domain
R
is a unique factorization domain (UFD) if nonzero non-invertible elements of
R
can be factored uniquely into irreducible elements up to renumbering and invertible factors. A domain
R
is a UFD if and only if any irreducible element in
R
is prime and any ascending chain of principal ideals in
R
terminates, see e.g.
[
7
, Section 0.2]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
R
be a domain and
f
be a nonzero non-invertible element in
R
. Then the domain
S
is a UFD if and only if
R
is a UFD and
f
is prime in
R
.

Proof.

Assume that
S
is factorial. By Lemma
3
, the element
u
is irreducible. By factoriality of
S
, the element
u
is prime. Proposition
1
implies that

f
is prime in
R
.

Let us show that any ascending chain of principal ideals in
R
terminates. Consider such a chain
Ra_{1}\subseteq Ra_{2}\subseteq\ldots
. Since
S
is factorial, the corresponding chain
Sa_{1}\subseteq Sa_{2}\subseteq\ldots
of principal ideals in
S
terminates. But if the principal ideals
Sa_{i}
and
Sa_{i+1}
coincide, then the elements
a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}

are associated in
S
. By Lemma
2
, the elements
a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}
are associated in
R
as well, and the original chain in
R
terminates.

It remains to check that any irreducible element
a\in R
is prime in
R
. If
a
is associated with
f
, then the assertion follows from Proposition
1
. Let us assume that
a
is not associated with
f
. By Lemma
4
(ii),
a
is irreducible in
S
, and so it is prime in
S
. If
a
divides
bc
with
b,c\in R
, then
we may assume that
a
divides
b
in
S
. Then
a
divides
b
in
R
, and so
a
is prime in
R
.

Assume now that
R
is factorial and
f
is prime in
R
. Then the polynomial ring
R[u,v]
is factorial as well. So, any ascending chain of principal ideals in
R[u,v]
and in its factor ring
S
terminates.

It remains to prove that any irreducible
g\in S
is prime. If
u
divides
g
, then
g
is associated with
u
. In this case
g
is prime by Proposition
1
. Further we assume that
u
does not divide
g
.

Take the smallest non-negative integer
l
such that
u^{l}g\in R[u]
.

Step 1
. Let us show that the element
u^{l}g
is prime in
R[u]
. Since
R[u]
is UFD, it suffices to prove that
u^{l}g
is irreducible in
R[u]
.

Let
u^{l}g=p_{1}p_{2}
with
p_{1},p_{2}\in R[u]
. By Proposition
1
, the element
u
is prime in
S
. Since
g
is irreducible in
S
, after dividing the right-hand side by
u^{l}
we cannot obtain a product of two
non-invertible elements. So we may assume that
p_{1}
is associated in
S
with
u^{t}
for some
0\leq t\leq l
. This means that
p_{1}=\lambda u^{t}
, where
\lambda\in S^{\times}=R^{\times}
. If
t>0
then

u^{l-t}g=\lambda p_{2}\in R[u]
, a contradiction with minimality of
l
. So
t=0
and
p_{1}\in R^{\times}
. This shows that
u^{l}g
is prime in
R[u]
.

Step 2
. Let us prove that
g
is prime in
S
. Let
h_{1}h_{2}=gs
with
h_{1},h_{2},s\in S
. Multiplying both sides by a suitable power of
u
, we obtain

h_{1}^{\prime}h_{2}^{\prime}=u^{l}gs^{\prime}
, where
h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime},s^{\prime}\in R[u]
. Since the element
u^{l}g
is prime in
R[u]
, we may assume that
h_{1}^{\prime}=u^{l}gp
with
p\in R[u]
. By construction,
we have
h_{1}^{\prime}=u^{r}h_{1}
with
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
.

If
r\leq l
then
h_{1}=u^{l-r}gp
, so
g
divides
h_{1}
in
S
.

If
r>l
then
u^{r-l}h_{1}=gp
. Since
u
does not divide
g
and
u
is prime in
S
, we conclude that
u^{r-l}
divides
p
in
S
. So
h_{1}=\frac{p}{u^{r-l}}g
,
and
g
divides
h_{1}
in
S
.

This proves that
g
is prime in
S
. The proof of Theorem
1
is completed.
∎

Remark 1
.

Let
A=\oplus_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}A_{i}
be a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-graded domain. We say that a nonzero non-invertible homogeneous element
a\in S
is
{\mathbb{Z}}
-prime if
a
divides a product
gh
of homogeneous elements
if and only if either
a
divides
g
or
a
divides
h
. Further,
A
is said to be factorially graded if any nonzero non-invertible homogeneous element in
A
is a product of
{\mathbb{Z}}
-primes.

Under some restrictions on the domain
A
it is known that
A
is factorially graded if and only if
A
is UFD; see
[
1
]
or
[
2
, Theorem 3.4.1.11 (ii)]
. This result can simplify the proof of Theorem
1
at the expense of loss of generality.

3.
Homogeneous varieties vs homogeneous spaces

Let us start with a direct corollary of Theorem
1
and the criterion of smoothness of suspensions
[
5
, Corollary 1]
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
Y
be a flexible affine variety. Then a suspension
\Susp(Y,f)
is a smooth flexible affine factorial variety if and only if
Y
is factorial and smooth, the function
f
is a prime element in
{\mathbb{K}}[Y]
, and
\{f=0\}
is a smooth subvariety in
Y
.

Theorem
2
provides an iterative procedure for constructing a large number of homogeneous affine factorial varieties in each dimension starting from
3
.

From now until the end of this section we assume that the ground field
{\mathbb{K}}
is the field of complex numbers
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proposition 3
.

Let an irreducible normal affine variety
Y
be simply connected and
f
be a non-invertible nonzero regular function on
Y
. Then the suspension
\Susp(Y,f)
is simply connected.

Proof.

By
[
15
, Theorem 7.1]
, any irreducible complex algebraic variety is connected in the complex topology. The variety
X=\Susp(Y,f)
is given by
uv=f(y)
. Consider the principal open subset
X_{u}

defined by
u\neq 0
. The variety
X_{u}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{*}\times Y
,
(u,v,y)\mapsto(u,y)
, and its fundamental group is generated by the loop
(l(t),y_{0})
,
t\in S^{1}
, where
l(t)
is a circle around the punctured point on

{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{*}
, and
y_{0}\in Y
. One may choose
y_{0}
such that
f(y_{0})=0
.

By Proposition
2
, the variety
X
is normal, and by
[
10
, Proposition 2.10.1]
, the inclusion
X_{u}\subseteq X
induces a surjection of fundamental groups. So the fundamental group of
X
is generated by the class of the image of the loop
(l(t),y_{0})
. This loop maps to
(l(t),0,y_{0})
, since
v=f(y_{0})/u=0
. So it is contained in the subvariety
(u,0,y_{0})
, which is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}
, hence this loop is contractible. We conclude that
X
is simply connected.
∎

More information on topology of suspensions over
Y={\mathbb{C}}^{n}
can be found
[
8
, Section 4]
. Let us formulate some of these results that we use below.

Let
X
be a topological space. First we recall relations between unreduced and reduced singular homology
H_{k}(X)
and
\tilde{H}_{k}(X)
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}
. We have

H_{k}(X)\cong\tilde{H}_{k}(X)\ \text{for}\ k\geq 1

and

H_{0}(X)\cong\tilde{H}_{0}(X)\oplus\mathbb{Z}.

Denote by
\pi_{0}(X)
the set of path-connected components of
X
. Then

H_{0}(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{0}(X)],

where
\mathbb{Z}[A]
for a set
A
denotes the free abelian group with basis
A
.

Let
(X,x)
be a pointed topological space. For each
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
there is a Hurewicz homomorphism

\Phi_{k}\colon\pi_{k}(X,x)\to H_{k}(X).

The Hurewicz Theorem states the following. If
X
is
(k-1)
-connected for some
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
, then
\Phi_{k}
induces an isomorphism

\pi_{k}^{\mathrm{ab}}(X,x)\cong H_{k}(X),

where
\pi_{k}^{\mathrm{ab}}(X,x)
is the abelianization of the homotopy group
\pi_{k}(X,x)
. Note that for
k\geq 2
, since the group
\pi_{k}(X,x)
is abelian, we obtain an isomorphism
\pi_{k}(X,x)\cong H_{k}(X)
.

Let
X
be a suspension
\{uv=f\}
over
Y={\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Assume that the hypersurface
\{f=0\}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is reduced, irreducible and smooth. By
[
8
, Proposition 4.1]
, there are isomorphisms

(
*
)

\tilde{H}_{*}(X)\cong\tilde{H}_{*-2}(X_{0}).

We have

\tilde{H}_{0}(X)=\tilde{H}_{0}(X_{0})=0.

We also know that
\pi_{1}(X)=0
, hence

H_{1}(X)\cong\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{ab}}(X)=0.

From (

∗

‣
3
) we obtain

H_{2}(X)=0\quad\text{and}\quad H_{3}(X)\cong H_{1}(X_{0}).

In particular,
\pi_{2}(X)=0
(so
X
is actually always
2
-connected) and

\pi_{3}(X)\cong H_{3}(X)\cong H_{1}(X_{0})\cong\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{ab}}(X_{0}).

We are ready to come to concrete examples and to construct a homogeneous affine factorial variety that is not a homogeneous space.

The only one-dimensional affine factorial variety
Y
without non-constant invertible functions is the affine line
{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
. The only irreducible polynomials
f
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
are linear functions. So the only smooth affine factorial surface
X
that can be obtained as
\Susp(Y,f)
is the affine plane
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. The plane is homogeneous with respect to several actions of linear algebraic groups.

The situation with flexible affine factorial 3-folds is more diverse.

Theorem 3
.

The hypersurface
x^{2}y-xy-uv+1=0
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{4}
is a homogeneous affine factorial 3-fold that is not a homogeneous space of an algebraic group.

Proof.

The hypersurface
x^{2}y-xy-uv+1=0
is the suspension

X=\Susp({\mathbb{C}}^{2},(x-1)xy+1).

By Theorem
2
,
X
is a smooth flexible affine factorial variety. In particular,
X
is homogeneous.

It follows from
[
14
, Theorem 2 and Proposition 19]
that there are precisely three pairwise non-isomorphic affine factorial 3-folds without non-constant invertible functions that are homogeneous spaces of algebraic groups: they are
{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
,
\SL(2)
and
\SL(2)/2I
, where
2I
is the binary icosahedral group. The last group has order
120
and is the universal perfect central extension of the simple group
A_{5}
.

By Proposition
3
, the hypersurface
X
is simply connected, so it is not isomorphic to
\SL(2)/2I
. Moreover, we know that
\pi_{2}(X)=0
and
\pi_{3}(X)\cong\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{ab}}(X_{0})
, where
X_{0}
is the curve
\{(x-1)xy+1=0\}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. This curve is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0,1\}
, so the group
\pi_{1}^{\mathrm{ab}}(X_{0})
is the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. We conclude that
X
is not isomorphic to
{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
, since the last variety is contractible. Moreover, the variety
\SL(2)
is homotopic to the real
3
-sphere, so the group
\pi_{3}(\SL(2))
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}
. This proves that
X
is not a homogeneous space of an algebraic group.
∎

It is an important problem to find algebraic invariants that would allow to distinguish between non-isomorphic smooth flexible affine factorial varieties of the same dimension.

5.
A remark on suspensions

Consider a suspension
X=\Susp(Y,f)
over a normal affine variety
Y
corresponding to a non-constant regular function
f
. Recall that
X
is given in the direct product
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}_{u,v}\times Y
by the equation
uv=f
. Denote by
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
the multiplicative group of the ground field
{\mathbb{K}}
.

The variety
X
carries a
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-action given by

t\cdot(u,v,y)=(tu,t^{-1}v,y).

The algebra of regular invariants
{\mathbb{K}}[X]^{{\mathbb{G}}_{m}}
coincides with the subalgebra
{\mathbb{K}}[Y]\subseteq{\mathbb{K}}[X]
and the quotient morphism

\pi\colon X\to X/\!/{\mathbb{G}}_{m}:=\Spec{\mathbb{K}}[X]^{{\mathbb{G}}_{m}}=Y

is the projection
(u,v,y)\mapsto y
. If
D
is the principal divisor
\divv_{Y}(f)
on
Y
, then the fiber
\pi^{-1}(y)
over
y\in Y\setminus\Supp(D)
is a hyperbola
uv=c
,
c\neq 0
, that is a closed
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-orbit on
X
. In turn, the fiber
\pi^{-1}(y)
over
y\in\Supp(D)
is the union
uv=0
of two lines that form three
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-orbits.

One may expect that this way we obtain a characterization of suspensions. Namely, let
X
be an affine variety with a
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-action and
\pi\colon X\to Y:=\Spec{\mathbb{K}}[X]^{{\mathbb{G}}_{m}}
be the quotient morphism. Assume that there is a principal divisor
D=\divv_{Y}(f)
on
Y
such that the fiber
\pi^{-1}(y)
with
y\in Y\setminus\Supp(D)
is one
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-orbit and
\pi^{-1}(y)
with
y\in\Supp(D)
is a union of two intersecting lines. Then
X
is the suspension
\Susp(Y,f)
over
Y
.

Let us show that this is not the case. Namely, let us consider the affine 3-fold
X
given as

\mathbb{V}(u_{1}v-y_{1}y_{2},\ u_{2}v-(y_{1}+1)y_{2},\ u_{1}(y_{1}+1)-u_{2}y_{1})\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{5}

with the
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-action

t\cdot(u_{1},u_{2},v,y_{1},y_{2})=(tu_{1},tu_{2},t^{-1}v,y_{1},y_{2}).

The quotient morphism
\pi
sends the point
(u_{1},u_{2},v,y_{1},y_{2})
to
(y_{1},y_{2})
. Hence
Y={\mathbb{A}}^{2}
and with the divisor
D=\divv_{Y}(y_{2})
we have the desired fiber structure. At the same time, the
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-action defines a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading on the algebra
A:={\mathbb{K}}[X]
and the homogeneous component

A_{1}=u_{1}{\mathbb{K}}[Y]+u_{2}{\mathbb{K}}[Y]

is not a cyclic
{\mathbb{K}}[Y]
-module. This proves that this
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-action on
X
cannot be induced by a suspension.
