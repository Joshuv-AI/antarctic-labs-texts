Chapman-Enskog expansion for chirally colliding disks
Ruben Lier, Paweł Matus

Abstract
We study a two-dimensional fluid of hard disks undergoing chiral, energy- and momentum-conserving collisions. We show that despite the microscopic breaking of time-reversal symmetry, the H H -theorem is obeyed, guaranteeing a relaxation towards equilibrium in the absence of external forces. In the dilute limit, a Chapman-Enskog expansion yields analytical expressions for the shear and odd viscosity and the thermal conductivity. Theoretical predictions are confirmed by nonequilibrium molecular dynamics simulations.

Introduction

The kinetic theory of gases was built on the image of colliding billiard balls, identical and symmetric, their interactions governed only by geometry, conservation laws, and discrete symmetries.
Although these microscopic encounters are reversible, an
arrow of time
emerges at macroscopic scales, breaking time-reversal symmetry. This breaking gives rise to dissipative transport such as shear viscosity, thermal conductivity, and the diffusivity of dyes.

chiral

Crucially, this chiral property modifies only the rate of left- versus right-handed collisions, while leaving the hard-disk collision rule itself unchanged. As a result, energy, momentum, and angular momentum are all conserved, meaning that additional degrees of freedom like the particles’ orientation are not necessary for a consistent description. Furthermore, although these chiral collisions violate parity and time-reversal symmetry,
H

With respect to this equilibrium state, we can expand the theory to obtain gradient corrections to the constitutive equations, allowing us to obtain first-order transport coefficients. III
Chapman-Enskog expansion

Due to their chiral nature, the collisions described in Sec.
II
break both parity and time reversal – two symmetries commonly assumed in the derivation of the
H
-theorem
degroot1984nonequilibrium
;
Cercignani1988
. What is more, the collision rules in our toy model do not follow from any Hamiltonian. Nevertheless, as long as the collisions conserve energy and momentum, the
H
-theorem must hold, as we rigorously prove in App.
B
. Therefore, the system possesses a well-defined thermal equilibrium in which the particles obey the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. This observation forms the foundation of the hydrodynamic approach to transport. The system is assumed to be in the vicinity of a
local
equilibrium state described by the distribution function

f_{0}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{n(\mathbf{x},t)}{2\pi{k_{\text{b}}}T(\mathbf{x},t)}\exp\left(-\frac{m(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}(x,t))^{2}}{2{k_{\text{b}}}T(\mathbf{x},t)}\right).

(9)

Here, velocity
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)
, disk density
n(\mathbf{x},t)
, and temperature
T(\mathbf{x},t)
are functions of time and space and are assumed to vary slowly in comparison to the typical relaxation time and the mean free path for collisions. Importantly, any distribution function of the form (
9
) is a solution of the collisional part of the Boltzmann equation:
\mathcal{C}[f_{0}]=0
, but the corresponding streaming term is generically nonzero:
\mathcal{L}[f_{0}]\neq 0
. The Chapman-Enskog expansion assumes that the actual solution to the Boltzmann equation can be approximated by a series of perturbations to
f_{0}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x},t)
:

f(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x},t)=f_{0}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x},t)\left[1+\Phi(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x},t)+\cdots\right],

(10)

where each successive term is proportional to higher gradients of
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)
and
T(\mathbf{x},t)
. The first-order correction
\Phi(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x},t)
is found by solving the equation

\ \mathcal{L}[f_{0}]=f_{0}C^{(1)}[\Phi],

(11)

where the right-hand side is the collision term
\mathcal{C}[f_{0}(1+\Phi)]
expanded to the linear order in
\Phi(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x},t)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&C^{(1)}[\Phi](\mathbf{v}_{1})=-\int d\mathbf{v}_{2}f_{0}(\mathbf{v}_{2})\sum_{\pm}\pm\frac{d(1\pm\varepsilon)}{2}\int_{0}^{\pm\pi}d\chi\sin(\chi/ 2 )|\mathbf{g}|\left[\Phi(\mathbf{v}_{1})+\Phi(\mathbf{v}_{2})-\Phi(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\prime})-\Phi(\mathbf{v}_{2}^{\prime})\right].\end{split}

(12)

In the equation above we dropped the arguments
\mathbf{x}
and
t
since they are equal for all the functions. Calculating the transport coefficients from (
11
) using the Chapman-Enskog theory involves a truncated expansion of
\Phi
in Sonine polynomials, which is worked out in App.
C
. We find the following viscosities
\eta_{{\text{e}},{\text{o}}}
and thermal conductivities
\kappa_{{\text{e}},{\text{o}}}
for a Sonine expansion truncated at order
N
:

\begin{split}\eta^{(N)}_{\text{e}}&=\eta^{(0)}_{\text{e}}N^{(N)}_{\text{e}}(\varepsilon),~~\eta^{(N)}_{\text{o}}=\eta^{(0)}_{\text{o}}N^{(N)}_{\text{o}}(\varepsilon),\\
\kappa^{(N)}_{\text{e}}&=\kappa^{(0)}_{\text{e}}K^{(N)}_{\text{e}}(\varepsilon),~~\kappa^{(N)}_{\text{o}}=\kappa^{(0)}_{\text{o}}K^{(N)}_{\text{o}}(\varepsilon),\end{split}

(13)

where the transport coefficients at zeroth order in a Sonine polynomial expansion are given by

\begin{split}\eta^{(0)}_{\text{e}}&=\frac{8}{d(16+\varepsilon^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{m{k_{\text{b}}}T}{\pi}},~~\eta^{(0)}_{\text{o}}=-\frac{2\varepsilon}{d(16+\varepsilon^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{m{k_{\text{b}}}T}{\pi}},\\
\kappa^{(0)}_{\text{e}}&=\frac{32{k_{\text{b}}}T}{d(16+\varepsilon^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{{k_{\text{b}}}T}{\pi m}},~~\kappa^{(0)}_{\text{o}}=-\frac{8\varepsilon{k_{\text{b}}}T}{d(16+\varepsilon^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{{k_{\text{b}}}T}{\pi m}},\end{split}

(14)

whereas the functions
N^{(N)}_{{\text{e}},{\text{o}}}(\varepsilon)
and
K^{(N)}_{{\text{e}},{\text{o}}}(\varepsilon)
are multiplicative corrections corresponding to the order at which the Sonine polynomial expansion is truncated. Taking
N=5
and
\varepsilon=0.5

\begin{split}N^{(5)}_{\text{e}}(0.5)&=1.02196,~~N^{(5)}_{\text{o}}(0.5)=1.02166,\\
K^{(5)}_{\text{e}}(0.5)&=1.02994,~~K^{(5)}_{\text{o}}(0.5)=1.02002.\end{split}

(15)

Since these multiplicative correction coefficients are close to one, the truncation error can be assumed to be small.

IV
Discussion

In this work, we introduce a new microscopic approach to odd viscosity, which relies neither on a background parity-odd field nor on the external inflow of angular momentum. Instead, the approach is based on hard disks with chiral effective radii. Because the collision rule obeys particle number, energy, and momentum conservation, it is possible to find the equilibrium state, with respect to which we expand to compute parity-even and parity-odd transport. The resulting expressions for viscosity are compared to a computation of viscosity using nonequilibrium molecular dynamics simulations. This work is the first one we are aware of where odd viscosity is calculated from first principles and coincides with the result of a many-particle simulation.

Appendix B
H
-theorem

To show that
H
-theorem holds, it is helpful to write the collision integral as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&C[f(\mathbf{v}_{1})]=\iiint d\mathbf{v}_{2}d\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{2}d\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{1}\left(W(\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2}|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{1},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{2})f(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{1})f(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{2})-W(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}_{2}^{\prime}|\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2})f(\mathbf{v}_{1})f(\mathbf{v}_{2})\right),\end{split}

(27)

with the transition probability
W(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{1},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{2}|\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2})
given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&W(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{1},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{2}|\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2})=|\mathbf{g}|\sum_{\pm}\pm\frac{d(1\pm\varepsilon)}{2}\int_{0}^{\pm\pi}d\chi\sin\left(\frac{\chi}{2}\right)\delta^{(2)}\left[\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\prime}-\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\prime}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\chi)\right]\delta^{(2)}\left[\mathbf{v}_{2}^{\prime}-\mathbf{v}_{2}^{\prime}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\chi)\right],\end{split}

(28)

Nonequilibrium molecular dynamics

In this Appendix we describe the nonequilibrium molecular dynamics computations that we perform to verify (

1
. We turn on shear using the SLLOD algorithm
Evans2007-dg
;
PhysRevA.30.1528
;
10.1063/1.2192775
;
han2021fluctuating
, which means that particles evolve according to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{x}_{i}&=u_{i}+\gamma y_{i},\qquad\dot{y}_{i}=v_{i}\,,\\
\dot{u}_{i}&=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j\neq i}f^{x}_{ij}-\gamma v_{i}+\alpha u_{i}\,,\quad\dot{v}_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}f^{y}_{ij}+\alpha v_{i}\,,\end{split}

(69)

where
\gamma
is the shear rate and
\mathbf{f}_{ij}
is the force on particle

due to collisions with particle

.
The coefficient
\alpha
is related to a thermostat mechanism introduced to prevent the system from heating up, which will be discussed shortly. We consider shear
\gamma
that acts with a constant magnitude during a single run, but its sign changes periodically, with the period long enough that the gas has enough time to thermalize. To extract viscosity, we compute

\displaystyle\eta=\left\langle\frac{\sigma_{xy}}{\gamma}\right\rangle\,,\quad\eta_{\text{o}}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\frac{\sigma_{xx}-\sigma_{yy}}{\gamma}\right\rangle\,,

(70)

where
\langle...\rangle
corresponds to an average over the run and
\bm{\sigma}
is the Irving-Kirkwood stress given by

\bm{\sigma}=-\frac{1}{A}\left[\sum_{i}m\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbf{f}_{ij}\mathbf{r}_{ij}\right]\,,

(71)

where

is the box area. Because of the simple hard disk nature of the collisions, the second term on the right hand side of (
71

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbf{f}_{ij}\mathbf{r}_{ij}=m\sum_{n_{c}}\frac{\Delta\mathbf{v}_{n_{c}}}{\Delta t}\Delta\mathbf{x}_{n_{c}}\,,

(72)

where
\Delta\mathbf{v}_{n_{c}}
is the change of velocity of the colliding particles caused by the

th collision of a time step of the numerical simulation and

is the width of this time step. Note that because

The shear induced by
\gamma

in (
