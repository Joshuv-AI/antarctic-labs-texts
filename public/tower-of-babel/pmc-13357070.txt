Si Ni Powder enhances chemotherapy efficacy in sleep-deprived breast cancer via modulation of the tumor microenvironment
Authors: Heyuan Jia, Ying Chen, Hao Cheng, Surui Yao, Qingwen Wang, Mengyuan Qian, Cun Ge, Zhaohui Huang, Yanyan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep disturbance, affecting up to 62% of breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy (CT), contributes to chemoresistance and tumor progression. However, the underlying mechanisms and potential interventions remain poorly defined. While Si Ni Powder (SNP) is known for its sleep-improving properties, its ability to mitigate CT resistance induced by sleep deprivation (SD) is unexplored. In this study, we aimed to investigate whether SNP could improve sleep, enhance the efficacy of doxorubicin (Dox) under SD conditions, and modulate tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling.MethodsAn orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer mouse model was used to evaluate the impact of SD on the efficacy of Dox and to assess the therapeutic potential of SNP. Tumor growth, serum melatonin (MT), and cortisol (Cor) levels were monitored. Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole-orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS) was employed to characterize the chemical constituents of SNP and to identify the compounds absorbed into tumor tissues. Transcriptomics, network pharmacology, and molecular docking were integrated to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.ResultsSD weakened the antitumor effect of Dox, which was effectively rescued by SNP. SD activated inflammatory signaling, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and lipid metabolic pathways, all of which were counteracted by SNP. A total of 168 chemical components were identified in SNP and 20 SNP-derived components were detected in tumor tissues. Integrated analyses revealed a multi-target regulatory network involving six key targets, including Stat3, Stat6, Ppara, Rxra, Ar, and Hes1. Molecular docking suggested strong interactions between these targets and five potential active compounds, namely neoeriocitrin, genistein, hesperetin, formononetin, and tetrahydroxyflavanone.ConclusionsSNP counteracts SD-induced chemoresistance via multi-faceted modulation of the TME, encompassing inflammatory suppression, normalization of the ECM, and reprogramming lipid metabolism.Keywords: Si Ni Powder (SNP), sleep deprivation, chemotherapy (CT), tumor microenvironment (TME), breast cancer

Highlight box

Key findings

• Sleep deprivation (SD) significantly reduced the antitumor efficacy of doxorubicin (Dox) in an orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer model.

• Si Ni Powder (SNP) restored chemotherapy (CT) sensitivity under SD conditions.

• Neoeriocitrin, genistein, hesperetin, formononetin, and tetrahydroxyflavanone may represent potential bioactive constituents of SNP.

• Mechanistically, SNP modulated the tumor microenvironment (TME) by suppressing inflammatory signaling, normalizing extracellular matrix remodeling, and regulating lipid metabolism through targets including Stat3, Stat6, Pparα, Rxrα, Ar, and Hes1.

What is known and what is new?

• Sleep disturbance is highly prevalent among breast cancer patients receiving CT and has been associated with poorer clinical outcomes. TME alterations, including inflammation and metabolic dysregulation, are known contributors to CT resistance.

• This study demonstrates that SD directly compromises the antitumor efficacy of Dox in vivo. It further reveals that SNP reverses SD-induced chemoresistance through multi-target regulation of TME pathways.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• SD may represent an important but underrecognized factor influencing CT efficacy.

• Interventions targeting sleep disorders may improve treatment outcomes in cancer patients.

• SNP may serve as a potential adjunctive strategy to enhance CT efficacy in patients experiencing SD, which warrants further clinical validation.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 16% of all new cancer cases (1). Although advances in screening and targeted therapies have improved outcomes, chemotherapy (CT) remains a cornerstone of breast cancer treatment (2). A significant clinical challenge during CT is the high prevalence of treatment-related symptom clusters, particularly sleep disturbances, which affect up to 62% of patients (3). Insomnia is reported more frequently in breast cancer than in other malignancies, driven by emotional distress, endocrine perturbations, and treatment toxicity (4,5).

While CT failure has traditionally been attributed to tumor-intrinsic mechanisms, increasing evidence highlights the critical role of host-related factors (6). Clinical studies have shown that breast cancer patients with sleep disturbance are more likely to undergo CT dose reduction due to treatment-related adverse events (7), suggesting that abnormal sleep may compromise CT tolerance and treatment intensity, thereby reducing therapeutic benefit and worsening prognosis. In addition, observational studies have reported that preoperative sleep disturbance, reduced sleep efficiency, and persistent sleep difficulties are associated with poorer overall survival in breast cancer patients (8). Together, these findings suggest that abnormal sleep may be closely associated with unfavorable outcomes in patients receiving CT. However, current evidence is derived mainly from epidemiological and observational studies, and direct experimental evidence demonstrating that sleep disturbance causally impairs CT efficacy remains lacking.

As a fundamental physiological process, sleep is essential for maintaining endocrine balance, immune function, and metabolic homeostasis (9). Chronic sleep deprivation (SD) disrupts circadian and neuroimmune balance, promotes systemic inflammatory remodeling, and weakens antitumor immunity (10). Increasing evidence suggests that insufficient sleep may contribute to tumor progression by reshaping the tumor microenvironment (TME) and impairing immune surveillance. In particular, SD has been associated with pro-tumoral macrophage polarization and inflammatory myeloid remodeling (11). Although these findings establish an association between sleep disturbance and tumor progression, how SD influences CT efficacy remains largely unclear, underscoring the need to clarify its biological impact and identify effective interventions.

The European Society for Medical Oncology Clinical Practice Guideline highlights insomnia in cancer patients as a common yet often neglected health issue. Conventional hypnotics such as dexzopiclone (Dex), estazolam (Est), and zolpidem (Zol) are commonly prescribed to alleviate sleep disorders (12). In recent years, the management of cancer related insomnia has increasingly shifted from sole reliance on hypnotic medications toward more comprehensive and multimodal interventions (13). Traditional Chinese medicine aligns with this paradigm, offering multi-component interventions capable of influencing integrated neuroendocrine, immune, and metabolic networks (14). Si Ni Powder (SNP), a classical traditional Chinese medicine prescription composed of Bupleurum chinense (Chaihu), Paeonia lactiflora (Shaoyao), Citrus aurantium (Zhishi), and Glycyrrhiza uralensis (Gancao), has long been used to soothe the liver, regulate mood, and improve sleep quality (15). Previous studies suggest that SNP and its major constituents possess anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and neurohormonal regulatory activities (16,17). However, whether sleep improvement can enhance CT efficacy remains unclear, and the role of SNP as a potential adjuvant for improving CT response has yet to be defined.

In this study, we established a breast cancer model with SD to evaluate whether improving sleep could enhance the antitumor efficacy of doxorubicin (Dox). We compared the effects of SNP and conventional hypnotics on sleep restoration and CT efficacy. Electroencephalography/electromyography (EEG/EMG) monitoring was used to confirm successful establishment of the SD model, while serum melatonin (MT) and cortisol (Cor) levels were measured to validate SD induction and assess treatment efficacy (18,19). Transcriptomic profiling was conducted to characterize the molecular alterations associated with SD and their reversal upon SNP treatment. The chemical constituents and tumor-penetrating constituents of SNP were analyzed using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole-orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS). Integrative analyses incorporating network pharmacology, transcription factor (TF) prediction, and molecular docking were subsequently applied to explore regulatory networks and potential targets influenced by SNP. These approaches aimed to provide mechanistic insights into the role of SD in CT response and to explore the potential of SNP as an adjuvant strategy in breast cancer management. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0517/rc).

Methods

Animals

Balb/c mice (female, 6 weeks old, 18–20 g) were purchased from Speford (Suzhou, China). Mice were maintained under standard laboratory conditions (22±2 ℃, 12 h light/dark cycle, 50%±10% humidity) with ad libitum access to food and water. The sample size was determined based on preliminary data and previous similar studies to ensure sufficient statistical power to detect treatment effects while minimizing animal use. Mice were euthanized immediately if they met any predefined humane endpoint criteria, including tumor volume >1,500 mm3, body weight loss >20%, or severe morbidity. No animals were lost during the study. Group allocation was performed by an independent investigator. The investigator administering treatments was aware of group allocation, while investigators responsible for outcome assessment and data analysis were blinded to the allocation. No potential confounding factors were identified in this study. All animal experiments were performed under a project license (No. JN.No20250228b0960430[074]) granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangnan University, in compliance with relevant ethical regulations for the care and use of animals.

Cell lines

4T1 breast cancer cells, obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Shanghai, China), were grown in RPMI-1640 medium (Eallbio, Beijing, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (VivaCell, Shanghai, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), under standard culture conditions of 37 ℃ and 5% CO2.

SD model based on breast cancer mice

Murine 4T1 breast cancer cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested and resuspended in a phosphate-buffered saline/Matrigel mixture (1:1, v/v). Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the right fourth mammary fat pad was sterilized with 70% ethanol. A total of 2×106 4T1 cells suspended in 40 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)/Matrigel mixture were injected using a fine needle. Tumor growth was monitored daily, and treatment was initiated when tumors reached approximately 50-80 mm3. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (length × width2)/2.

Mice were subjected to SD using the modified multiple platform method. The SD cages consisted of twelve small PVC platforms (3 cm in diameter) spaced 2 cm apart to restrict sustained sleep while allowing movement. Control cages contained six larger platforms (6 cm in diameter) placed close together, permitting normal sleep and mobility. Water levels in all cages were maintained approximately 1 cm below the platform tops. During the experimental period, mice were housed in these cages for 20 hours per day (from ZT6 to ZT2; 14:00–10:00 in the next day) to induce partial SD. The SD procedure began one week post-tumor inoculation, at a tumor volume of 50–80 mm3, and lasted for two weeks (Figure 1A).

Sleep-wake monitoring and analysis

Under avertin anesthesia (0.2 mL/10 g, i.p.) and carprofen analgesia (0.1 mL/10 g, s.c.), mice were placed in a stereotaxic frame. After scalp incision and skull exposure, the skull was leveled using Bregma and Lambda as references, with height differences maintained within 0.03 mm. Burr holes were drilled at predetermined sites, and custom EEG screw electrodes were implanted symmetrically. EMG electrodes were inserted into bilateral neck muscles and secured. Electrodes and skull were fixed with dental cement, and mice were allowed to recover for 3 days before recording. EEG/EMG monitoring was performed for 2 hours, and sleep-wake states were analyzed to determine total wake time.

Animal grouping and drug administration

A total of 122 mice were used in this study. Mice were randomly assigned to the following groups using a random number table: control group (Con): breast cancer-bearing mice received saline by oral gavage and intraperitoneal injection according to the corresponding treatment schedules; CT group (CT): breast cancer-bearing mice treated with intraperitoneal Dox (5 mg/kg) every three days for five cycles; SD group: breast cancer-bearing mice subjected to SD and administered saline; CTSD group: breast cancer-bearing mice subjected to SD and treated with the same Dox regimen as in the CT group; hypnotic-treated groups (CTSD + hypnotics): CTSD mice receiving daily oral gavage of hypnotics Dex (0.39 mg/kg), Est (0.13 mg/kg), or Zol (1.3 mg/kg); CTSD + SNP group: CTSD mice administered low (1.56 g/kg), medium (3.12 g/kg), or high (6.24 g/kg) doses of SNP extract via oral gavage. All treatments were administered once daily for 14 consecutive days. Body weight was recorded every three days as an indicator of systemic toxicity.

At the end of the treatment period, mice were fasted overnight and anesthetized with isoflurane. Blood samples were collected via retro-orbital puncture and centrifuged to obtain serum for MT and Cor assays. Tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed. Tumor tissues were collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 ℃ until molecular analyses.

Preparation of SNP

SNP was prepared according to the classical prescription, consisting of Chaihu (Liliangji, Suzhou, China), Shaoyao (Liliangji, Suzhou, China), Zhishi (Liliangji, Suzhou, China), and Gancao (Liliangji, Suzhou, China) in an equal weight ratio (1:1:1:1). Equal amounts of each herb were combined and soaked in 10 volumes of distilled water for 30 minutes, followed by decoction for 1.5 hours. The extract was filtered, and the remaining residue was reboiled in 8 volumes of water for an additional 1 hour. The two filtrates were then combined and concentrated to a final concentration of 0.62 g/mL using a rotary evaporator at 60 ℃. The resulting decoction was stored at 4 ℃ for short-term use or frozen at −80 ℃ and lyophilized for 96 hours to obtain the powdered extract.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Circulating Cor and MT levels in mice were assessed with ELISA kits (Mlbio, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After serum preparation by clotting and centrifugation, samples and standards were incubated with enzyme conjugate, developed with substrate, and terminated with stop solution, followed by absorbance measurement at 450 nm. Concentrations were calculated by interpolating from a 4-parameter logistic standard curve using ELISACalc software.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

A CCK-8 viability assay was performed by seeding 1,000 4T1 cells per well into a 96-well plate in triplicate. After cell adhesion, cells were treated with serial concentrations of Dex, Est, Zol, or SNP-containing serum (5%, 10%, 20%). SNP-containing serum was prepared by treating Balb/c mice with SNP decoction (0.31 g/mL) for 14 days and blood was collected at multiple time points (10 and 30 min; 1, 2, 4, 10, and 12 h) after the final dose and pooled for in vitro treatment. For the neoeriocitrin (Neo) viability assay, 293T cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well. After 24 h of incubation, cells were treated with different concentrations of Neo, including 0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 40 µM. Following treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was added to each well and the plate was incubated for 120 minutes at 37 ℃. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to assess living cell numbers.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. Following adhesion, cells were treated with test compounds, including various concentrations of Dex, Est, and Zol. Cultures were maintained for 10–14 days. Upon colony formation, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes at room temperature, stained with crystal violet for 30 minutes, gently rinsed, and photographed for analysis.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from tumors treated with the indicated drugs using the TRIzol Purification Kit. RNA purification, reverse transcription, library construction and sequencing were performed at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups, a significance threshold of P value <0.05 and |fold change| >2 was employed. These genes were then visualized with a volcano plot using Majorbio Cloud (20) and subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis via Metascape (21).

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was prepared using RNA isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription was carried out to generate cDNA with 4× HisyGo All-in-One qRT Red SuperMix (Vazyme, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using 2× ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) on a ViiATM 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin, and relative transcriptional changes were calculated accordingly. Primer information is provided in Table S1.

Western blot (WB)

To extract total protein, tumors were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer. Equal amounts of protein extracts were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the following primary antibodies: anti-matrix metalloproteinase-3 (Mmp3; 1:1,000; Zenbio, China), anti-pyruvate carboxylase (Pcx; 1:1,000; Zenbio, China), anti-interleukin-17a (IL17a; 1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; 1:10,000; ABclonal, Wuhan, China), and anti-β-actin (1:10,000; ABclonal, China). On the next day, membranes were treated with goat polyclonal secondary antibody to rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:5,000; Abmart, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using the SuperPico ECL chemiluminescence kit (Vazyme, China) and captured with a ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS

SNP decoction was subjected to protein precipitation using methanol, followed by centrifugation and dilution. Tumor samples harvested from SNP-treated mice were homogenized in an 80% methanol/water solution, subjected to prolonged vortexing, and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant for analysis.

Compound separation was performed using an UHPLC system equipped with a HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 µm) maintained at 40 ℃. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B), eluted at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min under a specified gradient program. The injection volume was 6.0 µL.

Analysis was performed on a hybrid Q-Orbitrap HRMS with a HESI-II source operating in both positive and negative ionization modes. Data were acquired in full mass spectrometry (MS)/data-dependent tandem mass spectrometry (dd-MS2) mode. Full scan MS data were collected at a resolution of 70,000, with the most intense precursor ions (top 10) selected for fragmentation at resolution 17,500 using stepped collision energies.

Raw data were processed using Progenesis QI 3.0 software for peak picking and alignment. Metabolite identification was conducted by matching experimental data against a proprietary reference substance database (TCM Pro 2.0) and a custom theoretical database, based on retention time, precursor mass accuracy, MS/MS spectral match, and isotope distribution.

Identification of key components and targets

The potential targets of SNP compounds were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction platform. The resulting target lists were compiled and harmonized using the tidyverse package (v2.0.0) in RStudio. Separately, TF enrichment analysis was conducted on the transcriptomic sequencing data using the JASPAR database. Finally, the predicted compound targets and transcriptomically inferred TFs were integrated, and their intersection was identified using the ggvenn package (v0.1.10) in R for subsequent analysis.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed to investigate the interactions between SNP compounds and six key target proteins. The three-dimensional structures of the ligands were retrieved from ChemDraw (version 23.1.1), and the target protein structures were obtained from the UniProt database (22) or generated using SWISS-MODEL (23). Docking simulations were conducted using AutoDockTools (version 1.5.7), and the resulting binding energies were used to evaluate the stability and affinity of the ligand-protein complexes. The docking conformations were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL software (version 4.6.0).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To assess the transcriptional activity of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (Stat3), cells were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured to 70–80% confluence. Cells were co-transfected with Stat3-Reporter and Renilla plasmids using a transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h of transfection, cells were treated with vehicle, 2.5 µM Neo, or 5 µM Neo for another 24 h. The cells were then harvested and lysed, and firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were detected using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit. Stat3 reporter activity was expressed as the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity and normalized to the vehicle group. Each assay was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analyses

All experiments were repeated at least three times (biological replicates). The code used in this study is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated before applying parametric tests. Unless otherwise specified, all datasets were normally distributed and met the assumption of homogeneity of variance. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Comparisons involving multiple groups were performed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as P<0.05, with the following markers: *P<0.05, **P<0.01, and ***P<0.001. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

SD weakens the anti-tumor efficacy of CT in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice

To determine whether SD compromises CT efficacy, a modified multiple-platform method was employed (Figure 1A). Successful establishment of the SD model was first confirmed by EMG/EEG monitoring, which demonstrated a significant increase in wake time during the 2-hour recording period (Figure 1B,1C). Consistently, endocrine assessment further validated the model, showing markedly decreased serum MT levels and elevated Cor concentrations in SD mice. In contrast, CT alone had no significant effect on either hormone (Figure 1D,1E).

SD significantly impaired the antitumor effect of the chemotherapeutic agent Dox (Figure 1F), as evidenced by a decline in the tumor growth inhibition rate from 46% in the CT-only group to 35% in the CT and SD group (Figure 1G). Consistently, tumor growth curves confirmed that SD significantly weakened the antitumor effect of Dox (Figure 1H). Although Dox induced a moderate reduction in body weight, SD did not further exacerbate this effect (Figure 1I). Collectively, these data demonstrate that SD compromises the therapeutic efficacy of Dox in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice.

Improving sleep enhances CT efficacy in SD tumor-bearing mice

Given that SD attenuated CT efficacy, we next investigated whether improving sleep could rescue this effect. In a mouse model of 4T1 breast cancer subjected to SD, we evaluated a traditional Chinese formula: SNP, as well as three clinically used hypnotics: Dex, Est, and Zol (Figure 2A). SNP and three hypnotics effectively reversed the hormonal imbalance induced by SD, as evidenced by increased MT and reduced Cor levels (Figure 2B,2C). These improvements were accompanied by significant decreases in tumor volume and tumor weight in Dox-treated mice (Figure 2D-2F), without altering body-weight trajectory (Figure 2G).

A dose-response evaluation further demonstrated that all three SNP doses normalized MT and Cor levels (Figure 2H,2I) and significantly suppressed tumor growth (Figure 2J-2L). Among them, the medium dose showed the strongest tumor inhibition, as reflected by tumor burden measurements. Meanwhile, none of the SNP treatments affected body weight (Figure 2M). These results demonstrate that improving sleep, particularly through SNP, effectively enhances CT efficacy in a sleep-deprived breast cancer mouse model.

Sleep-improving agents do not directly affect tumor proliferation or CT response

To clarify whether the observed antitumor benefit originated from direct cytotoxicity or indirectly via sleep restoration, in vitro and in vivo assays were performed. CCK-8 assays indicated that none of the four sleep-improving agents inhibited 4T1 cell proliferation at the tested concentrations (Figure 3A,3B). Colony formation assays using the three hypnotics yielded consistent results (Figure 3C).

In vivo, the most effective agents, SNP and Dex, were further tested in a mouse model of 4T1 breast cancer without SD (Figure 3D). Both agents had no significant effect on tumor weight and tumor growth, and their co-administration with Dox did not alter the chemotherapeutic outcome (Figure 3E-3G). Collectively, these data confirm that neither SNP nor Dex possesses intrinsic antitumor activity, suggesting that their therapeutic effect arises from sleep improvement rather than direct tumor suppression.

SNP broadly regulates inflammation, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and lipid metabolism, while Dex primarily affects inflammation

To elucidate the mechanism of SNP and Dex, transcriptomic profiling was performed on tumors from different groups. DEGs (|fold change| >2, P<0.05) were first identified among groups. Volcano plot analysis revealed a pronounced alteration of the transcriptomic landscape in response to SD, with a total of 230 upregulated and 299 downregulated genes (Figure 4A). Additional comparisons with the SNP- or Dex-treated groups highlighted distinct expression patterns (Figure 4B,4C).

GO enrichment analysis of biological processes was then conducted. Comparative analysis revealed that SD primarily upregulated pathways involved in inflammatory response, ECM organization, and lipid metabolism. In contrast, Dex treatment mainly downregulated the inflammatory response pathway, whereas SNP treatment not only suppressed the inflammatory response pathway but also downregulated ECM and lipid metabolic pathways (Figure 4D-4F). Heatmaps visualized representative gene clusters associated with these pathways (Figure 4G,4H).

To further identify key regulatory genes underlying SD-associated transcriptional alterations at the pathway level, representative genes from the significantly enriched pathways were first subjected to RT-qPCR validation. The screening criteria were defined as genes that were significantly upregulated under SD conditions but showed a marked downregulation trend following SD intervention. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that Il17a exhibited the most pronounced expression change among inflammation-related pathways (Figure 4I), Mmp3 showed the most significant upregulation among ECM-related pathways (Figure 4J), and Pcx displayed the most prominent alteration within lipid metabolism associated pathways (Figure 4K). Based on these findings, the protein expression levels of Il17a, Mmp3, and Pcx were further examined by WB analysis (Figure 4L). These results suggest that SNP enhances CT responsiveness by regulating inflammatory signaling, ECM and metabolic homeostasis within the TME.

Phytochemical profiling of SNP: identification of constituents present in tumor tissues

UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS revealed a chemically rich profile of SNP. Representative base peak intensity (BPI) chromatograms acquired in negative and positive ionization modes are shown (Figure 5A,5B). Based on precise molecular weights and comparisons with standards or databases, a total of 168 chemical constituents were identified or tentatively characterized (supplement file 1 available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-0517-1.xlsx). Among the herbs, Zhishi contributed 80 compounds, Gancao 50, Shaoyao 48, and Chaihu 47 (Figure 5D). This diverse phytochemical composition underscores the multi-component nature of SNP. We applied UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS to tumor tissues from SNP-treated mice to profile the chemical constituents present in tumor tissues. Representative BPI chromatograms of the tissues from blank and SNP-treated mice are presented in Figure 5E-5H. A total of 20 compounds were identified or tentatively characterized based on accurate mass, MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and comparison with reference standards or databases (supplement file 2 available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-0517-2.xlsx). These compounds encompass a wide range of structural classes, with flavonoids (10 compounds), prenol lipids (4 compounds), and isoflavonoids (3 compounds) being the predominant groups, highlighting the multi-component nature of SNP in the TME (Figure 5I). Zhishi was the primary contributor (11 compounds), followed by Gancao and Shaoyao (6 compounds each), and Chaihu (4 compounds) (Figure 5J). The structural diversity of these compounds (Figure 5K) suggests that SNP may contribute to therapeutic effects through a multi-component, multi-target mechanism in vivo.

Network pharmacology and molecular docking reveal SNP’s multi-target mechanism

To elucidate the mechanism of action of SNP, we integrated network analysis and computational docking to map its interactions from chemical components to TFs. A comprehensive interaction network was constructed to map the relationships among SNP, its identified constituents, and their predicted protein targets (Figure 6A). In the network, the central purple node represents SNP, encircled by four green nodes corresponding to its constituent herbs. These are connected to 20 orange nodes representing SNP-derived constituents, which in turn link to green nodes representing their target proteins, highlighting the multi-target regulatory effects of SNP.

To delineate the relationship between SNP’s predicted targets and TFs regulating Il17a, Pcx and Mmp3 from prior analyses, we constructed an interaction network (Figure 6B). The analysis revealed a set of shared targets (orange nodes) jointly associated with the three core elements (green nodes) and SNP (purple node), alongside other distinct targets (blue nodes). These shared targets included retinoid X receptor alpha (Rxrα), Stat3, signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (Stat6), Hes family bHLH transcription factor 1 (Hes1), androgen receptor (Ar), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (Pparα). This identification of shared targets indicates a potential mechanism of convergent action at the transcriptional level. We further used a Venn diagram to delineate the specific number of targets and their overlapping relationships (Figure 6C). Molecular docking was performed to assess the binding potential between key TFs and SNP-derived constituents. The heatmap shows binding energies (Figure 6D), supporting the multi-target modulation as the mechanistic basis of SNP’s effects.

Molecular docking of representative active compounds with key targets

Among tested compounds, Neo exhibited strong binding to Stat3 with an affinity of −9.0 kcal/mol, forming multiple hydrogen bonds with SER319, LYS348, LYS244, and THR456 (Figure 7A). Genistein showed the highest affinity toward Ar (−9.5 kcal/mol), mainly stabilized by hydrogen bonding with ARG752 (Figure 7B). Hesperetin bound to Rxrα with an affinity of −9.0 kcal/mol, interacting with GLN437 (Figure 7C). Neo also exhibited favorable binding to Pparα (−8.7 kcal/mol) through multiple hydrogen bonds with HIS416, ASP418, LYS148, LYS222, CYS105, and ARG326 (Figure 7D). Formononetin showed moderate binding to Hes1 with an affinity of −7.6 kcal/mol, forming a single hydrogen bond with ILE235 (Figure 7E). Finally, tetrahydroxyflavanone interacted with Stat6 with an affinity of −7.2 kcal/mol, stabilized by hydrogen bonds with ARG510, GLN479, and LYS480 (Figure 7F). These docking results indicate that specific SNP-derived compounds can target key signaling regulators involved in inflammation, metabolism, and ECM remodeling, thereby contributing to SNP’s effect. Based on the strong predicted binding affinity of Neo to Stat3 and the important role of Stat3 in inflammation-related transcriptional regulation, the Neo-Stat3 interaction was selected for further experimental validation. To determine suitable concentrations for subsequent assays, cell viability was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay (Figure 7G). Compared with the control group, 2.5 and 5 µM Neo did not significantly affect cell viability, whereas concentrations of 10 µM or higher significantly reduced cell viability, with more evident inhibitory effects at 20 and 40 µM. Therefore, 2.5 and 5 µM were selected for subsequent functional experiments to minimize the potential influence of cytotoxicity. Stat3 transcriptional activity was assessed using a dual-luciferase reporter assay. Compared with the vehicle-treated group, Neo treatment decreased Stat3 reporter activity, with a stronger inhibitory effect observed at 5 µM than at 2.5 µM (Figure 7H). These results preliminarily suggest that Neo may interfere with Stat3-mediated transcriptional activity at concentrations that do not significantly affect cell viability. Discussion

Our study provides the first experimental evidence that SD compromises the antitumor efficacy of Dox in a breast cancer model. Improving sleep quality-either through conventional hypnotics or the traditional formula SNP-significantly enhanced the CT efficacy of Dox. Importantly, these interventions did not exhibit intrinsic cytotoxicity toward tumor cells in vitro or in vivo, suggesting that their effects were mediated through systemic and microenvironmental modulation rather than direct tumor killing. These findings underscore sleep quality as a critical host-related determinant of CT efficacy and highlight SNP as a promising holistic adjuvant for breast cancer treatment.

Transcriptomic analyses revealed that SD profoundly reprogrammed the TME, including activation of inflammatory pathways, ECM remodeling, and dysregulated lipid metabolism. Notably, Il17a signaling—a key inflammatory mediator—was upregulated under SD, consistent with reports showing that SD exacerbates Il17-mediated inflammation response. Mechanistically, SD-induced stress responses have been reported to enhance pro-inflammatory signaling cascades, leading to increased expression of cytokines such as IL-17a, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, which further activate downstream pathways including NF-κB and STAT3 (24). Among these, IL-17a plays a central role in amplifying inflammatory responses and promoting tumor progression through STAT3 activation. In the present study, SNP treatment significantly reduced IL-17a expression, suggesting that SNP may attenuate SD-induced inflammation, potentially by suppressing the STAT3/IL-17a signaling axis. In addition to inflammation, SD markedly enhanced ECM remodeling, as evidenced by the upregulation of Mmp3. Mechanistically, inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB and STAT3 can drive the expression of matrix metalloproteinases, thereby promoting matrix degradation and tumor invasion (25). SNP treatment partially reversed Mmp3 expression, indicating that it may mitigate ECM remodeling by modulating upstream inflammatory signaling. Furthermore, SD induced metabolic reprogramming, particularly in lipid metabolism, as reflected by increased expression of Pcx. Dysregulated lipid metabolism is known to support tumor growth by providing energy and biosynthetic substrates, while also contributing to therapy resistance (26). SNP treatment downregulated Pcx expression, suggesting a role in restoring metabolic balance under SD conditions. Collectively, these alterations indicate that SD compromises CT effectiveness through a coordinated disruption of Il17a-mediated inflammation, Mmp3-dependent ECM remodeling, and Pcx-associated metabolic reprogramming. SNP treatment effectively reversed these perturbations, downregulating Il17a, Mmp3, and Pcx expression, thereby restoring TME homeostasis and enhancing Dox efficacy.

SNP exerts its effects through a multi-component, multi-target network that aligns with its traditional clinical use. UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap HRMS analysis identified 168 chemical constituents in SNP, with 20 bioavailable compounds detected in tumor tissue. These compounds encompass diverse structural classes, including flavonoids, isoflavonoids, prenol lipids, coumarins, and fatty acyls. Flavonoids have been reported to regulate oxidative stress, cell-cycle progression, apoptosis, and autophagy, which may contribute to the suppression of tumor cell proliferation and invasiveness (27). Isoflavones such as genistein have also demonstrated antiproliferative activity in prostate cancer cells through modulation of oncogenic signaling pathways (28). Moreover, prenol lipids have been associated with anticancer effects in prostate cancer models, likely through modulation of metabolic pathways (29). Increasing evidence further suggests that TCM can improve CT efficacy by remodeling the TME. For example, rhein enhances Dox activity in triple-negative breast cancer by inhibiting cancer-associated fibroblasts (30). In addition, Taohong Siwu Decoction improves Dox efficacy by promoting tumor vascular normalization (31). In this context, our study extends previous findings by providing experimental evidence that sleep disturbance can directly impair CT efficacy and by suggesting that SNP may restore chemosensitivity through sleep-centered, host-mediated remodeling of the TME, thereby linking sleep intervention with systemic and microenvironmental regulation in breast cancer CT.

Through network pharmacology analyses and molecular docking, we identified five compounds including Neo, genistein, hesperetin, formononetin, and tetrahydroxyflavanone, as potential interactors of key TFs, including Stat3, Stat6, Rxrα, Pparα, Ar, and Hes1. Neo exhibited strong binding to Stat3 and Pparα, while genistein bound to Ar and hesperetin to Rxrα, indicating a multi-node regulatory network. To provide preliminary functional support for this prediction, we further selected the Neo-STAT3 interaction for experimental validation. The dual-luciferase reporter assay demonstrated that Neo reduced Stat3 reporter activity. These findings preliminarily suggest that Neo may interfere with STAT3-mediated transcriptional activity, supporting the predicted involvement of the Stat3 axis in SNP-mediated regulation. In addition, these factors have been validated in prior literature as modulators of inflammation, lipid reprogramming, and ECM remodeling. Inflammatory pathways appear modulated through Stat3, Stat6, Pparα, and Hes1 (32-35); lipid metabolism is influenced via Rxrα, Pparα, and Ar (36-38); and ECM remodeling may be regulated by Stat3, Stat6, Ar, and Hes1 (35,39-41). In stress-related breast cancer models, SNP has been shown to inhibit Cor-induced GRP78 upregulation, thereby reducing tumor stemness (42). These data collectively delineate a coordinated pharmacological network through which SNP enhances CT responsiveness by modulating inflammatory, lipid-metabolic, and ECM-associated pathways rather than exerting direct cytotoxicity.

This study has several limitations. First, although EEG/EMG monitoring was conducted, it was limited to a 2-hour recording period rather than continuous 24-hour surveillance, which may have limited the accuracy and completeness of SD assessment (10). Second, this study focused on one breast cancer subtype; therefore, whether these findings generalize to other tumor types or clinical settings requires further validation. Third, only the Neo-Stat3 interaction was selected for preliminary functional validation. In addition, the specific in vivo contribution of each constituent to the overall therapeutic effects of SNP remains unclear, and the efficacy of these constituents, either alone or in combination, was not systematically evaluated.

Despite these limitations, our findings may have broader implications beyond this specific experimental setting. Sleep disturbance is highly prevalent in patients with cancer and is increasingly recognized as a clinically relevant factor associated with endocrine dysregulation, inflammatory activation, metabolic disturbance, and altered treatment response. In this context, our results suggest that SD may function as an important host-related determinant of CT efficacy by reshaping the TME rather than directly altering the cytotoxic action of chemotherapeutic agents. These mechanisms may also be relevant to other tumor types and therapeutic contexts in which sleep disruption is common. Nevertheless, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings directly to humans or other species, given interspecies differences in physiology, tumor biology, and treatment complexity. Further validation in additional animal models, breast cancer subtypes, and clinical cohorts will be necessary to establish the generalisability and translational relevance of these observations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that SD compromises the CT efficacy of Dox by inducing Il17a-driven inflammation, Mmp3-associated ECM remodeling, and Pcx-related lipid metabolic dysregulation, collectively reshaping the TME. These alterations may be associated with transcriptional regulation mediated by Stat3, Stat6, Pparα, Rxrα, Ar, and Hes1. SNP improved sleep and enhanced Dox responsiveness by partially reversing SD-induced TME remodeling, potentially through modulating the transcriptional activity of key TFs rather than through direct cytotoxicity. Molecular docking suggested that SNP-derived constituents, including Neo, genistein, hesperetin, formononetin, and tetrahydroxyflavanone, may interact with these regulatory TFs. Although the Neo-Stat3 interaction was preliminarily validated in this study, the remaining predicted interactions require further experimental confirmation.

From a translational perspective, SNP may have potential as an adjunctive strategy for improving CT response under sleep-disturbed conditions by modulating host–tumor interactions rather than directly targeting tumor cells. Nevertheless, the present evidence is limited to mouse models. The contribution of individual active constituents, the precise regulatory mechanisms, and the clinical efficacy and safety of SNP in patients with cancer-related sleep disturbance remain to be clarified. Therefore, SNP should be regarded as a potential, rather than confirmed, adjuvant approach, and further studies in additional tumor models and clinical settings are warranted. Five compounds (Neo, genistein, hesperetin, formononetin, tetrahydroxyflavanone) exhibited strong binding to core regulatory factors (Stat3/Stat6/Pparα/Rxrα/Ar/Hes1).
