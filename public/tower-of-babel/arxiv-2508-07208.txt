What One Cannot, Two Can: Two-Layer Transformers Provably Represent Induction Heads on Any-Order Markov Chains
Chanakya Ekbote cekbote@mit.edu, Marco Bondaschi, Nived Rajaraman, Jason D. Lee, Michael Gastpar, Ashok Vardhan Makkuva, Paul Pu Liang - Massachusetts Institute of Technology - École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne - University of California, Berkeley - Princeton University

Abstract
In-context learning (ICL) is a hallmark capability of transformers, through which trained models learn to adapt to new tasks by leveraging information from the input context. Prior work has shown that ICL emerges in transformers due to the presence of special circuits called induction heads. Given the equivalence between induction heads and conditional k k -grams, a recent line of work modeling sequential inputs as Markov processes has revealed the fundamental impact of model depth on its ICL capabilities: while a two-layer transformer can efficiently represent a conditional 1 1 -gram model, its single-layer counterpart cannot solve the task unless it is exponentially large. However, for higher order Markov sources, the best known constructions require at least three layers (each with a single attention head) — leaving open the question: can a two-layer single-head transformer represent any k th k^{\text{th}} -order Markov process? In this paper, we precisely address this and theoretically show that a two-layer transformer with one head per layer can indeed represent any conditional k k -gram. Thus, our result provides the tightest known characterization of the interplay between transformer depth and Markov order for ICL. Building on this, we further analyze the learning dynamics of our two-layer construction, focusing on a simplified variant for first-order Markov chains, illustrating how effective in-context representations emerge during training. Together, these results deepen our current understanding of transformer-based ICL and illustrate how even shallow architectures can surprisingly exhibit strong ICL capabilities on structured sequence modeling tasks. 2.1
Markov Processes and the Conditional
k
-gram Model

(a)

The conditional
k
-gram model (Def.
2.1
). It first (1) identifies the positions in the sequence where the preceding
k
tokens match the current context (blue), and (2) returns the empirical distribution over the symbols (green) that follow these matched positions.

(b)

k\textsuperscript{th}
-order induction head (Def.
3

k

A
k\textsuperscript{th}
-order Markov process is a time-homogeneous stochastic process over a finite state space, where the probability of the next symbol depends only on the most recent
k
symbols in the sequence
[
21
]
. More formally, it is defined as follows:

Definition 1.
(
k^{\text{th}}
-order Markov Chains) A stochastic process
x_{0:T}\coloneqq x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{T}
over a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
is said to be a
k\textsuperscript{th}
-order Markov Chain with transition kernel
\pi(\cdot)
if, for all
k\leq n\leq T
, the following condition holds:

\displaystyle\pi(x_{n+1}=s\mid x_{n},x_{n-1},\ldots,x_{0})=\pi(x_{n+1}=s\mid x_{n},x_{n-1},\ldots,x_{n-k+1}),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

We now define the empirical equivalent of the kernel
\pi
, the

conditional
k
-gram model
:

Definition 2.
(Conditional
{k}
-gram model) Given a sequence
x_{0:T}\coloneqq x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{T}
in
\mathcal{S}^{T+1}
, the conditional
k
-gram model
\widehat{\pi}_{k}(s\mid x_{T},x_{T-1},\ldots,x_{0})
computes the empirical probability of observing a symbol
s\in\mathcal{S}
matching the last
k
tokens, i.e.

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}_{k}(s\mid x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{T})\coloneqq\frac{\sum\limits_{i=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(x_{i}=s,\,x_{i-1}=x_{T},\,x_{i-2}=x_{T-1},\ldots,\,x_{i-k}=x_{T-k+1})}{\sum\limits_{i=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(x_{i-1}=x_{T},\,x_{i-2}=x_{T-1},\ldots,\,x_{i-k}=x_{T-k+1})},

assuming the denominator is non-zero. Thus conditional
k
-gram can be interpreted as a simple count-based estimator of the transition kernel
\pi
, using the in-context count estimate for the predictive probability distribution.

Data generation (Random Markov sequences).
To empirically generate the input data, i.e. random Markov sequences of order
k
, we first sample each row of the transition matrix
\pi
independently from a Dirichlet prior with parameter vector
\mathbf{1}
, which is equivalent to an uniform distribution on the
S
-dimensional probability simplex. The initial
k
tokens of each sequence are sampled uniformly from
\mathcal{S}^{k}
, and the remaining tokens are generated according to the sampled kernel
\pi
. To sample an input batch, we first sample multiple independent transition kernels, generate sequences from each, and then aggregate these sequences to form a batch for training or evaluation.

Warm Up: Construction with Two Heads in Layer One, One in Layer Two

As a warm up, in this section, we prove our first main result that we can represent
k^{\text{th}}
-order Markov processes with a
2
-layer,
2
-head transformer. Towards the same, we recall that the
k^{\text{th}}
-order in-context counting estimator, i.e. conditional
k
-gram model, defined in Sec.
2.1
is closely related to the classical Laplacian smoothing, which in turn is the optimal Bayes estimator for the next-token predictive distribution
[
12
,
28
]
. In view of this fact, in order to estimate the optimal conditional distribution of each token in-context, it is natural to ask:
how does a transformer represent this conditional
k
-gram model?
To this end,
Rajaraman et al. 2024
introduces the notion of a
k
-th order induction head
. This mechanism enables the attention layer of a transformer to assign at each time step the highest attention weight to past tokens whose length-
k
context matches that of the current token. For the sake of completeness, we recall the formal definition.

Definition 4.
(
k\textsuperscript{th}
-order induction head) An attention layer with a single head is said to implement a
k\textsuperscript{th}
order induction head if, for any input sequence

(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})\in\mathcal{S}^{T+1}
, and for any fixed index
i\leq T
, the attention score
\text{att}_{T,i}
at position
i
is maximized if and only if its preceding
k
tokens exactly match the final
k
tokens of the sequence. That is,
\text{att}_{T,i}
is maximized if and only if
x_{i-j}=x_{T-j+1}
for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
.

The salience of this higher-order induction head is immediately reflected by the fact that such a mechanism is indeed what is precisely needed to implement the conditional
k
-gram estimator from Def.
2.1
. Capitalizing on this,
Rajaraman et al. 2024
proves the best known result for higher-order Markov processes, through a
3
-layer,
1
-head transformer architecture, which we recall below to motivate our main result:

Theorem 1.
(Theorem 4 in
Rajaraman et al. 2024
): The conditional
k
-gram model can be represented using a transformer with three layers, each containing a single attention head. The layers are separated by MLP blocks and include relative positional encodings as well as layer normalization. The embedding dimension scales as
\mathcal{O}(S)
.

Proof sketch.

We provide a brief outline of the proof of Theorem
3
. For each position
n
in the sequence
s_{0:T}
, the first attention layer effectively learns a hard attention map concentrated on the indices
\{n,n-1,\ldots,n-k+1\}
. Specifically, within a subspace of its embedding space, this layer computes
\textbf{u}_{n}=\left({\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}\cdot e^{S}_{x_{n-i}}}\right)/\left({\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}}\right)
, with attention weights
\operatorname{attn}_{n,i}=3^{i}/\left({\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}3^{j}}\right)
for
i\in\{n,n-1,\ldots,n-k+1\}
, and zero otherwise. Intuitively, this
\textbf{u}_{n}
allows to capture the past-
k
context starting at position
n
. Similarly, the second attention layer focuses on the positions
\{n-1,n-2,\ldots,n-k\}
, and computes
\textbf{v}_{n}=\left({\sum_{i=1}^{k}3^{i}\cdot e^{S}_{x_{n-i}}}\right)/\left({\sum_{i=1}^{k}3^{i}}\right)
, capturing the context shifted by one position. After these two layers, the embedding at position
n
contains both
\textbf{v}_{n}/\|\textbf{v}_{n}\|_{2}
and the normalized vector
\textbf{u}_{n}/\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}
, assuming appropriate choices of embedding dimensions, query/key/value matrices, positional encodings, and MLP layers with layer normalization. In particular, the architecture can be configured to compute the
\ell_{2}
-norm via layer norm, as detailed in App.
A.1
. The third attention layer then functions as a
k
-th order induction head: at position
n
, the attention score is made proportional to the cosine similarity
{\langle\textbf{u}_{T},\textbf{v}_{n}\rangle}/\left({\|\textbf{u}_{T}\|_{2}\|\textbf{v}_{n}\|_{2}}\right)
. This quantity equals 1 when
\textbf{u}_{T}=\textbf{v}_{n}
, and is strictly less than 1 otherwise. As the softmax temperature tends to infinity, or equivalently, as the attention logits are scaled by a constant tending to infinity, the model approaches the behavior of a conditional
k
-gram estimator. Hence, the third layer effectively implements a
k
-th order induction head.
∎

We are now ready to prove our first main result. Specifically, we show that the aforementioned construction with
3
layers and a single head can be adapted to a two-layer counterpart with two heads in the first layer and one head in the second.

Theorem 2.
(Two heads in the first layer, one in the second): The conditional
k
-gram model can be represented using a transformer with two layers, the first containing two attention heads and the second containing a single attention head. The layers are separated by MLP blocks and include relative positional encodings as well as layer normalization. The embedding dimension is
6S+3
.

Remark 1
.

(Depth-width tradeoff)
Thm.
3
thus reveals an interesting tradeoff between depth (number of attention layers) and width (maximum number of attention heads per layer). While our construction here has both depth and width two, its counterpart in Thm.
3
instead has depth three and width one. Thus our new architecture trades off depth with an additional attention head, thereby increasing the width.

Proof sketch.

We provide a brief sketch of the proof for Theorem
3
, using the same notation as that of the Theorem
3
, and focusing on the key differences between the two architectures.

Layer One.

For each position
n
in the sequence
s_{0:T}
, the first attention head in the first layer learns a focused attention pattern over the positions
\{n,n-1,\ldots,n-k+1\}
. In a subspace of the embedding space, this head computes the representation
\textbf{u}_{n}=\left({\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}\cdot e^{S}_{x_{n-i}}}\right)/\left({\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}}\right)
. On the other hand, the second attention head in this layer focuses on the preceding context, attending to positions
\{n-1,n-2,\ldots,n-k\}
, and computes the representation
\textbf{v}_{n}=\left({\sum_{i=1}^{k}3^{i}\cdot e^{S}_{x_{n-i}}}\right)/\left({\sum_{i=1}^{k}3^{i}}\right)
. After this layer, the embedding at position
n
contains both the vectors
\textbf{u}_{n}/\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}
and
\textbf{v}_{n}/\|\textbf{v}_{n}\|_{2}
, achieved by choosing an appropriate choice of embedding dimensions, query, key, and value matrices, positional encodings, and MLPs with layer normalization.

Layer Two.

The second attention layer now plays the role of an induction head, analogous to the third layer in Theorem
3
. At position
n
, this head computes an attention score that is proportional to the cosine similarity
{\langle\textbf{u}_{T},\textbf{v}_{n}\rangle}/{\|\textbf{u}_{T}\|_{2}\|\textbf{v}_{n}\|_{2}}
. This score attains the maximum value of one when
\textbf{u}_{T}=\textbf{v}_{n}
, and strictly less otherwise. As the temperature parameter in the softmax becomes large, or equivalently as the inner product is multiplied by a large constant, the attention mechanism approaches the behavior of a conditional
k
-gram estimator. Therefore, this two-layer transformer, with two heads in the first layer and a single head in the second, is sufficient to represent the conditional
k
-gram model. We refer to App.
A
for the full proof.
∎

While Thm.

can a

In this section, we precisely address this gap and realize a

-layer,
1

-order Markov processes for any

The key idea behind our approach is to leverage the MLP—and specifically, the non-linearities such as ReLU and LayerNorm—to isolate symbols at particular positions that are useful for estimating induction heads. This contrasts with prior constructions, which primarily focused on the attention mechanism while underutilizing the role of the MLP and its non-linear components. As a result, our construction highlights how these non-linear MLP elements can play a critical role in enabling in-context learning, demonstrating their significance. We now present our main result.

Theorem 3.
(Two-layer, single-head construction): The conditional
k
-gram model can be represented using a transformer with two layers, both containing a single attention head. The layers are separated by MLP blocks and include relative positional encodings as well as layer normalization. The embedding dimension is
6S+3
.

in the sequence
s_{0:T}
, the attention head in the first layer attends to the preceding context, focusing on positions
\{n-1,n-2,\ldots,n-k\}
, and computes the representation
\textbf{v}_{n}=\left({\sum_{i=1}^{k}3^{i}\cdot e^{S}_{x_{n-i}}}\right)/\left({\sum_{i=1}^{k}3^{i}}\right)
, with attention weights
\operatorname{attn}_{n,i}=3^{i}/\left({\sum_{j=1}^{k}3^{j}}\right)
for
i\in\{n-1,\ldots,n-k\}
, and zero otherwise. This matches the optimal attention pattern for Layer -1 illustrated in Fig.
2
.

MLP.

In contrast to previous constructions, the MLP situated between the two attention layers plays a central role in our proof and is structured to reconstruct the complementary representation
\textbf{u}_{n}=\left({\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}\cdot e^{S}_{x_{n-i}}}\right)/\left({\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}}\right)
, which corresponds to a hard attention pattern over the positions
\{n,n-1,\ldots,n-k+1\}
. Recall that this
\textbf{u}_{n}
was constructed in
3
thanks to a second attention head, absent in our architecture.

Our main idea to tackle this issue is to isolate the one-hot embedding
e^{S}_{x_{n-k}}
from
\textbf{v}_{n}
computed in the first layer. This is achieved by embedding the entire one-hot vocabulary within the weight matrix of the first MLP layer. When combined with a suitable bias and followed by a ReLU activation and layer normalization, this configuration filters out all but the component aligned with
x_{n-k}
, thereby producing
e^{S}_{x_{n-k}}
. At this stage, the embedding also retains the representation
\textbf{v}_{n}
, computed by the first attention layer. Subsequent layers, along with appropriately configured skip connections, implement a linear transformation that combines
e^{S}_{x_{n-k}}
,
\textbf{v}_{n}
, and the token embedding
e^{S}_{x_{n}}
, the latter of which is introduced via a skip connection. Since
\textbf{v}_{n}
and
e^{S}_{x_{n-k}}
occupy orthogonal subspaces of the embedding, and all required components are explicitly available, this construction yields the intermediate representation
\textbf{u}_{n}=\left({e^{S}_{x_{n}}/\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}}\right)+\left({3e^{S}_{x_{n-k}}\textbf{v}_{n}}\right)/\left({\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}}\right)-\left({3^{k}\cdot e^{S}_{x_{n-k}}}\right)/\left({\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}}\right),
which simplifies exactly to the desired form
\textbf{u}_{n}=\left({\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}\cdot e^{S}_{x_{n-i}}}\right)/\left({\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}3^{i}}\right)
. With appropriately chosen weights, layer normalization, and skip connections, the resulting embedding at position
n
encodes both
\textbf{u}_{n}/\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}
and
\textbf{v}_{n}/\|\textbf{v}_{n}\|_{2}
.

Layer Two.

As in the second layer of Theorem
3
, the attention mechanism in the second layer is configured to act as a
k
th
-order induction head by computing an attention score at position
n
that is proportional to the cosine similarity
\left({\langle\textbf{u}_{T},\textbf{v}_{n}\rangle/\|\textbf{u}_{T}\|_{2}\|\textbf{v}_{n}\|_{2}}\right)
. This score reaches its maximum value of one when
\textbf{u}_{T}=\textbf{v}_{n}
, and is strictly less than one otherwise. As the softmax temperature tends to infinity, or equivalently as the attention logits are scaled by a constant that tends to infinity, the resulting distribution converges to that of a conditional
k
-gram estimator. App.
B
details the complete proof. ∎

Importance of non-linearities.
This construction demonstrates that non-linear components such as ReLU activations and layer normalization are not merely auxiliary, but play a critical role in enabling the transformer architecture to express higher-order inductive structures that are essential for in-context learning.

5
Gradient Descent Analysis

Thm.
4
establishes a representation result showing that a two-layer transformer with single attention heads can implement the conditional
k
-gram model. However, this does not guarantee that current optimization algorithms such as gradient descent (or its stochastic variants) can recover such a solution in practice. To this end, in this section we focus on first-order Markov chains and show that gradient descent on a reduced variant of our two-layer transformer indeed learns an induction head, and thereby the in-context conditional
1
-gram. On this note, we would like to emphasize that first-order Markov analysis a critical first-step and a building block for challenging higher-order analysis
[
20
]
.

Building towards our result, we quickly recall a few recent works
[
10
,
20
]
that also analyze the gradient dynamics on first-order Markov data with simplified transformer architectures. Specifically,
Edelman et al. 2024
study an attention-only linear transformer without MLPs and softmax operations, while
Nichani et al. 2024
analyze a disentangled transformer, with residual and attention streams split. On the other hand, our transformer architecture in Def.
2.2
consists of relative positional encodings, softmax attention, MLP with ReLU activations, and layer normalization. While non-linearities such as ReLU and layer normalization play a crucial role in our representation result, as illustrated in Sec.
4
, at the same time, they make the gradient analysis challenging and intractable even for a simple first-order Markov setup.

To tackle this, we study a reduced model wherein we only treat the salient components of our two-layer architecture in Thm.
4
, such as positional encodings and attention layer, as parameters, and treating the rest as approximately optimal. This enables for a a faithful reproduction of the ICL phenomenon exhibited by the parent model, whilst being tractable. This is akin to the reparameterization strategies of
Makkuva et al. 2024b
and
Nichani et al. 2024
for gradient-flow/gradient-descent analysis. Alternatively, this can also be viewed as a form of
good parameter initialization
, similar to that of
[
10
,
20
]
. We now start with our technical assumptions on the data distribution and the transformer architecture. We use notation from Sec.
2.2
, dropping the head-identifying superscript.

Assumptions.

(i)

Data distribution assumptions: Following
[
20
]
, we assume the prior over transition kernels enforces non-degeneracy, positive transitions, and constant mean (see App.
C
).

(ii)

The positional embeddings used for the keys in the first attention layer are scalar-valued, i.e.
\textbf{p}_{n}^{(1,K)}\propto\,p_{n}
, where
p_{n}
is a scalar.

(iii)

\mathbf{W}_{Q,K,V}^{(1)}
and
\textbf{p}^{(1,V)}
are chosen so that the attention weight for position
n-i
is given by
\operatorname{att}_{n,n-i}^{(1)}=\exp(p_{i})/\left({\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})}\right)\in\{0,1\}
, and the corresponding value output is
\textbf{v}_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\operatorname{att}_{n,n-i}^{(1)}\cdot\,e_{x_{n-i}}^{S}
.

(iv)

We assume the MLP is optimal—i.e., it outputs the correct
\textbf{u}_{n}
even from suboptimal
\textbf{v}_{n}
; this holds for first-order Markov chains via skip connections.

(v)

\mathbf{W}_{Q,K}^{(2)}
are chosen such that the attention in this layer is defined as
\operatorname{att}_{T,i}^{(2)}=\exp\left({a_{2}\cdot\langle\textbf{v}_{i},\textbf{u}_{T}\rangle}\right)/\left({{\sum_{j=0}^{T}\exp(a_{2}\cdot\langle\textbf{v}_{j},\textbf{u}_{n}\rangle)}}\right)
, where
a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
is the attention scalar.

(vi)

\mathbf{W}_{V}^{(2)}
is chosen so that the final logit
\operatorname{logit}_{T}=\sum_{i=0}^{T}\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}e_{x_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
,

Loss function.

We minimize the cross-entropy loss for next-token prediction
[
20
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}_{\pi},\ x_{0:T}\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\pi\left(x_{T+1}=s\mid x_{0:T}\right)\log\left(\operatorname{logit}_{T}(s)+\varepsilon\right)\right],

(1)

where
\varepsilon>0
is a small additive constant,
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\cdot)
denotes the prior over Markov transition kernels, and
\operatorname{logit}_{T}(s)
is the logit corresponding to the symbol
s
.

Training algorithm.
We denote the set of trainable parameters as
\theta=(\textbf{p},a_{2})
, where the positional scalars
\textbf{p}=[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{T}]^{T}
and
a_{2}
is the attention scalar. Similar to
[
20
]
, we adopt a two-stage training procedure. (i) In the first stage, we optimize the positional scalars
p
using gradient descent for
T_{1}
steps with learning rate
\eta_{1}
, (ii) in the second stage, we freeze
p
and train only
a_{2}
for an additional
T_{2}
steps using a separate learning rate
\eta_{2}
. A key distinction in our setup is how we treat layer normalization during training. While in the first stage we omit all layer norms in the MLP, during the second step we reintroduce them. This stage-wise enables a tractable theoretical analysis. We refer to App.
C
for further details. We now present our main result:

Theorem 4.
(Convergence of the training Algorithm): Suppose that Assumptions (i)- (vi) hold and that the Markov sequence length
T
satisfies
T+1\geq\mathrm{poly}(\gamma^{-1},S)
for some constant
\gamma>0
. Then there exist
\varepsilon>0
, learning rates
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
, and step counts
T_{1},T_{2}
, such that the output of the above two-stage training algorithm,
\hat{\theta}=(\{\hat{p}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{T},\hat{a}_{2})
, satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta})-\mathcal{L}^{*}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log(T+1)}{(T+1)^{c\gamma}},

for a constant
c
independent of
(\gamma,S)
, and the optimal loss
\mathcal{L}^{*}\coloneqq-\mathbb{E}\left[(1/S)\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\pi(s^{\prime}\mid s)\log\pi(s^{\prime}\mid s)\right]
.

Thus Thm.
5
illustrates that the transformer model trained via the two-stage algorithm achieves near-optimal loss asymptotically. Furthermore, we can also show that
\hat{\theta}
approximates the conditional
1
-gram model, i.e. first-order induction head, with vanishing error as sequence length grows (see App.
C
). For completeness, perturbative analysis of the architecture consisting of two attention heads in the first layer and one in the second (see Sec.
3
) for any-order Markov chains can be found in (App.
D
).

6
Conclusion

In this paper, improving over prior three-layer constructions, we show that a two-layer, single-head transformer can represent any
k
-th order Markov process. Thus our result gives the tightest known depth characterization for in-context learning of conditional
k
-grams. Additionally, we provide a gradient descent analysis of how such representations emerge during training on first-order Markov data. While these results deepen our theoretical understanding of ICL and show that compact transformer architectures can be both expressive and learnable, several open questions remain. In particular, fully characterizing the learning dynamics for higher-order processes is an important avenue of future research. Further, we view our contributions as a first step toward understanding the fundamental limits of ICL in compact transformer architectures, offering not only a tractable setting for theoretical analysis, but also a potential pathway toward more efficient model designs.

A.1
Modification of the Layer Norm

Following
Rajaraman et al. 2024
(Appendix C.1 in
Rajaraman et al. 2024
), we use a modified version of layer normalization to extract the
\ell_{2}
-normalized direction of the embedding
\textbf{x}_{n}
. Specifically, we augment the embedding by concatenating it with its negation to form
[\textbf{x}_{n};-\textbf{x}_{n}]
, which ensures that the mean of the features is zero. Applying standard layer normalization to this vector yields
\sqrt{d}\cdot\textbf{x}_{n}/\|\textbf{x}_{n}\|
in the first
d
components, since the standard deviation of the augmented vector is
\|\textbf{x}_{n}\|/\sqrt{d}
. To recover the unit-normalized embedding
\textbf{x}_{n}/\|\textbf{x}_{n}\|
, we simply divide the first
d
components of the output by
\sqrt{d}
, and extract the first
d
components. Throughout this work, we assume this normalization and scaling step is handled implicitly, and we interpret layer normalization as returning the normalized embedding direction. In order to avoid explicitly repeating this transformation at each layer, we redefine the layer norm to implicitly return
\textbf{v}/\|\textbf{v}\|_{2}
for any input vector
v
. Hence,
\textbf{v}/\|\textbf{v}\|_{2}=\mathrm{LN}(\textbf{v})
, where,
\mathrm{LN}(\cdot)
denotes the modified layer norm operator. Note that this modified definition is used throughout this paper.

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot Z_{i}Z_{n}+\frac{\kappa\cdot\langle\textbf{v}_{i},\textbf{u}_{n}\rangle}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}\cdot\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot Z_{i}Z_{n}+2-\kappa\cdot\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2}

Applying the softmax function, we obtain:

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}=\frac{\exp{(\kappa Z_{i}Z_{n}+2\kappa-\kappa\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2})}}{\sum_{j=0}^{n}\exp{(\kappa Z_{j}Z_{n}+2\kappa-\kappa\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{j}}{\|\textbf{v}_{j}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2})}}

We can see that for all
k+1\leq i\leq n
,
\textbf{v}_{i}=\textbf{u}_{n}
if and only if the local context around position
i
matches that of position
n
, i.e.,

\{x_{i-1-j}=x_{n-j}\text{ for all }j=0,\ldots,k\}
. Moreover, for any
k+1\leq i\leq n
, if
\textbf{v}_{i}\neq\textbf{u}_{n}
, then the normalized vectors are separated by a non-negligible margin:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|_{2}\geq\frac{1}{3^{k}}.

Note that while this gap is small, it is strictly non-zero. Recall that
Z_{i}=\frac{3^{k+1}}{5}
for
i\geq k+1
, and
Z_{i}\leq\frac{3^{k}}{5}
otherwise. As a result, the attention mechanism favors positions
i
such that
\textbf{v}_{i}=\textbf{u}_{n}
and
i\geq k+1
. In the limit as
\kappa\to\infty
, this results in the following attention pattern:

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}^{(2)}_{n,\cdot}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{I}_{n}),

where
\mathcal{I}_{n}
denotes the set of all indices
i\in\{k+1,\ldots,n\}
whose local context matches that of position
n
, formally defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}:=\left\{i\in\{k,\ldots,n\}\,\middle|\,x_{i-1-j}=x_{n-j}\text{ for all }j=0,\ldots,k\right\}.

Moreover,
\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
denotes the uniform distribution over the indices in
\mathcal{I}_{n}
; that is, each position
i\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
receives equal attention weight, and all other positions receive zero. We now choose the value projection matrix in the second attention layer as:

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{V}^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&I_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.

With this choice, the output of the second attention layer becomes:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}+\sum_{i=0}^{n}\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}\\
e_{x_{i}}^{S}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}\\
e_{x_{i}}^{S}\end{bmatrix}.

For the output linear layer, we choose:

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{o}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{S\times(5S+3)}&I_{S\times S}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\textbf{b}_{o}=\textbf{0}.

This yields the output logits:

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{n}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}e_{x_{i}}^{S}=\sum_{i=k}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{I}\left(\forall\,1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i-j}=x_{n-j+1}\right)}{\sum_{i^{\prime}=k}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left(\forall\,1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i^{\prime}-j}=x_{n-j+1}\right)}\cdot e_{x_{i}}^{S}.

Finally, by adjusting the notation and setting
n=T
to reflect prediction at the final time step, we obtain:

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{T}(x_{T+1})=\frac{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}\left(\forall\,0\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1}\right)}{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}\left(\forall\,1\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1}\right)},

which precisely computes the conditional
k
-gram probability.

With these choices, and adding a layer normalization after the output of the key linear layer and the query linear layer

\displaystyle=\frac{2\langle\textbf{v}_{i},\textbf{u}_{n}\rangle}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}\cdot\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle=2-\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2}

Taking the softmax with a temperature parameter
\kappa

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}=\frac{\exp{(2\kappa-\kappa\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2})}}{\sum_{j=0}^{n}\exp{(2\kappa-\kappa\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{j}}{\|\textbf{v}_{j}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2})}}

Note that although this gap is small, it remains non-zero. In particular, as
\kappa\to\infty
, the resulting attention pattern approaches

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}^{(2)}_{n,\cdot}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{I}_{n}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}),

where
\mathcal{I}_{n}
denotes the set of indices
i\in\{k,\ldots,n\}
whose local context matches that of position
n
. Formally,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\left\{i\in\{k,\ldots,n\}\,\middle|\,x_{i-1-j}=x_{n-j}\text{ for all }j=0,\ldots,k\right\}.

In contrast,
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
captures earlier positions
i\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
whose (normalized) key vectors are aligned with the normalized query at position
n

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{i\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\,\middle|\,\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}=\frac{\textbf{k}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\}.

On choosing,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{V}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&I_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.

We obtain,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(2)}=\textbf{x}_{n}^{(2)}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}\\
e_{x_{i}}^{S}\end{bmatrix}=\textbf{x}_{n}^{(3)}+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{n}|+|\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}\\
e_{x_{i}}^{S}\end{bmatrix}.

For the output linear layer, we choose:

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{o}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{S\times(5S+3)}&I_{S\times S}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\textbf{b}_{o}=\textbf{0}.

which results in,

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{n}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{n}|+|\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}e_{x_{i}}^{S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=k}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i-j}=x_{n-j+1})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}=k}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i^{\prime}-j}=x_{n-j+1})+\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}\cdot e_{x_{i}}^{S}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i\in{\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}}\frac{\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i-j}=x_{n-j+1})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}=k}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i^{\prime}-j}=x_{n-j+1})+\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}\cdot e_{x_{i}}^{S}

Now, by substituting
n=T
, and aiming to predict the next symbol given the current context, we obtain:

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{T}(x_{T+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall 0\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})}{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})+\hat{\mathcal{I}_{k}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sum_{i\in{\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}}\langle{e_{T+1}^{S}},{e_{i}^{S}}\rangle}{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})+\hat{\mathcal{I}_{k}}}

This corresponds to a
biased conditional

k
-gram model, where the bias originates from the second term in the expression above. Notably, this bias vanishes in the limit as
T\to\infty
. The reason is that the numerator of the second term remains constant with respect to sequence length, since
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
is fixed and does not scale with
T
. In contrast, the denominator, given by
\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall\,1\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})
, grows with
T
, assuming the underlying Markov chain is irreducible (i.e., every state is reachable from every other state). Consequently, under this assumption of irreducibility, the influence of the second term diminishes as
T
becomes large, yielding the approximation for a sufficiently large
T)
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{T}(x_{T+1})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall 0\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})}{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})}

Two-Layer Single-Head Construction

(Two-layer, single-head construction). We assert that there exists a two-layer, single-head attention Transformer model

, which can be configured in one of two equivalent ways: (1) with three MLP layers placed between the first and second attention blocks, or (2) with two MLP layers between the attention blocks, but with layer normalization applied to the query and key projections in the second attention block, such that:

\quad f_{\theta}(s_{1:T})_{s^{\prime}}\approx\pi\bigl(s^{\prime}\mid s_{1:T}\bigr)\quad\forall\,(s_{1:T},\,s^{\prime})\sim\pi,\pi\sim\mathbb{P}_{\pi}.

Note that
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}

\pi

It is important to note that in both configurations, the input embeddings and the first layer (

{\textbf{0}},&\text{if }i>k\end{cases},

Letting

\textbf{0}_{2S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{2S\times 4S}\\
\end{bmatrix}

The output of the attention layer (with the residual connection) is,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c
}Z_{n}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2}&e_{x_{n}}^{S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}&\textbf{v}_{n}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}\end{array}\right]^{T},

It is easy to verify that

for

, and that

. This distinction will be useful later, as the value of

B.1
Three Layer MLP followed by the Second Attention Layer

Figure 3

The MLP Architecture

The architecture of the MLP is illustrated in Fig.
3
. We now explain how the first layer of the MLP extracts the symbol

from

. To achieve this, the first layer of the MLP is designed as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}&=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{(3+S)\times(3+3S)}&\textbf{0}_{(3+S)\times(S)}&\textbf{0}_{(3+S)\times(2S)}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times(3+3S)}&\textbf{A}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times 2S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times(3+3S)}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times 2S}\\
\textbf{0}_{3S\times(3+3S)}&\textbf{0}_{3S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3S\times 2S}\end{bmatrix}\\
\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}&=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{1\times(3+S)}&-\frac{(3^{k-1}-1)}{2\cdot C_{\texttt{attn}}}\cdot\textbf{1}_{1\times S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\\
\text{where, }\textbf{A}&=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}^{T}\\
e_{2}^{T}\\
\vdots\\
e_{S}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\\
\end{split}

Note that on computing,
\hat{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}=\mathrm{LN}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}\left({\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}\ \widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}+\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}}\right)}\right)
, effectively isolates the component associated with the symbol at position
n-k
in the sequence. This, in turn, allows us to identify the value of
e^{S}_{x_{n-k}}
for a given

. Before demonstrating this explicitly, we first state a useful fact: in a triadic sum representation, the coefficient corresponding to position
n-k
is always greater than the sum of all coefficients at earlier positions. To illustrate this, consider the scenario in which several consecutive symbols, specifically those from positions
n-1
to
n-k+1

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}_{n,n-k}

\displaystyle=\frac{3^{k-1}}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\operatorname{att}_{n,n-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{3^{i-1}}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}=\frac{3^{k-1}-1}{2\cdot C_{\texttt{attn}}}

\displaystyle\text{Hence, }\operatorname{att}_{n,n-k}

\displaystyle>\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\operatorname{att}_{n,n-i}

The operation
\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}\ \widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}+\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}
computes the dot product of each symbol with
\textbf{v}_{n}
and subtracts
\frac{3^{k-1}}{2\cdot C_{\texttt{attn}}}

n-k
th

n-k
th

n-k
th

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}\ \widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}+\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{1\times(3+S)}&\textbf{c}_{1\times S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

Note that in
\widetilde{\textbf{c}}

n-k
; all other entries are zero. After applying the skip connection, the final result becomes:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}+\mathrm{LN}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}(\hat{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)})}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c|c
}Z_{n}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2}&e_{x_{n}}^{S}&\widetilde{\textbf{c}}_{1\times S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times S}&\textbf{v}_{n}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}\end{array}\right]^{T}

The second layer of the MLP is designed to compute
\textbf{u}_{n}

\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}

\textbf{v}_{n}

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{3\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}\\

\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&{\frac{1}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}\cdot I_{S\times S}}&{-\frac{3^{k}}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}\cdot\textbf{A}^{T}_{S\times S}}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&{\frac{3}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}I_{S\times S}}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}\\

\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&{\textbf{0}_{S\times S}}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}\\

\displaystyle\textbf{u}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}e_{x_{n}^{S}}+\frac{3}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}\textbf{v}_{n}-\frac{3^{k}}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}e^{S}_{x_{n-k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}e_{x_{n}}^{S}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{3^{i}}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}e_{x_{n}-i}^{S}-\frac{3^{k}}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}e_{x_{n-k}}^{S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{3^{i}}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}\cdot e^{S}_{x_{n-i}}

Hence, passing this through the normalization and with the skip connections we get:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}+\mathrm{LN}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}\left({\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}+\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c|c}Z_{n}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2}&e_{x_{n}}^{S}&{\textbf{0}_{n}}&\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}&\textbf{v}_{n}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}\end{array}\right]^{T}

Finally, on defining the third layer as follows:

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{3\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times 4S}\\
\textbf{0}_{3S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3S\times 4S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&I_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times 4S}\\
\textbf{0}_{2S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{2S\times 4S}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\textbf{0}_{(3+6S)\times 1}

Passing the input to the layer through the non-linearities along with the skip connections, we get:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}+\mathrm{LN}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}\left({\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}+\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c}Z_{n}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2}&e_{x_{n}}^{S}&{0_{1\times S}}&\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}&\textbf{v}_{n}&\frac{\textbf{v}_{n}}{\|\textbf{v}_{n}\|_{2}}&\textbf{0}_{1\times S}\end{array}\right]^{T}

Layer 2.

In this layer, all relative position encodings are set to
0
, and we use the following query and key matrices for the attention mechanism in the second layer:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{W}_{Q}^{(2)}&=\sqrt{\kappa}\begin{bmatrix}1&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}_{S\times(2+3S)}&I_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\\
\textbf{W}_{K}^{(2)}&=\sqrt{\kappa}\begin{bmatrix}1&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&{\textbf{0}_{S\times(2+4S)}}&{I_{S\times S}}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

With these choices in place, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\langle(\textbf{W}_{K}^{(2)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{i,\texttt{mlp}}^{(3)}),(\textbf{W}_{Q}^{(2)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(3)})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot Z_{i}Z_{n}+\frac{\kappa\cdot\langle\textbf{v}_{i},\textbf{u}_{n}\rangle}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}\cdot\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle=\kappa\cdot Z_{i}Z_{n}+2-\kappa\cdot\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2}

Applying the softmax function, we obtain:

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}=\frac{\exp{(\kappa Z_{i}Z_{n}+2\kappa-\kappa\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2})}}{\sum_{j=0}^{n}\exp{(\kappa Z_{j}Z_{n}+2\kappa-\kappa\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{j}}{\|\textbf{v}_{j}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2})}}

We can see that for all
k+1\leq i\leq n
,
\textbf{v}_{i}=\textbf{u}_{n}
if and only if the local context around position
i
matches that of position
n
, i.e.,

\{x_{i-1-j}=x_{n-j}\text{ for all }j=0,\ldots,k\}
. Moreover, for any
k+1\leq i\leq n
, if
\textbf{v}_{i}\neq\textbf{u}_{n}
, then the normalized vectors are separated by a non-negligible margin:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|_{2}\geq\frac{1}{3^{k}}.

Note that while this gap is small, it is strictly non-zero. Recall that
Z_{i}=\frac{3^{k+1}}{5}
for
i\geq k+1
, and
Z_{i}\leq\frac{3^{k}}{5}
otherwise. As a result, the attention mechanism favors positions
i
such that
\textbf{v}_{i}=\textbf{u}_{n}
and
i\geq k+1
. In the limit as
\kappa\to\infty
, this results in the following attention pattern:

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}^{(2)}_{n,\cdot}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{I}_{n}),

where
\mathcal{I}_{n}
denotes the set of all indices
i\in\{k+1,\ldots,n\}
whose local context matches that of position
n
, formally defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}:=\left\{i\in\{k,\ldots,n\}\,\middle|\,x_{i-1-j}=x_{n-j}\text{ for all }j=0,\ldots,k\right\}.

Moreover,
\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
denotes the uniform distribution over the indices in
\mathcal{I}_{n}
; that is, each position
i\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
receives equal attention weight, and all other positions receive zero. We now choose the value projection matrix in the second attention layer as:

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{V}^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&I_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.

With this choice, the output of the second attention layer becomes:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}+\sum_{i=0}^{n}\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}\\
e_{x_{i}}^{S}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}\\
e_{x_{i}}^{S}\end{bmatrix}.

For the output linear layer, we choose:

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{o}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{S\times(5S+3)}&I_{S\times S}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\textbf{b}_{o}=\textbf{0}.

This yields the output logits:

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{n}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}e_{x_{i}}^{S}=\sum_{i=k}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{I}\left(\forall\,1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i-j}=x_{n-j+1}\right)}{\sum_{i^{\prime}=k}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left(\forall\,1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i^{\prime}-j}=x_{n-j+1}\right)}\cdot e_{x_{i}}^{S}.

Finally, by adjusting the notation and setting
n=T
to reflect prediction at the final time step, we obtain:

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{T}(x_{T+1})=\frac{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}\left(\forall\,0\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1}\right)}{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}\left(\forall\,1\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1}\right)},

which precisely computes the conditional
k
-gram probability.

Note that on computing,

for a given

. Before demonstrating this explicitly, we first state a useful fact: in a triadic sum representation, the coefficient corresponding to position
n-k

n-1
to
n-k+1

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}_{n,n-k}

\displaystyle=\frac{3^{k-1}}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\operatorname{att}_{n,n-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{3^{i-1}}{C_{\texttt{attn}}}=\frac{3^{k-1}-1}{2\cdot C_{\texttt{attn}}}

\displaystyle\text{Hence, }\operatorname{att}_{n,n-k}

\displaystyle>\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\operatorname{att}_{n,n-i}

The operation
\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}\ \widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}+\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}
computes the dot product of each symbol with
\textbf{v}_{n}
and subtracts
\frac{3^{k-1}}{2\cdot C_{\texttt{attn}}}
from each coefficient. This ensures that only the
n-k
th

n-k

n-k
th

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{LN}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}(\hat{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)})}\right)

Note that in
\widetilde{\textbf{c}}

n-k
; all other entries are zero. After applying the skip connection, the final result becomes:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}+\mathrm{LN}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}(\hat{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)})}\right)

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}

\textbf{u}_{n}

,

In this layer, all the relative position encodings are set as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{W}_{Q}^{(2)}&=\begin{bmatrix}0&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\

\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\\

\textbf{0}&{\textbf{0}_{S\times(2+4S)}}&{I_{S\times S}}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

With these choices, and adding a layer normalization after the output of the key linear layer and the query linear layer

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathrm{LN}(\textbf{W}_{K}^{(2)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{i,\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}),\mathrm{LN}(\textbf{W}_{Q}^{(2)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2-\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2}

Taking the softmax with a temperature parameter
\kappa

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}=\frac{\exp{(2\kappa-\kappa\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2})}}{\sum_{j=0}^{n}\exp{(2\kappa-\kappa\left\|\frac{\textbf{v}_{j}}{\|\textbf{v}_{j}\|_{2}}-\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\|^{2})}}

Note that although this gap is small, it remains non-zero. In particular, as
\kappa\to\infty

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}^{(2)}_{n,\cdot}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{I}_{n}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}),

where
\mathcal{I}_{n}
denotes the set of indices
i\in\{k,\ldots,n\}
whose local context matches that of position
n
. Formally,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\left\{i\in\{k,\ldots,n\}\,\middle|\,x_{i-1-j}=x_{n-j}\text{ for all }j=0,\ldots,k\right\}.

In contrast,
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
captures earlier positions
i\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
whose (normalized) key vectors are aligned with the normalized query at position
n

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{i\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\,\middle|\,\frac{\textbf{v}_{i}}{\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}}=\frac{\textbf{k}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}\right\}.

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{V}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&I_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{o}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{S\times(5S+3)}&I_{S\times S}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\textbf{b}_{o}=\textbf{0}.

which results in,

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{n}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{n}|+|\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{n}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}e_{x_{i}}^{S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=k}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i-j}=x_{n-j+1})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}=k}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i^{\prime}-j}=x_{n-j+1})+\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}\cdot e_{x_{i}}^{S}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i\in{\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}}\frac{\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i-j}=x_{n-j+1})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}=k}^{n}\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq j\leq k,\ x_{i^{\prime}-j}=x_{n-j+1})+\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}\cdot e_{x_{i}}^{S}

Now, by substituting
n=T
, and aiming to predict the next symbol given the current context, we obtain:

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{T}(x_{T+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall 0\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})}{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})+\hat{\mathcal{I}_{k}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sum_{i\in{\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}}}\langle{e_{T+1}^{S}},{e_{i}^{S}}\rangle}{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})+\hat{\mathcal{I}_{k}}}

This corresponds to a
biased conditional

k
-gram model, where the bias originates from the second term in the expression above. Notably, this bias vanishes in the limit as
T\to\infty
. The reason is that the numerator of the second term remains constant with respect to sequence length, since
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
is fixed and does not scale with
T
. In contrast, the denominator, given by
\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall\,1\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})
, grows with
T
, assuming the underlying Markov chain is irreducible (i.e., every state is reachable from every other state). Consequently, under this assumption of irreducibility, the influence of the second term diminishes as
T
becomes large, yielding the approximation for a sufficiently large
T)
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{T}(x_{T+1})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall 0\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})}{\sum_{n=k}^{T}\mathbb{I}(\forall 1\leq i\leq k,\ x_{n-i}=x_{T-i+1})}

Appendix C
Two-Layer Single-Head Gradient Analysis

As discussed in Sec.
5
, we analyze a simplified model that retains only the key components of the two-layer architecture described in Thm.
4
—specifically, the positional encodings and the attention layer—while treating the remaining components as approximately optimal. This reduction enables a tractable yet faithful reproduction of the in-context learning (ICL) behavior exhibited by the full model. Our approach is closely related to the reparameterization techniques used by
Makkuva et al. 2024b
and
Nichani et al. 2024
in the context of gradient flow and gradient descent analysis. Alternatively, it can be viewed as a form of
optimal parameter initialization
, in the spirit of
Edelman et al. 2024
and
Nichani et al. 2024
. We reiterate the assumptions and provide additional details whenever necessary.

Data Assumptions.

Following
Nichani et al. 2024
, we assume a prior distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\cdot)
over irreducible and aperiodic Markov transition kernels
\pi
, such that:

Assumption 1.
(Assumption on
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
): We consider a prior distribution over transition matrices of first-order aperiodic and irreducible Markov chains such that there exists a constant
\gamma>0
for which, over transition kernels drawn from
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\cdot)
, the following conditions are satisfied:

1.

Minimum transition probability:
\displaystyle\min_{s,s^{\prime}}\pi(s^{\prime}\mid s)>\frac{\gamma}{S}

2.

Non-trivial mixing:
\displaystyle\sum_{s}\left\|\pi(\cdot\mid s)-\mu_{\pi}(\cdot)\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{S}

3.

Permutation invariance: For any permutation
\sigma
on
[S]
,
\displaystyle\sigma^{-1}\pi\sigma\overset{d}{=}\pi

4.

Uniform expected transition matrix:
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\pi]=\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{1}_{S}\mathbf{1}_{S}^{\top}

Model Simplifications

We simplify the model as follows. More details can be found in App.
C.1
.

(i)

The positional embeddings used for the keys in the first attention layer are scalar-valued, i.e.
\textbf{p}_{n}^{(1,K)}\propto\,p_{n}
, where
p_{n}
is a scalar.

(ii)

The query, key and value matrices (
\mathbf{W}_{Q,K,V}^{(1)}
) and positional encodings for the value (
\textbf{p}^{(1,V)}
) for the first layer, are chosen so that the attention weight for position

is given by
\operatorname{att}_{n,n-i}^{(1)}=\exp(p_{i})/\left({\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})}\right)\in\{0,1\}
, and the corresponding value output is
\textbf{v}_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\operatorname{att}_{n,n-i}^{(1)}\cdot\,e_{x_{n-i}}^{S}
.

(iii)

We assume the MLP is optimal—i.e., it outputs the correct
\textbf{u}_{n}
even from suboptimal
\textbf{v}_{n}
; this holds for first-order Markov chains via skip connections.

(iv)

The query and key matrices for the second layer (
\mathbf{W}_{Q,K}^{(2)}
) are chosen such that the attention in this layer is defined as
\operatorname{att}_{T,i}^{(2)}=\exp\left({a_{2}\cdot\langle\textbf{v}_{i},\textbf{u}_{T}\rangle}\right)/\left({{\sum_{j=0}^{T}\exp(a_{2}\cdot\langle\textbf{v}_{j},\textbf{u}_{n}\rangle)}}\right)
, where
a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
is the attention scalar.

(v)

\mathbf{W}_{V}^{(2)}
is chosen so that the final logit
\operatorname{logit}_{T}=\sum_{i=0}^{T}\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}e_{x_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
,

Loss function.

We minimize the cross-entropy loss for next-token prediction
[
20
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}_{\pi},\ x_{0:T}\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\pi\left(x_{T+1}=s\mid x_{0:T}\right)\log\left(\operatorname{logit}_{T}(s)+\varepsilon\right)\right],

(2)

where
\varepsilon>0
is a small additive constant,
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\cdot)
denotes the prior over Markov transition kernels, and
\operatorname{logit}_{T}(s)

Training algorithm.
We denote the set of trainable parameters as
\theta=(\textbf{p},a_{2})
, where
\textbf{p}=[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{T}]^{T}
represents the positional scalars and
a_{2}
is the attention scalar. Following the approach of
[
20
]
, we employ a two-stage training procedure. (i) In the first stage, we optimize the positional scalars
p
using gradient descent for
T_{1}
steps with a learning rate
\eta_{1}
; (ii) in the second stage, we freeze
p
and train only
a_{2}
for an additional
T_{2}
steps using a distinct learning rate
\eta_{2}
. A key difference in our setup lies in the treatment of layer normalization: during the first stage, all layer norms in the MLP are omitted, whereas in the second stage, they are reintroduced. This stage-wise strategy facilitates a more tractable theoretical analysis. The training algorithm is:

Algorithm 1.
(Training Algorithm).

Input:
learning rates
\eta_{1}
,
\eta_{2}
; steps
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
; small scalar
a_{2,0}

Initialize
p_{i}^{(0)}=0
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
;
a_{2}=a_{2,0}
;

Freeze all other parameters of the network

Stage 1: Train

p_{i}

without norms added to the MLP

for

t=1,\dots,T_{1}

do

\mathbf{p}^{(t)}\leftarrow\mathbf{p}^{(t-1)}-\eta_{1}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(t-1)})

\theta^{(t)}\leftarrow\left(\{p_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i=0}^{n},a_{2}=a_{2,0}\right)

end

for

Stage 2: Train

a_{2}

with norms added to the MLP

for

t=T_{1}+1,\dots,T_{1}+T_{2}

do

a_{2}^{(t)}\leftarrow a_{2}^{(t-1)}-\eta_{2}\cdot\nabla_{a_{2}}\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(t-1)})

\theta^{(t)}\leftarrow\left(\{p_{i}^{(T_{1})}\}_{i=0}^{n},a_{2}^{(t)}\right)

end

for

\hat{\theta}\leftarrow\theta^{(T_{1}+T_{2})}

Output:

\hat{\theta}

C.1
. Next, we demonstrate that during the first stage of training

, the model converges to the optimal attention map for the first layer—specifically, it learns to attend to the previous token, which is optimal under a first-order Markov chain. In the second stage of training
C.3
, the model sharpens its attention, ultimately converging asymptotically to a near-deterministic distribution over the desired token. Finally, we provide arguments for the result in the context of inductive generalization, utilizing results from
Nichani et al. 2024

C.1
Model Simplification

We adopt a low-parameter regime by isolating the core components of the Transformer architecture that are essential to our analysis, while keeping all remaining parameters fixed at their optimal values. Here, optimality is defined with respect to a first order Markov chain setting. Under this setup, we proceed by stating the following assumptions.

\displaystyle\textbf{x}^{(1)}_{n}=\texttt{Emb}(x_{n})=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{1\times 3}&e_{x_{n}}^{S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 5S}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{6S+3}

In the first layer, we define the positional encodings corresponding to the key as follows, while assuming that all other positional encodings remain fixed and set to zero.

\displaystyle\textbf{p}_{i}^{(1),K}=\begin{cases}{p_{i}}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}0&1&\textbf{0}_{1\times(1+6S)}\end{bmatrix}^{T},&\text{if }i\in\{{0},1,2,\cdots,{n}\},\\
\end{cases},

Layer 1.

We paramatrize the query and key matrice as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{W}^{(1)}_{K}&=\begin{bmatrix}0_{1\times 1}&\textbf{1}_{1\times 2}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 6S}\\
\textbf{0}_{(2+6S)\times 1}&\textbf{0}_{(2+6S)\times 2}&\textbf{0}_{(2+6S)\times(2+6S)}\end{bmatrix}\\
\textbf{W}_{Q}^{(1)}&=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{1\times 3}&\textbf{1}_{1\times S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 5S}\\
\textbf{0}_{(2+6S)\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{(2+6S)\times S}&\textbf{0}_{(2+6S)\times 5S}\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

This then leads to:

\displaystyle\left\langle\textbf{W}_{K}^{(1)}\big(\texttt{Emb}(x_{n-i})+\textbf{p}_{i}^{(1),K}\big),\textbf{W}_{Q}^{(1)}\texttt{Emb}(x_{n})\right\rangle=p_{i}

Hence, on passing it through the softmax function:

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}^{(1)}_{n,n-i}=\frac{\exp(p_{i})}{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\exp(p_{i})}

Choosing the value matrix as,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{V}^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{3\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times 4S}\\
\textbf{0}_{3S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3S\times 4S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&I_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times 4S}\\
\textbf{0}_{2S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{2S\times 4S}\\
\end{bmatrix}

Hence, we can obtain,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c
}\textbf{0}_{1\times 3}&e_{x_{n}}^{S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}&\textbf{v}_{n}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}\end{array}\right]^{T},

\displaystyle\text{ where, }\textbf{v}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\operatorname{att}_{n,n-i}e^{S}_{x_{n-i}}

MLP - Layer 1.

The first layer of the MLP is defined as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}&=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{(3+S)\times(3+3S)}&\textbf{0}_{(3+S)\times(S)}&\textbf{0}_{(3+S)\times(2S)}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times(3+3S)}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times 2S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times(3+3S)}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times 2S}\\
\textbf{0}_{3S\times(3+3S)}&\textbf{0}_{3S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3S\times 2S}\end{bmatrix}\\
\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}&=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{1\times(3+S)}&\textbf{0}_{1\times S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\end{split}

Hence, after applying the skip connections, we obtain:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}+\mathrm{LN}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}\left({\textbf{W}^{(1)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}+\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c
}\textbf{0}_{1\times 3}&e_{x_{n}}^{S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times S}&\textbf{v}_{n}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}\end{array}\right]^{T}

MLP - Layer 2.

The second layer of the MLP is defined as:

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{3\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&{\textbf{0}_{S\times S}}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&I_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&{\textbf{0}_{S\times S}}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\textbf{0}_{(3+6S)\times 1}

Hence, on computing the output, we obtain:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n}^{(1)}+\mathrm{LN}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}\left({\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}+\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c
}\textbf{0}_{1\times 3}&e_{x_{n}}^{S}&\textbf{0}_{1\times S}&\textbf{u}_{n}&\textbf{v}_{n}&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2S}\end{array}\right]^{T}

\displaystyle\text{Where, }\textbf{u}_{n}

\displaystyle=e^{S}_{x_{n}}

Note that, in this setting, the optimality condition is well defined due to the first order Markov property. In particular,
\textbf{u}_{n}
can be recovered directly via the skip connections, as it depends only on the current token.

MLP - Layer 3.

The third layer of the MLP is defined as:

\displaystyle\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}_{3\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3\times 4S}\\
\textbf{0}_{3S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{3S\times 4S}\\
\textbf{0}_{S\times 3}&I_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{S\times 4S}\\
\textbf{0}_{2S\times 3}&\textbf{0}_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}_{2S\times 4S}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\textbf{0}_{(3+6S)\times 1}

Passing the input to the layer through the non-linearities along with the skip connections, we get:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}+\mathrm{LN}\left({\mathrm{ReLU}\left({\textbf{W}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(1)}+\textbf{b}_{\texttt{mlp}}^{(2)}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c}0&\textbf{0}_{1\times 2}&e_{x_{n}}^{S}&{0_{1\times S}}&\frac{\textbf{u}_{n}}{\|\textbf{u}_{n}\|_{2}}&\textbf{v}_{n}&\frac{\textbf{v}_{n}}{\|\textbf{v}_{n}\|_{2}}&\textbf{0}_{1\times S}\end{array}\right]^{T}

Layer 2.

In this layer, all the relative position encodings are set as
0
and instead,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{W}_{Q}^{(2)}&=a_{2}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}1&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}_{S\times(2+3S)}&I_{S\times S}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\\
\textbf{W}_{K}^{(2)}&=\begin{bmatrix}1&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&{\textbf{0}_{S\times(2+4S)}}&{I_{S\times S}}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

With these choices, and adding a normalization after the output of the key linear layer

\displaystyle\left\langle\textbf{W}_{K}^{(2)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(3)},\textbf{W}_{Q}^{(2)}\widetilde{\textbf{x}}_{n,\texttt{mlp}}^{(3)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle={a_{2}}\cdot\langle\textbf{v}_{i},\textbf{u}_{n}\rangle

Taking the softmax, we obtain,

\displaystyle\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}=\frac{\exp\left({a_{2}\cdot\langle\textbf{v}_{i},\textbf{u}_{n}\rangle}\right)}{\sum_{j=0}^{n}\exp(a_{2}\cdot\langle\textbf{v}_{j},\textbf{u}_{n}\rangle)}

Finally the logit is computed as follows (selecting the optimal value and projection matrix):

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\operatorname{att}_{n,i}^{(2)}\cdot e_{x_{i}}

Motivated by this parameterization, we construct a simplified model that abstracts away the complex transformations occurring within the intermediate layers of the network. During stage one of training, we remove layer normalization; accordingly, we omit layer norms from the simplified model as well. These can be reintroduced later during stage two of training. However, in the case of a first order Markov chain, layer normalization does not affect the model’s behavior, since both
\textbf{u}_{n}
and
\textbf{v}_{n}
converge to one-hot embedding vectors. With these considerations, the model can be concretely reduced as follows. Let
Z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, where
Z_{i}=\langle{e_{x_{n}}},{v_{i}}\rangle
. Morever, let:

\displaystyle X_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}e_{x_{0}}&e_{x_{1}}&e_{x_{2}}&\cdots&e_{x_{n}}\end{bmatrix}

Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s},n}=e_{s^{\prime}}^{T}\left[{X_{n}\operatorname{softmax}\left({a_{2}Z_{n}}\right)}\right]

Note that the logit computed above corresponds to the case where
x_{n}=s
. Using a slightly different notation to account for causal masking, we obtain the following:

\displaystyle A^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}&-\infty&-\infty&-\infty&-\infty\\
p_{1}&p_{0}&-\infty&-\infty&-\infty\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
p_{n}&p_{n-1}&p_{n-1}&\cdots&p_{0}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle A^{(2)}

\displaystyle=a_{2}\cdot I

\displaystyle\text{Hence, }\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s},n}

\displaystyle=e_{s^{\prime}}^{T}\left[{X_{n}\operatorname{softmax}\left({a_{2}Z_{n}}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=e_{s^{\prime}}^{T}\left[{X_{n}\underbrace{\operatorname{softmax}\left({\operatorname{softmax}\left({A^{(1)}}\right)X_{n}^{T}A^{(2)}e_{x_{n}}}\right)}_{\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};1)}}\right]

We now use this simplified model for the gradient analysis.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}(\pi)}}\left[{g_{i,j}(\pi)}\right]

Hence, on substituting this back, we obtain,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{m}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{2,0}}{S\cdot kn}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}(\pi)}\Bigg[\sum_{i\geq m}^{n}\frac{\exp(p_{m})}{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})}g_{i,m}(\pi)-\sum_{i\geq m}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{i}\frac{\exp(p_{j})\,\exp(p_{m})}{\left(\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})\right)^{2}}g_{i,j}(\pi)\Bigg]

Therefore, rather than considering the entire expression, we focus on a specific row (i.e., for a given
i
), and obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{m}}\bigg|_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{2,0}}{S\cdot kn}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}(\pi)}\left[{\left({\frac{\exp(p_{m})}{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})}g_{i,m}(\pi)-\sum_{j=0}^{i}\frac{\exp(p_{j})\,\exp(p_{m})}{\left(\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})\right)^{2}}g_{i,j}(\pi)}\right)}\right]

To obtain the optimal attention map for a first-order Markov chain, the goal is to demonstrate that, for every row, the gradient with respect to
p_{1}
is greater than that of any other token and is also positive. Due to the properties of the softmax function, if this condition holds over the course of training, then the model will asymptotically attend to the previous token, thereby converging to the optimal attention map. Hence, the requirement is essentially, for every row:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{1}}\right|_{i}

\displaystyle>\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{k}}\right|_{i}\quad\forall\,k\in\{0,2,\ldots,n\}

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{1}}\right|_{i}

\displaystyle>0

In this section, we will show that given the prior assumptions, this is indeed true. Towards this, we denote
d_{g_{m,i}}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{m}}\right|_{i}
. With this notation, we can express:

\displaystyle d_{g_{m,i}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{2,0}}{S\cdot kn}\cdot\left[\frac{\exp(p_{m})}{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})}\cdot\left(g_{i,m}-\sum_{j=0}^{i}\frac{\exp(p_{j})}{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})}\cdot g_{i,j}\right)\right]

Now, suppose we initialize all positional encoding scalars to zero as defined in the training algorithm. Then, at initialization time
t=0
, we have:

\displaystyle d_{g_{m,i},t=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{2,0}}{S\cdot kn}\cdot\frac{1}{i+1}\left(g_{i,m}-\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{1}{i+1}\cdot g_{i,j}\right),\quad\forall\,i\geq 0

Due to Lemma
7
, we observe that at initialization time
t=0
(when all positional encoding scalars are initialized to zero), the gradient of the attention logits is maximized in the direction corresponding to position 1. This implies that the model is initially biased to attend more strongly to the immediately preceding token, aligning with the structure of a first-order Markov process. Concretely, we observe that:

\displaystyle-d_{g_{1,i},0}

\displaystyle>-d_{g_{k,i},0}\quad\forall\,k\in\{0,2,\ldots,n\},\quad\forall\,i\in\{k,\ldots,n\}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad-d_{g_{1,i},0}

\displaystyle\geq 0\ \forall\,i\in\{k,\ldots,n\}

Hence, under the given initialization and model assumptions, performing gradient descent yields the update rule:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}_{t}-\eta_{1}\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{t}}L(\theta),

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\mathbf{p}_{t}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}p_{0,t}&p_{1,t}&\cdots&p_{n,t}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

Here,
p_{i,t}
denotes the
i
-th positional encoding scalar at iteration
t
. Therefore, after the first update step (i.e., at
t=1
), we have:

p_{1,t=1}>p_{k,t=1}\quad\forall k\in\{0,2,\ldots,n\},

reflecting that the gradient is largest in the direction corresponding to position 1.

We need to show that this property holds for all iterates. By Lemma
3
, we can show that for each iterate of the preconditioned descent, the following holds:

\displaystyle p_{1,t}>p_{k,t}\quad\forall\,k\in\{0,2,\ldots,n\}

Furthermore, by Lemma
3
and Lemma
4
, we observe that
p_{1}
tends to infinity as it increases monotonically over time. In contrast, the other positional encodings do not diverge, as they do not exhibit similar growth. Due to the nature of the softmax function, the rapid increase of
p_{1}
suppresses the relative influence of the other components, effectively preventing their growth. In addition, Lemma
4
provides an estimate of the number of iterations required for the iterates to converge to the optimal attention configuration. Therefore, assuming a sufficiently long training period during the first stage, the model successfully recovers the ideal attention map corresponding to a second-order Markov chain:

\mathbf{v}_{i}\approx e_{x_{i-1}}

Hence, in the second stage of training, the inclusion of layer normalization does not significantly affect the outcome, provided the Markov chain is sufficiently long and the training duration is adequate, and hence, we will assume that
\textbf{v}_{i}/\|\textbf{v}_{i}\|\approx e_{x_{i-1}}
, which is the optimal attention map. We note that the gradient analysis for the first stage of training closely mirrors that of the first stage in
Nichani et al. 2024
, and is directly inspired by their approach.

Appendix D
Two Heads in Layer One, One in Layer Two Perturbative Analysis

Understanding the convergence behavior of a Transformer on Markov chains of arbitrary order in the prior setting (Appendix
C
) presents significant challenges. A central difficulty lies in the dependence of accurately estimating
\textbf{u}_{n}
on the precise recovery of
\textbf{v}_{n}
. In earlier constructions, this estimation was enabled by complex non-linearities such as layer normalization, which makes direct gradient-based analysis intractable. To facilitate analysis, we focus on a simplified architecture: a two-layer model where the first layer includes two attention heads, as described in Section
3
. Due to the intractability of analyzing the full model, we adopt a perturbative approach in which only a subset of parameters is trained, while the remainder of the network is fixed at optimal values. This aligns with our goal of exploring the behavior of a low-parameterized version of the model. Within this setup, we consider arbitrary-order Markov chains under the assumption that the first head is optimally initialized to recover
\textbf{u}_{n}
. Consistent with the simplification in Appendix
C
, we limit learning to the key positional vectors of the second head in the first layer, parameterized by a single scalar. All other network components remain fixed. The second layer of attention is not assumed to be learnable; instead, we begin with a softmax function at very low temperature, held constant during the initial training phase. This temperature is later increased to infinity. Even under these constraints, analyzing convergence of the positional encodings to their optimal values remains analytically intractable without strong assumptions about initialization, training procedures, and the data distribution. Nonetheless, we regard this work as a first step toward understanding convergence in higher-order Markov models within more realistic Transformer architectures.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{m}}\bigg|_{i}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-m}=s_{l})-\mathbb{P}(x_{i-m}=s_{l})\,\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle g_{i,j}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}g_{i,j,l}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{m}}\bigg|_{i}

D.2.1
Warmup: Second Order Markov Chain

We begin by presenting the dynamics for a second-order Markov chain as a warm-up. Since the vocabulary is binary, we have
|\mathcal{S}|=2
. Therefore,

\displaystyle g_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{k}g_{i,j,l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime},s_{1},s_{2}}\left(\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid s_{1},s_{2})}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=s_{1})+\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid s_{1},s_{2})}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=s_{2})\right)-4

Enumerating all possible values of
s_{1},s_{2}\in\{0,1\}
, we get:

\displaystyle g_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 0,0)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=0)+\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 0,0)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=0)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,0)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=1)+\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,0)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=0)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 0,1)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=0)+\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 0,1)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=0)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,1)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=1)+\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,1)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=1)\right)-4

Using marginalization, we can simplify terms such as:

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,0)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\,\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=1)+\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,0)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\,\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=0)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,0)\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=1)+\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=0)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,0)\cdot\frac{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\right)\qquad\text{(by marginalization)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}}\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,0)

\displaystyle=1

Similarly, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 0,1)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\,\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=0)+\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 0,1)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\,\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=1)\right)=1

Therefore, substituting these identities back into the expression for
g_{i,j}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle g_{i,j}

\displaystyle=2\cdot\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}}\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 0,0)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\,\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=0)+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,1)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\,\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=1)-1\right)

In an ideal second-order Markov chain scenario, we expect the positional scalars
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
to approach infinity, while the remaining positional weights remain relatively small. This reflects ideal attention behavior: the model should focus primarily on the two preceding tokens, consistent with the structure needed for an accurate
k
-gram (specifically, bigram) estimator. Hence, the requirement is essentially:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{1}}\right|_{i}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{2}}\right|_{i}>\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{k}}\right|_{i}\quad\forall\,k\in\{0,3,\ldots,n\}

We denote
d_{g_{m,i}}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}L(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}p_{m}}\right|_{i}
.
With this notation, we can express:

\displaystyle d_{g_{m,i}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{2,0}}{|\mathcal{S}|^{k}\cdot kn}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}(\pi)}\left[\frac{\exp(p_{m})}{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})}\cdot\left(g_{i,m}-\sum_{j=0}^{i}\frac{\exp(p_{j})}{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})}\cdot g_{i,j}\right)\right]

According to assumptions (Assump.
D
), the chain satisfies detailed balance.
Additionally, by Lemma.

decrease monotonically:

g_{i,0}>g_{i,1}>g_{i,2}>\cdots>g_{i,n}

Using this monotonicity, we can bound the weighted average:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i}\frac{\exp(p_{j})}{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})}\cdot g_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{i}\frac{\exp(p_{j})}{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\exp(p_{j})}\cdot\sup_{j}g_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{j}g_{i,j}=g_{i,0}\quad\text{(by monotonicity)}

Now, suppose we initialize all positional encoding scalars to zero except for
p_{0}=-\infty
. Then, at initialization time

\displaystyle d_{g_{m,i},t=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a_{2,0}}{|\mathcal{S}|^{k}\cdot kn}\cdot\frac{1}{i-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}(\pi)}[g_{i,m}]-\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{1}{i-1}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}(\pi)}[g_{i,j}]\right),\quad\forall\,i\geq 1

\displaystyle d_{g_{m,0},t=0}

\displaystyle=0

Since we assume detailed balance (Assump.
D

\displaystyle g_{i,j}

\displaystyle=2\cdot\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}}\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 0,0)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\,\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=0)+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\frac{\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,1)}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\,\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{i-j}=1)-1\right)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(x_{i-j}=0\mid x_{i}=s^{\prime})\cdot\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 0,0)+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(x_{i-j}=1\mid x_{i}=s^{\prime})\cdot\pi(s^{\prime}\mid 1,1)-1\right)

Under the assumptions on the Markov chain (Assump.
D
), we observe that at initialization time
t=0
(when all positional encoding scalars are initialized to zero, except for
p_{0}=-\infty

\displaystyle-d_{g_{1,i},0}

\displaystyle=-d_{g_{2,i},0}>-d_{g_{k,i},0},

\displaystyle\forall\,k\in\{0,3,\ldots,n\},\quad\forall\,i\in\{k,\ldots,n\},

\displaystyle p_{1,t}=p_{2,t}>p_{k,t}\quad\forall\,k\in\{0,3,\ldots,n\}

We first apply the triangle inequality to separate the difference between the two fractions:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}D(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))e_{i}}{\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\theta}+\epsilon}-\frac{e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}D(\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))e_{i}}{\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\hat{\theta}}+\epsilon}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left|\frac{1}{\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\theta}+\epsilon}-\frac{1}{\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\hat{\theta}}+\epsilon}\right|\cdot\left|e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}D(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))e_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left|\frac{1}{\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\hat{\theta}}+\epsilon}\right|\cdot\left|e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}\left(D(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))-D(\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))\right)e_{i}\right|.

To bound this expression, we begin by bounding the logit difference in the numerator of the first term.

\displaystyle|\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\theta}-\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\hat{\theta}}|

\displaystyle=\left|e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k)-\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k)-\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k)-\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))\|_{1},

where the last inequality uses the fact that
\|e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.

We now recall the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}
and analyze its properties.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{softmax}\left(a_{2,0}\cdot\operatorname{softmax}(A^{(1)})X_{n}^{T}\cdot\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}e_{x_{n-i}}\right)

In particular, if
a_{2,0}=0
, the argument to softmax is zero, and we obtain the uniform distribution:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{0})

Since the entries of the softmax input are in
[0,1]
, we can bound the minimum and maximum values of any softmax output entry as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n-1)e^{a_{2,0}}+1}\leq\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))_{i}\leq\frac{e^{a_{2,0}}}{(n-1)+e^{a_{2,0}}}

These bounds let us now quantify the maximum possible deviation between corresponding softmax outputs.

\displaystyle|\text{sup }\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))_{i}-\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))_{i}|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{e^{a_{2,0}}}{(n-1)+e^{a_{2,0}}}-\frac{1}{n}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{(n-1)(e^{a_{2,0}}-1)}{n((n-1)+e^{a_{2,0}})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{a_{2,0}}-1}{n}

Similarly, for the lower bound:

\displaystyle|\text{inf }\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))_{i}-\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))_{i}|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{(n-1)e^{a_{2,0}}+1}-\frac{1}{n}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{a_{2,0}}-1}{n}

Thus, we conclude:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))_{i}-\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))_{i}|\leq\frac{e^{a_{2,0}}-1}{n}

Next, we simplify the derivative expression using the identity for the softmax Jacobian.

\displaystyle e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}D(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))e_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k)_{i}(\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=e_{s^{\prime}}}-\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\theta})

We now bound the difference in these derivative expressions using triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\left|e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}D(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))e_{i}-e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}D(\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))e_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq|\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k)_{i}-\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k)_{i}|\cdot|\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=e_{s^{\prime}}}-\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\theta}|

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k)_{i}\cdot|\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\theta}-\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\hat{\theta}}|

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\cdot\frac{e^{a_{2,0}}-1}{n}

Here we used that
|\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=e_{s^{\prime}}}-\operatorname{logit}|\leq 1
and
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}\leq 1
.

Combining the bounds for the numerator and denominator differences, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}D(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))e_{i}}{\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\theta}+\epsilon}-\frac{e_{s^{\prime}}^{\top}X_{n}D(\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}}^{(2)}(X_{n};k))e_{i}}{\operatorname{logit}_{s^{\prime}_{s_{k}},n,\hat{\theta}}+\epsilon}\right|\leq 3\cdot\frac{e^{a_{2,0}}-1}{\epsilon^{2}n}

Finally, we bound the overall error
\|g^{*}_{i}-\hat{g}_{i}\|_{\infty}
by summing over all possible state sequences:

\displaystyle\|g^{*}_{i}-\hat{g}_{i}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq n\sum_{s^{\prime},s_{1},\cdots,s_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}(\pi)}\left[\pi(s^{\prime}\mid s_{1},\cdots,s_{k})\cdot\frac{3(e^{a_{2,0}}-1)}{\epsilon^{2}n}\right]

\displaystyle=3\cdot\frac{(e^{a_{2,0}}-1)}{\epsilon^{2}}\sum_{s^{\prime},s_{1},\cdots,s_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\pi(s^{\prime}\mid s_{1},\cdots,s_{k})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3S^{k+1}\cdot\epsilon^{-2}\cdot(e^{a_{2,0}}-1)

Using the inequality
e^{a_{2,0}}-1\leq 2a_{2,0}
for
a_{2,0}\in[0,1]
, we conclude:

\displaystyle\|g^{*}_{i}-\hat{g}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 6S^{k+1}\epsilon^{-2}a_{2,0}

∎

Lemma 4

(Extension of Lemma D.5 in

.

\displaystyle\tau_{1}\lesssim n\eta_{1}^{-1}a_{2,0}^{-1}\left(n\log(2n)+\alpha^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\left(\frac{1}{k}-\alpha\right)(n-k)}{1-k\left(\frac{1}{k}-\alpha\right)}\right)\right)

Lemma 5

(Successive Markov Chain Lemmas)
.

For a second-order Markov Chain, if
\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\geq\delta^{\prime}
and
\pi(1|1,1)-\pi(1|0,1)\geq\delta^{\prime}
then the probability
P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)
and
P^{i}(1\mid 1,1)
decreases with increasing
i
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[{P^{1}(0\mid 0,0)+P^{1}(1\mid 1,1)}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[{P^{k}(0\mid 0,0)+P^{k}(1\mid 1,1)}\right]\geq\delta\quad\forall k\geq 4

Proof.

We begin by establishing the condition for two steps and then generalize by induction.

\displaystyle P^{2}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle=\pi(0\mid 0,0)\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)\pi(1\mid 0,0)

Given the assumption
\pi(0\mid 0,0)>\pi(0\mid 1,0)
, we multiply both sides by
1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)
:

\displaystyle\pi(0\mid 0,0)\left(1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)\right)

\displaystyle>\pi(0\mid 1,0)\left(1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)\right)

\displaystyle\pi(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle>\pi(0\mid 0,0)^{2}+\pi(0\mid 1,0)\pi(1\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle\pi(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle>P^{2}(0\mid 0,0)\quad\text{(by definition)}

Now, we apply induction.

\displaystyle P^{3}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle=\pi(0\mid 0,0)P^{2}(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)(1-P^{2}(0\mid 0,0))

\displaystyle=\pi(0\mid 1,0)+\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)P^{2}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle<\pi(0\mid 1,0)+\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)\pi(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle<P^{2}(0\mid 0,0)

Hence, by induction:

\displaystyle P^{i+1}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle=\pi(0\mid 1,0)+\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle<\pi(0\mid 1,0)+\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)P^{i-1}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle<P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)

Now, using the recursive relation derived above, we can express
P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)
explicitly. The recurrence is:

\displaystyle P^{i+1}(0\mid 0,0)=\pi(0\mid 1,0)+\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)

We separate this into a homogeneous and a particular solution:

Homogeneous Solution:

\displaystyle P_{h}^{i+1}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle=\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)P_{h}^{i}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle P_{h}^{i}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle=C\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)^{i}

Particular Solution:

\displaystyle P_{p}

\displaystyle=\pi(0\mid 1,0)+\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)P_{p}

\displaystyle P_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(0\mid 1,0)}{1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)}

Using the initial condition
P^{1}(0\mid 0,0)=\pi(0\mid 0,0)
, we solve for the constant:

\displaystyle C=\frac{\pi(0\mid 0,0)-P_{p}}{\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)}

Final Solution

\displaystyle P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(0\mid 1,0)}{1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\frac{\pi(0\mid 1,0)}{1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)}\right)\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)^{i-1}

Similarly, we obtain:

\displaystyle P^{i+1}(1\mid 1,1)

\displaystyle<P^{i}(1\mid 1,1)\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{N}

Now, we can first express

\displaystyle P^{i}(1\mid 1,1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(1\mid 0,1)}{1-\pi(1\mid 1,1)+\pi(1\mid 0,1)}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\pi(1\mid 1,1)-\frac{\pi(1\mid 0,1)}{1-\pi(1\mid 1,1)+\pi(1\mid 0,1)}\right)\left(\pi(1\mid 1,1)-\pi(1\mid 0,1)\right)^{i-1}.

Now, define

\displaystyle G_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\frac{\pi(0\mid 1,0)}{1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)}\right),

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\pi(1\mid 1,1)-\frac{\pi(1\mid 0,1)}{1-\pi(1\mid 1,1)+\pi(1\mid 0,1)}\right).

Thus, we can write

\displaystyle P^{3}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(0\mid 1,0)}{1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)}+G_{0}\cdot\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)^{2},

\displaystyle P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(0\mid 1,0)}{1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)}+G_{0}\cdot\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)^{i-1},

and therefore

\displaystyle P^{3}(0\mid 0,0)-P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)

\displaystyle=G_{0}\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)\left(1-\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)^{i-2}\right).

We can further simplify
G_{0}
:

\displaystyle G_{0}

\displaystyle=\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\frac{\pi(0\mid 1,0)}{1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0))\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)}{1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\right)\left(1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)\right)}{1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)+\pi(0\mid 1,0)}.

By assumption,
\pi(0\mid 0,0)-\pi(0\mid 1,0)\geq\delta^{\prime}
and
1-\pi(0\mid 0,0)\geq\epsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle G_{0}\geq\epsilon^{\prime\prime}

for some positive constant
\epsilon^{\prime}
.

Hence, substituting back, we obtain

\displaystyle P^{3}(0\mid 0,0)-P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)\geq\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}\quad\text{(Where $\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}$ is a positive constant)}

Taking expectation over
\pi\sim\mathbb{P}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[P^{3}(0\mid 0,0)-P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)\right]\geq\delta,

where
\delta^{\prime\prime}
is defined formally as follows:

The constant
\delta
represents a uniform lower bound on the expected gap between the third-step and
\forall i
-th-step transition probabilities across all Markov chains sampled from the prior distribution
\mathbb{P}
. It is defined as

\delta^{\prime\prime}=\inf_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{E}\left[P^{3}(0\mid 0,0)-P^{i}(0\mid 0,0)\right],

and satisfies
\delta>0
. This ensures that, even after taking expectations, the separation between transition probabilities remains bounded away from zero across all sampled chains.

Similarly, for the other case, we obtain

\displaystyle P^{3}(1\mid 1,1)-P^{i}(1\mid 1,1)

\displaystyle\geq\delta^{\prime\prime\prime\prime},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[P^{3}(1\mid 1,1)-P^{i}(1\mid 1,1)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\delta.

Therefore, using linearity of expectation, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[P^{3}(0\mid 0,0)+P^{3}(1\mid 1,1)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[P^{k}(0\mid 0,0)+P^{k}(1\mid 1,1)\right]\geq\delta\quad\forall k\geq 4.

∎

Lemma 6

(Monotonicity via spectral decomposition)
.

Let
(X_{t})
be a reversible, irreducible, aperiodic
k
th-order Markov chain on state-space
S
, and let

\mathcal{A}=S^{k}
. For integers
i,\ell\geq 1
, define the return count

\displaystyle g_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in S^{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Pr\bigl(X_{i+\ell}=s_{j}\mid X_{i-1}=s_{1},\dots,X_{i-k}=s_{k}\bigr).

Equivalently, one can view the chain as a lifted first-order markov chain on
\mathcal{A}
, where

a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}),\ b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\in\mathcal{A}
and

\displaystyle P(a\to b)

\displaystyle=\Pr\bigl((X_{t},\dots,X_{t-k+1})=b\mid(X_{t-1},\dots,X_{t-k})=a\bigr),

\displaystyle f(a)

\displaystyle=|a|\text{ (where $|\cdot|$ denotes the cardinality)},

\displaystyle g_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}P^{\ell}(a\to b)\,f(a).

Under detailed balance with stationary distribution
\pi
. If
P
is self-adjoint on
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{A},\pi)
with eigenvalues

1=\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N}>0
and if
\beta_{m}>0
where orthonormal eigenfunctions are defined as
\{\phi_{m}\}
and
\beta_{m}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\beta_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\phi_{m}(a)\,\phi_{m}(b)\,\pi(b)\,f(a).

Then for all
\ell\geq 0
:

\displaystyle g_{i,\ell}-g_{i,\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=2}^{N}\lambda_{m}^{\ell}(1-\lambda_{m})\beta_{m}\geq 0,

\displaystyle g_{i,1}-g_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=2}^{N}(\lambda_{m}^{2}-\lambda_{m}^{\ell})\beta_{m}>0.

Hence,
g_{i,1}>g_{i,2}>g_{i,3}>\cdots
.

Moreover,
g_{i,1}\geq 2^{k-1}
if

\sum_{m=1}^{N}\lambda_{m}^{2}\beta_{m}\geq 2^{k-1}

Proof.

From spectral expansion, we know that:

\displaystyle P^{\ell}(a\to b)=\sum_{m}\lambda_{m}^{\ell}\phi_{m}(a)\phi_{m}(b)\pi(b)

Therefore, we obtain (on substitution)

\displaystyle g_{i,\ell}=\sum_{a,b}P^{\ell}(a\to b)f(a,b)=\sum_{m}\lambda_{m}^{\ell+1}\beta_{m}

Hence, finally we obtain monotonicity, due to the assumptions:

\displaystyle g_{i,\ell}-g_{i,\ell+1}=\sum_{m\geq 2}\lambda_{m}^{\ell+1}(1-\lambda_{m})\beta_{m}\geq 0

The last condition is obtained by direct substitution.

∎

Lemma 8

(
Levin and Peres 2017
)
.

Let
(z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a stationary, reversible, ergodic Markov chain with stationary distribution
\pi
, and let
P
denote its Markov operator on
L^{2}(\pi)
.
For any function
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f^{2}]<\infty
and all
t,t^{\prime}\geq 0
,

\operatorname{Cov}(f(z_{t}),f(z_{t^{\prime}}))=\left\langle\widetilde{f},P^{|t-t^{\prime}|}\widetilde{f}\right\rangle_{\pi},

where
\widetilde{f}=f-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f]
and
\langle g,h\rangle_{\pi}:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[g(z)h(z)]
.

Proof.

By stationarity and the Markov property,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(z_{t^{\prime}})\mid z_{t}]

\displaystyle=(P^{t^{\prime}-t}f)(z_{t}),

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f(z_{t})f(z_{t^{\prime}})]

\displaystyle=\langle f,P^{t^{\prime}-t}f\rangle_{\pi}.

(11)

Thus,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(f(z_{t}),f(z_{t^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=\langle f,P^{|t-t^{\prime}|}f\rangle_{\pi}-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f]\right)^{2}.

(12)

Expanding
f=\widetilde{f}+\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f]
and using that
P
preserves constants,

\displaystyle\langle f,P^{|t-t^{\prime}|}f\rangle_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{f},P^{|t-t^{\prime}|}\widetilde{f}\rangle_{\pi}+\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f]\right)^{2},

(13)

which gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(f(z_{t}),f(z_{t^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{f},P^{|t-t^{\prime}|}\widetilde{f}\rangle_{\pi}.

(14)

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{Cov}(f(z_{t}),f(z_{t^{\prime}}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\|P^{|t-t^{\prime}|}\widetilde{f}\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}.

(15)

If the spectral gap satisfies
\|P\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\lambda
on centered functions, then

\displaystyle\|P^{|t-t^{\prime}|}\widetilde{f}\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{|t-t^{\prime}|}\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L^{2}(\pi)},

(16)

thus

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{Cov}(f(z_{t}),f(z_{t^{\prime}}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{|t-t^{\prime}|}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(f).

(17)

∎

Lemma 9

(Variance Lemma similar to Cor. F.6 in
Nichani et al. 2024
)
.

Suppose:

1.

(s_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a stationary, irreducible, aperiodic
k
-th order Markov chain on a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
of size
S
,

2.

Define the lifted process
z_{t}=(s_{t},s_{t-1},\dots,s_{t-k+1})\in\mathcal{S}^{k}
,

3.

Assume the transition matrix
P
of
(z_{t})
has second-largest eigenvalue modulus
\lambda<1
.

Then, if

\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}_{z_{i}=s^{\prime}},

is the empirical frequency of observing
s^{\prime}
, we have the variance bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})-\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n(1-\lambda)},

where:

•

\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})=\pi_{Z}(\{z:z_{1}=s^{\prime}\})
is the stationary probability of
s^{\prime}
,

•

and the hidden constant is universal (independent of
T
).

Proof.

Let
z_{n}=(x_{n},x_{n-1},\cdots,)
.

Therefore, we can see that:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\pi,X}(s^{\prime})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}_{z_{i}=s^{\prime}}

(18)

Therefore:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\pi,X}(s^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}\left({\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}_{z_{t}=s^{\prime}}}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{n}\operatorname{Cov}\left({\mathbb{I}_{z_{t}=s^{\prime}},\mathbb{I}_{z_{t^{\prime}}=s^{\prime}}}\right)

(20)

From Lemma
8
, we can see that,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\pi,X}(s^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{t,t^{\prime}}\lambda^{|t-t^{\prime}|}

(21)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n(1-\lambda)}

(22)

∎

Lemma 10

(Extension-1 to k
th
-order of Lemma G.2 in
Nichani et al. 2024
)
.

For any
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
and any k
th
-order transition kernel,
\pi
with spectral gap
1-\lambda(\pi)\geq 1-\lambda
, and with
\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})\geq 0
, and
i>j
there exists a sufficiently large constant
C_{\gamma,S}
such that if
\epsilon\geq C_{\gamma,S}\left({n(1-\lambda)}\right)^{-1/2}
, then:

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{(\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=s^{\prime}}-\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime}))\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}}{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon}\right]-\left({\frac{\mathbb{P}_{X}[s_{i}=s^{\prime},s_{j}=s]}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}-\mathbb{P}_{X}[s_{j}=s]}\right)\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n(1-\lambda)}}.

Proof.

The proof is the same as the proof of Lemma G.2 in
Nichani et al. 2024
, where to control the variance term, we use
9
. For completeness, we desribe the proof below:

Define

\displaystyle E_{\pi}(s,s^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{(\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=s^{\prime}}-\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime}))\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}}{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon}\right]-\frac{\mathbb{P}_{X}[x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{j}=s]}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}+\mathbb{P}_{X}[x_{j}=s].

Expanding:

(\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=s^{\prime}}-\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime}))\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}=\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=s^{\prime}}\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}-\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}.

Thus:

\displaystyle E_{\pi}(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=s^{\prime}}\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}}{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon}\right]-\frac{\mathbb{P}_{X}[x_{i}=s^{\prime},x_{j}=s]}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}-\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}}{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon}\right]+\mathbb{P}_{X}[x_{j}=s].

Grouping terms:

\displaystyle E_{\pi}(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=s^{\prime}}\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}\left(\frac{1}{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon}-\frac{1}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}\left(1-\frac{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})}{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon}\right)\right].

Grouping terms:

\frac{1}{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon}-\frac{1}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\approx\frac{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})-\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})-\epsilon}{(\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon)\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})},

and

1-\frac{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})}{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon}=\frac{\epsilon}{\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon}.

Thus by the triangle inequality and boundedness of indicator variables
\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=s^{\prime}},\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}\in\{0,1\}
, we get

\displaystyle|E_{\pi}(s,s^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{|\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=s^{\prime}}\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}||\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})-\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})|+\epsilon(|\mathbb{I}_{x_{i}=s^{\prime}}\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}|+\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})|\mathbb{I}_{x_{j}=s}|)}{(\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon)\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{|\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})-\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})|+\epsilon}{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})^{2}}\right]+\epsilon^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{X}\left[\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})\leq\frac{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}{2}\right].

Using concentration inequalities (Lemma
9
):

\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[(\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})-\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime}))^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{1}{n(1-\lambda)},\quad\mathbb{P}_{X}\left[\hat{\mu}_{X}(s^{\prime})\leq\frac{\mu_{\pi}(s^{\prime})}{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{1}{n(1-\lambda)},

and
\epsilon\sim(n(1-\lambda))^{-1/2}
, we conclude:

|E_{\pi}(s,s^{\prime})|\lesssim\frac{1}{n(1-\lambda)}.

∎
