A Report of Two Cases of Primary Mucosa‐Associated Lymphoid Tissue Lymphoma of the Pharynx, Larynx, and Trachea
Authors: Jinqiu Li, Yingying Zhang, Xue Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study examines two cases of primary MALT lymphoma in the base of the tongue, larynx, and trachea. Both cases were finally diagnosed through pathological and immunohistochemical examinations, with common laryngoscopic features: scattered circumferential follicular‐like mucosal elevations with smooth surfaces on the affected mucosal areas.

Introduction

MALT lymphoma was first described by Isaacson and Wright in 1983 in the gastrointestinal tract [1]. It is a low‐grade B‐cell lymphoma that originates in mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue, accounting for around 8% of all non‐Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs) [2]. MALT lymphoma mostly occurs in the gastrointestinal tract, and it is particularly uncommon in the pharynx, larynx, and trachea. We reported two cases of primary MALT lymphoma at the base of the tongue, larynx, and trachea, which have the manifestation of hoarseness. They had extremely similar and characteristic changes in laryngoscopy and were diagnosed as MALT lymphoma based on the pathological results. The clinical characteristics of these two cases are summarized as follows, providing good diagnostic implications and guiding significance for the diagnosis of MALT lymphoma.

Case Presentation (Case History/Examination, Diagnosis, Treatment, and Follow‐Up)

Case 1

A 51‐year‐old female presented with hoarseness for 3 months. There was no cough, expectoration, or history of acid reflux. The results of electronic laryngoscopy were shown in the figure below (Figure 1). The pathological immunohistochemical staining of the biopsy specimen showed lymphoepithelial lesions within the epithelium. The uniformly proliferating lymphocytes beneath the epithelium were B cells, and lymphoid follicle implantation was visible locally. It was considered as mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue marginal zone lymphoma. The results of immunohistochemical staining were as follows: CK(AE1/AE3)(−), CD20(+), CD3(−), Ki67 (positive rate 10%), CD21(+), CD43 (−), CD23(−), CyclinD1(−), CD5(−), PAX‐5(+), EBER(−), TdT(−), CD10(−), Bcl‐2(+), Bcl‐6(−) (Figure 2). A whole‐body PET‐CT examination revealed multiple nodular thickenings of the tracheal wall without hypermetabolism, which was consistent with lymphoma. No other abnormalities were found. Since the lesion was confined to the main airway, local low‐dose radiotherapy (35 Gy) was administered. After 10 months of follow‐up, the voice has recovered well, the lesions in the trachea had disappeared, and the intratracheal mucosa had returned to normal.

Electronic laryngoscopy of CASE 1: The epiglottis showed no redness or swelling. Follicular‐like mucosal elevations were scattered circumferentially on the mucosal surface of the main airway beneath the bilateral vocal cords. The surface was smooth and extended from the glottis all the way to the tracheal carina. (Black arrow).

Pathological results of CASE 1 showed the uniformly proliferating lymphocytes beneath the epithelium were B cells, and lymphoid follicle implantation was visible locally (HEx200) (a). The immunohistochemical results were BCL‐2(+) (b), CD21(+) (c).

Case 2

A 47‐year‐old female presented with hoarseness for half a year. She had an occasional cough and expectoration, accompanied by a foreign body sensation in the pharynx and no acid reflux. The immunohistochemical results were as follows: Kappa (light chain restricted expression), Lambda (light chain restricted expression), Ki67 (positive rate 10%), CK(AE1/AE3)(−), CD20(+), PAX‐5(+), CD3 (a small amount +), CD5 (a small amount +), CD21 (FDC+), CD10 (focal +), Bcl‐6 (a small amount +), Bcl2(+), CyclinD1(−), LEF1(−), CD79a(+), IgD(−), EGFR(−) (Figure 4). The bilateral piriform fossae seemed to have lymphoid follicle proliferation. A light pink, smooth neoplasm protruded on the surface of the left ventricular band, laryngeal ventricle, and main airway. Discussion

Mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma is one of the subtypes of marginal zone B‐cell lymphoma in non‐Hodgkin lymphoma. It commonly occurs in the gastrointestinal tract (about 40%) [3]. Primary MALT lymphoma of the base of the tongue, larynx, and trachea is a rare disease. This disease is indolent, progresses slowly, and has a good prognosis.

MALT lymphoma is frequently associated with infection and chronic inflammation. Chronic inflammatory diseases can induce oligo‐ or monoclonal cell proliferation in mucosal lymphoid tissue, resulting in the development of lymphoma [4]. For example, the relationship between H. pylori chronic gastritis and gastric MALT lymphoma is well established and understood. More than 90% of cases of gastric MALT lymphoma can be induced by H. pylori infection. Moreover, H. pylori eradication therapy can lead to complete remission in about 80% of cases of low‐grade lymphoma [5, 6]. However, whether the etiology of MALT lymphoma in the pharynx, larynx, and trachea is related to H. pylori or laryngeal reflux has not been determined so far.

Analysis of the clinical characteristics of the two patients: Both patients were female, aged 40–50 years, and the initial symptom was hoarseness with no other remarkable findings. There was a completely negative autoimmune and inflammatory background. Case 1 mainly involved the larynx and trachea, while Case 2 involved multiple sites including the base of the tongue, larynx, trachea, and bronchus. Cases involving multiple sites of the respiratory tract are very uncommon. The two patients had similar characteristic manifestations during electronic laryngoscopy: Follicular‐like mucosal elevations with a smooth surface were scattered circumferentially on the mucosal surface of the affected areas (mucosa of the base of the tongue, larynx, and trachea), extending from the larynx all the way down to the tracheal carina. This was consistent with the results of lung CT, which showed evenly isodense nodules on the mucosal surface of the airway with no destruction of the tracheal ring bone. In the two cases reported by Daisuke Minami, the images seen by bronchoscopy were extremely similar to those of our electronic laryngoscopy. Therefore, we believe that the imaging diagnosis by endoscopic examination (including bronchoscopy and laryngoscopy) has good diagnostic implications and guiding significance for the diagnosis of MALT lymphoma.

The final diagnosis of MALT lymphoma still depends on pathological and immunohistochemical examinations. Its pathological characteristics are as follows: Under the microscope, the lymphoid tumor cells are diffusely distributed, presenting as infiltrations of small lymphocyte‐like cells, centrocyte‐like cells, and monocytoid B cells in varying numbers, with characteristic changes, such as lymphoepithelial lesions and follicle implantation visible. The typical immunophenotype: Tumor cells are positive for CD20, CD79a, and BCL‐2, and negative for CD3, CD5, CD10, CD23, and Cyclin D1. The Ki‐67 proliferation index of MALT lymphoma is usually low, and those with a high Ki‐67 proliferation index have a relatively poor prognosis [4].

Conclusion

Primary MALT lymphoma of the pharynx, larynx, and trachea is extremely rare. The two cases we collected have characteristic laryngoscopic manifestations (scattered circumferential follicular‐like mucosal elevations with smooth surfaces on the affected mucosal areas) which help to deepen our understanding of this disease and contribute to the ever‐growing body of knowledge in this field.
