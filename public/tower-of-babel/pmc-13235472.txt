A key SNP in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway gene CsaV3_6G009030 is highly correlated with cucumber fruit bitterness
Authors: Qianli Lu, Chuanxing Zhang, Hongyu Chen, Yue Yu, Zhimin Wang, Dayong Wei, Jiaqi Zou, Hongcheng Zhang, Hebing Wang, Qinglin Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cucumber fruit bitterness is a prominent issue affecting fruit quality and seriously compromises the commercial value of cucumbers. In this study, near-isogenic lines (NILs) were developed through six consecutive backcross generations using the bitter cucumber inbred line ‘5’ (P2) as the recurrent parent and the non-bitter cucumber inbred line ‘WK15’ (P1) as the donor parent, generating a BC6F2 population. Bulk segregant RNA-seq (BSR-seq) combined with metabolomics analysis was performed to screen for candidate regions and metabolic pathways significantly associated with fruit bitterness. A 5.8 Mb (2.7–8.5 Mb) candidate region for bitterness was mapped to chromosome 6. Functional analysis of the target region revealed significant enrichment of the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. Metabolic network analysis indicated that six flavonoid differential metabolites, including 4-methoxycinnamic acid and 2′-hydroxychalcone, were significantly enriched in bitter cucumbers, and their expression levels were highly consistent with the bitter phenotype. Through gene functional annotation and SNP validation, a key gene, CsaV3_6G009030, involved in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway was identified, and a functional dCAPS marker was developed based on this gene. This study reveals a potential association between flavonoid metabolism and bitterness formation in cucumber, providing a theoretical basis and technical support for the further application of marker-assisted selection in breeding high-quality, non-bitter cucumber varieties.

Introduction

Cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.), an annual climbing herb of the Cucurbitaceae family, is native to the Himalayan region of northwestern India (Sebastian et al., 2010). Rich in essential nutrients such as vitamins B2, C, and E (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2024), as well as minerals including calcium, phosphorus, and iron, cucumber is valued for its medicinal properties, including heat-clearing, thirst-quenching, diuretic, and detoxifying effects, making it a widely consumed health-promoting food (Gibson et al., 2016). Cucumber is also an important vegetable crop cultivated extensively worldwide, and its fruit flavor and quality directly affect its commercial value. Fruit bitterness in cucumber is a common quality defect during production, which directly impacts the edible taste and market acceptability. Elucidating the mechanism underlying bitterness formation holds significant theoretical and applied value for high-quality disease-resistant breeding.

The formation of bitterness in cucumber fruits is primarily associated with the accumulation of cucurbitacin triterpenoids. Cucurbitacins are generated from 2,3-oxidosqualene through multi-step catalysis including cyclization, hydroxylation, and acylation, in which genes such as Bi and Bt encode key biosynthetic enzymes and regulatory factors, constituting the core metabolic pathway underlying cucumber bitterness. (Shang et al., 2014; Shibuya et al., 2004). The cloning and mechanistic elucidation of these loci have provided important targets for non-bitter breeding. Li et al. (2015) identified 15 competitive allele-specific PCR markers flanking the bt locus (0.28–0.52 Mb) and successfully converted three of them into SSR markers (CsSSR6–1, CsSSR6–2, CsSSR6–3). Venkatesh et al. (2018) developed HRM and KASP markers based on a key SNP (G1178A) in the Bi gene, enabling precise differentiation between bitter (BiBi/Bibi) and non-bitter (bibi) cucumber genotypes using the Bi-KASP and *BiHyb-HRM1* markers.

Although the relationship between cucurbitacins and cucumber bitterness has been widely confirmed, plant bitterness is generally determined by multiple classes of secondary metabolites. In addition to triterpenoid cucurbitacins, polyphenolic compounds such as flavonoids also possess typical bitter characteristics. However, their functions, accumulation patterns, and regulatory mechanisms in the formation of cucumber bitterness have not yet been reported. Traditional research has mostly focused on the cucurbitacin pathway, overlooking the potential contribution of other secondary metabolites, including flavonoids, to the bitter phenotype, thereby limiting a comprehensive understanding of the regulatory network underlying cucumber bitterness. As important members of plant secondary metabolites, flavonoids play key roles in plant growth and development, color presentation, and stress defense (Zhuang et al., 2023). Flavonoids, particularly flavonols and flavones, can interact with chlorophyll to stabilize green color and contribute to fuller and brighter fruit skin coloration (Ma et al., 2024). Certain specific flavonoids, especially flavanones and flavonols, along with cucurbitacins, contribute to the bitter taste of cucumber fruits (Chen et al., 2023).

In this study, near-isogenic lines (NILs) with highly homozygous genetic backgrounds and stable differences only in the bitterness trait were used as materials, which can eliminate genetic background interference to the greatest extent and accurately reveal genetic and metabolic variations associated with bitterness, making them ideal materials for discovering new loci and mechanisms. Meanwhile, bulk segregant RNA-seq (BSR-seq) technology was employed to map candidate regions on cucumber chromosomes associated with the target trait. Combined metabolomics analysis was performed to screen for metabolic pathways and metabolites significantly correlated with the target candidate genes. These candidate genes were further screened and functional markers were developed. This study provides a solid foundation for identifying the genetic basis of the bitter flavor in cucumber fruits, which can provide practical guidance in the molecular breeding of non-bitter cucumbers.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Using the non-bitter cucumber inbred line ‘WK15’ (P₁) as the donor parent and the bitter cucumber inbred line ‘15’ (P₂) as the recurrent parent, a near-isogenic line (NIL) was constructed through six consecutive generations of backcrossing. In each backcross cycle, a single non-bitter plant was selected after one generation of selfing before being used for the next backcross. A total of 125 individuals from the BC6F1 generation and 925 materials from the BC6F2 generation of the same large population were obtained. These plant materials were provided by the Chongqing Municipal Academy of Agricultural Sciences as parental lines.

All plant materials were sown and planted on February 5, 2023, in the experimental field of Chongqing Academy of Agricultural Sciences under uniform conditions. In the 12-day period following pollination of the cucumber plants, three fruits were selected from each plant. The samples were evaluated for bitterness by six trained professional evaluators(Simiqueli et al., 2015; Talsma, 2016). The evaluation of bitterness referred to the grading standard of Ryland et al. (2017) with slight modification, as follows: level 1, virtually no bitterness with no detectable bitter sensation upon gustatory evaluation; level 2, slight bitterness detected through deliberate sensory analysis, yet remains insufficient to compromise the overall palatability or consumption experience; level 3, moderate bitterness with a distinct bitter perception emerging that moderately diminishes gustatory satisfaction, but remains within acceptable thresholds for routine ingestion; level 4, pronounced bitterness with an intensely bitter character that predominates, causing significant deterioration of sensory quality and inducing marked discomfort during mastication; and level 5, extreme bitterness, characterized by potent bitter attributes that reach physiologically intolerable levels, rendering the substance practically unsuitable for oral consumption.

BSR-seq analysis

BSR-seq analysis was performed to identify genomic regions associated with bitter taste in cucumber fruits. A total of 30 extreme bitter and 30 extreme non-bitter individuals were selected from the BC6F2 population. Fruit pulp samples were collected at 12 days post-anthesis from these 60 individuals, as well as from the two parental lines (‘15’ and ‘WK15’). RNA was extracted using the RNA TruSeq DNA PCR-free prep kit. Equal amounts of RNA from the 30 bitter individuals were pooled to form the 15–1 bulk, and similarly for the 30 non-bitter individuals to form the WK15–1 bulk. RNA samples from the two parents were processed individually. All four samples (15–1, WK15–1, 15, WK15) were subjected to RNA-seq using the Illumina NovaSeq™6000 platform, generating 150-bp paired-end reads. Each sample was sequenced to an average depth of 35×. Raw reads were filtered using fastp software (v0.20.0) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the cucumber (Chinese Long) v3 reference genome using BWA (0.7.12-r1039) mem (Li & Durbin, 2009) with default parameters. The “Fix Mate Information” command was used to ensure consistency between paired-end reads. Local realignment around insertions/deletions (InDels) was performed using the InDel Realigner tool from the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v3.8 (Zhu et al., 2014). SNP calling was then conducted using GATK HaplotypeCaller across all four samples. Raw variants were filtered to retain high-quality SNPs for subsequent analysis. SNP index and Δ(SNP index) values were calculated and visualized using sliding-window analysis with a window size of 600 kb and a step size of 60 kb. A total of 10,000 permutations were performed to establish 95% confidence intervals. Candidate genomic regions associated with bitter taste were identified based on these confidence intervals.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of target genes was performed using Top GO, in which the gene list and gene number of each term were calculated using the database established by the Gene Ontology Consortium (http://geneontology.org/). The associated pathways of genes in the candidate region were further annotated by the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.kegg.jp/) using a cluster profiler. Significant enrichment in comparison with the whole genome background was determined according to a P-value <0.05, calculated by the hypergeometric distribution method, highlighting the main biological functions of the candidate genes.

Metabolome analysis

Non-targeted metabolome analysis was performed on fruit pulp samples collected at 12 days after flowering from the BC6F2 generation of near-isogenic lines derived from the backcrossing of ‘WK15’ and ‘15’. Bitter cucumber samples were designated as K, and non-bitter samples as B, with six biological replicates per group. The raw data were obtained by metabolite extraction from the samples and detected by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The raw data were converted to mzXML format by Proteo Wizard, and then XCMS software was used for peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction. The data extracted by XCMS were first subjected to metabolite structure identification and data pre-processing, followed by experimental data quality evaluation and final data analysis. The metabolite identification analysis was performed with reference to Parsons Brinckerhoff's database and public libraries, including the Human Metabolome Database (http://www.hmdb.ca), Metlin (http://metlin.scripps.edu), Massbank (http://www.massbank.jp/), and Mzclound (https://www.mzcloud.org). The metabolites in the biological samples were structurally identified by matching according to the retention time, molecular mass (molecular mass error within <10 ppm), secondary fragmentation spectra, and collision energies of the metabolites in the databases. The identification results were subjected to strict manual secondary checking.

A range of supervised analysis methods was employed for all of the detected metabolites, including construction of an orthogonal partial least-squares–discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model. The relative importance of each metabolite to the OPLS-DA model was determined using variable importance for the projection (VIP) values. Strict criteria of OPLS-DA VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 were utilized for screening significant differential metabolites. The differential metabolites were subsequently mapped to the KEGG database to identify significant enrichment pathways. The screened differential metabolites were then used for agglomerate hierarchical clustering, whereby each object is grouped into one class and these classes are merged to form larger and larger objects until the end. The dataset was scaled by the Pheatmap package in R to obtain a hierarchical clustering map of relative quantitative values of differential metabolites. Subsequent visualization of the differences between the data was achieved by a heatmap using a color gradient.

Functional marker development and analysis

The SNP located at Chr6 at 7,268,346 base pairs (bp) within the candidate gene CsaV3_6G009030 was selected for the development of a derived cleaved amplified polymorphic sequence (dCAPS) molecular marker. To this end, specific primers were designed for amplification of the region containing the aforementioned SNP within the candidate gene CsaV3_6G009030 (Table S1). The amplification was then performed using DNA from the parental lines B and BK and 60 samples (30 each from bitter and non-bitter fruits) from the progeny. The amplified products were then digested with the restriction endonuclease MboII. The digested PCR products were separated by electrophoresis on a 3% agarose gel (100 V, 50 min) and observed under ultraviolet light. Following digestion, the PCR product of the non-bitter material was observed to cleave into two fragments of 46 bp and 160 bp, whereas the PCR product of the bitter material was a single band of 206 bp that was not cleaved.

Gene expression analysis using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The gene expression levels of the candidate gene CsaV3_6G009030 were verified in the parents and progeny using qRT-PCR. To this end, specific primers were designed for the candidate and internal reference genes using the National Center for Biotechnology Information website (Table S1). The qRT-PCR was performed using the Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and Novo-Start SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (Novoprotein, Shanghai, China). The relative expression levels were then quantified using the comparative 2-ΔΔCt method. This study incorporated three technical replicates and three experimental replicates to ensure the robustness of the experimental design.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated using Excel 2021 software. Analysis of variance and the Chi-square (χ2) test were carried out via IBM SPSS 26 software (https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics).

Results

BSR-seq analysis for determination of candidate regions contributing to the bitter taste trait in cucumber

To refine the localization of candidate genes related to the bitter taste trait in cucumber fruits, RNA was extracted from four samples (WK15, 15, WK15–1, and 15–1) for BSR-seq analysis. The raw data were filtered to obtain 53.6 GB of data with an average Q30 ratio of 95.77% and GC content of 43.94%. A substantial proportion of the reads, exceeding 99%, were successfully mapped to the cucumber (Chinese Long) v3 reference genome, attaining an average coverage depth of approximately 29-fold for the parental pool and of approximately 41-fold for the two progeny pools (Table S2). This observation signified that the quality and quantity of the obtained data were adequate for subsequent analyses.

In this study, genome-wide SNP detection was performed on the clean reads obtained from BSR-seq. After quality filtering, high-quality valid SNP sites were ultimately obtained. Based on the SNP genotyping results (Table S3), the SNP-index value for each SNP site was calculated, and the SNP-index distribution maps for chromosomes 1 to 7 were plotted. The results showed that, except for the target trait interval, the SNP-index (A) values were overall close to 1, and the SNP-index (B) values were also overall close to 1. Across the whole genome background, the difference in SNP-index between the two pools remained stable without obvious noise interference (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, Euclidean distance (ED) analysis was performed based on the SNP-index differences, and the genome-wide ED value distribution curve was plotted. The results revealed a significant ED peak only in the 2.7–8.5 Mb interval on chromosome 6, indicating that this region is the candidate association interval for the target trait (Fig. 1B). On the remaining chromosomes, ED values remained at background levels without significant abnormal fluctuations. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the target candidate region

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the target candidate gene region responsible for the bitter taste trait of cucumber fruit in Chr6 (2.7–8.5 Mb) were performed. The KEGG enrichment results showed that the candidate region genes were significantly enriched in the pentose phosphate pathway, carbon sequestration in photosynthetic organisms, protein processing, and flavonoid biosynthesis (Fig. 2A; Table S4). The GO enrichment results showed that the candidate region genes were significantly enriched in various biological processes, including pyrophosphodiester α-1,3-glucosyltransferase, ion transport, and nuclear envelope (Fig. 2B; Table S5). In this study, a total of 271 metabolites were identified in cucumber fruits through untargeted metabolomics analysis, which could be classified into nine major categories based on their chemical structures. Among these, lipids and lipid-like molecules accounted for the highest proportion (42.1%, 114 species), followed by phenylpropanoids and polyketides (15.9%, 43 species) (Fig. S1). Identification and functional analysis of metabolites in the two cucumber fruit varieties

A differential metabolite analysis was conducted on the two distinct cucumber varieties to identify significant differences in metabolite profiles based on OPLS-DA VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 as criteria for statistical significance. A total of 231 metabolites exhibited significant differences, with 210 metabolites demonstrating significant down-regulation and 21 metabolites exhibiting significant up-regulation (Fig. S3). The heat map (Fig. 4A) demonstrated that the 12 samples of bitter and non-bitter flavors were grouped into two primary categories, which was consistent with the data source (Table S6). The bidirectional clustering heatmap based on cluster analysis further classified the samples into nine distinct clusters based on the similarity of substance expression patterns. Notably, both cluster 2 and cluster 8 in the trend analysis exhibited a significant correlation with cucumber fruit bitterness (Fig. 4B). Within these two clusters, five phenylpropanes and polyketides, eight lipids and lipid-like molecules, and nine organic acids and their derivatives exhibited significant differences (Table S7). The top five KEGG pathways showing significant enrichment with these clusters were ABC transporter proteins, α-linolenic acid metabolism, anthocyanin biosynthesis, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, and arginine biosynthesis (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, flavonoids and terpenoids biosynthesis processes were also found to be significantly enriched (Table S8). Screening and identification of candidate genes

Based on the above BSR and metabolomic analyses, we tentatively identified five genes (CsaV3_6G006270, CsaV3_6G004010, CsaV3_6G009030, CsaV3_6G007580, and CsaV3_6G006720) from the significantly enriched pathways (including the pentose phosphate pathway, flavonoid biosynthesis, sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis, and alpha-1,3-glucosyltransferase activity) as candidate genes for cucumber fruit bitterness (Table S9). However, sequence alignment of the candidate genes revealed that, except for CsaV3_6G009030, the sequence polymorphisms in the remaining genes were predominantly synonymous mutations and lacked effective SNPs. As a result, we were unable to successfully develop molecular markers with practical discriminatory power for breeding (Table S10).

Integrative analysis of the flavonoid biosynthetic metabolic network using BSR-seq and metabolomics

Integrated analysis of BSR-seq and metabolomic sequencing revealed a close association between the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway and bitterness in cucumber fruits. In total, six differential metabolites were enriched in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, including one precursor (4-Methoxycinnamic acid), two chalcones (2-Hydroxychalcone and Bavachalcone), and three flavonoids/anthocyanins (3-Hydroxy-3′,4′-dimethoxyflavone, Apigenin 7-O-gentiobioside, and Kaempferol-3-O-glucoside). Based on differential metabolite enrichment and metabolic network analysis, these six flavonoid metabolites were found to be significantly accumulated in bitter accessions, and their abundance changes were highly consistent with the bitterness phenotype (Fig. 6), thus being identified as the major contributors to bitterness in cucumber fruits. The SNP-derived candidate gene CsaV3_6G009030 shows high correlation with bitter taste

Quantitative analysis of the CsaV3_6G009030 gene was performed in both bitter and non-bitter plant materials. Notably, the expression level of CsaV3_6G009030 was significantly higher in bitter materials than in non-bitter materials (Fig. 7A). In addition, BSR-seq results indicated that the CsaV3_6G009030 gene contains five SNPs, among which a non-synonymous SNP (Chr6:7,268,346 bp) showed a significant change in SNP-index (Table S10). Analysis revealed that BK aligned with the reference genome, exhibiting guanine (G) at this position, whereas K displayed a bias toward thymine (T). This SNP leads to an amino acid substitution from lysine (TTT, K) to asparagine (TTG, N).

Development and validation of dCAPS markers in the F2 populations

To ascertain the relationship between the CsaV3_6G009030 SNP (Chr6: 7,268,346 bp) and the bitter flavor trait in cucumber fruits, dCAPS markers were developed and evaluated in the parental lines and in extreme-phenotype individuals of the F2 generation. dCAPS markers are based on mismatches in PCR amplicon primers to construct a target-based restriction endonuclease cleavage site of the mutant. Specifically, the mismatch SNP (Chr6: 7,268,346 bp) (G) and the mutant site SNP (G) in the non-bitter parental material (WK15) led to the generation of the MboII restriction site (Fig. 8A). The MboII enzyme was able to successfully convert the PCR product (206 bp) of the CsaV3_6G009030 gene fragment from the non-bitter parental material (WK15) into two fragments. In contrast, the PCR-amplified fragment from the bitter parental material (15) could not be cleaved by the enzyme into the anticipated intact 206-bp fragment (Fig. 8B).

In the F2 generation of extreme phenotypic individuals, 75% of the PCR-amplified fragments of the non-bitter extreme material were enzymatically cleaved, resulting in the formation of two distinct fragments (46 bp and 160 bp). In contrast, the majority of the PCR-amplified fragments of the extreme bitter material exhibited a single uncut band. These data indicated that the SNP (Chr6: 7,268,346 bp) locus of the CsaV3_6G009030 gene is associated with cucumber fruit bitterness, providing a valuable functional marker for MAS breeding studies. This marker can be used to identify allelic variants associated with cucumber fruit bitterness and has the potential to assist in genotype selection in the future.

Discussion

Cucumber is a globally popular and highly economically valuable vegetable crop. The development and advancement of genomic and metabolomic sequencing technologies have accelerated the screening of candidate genes for target traits, facilitating research in this field.

Significant breakthroughs have been made in understanding the genetic regulation of cucumber bitterness: Huang Sanwen's team successively mapped the key genes Bi and Bt to a 35 kb region on chromosome 6 and a 442 kb region on chromosome 5, respectively (Qi et al., 2013; Shang et al., 2014). More recently, further research has revealed that the CsNPF1 gene, also located on chromosome 6, acts as a negative regulator in bitterness formation (Nie et al., 2025).

Unlike the materials used in the above studies, this study, based on the genetic background of South China-type cucumber, used two cucumber lines, ‘WK15’ and ‘15’, as the original parents to construct a near-isogenic line (BC₆F₂) targeting the fruit bitterness trait. The genetic mechanism underlying the bitterness trait in this material may differ from that in previous studies. Based on field statistics, the BC₆F₂ population exhibited a segregation ratio of 8: 1 for bitter versus non-bitter fruits (Table S11). We speculate that this may be due to fruit bitterness being a dominant trait controlled by two genes in this line, along with a recessive epistatic effect of dominant genes and a dominant homozygous lethal phenomenon. However, BSR-seq analysis revealed that the bitterness-related gene was mapped to only a single region on chromosome 6. This phenomenon is not uncommon in genetic studies and may be attributed to multiple factors. The statistical framework of BSR-seq is primarily designed to detect allele frequency differences for a single QTL. When multiple QTLs with minor effects are present, each locus individually may fail to reach the significance threshold, especially when interactions exist among these loci (Pang et al., 2012). This method typically uses extreme-phenotype individuals to construct bulked samples; however, when complex phenotypic distributions arise from digenic interactions, extreme bulks may not capture the full spectrum of genetic variation (Magwene et al., 2011). Furthermore, in a digenic interaction system, the relationship between phenotype and genotype is often nonlinear, and the allele frequency difference analysis on which BSR-seq relies cannot accurately capture such complex associations (Verhoeven et al., 2010). When epistatic interaction occurs between two genes, i.e., the effect of one gene depends on the genotype of the other, BSR-seq may only detect the gene with a larger major effect while missing its interacting partner (Kamphuis et al., 2019). In addition, if the two interacting genes are physically close to each other or in linkage disequilibrium, BSR-seq may mistakenly merge them into a single common QTL region (Ellis et al., 2019). Nevertheless, our subsequent BSR-seq analysis showed that the ED analysis and SNP-index analysis yielded consistent results, indicating that the BSR-seq findings are reliable. Even though only a single region was mapped, the SNPs within this region can still be used for molecular marker development. These findings not only expand our understanding of the genetic basis of cucumber bitterness but also highlight the innovation of this study in terms of plant materials and gene mapping strategy.

During plant growth, a range of compounds—including alkaloids, terpenoids, bitter peptides, catechins, and flavonoids—contribute to the formation of bitter flavor in fruits, with the terpenoid cucurbitacin playing a particularly prominent role (Luo et al., 2024). As the primary source of bitterness in cucumber fruit, cucurbitacin shares the upstream metabolic precursor acetyl-CoA with the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway. These two pathways may engage in both competitive and synergistic regulatory interactions for metabolic resources, thereby coordinately responding to environmental and developmental signals (Zhuang et al., 2023). Furthermore, through transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of cucumber varieties with different levels of astringency, Zhang et al. identified the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway as a key route underlying cucumber astringency and determined transcription factors such as CsMYB4, CsMYB111, and CsMYB32 as key candidate genes (Zhang et al., 2025). In a study on sponge gourd bitterness, Zhao et al. found that, in addition to the cucurbitacin biosynthesis pathway, the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway was also significantly enriched, with related genes exhibiting marked expression changes (Zhao et al., 2022).

Notably, in this study, field-based genetic analysis revealed an 8:1 segregation ratio for bitterness, and both CsaV3_6G009030 and the Bi gene (CsaV3_6G007580) were enriched in the candidate region. Although the Bi gene harbors five SNP sites, all of them are synonymous mutations and therefore cannot be converted into effective molecular markers. Nevertheless, we speculate that a functional connection may exist between these two genes; they may indirectly influence the formation of fruit bitterness by regulating associated metabolic pathways, coordinating antioxidant and defense responses, and participating in gene co-expression networks. Further investigation through functional validation is warranted to explore whether CsaV3_6G009030 interacts with the Bi gene, and progeny testing combined with fine mapping should be conducted to verify this genetic model. These hypotheses, of course, require in-depth exploration in future studies.

Conclusion

The present study adopted an integrated approach, incorporating BSR-Seq, metabolomics, gene expression trend analysis, and qRT-PCR to identify candidate genes associated with bitter taste traits in cucumber fruits. Following the implementation of rigorous screening criteria, CsaV3_6G009030 emerged as a strong candidate gene responsible for the formation of the bitter taste trait. A SNP within the CsaV3_6G009030 gene was effectively converted into a dCAPS molecular marker, thereby providing a practical tool for the selection of cucumber fruit varieties that are free of bitter taste.
