The evolving surface Cahn-Hilliard equation with a degenerate mobility
Charles M. Elliott and Thomas Sales

Abstract
. We consider the existence of suitable weak solutions to the Cahn-Hilliard equation with a non-constant (degenerate) mobility on a class of evolving surfaces. We also show weak-strong uniqueness for the case of a positive mobility function, and under some further assumptions on the initial data we show uniqueness for a class of strong solutions for a degenerate mobility function.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
35K65, 35K55, 35Q79

1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the Cahn-Hilliard equation on a sufficiently smooth, closed evolving surface,
\Gamma(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
moving with prescribed velocity
V
, with a non constant mobility
M(\cdot)
,

(1)

\displaystyle\partial^{\bullet}u+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)u=\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\left(M(u)\nabla_{\Gamma}\left(-\varepsilon\Delta_{\Gamma}u+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F^{\prime}(u)\right)\right),

as derived in
[
13
]
.
We note that we allow velocities
V
which have some non-zero tangential component rather than evolution purely in the normal direction — see also the discussion in
[
13
]
.
This equation is posed on the non-cylindrical space-time domain

\mathcal{G}_{T}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Gamma(t)\times\{t\},

which is known since the velocity field
V
is prescribed.
Here
\partial^{\bullet}
denotes the material derivative following the flow of the velocity field
V
,
\nabla_{\Gamma}
denotes the tangential gradient, and
\Delta_{\Gamma}
denotes the Laplace-Beltrami operator — all of which are defined in further detail in the following section.
In particular we are interested in the case where
M
is a degenerate mobility, that is
M(r)>0
on
(-1,1)
and
M(\pm 1)=0
— a typical example being
M(r)=1-r^{2}
.
We focus on potential functions of the form
F=F_{1}+F_{2}
where
F_{1}
is convex (and possibly singular), and
F_{2}^{\prime}
has at most linear growth.
We provide explicit assumptions later on.

Interest in the Cahn-Hilliard equation posed on an evolving surface stems from applications, for instance
[
19
,
27
,
33
,
34
]
and the references therein.
This has resulted in work from both an analytic perspective, see for instance
[
3
,
2
,
14
,
26
]
, and a computational perspective, see for instance
[
10
,
23
,
25
]
.
However, to the authors’ knowledge there has been no rigorous study of the Cahn-Hilliard equation with a degenerate mobility posed on an (evolving) surface.
We note also that the models in
[
19
,
27
,
33
]
involve degenerate mobilities.

The main contributions of this work are twofold.
Firstly we extend the existing analysis to a variable, and then degenerate, mobility function, by adapting the approach of
[
22
]
.
Secondly, and the more novel part of this work, we show a weak-strong uniqueness result for a positive mobility function, and a uniqueness result for a class of sufficiently smooth solutions for the degenerate mobility.
For some existing further literature concerning the degenerate Cahn-Hilliard equation on a stationary Euclidean domain we refer to
[
1
,
17
,
20
]
, and we refer the reader to the recent preprint
[
16
]
in which the authors also show a uniqueness result for a positive mobility function.
We also note that our existence theory for a degenerate mobility does not apply to general smoothly evolving surfaces without some further assumptions on the potential and mobility functions.

2.
Preliminaries

We assume
\Gamma(t)
is a
C^{3}
closed, orientable evolving surface, where there exist
C^{3}
diffeomorphisms
\Phi(t):\Gamma_{0}\rightarrow\Gamma(t)
such that for points
x_{0}\in\Gamma_{0}
one has

\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(x_{0},t)=V(\Phi(x_{0},t),t),

for
V
the velocity of
\Gamma(t)
.
We shall assume throughout that
V
is sufficiently smooth so that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|V\|_{C^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\leq C<\infty.

We denote the pushforward of a function
\eta
on
\Gamma_{0}
as
\Phi_{t}\eta=\eta\circ\Phi(t)^{-1}
, and the pullback of a function
\psi
on
\Gamma(t)
by
\Phi_{-t}\psi=\psi\circ\Phi(t)
.
For a sufficiently smooth function
\eta
the strong material derivative is defined as

\partial^{\bullet}\eta:=\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{-t}\eta\right),

and this is used to define suitable notion of a weak material derivative (see
[
4
,
5
]
).

Denoting the normal vector on
\Gamma(t)
by
\boldsymbol{\nu}
one defines the tangential gradient as

\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta:=(I-\boldsymbol{\nu}\otimes\boldsymbol{\nu})\nabla\eta^{e},

for
\eta^{e}
denoting some differentiable extension of
\eta
on a neighbourhood of
\Gamma(t)
.
Writing this componentwise as

\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{D}_{1}\eta\\
\underline{D}_{2}\eta\\
\underline{D}_{3}\eta\end{pmatrix},

one defines the tangential divergence of a vector field,
\boldsymbol{\eta}
as

\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\underline{D}_{i}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i},

and the Laplace-Beltrami operator is defined as
\Delta_{\Gamma}\eta=\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta
.
It can be shown that these operators are independent of the choice of extension.

2.1.
Sobolev spaces

Definition 2.1
.

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, the Sobolev space
H^{1,p}(\Gamma)
is then defined by

H^{1,p}(\Gamma):=\{\eta\in L^{p}(\Gamma)\mid\underline{D}_{i}\eta\in L^{p}(\Gamma),\ i=1,...,n+1\},

and higher order spaces (
k\in\mathbb{N}
) are defined recursively by

H^{k,p}(\Gamma):=\{\eta\in H^{k-1,p}(\Gamma)\mid\underline{D}_{i}\eta\in H^{k-1,p}(\Gamma),\ i=1,...,n+1\},

where
H^{0,p}(\Gamma):=L^{p}(\Gamma)
.
These Sobolev spaces are known to be Banach spaces when equipped with norm,

\|\eta\|_{H^{k,p}(\Gamma)}:=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{k}\|\underline{D}^{\alpha}\eta\|_{L^{p}(\Gamma)}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&p\in[1,\infty),\\
\max_{|\alpha|=1,...,k}\|\underline{D}^{\alpha}\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)},&p=\infty,\end{cases}

where we consider all weak derivatives of order
|\alpha|
.
We use shorthand notation,
H^{k}(\Gamma):=H^{k,2}(\Gamma)
, for the case
p=2
.

Next we introduce some notation which will be used throughout.

Notation 2.2
.

For a
\mathcal{H}^{2}-
measurable set,
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we denote the
\mathcal{H}^{2}
measure of
X
by

|X|:=\mathcal{H}^{2}(X).

For a function
\eta\in L^{1}(X)
we denote the mean value of
\eta
on
X
by

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{X}\eta:=\frac{1}{|X|}\int_{X}\eta.

We refer the reader to
[
6
,
30
]
for further details on Sobolev spaces defined on manifolds.

It can be shown that the pairs
(H^{m,p}(\Gamma(t)),\Phi_{t})
are compatible in the sense of
[
4
,
5
]
for
m=0,1,2
and
p\in[1,\infty]
.
Compatibility of these spaces allows one to obtain Sobolev inequalities on
\Gamma(t)
independent of
t
.

With these definitions, we can define time-dependent Bochner spaces.

Definition 2.3
.

In the following we let
X(t)
denote a Banach space dependent on
t
, for instance
H^{m,p}(\Gamma(t))
.
The space
L^{2}_{X}
consists of (equivalence classes of) functions

\displaystyle\eta:[0,T]\rightarrow\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}X(t)\times\{t\},

\displaystyle t\mapsto(\bar{\eta}(t),t),

such that
\Phi_{-(\cdot)}\bar{\eta}(\cdot)\in L^{2}(0,T;X(0))
.
We identify
\eta
with
\bar{\eta}
.
This space is equipped with norm

\|\eta\|_{L^{2}_{X}}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|\eta(t)\|_{X(t)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

for
\eta\in L^{2}_{X}
.
If
X(t)
is a family of Hilbert spaces then this norm is induced by the inner product

(\eta,\zeta)_{L^{2}_{X}}=\int_{0}^{T}(\eta(t),\zeta(t))_{X(t)},

for
\eta,\zeta\in L^{2}_{X}
.
In this case, as justified in
[
4
,
5
]
, we make the identification
(L^{2}_{X})^{*}\cong L^{2}_{X^{*}}
.
In particular for
X(t)=H^{1}(\Gamma(t))
we shall write
(L^{2}_{H^{1}})^{*}\cong L^{2}_{H^{-1}}
, for
H^{-1}(\Gamma(t))
the dual space of
H^{1}(\Gamma(t))
.

One can similarly define
L^{p}_{X}
for
p\in[1,\infty]
, which is equipped with a norm

\|\eta\|_{L^{p}_{X}}:=\begin{cases}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|\eta(t)\|^{p}_{X(t)}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&p\in[1,\infty),\\
\underset{t\in[0,T]}{\operatorname{ess\ sup\ }}\|\eta(t)\|_{X(t)},&p=\infty.\end{cases}

We refer the reader to
[
4
]
for further details.

We now state a transport theorem for quantities defined on an evolving surface.
Firstly, we use the following notation for bilinear forms,

\displaystyle m_{*}(t;\hat{\eta},\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\langle\hat{\eta},\zeta\rangle_{H^{-1}(\Gamma(t))\times H^{1}(\Gamma(t))},

\displaystyle m(t;\eta,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\eta\zeta,

\displaystyle g(t;\eta,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\eta\zeta\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V(t),

\displaystyle a(t;\eta,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\zeta,

where
\eta,\zeta\in H^{1}(\Gamma(t))
,
\hat{\eta}\in H^{-1}(\Gamma(t))
.
The argument in
t
will typically be omitted.
For weakly differentiable functions we have the following result.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
18
, Lemma 5.2]
)
.

For all
\eta,\zeta\in H^{1}_{H^{-1}}\cap L^{2}_{L^{2}}
the map
t\mapsto m(\eta(t),\zeta(t))
is absolutely continuous and such that

\frac{d}{dt}m(\eta,\zeta)=m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}\eta,\zeta)+m_{*}(\eta,\partial^{\bullet}\zeta)+g(\eta,\zeta).

Moreover, if
\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial^{\bullet}\eta,\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial^{\bullet}\zeta\in L^{2}_{L^{2}}
then
t\mapsto a(\eta(t),\zeta(t))
is absolutely continuous and such that

\frac{d}{dt}a(\eta,\zeta)=a(\partial^{\bullet}\eta,\zeta)+a(\eta,\partial^{\bullet}\zeta)+b(\eta,\zeta).

Here

b(\eta,\zeta)=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\mathbf{B}(V)\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\zeta,

where

\mathbf{B}(V)=\left((\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)\mathrm{id}-(\nabla_{\Gamma}V+(\nabla_{\Gamma}V)^{T})\right).

3.
Positive mobility functions

In this section we consider a positive mobility function,
M(\cdot)
, such that
M\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})
and

0<M_{1}\leq M(\cdot)\leq M_{2},

for some positive constants
M_{1},M_{2}
.
This will serve as a more regular mobility function which we can use to approximate the degenerate mobilities later (which vanish at
\pm 1
).
We also only consider the case of a regular potential with polynomial growth conditions, as in
[
13
]
.
More specifically we assume that
F_{1},F_{2}
are such that the following assumptions hold.

Assumption 1
.

(1)

There exists some constant
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
F(r)\geq\beta
for all
r\in\mathrm{dom}(F)
.

(2)

F_{1}
is convex.

(3)

F_{2}
is such that there exists some
\alpha>0
with

|F^{\prime}_{2}(r)|\leq\alpha(|r|+1).

(4)

There exists some
q\in[1,\infty)
and
\alpha>0
such that
|F_{1}^{\prime}(r)|\leq\alpha|r|^{q}+\alpha
.

(5)

There exists
\alpha>0
such that

|rF_{1}^{\prime}(r)|\leq\alpha(F_{1}(r)+1).

A standard example of such a potential is the quartic double well function given by

F(r)=\frac{(1-r^{2})^{2}}{4}=\frac{1+r^{4}}{4}-\frac{r^{2}}{2}.

Here we consider weak solutions to be a pair of functions
(u,w)\in L^{\infty}_{H^{1}}\cap H^{1}_{H^{-1}}\times L^{2}_{H^{1}}
such that

(2)

\displaystyle m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}u,\phi)+g(u,\phi)+\hat{a}(M(u),w,\phi)=0,

(3)

\displaystyle m(w,\phi)=\varepsilon a(u,\phi)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m(F^{\prime}(u),\phi),

for all
\phi\in H^{1}(\Gamma(t))
, almost all
t\in[0,T]
, and such that
u(0)=u_{0}
almost everywhere on
\Gamma_{0}
.
Here we have introduced a new trilinear form given by

\displaystyle\hat{a}(t;\phi,\psi,\chi)=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\phi\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\chi,

where
\phi,\psi,\chi
are sufficiently smooth functions.
We will omit the argument
t
throughout, as we have above.

The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists a solution pair
(u,w)\in L^{\infty}_{H^{1}}\cap H^{1}_{H^{-1}}\times L^{2}_{H^{1}}
solving (
2
), (
3
) for all
\phi\in H^{1}(\Gamma(t))
, almost all
t\in[0,T]
, and such that
u(0)=u_{0}
.

3.1.
Existence

3.1.1.
Galerkin Approximation

We proceed by Galerkin approximation, choosing a countable basis of functions given by
\Phi_{t}\psi_{i}
, where
\psi_{i}
are eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator,
-\Delta_{\Gamma}
, on
\Gamma_{0}
.
We denote the finite dimensional subspaces spanned by these (pushforwards of) eigenfunctions as

V^{K}(t):=\text{span}\{\Phi_{t}\psi_{i}\mid i=1,...,K\}\subset H^{1}(\Gamma(t)).

It is worth noting that by construction, these basis functions have vanishing material time derivative — that is
\partial^{\bullet}\Phi_{t}\psi_{i}=0
for all
i
.
We define a
H^{1}
-projection onto
V^{K}(t)
, denoted
P_{K}\phi
, as the unique solution of

(P_{K}\phi,\psi^{K})_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}=(\phi,\psi^{K})_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))},

for all
\psi^{K}\in V^{K}(t)
.
Now we consider the approximate solutions,
(u^{K},w^{K})
, solving

(4)

\displaystyle m(\partial^{\bullet}u^{K},\phi)+g(u^{K},\phi)+\hat{a}(M(u^{K}),w^{K},\phi)=0,

(5)

\displaystyle m(w^{K},\phi)=\varepsilon a(u^{K},\phi)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m(F^{\prime}(u^{K}),\phi),

for all
\phi\in V^{K}(t)
, almost all
t\in[0,T]
, and such that
u(0)=P_{K}u_{0}
almost everywhere on
\Gamma_{0}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists a short time solution
(u^{K},w^{K})
solving (
4
), (
5
) such that
u^{K}(0)=P_{K}u_{0}
.

Proof.

The proof of this result is standard, and follows from the usual appeal to standard ODE theory, namely the Peano existence theorem as the nonlinearities are continuous.
We refer the reader to
[
13
,
14
,
26
]
for details on analogous results.
∎

Next we show uniform energy estimates to establish long time existence for sufficiently large
K
, and let us pass to the limit
K\rightarrow\infty
.
For this we note the following properties of our choice of basis functions (see also the discussion in
[
13
,
26
]
):

(1)

The basis
(\psi_{i})_{i}
of
H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})
is such that
\psi_{1}
is constant on
\Gamma_{0}
.
This guarantees that
1\in V^{K}(t)
for all
K\in\mathbb{N},t\in[0,T]
.

(2)

We define
P_{2}^{K}(t):L^{2}(\Gamma(t))\rightarrow V^{K}(t)
to be the
L^{2}
projection defined by

(P^{K}_{2}(t)\phi,\psi)_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}=(\phi,\psi)_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))},\ \forall\psi\in V^{K}(t).

Then for
\eta\in H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})
that

\|P_{2}^{K}(0)\eta\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})}\leq C\|\eta\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})},

and given
\gamma>0
there exists
K^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
K>K^{*}
,

\|P_{2}^{K}(0)\eta-\eta\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{0})}\leq\gamma\|\eta\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})}.

We notice that this second property implies that for
\eta\in H^{1}(\Gamma(t))

\displaystyle\|P_{2}^{K}(t)\eta-\eta\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\leq C\gamma\|\eta\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))},

and likewise it is straightforward to see that the first property and compatibility of the pairs
(H^{1}(\Gamma(t)),\Phi_{t})
, in the sense of
[
4
,
5
]
, implies that

\|P_{2}^{K}(t)\eta\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}\leq C\|\eta\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}.

Lastly we note that
1\in V^{K}(t)
for all
K\in\mathbb{N},t\in[0,T]
(since it is the first eigenfunction of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on a closed surface), and so one may use the constant function
1
as a test function so that

\int_{\Gamma(t)}u^{K}(t)=\int_{\Gamma_{0}}u_{0},

We refer the reader to
[
26
]
for details on these calculations.

Lemma 3.3
.

For sufficiently large
K
, the pair
(u^{K},w^{K})
exists on
[0,T]
and is such that

(6)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}E^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t]+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}M(u^{K})\|\nabla_{\Gamma}w^{K}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\leq C,

for a constant,
C
, independent of
K
but depending on
T
.

Here we are using the Ginzburg-Landau functional,

(7)

\displaystyle E^{\text{CH}}[\phi;t]:=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\frac{\varepsilon|\nabla_{\Gamma}\phi|^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\phi).

It is known that for a stationary, Euclidean domain that the Cahn-Hilliard equation (with a constant mobility) is the
H^{-1}
gradient flow of the Ginzburg-Landau functional, we refer the reader to
[
7
]
for details, but even in our present setting this functional is still useful in the analysis.

Proof.

We begin by testing (
4
) with
w^{K}
so that

(8)

\displaystyle m(\partial^{\bullet}u^{K},w^{K})+g(u^{K},w^{K})+\hat{a}(M(u^{K}),w^{K},w^{K})=0,

and (
5
) with
\partial^{\bullet}u^{K}\in V^{K}(t)
,

m(\partial^{\bullet}u^{K},w^{K})=\varepsilon a(u^{K},\partial^{\bullet}u^{K})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m(F^{\prime}(u^{K}),\partial^{\bullet}u^{K}).

Using the transport theorem, Proposition
2.4
, on the latter equality one finds

(9)

\displaystyle m(\partial^{\bullet}u^{K},w^{K})=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}a(u^{K},u^{K})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}b(u^{K},u^{K})+\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m(F(u^{K}),1)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}g(F(u^{K}),1),

hence using (
9
) in (
8
) we find

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t]+\hat{a}(M(u^{K}),w^{K},w^{K})+g(u^{K},w^{K})=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}b(u^{K},u^{K})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}g(F(u^{K}),1).

It remains to bound these terms on the right.
Firstly we observe from the smoothness assumption on
V
, and the lower bound on
F(\cdot)
, that

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}b(u^{K},u^{K})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}g(F(u^{K}),1)\leq C+CE^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t],

for a constant,
C
, independent of
t,K
.
The problematic term here is
g(u^{K},w^{K})
, for which we use the same argument as in
[
13
,
26
]
.
By definition of
P_{2}^{K}
one has that

g(u^{K},w^{K})=m(w^{K},P_{2}^{K}(u^{K}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)),

where
P_{2}^{K}(u^{K}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)\in V^{K}(t)
so that one can use this in (
3
) for

g(u^{K},w^{K})=\varepsilon a(u^{K},P_{2}^{K}(u^{K}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V))+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m(F^{\prime}(u^{K}),P_{2}^{K}(u^{K}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)).

It is then clear that

\displaystyle g(u^{K},w^{K})\leq C\|u^{K}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}g(F^{\prime}(u^{K}),u^{K})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|m(F^{\prime}(u^{K}),P_{2}^{K}(u^{K}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)-u^{K}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)|,

where it follows from the assumptions on
P_{2}^{K}
, and the growth assumptions on
F^{\prime}
(Assumption
1
), that for any
\gamma>0
we can choose some sufficiently large
K
so that

\displaystyle|g(u^{K},w^{K})|

\displaystyle\leq C+C\|\nabla_{\Gamma}u^{K}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+C\int_{\Gamma(t)}F_{1}(u^{K})+C\gamma\|\nabla_{\Gamma}u^{K}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{q+1}

\displaystyle\leq C+CE^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t]+C\gamma E^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t]^{\frac{q+1}{2}}.

Hence we find that (
10
) becomes

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t]+\hat{a}(M(u^{K}),w^{K},w^{K})\leq C+CE^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t]+C\gamma E^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t]^{\frac{q+1}{2}},

where
\gamma>0
can be taken arbitrarily small by taking
K
sufficiently large.

We wish to use the Bihari-LaSalle inequality
[
12
]
to conclude, but we require that
E^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t]\geq 0
for this – which is not the case.
However, we know that
E^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t]
is bounded below, and so we can add some constant,
\tilde{C}
, (independent of
t
) so that

\widetilde{E^{\text{CH}}}[u^{K};t]:=E^{\text{CH}}[u^{K};t]+\tilde{C}\geq 0,

and consider (
11
) with
E^{\text{CH}}
replaced by
\widetilde{E^{\text{CH}}}
.
The result then follows as in the proof of
[
26
, Lemma 5.4]
.
We note that this gives an upper bound in terms of
\widetilde{E^{\text{CH}}}[P_{K}u_{0}]
, which we now bound independently of
K
.
Firstly we see that

{E^{\text{CH}}}[P_{K}u_{0}]\leq C+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}P_{K}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{0})}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|F_{1}(P_{K}u_{0})\|_{L^{1}(\Gamma_{0})}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|F_{2}(P_{K}u_{0}))\|_{L^{1}(\Gamma_{0})},

and by using the fact that
\|P_{K}u_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})}
as well as the growth conditions for
F_{1},F_{2}
we see that

{E^{\text{CH}}}[P_{K}u_{0}]\leq C+C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})}^{2}+\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})}^{q+1},

and so our bound is independent of
K
.
∎

3.1.2.
Passage to the limit

From this we have obtained uniform bounds for
u^{K}
in
L^{\infty}_{H^{1}}
and
w^{K}
in
L^{2}_{H^{1}}
, and hence there exist limits
u\in L^{\infty}_{H^{1}}
and
w\in L^{2}_{H^{1}}
such that, up to a subsequence, we have

\displaystyle u^{K}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}u,\text{ weak-}*\text{ in }L^{\infty}_{H^{1}},

\displaystyle w^{K}{\rightharpoonup}w,\text{ weakly in }L^{2}_{H^{1}}.

We cannot pass to the limit immediately however, since we do not have any uniform bounds on the time derivative
\partial^{\bullet}u^{K}
.
Hence to pass to the limit we firstly want to rewrite the equation with an alternate characterisation of the weak material time derivative.
Before we do this however, we observe that arguing along the lines of
[
13
]
Lemma 4.8 one can show that one has strong convergence
u^{K}\rightarrow u
in
L^{2}_{L^{2}}
, which will be useful in passing to the limit for the nonlinear terms.

We recall the following generalisations of the dominated convergence theorem.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
13
, Theorem B.2]
)
.

\displaystyle+C\left(\|U\|_{H^{1,4}(\Gamma(t))}+\|U\|_{H^{1,4}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\right)\|\partial^{\bullet}U\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\|\bar{U}\|_{U}^{2}.

The most difficult term to bound is

|\hat{a}(M(U)-M(u),w,\mathcal{S}_{U}\bar{U})|\leq C\|\bar{U}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}w\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\|\bar{U}\|_{U},

where we have used the Lipschitz continuity of
M(\cdot)
and Hölder’s inequality.
We now use Lemma
3.9
to see that

\displaystyle\|\bar{U}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t)}\left(1+\log\left(1+\frac{C\|\bar{U}\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}}{\|\bar{U}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t)}\left(1+\log\left(\frac{C\left(\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}+\|\bar{U}\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)}{\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

Using these inequalities, and Young’s inequality where needed, in (
20
) one finds

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\bar{U}\|_{U}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\leq K(t)\|\bar{U}\|_{U}^{2}\\
+C\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t)}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}w\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t)}\|\bar{U}\|_{U}\log\left(\frac{C\left(\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}+\|\bar{U}\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)}{\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where

\displaystyle K(t)

\displaystyle=C\left(1+\|\nabla_{\Gamma}w\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t)}^{2}+\|\partial^{\bullet}U\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+C\left(\|U\|_{H^{1,4}(\Gamma(t))}+\|U\|_{H^{1,4}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\right)\|\partial^{\bullet}U\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))},

and we conclude by using Lemma
3.8
.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

(1)

It follows that
K\in L^{1}([0,T])
from the same argument as used in
[
26
]
.

(2)

The above argument is only formal as we do not necessarily have that
\mathcal{S}_{U}\bar{U}\in H^{1}_{H^{1}}
as we only know that
\partial^{\bullet}U\in L^{2}_{L^{2}}
.
To mitigate this we instead consider
\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U}
for a sequence of sufficiently smooth functions
\eta_{n}
which converge to
U
in an appropriate sense.

To rigorously justify the previous result we require the following continuity result regarding the operator
\xi\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{\xi}
.

Lemma 3.13
.

(1)

Let
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))
and
\xi\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))
be such that
\eta_{n}\rightarrow\xi
strongly in
L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Then for all
z\in L^{2}_{0}(\Gamma(t))
we have strong convergence
\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}z\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z
in
H^{1}(\Gamma(t))
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

(2)

Let
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{2p}(\Gamma(t))
and
\xi\in L^{2p}(\Gamma(t))
such that
\eta_{n}\rightarrow\xi
strongly in
L^{2p}(\Gamma(t))
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, for some
p\in(1,\infty)
.
Then for all
z\in L^{2}_{0}(\Gamma(t))\cap H^{2}(\Gamma(t))
we have strong convergence
\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}z\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z
in
H^{1}(\Gamma(t))
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

We prove only the second statement, as the first statement is similar but slightly easier as it does not require use of elliptic regularity results.
By definition one has that

\hat{a}(M(\xi),\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z,\phi)=m(z,\phi)=\hat{a}(M(\eta_{n}),\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}z,\phi),

for all
\phi\in H^{1}(\Gamma(t))
and so one readily finds that

\hat{a}(M(\eta_{n}),\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}z-\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z,\phi)=\hat{a}(M(\xi)-M(\eta_{n}),\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z,\phi).

Thus taking
\phi=\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}z-\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z
, and using Hölder’s inequality and the assumptions on
M(\cdot)
we find

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}z-\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|(M(\xi)-M(\eta_{n}))\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z\|_{{L}^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\|\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}z-\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\xi-\eta_{n}\|_{L^{2p}(\Gamma(t))}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z\|_{L^{2p^{*}}(\Gamma(t))}\|\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}z-\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))},

for
p^{*}=\frac{p}{p-1}
.
Now as
z\in H^{2}(\Gamma(t))
we find
\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z\in H^{2}(\Gamma(t))
and so we use the Sobolev embedding
H^{2}(\Gamma(t))\hookrightarrow H^{1,2p^{*}}(\Gamma(t)))
for

\|\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}z-\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}\leq C\|\xi-\eta_{n}\|_{L^{2p}(\Gamma(t))}\|\mathcal{S}_{\xi}z\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))},

whence the result follows.
∎

It is then straightforward to show the following corollary.

Corollary 3.14
.

(1)

Let
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))
and
\xi\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))
be such that
\eta_{n}\rightarrow\xi
strongly in
L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))
as
n\rightarrow\infty
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.
Then for all
z\in C^{0}_{L^{2}_{0}}

\|z\|_{\eta_{n}}\rightarrow\|z\|_{\xi}
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.

(2)

Let
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{r}_{L^{2p}}
and
\xi\in L^{r}_{L^{2p}}
such that
\eta_{n}\rightarrow\xi
strongly in
L^{r}_{L^{2p}}
, for some given
r\in[1,\infty]
and
p\in(1,\infty)
.
Then for all
z\in L^{r^{*}}_{H^{2}}
, where
r^{*}=\frac{r}{r-1}
we have

\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{T}\|z\|_{\eta_{n}}^{r}\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}\|z\|_{\xi}^{r},&r\in[1,\infty),\\
\underset{t\in[0,T]}{\operatorname{ess\ sup\ }}\|z\|_{\eta_{n}}\rightarrow\underset{t\in[0,T]}{\operatorname{ess\ sup\ }}\|z\|_{\xi},&r=\infty.\end{cases}

Using these results we now justify the previous proof.
We consider smooth functions
\eta_{n}
on
\mathcal{G}_{T}
such that

\eta_{n}\rightarrow U\text{ strongly in }L^{2}_{H^{2}}\cap H^{1}_{L^{2}}\cap L^{\infty}_{H^{1}}\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

We observe that

m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U}\bar{U})+g(\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U}\bar{U})=\frac{d}{dt}m(\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U})-m(\bar{U},\partial^{\bullet}\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U})\\
+m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U}\bar{U}-\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U})+g(\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U}\bar{U}-\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U}),

where we find that
\partial^{\bullet}\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U}\in H^{1}_{H^{1}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
From the above proof we recall that

m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U})+g(\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U})=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\bar{U}\|_{\eta_{n}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{b}(M(\eta_{n}),\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U})\\
+\frac{1}{2}\hat{a}(M^{\prime}(\eta_{n})\partial^{\bullet}\eta_{n},\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{n}}\bar{U}).

and one can mirror the calculations above to find that

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\bar{U}\|_{\eta_{n}}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\leq K_{1}(t)\|\bar{U}\|_{\eta_{n}}^{2}+K_{2}(t)\|\bar{U}\|_{U}^{2}\\
+C\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}w\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\|\bar{U}\|_{U}\log\left(\frac{C\left(\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}+\|\bar{U}\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)}{\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where

\displaystyle K_{1}(t)=C\left(\|\eta_{n}\|_{H^{1,4}(\Gamma(t))}+\|\eta_{n}\|_{H^{1,4}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\|\partial^{\bullet}\eta_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)\|\partial^{\bullet}\eta_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))},

\displaystyle K_{2}(t)=C\left(1+\|\nabla_{\Gamma}w\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\right).

We now integrate the above in time, pass to the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
, and conclude by using an appropriate integral form of Lemma
3.8
, which we have included in the appendix (see Lemma
A.1
).

3.2.1.
Comparison to other uniqueness results

We now briefly compare Theorem
3.11
to existing uniqueness results in the literature — namely
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
16
, Theorem 1.2(B)]
.
Firstly, we compare to
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
.
The uniqueness result of
[
8
]
holds for weak solutions satisfying additional regularity properties:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\in L^{\frac{8}{8-d}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle u\in L^{2^{d}}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle w\in L^{\frac{8}{6-d}}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\leq 3
is either a convex polyhedron or
\partial\Omega
is
C^{1,1}
.
Inspecting the proof of this result one finds that this additional regularity is only required on one solution, so that the authors may prove a weak-strong uniqueness result for a class of sufficiently strong solutions.
Firstly, this result holds when one extends to 3 dimensional domains, whereas ours does not.
However, even in the 2 dimensional case this result requires a large amount of regularity, namely that
u\in L^{4}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
, in comparison to our assumptions on a strong solution (
u\in L^{2}_{H^{2}}
).
Their result however does require less regularity on the time derivative in two dimensions, namely
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\in L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
in contrast to our assumption that
\partial^{\bullet}u\in L^{2}_{L^{2}}
.

Next we compare to the recent uniqueness result of
[
16
]
, wherein the authors show that weak solutions are unique in two dimensions, provided that
\partial\Omega
is
C^{3}
, and the mobility
M(\cdot)
is
C^{2}
.
A key step in their uniqueness proof is establishing the equality
[
16
, Equation (3.16)]
, which in our notation would read

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{S}_{U}\frac{\partial\bar{U}}{\partial t}\bar{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\bar{U}\|_{U}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mathcal{G}\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}M^{\prime\prime}(U)\nabla U|\nabla\mathcal{S}_{U}\bar{U}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mathcal{G}\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}M^{\prime}(U)\left(D^{2}\mathcal{S}_{U}\bar{U}\nabla\mathcal{S}_{U}\bar{U}\right),

where
\mathcal{G}
is the inverse (Neumann) Laplacian.
This is justified by approximating by a sequence of smooth functions, similar to our considerations above — in particular this makes use of the
C^{3}
regularity of
\partial\Omega
(see
[
16
, (3.21) and (3.22)]
).
One immediately notices where the
C^{2}
regularity of
M(\cdot)
is required.
One may expect that their proof immediately transfers to the setting of a sufficiently evolving surface provided
M(\cdot)
is sufficiently smooth.
However, a subtle distinction between these two problems arises from the fact that while
\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t},1\rangle_{H^{-1}(\Omega),H^{1}(\Omega)}=0
on a stationary domain, one has that
\langle\partial^{\bullet}u,1\rangle_{H^{-1}(\Gamma(t)),H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}\neq 0
for a general evolving domain.
One may see this by testing (
2
) with
\phi=1
so that

\langle\partial^{\bullet}u,1\rangle_{H^{-1}(\Gamma(t)),H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}=-\int_{\Gamma(t)}u\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V.

Consequently one cannot define
\mathcal{G}\partial^{\bullet}u
without some further consideration, which we leave for another work.
Two questions naturally arise from the comparison of our uniqueness proof:

(1)

Under sufficient smoothness assumptions can one extend the uniqueness result of
[
16
]
to show the uniqueness of weak solutions to (
2
), (
3
)?

(2)

For
\Omega
(or respectively
\Gamma(t)
) a
C^{2}
domain (or a
C^{2}
evolving surface respectively), and
M(\cdot)
a
C^{0,1}
mobility function, are the weak solutions of (
2
), (
3
) unique or is one limited to a weak strong uniqueness result like Theorem
3.11
?

These are interesting issues to be resolved, among many others such as (weak-strong) uniqueness in higher dimensions (which to the authors knowledge only
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
has addressed).

4.
Degenerate mobility functions

We now use the results from the previous section to establish the existence of a weak solution to (
1
) for a degenerate mobility function
M(\cdot)
.
We proceed, as in
[
21
,
22
]
, by considering a regularised problem and passing to the limit in the regularisation variable.

Assumptions

In this section we consider a degenerate mobility function,
M
, in the sense that
M(r)
is positive when
r\in(-1,1)
and
M(\pm 1)=0
.
A standard example of this is the quadratic mobility,
M(r)=1-r^{2}
.
For our analysis we make the following assumption on the evolution of
\Gamma(t)
.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that the velocity,
V
, of
\Gamma(t)
is such that
\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V\geq 0
for all
(x,t)\in\mathcal{G}_{T}
.

This condition also appears in a recent preprint by the authors, concerning evolving surface finite element methods for the Cahn-Hilliard equation with a logarithmic potential
[
24
]
.
One can equivalently express this condition in more geometric terms, as we see in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V\geq 0
if, and only if, for every measurable region
\Sigma(t)\subset\Gamma(t)
,

\frac{d}{dt}|\Sigma(t)|\geq 0.

Proof.

Firstly we calculate that

\frac{d}{dt}|\Sigma(t)|=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Sigma(t)}1=\int_{\Sigma(t)}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V.

Hence, if
\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V\geq 0
then clearly
\frac{d}{dt}|\Sigma(t)|\geq 0.

Conversely, if we assume
\frac{d}{dt}|\Sigma(t)|\geq 0
for all regions
\Sigma(t)
, then we see

\int_{\Sigma(t)}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V\geq 0,

for all regions
\Sigma(t)
.
As this holds for any
\Sigma(t)
, and
V
is sufficiently smooth, the usual localisation arguments imply that
\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V\geq 0
everywhere on
\Gamma(t)
.
∎

If we assume that the evolution of
\Gamma(t)
is purely in the normal direction, that is to say
V=V_{N}\boldsymbol{\nu}
, then
\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V=HV_{N}
.
Hence an example of suitable evolution is if
V_{N}=f(H)
, as a function of the mean curvature, where
f(\cdot)
is sufficiently smooth, and such that
xf(x)\geq 0
.
An explicit example of such an evolution is the inverse mean curvature flow,
f(H)=\frac{1}{H}
.
We refer the reader to
[
11
]
for a discussion of geometric evolution equations of form
V_{N}=f(H)
.

This is not strictly necessary, as we shall see later, but it does allow one to obtain global in time solutions for a wider class of mobility functions.
Moreover, without this assumption one would expect that the existence result is only local in time, or global in time provided some further assumptions on the initial data.
We refer the reader to
[
13
, Proposition 5.1]
for details.

We also make the following assumption which relates the convex part of the potential,
F_{1}
, to the mobility function,
M(\cdot)
.

Assumption 3
.

We assume the mobility and potential functions are such that
M(r)F_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r)\in C^{0}([-1,1])
, and
rF_{1}^{\prime}(r)\geq 0
, where
F_{1}
is the convex part of the potential as before.

In particular we allow
F_{1}
to be singular at
\pm 1
, provided the singularities are of a lower order than
M
.
An explicit example of a singular potential and an appropriate mobility function such that this assumption holds is the logarithmic potential,

(21)

\displaystyle F_{1}(r)=\frac{\theta}{2}\left((1+r)\log(1+r)+(1-r)\log(1-r)\right),

for some fixed
\theta\in(0,1)
, and a mobility function

M(r)=(1-r^{2})^{k},

for
k\geq 1
.
Moreover, as
F_{1}(\cdot)
is convex one can often satisfy the condition
rF_{1}^{\prime}(r)\geq 0
by perturbing
F_{1}
with a quadratic function, and suitably redefining
F_{1},F_{2}
.

Weak formulation

Here we understand a weak solution to (
1
) to be a function
u\in L^{2}_{H^{-1}}\cap L^{2}_{H^{2}}\cap H^{1}_{H^{-1}}
such that

(22)

\displaystyle m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}u,\phi)+g(u,\phi)-\varepsilon\hat{c}(u,M(u),\phi)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\hat{a}(M(u)F^{\prime\prime}(u),u,\phi)=0,

for all
\phi\in H^{2}(\Gamma(t))
, almost all
t\in[0,T]
, and such that
u(0)=u_{0}
almost everywhere on
\Gamma_{0}
.
Here the trilinear form
\hat{c}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
is given by

\hat{c}(t;\phi,\psi,\chi)=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\Delta_{\Gamma}\phi\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot(\psi\nabla_{\Gamma}\chi),

where we suppress the dependence on
t
as usual.

This weak formulation follows from straightforward integration by parts on (
1
).
Unlike the weak formulation for a positive mobility, this has not been split into a system of two PDEs, and subsequently requires a more regular space of test functions.
Our main result of this section is the following existence result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let Assumptions
2
and
1
hold, and
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})
be such that

(23)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{0}}F(u_{0})+\Psi(u_{0})\leq C<\infty,

where
\Psi(\cdot)
is given by (
27
).
Then there exists at least one function
u\in H^{1}_{H^{-1}}\cap L^{\infty}_{H^{1}}\cap L^{2}_{H^{2}}
solving (
22
) for all
\phi\in H^{2}(\Gamma(t))
, for almost all
t\in[0,T]
, and such that
u(0)=u_{0}
almost everywhere on
\Gamma_{0}
.

4.1.
Existence

4.1.1.
Regularisation

We consider a regularised problem with a positive mobility as in the previous section.
Throughout we set
\delta\in(0,1)
as some regularisation parameter, where the limit
\delta\rightarrow 0
should recover degenerate problem.
We consider the regularised potential given by
F^{\delta}(r):=F_{1}^{\delta}(r)+F_{2}(r)
, where

(24)

\displaystyle F_{1}^{\delta}(r):=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=0}^{2}\frac{F_{1}^{(k)}(-1+\delta)}{k!}(r+1-\delta)^{k},&r\leq-1+\delta\\
F_{1}(r),&r\in(-1+\delta,1-\delta),\\
\sum_{k=0}^{2}\frac{F_{1}^{(k)}(1-\delta)}{k!}(r+1-\delta)^{k},&r\geq 1-\delta,\end{cases}

and the regularised mobility given by

(25)

\displaystyle M^{\delta}(r)=\begin{cases}M(-1+\delta),&r\leq-1+\delta\\
M(r),&r\in(-1+\delta,1-\delta),\\
M(1-\delta),&r\geq 1-\delta.\end{cases}

With this regularised mobility we define a function which will be used when we consider the limit
\delta\rightarrow 0
to establish that
|u(t)|\leq 1
almost everywhere.
For
\delta\in(0,1)
we define
\Psi^{\delta}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
to be the unique solution of the ODE,

(26)

\displaystyle(\Psi^{\delta})^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{M^{\delta}(r)},\qquad\Psi^{\delta}(0)=0=(\Psi^{\delta})^{\prime}(0).

We similarly define
\Psi:(-1,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
to be the unique solution of the ODE

(27)

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{M(r)},\qquad\Psi(0)=0=\Psi^{\prime}(0).

We observe that
\Psi^{\delta}(r)=\Psi(r)
for
r\in(-1+\delta,1-\delta)
.

From the above constructions it is clear that
M^{\delta}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})
, in fact it is Lipschitz continuous, and
F_{1}^{\delta}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and so the preceding theory applies for the existence of a regularised solution for some choice of initial data,
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})
, such that (
23
) holds.
We now show that the corresponding solution is appropriately bounded independent of
\delta
.
To do this, we repeat the energy estimates as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For sufficiently small
\delta
the solution pair
u^{\delta},w^{\delta}
is such that

(28)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}E^{\text{CH},\delta}[u^{\delta};t]+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma(t)}M^{\delta}(u^{\delta})|\nabla_{\Gamma}w^{\delta}|^{2}\leq C,

for a constant
C
independent of
\delta
but depending on
T
.

Proof.

Strictly speaking this proof is done at the level of Galerkin approximation, and then passing to the limit in the Galerkin parameter, but we avoid this for notational simplicity.
By arguing as in Lemma
3.3
it is straightforward to show that

(29)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E^{\text{CH},\delta}[u^{\delta};t]+\hat{a}(M^{\delta}(u^{\delta}),w^{\delta},w^{\delta})+g(u^{\delta},w^{\delta})=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}b(u^{\delta},u^{\delta})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}g(F^{\delta}(u),1).

The key term which is problematic is
g(u^{\delta},w^{\delta})
, but from our assumption that
\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V\geq 0
we will find that this term is dealt with in a straightforward way.
As
u^{\delta}\in L^{2}_{H^{2}}
we know that

w^{\delta}=-\varepsilon\Delta_{\Gamma}u^{\delta}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(F^{\delta})^{\prime}(u^{\delta}),

almost everywhere on
\Gamma(t)
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.
Hence we use this in
g(u^{\delta},w^{\delta})
for

g(u^{\delta},w^{\delta})=-\varepsilon\int_{\Gamma(t)}u^{\delta}\Delta_{\Gamma}u^{\delta}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Gamma(t)}u^{\delta}(F^{\delta})^{\prime}(u^{\delta})\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V,

and now by integration by parts and noting that
r(F_{1}^{\delta})^{\prime}(r)\geq 0
we see

g(u^{\delta},w^{\delta})\geq\underbrace{\varepsilon\int_{\Gamma(t)}|\nabla_{\Gamma}u^{\delta}|^{2}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V}_{\geq 0}+\varepsilon\int_{\Gamma(t)}u^{\delta}\nabla_{\Gamma}u^{\delta}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)\\
+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Gamma(t)}u^{\delta}(F_{1}^{\delta})^{\prime}(u^{\delta})\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V}_{\geq 0}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Gamma(t)}u^{\delta}F_{2}^{\prime}(u^{\delta})\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V.

Using this in (
29
) yields

(30)

\frac{d}{dt}E^{\text{CH},\delta}[u^{\delta};t]+\hat{a}(M^{\delta}(u^{\delta}),w^{\delta},w^{\delta})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}b(u^{\delta},u^{\delta})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}g(F^{\delta}(u),1)\\
-\varepsilon\int_{\Gamma(t)}u^{\delta}\nabla_{\Gamma}u^{\delta}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Gamma(t)}u^{\delta}F_{2}^{\prime}(u^{\delta})\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V.

It is then routine to show that one can appropriately bound this as

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}b(u^{\delta},u^{\delta})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}g(F^{\delta}(u),1)+\varepsilon\left|\int_{\Gamma(t)}u^{\delta}\nabla_{\Gamma}u^{\delta}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)\right|+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left|\int_{\Gamma(t)}u^{\delta}F_{2}^{\prime}(u^{\delta})\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V\right|\\
\leq C+CE^{\text{CH},\delta}[u^{\delta}],

after using the Poincaré inequality, bounds on
F_{2}^{\prime}
, and the smoothness of
V
.

Using bounds of this form in (
30
) and integrating in time we see

E^{\text{CH},\delta}[u^{\delta};t]+\int_{0}^{t}M^{\delta}(u^{\delta})\nabla_{\Gamma}w^{\delta}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}w^{\delta}\leq E^{\text{CH},\delta}[u_{0};0]+C+C\int_{0}^{t}E^{\text{CH},\delta}[u^{\delta}],

and so using the Grönwall inequality yields the desired result.

It remains to see that the bound is independent of
\delta
.
For this we note that

E^{\text{CH},\delta}[u_{0};0]=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\frac{\varepsilon|\nabla_{\Gamma}u_{0}|^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F^{\delta}(u_{0})=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Gamma_{0}}F_{2}(u_{0})\\
+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\{|u_{0}|<1-\delta\}}F_{1}(u_{0})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\{u_{0}\geq 1-\delta\}}\sum_{k=0}^{2}\frac{F_{1}^{(k)}(1-\delta)}{k!}(u_{0}-1+\delta)^{k}\\
+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\{u_{0}\leq-1+\delta\}}\sum_{k=0}^{2}\frac{F_{1}^{(k)}(-1+\delta)}{k!}(u_{0}+1-\delta)^{k},

and so it is only necessary to bound the last three terms.
This follows from the monotonicity of
F_{1}
and its derivatives near
\pm 1
, for which one can show that for sufficiently small
\delta

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\{|u_{0}|<1-\delta\}}F_{1}(u_{0})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\{u_{0}\geq 1-\delta\}}\sum_{k=0}^{2}\frac{F_{1}^{(k)}(1-\delta)}{k!}(u_{0}-1+\delta)^{k}\\
+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\{u_{0}\leq-1+\delta\}}\sum_{k=0}^{2}\frac{F_{1}^{(k)}(-1+\delta)}{k!}(u_{0}+1-\delta)^{k}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Gamma_{0}}F_{1}(u_{0}),

which is finite by assumption.
From this we conclude that the desired bound is in fact independent of
\delta
.
∎

Lemma 4.5

For sufficiently small

Remark 4.6

We can avoid the assumption that

Lemma 4.7

For sufficiently small

13
,
22
]
.
We recall that we have shown for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.

\int_{\Gamma(t)}\Psi^{\delta}(u^{\delta})\leq C,

for some constant independent of
\delta
.
From Fatou’s lemma one has that

\int_{\Gamma(t)}\liminf_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\Psi^{\delta}(u^{\delta})\leq\liminf_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\int_{\Gamma(t)}\Psi^{\delta}(u^{\delta})\leq C.

We now claim that

\liminf_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\Psi^{\delta}(u^{\delta})=\begin{cases}\Psi(u),&|u|<1,\\
\infty,&\mathrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

almost everywhere on
\Gamma(t)
and for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.
From this claim it follows that since
\int_{\Gamma(t)}\liminf_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\Psi^{\delta}(u^{\delta})<\infty
one must have that
|u|<1
almost everywhere on
\Gamma(t)
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.

We now prove the above claim.
Firstly, we recall that we have pointwise convergence of
u^{\delta}\rightarrow u
almost everywhere on
\Gamma(t)
for almost all
t
.
To begin we assume we have a point
(x,t)
such that the pointwise convergence holds and
|u(x,t)|<1
.
We fix
\gamma>0
to be some arbitrarily small constant.
By construction one can find
\delta_{1}
such that
\Psi(u(x,t))=\Psi^{\delta}(u(x,t))
for all
\delta<\delta_{1}
.
Similarly by continuity of
\Psi
on
(-1,1)
one can find
\delta_{2}
such that

|r-u(x,t)|<\delta_{2}\Rightarrow|\Psi(r)-\Psi(u(x,t))|<\gamma.

Lastly by pointwise convergence
u^{\delta}(x,t)\rightarrow u(x,t)
for a subsequence of
\delta\rightarrow 0
one has
\delta_{3}
such that

\delta<\delta_{3}\Rightarrow|u^{\delta}(x,t)-u(x,t)|<\delta_{2}.

Thus taking
\delta<\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3}\}
one finds

|\Psi^{\delta}(u^{\delta}(x,t))-\Psi(u(x,t))|<\gamma,

where
\gamma
was arbitrary.

Now we suppose that
|u(x,t)|=1
.
If
u(x,t)=1
then one has

\Psi^{\delta}(u^{\delta})\geq\min\{\Psi(1-\delta),\Psi(u^{\delta}(x,t))\}\rightarrow\infty,

as
\delta\rightarrow 0
.
A similar argument holds when
u(x,t)=-1
.
∎

4.2.
Strong uniqueness away from the pure phases

Here we use similar arguments to the proof of Theorem
3.11
to show uniqueness of strong solutions, with some restriction on the initial data.
We also assume that the function
r\mapsto M(r)F^{\prime\prime}(r)
is locally Lipschitz continuous.
This assumption is not particular restrictive and holds, for example, for the logarithmic potential (
21
) and mobility functions
M(r)=(1-r^{2})^{k}
for
k\geq 1
.

Definition 4.10
.

We call a function
U
a strong solution if it is a weak solution to (
22
) and one has additionally that
U\in H^{1}_{L^{2}}
and
\nabla_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}U\in L^{2}_{L^{2}}
.

Here we assume that
|u_{0}|\leq 1-\gamma_{1}
, for some small constant
\gamma_{1}>0
so that
\mathcal{S}_{u_{0}}
is a well-defined operator, and that a solution
u
with initial data
u_{0}
is such that
|u|\leq 1-\gamma_{2}
, almost everywhere on
\bigcup_{t\in[0,t^{*}]}\Gamma(t)\times\{t\}
, for some
t^{*}>0
, and a small constant
\gamma_{2}>0
.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
U_{1},U_{2}
be strong solutions of (
22
) with the same initial data,
u_{0}
, such that
|u_{0}|\leq 1-\gamma_{1}
almost everywhere on
\Gamma_{0}
for some constant
\gamma_{1}>0
.
Then if there exists some time interval
[0,t^{*}]\subseteq[0,T]
such that
|U_{i}|\leq 1-\gamma_{2}
almost everywhere on
\Gamma(t)
for almost all
t\in[0,t^{*}]
, for some constant
\gamma_{2}>0
and
i=1,2
, then we have
U_{1}=U_{2}
almost everywhere on
\mathcal{G}_{t^{*}}
.

Proof.

As before we present a formal argument for the sake of clarity, which can be rigorously justified as we did for Theorem
3.11
.
For ease of notation define
G(r):=M(r)F^{\prime\prime}(r)
.
Define
\bar{U}=U_{1}-U_{2}
, then one finds

(37)

m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}\bar{U},\phi)+g(\bar{U},\phi)-\varepsilon\hat{c}(U_{1},M(U_{1}),\phi)+\varepsilon\hat{c}(U_{2},M(U_{2}),\phi)\\
-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\hat{a}(G(U_{1}),U_{1},\phi)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\hat{a}(G(U_{2}),U_{2},\phi)=0,

where one then rewrites the final four terms as

\displaystyle\hat{c}(U_{1},M(U_{1}),\phi)-\hat{c}(U_{2},M(U_{2}),\phi)=\hat{c}(\bar{U},M(U_{1}),\phi)+\hat{c}(U_{2},M(U_{1})-M(U_{2}),\phi),

\displaystyle\hat{a}(G(U_{1}),U_{1},\phi)-\hat{a}(G(U_{2}),U_{2},\phi)=\hat{a}(G(U_{1})-G(U_{2}),U_{1},\phi)+\hat{a}(G(U_{2}),\bar{U},\phi).

Now by our smallness assumption we find that
\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U}
is well defined and so we may test (
37
) with
\phi=\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U}\in L^{2}_{H^{2}}
to obtain

m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})+g(\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})-\varepsilon\hat{c}(\bar{U},M(U_{1}),\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})\\
=\varepsilon\hat{c}(U_{2},M(U_{1})-M(U_{2}),\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\hat{a}(G(U_{1})-G(U_{2}),U_{1},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})\\
+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\hat{a}(G(U_{2}),\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U}),

We recall from the proof of Theorem
3.11
that if one assumes that
\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U}\in H^{1}_{H^{1}}
then

(38)

m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})+g(\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\bar{U}\|_{U_{1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{b}(M(U_{1}),\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})\\
+\frac{1}{2}\hat{a}(M^{\prime}(U_{1})\partial^{\bullet}U_{1},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U}),

where the two rightmost terms are handled in the exact same manner as in the proof of Theorem
3.11
.
Next we observe that

\displaystyle\hat{c}(\bar{U},M(U_{1}),\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\Delta_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot(M(U_{1})\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})

(39)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Gamma(t)}\bar{U}\Delta_{\Gamma}\bar{U}=\int_{\Gamma(t)}|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}|^{2}

where we have used the fact that
-\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot(M(U_{1})\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})=\bar{U}
almost everywhere on
\Gamma(t)
.

By Hölder’s inequality and the assumption that
G(\cdot)
is (locally) Lipschitz continuous one finds that

|\hat{a}(G(U_{1})-G(U_{2}),U_{1},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})|\leq C\|\bar{U}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}U_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\|\bar{U}\|_{U_{1}}.

By using Lemma
3.9
to control the
\|\bar{U}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))}
term, and then using Young’s inequality one obtains

(40)

\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\hat{a}(G(U_{1})-G(U_{2}),U_{1},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})\right|\leq C\|\nabla_{\Gamma}U_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\|\bar{U}\|_{U_{1}}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\\
+C\|\nabla_{\Gamma}U_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\|\bar{U}\|_{U_{1}}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\log\left(1+\frac{C\|\bar{U}\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}}{\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Next using the boundedness of
G(\cdot)
one finds

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\hat{a}(G(U_{2}),\bar{U},\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})\leq C\|\bar{U}\|_{U_{1}}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}.

The final, and most troublesome, term to deal with is
\hat{c}(U_{2},M(U_{1})-M(U_{2}),\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})
.
Using integration by parts, and the Lipschitz continuity of
M(\cdot)
, one has

\displaystyle\hat{c}(U_{2},M(U_{1})-M(U_{2}),\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma(t)}(M(U_{1})-M(U_{2}))\nabla_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}U_{2}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bar{U}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}U_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\|\bar{U}\|_{U_{1}}.

One then uses Lemma
3.9
to control the
\|\bar{U}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma(t))}
term, as we did above, and Young’s inequality to see that

(42)

\varepsilon\hat{c}(U_{2},M(U_{1})-M(U_{2}),\mathcal{S}_{U_{1}}\bar{U})\leq\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}U_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\|\bar{U}\|_{U_{1}}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\\
+C\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}U_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\|\bar{U}\|_{U_{1}}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\log\left(1+\frac{C\|\bar{U}\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}}{\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{U}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Uniqueness follows by using (
38
)–(
42
) in (
37
) and concluding by using Lemma
3.8
, similar to the proof of Theorem
3.11
.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

(1)

Our assumption that
|U|\leq 1-\gamma_{2}
is known as
separation from the pure phases
, and has been shown to occur on an evolving surface when
M\equiv 1
— see
[
14
]
.
To the authors’ knowledge there are no such results of this kind for a degenerate mobility function, although a recent result of this kind has been shown for a positive, variable mobility in
[
16
]
.
Despite this, Lemma
4.9
shows that
|U|<1
almost everywhere, provided that
\Psi
blows up at
\pm 1
.
This is the case when
M(r)=(1-r^{2})^{k}
for
k\geq 2
, as remarked in
[
22
]
.

(2)

If one additionally assumes that
F^{\prime\prime}
is bounded then the condition that
\nabla_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}U\in L^{2}_{L^{2}}
actually follows from the assumption that
|U|\leq 1-\gamma_{2}
almost everywhere.
To see this one can argue as in
[
22
, Theorem 3]
to show that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma(t)}M^{\delta}(u^{\delta})|\nabla_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}u^{\delta}|^{2}<C,

for
C
independent of
\delta
.
Then if
|U|\leq 1-\gamma_{2}
and
U(t)\in C^{0}(\Gamma(t))
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
, one finds that
M(U)>0
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
, and so one can use lower semicontinuity to see
\nabla_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}U\in L^{2}_{L^{2}}
.
As such, this result only requires
U_{1}
to be a strong solution — thus Theorem
4.11
becomes a weak-strong uniqueness result rather than strong-strong uniqueness.

The main take away from this result is that although the weak formulation of (
22
) requires a stronger class of test functions than (
2
), (
3
) one can still argue along the same lines as in Theorem
3.11
, given sufficiently strong solutions.
Moreover, this result implies that if one wishes to investigate potential non-uniqueness phenomena for the degenerate Cahn-Hilliard equation, one should look at initial data which attains
\pm 1
and/or a mobility such that
\lim_{r\rightarrow\pm 1}\Psi(r)<\infty
.
This is the case in the numerical experiments of
[
9
]
.

4.3.
A deep quench limit of the logarithmic potential

Here we briefly discuss a special case where the mobility is given by
M(r)=1-r^{2}
and the potential is

F_{\theta}(r)=\frac{\theta}{2}\left((1+r)\log(1+r)+(1-r)\log(1-r)\right)+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2},

for
\theta\in(0,1)
.
In particular we study the limit
\theta\rightarrow 0
, similar to
[
13
]
where the authors show that (for a constant mobility) this limit leads to a unique solution of a variational inequality where the potential is given by the double obstacle potential

F_{o}(r):=\begin{cases}\frac{1-r^{2}}{2},&r\in[-1,1],\\
\infty,&|r|>1.\end{cases}

This deep quench limit has also been studied in
[
15
,
22
]
.

We denote the solution of the degenerate problem, in the sense of (
22
), with a fixed
\theta\in(0,1)
as
u^{\theta}
, and note from (
28
), (
31
), (
36
) that the sequence
u^{\theta}
is uniformly bounded (independent of
\theta
) in
H^{1}_{H^{-1}}\cap L^{\infty}_{H^{1}}\cap L^{2}_{H^{2}}
.
Denoting the corresponding weak (or weak-
*
) limit by
u
one can repeat arguments from the previous section to obtain that

\lim_{\theta\rightarrow 0}m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}u^{\theta},\phi)+g(u^{\theta},\phi)-\varepsilon\hat{c}(u^{\theta},M(u^{\theta}),\phi)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\hat{a}(M(u^{\theta})F_{\theta}^{\prime\prime}(u^{\theta}),u^{\theta},\phi)\\
=m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}u,\phi)+g(u,\phi)-\varepsilon\hat{c}(u,M(u),\phi)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\hat{a}(M(u),u,\phi),

for some subsequence of
\theta\rightarrow 0
, since

\hat{a}(M(u^{\theta})F_{\theta}^{\prime\prime}(u^{\theta}),u^{\theta},\phi)=\theta a(u^{\theta},\phi)-\hat{a}(M(u^{\theta}),u^{\theta},\phi)\rightarrow-\hat{a}(M(u^{\theta}),u^{\theta},\phi).

From this we conclude that
u
solves

m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}u,\phi)+g(u,\phi)-\varepsilon\hat{c}(u,M(u),\phi)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\hat{a}(M(u),u,\phi)=0,

for all
\phi\in H^{2}(\Gamma(t))
, almost all
t\in[0,T]
, and such that
u(0)=u_{0}
almost everywhere on
\Gamma_{0}
.
We note that our uniqueness result does not necessarily mean that the whole sequence
(u^{\theta})_{\theta}
converges to
u
as we have not shown the hypotheses of Theorem
4.11
hold for
u
.

Appendix A
An integral form of Lemma
3.8

In this appendix we outline how one establishes an appropriate integral form of Lemma
3.8
, which is required to justify the proofs of Theorem
3.11
and Theorem
4.11
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
m_{1},m_{2},S
be non-negative functions on
(0,T)
such that
m_{1},S\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
m_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T)
, with
S>0
a.e. on
(0,T)
.
Now suppose
f,g
are non-negative, integrable functions on
(0,T)
, and
f
is continuous on
[0,T)
, such that

\displaystyle f(t)+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}m_{1}(s)f(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}m_{2}(s)\left(f(s)g(s)\log^{+}\left(\frac{S(s)}{g(s)}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,ds,

holds on
(0,T)
, and
f(0)=0
.
Then
f\equiv 0
on
[0,T)
.

Proof.

The proof of this result is largely the same as that of
[
31
, Lemma 2.2]
.
To begin we recall that for
\sigma\in(0,\infty)
one has

\log^{+}(z)\leq\frac{z^{\sigma}}{\sigma e},\text{ for }z\in(0,\infty).

Using this inequality one observes that we have

f(t)+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}m_{1}(s)f(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}m_{2}(s)S(s)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}g(s)^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2}}\left(\frac{f(s)}{e\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,ds.

Now using Young’s inequality we find that for any
\sigma\in(0,1)
one has, for
a,b\geq 0
,

ab\leq\frac{1-\sigma}{2}a^{\frac{2}{1-\sigma}}+\frac{1+\sigma}{2}b^{\frac{2}{1+\sigma}},

which we use in the above to obtain

\displaystyle f(t)+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}m_{1}(s)f(s)\,ds+\frac{1-\sigma}{2}\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1+\sigma}{2}\int_{0}^{t}m_{2}(s)^{\frac{2}{1+\sigma}}S(s)^{\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}}\left(\frac{f(s)}{e\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\sigma}}\,ds.

We note that this final integral is well defined by using Hölder’s inequality since
f\in L^{\infty}(0,T),m_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T),S\in L^{1}(0,T)
.
Since
\sigma\in(0,1)
one then finds that

\displaystyle f(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}m_{1}(s)f(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}m_{2}(s)^{\frac{2}{1+\sigma}}S(s)^{\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}}\left(\frac{f(s)}{e\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\sigma}}\,ds.

Now we argue by contradiction and suppose that there is some maximal
t_{0}\in[0,T)
such that
f(t)=0
for
t\in[0,t_{0}]
, and
f(t)>0
for
t\in(t_{0},T)
.
Then clearly the above inequality yields that

\displaystyle f(t)\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}m_{1}(s)f(s)\,ds+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}m_{2}(s)^{\frac{2}{1+\sigma}}S(s)^{\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}}\left(\frac{f(s)}{e\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\sigma}}\,ds,

for
t\geq t_{0}
.
Moreover, using the fact that
z\leq\left(\frac{z}{\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\sigma}}
for
z\in(0,\sigma^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}})
we find that

\displaystyle f(t)\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(m_{1}(s)+m_{2}(s)^{\frac{2}{1+\sigma}}S(s)^{\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}}\right)\left(\frac{f(s)}{\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\sigma}}\,ds,

for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
where
t_{1}
is chosen such that

\max_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}f(t)\leq\sigma^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}}.

By continuity of
f
such a
t_{1}
exists and moreover we find that
t_{1}\rightarrow T
as
\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
We wish to conclude by using the Bihari-LaSalle inequality
[
12
]
.
To this end we firstly define the (bijective) function
\Lambda_{\sigma}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
by

\Lambda_{\sigma}(y):=\int_{0}^{y}\left(\frac{\sigma}{z}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\sigma}}\,dz=(1+\sigma)\sigma^{\frac{-\sigma}{1+\sigma}}y^{\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}},

with inverse

\Lambda_{\sigma}^{-1}(z):=\frac{\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{\frac{1+\sigma}{\sigma}}}z^{\frac{1+\sigma}{\sigma}}.

Now we may apply the Bihari-LaSalle inequality to see that

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda_{\sigma}^{-1}\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(m_{1}(s)+m_{2}(s)^{\frac{2}{1+\sigma}}S(s)^{\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}}\right)\,ds\right),

for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
.
We then notice that

\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Lambda_{\sigma}^{-1}(z)=\begin{cases}0,&z\in(0,1],\\
\infty,&z>1,\end{cases}

and we may choose a
t^{*}\in(t_{0},t_{1}]
(independent of
\sigma
) such that

\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{*}}\left(m_{1}(s)+m_{2}(s)^{\frac{2}{1+\sigma}}S(s)^{\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}}\right)\,ds\leq\frac{1}{2}.

The existence of such a
t^{*}
follows from Young’s inequality since
m_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T),S\in L^{1}(0,T)
so that

\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{*}}\left(m_{1}(s)+m_{2}(s)^{\frac{2}{1+\sigma}}S(s)^{\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}}\right)\,ds\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{*}}m_{1}(s)+(1+\sigma)m_{2}(s)^{2}+\frac{1}{1+\sigma}S(s)\,ds.

Then since
\Lambda_{\sigma}^{-1}(\cdot)
is increasing we find that

f(t)\leq\Lambda_{\sigma}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right),\text{ for }t\in[t_{0},t^{*}],

and hence we may pass to the limit
\sigma\rightarrow 0^{+}
to see that
f(t)=0
for
t\in[t_{0},t^{*}]
.
This contradicts the maximality of
t_{0}
, and hence
f\equiv 0
on
[0,T)
.

∎
