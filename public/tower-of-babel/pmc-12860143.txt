Narrative review on decoding the crosstalk between lipid homeostasis and immune pathways in membranous nephropathy
Authors: Sichao Ma, Mingxin Chang, Shukun Chen, Tingting Han, Shoulin Zhang, Hong’an Wang, Guanyu Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Membranous nephropathy (MN) is a leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults and is characterized by subepithelial immune deposits driven by autoantibodies, most commonly anti-PLA2R, with subsequent complement activation and C5b-9–mediated podocyte injury. Emerging evidence suggests that this immune-mediated process is closely intertwined with disturbances in lipid homeostasis. In this review, we propose an immunometabolic framework for MN that highlights the reciprocal interactions between lipid dysregulation and immune activation. Three interrelated mechanistic domains are emphasized. First, cholesterol-enriched lipid rafts on podocyte membranes may facilitate autoantibody binding and immune complex organization, thereby influencing complement activation. Second, systemic dyslipidemia, which is highly prevalent in MN, is associated with increased generation of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL), a lipid species that can amplify complement activity and glomerular inflammation. Third, intracellular lipid accumulation and impaired ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux may contribute to metabolic reprogramming of adaptive immune cells, including T follicular helper cells, supporting sustained autoantibody production. Clinically, dyslipidemia correlates with proteinuria severity and adverse renal and cardiovascular outcomes, and lipid-lowering therapies have been associated with modest reductions in proteinuria in selected studies. Collectively, these observations support the concept that immune and metabolic pathways are functionally interconnected in MN. Future studies integrating longitudinal multi-omics profiling and computational approaches may help refine patient stratification and guide the development of combined immunologic and metabolic therapeutic strategies.Clinical trials Not applicable.

Introduction

Membranous nephropathy (MN) is among the most common causes of nephrotic syndrome in adults worldwide and represents a major contributor to chronic kidney disease burden [1]. Histopathologically, MN is defined by subepithelial immune complex deposition along the glomerular basement membrane, leading to progressive proteinuria, edema, dyslipidemia, and an increased risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) [2]. Epidemiological data indicate that MN accounts for approximately 30–40% of primary nephrotic syndrome cases in adults, with a rising global incidence that parallels population aging and increased exposure to environmental risk factors such as air pollution and heavy metals [3]. Long-term observational studies further demonstrate that nearly one-third of untreated patients with persistent nephrotic-range proteinuria progress to ESKD within a decade, underscoring the substantial clinical and societal impact of this disease [3]. From a pathogenic standpoint, MN is now firmly established as an autoimmune glomerular disorder, characterized by the formation of circulating autoantibodies directed against podocyte-expressed antigens. The identification of the M-type phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) as the dominant target antigen in 2009 fundamentally reshaped the field, revealing that anti-PLA2R antibodies are detectable in approximately 70–80% of patients with primary MN [3]. Subsequent discoveries of additional antigens, including thrombospondin type 1 domain–containing 7A (THSD7A) and neural epidermal growth factor–like 1 protein (NELL1), further expanded the antigenic landscape of the disease [3, 4]. Binding of these autoantibodies to podocyte surface antigens initiates complement activation, predominantly via the classical pathway, leading to the assembly of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) and consequent podocyte injury [4]. Although this autoantibody–complement paradigm remains central to current disease models, it does not fully account for the marked heterogeneity in disease severity, progression, and therapeutic responsiveness observed among patients. An emerging body of evidence suggests that metabolic factors, particularly disturbances in lipid homeostasis, substantially modulate MN pathobiology and may act as pathogenic amplifiers rather than passive epiphenomena [1, 5]. The reframing of MN within an immunometabolic context is motivated by observations that dyslipidemia, lipid oxidation, and altered cholesterol handling correlate with disease activity and treatment outcomes, yet remain insufficiently integrated into traditional immune-centric models. Similar immunometabolic intersections have been described in other chronic inflammatory disorders, including lupus nephritis and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, where metabolic stress and immune activation operate as interdependent drivers of tissue injury [6, 7]. Within this broader framework of metabolic autoimmunity, lipid-derived danger signals such as oxidized low-density lipoprotein, ceramides, and cholesterol crystals have been shown in other proteinuric and inflammatory settings to activate innate immune pathways, including inflammasome complexes such as NLRP3, thereby linking metabolic perturbations to inflammatory signaling cascades [8]. In the context of MN, available experimental and clinical evidence suggests that lipid abnormalities may influence disease biology through several converging mechanisms. Lipid oxidation products and altered cholesterol distribution can modify podocyte membrane microdomains, including lipid rafts, potentially affecting antigen presentation, complement assembly, and susceptibility to immune-mediated injury [9, 10]. In parallel, lipid-mediated signals may shape the intraglomerular inflammatory milieu by enhancing complement activation and sustaining low-grade innate immune responses. Beyond local effects on podocytes, metabolic reprogramming of immune cells represents an additional layer of interaction, as altered lipid availability and utilization can influence T cell and B cell metabolism, favoring proinflammatory phenotypes and potentially supporting persistent autoantibody production, as suggested by studies in related autoimmune contexts [11, 12]. These observations collectively indicate that MN is unlikely to represent a purely localized autoimmune process confined to the glomerular filtration barrier. Taken together, MN can be more comprehensively conceptualized as a multidimensional immunometabolic disease in which reciprocal interactions between immune mechanisms and lipid dysregulation contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle of inflammation, structural injury, and metabolic stress [13]. Building on this premise, the present review synthesizes current evidence linking lipid homeostasis and immune activation in MN, proposes an integrated immunometabolic framework that highlights key mechanistic nodes connecting these systems, and discusses how this perspective may inform future hypothesis-driven research and therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring immunometabolic balance rather than targeting immune pathways in isolation.

Lipid homeostasis in kidney health

Lipid homeostasis within the kidney, and particularly within the glomerulus, is essential for preserving the structural integrity and metabolic stability of the filtration barrier [14, 15]. Each cellular compartment of the glomerulus, including podocytes, mesangial cells, and endothelial cells, maintains a distinct and tightly regulated lipid composition that supports its specialized physiological functions [16]. Among these, podocytes exhibit a high enrichment of cholesterol and sphingolipids organized into membrane microdomains, commonly referred to as lipid rafts, which are critical for maintaining slit diaphragm architecture and coordinating signal transduction across the filtration barrier [17]. Continuous regulation of lipid uptake, biosynthesis, oxidation, and efflux is required to sustain appropriate membrane fluidity and mechanical resilience, enabling the glomerulus to tolerate hemodynamic stress while preserving selective permeability [18]. Perturbation of this finely balanced system, whether driven by intrinsic metabolic alterations or systemic lipid disturbances, can rapidly translate into glomerular dysfunction and progressive renal injury [19, 20].

Renal lipid homeostasis should therefore be viewed not as a passive reflection of circulating lipid levels but as an active, cell-autonomous process governed by coordinated transcriptional and enzymatic programs [21]. Key regulatory pathways, including sterol regulatory element–binding proteins and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARα and PPARγ), orchestrate the balance between lipid synthesis, fatty acid oxidation, and storage, thereby preventing excessive intracellular lipid accumulation [22]. In parallel, cholesterol efflux systems mediated by ATP-binding cassette transporters such as ABCA1 and ABCG1, together with apolipoproteins including ApoE and ApoAI, play a central role in maintaining intracellular cholesterol homeostasis and podocyte viability [23, 24]. Disruption of these regulatory mechanisms promotes lipotoxic stress, characterized by the accumulation of ceramides, oxidized phospholipids, and cholesterol crystals, which in turn impairs mitochondrial function, induces endoplasmic reticulum stress, and increases oxidative burden [14, 15].

These metabolic perturbations compromise cellular energy production, activate unfolded protein response pathways, and engage inflammatory signaling cascades involving NFκB and NLRP3, as demonstrated in multiple experimental and proteinuric disease models [25, 26]. Podocytes are particularly susceptible to such stress, given their limited capacity for lipid storage and regenerative renewal; even modest intracellular lipid overload can destabilize the actin cytoskeleton, weaken adhesion to the glomerular basement membrane, and promote podocyte detachment [27]. In addition, modified lipoproteins such as oxidized low-density lipoprotein can interact with scavenger receptors, including CD36 and LOX-1, expressed on podocytes and infiltrating macrophages, thereby amplifying inflammatory signaling and potentially enhancing complement activation within the glomerular microenvironment [18].

Advances in multi-omics approaches and spatial lipidomics have further refined this view by revealing marked compartmentalization of renal lipid species. Glomerular regions are preferentially enriched in phosphatidylcholines and sphingomyelins, whereas tubulointerstitial compartments contain higher proportions of neutral lipids and triglycerides [28]. This spatial heterogeneity provides a mechanistic basis for the observation that similar systemic dyslipidemic states can give rise to distinct patterns of renal injury, manifesting predominantly as podocyte dysfunction in some settings and tubular lipotoxicity in others [29]. Once intrinsic renal lipid handling becomes impaired, disturbances in systemic lipid recycling may ensue, fostering a bidirectional and self-reinforcing loop in which glomerular injury exacerbates dyslipidemia and metabolic stress further accelerates renal damage [30, 31].

Collectively, intact lipid homeostasis constitutes a fundamental pillar of renal resilience, supporting podocyte integrity, endothelial stability, and relative immunologic quiescence within the glomerulus. Conversely, its disruption establishes a permissive biochemical environment for inflammatory activation and tissue injury, providing the substrate upon which lipid–immune interactions can operate. This conceptual foundation sets the stage for understanding how dysregulated lipid metabolism intersects with immune mechanisms in MN, a theme that is developed in subsequent sections [32, 33].

Role of lipids in podocyte structure and function

Podocytes depend on a highly specialized membrane architecture to preserve their complex foot process organization and to sustain the dynamic functions of the glomerular filtration barrier. Central to this architecture are cholesterol- and sphingolipid-enriched membrane microdomains, commonly referred to as lipid rafts, which serve as nanoscale platforms for the spatial organization of key slit diaphragm components, including nephrin, podocin, and CD2-associated protein [1, 34]. By concentrating these proteins within defined membrane regions, lipid rafts facilitate coordinated mechanotransduction and signal integration in response to shear stress and filtration forces, thereby supporting cytoskeletal tension and foot process stability [35, 36].

Beyond their structural role, lipids function as active biochemical regulators of podocyte behavior. Sphingolipids, particularly ceramides, have emerged as potent mediators of cellular stress, capable of inducing caspase-dependent apoptosis, cytoskeletal disassembly, and eventual detachment of podocytes from the glomerular basement membrane [10, 37]. Excess ceramide accumulation or dysregulation of its metabolic derivatives alters membrane viscosity and interferes with phosphorylation-dependent signaling of slit diaphragm proteins such as nephrin, leading to progressive destabilization of the filtration interface [38]. In contrast, glycerophospholipids and unsaturated fatty acids tend to enhance membrane fluidity and support the endocytic turnover of structural proteins, including nephrin and synaptopodin, underscoring the divergent and context-dependent effects of distinct lipid classes on podocyte resilience [39].

Maintenance of an appropriate cholesterol-to-phospholipid ratio is particularly critical for podocyte function. Excessive free cholesterol accumulation rigidifies the plasma membrane and disrupts actin–nephrin signaling pathways, whereas cholesterol depletion promotes lipid raft disassembly and perturbs Rho GTPase–dependent regulation of foot process motility [40]. Experimental manipulation of membrane cholesterol levels further illustrates this sensitivity; pharmacological depletion using agents such as methyl-β-cyclodextrin or, in certain settings, statins has been shown to induce marked retraction of podocyte foot processes, highlighting the narrow range within which cholesterol homeostasis supports barrier integrity [41]. Conversely, interventions that restore cholesterol balance, including activation of PPARγ or liver X receptor signaling, have been reported to promote reorganization of lipid rafts and facilitate structural recovery following podocyte injury [42].

Ceramide metabolism represents an additional axis through which lipid composition intersects with stress signaling and mitochondrial function. Enhanced ceramide generation via neutral sphingomyelinase activation under oxidative or inflammatory conditions compromises mitochondrial integrity and augments reactive oxygen species production, thereby amplifying cellular injury [43]. In experimental models, inhibition of sphingomyelinase activity or diversion of ceramide toward sphingosine-1-phosphate synthesis has been associated with cytoprotective effects, further emphasizing the dynamic interplay between structural lipids and intracellular signaling pathways [44]. Collectively, these observations support the concept that podocytes are not passive structural elements of the filtration barrier but rather lipid-responsive cells that continuously remodel their membrane lipidome to adapt to biomechanical and immunologic stressors [11, 45]. Sustained disruption of these tightly regulated processes predisposes podocytes to lipotoxic injury, sensitization to immune-mediated damage, and progressive glomerular dysfunction, thereby linking lipid dysregulation directly to the pathogenesis of proteinuric kidney disease [46].

Intracellular lipid metabolism pathways in renal cells

Renal parenchymal cells, particularly podocytes and proximal tubular epithelial cells, are equipped with highly specialized intracellular metabolic programs designed to preserve lipid equilibrium and prevent lipotoxic accumulation. The kidney ranks second only to the heart in its dependence on fatty acid oxidation as a primary energy source, rendering even modest disturbances in lipid turnover sufficient to precipitate cellular stress and dysfunction [13, 47]. Within this framework, cholesterol efflux represents a critical protective mechanism. ATP-binding cassette transporters ABCA1 and ABCG1, together with scavenger receptor class B type I, mediate the export of excess free cholesterol to apolipoproteins such as ApoE and ApoAI, thereby preserving membrane fluidity, limiting oxidative burden, and maintaining intracellular redox balance [12]. Disruption of these efflux pathways results in intracellular accumulation of unesterified cholesterol within lipid droplets, fostering lipid peroxidation and activating oxidative stress–responsive signaling cascades that propagate cellular injury [30].

Fatty acid oxidation is predominantly carried out in mitochondrial and peroxisomal compartments, where long-chain acyl-CoA species are converted into acetyl-CoA to sustain ATP production. In renal cortical cells, this oxidative program is tightly regulated by peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α, carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A, and the transcriptional coactivator PGC-1α. Downregulation or functional impairment of any component of this regulatory axis, as documented in proteinuric and inflammatory states, leads to incomplete fatty acid oxidation, excessive reactive oxygen species generation, and secondary mitochondrial damage [48, 49]. Podocytes are particularly vulnerable to such perturbations because of their high energetic demands coupled with limited glycolytic flexibility. Inhibition of fatty acid oxidation in these cells promotes a metabolic shift toward lipid storage, accompanied by endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial fragmentation, and progressive compromise of slit diaphragm architecture [16, 50, 51].

In parallel with mitochondrial β-oxidation, peroxisomes fulfill a complementary role by degrading very-long-chain fatty acids that are not efficiently processed by mitochondria. Impairment of peroxisomal function, including defects in enzymes such as acyl-CoA oxidase 1 or peroxisomal biogenesis factors such as PEX5, leads to accumulation of very-long-chain fatty acids and activation of proinflammatory lipid signaling pathways, including NFκB-dependent transcriptional programs [52]. Recent lipidomic and isotope-tracing studies further indicate that defective fatty acid oxidation in renal cells is closely linked to aberrant activation of sterol regulatory element–binding protein 1, which drives de novo lipogenesis even in the setting of lipid excess [53]. This maladaptive metabolic response accelerates intracellular cholesterol and triglyceride deposition, reinforcing a feedforward cycle of oxidative stress and inflammation.

Autophagy constitutes an additional layer of metabolic control that intersects directly with lipid handling in renal cells. Under conditions of nutrient deprivation or oxidative stress, autophagy supports lipophagy, the selective degradation of lipid droplets to liberate fatty acids for oxidative metabolism [54]. In glomerular disease states, however, chronic exposure to saturated fatty acids impairs lysosomal acid lipase activity, disrupts lipophagic flux, and exacerbates intracellular lipid overload [24]. Experimental studies have shown that therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring fatty acid oxidation and autophagic clearance, including activation of PPARα or AMPK signaling pathways or supplementation with carnitine, can improve mitochondrial function and attenuate proteinuria in models of membranous nephropathy [23, 55]. Collectively, these observations underscore that intracellular lipid metabolism in renal cells serves not only an energetic function but also a critical cytoprotective role. Failure of coordinated lipid efflux, oxidation, or autophagic clearance transforms lipid handling from a homeostatic process into a driver of inflammation, immune activation, and progressive glomerular injury.

Dyslipidemia in nephrotic syndrome: cause and consequence

The profound dyslipidemia observed in nephrotic syndrome (NS), characterized by elevations in total cholesterol, triglycerides, and apolipoprotein B–containing lipoproteins, has classically been interpreted as a secondary metabolic adaptation to hypoalbuminemia [17]. Reduced plasma oncotic pressure stimulates hepatic synthesis of very-low-density lipoprotein and low-density lipoprotein, while urinary loss of key regulatory proteins, including lipoprotein lipase and lecithin–cholesterol acyltransferase, compromises peripheral lipid catabolism [16]. Lipid abnormalities in membranous nephropathy and their clinical significance

Atherogenic lipoproteins, particularly oxidized low-density lipoprotein, accumulate within the glomerular capillary wall and mesangial regions, where they are taken up by macrophages and podocytes through scavenger receptors such as CD36 and LOX-1, promoting foam cell–like phenotypes [14, 19]. The presence of lipid-laden cells within the glomerulus is associated with the release of proinflammatory cytokines and matrix-remodeling enzymes, processes that have been linked to acceleration of glomerulosclerosis in experimental settings [26]. In parallel, dyslipidemia adversely affects the glomerular endothelium by impairing nitric oxide bioavailability and increasing vascular stiffness, thereby exacerbating local ischemic and hemodynamic stress within the microvasculature [63]. Through these mechanisms, lipid imbalance in NS is increasingly recognized as a contributor to intrarenal inflammation, oxidative stress, and structural injury rather than a neutral epiphenomenon.

The interaction between proteinuria and dyslipidemia is strongly self-reinforcing. Persistent urinary protein loss sustains hepatic lipoprotein overproduction, while accumulated atherogenic lipids further injure podocytes and tubular epithelial cells, amplifying protein leakage and metabolic stress [15, 18]. Several studies in nephrotic and other proteinuric models have further linked systemic lipid overload to renal mitochondrial dysfunction, in part through ceramide accumulation and inhibition of β-oxidation, thereby connecting circulating dyslipidemia with intracellular energetic failure [48, 64]. Consistent with this concept, pharmacologic modulation of lipid metabolism using statins, fibrates, or PCSK9 inhibitors has been shown in experimental systems to attenuate hyperlipidemia and reduce renal inflammatory markers, effects that appear at least partially independent of direct immunosuppression [17, 65].

Clinically, the dyslipidemic profile associated with NS exhibits distinctive qualitative features, including marked elevations in LDL cholesterol and lipoprotein(a), relatively preserved or mildly reduced HDL cholesterol levels, and functional impairment of HDL particles [66]. Dysfunctional HDL demonstrates a reduced capacity to promote cholesterol efflux via ABCA1 and loses key antioxidative and anti-inflammatory properties, thereby diminishing its protective role within the vascular and glomerular compartments [67]. Together, these quantitative and qualitative lipid abnormalities favor intracellular cholesterol retention, endothelial dysfunction, and recruitment of immune effector cells. As a result, NS is characterized by a vicious cycle in which proteinuria exacerbates lipid derangements, and lipid abnormalities, in turn, accelerate renal injury and disease progression [45, 68].

Immune pathways in membranous nephropathy

Membranous nephropathy (MN) is classically defined as an organ-specific autoimmune glomerulopathy in which immune responses are directed against antigens expressed on podocytes within the glomerular filtration barrier [5]. The pathological hallmark of MN is the in situ formation of subepithelial immune complexes, followed by complement activation and progressive podocyte injury. Although MN has long been viewed predominantly as a humoral immune disorder, accumulating evidence suggests that its immunopathology is shaped by a broader context in which metabolic factors, particularly lipid dysregulation, modulate immune signaling, antigen organization, and effector mechanisms [49, 69].

The autoimmune cascade in MN is initiated when podocyte-associated antigens, most prominently PLA2R1, but also THSD7A and NELL1, become immunologically accessible, potentially through conformational changes or post-translational modifications [11, 28]. These antigens are presented to autoreactive B cells with support from cognate T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, leading to the generation of high-affinity, predominantly IgG4 autoantibodies [70]. Circulating autoantibodies subsequently bind their target antigens on the podocyte surface, resulting in the formation of subepithelial immune deposits. The extent and spatial organization of these deposits appear to influence downstream complement activation and are increasingly recognized to be modulated by the lipid composition of the podocyte membrane [71]. Experimental observations, including high-resolution imaging studies, suggest that cholesterol- and sphingolipid-enriched membrane nanodomains can facilitate antigen clustering and immune complex assembly, thereby shaping the efficiency of local immune activation [44].

Following immune complex deposition, complement pathways are engaged, primarily through the classical and lectin routes [32]. Although IgG4 is intrinsically a weak complement activator, the presence of mixed IgG subclasses and lipid-associated immune complexes enhances complement recruitment and amplification [72]. This process culminates in the formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), which, when inserted in a sublytic manner into the podocyte membrane, induces calcium influx, generation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, and disruption of cytoskeletal organization, ultimately leading to foot process effacement and loss of barrier function [73, 74]. In parallel, complement-derived anaphylatoxins such as C5a promote recruitment and activation of macrophages, sustaining local oxidative stress and inflammation even in phases when circulating autoantibody titers decline [75].

The immune landscape of MN further reflects coordinated interactions between innate and adaptive immune compartments. Antigen-presenting cells, including dendritic cell subsets within the renal interstitium, can internalize podocyte-derived antigens and perpetuate autoreactive T-cell responses, thereby reinforcing disease chronicity [76]. Importantly, the functional behavior of these innate immune cells is influenced by their metabolic state; variations in lipid droplet accumulation and mitochondrial redox balance have been shown in related inflammatory settings to bias immune responses toward tolerance or inflammation [77]. On the adaptive side, activated Tfh cells rely on glycolytic and lipid-dependent metabolic programs to sustain IL-21–mediated support of autoreactive B cells [15]. Concurrently, impairment of regulatory T-cell metabolic pathways, including fatty acid oxidation and sterol regulatory element–binding protein control, may weaken immune restraint and favor effector dominance [78].

Recent integrative analyses combining immune profiling with lipidomic data have proposed the existence of an “immunolipidic fingerprint” in MN, characterized by the co-occurrence of oxidized lipid enrichment, enhanced C5b-9 deposition, and elevated PLA2R1 autoantibody levels, which correlates with disease activity and therapeutic responsiveness [79]. This concept is best regarded as a provisional analytical framework rather than a validated clinical classification, intended to capture the coordinated immune–metabolic features of the disease. Taken together, these observations support a reframed view of MN not solely as an antibody–complement–driven disorder, but as a condition in which immune effector pathways are modulated and amplified by the renal lipid milieu. This immunometabolic perspective helps explain why immunosuppressive strategies alone may yield incomplete or transient responses in some patients and highlights the potential relevance of metabolic context in shaping disease course and treatment outcomes [80].

Autoantibody-mediated immune response

The defining immunopathologic feature of primary membranous nephropathy (MN) is the presence of circulating autoantibodies directed against podocyte surface antigens, which initiate subepithelial immune complex formation and downstream complement activation. The identification of the M-type phospholipase A₂ receptor (PLA₂R1) as the dominant target antigen represented a pivotal advance in understanding MN pathogenesis [5]. Anti-PLA₂R antibodies, predominantly of the IgG4 subclass, bind in situ to conformational epitopes on PLA₂R1 expressed on podocyte membranes, leading to the characteristic subepithelial immune deposits that define MN histologically. These antigen–antibody interactions occur within cholesterol- and sphingolipid-enriched membrane microdomains, implicating the local lipid environment as a determinant of immune complex organization and pathogenic potential.

Although IgG4 antibodies are traditionally regarded as inefficient activators of the classical complement pathway, structural and cryo-electron microscopy studies indicate that PLA₂R1–IgG4 immune complexes can adopt multivalent conformations capable of weak C1q engagement under specific conditions [81]. This process appears to be influenced by the surrounding membrane lipid composition, supporting the concept that podocyte lipid microdomains modulate the functional consequences of autoantibody binding. Such observations highlight an immunometabolic interface in which lipid remodeling of the podocyte membrane may influence the stability, density, and complement-activating capacity of immune complexes, thereby shaping disease expression [41].

Beyond PLA₂R1, a growing spectrum of target antigens has been identified, including THSD7A, NELL1, exostosin 1/2 (EXT1/2), protocadherin 7 (PCDH7), and semaphorin 3B [11, 28]. These antigenic variants are associated with distinct clinicopathologic patterns and likely reflect differences in upstream immune activation and antigenic context. For example, THSD7A-associated MN has been linked to a more pronounced Th2-skewed immune response and a higher frequency of concurrent malignancy, whereas NELL1- or EXT1/2-associated forms are more often observed in secondary or autoimmune settings, such as lupus nephritis or IgG4-related disease [82]. Such heterogeneity suggests that antigen identity influences not only immune targeting but also the broader immunologic and metabolic milieu in which MN develops.

Autoantibody generation in MN follows a classical germinal center–dependent pathway that requires coordinated interactions between B cells and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells. Tfh-derived cytokines, including interleukin-21 and interleukin-4, promote class switching toward IgG4 and drive affinity maturation within secondary lymphoid tissues [83]. Emerging multi-omics studies further suggest that autoreactive B-cell clones, particularly those specific for PLA₂R1, exhibit enhanced metabolic activity characterized by increased glycolytic flux and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation [84]. This metabolically activated state may support clonal persistence and help explain the incomplete or transient suppression of autoantibody production observed after B-cell–depleting therapies in some patients.

From a clinical perspective, the circulating levels and qualitative features of MN-associated autoantibodies closely parallel disease activity and therapeutic response. Elevated titers of anti-PLA₂R antibodies are associated with ongoing immune complex deposition, complement activation, and active proteinuria, whereas declining or undetectable titers reliably precede or accompany immunologic remission [22, 29, 85]. In addition, assessment of epitope spreading within PLA₂R1, from the dominant cysteine-rich domain to secondary C-type lectin-like domains, has emerged as a marker of disease progression, with broader epitope recognition reflecting a more entrenched and resilient autoimmune response [86]. At the tissue level, immunohistochemical analyses and spatial transcriptomic studies demonstrate colocalization of podocyte antigens and bound autoantibodies, accompanied by upregulation of oxidative stress–responsive and interferon-stimulated gene programs in adjacent cells [87]. Collectively, these findings indicate that autoantibody-mediated injury in MN extends beyond static antigen binding and instead reflects a dynamic, lipid-influenced, and metabolically primed immune interface in which podocytes function both as immune targets and active participants in disease propagation.

Complement system activation

Activation of the complement system constitutes the principal effector mechanism through which autoantibody binding is translated into podocyte injury in membranous nephropathy (MN). Following the formation of subepithelial immune complexes containing PLA₂R1, THSD7A, or NELL1 antigens, multiple complement pathways may be engaged, including the classical and lectin pathways, with contributions from the alternative pathway under specific conditions [32]. Although IgG4, the predominant immunoglobulin subclass in MN, is traditionally considered inefficient at activating complement, it frequently coexists with IgG1 and IgG3 within mixed immune complexes, thereby facilitating C1q binding and initiation of the classical pathway [9, 88]. In parallel, mannan-binding lectin can recognize carbohydrate structures exposed on immune complexes and activate the lectin pathway independently of C1q, providing an additional route for complement engagement even when classical activation is relatively weak [64].

Complement activation initiates a cascade of proteolytic events that generate a highly reactive inflammatory microenvironment at the podocyte surface. Formation of C3 convertase leads to local deposition of C3b along the subepithelial aspect of the glomerular basement membrane, promoting amplification of the cascade through C5 convertase assembly and culminating in generation of the terminal membrane attack complex (C5b-9) [33]. In MN, C5b-9 insertion into the podocyte membrane is typically sublytic rather than overtly cytolytic. These non-lytic pores permit dysregulated ion fluxes, particularly calcium influx, which in turn induce mitochondrial depolarization, increased production of reactive oxygen species, and activation of intracellular phospholipase A₂ and protease pathways [89]. The downstream consequence is reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton and effacement of podocyte foot processes, a central structural correlate of proteinuria.

In addition to direct effects on podocyte integrity, complement-derived anaphylatoxins such as C3a and C5a exert potent immunomodulatory actions. By engaging their cognate receptors on podocytes, glomerular endothelial cells, and infiltrating macrophages, these mediators enhance oxidative stress, stimulate the production of proinflammatory cytokines including interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor α, and promote propagation of tubulointerstitial inflammation [1]. Experimental studies further suggest that C5a signaling can interfere with intracellular metabolic pathways in podocytes, including insulin and lipid signaling, thereby linking complement activation to alterations in cellular metabolic homeostasis [20]. Consistent with this concept, metabolomic analyses in proteinuric disease states have demonstrated associations between lipid peroxidation products, enhanced C5b-9 deposition, and persistent autoantibody activity, although direct causal relationships in MN remain to be fully established [85].

Under physiological conditions, complement activation at the glomerular surface is tightly constrained by membrane-bound regulatory proteins such as CD46 (membrane cofactor protein), CD55 (decay-accelerating factor), and CD59 (protectin). These regulators are expressed by podocytes and serve to limit uncontrolled complement amplification and MAC assembly. In MN and related proteinuric settings, oxidative stress and lipid-induced membrane perturbations have been associated with reduced expression or functional impairment of these protective molecules, rendering podocytes more susceptible to complement-mediated injury [90]. The convergence of dense immune complex formation with compromised complement regulation thus creates a permissive environment for sustained C5b-9 activity.

This interplay between immune complex density, complement effector mechanisms, and the metabolic state of the podocyte membrane represents a critical node in the pathophysiology of MN. From a therapeutic perspective, modulation of this effector axis through inhibition of terminal complement components or interference with C5a–C5a receptor signaling has demonstrated the capacity to reduce proteinuria and attenuate podocyte stress in experimental models, while clinical translation remains an area of ongoing investigation [91, 92]. Collectively, these observations support the view that complement activation in MN is not a simple linear extension of antibody binding but a context-dependent process influenced by immune composition, regulatory capacity, and metabolic resilience of the glomerular membrane, with these factors jointly determining the extent of podocyte injury and clinical disease expression.

Podocyte injury, remodeling, and secondary MN

Sustained sublytic insertion of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) induces a profound phenotypic transformation in podocytes, shifting them from specialized structural components of the filtration barrier toward metabolically stressed and proinflammatory effector cells. Recurrent episodes of complement activation lead to intracellular calcium overload, which activates calcineurin- and Rho GTPase–dependent signaling pathways, resulting in actin cytoskeletal disorganization and progressive collapse of foot process architecture [35]. Key slit diaphragm and cytoskeletal proteins, including nephrin, podocin, and synaptopodin, undergo ubiquitin-mediated degradation under these conditions, producing widespread foot process effacement that closely correlates with the severity of proteinuria and serves as a major determinant of long-term renal outcome [36]. Given the limited regenerative capacity of podocytes, such injury is largely irreversible, and surviving cells often enter a state of metabolic dedifferentiation characterized by increased reliance on glycolytic pathways and suppression of fatty acid oxidation [93].

Beyond structural disruption, non-lytic C5b-9 signaling activates intracellular inflammatory pathways within podocytes, including NFκB and MAPK cascades, leading to increased expression of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor α, transforming growth factor β1, and interleukin-6, as well as matrix-remodeling enzymes including matrix metalloproteinases 2 and 9 [94]. These responses promote progressive remodeling of the glomerular basement membrane and contribute to glomerulosclerosis. Importantly, these signaling events are closely intertwined with alterations in lipid metabolism. Injured podocytes exhibit accumulation of oxidized lipid species and free cholesterol, which exacerbate reactive oxygen species generation and endoplasmic reticulum stress, thereby amplifying cellular dysfunction [95]. Impaired cholesterol efflux, particularly through downregulation or dysfunction of ABCA1-mediated pathways, further promotes membrane lipid rigidity, interferes with repair mechanisms, and increases susceptibility to podocyte detachment [96]. This convergence of immune-mediated stress and lipid dysregulation provides a mechanistic basis for clinical observations in which hyperlipidemia is associated with persistent proteinuria despite apparent immunologic remission [97].

Chronic exposure to complement-derived stressors and lipotoxic signals also drives phenotypic reprogramming of podocytes toward a proinflammatory and profibrotic state. Under sustained injury conditions, podocytes upregulate markers such as vimentin, α–smooth muscle actin, and integrins, features consistent with partial epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition–like remodeling. This process facilitates loss of adhesion to the glomerular basement membrane, migration toward Bowman’s space, and formation of so-called pseudocrescents, which reflect maladaptive attempts at cellular survival rather than true regenerative responses. Ultrastructural analyses of renal biopsy specimens have demonstrated colocalization of C5b-9–positive deposits with lipid-laden lysosomal structures, supporting the coexistence of immune-mediated and metabolic injury within affected podocytes [85]. Such remodeling can therefore be viewed as an adaptive response to sustained stress that ultimately compromises filtration function and accelerates disease progression.

Secondary forms of membranous nephropathy, including those associated with systemic autoimmune diseases, chronic infections, or malignancy, converge on similar downstream pathways of podocyte injury despite distinct initiating triggers [38]. In lupus-associated MN, a broader autoantibody repertoire, including anti–double-stranded DNA and anti-nucleosome antibodies, promotes robust classical pathway activation with prominent C1q deposition, while the accompanying systemic inflammatory environment exacerbates dyslipidemia and mitochondrial dysfunction within renal cells [98, 99]. Malignancy-associated MN likewise exhibits enhanced susceptibility to podocyte injury, potentially influenced by oxidized lipid byproducts generated in tumor-associated metabolic states that alter lipid raft organization and facilitate immune complex anchoring [55]. Across these diverse clinical contexts, a unifying theme emerges in which podocytes function simultaneously as targets of immune attack and amplifiers of injury through metabolic vulnerability. This convergence of complement-driven damage and lipid dysregulation establishes a self-sustaining pathogenic loop that underlies both primary and secondary forms of MN and contributes to their shared histopathologic and clinical features [100].

Involvement of adaptive immunity: B cells, Tfh cells, and cytokine networks

The adaptive immune system constitutes the central driver sustaining membranous nephropathy (MN) through continuous generation of pathogenic autoantibodies. Activated B cells, particularly within renal-draining lymph nodes and tertiary lymphoid structures, differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells that maintain circulating anti-podocyte antibody pools [38]. This mechanism underlies the clinical efficacy of B-cell–depleting therapies such as rituximab and obinutuzumab, which target CD20-positive B cells and frequently induce durable immunologic and clinical remissions [101]. Nevertheless, relapse after immune reconstitution is common, often accompanied by re-emergence of the same antigen-specific autoantibody clones, indicating that B-cell depletion alone does not fully dismantle the autoreactive immune network and that upstream regulatory pathways remain active.

T follicular helper (Tfh) cells play a pivotal role in this process by orchestrating B-cell activation, class switching, and affinity maturation within germinal centers. These specialized CD4+ T cells provide costimulatory signals through CD40L, ICOS, and PD-1, and secrete key cytokines, most notably interleukin-21 and interleukin-4, which together promote IgG4 class switching and plasma cell differentiation [35, 36]. In MN, expanded populations of circulating IL-21–producing Tfh cells have been reported, with levels correlating with anti-PLA₂R antibody titers and disease activity [102]. Multi-omics profiling suggests that these Tfh cells exhibit metabolic reprogramming toward enhanced glycolytic flux and oxidative metabolism regulated by mTORC1 and HIF-1α signaling pathways, a state that supports sustained cytokine production even under metabolically constrained or hypoxic conditions [103]. In parallel, T follicular regulatory (Tfr) cells, which normally restrain excessive germinal center reactions, appear reduced in number or functional capacity in MN, potentially due to oxidative and lipid-induced stress within lymphoid microenvironments [104]. This imbalance between Tfh and Tfr populations favors persistent autoreactive B-cell responses.

The cytokine milieu sustaining adaptive immune activation in MN extends beyond IL-21 and IL-4 and includes interleukin-6, B-cell activating factor (BAFF), and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL), each contributing to B-cell survival and differentiation through distinct signaling pathways. IL-6 reinforces STAT3 and Bcl6-dependent programs that stabilize the Tfh phenotype and enhance B-cell viability [105]. Elevated circulating BAFF levels have been observed in patients with PLA₂R- and THSD7A-associated MN and tend to decline with immunologic remission, suggesting participation in a feedback loop that supports autoreactive B-cell persistence [106]. On this basis, therapeutic strategies targeting BAFF alone or in combination with APRIL, such as belimumab or telitacicept, are being explored in MN, with the aim of modulating B-cell survival signals rather than inducing broad immunosuppression [107].

Recognition of long-lived plasma cells as a major source of refractory autoantibody production has further expanded the therapeutic landscape. Plasma cells are largely resistant to CD20-directed therapies, prompting interest in agents that target later stages of B-cell differentiation. Felzartamab, a monoclonal antibody directed against CD38, has recently entered clinical investigation in MN and offers a strategy to suppress plasmablasts and plasma cells that sustain autoantibody production despite prior B-cell depletion [108]. These developments underscore the hierarchical complexity of adaptive immune dysregulation in MN and the need for approaches that address multiple levels of the B-cell lineage.

At the interface of immunity and metabolism, lipid availability and intracellular cholesterol handling exert significant influence on adaptive immune function. Excess cholesterol within lymphocytes enhances B-cell receptor and T-cell receptor clustering within lipid rafts, amplifying downstream signaling and lowering activation thresholds [109]. Conversely, modulation of lipid-sensing pathways such as liver X receptor or sterol regulatory element–binding protein signaling can reprogram immune cell metabolism and alter the balance between effector and regulatory responses [110]. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses of MN kidney tissue have identified infiltrating Tfh-like cells with coordinated upregulation of Bcl6, ICOS, and oxidative phosphorylation–related genes, closely linked to pathways involved in cholesterol biosynthesis and lipid metabolism [111]. These findings suggest that adaptive immune activation in MN is not solely antigen driven but is also conditioned by the metabolic environment.

Collectively, the adaptive immune arm of MN, encompassing B cells, Tfh cells, regulatory counterparts, and their cytokine networks, operates as a metabolically influenced system that sustains autoantibody production and disease chronicity. This perspective provides a rationale for therapeutic strategies that combine immune modulation with targeted correction of metabolic and lipid-associated imbalances. Such approaches remain largely investigational but represent a logical extension of the immunometabolic framework proposed for MN pathogenesis and warrant systematic evaluation in future studies [112]. Crosstalk between lipid metabolism and immune pathways

The pathogenesis of membranous nephropathy (MN) cannot be adequately explained by autoimmune mechanisms or metabolic disturbances considered in isolation. Increasing evidence supports the view that MN lies at the intersection of immune regulation and cellular metabolism, consistent with the broader framework of immunometabolism, which examines how metabolic states influence immune effector functions [42]. In this context, dysregulated lipid homeostasis and immune activation in MN appear to engage in a bidirectional and self-reinforcing interaction, whereby immune-mediated podocyte injury perturbs intrinsic lipid handling, while lipid accumulation and modification may, in turn, potentiate immune activation and tissue damage [113]. This reciprocal relationship provides a unifying lens through which many previously disconnected observations in MN can be integrated.

At the molecular level, cholesterol and sphingolipids serve dual and interconnected roles. As essential structural components of the glomerular filtration barrier, these lipids contribute to membrane integrity and mechanical stability. Simultaneously, they modulate immune signaling by organizing membrane microdomains that influence receptor localization and signal transduction. Cholesterol-enriched lipid rafts on podocyte membranes have been implicated in the spatial clustering of antigen–antibody complexes, a process that may facilitate complement recruitment and stabilization of subepithelial immune deposits. Although much of the mechanistic insight into raft-mediated immune signaling derives from other proteinuric and autoimmune systems, analogous principles are increasingly being applied to MN to explain how membrane composition could shape the efficiency and persistence of immune injury [114].

Lipid-dependent signaling platforms are not confined to the kidney but extend to circulating and lymphoid immune cells. In T and B lymphocytes, lipid raft integrity influences T-cell receptor and B-cell receptor clustering and downstream activation thresholds. In settings of systemic dyslipidemia, altered cholesterol availability has been shown in other inflammatory diseases to enhance receptor signaling and immune responsiveness, raising the possibility that similar mechanisms may contribute to sustained adaptive immune activation in MN [114]. Within the glomerulus, accumulation of oxidized lipoproteins and bioactive lipid species such as ceramides can act as danger-associated molecular patterns, engaging innate immune sensors including toll-like receptor 4 and inflammasome components such as NLRP3. Activation of these pathways promotes the release of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β and interleukin-18, thereby linking metabolic stress to amplification of both innate and adaptive immune responses [115].

These observations suggest that metabolic toxicity in MN should not be regarded solely as a downstream consequence of proteinuria. Rather, lipid dysregulation may function as an active modifier of immunopathology, influencing antigen presentation, complement activation, and immune cell behavior. While definitive causal relationships in MN remain an area of ongoing investigation, clinical associations between hyperlipidemia, disease severity, and treatment responsiveness support the relevance of lipid–immune interactions to disease course [116]. Recognition of this integrated pathogenic network provides the conceptual basis for examining discrete mechanistic nodes through which lipid metabolism and immune pathways intersect, including membrane microdomain organization, oxidized lipid signaling, cholesterol efflux capacity, and lipid-derived inflammatory mediators. These interconnected processes, schematically summarized in Fig. 2, form the foundation for understanding MN as an immunometabolic disorder rather than a purely antibody-driven disease [116]. Lipid rafts as platforms for immune complex deposition

Lipid rafts within podocyte membranes, defined as cholesterol- and sphingolipid-enriched microdomains containing glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins, function as dynamic signaling platforms rather than inert structural elements [48]. In membranous nephropathy (MN), these microdomains provide preferential sites for anchoring major target antigens, including PLA2R1 and THSD7A, thereby creating localized environments that favor in situ autoantibody binding. The tightly ordered lipid architecture of rafts promotes antigen clustering and increases antibody accessibility, facilitating immune complex formation at the podocyte surface. Upon antibody engagement, these domains initiate localized signaling events mediated by Src-family kinases and raft-associated adaptor proteins, triggering intracellular stress pathways that prime podocytes for complement deposition and injury [78].

Beyond their role in antigen anchoring, lipid rafts actively coordinate podocyte immune signaling. Antigen–antibody complexes formed within cholesterol-dense nanodomains are stabilized by scaffolding proteins such as flotillin-1 and flotillin-2, as well as caveolin-1, which colocalize with podocin and nephrin within slit diaphragm–associated regions [78]. This spatial organization enables sustained signal amplification, including activation of phospholipase Cγ, induction of intracellular calcium flux, and nuclear translocation of NFκB, collectively biasing podocytes toward proinflammatory and proapoptotic responses [117]. Experimental evidence from cellular and animal models supports a functional requirement for raft integrity in these processes, as pharmacologic depletion of membrane cholesterol with agents such as methyl-β-cyclodextrin disrupts PLA2R1 microclustering and markedly attenuates subsequent C5b-9 formation [118].

Chronic lipid imbalance, a hallmark of MN, further modifies the composition and biophysical properties of podocyte lipid rafts. Sustained hypercholesterolemia and uptake of oxidized lipids lead to accumulation of oxysterols, such as 7-ketocholesterol, and ceramide species within the plasma membrane. These changes alter liquid-ordered phase stability and promote fusion of smaller nanodomains into enlarged raft structures, sometimes referred to as macrorafts, which facilitate aberrant immune complex aggregation and enhance binding of complement components such as C1q [119]. Complement activation within these expanded platforms generates anaphylatoxins, including C3a and C5a, which recruit monocytes and macrophages to the glomerular capillary wall and amplify local inflammatory responses [120].

From a mechanistic standpoint, lipid rafts represent a critical biointerface where lipid metabolism converges with immune signaling. The lipid composition of these domains is continuously regulated by cholesterol transport and trafficking proteins, including ABCA1 and NPC1L1, and disruption of these regulatory pathways compromises membrane homeostasis and antigen tolerance [121]. Advanced molecular imaging and super-resolution microscopy studies in experimental MN have demonstrated colocalization of antigen–antibody complexes with raft-associated lipid transporters and complement regulatory proteins such as CD55 and CD59 [122]. Oxidative modification of membrane cholesterol and impaired cholesterol efflux reduce the density and functionality of CD59, thereby diminishing resistance to C5b-9–mediated injury and lowering the threshold for complement-driven podocyte damage [6].

Persistent lipid derangement thus reprograms lipid raft composition and function, converting a finely tuned signaling matrix that supports filtration barrier integrity into a pathogenic platform for immune complex stabilization and complement activation. Similar principles have been described in other immune-mediated and metabolic diseases, including lupus nephritis and atherosclerosis, where cholesterol-enriched rafts promote immune complex formation and immune cell hyperactivation [123]. In MN, podocyte lipid rafts undergo a comparable transformation, evolving from regulators of cellular architecture into metabolically conditioned immunologic synapses that link systemic dyslipidemia with localized autoimmune injury [34, 124]. This raft-centered remodeling process represents an early and critical node in the immunometabolic cascade of MN, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 2.

Oxidized LDL fueling complement activation

Systemic dyslipidemia, combined with the oxidative stress imposed by sustained proteinuria, creates a biochemical milieu that favors lipid peroxidation in membranous nephropathy (MN) [21]. Among the spectrum of oxidatively modified lipids, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) has emerged as a particularly relevant immunometabolic mediator. OxLDL particles contain structurally altered apolipoprotein B100 and reactive aldehyde adducts such as malondialdehyde and 4-hydroxynonenal, which modify surface charge and epitope configuration, thereby enabling interactions with components of the innate immune system [94]. Through these properties, oxLDL provides a mechanistic link between metabolic oxidative injury and complement activation in the absence of overt infection.

At the molecular level, oxLDL can associate with complement-interacting proteins such as C-reactive protein and complement factor H (CFH), thereby influencing the balance between complement activation and regulation [125]. Under physiological conditions, CFH restrains excessive activation of the alternative pathway by promoting the inactivation of C3b. However, binding of CFH to oxLDL alters its regulatory capacity, favoring persistence of C3 convertase activity and facilitating downstream C5b-9 assembly [126]. Within the glomerulus, deposited oxLDL has been shown in experimental settings to interact with phosphatidylserine-enriched membrane microdomains on podocytes, which may stabilize alternative pathway components and support local amplification of complement activation [127]. These observations provide a potential explanation for continued complement activity in MN even during periods of reduced circulating autoantibody levels, suggesting that oxidized lipids can function as non-antibody–dependent drivers of immune-mediated injury [12].

Evidence supporting a pathogenic association between oxidized lipids and complement activation derives from both clinical correlations and experimental models. In patients with MN, elevated markers of lipid peroxidation have been reported to correlate with increased glomerular C5b-9 deposition and disease activity [85]. In animal models of nephrotic syndrome, interventions that neutralize oxLDL or enhance lipoprotein clearance, including administration of anti–oxLDL antibodies or ApoE-mimetic peptides, have been associated with reductions in C3 and C5b-9 deposition and preservation of podocyte structure [128, 129]. While these findings do not establish causality in human MN, they support the concept that oxidized lipids can actively modulate complement-dependent injury in proteinuric states.

In addition to direct effects on complement regulation, oxLDL functions as a danger-associated molecular pattern capable of engaging pattern-recognition receptors such as toll-like receptor 2 and toll-like receptor 4 on podocytes and resident immune cells. Activation of these receptors promotes reactive oxygen species generation through NADPH oxidase and stimulates inflammasome pathways, including NLRP3, resulting in the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β, interleukin-18, and tumor necrosis factor α [130]. These mediators may further enhance local expression of complement components, including C3 and factor B, thereby reinforcing a feedforward loop between oxidative stress, lipid toxicity, and complement activation within the glomerular microenvironment [85]. Oxidative modification of membrane lipids can also impair the function of complement regulatory proteins anchored in lipid rafts, such as CD59, increasing susceptibility to sustained sublytic C5b-9 insertion and podocyte injury [131].

Histopathologic analyses of renal biopsy specimens from patients with MN have demonstrated spatial colocalization of oxLDL and C5b-9 along the glomerular capillary wall, lending morphologic support to the concept of coordinated metabolic and immune injury [64]. Collectively, these observations position oxidized lipids not simply as downstream byproducts of metabolic disturbance, but as active modulators of complement activation and inflammatory signaling in MN [121]. Although the therapeutic implications remain to be fully defined, these findings provide a rationale for exploring strategies that reduce lipid oxidation or limit oxLDL accumulation, potentially in combination with immunomodulatory approaches, as part of a broader immunometabolic framework for MN management.

Dysregulated lipid metabolism modulating adaptive immunity

Adaptive immune cell function and fate are tightly coupled to intracellular metabolic programs, rendering these cells highly sensitive to fluctuations in lipid availability and composition. In membranous nephropathy (MN), systemic dyslipidemia and increased exposure to cholesterol and fatty acids reshape the metabolic landscape of adaptive immunity, thereby modulating both the magnitude and persistence of autoreactive responses. Excess lipid availability alters membrane fluidity and signaling competence in B cells, favoring differentiation toward antibody-secreting phenotypes and sustaining autoantibody production [50, 52]. In parallel, metabolic reprogramming of T follicular helper (Tfh) cells biases immune regulation toward a pathogenic, pro-autoimmune state that reinforces ongoing disease activity [41, 132].

Recent experimental and translational studies indicate that B-cell immunometabolism plays a central role in determining antibody quality, durability, and autoreactive potential [133]. Intracellular cholesterol accumulation stabilizes lipid raft–associated signaling complexes containing BLNK, SYK, and PI3K, prolonging B-cell receptor signaling and lowering activation thresholds [84]. In addition, uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein via scavenger receptors such as SR-B1 and CD36 induces endoplasmic reticulum stress and unfolded protein responses in B cells, promoting differentiation toward highly secretory plasmablast phenotypes. This metabolically stressed state resembles immune profiles observed during chronic autoimmune flares, suggesting that lipid toxicity may function as a persistent stimulus for antibody secretion rather than a transient metabolic disturbance [19]. Supporting this concept, in vitro studies demonstrate that experimental restriction of cholesterol availability or pharmacologic modulation with statins reduces the frequency of CD27+ memory B cells and suppresses anti-PLA2R antibody production, providing direct evidence for a lipid–B-cell regulatory axis [134].

T-cell responses in MN are similarly shaped by metabolic cues. Tfh cells depend on balanced fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial fitness to sustain germinal center help functions [135]. Chronic exposure to elevated circulating lipids and oxidative stress disrupts this balance, favoring a metabolic shift toward glycolysis and de novo lipid synthesis driven by activation of sterol regulatory element–binding protein 2 and mTORC1 signaling. This reprogramming stabilizes transcriptional programs dominated by BCL6 and c-MYC, characteristic of pathogenic Tfh cells that promote persistent B-cell activation and autoantibody production [136]. Consistent with these mechanisms, flow cytometric analyses of patients with active MN reveal expanded populations of circulating PD1+ ICOS+ Tfh cells expressing the lipid uptake receptor CD36, linking fatty acid availability directly to disease activity and humoral immune amplification [137].

Metabolic dysregulation also affects immune subsets that normally restrain autoimmunity. Regulatory T cells and memory B cells rely on intact AMPK and PPARα signaling to maintain suppressive capacity and lineage stability. In vitro studies indicate that lipid overload inhibits these pathways, leading to reduced FOXP3 expression and diminished regulatory T-cell stability [138]. Aberrant cholesterol accumulation further impairs regulatory T-cell migratory capacity and their ability to suppress autoreactive B-cell responses within inflamed renal tissue, thereby shifting immune homeostasis toward effector dominance [139]. Complementary transcriptomic analyses of renal biopsies from patients with MN demonstrate overexpression of lipid metabolism–associated genes, including FASN, ACLY, and SCD1, within infiltrating B cells and T cells, reinforcing the concept of metabolically activated adaptive immunity in situ [49].

Collectively, these findings position dysregulated lipid metabolism as a critical metabolic checkpoint governing adaptive immune cell plasticity in MN. Hypercholesterolemia and exposure to oxidized lipid species do not merely accompany autoimmunity but actively sustain and amplify autoreactive networks, transforming adaptive immunity into a self-reinforcing metabolic circuit [140]. This framework suggests that selective metabolic reprogramming of immune cells, through modulation of pathways such as AMPK and mTOR or through targeted lipid management strategies, may offer a means to attenuate pathogenic autoimmunity while avoiding the consequences of broad immunosuppression.

Role of cholesterol efflux pathways in immune regulation

Cholesterol efflux pathways, particularly those mediated by ABCA1 and apolipoprotein E (ApoE), play a central role in maintaining cellular lipid balance and exert important modulatory effects on inflammatory and immune responses [40]. In podocytes, an intact ABCA1/ApoE axis limits intracellular cholesterol accumulation and protects against lipotoxic stress, thereby supporting cytoskeletal stability and filtration barrier integrity [54]. In inflammatory settings, including membranous nephropathy (MN), suppression or dysfunction of ABCA1 activity has been observed and is associated with intracellular cholesterol retention, heightened inflammatory signaling, and impairment of cellular stress-response mechanisms [23, 24]. Under physiological conditions, the export of excess membrane cholesterol to extracellular acceptors such as ApoAI and high-density lipoprotein via ABCA1 and ABCG1 constitutes a critical homeostatic process that preserves membrane fluidity, constrains reactive oxygen species generation, and supports relative immune cell quiescence [141].

Disruption of cholesterol efflux in MN creates a metabolic environment in which both podocytes and infiltrating immune cells accumulate unesterified cholesterol. Within lysosomal compartments, this excess cholesterol can crystallize and activate innate immune sensors such as the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to increased production of interleukin-1β and interleukin-18 and amplification of local inflammatory signaling [142]. In podocytes, reduced ABCA1 function has been linked to cytoskeletal instability, compromised autophagic flux, and progressive loss of filtration barrier integrity, thereby reinforcing a cycle of lipid accumulation and inflammation [143]. These processes suggest that impaired cholesterol handling does not merely accompany immune injury but may actively modulate cellular susceptibility to complement- and cytokine-mediated stress.

From an immunologic perspective, cholesterol efflux pathways function as metabolic checkpoints that influence immune cell phenotype and responsiveness. Macrophages with defective ABCA1 or ABCG1-mediated transport exhibit enhanced toll-like receptor signaling, increased secretion of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor α, and a bias toward an M1-like inflammatory polarization [144]. In contrast, efficient cholesterol export facilitated by ApoE-rich high-density lipoprotein promotes activation of lipid-sensing transcription factors, including PPARγ and liver X receptor α, which are associated with anti-inflammatory or regulatory macrophage phenotypes [145]. In renal tissue from patients with MN, reduced ApoE expression has been reported in association with increased C5b-9 deposition and markers of podocyte injury, consistent with a link between impaired lipid handling and complement-mediated damage [146].

T lymphocytes likewise depend on tightly regulated cholesterol flux for appropriate activation and signal termination. Increased membrane cholesterol stabilizes T-cell receptor nanoclusters and lowers activation thresholds, whereas induction of ABCA1 and ABCG1 expression through liver X receptor signaling disrupts these clusters and attenuates excessive effector responses [147]. Experimental studies further indicate that loss of ApoE skews T-cell differentiation toward Th1 and Th17 phenotypes at the expense of regulatory T-cell development, thereby favoring autoimmune responses [148]. In regulatory T cells, ABCA1-dependent cholesterol efflux supports mitochondrial respiration and cellular longevity; impairment of this pathway during oxidative or cytokine stress is associated with metabolic exhaustion and reduced suppressive capacity, limiting control over autoreactive B-cell activity [149].

Omics-based analyses and experimental models also link the ABCA1/ApoE axis to regulation of membrane microarchitecture, including lipid raft organization. Reduced cholesterol efflux enhances raft stability and immunological synapse formation, facilitating heightened signaling through B-cell and T-cell receptor complexes [150]. Conversely, pharmacologic or genetic interventions that augment ABCA1 expression, including activation of liver X receptor or AMP-activated protein kinase pathways, have been associated in murine models with reductions in autoantibody levels and attenuation of glomerular injury, although direct translation to human MN remains to be established [151]. Collectively, these observations position cholesterol efflux pathways as integrators of lipid homeostasis and immune regulation. Within the proposed immunometabolic framework of MN, impaired efflux amplifies inflammatory self-activation in lipid-rich cells, whereas restoration of cholesterol export may contribute to re-establishing immune restraint and cellular resilience [152].

Interplay between lipid-derived mediators and immune responses

Lipid-derived inflammatory mediators constitute an additional layer through which lipid metabolism intersects with immune regulation in membranous nephropathy (MN). Bioactive lipids such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes are generated from polyunsaturated fatty acids and are markedly influenced by the inflammatory milieu characteristic of proteinuric kidney disease [39]. In MN, enhanced lipid oxidation and enzymatic remodeling favor the production of proinflammatory mediators, including leukotriene B4 and prostaglandin E2, which modulate glomerular hemodynamics, promote immune cell recruitment, and reinforce local cytokine networks, thereby directly linking lipid metabolic flux to the intensity and persistence of immune activation [17, 153].

At the core of this signaling network lies phospholipase A2, which catalyzes the release of arachidonic acid from membrane phospholipids, providing substrate for downstream cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways [154]. Within the glomerular microenvironment of MN, increased activity of these enzymatic cascades leads to elevated levels of prostaglandins and leukotrienes. Prostaglandin E2, acting through EP2 and EP4 receptors expressed on podocytes and mesangial cells, influences glomerular perfusion and vascular permeability, effects that can exacerbate proteinuria and mechanical stress on the filtration barrier [155]. Concurrently, leukotriene B4 functions as a potent chemoattractant for monocytes and T lymphocytes, amplifying immune cell infiltration and sustaining inflammatory circuits within the kidney [156].

The influence of lipid mediators extends beyond innate immune recruitment to shape adaptive immune responses. Prostaglandin E2 has been shown in experimental systems to favor differentiation of pathogenic T follicular helper and Th17 cells through cAMP–protein kinase A and mTOR-dependent signaling pathways, while simultaneously suppressing regulatory T-cell development and function [157]. This dual effect links lipid oxygenation pathways to sustained autoantibody production in MN. In vitro studies further indicate that arachidonic acid–derived oxidation products can enhance immunoglobulin class switching and interleukin-21 secretion by B cells through cyclooxygenase-2–dependent mechanisms, providing an additional route by which lipid metabolism may reinforce humoral autoimmunity [158].

Counterbalancing these proinflammatory signals, a parallel set of lipid mediators generated through alternative lipoxygenase pathways exert anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving effects. Lipoxin A4 and specialized pro-resolving mediators such as resolvins from the E and D series suppress NFκB activation, reduce cytokine production, and inhibit complement gene expression within glomerular cells in experimental models [159]. The relative balance between proinflammatory mediators, including prostaglandin E2 and leukotriene B4, and pro-resolving lipids such as lipoxins and resolvins thus functions as a molecular switch that influences disease trajectory and resolution capacity [160]. In MN, this balance appears to be shifted toward sustained inflammation, favoring persistence rather than resolution of immune-mediated injury.

Additional complexity arises from the involvement of cytochrome P450 epoxygenase pathways, which convert arachidonic acid into epoxyeicosatrienoic acids and hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids. Epoxyeicosatrienoic acids exert vasoprotective, antioxidant, and antiapoptotic effects in glomerular cells, but their bioavailability is reduced in chronic inflammatory states due to increased degradation by soluble epoxide hydrolase [161]. Inhibition of this enzyme has been shown in experimental models to restore epoxyeicosatrienoic acid levels, attenuate immune complex deposition, and reduce proteinuria, suggesting a protective role for this arm of lipid signaling [162]. Consistent with these findings, renal metabolomic analyses in MN have identified a decline in pro-resolving mediator signatures accompanied by excess prostaglandin E2 and leukotriene B4 during disease flares, indicating impaired endogenous resolution mechanisms [121].

Collectively, these observations underscore that lipid-derived mediators function not merely as downstream effectors of inflammation but as active regulators of immune responses in MN. Although most evidence derives from experimental and associative studies, the lipid mediator axis represents a potential therapeutic complement to immunosuppression. Pharmacologic strategies targeting cyclooxygenase-2 activity, leukotriene signaling, or enhancing pro-resolving lipid pathways have shown efficacy in reducing inflammation in proteinuric disease models and may, in principle, mitigate the feedback loop between lipid oxidation and immune activation in MN [19, 163]. Within an immunometabolic framework, modulation of lipid-derived mediator networks thus emerges as a plausible avenue to temper glomerular inflammation and slow progression toward end-stage kidney disease, while preserving essential immune functions.

From a diagnostic and monitoring perspective, circulating lipid profiles in MN provide insight into the underlying immunologic and metabolic stress state. Marked hypercholesterolemia and elevations in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol are common during active disease and have been shown to parallel immunologic activity, including circulating anti-PLA2R antibody levels, in a substantial proportion of patients [165, 166]. Although dyslipidemia alone lacks disease specificity, its dynamic changes over time may complement serologic and clinical markers by reflecting the intensity of ongoing immune-mediated podocyte injury and metabolic burden.

The degree and persistence of dyslipidemia also appear to carry prognostic relevance. Observational studies suggest that more severe or sustained lipid abnormalities are associated with greater glomerular injury and a higher likelihood of delayed or incomplete remission. Patients in whom hyperlipidemia remains poorly controlled during immunosuppressive therapy often demonstrate slower reductions in proteinuria and prolonged disease activity, indicating that metabolic factors may modulate treatment responsiveness rather than merely mirror it [7]. While causality cannot be firmly established from these associations, they support the concept that lipid homeostasis may influence the renal recovery trajectory in MN.

These observations have therapeutic implications that favor an integrated management strategy. Interventions aimed solely at immune suppression may be insufficient to fully address disease drivers in some patients if underlying metabolic stress persists. Conversely, correction of lipid abnormalities in isolation is unlikely to induce immunologic remission. Clinical data increasingly suggest that concurrent targeting of immune and lipid dysregulation may offer additive or synergistic benefits, improving renal outcomes and potentially stabilizing disease course when compared with unimodal approaches [167]. Dyslipidemia as a biomarker of disease activity and progression

Dyslipidemia, most prominently manifested as hypercholesterolemia and elevated low-density lipoprotein levels, is a nearly universal feature of nephrotic syndrome in patients with membranous nephropathy (MN), with more than 90% of individuals exhibiting significant cholesterol elevations during nephrotic-range proteinuria [43]. The severity of these lipid abnormalities frequently parallels the degree of proteinuria and has therefore been regarded as a non-immunologic indicator of disease activity [137]. Importantly, persistently elevated total cholesterol levels have been identified as independent predictors of progression to end-stage kidney disease, underscoring the prognostic relevance of lipid disturbances beyond their cardiovascular implications [44]. In this context, the systemic lipid profile provides an accessible yet biologically informative window into the metabolic stress state that accompanies and potentially amplifies glomerular injury.

Beyond traditional clinical correlations, recent omics-based investigations have refined this perspective by demonstrating that both the quantitative and qualitative composition of circulating lipids reflects discrete phases of MN activity. Lipidomic profiling has revealed marked enrichment of oxidized phospholipids, apolipoprotein B–containing particles, and unesterified cholesterol during immunologically active disease [176]. These alterations cannot be fully explained by hepatic compensatory responses to hypoalbuminemia alone, but also appear to reflect podocyte-derived stress signals and complement activation within the glomerulus [177]. Dyslipidemia thus emerges as an integrated readout of systemic metabolic adaptation and local immune-mediated injury, linking circulating biomarkers to intrarenal pathophysiology.

Longitudinal clinical studies further suggest that changes in lipid parameters may precede conventional immunologic or clinical markers of relapse. In several patient cohorts, rising levels of LDL cholesterol or oxidized LDL have been observed to anticipate incremental increases in anti-PLA2R antibody titers by several weeks, indicating that lipid perturbations may serve as early indicators of immune reactivation [178]. Similarly, reductions in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and specific ApoE-containing subfractions during partial remission have been associated with ongoing low-grade complement activity, even in the absence of overt clinical deterioration [179]. These observations raise the possibility that routine lipid measurements, when interpreted dynamically, could complement serologic monitoring and assist in tailoring the intensity and timing of therapeutic interventions.

The pattern of dyslipidemia observed in MN also appears to differ qualitatively from that seen in other causes of nephrotic syndrome. Whereas hypertriglyceridemia is more prominent in conditions such as diabetic nephropathy or minimal change disease, MN is characterized by a predominantly cholesterol-rich, LDL-driven lipid profile with pronounced oxidative modification [99]. Mechanistically, this phenotype has been linked to enhanced hepatic sterol regulatory element–binding protein 2 activity and impaired ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux, reinforcing the connection between lipid biomarkers and disease-specific molecular pathways [180]. Integration of lipidomic variables with clinical and immunologic data has further improved disease stratification, with machine-learning approaches incorporating indices such as LDL oxidation, HDL functionality, and ApoE status achieving high accuracy in distinguishing active disease from partial or complete remission states [181].

From a broader clinical standpoint, dyslipidemia in MN conveys information not only about renal disease activity but also about systemic cardiometabolic risk. Patients with persistently uncontrolled cholesterol levels experience a substantially higher incidence of major cardiovascular events, highlighting the dual prognostic significance of lipid abnormalities in this population [182]. Taken together, these findings support a reappraisal of dyslipidemia in MN as a dynamic biomarker that translates underlying immunometabolic stress into clinically observable signals. Incorporation of lipid profiling into routine disease surveillance may therefore enhance diagnostic precision, refine risk stratification, and inform more individualized therapeutic decision-making in MN [183].

Association between lipid abnormalities and proteinuria severity

The relationship between lipid abnormalities and proteinuria in membranous nephropathy (MN) is reciprocal and self-reinforcing, forming a pathogenic cycle that links metabolic disturbance to progressive glomerular injury. Heavy proteinuria stimulates hepatic lipoprotein synthesis and impairs lipid catabolism, while the resulting dyslipidemia, particularly the accumulation of modified lipoproteins such as oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL), feeds back to aggravate podocyte damage and increase glomerular permeability [29]. Through this mechanism, lipid toxicity and inflammation act to sustain protein leakage even when upstream immune activity is partially controlled.

Clinical observations support a contributory role for dyslipidemia in determining proteinuria severity. Lipid-lowering interventions, most notably statins, have been shown to reduce proteinuria by approximately 15–20% in some studies, with effects that appear partly independent of immunosuppressive therapy [63]. These findings suggest that metabolic factors are not merely epiphenomena but influence the clinical expression of MN. In addition, qualitative abnormalities in high-density lipoprotein, characterized by reduced antioxidative and cholesterol efflux capacity, have been associated with enhanced oxidative stress and accelerated glomerular injury [30].

Mechanistic studies have identified oxLDL–CD36 signaling as an important link between lipid toxicity and structural damage to the filtration barrier. Podocytes internalize oxLDL through CD36, leading to endoplasmic reticulum stress, cytoskeletal disorganization, and activation of proapoptotic pathways [145]. These events promote matrix remodeling and expansion of the glomerular basement membrane, thereby amplifying protein leakage [184]. In parallel, accumulation of very-low-density and intermediate-density lipoproteins can activate toll-like receptor 4 signaling in podocytes and resident macrophages, increasing local production of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor α [185]. The convergence of oxidative lipid stress and innate immune activation thus establishes a maladaptive loop that perpetuates proteinuria despite partial immunologic control.

Distinct lipid profiles have been reported to associate with different patterns and severities of proteinuria. Patients with predominantly selective albuminuria often exhibit disproportionately elevated LDL cholesterol, whereas nonselective proteinuria has been linked to triglyceride-rich lipoprotein accumulation and increased apolipoprotein CIII levels [186]. These differences likely reflect divergent hepatic transcriptional responses to albumin loss, involving sterol regulatory element–binding protein and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α signaling pathways [187]. Composite indices, such as the oxLDL-to-HDL ratio, have emerged as predictors of renal functional decline, showing strong associations with longitudinal reductions in estimated glomerular filtration rate [188].

Experimental evidence further supports a contributory role for disrupted lipid handling in sustaining proteinuria. In animal models, genetic deficiency of ApoE or ABCA1 accelerates albuminuria and foot process effacement under proteinuric stress, whereas pharmacologic or genetic enhancement of ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux reduces glomerular lipid deposition and attenuates proteinuria [189, 190]. Clinically, lipid profiles also appear to influence therapeutic responsiveness. Failure to normalize LDL cholesterol during treatment has been associated with persistent subnephrotic proteinuria, while recovery of HDL functionality correlates with more rapid declines in anti-PLA2R antibody titers and proteinuria [191, 192]. Together, these observations support the concept that lipid abnormalities participate in a closed pathogenic cycle with glomerular leak, and that durable disease control may require concurrent correction of both immunologic activity and metabolic dysregulation [193].

Impact of lipid-lowering therapies on immune activity and renal outcomes

Lipid-lowering therapies, most notably inhibitors of HMG-CoA reductase, are routinely prescribed in nephrotic syndrome to reduce cardiovascular risk associated with severe dyslipidemia [48]. In the context of membranous nephropathy (MN), however, their potential clinical relevance extends beyond lipid reduction, owing to a range of pleiotropic effects on inflammatory and immune pathways [64]. Experimental and translational studies indicate that statins can modulate T-cell costimulatory signaling, suppress the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor α, and preserve endothelial function through stabilization of nitric oxide bioavailability. Through these mechanisms, statins may influence elements of the autoantibody–complement cascade that drives podocyte injury in MN, although direct causal effects on immune remission remain to be fully delineated [8].

At the molecular level, transcriptomic analyses of renal tissue have shown that statin exposure is associated with downregulation of immune-related gene signatures, including reduced expression of major histocompatibility complex class II molecules and attenuation of NFκB pathway activation within glomerular compartments [194]. These changes are consistent with observed clinical associations between statin use and reductions in proteinuria, as well as modest declines in circulating autoantibody levels in some patient cohorts [195]. Certain statins, such as pravastatin and rosuvastatin, have demonstrated renoprotective effects in experimental models that appear only partially dependent on cholesterol lowering, with reported reductions in oxidative stress, macrophage infiltration, and podocyte injury [196]. Mechanistically, inhibition of RhoA and downstream ROCK signaling by statins has been implicated in stabilization of podocyte cytoskeletal dynamics, a pathway known to be activated during immune-mediated glomerular injury [197]. Collectively, these observations suggest that HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors may intersect with the immunometabolic network of MN at multiple levels, although the magnitude and consistency of these effects in clinical practice require further clarification.

Beyond statins, newer lipid-lowering agents have attracted interest for their potential immunomodulatory properties. Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, including evolocumab and alirocumab, achieve profound reductions in LDL cholesterol and have shown anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects in experimental models of proteinuric kidney disease [64]. PCSK9 regulates low-density lipoprotein receptor recycling and has also been implicated in modulation of immune signaling pathways, including type I interferon responses [133]. In animal studies relevant to MN, pharmacologic inhibition of PCSK9 has been associated with reduced glomerular C5b-9 deposition and attenuation of extracellular matrix expansion, suggesting potential effects that extend beyond lipid lowering alone [19]. Whether such mechanisms translate into clinically meaningful immunologic modulation in human MN remains an area of active investigation.

Fibrates provide an additional example of how metabolic therapies may influence immune pathways. Acting primarily through activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α, fibrates enhance fatty acid oxidation and have been shown to suppress NFκB-dependent cytokine expression while promoting a shift toward anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotypes in experimental systems [198]. In small pilot studies, combination strategies integrating PPAR agonists with low-dose immunosuppressive agents have been associated with greater reductions in proteinuria compared with monotherapy, although these findings require validation in larger controlled trials [199]. Emerging immunometabolic modulators, including activators of AMP-activated protein kinase, have also demonstrated the capacity to enhance autophagic clearance of lipid droplets and dampen NLRP3 inflammasome activation in podocytes in preclinical models, linking cellular energy sensing to immune regulation [123].

Potential of immunometabolic interventions

The accumulating evidence for sustained interaction between lipid metabolism and immune activation in membranous nephropathy (MN) has prompted increasing interest in immunometabolic modulation as a complementary therapeutic concept. Rather than viewing metabolic abnormalities solely as downstream consequences of proteinuria, multiple experimental and clinical observations suggest that lipid dysregulation may contribute to disease persistence and heterogeneity, positioning metabolic pathways as potential modifiers of immune-driven injury [67]. Within this framework, simultaneous attention to immune activity and metabolic stress is hypothesized to offer a more comprehensive approach to disease control by interrupting reinforcing cycles of lipid toxicity and inflammation.

In practical terms, immunometabolic intervention refers to the coordinated use of established immunosuppressive therapies together with agents that modulate lipid handling, mitochondrial function, or oxidative balance. Clinical observations indicate that combining B-cell–directed therapies, such as anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, with lipid-lowering or metabolic agents may be associated with more sustained reductions in proteinuria compared with immunosuppression alone, although these findings are largely derived from observational studies and small cohorts [200]. Mechanistically, correction of metabolic imbalance may reduce the burden of oxidized lipid species and proinflammatory lipid mediators, thereby limiting immune amplification and lowering the likelihood of continued autoantibody activity or epitope diversification [201]. This conceptual integration frames immunologic and metabolic pathways as interconnected components of a single disease network rather than parallel, independent targets.

Several intracellular checkpoints have been proposed as potential nodes for immunometabolic modulation. Activation of AMP-activated protein kinase has been shown in experimental models to enhance fatty acid oxidation, improve mitochondrial efficiency, and attenuate inflammatory signaling in metabolically stressed podocytes. Similarly, Sirtuin 1 activation has been linked to deacetylation of transcriptional regulators involved in inflammatory and stress responses, including NFκB and p53, resulting in reduced inflammatory signaling under certain conditions [202]. Inhibition of sterol regulatory element–binding protein 2 has also been reported in preclinical settings to suppress lipid biosynthesis and attenuate complement-related pathways, suggesting a dual effect on metabolic and immune processes, although its relevance to human MN remains to be established [203]. Collectively, these pathways illustrate how restoring metabolic flexibility in renal and immune cells could, in principle, modify the inflammatory environment that sustains disease activity.

From a systems-level perspective, integration of metabolic and immunologic biomarkers offers opportunities for refined disease monitoring rather than definitive therapeutic guidance. Lipidomic profiling combined with autoantibody measurements has been explored as a means to stratify disease activity and identify residual metabolic stress in patients who otherwise appear to be in immunologic remission [204]. Early proof-of-concept studies using machine-learning approaches suggest that such integrative analyses may improve detection of ongoing subclinical activity, although their clinical utility and generalizability require further validation [205]. These tools are best viewed as exploratory aids that may inform future adaptive treatment strategies rather than as established decision-making frameworks.

In parallel, the concept of disease remission in MN is being reconsidered to include metabolic parameters alongside traditional immunologic and clinical endpoints. Beyond reduction of proteinuria or disappearance of circulating autoantibodies, a more comprehensive remission state has been proposed to encompass normalization of lipid handling and attenuation of oxidative stress, reflecting a broader restoration of cellular homeostasis [206]. Observational data suggest that patients achieving such combined improvements may experience lower relapse rates and more stable long-term renal function, although causality remains unproven [207]. Accordingly, future interventional studies may benefit from incorporating metabolic readouts, such as oxidized lipid burden or markers of cholesterol efflux capacity, as secondary or exploratory endpoints alongside established immunologic measures [208].

Overall, immunometabolic intervention in MN should currently be regarded as a hypothesis-driven extension of existing therapeutic strategies rather than a validated treatment paradigm. By acknowledging the intertwined roles of lipid metabolism and immune activation, this approach offers a framework for understanding disease variability and treatment response. Whether integrated metabolic and immune modulation can deliver durable disease modification beyond conventional immunosuppression remains an open question that will require rigorous evaluation in well-designed clinical trials [141].

Emerging therapeutic perspectives

The expanding recognition of sustained crosstalk between lipid metabolism and immune activation in membranous nephropathy (MN) is reshaping therapeutic perspectives and prompting a gradual transition from purely immune-centered strategies toward more integrated immunometabolic approaches. This evolving view reflects the understanding that immune effector mechanisms and metabolic disturbances are not parallel phenomena but mutually reinforcing layers of pathology that together influence disease persistence, heterogeneity, and long-term outcomes. Consequently, emerging therapeutic concepts aim not only to attenuate pathogenic immune responses but also to restore systemic and cellular metabolic balance, with the goal of achieving more durable remission while simultaneously addressing the heightened cardiometabolic risk associated with MN [75].

Within this conceptual framework, future treatment strategies can be viewed as comprising complementary dimensions rather than discrete or competing options. Continued refinement of conventional immunosuppressive therapies remains central, with increasing emphasis on achieving precise immune modulation while minimizing unintended metabolic disruption. In parallel, growing attention is being directed toward the incorporation of metabolic modulators as adjunctive components of therapy, based on the premise that correcting lipid-driven amplification of immune injury may enhance and stabilize the effects of immunosuppression. These approaches reflect an appreciation that immune silencing in a persistently dysregulated metabolic environment may be insufficient to prevent relapse or progressive injury.

A further emerging dimension involves the application of integrative analytical tools to support individualized treatment strategies. Advances in lipidomics, immunophenotyping, and computational modeling have enabled more detailed characterization of disease states that encompass both immune activity and metabolic stress. The use of data-driven platforms, including artificial intelligence–assisted approaches, has been proposed as a means to synthesize these multidimensional datasets and to inform therapeutic selection and monitoring at the individual patient level. Although these strategies remain largely exploratory, they highlight a shift toward dynamic, patient-specific management paradigms that align therapeutic intensity and modality with evolving immunometabolic profiles. Dual effects of immunosuppressive agents

Conventional immunosuppressive agents remain central to the management of membranous nephropathy (MN), yet their effects extend beyond immune modulation to encompass significant influences on systemic and cellular metabolism. Corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors, including cyclosporine and tacrolimus, are particularly associated with adverse metabolic consequences that may inadvertently intensify renal stress and increase long-term cardiovascular risk [209, 210]. These agents impair insulin sensitivity, stimulate hepatic very-low-density lipoprotein production, and downregulate low-density lipoprotein receptor expression, leading to hypertriglyceridemia and elevated LDL cholesterol levels [27]. At the cellular level, suppression of AMP-activated protein kinase and activation of mTOR signaling by steroids and calcineurin inhibitors shift energy metabolism toward lipogenesis, thereby promoting lipotoxic stress and increasing podocyte vulnerability [75]. Such metabolic disturbances, including dyslipidemia, new-onset diabetes, and hypertension, may silently reinforce the pathogenic cycle of MN, as accumulated oxidized lipids enhance complement activation and amplify immune-mediated injury [209].

In contrast, more recently adopted B-cell–directed therapies, such as rituximab and obinutuzumab, achieve immunologic control primarily through depletion of CD20-positive B cells and are not typically associated with comparable metabolic toxicity. Beyond their effects on autoantibody production, these agents have been associated in clinical observations with improvements in lipid profiles, reductions in circulating oxidized LDL, and attenuation of systemic oxidative stress markers [211]. Such changes are generally interpreted as downstream consequences of effective immune suppression rather than direct metabolic actions, yet they support the concept that metabolic normalization may accompany successful immunologic remission. This pattern suggests that restoration of immune homeostasis can indirectly alleviate metabolic stress, reinforcing the interdependence of immune and lipid pathways in MN.

Emerging immunotherapies further extend this paradigm by targeting long-lived antibody-secreting cells that are relatively resistant to standard CD20-directed approaches. Felzartamab, an anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody currently under clinical investigation for refractory MN, selectively depletes plasma cells and plasmablasts, addressing a persistent source of autoantibody production that may sustain disease activity despite apparent B-cell reconstitution [108]. By more completely suppressing chronic autoantibody generation, such strategies have the potential to attenuate downstream inflammatory signaling, including tumor necrosis factor α and interleukin-6 pathways, which are known to influence hepatic lipid synthesis and cholesterol handling. Transcriptomic analyses in patients achieving immunologic remission after B-cell–depleting therapy have reported normalization of pathways involved in lipid metabolism, including those regulating ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux, providing indirect support for this linkage [181].

Taken together, these observations highlight that immunosuppressive agents exert heterogeneous metabolic effects, ranging from potentially deleterious consequences associated with corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors to metabolically neutral or indirectly restorative effects observed with targeted B-cell and plasma cell therapies. Recognition of these divergent profiles is critical for individualized treatment selection and for minimizing unintended amplification of metabolic stress during long-term disease management. Metabolic modulators as adjunct therapies

Targeting dysregulated metabolic checkpoints has emerged as a plausible adjunctive strategy to conventional immunosuppressive therapy in membranous nephropathy (MN), based on the recognition that metabolic stress, oxidative injury, and inflammation are tightly interconnected components of disease pathophysiology. Rather than functioning as nonspecific supportive measures, metabolic modulators have the potential to influence core cellular processes by rebalancing energy utilization, lipid handling, and redox homeostasis within both podocytes and infiltrating immune cells. Through these mechanisms, such agents may attenuate the biochemical conditions that favor persistent immune activation and tissue injury.

Experimental and translational studies suggest that metabolic interventions can either enhance lipid catabolism or suppress lipid-driven inflammatory signaling, thereby reducing intracellular lipid accumulation and limiting oxidative stress [181]. By improving mitochondrial function, promoting fatty acid oxidation, or facilitating cholesterol efflux, these approaches may restore a more resilient cellular phenotype that is less permissive to immune-mediated damage. In immune cells, similar metabolic recalibration has been associated with dampened proinflammatory signaling and improved regulatory capacity, indicating that metabolic modulation can influence both effector and target compartments of the disease process.

Within this framework, metabolic modulators are best conceptualized as complementary agents that may reinforce the effects of immunotherapy rather than replace immune-directed treatment. By mitigating lipid-induced amplification of inflammatory pathways, these interventions could help stabilize remission and reduce the likelihood of relapse in selected patients. Although most supporting evidence currently derives from preclinical models and associative clinical observations, the integration of metabolic modulation into MN management represents a biologically grounded hypothesis that warrants systematic evaluation in controlled clinical studies.

Established modulators (AMPK activators and PPAR agonists)

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) functions as a central cellular energy sensor that coordinates fatty acid oxidation, glucose utilization, and autophagic flux, thereby maintaining metabolic homeostasis under conditions of stress. In membranous nephropathy (MN), pharmacologic activation of AMPK with agents such as metformin or AICAR has been shown in experimental systems to shift cellular metabolism away from lipid accumulation toward enhanced β-oxidation, suppress sterol regulatory element–binding protein 1–driven lipogenesis, and reduce oxidative stress within podocytes [182]. In parallel, AMPK activation has been associated with attenuation of NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, suggesting a mechanism by which lipid overload–induced sterile inflammation may be mitigated. In preclinical MN models, metformin has also been reported to upregulate autophagy-related proteins, including LC3-II and Beclin-1, and to preserve glomerular filtration barrier integrity independently of its systemic glycemic effects [212].

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors, particularly PPARα and PPARδ, act in a complementary manner to AMPK by transcriptionally regulating pathways involved in lipid catabolism and cholesterol handling. Activation of these nuclear receptors by agents such as fibrates, pioglitazone, or rosiglitazone induces expression of key enzymes required for fatty acid oxidation, including CPT1A and ACOX1, while simultaneously enhancing ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux. Through these effects, PPAR activation reduces intracellular cholesterol accumulation and limits formation of cholesterol crystals, processes that have been implicated in inflammatory activation within renal cells [212]. In addition to their metabolic actions, PPAR agonists have been shown in experimental and translational studies to suppress NFκB- and STAT-dependent cytokine production, including interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor α, and to bias macrophage polarization away from proinflammatory phenotypes within the glomerular environment.

Clinical observations from small studies and pilot cohorts suggest that PPAR agonist therapy is associated with reductions in circulating oxidized LDL levels and improvements in high-density lipoprotein functionality, changes that correlate with decreases in proteinuria and anti-PLA2R antibody titers in selected patients [213]. While these findings remain preliminary and do not establish causality, they illustrate how AMPK activators and PPAR agonists exert convergent metabolic and immunomodulatory effects. Collectively, these agents exemplify established metabolic modulators that may influence both lipid handling and inflammatory signaling, positioning them as candidate adjuncts within an immunometabolic framework for MN rather than as stand-alone disease-modifying therapies.

Novel and hypothesized pathways

Beyond established metabolic modulators, a number of emerging and still largely exploratory therapeutic strategies aim to directly interfere with lipid biosynthesis pathways or to indirectly recalibrate renal energy metabolism. One such target is the sterol regulatory element–binding protein (SREBP) pathway, particularly SREBP2, which functions as a central transcriptional regulator of cholesterol synthesis and intracellular esterification. Experimental studies suggest that aberrant activation of SREBP2 in proteinuric states contributes to intraglomerular cholesterol accumulation and may facilitate downstream inflammatory signaling, including inflammasome activation. In this context, pharmacologic inhibitors of SREBP signaling, such as fatostatin and betulin derivatives, have been shown in experimental nephropathy models to suppress expression of cholesterol biosynthetic enzymes, including HMG-CoA reductase, and to attenuate complement-related injury markers such as C5b-9 deposition [214]. While these findings remain preclinical, they highlight a potential strategy for simultaneously reducing lipid burden and complement amplification within the glomerulus.

Additional interest has focused on agents originally developed for metabolic disorders that exert pleiotropic effects on renal energy handling and inflammatory pathways. Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists, although primarily indicated for glycemic control, have demonstrated nephroprotective properties that may extend beyond diabetic kidney disease. Experimental and early clinical observations suggest that these agents can reduce proximal tubular oxidative stress, enhance mitochondrial biogenesis through activation of PGC-1α–dependent pathways, and suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activity in renal parenchymal and immune cells [213]. In small observational settings, such interventions have been associated with reductions in albuminuria and systemic oxidative stress markers that appear partially independent of glucose lowering, raising the possibility that they may influence lipid–immune interactions relevant to MN.

At present, direct evidence supporting the use of SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists in MN is limited, and their effects on disease-specific immune mechanisms have not been systematically evaluated. Nevertheless, the mechanistic alignment of these agents with key features of MN pathophysiology, including mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammasome activation, positions them as candidates for future investigation within combination therapeutic strategies. As such, these approaches should be regarded as hypothesis-generating avenues that warrant rigorous testing in controlled experimental and clinical studies before their role in MN management can be defined [215].

Precision medicine and AI-assisted profiling

The marked clinical and biological heterogeneity of membranous nephropathy (MN), arising from diversity in target antigens such as PLA₂R, THSD7A, and NELL1, as well as variability in immune activation patterns and metabolic phenotypes, has highlighted the limitations of uniform therapeutic strategies and prompted interest in more individualized approaches. Patients differ substantially with respect to autoantibody subclass distribution, complement pathway engagement, and the extent and nature of lipid dysregulation, suggesting that disease course and treatment response are shaped by distinct immunometabolic trajectories rather than a single dominant mechanism [75]. Within this context, precision medicine approaches seek to integrate immunologic and metabolic dimensions to better characterize disease states and guide therapeutic decisions.

The proposed clinical application of an immunometabolic framework relies on the integration of multi-omics datasets, including lipidomics, proteomics, and single-cell transcriptomics, to delineate patient-specific pathways associated with disease activity and treatment resistance [137]. Advances in lipidomic technologies now permit discrimination among structurally related lipid species and isomers, enabling the generation of detailed metabolic profiles that may reflect underlying cellular stress responses. When such biochemical information is analyzed alongside immune phenotyping data, including autoantibody characteristics and immune cell signatures, a multidimensional dataset emerges that is amenable to computational analysis [187]. These integrated datasets provide an opportunity to explore complex relationships between lipid metabolism and immune activation that may not be readily captured through conventional univariate statistical approaches.

Artificial intelligence and machine-learning methodologies have been proposed as tools to interrogate these high-dimensional datasets, with the aim of identifying patterns or clusters that correspond to shared immunometabolic features. Supervised and unsupervised analytical frameworks have been applied in exploratory studies to group patients into putative endotypes defined by combinations of metabolic and immune parameters rather than by autoantibody status alone. Such classifications remain conceptual and hypothesis-generating, but they suggest that distinct immunometabolic profiles may be associated with differential responses to immunosuppressive or metabolic interventions [216]. For example, a profile characterized by prominent lipid biosynthetic signatures and oxidized lipid burden may, in theory, respond differently to adjunct metabolic modulation than a profile dominated by robust complement activation and specific immunoglobulin subclasses. These associations, however, require validation in prospective clinical studies before they can be translated into treatment algorithms.

Beyond therapeutic selection, AI-assisted profiling has also been explored as a means of longitudinal disease monitoring. Integrative models that track changes in lipidomic and immunologic parameters over time may help identify residual metabolic stress or subclinical immune activity during apparent remission, potentially informing closer surveillance or adjustment of therapy [217]. At present, such applications should be viewed as investigational tools that complement, rather than replace, established clinical and serologic assessments. Nonetheless, the combination of high-resolution lipid profiling with computational analytics represents a shift toward conceptualizing MN as a spectrum of individualized immunometabolic states rather than a uniform autoimmune condition. Whether this approach will yield clinically actionable stratification and improved outcomes remains to be determined through systematic evaluation and validation in larger patient cohorts [218].

Barriers to implementation

Despite the conceptual appeal of immunometabolic strategies in membranous nephropathy (MN), several substantial barriers currently limit their translation from experimental and theoretical frameworks into routine clinical practice. A primary challenge lies in the practical integration of multi-omic profiling approaches, including lipidomics, metabolomics, and detailed immune phenotyping. These technologies remain constrained by high costs, technical complexity, and limited reproducibility across laboratories [219]. High-throughput platforms such as LC–MS/MS and NMR-based metabolomics require specialized infrastructure, rigorous sample handling, and standardized analytical pipelines to ensure data reliability. The lack of universally accepted protocols for normalization, batch correction, and cross-platform harmonization has further impeded their incorporation into standard nephrology workflows [48]. As a result, most clinical settings continue to rely on conventional lipid panels, which, while accessible, provide only coarse information and fail to capture the specific lipid species and oxidative modifications implicated in immune activation and therapeutic resistance.

A second major obstacle is the limited clinical evidence supporting metabolic modulation as a therapeutic adjunct in MN. Although preclinical and small translational studies suggest that agents such as AMPK activators and PPAR agonists may exert anti-inflammatory and lipid-corrective effects, robust multicenter clinical trials demonstrating their added benefit in combination with established immunosuppressive regimens are lacking [64]. Existing studies are often limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous inclusion criteria, and relatively short follow-up periods, reducing their ability to assess long-term renal outcomes and relapse prevention [220]. Moreover, most clinical trial designs have not incorporated integrated endpoints capable of capturing concurrent changes in proteinuria, immune activity, and lipid-mediated toxicity, making it difficult to rigorously evaluate dual immunometabolic effects. Regulatory frameworks similarly lag behind scientific developments, as current guidelines provide limited direction for the assessment and approval of combination strategies that target both immune and metabolic pathways.

Economic and logistical considerations further complicate implementation. Comprehensive lipidomic analyses remain costly, with per-sample expenses that may be prohibitive for routine use, particularly when bioinformatic processing and data storage are included [221]. The deployment of computational infrastructure required for advanced data integration and AI-assisted analysis introduces additional financial burdens and raises concerns related to data security and regulatory compliance, especially in healthcare systems governed by strict data-protection policies [16]. The absence of standardized biorepositories, shared reference datasets, and interoperable data platforms also limits large-scale validation and cross-institutional collaboration, which are essential for establishing clinical robustness.

Beyond technical and economic barriers, a translational gap persists between data generation and clinical decision-making. Omics-derived biomarkers and AI-based predictive models often lack transparency and interpretability, reducing clinician confidence and limiting their adoption in practice [222]. Many nephrologists have limited training in computational analytics, and without user-friendly interfaces or validated clinical decision support systems, complex immunometabolic signatures remain difficult to translate into actionable treatment choices. Addressing this gap will require coordinated educational initiatives and sustained collaboration among clinicians, bioinformaticians, and systems biologists to ensure that analytical outputs are clinically meaningful and operationally feasible [223].

Overall, the transition of immunometabolic precision medicine in MN from concept to clinical reality will depend on parallel advances in methodological standardization, clinical trial design, regulatory adaptation, and workforce training. Only through coordinated system-level efforts, coupled with prospective validation of combined immunometabolic interventions, can these approaches be meaningfully integrated into patient care [1].

Future directions

Despite considerable advances in the identification of antigenic drivers and the characterization of reciprocal interactions between lipid metabolism and immune regulation in membranous nephropathy (MN), important knowledge gaps remain that limit translation of these insights into clinically actionable strategies. Accumulating evidence supports the view of MN as an immunometabolic disorder in which immune dysregulation and lipid perturbations mutually reinforce podocyte injury, yet the temporal hierarchy and causal weight of these processes are incompletely defined. Addressing these uncertainties will require a shift from predominantly descriptive analyses toward integrated, high-resolution approaches capable of linking molecular dynamics with clinical trajectories [94].

Future research efforts should prioritize longitudinal study designs that capture immunologic and metabolic parameters across the full disease continuum, encompassing onset, active flares, partial remission, and sustained remission. Such cohorts, when coupled with repeated sampling, would allow assessment of how changes in autoantibody profiles, complement activity, lipid species, and metabolic stress markers evolve over time and relate to subsequent clinical outcomes [224]. In parallel, functional validation using experimental models and ex vivo human systems will be necessary to test causality and to distinguish primary drivers from secondary adaptations within the immunometabolic network. By integrating longitudinal clinical data with mechanistic experimentation, future studies can better delineate the sequence of events that governs disease initiation, persistence, and resolution, thereby informing more precise and durable therapeutic strategies in MN.

Prioritizing longitudinal studies and actionable targets

The effective translation of mechanistic insights into clinical benefit in membranous nephropathy (MN) will depend on the development of robust longitudinal cohort studies that capture the temporal dynamics of immunometabolic interactions. Cross-sectional analyses, while informative, provide only static representations of a disease process that evolves over time. In contrast, time-resolved investigation is required to delineate how autoantibody production, complement activation, and lipid metabolic disturbances interact and fluctuate across different disease states, including initial onset, active flares, remission, and relapse [159]. Longitudinal monitoring is therefore essential to define the sequence and interdependence of these processes and to distinguish early drivers from downstream consequences.

Such studies should incorporate serial assessment of disease-specific autoantibodies, including anti-PLA₂R and anti-THSD7A titers, alongside comprehensive lipidomic, metabolomic, and inflammatory biomarker profiling throughout the disease course [225]. Integrating these datasets over time would enable construction of an immunometabolic trajectory that captures transitional phases preceding clinically apparent proteinuria or decline in glomerular filtration. Identifying these preclinical inflection points could improve understanding of disease kinetics and inform earlier, more targeted intervention strategies.

Within this temporal framework, the identification of actionable molecular targets represents a key objective. Emerging evidence suggests that specific lipid signatures, such as defined oxidized LDL epitopes or alterations in ceramide subclass composition, may precede immunologic reactivation and thus serve as early indicators of disease flare. Systematic application of targeted and untargeted lipidomic approaches will be necessary to isolate these lipid species and to evaluate their predictive value in relation to established markers such as autoantibody titers and complement activation indices [85]. In parallel, detailed characterization of metabolic checkpoints within podocytes and immune cells, including pathways governing fatty acid oxidation, cholesterol biosynthesis mediated by sterol regulatory element–binding proteins, and mitochondrial redox balance, will help identify processes that are dynamically and potentially reversibly dysregulated during active disease.

Equally important is the validation of candidate lipid and metabolic biomarkers against established immunologic measures of MN activity, including immunoglobulin subclass distribution, complement activation products such as C3 fragments and C5b-9, and inflammatory cytokine signatures. Integration of these parameters into composite biomarker indices may improve risk stratification and provide more sensitive measures of disease trajectory than any single marker alone. Such validated frameworks could also serve as surrogate endpoints in future clinical trials, enabling earlier assessment of therapeutic response and disease stabilization before irreversible structural damage occurs. In this context, well-designed longitudinal studies represent a critical bridge between immunometabolic discovery and clinically meaningful therapeutic translation in MN.

Multi-omics approaches and functional validation

The complexity of immunometabolic dysregulation in membranous nephropathy (MN) necessitates analytical strategies that extend beyond isolated, single-target investigations. Given the multilayered nature of lipid–immune interactions, future translational progress will depend on integrative multi-omics approaches that combine lipidomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data into a unified systems-level framework [226]. Such integration is required to capture the coordinated molecular networks that govern immune activation, metabolic stress, and podocyte injury with sufficient resolution to inform therapeutic development.

Lipidomics represents a foundational component of this approach, enabling quantitative profiling of a wide array of lipid species that differ in acyl-chain length, saturation, and oxidative modification. When lipidomic datasets are aligned with single-cell RNA sequencing analyses, it becomes possible to associate specific lipid perturbations with distinct transcriptional states of podocytes and infiltrating immune populations, including T cells and B cells, during different phases of disease activity [142]. This integration can resolve cell-specific metabolic phenotypes, such as enhanced sterol regulatory element–binding protein 2–driven lipogenesis in podocytes or impaired peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α signaling in regulatory T cells, thereby clarifying the cellular sources of lipotoxic and inflammatory signaling.

Complementary spatial transcriptomic and proteomic imaging technologies further add contextual information by defining how these metabolic and immune alterations are distributed within the glomerular microenvironment. Mapping the spatial relationships between resident glomerular cells, immune infiltrates, and sites of complement deposition can elucidate intercellular communication networks that are not apparent from bulk or dissociated-cell analyses alone. Integration of these multilayered datasets through machine-learning algorithms and network modeling approaches provides a means to identify key regulatory hubs and lipid nodes that coordinate immune and metabolic signaling. Computational analyses have been proposed to prioritize axes such as sterol regulatory element–binding protein 2–NLRP3 or ABCA1–PPARγ as candidate drivers of disease activity, although these predictions remain to be validated experimentally [227].

Systematic functional validation is essential to bridge the gap between correlation and causation. Candidate pathways identified through computational inference should be interrogated across multiple experimental platforms, including immortalized podocyte lines, primary immune cell cultures, genetically modified animal models, and human kidney organoids. Targeted loss- and gain-of-function studies involving pathways such as ABCA1, SREBP2, NLRP3, or complement factor H, conducted under controlled lipid conditions, can determine whether modulation of specific metabolic nodes alters immune activation, complement amplification, or cellular injury. Incorporation of metabolomic flux analyses and live-cell imaging techniques will further allow quantitative assessment of how such interventions influence energy utilization, lipid trafficking, and cytokine secretion dynamics. Through the combined application of integrative omics, computational modeling, and rigorous experimental validation, MN research can progress from descriptive characterization toward mechanistically grounded, intervention-ready understanding [228, 229].

Targeting immunometabolism for personalized therapy in MN

The long-term objective of integrated research in membranous nephropathy (MN) is to move beyond uniform immunosuppressive strategies toward a personalized immunometabolic therapeutic framework that simultaneously addresses immune dysregulation and lipid homeostasis [230]. This perspective is grounded in the recognition that autoimmunity and lipotoxic stress represent interconnected components of a single pathogenic circuit rather than independent processes. Accordingly, future therapeutic development is increasingly oriented toward coordinated modulation of immune activity and metabolic state, with the aim of achieving more stable and durable disease control than is typically observed with immune-centric interventions alone.

One proposed axis of personalization focuses on correcting maladaptive metabolic reprogramming within renal parenchymal cells and immune compartments. Advanced profiling studies have indicated that podocytes and autoreactive B cells during active MN frequently display a shift toward lipogenic and energetically stressed states, characterized by increased sterol regulatory element–binding protein 2 activity and reduced fatty acid oxidation capacity. Experimental data suggest that pharmacologic activation of AMP-activated protein kinase or peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α, as well as inhibition of excessive SREBP2 signaling, can improve cellular energy efficiency and attenuate downstream inflammatory pathways, including NLRP3 inflammasome priming [231]. When combined with established immune-directed therapies, such as B-cell depletion or complement inhibition, such metabolic modulation may facilitate more coordinated resolution of immune and metabolic stress rather than isolated suppression of autoreactivity.

A complementary dimension of personalized immunometabolic therapy involves consideration of nutritional and microbiome-related influences on lipid metabolism and immune balance. Gut-derived metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate and propionate, have been shown in experimental systems to influence regulatory T-cell stability, epithelial barrier integrity, and lipid-sensing nuclear receptor signaling. Alterations in gut microbial composition that favor proinflammatory or endotoxin-producing taxa may contribute to systemic inflammation and metabolic imbalance, potentially modulating disease expression in MN [232]. Although direct evidence in MN remains limited, these observations suggest that dietary interventions or microbiome-targeted strategies could, in principle, serve as adjunctive approaches to support immune regulation and metabolic stability.

Implementation of such personalized strategies will require adaptive clinical trial designs that integrate molecular profiling with therapeutic decision-making. The use of omics-defined biomarkers encompassing immune, lipidomic, and, where relevant, microbial parameters may enable stratification of patients according to dominant pathogenic features and facilitate more tailored treatment allocation. Iterative feedback between molecular readouts and clinical outcomes could support dynamic adjustment of therapy intensity or combination, potentially aided by computational decision-support tools. At present, these approaches remain investigational and hypothesis-driven, but they outline a trajectory toward individualized management in which treatment is aligned with a patient’s evolving immunometabolic profile rather than fixed disease categories. Within this framework, the goal is not the assertion of definitive cure, but the pursuit of sustained immunologic quiescence accompanied by restoration of metabolic balance, thereby reducing relapse risk and preserving long-term renal function in MN [233].

Current limitations of evidence

Despite the conceptual coherence and mechanistic support for an immunometabolic framework in membranous nephropathy (MN), important limitations in the current evidence base constrain its translation into routine clinical practice. A major limitation is the paucity of long-term, longitudinal lipidomic data derived from well-characterized MN cohorts. Most available studies rely on cross-sectional sampling or short follow-up periods, which limits the ability to validate candidate lipid and metabolic biomarkers as reliable early predictors of relapse, treatment response, or long-term renal outcomes. Without sustained temporal data, it remains difficult to distinguish causal metabolic drivers from secondary or adaptive changes accompanying disease activity.

In addition, although causal links between lipid dysregulation and immune activation are convincingly demonstrated in cellular systems and animal models, corresponding evidence in humans remains largely indirect or associative. Critically, there is a shortage of large, randomized, controlled clinical trials specifically designed to evaluate the added benefit of combining immunosuppressive therapies, such as B-cell–directed agents, with targeted metabolic modulators, including PPAR agonists or AMPK activators. Existing clinical studies are often underpowered, heterogeneous in design, or focused on single endpoints, limiting their capacity to assess synergistic immunometabolic effects or durable disease modification.

This disconnect between strong mechanistic rationale and limited clinical validation represents a key barrier to adoption of immunometabolic strategies as standard care in MN. Addressing this gap will require prospective, adequately powered trials with integrated immunologic and metabolic endpoints, as well as standardized approaches to biomarker collection and analysis. Only through such rigorously designed studies can the clinical relevance of immunometabolic modulation be definitively established and translated into evidence-based therapeutic frameworks.

Conclusion

Membranous nephropathy (MN), historically conceptualized as an organ-specific autoimmune glomerulopathy driven predominantly by autoantibodies against podocyte antigens such as PLA2R and THSD7A, is increasingly recognized as a disease in which immune dysregulation and metabolic disturbance are tightly interconnected [234]. Evidence synthesized in this review indicates that lipid abnormalities are not simply secondary consequences of nephrotic protein loss but are closely interwoven with immune-mediated injury pathways. Immune complex deposition and complement activation at the podocyte surface are accompanied by alterations in lipid handling and oxidative stress, while impaired lipid efflux and lipid oxidation may, in turn, amplify local inflammatory signaling, including inflammasome activation and complement-mediated injury [205]. Together, these observations support a bidirectional pathogenic loop in which immune and metabolic processes reinforce one another and contribute to progressive glomerular dysfunction.

Mapping this lipid–immune crosstalk highlights several mechanistic themes with potential translational relevance. First, cholesterol-enriched membrane microdomains appear to facilitate spatial organization of podocyte antigens and immune complexes, thereby influencing the efficiency of complement activation. Second, accumulation of oxidized lipid species, particularly oxidized low-density lipoprotein, may act as inflammatory amplifiers that prolong complement activity and cellular stress independently of ongoing autoantibody production. Third, disruption of cholesterol efflux and lipid catabolic pathways, involving regulators such as ABCA1, PPARα, and SREBP2, is associated with metabolic reprogramming of both podocytes and immune cells toward proinflammatory states [12]. Collectively, these mechanisms position lipid metabolism as both a modifier of immune effector function and a downstream target affected by immune-mediated injury in MN.

From a translational perspective, this framework argues for consideration of therapeutic strategies that address metabolic stress alongside immune suppression, rather than relying on immunomodulation alone. Adjunctive approaches targeting metabolic checkpoints, including PPAR agonists, AMP-activated protein kinase activators, or inhibitors of excessive lipid biosynthesis, have shown anti-inflammatory and renoprotective effects in experimental settings and small clinical studies, although their efficacy and safety in MN require validation in adequately powered trials [235]. Similarly, emerging immune-directed therapies, such as agents targeting long-lived plasma cells, may achieve more durable disease control when combined with interventions that stabilize lipid handling and cellular energy balance, potentially attenuating cycles of inflammation and lipotoxicity.

The implications of an immunometabolic perspective extend beyond MN, as analogous interactions between lipid dysregulation, inflammasome activation, and immune cell metabolic reprogramming have been described in other immune-mediated glomerular diseases, including lupus nephritis and IgA nephropathy [99, 155]. Looking ahead, integration of longitudinal multi-omics profiling with computational analyses may facilitate identification of immunometabolic patterns associated with disease activity and treatment response. While such approaches remain investigational, they offer a conceptual basis for refining patient stratification and for designing adaptive clinical studies that test combined immune and metabolic interventions. In this context, the immunometabolic framework does not redefine MN management as established practice but provides a structured lens through which future research may move toward more precise, preventive, and individualized therapeutic strategies [236, 237].
