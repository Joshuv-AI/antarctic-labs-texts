Integrative meta-analysis and experimental validation reveal the oncogenic role of SACS and its therapeutic targeting potential in colorectal cancer
Authors: Chunmei Chen, Jun Li, Beibei Wen, Wenwei Dou, Haisheng Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to identify core genes closely associated with the diagnosis and prognosis of colorectal cancer (CRC) using transcriptome-based meta-analysis approach and machine learning algorithms. Nine CRC datasets from the GEO database were integrated for differential gene expression analysis and WGCNA to identify key genes. Ninety-six combinations of machine learning algorithms were employed to further refine the selection of core genes and validate their diagnostic performance. Functional enrichment, molecular pathways, and associations with the immune microenvironment of core genes were analyzed using GSEA, CIBERSORT, and ssGSEA. Drug sensitivity predictions were performed to evaluate the impact of core genes on CRC drug response, and molecular docking simulations were used to identify candidate compounds targeting the core genes. A total of 26 core genes were identified, among which the high expression of the SACS gene was significantly associated with poor prognosis, advanced stage, and specific pathological subtypes in CRC patients. GSEA revealed that high SACS expression prominently activates cell cycle regulatory pathways and immune pathways while suppressing metabolic pathways. Furthermore, in vitro experiments demonstrated that SACS is highly expressed in CRC cells and that its knockdown significantly inhibits CRC cell proliferation, suggesting its functional role in tumor growth. Immune analysis showed that high SACS expression was positively correlated with activated NK cells but negatively correlated with Tregs and resting NK cells. Drug sensitivity analysis indicated that high SACS expression reduces sensitivity to oxaliplatin. Molecular docking identified coumestrol and quercetin as potential compounds targeting SACS. SACS promotes CRC progression by regulating cell cycle pathways, the immune microenvironment, and metabolic pathways. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent and fatal malignancies globally, ranking among the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality1. Although advancements in surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy have significantly improved the outcomes for some patients with CRC, the inherent heterogeneity and complex molecular mechanisms of the disease continue to pose challenges. Many patients experience suboptimal treatment efficacy and a high risk of recurrence2,3. Therefore, identifying key driver genes and associated molecular pathways is critical to understanding CRC pathogenesis and progression.

Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing and machine learning technologies have substantially enhanced our understanding of CRC molecular mechanisms. Numerous genes and signalling pathways have been implicated in CRC, including the activation of pathways associated with E2F target genes, G2/M checkpoints, and MYC target genes, which are involved in cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and immune responses4–6. In addition, abnormal metabolic reprogramming has been widely recognised as a hallmark of CRC7. These findings not only provide a molecular foundation for CRC pathogenesis and progression but also offer valuable insights for further elucidation of its underlying mechanisms. Moreover, the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) has been shown to play a pivotal role in CRC initiation and progression. Mechanisms such as the enrichment of regulatory T cells (Tregs), dysfunction of antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and inhibition of natural killer (NK) cells contribute to immune evasion in CRC8,9. However, current therapeutic approaches targeting the immune microenvironment exhibit considerable variability in efficacy among patients, underscoring the importance of identifying TIME-associated key genes to guide treatment strategies.

In this context, the present study aimed to systematically examine the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC. Transcriptomic data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database were integrated and analysed, with CRC-associated key genes identified using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and differential gene expression analysis. Machine learning models were used to uncover core genes closely associated with CRC diagnosis and prognosis. Subsequently, a gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to investigate the effects of core gene expression on molecular pathways, while CIBERSORT and single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) were used to assess the relationships between the core genes, tumor immune infiltration, and immune regulatory functions. Furthermore, drug sensitivity prediction models were used to evaluate the impacts of the core gene expressions on the response to various anti-CRC drugs, and molecular docking simulations were conducted to identify potential traditional Chinese medicine compounds that could be used to target these core genes. To further validate the biological relevance of the identified core genes, in vitro experiments were conducted using human normal colonic epithelial cell line NCM460 and the CRC cell line SW480. The mRNA expression levels of selected genes were detected via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), and their functional roles in regulating CRC cell proliferation were assessed through siRNA-mediated knockdown and Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays. Through these comprehensive analyses, this study seeks to systematically elucidate the mechanisms of CRC initiation, progression, and therapy, thereby providing novel perspectives and actionable strategies for advancing personalised medicine in CRC.

Methods

Dataset selection and normalisation

Seven CRC-related datasets (GSE15960, GSE21510, GSE32323, GSE33113, GSE37364, GSE62932, and GSE110224) based on the GPL570 platform were retrieved from the GEO database. Gene expression matrices were normalised and batch effects were corrected to ensure data consistency. Normalisation was performed using the normalizeBetweenArrays function in the limma package (version 3.58.1, https://bioconductor.org/packages/limma). Initially, unlogged expression data were log2-transformed, followed by normalisation of expression levels across samples. Subsequently, the datasets were divided into a training set (GSE21510, GSE32323, GSE62932, and GSE110224) and a testing set (GSE15960, GSE33113, and GSE37364). Batch effects in the training set were corrected using the ComBat algorithm in the sva package (version 3.50.0, https://bioconductor.org/packages/sva), minimising systematic biases while preserving genuine biological variability. The effectiveness of the batch effect correction was assessed using box plots and principal component analysis (PCA).

Differential gene expression analysis

To identify differences in gene expression levels between the tumor and normal groups, a differential expression analysis was performed on the training set data using the limma package. This involved linear modelling and Bayesian statistics to calculate log2 fold changes (logFC) and adjusted p values (adj.P.Val) for each gene. Significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those that meet the criteria of |logFC| > 1 and adj.P.Val < 0.05. A volcano plot was generated to visualise DEG distributions, with significantly upregulated and downregulated genes highlighted in orange and green, respectively, and non-significant genes highlighted in grey.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

A WGCNA was performed to identify gene modules that exhibited coordinated expressions and to determine their association with clinical traits. Genes with the highest variance (top 25%) in the training set were selected for network construction. Outlier samples were detected via clustering, and the optimal soft-threshold power was determined to build a scale-free network. A weighted adjacency matrix was converted to a topological overlap matrix to calculate network connectivity. Gene clustering was performed using hierarchical clustering, and modules were identified using the Dynamic TreeCut algorithm, followed by merging highly similar modules. Module-trait correlations were evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients and visualised in a heatmap. Scatterplots of module membership and gene significance were used to identify key genes within clinically relevant modules.

Functional enrichment and pathway analyses

To elucidate the biological functions of CRC-associated DEGs, genes from the most relevant WGCNA modules were intersected with DEGs to obtain a CRC-relevant gene set. A Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (including biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.10.1, https://bioconductor.org/packages/clusterProfiler). Terms and pathways with p.adjust values < 0.05 were considered significant. In addition, a comprehensive pathway analysis was performed using the Metascape platform (https://metascape.org), providing an integrated overview of the biological processes and pathways in which the intersecting genes were involved.

Core gene selection and diagnostic performance validation

Core genes strongly associated with CRC were identified using 96 combinations of machine learning algorithms. The performance of each model was evaluated on the basis of the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) in both the training and testing sets. Models with fewer than five selected features were excluded, and the model with the highest mean AUC was identified as the optimal model. The expressions of the selected core genes were validated by differential expression analysis, and a Pearson correlation analysis was performed to construct a co-expression matrix. To assess the diagnostic performance of the individual core genes, ROC curves were plotted, and the AUC for each gene was calculated.

Prognostic value assessment and Meta-analysis validation

To evaluate the prognostic significance of the core genes, the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) was queried to identify genes significantly associated with overall survival (OS). Candidate genes were selected by intersecting prognostic genes with upregulated core genes. A Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database to assess the prognostic relevance of the candidate genes. A meta-analysis was performed using seven datasets (GSE15781, GSE20842, GSE28000, GSE41011, GSE44076, GSE47063, and GSE87211) comprising 426 CRC and 365 normal samples to validate the differential expressions of the candidate genes. The relationships between the candidate gene expressions and clinical features were further examined using TCGA clinical data. Box plots were used to display expression differences, and Wilcoxon tests were applied to assess statistical significance.

Candidate gene expression analysis and functional validation

To validate the expression characteristics of the candidate gene in CRC and its potential regulatory effects on cellular functions, in vitro experiments were conducted using the human normal colonic epithelial cell line NCM460 and the CRC cell line SW480. Both cell lines were purchased from Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Total RNA was extracted from each cell line, and the mRNA expression level of the candidate gene was measured using qRT-PCR. Subsequently, siRNA was employed to knock down the candidate gene in SW480 cells, with a negative control group (NC) included. The knockdown efficiency was validated via qRT-PCR. The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR and the siRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Tables S1. To assess the impact of the candidate gene on CRC cell proliferation, CCK-8 assays were performed at 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection. All experiments were independently repeated three times. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.0), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Gene set enrichment analysis

To examine the impacts of the candidate gene expressions on molecular pathways in CRC, a GSEA was performed on the training set samples. The CRC samples were divided into high- and low-expression groups according to the median expression level of the candidate genes. A ranked gene list was generated on the basis of logFC, and a GSEA was performed using the Hallmark pathway gene set (h.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt), KEGG pathway gene set (c2.cp.kegg.Hs.symbols.gmt), and GO gene set (c5.go.Hs.symbols.gmt). Pathways with p values < 0.05 were deemed significant, and enrichment plots for the top five pathways were generated to illustrate regulatory patterns.

Immune infiltration and function analysis

The relationships between the candidate gene expressions and TIME were assessed using CIBERSORT to quantify immune cell infiltration. Differences in immune infiltration between the CRC and normal samples were evaluated, and a Spearman correlation analysis was performed to determine the associations between the candidate gene expressions and the immune cell proportions in CRC samples. In addition, to investigate the potential regulatory effects of the candidate gene expressions on immune function, an ssGSEA was performed using an immune-related gene set (immune.gmt) to analyse the CRC samples. Immune function enrichment scores were calculated, and Wilcoxon tests were used to compare immune function activity between the high- and low-expression groups.

Drug sensitivity prediction and molecular docking simulation

The potential therapeutic relevance of the candidate genes was evaluated through drug sensitivity prediction and molecular docking simulations. The oncoPredict package (version 1.2, https://github.com/HuangLabUMN/oncoPredict) was used to predict the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of anticancer drugs based on TCGA expression data. Differences in drug sensitivity between the high- and low-expression groups were assessed using Wilcoxon tests. Potential therapeutic compounds for targeting the candidate genes were identified using HERB (http://herb.ac.cn/), a high-throughput experiment- and reference-guided database of traditional Chinese medicine. Three-dimensional structures of the compounds and gene-encoded proteins were retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). Energy optimisation of the candidate compounds was performed using ChemBio3D Ultra 14.0, and molecular structures were verified with PyMOL (version 2.4.0). Molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.6) to assess the interactions between the candidate compounds and the active sites of the proteins encoded by the candidate genes. Binding energies and docking results were analysed to evaluate the binding capacity of the candidate compounds.

Results

Standardisation and batch effect correction enhanced data consistency

Normalisation and batch effect correction significantly improved the consistency of the gene expression data. Prior to correction, the box plot revealed substantial differences in expression distributions across the datasets (Fig. 1A). Post-correction, expression medians, and ranges were aligned across the datasets (Fig. 1B). The PCA further validated the effectiveness of batch effect correction. Before correction, the samples from different datasets clustered distinctly in the principal component space (Fig. 1C). After correction, the sample distributions in the principal component space became more homogeneous, with samples no longer clustering by batch (Fig. 1D).

Expression characteristics of the DEGs between the CRC and normal samples

A volcano plot illustrated the distributions of significant DEGs, with upregulated genes concentrated on the right and downregulated genes concentrated on the left (Fig. 1E). A total of 989 DEGs were identified, comprising 480 significantly upregulated genes and 509 significantly downregulated genes in the CRC samples. A heatmap of the top 50 upregulated and downregulated DEGs highlighted their distinct expression patterns across the samples (Fig. 1F). These findings confirmed the substantial differences in the gene expressions between the tumor and normal groups.

Identification of CRC-related modules using a WGCNA

A scale-free gene co-expression network was constructed using a WGCNA, with the optimal soft threshold power determined as 6 (Fig. 1H). Multiple modules were identified using a dynamic tree-cutting algorithm. The clustering results demonstrated reasonable module delineation, with significant independence among the module eigengenes (Figs. 1G and 2A, B). A correlation analysis revealed that the black and blue modules were significantly associated with CRC (black module: r = 0.46, p = 4e–16; blue module: r = − 0.42, p = 9e-14), with the black module showing the highest correlation (Fig. 2C). Scatterplots further confirmed that the genes within the black and blue modules exhibited high module membership and gene significance values, which suggests their potential roles in CRC progression (Fig. 2D, E). Other modules showed weaker correlations with CRC, indicating limited biological relevance (Fig. 2C, F–I).

Key pathways and biological functions of CRC-related genes

The intersection of the genes from the black module with DEGs resulted in 485 CRC-related genes (Fig. 3A). Functional enrichment analysis via GO, KEGG, and Metascape highlighted significant biological processes and pathways. The GO analysis revealed that these genes were primarily involved in cell cycle regulation, DNA replication and repair, chromatin remodelling, stress response signalling, ribosome biogenesis, and transcriptional regulation (Fig. 3B, C). The KEGG pathway analysis identified key pathways such as cell cycle, DNA replication, and the p53 signalling pathway, all closely related to cancer progression (Fig. 3D). Functional annotations from Metascape further corroborated these findings, indicating significant involvement in cell cycle regulation, DNA metabolism, cell proliferation, and core cancer-related pathways (Fig. 3E).

Selection of core genes and their diagnostic value in CRC

The evaluation of 96 machine learning model combinations identified the lassoStep[backward] + GBM model as the optimal classifier, with 0.996 as the highest mean AUC for both the training and testing sets (Fig. 4A, B). Using this model, 26 core genes were identified from the 485 CRC-related DEGs. Validation in the testing set confirmed the high diagnostic accuracy of the lassoStep[backward] + GBM model, as shown by its ROC curves (Fig. 4C–E). The differential expression analysis results indicated that 16 core genes were significantly upregulated and 10 were significantly downregulated in the CRC samples (Fig. 4F, G). The correlation analysis revealed co-expression relationships among most core genes, which suggests their coordinated involvement in CRC progression (Fig. 4H). The independent ROC analysis for the 26 core genes demonstrated their strong diagnostic capability, with significant AUC values supporting their standalone diagnostic utility in CRC (Fig. 5A).

Associations of the SACS gene with CRC prognosis, clinical features, and pathological subtypes

Among the 16 upregulated core genes, SACS was identified as a key gene associated with OS (Fig. 5B). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis using TCGA data revealed that high SACS expression levels were significantly associated with poorer OS in CRC patients, suggesting the potential of the gene as a prognostic biomarker (Fig. 5C). A meta-analysis of the seven independent datasets validated the consistent upregulation of SACS in the CRC samples (Fig. 5D). The clinical correlation analysis from TCGA indicated that the SACS expression level was significantly higher in the T3 and T4 stages than in the T2 stage (Fig. 5E) and in Stage II than in Stage I CRC (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, the SACS expression level was notably higher in colon mucinous adenocarcinoma than in other pathological subtypes, including colon adenocarcinoma, rectal adenocarcinoma, and rectal mucinous adenocarcinoma (Fig. 5G). These findings suggest that SACS expression is closely related to CRC progression and its pathological subtype, highlighting its potential as a molecular marker for diagnosis and staging.

SACS is upregulated in CRC cells and regulates cell proliferation

To investigate the expression of SACS in CRC cells, qRT-PCR was first performed to detect its mRNA levels in the normal colonic epithelial cell line NCM460 and the CRC cell line SW480. The results indicated that SACS expression was significantly upregulated in SW480 cells (Fig. 5H, ****p < 0.0001), suggesting a high expression status in CRC cells. Subsequently, siRNA targeting SACS was transfected into SW480 cells. qRT-PCR validation demonstrated that SACS mRNA expression was significantly decreased in the siSACS group compared to the negative control group (Fig. 5I, *p < 0.05), confirming effective gene knockdown. To further assess the role of SACS in regulating cellular behavior, CCK-8 assays were conducted at 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection to evaluate cell proliferation. The results showed that the proliferation of cells in the siSACS group was significantly lower than that in the siNC group at all three time points, with more pronounced differences observed at 48 and 72 h (Fig. 5J, *p < 0.05, ****p < 0.0001). These findings indicate that SACS knockdown effectively inhibits the proliferative activity of CRC cells. In summary, the candidate gene is upregulated in CRC cells, and its expression level significantly influences tumor cell proliferation, suggesting that SACS may function as a potential oncogenic factor in CRC.

Impact of SACS expression on key pathway activities

The GSEA revealed that high SACS expression levels significantly influenced multiple molecular pathways. The Hallmark gene set analysis revealed activation of pathways related to cell cycle regulation, tumor cell proliferation, and immune responses, including E2F targets, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, G2/M checkpoint, interferon-gamma response, and MYC targets (Fig. 6A, B). Conversely, metabolic pathways such as bile acid, fatty acid, and xenobiotic metabolisms were significantly suppressed. In the GO gene set analysis, activation of key biological processes was identified, including DNA replication and chromatid segregation, while metabolic processes were suppressed (Fig. 6C, D). The KEGG gene set analysis further highlighted the activation of cell cycle, complement and coagulation cascades, cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, ECM-receptor interactions, and systemic lupus erythematosus pathways, alongside the suppression of axon guidance, cytochrome P450 drug metabolism, and PPAR signalling pathways (Fig. 6E, F). Collectively, these findings suggest that SACS expression promotes CRC progression by enhancing the cell cycle and immune regulatory pathways while suppressing metabolic pathways.

Correlation between SACS expression and immune infiltration patterns

The CIBERSORT analysis revealed significant differences in immune infiltration between the CRC and normal samples. In the CRC samples, M1 macrophages and Tregs were more prevalent, whereas activated mast cells and monocytes were reduced (Fig. 6G–H). SACS expression positively correlated with activated NK and CD4 + memory T cells but negatively correlated with Tregs and resting NK cells (Fig. 6I). The immune function analysis using ssGSEA demonstrated that high SACS expression levels were associated with the activation of immune functions such as dendritic cell activation, APC co-stimulation, C-C chemokine receptor signalling, immune checkpoints, cytolytic activity, para-inflammation, T cell co-inhibition, and Th1/Th2 cell responses, while suppressing T helper cell activity (Fig. 6K). These findings indicate that SACS expression modulates immune infiltration and function.

Drug sensitivity and identification of targeting compounds for SACS

The drug sensitivity analysis revealed that high SACS expression levels were associated with increased IC50 values for oxaliplatin, which suggests reduced drug sensitivity in CRC (Fig. 6J). This highlights SACS expression as a potential biomarker for predicting chemotherapy responses and guiding personalised treatment strategies. Through HERB database screening and molecular docking simulations, coumestrol and quercetin were identified as potential compounds for targeting the SACS gene. The docking results indicated a strong binding affinity between these compounds and SACS protein active sites, with stable complexes formed through hydrogen-bonding interactions (Fig. 6L–O; Table 1). These findings provide a basis for investigating the therapeutic potential of traditional Chinese medicine compounds in targeting SACS.

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the potential role and clinical significance of the SACS gene in CRC through comprehensive transcriptomic data integration and bioinformatics analysis. The findings demonstrate that SACS expression is significantly upregulated in CRC tissues, closely associated with poor prognosis, and involved in the dysregulation of critical molecular pathways. Furthermore, SACS expression modulated TIME and influenced drug sensitivity. These results not only enhance the understanding of the mechanistic role of SACS in CRC but also provide important theoretical evidence supporting its potential clinical application as a therapeutic target.

Through differential gene analysis and WGCNA, 485 genes significantly associated with CRC were identified. The GO analysis revealed that 485 genes are enriched in key biological processes, including cell cycle regulation, DNA replication, signal transduction, and stress response. The KEGG pathway analysis results further corroborated these findings, demonstrating that the 485 genes are enriched in cancer-related pathways such as the cell cycle regulatory pathway, p53 signalling pathway, and DNA replication. Cell cycle regulation is closely associated with tumor proliferation. The p53 signalling pathway, as a classical tumor suppressor mechanism, inhibits tumorigenesis by inducing cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, and apoptosis10. However, the mutation rate of the p53 gene in CRC is as high as 60%. Mutated p53 not only loses its tumor suppressor function but may also acquire tumor-promoting effects, thereby exacerbating cell cycle dysregulation and accelerating tumor progression11. The significant enrichment of DNA replication-related pathways further underscores the abnormal increase in the demand for genetic material replication in CRC cells, which is consistent with their high proliferation rates. Moreover, in the KEGG analysis, enrichment in pathways related to human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection was observed, which suggests that CRC may promote the formation of the tumor microenvironment by modulating immune evasion mechanisms associated with viral infection12–14. The results of the analysis using the Metascape database further validated and complemented the results of the GO and KEGG analyses, showing significant enrichment of the 485 genes in the FOXM1 pathway, retinoblastoma-related pathways, and DNA metabolism. Activation of the FOXM1 pathway is considered a key driver of cell cycle regulation in various cancers15,16. FOXM1 is highly expressed during the G1/S and G2/M phases, promoting cell division and proliferation by upregulating target genes such as cyclin B1 and PLK117–19.

Using an integrated machine learning approach, 26 core genes were identified, among which SACS emerged as a key gene significantly associated with CRC prognosis. As the primary target gene of this study, SACS was significantly upregulated in CRC tissues and strongly correlated with poor patient prognosis. Previous studies have reported that SACS is involved in cytoskeletal homeostasis and protein folding in neurodegenerative diseases, but its specific role in tumor biology remains unclear20,21. The meta-analysis confirmed the consistent aberrant expression of SACS across independent datasets, highlighting its potential as a CRC-specific biomarker. Elevated SACS expression levels were observed in advanced CRC stages (e.g. T3/T4 and Stage II) and specific pathological subtypes (e.g. colonic mucinous adenocarcinoma), which suggests its involvement in promoting tumor progression and invasive phenotypes. Mechanistically, GSEA revealed that high SACS expression levels significantly activated multiple pathways associated with cell cycle regulation, including E2F targets, G2/M checkpoint, and MYC targets. E2F transcription factors are central regulators of the cell cycle22,23. Overactivation of E2F transcription factors in tumor cells leads to cell cycle dysregulation and promotes tumor proliferation23. Disruption of G2/M checkpoints further exacerbates chromosomal segregation abnormalities and genomic instability24,25. Activation of the MYC pathway supports tumor growth by enhancing glycolysis and nucleotide metabolism, providing essential energy and biosynthetic precursors for rapid tumor proliferation26–28. In addition to cell cycle regulatory pathways, high SACS expression levels suppress bile acid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and xenobiotic metabolism, contributing to metabolic reprogramming. These metabolic alterations are hallmark features of CRC cells adapting to tumor microenvironmental changes29. Disruption of bile acid metabolism has been demonstrated to be closely associated with CRC progression, promoting tumorigenesis by inducing oxidative stress and DNA damage30,31. The synergistic effects of these pathways suggest that SACS may drive the rapid proliferation of CRC cells by regulating the expression of key genes at critical cell cycle checkpoints and disrupting the metabolic balance of tumor cells.

CRC progression involves not only intrinsic tumor cell changes but also complex alterations in the immune microenvironment. CIBERSORT analysis and ssGSEA revealed significant differences in immune cell infiltration between the CRC and normal tissues. Higher proportions of M1 macrophages and Tregs were observed in the CRC tissues, while activated mast cells and monocytes were reduced. Tregs suppress antitumor immunity by secreting cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, impairing T-cell activity and dendritic cell antigen presentation8. Although M1 macrophages exhibit pro-inflammatory roles, their reprogramming within the tumor microenvironment can enhance tumor-promoting activities32. Previous studies have demonstrated that M1 macrophages can enhance the survival and invasiveness of oral squamous cell carcinomas through the GDF15-mediated phosphorylation of ErbB233. These findings suggest that the TIME in CRC is characterised by significant inflammation and immune suppression, which are closely linked to tumor progression and immune evasion mechanisms. Further analysis demonstrated a strong association between SACS expression and immune cell infiltration. High SACS expression levels positively correlated with activated NK cells and activated memory CD4 + T cells but negatively correlated with Tregs and resting NK cells. These findings suggest that SACS expression may influence immune regulation by promoting antitumor immune responses by modulating NK cells and Treg infiltration. The functional analysis further revealed that high SACS expression levels enhanced critical immune functions, including APC co-stimulation, C-C chemokine receptor signalling, cytolytic activity, and dendritic cell activation. Such functional enhancements may reflect heightened immune activity within the tumor microenvironment. As key APCs, dendritic cell activation is directly linked to the efficacy of T-cell responses34. Moreover, C-C chemokine receptor signalling plays a pivotal role in immune cell migration and chemotaxis, with its activation potentially facilitating the redistribution of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment35. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown promising therapeutic potential in CRC. However, their efficacy remains limited by interindividual variability. The findings of this study suggest that high SACS expression levels significantly enhance immune activity, indicating that patients with CRC who have elevated SACS expression levels may exhibit increased responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Nevertheless, further experimental and clinical studies are required to validate the relationship between SACS expression and the therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint blockades.

In the drug sensitivity analysis, high SACS expression levels were associated with resistance to oxaliplatin. As a first-line chemotherapeutic agent, oxaliplatin exerts its anticancer effects primarily through the induction of DNA damage and apoptosis; however, tumor cells can develop resistance by enhancing DNA repair mechanisms and inhibiting apoptosis36,37. In addition, molecular docking simulations identified coumestrol and quercetin as potential natural compounds targeting SACS. Coumestrol has been demonstrated to exert anticancer effects in various malignancies by inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. For example, Lim et al. found that coumestrol inhibits prostate cancer cell proliferation and migration through the regulation of the AKT, ERK1/2, and JNK/MAPK signalling pathways38. Similarly, quercetin has been shown to modulate oxidative stress, suppress inflammation, and inhibit tumor progression through its multi-targeted actions39,40. These findings provide important insights into the potential development of novel anticancer therapies based on natural products that target SACS.

To integrate these molecular, immune, and therapeutic associations, we propose a unified clinical hypothesis: SACS defines a CRC molecular subtype characterized by enhanced proliferative signaling, metabolic reprogramming, chemoresistance, and a functionally active immune microenvironment that may partially respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Specifically, high SACS expression is associated with the activation of oncogenic pathways such as E2F and MYC, suppression of bile acid and fatty acid metabolism, and increased resistance to oxaliplatin. Concurrently, SACS expression is positively correlated with the infiltration of activated NK cells and memory CD4⁺ T cells, and with heightened immune functions such as dendritic cell activation, APC co-stimulation, and cytolytic activity. These findings suggest that, while SACS-overexpressing tumors demonstrate aggressive phenotypes and chemoresistance, their association with a functionally active immune microenvironment may indicate increased susceptibility to immune checkpoint inhibitors, thereby highlighting the potential of SACS as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy responsiveness. This hypothesis underscores the potential utility of SACS not only as a biomarker for prognosis and treatment resistance but also as a stratification marker to identify CRC patients who may benefit from immunotherapy. In conclusion, this study systematically elucidates the critical role and molecular mechanisms of the SACS gene in CRC through comprehensive transcriptomic meta-analysis and experimental validation. However, this study has limitations. Although in vitro experiments were performed to validate the oncogenic role of SACS, further in vivo studies are needed to fully confirm its biological functions. Additionally, the findings related to drug sensitivity and molecular docking require subsequent pharmacological experiments and clinical studies to verify their therapeutic feasibility and clinical applicability.

Conclusion

This study reveals the critical role and clinical significance of SACS expression in CRC. High SACS expression levels are closely associated with poor prognosis, advanced staging, and specific pathological subtypes. By activating cell cycle regulatory pathways and reshaping the TIME, SACS expression drives CRC progression. In addition, SACS influences drug sensitivity, and through molecular docking, coumestrol and quercetin were identified as potential targeted therapeutic candidates. These findings highlight the potential of the SACS gene as a promising diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic target in CRC. However, further experimental and clinical validations are required to confirm its clinical usefulness.
