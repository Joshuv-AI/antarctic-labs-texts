Radiographic Assessment of the Marginal Bone Loss Around the Implant Before and After the Prosthesis Placement—A Comparative Study
Authors: Mohammad Jalaluddin, Aradhana Rathod, Shilpa Mailankote, Liya Neha Bipinchandra, BS Harsha Raj, Anupama Ahirwar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:The present investigation performed the CBCT to measure the marginal bone loss surrounding the implant both before and after the prosthesis was placed.Materials and Methods:Within the age range of 21 to 45 years, a total of 20 patients were involved, and all participants gave their informed consent. Immediate implant was inserted and ceramic prosthesis was prepared and given to the patients. Radiographic assessment was carried out using the CBCT at baseline, three months, and six months following the prosthesis. The circumference of the crestal bone level around the implant was measured in all directions (mesial, distal, buccal, and palatal), using the implant’s first thread as a standard reference point.Results:The change of the mean marginal bone level at the time of implant placement to three months after the prosthesis was at mesial 1.32 ± 0.02 to 1.46 ± 0.10, at distal 1.46 ± 0.08 to 1.94 ± 0.14, at buccal 1.28 ± 0.01 to 1.40 ± 0.06, at palatal 1.20 ± 0.03 to 1.22 ± 0.04. The change of the mean marginal bone level at the time of implant placement to six months after the prosthesis was at mesial 1.32 ± 0.02 to 1.84 ± 0.12, at distal 1.46 ± 0.08 to 2.38 ± 0.02, at buccal 1.28 ± 0.01 to 1.78 ± 0.04, at palatal 1.20 ± 0.03 to 1.34 ± 0.01.Conclusion:The current investigation came to the conclusion that the CBCT provides all the diagnostic information required for an implant to be placed successfully.

INTRODUCTION

Dental implants are an essential procedure for restoring function for individuals who are completely or partially edentulous. Traditionally, dental implant therapy has involved placing a dental implant in a healed bone and letting it grow back completely. Due to advancements in implant surgery throughout time, immediate implantation of dental implants in extraction sockets has emerged as a safe and effective therapeutic option. Immediate implant placement has several clear advantages, such as improved preservation of soft tissue and alveolar morphology, fewer surgical procedures needed, a shorter recovery period, reduced patient stress, and immediate benefits.[1]

A number of variables, including follow-ups during the healing phase to assess osseointegration—the key performance indicator in implant dentistry—determine whether an implant succeeds or fails. The primary clinical indicators for evaluating the success of an implant are the marginal bone loss and alveolar bone loss of less than 0.2 mm annually after the first year. Radiologically, it has been discovered that prosthetic loading could exacerbate the loss of crestal bone. Smaller alterations in the bone cannot be seen with traditional X-ray methods.[2]

The precision of 3D cone-beam CT imaging (CBCT) in displaying peri-implant bone was observed. With variable precision, 3D CBCT offers valuable information about the bone surrounding implants in all dimensions. Since its inception in 1998, computed tomography (CBCT) has been used to assess implant sites, evaluate the temporomandibular joint, visualize the periodontal osseous condition, and identify periodontal ligament gaps.[3] While the CBCT may evaluate oral and buccal bone, its performance in evaluating MBL and DBL is similar. Partially relevant diagnostic information for implant planning is provided by traditional dental imaging modalities. Periapical radiographs are not useful for evaluating the marginal bone on the buccal and lingual/palatal surface of the implant, the distance between the implant and the buccal and lingual/palatal plates, or the possibility of plate perforation.[4] So, the goal of the current investigation was to compare, using a CBCT analysis, the peri-implant marginal bone level changes around dental implants before and after the prosthesis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The current investigation was conducted in the Department of Periodontics and Oral Implantology, Kalinga Institute of Dental Sciences, Bhubaneswar, India. Within the age range of 21 to 45 years, a total of 20 patients were involved, and all participants gave their informed consent. The study included patients of either sex who were between the ages of 21 and 45, had a single missing tooth that was in a healthy edentulous site with neighboring, healthy teeth that were intact, were in good systemic health and did not have any contraindications to surgery, and maintained good oral hygiene. Patients with a history of smoking, insufficient bone quantity and quality, bruxism in the dental history, parafunctional behaviors and/or unstable posterior occlusion, and pregnant or nursing women were excluded from the current study.

Presurgical protocol

For every patient, wax patterns and study models were made. The study models were subjected to occlusal analysis, and surgical stents were made. The oral prophylaxis was carried out seven days prior to the scheduled implantation. For a duration of seven days, patients were instructed to use mouthwash containing 0.2% chlorhexidine gluconate twice a day. Sufficient guidance was provided regarding maintaining dental hygiene and its significance for the efficacy of implant treatment.

Surgical procedures

After the induction of local anesthesia, to preserve the bony socket walls, the teeth were retrieved with a set of forceps after being loosened using an elevator. Dental cavities were thoroughly checked for wall cracks following debridement and cleaning. At the mesial and distal aspects of the socket, a crestal incision was made to raise a periosteal flap. Using a caliper, the depth, mesiodistal, and buccolingual dimensions of the socket were measured. Implants with the right diameter and platform size that best matched the socket walls were chosen. Finally, the appropriate cover screw was drilled one after the other until it was level with the nearby bone. Finally, the area was sutured (silk 4–0) once the primary soft tissue closure of the region was completed. Every patient received antibiotic prophylaxis an hour prior to surgery, and this treatment was repeated twice a day for a week. Patients were prescribed postoperative analgesics and relevant health recommendations. In a week, the sutures were taken out. A mid-crestal incision was made to seal the healing abutment four months after the implant was placed.

Prosthesis stage

Following the four- to six-week period of soft tissue healing, the gingival former was inserted and the cover screw was removed. They used simple interrupted sutures. One week later, the gingival former was taken out, the imprint coping was put in place, and the details were recorded using the closed tray method using the rubber base impression material. The gingival former was reinstalled following the removal of the impression coping. The laboratory received the impression along with the impression coping and the implant analogue. A screw-retained platform switched ceramic prosthesis was then created and handed to the patients, with the screw being tightened to a depth of around 25 Ncm.

Evaluation of the marginal bone loss around the dental implant

Radiographic examination was carried out using the CBCT at baseline, three months, and six months following the prosthesis. The circumference of the crestal bone level around the implant was measured in all directions (mesial, distal, buccal, and palatal), using the implant’s first thread as a standard reference point.

Statistical analysis

After being tabulated in an Excel sheet, the data was transferred into IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) for analysis. The measures were then compared using the Student’s unpaired t test. A statistically significant P value was defined as one that was less than 0.05.

DISCUSSION

One of the most cutting-edge methods of dentistry practiced nowadays is the use of endosteal implants for rehabilitation. Endosteal implants are suitable and efficient for both restoring edentulous arches and replacing a single tooth. Marginal bone area is typically a key predictor of implant health when success and durability are taken into account.[5] One crucial and practical method of determining the integrity and health of the bone surrounding the peri-implant hard tissue is radiographic assessment of the bone. A reduction in the level of crestal bone signifies that the implant’s bony anchoring is becoming less secure. It is evident that employing accurate radiography techniques is crucial to accurately determine the height of bone surrounding dental implants to proceed with other procedures.[6]

Peri-implantitis is the most frequent cause of late implant loss, accounting for about 50% of cases. The marginal bone loss surrounding implants should be the primary parameter in the definition of peri-implantitis, above and above the clinical parameters suggested in the existing classification. In implant dentistry, it is critical to distinguish between pathological and healthy bone loss because it is still uncertain how best to treat peri-implantitis.[7]

A tooth replacement technique that is becoming more favored is immediate implant placement, in which a dental implant is placed into the extraction socket at the time of extraction. The conventional projection consists of the intraoral periapical extraoral, lateral cephalometric, and panoramic radiographs. The following are examples of advanced radiography techniques: interactive computer-guided implantology, spiral CT, CBCT, and CT. The CBCT is useful in defining the safety zone because it offers an accurate assessment of the distance between the alveolar ridge and significant structures, such as the maxillary sinus and mandibular canal. Schulze et al. demonstrated how to measure the buccal and lingual bone plates using the CBCT.[8]

Cardaropoli et al.[9] and Vera et al.[10] found that after a year of implant insertion in new extraction sockets, marginal bone levels remained constant as assessed by a CBCT evaluation of horizontal ridge diameters and marginal bone levels. Its level girth around the top remained mostly unchanged. A CBCT examination in the trial by Raes et al.[11] revealed that, one year later, the average circumferential bone level surrounding implants implanted in a new extraction socket with immediate loading was 0.21 mm. They found that the CBCT outperforms IOPA in terms of precision. After a year, Huber et al.[12] conducted more research to determine the impact of implants implanted in newly extracted sockets on the crestal bone level, and they discovered a mean loss of 0.49 mm.

CONCLUSION

The current investigation came to the conclusion that the CBCT provides all the diagnostic information required for an implant to be placed successfully. Additionally, the CBCT assists in precisely identifying changes in the bone level at each follow-up appointment. This enables the patient to take corrective measurements at the appropriate time, preventing implant failure.
