Metabolic Reprogramming of Cancer Stem Cells: Targeting Lipid Flux and Mitochondrial Plasticity to Overcome Therapeutic Resistance
Authors: Daniel Ejim Uti, Esther Ugo Alum, Josephine E. Egbung, Zuhair M. Mohammedsaleh, Manpreet Kaur, Kranti Kiran Reddy Ealla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer stem cells (CSCs) are increasingly recognized as metabolically plastic subpopulations within malignant tissues that drive tumor initiation, metastatic dissemination, and relapse after therapy. Although traditional models of cancer metabolism have emphasized aerobic glycolysis, CSCs rarely exhibit a single, clearly defined bioenergetic phenotype. Rather, they dynamically remodel glucose utilization, oxidative phosphorylation, redox regulation, de novo fatty acid synthesis, lipid uptake, lipid sequestration, and fatty acid oxidation in response to hypoxic conditions, nutrient restriction, stromal interactions, and therapeutic perturbations. This review focuses on two interconnected aspects of metabolic flexibility: lipid flux and mitochondrial plasticity. We examine how de novo lipogenesis, CD36‐mediated fatty acid uptake, fatty acid‐binding protein trafficking, cholesterol biosynthesis, and lipid‐droplet turnover contribute to stemness, membrane remodeling, metastatic potential, and resistance to cytotoxic agents. We also examine how mitochondrial dynamics, including fusion, fission, mitophagy, and biogenesis, together with reactive oxygen species buffering and shifts in oxidative phosphorylation, facilitate CSC survival during chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immune‐mediated cytotoxicity. Particular emphasis is placed on the integration of fatty acid oxidation to respiratory metabolism, on the epigenetic consequences associated with the acetyl‐CoA availability, and the metabolic crosstalk linking CSCs to adipocytes, fibroblasts, mesenchymal cells, and immune cell populations in the tumor microenvironment. Finally, we evaluate therapeutic strategies involving inhibitors of fatty acid synthase (FASN), acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC), stearoyl‐CoA desaturase‐1 (SCD1), carnitine palmitoyltransferase‐1 (CPT1), and OXPHOS. We also discuss combination therapies, nanotechnology‐based drug delivery, and emerging artificial intelligence (AI)‐guided approaches. Taken together, current evidence identifies the lipid–mitochondrial axis as a critical systems‐level driver of therapeutic resistance and a promising target for improving long‐term cancer control.

Introduction

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) represent a functional subset of tumor cells that have the self‐renewal ability, multi‐lineage differentiation potential, and disproportionately large influence in the process of tumor initiation, tumor growth, metastatic seeding and disease recurrence [1, 2]. CSCs are operationally characterized by tumor‐initiating capacity, sphere‐forming ability, expression of specific markers, and resistance to therapy; however, they do not constitute a fixed clonal population. Rather, CSC states are dynamic and reversible and are strongly influenced by oncogenic signaling pathways and microenvironmental stresses [3]. This plasticity may contribute to the pattern observed in many cancers, in which an initial clinical response is followed by disease progression and the emergence of a more aggressive tumor phenotype [4, 5]. Surviving cells often exhibit stem‐like properties, including slow cell‐cycle kinetics, enhanced DNA‐damage responses, and robust stress‐adaptation programs that permit tumor regrowth after treatment is discontinued [6]. One of the most important mechanisms that have been associated with this persistence is metabolic reprogramming. Metabolic reprogramming can be defined as the coordinated redirection of nutrient acquisition, energy production, biosynthesis, and redox regulation to support malignant fitness [7, 8]. Although bulk tumor‐cell populations may exhibit high glycolytic flux under oxygenated conditions, CSCs do not conform to a single Warburg‐like metabolic phenotype. Across leukemia, breast, ovarian, pancreatic, colorectal, liver and brain tumors, CSCs have been reported to have glycolysis, mitochondrial respiration or combined metabolic programs based on anatomical context, oncogenic background and, of course, therapeutic exposure [9, 10]. The defining feature is not reliance on a single metabolic pathway but the ability to switch between pathways while retaining stemness, survival, and regenerative potential.

Two interconnected metabolic systems have emerged as particularly important in CSC biology: lipid metabolism and mitochondrial plasticity (Figure 1). Lipids are no longer regarded merely as structural membrane components; in CSCs, they also function as metabolic substrates, signaling molecules, membrane organizers, and precursors of epigenetically active metabolites [11]. De novo lipogenesis produces phospholipids, triglycerides, and cholesterol required for proliferation and membrane reconfiguration [11]. Transporters such as CD36 and fatty acid‐binding proteins facilitate the uptake and intracellular trafficking of fatty acids derived from the circulation or tumor microenvironment [12]. Lipid droplets provide a buffered energy store that can be mobilized during metabolic stress. Fatty acid oxidation generates acetyl‐CoA, NADH, and FADH2, which support the tricarboxylic acid cycle and the electron transport chain [13]. Remodeling of cholesterol and sphingolipids stabilizes lipid rafts that support stemness‐related signaling through pathways such as Wnt, Notch, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR [13].

In CSCs, mitochondria perform functions that extend well beyond ATP production. Mitochondrial architecture, abundance, and signaling output influence whether a cell can survive hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, oxidative stress, and drug‐induced injury [14, 15]. CSCs have often been shown to exhibit plasticity in mitochondrial mass, membrane potential, cristae organization, as well as quality control programs (Figure 1). CSCs may preferentially use oxidative phosphorylation in quiescent or slow‐cycling states but shift toward glycolysis during rapid proliferation or ischemic stress [16].

Fusion and fission of mitochondria are involved in the redistribution of metabolites and in the elimination of impaired organelles [17]. Mitophagy maintains respiratory competence along with the prevention of an accumulation of toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS). Controlled ROS production may also act as a stemness supporting signal if antioxidant buffering is not impaired [18, 19]. Lipid flux and mitochondrial function intersect at several levels. Mitochondrial respiration is directly fueled by FAO. The export of citrate and the generation of acetyl‐CoA couple mitochondrial metabolism to cytosolic lipogenesis and histone acetylation. Redox equivalents produced during lipid degradation affect glutathione homeostasis, susceptibility to ferroptosis, and threshold for treatment‐induced apoptosis [20]. Mitochondrial dysfunction may induce compensatory lipid uptake, whereas lipid storage can buffer cells against oxidative and endoplasmic‐reticulum stress [21]. These reciprocal interactions form a metabolic network that allows CSCs to survive conditions that eliminate less metabolically plastic tumor cells.

This biology is important because resistance to therapy is the major unsolved problem in oncologic medicine. Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy may effectively reduce tumor burden, but metabolically flexible, stem‐like cells often persist in residual disease [22, 23]. Lipid remodeling may reduce membrane permeability to drugs, increase drug efflux, and decrease susceptibility to ferroptosis. Mitochondrial rewiring is able to maintain ATP synthesis, enhance the amplitude of DNA repair, induce cell quiescence, and inhibit apoptosis [24]. Tumors with OXPHOS‐high, FAO‐enriched, or lipid‐loaded phenotypes are increasingly associated with poor treatment response, metastasis, and shorter survival [25]. This review synthesizes current evidence on how CSCs exploit lipid metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial plasticity to maintain stemness and resist therapy. First, the biological underpinnings of the heterogeneity and metabolic plasticity of CSC are described. Second, lipid metabolism, mitochondrial bioenergetics and their integration in the tumor microenvironment are investigated. Third, the processes that lead to resistance to major anticancer modalities are discussed. Finally, therapeutic strategies addressed to FASN, ACC, and SCD1, as well to CD36‐associated lipid acquisition, FAO, OXPHOS, ROS signaling, and mitophagy, are evaluated while thoughts of opportunities provided by single‐cell omics, and synthetic lethality as well as nanotechnological and AI‐guided metabolic profiling are highlighted. By placing lipoflux, as well as mitochondrial plasticity, within the same conceptual framework, the review proposes that sustained cancer control may require the coordinated disruption of adaptive metabolic programs that protect CSCs from eradication.

Biological Foundations of Cancer Stem Cells and Metabolic Plasticity

CSCs are best conceptualized as dynamic cellular states within a tumor ecosystem and hierarchical organization rather than as a homogeneous population [26]. In many malignancies, there is a subset of tumor cells with rare and unique capacity for self‐renewal, asymmetric cell division, multi‐lineage differentiation, and tumor‐initiating function, but these characteristics are not discretely contained within an already predefined marker compartment [26]. Non‐CSCs can regain stem‐like properties in response to conditions of hypoxia, inflammatory signaling, epithelial‐mesenchymal transition, or in response to therapeutic stress [27]. Experimental evidence also demonstrates that non‐stem cancer cells can spontaneously convert into stem‐like cells in vitro and in vivo, supporting a bidirectional rather than strictly hierarchical CSC model [28]. Consequently, CSC heterogeneity arises from genetic diversity, epigenetic memory, tissue‐of‐origin programs, and microenvironmental context; therefore, no single metabolic profile is likely to characterize all CSCs. Instead, different niches generate selection for different energetic solutions [27].

Niche dependence is of paramount importance to this biology. Perivascular niches may expose CSCs to oxygen, lipids, and growth factors that support mitochondrial respiration and biosynthesis, whereas hypoxic or nutrient‐deprived compartments favor glycolysis, autophagy, and nutrient‐scavenging pathways [29]. Interactions with cancer‐associated fibroblasts, adipocytes, mesenchymal cells, endothelial cells, and immune populations increase the nutrient landscape even more. Lactate, fatty acids, ketone bodies, cholesterol‐fat‐rich vesicles, and even donated mitochondria may be available locally as fuels. As a result, CSCs do not merely respond to stress; they integrate spatial signals from the niche to determine whether to proliferate, remain quiescent, invade, or resist treatment [30, 31].

Canonical developmental pathways are used to maintain this state. Wnt/β‐catenin signaling supports self‐renewal, cell‐fate specification, and metastatic competence which is also enhanced by lipid raft composition and by enzymes (e.g., SCD1) involved in monounsaturated fatty acid availability [32, 33]. Notch signaling contributes to dormancy, cell‐to‐cell communication, and therapeutic persistence, particularly within perivascular niches [34]. Hedgehog signaling determines stemness, matrix remodeling and metabolic plasticity in multiple solid tumors. PI3K/AKT/mTOR is responsible for coordinating nutrient sensing, anabolism and autophagy and connecting signals from the extracellular environment, including growth signals, to glucose metabolism, lipogenesis, mitochondrial biogenesis and cell survival [35]. Because of the role these pathways have in controlling both stemness and metabolism, the distinction between signaling and metabolism in CSCs is often artificial. Stemness programmes are encoded metabolically, whereas metabolic states reinforce signaling amplitude [35].

Metabolic plasticity refers to the ability of CSCs to switch among glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, glutamine utilization, and lipid‐centered metabolism without losing viability [36, 37]. This adaptability could be in the form of a slow spectrum, rather than an on/off switch. Some CSCs exhibit a hybrid metabolic state in which glycolysis supplies biosynthetic intermediates while oxidative phosphorylation provides ATP and redox support [38]. Others switch states with respect to the cell cycle or exposure to therapeutics. In pancreatic CSCs, the MYC–PGC‐1α balance was shown to regulate OXPHOS dependence and the emergence of metabolically flexible, treatment‐resistant CSC populations [39]. Importantly, metabolic plasticity is not just a response to deprivation, but rather it brings selective advantages during dissemination, colonization of distant organs, and recovery following cytotoxic injury [37].

Crosstalk between metabolic states and stemness is being supported by more and more mechanistic evidence. Enhanced OXPHOS can maintain ATP supply in slow cycling CSCs and thus allow for survival during chemotherapy [40]. Consistent with this concept, quiescent ROS‐low human leukemia stem cells were found to depend on BCL‐2‐supported OXPHOS and were selectively eliminated by BCL‐2 inhibition [41]. Lipid metabolism can modify chromatin accessibility and maintain transcriptional programs associated with self‐renewal. Controlled production of ROS is able to promote hypoxia and redox‐sensitive pathways, whereas uncontrolled production of ROS can promote differentiation or cell death [10]. As a result, CSCs have a tendency to stay in an intermediate redox zone: high enough for signaling, but not so high that it is exposed to lethal oxidative damage. This balance is maintained through mitochondrial quality control, ROS detoxification, glutathione production, and flexible substrate utilization [26].

Temporal plasticity is one other relevant dimension. During early tumor growth or metastatic seeding, transient or fluctuating hypoxia may favor glycolysis, enabling CSCs to survive changes in oxygen and nutrient availability [10]. As niches become more stable, they could strengthen positive effects of mitochondrial biogenesis and long‐chain fatty acid oxidation to facilitate persistence and long‐term repopulation [42]. These trajectories can further be remodeled by therapy. Cells that survive kinase inhibition or DNA‐damaging therapy frequently exhibit increased respiratory dependence, altered mitochondrial dynamics, and enhanced lipid scavenging. Hence, metabolic identity in CSCs should be viewed as a dynamic and progressive process rather than a static description [42].

This systems view has some practical implications. Marker‐based CSC targeting may be ineffective if metabolically adaptable non‐CSCs can regenerate the stem‐like compartment. Conversely, purely metabolic strategies may be circumvented if alternative stemness pathways remain intact [43]. A more fruitful conceptualization is of CSCs as signaling‐metabolic units whose behavior is dependent on niche inputs, organellar state, and developmental circuitry. Under this model, therapeutic vulnerabilities are most likely to emerge when stemness pathways and metabolic dependencies are targeted simultaneously [43]. The remainder of this review explores the two major modules that are observed in common to tumor types at large: lipid metabolic rewiring and mitochondrial plasticity (Figure 2).

Lipid Metabolic Reprogramming in Cancer Stem Cells

Lipid metabolic reprogramming is one of the most commonly observed features of CSCs across a broad range of solid and hematologic malignancies [11]. While in the past the predominant paradigm of cancer metabolism was focused on glucose utilization, the emerging evidence suggests that CSCs preferentially make use of lipids as multifunctional substrates that are used in membrane biosynthesis, ATP production, signal transduction, oxidative stress mitigation and epigenetic modulation [44]. Lipid metabolic programs provide an advantage over rigid glucose‐dependent metabolism because they support both anabolic and catabolic flexibility [11]. Accordingly, a CSC is able to synthesize fatty acids in nutrient‐rich conditions, scavenge exogenous lipids during periods of nutrient availability, sequester exogenous lipids in cytosolic droplets during periods of nutrient excess, and oxidize exogenous lipids as a source of energy during metabolic stress (Figure 3A) [25].

De novo lipogenesis is a major entry point into the lipid metabolic network of CSCs [45]. Key enzymes, including ATP citrate lyase, acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC), and fatty acid synthase (FASN), channel citrate‐derived carbon toward palmitate synthesis, which then undergoes further processing for elongation, desaturation, and incorporation into phospholipids, triglycerides, sphingolipids, and mediators of the signaling pathways [45]. Activation of sterol regulatory element binding proteins (SREBs) enhances transcription of these anabolic genes, and these can be linked to oncogenic PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling [46]. In CSCs, the high rate of lipogenesis for quick remodeling of membrane and de novo invasion depends on the concomitant stabilization of lipid rafts, which are potentiators of the Wnt, Notch, and receptor‐tyrosine kinase signaling [47]. Thus, lipogenesis has a much greater role than it simply serves as a biomass‐generating mechanism; it is responsible for orchestrating the signaling topology needed for self‐renewal [47].

Desaturation is then an extra level of regulation. Stearoyl CoA desaturase‐1 (SCD 1) catalyzes monounsaturated fatty acid formation, which maintains membrane fluidity, relieves lipotoxic saturation stress and modulates the stemness signaling [48]. Experimental studies in colorectal and other cancers have shown that SCD1 inhibition reduces CSC viability and suppresses Wnt and Notch signaling highlighting that it is the quality rather than the quantity of lipids which is as important in the biology of CSCs [49]. Ovarian CSCs exhibit increased lipid unsaturation, and genetic or pharmacological inhibition of lipid desaturases suppresses sphere formation and tumor‐initiating capacity [50]. Nevertheless, the ability of CSCs to regulate unsaturated lipid synthesis in response to, for example, by‐passed miR‐21, may occur through the development of resistance through compensatory mechanisms such as FADS2‐dependent sapienate synthesis (Figure 3B), thus describing the regenerative capacity of CSCs to re‐wire the synthesis of unsaturated lipids in response to the disruption of a single enzymatic node [51]. The illustrated pathway (Figure 3B), represents an alternative monounsaturated fatty‐acid synthesis route used by some cancer cells. Palmitate (16:0) is desaturated by fatty acid desaturase 2 (FADS2; Δ6‐desaturase), which introduces a cis double bond at the Δ6 position to form sapienate (cis‐6‐hexadecenoate; 16:1 n‐10). Sapienate can then undergo two‐carbon chain elongation by an ELOVL‐family elongase to generate cis‐8‐octadecenoate (18:1 n‐10). Both fatty acids can be incorporated into membrane phospholipids, supplying monounsaturated acyl chains required for membrane formation during rapid cancer‐cell proliferation [11].

FADS2‐mediated removal of excess palmitate may also reduce endoplasmic‐reticulum stress and contribute to resistance to SCD1‐targeted therapy.

This pathway is particularly important when the conventional stearoyl‐CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) pathway is restricted. By converting excess palmitate into sapienate, FADS2 can prevent saturated fatty acid accumulation, maintain membrane lipid homeostasis and allow selected cancer cells to continue proliferating under lipid‐limited conditions or during SCD1 inhibition. Evidence for this pathway has been reported in cancer cell lines, mouse hepatocellular carcinomas and primary human liver and lung cancers, although its activity varies among tumor types [11].

A 2024 study further showed that FADS2‐mediated sapienate production can confer resistance to SCD1 inhibitors. SCD1‐sensitive cells accumulated palmitate and developed endoplasmic‐reticulum stress, whereas resistant cells increased FADS2 activity, converted palmitate into sapienate, and avoided ER‐stress‐dependent cell death. Simultaneous suppression of SCD1 and FADS2 restored sensitivity in resistant cancer cells, suggesting that the sapienate pathway may represent both a metabolic escape mechanism and a potential combination‐therapy target [52].

Acquisition of extracellular lipids is another important part of CSC metabolism. CD36 has become a key fatty acid uptake, metastasis and therapeutic resistance transporter/receptor [25, 53]. A CD36‐high, slow‐cycling population of human carcinoma cells was shown to initiate metastasis, while CD36 blockade markedly inhibited metastatic dissemination in experimental models [54]. Elevated expression of CD36 on tumor cells or stem like subpopulations is associated with a greater dependence on exogenous lipids, migratory behavior, and metastasis colonization in the body [25]. Fatty acid‐binding proteins (FABPs) mediate the intracellular trafficking of fatty acids to storage organelles, signaling compartments, or mitochondria [55]. In patient‐derived glioblastoma models, CD36 was enriched in CSCs, and its suppression reduced self‐renewal and tumor‐initiation capacity [56]. This scavenging strategy is especially relevant in adhesion sites which contain a high concentration of adipocytes, that is, the omentum, the breast, the marrow and obese areas, in which host‐derived lipids can constitute a rich form of metabolic currency [57]. In such environments, CSCs have evolved the effective externalization of a part of their anabolic burden onto the host.

Fatty acid β‐oxidation (FAO) is the principal catabolic counterpart to fatty acid uptake and synthesis [31]. Transport of long‐chain fatty acids into mitochondria through carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) enables the production of acetyl‐CoA, NADH, and FADH2, which supply the TCA cycle and respiratory chain [58]. FAO is especially beneficial for those CSCs that are in the quiescent or stress‐resistant state; it provides the sustained supply of ATP and reducing equivalents without requiring high glucose flux [58]. In breast CSCs, adipocyte‐derived leptin activated a JAK/STAT3–CPT1B–FAO pathway that promoted self‐renewal and chemoresistance, whereas FAO inhibition restored treatment sensitivity [59]. Moreover, FAO has been linked to maintenance of NADPH homeostasis, suppression of anoikis, and survival of detachment and dissemination. Recent reviews highlight the FAO as a driver of the persistence of CSC and contributor to chemotherapeutic and targeted resistance in a variety of diseases [58].

Cholesterol metabolism needs to be further stressed. The mevalonate pathway is the source of cholesterol and isoprenoid second‐messengers that are required for membrane order as well as protein prenylation and lipid‐raft integrity [60]. Cholesterol‐rich lipid rafts cluster receptors involved in stemness and survival, thereby facilitating Wnt, Hedgehog, EGFR, and integrin signaling [60]. Interruption of cholesterol synthesis or raft organization can consequently attenuate signaling networks which are responsible for the CSC identity. The general implication is that lipid metabolism of CSCs is beyond the scope of simple fatty acids; the structural lipids and sterols composition work together to restructure signal transduction and membrane biophysics.

Lipid droplets form an important storage module in this system. Once viewed as inert fat reservoirs, lipid droplets are now recognized as dynamic organelles that buffer lipotoxicity, protect polyunsaturated lipids from oxidation, and release substrates during metabolic stress [25]. Lipid‐droplet‐enriched CSCs often exhibit greater sphere‐forming capacity, increased invasive potential, and enhanced resistance to therapy. Label‐free spectroscopic imaging demonstrated that high lipid‐droplet abundance characterizes colorectal CSCs, correlates with CD133 and Wnt activity, and identifies cells with increased tumor‐initiating potential [61]. Droplet‐associated lipases have the capacity to release fatty acids to ensure FAO and lipid stored in the cell can simultaneously modulate signaling and susceptibility to ferroptosis. Practically, lipid droplets help the CSCs to overcome the variation of nutrient availability and maintain survival under acute stress circumstances [62].

Collectively, lipid reprogramming maintains two related phenotypes of CSC: self‐renewal and metastasis [63]. Self‐renewal finds support in lipogenic support of membrane biosynthesis, raft‐mediated signaling, and epigenetic substrate generation [64]. Metastatic competence is supported by CD36‐mediated lipid uptake, FAO‐dependent survival during dissemination, and the capacity to withstand metabolic stress at distant sites [25, 65]. These multifaceted functions explain why lipid pathways are linked repeatedly to relapse and poor prognosis and suggest that effective therapy will likely need to target both lipid acquisition and utilization because inhibiting a particular branch is often compensated by a compensatory activation of the alternative pathways [66, 67]. Mitochondrial Plasticity and Bioenergetics in CSCs

Mitochondrial plasticity is the second major metabolic axis that is responsible for the persistence of the cancer stem cell (CSC) (Figure 4). Although traditional models contrast glycolytic tumor cells with oxidative normal cells, CSCs often occupy intermediate metabolic states, retain substantial mitochondrial activity, and display pronounced metabolic plasticity [36]. Nevertheless, this dependence is rather contextual and by no means absolute: the success of CSCs is not determined by their habitual maximizing of respiration, but by their ability to re‐organize mitochondrial architecture and substrate utilization in response to changes in environmental conditions [84].

Mitochondrial plasticity and bioenergetic adaptation in cancer stem cells. Compared with differentiated tumor cells and normal cells, the CSC is shown as an organelle‐rich, stress‐adapted state capable of reorganizing energy production and mitochondrial quality control. Nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and therapy drive switching between glycolysis and OXPHOS; hypoxia favors glycolysis and FAO, whereas treatment can select OXPHOS‐high survivors. Mitochondrial fusion, regulated by MFN1/2 and OPA1, supports content mixing and respiratory efficiency, while DRP1‐dependent fission enables organelle redistribution and segregation of damaged mitochondria. PINK1/Parkin‐dependent and receptor‐mediated mitophagy remove dysfunctional organelles. ROS are maintained within a signaling range: low‐to‐moderate ROS support HIF and redox‐sensitive stemness pathways, whereas excessive ROS can trigger apoptosis, ferroptosis, or differentiation. Glutathione, thioredoxin, and NAD + ‐linked redox systems buffer oxidative stress. Acetyl‐CoA, ROS, the NAD+/NADH balance, AMPK/mTOR signaling, and TCA‐cycle intermediates such as citrate, alpha‐ketoglutarate, succinate, and fumarate connect mitochondrial metabolism to histone and DNA regulation. This adaptability is enabled by mitochondrial dynamics, that is, fusion and fission. Fusion facilitates the mixing of mitochondrial contents, maintenance of membrane potential, and efficient oxidative metabolism, whereas fission supports mitochondrial distribution during cell division and the segregation of damaged organelles for autophagic removal [85]. CSCs seems to be modulating this balance to suit their changing requirements. More marked fusion may aid sustained respiration and quiescence, while greater fission may allow rapid remodeling in cellular stress and mitigate this stress or as an adaptation during mitosis or in migration [86]. Dysregulated signaling of DRP1, MFN1/2 and OPA1 has thus been implicated in stemness behavior, in metastatic behavior, and response to therapy [87].

OXPHOS dependence versus glycolytic flexibility is thus one of the clinically highly relevant manifestations of this plasticity [87]. Multiple treatment‐resistant CSC populations become increasingly dependent on OXPHOS following exposure to chemotherapy or targeted therapy, indicating that mitochondrial respiration is a salvage mechanism when proliferative programmes can be suppressed. However, complete dependence on oxidative metabolism is uncommon and context‐dependent [88]. Hypoxic niches, glucose (which is scarce), or abrupt vascular disruption can nevertheless force CSCs to use glycolysis, glutamine metabolism, or scrounged substrates. The necessary attribute is the ability to reallocate metabolic flux without massive energetic collapse [89].

A signaling dimension that is added to this bioenergetic role is the reactive oxygen species (ROS). Mitochondria are major sources of ROS, and CSCs therefore must strike a delicate balance between redox signaling and oxidative damage. Low to moderate concentrations of ROS could induce activation of pathways related to adaptation, angiogenesis, and stemness, such as HIF‐dependent response and redox‐sensitive transcription factors [90]. Excessive ROS, on the other hand, lead to damage to lipids, proteins, DNA, and iron–sulfur clusters and may lead to apoptosis, differentiation, or ferroptosis of the cells. Consequently, CSCs often upregulate antioxidant defenses, including glutathione and thioredoxin systems, NADPH‐generating enzymes, and mitophagy [91].

Mitophagy and mitochondrial quality control are especially important for cells that are resistant to stress by selectively removing damaged mitochondria; CSCs preserve mitochondrial function and prevent the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species, and Both PINK1/Parkin dependent, and receptor mediated mitophagy pathways have been involved in cancer adaptation [92]. However, under the condition of chemotherapy to hypoxia to nutrient stress, increased mitophagy can provide a healthier pool of mitochondria and promote survival. Thus, mitophagy may act as a double‐edged process: it can protect resistant CSCs but may also represent a therapeutic vulnerability when these cells become highly dependent on it [93].

Adaptation to hypoxia and limitation of nutrients further explains the role of mitochondria in determining the fate of CSC. Hypoxic microenvironments result in a suppression of the entry of pyruvate into mitochondria and of glycolysis, whereas chronic or cyclic hypoxia also may act by elevating selection for cells equipped with a better respiratory control, increased ability to oxidize fatty acids, or enhanced reductive carboxylation and scavenging programs [94]. When amino acids or glucose are limited, mitochondrial metabolism integrates signals from AMPK, mTOR, and redox sensors to regulate autophagy, quiescence, and selective biosynthesis [95]. This flexibility allows the ability of CSCs to survive in poorly perfused microenvironments as well as after anti‐angiogenic therapy [96].

Mitochondria are also responsible for control of stemness at transcriptional and epigenetic levels [97]. TCA‐cycle intermediates, including citrate, α‐ketoglutarate, succinate, and fumarate, regulate histone and DNA‐modifying enzymes, thereby linking mitochondrial metabolism to gene‐expression programs [98]. Variations in acetyl‐CoA can help to change the acetylation of histones whereas changes in ROS or NAD+/NADH ratios can also affect the activity of sirtuins and accessibility of chromatin [99]. Mitochondrial retrograde signaling, in contrast, can be both an element that reinforces stem‐like transcriptional networks rather than simply responding to them [99].

From a therapeutic perspective, mitochondrial plasticity helps explain why strategies targeting glycolysis alone often produce only transient effects. Even without glycolysis, the CSCs can increase fatty‐acid oxidation, glutamine oxidation, or respiratory efficiency. Vice versa, the widespread inhibition of the OXPHOS pathway could be constrained by toxicity and the development of glycolytic escape [100]. It is therefore important to identify the periods during which mitochondria function as indispensable centers of stress adaptation. During these windows of vulnerability, interventions targeting complex I, redox buffering, fusion–fission regulators, or mitophagy may disrupt the organellar plasticity that enables CSC survival under therapeutic pressure [101].

Interplay Between Lipid Flux and Mitochondrial Function

The issue of the biological importance of lipid metabolism and mitochondrial plasticity is best illustrated when the two are considered together as a coupled system as opposed to two separate systems. FAO is a major point of integration [102]: fatty acids derived from the extracellular environment, lipid droplets, or de novo synthesis are converted into long‐chain acyl‐CoA molecules and transported into mitochondria for oxidation. Within mitochondria, these fatty acids are oxidized to generate acetyl‐CoA, NADH, and FADH2 [25, 103]. These metabolites are used to fuel the tricarboxylic acid cycle as well as the electron transport chain which will continue ATP synthesis and respiratory strength under glucose constraint or therapeutic stress [103]. In cancer stem cells (CSCs), such a linkage is especially critical, since the drive to long‐term survival is posed without requiring rapid biomass accumulation.

A number of functional consequences are derived from this integration. First, FAO provides an energetically efficient fuel for the quiescent or slow‐cycling CSCs, which usually need persistent ATP production capacities instead of exuberant biosynthesis capacities. Second, the generation of NADH and FADH2 through FAO helps maintain spare respiratory capacity, which is frequently associated with treatment resistance. Third, this lipid oxidation interacts with the redox homeostasis [104]. Although FAO can promote electron flow and potentially produce higher levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), it also promotes pathways that maintain the integrity of the reducing antioxidant system (NADPH) and antioxidant strategy in an indirect manner, which allows mediated oxidative signaling without inducing catastrophic injury [105, 106]. Consequently, CSCs enter a metabolic state that favors dormancy, dissemination, and regrowth.

Lipid–mitochondrial coupling also influences epigenetic regulation [107, 108]. Acetyl‐CoA from the export of citrate, from the processes of beta‐oxidation, and from acetate utilization are substrates for histone acetylation, and mitochondrial regulation of one‐carbon metabolism and the triangular acid cycle control the methylation and demethylation reactions [109]. In CSCs, these metabolite‐sensitive chromatin changes may reinforce stemness networks, cellular plasticity, and drug‐tolerant states. Thus, the lipid–mitochondrial axis is not merely a fuel‐supply pathway but also a signaling and epigenetic circuit that influences gene accessibility and the persistence of stem‐like states.

This is an integrated behavior that is enhanced by the tumor microenvironment. Stromal cells, including adipocytes, mesenchymal cells, and immune cells, participate in metabolic symbiosis with CSC. Adipocytes have the ability to release fatty acids and exosomes that induce tumor aggressiveness through FAO‐related programs [110]. Direct co‐culture experiments demonstrated transfer of adipocyte‐derived lipids to ovarian cancer cells, induction of tumor‐cell β‐oxidation and promotion of metastatic tumor growth [111]. Tumor‐associated mesenchymal populations can potentially contribute to mitochondria donation uncovering ways to improve respiratory capacity and chemo‐resistance in recipient cancer cells [112]. Bone‐marrow stromal cells were experimentally shown to transfer functional mitochondria to AML blasts and leukemia‐initiating cells, increasing mitochondrial ATP production and survival following chemotherapy [113]. Macrophages and other cells in the immune system regulated by CD36 and lipid loading may be immunosuppressive and indirectly protect glycolytic CSCs niches while competing and redistributing metabolites [114]. These interactions demonstrate that CSC metabolism is not exclusively cell‐autonomous but is also shaped by the broader tumor ecosystem.

Those that are rich in fat are especially illustrative. Omental, mammary, marrow, and visceral fat microenvironments have an abundant source of exogenous lipids that may be taken up through CD36 and fatty acid binding proteins, esterified lipid droplets, or efficiently committed to beta (β)‐oxidation. Such sites are therefore potential metabolic sites for metastatic CSCs [25]. Similarly, cancer associated fibroblasts could secrete lactate, amino acids, or lipids which abrogate cancer cell biosynthetic burdens that enable CSCs to save internal resources for survival and invasion. Consequently, the nutritional landscape of metastatic sites is shaped, at least in part, by compatible metabolic partnerships between CSCs and stromal cells [115].

A systems‐level view makes obvious why single pathway targeting usually fails. Inhibition of fatty‐acid synthase (FASN) can potentially augment exogenous lipid uptake; blocking of fat according to FAO, which systemically decreases glutamine oxidation and leads to enhanced glycolysis; inhibiting oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) can lead to increased lipid droplet formation, mitophagy or stromal dependency; arguably even inhibition of stearoyl‐CoA desaturase‐1 (SCD1) could be overcome by alternative desaturation pathways of FADS2‐mediated sapienate synthesis [116, 117]. This compensatory mechanism was experimentally demonstrated by the identification of a FADS2‐dependent sapienate pathway that allowed cancer cells to bypass conventional SCD‐dependent fatty‐acid desaturation [11]. These compensatory mechanisms are not incidental, but are built into the network architecture of CSC metabolism, where there are parallel routes that sustain stemness in the face of the intellectual and physical disruption of one of the nutrient‐processing branches of the metabolism (Figure 5, and Table 2).

This networked action has implications in biomarker development. Measuring one enzyme at a time is at risk of misclassifying the tumors because tumor vulnerability may be in pathway coordination but not that of absolute expression [117]. More informative indicators may include combined signatures of lipid uptake, abundance of droplets, mitochondrial mass, respiratory reserve, redox buffering ability and composition of the niche. Single cell‐multi‐omics and spatial metabolomics have particular value, for they are in a position to outline whether the cells at biggest relapse danger are OXPHOS‐high, FAO‐high, lipogenic, or hybrid. Therapeutic design can then be refined from a mixture of empiricism to every specific genome [128].

In summary, the lipid flux and the mitochondria function are part of a system feedback bidirectional adaptive module. Lipids fuel mitochondrial metabolism, while mitochondria reciprocally reshape lipid utilization; together, these processes regulate signaling, chromatin state, redox balance, and microenvironmental interactions. This integrated outlook gives a reason why CSCs are still able to survive under severe stress and that their elimination has been tricky. It also implies that the most effective therapies in the future may target destabilization of the relationships between quality control of the substrate supply organs and organelles, and maintenance of stemness rather than targeting any one of the metabolic enzymes in isolation [129]. Metabolic Mechanisms Underlying Therapeutic Resistance

Therapeutic resistance is the clinical manifestation of metabolic plasticity of the cancer stem cells (CSCs). A cell population with the capacity to change between lipid synthesis, fatty acid uptake, fatty acid oxidation (FAO), glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) may survive many therapeutic interventions which are effective against more metabolically rigid tumors. The lipid–mitochondrial axis contributes to resistance at several levels, including membrane organization, ATP production, quiescence, immune evasion, and post‐treatment repopulation [130, 131].

Lipid‐mediated drug resistance primarily involves remodeling of cellular membranes. Increased synthesis of phospholipids, cholesterol, and monounsaturated fatty acids alters membrane fluidity, lipid‐raft organization, receptor clustering, and intracellular drug distribution. Such changes can reduce drug penetration and increase transporter activity of the drug, as well as increase survival signaling [132]. Lipid rafts cluster receptors and kinases that activate downstream anti‐apoptotic pathways, thereby reducing sensitivity to chemotherapy and targeted therapies [25]. By accumulating lipid droplets, CSCs gain an additional buffer that can sequester lipotoxic species and protect membranes during oxidative or pharmacological stress [133].

FAO creates a secondary defensive level. Because of the production of lasting ATP and reducing equivalents, beta‐oxidation can help resistant CSCs survive when the proliferation metabolism is suspended by therapy. FAO has been linked to resistance to chemotherapy, endocrine and targeted agents, and it is now reported that aberrant FAO is a recurrent cause of poor response in benign and malignant tumors [25]. By supporting mitochondrial respiration and redox homeostasis, FAO also helps cells tolerate DNA damage and retain repair capability after genotoxic stress. This is particularly relevant following radiotherapy, where the need for ATP, oxidative injury, as well as membrane repair are all heightened.

Mitochondrial adaptations provide an additional mechanism that reinforces therapeutic resistance. CSCs that survive treatment often have been shown to have increased respiratory dependency, upregulated mitochondrial biogenesis, disturbed fusion‐fission balance, and increased spare respiratory capacity [134]. These characteristics allow ATP synthesis to be maintained under conditions of impaired cytosolic pathways and, once again, inhibition of apoptosis by maintenance of membrane potential and limitation of cytochrome c release. In some contexts, therapy itself selects for OXPHOS‐high clones, thereby generating resistant populations that are more difficult to eliminate [134]. In AML patient‐derived xenografts, cytarabine‐persistent cells displayed increased mitochondrial mass, CD36 expression, FAO and OXPHOS, and inhibition of mitochondrial metabolism improved the antileukaemic effect of chemotherapy [135]. Mitochondrial retrograde signaling may subsequently reinforce stemness and pro‐survival transcriptional programs, converting an acute stress response into stable acquired resistance.

Dormancy and quiescence are very similar states of metabolic resistance. Many CSCs are resistant to therapy not only by actively countering a drug but also by entering into low proliferation programs whereby the agents targeted at DNA replication/mitosis become less effective [136]. Slow‐cycling JARID1B‐high melanoma cells were shown to upregulate mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, and respiratory‐chain inhibition prevented their enrichment during treatment [137]. These dormant cells nevertheless have the same need for energy and redox control, but at lower absolute levels of demand. FAO, regulated OXPHOS, and efficient mitophagy are well suited to this state because they provide maintenance‐level energy while preserving organelle quality [138, 139]. Upon release of therapeutic pressure, these cells are able to reactivate and regenerate the tumor.

Immune evasion is now also increasingly associated with the same metabolic structure. Lipid accumulation within the tumor microenvironment can promote immunosuppressive macrophage and myeloid phenotypes, whereas CD36 with its dependent pathways remodel both cancer cells and the immune cells infiltrating the tumor [140, 141]. OXPHOS inhibition can in some contexts enhance tumor oxygenation and make tumors more susceptible to radiotherapy or immune attack; however, mitochondrial inhibition may also result in inhibiting beneficial immune cells [142]. Consequently, CSC metabolism modulates therapeutic outcome not only by providing intrinsic tumor cell protection but also by modulating the environment encompassing the immunity of the treated patient.

Clinical observations also support these mechanistic linkages. Tumors with signatures of OXPHOS, FAO‐related genes or lipid metabolic markers have often been shown to have inferior response and shorter survival in modern studies reviewed in recent papers [143, 144]. Drawing on several contexts, the expression of CD36 is linked with metastasis and resistance, whereas a high activity of another protein, FASN, ACC, and SCD1 often correlates with aggressive disease [114, 140]. Despite the fact that these patterns do not validate a universal pathway in all tumors, they show consistently that there is prognostic and therapeutic interest in metabolic phenotypes.

Therapeutic resistance rarely depends on a single pathway. Instead, CSCs combine the membrane remodeling, redox regulation, stress buffering, quiescence, and micro‐environmental symbiosis for layering defenses (Figure 6). This helps explain why single‐agent treatments, despite producing substantial initial responses, often fail to prevent relapse. Residual disease is metabolically selected for cells that efficiently use lipids, maintain mitochondrial quality, and switch between biosynthetic and maintenance states under therapeutic pressure [145].

Multilayered metabolic mechanisms that protect residual cancer stem cells and promote relapse after therapy. The central CSC integrates several cooperating resistance mechanisms. Increased phospholipid, cholesterol, and monounsaturated‐fatty‐acid synthesis remodels the plasma membrane and lipid rafts, strengthening survival‐receptor signaling and altering drug partitioning or efflux. The practical implication of this is that therapeutic resistance ought to be looked at, at least in part, as a failure of the metabolic systems in current designs of treatment. Standard regimens are generally designed to debulk proliferating tumor cells but may fail to eliminate the adaptive circuitry that maintains CSC viability. Overcoming resistance will thus need regimens that interrupt metabolic pathways of efflux but leave tolerability and preferably strengthen anti‐tumor immunity intact. Therapeutic Targeting Strategies

Therapeutic interventions targeting the lipid–mitochondrial axis are progressing from conceptual models toward clinical translation, although major challenges remain. Within the lipid domain, inhibitors of fatty acid synthase (FASN), acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC), stearoyl‐CoA desaturase‐1 (SCD1), and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) are the most advanced classes. FASN inhibition is particularly attractive because many CSC populations depend on de novo lipogenesis for membrane biogenesis, signaling, and survival. The drug denifanstat (TVB‐2640) has shown a tolerable degree of safety in early trials in humans, and represents an example of a clinically advanced approach to lipid metabolism [146, 147]. Nevertheless, FASN blockade alone may be insufficient to eradicate CSCs when tumor cells can scavenge host‐derived lipids or redirect carbon through alternative biosynthetic pathways.

ACC inhibition reduces malonyl‐CoA production, thereby limiting fatty acid synthesis and indirectly influencing fatty acid oxidation [148]. Inhibition of stearoyl‐CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) is conceptually attractive because it inhibits lipid desaturation, a process that is involved in membrane fluidity, alleviation of endoplasmic reticulum stress, and Wnt/Notch signaling in CSCs [149]. However, counteracting mechanisms mediated by the transcription factor, fatty acid desaturase 1 (FADS1), are why monotherapy may lose efficacy with time [150]. These observations imply the need for rational combination approaches and not isolated single‐enzyme suppression.

FAO inhibition provides an alternative pathway by inhibiting oxidation instead of synthesis of lipid metabolism. Agents that inhibit carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) and other related pathways can reduce ATP production, redox fitness, and quiescent maintenance in resistant CSCs [151]. In pancreatic CSC models, blocking fatty‐acid uptake, storage, or oxidation reduced OXPHOS, depleted functional CSC populations, and enhanced the activity of gemcitabine in vitro and in vivo [152]. This strategy is particularly relevant in therapy arms that are persistent oxidative respiration reliant, and adenosine triphosphate dependent, and then in adipocytes supported metastases. Nonetheless, FAO is also indispensable in normal tissues and subsets of immune cells, raising concerns with respect to tolerability and context‐specificity [151]. Biomarker applications guided patient selection probably will be crucial for FAO inhibitor to have a therapeutically useful window.

Mitochondria‐targeted therapeutic strategies are equally diverse. Inhibitors of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), including strategies directed at complex II of the respiratory chain, are being investigated in order to disable respiration‐dependent resistant populations [153]. The major challenge to this is how to reconcile antitumor efficacy with systemic toxicity given the ubiquity and essentiality of the mitochondria. Therefore, the newer approaches have focused on better delivery, selectivity of dependency mapping of combinations, and lower dosages followed by combination use as opposed to maximal continuous blockade. ROS‐modulating agents represent another strategy. By increasing oxidative stress beyond the buffering capacity of CSCs or inhibiting antioxidant defenses, these agents may compromise CSC survival [154, 155]. The risk associated with this is, again, the collateral damage to the normal cells.

Mitophagy inhibitors are receiving attention because resistant CSCs often rely on the quality control of mitochondria to escape death from therapy‐inflicted organelle damage [156]. Blocking mitophagy while simultaneously using chemotherapy or radiotherapy may allow accumulation of dysfunctional organelles, uncontrolled increase in ROS, and reduced apoptosis thresholds [157]. Supporting this therapeutic concept, ovarian CSCs exhibited elevated mitophagy, and inhibition of PINK1/Parkin signaling increased their sensitivity to PARP inhibition [158]. However, because mitophagy may have context‐dependent tumor‐suppressive functions, careful treatment timing and patient selection are required. The same logic applies to agents that target fusion‐fission machinery; disruption in the normal dynamics of mitochondria may affect metabolic adaptation, but at the same time also affects normal tissue homeostasis conditions.

Consequently, combination therapy is the most promising general framework (Figure 7, Table 3). Lipid‐metabolism inhibitors may sensitize CSCs to chemotherapy by disrupting membrane remodeling and ATP production. OXPHOS inhibitors could be used to improve radiotherapy or immunotherapy by changing the oxygen consumption and redox state [167]. Dual blockade of lipid synthesis and utilization; for example, FASN inhibition combined with FAO inhibition, or SCD1 inhibition combined with mitochondrial targeting, may more effectively prevent compensatory escape. Importantly, combinations should be scheduled in accordance to the metabolic status of the cells [8, 168]. For instance, a therapy that initially pushes cells toward respiratory dependence may create a vulnerability to OXPHOS inhibition that was not present at baseline.

Nanotechnology and the delivery of therapeutics have the potential to improve therapeutic indices. Nanocarriers may increase intratumoral drug concentrations, reduce systemic toxicity, and enable the co‐delivery of multiple agents to resistant niches [169]. This is especially useful for the development of metabolic inhibitors for which the on‐target toxicities limit the development. Emerging approaches also involve the use of target validation by means of so‐called proof‐of‐concept studies using technologies such as the CRISPR gene‐editing method [170, 171]; synthetic lethality screening; and artificial intelligence (AI)‐guided drug discovery pipelines that consolidate transcriptomic, metabolomic, and phenotypic data to identify which metabolic pathways should be co‐targeted in specific tumors [172].

Despite these opportunities, there are still a number of barriers. Tumor heterogeneity means that not all the CSCs of a particular tumor have the same metabolic dependencies. Metabolic compensation may occur very quickly [173]. Normal stem cells and immune cells share several metabolic pathways with CSCs, creating challenges for therapeutic selectivity. Spatial metabolic gradients within tumors may make a single biopsy an unreliable indicator of pathway dependence [173]. For these reasons, future success will not depend on the discovery of a universal “metabolism drug” but rather on the creation of adaptive and biomarker‐targeted regimens directed against the prevailing metabolic fallback pathways in the respective clinical context. The most durable benefit is likely to be due to therapies that interrupt lipid flux, mitochondrial quality control, and bulk tumor population simultaneously [174].

Future Perspectives

Future progress in this field depends on resolving CSC metabolic heterogeneity across space, time, and lineage, which remains poorly characterized because most studies average signals across mixed cell populations. Accordingly, a central goal for the next decade should be to characterize metabolic heterogeneity at single‐cell and spatial levels. Single‐cell RNA sequencing alone is insufficient because transcript abundance does not fully reflect metabolic flux, substrate origin, or organelle state; therefore, coupling of single‐cellular transcriptomics with spatial metabolomics, lipidomics and isotope tracing, imaging mass spectrometry, and functional respiration assays will provide a more precise map of what CSC states are actually lipogenic, FAO‐dependent, OXPHOS‐high or hybrid [175]. Such investigations should be conducted longitudinally before therapy, during treatment, and in minimal residual disease because often the clinically relevant metabolic state occurs under therapeutic pressure rather than it is present at the moment of diagnosis.

A second major gap is the difficulty of distinguishing CSC‐specific adaptations from general stress responses shared by non‐stem tumor cells and normal regenerative cells [25]. Many pathways discussed here (including FAO, mitophagy, redox control and SCD1‐mediated lipid desaturation) are not cancerous per se; it's likely the difference in CSCs is the extent to which these pathways are integrated into regulatory circuits governing self‐renewal, niche communication, and relapse [25]. Future work should therefore be focused on causal experiments that establish a link between inhibition of metabolic nodes and extinction of tumor‐initiating potential and long‐term suppression of recurrence, as opposed to only short‐term inhibition of proliferation [1, 4, 5]. This approach will counter the issue of over‐interpretation of pathways that are active in tumors but not critical for sustaining CSC maintenance.

The tumor microenvironment is yet another area where the lack of resolution of mechanisms is inadequate. Current evidence strongly supports metabolic symbiosis between CSCs, adipocytes, fibroblasts, mesenchymal cells, endothelial niches and immune populations; however, it is incompletely described which metabolites are exchanged in which direction and at which time [176]. Key questions are whether fatty acids are continuously offered to CSCs or during certain periods of stress, whether mitochondrial transfer mainly takes place at the time of metastasis colonization, recovery from treatment or both, and which immune states are most affected by lipid competition or oxygen consumption. Answers to these questions will help determine whether treatment should directly target CSC metabolism, disrupt cooperation between tumor cells and stroma, or combine both approaches.

Artificial intelligence can provide useful tools for handling this complexity. Machine‐learning models have the ability to combine imaging, genomics, transcriptomics, metabolism, clinical response, etc. that may reveal latent patterns that may not be obvious in single‐modality analysis. In practice, AI may facilitate the classification of tumors into metabolically defined subtypes, predict therapy‐induced shifts toward OXPHOS dependence, and identify synthetic‐lethal combinations involving lipid, redox, and mitochondrial pathways [177]. Nevertheless, the usefulness of these methods depends on the quality, breadth, and biological relevance of the datasets used for model training; therefore, prospective validation and mechanistic interpretability must accompany any AI‐guided clinical strategy.

Translational development should also incorporate adaptive clinical‐trial designs. Rather than testing the effectiveness of metabolic inhibitors as generic add‐on therapies in unselected patients with advanced cancer, future clinical trials should consider stratifying patients according to metabolic phenotype and potentially using composite biomarkers such as CD36 expression, droplet imaging, respiratory signatures, and treatment‐associated changes in circulating metabolites. Serial monitoring will likely be critical, given that CSC metabolic dependencies may change during therapy; window of opportunity studies and neoadjuvant regimens and minimal residual disease settings may turn out to be more informative than late stage refractory trials where extensive evolutionary diversification has already occurred [178].

Finally, the field should move beyond the search for individual “master targets.” The lipid‐mitochondrial axis functions as a network; therefore, the clinically meaningful unit of vulnerability may be a pathway pair or triad rather than a single enzyme. Examples include lipid uptake with coupled FAO, SCD1 with alternative desaturation suppressing, or OXPHOS with mitophagy suppressing, or inhibition of the mitochondria with immunotherapy [179]. Optimal strategies will be different depending on the type of tumor and the phase of treatment, but the overarching principle is that relapse reflects persistent metabolic adaptability, which may be most effectively disrupted by simultaneously inhibiting coordinated metabolic nodes [180].

Overall, the translational outlook is encouraging but scientifically and clinically demanding. The practice of design and inventions in the field is moving beyond descriptive statements that CSCs are “metabolically flexible” toward actionable maps showing how this flexibility arises, when it is used, and how it can be disrupted. As these maps become more accurate, targeting the lipid–mitochondrial axis may progress from an attractive concept to a practical strategy for preventing relapse and achieving durable remission in challenging cancers. Conclusion

In conclusion, the persistence of CSCs is better explained by metabolic adaptability than by a single homogeneous metabolic phenotype. Lipid synthesis, uptake, storage, and oxidation provide essential structural components, signaling mediators, and stress‐responsive energy substrates, whereas mitochondrial plasticity maintains ATP production, redox homeostasis, and cell survival during therapeutic stress. The reciprocally intertwined nature of these metabolic pathways is responsible for the engendering of a resilient network to support quiescence, metastasis, immuno‐evasion, and relapse. Current evidence suggests that the lipid–mitochondrial axis is a central driver of therapeutic failure rather than a secondary consequence of tumor progression. Consequently, future therapeutic strategies should move beyond bulk‐tumor debulking toward biomarker‐guided combinations that simultaneously disrupt lipid acquisition and utilization, mitochondrial quality control, and adaptive metabolic fallback pathways. The integration of single‐cell omics, spatial metabolic profiling, and well‐designed combination trials will be essential for translating these findings into durable improvements in patient outcomes.
