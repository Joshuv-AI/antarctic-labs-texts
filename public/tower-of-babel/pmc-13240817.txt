Use of Proton Pump Inhibitors in Infants: Nationwide Cohort Study Based on the French EPI-MERES Register
Authors: Marion Lassalle, Sydney Sebban, Cécile Talbotec, Rosemary Dray-Spira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe the frequency and characteristics of proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use before the age of 1 year and its evolution over time in France.Study designThis cohort study, based on the EPI-MERES register built from the French National Health Data System, included children born between 2010 and 2021. PPI use was identified through prescriptions before age 1 year. Logistic regression models were used to assess factors associated with PPI use, including demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, medical characteristics, and healthcare use indicators.ResultsAmong the 8 222 100 children included, 703 891 were PPI users. The incidence of PPI use was 54.4 per 100 person-years among children with hospital-diagnosed gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) (+ 33.6% between 2010 and 2021), and 7.9 per 100 person-years in those without (+ 63.6%). In children without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD, PPI use was associated with known risk factors of GERD (extreme prematurity, aOR, 1.91 [95% CI, 1.82-2.01]; digestive diseases, aOR, 5.60 [95% CI, 5.49-5.71]; respiratory diseases, aOR, 2.73 [95% CI, 2.70-2.75]; neurological diseases, aOR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.56-1.62]), and with high maternal socioeconomic level (first quintile of deprivation index [least deprived] vs fifth quintile [most deprived], aOR, 1.80 [95% CI, 1.79-1.82]; and monthly salary of ≥€2000 vs beneficiaries of the complementary solidarity health insurance, aOR, 1.92 [95% CI, 1.91-1.94]).ConclusionsPPI use before age 1 has increased sharply in France since 2010, particularly in infants without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD. Beyond known risk factors for GERD, it was associated with high maternal socioeconomic level. Further work to understand the determinants of PPI prescribing is essential to reduce unnecessary exposure.Keywords: children, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, proton pump inhibitor

In infants, gastroesophageal reflux (GER) is the passage of gastric contents into the esophagus, with or without regurgitation.1 Regurgitation of ≥1 episode per day occurs in one-half of children aged 0-3 months, peaks at approximately 70% at around 4 months of age, and spontaneously resolves by 10-12 months of age.2,3

GER disease (GERD) is a pathological reflux defined by troublesome symptoms or complications.1 Symptoms are diverse and mostly nonspecific, including overt regurgitation, excessive crying, and irritability.1 Consequently, it can be difficult to distinguish GERD from GER.1 GERD is more frequent in children with underlying pathologies such as prematurity, gastrointestinal malformations, severe neurodisabilities, and respiratory diseases.1

Pharmacological treatment with antacids with or without alginate, or with antisecretory drugs, namely proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and histamine-2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs), may be considered if the disease interferes with feeding, growth, or achievement of developmental milestones,1 although there is very low certainty evidence for symptom improvement.3 PPIs are recommended as the first-line treatment for GERD complicated by esophagitis.1

Children under the age of 1 year are at risk of PPI overuse, likely due to overdiagnosis of GERD and heightened concerns regarding the severity of the complications. This population is particularly vulnerable to the potential adverse effects of these drugs.4 Close monitoring of PPI use in this population is, therefore, crucial. However, few investigations have been conducted to date. Studies have shown heterogeneous levels of use across countries, but consistent substantial increases over time.5, 6, 7

The aim of this study was to describe the frequency and characteristics of PPI use before the age of 1 year and its evolution over time in France.

Methods

Data Sources

The French national health data system (SNDS) covers 99% of the population (>67 million individuals) in France. It comprises individual data from the national outpatient claims database, and the national hospital discharge (PMSI) database. These databases have been linked since 2006 through a unique pseudonymized personal identifier assigned to each individual. The national outpatient claims database includes demographic data such as sex, dates of birth and death, eligibility for complementary solidarity health (CSS, which provides free access to healthcare for low-income people), and medical information including dates of medical visits, dispensed drugs and medical devices, performance (but not the results) of laboratory tests, imaging procedures, and other complementary examinations. It also provides information on serious and costly long-term diseases eligible for full health insurance coverage, coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10). The PMSI database contains data on public and private hospitalizations, including ICD-10 codes for diagnoses, dates of admission and discharge, high-cost drugs, and procedures.

The EPI-MERES register was built by EPI-PHARE from the SNDS to conduct observational studies of drug exposure in pregnant women and young children.8 It includes pregnancies that occurred in France since January 2010. Children are linked to their mothers with a matching rate of 99%. Algorithms were developed for the characterization of women, pregnancies, and children.9, 10, 11 Under the permanent regulatory access granted to EPI-PHARE, this work did not require specific authorization from the French data protection authority (CNIL). The SNDS is a strictly anonymous database, so informed consent was not needed.

Study Population

Children born alive between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2021, were included in this cohort study and followed from birth. Same-sex twins were excluded, because they cannot be individually distinguished in the PMSI database. PPI users were identified by a dispensed prescription (ATC code A02BC) in the first year of life. The indication for PPI treatment is not recorded in the SNDS. However, we identified children with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD (ICD-10 code K21).

Population and PPI Treatment Characteristics

Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, assessed at birth, were calendar year, sex, mother's age at the start of pregnancy, sibling rank, French deprivation index of the residential area (in quintiles),12 and mother's income level, measured by affiliation to the complementary solidarity health (CSS, which provides free access to healthcare for low-income people), and, for mothers who are not CSS beneficiaries but salaried, by their monthly salary.

Medical characteristics included prematurity (calculated based on gestational age: full term [37-41 weeks of gestation] or post term ≥42 weeks, moderate to late preterm [32-36 weeks], very preterm [28-31 weeks], and extremely preterm [22-27 weeks]), birth weight z-score adjusted for gestational age and sex (in percentiles: severe macrosomia [>97th percentile], moderate macrosomia [90th-97th percentile], normal birth weight [10th-90th percentile], low birth weight [3rd-10th percentile], and very low birth weight [<3rd percentile]),13 existence of digestive diseases other than GERD (esophageal atresia [CIM-10 codes Q390-Q391], tracheoesophageal fistula [CIM-10 code Q392], diaphragmatic hernia [CIM-10 codes K44, Q790], hiatal hernia [CIM-10 code Q401], achalasia [CIM-10 code K220], esophageal burn and corrosion [CIM-10 codes T281, T286], and others [CIM-10 codes B980, E164, K20, K22 - excluding K220 -, K25-K29, P788, Q393-Q399); neurological diseases (epilepsy, cerebral palsy, neurodegenerative, genetic and metabolic diseases, movement disorders, muscular dystrophies and myopathies, brain and spinal cord malformations, malignancy, others [codes published in14,15]); or respiratory diseases (asthma [CIM-10 codes J45-J46; ATC code R03], bronchopulmonary dysplasia [CIM-10 code P271], bronchiectasis [CIM-10 codes J47, Q334], idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [CIM-10 code J841], cystic fibrosis [CIM-10 codes E84, P75], lung transplantation [CIM-10 codes Z942-Z943], and others [CIM-10 codes J40-J44, J961-J969, Q31-Q32, Q330-Q333, Q335-Q339, Q34] identified in the first year of life by a long-term disease support or a hospital discharge diagnosis, or ≥3 drug dispensings.

Healthcare use was estimated using indicators for both mothers and children. Maternal healthcare use was measured by dispensing of preventive medicines in the 3 months before conception (folic acid, ATC codes B03BB and B03AD) or during pregnancy (vitamins, ATC codes A11; mineral supplements, ATC codes A12; antianemic preparations, ATC codes B03; vaccines, ATC codes J07), and use of assisted reproductive technology. Child's healthcare use was measured in the first 3 months of life by identifying a visit to a private general practitioner or a pediatrician, the number of drugs dispensed (count per ATC fifth-level codes; excluding GERD drugs), and a history of hospital stay (excluding stays with a GERD diagnosis).

Characteristics of PPI treatment at initiation were child's age, prescriber status (hospital employed or private practice) and specialty (available for private practitioners only), and any prior treatment with other GERD drug (H2RAs, ATC codes A02BA; antacids alone, ATC codes A02 A, or combined with alginate, ATC code A02BX13). During the first year of life, we calculated the number of PPI prescriptions dispensed, pharmaceutical forms dispensed (notably esomeprazole 10 mg granules for oral suspension in sachets, the only pediatric formulation available in France), and medical procedures suggestive of GERD investigation (ie, ultrasound examination, barium contrast study, pH-metry, esophagogastroduodenoscopy).1 Continuation of PPI treatment was defined by a new PPI prescription dispensed in the second year of life.

Statistical Analyses

The annual incidence of PPI use was calculated as the number of children dispensed a PPI before the age of 1 year divided by the total number of person-years among children born during that year.

Descriptive statistics were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables, because they were not normally distributed, and as counts and percentages for categorical variables. Given the lack of prior studies specifically addressing the determinants of PPI prescribing in the general infant population, potential factors considered clinically plausible were examined, grouped into 3 categories: medical characteristics, healthcare use, and demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. Logistic regression models were employed to estimate ORs, with their 95%. Variance inflation factor values were <2.5, suggesting no substantial multicollinearity between covariates. For each variable, missing values, if any, were considered a separate category. Data completeness was 95.5% for the study population. In the main analysis, the characteristics of PPI users were compared with those of nonusers of GERD drugs. We conducted two complementary analyses. First, the characteristics of either first-line PPI users (ie, without a history of treatment with another GERD drug before the start of PPI treatment), or second-line PPI users (ie, with a history of treatment with another GERD drug before the start of PPI treatment) were compared with those of nonusers of GERD drugs. In a second complementary analysis, we compared the characteristics of PPI users with those of users of other GERD drugs. Finally, in a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the main analysis after additional exclusion of children with suspected GERD, identified by continuation or initiation of PPI therapy after 1 year of age, or performance of a medical procedure suggestive of GERD or use of prokinetics (metoclopramide, ATC code A03FA01; domperidone, ATC code A03FA03)1 in the first year of life. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute).

Results

The EPI-MERES register contained information on 8 423 557 children born alive between 2010 and 2021. After exclusion of 201 457 same-sex twins, the study population comprised 8 222 100 children (1.4% with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD). Among them, 703 891 (8.6%) were PPI users (61 918 with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD and 641 973 without) (Figure 1).

Study flow chart. ∗Other GERD drugs include H2RAs and antacids with or without alginate.

The overall cohort incidence of PPI use was 8.6 per 100 person-years. It increased by 55.7%, from 6.1 per 100 person-years for children born in 2010 to 9.5 per 100 in 2021, with a slight peak in 2020 (9.9 per 100 person-years). Comparatively, the incidence of antacids with or without alginate use was almost twice that of PPIs (15.5 per 100 person-years in the overall cohort), and remained stable over the period. H2RAs were rarely used (decreasing from 0.9 per 100 person-years in 2010 to negligible use from 2020 onward). The incidence of PPI use was 54.4 per 100 person-years among children with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD. It increased by 33.6% between 2010 and 2021 from 43.1 to 57.6 per 100 person-years, with a strong upward trend between 2010 and 2014, followed by stabilization from 2015 onward. The incidence of PPI use was 7.9 per 100 person-years in children without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD, increasing by 63.6% steadily over the entire study period, from 5.5 to 9.0 per 100 person-years (Figure 2).

Incidence of PPI use in the first year of life in children born between 2010 and 2021, with and without inpatient diagnosis of GERD.

Table I presents the characteristics of the study population according to PPI use and inpatient GERD diagnosis. Of the PPI users with or without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD, 54.2% and 55.2% were boys, respectively. The distribution of PPI users with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD was relatively balanced with regard to the deprivation index. In contrast, more than one-quarter of PPI users without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD resided in the less deprived areas (first quintile: 27.9%). Prematurity was more common among children with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD (<37 weeks of gestation: 21.6% vs 9.0%), as were low birth weight (z-score <10th percentile 17.7% vs 12.2%) and digestive (15.0% vs 3.3%), respiratory (25.8% vs 15.6%), and neurological comorbidities (9.1% vs 2.4%). Most mothers of PPI users with or without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD were dispensed preventive drugs in the 3 months before conception or during pregnancy (87.4% and 89.3%, respectively). Children's healthcare use was measured in the first 3 months of life. Most had ≥1 outpatient visit (86.0% and 93.7%) and a median of 7 dispensed drugs; hospitalizations were more frequent among children with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD (51.4% vs 17.3%).

Table II describes PPI treatments. The median age at treatment initiation was 62 days for PPI users with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD and 83 days for those without. It decreased between 2010 and 2021 in both groups (from 76-59 days and from 106-74 days, respectively). PPI treatment was mostly initiated by a hospital physician in children with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD (62.7%), although a decrease was observed over time (from 65.7% in 2010-59.3% in 2020). In children without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD, treatment was frequently initiated by a private physician, either a pediatrician (47.6%) or a general practitioner (26.3%). General practitioner prescribing increased between 2010 and 2021 (from 19.9%-31.6%) at the expense of pediatricians (from 54.8%-41.2%). PPIs were prescribed as first-line therapy for 68.6% of users with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD and 67.9% of those without. Respectively, PPI users with and without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD were dispensed a median of 3 and 2 PPI prescriptions in the first year of life, and these figures remained constant over time; 22.4% and 15.0% of them continued treatment in the second year, respectively. The pediatric formulation was the most frequently administered form in the first year of life, for 95.1% and 91.1% of users with and without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD. The proportion of its use increased between 2010 and 2021 in both groups (from 70.5%-99.6% and from 55.6%-97.9%, respectively). More than one-third of PPI users (36.3%) with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD and 7.4% of those without underwent a medical procedure suggestive of GERD in the first year of life.

To assess factors associated with PPI use among children without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD, PPI users (n = 641 973) were compared with nonusers of GERD drugs (n = 6 609 184) (Table III). All variables showed univariable significance and were introduced in multivariate models. PPI users were more often born prematurely (vs full-term: moderate to late preterm, aOR, 1.45 [95% CI, 1.44-1.47]; very preterm, aOR, 1.49 [95% CI, 1.45-1.54]; extremely preterm, aOR, 1.91 [95% CI, 1.82-2.01]), and with a low weight (vs normal birth weight: low birth weight, aOR, 1.04 [95% CI, 1.03-1.05]; very low birth weight, aOR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.08-1.11]). They were also more likely to present with digestive (aOR, 5.60 [95% CI, 5.49-5.71]), respiratory (aOR, 2.73 [95% CI, 2.70-2.75]), and neurological diseases (aOR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.56-1.62]). Both PPI users and their mothers had higher levels of healthcare use than nonusers of GERD drugs. Mothers were more likely to have been dispensed preventive drugs in the 3 months before conception or during pregnancy (aOR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.21-1.23]), and to have used assisted reproductive technology (aOR, 1.33 [95% CI, 1.31-1.35]). In the first 3 months of life, PPI users were more likely to visit a pediatrician or general practitioner (aOR, 2.27 [95% CI, 2.25-2.30]), were dispensed a greater number of drugs (≥7 vs < 7: aOR, 1.61 [95% CI, 1.60-1.62]), and were more frequently hospitalized (aOR, 1.70 [95% CI, 1.69-1.72]). After adjustment for medical characteristics and healthcare use, PPI use was independently associated with demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. PPI users were more likely to be boys (aOR, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.11-1.12]) and born to mothers aged 25-34 years (vs <25 years: aOR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.15-1.17]). PPI use was associated with a higher maternal socioeconomic level, whether measured by the deprivation index (vs fifth quintile [most deprived]: fourth quintile, aOR, 1.07 [95% CI, 1.06-1.08]; third quintile, aOR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.15-1.17]; second quintile, aOR, 1.34 [95% CI, 1.32-1.35]; first quintile [least deprived], aOR, 1.80 [95% CI, 1.79-1.82]) or by the income level (vs CSS beneficiaries: non-CSS beneficiary, nonsalaried, or unknown salary aOR, 1.62 [95% CI, 1.61-1.64]; non-CSS beneficiaries with salary <€2000, aOR, 1.61 [95% CI, 1.59-1.62]; non-CSS beneficiaries with salary ≥€2000, aOR, 1.92 [95% CI, 1.91-1.94]).

Discussion

This study, based on the national EPI-MERES register, covered >8 million children born in France between 2010 and 2021. Nearly 1 in 10 children born in 2021 used a PPI in the first year of life.

Reliable prevalence estimates for GERD are limited due to diagnostic challenges in infants,1 but the burden of the disease alone is unlikely to account for either the overall level or the marked increase in PPI prescriptions. Eosinophilic esophagitis is another condition managed with PPIs, which has become more frequent. However, given the rarity of this condition, its increase cannot, on its own, fully account for the overall increase in PPI prescriptions observed in children before the age of 1 year. Although it may not be identified through the SNDS, the number of affected children in our study is expected to be very small; a meta-analysis reported an incidence of 3.5 per 100 000 children in Europe, with a mean age at diagnosis ranging from 6 to 13 years.16

The high level of PPI consumption reported in French adults17 was also observed in the pediatric population. Overall, the incidence of PPI use before the age of 1 years was higher in France than in the United States (0.5% in 2004),7 Norway (1.1% in 2020),6 or New Zealand (5.2% in 2012).5 We observed an upward trend, consistent with other countries5, 6, 7: the frequency of PPI use increased 7.5-fold between 1999 and 2004 in the United States,7 7-fold between 2007 and 2020 in Norway, and 2-fold between 2005 and 2012 in New Zealand.5 However, a direct comparison across countries remains difficult due to differences in study periods and baseline levels of use. Notably, in the United States, use may have increased from 2011 onward, after esomeprazole was approved in children aged ≥1 month.18 In France, the sharp increase observed between 2010 and 2014 can partly be explained by the market introduction of a pediatric formulation of esomeprazole, although, like all other PPIs, it was not approved for use in children aged <1 year.

Between 2015 and 2021, PPI use stabilized in France among children with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD. In contrast, in those without a GERD diagnosis, a slight increase persisted beyond 2015. This finding, in line with a previous study,19 suggests that recommendations issued at the national level in 2014,20 and at the international level in 2018,1 were insufficient to curb PPI use in toddlers.

The median age at initiation of PPI treatment decreased over time, supporting the idea that PPI use may have become more commonplace. In addition, although there is currently no clear recommendation regarding the stepwise escalation of therapy,1 PPIs were used as first-line treatment in two-thirds of users, and as second-line treatment in one-third of cases. Similar figures were reported in New Zealand.5 By 1 year of age, children had received a median of 2-3 courses of a PPI, a duration comparable with that reported in the United States.7 The proportion of prescriptions issued by general practitioners had increased at the expense of pediatricians. This finding could reflect changes in general practitioners' prescribing practices, and evolving medical demographics, with increasingly difficult access to pediatricians.

Known risk factors for severe GERD, namely severe prematurity, digestive and respiratory or neurological diseases,1 were strongly associated with PPI use, supporting the internal validity of our findings. Consistent with the hypothesis that more frequent interactions with the healthcare system may be closely associated with PPI prescribing practices, both PPI users and their mothers showed higher levels of healthcare use than nonusers of GERD drugs. Finally, after adjustment for medical characteristics and healthcare use, PPI use appeared to be independently associated with a higher maternal socioeconomic level, whether measured by deprivation index or income. These associations were unchanged in complementary and sensitivity analyses. It is plausible that parents from higher socioeconomic backgrounds, with better access to information about pharmacological treatments, may express more concern about their infants' symptoms, influencing PPI prescribing.

The most well-known and common adverse events associated with PPIs are headache, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and nausea. However, by directly altering gastric pH, PPIs can affect the composition of the gut microbiota,21,22 raising concerns about their use in infants without documented GERD, particularly during a critical period of microbiome development.23,24 Consistently, early life PPI exposure has been associated with an increased risk of respiratory and gastrointestinal infections.9 Observational studies have also suggested associations with immune-mediated diseases potentially mediated by microbiome alterations, including asthma,25,26 allergic disorders,26,27 and inflammatory bowel disease.28 However, some studies were conducted in small populations,28 exposures were not limited to infancy,25,26,28 and the results may be influenced by residual confounding. More recently, possible links with malignancy have been reported, although evidence in children remains limited.29

It remains essential to inform both healthcare professionals and parents about the benign nature of physiological GER, to avoid unnecessary pharmacological interventions. PPI use in children aged <1 year may be appropriate, especially for esophagitis,1 but is not needed if the symptoms do not interfere with growth and development,1 and there is no evidence supporting empirical PPI therapy for diagnosing GERD in infants.1

This study provides, for the first time, a detailed description of PPI use in children aged <1 year in France. Key strengths of this study stem from the quality, reliability, and comprehensiveness of the data source. The EPI-MERES national register, built from the SNDS, contains data on almost the entire population of children born since 2010 and their mothers.

Nevertheless, this study also has some limitations. First, it included children born between 2010 and 2021. Given that the international clinical practice guidelines were issued in 2018,1 the limited postimplementation follow-up precludes an assessment of their potential long-term impact on prescribing patterns. Future analyses will be necessary to evaluate more recent trends. Secondly, the indication for PPI treatment is not recorded in the SNDS. We considered separately children with an inpatient diagnosis of GERD, although this diagnosis was not externally validated by comparison with medical charts. The true prevalence of the disease was underestimated, because milder forms are often managed in outpatient settings. Third, hospital prescriber specialty was not available. Fourth, PPIs administered during hospitalization are not captured in the SNDS. Our study focused solely on outpatient use, which accounts for the vast majority of PPI use (96% of all sales of the pediatric formulation of esomeprazole in 2022). Nevertheless, the dispensing of medication may not be an accurate representation of use because patients may not strictly adhere to dosing instructions. Finally, parental anxiety, which has been reported to influence prescribing decisions, even in children without GERD,1 is not measurable in the SNDS. The slight peak in PPI incidence among children born in 2020, at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, may reflect this phenomenon.30

Conclusions

PPI use in children aged <1 year is common in France and increased sharply from 2010 to 2021, particularly among those without an inpatient diagnosis of GERD. Although PPI use generally remains of limited duration, it increasingly affects younger infants. Factors of a nonstrictly medical nature, namely, higher maternal socioeconomic level, could influence the prescription. Further work to understand the determinants of PPI prescribing is essential to decrease unnecessary exposure.
