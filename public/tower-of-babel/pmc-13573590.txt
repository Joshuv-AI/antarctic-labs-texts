Phenotypic spectrum and quality of life in pediatric Bruck syndrome due to FKBP10 and PLOD2 variants: a 2-center United Arab Emirates experience
Authors: Mohammad Hosny Awad, Rania Eladl, Asmahan Tagelsir Abdalla, Ibrar Majid, Mohamed Kenaway, Mason Alnouri, Sattar Alshryda, Manal Mustafa, Sarah Ehtisham, Asma Deeb, Mohamed Zulficar Mughal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBruck syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive form of OI caused by biallelic variants in FKBP10 or PLOD2, and characterized by fragility fractures with congenital or progressive joint contractures. We describe 13 patients from 2 tertiary pediatric centers in the United Arab Emirates and report exploratory health-related quality-of-life data using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL). Published molecularly confirmed cases were reviewed solely to contextualize the cohort. The cohort comprised 13 patients (7 female and 6 male; median age, 4 yr; range, 13 mo-14 yr) from 11 unrelated families, including 2 sibling pairs. Twelve patients had FKBP10 variants and 1 had PLOD2 variants. FKBP10 c.831dup was identified in 10 patients from 8 unrelated families of Emirati, Sudanese, and Indian backgrounds. Recurrent long-bone fractures occurred in 11 patients (85%), joint contractures in 6 (46%), and vertebral deformity in 9 (69%). Mobility ranged from independent ambulation to nonambulatory status, including among patients carrying c.831dup. The PedsQL domain scores (mean ± SD) were 47.2 ± 23.7 for physical functioning, 72.7 ± 19.3 for emotional functioning, 57.7 ± 22.5 for social functioning, and 64.5 ± 25.0 for school functioning. Physical functioning had the lowest mean domain score; these findings are descriptive because of the small, age-heterogeneous cohort, and the limitations of a single generic instrument. This 2-center case series expands the reported phenotypic and functional spectrum of FKBP10- and PLOD2-related diseases. The FKBP10 c.831dup variant was identified across 3 national backgrounds in the UAE cohort and has been reported in other populations, supporting its interpretation as a recurrent variant brought into homozygosity by regional consanguinity rather than a UAE-specific founder mutation. The variability in skeletal severity, mobility, and functional outcome, including among patients sharing the same variant, highlights the need for individualized multidisciplinary care addressing fracture prevention, mobility, spinal surveillance, rehabilitation, and long-term function.

Introduction

Pathogenic mutations in the FKBP10 and PLOD2 genes disrupt 2 essential components of collagen maturation, protein folding, and telopeptide hydroxylation, and result in a distinct autosomal recessive bone fragility disorder historically described as Bruck syndrome.1–4

FKBP10 encodes FKBP65, an endoplasmic reticulum-resident collagen-specific chaperone that assists in the folding and stabilization of type I procollagen. FKBP65 also plays a critical regulatory role in collagen cross-link formation by stabilizing and supporting the enzymatic activity of lysyl hydroxylase 2 (LH2), the protein product of PLOD2. LH2 hydroxylates specific lysine residues within collagen telopeptides, a prerequisite for forming mature intermolecular cross-links. Deficiency of either FKBP65 or LH2 therefore disrupts collagen processing at complementary points in the same pathway, producing a shared phenotype characterized by bone fragility, congenital, or progressive joint contractures, and abnormal cross-linking of extracellular matrix collagen.5,6

Biallelic FKBP10 and PLOD2 variants produce a phenotypic continuum that includes classical Bruck syndrome, characterized by bone fragility and congenital or progressive contractures, and recessive OI phenotypes in which contractures may be absent or mild.7–11 In this report, the term “Bruck syndrome spectrum” is used for genetically confirmed FKBP10- or PLOD2-related diseases with compatible skeletal fragility. Classical OI features, including blue sclerae and dentinogenesis imperfecta, are variably present.3,10 This heterogeneity complicates diagnosis, prognostication, and management planning.

Despite improved molecular recognition, patient-centered outcome data remain limited. Bruck syndrome differs from many other forms of OI because skeletal fragility may coexist with congenital or progressive contractures, potentially adding burdens in mobility, self-care, participation, and school attendance. A mixed-methods systematic review described the effects of OI across physical, emotional, social, and school domains.12 Pediatric OI cohorts using the PedsQL have generally reported the greatest limitations in physical functioning, although estimates vary by phenotype, age, respondent, and study design.13–15 A 2023 meta-analysis found lower overall quality of life in children and adults with OI than in norms or controls, with pediatric deficits also reported in emotional, social, and school functioning.16 Comparable cohort-level data in Bruck syndrome are scarce, supporting a cautious exploratory assessment of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in this population.

Documentation of phenotypic variability and functional outcomes is particularly important in rare skeletal disorders such as Bruck syndrome, where small cohort sizes and dispersed reporting limit the precision of genotype-phenotype correlations and the development of evidence-based management.17–19

We report a 2-center case series of 13 patients with genetically confirmed disease within the Bruck syndrome spectrum. The aims were to describe clinical, genetic, and functional features and to report exploratory HRQoL data using the PedsQL. A targeted review of molecularly confirmed published cases was used to contextualize the cohort in the “Discussion.”

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Dubai Scientific Research Ethics Committee (DSREC-SR-06/2025_04), the Mohammed Bin Rashid University Institutional Review Board (MBRU-IRB-2024-766), and the Sheikh Shakhbout Medical City Research Ethics Committee (SSMCREC668). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Informed consent

In accordance with the IRB-approved protocols, informed consent was obtained from all parents or legal guardians after full explanation of the study purpose, procedures, and confidentiality safeguards.

Patient cohort

This retrospective chart review included all patients with genetically confirmed disease within the Bruck syndrome spectrum who were followed at Al Jalila Children’s Hospital (Dubai, UAE) and Sheikh Shakhbout Medical City (Abu Dhabi, UAE) between June 2025 and June 2026. Demographic characteristics, clinical features, molecular findings, treatment history, and functional outcomes were extracted from electronic medical records. Two sibling pairs were identified (Patients 3 and 13; Patients 10 and 11); the remaining 9 patients were from separate families, giving 13 patients from 11 unrelated families.

Molecular diagnosis and variant interpretation

Molecular diagnoses were established through clinical genetic testing performed as part of routine diagnostic care, and results were retrieved from patients’ medical records. Patients evaluated at Al Jalila Children’s Hospital underwent targeted next-generation sequencing based on clinical exome capture (SureSelect XT Clinical Research Exome, Agilent) with paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform; reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) and processed through a validated pipeline for variant calling, annotation, and filtering across skeletal-dysplasia- and OI-associated genes (including FKBP10 and PLOD2), with Sanger confirmation of clinically significant variants of questionable quality. Patients evaluated at Sheikh Shakhbout Medical City underwent diagnostic whole-exome sequencing with probe-based exome enrichment and paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform; reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37), and copy-number variants were assessed by read-depth analysis. At both centers, variants were classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, of uncertain significance, likely benign, or benign according to ACMG/AMP guidelines.

Quality-of-life assessment

Health-related quality of life was assessed using age-appropriate PedsQL 4.0 Generic Core Scales. The parent-proxy forms cover ages 2-4, 5-7, 8-12, and 13-18 yr; child self-report forms are available for ages 5-7, 8-12, and 13-18 yr. The toddler parent-proxy form contains 21 items, whereas the older Generic Core forms contain 23 items.20,21 Patient 13 was 13 mo old and therefore below the validated age range of the Generic Core Scales and was excluded from PedsQL scoring. Valid domain scores were available for physical functioning (n = 11), emotional functioning (n = 11), social functioning (n = 11), and school functioning (n = 7). All assessments were parent-proxy report, completed in Arabic or English according to family preference. Scores were linearly transformed to 0-100, with higher scores indicating better HRQoL.

Targeted literature review

A targeted narrative search was performed in PubMed and RARe-SOURCE using “Bruck syndrome,” “FKBP10,” and “PLOD2,” supplemented by reference-list screening. Published individuals with biallelic pathogenic or likely pathogenic FKBP10 or PLOD2 variants within the OI-Bruck syndrome phenotypic spectrum were eligible. The review was not conducted or reported as a systematic review; the published cases were not treated as a co-equal dataset. Family relationships were recorded where reported, and patient-level details of the published cases were tabulated.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were descriptive; no hypothesis testing or gene-level severity comparison was performed. Categorical variables are reported as n (%), and continuous variables as mean ± SD unless otherwise specified; age is also reported as median and range. PedsQL summaries are exploratory because of the small sample, differing age forms, and respondent-mode considerations. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0 (IBM Corp).

Results

Cohort analysis (n = 13)

Thirteen patients (7 female, 6 male; median age 4 yr, range 13 mo to 14 yr) with genetically confirmed Bruck syndrome were identified. The 13 patients originated from 11 unrelated families and were of Emirati (n = 9), Sudanese (n = 2), and Indian (n = 2) background. Patients 3 and 13 are sisters of Sudanese origin; Patients 10 and 11 are sisters of Emirati origin. Clinical and genetic characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Genetic findings

FKBP10 variants were identified in 12 of 13 patients (92%) and PLOD2 in 1 of 13 (8%). The FKBP10 c.831dup variant (NM_021939.4: c.831dup, p.[Gly278Argfs*95]) accounted for 10 of 13 cases (77%), present in 8 unrelated families across Emirati, Sudanese and Indian backgrounds; this total includes 1 patient who was compound heterozygous for c.831dup and c.958_1009dup (Patient 4). FKBP10 c.972dup and c.354del were each present in 1 further patient. The single PLOD2 patient (Patient 6) was compound heterozygous.

Skeletal phenotype

Recurrent long-bone fractures occurred in 11 patients (85%). Joint contractures were documented in 6 patients (46%), and vertebral deformities in 9 patients (69%). Ocular or dental abnormalities were noted in 1 patient.

Mobility

Functional status varied within the cohort, including both ambulatory and non-ambulatory individuals among those with the FKBP10 c.831dup mutation. Functional abilities ranged from full independent ambulation to limited mobility requiring assistance. The clearest example of within-family variability was the Sudanese sister pair (Patients 3 and 13), both homozygous for c.831dup: Patient 3 has a fracture-predominant, ambulant phenotype, while Patient 13 has a contracture-predominant, non-ambulant phenotype.

PLOD2-related disease

The patient with compound-heterozygous PLOD2 variants had severe 4-limb contractures. Imaging at approximately 1 yr of age showed C3-C4 spinal cord compression. She subsequently underwent anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion followed by posterior occipitocervical fusion. Postoperative complications included paralysis and respiratory failure. She remains tracheostomy- and feeding-tube dependent, with severe scoliosis and joint contractures.

FKBP10 versus PLOD2 phenotype within the cohort

Because the cohort includes only 1 patient with PLOD2-related disease and 12 with FKBP10-related disease, it cannot determine whether PLOD2-related disease is generally more severe. Descriptively, the PLOD2 patient had the most severe contracture-dominant and cervical phenotype in this cohort, whereas FKBP10-related phenotypes ranged from full ambulation to nonambulatory status.

Treatment and observed outcomes

Bisphosphonate therapy, predominantly intravenous zoledronic acid, was administered to 11 of 13 patients. At the data cut-off, treated patients continued to show a wide range of skeletal severity and mobility (Table 1). However, treatment had been initiated at different ages, and the dose, duration, and pre-treatment disease trajectory were incompletely documented. Annualized fracture incidence, pain scores, standardized mobility measures and DXA Z-scores were not available in a uniform paired pre-/post-treatment format. Consequently, the treatment response could not be quantified at the patient or cohort level or compared between genotypes. Two patients had not started pharmacological therapy because of young age or recent diagnosis. Orthopedic interventions, including corrective surgery for deformity, were performed in selected cases, and physiotherapy was routinely incorporated to address joint contractures and support mobility.

Discussion

We report a 2-center case series of 13 patients with genetically confirmed disease within the Bruck syndrome spectrum, including exploratory HRQoL data. Published molecularly confirmed cases are used only to contextualize the cohort.

Cohort phenotype and intrafamilial variability

Phenotypic variability was observed within the cohort, including among patients carrying FKBP10 c.831dup. The clearest within-family contrast was between Patients 3 and 13, Sudanese sisters homozygous for c.831dup: Patient 3 had a fracture-predominant ambulant phenotype (Figures 1 and 2), whereas Patient 13 had a contracture-predominant nonambulatory phenotype (Figures 3-5). Intrafamilial variability in FKBP10-related disease was previously described by Schwarze et al.,22 and marked differences between siblings with a shared PLOD2 genotype were reported by Puig-Hervás et al.10 Our family adds a further example but does not establish a novel phenomenon.

FKBP10 c.831dup in the UAE: a recurrent variant

FKBP10 c.831dup, p.(Gly278Argfs*95), was present in 10 patients from 8 unrelated families of Emirati, Sudanese, and Indian backgrounds, including 1 compound-heterozygous patient. The variant has also been reported in several other populations.1,22–24 This cross-population distribution does not support a UAE-specific founder effect. Enrichment among affected individuals from populations in which consanguinity is common may increase the probability of homozygosity; however, the present study does not include a formal analysis of consanguinity or haplotypes. Haplotype analysis would be required to distinguish independent mutational events from an older shared founder allele. This differs from better-supported population-restricted founder alleles, including the Samoan c.948dup variant reported across 10 families.22

The cohort in the context of published cases

The targeted literature review identified 148 published individuals from 30 reports, representing approximately 97 families after accounting for reported relatedness (Table 3). Several large reports included multiple affected individuals from single extended kindreds, including the Palestinian family described by Essawi et al.8 and the Samoan families described by Schwarze et al.22 Family-level interpretation is therefore more appropriate than treating each individual as an independent mutation event.

Among published individuals for whom each feature was reported, fractures were described in 139 of 145 (95.9%), contractures in 109 of 144 (75.7%), spinal deformity in 108 of 127 (85.0%), blue sclerae in 21 of 132 (15.9%), and dentinogenesis imperfecta in 12 of 122 (9.8%). In the UAE cohort, the corresponding proportions were 85%, 46%, 69%, 8%, and 8%, respectively. These are descriptive contextual comparisons rather than comparative statistics. The UAE cohort is small, pediatric, and cross-sectional, while published series are vulnerable to referral, publication, age, and severity biases and use heterogeneous reporting standards.

FKBP10 versus PLOD2 phenotype in the literature

Published PLOD2-related phenotypes are heterogeneous and include severe contracture-dominant disease, cervical involvement, and multisystem presentations.10,25–27 FKBP10-related disease also spans severe contracture-dominant and milder fracture-predominant phenotypes. The present cohort therefore supports phenotypic overlap but cannot establish a general difference in severity between the 2 genes.

Bisphosphonate treatment and outcomes

Eleven patients in our cohort had received bisphosphonate therapy. The retrospective records do not support a causal estimate of effectiveness because treatment regimens and follow-up varied, treated children generally had established skeletal disease, and standardized paired outcome measures and an untreated comparator were absent. Current mobility remained heterogeneous among treated patients; however, mobility in Bruck syndrome is strongly influenced by congenital contractures, vertebral deformity, orthopedic complications and rehabilitation, and therefore cannot be interpreted as a drug-response endpoint in this dataset.

The published treatment evidence is similarly limited but suggests potential skeletal benefit with incomplete disease control. In a 2-yr prospective observational study, Otaify et al.28 treated 33 children with OI and 5 with Bruck syndrome using intravenous zoledronic acid every 6 mo; the combined cohort showed increased spine and hip BMD Z-scores, fewer fractures and less pain, and improved ambulation, although the small Bruck syndrome subgroup and non-randomized design limit disease-specific inference. Santana et al.7 reported improved DXA-measured bone density in 3 treated patients. Conversely, in 3 patients with PLOD2-related Bruck syndrome, Lv et al.29 observed increased BMD Z-scores during zoledronic acid treatment, but new fractures occurred and scoliosis progressed. A recent single-patient report described 3 fracture-free years and a total-body-less-head BMD Z-score of −0.5 after 5 zoledronate infusions.30 Collectively, these small observational reports indicate that bisphosphonates can increase BMD and may reduce fracture and pain burden in some patients, but they do not correct the underlying collagen cross-linking defect or established contractures and deformity, and skeletal progression may continue. Evidence is insufficient to define gene- or variant-specific responsiveness; prospective multicenter studies should use annualized fracture rate, pain, mobility, vertebral morphometry, and size-adjusted DXA outcomes.

Functional outcomes and quality of life

To our knowledge, this is the first cohort-level report of PedsQL data in the Bruck syndrome spectrum. Physical functioning had the lowest mean domain score. Direct comparison with published OI cohorts is limited by differences in age, phenotype, respondent type, language, PedsQL form, and study design; no inferential between-study comparison was performed.

The mean physical functioning score (47.2 ± 23.7) was numerically lower than values reported in the Brazilian and Chinese OI cohorts.13,14 This difference cannot be attributed specifically to contractures because the present study did not include a matched comparator or a prespecified analysis by contracture status, mobility, or treatment exposure. The wide SD is consistent with substantial heterogeneity but does not identify its determinants.

Mean emotional, social, and school scores were 72.7 ± 19.3, 57.7 ± 22.5, and 64.5 ± 25.0, respectively. Although these can be described alongside published OI data, the current study does not establish preservation of psychosocial functioning or determine the causes of between-domain differences. The Wehrli meta-analysis reported lower overall quality of life in children and adults with OI than in norms or controls, with pediatric limitations also evident in emotional, social, and school functioning.16 Explanations such as adaptive coping, family support, or restricted participation should therefore be presented as hypotheses for future qualitative study, not as conclusions from the present data.

Clinically, the findings support routine multidimensional assessment rather than a narrow focus on fractures. Physiotherapy, orthopedic management, mobility aids, school accommodations, and psychosocial support remain relevant components of multidisciplinary care, but the present study did not evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, N = 13 is small and limits precision of frequency estimates and any genotype-phenotype inference, particularly for PLOD2 where only 1 patient is included; formal statistical comparison between FKBP10- and PLOD2-related phenotypes is therefore not possible. Second, HRQoL was assessed with a single generic instrument (PedsQL) without complementary disease-specific or qualitative measures, restricting the depth of QoL characterization; future work should incorporate Bruck- or OI-specific instruments and longitudinal assessment. Third, the retrospective design did not uniformly capture bisphosphonate dose and duration or paired pre- and post-treatment annualized fracture rate, pain, mobility and size-adjusted DXA Z-score data; treatment effectiveness therefore cannot be quantified, and confounding by indication is likely. Fourth, the literature search was performed for cohort contextualization and is not a formal systematic review; heterogeneity in reporting standards across the source publications limits the precision of cohort versus literature comparison.

Conclusion

This 2-center UAE case series adds phenotypic, mobility, and exploratory HRQoL data for 13 patients from 11 families within the Bruck syndrome spectrum. Intrafamilial and interindividual variability were observed, including among patients with FKBP10 c.831dup. The variant’s cross-population distribution argues against a UAE-specific founder effect, but the underlying population-genetic mechanism cannot be determined without haplotype data. Physical functioning had the lowest mean PedsQL domain score; all HRQoL findings should be interpreted descriptively because of the small sample and methodological limitations. Prospective multicenter studies should use standardized fracture, spine, mobility, treatment-response, and age-appropriate patient-reported outcomes.
