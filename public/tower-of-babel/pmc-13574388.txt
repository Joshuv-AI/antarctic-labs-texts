Post-cannulation prognostic reassessment in ECPR
Authors: Taekwon Kim, Sang-hun Lee, Kyungsub Song, Jonghoon Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Static prognostic scores for extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation have been developed for pre-cannulation risk estimation and are frequently repurposed for post-cannulation decisions, where early treatment response may be more informative. We evaluated 3 widely used pre extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) scores (TiPS65, RESCUE-in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA), and Survival After Veno-Arterial ECMO) for in-hospital mortality in a single-center cohort of 158 adults treated with extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (2015-2022). Using a prespecified 24-hour landmark cohort, we assessed whether 24-hour lactate clearance (LC) provides incremental prognostic information beyond a static score. All 3 scores showed only modest post-cannulation discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.574–0.668). In the landmark cohort (n = 105; complete cases n = 103), LC ≥65% was independently associated with survival after adjustment for RESCUE-IHCA (adjusted OR 3.34, 95% CI 1.45-7.68; P = .004). Adding LC to RESCUE-IHCA produced a modest improvement in discrimination (Δarea under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.069) with mixed reclassification signals (integrated discrimination improvement 0.037, P = .032; category-based net reclassification improvement 0.178, P = .18). On decision curve analysis, the combined model showed a slightly higher net benefit at threshold probabilities of 40%–60%, although bootstrap confidence intervals for the difference included zero across all thresholds examined. These exploratory findings highlight the limitations of static pre-ECMO scores for post-cannulation prognostication and are hypothesis-generating: a pragmatic 2-step concept baseline risk estimation followed by early dynamic reassessment requires confirmation in prospective, multicenter cohorts before any clinical use.

1. Introduction

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) has emerged as a rescue therapy for refractory cardiac arrest, with recent randomized controlled trials demonstrating variable results regarding neurologically favorable survival.[1–3] Given the substantial resource requirements and clinical complexity of ECPR, accurate prognostication is essential for appropriate patient selection and clinical decision-making support.[4,5] However, prognostication serves different purposes at different time points and pre-cannulation selection and post-cannulation reassessment represent distinct clinical questions that may require different prognostic approaches.

Several prognostic scoring systems have been developed to predict the outcomes in ECPR patients undergoing ECPR. The TiPS65 was designed specifically for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) patients with shockable rhythms, predicting neurologically favorable survival.[6] RESCUE-in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) addresses IHCA and predicts in-hospital mortality.[7] Survival After Veno-Arterial ECMO (SAVE) score, which was developed for general veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) cohorts with cardiogenic shock, has been applied in ECPR settings.[8] Critically, these scoring systems share a common structural characteristic; all predictive variables are captured at or before extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) cannulation. By design, they were developed for pre-ECMO risk estimation and cannot incorporate the patient’s physiological response to mechanical circulatory support–information that becomes available only after therapy initiation. This raises an important question: Are these static pre-ECMO scores sufficient for post-cannulation risk reassessment?

Early physiological indicators have been proposed as markers of treatment responses in critically ill patients.[9–11] Among these, lactate clearance (LC) reflects the integrated physiological response to circulatory support, encompassing cardiac output adequacy, microcirculatory perfusion, and cellular metabolic recovery.[12,13] More recently, early physiological indicators, such as lactate kinetics, have been associated with outcomes after ECPR in multicenter cohorts.[14,15] However, an important and distinct gap remains; these studies do not address the post-cannulation performance of widely used pre-ECMO risk scores when repurposed for bedside prognostication, nor do they provide a pragmatic framework integrating baseline score-based risk with early treatment-response signals for structured reassessment.

We hypothesized that, among patients surviving to a 24-hour landmark, an early physiological response indicator exemplified by 24-hour LC provides modest incremental prognostic information beyond static pre-ECMO scores for post-cannulation reassessment. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the performance of 3 established prognostic scores (TiPS65, RESCUE-IHCA, and SAVE) in a contemporary ECPR cohort, characterize their discrimination limitations in a post-cannulation context, and explore whether adding an early physiological response indicator, illustrated by 24-hour LC in a landmark cohort, provides incremental prognostic information beyond these static prediction tools.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary academic medical center. We included consecutive adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) who underwent ECPR for refractory cardiac arrest between January 2015 and December 2022. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (Supplementary STROBE Checklist).[16] ECPR was defined according to the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization guidelines as VA-ECMO initiated during active cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).[17] The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Keimyung University Dongsan Medical Center (IRB No. 2024-10-047). Informed consent was waived because the study was retrospective in design, obtaining consent was impracticable given the high mortality rate and historical nature of the cohort (2015–2022), and the research posed minimal risk to participants. Data were accessed for research purposes in October 2024. Data were extracted from identifiable records by authorized personnel. All direct identifiers (names, medical record numbers, dates of birth, and contact information) were removed, and indirect identifiers (exact admission and event dates) were converted to time intervals before analysis. All analyses were performed using a fully de-identified dataset. The de-identified dataset was maintained on a password-protected institutional server, with access restricted to the research team.

This cohort is also being used for separate analyses examining how renal dysfunction modifies the interpretation of lactate-based metrics and for comparing alternative lactate summary measures. The present study focused specifically on the limitations of static prognostic scores for post-cannulation outcomes and the rationale for dynamic reassessment.

2.2. Data collection and definitions

Baseline characteristics, arrest details, and laboratory values were extracted from the electronic medical records. Cardiac arrest variables were defined according to the Utstein definitions.[18] Pre-ECMO lactate level was defined as the last arterial lactate level measured before ECMO initiation. Prognostic scores (TiPS65,[6] RESCUE-IHCA,[7] and SAVE[8]) were calculated using variables collected at or before ECMO cannulation.

2.3. Prognostic score validation

Because these scores were developed for different populations and endpoints, we evaluated their discrimination for discharge outcomes in our mixed ECPR cohort as an independent cohort evaluation (retrospective single-center validation), explicitly acknowledging the potential domain mismatch. TiPS65 was originally developed for OHCA patients with shockable rhythm, predicting neurologically favorable survival; RESCUE-IHCA was developed for IHCA patients, predicting in-hospital mortality; and SAVE was developed for cardiogenic shock VA-ECMO patients, predicting survival to discharge. For consistency, all scores were oriented such that higher values corresponded to a higher predicted mortality risk before calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The original score directionality and intended population are specified in Supplementary Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1.

2.4. LC calculation and landmark analysis

As an example of an early physiological response to ECMO support, 24-hour LC was calculated as follows: ([initial lactate−24-hour lactate]/initial lactate) × 100%. The initial lactate level was defined as the first measurement after the ECMO pump-on. We selected the 24-hour timepoint based on previous literature demonstrating its association with outcomes in VA-ECMO patients.[9,14]

To mitigate immortal time bias, incremental analyses involving 24-hour LC were conducted as a 24-hour landmark analysis, restricting the analytic cohort to patients who were alive at 24 hours and had paired lactate measurements. Outcomes were assessed from the 24-hour landmark onward. This landmark cohort represents a conditional analysis; results should be interpreted as being applicable to patients who survive the initial 24 hours of ECMO support.

2.5. Outcomes

The primary outcome was survival to hospital discharge.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, given the small subgroup sizes and non-normal distributions; categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical tests were 2-tailed. For ROC analyses, the outcome was defined as non-survival (in-hospital death), and all scores were oriented so that higher values indicated a higher mortality risk. Discrimination between prognostic scores was assessed using the AUC and compared using DeLong’s test.[19] Calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and calibration plots. An exploratory LC threshold was determined using the Youden index; this data-driven threshold should be interpreted with caution given potential optimism and requires prospective validation in independent cohorts. We performed bootstrap resampling (500 iterations) to estimate optimism-corrected performance. To address the potential loss of information from dichotomizing LC, we also modeled the LC as a continuous variable (per 10% increase). Nonlinearity was assessed by comparing models with restricted cubic splines (three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) against the linear model using a likelihood ratio test. Calibration was evaluated using an optimism-corrected calibration slope derived from bootstrap resampling. For the decision curve analysis (DCA), bootstrap confidence intervals (500 iterations) were calculated for the difference in net benefit between the models. To evaluate the potential impact of missing 24-hour lactate data, we compared baseline characteristics between patients with and without 24-hour lactate measurements; a sensitivity analysis using best–worst case bounds was performed. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess independent associations, with survival to discharge as the dependent variable. The model outcome was survival to discharge. Consequently, a mortality risk score of OR < 1 indicated that higher scores were associated with decreased odds of survival. The incremental prognostic value was quantified using net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). For category-based NRI, risk categories were defined as <30%, 30 to 60%, and >60% predicted non-survival probability (reclassification details in Supplementary Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 2). Confidence intervals for ΔAUC were obtained using DeLong’s method for correlated ROC curves. DCA was performed to compare net benefit of combined versus single-score models across threshold probabilities; net benefit quantifies the trade-off between true positives and false positives weighted by the threshold probability, with comparison to “treat all” and “treat none” strategies as reference.[20] Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Analyses were performed using R, version 4.2.0. Large language models (Claude, Anthropic, ChatGPT, OpenAI) were used to assist with language polishing. The authors have taken full responsibility for the content and accuracy of the final manuscript.

3. Results

3.1. Study population

Of the 192 ECMO patients, 34 were non-ECPR, leaving 158 patients in the ECPR cohort (Fig. 1). The median age was 58 years (IQR 47-67), 71.5% were male, and 63.3% had IHCA. The baseline characteristics stratified by survival status are presented in Table 1 (score distributions in Supplementary Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 3). Non-survivors had longer CPR duration (36.0 vs 27.5 minutes, P = .005) and were less likely to have IHCA (57.0% vs 74.1%, P = .047). Pre-ECMO lactate levels were similar between survivors and non-survivors (P = .476). Of 158 ECPR patients, 53 were excluded from the landmark cohort: 9 lacked initial lactate (six died before 24 hours, 3 survived), 39 died before the 24-hour landmark timepoint, and 5 were alive but had missing 24-hour lactate values. The remaining 105 patients with paired lactate measurements comprised the 24-hour landmark cohort. Because 24-hour lactate is structurally missing for early deaths and potentially informative among survivors, multiple imputations may be inappropriate; therefore, we conducted bounded sensitivity analyses under extreme assumptions (Supplementary Table S9, Supplemental Digital Content 4). The baseline characteristics of included versus excluded patients are compared in Supplementary Tables S4 and S9, Supplemental Digital Content 5.

3.2. External validation of static prognostic scores

All 3 static prognostic scores demonstrated modest discrimination in predicting non-survival in our cohort (Fig. 2). TiPS65 showed an AUC of 0.574 (95% CI 0.484–0.668), RESCUE-IHCA 0.668 (95% CI 0.579–0.755), and SAVE 0.594 (95% CI 0.504–0.681). RESCUE-IHCA demonstrated the highest discrimination but remained modest. Calibration analysis revealed an acceptable fit for RESCUE-IHCA (Hosmer-Lemeshow P = .342) but suggested possible miscalibration for TiPS65 (P = .018) and SAVE (P = .041) (Supplementary Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content 6). These findings suggest that static scores, although potentially useful for pre-cannulation risk estimation, showed only a modest ability to discriminate post-cannulation outcomes in this cohort. Sensitivity analyses restricted to the intended populations (OHCA-shockable for TiPS65; IHCA for RESCUE-IHCA) are presented in Supplementary Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 7.

Receiver operating characteristic curves for static prognostic scores (TiPS65, RESCUE-IHCA, SAVE) predicting non-survival. All scores demonstrated limited discrimination (AUC range 0.574-0.668). AUC = area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, IHCA = in-hospital cardiac arrest.

3.3. Early physiologic response in the landmark cohort

In the 24-hour landmark cohort (n = 105), LC was significantly higher in those who ultimately survived to discharge (72.2% vs 50.8%, P = .006). Notably, while pre-ECMO lactate levels showed no difference between survivors and non-survivors, the trajectory of lactate change after ECMO initiation diverged substantially between the groups (Fig 3; Supplementary Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 8). Using the Youden index, an exploratory LC threshold of ≥65% was identified. We present this threshold as an illustrative, interpretable reassessment indicator rather than a validated clinical cutoff point. Patients who achieved ≥65% clearance had higher survival rates (63.3% vs 33.9%). In complete cases with available RESCUE-IHCA (n = 103), LC ≥ 65% was associated with survival (unadjusted OR 3.44, 95% CI, 1.53–7.75; P = .003). This contrast: similar pre-ECMO values but divergent post-ECMO trajectories: illustrates the type of response-phase information that static scores cannot capture by design.

3.4. Incremental prognostic value of early response assessment

In the landmark cohort, multivariate analysis adjusted for the RESCUE-IHCA score (Table 2, Panel A) showed that each 1-point increase in RESCUE-IHCA was associated with lower odds of survival (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.88–0.99, P = .030), while LC ≥ 65% was independently associated with survival (OR 3.34, 95% CI 1.45–7.68, P = .004). Although reclassification estimates were imprecise and category-based NRI was not statistically significant, adding LC ≥ 65% to RESCUE-IHCA showed modest incremental signals in complete cases (n = 103): category-based NRI 0.178 (95% CI –0.09 to 0.51, P = .18; reclassification details in Supplementary Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 2) and IDI 0.037 (95% CI 0.003–0.071, P = .032). When LC was modeled as a continuous variable (per 10% increase), the adjusted OR for survival was 1.08 (95% CI 1.02–1.15), with similar discrimination (AUC 0.684; ΔAUC 0.053, 95% CI −0.010 to +0.181). A likelihood ratio test comparing models with restricted cubic splines against the linear model suggested modest nonlinearity (χ2 = 3.93, df = 1, P = .047), although the main conclusions remained unchanged (Supplementary Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content 9). The optimism-corrected calibration slopes were 0.977 for the dichotomized model and 0.891 for the continuous model, suggesting overfitting in the latter (Supplementary Table S11, Supplemental Digital Content 10). For reclassification, calibration, and DCA, an exploratory dichotomized indicator (LC ≥ 65%) was used to facilitate clinical interpretability. The combined model showed acceptable calibration in this dataset (slope 1.01, Hosmer-Lemeshow P = .605; Supplementary Table S8, Supplemental Digital Content 11), although estimates were imprecise given the limited sample size; optimism-corrected AUC was 0.686 (Table 2, Panel C). DCA suggested that the combined model showed a higher net benefit than the static score alone across threshold probabilities of 40% to 60% (Supplementary Table S12, Supplemental Digital Content 12); at lower thresholds (20%–30%) the net-benefit difference was negative, and bootstrap confidence intervals for the difference included zero across all thresholds examined (20%–60%), indicating modest and uncertain improvement (Supplementary Table S12, Supplemental Digital Content 12). We selected this range as an illustrative decision zone for post-cannulation reassessment; differences should be interpreted cautiously given the sample size and single-center design. Within this window, additional prognostic separation may support structured communication (e.g., time-limited trial framing or goals-of-care discussions) rather than mandate specific treatment limitation decisions (Fig. 4).

4. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 158 ECPR patients, we found that the established static prognostic scores demonstrated only modest discrimination for predicting post-cannulation mortality. In the 24-hour landmark cohort, adding an early physiological response indicator, illustrated by LC, showed modest signals of incremental risk reclassification beyond static scores alone. These findings highlight the potential gap between pre-ECMO risk estimation and post-cannulation prognostic reassessment.

The modest discrimination of static scores in our cohort (AUC 0.57–0.67) likely reflects their design scope rather than model failure. Notably, the prognostic value of LC after ECPR has been supported by recent multicenter studies,[14,15,21] including a 36-hospital Japanese cohort that demonstrated associations between modified 6-hour LC and neurological outcomes. Our study was not designed to replicate these findings; rather, we aimed to address why pre-ECMO scores perform modestly when repurposed for post-cannulation contexts and how treatment response indicators might be integrated into a reassessment framework. TiPS65, RESCUE-IHCA, and SAVE were developed for pre-ECMO risk estimation to predict who might benefit from ECPR initiation.[6–8] By construction, these tools cannot incorporate treatment response information that only becomes available after cannulation. External validation studies of critical care prediction models have consistently shown performance heterogeneity across different populations and contexts,[22] suggesting that applying pretreatment models to post-treatment questions may inherently limit their utility. In our cohort, TiPS65 showed particularly limited discrimination, consistent with its development in OHCA patients with shockable rhythm,[6,23] a population comprising only a subset of our mixed IHCA/OHCA cohort (Supplementary Table S7, Supplemental Digital Content 13).

The divergence between pre-ECMO lactate (no difference between survivors and non-survivors) and 24-hour LC (significant difference) illustrates the value of response-phase information. Initial lactate elevation in cardiac arrest patients reflects arrest severity and ischemic burden, factors already captured, at least partially, by static scores through variables such as arrest duration and rhythm. In contrast, the lactate trajectory after ECMO initiation reflects a distinct phenomenon: the integrated physiological response to mechanical circulatory support, including the restoration of end-organ perfusion and cellular metabolic recovery.[12,13,24] This type of dynamic information, by definition, unavailable at the time of cannulation, represents a potential prognostic dimension that static pre-ECMO models cannot address.

Twenty-four-hour LC served as a pragmatic example of early physiological response assessment, not as a definitive dynamic marker for ECPR prognostication. The modest reclassification signals (category-based NRI 0.178; IDI 0.037) suggest that incorporating response-phase information may offer incremental prognostic separation beyond static score prediction alone, consistent with the uncertainty from a limited sample size. Other dynamic indicators, such as changes in vasopressor requirements, echocardiographic parameters, and organ function biomarkers, may provide similar or complementary information.[25,26] The broader implication is that post-cannulation prognostication may benefit from a 2-step framework: baseline risk estimation using validated static scores, followed by early reassessment incorporating treatment response indicators.[27]

The incremental value of LC demonstrated here applies specifically to the 24-hour landmark cohort of patients who survived the initial day of ECMO support. This conditional interpretation is important: our findings do not address very early prognostication (before 24 hours) and should not be extrapolated to patients who deteriorate rapidly after cannulation. In clinical practice, the 24-hour timepoint represents a natural reassessment opportunity when initial stabilization has been attempted, and early treatment response can be evaluated.

Our findings raise the hypothesis that static prognostic scores, although valuable for initial patient selection, may be insufficient as stand-alone tools for post-cannulation risk communication. Whether incorporating early response assessment improves prognostic discussions will require prospective, multicenter validation before any clinical adoption. These findings support the development and validation of prognostic frameworks that explicitly incorporate post-cannulation physiological responses, rather than relying solely on pre-ECMO variables.

This study had several limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design limits generalizability and introduces potential selection bias. Second, our sample size (n = 158 overall; n = 105 in the landmark cohort) may have limited the statistical power for subgroup analyses and contributed to the wide confidence intervals in multivariable analyses. Third, we did not comprehensively assess neurological outcomes beyond Cerebral Performance Category at discharge. Fourth, the 24-hour landmark approach, while mitigating immortal time bias, restricts applicability to patients surviving the initial 24 hours; results should not be extrapolated to earlier time points. Fifth, the exploratory threshold of 65% clearance was derived using the Youden index and should be considered exploratory rather than definitive. When LC was modeled continuously, the association with survival remained significant, and allowing for nonlinearity did not materially change the conclusions. Nevertheless, externally validated thresholds are preferable for clinical implementations. Sixth, 24-hour lactate data were unavailable for 53 patients: 45 died (presumed before 24 hours) and 8 survived. Among the 8 survivors, 5 had initial lactate available (mean 6.1 mmol/L) but missing 24-hour values, and 3 lacked initial lactate measurement entirely. These survivors had more favorable baseline characteristics (lower RESCUE-IHCA scores; Supplementary Table S13, Supplemental Digital Content 14), suggesting that missingness may reflect early clinical improvement or workflow factors rather than random loss. Sensitivity analyses under extreme assumptions showed that the positive association between LC and survival was robust (OR range 2.72–3.80; Supplementary Table S14, Supplemental Digital Content 15). Our multivariable models adjusted for available confounders but did not incorporate several variables that were not systematically captured and that may influence lactate kinetics and outcomes, including vasoactive-inotrope dosing, hepatic function, ECMO flow settings, and the timing of neurological prognostication. In addition, withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy may have influenced outcomes among patients with poor lactate trajectories, potentially creating a self-fulfilling prophecy that could not be resolved retrospectively because standardized criteria and timing of such decisions were not recorded. Finally, LC may be influenced by factors beyond ECMO efficacy, including hepatic dysfunction, ongoing ischemia, and medications, which warrants consideration in clinical interpretation.

5. Conclusions

In this single-center ECPR cohort, static pre-ECMO prognostic scores demonstrated modest discrimination of post-cannulation outcomes, highlighting a potential gap between pretreatment risk estimation and post-treatment prognostic reassessment. The 24-hour landmark cohort, incorporating early physiological response indicators, as illustrated by LC, showed modest reclassification signals beyond static scores alone. A 2-step approach to post-cannulation prognostication, baseline risk estimation followed by early response assessment, may better align prognostic estimates with evolving clinical trajectories. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for additional available confounders (age, CPR duration, initial lactate, CRRT) showed consistent results (Supplementary Table S10, Supplemental Digital Content 16). Prospective multicenter validation is needed to confirm these findings and determine the optimal combination of static and dynamic prognostic indicators for ECPR patients.
