Factors Associated with Sedentary Behavior in Pregnant Women: Insights from Multiple Linear Regression and Random Forest Model
Authors: Lei Zeng, Rongrong Han, Jiayuan Ma, Lingling Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHigh levels of sedentary behavior (sitting for more than 6 hours a day) during pregnancy are a public health concern. Understanding the prevalence, patterns, and associated factors of sedentary behavior is important to develop targeted interventions, yet contemporary evidence remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence and patterns of sedentary behavior and identify its associated factors among pregnant women using the Systems of Sedentary Behavior framework.Patients and MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Guangzhou, China, from October 2024 to April 2025. We recruited 655 pregnant women from a single tertiary hospital who completed the sedentary behavior subscale and physical activity subscale of the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire, the Pregnancy Discomforts Questionnaire, the Physical Activity Neighborhood Environment Scale, and a sociodemographic and obstetrics data sheet. Multiple linear regression and random forest models were conducted to explore the associated factors.ResultsThe prevalence of high sedentary behavior in pregnant women was 57.3%, with a mean sedentary time of 44.11 (27.71) hours per week. Occupational sedentary behavior consumed the most time. Both models identified household physical activity, pregnancy discomfort, access to destinations, occupation, and insurance as significant factors. Additionally, age and residence were specific to the multiple linear regression model, while income was unique to the random forest model.ConclusionHealthcare providers should focus on younger, employed, high-income, and urban women who are at high risk for high sedentary behavior. Increasing household physical activity and alleviating pregnancy-related discomfort may be associated with less sedentary time, while improving access to exercise facilities and insurance coverage may also warrant consideration; however, these should be framed as potential targets requiring further prospective evaluation.Keywords: sedentary behavior, pregnancy, physical activity, discomfort

Introduction

Sedentary behavior refers to waking time spent sitting or lying down with energy expenditure below 1.5 metabolic equivalents of task (METs).1 Sitting for more than 6 hours a day in adults is associated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular disease mortality and is considered high sedentary behavior.2,3 In pregnant women, a duration of approximately 6 hours/day has been identified as the minimum cutoff for adverse maternal outcomes, especially hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.4 Therefore, this study adopted 6 hours/day as the cutoff for high sedentary behavior during pregnancy. Notably, sedentary behavior and physical inactivity are conceptually distinct constructs. Physical activity refers to bodily movement that increases energy expenditure above basal levels.5 World Health Organization guidelines recommend that adults, including healthy pregnant women, spread their activity throughout the week by doing at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity, or equivalent.1 Not meeting this physical activity guideline refers to physical inactivity.1

High levels of sedentary behavior are a significant public health concern. A meta-analysis involving over 1 million participants found that high sedentary behavior was associated with a 15% increased risk of all-cause mortality, even among the most physically active individuals.6 Apart from the adverse outcomes observed in the general population, high sedentary behavior during pregnancy can increase the risk of gestational diabetes mellitus, gestational hypertension, and postpartum depression, as well as a higher risk of preterm birth for the fetus.7–9 With the development of modern society and technology, sedentary behavior has become increasingly prevalent across daily life, including leisure time, transportation, and occupational contexts. Pregnant women are more vulnerable to high sedentary behavior due to physical and psychological changes, pregnancy discomfort, and social expectations.10 Previous studies have indicated that pregnant women spend 7.07 to 18.30 hours per day in sedentary behavior, accounting for 51.7% to 78.0% of their waking hours.11

The Systems of Sedentary Behavior framework is transdisciplinary and offers multiple perspectives on sedentary behavior.12 According to this framework, sedentary behavior is influenced by a cluster of determinants, including physical health and well-being, the built and natural environment, political and economic conditions, and other behavioral factors. Physical health and well-being encompass factors related to a pregnant woman’s health status, such as pregnancy-related discomfort and parity.11,13 Built and natural environment factors, such as residence and recreational facilities, may increase sedentary time in non-pregnant women,14 but their impact during pregnancy remains unclear. Political and economic factors, including insurance coverage and household income, can affect access to resources across socioeconomic groups and, in turn, influence sedentary behavior.15,16 Behaviors such as physical activity, sleep time, smoking, and drinking alcohol are related to sedentary behavior.11,12 Additionally, other sociodemographic characteristics (eg, age, education, occupation) should also be considered.11,16 Figure 1 illustrates the conceptual framework of this study.

Adverse outcomes can be potentially averted by small changes in sedentary time.17 Pregnancy represents a critical window connecting maternal and fetal health across two generations. Previous Chinese studies have predominantly examined the adverse consequences of sedentary behavior during pregnancy;18,19 however, research on the factors associated with sedentary behavior during this unique stage remains scarce. While one study in Macau, China examined demographic correlates of sedentary behavior,20 other dimensions of the Systems of Sedentary Behavior framework, including environmental, behavioral, and policy factors, remain unresolved. Therefore, this study aims to: (1) investigate the prevalence and patterns of sedentary behavior, and (2) identify its associated factors during pregnancy.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This cross-sectional study was implemented from October 2024 to April 2025 to investigate physical activity and sedentary behavior during pregnancy. The paper about factors associated with physical activity and sedentary behavior patterns was published elsewhere.21 The present paper focused on factors independently associated with sedentary behavior among Chinese pregnant women, thereby contributing to future research aimed at reducing sedentary behavior in this population. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Checklist.

Setting and Participants

This study was conducted in the antenatal clinic of a single tertiary hospital in Guangzhou, China. The hospital is situated in a highly developed district of Guangzhou and experiences approximately 3000 live births annually, with a predominantly urban and insured pregnant population. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach. All pregnant women attending the antenatal clinic for routine antenatal care during the recruitment period were consecutively screened for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were: (1) pregnant women aged 18 years or older, and (2) single pregnancies. Participants were excluded if they had serious pregnancy complications and chronic diseases, or had a history of psychiatric disorder. Notably, this study excluded pregnant women under 18 years because this population constitutes adolescent pregnancy in China, and their pregnancy risks and psychosocial factors may differ from those of adults.22

Assuming a 40.7% prevalence (P) of high sedentary behavior in pregnant women,23 a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a margin of error of d = 0.1×P, and a potential 10% invalid response rate. The minimum sample size was 627 pregnant women.

Measurements

Outcome Variable

Sedentary behavior is the outcome variable in the present study and was measured using the sedentary behavior subscale of the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ). The Chinese version of PPAQ has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with a content validity of 0.94 and a test-retest reliability of 0.94. The Chinese version of the PPAQ comprises 31 items, of which 5 specifically assess sedentary behavior.24 The 5-item sedentary behavior subscale covers leisure time, transportational, and occupational sedentary activities. Each item provides response options corresponding to various weekly durations (0 to 21 hours per week, or 0 to 42 hours per week). The total weekly sedentary behavior time is calculated by summing the duration indicated for each item. In this study, high sedentary behavior was defined as sitting time exceeding 6 hours/day (42 hours/week) in pregnant women.3,4 The Cronbach’s alpha of the sedentary behavior subscale was 0.630 in the present study.

Study Factors

Pregnancy discomfort was assessed using the Pregnancy Discomforts Questionnaire (PDQ). This 7-item questionnaire inquires about symptoms experienced by pregnant women over the past week.25 Responses were scored on a 4-point Likert scale, from “no discomfort” (0 points) to “severe discomfort” (3 points). The total score ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating more severe discomfort. The PDQ showed acceptable reliability in this study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.719.

Physical activity was evaluated using the physical activity subscale of the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ).The Chinese version of the PPAQ comprises 31 items, of which 26 specifically assess physical activity, categorized into four types: household (10 items), transportational (4 items), sports (8 items), and occupational (4 items).24 Response options represent weekly durations in three formats: 0 to 21 hours per week, 0 to 42 hours per week, or 0 to 3 hours per week. The total weekly physical activity time is calculated by summing the durations selected for each item, providing an estimate of the total time spent on physical activity during pregnancy. The Cronbach’s alpha of the physical activity subscale was 0.696 in the present study.

Built environment was measured using the Physical Activity Neighborhood Environment Scale (PANES). Among the 17 items, item 11 (vehicles in household) was excluded because it is not a built-environment attribute, in accordance with the recommended scoring procedures.26 The remaining items are categorized into 7 dimensions: residential density, access to destinations, neighborhood infrastructure, neighborhood safety, social environment, aesthetic qualities, and street connectivity. All items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (4 points), with 5 items reverse-coded. Dimension scores were calculated by averaging the item scores within each dimension, and a total PANES score was calculated as the mean of all valid items, with higher scores indicating greater built environment. The PANES showed acceptable reliability in this study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.799.

Covariates were assessed using a sociodemographic and obstetrics data sheet, which was designed based on previous studies and the theoretical framework derived from the Systems of Sedentary Behavior. The physical health and well-being domain included pregnancy trimester, parity, body mass index (BMI), planned mode of birth, method of conception, and history of adverse pregnancy outcomes. The built and natural environment domain included the type of residence. The political and economic domain comprised insurance status and monthly household income. The behavioral domain included sleep time, smoking status, and drinking alcohol. Sleep time was assessed by a self-reported open question: “How many hours do you usually sleep per night during the past week?” Other sociodemographic variables included age, education, occupation, and marital status.

Data Collection

The first author served as a research assistant (RA) at the study hospital, where the data were collected. Eligible pregnant women who attended the hospital for routine antenatal care were invited to participate in the study. Questionnaires were administered in a paper-based format. After providing informed consent, participants were asked to complete the questionnaires in a quiet room at the clinic. The RA remained on site to collect the returned questionnaires personally and address any inquiries that arose during this time.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25.0 and R version 4.4.1. All statistical tests were two-tailed. Significance (α) was set at 0.05. Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ characteristics using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. The amount of sedentary behavior was quantified based on time spent (hours/week). Pearson correlation was used to assess associations between continuous variables and sedentary behavior. The primary analysis treated sedentary time as a continuous rather than a dichotomous variable to avoid information loss, reduced statistical power, and obscuring true relationships.27

Multiple linear regression and random forest models were employed to identify potential factors associated with sedentary behavior. Multiple linear regression is suitable for analyzing linear relationships and offers strong interpretability, whereas the random forest model can identify variable importance and potential nonlinear associations.28 Based on the Systems of Sedentary Behavior framework, relevant confounders were considered for inclusion in the models. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted using a stepwise approach. Variable importance was assessed through the standardized regression coefficient (β). Model fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2). Regression assumptions were tested prior to model construction, using scatterplots for linearity and homoscedasticity, histograms and P–P plots for normality, Cook’s distance (< 1) and leverage values (< 0.5) for influential outliers, and variance inflation factor (VIF) values (< 5) for multicollinearity.

A random forest regression model was built using the randomForest package. Missing values were handled prior to modeling: continuous variables were imputed using the observed mean, and categorical variables using the mode. All categorical variables were one-hot encoded. A fixed random seed = 123 was used to ensure reproducibility. Model performance was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation. The dataset was randomly partitioned into five approximately equal-sized folds using the createFolds function in the caret package. In each iteration, one fold served as the test set and the remaining four folds as the training set. A random forest model was fitted on the training set with ntree = 500 and default nodesize (5 for regression), and predictions were generated for the test set. No explicit hyperparameter tuning was performed; default settings were adopted to maintain simplicity. Model performance was evaluated by average R2. Variable significance was assessed using the rfPermute package through permutation tests, ranked by the percent increase in mean squared error (%IncMSE). Partial dependence plots (PDPs) were used to visualize the marginal effects of significant factors on sedentary behavior.

Results

Characteristics of Participants

We initially enrolled 710 eligible women. After excluding 23 women who were uninterested in participating and 32 who did not finish the entire questionnaire, the final sample comprised 655 pregnant women (valid response rate: 92.3%).

Table 1 displays an overview of participants’ characteristics. The mean age was 31.84 (4.41) years (range, 18–46 years), with 36.8% in the ≥ 35-year age group. Pregnant women aged 18–34 years had a mean sedentary time of 46.65 (28.32) hours/week, whereas those aged more than 35 years had a mean of 39.75 (26.11) hours/week. Most pregnant women (36.8%) held an undergraduate degree, and 70.5% were employed. 93.9% had medical insurance, 40% reported a monthly household income of ¥6,001–¥10,000 (about US$840–US$1400), and 90.1% resided in urban areas. Regarding obstetric characteristics, over half of women were primiparous (59.2%). Most women had a planned pregnancy (80.8%), conceived naturally (88.5%), and preferred vaginal birth (65.3%).

Descriptive Statistics of Sedentary Behavior During Pregnancy

Table 2 presents the descriptive statistics of sedentary behavior. 57.3% of pregnant women reported engaging in high levels of sedentary behavior (≥42 hours per week). The average sedentary time was 44.11 (27.71) hours/week, with a corresponding energy expenditure of 62.38 (40.11) MET-hours/week. The sedentary behavior that was spent the most time was sitting while working or attending class, with a mean of 21.44 (18.96) hours per week, followed by screen-based leisure activities (eg, watching TV or videos) and other leisure activities (eg, reading, using the phone or computer). The least time reported was transportation-related sitting (eg, driving or riding), with a mean of 2.00 (3.21) hours/week.

Multiple Linear Regression Results

Regression assumptions were tested prior to linear regression. Scatterplots of standardized residuals vs predicted values showed no obvious violations of linearity or homoscedasticity. The histogram and P–P plot suggested approximate normality of residuals. Cook’s distance (range: 0–0.037) and leverage values (range: 0.002–0.104) were both below established thresholds (1 and 0.5, respectively), indicating no influential outliers. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values ranged from 1.030 to 1.133, all below 5, confirming no multicollinearity.

Table 4 presents the results of a multiple regression analysis of sedentary behavior. Using a stepwise approach within a prespecified multivariable model informed by the Systems of Sedentary Behavior framework and adjusted for relevant confounders, this study identified seven significantly associated factors. The final model was significant (F = 30.32, p < 0.001), explaining 24.1% of the variance in sedentary behavior. Pregnancy discomfort (β = 0.184, p < 0.001), access to destinations (β = 0.138, p < 0.001), employment (β = 0.095, p = 0.009), and urban residence (β = 0.082, p = 0.018) were positively associated with sedentary behavior. Compared with self-payment, having insurance (β = 0.100, p = 0.005) was associated with higher levels of sedentary behavior. Regarding maternal age, the age group of 18–34 years (β = 0.092, p = 0.010) was significantly associated with sedentary. In addition, household physical activity (β = −0.780, p < 0.001) was negatively associated with sedentary behavior.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to utilize multiple linear regression and a random forest model to investigate factors associated with sedentary behavior in pregnant women. Associated factors identified by both models included household physical activity, pregnancy-related discomfort, access to destinations, occupation, and insurance. Additionally, age and residence were unique to the multiple linear regression model, whereas income was specific to the random forest model.

Prevalence and Patterns of Sedentary Behavior Among Pregnant Women

The present study found that 57.3% of pregnant women exhibited high sedentary behavior, with an average sedentary time of 44.11 (27.71) hours per week, equivalent to approximately 6.30 hours per day, which exceeds the 6-hour threshold. This highlights the potential maternal-fetal health risks and the need for targeted interventions during pregnancy in China. Although national efforts in China have focused on promoting moderate-intensity physical activity, sedentary behavior has received limited attention. Importantly, high physical activity does not necessarily mean low sedentary time, as some pregnant women may engage in sufficient activity but remain highly sedentary at the same time. The findings of this study suggest that addressing sedentary behavior should be incorporated into routine antenatal care for comprehensive maternal health management.

We have compared these results with those from other countries. A previous Spanish study using accelerometers reported that pregnant women spent 8.54 hours per day on sedentary behavior,29 which is higher than the time reported in our study. The differences may be attributed to different measurements. The accelerometers are more accurate, while self-reported sedentary behavior may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Our result was higher than the 5.90 hours observed in a Japanese study.16 Although both studies used self-reported questionnaires, differences in the content and structure of the questionnaires may be one of the reasons to explain this discrepancy. Additionally, cultural factors may also contribute, as pregnancy is considered a period of fragility during which women are encouraged to minimize energy consumption to ensure maternal and fetal safety in Chinese culture.30 Further studies or interventions in Chinese settings should take cultural factors into account, particularly by engaging pregnant women and their families, especially partners, to correct the misguided social belief that pregnant women should remain sedentary, thereby effectively reducing sedentary time.

Further examination of sedentary behaviors revealed that pregnant women spent the most time on occupational sedentary activities compared with other types of sedentary behavior. This may be related to the fact that 70.5% of pregnant women in the present study were employed. Furthermore, pregnant women are usually assigned to sedentary work because of traditional beliefs and labor laws in China.30,31 The second most common type was leisure-time sedentary behavior, including screen-based activities, reading, and talking. With easy access to electronic devices, many women use them for relaxation and obtaining emotional support, which may increase their sedentary time. Lastly, women spend the least time on transportational sedentary behavior, possibly due to reduced use of public transport or shorter commutes during pregnancy.32 These findings suggest the need for targeted strategies by behavior type. Specifically, strategies such as stretching or exercising during work breaks may help reduce prolonged sitting at work.

Factors Associated with Sedentary Behavior During Pregnancy

Household physical activity was negatively associated with sedentary behavior in both models. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Singapore, which suggested that women who spend more time on household activities tend to be less sedentary.33 As a primary source of physical activity during pregnancy, housework may play a central role in reducing sedentary behavior. An exploratory nonlinear pattern was observed, with sedentary behavior appearing to plateau at approximately 25 hours of housework per week. As this finding is derived from a cross-sectional partial dependence plot, it should be regarded as exploratory and requires prospective confirmation. Physical activity is recognized as a “vital sign” for maternal health,34 and any intensity of it that replaces sedentary behavior can be associated with better health. However, both sedentary behavior and household physical activity are derived from the PPAQ. Since time allocated to activity necessarily competes with sedentary time, possible time-use and mathematical coupling should be acknowledged. This finding should be interpreted with caution, and future longitudinal and intervention studies using objective measures are needed to confirm whether increasing physical activity, particularly household physical activity, can reduce sedentary behavior in pregnant women.

This study found pregnancy discomfort was positively associated with sedentary behavior. When the score reached around 7.50, the impact tended to plateau, with only 15.3% of women scoring above this level. A previous study has also supported that discomfort during pregnancy can increase sedentary behavior.33 Possible reasons include pain, fatigue, and nausea, which may make it harder for women to stay active,13,35 leading to more passive sitting to avoid worsening the discomfort. However, few studies have explored the possible threshold effect in this relationship. When the discomfort becomes too severe, sitting may no longer help, and women may need to lie down to regain energy. Pregnancy discomfort is a modifiable factor and may represent a potential intervention target for improving maternal well-being and reducing sedentary behavior, which needs further confirmation.

Pregnant women aged 18–34 spent more time in sedentary behavior in this study. It was partially consistent with the findings of Hailemariam et al, who reported that women aged 25–35 were more likely to be sedentary than those over 35.36 One possible explanation is that older women are often multiparous,37 spending more time caring for children or doing housework, which may partly explain their lower sedentary time. These age-related differences warrant attention, as women in age of 18–34 years are in the early stage of their reproductive years, sedentary behavior in this group may affect current outcomes, future fertility, and long-term health,7–9 although these associations should be explored further. Moreover, employed pregnant women tended to have longer periods of sedentary time, consistent with previous research.16 However, a study from Macau found the opposite result.20 This difference may be speculatively related to gestational age. Over half of the Macau participants were in the third trimester, when sitting time might decrease due to fetal pressure, edema, or medical advice for blood sugar control and birth preparation. In contrast, women in the first and second trimesters might lack awareness and maintain sedentary work routines. Although age and occupation are not easily modifiable, they are useful for identifying high-risk populations.

This study revealed a positive correlation between access to destinations and sedentary behavior. Access to destinations is a dimension of the built environment that reflects the convenience of walking to daily destinations.38 Similar findings have been reported among Belgian adults and Ethiopian women, where higher walkability was associated with greater sedentary time.36,39 However, prior research also found opposite results, showing that better access to destinations may link with lower sedentary time.40 These mixed findings suggest that the relationship between access to destinations and sedentary behavior may vary across settings. Guangzhou is a high-density city with convenient public transportation and community infrastructure, where daily destinations are often within a short walking distance in most districts. For pregnant women, high accessibility may co-occur with less physical activity for reaching destinations, and this may partly explain the higher sedentary time observed. Residence is another environmental factor. Multiple linear regression showed that urban pregnant women spend more time sitting. Kaur et al reported a similar result,41 noting urban women tended to engage in less household and sports activities but more occupational activities during pregnancy. Thus, efforts to reduce sedentary behavior should focus more on urban pregnant women. Further studies are needed to determine whether built-environment features that promote physical activity, such as activity signs, exercise facilities, and accessible walkways, effectively reduce sedentary time during pregnancy.

This study found that women with medical insurance had longer sedentary time. At the macro level, insurance provides higher reimbursement and postnatal allowances, offering more substantial financial support.42 A previous study has shown that individuals with medical insurance tend to exhibit more extended periods of sedentary time.15 One possibility is that insured women are more likely to be employed, and office-based work often involves longer periods of sitting. Another explanation is that financial compensation may reduce perceived health risks. With fewer economic concerns over potential complications, pregnant women may lack motivation to adopt healthier behaviors.43 Similarly, the random forest model also revealed that women with higher household income spent more time being sedentary. This suggests that, in clinical practice, greater attention should be paid to pregnant women with higher socioeconomic status.

Implications for Practice and Research

This study revealed a high prevalence of sedentary behavior among pregnant women, underscoring the need to reduce sedentary time. Occupational sedentary behavior is considered a primary concern. When prolonged sitting is unavoidable, pregnant women should be encouraged to interrupt their sitting time by standing or engaging in physical activity. Companies are also recommended to promote activity at work, such as doing stretching exercises during breaks. For leisure-time sedentary behavior, interventions should focus on raising health awareness, reducing screen time, and promoting more physical activity. Future practice for Chinese pregnant women may also need to consider cultural factors, particularly in correcting the misconception that pregnant women should remain sedentary.

Eight associated factors were explored in this study. Non-modifiable factors, including age, occupation, income, and residence, may help identify women at high risk of sedentary behavior. Modifiable factors, especially household physical activity and pregnancy discomfort, may represent key targets for future research. Pregnant women can be encouraged to engage in moderate-intensity physical activity, especially light household chores, such as sweeping the floor, drying clothes, and washing dishes. Although pregnancy discomfort assessment is part of routine antenatal visits, its potential role in sedentary behavior interventions deserves further investigation. While these factors are modifiable, others are structural or contextual (including access to destinations and insurance), suggesting the need for multi-level interventions. Reducing sedentary behavior during pregnancy not only benefits short-term maternal-fetal health but also helps women foster lifelong healthy habits. However, it should be emphasized that these implications are exploratory and require future testing.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. Firstly, it used a relatively large sample size, enhancing the robustness of the findings in pregnant women. Secondly, under the Systems of Sedentary Behavior framework, non-modifiable and modifiable factors were considered comprehensively in this study. Thirdly, from the combined insights of multiple linear regression and random forest model, important factors and potential nonlinear relationships can be identified, improving the interpretability of the results.

Several limitations should also be noted. Firstly, this study used a cross-sectional design. The timeframe is sufficient for estimating contemporaneous sedentary behavior and its correlates, but cannot establish temporality, causation, or true prospective prediction. Therefore, findings of this study should be interpreted with caution. Longitudinal studies are necessary to further confirm these findings. Secondly, self-reported sedentary behavior using the PPAQ may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Both sedentary behavior and household physical activity were derived from the PPAQ, possible time-use and mathematical coupling should also be acknowledged when interpreting key findings. Future research should consider using objective measures such as accelerometers and wearable devices to assess. Thirdly, participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital in a highly developed area of Guangzhou, with a predominantly urban and insured population. These may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future multi-center studies involving diverse geographic and socioeconomic populations are needed to confirm the external validity of our results. Lastly, the R2 of both models were modest. This is consistent with previous behavioral research, as sedentary behavior is influenced by multiple factors. The factors identified in this study explain part of the variability, which further emphasizes that sedentary behavior cannot be fully understood at a single level. Future studies should include additional factors and explore complex interactions among variables.

Conclusion

This study found that behavioral, physical health and well-being, environmental, political, and economic factors were associated with sedentary behavior among pregnant women in the Chinese cultural context. Specifically, younger age, employment, higher income, and urban residence were associated with higher sedentary behavior, while household physical activity and pregnancy discomfort were also correlates. Access to destinations and insurance showed positive associations with sedentary behavior that require further prospective evaluation.
