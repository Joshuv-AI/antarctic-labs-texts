Deciphering differential mRNA and lncRNA expression profiles in response to PEG simulated drought stress in cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.)

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Studied the transcriptomic landscape and identified the differential expression of genes, mRNAs and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs).•A total of 155 transcription factors (TFs) from various families were under drought stress.•Identified 774 differentially expressed lncRNAs, in response to drought that regulated gene expression through cis and trans mechanisms.•WAT1-related protein, WRKY transcription factor, and pathogenesis-related protein 1 are possible candidate genes.•A comprehensive database, ‘CsDTDb; https://backlin.cabgrid.res.in/csdtdb’ developed as readt reference.

Introduction

Drought stress poses a significant threat to global agricultural productivity and food security. So, developing drought-resistant crop cultivars has therefore become an urgent priority. Extensive research has documented drought tolerance across various taxa, including cucumber; however, further investigation is needed to fully elucidate the intricate molecular pathways unique to Cucumis sativus. Under drought stress, plants close stomata to prevent water loss, accumulate compatible solutes to support osmotic adjustment, enhance antioxidant systems, and activate molecular chaperones to protect against oxidative damage. Water deficit also directly reduces photosynthetic rates.1 Signal transduction begins when stress-induced signals such as osmotic imbalance, membrane turgor changes, or hormonal cues are detected by plasma membrane receptors (RLKs, GPCRs, hormone receptors). This perception triggers secondary messengers (calcium, ROS, abscisic acid) that activate downstream components, including CDPKs, MAPKs, and transcription factors, leading to metabolic changes and the activation of genes and proteins involved in tolerance responses, such as protein protection, antioxidation, osmotic regulation, and stomatal adjustment.2 Under water deficit, the plant increases ABA synthesis, which is perceived by PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors. These receptors inhibit PP2C phosphatases, allowing SnRK2 kinases to phosphorylate and activate key transcription factors (TFs) such as AREB/ABFs (ABA-responsive element binding factors). These TFs bind to the promoters of drought-responsive genes, such as RD29A and COR15A, initiating a cascade that activates ion channels (e.g., SLAC1) and inhibits inward-rectifying K+ channels. This leads to an efflux of ions and water, decreasing turgor pressure and causing stomatal closure.3, 4 As ABA signaling activates the NADPH oxidase (Rboh) at the plasma membrane, a controlled “oxidative burst” occurs. This H2O2 serves as a secondary messenger to activate Ca2+ channels, thereby triggering the ion efflux required for stomatal closure. To prevent this ROS signaling from escalating into cellular damage, transcription factors (TFs) like DREB2A (Dehydration-Responsive Element-Binding) and WRKYs upregulate the expression of antioxidant enzymes, including Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), Catalase (CAT), and Ascorbate Peroxidase (APX), protecting the cell from oxidative stress.4 Simultaneously, osmotic adjustment is managed by TFs that divert metabolic flux toward the production of compatible solutes. For instance, MYB transcription factors and bZIPs regulate the expression of the P5CS (Delta-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase) gene, which is the rate-limiting enzyme in proline biosynthesis. The accumulation of proline and soluble sugars (like trehalose) lowers the cellular osmotic potential, allowing the plant to maintain turgor pressure even as soil water potential decreases.5.

Identifying candidate genes that regulate drought response can provide valuable gene resources and a genetic foundation for molecular breeding of cucumbers that withstand drought. Transcriptome analysis is essential for understanding drought stress, as it enables the genome-wide identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and reveals metabolic pathways, stress signaling mechanisms, and biosynthetic processes. The use of high-throughput sequencing assays has enhanced the detection specificity of ncRNAs, and transcriptomic analyses have been extensively employed to explore the response of cucumbers to stress conditions.6 RNA sequencing has been instrumental in identifying differentially regulated genes under various abiotic stress, offering new perspectives on cucumber stress responses.6

Noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) are a category of non-protein-coding RNAs. Based on their structure, ncRNAs are classified into linear ncRNAs (linear-ncRNAs) and circular ncRNAs (circRNAs). Linear ncRNAs include microRNAs (miRNAs) and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs). Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a class of RNA transcripts that surpass 200 nucleotides in length and do not code for protein but regulate plant response to unfavorable conditions at the post-transcriptional and translational levels. MicroRNAs are small, non-coding RNA molecules of 21–25 nucleotides that serve as key regulators of gene expression in plants in the same way. Competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks are complex, post-transcriptional regulatory systems where various RNA transcripts—including long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), circular RNAs (circRNAs), and messenger RNAs (mRNAs)—compete for binding to a shared pool of microRNAs (miRNAs miRNAs execute post-transcriptional control by targeting drought-responsive genes through mRNA cleavage or translational repression, regulating key processes including ABA signaling, stomatal closure, ROS detoxification, and osmotic adjustment.7 Circular RNAs (circRNAs) function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) by sequestering microRNAs (miRNAs), which shields target mRNAs from degradation (Function as sponge). These circRNAs influence specific miRNA–mRNA modules and signaling pathways to enhance drought resilience—improving survival, water retention, and antioxidant defenses during periods of water scarcity.8, 9

Drought-responsive miRNA families such as miR169, miR398, and miR393 modulate transcription factors, antioxidant enzymes, and hormone receptors to enhance stress adaptation.10 Numerous miRNAs in tomatoes have been shown to respond to drought stress, regulating stomatal activity, root development, and hormone and secondary metabolite biosynthesis.11 To date, the functions of various lncRNAs, such as regulating membrane repair, plant hormone signal transduction, kinase signaling, stomatal opening, and ascorbate/aldarate metabolism, and acting as central nodes of the competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network, have been elucidated.12 The drought-induced lncRNA DRIR enhances tolerance by upregulating ABA-responsive genes, aquaporins, and stress transcription factors, thereby coordinating stomatal behavior and water transport in Arabidopsis.13 lncRNA DANA1 promotes drought tolerance through epigenetic regulation by recruiting histone deacetylase complexes to suppress ABA catabolism and elevate endogenous ABA levels, and long non-coding RNA DANA2 positively regulates drought tolerance by recruiting ethylene responsive factors to promote JMJ29-mediated histone demethylation in Arabidopsis.14, 15

The cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) is one of the most widely grown worldwide horticultural crops, which is very sensitive to drought stress due to its high-water demand (400–600 nm), particularly at the fruiting stage, shallow root system, and unfavorable soil moisture conditions, which may limit the quality and thus limit the yield parameters.16 Building on existing physiological studies of cucumber, there remains a significant opportunity to refine the current understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying drought resilience in this species. Previous transcriptomics studies have been done in cucumber for drought, but they have some limitations: Wang et al. (2018) focused on analyzing the mRNA-based transcriptomic responses of contrasting cucumber genotypes (drought-tolerant L-9 and drought-sensitive L-4) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and key pathways like ABA signaling and sucrose metabolism.17 Du et al. (2021) examined the response of cucumber seeds during post-germinative development, integrating transcriptomics (mRNA) and proteomics to highlight the role of arginine metabolism and ethylene signaling.18 While these studies provide extensive data on protein-coding genes (mRNAs) and proteins, they leave a significant gap regarding non-coding regulatory elements: neither study explores the role of long non-coding RNAs, which are known to be vital regulators of plant stress responses. Without lncRNA data, the full regulatory architecture involving lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA interactions remains incomplete in the context of cucumber drought tolerance. Wang et al. (2018) used contrasting genotypes to identify the basis of drought tolerance, but it is missing in the study by Du et al. (2021).17, 18 Again, their findings are not sufficiently backed by a physiological basis/mechanism.

The current study addresses these gaps by comparing transcriptomic landscapes of drought-susceptible and drought-tolerant cucumber genotypes during the vegetative stage and by identifying differentially expressed genes, transcription factors, mRNAs, lncRNAs, and miRNAs. This research seeks to integrate physiological responses with their underlying molecular mechanisms, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the plant’s stress profile. These findings will provide theoretical foundations for molecular breeding programs, enabling the development of drought-resistant cucumber varieties through marker-assisted selection and genetic engineering by identifying candidate genes and regulatory networks.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and drought stress treatment

Two cucumber genotypes, WBC-23-2, with drought tolerance, and DGPC-59, with extreme drought sensitivity, were used for RNA-seq analysis. These two lines were selected based on our previous studies, as reported by Das et al. (2023).19 WBC-23-2 is a breeding line collected from West Bengal, India, and maintained at ICAR-IARI, New Delhi, while DGPC-59 (DG-59) is a gynoecious picking cucumber inbred developed at ICAR-IARI, New Delhi.

The study was carried out at the National Phytotron Facility, Indian Agricultural Research Institute (ICAR-IARI), New Delhi, India, in a completely randomized block design (CRBD) with three replications. The air temperature was maintained at 30/25°C Day/night; the photoperiod was 14/10 h light/dark, and the relative humidity was approximately 60%. Before germination on filter paper, seeds were disinfected with 1% sodium hypochlorite for 2–3 min and rinsed with distilled water. The six-day-old seedlings were transferred to a half-strength hydroponic solution. Drought stress was imposed on 12-day-old seedlings (fully developed first true leaf and the 2nd true leaf had started to emerge), and these healthy seedlings were then transferred to 8% PEG-6000 solution and kept for 48 h.19, 20 After 48 h, leaf canopy temperature was measured by using an Infrared thermal imager (Testo 890, India), producing an image resolution of 640 × 480 pixels with a spectral range of 7.5–14 µm. Images were then processed in Testo IRsoft software v.4.3 to determine the average canopy temperature of the leaf. Finally, stress was quantified as canopy temperature depression (CTD): CTD = leaf canopy temperature surrounding air temperature. Gas exchange measurements were conducted on the first true leaf from 9:00 to 10:30 AM using a portable photosynthesis system (Li-6400; LI-COR, Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA). The air temperature ranged between 30 °C and 32 °C, matching the growth chamber’s temperature settings. During the observation of photosynthesis, light-response curves were recorded at ambient CO2 concentrations (350–400 mmol). The leaves were exposed to a photon flux density of 1500 mmol photons m−2 s−1, and measurements were taken for the net photosynthetic rate.

Collection of leaf samples

After 48 h of drought stress treatment, we sampled 10 leaves each from drought-treated plants and control plants grown in a hydroponic medium. For consistency, the first true leaf of each plant was collected, with a single leaf from each plant used for sampling. From the 10 collected leaves, three replicates were randomly selected. Sampling was conducted from both WBC-23-2 and DGPC-59 (DG-59) plants across each replication, totaling 12 samples. Liquid N2 was used to freeze the samples immediately after harvesting, and the samples were stored at −80°C for use in the current study.

Quantitative analysis of different physiological and biochemical traits

Following stress application, the number of wilted plants was visually assessed in both genotypes. Leaf relative water content (RWC) was measured using the formula: RWC (%) = [(FW − DW) / (TW − DW)] × 100.21 Here, FW represents the fresh leaf weight, DW is the dry leaf weight, and TW is the turgid weight obtained after 4 h of floating in distilled water at 4 °C in darkness. Plant dry weight was calculated by oven-drying at 70°C for 48 h. The total soluble protein content was determined according to Bradford et al. (1976).22

RNA isolation and transcriptome sequencing

Using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) as a reagent, total RNA was extracted from the first true leaf of both genotypes (3 replications) for transcriptome analysis, revealing differences in drought response. The concentration (optical density 260 nm/280 nm ratio) and quality (optical density 260 nm/230 nm ratio) of the extracted RNA were determined using a Bio-analyzer (Agilent, UK). Whole-genome RNA sequencing was performed using the Illumina HiSeq X10 (after adjusting the final RNA concentration to 100 ng/μL). To remove genomic DNA contamination, total RNA was treated with DNaseI using the DNA-free kit (Promega Corporation, USA). The DNA-free RNA was then verified by PCR to confirm the absence of genomic DNA contamination before use in quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). For c-DNA first strand synthesis, the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit from Promega Corporation, USA was used following the instructions from the kit.

Evaluation of transcriptome datasets

The transcriptome sequence quality reads were assessed using fastqc-0.11.8.23 Reads containing adapter sequences, those with Phred quality scores (Q-score) <30, and reads shorter than 35 base pairs (bp) after quality clipping were removed using the Trimmomatic tool in paired-end (PE) mode.24 The high-quality sequencing data obtained after pre-processing were used for further downstream analysis. The genome sequence and gene annotation of Cucumis sativus were retrieved from NCBI-RefSeq (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_000004075.3/). The high-quality cleaned reads of samples were aligned to the genome using HISAT2 (version 2.1.1).25 FeatureCount was used to obtain gene-level feature counts.26

Genome-wide identification of lncRNAs in cucumber

Transcriptome assembly through StringTie version 2.1.4 processed each aligned BAM file to produce GTF files for every sample to identify lncRNA.27 To consolidate the data for each tissue, Individual files were combined using StringTie's −-merge function, resulting in a single combined GTF file. The workflow of identifying potential lncRNA candidate transcripts from the assembled transcripts is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. First, gffread (version 0.12.3) was used to retrieve the FASTA sequences of all transcripts from the combined transcript annotation file (GTF). To identify and exclude Transcripts with ORFs exceeding 300 nucleotides, we employed ORFPredictor,28 and transcripts shorter than 200 were filtered out. To further classify the remaining sequences as coding or non-coding, binary classifiers such as CPC2 version 1.0.1 and PLEK were utilized.29, 30 Transcripts identified as coding by all classifiers were excluded and classified as protein-coding. To identify previously uncharacterized non-coding RNAs, a BlastN search was performed using resources like the SILVA repository (release 138.1), GtRNAdb (release 18.1), and RNAcentral (release 16.0). To identify previously uncharacterized non-coding RNAs, a BlastN search was performed using resources like the SILVA repository (https://www.arb-silva.de/download/arb-files/release138.1), GtRNAdb (https://gtrnadb.ucsc.edu/release18.1), and RNACentral (https://rnacentral.org/release16.0). Sequences showing a similarity of 95% or greater were excluded. Additionally, Blast searches against the NCBI-nr protein database and the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/release33.1) were used to exclude transcripts matching known proteins or protein families. StringTie with the −e option was utilized to estimate the lncRNA transcript abundance, and the PrepDE.py script from the StringTie suite was used to generate the count matrix of each sample.

Conservation analysis with known plant lncRNAs in CANTATAdb

lncRNAs exhibit a reduced level of conservation in comparison to protein-coding transcripts across different species. However, to assess the level of conservation of lncRNAs in cucumber, we obtained a collection of previously identified lncRNAs from thirty-eight species of plants, accessible from the CANTATAdb version 2.0 database (https://cantata.amu.edu.pl/).31 We conducted a localized BlastN search, using our identified cucumber lncRNAs as the query with a cutoff e-value of 10−10.

Identification of lncRNAs regulating neighboring cucumber mRNAs

lncRNAs themselves do not produce any functional genes but can regulate the genes by interacting either directly with the neighbouring genes, causing the cis effect, or interfering with the miRNA-mRNA interaction, causing a trans regulation. Bedtools was used with the window option to identify genes within 10 kb upstream and downstream of differentially expressed lncRNAs.

Identification of lncRNAs acting as ceRNAs

Further, to identify the trans-regulatory effects of lncRNAs, miRNA-mediated mRNA regulation was studied. To identify potential miRNAs that might use cucumber long non-coding RNAs acting as mimics of their target molecules, psRNATarget server (https://plantgrn.noble.org/psRNATarget/) was applied. To identify the mRNA target of the miRNA targeting the lncRNA, PsRNATarget was again used with the identified miRNA sequences and the cucumber protein-coding transcript as input. To ensure the reliability and significance of our findings, stringent criteria like an expectation value (E-value) of ≤ 2 and a maximum energy to unpair the target site (UPE) equal to 25 were used as a threshold.

Differential gene expression analysis, gene ontology and gene set enrichment analysis

The differential gene expression analysis was conducted in R software (version 4.2.1) using DESeq.32, 33 A p-adjusted value of 0.05 and a log2FC value of ± 2-fold were considered significant. The heatmaps were prepared using the pHeatmap package, and the basic graph was prepared using the ggplot2 package of R software (version 4.2.1) and a spreadsheet. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using the enrichGO function of the R package clusterProfiler. In the enrichment analysis, GO terms with adjusted p-values <0.1 were considered significant. For gene set enrichment analysis, the gseGO function was used; sets with an adjusted p-value <0.1 were considered significant. The redundant enriched gene ontology terms in both analyses were simplified using a 0.7 similarity cutoff.34

Validation of differentially expressed genes with qPCR

To validate differentially expressed genes, researchers used qRT-PCR on 17 selected gene targets (Sequences retrieved from the cucurbits genomic database at https://cucurbitgenomics.org). The IDT PrimerQuest software (https://www.idtdna.com/) was used to design the primers, followed by cDNA preparation from DNase-treated total RNA (2 μg) using the GoScript™ Reverse Transcription System Kit (Promega, USA). The cDNA solution was diluted to 20 times its original volume with nuclease-free water. A total volume of 10 μL contained 1 μL diluted cDNA (200 ng), whereas 5 μL 2xSYBR Green (Go Taq qPCR system, USA) and 0.4 μL each of forward and reverse primer (10 μM) were used with 3.2 μL RNase-free water according to the manufacturer's instructions for performing qRT-PCR using the LightCycler 96 system Real-Time PCR (Roche, USA). The temperature protocol for cycling involved one minute at 95 °C followed by 40 repeating cycles at 95 °C during 10 s, then 58 °C for 30 s before completing at 72 °C during another 30 s. The 2−ΔΔCT method was used to determine gene expression relative to Actin, with normalization. The researchers employed Supplementary Table 1 to display all qRT-PCR primer information and descriptions.

Establishment of web-based resources for the cucumber

The Transcriptome-based Cucumis sativus Database for Drought (CsDTDb) is a comprehensive web resource for this crop. It is a specialized relational database with an easy, user-friendly approach to data retrieval and management. It features a three-tier architecture comprising client, server, and database layers. The system uses Apache (2.4.58) for web serving, MySQL (8.0.32) for data management, and PHP (8.2.12) for server-side scripting. The MySQL database contains 20 tables that store information on counts, enrichment, interactions, and differential expression for both lncRNA and transcriptomic data. The web interface, developed with PHP, HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and Bootstrap, provides a dynamic and interactive user experience. When a user interacts with the interface, a request is sent from the user's web page to the server via a PHP script. This script generates an SQL query to fetch the relevant data from the MySQL database. The retrieved data is then returned in JSON format, converted into tables, and displayed on the user's screen. The server dynamically generates HTML pages with the results, ensuring efficient and accurate information delivery for research purposes (Supplementary figure 5).

Results

Physiochemical response in relation to drought stress

Two genotypes, WBC-23-2 and DGPC-59, showed significant differences in leaf wilting and canopy temperature depression under stress. After 48 h of PEG-induced stress, all DGPC-59 seedlings (100%) exhibited severe leaf wilting, whereas over 95% of WBC-23-2 seedlings showed no signs of wilting (Fig. 1E). Canopy temperature depression (CTD) values significantly decreased under stress. DGPC-59 showed a higher decrease (Control −7.79 degree C, PEG-3.34 degree C) than WBC-23-2 (control-8.42 degree C, PEG-6.78 degree C) (Fig. 1D) (Supplementary figure 9). Relative water content (RWC) of the leaves significantly reduced in DGPC-59 under stress (Conrol-91.79%, PEG-28.34%), while RWC in WBC-23-2 remained stable compared to the control (Control-93.13%, PEG-91.28%) (Fig. 1 B) (Supplementary figure 7). Total soluble protein levels significantly increased in WBC-23-2 under drought stress (Control-2.26 mg/g FW, PEG-2.89 mg/g FW), while they decreased by 1.3-fold in DGPC-59 (Control-2.04 mg/g FW, PEG-1.43 mg/g FW) (Fig. 1C) (Supplementary Fig. 8). Analysis of RNA-seq data to identify expressed mRNA and lncRNA

Read and alignment statistics of transcriptome data

A total of 23,44,19,390 paired-end reads were generated. After quality trimming, 21,46,88,165 paired reads were retained. The alignment of samples ranged from 96.20% to 99.10% (average 98.46%) (Supplementary Table 1). The mRNA gene count with at least 1 raw read ranged from 16,329 to 18,204 in the sequenced samples.

Identification of lncRNA

We followed a stringent filtering pipeline for the assembled transcripts to identify lncRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 1). After that, StringTie assembly was completed for each sample, and the StringTie merge module was used to unify all GTF files into a single file. To extract the FASTA sequences of the corresponding 20,849 transcripts from the cucumber genome file, Gffread35 was used. To select a sequence of larger than 200 bp, In-house Perl scripts were used. ORF predictor was utilized in identifying the ORFs present in the sequences, and all the ORFs with a length of > 300 bp were removed. The remaining 2297 transcripts were analyzed for coding potential using CPC2 and PLEK software, and those predicted to be polypeptide-coding were removed. BlastN hits against known ncRNA databases were also removed to obtain novel ncRNAs. At last, the probable protein-coding genes were removed by conducting a similarity search against the Pfam and NCBI-nr databases and removing hits with e-values lower than 10−5, yielding 774 most probable cucumber lncRNAs (Supplementary Table 2). The allocation of identified lncRNAs under various conditions of cucumber is presented in Fig. 2A. Identified cucumber lncRNAs were evenly distributed among the chromosomes (Fig. 2B). Compared to mRNAs, which had a range of 141 to 16,715 nucleotides, the length of the detected lncRNAs varied between 200 and 7334 nucleotides, making them shorter. Only 5% of the lncRNAs were longer than 2200 nucleotides, whereas over 80% of them were shorter than 1400 nucleotides (Fig. 2C). Differential expression profile of mRNA and lncRNA

Of the cucumber genes in drought versus control samples, 19.73% were represented by the 4,736 distinct genes (FDR < 0.05 and fold change>= |±2|) that showed differential expression. 4,340 of the 4,736 genes were those that coded for mRNA or proteins (Supplementary Table 3). There were 3395 differentially expressed genes in the tolerant genotype and 2015 in the susceptible genotype. In contrast, the tolerant and susceptible genotypes shared only 14.23% of DEGs, whereas 57.45% and 28.31% of genes were specific to the tolerant and susceptible genotypes, respectively (Fig. 4C). Genes that were upregulated (UR) and downregulated (DR) in tolerant genotypes were 2225 and 1170, respectively. In the Susceptible genotypes, UR and DR genes were 856 and 1159, respectively. According to a differential expression analysis of 413 lncRNAs, susceptible and tolerant cultivars had 170 and 243 lncRNAs, respectively, that were differently regulated. Fig. 4D shows how cucumber lncRNAs that were up- and down-regulated were distributed among the four situations (Tolerant and susceptible genotype in both control and stressed conditions).

Expression profile of transcription factor in two cultivars

The role of transcription factors as both enhancers and suppressors of the stress response is widely acknowledged, and in this study, a total of 155 transcription factors (TFs) were regulated by drought stress. Detection of DE lncRNAs as endogenous target mimics (eTM) of miRNAs and gene ontology enrichment analysis of their targets

By binding with the 3′ UTR of the protein-coding mRNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs), a type of small non-coding RNA, play a crucial role in the regulation of mRNA expression. The complementarity between miRNA and mRNA determines whether expression is muted or inhibited. While partial binding lowers mRNA production and downregulates gene expression, full complementarity results in mRNA breakdown and, thus, silencing. Sometimes, LncRNAs obstruct the process by functioning as miRNA traps, preventing miRNA binding to mRNA. For this investigation, known plant miRNAs and identified lncRNAs accessible at the psRNA target server were used. lncRNA-mRNA regulatory dynamics under drought stress

To determine the cis-regulatory relationship between lncRNAs and mRNAs between a Drought Susceptible (DS) and a Drought Tolerant (DT) genotype, the log2 fold change value of differentially expressed mRNA and lncRNA pairs (195) was extracted to determine whether any relation exists for their divergent survival phenotypes in drought stress. Discussion

Cucumber is frequently used to study and understand fundamental molecular and physiological responses to biotic and abiotic stressors. In a previous study, we found a group of cucumber genotypes from a core collection that exhibited contrasting responses to drought.19 The genotype WBC-23-2 showed the highest tolerance among these, but DGPC-59 was found to be very susceptible to drought stress, during the seedling stage (in hydroponic) and the reproductive stage (in field).19, 20 So, these two contrasting genotypes could serve as valuable candidates for uncovering the molecular mechanisms and regulatory networks underlying drought response in cucumbers, a field that remains underexplored.

Physiological basis of drought resilience in the tolerant genotype

Osmotic stress can be induced by adding polyethylene glycol to the nutrient medium, leading to changes in tissue water content and affecting physiological activities.20 The tolerant line has been shown to maintain higher relative water content (RWC) in numerous studies.36, 37,38 When drought stress is present, soluble protein levels may rise due to the synthesis of hydrophilic proteins that resist dehydration.39 Das et al. (2024) reported elevated soluble protein levels in a drought-tolerant cucumber variety that provides protection against oxidative damage, further corroborating our results, which show higher soluble protein levels in the tolerant genotype.40 Ability to use available moisture indicated by cooler canopy temperature and higher canopy temperature depression.41 Tolerant genotype WBC-23-2 was able to maintain cooler canopy temperature than drought susceptible DGPC-59, indicating its inherent ability to take up water from the medium or use water more efficiently. Both the light-dependent and dark reactions of photosynthesis are negatively affected by water deficit, resulting in a reduction in overall photosynthetic capacity. The tolerant genotype showed a higher net photosynthesis rate than the susceptible genotype, even under stress, indicating its greater efficiency in light harvesting. It is further supported by our transcriptomics study, where photosynthesis-light harvesting in photosystem 1 is highly upregulated in the tolerant genotype.42

Upregulation and downregulation of drought-responsive genes

In our study, 4,736 unique genes showed differential expression, accounting for 19.73% of the cucumber genome when comparing drought-affected samples to controls. Of these, 4,340 were mRNA/protein-coding genes. Notably, 2,225 genes showed upregulation in the drought-tolerant genotype, while 856 genes were upregulated in the drought-susceptible genotype. Additionally, 1,170 genes were downregulated in the drought-tolerant genotype, while 1,159 genes were downregulated in the drought-susceptible genotype. The upregulation of drought-responsive genes (DRGs) is likely crucial for drought tolerance (DT), as tolerant genotypes exhibit a greater number of upregulated DRGs. This observation supports previous transcriptomic studies, which identified upregulated DEGs as genes associated with stress tolerance.43

In the process of photosynthesis, carbon is reduced by light energy absorbed by light-harvesting complex (LHC) proteins and transferred to the Calvin-Benson cycle for carbon reduction.44,45 Among the eleven photosynthesis-related genes identified, the transcript encoding ferrochelatase-2 (FC2) is particularly significant and is associated with light-harvesting complex (LHC) protein function and light absorption efficiency in cucumber. Our comparative analysis between genotypes further illuminates these adaptive strategies. While the susceptible genotype exhibited a marked downregulation of cytochrome-related genes—potentially leading to a collapse of linear electron flow—the tolerant genotype demonstrated a strategic upregulation of photosystem I (PSI) light-harvesting. This genotypic divergence suggests that tolerant plants may maintain cyclic electron flow or enhance PSI protection to mitigate dark reaction inhibition, a typical feature of severe water deficit. Maintaining glutathione (GSH) homeostasis through the γ-glutamyl cycle is fundamental to a plant's survival strategy. In tolerant genotypes, including WBC 23-2 of the present study, the enrichment of these metabolic processes suggests a superior capacity to neutralize drought-induced Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS).46 Plants respond to water deficit by rapidly producing biochemical signals, such as Ca2+, ROS, and ABA, which collectively minimize water loss and activate stress-tolerance programs. ABA serves as the central regulatory hub in this process, modulating the expression of genes involved in osmotic adjustment—such as proline and sugar transporters—as well as structural stabilizers, such as LEA proteins and chaperones. Furthermore, ABA-mediated pathways enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes, thereby mitigating oxidative damage caused by ROS accumulation.47 In this study, a tolerant cultivar showed upregulation of genes involved in defense responses. This is further supported by the study of Wang et al. (2018), which shows that the distinction between the tolerant (L-9) and susceptible (A-16) genotypes lies in their ability to decode and respond to stress signals. In L-9, we observed a synchronized increase in NAC transcription factors and Calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs), both of which are pivotal for ABA-mediated stomatal control and drought adaptation.17 The tolerant genotype in our study showed upregulation of genes involved in defense responses to other organisms and in response to wounding. Drought-induced accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) appears to have acted as a priming signal, activating endogenous defense pathways that may have enhanced the plant's resistance to the tested pathogens.48,49 PAL affects the synthesis of diverse secondary metabolites in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway.7 Phenylalanine serves as a precursor for phenylpropanoid metabolism, playing a vital role in plant development and environmental adaptation by driving the synthesis of protective secondary metabolites such as lignin and flavonoids.50 It is involved in plant growth, development, and plant–environment interactions.51 Additionally, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), an enzyme involved in phenylpropanoid metabolism, was found to be upregulated under stress conditions, particularly in tolerant genotypes.52, 53 Genetic research highlights the functional role of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), demonstrating that its overexpression significantly increases seed germination rates in Arabidopsis under salt and drought conditions.54,55 This metabolic advantage supports our research in cucumber cultivars, where a substantial majority—seven out of ten—of identified PAL genes were exclusively upregulated in drought-tolerant varieties, suggesting that specific PAL isoforms are primary drivers of environmental resilience. The HSP20/alpha-crystallin family of heat shock proteins (HSPs) functions as molecular chaperones, protecting proteins from denaturation and helping other chaperones maintain the native structure of newly synthesized polypeptide chains or facilitate the refolding of denatured proteins.56 Cytochromes, including cytochrome P450, play roles in phytohormone homeostasis, signal transduction, and the metabolism of antioxidant flavonoids. They increase ROS-scavenging activities, which help people withstand drought.57 Similarly, our study observed a higher number of upregulated HSP and cytochrome-related genes in the drought-tolerant cucumber genotype. This suggests that the simultaneous reinforcement of protein stability and antioxidant capacity is a core mechanism underlying the tolerant genotype's superior drought resilience.

Water availability is reduced under drought stress, which lowers stomatal conductance and CO2 absorption, ultimately upsetting the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio and hindering nitrogen metabolism. Du et al. (2021) reported genes involved in nitrogen metabolism were uniformly down-regulated under post-germinative drought and salinity stress conditions in cucumber.18 Similarly, genes associated with organic nitrogen compound metabolism showed higher expression in drought-susceptible genotypes in the present study, indicating that nitrogen metabolism is a critical component of drought susceptibility. Under drought stress, plants typically use ABA signaling to reconfigure their metabolism; however, our results indicate a fundamental divergence in how tolerant and susceptible genotypes manage their macromolecular reserves. In the tolerant cultivar L-9, the significant upregulation of starch and sucrose biosynthetic pathways was observed, indicating the plant builds metabolic buffers to maintain osmotic potential and energy availability.17 Conversely, the susceptible genotype in our study exhibited pronounced upregulation of proteasomal protein catabolism and modification-dependent macromolecular catabolism. We infer that while L-9 engages in proactive resource accumulation, the susceptible genotype DGPC-59 undergoes uncontrolled metabolic collapse and senescence; rather than a strategic release of nutrients, its excessive proteolysis represents the terminal breakdown of functional cellular machinery. While Wang et al.17 identified a correlation between wax biosynthesis genes and drought tolerance, our findings did not reveal a similar relationship.

Regulation of transcription factors

TFs are implicated in ABA- and Jasmonate-mediated responses that regulate tolerance to drought and other abiotic stress.58 In the present study, a total of 155 TFs were found to be regulated under drought stress. Consistent with these findings, previous research has identified several TF families, including MYB, bHLH, WRKY, ERF, NFY, GATA, PIF, ABA-INDUCIBLE BHLH-TYPE TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR (AIB), HSF, and bZIP, as responsive to various abiotic stresses.59,60 Wang et al. (2018) found that the expression of two genes, encoding the NAC domain-containing protein, increased prominently in the drought-tolerant cultivar in cucumber.17 NAC proteins act as critical intermediaries in this process; they are often induced by ABA and, in turn, regulate the expression of downstream stress-responsive genes, creating a robust feedback loop that enhances dehydration tolerance. This synergy is exemplified by the binding of NAC factors to specific promoter elements of ABA-biosynthetic genes, such as NCED3, thereby amplifying the hormonal signal under water deficit. Similarly, WRKY TFs can directly bind the W-boxes of the RD29A and NCED3 promoters, thereby activating ABA-dependent genes under stress conditions.61 Similar studies across different crops have confirmed the crucial role of WRKY TFs in stress responses.62, 63 Additionally, under stress, specific bHLH transcription factors interact with CBF genes (DREB1 and DREB2), contributing to tolerance.64 This interaction may account for the differing regulation patterns observed between the two contrasting genotypes in response to drought stress. bHLH transcription factors as positive regulators of drought tolerance.65 In our study, bHLH78 and WRKY 51 (LOC101216346) were exclusively regulated in the drought-tolerant cultivar, further confirming their role in stress tolerance. In the drought-tolerant genotype, the R2R3 transcription factor MYB59 is specifically regulated and participates in the jasmonic acid pathway. Its contribution to these physiological processes is well-documented, with overexpression studies validating its regulatory role.66 Evidence indicates that numerous abiotic stresses affect the genes of the ERF family and related signaling pathways.67 Several ERF proteins function as molecular nodes that integrate signals from the ABA and ethylene signaling pathways. In Arabidopsis, the ERF109 (RRTF) gene plays a critical role in stress-induced redox regulation via the ethylene and jasmonic acid signaling pathways.68 While previous studies demonstrated that its overexpression enhances oxidative stress defenses, our results specifically highlight its exclusive induction in drought-tolerant lines, suggesting a similar protective role in this context.69

Regulatory role of non-coding RNAs

These lncRNAs primarily function as regulators by promoting gene silencing, which is essential for controlling transcriptional regulation and genome imprinting. They play significant roles in various biological processes, including root organogenesis, photomorphogenesis, flowering time control, reproduction, and nutrient homeostasis.70 It was confirmed from previous studies that lncRNAs role in drought stress through a co-expression network that includes lncRNAs, miRNAs, protein-coding genes, and transcription factors.71 In this research, lncRNAs that were identified ranged from 200 to 7,334 nucleotides in length, which is shorter compared to mRNAs, which ranged from 141 to 16,715 nucleotides and were distributed evenly across the chromosomes. Similarly, when rice was stressed by drought, mRNAs were, on average, longer than lncRNAs, and lncRNAs showed significantly lower expression levels than mRNAs.71

The RNA-induced silencing complex mediates gene silencing via miRNAs. In plants, some long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) may act as decoys because they have miRNA recognition areas that resemble those of miRNA targets. These lncRNAs bind to miRNAs, preventing them from interacting with their actual targets and effectively inhibiting miRNA activity. Such lncRNAs are known as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) or lncRNA sponges and play a vital role in modulating gene expression.72 For example, it was discovered that 849 lncRNAs in wheat (Triticum aestivum) changed their expression in response to alkaline stress and functioned as decoys for 115 conserved miRNAs.73 In our study of cucumber under drought stress, we identified 844 interactions involving 255 unique miRNAs and 188 unique lncRNAs. By using psRNA target to analyze cucumber coding sequences (CDS) as targets, we detected 1690 interactions between cucumber genes and miRNAs, suggesting potential gene regulatory mechanisms.

In this study we identified a Genotype-Specific Transcriptional Switch governed by lncRNAs. Our data suggests that the difference between surviving desiccation and succumbing to it lies not merely in the presence of stress-response genes, but in how the plant's chromatin architecture deploys non-coding RNAs to regulate them. In the drought-susceptible genotype, lncRNAs appear to function primarily as transcriptional repressors under stress. The strong antagonistic relationship implies a maladaptive “panic” response, in which the plant uses cis-acting lncRNAs to rapidly shut down normal cellular processes (such as water transport via PIP2-1). While this limits water loss, the pervasive silencing environment prevents the plant from mounting an active, structural defense, ultimately rendering it susceptible. Conversely, the drought-tolerant genotype utilizes an entirely different regulatory logic. The shift toward synergistic regulation suggests that in the DT line, these lncRNAs are acting as enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) or transcriptional co-activators. By opening chromatin loci to simultaneously transcribe lncRNAs and their neighboring defense genes (DIR22, DnaJ2, HSC80), the tolerant plant actively reinforces its cell walls and protects its proteome, conferring resilience to the drought condition

CsDTDb

CsDTDb is a cucumber-specific web resource available at [CsDTDb](https://backlin.cabgrid.res.in/csdtdb/index.html). It provides access to the analyzed data from this study on two contrasting cucumber cultivars: drought-tolerant and drought-sensitive. This platform is designed to enhance global researchers' understanding of drought-stress biology and to support future cucumber crop improvement programs by making the study's findings easily accessible.

Conclusion

This study investigated the drought stress response in cucumber using two contrasting genotypes, WBC-23-2 (drought-tolerant) and DGPC-59 (drought-sensitive). Through transcriptomic analysis, we identified 4,736 differentially expressed protein-coding genes and 774 differentially expressed long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in response to drought. The drought-tolerant line exhibited reduced leaf wilting and lower canopy temperature depression under stress, with upregulated genes associated with defense response, photosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism. In contrast, the sensitive line showed enrichment in organonitrogen catabolic processes and water deprivation response pathways. Furthermore, 155 transcription factors (TFs) were found to differentially regulate across genotypes, suggesting unique regulatory networks linked to drought tolerance. Importantly, candidate genes like WAT1-related protein, thaumatin-like protein, WRKY transcription factor, and pathogenesis-related protein 1 emerged as probable contributors to drought resilience. The findings provide a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying drought tolerance and offer valuable genomic resources. These candidate genes and regulatory RNAs can act as a basis for functional validation and developing drought-tolerant varieties through genetic engineering or CRISPR-based editing.74,75 By integrating this transcriptome data with genomic variation, it is possible to identify genetic markers that correlate with specific expression levels of stress-responsive genes. This can allow breeders to select lines that not only have the tolerance gene but also express it at the right time and magnitude. Additionally, the CsDTDb database is intended to support future cucumber breeding efforts to enhance drought resilience.
