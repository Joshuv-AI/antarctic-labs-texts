Evaluation of Hyaloid Artery Persistence as a Biomarker in Retinopathy of Prematurity Using OCT
Authors: Hanna N. Luong, Aaron S. Coyner, Elizabeth V. Roti, David A. Sutter, Susan Ostmo, John Jackson, Mani Woodward, Yakub Bayhaqi, Shuibin Ni, Yifan Jian, J Peter Campbell, Benjamin K. Young
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate whether hyaloid artery persistence, detected by handheld OCT, is associated with retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) severity.DesignA retrospective cohort study.SubjectsA total of 162 eyes of preterm infants undergoing ROP screening at a tertiary care neonatal intensive care unit were included.MethodsHandheld ultra-widefield OCT images were reviewed at each clinical encounter for the presence or absence of the hyaloid artery. Time to hyaloid artery disappearance was modeled using a Weibull accelerated failure time survival analysis incorporating interval censoring and clustering within subjects. Covariates included ROP severity (mild, moderate, or severe) and gestational age (GA). Leave-one-eye-out cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROCs) were calculated to evaluate the discriminative ability of hyaloid regression timing for ROP severity.Main Outcome MeasuresThe primary outcomes were postnatal age (PNA) at hyaloid artery disappearance and AUROC values for No vs. Mild, No vs. Severe, and Mild vs. Severe ROP.ResultsThe hyaloid artery was detectable by OCT in 152 of 162 (94%) eyes. Compared with eyes with No ROP, hyaloid artery persistence was significantly prolonged in Mild ROP (acceleration factor [AF], 1.354; P = 0.002) and Severe ROP (AF, 1.414; P = 0.006). Median PNA of hyaloid artery disappearance, adjusted for mean GA, was 9.1 weeks for No ROP, 11.7 weeks for Mild ROP, and 12 weeks for Severe ROP. Leave-one-eye-out cross-validated AUROCs demonstrated moderate discrimination between No versus Mild ROP (AUROC = 0.71) and Mild versus Severe ROP (AUROC = 0.76), and strong discrimination between No vs. Severe ROP (AUROC = 0.96).ConclusionsThe hyaloid artery is commonly detectable in preterm infants using OCT. Persistence of the hyaloid artery is significantly associated with increasing ROP severity and may serve as a novel, quantifiable biomarker of disease severity and retinal maturation.Financial DisclosuresProprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.

The hyaloid artery is a transient embryologic vessel that supplies the developing lens and retina during gestation. It typically regresses in the third trimester, but persistence may be more common in preterm infants.1,2 Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), the leading cause of childhood blindness, is a vasoproliferative disorder of the immature retina. Human data characterizing persistent hyaloid artery visibility in the setting of ROP has been limited to 1 observational study, which noted persistent hyaloid vasculature in 14 out of 160 infants (9%) using indirect ophthalmoscopy.2 The anterior contribution of the hyaloid artery, the tunica vasculosa lentis, has been shown to be a biomarker for increased treatment need and zone I disease.3

Handheld OCT has enabled noninvasive, high-resolution in vivo imaging of the retina in preterm infants.4 Although ROP standard of care remains indirect ophthalmoscopy or fundus photography, handheld OCT may provide enhanced resolution, patient comfort, objectivity, and identification of potential novel biomarkers.4, 5, 6, 7 Herein, we assess whether the hyaloid artery can be consistently visualized using OCT during routine ROP screening and use survival analysis to model its relation to ROP severity.

Methods

Oregon Health & Science University Casey Eye Institute Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee approval was obtained. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All infants seen for ROP screening and evaluation from a single tertiary ophthalmology center from August 2021 to November 2024 were included. Exclusion criteria included lack of quality OCT images recorded. All consented infants had imaging with an investigational 800 kHz ultra-widefield OCT device, whose operational details are described elsewhere,8 at each ROP screening/monitoring visit. Retinopathy of prematurity severity was determined by clinician grading and divided into No, Mild, type 2, or type 1 disease based on definitions from the Early Treatment for Retinopathy of Prematurity Study9 and International Classification of ROP.10 Given their relative rarity, type 1 and type 2 disease were combined into a single “Severe” disease category. Author H.N.L. reviewed all images from each imaging encounter for the presence or absence of the hyaloid artery. Identification of the hyaloid artery was based on specific OCT features: a tubular or linear structure extending from the optic nerve head into the vitreous, and continuity across multiple scans through time. We were not able to assess perfusion of this structure to know whether it was a true artery. To assess intergrader reliability, a random 20% of OCT images were independently reviewed by a second grader for the presence or absence of a persistent hyaloid artery. Agreement was quantified using Cohen’s kappa statistic.

Time to hyaloid artery disappearance (measured in postnatal age [PNA], weeks) was modeled using an accelerated failure time (AFT) survival analysis with a Weibull distribution. An AFT model rather than Kaplan–Meier methods was used because the timing of hyaloid artery regression was interval-censored. This parametric approach also allowed adjustment for key covariates such as gestational age (GA). Time-to-event data included left censoring for eyes where the hyaloid artery had resolved before the first observation, right censoring for eyes where the hyaloid artery persisted at the last visit, and exact event times for known disappearance. The primary outcome was PNA at hyaloid artery disappearance, either observed directly or censored. Timing was measured in PNA to avoid multicollinearity effects that could inflate variance and distort estimates between postmenstrual age and GA. In addition, modeling to PNA aligns with important modifiable exposures, including oxygen and ventilation needs that are gauged in the neonatal intensive care unit.11 Coefficient estimates were interpreted as acceleration factors (AFs), where an AF >1 indicates delayed hyaloid regression and AF <1 indicates faster resolution. Predicted survival curves were generated for each severity group.

Cluster-robust standard errors were used to account for correlation between eyes from the same subject. Median PNA at hyaloid regression for each severity group was estimated using the Turnbull method, an equivalent of the Kaplan–Meier estimator used for interval-censored data without a specific time-to-event distribution. To quantify the discriminative ability of hyaloid regression timing for ROP severity, pairwise contrasts between ROP severity groups (No vs. Mild, No vs. Severe, and Mild vs. Severe) were evaluated using leave-one-eye-out cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROCs) at PNA 10 weeks. For each iteration, 1 eye was excluded, the interval-censored Weibull AFT model was fit using the remaining dataset, and a predicted probability of hyaloid regression by PNA 10 weeks was generated for the excluded eye using GA and interval-censored regression timing information only. Retinopathy of prematurity severity was not included as a predictor in AUROC model generation and was used solely as the classification outcome. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curves were then calculated for each pairwise severity contrast using the aggregated predictions. Bootstrap resampling (500 iterations) was performed as a secondary internal validation measure to assess optimism in apparent AUROC estimates. Primary discrimination results are reported using leave-one-eye-out cross-validation. The number of observed regression events and censored observations contributing to each severity group and pairwise contrast were tabulated. All analyses were performed in R (version 3.7.0) using the survival, lmtest, sandwich, and pROC packages.

Results

Of 162 eyes reviewed, 152 (94%) had a hyaloid artery visualized at some point during routine ROP screening/monitoring. A representative case is shown in Figure 1. Intergrader agreement for hyaloid artery presence on OCT was high (Cohen’s κ = 0.86; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.67 to 1.00). Of the eyes where a hyaloid artery was noted, 39 eyes had No ROP, 49 had Mild ROP, and 64 had Severe ROP (Table 1). Compared with in eyes with No ROP, the hyaloid artery persisted 3.2 weeks (35%) and 3.8 weeks (41%) longer in Mild and Severe ROP, respectively (standard error = 0.099, z = 3.05, P = 0.002; standard error = 0.126, z = 2.75, P = 0.006) (Fig. 2). The median PNA at which the hyaloid artery resolved for an eye with average GA (27.5 weeks) was 9.1, 11.7, and 12 weeks for No, Mild, and Severe ROP, respectively. Leave-one-eye-out cross-validated AUROCs demonstrated moderate discrimination between No vs. Mild ROP (AUROC = 0.71; 95% CI, 0.60–0.81) and Mild vs. Severe ROP (AUROC = 0.76; 95% CI, 0.66–0.84), and strong discrimination between No vs. Severe ROP (AUROC = 0.96; 95% CI, 0.93–0.99). The number of observed regression events and censored observations differed across severity groups. Regression was observed in 13 of 39 eyes with No ROP, 18 of 49 eyes with Mild ROP, and 55 of 64 eyes with Severe ROP, with corresponding right-censoring in 17, 23, and 5 eyes, respectively. Unadjusted boxplots demonstrated partial overlap in regression timing between severity groups (Supplemental Figure 1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), although separation between No and Severe ROP remained substantial.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that the hyaloid artery can be visualized in most preterm infants using OCT, and its persistence is associated with ROP severity. In this cohort, hyaloid artery regression was substantially delayed in eyes with ROP, even in mild disease, compared with eyes without ROP. These findings support the biological relevance of persistent hyaloid vasculature as a marker of altered postnatal vitreoretinal maturation in infants who develop ROP.

Leave-one-eye-out cross-validated discrimination analysis demonstrated the strongest performance for distinguishing No vs. Severe ROP, whereas discrimination between adjacent severity groups was more modest. This pattern is biologically plausible given the expected overlap in retinal maturation and VEGF-mediated disease pathways across the ROP severity spectrum. Although delayed hyaloid regression was associated with increasing disease severity, overlap between Mild and Severe ROP suggests that hyaloid persistence alone is unlikely to fully capture the biological heterogeneity of ROP progression.

Delayed hyaloid regression in more severe ROP may reflect dysregulation of shared molecular and cellular pathways that govern both fetal vessel involution and retinal angiogenesis. Normal hyaloid regression is a programmed process involving macrophage-mediated signaling, neuronal sequestration of VEGF, and endothelial apoptosis as the retina vascularizes.1,12,13 Disruption of these cues can therefore preserve hyaloid vessels while promoting aberrant retinal vascular growth. These mechanisms may include impaired regulation of VEGF by retinal neurons and macrophages during the transition from fetal to postnatal circulation, as well as perturbations in oxygen sensing, growth factor availability, and immune cell function.

These findings extend earlier observations of persistent anterior hyaloid vessels as a risk factor for advanced ROP.2,3 Herein, volumetric handheld OCT detected the hyaloid artery in 94% of premature infants during routine screening, which is far higher than had been noted previously with ophthalmoscopy (9%).2 Interestingly, we did not find a statistically significant association between GA and regression timing. This may reflect inadequate statistical power and a dominant influence of postnatal vitreoretinal maturation and VEGF exposure over prenatal factors.

Several limitations exist. The retrospective design and absence of detailed adjustment for neonatal factors, such as systemic illness or oxygen therapy, introduce the potential for residual confounding. In the AFT model, omission of these factors may have inflated the estimated delay in hyaloid regression associated with ROP and contributed to greater heterogeneity in regression timing within severity groups. All patients were evaluated at a single tertiary center for ROP screening, limiting generalizability to other populations or care settings. In addition, OCT imaging from healthy term infants was unavailable, reflecting the ethical and practical constraints of imaging infants who do not meet clinical screening criteria. Although leave-one-eye-out cross-validation and bootstrap resampling were performed to reduce optimism bias, discrimination estimates may still have been influenced by limited sample size, censoring patterns, and cohort-specific distributions of GA and postnatal imaging times. Finally, this study used an investigational handheld ultra-widefield OCT system, though the narrow field of view required to visualize the hyaloid artery makes detection theoretically possible with commercially available handheld OCT. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating standardized longitudinal imaging and detailed neonatal exposure data are warranted to assess whether OCT-based assessment of hyaloid regression can enhance ROP risk stratification.
