Clinical characteristics of patients who attempt suicide by drug overdose in Western China: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Lingyan Li, Huaiyi Zhu, Lin Wang, Zuolin Wei, Xiaoli Wang, Yigang Li, Mingjin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOverdose is a prevalent method of suicide, and a thorough analysis of the demographic characteristics and risk behaviour patterns within the drug overdose population might facilitate the development of effective treatments and prevention methods.MethodsThis retrospective study analysed patients hospitalized for attempted suicide by drug overdose (2020–2023) at a tertiary care hospital in Western China. Data on sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were analysed using descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsA total of 631 overdose cases originating from 604 patients were analysed. The resulting patient cohort had a male-to-female ratio of 1:3.1. The patient age range was 12–88 years, with more than half (57.0%) aged ≤ 20 years. The number of female patients was greater than that of male patients across all age groups, with this sex disparity decreasing with increasing age. Among 631 overdose cases, 84.2% involved psychiatric drugs, with antidepressants being the predominant drug type (296 cases, 46.9%). Adult patients predominantly used benzodiazepines and had a higher rate of alcohol co-ingestion, whereas adolescent patients used antidepressants and often combined multiple medications (≥3). The number of overdose cases showed a bimodal distribution and was more pronounced in the adolescent population, with peak drug use occurring in April and December, which coincided with academic cycles. A total of 52.0% of the patients had a history of psychiatric hospitalization, with depressive disorders being the predominant mental illness. Among the follow-up destinations, 36.7% of the adult patients returned home without psychiatric follow-up after treatment, which was significantly greater than the percentage of adolescents who returned home (19.9%, p < 0.05). Multivariable analysis identified female sex (aOR = 0.56) and prior psychiatric contact (outpatient aOR = 0.12; hospitalization aOR = 0.21) as protective factors against loss to follow-up.ConclusionThis study identifies adolescent females and individuals with depressive disorders as key populations for targeted suicide prevention. A bimodal peak in attempts coinciding with academic examination periods (April/December) supports implementing school-based screenings and stress management. Adults showed significantly higher disengagement from follow-up, with prior psychiatric contact emerging as a protective factor. These findings underscore the need for improved care continuity, especially for individuals without established mental health pathways.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07715-1.Keywords: Suicide attempt, Drug overdose, Adolescents, Health policy, Mental health services

Introduction

Drug overdose is a prevalent method of suicide and constitutes an escalating public health issue globally, with its epidemiological picture showing considerable geographic and cultural disparities [1, 2]. While recent national data confirms considerable success in China’s suicide prevention efforts, demonstrating a significant decline in the overall age-standardized suicide mortality rate from 10.88 to 5.25 per 100,000 population between 2010 and 2021 [3], detailed clinical characterization of specific suicide methods within sub-populations and regions remains limited. This is particularly relevant for the emerging trend of drug overdose. Evidence confirms that the decline in pesticide suicide rates has coincided with the implementation of regulatory bans and restrictions, a trend especially pronounced in urban areas [4–6]. Concurrently, poisoning by medicinal substances has emerged as a predominant method of suicide [7, 8], indicating a novel trend of risk transfer. This transition underscores the urgency of characterizing the clinical profile of the emerging drug overdose population, which is the primary focus of this study in Western China.

Overdose endangers patients’ lives and significantly strains their societal–medical resources. Research indicates that the predominant cause of suicide globally is psychiatric disorders, with depression, substance abuse, and psychosis being the most significant risk factors [9]. Consequently, overdose frequently necessitates psychological therapies alongside emergency resuscitation and specialized care. In addition to exacerbating the demand on the healthcare system, overdoses provide a significant challenge to the mental health service framework. Currently, overdose prevention and intervention involve numerous challenges. Drugs are defined by their dual attributes of accessibility and confidentiality, with patients frequently acquiring high-risk drugs for therapeutic purposes, including antidepressants and benzodiazepines for individuals with psychiatric conditions. The general population can readily access various over-the-counter drugs from pharmacies and online, complicating efforts to eliminate the risk of purposeful or impulsive overdose. Second, discerning patients’ intentions regarding drug use is challenging; overdose may stem from suicidal behaviour, self-harm, or accidental ingestion. Children and adolescents seeking medical assistance may deliberately obscure their true motives for injury, often misreporting it as unintentional owing to the stigma associated with intentional harm, which complicates the identification of overdose intent in clinical environments [10]. The disproportionate distribution of mental health service resources and the absence of professional identification, assessment, and intervention processes in certain regions have led to numerous at-risk individuals not being recognized and receiving prompt action [11].

Research on overdose primarily emphasizes epidemiological descriptions or emergency clinical interventions at a singular moment, while research that focuses on understanding long-term trends, features of at-risk populations, and patterns of drug use remains insufficient. This study aimed to systematically analyse the demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, seasonal distributions, and trends of patients hospitalized for overdose from January 2020 to December 2023 in a western region of China through retrospective analyses to identify high-risk populations and critical time points, thereby providing theoretical support and practical foundations for subsequent interventions and prevention strategies.

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically characterize patients hospitalized for intentional drug overdose in Western China by analyzing their demographic profiles, temporal patterns (including seasonal and monthly variations), and clinical features (including medications used and patterns of co-ingestion). A primary analytical goal was to identify predictors associated with loss to psychiatric follow-up after the overdose event. Ultimately, we sought to delineate high-risk populations and critical risk periods to provide an empirical foundation for tailoring targeted interventions and suicide prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study was conducted at The Third Hospital of Mianyang, a tertiary care hospital that also serves as the Mental Health Center, functioning as a regional diagnosis and treatment center that provides comprehensive psychiatric care and receives referred cases from surrounding areas. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third Hospital of Mianyang, Sichuan Mental Health Center (Approval number: 2023–96), which also waived the requirement for written informed consent due to the retrospective design and the use of anonymized patient data. To ensure patient confidentiality, all personally identifiable information was removed from the analytical dataset and replaced with a unique study code. The anonymized data were stored securely with access restricted to the principal investigators.

This retrospective cohort study analysed data from all patients hospitalized for intentional drug overdose suicide attempts at the research hospital between January 2020 to December 2023. Patients with a discharge diagnosis of drug overdose were screened using the electronic case record system. Case identification was based on medical record documentation of intentional overdose. Intentional overdose with suicidal intent was identified based on ICD-10 codes for drug poisoning/overdose, confirmed by the attending physician’s documentation in the medical record and corroborated by patient or family report. The exclusion criteria were inadvertent overdose and overdose involving a nondrug toxin (e.g., chemicals). Details are provided in the supplementary figure S1 of the flowchart.

Analyses were stratified into two distinct levels: patient-level analyses (N = 604) focusing on demographic and clinical characteristics of individual patients (Table 1), and case-level analyses (N = 631) examining patterns across all overdose cases, including medication classes and temporal factors (Table 2). Polypharmacy was defined as ingestion of ≥ 3 distinct drug classes based on the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system, rather than the number of pharmaceutical products. Clinical information regarding overdose ingestion characteristics was collected for the included patients, including the timing and day of administration (daytime [8:00 AM − 7:00 PM], early night [7:00 PM − 12:00 AM], or late night [12:00 AM − 8:00 AM]), month, duration from overdose attempt to admission, length of hospital stay, alcohol consumption, type of medication (psychotropic medications such as antidepressants, Anxiolytics(non-BZD), antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, and nonpsychotropic medications), and the quantity of different drug types utilized. Demographic and other data, including sex, age, marital status, education level, residential address, prior psychiatric contact, and medical history, as well as the patient’s subsequent disposition following emergency treatment, were documented.

Diagnostic ascertainment

Diagnostic ascertainment comprised: (1) ICD-10 codes at discharge for hospitalization; (2) diagnostic consensus reached by two attending psychiatrists (both at attending level or higher); (3) collateral history from patients/families for select cases. While formal inter-rater reliability statistics were not calculated, the use of standardized diagnostic procedures and independent assessment by experienced psychiatrists ensured diagnostic consistency throughout the study.

Statistical analyses

The patients were classified into decadal intervals (≤20, 21–30, 31–40, 41–50, 51–60, and > 60 years) to analyse the gender features of various age cohorts, followed by a sex-specific evaluation of age distribution. Furthermore, as the adolescent cohort aged 12–18 years constituted nearly 50% of the total sample, this subgroup was examined independently in comparison to the adult cohort. The monthly distribution of medication use was examined, particularly among adolescents, to evaluate potential seasonal patterns. Missing data were handled as an ‘Unknown’ category (N = 631) to preserve statistical power and include all available information; a complete-case analysis (n = 611) yielded fully comparable results, confirming the robustness of our primary findings.

To identify predictors of loss to follow-up, a binary logistic regression model was employed using the Enter method in SPSS. The outcome was failure to attend follow-up (coded 1 vs. attendance 0). Predictors included gender, age group, history of psychiatric care (outpatient/hospitalization), concomitant alcohol use, and polypharmacy (≥3 ATC drugs). Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (χ2 = 5.906, df = 8, p = 0.658), and the model explained approximately 40% of the variance in follow-up status (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.400). Multicollinearity was assessed via tolerance and VIF statistics. The monthly overdose incidence was assessed using a chi-square goodness-of-fit test (df = 11) to compare the observed monthly distribution against the expected uniform distribution (equal cases per month).

The statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 for Windows (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL). Continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) or minimum–maximum values. Continuous variables were used for descriptive purposes only and were not included in parametric tests. Inferential analyses involved chi-square tests and binary logistic regression, with VIF used to evaluate multicollinearity. All p values were two-tailed, and a P-value of 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 631 overdose cases met the inclusion criteria, originating from 604 unique patients (as 20 patients had repeat overdoses). Subsequent analyses are stratified accordingly: patient-level analyses (N = 604) focus on demographic and clinical history, while case-level analyses (N = 631) examine ingestion patterns and temporal factors. Among the 604 participants, 457 (75.7%, 95% CI 72.1–78.9) were female and 147 (24.3%, 95% CI 21.1–27.9) were male, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 1:3.1 (95% CI 1:2.6–1:3.8). The patients in the overall case sample were aged between 12 and 88 years, with an average age of 27.52 ± 18.14 years. A total of 296 patients (49.0%, 95% CI 45.0–53.0) were adolescents (≤18 years), whereas 308 patients (51.0%, 95% CI 47.0–55.0) were adults ( > 18 years). Additionally, more than half of the patients (57.0%) were ≤ 20 years of age. Furthermore, the number of individuals utilizing medication gradually decreased with increasing age. Importantly, across all the age groups, the incidence of overdose was greater in females than in males, with this sex disparity decreasing with increasing age (Fig. 1). This difference was statistically significant among adolescents and adults (p = 0.022). A total of 20 patients had repeat overdoses (≥2), including 11 adults and 9 adolescents, and there was no significant difference in the rate of repeat overdoses (p = 0.586). Among the cohort of adult patients, 126 (40.9%) were unmarried, 151 (49.0%) were married, 16 (5.2%) were divorced, and 15 (4.9%) were widowed. A total of 72 patients (24.7%) had a low educational level, defined as an elementary school education and below. Additionally, 131 patients (42.5%) had a moderate educational level, which included a middle school or high school education. Finally, 101 patients (32.8%) were classified as having a high educational level, encompassing a college education and above. From 2020 to 2023, the data on overdoses across various educational levels indicated that individuals with a moderate educational level consistently accounted for the largest percentage (Fig. 2).

Time patterns

Temporal patterns were analyzed at the case level (N = 631). The minimum duration from the overdose attempt to hospitalization was 1 hour, whereas the maximum duration was 72 hours, with a mean of 6.96 ± 8.76 hours. The minimum hospitalization duration was 1 day, and the maximum hospitalization duration was 23 days, with a mean of 2.17 ± 2.27 days. There were 172 overdose cases (27.3%) in spring, 135 cases (21.4%) in summer, 187 cases (29.6%) in fall, and 137 cases (21.7%) in winter. The monthly distribution of overdose cases showed a bimodal pattern, with peaks occurring in April and December. The result was statistically significant, χ2 (11, N = 631) = 34.33, p < 0.001, indicating a non-uniform distribution of cases across months. This pattern was more pronounced in the adolescent population, demonstrating a decreasing tendency from May to July after attaining an initial peak in April, followed by a second peak in December (Fig. 3). A total of 321 (50.9%) patients opted to attempt suicide by drug overdose during daytime hours [8:00 AM-7:00 PM]. Adolescents and adults did not show significant differences in the time of day for overdose attempts. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of this finding, with consistent non-significant results in both the all-case analysis (including missing data, n = 631, p = 0.179) and the complete-case analysis (n = 611, p = 0.087).

Patterns of drug use

Among the 631 overdose cases, 531 (84.2%) involved psychiatric medications; 100 (15.8%) involved nonpsychiatric drugs, including antibiotics and antihypertensives. The prevalence of nonpsychiatric drug use was significantly greater among adults than among adolescents (p < 0.05). Antidepressants were the most frequently prescribed psychiatric drugs, accounting for 296 patients (46.9%, 95% CI 43.0–51.0). This was followed by benzodiazepines in 198 patients (31.4%), antipsychotics in 196 patients (31.1%), anxiolytics (non-BZD) in 114 patients (18.1%), mood stabilizers in 56 patients (8.9%), and Z-drugs in 50 patients (7.9%). The drug use patterns differed between adolescents and adults. Among adults, benzodiazepines were the most frequently used substance (138 cases, 42.7%), with clonazepam being the predominant agent. In contrast, antidepressants were most prevalent among adolescents (205 cases, 66.6%), particularly sertraline. Detailed ATC mapping, specific agents, and additional pharmacological characteristics are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. No significant difference was observed in the selection of a single-use drug between adolescents and adults. Adolescents exhibited a greater propensity for concurrent use of multiple drugs (p < 0.05), whereas adults were more likely to consume alcohol in conjunction with drug use (p = 0.003). Mental illness history and follow-up

Among the 604 patients, 514 (85.1%, 95% CI 81.8–87.9) had a psychiatric outpatient visit or consultation before their first overdose, whereas 314 (52.0%, 95% CI 48.0–56.0) had a history of psychiatric hospitalization. Compared with adults, adolescents presented significantly higher rates of outpatient (94.3% vs. 76.3%, p < 0.001) and hospitalization (62.8% vs. 41.6%, p < 0.001) visits.

Patients who presented to the hospital following intentional overdose were discharged after treatment. Among all 604 patients with follow-up, destinations were mutually exclusive: 24.0% opted for psychiatric hospitalization, 47.7% received care at a psychiatric outpatient clinic, and 28.5% did not pursue any further psychiatric contact at the hospital postdischarge. Among these patients (based on the initial event per patient), 28.7% were adolescents who were hospitalized in a psychiatric hospital, and 36.7% were adults who went straight home after discharge with no further psychiatric follow-up. The majority of patients experienced favourable clinical outcomes following emergency treatment. A 57-year-old male patient with a history of three previous suicide attempts died shortly after discharge. The types and dosages of medications involved in this case were undocumented. Upon admission, the patient presented in a comatose state and had a history of chronic heavy alcohol consumption accompanied by significant emaciation. The family declined aggressive interventions, including haemoperfusion, and the patient passed away following discharge.

Among the 604 patients, 336 (55.6%) were diagnosed with depressive disorder, 52 (8.6%) were diagnosed with a childhood mood disorder, 35 (5.8%) were diagnosed with bipolar disorder, 34 (5.6%) were diagnosed with anxiety disorder, and 30 (5.0%) were diagnosed with schizophrenia. Depressive disorders were the predominant mental illness in both the adolescent and adult populations. Figure 4 illustrates the various mental illness diagnoses within the overdose population.

Predictors of loss to psychiatric follow-up and repeated overdose

Binary logistic regression (N = 604 patients) identified significant predictors of follow-up failure (χ2 (6) = 197.489, p < 0.001; Nagelkerke R2 = 40.0%). Female sex (aOR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.34–0.91, p = 0.019) and prior psychiatric contact (outpatient aOR = 0.12; hospitalization aOR = 0.21, both p < 0.001) were protective factors, while age, alcohol use, and polypharmacy showed no significant associations. Multicollinearity was acceptable (tolerance 0.763–0.958; VIF 1.044–1.310).

Analysis of repeated overdose predictors (n = 20 events) revealed no statistically significant associations in the final model (Omnibus test, p = 0.498). Given the limited number of events, this analysis should be considered exploratory, as the small sample size constrains statistical power and may limit the detection of potentially significant associations. The limited event size and model fit issues suggest that repeated overdose behaviour may be influenced by factors beyond the clinical variables measured in this study (Table 3).

Discussion

This study systematically analyzes drug overdose patterns in Western China, revealing critical insights into temporal trends and care discontinuation. A prominent finding is the bimodal distribution of overdoses, with April and December peaks coinciding with academic cycles, suggesting a link between academic stress and adolescent suicidality. Equally important is the substantially higher rate of post-discharge disengagement from psychiatric care among adults compared to adolescents, highlighting a crucial gap in maintaining mental health support for adult populations. These findings, complemented by our predictive analysis identifying prior psychiatric contact as protective against loss to follow-up, underscore the need for targeted interventions, including school-based screenings during high-risk periods and enhanced care continuity strategies for vulnerable adults.

In the late 1990s, the overall suicide rate in China was approximately 23 cases per 100,000 people, ranking among the highest globally [12]. The female suicide rate exceeds that of males, as indicated by a sex ratio of 0.88, which suggests an increased risk of suicide among females [13]. There is increasing evidence indicating a high risk of suicide among young individuals and adolescents, particularly young women [14, 15]. The present study revealed comparable findings; specifically, within the overdose population, female patients constituted approximately three-quarters of the overall sample, significantly exceeding the number of male patients across all age groups. The sex difference decreased with age and was statistically significant between adolescents and adults. The results indicate that adolescent females should be prioritized for inclusion in interventions targeting overdose behaviour and suggest that sex-related risk factors may decrease with age. Research indicates that adolescents and young adults are more susceptible to suicidal ideation and behaviour, which is largely attributed to significant mental health issues [16]. The period of adolescence is a unique phase of life characterized by rapid physical, emotional and developmental changes [17]. Adolescents frequently exhibit insufficient psychological coping strategies. Without adequate family and social support, this population is at increased risk of suicide attempts [18]. Intentional drug overdose represents the predominant method of suicide among adolescents [19].

The monthly distribution of overdose cases showed elevated rates during spring and fall, a pattern that coincides with seasonal variations in mental health disorders and academic pressures. Research indicates that hospitalizations for depression and bipolar disorder peak in the fall and spring, whereas admissions for childhood mood disorders peak in the fall [20]. The concentration of cases during April and December corresponds closely with major academic examination periods in the Chinese education system, suggesting that academic stress may serve as an important contextual factor influencing the timing of overdose incidents, particularly among adolescents. A monthly distribution plot of adolescent overdoses indicates fluctuations in case numbers corresponding to the academic calendar. Specifically, the lowest incidence occurs in January and July, with a notable increase following the commencement of the school year, culminating in a peak two months thereafter. Studies indicate that American adolescents experience a comparable increase in self-poisoning incidents during the academic year [21]. Academic stress is closely associated with the emergence of behavioural issues and adverse emotional states in adolescents [22]. Moreover, many studies have suggested that Chinese adolescents experience more academic stress than do adolescents in other countries, such as the U.S., Japan, and Korea [23, 24]. The results of this study indicate a correlation between academic stress and attempted suicide by drug overdose among adolescents. Academic stress was associated with increased psychological burden in adolescents, which correlated with engagement in maladaptive and extreme coping mechanisms for emotional distress. This finding underscores the essential function of school-based mental health support and intervention strategies. The reduction in case numbers during February and July, which are periods that align with Chinese holidays and traditional festivals, suggests a potential association between social interactions/shared positive emotions and reduced suicide attempt risk. While this temporal coincidence is noteworthy, we caution that our study design cannot determine whether these patterns share common underlying seasonal factors or represent independent phenomena. The observed bimodal distribution may reflect a combination of factors specific to drug overdose help-seeking behaviours.

This study demonstrated that the proportion of patients with a middle or high school education was consistently the highest among adults who experienced an overdose. Education is a crucial element of socioeconomic status and significantly affects various socioeconomic indicators, such as income level and occupational standing [25]. Multiple studies indicate a negative correlation between educational attainment and suicide attempts, suggesting that higher education levels confer a protective effect, resulting in a lower risk of suicide attempts [26]. Individuals with lower educational attainment experience higher rates of relationship issues and substance abuse. Research indicates that individuals with a high school education have the highest suicide rates [27]. The present research yielded comparable findings. This may be associated with elevated psychological and mental stress in individuals with lower literacy and income levels [28]. Individuals experiencing significant economic and psychological stress, coupled with inadequate health literacy and psychological resources, are more prone to engage in self-injurious behaviours. This finding may partially elucidate the relationship between educational attainment and overdose behaviour. This study indicates that individuals with a moderate level of education are more prone to utilize drug overdose as a method of suicide. This group demonstrates improved access to medications and the ability to process basic medical information; however, they may misjudge medication toxicity and overestimate the effects of medical interventions. Conversely, adults with lower educational attainment tend to utilize more lethal methods for suicide, such as overdose by pesticides. Consequently, variations in educational attainment must be accounted for in mental health interventions and public health policies to ensure more tailored and effective support [29].

This study demonstrated that patients who attempt suicide via overdose commonly utilize psychiatric medications, with antidepressants being the most frequently employed. This finding aligns with earlier research indicating that individuals with mood disorders exhibit an increased risk of drug overdose [9, 30]. Patients frequently utilize their daily medications to alleviate psychological distress or attain emotional relief, resulting in behaviours associated with overdose. The pattern of medication use differs significantly between adolescents and adults, with adolescents more frequently utilizing antidepressants, whereas adults tend to favour benzodiazepines, which aligns with previous research [31]. Depressive disorders are a significant factor in the aetiology of adolescent suicide [32]. The use of antidepressants among adolescents may indicate adolescents’ susceptibility to emotion regulation and stress-coping mechanisms. The preference for benzodiazepines among adults may be associated with the need to mitigate anxiety symptoms and facilitate sleep [33]. Furthermore, the selection of nonpsychiatric medications was notably greater in adults than in adolescents. This phenomenon highlights the greater degree of mental health problems and history of psychiatric medication use in the adolescent group, as well as the accessibility of common over-the-counter medications. From a clinical perspective, variations in medication selection between adolescents and adults indicate distinct requirements for preventing overdose attempts in cases of suicide. Adolescents require not only effective management and antidepressant monitoring but also an emphasis on the development of mood management and coping skills. For adult patients, it is essential to consider the potential suicide risk associated with daily medications alongside psychiatric medication management. Furthermore, the sharing of drugs and alcohol among adults constitutes one-tenth of the total cases, indicating that alcohol misuse may increase the risk of drug overdose [34]. Targeted mental health interventions and medication management for the prevention of suicide attempts may effectively reduce the risk of suicide by drug overdose in high-risk populations.

Over half of the overdose cases in this study were associated with a history of psychiatric hospitalization, indicating that the risk of suicide in patients with psychiatric disorders warrants serious consideration [35]. Depressive disorders represent the most prevalent category of psychiatric disorders. This finding aligns with previous research and indicates that mood disorders represent a significant risk factor for self-harm [36]. Patients with schizophrenia are included in the overdose demographic group, potentially owing to their hallucinatory and delusional symptoms, which are positive symptoms linked to suicidal ideation. Additionally, the impaired reality-testing capabilities of patients which schizophrenia complicate the regulation of their behaviour [37, 38]. Although it accounts for a relatively small proportion of patients, borderline personality disorder is characterized by emotional impulsivity and interpersonal instability, which are significant risk factors for suicidal behaviour [39].

Most patients undergoing overdose had prior psychiatric contact, reflecting preexisting mental health issues and help-seeking attempts, particularly among adolescents who demonstrated higher visit rates. Conversely, adults exhibited fewer pre-overdose psychiatric visits and a greater tendency to return home directly after emergency care, suggesting divergent care-engagement behaviours. A qualitative review of discharge documentation indicated that patient refusal of the provided psychiatric referral was a predominant recorded reason, despite clear recommendations from treating physicians. This finding is further contextualized by our predictive model, which identified prior psychiatric contact as a significant protective factor (outpatient aOR = 0.12; hospitalization aOR = 0.21). The synergy between these findings suggests that refusal may often reflect absence of established care relationships rather than purely patient-level factors. While the retrospective design limited deeper exploration of refusal determinants, these results underscore the need for future prospective studies to employ mixed-methods approaches, combining quantitative assessments with patient interviews to better understand barriers to care engagement, particularly for individuals without prior mental health service contact.

Adults frequently neglect their emotional issues because of social obligations and life pressures, resulting in an underestimation of their mental health needs. This lack of timely and effective mental health support can lead to self-harming behaviours as individuals seek extreme solutions. Additionally, certain patients may conceal their suicidal intent, complicating the timely recognition and intervention of their mental health issues [40]. This study indicated that the repeat overdose rate among adults was not significantly different from that among adolescents; however, adults received less psychological care support after the overdose event than adolescents did, implying potential neglect of suicide risk among adult patients. Concerns regarding adolescent mental health necessitate an increased provision of psychological support for adult patients, aimed at fostering more effective coping mechanisms, particularly following emergency treatment.

Logistic regression confirmed female sex (B = −0.584, p = 0.019) and prior psychiatric contact (outpatient B = −2.113, p < 0.001; hospitalization B = −1.554, p < 0.001) as significant protective factors against loss to follow-up. This aligns with adolescents’ higher service utilization and suggests that gendered help-seeking behaviours (females: 75.7% of cohort) and established care continuity mitigate disengagement. Notably, age was not an independent predictor (p = 0.447), implying that higher adult disengagement rates may stem from lower baseline service utilization—consistent with adults’ tendency to neglect mental health needs due to social pressures, as noted previously. These findings underscore the urgency of targeting improved referral systems for individuals without prior mental health engagement, addressing the neglect of suicide risk in adults highlighted in earlier analyses.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a systematic analysis of clinical characteristics among 604 patients attempting suicide by drug overdose in Western China. It has revealed significant differences in medication patterns, temporal distribution, and follow-up adherence between adolescent and adult populations. The application of multivariable logistic regression has identified independent predictors of loss to follow-up, providing an empirical basis for early intervention in high-risk groups. Despite the important findings outlined above, the results of this study should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, the single-center design, while providing valuable insights into overdose patterns in Western China, may limit the generalizability of our findings to other geographic regions with different healthcare systems and patient populations. Future multicenter studies spanning diverse healthcare settings would help validate these results. Second, the lack of systematically recorded dosage and toxicity data precluded analysis of dose-response relationships or assessment of overdose lethality, which represents an important aspect of suicidal behavior that warrants investigation in future studies. Third, our approach of categorizing missing values as ‘Unknown’ (applied to 20 cases for medication time data), while preserving statistical power, may introduce potential misclassification bias, particularly if the missingness was not completely at random. Fourth, by focusing exclusively on drug overdose cases, we cannot determine whether the observed patterns reflect method-specific factors or broader suicidal behaviors. Finally, diagnoses were based on clinical assessments rather than standardized research interviews, which may introduce non-differential misclassification bias. Despite these limitations, our study provides a comprehensive characterization of overdose patterns in a previously understudied population.

Conclusion

This study identifies adolescent females and individuals with depressive disorders as key high-risk populations for targeted suicide prevention efforts. The bimodal peak in attempts coinciding with academic examination periods underscores the urgency of implementing school-based interventions, including mental health screenings and stress management programs timed to semester cycles. A critical finding is the substantially higher rate of disengagement from psychiatric follow-up among adults compared to adolescents, highlighting a care gap that necessitates further exploration. Multivariable analysis confirmed prior psychiatric contact as a significant protective factor against loss to follow-up, suggesting that this gap predominantly affects individuals without established mental health pathways. Future research should investigate systemic strategies such as improved referral protocols and follow-up mechanisms to enhance care continuity. These findings underscore the importance of public health initiatives focused on school-based screenings during high-risk periods and reinforced support for adults transitioning from emergency care.
