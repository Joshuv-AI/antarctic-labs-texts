Bypassing the imaging thickness limit with a translationally-invariant nonlocal thin film
Authors: Bohan Zhang, Liu Li, Xinyuan Xue, Yibao Xu, Qitong Li, Hao Zhang, Liqun Sun, Liangcai Cao, Yuanmu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional imaging relies on both lenses and free-space propagation. Despite recent advancements, a thickness limit was established for conventional translationally-variant imaging systems based on the nonlocality required for image formation. We bypass this limit with an imaging system composed of a single, 12.2-µm-thick dielectric film placed directly on an image sensor, eliminating both lenses and free space. By engineering nonlocal Fabry–Pérot resonances, which allows light to spread and interfere constructively across the film’s surface, our multilayer film effectively assigns an individual aperture to each object point, bypassing the single-aperture assumption that dictates the thickness limit in conventional optics. We experimentally demonstrate deterministic, computation-free 2D imaging with sub-100-μm resolution. Furthermore, the system’s functionality is dynamically reconfigurable; by simply tuning the illumination wavelength, we achieve depth-resolved 3D imaging and perform optical diffraction tomography to reconstruct 3D refractive index profiles. This lensless platform establishes a new paradigm for ultracompact, multifunctional optical systems with potential applications in endoscopy, machine vision, and wearable sensors.

Introduction

For centuries, optical imaging has relied on lenses—spatially-variant phase elements that manipulate light via free-space propagation to form images1. This principle imposes an irreducible trade-off: achieving high-fidelity imaging requires bulky, multi-element optics with precisely aligned air gaps, limiting scalability in a vast array of size-constrained applications, from medical endoscopy2, wearable sensors3 to advanced machine vision systems4.

While recent advances in diffractive optics5,6 and metalenses7–9 promise thinner lenses, the total track length (TTL), defined as the thickness from the front surface of the lens module to the image plane, remains fundamentally constrained by the need for free-space propagation10. Space plates11–13 offer an alternative by compressing free-space propagation distance, yet they still require integration with lenses with spatially varying phase response for wavefront collection. A thickness limit for conventional translationally-variant imaging systems has been established by analyzing the inherent overlapping nonlocality, which quantifies the number of independent lateral communication channels required across a single, shared aperture10.

Computational lensless imaging offers one alternative by replacing optics with coded apertures and intensive reconstruction algorithms14. Though promising for miniaturization, this approach often relies on strong priors and heavy computation, leading to ill-posed problems that compromise real-time performance and generality. Consequently, achieving deterministic, computation-free 2D imaging that truly bypasses the conventional thickness limit has remained a major unsolved challenge.

Here, we introduce an imaging platform that bypasses the thickness limit of conventional imaging systems using only a simple, translationally invariant dielectric thin film attached directly to an image sensor. By exploiting nonlocal Fabry–Pérot resonance within the dielectric stack, our system enables the constructive interference necessary for image formation without requiring lenses or free-space propagation. We experimentally demonstrate direct, computation-free 2D imaging. Furthermore, this nonlocal mechanism unlocks remarkable multifunctionality from a single passive element. Simply tuning the illumination wavelength dynamically reconfigures the system’s point spread function (PSF) for depth-resolved 3D imaging. This inherent angular selectivity also facilitates optical diffraction tomography (ODT), enabling 3D refractive index reconstruction through wavelength-controlled annular illumination.

Results

Principle of deterministic 2D thin-film imaging

Conventional imaging relies on both lenses and free-space propagation (Fig. 1a). Under the Fresnel approximation, a lens maps each coordinate in its aperture to a specific object spatial frequency, with lenses and free space working together to ensure constructive interference at the image plane. Since traditional optical elements exhibit only local responses, interference between distinct spatial points requires lateral wavefront diffusion through a free-space propagation kernel (Fig. 1a, dashed box). Correcting wavefront aberrations further necessitates position-dependent phase gradients, with complexity increasing as the system’s TTL decreases. To enable interference without free-space propagation, the optical element must possess a nonlocal response16–23 that couples incident fields across spatial positions. A simple yet effective realization of such a nonlocal optical element is a multilayer dielectric thin film stack structured as a Bragg microcavity (Fig. 1b, inset). Its Fabry–Pérot response creates a laterally distributed impulse function h (Supplementary Note 1), effectively creating an ultrathin spatially-distributed communication channel, allowing constructive interference of light from a continuous spatial range to a single output point, thus eliminating lens and free-space propagation. The lateral spreading length W24 of the nonlocal thin film quantifies the spatial coupling range and determines resolution. W scales with the cavity Q-factor (Supplementary Note 2).

The nonlocal Fabry–Pérot multi-beam interference kernel selectively transmits wavefront components near normal incidence, with its transmission function t reciprocal to h (Fig. 1b, inset). The resulting PSF shifts proportionally with object displacement at 1:1 magnification—inverting the behavior of lens-based systems. To understand how the nonlocal thin film architecture bypasses conventional limitations, we must first re-examine the thickness constraints of traditional imaging. Conventional, translationally-variant systems rely on a single, finite aperture. The sampling theorem dictates that to resolve N distinct object points, an equal number of independent samples must be collected across the aperture. As shown in Fig. 1c, interactions between aperture points in the upper-left half and image points in the lower-right half define a set of mutually orthogonal channels across the dividing plane S. This requires a minimal thickness that scales with the number of independent internal communication channels, expressed as Nλ0/(4nmax), where λ0 is the incident wavelength and nmax is the maximum refractive index between the aperture and image plane10.

In contrast, our translationally-invariant system (Fig. 1d) bypasses the single-aperture constraint by effectively assigning an individual aperture to each object point. Optical interactions between aperture points and image points do not need to be mutually orthogonal. This design simplifies internal optical communication and imposes a minimal thickness of just λ0/(2nmax). A critical advantage is that the number of required channels remains constant regardless of the pixel count, allowing for increased resolution without a corresponding increase in system thickness. This decoupling of resolution and thickness demonstrates that our architecture circumvents the structural constraints of conventional optics without violating the underlying physical principles of overlapping nonlocality, thereby confirming that the required thickness is indeed minimal (Supplementary Note 4).

Experimental deterministic 2D imaging

We experimentally validated the 2D imaging capability by attaching the nonlocal thin film directly on an image sensor (QHYCCD QHY991). Fig. 2a shows the assembled imaging system, with the field-of-view (FOV) determined by the active area of the image sensor to be 3.28 × 2.60 mm2. The 1-inch-diameter film comprising a 13-pair SiO2/TiO2 distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) stack was fabricated via a standard ion-assisted deposition process (see Methods). The nonlocal thin film can, in principle, be directly deposited onto the image sensor, thus eliminating the conventional lens assembly process. Its thickness, typically on the order of 10 μm, is also negligible compared to conventional lens modules. More interestingly, unlike conventional lens-based systems, the nonlocal thin film requires no precise axial or lateral alignment and can be placed at any location within the imaging system. To avoid damage to the sensor, here the nonlocal thin film is attached to the protective spacer of the image sensor. Nonetheless, the transfer functions of the nonlocal thin film and the protective spacer are both linear operators in k-space and can therefore commute (Supplementary Note 6)1,18.

Fig. 2c quantifies how increasing the pair number M of a DBR made of SiO2/TiO2 can enhance the cavity Q-factor, thereby improving the system’s resolution, defined by the full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) of the PSF, at object distances ρ = 2.25 mm, ρ = 4.25 mm, and infinity, respectively (Supplementary Note 7). Simulated PSFs show progressive narrowing with increasing M (Fig. 2d). Further imaging simulations of a letter “E” with a line spacing of 150 μm also demonstrate resolution improvement with increasing M (Fig. 2e), consistent with the predicted scaling.

Under incoherent narrowband illumination generated by a tunable laser and a rotating diffuser, each point object directly forms a PSF with 1:1 magnification independent of the distance from the sensor. The illumination is slightly detuned toward a longer wavelength by the resonance’s FWHM to enhance imaging stability and resolution. At M = 13, with a simulated Q-factor of 54621, the theoretical image resolution (PSF’s FWHM) is 86.8 μm and 121.9 μm at ρ = 2.25 mm and ρ = 4.25 mm, respectively. Although the experimentally measured Q-factor of the Fabry–Pérot resonance at normal incidence is reduced to 19518, with the resolution being relatively insensitive to Q-factor variations in the high-Q regime (Fig. 2c), the measured image resolution still reaches 89.7 μm and 124.5 μm at ρ = 2.25 mm and ρ = 4.25 mm, respectively (Fig. 2f, g).

We further experimentally demonstrated telecentric imaging of a USAF resolution test chart. At ρ = 2.25 mm, the system can resolve Group 3 Element 4 with 88.5-μm line spacing with 0.14 contrast—above the 0.13 Rayleigh threshold (Fig. 2h). At ρ = 4.25 mm, the system can resolve Group 3 Element 1 and Group 2 Element 2, with only a slight fall in resolution, maintaining ~ 1:1 magnification and extended depth-of-field characteristics (Fig. 2i). The brightness of the captured image exhibits subtle spatial variations, which is caused by the spatially-varying resonance wavelength of the thin film due to thickness non-uniformity and can be improved by epitaxial thin film growth25.

Our system achieves a TTL of just 12.2 µm, over two orders of magnitude thinner than conventional millimeter-scale miniature lens modules, while providing sub-100 μm resolution without the computational overhead required by other lensless imaging techniques (Supplementary Note 8). The system achieves a volumetric channel density26 approximately 8.2 times greater than that of conventional lens-based systems (Supplementary Note 9). Based on this performance profile, the current implementation is not intended to serve as a direct replacement for microscope objectives, but rather to extend image formation into a regime of extreme compactness inaccessible to conventional optics. The imaging resolution can be further enhanced by reducing the object distance or by shifting to shorter operational wavelengths, which involves a straightforward scaling of the film’s layer thicknesses. In addition, cascading two tilted thin films can reduce the angular linewidth of the transmission peak by roughly an order of magnitude compared to a single, upright film, leading to a substantial improvement in resolution without enhancing the cavity Q-factor (Supplementary Note 10). Furthermore, the FOV and magnification can be custom-tailored using a curved detector (Supplementary Note 11).

3D imaging via wavelength switching

Beyond 2D imaging, the nonlocal thin film can further enable depth-resolved 3D imaging by PSF engineering via simply switching between two illumination wavelengths. At the central resonance wavelength, it maintains a point-like PSF with an extended depth-of-field. Conversely, with a slight blue-shift of the incident wavelength, the PSF becomes highly depth-dependent, adopting a ring-shaped structure whose radius scales monotonically with object distance ρ.

Fig. 3a, b confirms close agreement between simulated and measured depth-dependent PSFs for both operation modes, with the experimental operation wavelength of 1607.4 nm (dot-mode) and 1606.1 nm (ring-mode). We validated 3D reconstruction of a 60°-tilted letter “E” target (Fig. 3c), with ground truth albedo and depth maps shown in Fig. 3d, e, respectively. Under spatially incoherent illumination, two raw images were captured at distinct wavelengths (Fig. 3f, g). Remaining errors mainly stem from the thin film’s temperature-induced resonance wavelength drifts, thickness non-uniformity, and PSF truncation due to the finite sensor size, which can be mitigated with further refined material selection, fabrication, and PSF calibration (Supplementary Note 14).

3D refractive index reconstruction

Building upon the previously demonstrated capabilities for deterministic 2D imaging and depth-resolved 3D imaging, the nonlocal thin film’s angular selectivity can further enable ODT through wavelength-controlled annular illumination, facilitating ultra-compact 3D refractive index reconstruction. To overcome limitations of coherent illumination in ODT—including speckle noise, vibration sensitivity, and requirement for precise interferometric alignment—we implemented partially coherent illumination29,30. As shown in Fig. 4a, the nonlocal thin film can serve as a cover slip of the sample, which is directly placed on the image sensor, and modulate the annular illumination profile via wavelength scanning. Angular diversity sequentially fills the frequency support domain via the superposition of Ewald spherical shells under the weak scattering approximation, enabling refractive index reconstruction (Fig. 4b)31. We experimentally validated this approach by capturing diffraction intensity patterns under different annular illumination angles ranging from 0° to 20° (Fig. 4c) and reconstructing polystyrene beads with a nominal diameter of 200 μm immersed in index-matching liquid with n = 1.561. The volume-rendered reconstruction result is depicted in Fig. 4d. Lateral and axial slices through the representative bead (Fig. 4e) show reconstructed dimensions of 215.3 μm (lateral) × 266.6 μm (axial), respectively. The corresponding refractive index contrasts are reconstructed as Δn = 0.0072, with the ground-truth refractive index contrast of 0.006732. Due to the well-known missing-cone effect originating from limited illumination angle coverage33–35, axial elongation occurs, leading to inaccuracies in reconstructing the beads’ geometric parameters and refractive index contrasts. In our experiments, the usable angular range is constrained by the sensor’s finite area, bit precision (8-bit) and image clipping caused by the spacer thickness (Supplementary Note 16). These constraints can be mitigated by using image sensors with larger areas and higher bit precision and by directly depositing the thin film on the image sensor chip. The accuracy of the reconstruction can be further improved by increasing the number of imaging wavelengths36,37 or by incorporating deep learning38,39.

Discussion

Our work establishes a new paradigm for optical imaging, replacing the architecture of lenses and free-space propagation with a single, passive dielectric thin film. By engineering nonlocal, angle-selective Fabry–Pérot resonances, we have demonstrated that a simple film can form an image directly on a sensor, bypassing the thickness limit of conventional systems. This platform achieves computation-free 2D imaging. Furthermore, the thin film’s narrowband and dispersive nature can be leveraged to unlock advanced computational modalities, including depth-resolved 3D imaging and microtomography, with a simple scan of the illumination wavelength.

The versatility of this platform points toward significant future advancements. The operating wavelength can be readily scaled from the ultraviolet to the infrared simply by adjusting the film’s layer thicknesses. Performance can be further enhanced by directly depositing the dielectric stack onto the image sensor, a step that promises higher quality factors and streamlined integration. In its present form, the nonlocal thin film is a narrowband device operating under active laser illumination. It has an effective f-number of 47.0, approximately six times the typical value of 8 for telecentric lenses40–42 (Supplementary Note 17). However, optimizing the film’s spatial dispersion and angular selectivity could enable broadband operation or the engineering of more complex point spread functions for new imaging modes43, a strategy that can also be leveraged to enhance the system’s efficiency (Supplementary Note 17). Alternatively, the thin film can be engineered for polychromatic operation, enabling multiwavelength imaging within a single ultrathin element (Supplementary Note 18). Furthermore, moderate-bandwidth sources, such as LEDs, may suffice for applications like localization metrology that rely on PSF centroid estimation rather than size44–46. The field of view and magnification are not fixed; they can be custom-tailored using curved detectors47, opening possibilities for radical new designs like lensless telescopes that do not require long focal lengths or microscopic imagers capable of geometric magnification (Supplementary Note 11).

This technology offers a scalable pathway to extreme miniaturization, poised to enable imaging in applications where it was previously impractical or impossible. In medicine, it could lead to sub-millimeter endoscopic probes capable of combined structural and refractive index tomography48. For industrial automation, it could allow for compact 3D machine vision systems that operate in confined spaces49. Its flat and potentially flexible form factor is ideal for seamless integration into wearable sensors50. The core principle even extends beyond optics to fields like matter-wave imaging, where potential-barrier Fabry–Pérot cavities could replace the bulky magnetic lenses currently required to manipulate neutrons51,52.

Methods

Thin film design and fabrication

The nonlocal thin film was designed as a multilayer Bragg microcavity structure. Using SiO2 (refractive index n = 1.44) and TiO2 (n = 2.15) layers, distributed Bragg reflectors (DBRs) were designed for a target wavelength of 1550 nm, resulting in layer thicknesses of 269.1 nm for SiO2 and 180.2 nm for TiO2. Theoretical simulations (Fig. 2) determined the optimal number of DBR pairs (M) for the desired imaging distance. Accounting for practical factors like scattering loss due to fabrication-induced surface roughness, M = 13 was selected. For the central cavity layer, its normal thickness was fine-tuned to 553.8 nm to compensate for anticipated manufacturing deviations based on fabrication experience.

This design yielded a stack with the central microcavity sandwiched between 13 pairs of alternating SiO2/TiO2 layers, with a total thickness of 12.2 µm. The film was deposited onto a one-inch-diameter fused silica substrate via ion-assisted deposition by Beijing Qifenglanda Inc.

Angle-resolved transmittance characterization

The angle-resolved transmittance spectra of the fabricated film were characterized by directly placing it in front of a photodetector (Thorlabs DET10N/M) mounted on a motorized rotary stage. Illumination was provided by a tunable laser source (Santec TSL-550), with its output collimated and passed through a polarizer. A chopping wheel synchronized to a lock-in amplifier (Zurich Instruments MFLI) was used to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio during detection. By sequentially tuning the laser wavelength and rotating the stage to vary the incidence angle, we obtained the normalized angle-resolved transmittance spectra as shown in Fig. 2b.

Reconstruction algorithm

The 3D imaging reconstruction algorithm via wavelength switching integrates regularized deconvolution with geometric calibration to optimize albedo and depth maps. Its objective function minimizes the mean squared residual (MSR) between captured images and depth-dependent forward model predictions, augmented with regularization terms. Optimization employs an Adam optimizer on an RTX 3060 GPU.

For optical diffraction tomography (ODT), the reconstruction uses a wave-propagation forward model53 with gradient-descent optimization to reduce intensity mismatches between simulated and measured diffraction patterns across all illumination angles. Hybrid regularization—combining non-negativity and sparsity priors—addresses the missing-cone problem, enabling high-fidelity reconstructions.
