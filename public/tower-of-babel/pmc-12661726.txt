The health-driven security for older adults with partial dependency in activities of daily living who experience loneliness: a research and development
Authors: Phatchanun Vivarakanon, Puangpet Meesiri, Wanida Intharacha, Jitaree Chatmontree, Sriprapi Inchithep
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth security is an important aspects affecting quality of life, particularly older people who live independently. This research and development aimed to: 1) assess the current health security situation, 2) develop a health-driven security model, and 3) evaluate the effectiveness of the developed model.MethodologyThe study consisted of four phases: 1) assessing the health safety and security of 377 older adults and conducting a focus group discussion with 15 stakeholders in Mueang District, Lampang Province; 2) developing a health-driven security model based on the human security concept; 3) implementing the developed model with 40 older adults selected through purposive sampling, calculated by G* Power statistics; and 4) evaluating the model’s effectiveness. Research tools included the developed model and data collection instruments with an IOC greater than 0.80.ResultsThe overall health safety and security situation in older adults aged 60–69 years old in Lampang Province was found to be at an average level (X = 76.50, SD = 4.90). The developed model comprises the Older Adults’ Safety and Security Questionnaire (OA-SQs) and a framework for health-driven security, covering seven aspects: environment, health, personal, knowledge for living, political, community, and economic security. Evaluation of the model’s effectiveness revealed significant improvements, particularly in environmental, health, and personal security domains, with statistical significance before and after participating in learning model (p < .05).ConclusionThe developed model is suitable for promoting the security of older adults and can be applied to both healthy and frail elderly populations.Keywords: Older adults, Security, Loneliness

Introduction

The United Nations Development Programme's Human Development Report Office [1] formalized the concept of "Human Security" to embody a comprehensive, individual-focused approach to risk prevention that transcends various domains, including economic, health, personal, political, food, environmental, and community security. These dimensions include economic security, health security, personal security, political security, food security, environmental security, and community security [1]. Economic security encompasses the ability to access flexible income sources, stable employment opportunities, financial services, and other economic opportunities [1]. Health security involves protection from pandemics, good health status, quality healthcare, access to essential health services, and comprehensive health insurance [1]. Personal security pertains to living safety in all physical forms, being free from physical harm, avoiding threats from individuals or child labor, and ensuring personal safety [1]. Political security encompasses safeguarding against political oppression, upholding human rights, promoting fair and equitable governance, and adhering to both legal and ethical principles [1]. Food security encompasses access to nutritious and adequate sustenance, access to potable water for every meal, affordability of provisions, and straightforward access to such resources [1]. Environmental security includes the preservation of a stable ecosystem, the provision of suitable living conditions in accordance with the geographical location, and protection from natural disasters, as outlined by the United Nations Development Program [1]. Lastly, community security includes ensuring safety and protection in diverse communities, managing religious and cultural tensions, promoting secure living amidst various identities, and safeguarding against crime or violence [1]. The imperative for health security is of utmost significance to older adults in the latter stages of life and those residing independently, which diverges from the prioritization presented in Maslow’s Theory [2–4]. This matter has significant implications for the fundamental security requirements of older adults who reside independently in community settings.

In Thailand, the notion of security encompasses a comprehensive state of overall well-being and interconnectedness across the physical, mental, social, and spiritual dimensions [5]. The Ministry of Social Development and Human Security has acknowledged, in accordance with the National Health Act [6], that it has established mechanisms in partnership with multiple governmental and non-governmental entities within the Ministry of Public Health. The Ministry of Public Health [7] outlined five strategies pertaining to older adults, which include: 1) promoting health security, 2) strengthening a just and equitable health system, 3) promoting Thailand’s leadership and responsibility in the global community, 4) promoting alignment between domestic and international health policies, and 5) enhancing the capability of personal and organizations in international health and coordination. These strategies aim to ensure that older adults receive the care they need and are able to live healthy and fulfilling lives. However, the lack of integration among different has hindered the full success of these endeavors.

Based on recent studies conducted by demographic and social research institutions at Mahidol University, the issue of unmet security needs among the older adults is a complex and multifaceted problem. The aforementioned dimensions entail physical, mental, social, and economic aspects, in addition to risks of chronic illnesses, abandonment, lack of social communication, and mental health problems [8, 9]. It is imperative to address the growing dependence of older adults, particularly those who live independently, as this demographic is more vulnerable than the general older population. The issues highlighted underscore this increasing dependency and its impact on older adults. Older adults who lived independently in the community tend to exhibit frailty more than those who lived with family and maintain regular activities [10]. Furthermore, negative perceptions of living alone affected 13% of individuals [11]. The physical decline associated with aging, which is a natural process, decreases the capacity of elderly individuals to care for themselves, thereby increasing the likelihood of health issues such as osteoporosis, diabetes, and hypertension. The state of mental health in older adults is often at risk, with conditions such as stress, depression, and anxiety being prevalent [8]. This increased the likelihood of accidents, falls, and mobility issues. Frailty in older adults significantly impact their quality of life in later years. Regarding social aspects, approximately 10.05% of the older population live without caregivers, and 2.51% lack caregivers in the morning [12]. Some individuals, particularly older adults, may experience income insecurity and rely on government assistance and welfare programs for daily allowances [12]. This can lead to psychological regression, loss of self-esteem, and diminished sense of pride. Given the prevalence of these problems among older adults, it is crucial to address them comprehensively [12]. This is especially important considering the recognized significance of security and safety in various contexts, as outlined above. Developing strategies aligned with international and national frameworks is vital in addressing these issues. Implementating such strategies would enable self-sufficient older individuals (ADL-2) residing in communities to experience enhanced human security in later year while also improving their overall quality lives. Thus, this research and development design aimed to: 1) examine the health safety situation of aging individuals with Activity of Daily Living – 2 (ADL-2) who live independently in the community, 2) develop a health-driven safety model, 3) implement the developed model, and 4) evaluate its effectiveness for older individuals with ADL-2 who live independently.

Definition

Older adults

Individuals aged 60 years and above who possess partial self-sufficiency in activities of daily living (ADL-2) and reside independently.

Health security

Health security signify a state of complete well-being and the harmonious interconnection of physical, mental, social, and spiritual aspects [15]. The United Nations Development Program (UNDP, 2017) outlines seven dimensions of health security: 1) economic security, ensuring financial stability; 2) health security, ensuring well-being; 3) personal security, safeguarding individual; 4) political and governance security, ensuring political stability; 5) food security, maintaining nutritional well-being; 6) environmental security, ensuring a safe environment; and 7) community security, promoting social and community well-being.

Activities of daily living 2 (ADL-2)

This study utilized the Barthel Index for Activities of Daily Living, first published in 1965 [13]. The Barthel Index assesses an individual’s ability to perform ADLs and includes 10 items: 1) feeding, 2) bathing, 3) grooming, 4) dressing, 5) bowel control, 6) bladder control, 7) toilet use, 8) transferring from bed to chair and back, 9) mobility on level surfaces, and 10) stair negotiation. An ADL-2 classificantion refer to partially dependent status, corresponding to a score ranging from 8 to 11.

Conceptual framework

This conceptual framework utilizes Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs theory and the United Nations Development Program's Human Development Report Office to address the well-being of older adults and the health-related safety and security of older individuals [1, 14] as showen in Fig. 1. It is grounded in Human Security framework proposed by the United Nations Development Program [1], which emphasizes seven essential dimensions-economic, health, political, personal, food, environmental, and community security—that are crucial to ensuring the overall well-being of individuals, particularly older adults. These dimensions, with Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs, inform the development of the Health-Driven Safety and Security Model, which serves as a guiding structure for addressing the multifaceted needs of older adults with partial dependency in activities of daily living. By integrating both human security principles and the hierarchy of human motivation, the model seeks to provide a comprehensive and person-centered approach that ultimately promotes health safety and security—the desired outcome in which older adults feel physically protected, emotionally supported, and socially connected within their living environments.

Methodology

Study design

This research and development (R&D) design aimed to investigate the current state health and security situation in older people with ADL-2 who reside alone in Mae Kong and Ton Tong subdistricts of Lampang province, Thailand.

Data collection

This study followed four main steps: 1) assessing the health, safety and security situation in older individuals, 2) developing a model of health-driven security, 3) implementing the developed model in a cohort study of older adults with ADL-2 who live alone, and 4) evaluating the effectiveness of the model.

Step 1: studying the health security situation of aging people

In this phase, the investigation focused on analyzing the health security conditions of partially independently older individuals (ADL-2) who live alone. A systematic literature review was conducted, drawing from Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs [2], the UNDP human security framework [1], and additional frameworks on health status and security in older populations. For the qualitative approach, data was collected through general information, gathering referencing health security frameworks, and posing closed-ended questions related to health safety and security. Data were collected from 30 older adults and continued until data saturation was reached using criterion-based sampling. Researchers collaborated to verify the alignment between data characteristics, emerging problmes and situations, and the needs of older individuals with ADL-2 who experience loneliness.

Participants and settings

Older adults aged 60 or over who live alone were identified using the ADL scale. Participnats were selected purposive sampling in Lampang Province to reach 30 participants.

Instrument

Closed-ended interview questions were used to share detailed responses, based on the seven dimensions of human security defined by the UNDP Human Development Report Office [1].

Methodological rigor

The qualitative instrument was reviewed by five experienced evaluators, with an Index of Item-Objective Congruence (IOC) ranging from 0.8 to 1.0. A three-step back-translation process was followed to ensure the accuracy and cultural relevance of the Thai instrument: forward translation by a bilingual expert, back-translation by an independent translator, and comparison by a panel of experts to resolve discrepancies.

Step 2: Initiating a developed model of health-driven security

The model was developed through a systematic process integrating both qualitative and theoretical data. Quantitative data were collected using the Older Adult Safety and Security Questionnaires (OA-SQs), which assessed seven key perspecitves of safety and security in older adults with partial dependency and loneliness. These data helped core areas for inclusion in the model.

The theoretical framework consisted of five perspectives – health- diven patterns, goals, implementation, concepts, and health impact – aligned with the seven safety and security OA-SQ dimensions.

Content analysis was conduceted on qualitative data from the literature review, expert consultation, and responses from closed-ended questionnaire. Emergent themes were mapped on to the framework components to ensure alignment with empirical and theoretical insights.

Patterns from the OA-SQs were integrated with conceptual insights to refine the model’s domain and relationships. This ensured that the model was both data-driven and theoretically coherent.

Instrument

The model included the OA-SQs, which comprised 10 sociodemographic items and 30 items on health safety and security across seven dimensions: 1) environmental security, 2) health security, 3) personal security, 4) basic education for living, 5) political security, 6) community security, and 7) economic security.

Quality assessment of instrument

Content validity was assessed by three experts. The OA-SQs showed a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87. The framework was validated by five peer reviewers, with IOC scores ranging from 0.8 to 1.0.

Step 3: Implementing the health-driven safety model

Community participation was emphasized during model implementation in Ban Mae Kong and Ton Tong, Mueang District, Lampang Province.

Week 1: The OA-SQs and health-driven security framework were intruduced to participants. Baseline assessments were conducted using the OA-SQs, and the framework was explained, focusing on its seven key dimensions.

Weeks 2–15: Daily activities of participants were monitored via phone calls and site visits to ensure consistency with health-driven security principles.

Week 16: Follow-up assessments were conducted using the OA-SQs to evaluate changes in health, safety and security level afeter the 16-week intervention.

Participant and settings

Forty older adults with ADL-2 who live alone were divided into two groups of 20 participants each from Ton Tong and Mae Kong subdistricts. The sample size was calculated using G*power with an effect size of 0.50, confidence level of 0.95 and margin of error 0.05.

Instrument

The developed, including the OA-SQs and the health-driven security framework, was implemented in a cohort study between April and September 2023 using a six-month pre-posttest design.

Step 4: evaluating the developed model of health-driven safety

Evaluation involved assessing community collaboration and outcomes using a focus group discusion with stakeholders. Feedback from older adults with ADL-2 who live alone was collected trhough closed-ended questions regarding their satisfaction and opinions after participating in the model. The goal was to assess the model’s effectiveness and gather suggestions or improving security interventions.

Participant and settings

Fifteen stakeholders in Lampang Province, who participated in planning the health-driven security interventions, joined a focus group discussion at Boromarajonani College of Nursing, Nakhon Lampang.

Instrument

Open-ended questions were used to collect participant feedback following implementation of the model.

>Methodological rigor

To strengthen the interview guide, five peer reviewers assessed content validity. The IOC ranged from 0.85 to 0.90.

Data extraction

Step 1: studying the health security situation of older people

For the systematic literature review, the researcher screened databases such as PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, and EBSCO to gather data relevant to the health safety and security of the older population. This included both qualitative and quantitative studies published between 2018 and 2022. For the qualitative approach, participants were interviewed face-to-face for 20 to 30 min (80% of interview lasted 25 min) using closed-ended questions to explore their perspectives on health security in the community settings.

Step 2: developing a health-driven security model

To develop a health-driven security model, data from the OA-SQs tool, based on the UNDP’s human security concept (2017) [1], were collected via survey from January to March 2022. A pilot study was conducted using the same process as the actual survey to train the research team. Eligible participants were recruited and gave informed consent. The health-driven security framework was developed using data collected from the literature review and content analysis to better explain the implementation of the model in the community setting.

Step 3: implementing the health-driven security model

Prior to implementation, a pre-assessment conducted to evaluate participants’ health security levels over six months. Based on the pre-test results, the model was refined and implemented. A post-test was then conducted to assess changes in health safety and security following the intervention.

Step 4: evaluating the health-driven security model

In the final step, each participants was invited to a face-to-face group discussion interview. Group interview lasted 1–2 h, guided by open-ended questions to explore perspectives on the effectiveness of the developed model (See Table 1).

Patient and public involvement

No patients were involved.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of Boromarajonani College of Nursing, Nakhon Lampang, Thailand. The study ensure anonymity, privacy, and confidentiality. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. No therapies, training courses, or invasive procedures were employed. Approval was granted under The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Boromarajonanic College of Nursing, Nakhon Lampang granted approval (approval number E2566-031).

Data analysis

Step 1: studying the health security situation

Data was synthesized using content analysis to extract key concepts and hypotheses. Interviewing data were transcribed, coded, and analyzed. Transcripts were carefully reviewed, and MAXQDA 2020 software was used for data management and analysis.

Step 2: developing the model

Data from the OA-SQs were analyzed using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), followed by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The sample was split into two groups: EFA (n = 188) and CFA (n = 189), and analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 and AMOS version 26 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY USA). MAXQDA Analysis Pro 2020 was used for content analysis. The framework was structured around seven perspectives – environmental, health, personal, educational, political, community, and economic security – and included patterns, goals, borders, implementation steps, concepts, and health impacts.

Step 3: implementing the health-driven security model

Quantitative data analysis included sociodemographic variables such as age, gender, religion, marital status, educational attainment, occupation, income, pre-existing conditions, and residential address. A pre-post test design was used, with paired t-test or Wilcoxon singed-rank test for non-normally distributed data, with statistical significance (p < 0.05).

Step 4: evaluating the health-driven security model

Focus group discussions were transcribed, coded, and analyzed thematically to explore meanings, beliefs, and cultural influences on health-driven safety and security. Each transcript was carefully reviewed to identify meaningful patterns. (see Table 1).

Development of the health-driven security model

The health-driven security model consisted of: 1) the OA-SQs tool and 2) a framework for older adults with ADL-2 who live alone. The OA-SQs comprised 42 items across seven aspects, supported by EFA (Bartlett’s sphericity test ((X2 = 11,551.453, p < 0.001); the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin test [KMO] = 0.89). CFA indicted good model fit: X2/df = 2.01, RMSEA = 0.05, TLI = 0.93, GFI = 0.91, AGFI = 0.90, CFI = 0.94, ACI = 561, and BIC = 898. Cronbach’s alpha > 0.75, CR value > 0.73, and average variance extracted [AVE] > 0.50. The final model’s p-value approximated 0.5, confirming acceptable fit.

Outcomes

Pre-post test in Mae Kong and Ton Tong showed statistically significant improvement in environmental (t = −3.49, p-value = 0.002), health (t = −2.85, p-value = 0.010), and personal security (t = −4.93, p-value = 0.000). No significant improvement were found in knowledge for living, political, community, and economic security (p > 0.05).

In comparison after six months, there were discrepancies in the results obtained from begining. In the one area setting, which is a hybrid of rural and urban areas, statistically significant differences have been observed in the personal, knowledge, political, and community security domains. Specifically, at a p-value of 0.000, the t-tests revealed that personal security (−6.343, p-value = 0.001), knowledge security (−6.332, p-value = 0.001), and community security (−6.212, p-value = 0.001) were statistically significant. Additionally, economic security had a p-value of 0.001, but its significance was not statistically significant compared to the pre-test shown in Table 4.

Discussion

Health security of older adults with ADL-2 who live independently

The demographic composition of the study population primarily consisted of females aged 60–69 years, constituting a majority of participant as shown in Table 2. It is noteworthy that the age range of the majority of older Thai individuals falls within the range of 60–67 years, with females accounting for more than 50% of the population [22]. An individual aged 65 years or above had received primary education and suffered from one to two chronic illnesses, with a reliance on government-issued pensions. There were 792,581 individuals aged 60 and above who received government pensions, and 10,488,013 individuals received old-age allowances [14]. The well-being of older adults is of critical importance to the Thai government, and has a significant impact on their quality of life [23]. The majority of older adults in the northern region of Thailand resided with their children or grandchildren while a minority lived independently [24]. It is also worth noting that the previous study highlighted the prevalence of multi-generational living arrangements among older adults in the region.

A model of the health-driven security

The "Older Adult Safety and Security Questionnaire (OA-SQs)" was utilized to survey older adults with ADL-2 and those who reside independently in the community. The highest level of health and political safety and security was observed among older adults who are partially dependent on activities of daily living (ADL2) and live independently. Ensuring the well-being of these individuals was of utmost importance, as they aim to maintain good health, manage chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension, and participate in social and mental activities. The physical health of these individuals had a significant impact on their mental and social well-being. The literature has noted that older individuals who require care services have diverse health profiles ranging from excellent general health to multiple chronic illnesses that demand substantial resources [25]. The older demographic expressed contentment with their healthcare entitlements, with a particular emphasis on the "Goal Card System," which they perceived as a government-backed health insurance program in Thailand. The continuation of the old-age allowance system is imperative to ensure basic income security for all older individuals, which is essential for their health and well-being [26]. This implies that access to good health, active participation in society, and the preservation of cognitive functions and independence are motivational factors for ensuring health and safety in older individuals. On the other hand, political safety and security are closely linked to issues of law and justice, particularly effective food policy, healthy eating, and the protection of human rights under numerous international and regional human rights agreements [27]. The importance of political stability in relation to nutrition and food consumption [28]. The implementation of measures aimed at fostering a peaceful and harmonious environment within a nation can prevent crime and promote political safety and security [29].

Outcomes and evaluation

The results of the study indicated that personal security in relation to a life of happiness, living without the emotional burden of being evaluated, acknowledgement of other mortal selves, older people's right to independent living, and avoiding any form of bullying, threat, dishonesty, negligence, or offensive behavior was reported as a second-level factor when compared to others (Tables 4, 5). The older demographic has had a significant influence on their health during the ongoing Covid-19 pandemic, as their personal habits, social interactions, and emotional resilience have played a key role in shaping their health outcomes [30]. Additionally, the provision of personal security also contributes to the public protection afforded by organizations or individuals in positions of authority, resulting in enhanced security. The prevention of violations of human rights and freedoms has been recognized as a key factor in improving the quality of life for older individuals [31]. This understanding promotes the availability of opportunities for older individuals to attain good health, engage in societal activities, and maintain long-term well-being.

The environmental, community, and economic factors were determined to be of equal importance. The economic aspect encompassed the need for readily accessible funds to cover living expenses, the desire for employment that aligns with one's age, and satisfaction with the means of earning. The advancement in age is accompanied by transformations in both the physical and mental aspects, as well as the home environment and caregiving, which can either contribute to or obstruct the onset of health issues in older individuals [15]. The arrangement of the living environment for the elderly has a profound impact on their personal well-being, encompassing social cleanliness and stability in life. The influence of this arrangement extends to the family level, where it affects both the environmental and social aspects, including the health status of the older adults and their physical living conditions. These factors collectively contribute to the overall quality of life of the elderly population [32]. Older adults who receive formal and informal visits from caregivers, such as volunteers who visit four times a month, benefit significantly from various relationships that assist in their care. These relationships may involve neighbors and extended family members [33]. Based on the results of the regression analysis, it was found that the good and safety environment had the most significant impact on the quality of life (46.2%), followed by the psychological (43.7%), physical (31%), and social (20.4%) domains [34]. A secure environment is characterized by a structured society with provisions for the safety and security of lives and property, an economically stable population with stable occupations and incomes, and resilient communities with mutual support. The matter of community safety and security is of utmost importance, as it pertains to the overall well-being of society. This includes a comprehensive analysis of social, political, and psychological factors that influence the quality of life of individuals and the tangible and intangible aspects of community cohesion, such as identity, acceptance, participation, and self-governance rights. Recent research has demonstrated that a strong perception of community cohesion can significantly enhance the mental health of older adults and diminish the mediating effect of perceived community safety Individuals' capacities to consider community benefits, maintain physical health, and maintain a healthy emotional state, have a positive impact on economic security [35]. Furthermore, the majority of older individuals prefer to reside in their own homes, as highlighted in 34 studies that examined the community safety and security aspects of "aging in place," which refers to remaining in one's own home and preserving social well-being for as long as possible without having to relocate to long-term care facilities [35].

New perspectives: knowledge for security

Thai older adults with ADL-2 had limited safety knowledge. Food security emerged as a separated factor from the original UNDP 2017. Lifelong learning, eHealth literacy, and self-care education were critical to improving outcomes, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic [36, 37].

Strengths and limitations

This study developed a health-driven security model using older adults based on the UNDP [1] framework. Strengths include theoretical-practical integration, local tool development with validation, real community settings, and stakeholder involvement. Limitations include purposive sampling, small sample size, partial validation, short intervention duration, and uncontrolled confounders. Future studies should address these with larger samples, longer follow-up, and more controls.

Conclusion

The preservation of the quality of life for older individuals with partially diminished activity of daily living (ADL2) through health-oriented safety and security measures is of utmost importance. Preserving the qualitaty of life for older adults with partial dependence in activities of daily living required targeted, health-oreinted security interventions. This model showed significant improvements environment, health, and personal safety. Limitations include purposive sampling, a small size, partial validation of the instrument, a short intervention perios, and lack of control for confunders.
