Linking compression mechanics and pressure redistribution in hybrid support surfaces for pressure ulcer prevention
Authors: Melika Badin Dahesh, Azita Asayesh, Ali Asghar Asgharian Jeddi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pressure ulcers are a major concern for individuals with limited mobility. This study developed six hybrid textile-based support surfaces as low-cost, non-electric alternatives for pressure redistribution. Each surface was constructed by layering cotton pile and spacer fabrics to achieve mechanical gradation and improved body conformity. Compression behavior and interface pressure distribution were systematically analyzed in supine and lateral postures using a proof-of-concept model. To ensure reliability, triplicate measurements were averaged after a 30-second stabilization period. The results demonstrated that higher compression work and elastic modulus were associated with lower peak interface pressures, while greater yield strain increased contact area and enhanced conformity. All investigated hybrid designs showed superior pressure redistribution performance compared to the control surface, effectively mitigating pressure concentration. These findings provide evidence-based design guidelines for affordable, ergonomically optimized support surfaces that can improve safety and quality of life for individuals at risk of pressure ulcers. By linking textile mechanics with clinical performance, this work advances the translation of textile engineering innovations into practical assistive technologies for institutional and home care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51596-7.

Introduction

Pressure ulcers(i.e., bedsores, pressure sores, pressure injuries, decubitus ulcers) remain a global healthcare challenge, particularly in individuals with limited mobility who rely on support surfaces such as beds, mattresses, and wheelchair cushions. They reduce quality of life and increase healthcare costs. Ulcers develop when soft tissues are compressed between bony prominences and surfaces, with risk factors including immobility, malnutrition, aging, pressure, and adverse microclimate conditions1–7. Clinical management strategies include Pressure-relieving support surfaces, wound care, and regular repositioning. Among these, Pressure-relieving support surfaces(e.g., beds, mattresses, cushions) play a critical role by redistributing pressure, increasing contact area, and maintaining an optimal skin microclimate, thereby reducing the risk of ulcer development and helping to prevent ulcer development1,3,5–8.

A variety of support surfaces are now available on the market to prevent pressure ulcers. However, existing options have notable limitations: Hospital foam mattresses, commonly referred to as standard mattresses, often offer minimal pressure relief and retain heat, which can lead to excessive sweating. Low and high air loss beds redistribute pressure, and their airbags let air pass across and control the skin’s microclimate. They are suitable for high-risk people but may be uncomfortable to rest on and need electricity, which can be challenging and costly. Static air mattresses, having chambers for air exchange during compression, are suitable for moderate-risk individuals but can be damaged by sharp objects and lose air over time. Alternating pressure surfaces use air cells to relieve pressure. Their ability to adjust to patient’s weight and position makes them suitable for individuals at medium to high risk of complications. They have mechanical parts and produce periodic pressure, which may bother somebody. These drawbacks highlight the need for low-cost, user-friendly, and effective support surfaces suitable for both institutional and home care settings5–17.

Ren et al.14 demonstrated that multilayer mattresses with increasing stiffness from top to bottom improve comfort and body support. Arumugam and Ranganathan16 used additive manufacturing to create surfaces that match or exceed commercial cushions in pressure reduction. Dénes et al.18 highlighted the importance of surface elasticity in pressure distribution. The structural parameters of support surfaces, particularly spacer fabrics, have been shown to significantly influence compression behavior, thermal resistance, and moisture management19–25. Basal and Ilgaz19 demonstrated that compression characteristics in spacer fabrics depend on yarn type and fabric height, which in turn affect comfort and microclimate regulation. Wu et al.20 reported that lower ground-layer density and larger connecting thread diameters improve pressure redistribution for ulcer prevention. Shim et al.21 found that a multi-foam mattress with a laminated cover outperformed alternating air mattresses in preventing pressure ulcers, while Ghaderi et al.22 showed that a wavy mattress design reduced recovery time in long-term hospitalized patients. Özer et al.23 reported that multilayer knitted substrates exhibit higher air permeability than woven structures, although increased thickness improves compression resilience at the expense of breathability.

Mohammadi et al.24 demonstrated that sleeping mats, used alone or with sleeping bags, significantly reduce body pressure, with gender-dependent pressure distribution patterns. Asayesh and Amini25 investigated the compression properties of weft-knitted spacer fabrics. The results reveal that spacer fabrics having knit stitches on their surface layers are ideal for cushioning applications because they absorb more compression energy and have better resiliency. Amirifard and Asayesh26 investigated the effects of elastic yarn tension and alignment on the tensile and compressive properties of weft-knitted spacer fabrics intended for cushioning applications. Their results showed that using elastic yarns under higher tension, along with a reduced number of miss stitches in the surface layers, enhances the cushioning performance of spacer fabrics. Asayesh and Amini27 analyzed the compression performance of spacer fabrics with respect to surface layer structure. The results indicated that spacer fabrics composed of knit stitches in their surface layers absorb more energy and exhibit better resiliency and relative compressibility than those containing a combination of knit and miss stitches. Consequently, these structures are more suitable for cushioning applications.

Despite these advances, existing support surface designs largely rely on foam- or air-based systems and rarely integrate material mechanics with clinically relevant pressure-redistribution performance. In particular, the relationship between fabric compression behavior and human–support interface pressure under realistic postures remains insufficiently explored, especially for non-electric, low-cost textile-based solutions. To address this gap, this study develops and biomechanically evaluates hybrid textile support surfaces that combine cotton pile and spacer fabrics. By systematically linking material compression characteristics to interface pressure distributions in both supine and lateral postures, this work provides new evidence for the biomechanical feasibility of textile-based support surfaces as practical, low-cost alternatives for pressure ulcer prevention.

Methods

Development and production of hybrid surfaces

The hybrid support surfaces combine a soft cotton pile upper layer with a resilient polyester spacer layer, providing softness, microclimate control, and pressure redistribution at low cost. This user-centered design supports use in wheelchair cushions, hospital beds, and home-care mattresses. Thus, six double-layer support surfaces were produced by layering cotton weft-knitted pile fabric over warp-knitted polyester spacer fabric without binding(Fig. 1.(a)).

Compression test

Compression, recovery, and thickness were measured using an Instron 5566(Fig. 1.(c), (d)). Samples (15 × 15 cm²) were compressed at 12 mm/min to 80% strain following ASTM D575, with five samples per condition. where Pc(N) is the compression force, T0(mm) is initial fabric thickness, Tm(mm) is thickness at maximum pressure25–28. Compression elastic modulus was the slope of the second region of the stress-strain curve of the support surfaces. Yield strain was identified at the end of elastic deformation before plateau onset(Fig. 2). In this study, the KES-F system was therefore used as a complementary technique to ASTM D575 rather than as an alternative method. While ASTM D575 characterizes the overall compressive performance at higher loads, the KES-F-derived compression work provides additional insight into the energy absorption behavior that is particularly relevant for spacer fabric cushioning applications.

Body pressure distribution test

Interface pressure distribution was measured using the PT-Mat-F10L2000 system(Payafanavaran Ferdowsi Co.) placed between the participant and support surface(sample size: 140 × 60 cm²)(Fig. 1.(e), (f)). Tests involved a healthy child participant(Gender: female, Age: 6 years, Weight: 20 kg, Height: 118 cm, BMI: 14.36 kg/m2(13.4 < Normal BMI of 6-years female child29< 17.1)) in supine and lateral positions(Table 3), as these are most relevant to pressure ulcer risk. To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the interface pressure data, a rigorous standardized protocol was followed for each of the six hybrid construction types. Measurements were performed in both supine and lateral positions. Upon positioning the participant on each support surface, a 30-second stabilization period was strictly observed before data recording to allow for initial material compression and postural settling. To minimize potential positioning variability and experimental noise, three separate samples of each hybrid structure were tested. These three repetitions were performed on three distinct samples of the same construction type to account for potential material consistency variations. The interface pressure values reported in this study represent the mean of these measurements. All data collection was completed within a single controlled session to eliminate environmental or temporal variables, ensuring that the observed differences were primarily attributable to the mechanical properties of the hybrid structures.

In this study a preliminary human trial was conducted with a single participant to evaluate the behavior of the developed support surfaces under controlled conditions. This study was designed solely to illustrate feasibility and maintain consistent testing conditions for material performance assessment. It is important to note that this manuscript primarily addresses the material science aspects of the support surfaces rather than clinical outcomes. Consequently, statistical generalization from this single-subject trial is not possible.

The decision to employ static interface pressure testing is grounded in the clinical reality that prolonged, immobile positioning is a fundamental precursor to pressure ulcer formation. In individuals with significantly limited mobility, sustained mechanical loading of soft tissues against a support surface represents the primary physiological challenge. Consequently, static evaluation—a methodology widely adopted in biomechanical research for mattresses and cushions—offers a representative simulation of the critical conditions under which pressure redistribution is most vital. While dynamic factors like repositioning and shear forces are important, our findings provide essential baseline evidence on the foundational ability of these hybrid structures to mitigate sustained pressure. Future investigations will incorporate cyclic loading and shear stress analysis to further enhance clinical generalizability.

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA assessed the effect of surface structure on compression properties(α = 0.05), and two-way ANOVA evaluated the combined effects of structure and posture on pressure distribution outcomes(α = 0.05). Pearson’s correlation quantified relationships between compression properties and pressure distribution indices. While advanced statistical models and optimization algorithms (e.g., machine learning and hybrid metaheuristics) offer powerful predictive capabilities, they were not incorporated into this preliminary study to avoid overcomplicating the interpretation of the initial material-based results. These methods represent a promising direction for our future research as we transition to larger datasets. Drawing inspiration from these robust analytical methodologies, the present study employs systematic statistical tools to evaluate the mechanical performance of hybrid support surfaces.

Relation between body pressure distribution and compression properties

Average pressure(PA)

Statistical results reveal a weak negative preliminary correlation(R=−0.272; p-value = 0.602) between compression work(WC) and average pressure(PA) in the supine position, while a medium negative preliminary correlation(R=−0.552; p-value = 0.256) exists between WC and PA in the lateral position(Table 4). These preliminary correlations were not statistically significant(p-value > 0.05). This lack of significance is likely influenced by sample size and variability within the dataset. Most cases show decreasing PA as WC increases, with exceptions(Fig. 3.(a)). These small number of observations deviated from the observed trend, contributing to reduced statistical power. Despite the absence of statistical significance, the observed correlations suggest potentially meaningful correlations that warrant further investigation in larger and more homogeneous datasets. In the supine position, Spacer+S2, Spacer+S4, and Spacer+S5 disrupt the PA reduction trend with increasing WC. PA reduction relies on adjusting pressure and increasing the body contact area. On the other hands, body support surfaces help relieve contact interface pressure by redistributing body weight and enlarging contact areas. Therefore, because the AC of Spacer+S2 is greater than Spacer+S1, even though the WC of Spacer+S1 is greater than Spacer+S2, the PA of Spacer+S2 is less than Spacer+S1. The asymmetric pile placement of S4 causes stress concentration due to reversed piles at the front and back(Table 1). Consequently, Spacer+S4, despite having more WC than Spacer+S3, experiences higher PA.

WC measures the energy required to compress a support surface. A higher WC indicates that the surface needs more energy to deform under body weight, which reduces contact pressure at all points and lowers the PA.

As the modulus increases, the surface requires more force to deform, which reduces the PA from the weight.

Peak pressure(PP)

The statistical analysis shows a strong negative preliminary correlation exists between compression work(WC) and peak pressure(PP) in the supine(R=−0.758; p-value = 0.081) and lateral(R=−0.646; p-value = 0.165) positions(Table 4). These preliminary correlations were not statistically significant(p-value > 0.05). The possible reasons for the non-significant correlations have been addressed earlier. When examining Fig. 3.(c), most cases illustrate a decrease in PP as the WC increases, although there are some exceptions.

In the supine position, Spacer+S3, Spacer+S4 and Spacer+S5 disrupt the trend of decreasing PP with increasing WC due to the larger unit cell dimensions of S3, reversed placement of the S4 piles, and higher unevenness of S5’s contact surface compared to S3 as described. In the lateral position, Spacer+S3 and Spacer+S6 disrupt the trend of decreasing PP with increasing WC due to the structural reasons described.

A higher WC means that the support surface requires more energy to deform under body weight, which reduces PP. The increased body penetration into the surface in the lateral position enhances the negative correlation between WC and pressure compared to the supine position. The PP occurs at bony prominences, so the penetration depth when lying on the back versus the side does not significantly affect it. So, there is a strong correlation between the PP and WC in both positions.

The statistical analysis shows a very strong negative preliminary correlation between compression elastic modulus and peak pressure(PP) in the supine(R=−0.861; p-value = 0.027) and lateral(R=−0.866; p-value = 0.026) positions(Table 4). These preliminary correlations were statistically significant(p-value < 0.05). When examining Fig. 3.(d) and (d), most cases illustrate a decrease in PP as the compression elastic modulus increases, although there are some exceptions. In the lateral position, Spacer+S3 and Spacer+S6 disrupt the decrease in PP with increasing compression elastic modulus due to the lower pile density coefficient(λ) of S6 compared to S5, which can lead to higher PP of the Spacer+S6 and larger AC of S2 than S3 leads to lower PP of the Spacer+S2.

Total contact surface(AC)

Statistical results reveal a very strong positive preliminary correlation(R = 0.812; p-value = 0.050) between yield strain and total contact surface(AC) in the supine position. A medium positive preliminary correlation (R = 0.540; p-value = 0.269) exists between yield strain and AC in the lateral position(Table 4). These preliminary correlations were statistically significant(p-value < 0.05). The statistical results show that with increasing yield strain, the AC increases.

Figure 4. (a) reveals that the change trend of AC in all postures has a perfect similarity with the yield strain. A higher yield strain indicates better deformability of the support surface, which enhances body conformity and results in a higher AC. Only Spacer+S6 disrupts the trend of increasing AC by boosting yield strain. The S5’s higher pile density coefficient(λ) results in greater contact friction, limiting Spacer+S5’s deformability(Table 1). Thus, despite Spacer+S5 having a higher yield strain, its AC is less than or equal to that of Spacer+S6.

Statistical results show a medium negative preliminary correlation(R=−0.506; p-value = 0.306) between compression elastic modulus and AC in the supine position, while a medium negative preliminary correlation(R=−0.552; p-value = 0.234) exists between compression elastic modulus and AC in the lateral position(Table 4). These preliminary correlations were not statistically significant(p-value > 0.05). The possible reasons for the non-significant correlations have been addressed earlier. The decreasing of compression elastic modulus leades to increase AC, although there are some exceptions(Fig. 4.(b)). Reducing the elastic modulus allows the support surface to deform more easily under body weight, enhancing conformity to the body’s shape and increasing contact area.

In the supine position, Spacer+S4’s compression elastic modulus is lower than that of Spacer+S2, but its AC is lower than that of Spacer+S2. S4 has a higher pile density coefficient(λ) than S2(Table 1). So, Spacer+S4 experiences greater contact friction than Spacer+S2, which increases the fabric’s resistance to deformation and decreases its conformity to body shape. Thus, the AC of Spacer+S4 is reduced compared to Spacer+S2. In the lateral position, Spacer+S5 and Spacer+S6 disrupt the trend of decreasing PP with increasing compression elastic modulus. The compression elastic modulus of Spacer+S5 is lower than that of Spacer+S2 and Spacer+S6, but its AC is lower than that of Spacer+S2 and Spacer+S6. Its higher pile density coefficient (λ) than S2 and S6(Table 1) leads to a decrease in AC of Spacer+S5 compared to Spacer+S2 and Spacer+S6. The compression elastic modulus of Spacer+S6 is approximately 0.283% higher than Spacer+S2, and its AC is about 2.81% higher. The S6 is a single-sided pile fabric, so it has lower friction than S2 with a Spacer layer in the support surface(Table 1). This results in Spacer+S2 having lower conformity to body shape and a lower AC than Spacer+S6.

It should be noted that while flat-plate compression testing is a foundational standard for characterizing material properties such as yield strain, the mechanical behavior under these conditions differs from multi-axial loading against curved anatomical structures. Although yield strain serves as a valuable proxy for initial material deformation, it may not fully reflect the complex conformity required for the human torso. Consequently, these findings represent indicators of material performance, with further investigation into multi-axial loading being a subject for future research.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge that as a proof-of-concept and exploratory study, the present research utilized a focused sample size, which inherently limits statistical power. Consequently, some observed interactions between material properties and pressure distribution did not reach the conventional threshold of statistical significance(p < 0.05). However, within the framework of pilot biomechanical investigations, these non-significant numerical trends remain highly valuable as they provide a foundational baseline for hypothesis generation in future research. Rather than presenting these patterns as definitive statistical phenomena, they are reported here as preliminary structural indicators that warrant further validation in larger, multi-center cohorts. By documenting these initial trends, this study establishes a necessary physical and mechanical framework for the future redesign and optimization of advanced hybrid support surfaces in clinical applications.

Compression properties

As shown in Fig. 3.(a), the Spacer+S1 has the highest and the Spacer+S3 has the lowest WC. The statistical results confirm that the structure of support surfaces has no significant influence on the WC(p = 0.610 > 0.05). If one compares the WC of these surfaces with their constituents, it becomes clear that the spacer more strongly influences it. Thus, since the pile fabric plays a lesser role in the WC, statistically, there is no significant difference between the WC of the support surfaces despite their structural differences. The Spacer+S1 has the highest compression elastic modulus, while Spacer+S4 has the lowest. Statistical analysis indicates that the structure of the support surfaces significantly affects compression elastic modulus (p = 0.000 < 0.05). The results indicate that increasing the pile density coefficient(λ) in the pile fabric unit cell leads to a higher compression elastic modulus of the support surface made up of it(R2 = 0.8524)(Table 1; Fig. 4.(c)). The Spacer+S2 has the highest yield strain, while Spacer+S3 has the lowest(Fig. 4.(a)). Statistical analysis indicates that the structure of the support surfaces significantly affects yield strain(p = 0.018 < 0.05).

Pressure distribution characteristics

The average pressure(PA) was lower for all support surfaces than for the reference condition(no support surface) in every position(Fig. 3.(a) and Table 5). In the supine position, Spacer+S4 produced the highest PA and Spacer+S6 the lowest. Because pressure is a key factor in pressure-ulcer formation, all surfaces effectively reduced PA. As participant weight was constant, larger contact areas resulted in lower PA, consistent with AC trends across all positions(Figs. 3.(a) and 4.(a). All support surfaces also showed lower peak pressure(PP) than the reference state in the supine position (Fig. 3.(c); Table 5). Spacer+S4 had the highest PP and Spacer+S1 the lowest. In the lateral position, only Spacer+S1 reduced PP relative to the reference. Spacer+S6 showed the highest PP laterally, while Spacer+S1 remained the lowest across all positions, indicating superior PP performance. Surfaces with smaller or uneven pile-covered regions tended to concentrate stress and increase PP, whereas smoother surfaces reduced PP.

The total contact area(AC) of all support surfaces exceeded that of the reference condition in all positions(Fig. 4.(a); Table 5). A larger contact area distributes body weight more effectively, lowering pressure-ulcer risk. In the supine position, Spacer+S2 yielded the greatest AC, whereas the Spacer+S1 showed the smallest. In the lateral position, Spacer+S6 had the highest AC and the Spacer+S3 the lowest. AC was generally greater in supine than lateral, likely because lateral positioning increases body curvature and decreases the effective contact area.

In the supine position, all surfaces reduced the area prone to developing pressure ulcers(AD) compared with the reference state(Fig. 4.(d); Table 5). In the lateral position, all surfaces except Spacer+S2 and Spacer+S4 lowered AD. Spacer+S4 showed the highest AD in supine and Spacer+S6 the lowest; laterally, Spacer+S2 produced the highest AD and Spacer+S5 the lowest. Surfaces that reduced PA and increased AC generally showed lower AD. Compared with the reference condition, support surfaces reduced high-risk areas by up to 44.6%(supine) and 20.12%(lateral). Statistical analysis indicated that surface structure significantly affected AC(p = 0.000 < 0.05), whereas posture significantly affected all pressure indices except PP(p = 0.000 < 0.05). A structure–posture interaction was found only for AC(p = 0.000 < 0.05), underscoring the need to integrate material design with posture-specific ergonomics. From an assistive technology perspective, these hybrid surfaces demonstrate potential for:

Wheelchair cushions:

reducing ischial pressure in long-term seated users.

Therapeutic mattresses:

providing affordable, non-electric options for long-term care.

Low-resource settings:

offering lightweight, portable, and cost-effective pressure-relief solutions.

Limitations and future work

This proof-of-concept study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, the use of a single healthy participant as a representative model for pediatric-scale loading limits the generalizability of the findings to broader adult populations or diverse clinical conditions. While this controlled setup enabled a clear assessment of mechanical–pressure relationships, future research involving larger, more heterogeneous cohorts—including individuals with limited mobility—is essential for clinical validation. Second, measurements were restricted to supine and lateral positions; incorporating prone and seated postures would broaden the applicability of these hybrid surfaces. Additionally, the spatial resolution of the pressure sensor pad (3.5 cm²/cell) may not capture fine-grained pressure gradients over smaller anatomical regions. Future investigations should utilize high-density sensor arrays and include physiological measurements, such as tissue perfusion and oxygenation, to provide a more comprehensive clinical evaluation. A limitation of this study is the small sample size used for correlation analysis. While sufficient for identifying preliminary trends in biomechanical testing, the statistical power is limited for broad generalizations. Therefore, these correlations should be interpreted as early indicators, requiring further validation with larger datasets in future research. Subsequent work will also focus on refining fabric combinations based on long-term monitoring and user feedback in real-world hospital and home-care settings.

Conclusions

Pressure ulcers remain a major global challenge affecting health, independence, and healthcare resources. This study developed and biomechanically evaluated hybrid textile support surfaces that combine cotton pile and spacer fabrics as non-electric, low-cost alternatives for pressure redistribution. Higher work of compression and elastic modulus were associated with lower average and peak interface pressures, while greater yield strain increased total contact area and body conformity. Hybrid surfaces reduced high-risk areas by up to 44.6% in the supine and 20.1% in the lateral posture, confirming their mechanical pressure redistribution performance for pressure ulcer prevention.

By linking material mechanics with clinical performance, this study provides quantitative evidence for designing affordable, portable, and ergonomically optimized support systems suitable for hospitals, rehabilitation centers, and home care. Future work will extend validation to diverse user populations and clinical environments, advancing the translation of textile engineering innovations into practical assistive technologies that enhance comfort, safety, and quality of life.
