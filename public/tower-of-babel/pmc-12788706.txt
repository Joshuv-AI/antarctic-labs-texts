Correlation between anterior teeth retraction and pharyngeal airway dimension changes in bimaxillary protrusion cases
Authors: Amelia Priscilla Sugiarta, Haru Setyo Anggani, Maria Purbiati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVE:This study aims to demonstrate the correlation between anterior tooth retraction and changes in the dimensions of the pharyngeal airway in cases of bimaxillary protrusion.MATERIALS AND METHODS:Changes in dental parameters and the width of the upper and lower pharyngeal airways from 46 lateral cephalometric radiographs that met the inclusion criteria were measured. Changes were tested for significance using the paired t-test, and correlations were tested using the Pearson correlation test.RESULTS:Significant changes were observed in dental parameters and in the width of the upper pharyngeal airway following orthodontic treatment (P < 0.05). However, no significant change was found in the width of the lower pharyngeal airway (P = 0.166). There was no significant correlation between changes in the upper incisor parameters and upper pharyngeal airway width (P = 0.130 and P = 0.738), nor between changes in the lower incisor parameters and lower pharyngeal airway width (P = 0.911 and P = 0.614).CONCLUSION:Anterior tooth retraction does not significantly correlate with changes in the width of the pharyngeal airway. Therefore, premolar extraction for anterior tooth retraction can be considered a safe orthodontic approach in cases of bimaxillary protrusion.

Introduction

The relationship between dentocraniofacial morphology and airway dimensions has long been of interest since the 19th century due to their anatomical proximity.[1] The upper airway plays a crucial role in the growth and development of the dentocraniofacial complex, as well as in respiration, swallowing, and speech functions. The pharynx is an organ connecting the posterior parts of the nasal and oral cavities with the larynx and esophagus and is part of the upper respiratory tract. Several factors influencing upper airway dimensions have been outlined in several studies, including age, skeletal maturation, and craniofacial morphology.[234] Analysis of the upper airway can be performed through various imaging techniques, including lateral cephalometry, fluoroscopy, fiber-optic pharyngoscopy, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging, which aid in diagnosis and orthodontic treatment planning.[56]

Deviations in craniofacial growth can result in malocclusion, which may be treated through growth modification, camouflage orthodontics, or orthognathic surgery, depending on severity and skeletal pattern.[78] Surgical interventions, such as mandibular setback surgery, have been associated with reductions in upper airway dimensions, with previous systematic review confirming a significant narrowing of oropharyngeal space postoperatively[9] A reduction in facial skeletal dimensions following a combination of orthodontic and orthognathic surgery is a contributing factor to airway obstruction, as orthognathic surgery alters the hard tissue anatomy of the facial area and surrounding structures to correct craniofacial deformities. The upper airway, being in close proximity to the teeth and jaws, is likely to have its dimensions altered after orthognathic surgery.[1011] Zhang et al. (2023)[12] reported that mandibular setbacks during orthognathic surgery can significantly reduce the volume of the upper airway. However, the extent of this reduction may vary depending on whether the surgery involves one or both jaws.[13] In contrast, the influence of nonsurgical orthodontic interventions on airway dimensions is still debated.

Bimaxillary protrusion is a common dentofacial condition, particularly in Class I malocclusion. This condition often presents several aesthetic issues, such as a convex facial profile, incompetent lips, excessive gingival display, and protrusive upper and lower incisors, along with protrusive lips. The negative perception of this condition and its impact on quality of life often leads patients to seek orthodontic treatment. A typical approach for managing bimaxillary protrusion is the extraction of all four first premolars, followed by anterior tooth retraction, aimed at creating sufficient space to improve the aesthetic appearance of the dentocraniofacial complex.[14]

Lateral cephalometry is a commonly used method for assessing the upper airway due to its simplicity, accessibility, low cost, and minimal radiation exposure. Lateral cephalometry can be performed using manual or digital methods. Digital methods involve uploading cephalometric images to software. WinCeph is orthodontic analysis software developed in 1995 by Tohoku University in Japan. This software is widely used in Japan and around the world. Currently, WinCeph is at version 11 (WinCeph Ver. 11) and is compatible with Windows 10.[1516] Measurement of the pharyngeal airway via lateral cephalometric analysis can be conducted based on McNamara’s theory. The width of the nasopharynx is measured linearly as the shortest distance from the posterior wall of the soft palate to the posterior wall of the pharynx. The width of the oropharynx is evaluated radiographically at the point where the mandibular plane intersects the anterior and posterior walls of the pharynx.[1718]

While the effects of orthognathic surgery on airway morphology are well-documented, the impact of nonsurgical orthodontic interventions—specifically anterior tooth retraction following premolar extraction—remains underexplored. Most existing studies have focused on skeletal Class II or III cases, or on volumetric airway analysis using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT). This study addresses a critical research gap by evaluating whether anterior tooth retraction alone alters pharyngeal airway dimensions in Class I bimaxillary protrusion patients. Understanding this relationship is essential for ensuring functional safety during aesthetic orthodontic treatment planning. Previous studies have demonstrated that orthodontic treatment involving extractions in cases of bimaxillary protrusion results in statistically significant changes in the relationship between the maxilla and mandible relative to the cranial base.[1920] However, the correlation between anterior tooth retraction and changes in pharyngeal airway dimensions remains inconclusive. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate changes in the pharyngeal airway following anterior tooth retraction in bimaxillary protrusion cases and to determine whether a correlation exists between incisor retraction and airway dimension changes.

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry. The study was a retrospective, observational, and descriptive–analytic study with a cross-sectional design at the Dental Hospital and was conducted from February to April 2024. The sample for this study comprises 46 lateral cephalograms from 23 orthodontic patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cephalograms from orthodontic patients with skeletal and dental Class I malocclusion angle 3° ±2°) accompanied by bimaxillary protrusion (protrusive maxillary incisors relative to the maxillary plane [upper incisors relative to the maxillary plane (UI-PP) >115°] and protrusive mandibular incisors relative to the mandibular plane [lower incisors relative to the mandibular plane (LI-MP) >94°]) with a vertical skeletal pattern (Sella Nasion-Mandibular plane (SN-MP) 32° ± 3°) normodivergent. Mild crowding (<4 mm).[78] The patients should have undergone fixed orthodontic treatment, including the extraction of four permanent first premolars and the retraction of the upper and lower anterior teeth. (2) Cephalograms taken before and after fixed orthodontic treatment from orthodontic patients at the Dental Hospital. T he cephalograms must be of good quality and suitable for analysis. (3) Cephalograms of male and female patients. (4) Cephalograms from patients above 20 years old at the start of fixed orthodontic treatment. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Cephalograms of patients with syndromes or dentocraniofacial anomalies. (2) Cephalograms of patients with a history of orthognathic surgery. (3) Cephalograms of patients with airway obstruction. (4) Cephalograms of patients with a history of tooth extractions other than first premolars and third molars. All samples were scanned using a Microtek Scanner TMA 1600 to obtain digital lateral cephalometric data. The digital cephalograms were then measured using WinCeph Ver. 11 software for analysis as seen on Figure 1. The samples were calibrated according to the software’s instructions, and reference points and angular measurements were established for lateral cephalometric analysis.

Lateral cephalometric analysis

The digital lateral cephalograms, once uploaded to WinCeph Ver. 11 software, underwent measurement for the skeletal pattern (ANB) and vertical skeletal pattern (SN-MP) to confirm that the study subjects had Class I malocclusion with a normodivergent vertical skeletal pattern. Measurements included the inclination of the UI-PP, the inclination of the LI-MP, the position of the upper incisors relative to the profile (UI-APg), and the position of the lower incisors relative to the profile (LI-APg). For these measurements, reference points such as Anterior Nasal Spine, Posterior Nasal Spine, A, Pog, Go, and Me, as well as the lines for UIs along the tooth axis and LIs along the tooth axis, were determined. Measurements of the upper pharyngeal airway width (UPAW) and lower pharyngeal airway width (LPAW) were conducted according to McNamara’s analysis.[18] Two observers, including an expert, conducted measurements on four cephalometric samples within a single day, and the researcher performed repeated measurements after 1 week. Interobserver and intraobserver reliability tests were subsequently conducted to assess the consistency of point, line, and angle determination in the measurements.

Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 27. Reliability analysis was conducted using one sample t-test and a Bland–Altman plot to assess intraobserver and interobserver reliability. Normality testing of the data was carried out with the Shapiro–Wilk test to evaluate the frequency distribution and data spread for each test group. A paired t-test was used to analyze the changes in the width of the Upper Pharyngeal Airway and Lower Pharyngeal Airway and the length of the pharyngeal airway, which are described in Table 1. A Pearson correlation test was then performed to determine the correlation between anterior tooth retraction and changes in the pharyngeal airway dimensions in cases of bimaxillary protrusion.

Results

The sample consisted of 46 lateral cephalometric radiographs from 23 orthodontic patients at the Dental Hospital of the Faculty of Dentistry. The subjects included 4 males (17%) and 19 females (83%), with ages ranging from 20 to 33 years. The orthodontic treatment involved fixed appliances for Class I bimaxillary dental protrusion malocclusion (mean ANB 2.44° ±1.13°) with a normodivergent vertical skeletal pattern (mean SN-MP 32.58° ±2.24°) and included the extraction of four first permanent premolars followed by the retraction of the anterior teeth. Four samples were randomly selected for intra-observer reliability testing by the primary researcher and inter-observer reliability testing by an orthodontist deemed an expert in using WinCeph Ver. 11. Intra- and interobserver reliability tests were conducted using one sample t-test and a Bland–Altman plot. The one-sample t-test for interobserver reliability yielded a P-value of 0.251, indicating that the measurements between the expert and the researcher were not significantly different. The mean bias was 0.04, with a lower limit of -0.1217 and an upper limit of 0.325. Figure 2 shows the Bland–Altman plot, where most of the measurement points between the expert and the researcher are close to the mean line (green line) and within the upper and lower limits of agreement (red lines), indicating good agreement between interobserver measurements.

The one-sample t-test for intraobserver reliability yielded a P-value of 0.737. This result indicates that the measurements taken by the researcher at two different times were not significantly different. The two measurements, taken two weeks apart, demonstrated good consistency. The mean bias was 0.0583, with an upper limit of 0.1365 and a lower limit of -0.972, as illustrated in the Bland–Altman plot shown in Figure 3.

As all variables were normally distributed, Pearson’s correlation was deemed appropriate for this analysis. Although not statistically significant (P > 0.05), this suggests that greater lingual inclination of the UIs may be associated with a modest increase in the width of the upper airway. Similarly, a weak positive correlation was found, suggesting that as the position of the UIs relative to the profile changes, there is a slight increase in the UPAW (r = 0.074). Although none of the correlations were statistically significant, these findings highlight subtle trends that may warrant further investigation in larger sample populations. The absence of strong correlations in this study supports the conclusion that anterior tooth retraction does not significantly impact pharyngeal airway dimensions in adult bimaxillary protrusion cases.

Discussion

Pharyngeal airway dimensions can be influenced by several factors, including age, skeletal maturity, and dentocraniofacial morphology. Gu et al. (2016)[21] conducted a study using CBCT to examine pharyngeal airway dimensions during growth and found that nasopharyngeal volume continuously increased from ages 6 to 18 years. The rate of increase peaked at ages 6–9 and 12–15, then slowed until age 19.[22] This study utilized cephalometric radiographs from subjects over 20 years old (average age 24.57 ± 3.66 years) to minimize confounding variables in measurement outcomes. Blancas et al. (2017)[17] investigated pharyngeal airway dimensions in Class I malocclusions with different vertical skeletal patterns and found that nasopharyngeal width was greater in individuals with hypodivergent growth patterns compared to those with normodivergent and hyperdivergent patterns. In this study, subjects with normodivergent vertical skeletal patterns (average SN-MP 32.58° ± 2.24°) were selected to ensure more homogeneous data.

This retrospective study aimed to evaluate changes in pharyngeal airway dimensions following the extraction of four premolars and anterior tooth retraction in cases of bimaxillary protrusion. Premolar extraction is often necessary in orthodontic treatment to correct dentocraniofacial deformities. Miles et al. (2018)[23] found that 56–66% of the space created by the extraction of first permanent premolars is used by the anterior segment after anterior tooth retraction, with the remaining space occupied by the posterior segment moving mesially. This study’s findings align with the results of Miles et al. (2018),[23] Aldosari (2019),[24] and Joy et al. (2020),[25] showing significant changes in dentoalveolar parameters, including incisor inclination and position, after extracting the first four permanent premolars and retracting anterior teeth. The significant changes are also supported by the inclusion criteria of subjects with mild crowding, allowing the space from premolar extraction to be maximally utilized for anterior tooth retraction.

Significant changes were observed in the UPAW but not in the LPAW. This is consistent with the findings of Bhatia et al. (2016), who studied the effects of anterior tooth retraction on the pharyngeal airway. Bhatia et al.[26] reported that the LPA is less prone to deformation due to its support from bone and cartilage, whereas the UPA, surrounded by soft tissues like the soft palate, is more affected. Anterior tooth retraction reduces oral cavity size, shifting the tongue posteriorly and compressing the soft palate, leading to a reduction in UPA size. The significant changes in UPAW found in this study contradict the results reported by Pervez et al. (2021)[14] and Stefanovic (2013),[27] who included subjects with different vertical skeletal patterns and used different dentoalveolar parameters. The lack of significant changes in the LPAW contrasts with Nagmode et al. (2017), who included subjects undergoing orthodontic treatment with maximum anchorage. The nonsignificant changes in LPAW after anterior tooth retraction could also be due to varying anchorage mechanics in different subjects, potentially leading to loss of anchorage during anterior tooth retraction.[2829]

This study found no significant correlation between anterior tooth retraction and changes in both UPAW and LPAW, with correlation strengths ranging from weak to moderate. This is in line with the systematic review and meta-analysis by Orabi et al. (2021), which concluded that there is no strong evidence supporting the notion that premolar extraction and anterior tooth retraction in bimaxillary protrusion cases reduce pharyngeal airway dimensions. Changes in pharyngeal airway width can be influenced by various factors, including breathing mode, tongue position during lateral cephalometric radiography, inflammation in surrounding airway structures, and changes in body mass index during orthodontic treatment.[253031]

Pharyngeal airway width measurements in this study were conducted using two-dimensional lateral cephalometric analysis with WinCeph Ver. 11. Although this method is considered reliable and reproducible, it cannot thoroughly investigate the dynamic function or volume of the pharyngeal airway, which is a three-dimensional structure.[28] Narrowing of the pharyngeal airway does not necessarily indicate an increased risk of airway obstruction or obstructive sleep apnea. Zhang et al. (2015) reported that the airway has self-regulating properties; when narrowing occurs in the anteroposterior direction, the airway expands laterally to ensure sufficient space for breathing. The pharyngeal airway adapts to changes in surrounding structural morphology, so its volume, height, and cross-sectional area do not change significantly.[3233]

The limitations of this study include variability in how the subjects positioned themselves during lateral cephalometric radiography. Standardizing instructions for tongue position and breathing mode during radiography could provide more homogeneous sample data. Additionally, body mass index and inflammation in subjects should be recorded to minimize potential bias in measurement variables. The anchor mechanics used in orthodontic treatment should also be identified and standardized to obtain more consistent data. The presence of airway obstruction in subjects could be excluded by examining the adenoid and tonsil morphology on lateral cephalometric radiographs. Furthermore, the use of two-dimensional cephalometric analysis for studying a three-dimensional structure, such as the upper airway, is a limitation. Three-dimensional imaging techniques, such as CBCT, provide more detailed measurements of the pharyngeal airway. A larger sample size is also needed to ensure a clearer understanding of the correlation between anterior tooth retraction and changes in pharyngeal airway dimensions.

Conclusion

Anterior tooth retraction in the treatment of Class I bimaxillary protrusion with a normodivergent vertical skeletal pattern, involving the extraction of all first permanent premolars, results in significant changes in dentoalveolar parameters, including UI-PP, UI-APg, LI-MP, and LI-APg. Additionally, a significant change is observed in the UPAW. However, the LPAW does not exhibit significant changes after orthodontic treatment. Pearson correlation tests in this study indicate that anterior tooth retraction, represented by changes in dentoalveolar parameters, does not show a significant correlation with changes in either the UPAW or the LPAW. These findings reinforce the evidence that the extraction of the first four permanent premolars and anterior teeth retraction for correcting bimaxillary dental protrusion with fixed orthodontic appliances does not have a significant correlation with changes in the UPAW and LPAW.

Criteria for inclusion

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cephalograms from orthodontic patients with skeletal and dental Class I malocclusion (ANB 3° ±2°) accompanied by bimaxillary protrusion (protrusive maxillary incisors relative to the maxillary plane [UI-PP > 115°] and protrusive mandibular incisors relative to the mandibular plane [LI-MP > 94°]) with a vertical skeletal pattern (SN-MP 32° ±3°) normodivergent. Mild crowding (<4 mm). The patients should have undergone fixed orthodontic treatment, including the extraction of four permanent first premolars and the retraction of the upper and lower anterior teeth. (2) Cephalograms taken before and after fixed orthodontic treatment from orthodontic patients at the Dental Hospital, Universitas Indonesia. The cephalograms must be of good quality and suitable for analysis. (3) Cephalograms of male and female patients. (4) Cephalograms from patients above 20 years old at the start of fixed orthodontic treatment.
