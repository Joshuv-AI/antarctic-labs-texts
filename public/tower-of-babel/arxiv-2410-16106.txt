Statistical Inference for Policy Evaluation with Temporal Difference Learning
Weichen Wu, Gen Li, Yuting Wei, Alessandro Rinaldo

Abstract
We investigate the statistical properties of Temporal Difference (TD) learning with Polyak-Ruppert averaging, arguably one of the most widely used algorithms in reinforcement learning, for the task of estimating the parameters of the optimal linear approximation to the value function. Assuming independent samples, we make three theoretical contributions that improve upon the current state-of-the-art results: (i) we establish refined high-dimensional Berry-Esseen bounds over the class of convex sets, achieving faster rates than the best known results, and (ii) we propose and analyze a novel, computationally efficient online plug-in estimator of the asymptotic covariance matrix; (iii) we derive sharper high probability convergence guarantees that depend explicitly on the asymptotic variance and hold under weaker conditions than those adopted in the literature. These results enable the construction of confidence regions and simultaneous confidence intervals for the linear parameters of the value function approximation, with guaranteed finite-sample coverage. We demonstrate the applicability of our theoretical findings through numerical experiments.

Introduction

The feasibility and accuracy of statistical inference tasks, such as hypothesis testing or constructing confidence regions or simultaneous confidence intervals, are traditionally predicated on using estimators that are not only consistent but also satisfy some type of distributional approximation. In most cases, this is accomplished via Central Limit Theorems (CLTs), which ensure that the procedures in use admit a Gaussian approximation. Due to the high dimensionality of modern scientific and engineering applications, there has been a surge of interest in establishing new
high-dimensional Berry-Esseen bounds,
CLTs that hold in high dimensions and have a finite-sample guarantee. While efforts in this area have focused on Berry-Esseen bounds for normalized sums, few results are available to handle more complicated scenarios, where estimators are computed in a nonlinear, iterative manner. In this paper, we investigate one such scenario and study in detail the consistency, Gaussian approximations, and statistical inference for the output of the Temporal Difference (TD) learning algorithm for policy evaluation with linear function approximation in Reinforcement Learning (RL).

A critical task in RL is to evaluate with confidence the quality of a given policy, measured by its value function.
As RL models are based on Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), statistical inference must accommodate the online nature of the sampling mechanism.
TD learning
(
Sutton, 1988
, see, e.g.,)
, an iterative procedure that can be framed as an instance of stochastic approximation (SA), is arguably the most widely used and effective algorithm for policy evaluation in RL. The last several years have witnessed a flurry of activities within the statistical machine learning community to derive finite sample consistency rates of TD iterates, leading to deep results and a much improved understanding of the performance and limitations of TD learning. See, e.g.,
Khamaru et al. (2020)
;
Li et al. (2024a)
;
Bhandari et al. (2021)
;
Dalal et al. (2018)
;
Srikant and Ying (2019)
;
Lakshminarayanan and Szepesvari (2018)
;
Mou et al. (2020)
;
Durmus et al. (2022)
;
Li et al. (2024b)
;
Samsonov et al. (2024b)
;
Prashanth et al. (2013)
;
Borkar et al. (2025)
for a partial and growing list of relevant references.
Though for the tabular case (finite state and action spaces) the TD algorithm is known to be minimax rate optimal
(
Li et al., 2024a
)
, its properties – and, in particular, its sample complexity – in more complex and high-dimensional settings have not been fully understood.

In a more specialized line of work, concerned with establishing distributional approximations of the TD iterates, it has been shown that, using Polyak-Ruppert averaging
(
Polyak and Juditsky, 1992
;
Ruppert, 1998
)
, the TD estimation error converges to a Gaussian distribution in the large sample limit; see, e.g.,
Fort (2015)
;
Mou et al. (2020)
;
Li et al. (2023)
. While these results provide a formal justification for the validity of statistical inference, the guarantees from those contributions are non-quantitative, in the sense that they are of asymptotic nature and hold only in fixed dimensions. As a result, they do not adequately capture the dependence on the sample size and other problem-related quantities, most notably the dimensionality, all of which affect the accuracy of statistical inference.
In this paper, we partially address this gap. We focus on TD learning with Polyak-Ruppert averaging for estimating the coefficients of the best linear approximation of the value function, assuming i.i.d. samples. We make the following theoretical and methodological contributions.

1.

Sharper Berry-Esseen bounds.
We derive a new high-dimensional Berry-Esseen bound for the estimation error over the class of convex sets and using an estimated variance, achieving an
O(T^{-\frac{1}{3}})
rate as a function of the number of iterations, which appears to be the fastest in the literature. While completing this work, we became aware of the recent related paper by
Samsonov et al. (2024a)
, which, under the same settings, derived a high-dimensional Berry-Esseen bound for linear SA with Polyak-Ruppert averaging, obtaining the slower rate
O(T^{-\frac{1}{4}})
. See Section
3.1
for a discussion and detailed comparisons.

2.

Improved high-probability guarantees.
We show that, for an appropriate choice of the initial stepsize, the Euclidean norm of the estimation error for the TD learning algorithm is of order

\displaystyle O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{\Lambda}^{\star})}{T}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right),

with probability at least
1-\delta
, where
T
denotes the number of iterations,
\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}
represents the asymptotic variance matrix of the estimation error, and
\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{\Lambda}^{\star})
its trace. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first high-probability convergence guarantee for this problem that matches the asymptotic variance, up to logarithm factors, without requiring a projection step to control the iterative trajectory. Furthermore, our assumptions are weaker than those used in the literature. Table
1
compares our high-probability convergence guarantees with the latest available results.

3.

Novel, computationally efficient variance estimator.
We develop a computationally efficient online algorithm to construct an estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}
of the asymptotic variance
\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}
. We also show that the total variation distance between the (computable) distribution
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T})
and the asymptotic distribution
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}^{\star})
vanishes with high probability at a rate
O(T^{-\frac{1}{3}})
, matching the order of our Berry-Esseen rate. As a result, estimating the variance, a crucial step for constructing confidence sets, does not lead to worse rates.

4.

Statistical inference.
Based on the Berry-Esseen bound and the variance estimator, we design an online, efficient algorithm to construct confidence regions and simultaneous confidence intervals for the linear parameters of the value function approximation. We prove that the finite-sample coverage rate of proposed confidence region converges to the target level at a rate of
O(T^{-\frac{1}{3}})
.

Background

Markov decision process.

An infinite-horizon discounted Markov Decision Process (MDP)
(
Bertsekas, 2017
;
Sutton and Barto, 2018
)
is the tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,\gamma)
where
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
represent the
state space
of the environment and the
action space
available to an agent, respectively, both assumed for simplicity to be finite (though our results hold for countable spaces). The function
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\mapsto\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is a transition kernel that associates to any state-action pair
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
the probability distribution
P(\cdot\mid s,a)
of the next state of the environment when the agent executes action
a
while the environment is in state
s
. The
reward function

r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\mapsto[0,1]
measures the immediate reward to the agent after taking action
a
at state
s
; finally,
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the
discount factor
, used to assess the impact of future rewards.

In choosing their actions, the agent follows a randomized action selection rule, or
policy
, represented as a (time-invariant) conditional probability distribution
\pi:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\Delta(\mathcal{A})
over the action space given the state of the environment. For any policy
\pi
, its associated reward function
r:\mathcal{S}\mapsto[0,1]
is the expected reward of the next action selected according to the policy
\pi
, i.e.

r(s):=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}[r(s,a)],
for
s\in\mathcal{S}.

Any policy
\pi
induces a transition kernel
P
, representing the probability distribution of the next state given the current state
s
and the policy
\pi
, given by

P(s^{\prime}\mid s)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
for
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}.

We assume throughout that there exists a unique stationary distribution
\mu
for the transition matrix
{P}
; that is,
\mu(i)=\mathbb{P}_{s\sim\mu,s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)}(s^{\prime}=i)
,
i\in\mathcal{S}
.

The value function and the Bellman equation.

The worth of a policy
\pi
is measured by its
value function

s\in\mathcal{S}\mapsto V(s):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\,\big|\,s_{0}=s\right]
returning, for each initial state, the expected value of all cumulative future rewards, appropriately discounted. A fundamental, defining feature of the value function is that it arises as the
unique
solution to the
Bellman equation
, a functional fixed-point equation of the form

\displaystyle V(s)=r(s)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)}V(s^{\prime})=:\mathcal{T}V(s).

(1)

where
\mathcal{T}
is known as the
Bellman’s operator
.

Linear policy approximation and the projected Bellman equation.

In most practical scenarios, the value function is very complex and impossible to compute due to the prohibitively large size of the state space
\mathcal{S}
.
A common way to overcome this issue is to consider a parametric approximation of
V
through a linear function, which is both computationally tractable and amenable to statistical estimation. In detail, for a given feature map
\bm{\phi}:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
, mapping each state in
\mathcal{S}
to a centered feature vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d
is typically smaller than
|\mathcal{S}|
, a linear value function approximation is given by

\displaystyle s\in\mathcal{S}\mapsto V_{\bm{\theta}}(s):={\bm{\phi}}(s)^{\top}{\bm{\theta}},

(2)

where
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector of linear coefficients. Throughout this paper, we assume that the Euclidean norm of the features is uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\|\bm{\phi}(s)\|_{2}\leq 1
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Throughout, we let

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}:=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}^{\top}(s)]

(3)

be the feature covariance matrix and use
\lambda_{0}
,
\lambda_{\Sigma}
to denote its smallest and largest eigenvalues, respectively. We assume
\lambda_{0}>0
.

The best approximating linear function is the one parametrized by the coefficient
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
solving the
projected Bellman equation

(
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy, 1997
)

V_{\bm{\theta}^{\star}}=\Pi\mathcal{T}V_{\bm{\theta}^{\star}},

with
\Pi
the orthogonal projection onto the subspace of functions that can be represented by linear combinations of the features. See, e.g.,
Duan et al. (2021)
.
In turn, this (unique!) solution is given by

The Temporal Difference (TD) learning with Polyak-Ruppert averaging.

Although in most real-world RL scenarios the state-action-reward tuples follow a Markov decision process, many of the most recent theoretical contributions – including those delivering the sharpest guaranties listed in Table
1
and
Samsonov et al. (2024a)
– have made the simplifying but technically very convenient assumption of
i.i.d.
data. Here we follow the same convention and analyze the properties of the TD learning algorithm applied to a sequence of
T

i.i.d.
state-action-reward tuples of the form

\displaystyle\{(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t}^{\prime},r_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T},

(7)

where
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{T})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mu
, the stationary distribution and, for each
t
,
a_{t}\sim\pi(s_{t}),r_{t}=r(s_{t},a_{t})
and
s_{t}^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s_{t},a_{t})
.

The TD learning algorithm is a stochastic approximation method to find, in an iterative manner, the solution (
4
). In detail, for a sequence of pre-selected step sizes
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 1},
TD proceeds using the update rule

\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}(\bm{A}_{t}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\bm{b}_{t}),

(8)

where

\bm{A}_{t}:=\bm{\phi}(s_{t})\left(\bm{\phi}(s_{t})-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s_{t}^{\prime})\right)^{\top}\quad\text{and }\quad\bm{b}_{t}:=r_{t}\bm{\phi}(s_{t}).

(9)

Rather than the final TD iterate
\bm{\theta}_{T}
, to estimate
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
it is often preferred to use instead the average of all the iterates

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\theta}_{t}.

(10)

This estimator is an instance of the Polyak-Ruppert averaging scheme
(
Polyak and Juditsky, 1992
;
Ruppert, 1998
)
, a stochastic approximation methodology that provably reduces the variability and accelerates convergence. Furthermore, with a proper choice of the stepsizes, the averaged iterates converge to a Gaussian distribution, thus enabling statistical inference.
To date, the best Berry-Esseen bounds rates for stochastic approximation methods and TD learning with Polyak-Ruppert averaging are due to
Samsonov et al. (2024a)
, which in turn are based on general non-linear Berry-Esseen bounds by
Shao and Zhang (2022)
. In this paper, we improve on these results by deriving novel and sharper high-probability and Berry-Esseen bounds for the estimation error of the TD algorithm

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T}=\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}.

(11)

We conclude by mentioning that the choice of learning rates
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
in the updates (
8
) plays a critical role in determining the statistical properties of (both the original and) the averaged TD estimator (
10
).
Here we assume a
polynomial-decaying
stepsize schedule
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}t^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
.

3
Results

It is known
(
Polyak and Juditsky, 1992
;
Fort, 2015
)
that, in fixed-dimensional settings, the TD estimation error of (
11
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}\,\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}),

(12)

where the asymptotic covariance matrix
\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}=\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{A}^{-\top},\quad\text{with}

(13)

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}=\mathbb{E}[(\bm{A}_{t}\bm{\theta}^{\star}-\bm{b}_{t})(\bm{A}_{t}\bm{\theta}^{\star}-\bm{b}_{t})^{\top}].

(14)

Thus, the above result implies the familiar parametric rate

\displaystyle\|\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T}\|_{2}=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{\Lambda}^{\star})}{T}}\right),

(15)

which may serve as a benchmark.
Below, we will extend these guarantees to a high-dimensional scenario by demonstrating an explicit dependence on all model-related parameters including, in particular, the dimension of the feature space. Our main goal is to show how the TD algorithm can be used to construct non-asymptotic confidence regions and simultaneous confidence intervals for
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
in high dimensions.
To that effect, we will establish a high-probability convergence guarantee for
\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T}
, develop high-dimensional Berry-Esseen bounds over the class of convex sets, and finally build confidence regions for
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
using
\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T}
and a novel, online estimator of the asymptotic variance.

3.1
High-dimensional Berry-Esseen bounds

We start with a novel high-dimensional Berry-Esseen bounds for the TD learning estimator, certifying that, uniformly over the class of
d

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the TD algorithm with Polyak-Ruppert averaging (

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{C}}(\sqrt{T}\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T},\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}))\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\eta_{0}}{(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}}}\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{\Gamma})\|\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}\|T^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{d\mathsf{cond}(\bm{\Gamma})}}{(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}\eta_{0}}T^{\alpha-1}+\tilde{C}\;T^{-\frac{1}{2}}+\tilde{C}^{\prime}\;T^{2\alpha-2}

where

On the final rate.
By choosing

3.2
High-probability bounds

Our next result presents a high-dimensional, high-probability bound on the size of the TD estimation error.

.

As it turns out, the dependence of
\eta_{0}
on the target probability coverage
\delta
can be removed in the special case of
tabular settings
in which the state space is finite. See Appendix
7.1.4
, where we also discuss related (but not applicable to our problem!) high probability bounds for TD-learning in tabular settings by
Chen et al. (2024)
and
Khodadadian and Zubeldia (2025)
, in which the stepsize is also independent of the choice of
\delta
.

3.3
Online estimation of the covariance

In the last section, we obtained finite sample bounds on the convex distance between
\sqrt{T}\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T}
and its Gaussian approximation, which features an
unknown
variance.
To construct confidence regions for
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
, it is necessary to estimate the covariance matrix
\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}
.
Two types of approaches have recently been proposed:
plug-in estimation

(
Chen et al., 2020
)
and
bootstrapping

(
White and White, 2010
;
Hanna et al., 2017
;
Hao et al., 2021
;
Ramprasad et al., 2022
;
Zhu et al., 2023
;
Samsonov et al., 2024a
)
.

Here we propose to use a computationally feasible online plug-in estimator for
\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}
with superior computational efficiency to existing methodologies and establish its non-asymptotic rate of convergence. Specifically, we consider the following

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{A}}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{A}_{t},\quad\text{and}

(16)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t,T}(\bm{A}_{t}\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{b}_{t})(\bm{A}_{t}\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{b}_{t})^{\top}

(17)

as estimators for
\bm{A}
and
\bm{\Gamma}
respectively, where the
weights

\{w_{t,T}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
in
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{T}
are chosen as

\displaystyle w_{t,T}:=\frac{t}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}t}=\frac{2t}{T(T+1)},

following the intuition that as
t
grows larger,
\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
is a more accurate estimator of
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
and hence the corresponding term should be assigned higher weights. We then consider

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}=\overline{\bm{A}}_{T}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{T}\overline{\bm{A}}_{T}^{-\top}

(18)

as the final estimator of
\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}
.

Crucially, the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}
can be evaluated in an online fashion.
See Algorithm
1
in Appendix
9.1
for a detailed description of the procedure, which does
not
require a priori knowledge of the total round of iterations
T
, and has time and space complexities of order
O(Td^{2}+d^{3})
and
O(d^{2})
, respectively.
The estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}
also enjoys nice statistical properties. This is made precise by the following Gaussian comparison result, showing that the unknown asymptotic variance
\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}
in the Berry-Esseen bound of Theorem
7.4
can be replaced by
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}
without compromising the accuracy of the approximation (i.e., without affecting the rate in
T
).
The proof of this result can be found in Appendix
7.3
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider the TD algorithm (
8
) with independent samples and decaying stepsizes
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}t^{-\alpha}
for all
t>0
, any
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
and
\eta_{0}<\frac{1}{4}
, and let
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}
,
\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}
be defined as in (
18
), (
14
) respectively. Then,

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}),\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}))\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{\Gamma})}}{\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)}(\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}+1)\|\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}\|T^{-\frac{1}{3}}+o(T^{-\frac{1}{3}}).

with probability at least
1-3T^{-\frac{1}{3}}-\frac{\sqrt{\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{\Gamma})}}{\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)\sqrt{T}}
.

Above, the term
o(T^{-\frac{1}{3}})
depends on all the parameters of the problem (and, in particular, on
\alpha
) but, as a function of
T
alone, it is of order
O(T^{-\frac{1}{3}})
. See Appendix
7.3
for the proof and details.

Comparison with
Chen et al. (2020)
.

In different settings,
Chen et al. (2020)
considered the problem of carrying out statistical inference for the true model parameters using stochastic gradient descent and assuming a strongly convex and smooth population loss function.
In their work, they consider the slightly different plug-in estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}^{\prime}=\overline{\bm{A}}_{T}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{T}^{\prime}\overline{\bm{A}}_{T}^{-\top}
, where
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{T}^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\bm{A}_{t}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{b}_{t})(\bm{A}_{t}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{b}_{t})^{\top}
.
The authors proved that the estimation error of
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}^{\prime}
is bounded in expectation as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}^{\prime}-\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}\|\leq\|\bm{\Gamma}\|\cdot(\widetilde{C}_{1}T^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}+\widetilde{C}_{2}T^{-\alpha}),

(19)

for problem-related constants
\widetilde{C}_{1}
and
\widetilde{C}_{2}
.
It is possible to modify the proof of Theorem
3.3
to obtain a guarantee in expectation for our problem as well, albeit in the TV metric. Specifically, we can show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}),\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}))\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\widetilde{C}\|\bm{\Gamma}\|T^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(20)

where
\widetilde{C}
depends on
\lambda_{0},\gamma,\eta_{0},\alpha
and
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
.
Comparing (
19
) with (
20
), we have sharpened the rate from
O\left(T^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right)
to
O\left(T^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)
,
independent of
the choice of
\alpha
.

Comparison with
Samsonov et al. (2024a)
.

Leveraging the multiplier-bootstrap technique proposed by
Zhu et al. (2023)
,
Samsonov et al. (2024a)
construct a randomized estimator
\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T}^{(b)}
based on the samples
\mathcal{S}_{T}:=\{s_{t},s_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Their Theorems 4 and 5 yield that

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{C}}(\sqrt{T}(\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T}^{(b)}-\bm{\theta}^{\star})|\mathcal{S}_{T},\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}))\lesssim\frac{\|\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}\|}{[\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)]^{\frac{3}{2}}}(2\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}+1)^{2}\frac{\log T}{T^{\frac{1}{4}}}+o(T^{-\frac{1}{4}})

(21)

Compared with this bootstrap-based procedure, our methodology is advantageous in the following ways: (i) the upper bound in Theorem
3.3
vanishes, as a function of
T
, at the rate of
O(T^{-\frac{1}{3}})
, which is faster than the
O(T^{-\frac{1}{4}}\log T)
rate from the bound (
21
). (ii) The leading term in the upper bound in Theorem
3.3
entails a smaller problem-related coefficient than that of (
21
). (iii) Perhaps most importantly, in order to estimate the bootstrap distribution with an
O(T^{-\frac{1}{4}})
error rate, it is necessary to store the sample trajectory
\mathcal{S}_{T}
and generate
O(T^{\frac{1}{2}})
perturbed trajectories. Consequently, the bootstrap procedure has a time complexity of order
O(T^{\frac{3}{2}}d^{2})
and a space complexity of order
O(T)
. This amounts to a much higher computational burden than our online Algorithm
1
.

3.4
Statistical inference

Combining the results from Sections
3.1
and
3.3
, we describe a general methodology for constructing convex confidence sets for
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
with guaranteed finite sample coverage. Based on the TD estimator
\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T}
in (
10
) and the variance estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}
(
18
), and given any tolerance level
\delta\in(0,1)
, it is enough to pick any convex set
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
that has probability at least
1-\delta
under the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T},\frac{\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}}{T})
with random mean and covariance; that is, satisfying the condition

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T},\frac{\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}}{T})}\left(\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\bigg|\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T},\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

(22)

Natural choices of
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
are (i) hyper-rectangles, leading to
simultaneous confidence intervals
of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\delta}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}[(\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T})_{j}-\widehat{R}/\sqrt{T},(\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T})_{j}+\widehat{R}/\sqrt{T}],

(23)

where
\widehat{R}>0
is the (random) quantile of the
\ell_{\infty}
norm of a centered Gaussian random vectors with covariance
(\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}/{T})
, which can be calculated via simulations; and (ii)
ellipsoids
or
balls
, for example

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\delta}=\left\{\bm{\theta}:\sqrt{T}\|\bm{\theta}-\overline{\bm{\theta}}_{T}\|_{\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}^{-1}}^{2}\leq R\right\},

(24)

where
R
is the
1-\delta
quantile of the
\chi^{2}(d)
distribution.

The next result, whose proof is in Appendix
7.4
, shows that the coverage of
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
converges to the nominal level of
1-\delta
at a rate
O(T^{-1/3})
, as a function of
T
alone.

Theorem 3.4
.

Consider TD with Polyak-Ruppert averaging (
8
) with independent samples and decaying stepsizes
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}t^{-\alpha}
, for all
t>0
with
\alpha=2/3
and
\eta_{0}<\frac{1}{4}
.For a given significance level
\delta\in(0,1)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}^{\star}\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\delta-\frac{\sqrt{\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{\Gamma})}}{\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)}(2\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}+1)\|\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}\|T^{-\frac{1}{3}}-\frac{\sqrt{d\mathsf{cond}(\bm{\Gamma})}}{2(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}\eta_{0}}T^{-\frac{1}{3}}-o(T^{-\frac{1}{3}}),

uniformly over all convex sets
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying (
22
).

We highlight two important features of the above result. First, the coverage guarantee holds
uniformly
over all choices of the convex set
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
meeting condition (
22
), such as those in (
23
) and (
24
). Secondly, the size (i.e., volume) of the confidence sets vanishes at the standard (and optimal) parametric rate of
O(T^{-1/2})
, keeping all the other variables constant.

5
Discussion

We develop high-probability convergence guarantees and Berry-Esseen bounds for TD learning with linear function approximation and
i.i.d.
samples. We also propose an efficient way to construct a covariance estimator and derive its consistency rates. These results lead to a practicable methodology for constructing convex confidence sets for the target parameter, with its size vanishing at a parametric rate
O(T^{-1/2})
. An important direction for future research is to investigate whether the order
O(T^{-1/3})
for the convergence speed of coverage rate in Theorem
3.4
is tight. Another interesting extension would be to derive specialized Berry-Esseen bounds for the sub-class of hyper-rectangles, where one would expect to find a better dependence on the dimension
d
of the feature space, possibly of poly-logarithmic, as opposed to polynomial, order.

Our analysis and results can be generalized to cover other iterative algorithms, such as stochastic gradient descent and stochastic approximation methods. In Appendix
10
, we describe conditions under which our bounds and rates apply to the output of a general linear stochastic approximation algorithm for i.i.d. data, a setting that has received attention in the recent literature
(
Sheshukova et al., 2025
;
Samsonov et al., 2024a
, see, e.g.,)
.

\widetilde{C}

\displaystyle\|\bm{F}_{t}\|\leq\widetilde{C}f_{t}\|\bm{X}\|,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{F}_{t})\leq\widetilde{C}f_{t}\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{X})

(25)

\bm{F}_{t}=O(f_{t})\bm{X}

The following lemma captures some basic properties in linear algebra regarding the relationship between Frobenius and operator norms of matrices.

Lemma 6.1
.

The following properties hold true:

1.

For any sequence of matrices

\displaystyle\left\|\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bm{X}_{i}\right\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\|\bm{X}_{j}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq n\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\|\bm{X}_{i}\|.

(26)

2.

For any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\displaystyle\|\bm{X}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\sqrt{d}\|\bm{X}\|.

(27)

Lemma 6.2

For any matrix

\displaystyle\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{XY})=\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{YX})\leq\|\bm{X}\|\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{Y}).

Lemma

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{t}\bm{X}=O(\|\bm{F}_{t}\|)\bm{X},\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{X}\bm{F}_{t}=O(\|\bm{F}_{t}\|)\bm{X}.

(28)

Lemma 6.3

The following lemma reveals several critical algebraic features of the matrix

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\bm{A}_{t},\bm{A}
be defined as in (

5

\displaystyle 2(1-\gamma)\bm{\Sigma}\preceq\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top}\preceq 2(1+\gamma)\bm{\Sigma};

(29)

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\mathsf{Re}(\lambda_{i}(\bm{A}))\geq(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0};

(30)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{t}^{\top}\bm{A}_{t}]\preceq\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top};

(31)

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}\preceq\lambda_{\Sigma}(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top});

(32)

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}\right\|\leq 1-\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\lambda_{0}\eta,\quad\forall\eta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2\lambda_{\Sigma}}\right);

(33)

\displaystyle\|\bm{A}^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)}.

(34)

Proof
: We address these properties in order.

Proof of Equation (
29
).

By definition,
\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top}
is featured by

\displaystyle\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu,s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)}[\bm{\phi}(s)(\bm{\phi}(s)-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime}))^{\top}+(\bm{\phi}(s)-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime}))\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}]-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu,s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})^{\top}+\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}].

By Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, the second term is bounded by

\displaystyle-\left(\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}+\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})^{\top}\right)\preceq\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})^{\top}+\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}\preceq\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}+\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})^{\top};

Hence,
\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top}
is bounded above by

\displaystyle\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top}

\displaystyle\preceq 2\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}]+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu,s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)}\left[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}+\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}]+\gamma\left(\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}]+\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mu}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}]\right)

\displaystyle=(2+2\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}]=2(1+\gamma)\bm{\Sigma},

and similarly below by

\displaystyle\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top}\succeq 2(1-\gamma)\bm{\Sigma}.

Notice we applied the fact that when
s\sim\mu
and
s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)
, the marginal distribution of
s
is also
\mu
.

Proof of Equation (
30
).

This result is a direct corollary of (
29
). Specifically, let
z=\lambda+i\mu\in\mathbb{C}
denote any eigenvalue of
\bm{A}
, where
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}

\bm{x}+i\bm{y}
denote the corresponding eigenvector in
\mathbb{C}^{d}

\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=\|\bm{y}\|_{2}=1
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\bm{A}(\bm{x}+i\bm{y})=(\lambda+i\mu)(\bm{x}+i\bm{y})=(\lambda\bm{x}-\mu\bm{y})+i(\mu\bm{x}+\lambda\bm{y}).

Comparing the real and imaginary parts of the left-most and right-most terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{A}\bm{x}=\lambda\bm{x}-\mu\bm{y},\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{A}\bm{y}=\mu\bm{x}+\lambda\bm{y}.

Direct calculation yields

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}=\lambda\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\mu\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{y}=\lambda-\mu\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{y},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{y}=\mu\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{x}+\lambda\|\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}=\mu\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{x}+\lambda.

Hence, Equation (
29

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}+\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top})\bm{x}+\frac{1}{4}\bm{y}^{\top}(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top})\bm{y}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\bm{x}^{\top}(1-\gamma)\bm{\Sigma}\bm{x}+\frac{1}{4}\bm{y}^{\top}(1-\gamma)\bm{\Sigma}\bm{y}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}(2(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}+2(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0})=(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}.

This completes the proof of (
30
).

Proof of Equation (
31

The left-hand-side of (
31

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{t}^{\top}\bm{A}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\bm{\phi}(s_{t})-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s_{t}^{\prime}))\bm{\phi}(s_{t})^{\top}\bm{\phi}(s_{t})(\bm{\phi}(s_{t})-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s_{t}^{\prime}))^{\top}]

\displaystyle\preceq\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(s_{t})\bm{\phi}(s_{t})^{\top}]-\gamma\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(s_{t})\bm{\phi}(s_{t}^{\prime})^{\top}+\bm{\phi}(s_{t}^{\prime})\bm{\phi}(s_{t})^{\top}]+\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(s_{t}^{\prime})\bm{\phi}(s_{t}^{\prime})^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top},

Notice that the inequality on the fourth line follows from the assumption that

and the inequality on the fifth line follows from the fact that since

and
s_{t}^{\prime}
and both drawn from the stationary distribution,

Proof of Equation (
32
).

It suffices to show that for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Ax}\leq\lambda_{\Sigma}\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top})\bm{x}.

By definition, the left hand side of this inequality is featured by

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Ax}=\|\bm{Ax}\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(s)(\bm{\phi}(s)-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime}))^{\top}\bm{x}]\right\|_{2}^{2},

where the expectation is taken over the distribution
s\sim\mu,s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)
. For simplicity, denote

\displaystyle f(s)=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)}[(\bm{\phi}(s)-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime}))^{\top}\bm{x}];

then the law of total expectations yields

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Ax}=\left\|\mathbb{E}[f(s)\bm{\phi}(s)]\right\|_{2}^{2}.

In what follows, we aim to show that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}[f(s)\bm{\phi}(s)]\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\Sigma}\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(s)].

(35)

Observe that for any vector
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it can always be guaranteed that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(s)-\bm{\phi}^{\top}(s)\bm{y}]^{2}\geq 0.

A direct expansion yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(s)]-2\mathbb{E}[f(s)\bm{\phi}^{\top}(s)\bm{y}]+\bm{y}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}(s)^{\top}]\bm{y}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(s)]-2\mathbb{E}[f(s)\bm{\phi}^{\top}(s)\bm{y}]+\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{y}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(s)]-2\mathbb{E}[f(s)\bm{\phi}^{\top}(s)\bm{y}]+\lambda_{\Sigma}\|\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\forall\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

By taking

\displaystyle y=\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(s)\bm{\phi}(s)]}{\lambda_{\Sigma}},

we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(s)]-\frac{\left\|\mathbb{E}[f(s)\bm{\phi}(s)]\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\lambda_{\Sigma}},

and (
35

\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Ax}\leq\lambda_{\Sigma}\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(s)].

and we now proceed to bounding
\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(s)]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(s)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu}\left\{\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)}[\bm{\phi}(s)-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})]\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)}[\bm{\phi}(s)-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})]^{\top}\bm{x}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu}\left\{\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid s)}[\bm{\phi}(s)-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})][\bm{\phi}(s)-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})]^{\top}\bm{x}\right\}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left\{[\bm{\phi}(s)-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})][\bm{\phi}(s)-\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})]^{\top}\right\}\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top})\bm{x},

where the last inequality follows from the same logic as in the proof of Equation (
31
). Equation (
32
) follows immediately.

Proof of Equation (
33
).

We firstly notice that

\displaystyle(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A})^{\top}(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A})=\bm{I}-\eta(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top})+\eta^{2}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}.

By invoking (

\displaystyle(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A})^{\top}(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A})\preceq\bm{I}-(\eta-\lambda_{\Sigma}\eta^{2})(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top}).

For any

\displaystyle(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A})^{\top}(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A})\preceq\bm{I}-\frac{\eta}{2}(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top})\preceq(1-(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}\eta)\bm{I}.

Proof of Equation (

For any unit vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{A}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}^{\top}(s)]\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{x}-\gamma\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}^{\top}(s^{\prime})]\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\gamma\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\phi}(s))^{2}]\mathbb{E}[(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime}))^{2}]}

\displaystyle=1-\gamma\sqrt{(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(s)\bm{\phi}^{\top}(s)]\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{x})\cdot(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime})\bm{\phi}^{\top}(s^{\prime})]\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{x})}

\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{A}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{x}\|_{2}

\|\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{A}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\|\bm{A}^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma})}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)}.

Lemma 6.5
.

For any matrix

, there exists a unique positive definite matrix

\displaystyle\|\bm{X}\|\leq\frac{1}{2(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}}\|\bm{E}\|,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{X})\leq\frac{1}{2(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}}\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{E}).

Proof:
The existence of
\bm{X}
follows from the basic property of Lyapunov equation and (
30
); we focus on bounding the norm and trace of
\bm{X}
.

Bounding the operator norm of
\bm{X}
.

Let
\lambda=\lambda_{\max}(\bm{X})
and
\bm{x}
denote a corresponding eigen vector with
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=1
. By definition,
\bm{Xx}=\lambda\bm{x}

\displaystyle\|\bm{E}\|\geq\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Ex}=\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{AX}+\bm{XA}^{\top})\bm{x}=\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top})\bm{x}\geq[2(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}]\cdot\lambda,

where the last inequality follows from (
29

\|\bm{X}\|
follows naturally.

Bounding the norm of
\bm{X}

. Since
\bm{X}\succeq\bm{0}

\displaystyle\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{E})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{AX}+\bm{XA}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{X}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{AX}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\bm{X}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\frac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{X}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{A}+\bm{A}^{\top})\bm{X}^{\frac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle(t+1)^{\alpha}-t^{\alpha}\leq\frac{\beta}{2},

for which we observe

\displaystyle(t+1)^{\alpha}-t^{\alpha}<\alpha t^{\alpha-1}\leq\alpha\left(t^{\star}\right)^{\alpha-1}=\frac{2}{\beta}.

In this way, we prove that
Y_{t}\leq\frac{2}{\beta}
for every
t\geq t^{\star}
.

Bounding
Y_{t}
for
1\leq t<t^{\star}
.

For every positive integer
t
satisfying
1\leq t<t^{\star}
, the iterative relation (
45
)

\displaystyle Y_{t}=t^{-\alpha}X_{t}<X_{t}<X_{t^{\star}}+(t^{\star}-t)

\displaystyle<X_{t^{\star}}+t^{\star}

\displaystyle=(t^{\star})^{\alpha}Y_{t^{\star}}+t^{\star}

\displaystyle<\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\frac{2}{\beta}+\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}(\frac{1}{\alpha}+1).

Proof of (
44
).

We firstly expand the left-hand-side of (
44
) as

\displaystyle t^{-\alpha}\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}(j+1)^{-\alpha}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})+\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}(t^{-\alpha}-(j+1)^{-\alpha})\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha}).

(47)

Here, the first term is bounded by applying the telescoping technique

\displaystyle\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}(j+1)^{-\alpha}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\left[1-(1-\beta(j+1)^{-\alpha})\right]\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}\left(\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})-\prod_{k=t+1}^{j+1}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})-\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j+1}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\left[\left(\prod_{k=t+1}^{t}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})+\sum_{j=t+1}^{\infty}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})\right)-\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j+1}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\frac{1}{\beta}\left(1+\sum_{i=t}^{\infty}\prod_{k=t+1}^{i+1}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})-\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j+1}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})\right)=\frac{1}{\beta}.

(48)

Notice that in (i) we invoked the convention

\displaystyle\prod_{k=t+1}^{t}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})=1,

and a change of variable
i=j-1
.

Therefore, the proof boils down to showing that the second term is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}((j+1)^{-\alpha}-t^{-\alpha})\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})=O(t^{\alpha-1}).

(49)

Towards this end, we denote

\displaystyle m_{t}^{j}=\sum_{k=t}^{j}k^{-\alpha}.

(50)

In terms of
m_{t}^{j}
, the left-hand-side of (
49
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}((j+1)^{-\alpha}-t^{-\alpha})\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})

\displaystyle\asymp\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}((j+1)^{-\alpha}-t^{-\alpha})\exp\left(-\beta m_{t}^{j}\right).

Furthermore, we observe that on one hand,
m_{t}^{j}
is bounded by

\displaystyle m_{t}^{j}=\sum_{k=t}^{j}k^{-\alpha}>\int_{t}^{j+1}x^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{(j+1)^{1-\alpha}-t^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha};

(51)

on the other hand, the term
(j+1)^{-\alpha}-t^{-\alpha}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle t^{-\alpha}-(j+1)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle=(j+1)^{-\alpha}\left[\left(\frac{j+1}{t}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right]

\displaystyle=(m_{t}^{j+1}-m_{t}^{j})\cdot\left[\left(\frac{j+1}{t}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right].

In what follows, we aim to bound the last term in terms of
m_{t}^{j}
. Specifically, since

\displaystyle\left(\frac{j+1}{t}\right)^{1-\alpha}-1<\frac{(1-\alpha)m_{t}^{j}}{t^{1-\alpha}},

we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\frac{j+1}{t}\right)^{\alpha}-1

\displaystyle<\left[\frac{(1-\alpha)m_{t}^{j}}{t^{1-\alpha}}+1\right]^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}-1

\displaystyle<t^{\alpha-1}\left((1-\alpha)m_{t}^{j}+1\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.

Therefore, the left-hand-side of (
49
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}((j+1)^{-\alpha}-t^{-\alpha})\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})

\displaystyle<\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}(m_{t}^{j+1}-m_{t}^{j})t^{\alpha-1}\left((1-\alpha)m_{t}^{j}+1\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\exp\left(\beta t^{-\alpha}-\beta m_{t}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle<t^{\alpha-1}\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}\left((1-\alpha)m_{t}^{j}+1\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\exp\left(-\beta m_{t}^{j}\right)(m_{t}^{j+1}-m_{t}^{j})

\displaystyle\asymp t^{\alpha-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left((1-\alpha)m+1\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\exp(-\beta m)\mathrm{d}m=O(t^{\alpha-1});

the integral on the last line is further bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left((1-\alpha)m+1\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\exp(-\beta m)\mathrm{d}m

\displaystyle<\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(m^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}+2^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)\exp(-\beta m)\mathrm{d}m

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\frac{1}{\beta}+\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right)\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right).

The bound (
44
) follows naturally. ∎

(52)

where

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}t^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and
0\leq\eta_{0}\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda_{\Sigma}}
. Recall that
\bm{Q}_{t}
is defined as in (
53
). Then for any positive integer
\ell<T
, the following relation hold:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\bm{Q}_{t}-\bm{A}^{-1})=-\bm{A}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\prod_{k=1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}),

(54a)

Furthermore, there exist a sequence of matrix
\{\bm{S}_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
, along with a constant
\widetilde{C}
, depending on
\alpha,\eta_{0}
and
\gamma
, such that the difference between
\bm{Q}_{t}
and
\bm{A}^{-1}
can be represented as

\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{t}-\bm{A}^{-1}=\bm{S}_{t}-\bm{A}^{-1}\prod_{k=t}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}),

(54b)

in which
\bm{S}_{t}
is independent of
T
, and its norm is bounded by

\displaystyle\|\bm{S}_{t}\|\lesssim\eta_{0}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{2}{(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}\eta_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}t^{\alpha-1}.

(54c)

Proof
: We address these equations and bounds separately.

Proof of (
54a
)

By definition, the sum of
\bm{Q}_{t}
for
1\leq t\leq T
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{Q}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\sum_{j=t}^{T}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{t=1}^{j}\eta_{t}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}),

where the second line follows by switching the order of summation. Here, the summand can be represented as

\displaystyle\eta_{t}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\bm{A}^{-1}[\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta_{t}\bm{A})]\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\bm{A}^{-1}\left[\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})-\prod_{k=t}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\right].

(55)

Notice that summing the difference in the bracket from
t=1
to
t=j
would cancel all the terms in the middle and only the first and last terms would remain. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{Q}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{t=1}^{j}\eta_{t}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\sum_{t=1}^{j}\bm{A}^{-1}\left[\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})-\prod_{k=t}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{A}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\left[\bm{I}-\prod_{k=1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\right],

which directly implies (
54a
).

Construction of the decomposition (
54b
).

The definition of
\bm{Q}_{t}
(
53
) directly implies that on one hand,

\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{t}

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}\sum_{j=t}^{T}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})=\eta_{t}\bm{I}+\eta_{t}\sum_{j=t+1}^{T}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}),

and that

\displaystyle(\bm{I}-\eta_{t}\bm{A})\bm{Q}_{t}=\eta_{t}\sum_{j=t}^{T}\prod_{k=t}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}\sum_{j=t}^{T-1}\prod_{k=t}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})+\eta_{t}\prod_{k=t}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}\sum_{j=t+1}^{T-1}\prod_{k=t}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})+\eta_{t}\prod_{k=t}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})-\prod_{k=t}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\right](\bm{I}-\eta_{j}\bm{A})-(\bm{I}-\eta_{t}\bm{A})\left[\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})-\eta_{j}\left[\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\right]\bm{A}-\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})+\eta_{t}\bm{A}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}\bm{A}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})-\eta_{j}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\bm{A}

\displaystyle=(\eta_{t}-\eta_{j})\bm{A}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}).

Therefore, by taking a difference between
\bm{Q}_{t}
and
(\bm{I}-\eta_{t}\bm{A})\bm{Q}_{t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\eta_{t}\bm{A}\bm{Q}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{Q}_{t}-(\bm{I}-\eta_{t}\bm{A})\bm{Q}_{t}

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}\bm{I}+\eta_{t}\sum_{j=t+1}^{T-1}\left[\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})-\prod_{k=t}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\right]-\eta_{t}\prod_{k=t}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}\bm{I}+\eta_{t}\sum_{j=t+1}^{T-1}(\eta_{t}-\eta_{j})\bm{A}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})-\eta_{t}\prod_{k=t}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}).

Consequently, the difference between
\bm{Q}_{t}
and
\bm{A}^{-1}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{t}-\bm{A}^{-1}=\underset{\bm{S}_{t}}{\underbrace{\sum_{j=t+1}^{T-1}(\eta_{t}-\eta_{j})\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})}}-\bm{A}^{-1}\prod_{k=t}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}),

(56)

which takes the form of (
54b
).

Proof of (
54c
).

Lemma
6.4
implies that

\displaystyle\left\|\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\eta_{k}(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\eta_{k}(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}\sum_{k=t+1}^{j-1}\eta_{k}\right).

Recalling the definition of
m_{t}^{j}
(
50
),(
51
), we can bound the norm
\bm{S}_{t}
by

\displaystyle\|\bm{S}_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}(\eta_{t}-\eta_{j})\left\|\prod_{k=t+1}^{j-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}(\eta_{t}-\eta_{j})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}\sum_{k=t+1}^{j-1}\eta_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=t}^{\infty}\eta_{j}\left[\left(\frac{j}{t}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right]\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}\eta_{0}m_{t}^{j}\right).

The right-hand-side can then be bounded by a similar argument to that of Lemma

. ∎

Lemma 6.9
.

When
\eta_{0}<\frac{1}{2\lambda_{\Sigma}}
, for every positive definite matrix
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, it can be guaranteed that

\displaystyle T^{-\alpha}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\prod_{k=t}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\bm{Y}\prod_{k=t}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}^{\top})\right)-\bm{X}\leq O(T^{\alpha-1})\bm{Y}

(57)

where
\bm{X}\succeq\bm{0}
is the solution to the Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle\eta_{0}(\bm{A}\bm{X}+\bm{XA}^{\top})=\bm{Y},

(58)

and the big
O
notation for matrix sequences is defined as in (
25
).

Proof
: For simplicity, we define a sequence of matrices
\{\bm{X}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{T}
as

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{i}=\begin{cases}&i^{-\alpha}\sum_{t=1}^{i}\left(\prod_{k=t}^{i}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\bm{Y}\prod_{k=t}^{i}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}^{\top})\right),\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq T;\\
&\bm{0},\quad\text{for}\quad i=0.\end{cases}

in this way, the left-hand-side of (
57
) can be represented by
\bm{X}_{T}-\bm{X}
. It can be easily verified that the sequence
\{\bm{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
is featured by the iterative relation

\displaystyle i^{\alpha}\bm{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=(i-1)^{\alpha}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A})\bm{X}_{i-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A}^{\top})+(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A})\bm{Y}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A}^{\top}),\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq T.

Direct calculation yields that for every
1\leq i\leq T
,

\displaystyle i^{\alpha}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X})

\displaystyle=(i-1)^{\alpha}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A})\bm{X}_{i-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A}^{\top})+(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A})\bm{Y}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A}^{\top})-i^{\alpha}\bm{X}

\displaystyle=(i-1)^{\alpha}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A})(\bm{X}_{i-1}-\bm{X})(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A}^{\top})

\displaystyle+(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A})\bm{Y}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A}^{\top})-[i^{\alpha}-(i-1)^{\alpha}]\bm{X}-(i-1)^{\alpha}[\eta_{i}(\bm{AX}+\bm{XA}^{\top})+\eta_{i}^{2}\bm{AXA}^{\top}].

We now feature the terms on the last line. Firstly, we notice that (
58
) guarantees

\displaystyle\eta_{i}(\bm{AX}+\bm{XA}^{\top})=\eta_{0}i^{-\alpha}(\bm{AX}+\bm{XA}^{\top})=i^{-\alpha}\bm{Y};

therefore, a simple reorganization yields

\displaystyle(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A})\bm{Y}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A}^{\top})-[i^{\alpha}-(i-1)^{\alpha}]\bm{X}-(i-1)^{\alpha}[\eta_{i}(\bm{AX}+\bm{XA}^{\top})+\eta_{i}^{2}\bm{AXA}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\left[1-\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\bm{Y}-[i^{\alpha}-(i-1)^{\alpha}]\bm{X}-\eta_{i}(\bm{AY}+\bm{YA}^{\top})+\eta_{i}^{2}\bm{A}(\bm{Y}-(i-1)^{\alpha}\bm{X})\bm{A}^{\top}.

For these terms, we observe

\displaystyle\left[1-\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\bm{Y}\preceq\left[1-\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)\right]\bm{Y}=\frac{1}{i}\bm{Y};

\displaystyle[i^{\alpha}-(i-1)^{\alpha}]\bm{X}=i^{\alpha}\left[1-\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\bm{X}\preceq i^{\alpha}\left[1-\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)\right]\bm{X}=i^{\alpha-1}\bm{X}=O(i^{\alpha-1})\bm{Y};

\displaystyle\eta_{i}(\bm{AY}+\bm{YA}^{\top})=O(i^{-\alpha})\bm{Y};

\displaystyle\eta_{i}^{2}\bm{A}(\bm{Y}-(i-1)^{\alpha}\bm{X})\bm{A}^{\top}=O(i^{-\alpha})\bm{Y}.

Hence, the leading term decreases by the rate of
O(i^{\alpha-1})\bm{Y}
. As a result,

\displaystyle i^{\alpha}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X})=(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A})[(i-1)^{\alpha}(\bm{X}_{i-1}-\bm{X})](\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A}^{\top})+O(i^{\alpha-1})\bm{Y}

For simplicity, we denote
\bm{Z}_{i}=i^{\alpha}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X})
. The relation above can then be represented as

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{i}=(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A})\bm{Z}_{i-1}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{A}^{\top})+O(i^{\alpha-1})\bm{Y}.

It is easy to verify by iteration that, there exists a quantity
\widetilde{C}
independent of
T
, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{T}i^{\alpha-1}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\bm{Y}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}^{\top})

\displaystyle\preceq\bm{Z}_{T}\preceq\sum_{i=1}^{T}i^{\alpha-1}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\bm{Y}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}^{\top});

In other words,

\displaystyle T^{-\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{T}i^{\alpha-1}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\bm{Y}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}^{\top})

\displaystyle\preceq\bm{X}_{T}-\bm{X}

\displaystyle\preceq T^{-\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{T}i^{\alpha-1}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\bm{Y}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}^{\top}).

By letting
\beta=\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\lambda_{0}\eta_{0}
, Lemma
6.4
implies

\displaystyle\left\|T^{-\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{T}i^{\alpha-1}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}^{\top})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq T^{-\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{T}i^{\alpha-1}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}\|\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}\|\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}\|\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}^{\top}\|

\displaystyle\leq T^{-\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{T}i^{\alpha-1}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})^{2}

\displaystyle<T^{\alpha-1}\sum_{i=1}^{T}i^{-\alpha}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})

\displaystyle=T^{\alpha-1}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left(\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})-\prod_{k=i}^{T}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}T^{\alpha-1}\left(\prod_{k=T+1}^{T}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})-\prod_{k=1}^{T}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})\right)<\frac{1}{\beta}T^{\alpha-1};

notice that in the last two lines we invoked a similar approach to the telescoping method (
48
). Combining this with (
28
), we obtain

\displaystyle T^{-\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{T}i^{\alpha-1}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\bm{Y}\prod_{k=i+1}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}^{\top})=O(T^{\alpha-1})\bm{Y}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}t^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
and
0\leq\eta\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda_{\Sigma}}
. For
\bm{Q}_{t}
defined as in (
53

\displaystyle\|\bm{Q}_{t}\|\leq 3\eta_{0}^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\left(\frac{4\alpha}{\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},\quad\forall t\in[T];

(59)

\displaystyle\|\bm{A}\bm{Q}_{t}\|\leq 2+\eta_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right)t^{\alpha-1};\quad\text{and}

(60)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\bm{Q}_{t}-\bm{A}^{-1}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)}T^{\alpha-1}+\widetilde{C}T^{2\alpha-2}

(61)

where
\beta=\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\lambda_{0}\eta_{0}
and
\widetilde{C}
depends on
\alpha,\eta_{0},\lambda_{0},\gamma
.

Proof
: According to Lemma
6.4
, since
0<\eta_{t}\leq\eta_{0}\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda_{\Sigma}}
for all
t
, the norm of
\bm{Q}_{t}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\|\bm{Q}_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{t}\sum_{j=t}^{T}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)\eta_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}t^{-\alpha}\cdot\sum_{j=t}^{T}\prod_{k=t+1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}(1-\gamma)\eta_{0}k^{-\alpha}\right).

The bound (
59
) follows immediately by taking
\beta=\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\lambda_{0}\eta_{0}
in (
43
).
For the bound (
60
), observe due to (
54b
) that

\displaystyle\bm{AQ}_{t}-\bm{I}=\bm{AS}_{t}-\prod_{k=t}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A}).

54c
) directly imply

\displaystyle\|\bm{AQ}_{t}\|\leq\|\bm{I}\|+\|\bm{AS}_{t}\|+\left\|\prod_{k=t}^{T}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\eta_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right)t^{\alpha-1}+1

\displaystyle=2+\eta_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right)t^{\alpha-1}.

6.2.2
Properties of multivariate Gaussian distributions

The following theorem by
Devroye et al. (2018)
gives an upper bound for the total variation (TV) distance between two Gaussian random variables with same means and different covariance matrices.

Theorem 6.14

(
Devroye et al. (2018)
)
.

Let
\bm{\Lambda}_{1},\bm{\Lambda}_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
be two positive definite matrices, and
\bm{\mu}
be any vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the TV distance between Gaussian distributions
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{1})
and
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{2})
is bounded by

\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{100}\left\|\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{-1/2}\bm{\Lambda}_{2}\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{-1/2}-\bm{I}_{d}\right\|_{\mathsf{F}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{1}),\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\left\|\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{-1/2}\bm{\Lambda}_{2}\bm{\Lambda}_{1}^{-1/2}-\bm{I}_{d}\right\|_{\mathsf{F}}.

In the case of
\bm{\Lambda}_{1}\succ\bm{\Lambda}_{2}
, the following proposition further specifies that the TV distance and the distance on convex sets are the same between
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}_{1})
and
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}_{2})
.

Proposition 6.15
.

Let
\bm{\Lambda}_{1},\bm{\Lambda}_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}
be two positive definite matrices, and
\bm{\mu}
be any vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When
\bm{\Lambda}_{1}\succ\bm{\Lambda_{2}}
, it can be guaranteed that

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{C}}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{1}),\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{2}))=d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{1}),\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{2})).

Proof
: Without loss of generality, let
\bm{\mu}=\bm{0}
. Define
\mathcal{C}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid p_{1}(\bm{x})<p_{2}(\bm{x})\},

where
p_{1}(\cdot)
and
p_{2}(\cdot)
denote the probability density functions of
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}_{1})
and
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}_{2})
respectively; we aim to show that
\mathcal{C}
is a convex set. Notice that the ratio between these two density functions is featured by

\displaystyle\frac{p_{1}(\bm{x})}{p_{2}(\bm{x})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathsf{det}(\bm{\Lambda_{1}})}}\exp(-\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda_{1}}^{-1}\bm{x})}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathsf{det}(\bm{\Lambda_{2}})}}\exp(-\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda_{2}}^{-1}\bm{x})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{det}(\bm{\Lambda}_{2})}{\mathsf{det}(\bm{\Lambda_{1}})}}\exp(\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{\Lambda_{2}}^{-1}-\bm{\Lambda_{1}}^{-1})\bm{x}).

Therefore by definition, the set
\mathcal{C}
is characterized by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigg|\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{\Lambda_{2}}^{-1}-\bm{\Lambda_{1}}^{-1})\bm{x}<\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\mathsf{det}(\bm{\Lambda}_{1})}{\mathsf{det}(\bm{\Lambda}_{2})}\right\}.

Since
\bm{\Lambda_{1}}\succ\bm{\Lambda}_{2}
, the matrix
(\bm{\Lambda_{2}}^{-1}-\bm{\Lambda_{1}}^{-1})
must be positive definite; consequently,
\mathcal{C}
is a
d
-dimensional hyper-ellipsoid and is therefore convex. With
\mathcal{C}
being convex, it is seen that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}_{1})}(\bm{z}\in\mathcal{C})-\mathbb{P}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}_{2})}(\bm{z}\in\mathcal{C})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{C}_{d}}\left|\mathbb{P}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{1})}(\bm{z}\in\mathcal{A})-\mathbb{P}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{2})}(\bm{z}\in\mathcal{A})\right|

\displaystyle=d_{\mathsf{C}}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}_{1}),\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}_{2})),

while in the meantime

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{1})}(\bm{z}\in\mathcal{C})-\mathbb{P}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{2})}(\bm{z}\in\mathcal{C})\right|

\displaystyle=d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{1}),\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda}_{2}))

\displaystyle\geq d_{\mathsf{C}}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}_{1}),\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}_{2})).

Combining these two inequalities completes the proof. ∎

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 4R+2\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}+1.

In what follows, we prove the desired result in the following 4 steps:

1.

Showing the existence of
t^{\star}(\delta)
and featuring its relationship with

;

2.

Showing that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{H}_{t^{\star}})\geq 1-\frac{1}{3}\delta
;

3.

Showing that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{H}_{\infty})\geq 1-\frac{2}{3}\delta
;

4.

Showing the high-probability bound (

Step 1: the existence of
t^{\star}(\delta)
.

By definition,
t^{\star}
depends on the integral

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\exp(-Cx^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}x,

which we handle with a change of variable

\displaystyle u=x^{\alpha}.

It is easy to verify that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\alpha}u^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}u,

and hence

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\exp(-Cx^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{t^{\alpha}}^{\infty}u^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\exp(-Cu)\mathrm{d}u.

since
\alpha>0.5
, it can be guaranteed that
u^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}<u
, for any
u>t^{\alpha}\geq 1
. Hence, this integral is bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\exp(-Cx^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{t^{\alpha}}^{\infty}u\exp(-Cu)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha C^{2}}(Cu+1)\exp(-Cu).

Consequently, in order that

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\exp(-Cx^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{\delta}{9},

it suffices to let

\displaystyle(Ct^{\alpha}+1)\exp(-Ct^{\alpha})\leq\frac{\alpha C^{2}\delta}{9};

It is easy to verify that this condition is satisfied by taking

\displaystyle t=\left[\frac{2}{C}\left(\log\frac{9}{\alpha C^{2}\delta}\vee\frac{5}{2}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

In other words,
t^{\star}(\delta)
is a well-defined function of
\delta
that scales as
O\left(\log^{\alpha}\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
as
\delta\to 0
.

Step 2: Bounding
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{H}_{t^{\star}})
.

We aim to prove, through an induction argument, that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{H}_{t})\geq 1-\frac{t}{t^{\star}}\cdot\frac{\delta}{3}.

(75)

Since
\bm{\theta}_{0}=0

We firstly observe that

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}\bm{R}_{i}^{t}\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle<\eta_{0}^{2}(4R+2\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}+1)^{2}\underset{W_{t}}{\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{t}i^{-2\alpha}\prod_{k=1}^{t}(1-\beta k^{-\alpha})}}.

The term

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}\bm{R}_{i}^{t}\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}_{i}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{8}(4R+2\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}+1),

where we invoked the condition (

\displaystyle\|\bm{\Delta}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\prod_{k=1}^{t}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\bm{\Delta}_{0}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}\bm{R}_{i}^{t}\bm{\zeta}_{i}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\prod_{k=1}^{t}(\bm{I}-\eta_{k}\bm{A})\bm{\Delta}_{0}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}\bm{R}_{i}^{t}\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}_{i}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=1.191+\frac{5}{4\eta_{0}}T^{-\alpha-1}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{T}(1+0.2\eta_{0}k^{-\alpha})-1\right).

Here, since
k^{-\alpha}\leq 1
, it can be guaranteed that

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{T}(1+0.2\eta_{0}k^{-\alpha})

\displaystyle>\prod_{k=1}^{T}(1+0.2\eta_{0})^{k^{-\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\log(1+0.2\eta_{0})\sum_{k=1}^{T}k^{-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle>\exp\left(\log(1+0.2\eta_{0})\cdot\frac{T^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\right).

Take
u=T^{1-\alpha}
, then the norm of
\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T}
is bounded below by

\displaystyle\left\|\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T}\right\|_{2}>1.191+\frac{5}{4\eta_{0}}u^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{1-\alpha}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\log(1+0.2\eta_{0})}{1-\alpha}u\right)-1\right],

which grows exponentially with
u
and hence faster than any polynomial function of
T
. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

) and taking the supremum of

It is easy to verify that by definition (

9.2
The Markov Decision Process and the feature map

Given a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
, define a sequence of MDP
\{\mathcal{M}_{\bm{q}}\}
indexed by
\bm{q}\in\mathcal{Q}\subset\{q_{+},q_{-}\}^{d-1}
where for each
\bm{q}
, the transition kernel equals

\displaystyle P_{\bm{q}}(s^{\prime}\mid s)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}q_{s}\mathds{1}(s^{\prime}=s)+\frac{1-q_{s}}{|\mathcal{S}|-d+1}\mathds{1}(s^{\prime}\geq d)&\text{for}&s<d;\\[5.69046pt]
\frac{\gamma}{|\mathcal{S}|-d+1}\mathds{1}(s^{\prime}\geq d)+\frac{1-q_{s^{\prime}}}{d-1}\mathds{1}(s^{\prime}<d)&\text{for}&s\geq d.\end{array}\right.

and the reward function equals
r(s)=\mathds{1}(s\geq d)
.

Here, for each
i\in[d-1]
,
q_{i}
is taken to be either
q_{+}
or
q_{-}
where

\displaystyle q_{+}:=\gamma+(1-\gamma)^{2}\varepsilon,\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{-}:=\gamma-(1-\gamma)^{2}\varepsilon.

We further impose the constraint that the number of
q_{+}
’s and
q_{-}
’s in
\bm{q}
are the same, namely,

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{d-1}\mathds{1}(q_{s}=q_{+})=\sum_{s=1}^{d-1}\mathds{1}(q_{s}=q_{-})=(d-1)/2.

(118)

Here without loss of generality, assume
d
is an odd number.
With these definitions in place, it can be easily verified that the stationary distribution for
\bm{P}
obeys

\displaystyle\mu(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}\frac{1}{2(d-1)}&\text{for}&s<d;\\[5.69046pt]
\frac{1}{2(|\mathcal{S}|-d+1)}&\text{for}&s\geq d.\end{array}\right.

Moreover, suppose the feature map is taken to be

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}(s)=\bm{e}_{s\wedge d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

then one can further verify that

\displaystyle\theta^{\star}(d)

\displaystyle=V^{\star}(s)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\frac{\gamma^{2}(1-q_{i})^{2}}{(d-1)(1-\gamma q_{i})}},

\displaystyle\theta^{\star}(i)

\displaystyle=V^{\star}(i)=\frac{\gamma(1-q_{i})}{1-\gamma q_{i}}V^{\star}(s),\text{ for }s\geq d\text{ and }i<d.

(121)

From the expressions above, we remark that, the values of
q
and
V^{\star}(s)
with
s\geq d
are fixed for all
\bm{q}\in\mathcal{Q}
which is ensured by the construction (
118
). It is shown in Theorem 2 of
Li et al. (2024b)
that the sequence
\{\mathcal{M}_{\bm{q}}\}_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}
is hard to distinguish for a considerably large set
\mathcal{Q}
.

9.5
Decomposition of TD estimation error

In this section, we aim to further explore the emergence of the TD estimation error
\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T}
in numerical simulations, in order to verify our key claims in the proofs of our theoretical results. Specifically, we aim to explore the role played by the choice of
\alpha
through decomposing the TD estimation error. Recall that throughout our proofs, we repetitively used the decomposition

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T}=\underset{I_{1}}{\underbrace{\frac{1}{T\eta_{0}}\bm{Q}_{0}\bm{\Delta}_{0}}}-\underset{I_{2}}{\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bm{Q}_{i}(\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\theta}^{\star}-\bm{b}_{i})}}-\underset{I_{3}}{\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bm{Q}_{i}(\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{A})\bm{\Delta}_{i-1}}},

where the matrices
\{\bm{Q}_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq T}
are defined by (
53
), and therefore decided by the matrix
\bm{A}
as well as the choice of stepsizes
\{\eta_{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
. Of the three terms on the right-hand-side:

1.

The term
I_{1}
is related only to the initial estimator
\bm{\theta}_{0}
through
\bm{\Delta}_{0}
and not to the samples
\{(\bm{A}_{t},\bm{b}_{t})\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
. Therefore, we refer to this term as the
initialization error
. This term is deterministic, and according to our analysis, converges by the rate of

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T\eta_{0}}\bm{Q}_{0}\bm{\Delta}_{0}\right\|_{2}\lesssim O(T^{-1}).

2.

The term
I_{2}
is related only to the samples
\{(\bm{A}_{t},\bm{b}_{t})\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
and not to any of the iterations
\{\bm{\theta}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
. Therefore, we refer to this term as the
sampling error
. According to our analysis, this term corresponds to the asymptotic error:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bm{Q}_{i}(\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\theta}^{\star}-\bm{b}_{i})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}^{\star})

when
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
; non-asymptotically speaking, the variance of this term is
\overline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}/T
by definition, and
\overline{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}-\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}=\bm{O}(T^{\alpha-1})
;

3.

The term
I_{3}
is related to both the samples
\{(\bm{A}_{t},\bm{b}_{t})\}_{1\leq t\leq T}
and the intermediate iterations
\{\bm{\theta}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
. Hence, we refer to this term as the
iterative error
. According to our analysis, this term converges by the rate of

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bm{Q}_{i}(\bm{A}_{i}-\bm{A})\bm{\Delta}_{i-1}\right\|_{2}\lesssim O(T^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}).

Extension to general linear stochastic approximation

In this section, we explore on the conditions under which our theoretical analysis and statistical inference procedure can be extended to general Linear Stochastic Approximation (LSA) for the linear system

\displaystyle\bm{A\theta}=\bm{b},

where a sample matrix
\bm{A}_{t}
and a sample vector
\bm{b}_{t}
are observed
i.i.d.
at each time
t
, with expectations being
\bm{A}
and
\bm{b}
respectively. Similar to TD learning, we also run

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}(\bm{A}_{t}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\bm{b}_{t}),

with the same choice of stepsizes
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}t^{-\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
.

We first make the following assumptions on the matrix
\bm{A}
:

Assumption 10.1

(Stability)
.

There exists a
d\times d
symmetric matrix
\bm{U}\succeq\bm{0}
and a constant
\beta_{0}>0
, such that
\bm{AU}+\bm{UA}^{\top}\succeq\beta_{0}\bm{I}
;

Notice that in our setting of TD learning, this assumption is satisfied with
\bm{U}=\bm{I}
, and
\beta_{0}=2(1-\gamma)\lambda_{0}
; for general LSA, it is guaranteed as long as
-\bm{A}
is
Hurwitz
, i.e., all the eigenvalues of
-\bm{A}
has strictly negative real-parts. Under that condition,
\beta_{0}
can be set to
1
, and
\bm{U}
is the unique solution to the Lyapunov equation
\bm{AU}+\bm{UA}^{\top}=\bm{I}
.
Meanwhile, we need the following constraints on the samples
\{(\bm{A}_{t},\bm{b}_{t})\}
:

Assumption 10.2

(Sampling constraints)
.

With
\bm{U}
specified as in Assumption
10.1
,

1.

There exists a constant
c_{U}>0
, such that
\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{t}\bm{U}\bm{A}_{t}^{\top}]\preceq c_{U}(\bm{AU}+\bm{UA}^{\top})
;

2.

There exists constants
c_{A}>0,c_{b}>0
, such that
\|\bm{A}_{t}\|_{\bm{U}}\leq c_{A}
and
\|\bm{b}_{t}\|_{\bm{U}}\leq c_{b}
almost surely.

Under our setting of TD learning, Assumption
10.2
is satisfied with
c_{U}=1,c_{A}=2
and
c_{b}=1
by Lemma
6.4
. For general LSA with
-\bm{A}
being Hurwitz, as long as (2) is satisfied, (1) can also be guaranteed by setting
c_{U}=c_{A}\|\bm{U}\|
.
As long as both Assumption
10.1
and Assumption
10.2
are satisfied, it is immediate that
\|\bm{A}_{t}\bm{\theta}^{\star}-\bm{b}_{t}\|_{\bm{U}}\leq c_{A}\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{\bm{U}}+c_{b}
almost surely, and that for every
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A})\bm{x}\|_{\bm{U}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\|(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}_{t})\bm{x}\|_{\bm{U}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{x}\|_{\bm{U}}^{2}-\eta\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{AU}+\bm{UA}^{\top})\bm{x}+\eta^{2}\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{t}\bm{U}\bm{A}_{t}^{\top}]\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{x}\|_{\bm{U}}^{2}-(\eta-c_{U}\eta^{2})\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{AU}+\bm{UA}^{\top})\bm{x};

as long as

\displaystyle\eta\leq\frac{1}{2c_{U}}=:\eta_{\max},

this bound can be further elaborated as

\displaystyle\|(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A})\bm{x}\|_{\bm{U}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\|(\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}_{t})\bm{x}\|_{\bm{U}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{x}\|_{\bm{U}}^{2}-\frac{\eta}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{AU}+\bm{UA}^{\top})\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{x}\|_{\bm{U}}^{2}-\frac{\beta_{0}\eta}{2}\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{\beta_{0}\eta}{2\|\bm{U}\|}\right)\|\bm{x}\|_{\bm{U}}^{2};

Consequently, as long as
\eta\leq\eta_{\max}
,

\displaystyle\|\bm{I}-\eta\bm{A}\|_{\bm{U}}\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{\beta_{0}\eta}{2\|\bm{U}\|}}\leq 1-\frac{\beta_{0}\eta}{4\|\bm{U}\|}.

This is a direct generalization of Lemma
6.4
.

While the detailed derivations are well beyond the scope of this paper, our theoretical analysis in Theorems
3.2
,
3.1
,
3.3
can all be extended to general LSA with this generalized version of Lemma
6.4
. Specifically:

1.

For every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists a proper choice of
\eta_{0}
, such that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\|\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T}\|_{\bm{U}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{Tr}(\bm{U\Lambda}^{\star})}{T}\log\frac{1}{\delta}};

2.

As long as
\eta_{0}\leq\eta_{\max}
, it can be guaranteed that

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{C}}(\sqrt{T}\overline{\bm{\Delta}}_{T},\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}))\lesssim C_{1}T^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}+C_{2}T^{\alpha-1},

with coefficients
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
related to
\bm{U},\beta_{0},c_{U},c_{A},c_{b}
. Therefore, choosing
\alpha=\frac{2}{3}
would still yield an
O(T^{-1/3})
convergence rate;

3.

With variance estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}
defined in the same way as (
18
),

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}_{T}),\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}))\lesssim O(T^{-\frac{1}{3}})

with probability at least
1-O(T^{-\frac{1}{3}})
;

4.

Under the same statistical inference procedure as described in Section
3.4
, the confidence region
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
still satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta}^{\star}\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\delta-O(T^{-\frac{1}{3}}).
