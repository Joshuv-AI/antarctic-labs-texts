Peer review of the pesticide risk assessment of the active substance proquinazid
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Fernando Álvarez, Maria Arena, Domenica Auteri, Sofia Batista Leite, Marco Binaglia, Anna Federica Castoldi, Arianna Chiusolo, Angelo Colagiorgi, Mathilde Colas, Federica Crivellente, Chloe De Lentdecker, Isabella De Magistris, Mark Egsmose, Gabriella Fait, Franco Ferilli, German Giner Santonja, Varvara Gouliarmou, Alessio Ippolito, Frederique Istace, Dimitra Kardassi, Aude Kienzler, Anna Lanzoni, Roberto Lava, Renata Leuschner, Alberto Linguadoca, Mariano Lopez Romano, Jochem Louisse, Christopher Lythgo, Oriol Magrans, Iris Mangas, Silvia Mazzega, Andrea Mioč, Ileana Miron, Tunde Molnar, Laura Padovani, Vincenzo Padricello, Martina Panzarea, Juan Manuel Parra Morte, Simone Rizzuto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe conclusions of the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) following the peer review of the initial risk assessments carried out by the competent authorities of the rapporteur Member State Sweden and co‐rapporteur Member State Lithuania for the pesticide active substance proquinazid are reported. The context of the peer review was that required by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659. The conclusions were reached on the basis of the evaluation of the representative uses of proquinazid as a fungicide on powdery mildew diseases on cereals and grapes. The reliable end points, appropriate for use in regulatory risk assessment are presented. Missing information identified as being required by the regulatory framework is listed. Concerns are reported.Keywords: fungicide, peer review, pesticide, Proquinazid, risk assessment

SUMMARY

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659, lays down the procedure for the renewal of the approval of active substances submitted under Article 14 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. The list of those substances is established in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 686/2012 as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2016/183. Proquinazid is one of the active substances listed in that Regulation.

In accordance with Article 1 of Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, the rapporteur Member State (RMS), Sweden and co‐rapporteur Member State (co‐RMS), Lithuania, received an application from Corteva Agriscience International Sàrl for the renewal of approval of the active substance proquinazid.

An initial evaluation of the dossier on proquinazid was provided by the RMS in the renewal assessment report (RAR) and, subsequently, a peer review of the pesticide risk assessment on the RMS evaluation was conducted by EFSA in accordance with Article 13 of Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659. The following conclusions are derived.

The uses of proquinazid as a fungicide on cereals and grapes by foliar spraying in field, as proposed at EU level, result in a sufficient fungicidal efficacy against the target pests (Blumeria graminis and Uncinula necator).

The assessment of the data package revealed no issues that could not be finalised or that need to be included as critical areas of concern with respect to identity, physical and chemical properties and analytical methods.

In the area of mammalian toxicology, data gaps are identified to characterise the toxicological profile of an impurity and some metabolites, leading to issues that could not be finalised.

In the area of residues, due to the multiple data gaps identified – including insufficient characterisation of toxicity of major residue metabolites – the consumer dietary risk assessment for all representative uses could not be finalised. Also, the consumer risk assessment from the consumption of drinking water could not be finalised due to incomplete data.

In the area of environmental fate and behaviour, a data gap remains regarding the effect of water treatment processes on the nature of the identified metabolite residues potentially present in surface water, when surface water is abstracted for the production of drinking water, preventing the finalisation of the consumer risk assessment as indicated above.

In the area of ecotoxicology, a low risk was concluded for birds, mammals (acute), aquatic organisms, bees, soil organisms, non‐target terrestrial plants and organisms involved in biological methods for sewage treatment. A high long‐term risk to mammals and a high in‐field risk to non‐target arthropods other than bees was concluded for several representative uses.

Regarding the assessment of the endocrine disruption (ED) properties, based on the available information, it can be concluded that proquinazid is an endocrine disruptor in humans according to point 3.6.5 of Annex II to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, as amended by Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605, leading to a critical area of concern. For non‐target organisms, it could be concluded that the substance does not meet the ED criteria according to point 3.8.2 of Annex II to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, as amended by Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605.

BACKGROUND

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, 1 as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659, 2 (hereinafter referred to as ‘the Regulation’), lays down the provisions for the procedure of the renewal of the approval of active substances, submitted under Article 14 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009. 3 This regulates for the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) the procedure for organising the consultation of Member States, the applicant(s) and the public on the initial evaluation provided by the rapporteur Member State (RMS) and/or co‐rapporteur Member State (co‐RMS) in the renewal assessment report (RAR), and the organisation of an expert consultation where appropriate.

In accordance with Article 13 of the Regulation, unless formally informed by the European Commission that a conclusion is not necessary, EFSA is required to adopt a conclusion on whether the active substance can be expected to meet the approval criteria provided for in Article 4 of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 within 5 months from the end of the period provided for the submission of written comments, subject to an extension of an additional 3 months where additional information is required to be submitted by the applicant(s) in accordance with Article 13(3). Furthermore, in accordance with Article 13(3a), where the information available in the dossier is not sufficient to conclude the assessment on whether the approval criteria for endocrine disruption are met, additional information can be requested to be submitted in a period of minimum 3 months, not exceeding 30 months, depending on the type of information requested.

In accordance with Article 1 of the Regulation, the RMS Sweden and co‐RMS Lithuania received an application from Corteva Agriscience International Sàrl for the renewal of approval of the active substance Proquinazid. Complying with Article 8 of the Regulation, the RMS checked the completeness of the dossier and informed the applicant, the co‐RMS Lithuania, the European Commission and EFSA about the admissibility.

The RMS provided its initial evaluation of the dossier on proquinazid in the RAR, which was received by EFSA on 14 February 2022 (Sweden, 2022).

In accordance with Article 12 of the Regulation, EFSA distributed the RAR to the Member States and the applicant, Corteva Agriscience International Sàrl, for consultation and comments on 5 December 2022. EFSA also provided comments. In addition, EFSA conducted a public consultation on the RAR. EFSA collated and forwarded all comments received to the European Commission on 14 February 2023. At the same time, the collated comments were forwarded to the RMS for compilation and evaluation in the format of reporting table. In addition, the applicant was invited to respond to the comments received. The comments and the applicant's response were evaluated by the RMS in column 3.

The need for expert consultation and the necessity for additional information to be submitted by the applicant in accordance with Article 13(3) of the Regulation were considered in a telephone conference between EFSA, the RMS and ECHA on 12 May 2023. On the basis of the comments received, the applicant's response to the comments and the RMS's evaluation thereof, it was concluded that additional information should be requested from the applicant, and that EFSA should conduct an expert consultation in the areas of mammalian toxicology, residues, environmental fate and behaviour, and ecotoxicology.

In addition, following a consultation with Member States in the Pesticides Peer Review Expert meeting TC 131 (March 2024), it was considered necessary to apply an additional clock stop of 3–30 month in accordance with Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659, to be able to conclude whether the approval criteria for endocrine disruption in line with the scientific criteria for the determination of endocrine disrupting properties, as laid down in Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605, 4 are met.

In addition, in accordance with the provisions of Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659, following a consultation with Member States in the Pesticides Peer Review Expert meeting TC 131 (March 2024), the applicant was given the opportunity to submit, within a period of 3 months, additional information to address the approval criteria set out in point 3.6.5 and/or point 3.8.2 of Annex II to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, as amended by Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605, and/or documentary evidence demonstrating that Proquinazid may be used such that exposure is negligible, or the conditions for the application of the derogation under Art.4(7) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 are met.

The additional information submitted by the applicant was subsequently evaluated by the RMS. Furthermore, the applicant also requested a derogation under Article 4(7) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, submitting evidence regarding the necessity of proquinazid as fungicide to control a serious danger to plant health. The evaluation of the data regarding this derogation request is presented in Appendices D and E of this conclusion.

A public consultation on the draft Article 4(7) scientific report and the revised RAR on the ED made available after the 3‐month clock stop was conducted between April and June 2025. All comments received, including the ones from the applicant and the Member States, were collated in the format of a commenting table (on the draft Art 4(7) scientific report) and of a reporting table (on the revised RAR on the assessment of the endocrine‐disrupting properties). As a result of the public consultation, the need for an additional experts' consultation in the area of mammalian toxicology and ecotoxicology was identified.

The outcome of the telephone conference, together with EFSA's further consideration of the comments, is reflected in the conclusions set out in column 4 of the reporting table. All points that were identified as unresolved at the end of the comment evaluation phase and which required further consideration, including those issues to be considered in an expert consultation, were compiled by EFSA in the format of an evaluation table.

The conclusions arising from the consideration by EFSA, and as appropriate by the RMS, of the points identified in the evaluation table, together with the outcome of the expert consultation and the written consultation on the assessment of additional information, where these took place, were reported in the final column of the evaluation table.

A final consultation on the conclusions arising from the peer review of the risk assessment took place with Member States via a written procedure in February–March 2026.

This conclusion report summarises the outcome of the peer review of the risk assessment of the active substance and the formulation for representative uses, evaluated on the basis of the representative uses of proquinazid as a fungicide active on powdery mildew diseases on cereals (winter wheat, spring wheat, winter barley, spring barley, winter rye, oats, triticale) and grapes (wine and table), as proposed by the applicant. In accordance with Article 12(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, risk mitigation options identified in the RAR and considered during the peer review, if any, are presented in the conclusion.

THE ACTIVE SUBSTANCE AND THE FORMULATION FOR REPRESENTATIVE USES

Proquinazid is an ISO common name for 6‐iodo‐2‐propoxy‐3‐propylquinazolin‐4(3H)‐one (IUPAC). The formulated product for the evaluation was ‘Proquinazid 200 g/L EC’, an emulsifiable concentrate (EC) containing 200 g/L pure proquinazid.

The information on the active substance and the formulation for representative uses, including the co‐formulants in these formulations, was considered in the overall assessment during the peer review. None of the co‐formulants is an unacceptable co‐formulant listed in Annex III of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, 5 and one co‐formulant, present in significant amount in the formulation for representative uses, is a non‐approved active substance under Regulation (EC) 1107/2009 6 , 7 (not linked to safety concerns).

Details on the composition of the formulations cannot be reported in conclusions because of the provisions in Article 63(2)(d) of Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, however this information was fully available and evaluated during the peer review. A proposal for classification of the formulation(s) according to Regulation (EC) 1272/2008 was provided by the applicant and assessed by the RMS (please see Volumes 3 CP of the RAR).

The representative uses evaluated were field spray foliar application on cereals (such as winter and spring wheat, winter and spring barley, winter rye oats and triticale) for control of cereals powdery mildew (Blumeria graminis) and on grapes (wine and table) for control of grape powdery mildew (Uncinula necator). Full details of the GAPs can be found in the list of end points in Appendix B.

Data were submitted to conclude that the uses of proquinazid according to the representative uses proposed at EU level result in a sufficient fungicidal efficacy against the target pathogens, following the guidance document SANCO/2012/11251‐rev. 4 (European Commission, 2014b).

CONCLUSIONS OF THE EVALUATION

General aspects

With regard to the mammalian toxicity information available for the formulation for representative uses ‘Proquinazid 200 g/L EC, studies were performed for acute toxicity endpoints. With regard to the co‐formulants contained in ‘A10788A’, sufficient toxicological data were available for all components. The RMS considered that the available toxicological information did sufficiently address genotoxicity, and repeated dose toxicity potential of ‘A10788A’ over the short‐ and long‐term and no concern was identified. 8

The availability of ecotoxicity data with the formulation for representative uses was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Teleconference 134. 9 Based on the available data (see Section 5), it was noted that the formulation for representative uses was not more toxic than the active substance. Furthermore, the experts discussed the data retrieval search and the available data for the individual components. Considering the available data, the experts could not conclude on the ecotoxicological profile of the representative formulation and agreed that further chronic toxicity considerations to certain non‐target organisms (aquatic organisms) might be necessary to complement these assessments (data gap, see Section 10).

IDENTITY, PHYSICAL/CHEMICAL/TECHNICAL PROPERTIES AND METHODS OF ANALYSIS

The following guidance documents were followed in the production of this conclusion: European Commission (2000, 2010a, 2010b, 2019).

The proposed specification for proquinazid is based on batch data from industrial plant production and quality control (QC) data. Based on the data submitted for the renewal procedure it was proposed the minimum purity of 950 g/kg to be maintained, however the maximum level of an impurity to be lowered and an impurity not to be specified. EFSA is of the opinion that based on the data submitted higher minimum purity for the active substance and lower levels for some other impurities could have been set. It should be noted that evaluation of the toxicological relevance of the impurities is open (see Section 2) and as a consequence, new data such as spectral data, content of the impurities before and after the storage of the formulation and methods for analysis of the relevant impurities in the formulation might be required. The batches used in the toxicological assessment are not fully representative of the original and newly proposed reference specification (see Section 2). The available data were insufficient to confirm the compliance of the batches used in the ecotoxicity studies with the current and updated reference specification (see Section 5). There was not FAO specification available for proquinazid.

The main data regarding the identity of proquinazid and its physical and chemical properties are given in Appendix B.

Adequate methods are available for the generation of data required for the risk assessment. A data gap was identified for a summary (description and validation data) of the analytical method used in vapour pressure study (see Section 10). Methods of analysis are available for the determination of the active substance in the technical material and in the representative formulation and for the determination of the respective impurities in the technical material. A data gap for additional validation data for the method for quantification of two impurities in the technical material was identified (see data gap in Section 10). 10

Proquinazid residue can be monitored in food and feed of plant origin by DFG S19 using liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) with limit of quantification (LOQ) of 0.01 mg/kg in all commodity groups. For dry, high‐water, high‐acid content commodities, efficiency of the extraction procedure used was demonstrated. Extraction efficiency was not verified for high‐oil content commodities (not needed, considering the representative uses and lack of metabolism studies in these commodities). Proquinazid and metabolite IN‐MU210 residues in food and feed of animal origin can be determined by LC–MS/MS with LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg for each analyte in all animal matrices. Proquinazid and IN‐MW977 can be monitored in honey by LC–MS/MS with LOQs of 0.01 mg/kg.

Proquinazid residue in soil and water can be monitored by LC–MS/MS with LOQs 1.0 μg/kg and 0.1 μg/L, respectively. Appropriate LC–MS/MS method exists for monitoring of proquinazid residue in air with a LOQ of 0.80 μg/m3.

LC–MS/MS can be used for monitoring of proquinazid and IN‐MU210 in body fluids (plasma and urine) with LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg. Proquinazid and IN‐MU210 residues in body tissues can be determined by using the monitoring methods for residue in food of animal origin.

MAMMALIAN TOXICITY

Proquinazid was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' Teleconference (TC) 131 (4–8 March 2024). The assessment is based on the following guidance documents: European Commission (2003, 2012), EFSA (2014b).

The test material used in toxicity studies is not fully representative of the original and newly proposed reference specification for the active substance and associated impurities. Further data are needed to define the relevance of an impurity and its maximum content 11 (issue not finalised, see Section 1 and 9.1).

The analytical methods used in feed, body fluids and tissues, air and any additional matrices used in support of the key toxicity studies for risk assessment are considered fit‐for‐purpose (see Section 1).

Proquinazid is extensively absorbed (higher than 86%), following oral administration to rats. Metabolism is extensive. No disproportionate or unique human metabolites are identified in vitro (hepatocytes). The residue definition for body fluids and tissues includes proquinazid and IN‐MU210.

Proquinazid is of low acute toxicity to rats following oral, dermal or inhalation exposure; it is not a skin or eye irritant nor a skin sensitiser.

Based on its UV–vis absorption spectra, phototoxicity and photomutagenicity testing is required for proquinazid. Under the conditions of the available studies (OECD TG 432 positive and TG 498, negative with topical application), proquinazid is considered unlikely to be phototoxic. The experts agreed that the requirement to address photomutagenicity was not properly addressed since a rationale for a data waiver was not provided. 12 Based on the current state of the art and additional considerations as discussed by the expert meeting, EFSA concluded that phototoxicity can be considered as a surrogate for photomutagenicity and a data gap is not set.

After short‐term oral exposure to proquinazid, the relevant no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) is 1.89 mg/kg bw per day, based on liver and thyroid toxicity in the combined subchronic and neurotoxicity study in rats. In dogs, a lowest observable adverse effect leve (LOAEL) of 17 mg/kg bw per day based on eye and liver toxicity was identified in the 90‐day study (a NOAEL was not identified).

Based on weight‐of‐evidence, proquinazid is unlikely to be genotoxic, in line with the ECHA Risk Assessment Committee (RAC) opinion (ATP05; ECHA, 2023; see Appendix A).

Long‐term toxicity studies in rats and mice indicate liver and thyroid toxicity. The relevant NOAEL for long‐term toxicity is 1.16 mg/kg bw per day from the 2‐year rat study. The relevant NOAEL for carcinogenicity is 12 mg/kg bw per day based on cholangiocarcinomas and hepatocellular adenomas in female rats; and thyroid follicular cell adenomas in male rats. The ECHA RAC proposed to retain the currently legally in place harmonised classification for proquinazid as carcinogenic category 2 (ATP 05; ECHA, 2023, see Appendix A).

No relevant effects related to fertility or sexual function are observed in the available two‐generation studies in rats. The relevant offspring and reproductive NOAELs are 13.7 mg/kg bw per day based on reduced pup weight and 35 mg/kg bw per day (highest dose level tested), respectively. The parental NOAEL is 1.80 mg/kg bw per day based on thyroid toxicity. Proquinazid is not a developmental toxicant. The maternal NOAELs in rat and rabbit are 30 mg/kg bw per day and 2.5 mg/kg bw per day, respectively, based on reduced food consumption and body weight. The developmental NOAELs in rat and rabbits are 30 mg/kg bw per day and 2.5 mg/kg bw per day, respectively, based on reduced body weight and foetal variations.

The ECHA RAC concluded proquinazid warrants no classification for adverse effects on sexual function and fertility and for development (ATP05; ECHA, 2023; see Appendix A).

Acute neurotoxicity studies indicate decreased motor activity at 100 and 500 mg/kg bw in females and males, respectively, but no structural damage to the nervous system. Repeated dose studies, including the combined subchronic and neurotoxicity study in rats, do not show neurotoxic effects up to 135 mg/kg bw per day and 49.7 mg/kg bw per day in males and females respectively. The requirement for information on developmental neurotoxicity (DNT) was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review TC 131 13 and the experts did not consider a DNT study necessary. While experts agreed that the Uridine 5′‐diphospho‐glucuronosyltransferase(UGT) Mode of action has not been proven to be rodent specific and that observed thyroid effects may be relevant to humans, there was agreement that the young adult rat lacking thyroid binding globulin is likely more sensitive than humans regarding this effect and the intraspecies uncertainty factor of 10 is – in this specific case – expected to cover for the lack of additional investigation in the developing animal.

Immunotoxicity studies do not show impact on immune function.

Health‐based guidance values (HBGVs) were established: the acceptable daily intake (ADI) is 0.012 mg/kg bw per day. This value is based on a NOAEL of 1.16 mg/kg bw per day, identified in a 2‐year rat study, in which histopathological changes in the liver and thyroid along with alterations in thyroid hormone homeostasis were observed at the dose of 12.08 mg/kg bw per day. An uncertainty factor (UF) of 100 was applied. This ADI is the same as in the previous peer review (EFSA, 2009).

The acceptable operator exposure level (AOEL) is 0.02 mg/kg body weight per day. This value is derived from a NOAEL of 1.89 mg/kg bw per day, identified in a combined subchronic and neurotoxicity study in rats, in which morphological changes in the liver and thyroid, alterations in thyroid hormone homeostasis and clinical pathology changes in males were observed at a dose of 6.22 mg/kg bw per day. An uncertainty factor of 100 was applied, with no correction for oral absorption. This AOEL is the same as in the previous peer review (EFSA, 2009).

The acute reference dose (ARfD) is 0.06 mg/kg body weight. This value is based on a LOAEL of 17 mg/kg bw per day, identified in a 90‐day dog study, in which ocular discharge after the initial doses were observed. A standard UF of 100 was applied, along with an additional UF of 3, due to the absence of a NOAEL. This ARfD differs from the value established in the previous peer review (0.2 mg/kg bw; 90‐day dog).

The same basis as the ARfD was applied to derive the acute AOEL (AAOEL), with no correction for oral absorption. An AAOEL was not established during the previous peer review.

Proquinazid meets the criteria for endocrine disruption for T‐modality for humans (Scenario 1b) (see Section 6). The lowest NOAEL for the endocrine disruption endpoint is 1.89 mg/kg bw per day (see Section 6). Consequently, the established HBGVs are considered covering the endocrine effects.

Dermal absorption rates of proquinazid in the formulation for representative uses are 1% for concentrate and 6.5% for a formulation diluted to 0.033 g/L, and 21% (after prorata correction) for a formulation diluted to 0.01 g/L.

Non‐dietary exposure estimates, calculated with the EFSA (EFSA, 2014b), showed that operator, worker, resident and bystander exposure to proquinazid was below (A)AOEL values (see Appendix B) considering risk mitigation measures in some scenarios (See Section 8) or using refined input parameters (i.e. measured DFR of 0.53 μg/cm2/kg a.s per hectare in worker hand harvesting grapes).

Toxicological studies and information have been provided for metabolites sulfate conjugate var. 1, Sulfate conjugate var. 2, IN‐MW977 (including its major and minor isomers and their respective glucosides), A1, A4, B3, B4, CRC‐Soy‐01, IN‐MM671, IN‐MU210, IN‐MY340, IN‐MY341, IN‐MY788, IN‐MM986, IN‐MM991, IN‐MU715, IN‐MW397, IN‐MW398, IN‐NA250, IN‐NA251, IN‐NA252, A2 (see Appendix B).

There are no metabolites found in groundwater above 0.1 μg/L (see Section 4 and Table 3).

RESIDUES

Representative use residues

The assessment in the residue section is based on the following guidance documents: OECD (2009, 2011), European Commission (2011) and JMPR (2004, 2007).

Proquinazid was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' TC 133 (12–14 March 2024).

Metabolism studies for the crop categories cereal/grass crops (wheat) and fruit (apple and grape) were available to cover the GAPs of the representative uses. However, major shortcomings were observed, i.e. the fate of proquinazid was not fully elucidated as the studies were performed with only the phenyl ring labelled. Together with the low identification rate it remains unclear whether cleavage can take place and metabolites bearing the pyrimidinyl ring could be expected. Furthermore, the metabolite IN‐MW977, which was quantified in residue field trials with grapes, was not used as standard in the metabolism studies with fruits. The identification in fruit leaves was low despite high total residues and therefore the studies cannot be used to conclude on the metabolic pattern in grape leaves for which a MRL application is reported to be submitted. 14 A new metabolism study with grape with proquinazid labelled on the pyrimidine ring of the molecule, attempting an identification rate as described in OECD TG 501 (OECD, 2007) and also addressing the metabolism in grape leaves, is requested to confidently conclude on the metabolism of proquinazid in fruit and cereal (data gap leading to an issue not finalised, see Section 9.1). If cleavage of the molecule is observed in this requested study, another metabolism study with the pyrimidine label, performed with a cereal crop, should also be conducted. Based on the considerations above and in the absence of data on the general toxicological profile of IN‐MW977 (see Sections 2 and 9.1), the residue definition for risk assessment in plants is set provisionally as proquinazid and IN‐MW977 (for fruits and cereals including cereal straw). Proquinazid was recovered in all plant parts and is a good marker. Therefore, the residue definition for enforcement is proposed as proquinazid (for fruits and cereals).

A sufficient number of residue field trials is available to cover the representative uses on cereals and grapes analysing for proquinazid and its metabolite IN‐MW977. These trials are considered conditionally valid until the risk assessment residue definition for plants will be confirmed by additional metabolism study(ies) and toxicological data for IN‐MW977 become available (see data gaps above).

The persistence in soil of proquinazid and its soil metabolites IN‐MM671, IN‐MM986 and IN‐MM991 (see Section 4) triggered the investigation of residues also in rotational crops. Two confined rotational crop metabolisms studies were presented. The first study did not allow to fully identify the residues and therefore a second study was submitted to also address the confirmatory data requested in the context of the review of the existing maximum residue levels for proquinazid according to Article 12 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 (EFSA, 2020) (see also Section 3.2). Out of the three persistent soil metabolites, only IN‐MM671 and IN‐MM991 were found in low concentrations in soil and some crops, whereas the other soil metabolite IN‐MM986 was not detected. A specific major metabolite, CRC‐Soy‐1, was only found in the rotated soyabean, both in straw and seed. Information on their toxicity (genotoxicity and general toxicity) is not or only partially available (data gap, see Section 2). As both studies were underdosed with respect to the PECplateau for proquinazid, IN‐MM671 and IN‐MM986 and because of the different findings observed among them, a confident decision on the metabolic pattern cannot be established. Therefore, a new confined rotational metabolism study, with proquinazid labelled in the phenyl and pyrimidine rings, investigating parent proquinazid and all relevant metabolites and covering the PECplateau calculated for these compounds is requested (data gap leading to an issue not finalised, see Section 9.1).

Proquinazid is stable under household processing conditions. Processing factors were derived (see Appendix B).

The outcome of the dietary burden calculation for the sum of proquinazid and IN‐MW977 indicates that for all species except swine animal studies are triggered by cereals, the only representative use which results in feed items. The calculation was carried out by using the provisional residue definition, i.e. expressed as proquinazid, and that the potential contribution of residues from rotated crops is not considered in this calculation. The presented metabolism studies with proquinazid in poultry and ruminant were found to be valid. Proquinazid was extensively metabolised in ruminants but it was the major residue in poultry tissues. IN‐MU210 and IN‐MW977 were reported in all ruminant matrices but not found in poultry. For the plant metabolite IN‐MW977, it remains unclear whether it is not formed in poultry or could not be detected because no analytical standard was used. An indicative dietary burden calculation for this metabolite alone exceeded the trigger also for poultry. 15 Pending the finalisation of the residue definition for risk assessment in primary and rotational crops (see data gaps above), a follow‐up investigation on the behaviour of this metabolite in poultry might become necessary.

A feeding study with dairy cows and analysing for proquinazid, IN‐MU210 and IN‐MW977 was submitted. Proquinazid was recovered only at the highest dose level in liver and fat and IN‐MW977 was reported only in liver at the highest dose level. The residue of IN‐MU210 ranged from 0.011 mg/kg in muscle to 0.53 mg/kg in kidney.

Based on the results presented above from the animal studies, the existing residue definition for enforcement was confirmed as sum of proquinazid and metabolite 3‐[(6‐iodo‐4‐oxo‐3‐propyl‐3,4‐dihydroquinazolin‐2‐yl)oxy]propanoic acid (IN‐MU210), expressed as proquinazid; and the residue definition for risk assessment in livestock as sum of proquinazid, IN‐MU210 and IN‐MW977, expressed as proquinazid. The latter is provisional, pending the toxicity data for the metabolites IN‐MU210 and IN‐MW977 (data gap, see Section 2). Dietary burden calculations in fish indicated that the maximum dietary burden for fish species will be below the threshold of 0.1 mg/kg feed and thus not triggering feeding studies with fish.

Residues of both, proquinazid and IN‐MW977, in honey were below the LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg.

Chronic and acute consumer dietary intake assessment was performed using the revision 3.1 of the EFSA pesticide residue intake model (PRIMo). The consumer exposure corresponds to 8% of the ADI and to 20% (for table grapes, FI child) with the newly derived ARfD (see section 2). However, pending several data gaps, the consumer risk assessment remains provisional (issue not finalised, see Section 9.1). When considering all uses evaluated in the Article 12 MRL review of proquinazid (EFSA, 2020), the consumer exposure corresponds to 29% of the ADI and to 54% of the ARfD (for strawberries, NL child).

The consumer risk assessment from the consumption of drinking water is not finalised considering the lack of appropriate information to address the effect of water treatment processes on the nature of residues, potentially present in surface water, when surface water is abstracted for the production of drinking water (see Sections 4 and 9.1).

Proquinazid meets the criteria for endocrine disruption for T‐modality for humans (see Section 6). An assessment of negligible dietary exposure was requested but not provided. Therefore, it could not be demonstrated that one or more of the representative uses could satisfy the requirements regarding negligible dietary exposure as set out in point 3.6.5 of Annex II to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009.

Maximum residue levels and confirmatory data MRL review

Data requirements identified for the MRL requests (residue field trials with barley and oats, pumpkins, grass) were partly addressed, and the additional information was assessed (see data requirement 3.9 and Experts' consultation 3.4 in the evaluation table part 3 residue (EFSA, 2026)).

In the Article 12 MRL review assessment of proquinazid (EFSA, 2020), 8 residue trials on barley and oats in the SEU zone were requested as confirmatory data. A study with 5 GAP compliant and valid residue trials in the SEU zone with barley were submitted and partly addressed the data gap set in the Article 12 MRL review.

ENVIRONMENTAL FATE AND BEHAVIOUR

Proquinazid was discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Meeting Experts' TC 143 (6–7 March 2024).

The rates of dissipation and degradation in the environmental matrices investigated were estimated using FOCUS (2006) kinetics guidance. In soil laboratory incubations under aerobic conditions in the dark, proquinazid exhibited moderate to high persistence, forming the major (> 10% applied radioactivity (AR)) metabolite IN‐MM671 (max. 65.3% AR) which exhibited high persistence, metabolite IN‐MM986 (max. 8.1% AR), which exhibited moderate to very high persistence, and metabolite IN‐MM991 (max. 6.7% AR), exhibiting moderate to high persistence. Mineralisation of the phenyl 14C radiolabel to carbon dioxide accounted for 2.4%–9.5% AR after 120 days. The formation of unextractable residues (not extracted by acetonitrile/water) for this radiolabel accounted for 6.4%–12.5% AR after 120 days. In anaerobic soil incubations, proquinazid degraded faster than under aerobic conditions with the route of degradation being the same as in the aerobic investigations. In soil photolysis conditions, proquinazid degradation was faster than in the dark control, but no novel metabolites were formed. Proquinazid exhibited immobility in soil. Metabolite IN‐MM671 exhibited low to slight soil mobility, metabolite IN‐MM986 exhibited low soil mobility to immobility and metabolite IN‐MM991 exhibited high to low soil mobility. It was concluded that the adsorption of proquinazid and its soil metabolites was not pH dependent. In satisfactory field dissipation studies carried out at 8 sites from UK, France, Italy and Germany (spray application to the soil surface on bare soil plots), proquinazid exhibited low to medium persistence. Sample analyses were carried out for the parent proquinazid and metabolites IN‐MM671, IN‐MM986 and IN‐MM991. Field study DegT50 values were derived following normalisation to FOCUS reference conditions (20°C and pF2 soil moisture) following the EFSA (2014a) DegT50 guidance.

In laboratory incubations in dark aerobic natural sediment water systems, proquinazid exhibited moderate to high persistence, forming the major metabolite IN‐MM671 (max. 7% AR in water and max. 32% in sediment, exhibiting high persistence). The unextractable sediment fraction (not extracted by acetonitrile/water) was the major sink for the phenyl 14C radiolabel, accounting for 7% AR at study end (100 days). Mineralisation of this radiolabel accounted for 0.2%–1.4% AR at the end of the study. In a laboratory sterile aqueous photolysis experiment, the rate of decline of proquinazid was higher compared to the observed rate in the aerobic sediment water incubations. Chromatographically resolved components accounting for > 10% AR were metabolites IN‐MT884 (max. 30.5% AR), IN‐MM671 (max. 19.5% AR), metabolite IN‐MM986 (max. 14.5% AR) and metabolite IN‐MM991 (max. 14.2% AR). The necessary surface water and sediment exposure assessments (predicted environmental concentrations (PEC) calculations) were carried out for proquinazid and metabolites IN‐MT884, IN‐MM671, IN‐MM986 and IN‐MM991 using the FOCUS (FOCUS, 2001) step 1 and step 2 approach (version 3.2 of the Steps 1–2 in FOCUS calculator). For the active substance proquinazid and metabolites IN‐MT884, IN‐MM671 IN‐MM986 IN‐MM991, appropriate step 3 (FOCUS, 2001) and step 4 calculations were available. 16 The step 4 calculations appropriately followed the FOCUS (FOCUS, 2007) guidance and applying appropriate mitigation measures, with no‐spray drift buffer zones of up to 20 m being implemented for the drainage scenarios (representing a 60%–93% spray drift reduction) and combined no‐spray buffer zones with vegetative buffer strips of up to 20 m (reducing solute flux in run‐off by 80% and erosion run‐off of mass adsorbed to soil by 95%) being implemented for the run‐off scenarios. The SWAN tool (version 5.0.1) was appropriately used to implement these mitigation measures in the simulations. However, risk managers and others may wish to note that whilst run‐off mitigation is included in the step 4 calculations available, the FOCUS (FOCUS, 2007) report acknowledges that for substances with K Foc < 2000 mL/g (i.e. IN‐MM671 and IN‐MM991), the general applicability and effectiveness of run‐off mitigation measures had been less clearly demonstrated in the available scientific literature, than for more strongly adsorbed compounds.

The necessary groundwater exposure assessments were appropriately carried out using FOCUS (European Commission, 2014a) scenarios and the models PEARL 4.4.4, PELMO 5.5.3 and MACRO 5.5.4.16 The potential for groundwater exposure from the representative uses by proquinazid and its soil metabolites IN‐MM671, IN‐MM986 and IN‐MM991 above the parametric drinking water limit of 0.1 μg/L was concluded to be low in geoclimatic situations that are represented by 9 out of 9 FOCUS groundwater scenarios.

The applicant did not provide appropriate information to address the effect of water treatments processes on the nature of the residues that might be present in surface water, when surface water is abstracted for drinking water. This has led to the identification of a data gap and results in the consumer risk assessment not being finalised (see Sections 3 and 9.1).

The PEC in soil, surface water, sediment and groundwater covering the representative uses assessed can be found in Appendix B of this conclusion. A key to the wording used to describe the persistence and mobility of the compounds assessed can be found in Appendix C of this conclusion.

ECOTOXICOLOGY

The risk assessment was based on the following documents: European Commission (2002), SETAC (2001), EFSA (2009, 2013) and EFSA PPR Panel (2013) and EFSA (2013).

Proquinazid was discussed at the Pesticide Peer Review Experts' TC 134 (11–14 March 2024) and 146 (4–12 July 2024).

The available data were insufficient to confirm the compliance of the batches used in the ecotoxicity studies with the current and updated reference specification.

Acute and long‐term toxicity studies with birds and mammals were available for the active substance. In addition, acute toxicity studies with the formulation for representative uses ‘proquinazid 200 g/L EC’ were also available for both taxonomic groups. Long‐term endpoints for birds and mammals were agreed during the experts' meeting. 17 An acute toxicity study with rats was also available for the metabolite IN‐MM671.

Based on the available data and the screening‐level risk assessment, a low acute risk to birds and mammals was indicated for all representative uses. A low long‐term risk to birds was indicated at the screening step for the use in grapes at 50 g a.s./ha, whereas a low risk was concluded at Tier 1 for the uses in cereals and in grapes at 75 g a.s./ha. A high long‐term risk to small herbivorous mammals was identified at Tier 1 for all representative uses: in cereals at BBCH ≥ 40 and in grapes at BBCH 10–39. Several refinement options for the long‐term risk to mammals were discussed at the experts meeting. 18 A refinement of exposure based on crop interception was agreed. In addition, the use of a mixed diet consisting of monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants for small herbivorous mammals was considered scientifically justified to refine the dietary composition. Based on the refined risk assessment, a low long‐term risk to mammals was concluded for representative uses in cereals, whereas the high long‐term risk for uses in grapes remained. A low acute risk to birds and mammals could also be concluded for the plant metabolite IN‐MW977 considering the high margins of safety relative to the parent compound. The long‐term risk for IN‐MW977 could not be finalised (data gap; see Section 10). For all representative uses, a low risk to birds and mammals from exposure via consumption of contaminated water was concluded. A low risk from exposure via secondary poisoning for proquinazid and the metabolites IN‐MM671 and IN‐MM986 19 was also indicated.

To evaluate the risk for aquatic organisms, toxicity data for fish and aquatic invertebrates (acute and chronic), sediment dwelling organisms and algae were available for proquinazid. In addition, acute studies with fish and aquatic invertebrates and with algae were available with the formulation for representative uses ‘Proquinazid 200 g/L EC’. No chronic data for fish and aquatic invertebrates were available with ‘Proquinazid 200 g/L EC’ (see data gap under General aspects). ENDOCRINE DISRUPTION PROPERTIES

The endocrine disruption properties (ED) of proquinazid for human and non‐target organisms were discussed at the Pesticides Peer Review Experts' TC 131 (4–8 March 2024). 25

With regard to the assessment of the endocrine disruption (ED) potential of proquinazid for humans according to the ECHA and EFSA guidance (2018), in determining whether proquinazid interacts with the oestrogen, androgen and steroidogenesis‐ (EAS) and thyroid‐ (T) mediated pathways, the number and type of effects induced; and the magnitude and pattern of responses observed across studies were considered. Additionally, the conditions under which effects occur were considered, in particular, whether or not endocrine‐related responses occurred at dose(s) that also resulted in overt toxicity. The assessment is therefore providing a weight‐of‐evidence analysis of the potential interaction of proquinazid with the EAS and T signalling pathways using the available evidence in the dataset.

The T‐modality has been sufficiently investigated and a pattern of T‐mediated adversity i.e. changes in thyroid weight and thyroid histopathology (including increased incidence of follicular cell hypertrophy/hyperplasia and adenomas), and T‐mediated endocrine activity (changes in THs and TSH) has been observed in studies of varying duration and conducted with different species (i.e. rat and mouse). The postulated mode of action (MoA) indicated CAR/PXR induction as a plausible molecular initiating event (MIE) (not experimentally proven) and Phase I/II enzymes induction as plausible downstream intermediate key events (KEs), supported by in vitro (human and rats) and in vivo (rats) evidence. An in vitro comparative liver enzyme induction study, using human and rat hepatocytes, was submitted to explore species‐specific induction of phase II enzymes (namely T4‐UGT). However, due to the number of uncertainties regarding the reliability and relevance of the method used, the available information does not allow the dismissal of human relevance of the MoA. In line with the criteria established in the ECHA/EFSA guidance on ED, ‘relevance to humans should be assumed by default in the absence of appropriate scientific data demonstrating non‐relevance’.

In addition, although the hepatic mediated MoA is plausible, it was concluded that a concomitant MoA dealing with hepatic deiodinase (DIO1) inhibition observed in rats could not be excluded. Proquinazid was shown not to inhibit NIS or TPO activity, and not to act as a TR agonist in vitro.

Upon request for further information in accordance with Article 13(3a) of Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 844/2012, as amended by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659, related to the implementation of the scientific criteria for the determination of endocrine‐disrupting properties introduced by Regulation (EU) No 2018/605, 26 no additional data were submitted by the applicant to support the ED assessment. The applicant did submit some information after the legal deadline established in the Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 2018/1659; consequently, this information could not be considered within the peer review process. Nevertheless, for completeness, EFSA reviewed the late submitted information and concluded that it would not impact the conclusion reached by the experts during TC 131.

Overall, based on the available and sufficient data set and on the MoA analysis, it was concluded that the ED criteria for the T‐modality are met for proquinazid (Scenario 1b of the ECHA and EFSA (2018)).

In the studies conducted with proquinazid, the LOAEL where T‐mediated adversity (i.e. increased incidence of thyroid follicular cell hypertrophy) and endocrine activity (i.e. changes in THs and TSH) were observed in male rats, is 6.22 mg/kg bw per day from an oral 90‐day combined subchronic oral and neurotoxicity study. A NOAEL of 1.89 mg/kg bw per day is derived based on these effects (see Section 2). This NOAEL is supported from the 2‐year combined chronic toxicity and carcinogenicity study in rats in which the NOAELs of 1.16 and 1.39 mg/kg bw per day, for males and females respectively, were established based on thyroid altered colloid and follicular cell hypertrophy at the LOAEL 12.08 and 15.66 mg/kg bw per day, for males and females respectively (see Section 2).

With regard to the EAS‐modalities, the dataset was considered complete, and a pattern of EAS‐mediated adversity was not observed. Therefore, based on the available and sufficient dataset, it was concluded that the ED criteria are not met for the EAS‐modalities (Scenario 1a of the ECHA/EFSA (2018)).

For the EAS‐modalities, the outcome of the assessment reported above for humans also applies to wild mammals as non‐target organisms. 27 For the T‐modality, the outcome of the assessment for humans (ED criteria met) does not apply to wild mammals as non‐target organisms since the relevance of the T‐mediated adversity observed in mammals could not be confirmed at population level.

For non‐mammalian species, an amphibian metamorphosis assay (AMA, OECD TG 231) and a Fish short‐term reproduction assay (FSTRA, OECD TG 229) were available. Although some effects suggested a possible degree of adversity (e.g. accelerated amphibian development and impairment on fish reproduction), a clear pattern of EATS‐mediated endocrine activity/adversity could not be identified.

As consequence of the above‐assessment for non‐target organisms, it is concluded that proquinazid does not meet the ED criteria for EATS‐modalities as laid down in point 3.8.2 of Annex II to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, as amended by Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/605.

In view of the fact that ED is an approval criterion, the applicant should have provided further information aimed at demonstrating that the exposure of humans to proquinazid was negligible under realistic conditions of use.
