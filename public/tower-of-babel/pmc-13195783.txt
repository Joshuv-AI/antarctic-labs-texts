Mitigating climate change in sports leagues: A scenario-based analysis of travel distances in women’s and men’s amateur soccer in Germany
Authors: Karim Abu-Omar, Tobias Völk, Antonina Tcymbal, Julian Resch, Dogukan Özer, Peter Gelius, Sven Messing, Heiko Ziemainz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The environmental impact of motorized travel to away games in amateur sport is an understudied research topic.•Analysis of 384 teams over one full season revealed more than 93 tons of GHG emissions from travel to away games.•The study demonstrated that optimizing game schedules and formats could result in GHG savings of up to 33 tons.•Optimizing league play in amateur sport has great potential to reduce GHG.Keywords: Sport, Planetary health, Carbon emissions, Football, Soccer

Introduction

Providing people with opportunities to practice sport and increasing participation rates are seen as the cornerstones of many physical activity promotion strategies.1 This view rests on the assumption that engaging in grassroots sport comes with many individual-level health benefits that outweigh the risk of injury that can occur when engaging in sport. While such an individualistic perspective on health is very common in the field of sport, it has recently been challenged by more holistic views such as One Health2 and Planetary Health.3,4 Planetary Health is defined, for example, as “the health of human civilization and the state of the natural systems on which it depends”.4 Adopting such a broad understanding of health raises important questions about the planetary health benefits of sport participation, particularly when considering the greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions that accompany it.5,6 Climate change and the deterioration of the natural environment have amplified these questions. As a consequence, the United Nations has urged sport organizations to “take responsibility for their climate footprint” and to “take meaningful and transformative climate action” in order to become “climate leaders”.7

Such calls on sport organizations to become more sustainable are based on increasing evidence of the negative environmental impact of sport. These impacts include the building and maintenance of sport infrastructure, habitat destruction in outdoor sports, and travel emissions caused by athletes and fans.8,9 While some studies have calculated the carbon emissions of mega sporting events, such as the Summer Olympics or the Fédération Internationale de Football Association (FIFA) Football World Cup, and estimated GHG emissions of millions of tons,6,10, 11, 12 there are only a few studies that have investigated the negative effects of grassroots sport on the environment.

One source of GHG emissions from sporting events is the travel emissions of athletes. While it is obvious that professional athletes who travel by plane cause large amounts of GHG emissions,13,14 the GHG emissions in grassroots sport have rarely been investigated. One of the few studies on this topic showed that the travel-related emissions of sport participants varied largely across different types of sports: at the upper end of the scale, a person emits an average of 2.2 tons of CO2 per year playing recreational golf and 2.1 tons of CO2 when surfing; recreational sports with lower estimated CO2 emissions were tennis and gym-based exercise (both 0.2 tons of CO2 per year).5 These findings are supported by other studies. Grofelnik et al.15 have estimated travel-related CO2 as being close to 95 kg per person for participating in a single ultra-trail run. Similarly, Dolf and Teehan16 have reported significant GHG emissions from travel by athletes and spectators to collegiate league play in Canada. In general, these estimates are much lower than those for professional athletes (who have high relative emissions, which is also relevant for thinking about potential measures for cutting GHG emissions). Nevertheless, it needs to be considered that the number of people participating in grassroots sports is much higher. Consequently, the absolute emissions of grassroots sports are also highly relevant in this context.

This study aimed to calculate travel distances for a full season of amateur soccer for 35 leagues in 3 regions of Germany, and to model scenarios for minimizing travel distances by optimizing group allocation and changing game formats. All calculations and scenarios are based on the travel distances in these 35 amateur soccer leagues during the 2022/23 season and include both urban and rural areas. As the methodology is transferable to other types of sport and countries, the results of this study may inform future initiatives to mitigate climate change in the sport sector.

Methods

Amateur soccer in Germany

According to the German Olympic Sport Federation, soccer is the most popular sport among men and the second most popular sport among women in Germany.17 About 24,000 soccer clubs take part in league play at the professional and amateur levels. Play is organized following the European model of sport.18 Teams can get relegated or promoted to a lower or higher league at the end of the season depending on their final standing in the league table. Teams compete in a double-round-robin format, meeting each opponent twice per season (home and away).

In describing league play in German soccer, we use the term “league” to indicate the different levels of play (e.g., 1. Bundesliga, 2. Bundesliga) and “group” to indicate the grouping of teams who play on the same level (e.g., Group A, Group B). It is evident that there is an absence of consensus on the nomenclature of these groups on the international stage. The designation of these groups as “divisions” (for instance, the American League East Division of Major League Baseball in the USA) or “conferences” (such as the Western Conference of the National Basketball Association in the USA) varies depending on the specific sporting discipline.

Soccer in Germany is organized in up to 12 different levels of play, ranging from the highest professional level (1. Bundesliga) to the lowest amateur level (Kreisliga C/D). Since there are thousands of teams on the lower amateur levels, teams on the same level are organized into groups. For example (and as part of our analysis), in the state of Berlin, 79 men’s teams play on the lowest level (Kreisliga C). These teams are sorted into 6 groups of 12 to 15 teams. During a regular season of gameplay, each team plays double-round-robin against each other team in the same group.

The manner in which teams are sorted into groups varies between regions and leagues. Federations commonly use a highly decentralized system to organize teams in the lower leagues into groups. Within this system, the grouping of teams and the creation of a game schedule for the season will entirely fall upon volunteers. Most often, there will be no monitoring of how groups are formed by the federation.

As no central database on leagues, groups, and game schedules for amateur soccer in Germany exists, data for the 2022/23 season were extracted manually from official websites of the 3 regional soccer federations (Berliner Fußball-Verband e.V.; Württembergischer Fußballverband e.V.; Landesverband Mecklenburg-Vorpommern e.V.). The websites contained information on which teams played in the selected leagues, their group allocation, the complete schedule for each group for the 2022/23 season, and the addresses of the teams’ home ground premises. The addresses were converted into geographical coordinates (latitude and longitude) using the Google Maps Platform (Geocoding API; Google Maps Platform, Dublin, Ireland) and entered into a database. The coordinates were then displayed on a map using Mapbox GL Javascript (Mapbox GL JS v3.12.0; Mapbox, Washington D.C., USA) and manually checked for plausibility. The shortest driving distances and round-trip travel times were computed using Geoapify (Geocoding API and Routing API; KEPTAGO LTD, Paphos, Cyprus).

Scenarios for optimization of league play to reduce travel distances

Two scenarios were inductively developed and tested to model the effects of a different league format on travel distances.

Scenario A (optimized group allocation)

The objective of this scenario was to optimize the allocation of teams to groups, with the aim of minimizing the aggregate travel distances for all teams. The game format does not change in this scenario. Teams are organized into groups based on geographical region and each team plays 2 games against each opponent (1 home and 1 away). The number of teams in a group remains within the range of the original solution, thus ensuring that each team plays the same number of games as before. In optimization science, this scenario is known as the max-k-cut problem with restrictions on the size of the groups.20 A near-optimal allocation of teams to groups was computed with an algorithm developed for this study, as the Gurobi Optimizer software (Gurobi 10.0.0; Gurobi Optimization, Beaverton, OR, USA) was unable to obtain good solutions for large instances within a reasonable timeframe.

Scenario B (optimized game format)

The objective of this scenario was to optimize the game format by placing all teams in a single group, aiming to further minimize the aggregate travel distances for all teams. The scheduling format of this scenario is unbalanced. This means each team plays opponents located close to its home premises but does not play all teams in the group. Teams play as many games as in the original format. If a team plays an opponent, they do so twice (1 home and 1 away). An optimized league schedule was computed using the Gurobi Optimizer software by formulating Scenario B as a mixed integer program.

Results

Travel distances in the 2022/23 season of league play

In total, 35 groups in 6 leagues in Berlin (Leagues 1–2), Baden-Württemberg (Leagues 3–4), and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern (Leagues 5–6) were analyzed. The 384 teams included in the analysis had to travel 156,557.57 km to away games in the 2022/23 season, an average of 407.7 km per team (Table 1). Average travel distances per away game ranged from 6.13 km to 74.02 km. Teams in League 5 had the highest average travel distance with 626.99 km for the full season, teams in League 1 had the lowest with 194.46 km. Estimate of GHG emissions caused by team travel

Taking the original game schedule for the 2022/23 season across all leagues, each petrol car traveling the total distance of 156,557.57 km (Table 1) would cause GHG emissions of approximately 26.6 tons. Estimating that teams use on average 4, 5, or 6 cars to travel to away games, GHG emissions for the 2022/23 season of game play were calculated as 106.5 tons (4 cars), 133.1 tons (5 cars), or 159.7 tons (6 cars), respectively.

Scenario A would result in traveling a total distance of 117,428 km (a reduction of 25%) or emitting 79.9–119.8 tons of GHG (depending on the number of cars taken), and for Scenario B, 101,333 km (a reduction of 35%) or 68.9–103.4 tons of GHG.

Discussion

Summary of main results

Our results indicate that certain types of grassroots sport—in our study, soccer in the lowest leagues—cause substantial travel for teams. The 384 teams in our analysis had to travel 156,557.57 km round-trip to 4084 league games in the 2022/23 season. Teams located in less densely populated areas had to travel further than teams in more densely populated areas, on average.

Using an algorithm from optimization science, modeling shows that team travel can be reduced by optimizing group allocation (Scenario A) or by optimizing the game format (Scenario B). In both scenarios, the geographical location of teams influences to which group they are assigned or which opponents they play. This reduces total travel distances by 39,129 km in Scenario A and by 55,221 km in Scenario B.

GHG emissions and the environmental impact of grassroots sport

These results add to the growing body of literature on the GHG emissions of travel in grassroots sport. Globally, travel accounts for one-fifth of total GHG emissions, and for Europe, GHG emissions from the transport sector have increased by more than 33% from 1990 to 2019.22 There has also been a 5.9% increase in the emissions caused by passenger cars from 2000 to 2020.23

The 79.8–133.1 metric tons of GHG that were calculated for the 2022/23 season do not include travel of coaches and players to meeting points, to practices, and to home games nor do these numbers include travel by referees to games or travel of spectators (although there may be very few spectators attending matches on this level of play). They also do not account for other environmental costs associated with driving cars (e.g., tire abrasion resulting in micro-plastics). Likewise, other environmental costs associated with playing grassroots soccer, such as the use of fertilizers and pesticides on soccer pitches, irrigation of pitches, heating or cooling of changing rooms, heating water for showers and the production process of players’ jerseys, have not been calculated. Overall, this raises questions about the total negative impact of grassroots soccer on the environment. Also, if such an impact could be calculated, how would it be weighed against the individual-level health benefits of engaging in grassroots sport?

Can the external costs associated with 106.4–159.7 tons of GHG emissions be calculated using the results from this study? Estimates from the European Commission24 indicate that the negative environmental effects of 1 ton of CO2 result in a total cost of 160 Euros. Using this estimate, 1 season of league play would result in an environmental cost of 17,024–25,552 Euros. Additionally, based on the modeling of Bressler,25 adding 106.4–159.7 metric tons of CO2 to the atmosphere will cause 0.024–0.036 excess deaths globally between 2020–2100 (4434 metric tons causes one excess death). However, a much more detailed analysis would need to be conducted to compare the potential of “excess death”, along with the risk of injury in playing football, to its many health benefits.

From a more general perspective of public health ethics, the excess deaths due to the carbon emissions from travel to grassroots sport league games would, in all likelihood, be acceptable. The potential burdens of grassroots sport leagues would need to be recognized and attempts made to minimize them, but if the benefits and burdens were fairly balanced, league play could continue (if it were a public health program).26 It could also be argued that any attempts to limit grassroots sport league play would infringe on the human rights of participants,27 and that participation in league play is linked to the (human) right to be physically active.28 However, even from this perspective, questions regarding the environmental burdens of league play and whether benefits and burdens are shared equally among the population would need to be addressed.26

Taking an ethical perspective that aligns closer with concepts of planetary health, an even more complex picture emerges. Within the ethical concept of climate justice, it would need to be recognized that the health benefits of engaging in grassroots league play are reaped in the global north (in our example in Germany), while the negative effects of the emitted GHG emissions are felt most in the global south among vulnerable populations who are not responsible for releasing them.29 It would need to be considered whether what Shue30 calls “luxury emissions” caused by teams driving to other cities when local pick-up games could be organized that provide the same individual health benefits are acceptable at all on ethical grounds. According to this argumentation, luxury emissions should be cut first so that “subsistence emissions”, those on which the livelihoods of people depend, can continue. Thus, when planetary health is taken as the point of reference, difficult questions about the negative environmental impacts of grassroots sport emerge.

Implications of results for the optimization of amateur league play

Results show that for most regions, there is potential to reduce travel distances by changing how teams are organized in leagues and groups. In particular, for the region of Berlin, the estimated reduction in travel distances is substantial. Employing Scenario A (optimized group allocation) across leagues has the potential to minimize travel distances and therefore GHG emissions. At the same time, not much would change for the teams, groups, and federations organizing league play, apart from the composition of groups. An adoption of Scenario B (optimized game format, all teams are placed in one league) brings more substantial change to grassroots league play. In this format, it could be argued that the principle of fairness in sport is disregarded as a team does not face every other team in the group in a round-robin format. Theoretically, the format could result in a team winning the league by being “lucky” to play against a weaker set of teams compared to some of the runners-up. It is therefore more questionable whether Scenario B is suitable for teams and federations, although the analysis indicates that it might reduce travel distances even more than Scenario A. Our results are in line with those of a study from Devriesere and Goossens,31 who have shown for youth field-hockey in Belgium that travel distances can be reduced by 25% when alternative game formats (in this case an incomplete round-robin) are chosen. Such pronounced reductions in travel emissions through the employment of alternative game formats or geographical optimization show great potential for the reduction of GHG emissions in the sport sector. Federations should prioritize working toward the implementation of such measures to reduce their carbon footprint.

In general, the software used for the analysis does not optimize for fairness (i.e., it might make some teams travel considerably further in order to reduce travel distances for many other teams). However, the optimization could be modified to incorporate fairness aspects. Such issues are inherent in optimization science, and there is a body of literature that deals with these problems.27 It is also commonly understood that some teams might want to play (or not play) against certain other teams for other reasons, including tradition (at least in Germany). This has not been considered for this optimization either but could easily be included in future modeling studies. In fact, such restrictions might facilitate the optimization computationally since they reduce the number of possible solutions.

Limitations

The analysis was conducted based on publicly available information on game-schedules and limited data on team travel. Potentially, some teams might not play at the address given on their websites but at other locations. However, it is reasonable to assume that this would not change the results significantly. GHG emissions were calculated rather crudely as no information was available on the number of cars used by teams to travel to away games. The estimate of 4–6 cars per team utilized for the analysis seems to be at the more conservative end, and anecdotal evidence suggests that teams might not car-pool at all for some nearby away-games, potentially increasing the number of cars used by the factor 2.

Our analysis was only based on play in 3 regions of Germany. In order to convince federations, more regions would need to be analyzed to assess the full potential of our approach in reducing travel emissions.

At this stage, the specific optimization scenarios we developed serve merely as a proof of concept, and it is not clear whether soccer federations would be willing to implement them in practice. A high-priority next step would be to engage with federations to ascertain the opinion of officials, club representatives, players (including parents of children playing in junior leagues), coaches, and fans on this topic. Also, a number of other scenarios could be explored to assess their impact on travel distances. For example, a scenario where teams play in geographically allocated groups but skip games with the opponents who are furthest away might reduce travel distances even more; this would most likely be perceived as a less radical departure from the double-round-robin format compared to Scenario B (where all teams are placed in one league).

Integrating planetary health into sport science research

Graeber32 coined the phrase “dead zones of the imagination”, meaning spaces where people stop imagining alternatives to the systems they are in. Such dead zones are created by bureaucracies and power structures but also by traditions. Attempting to integrate planetary health into sport science research has the potential to create such a dead zone of imagination. This seems to be partly due to tradition—the tradition to use human health as a point of departure for all research and modeling, which may be limiting our understanding of the negative environmental consequences of practicing sport. However, it may also be due to the “inconveniences” of sport science research being confronted with potential evidence that many sport activities, depending on how and where they are practiced, might simultaneously cause health benefits (for the individual practicing a particular sport) and harm (for the individual suffering from global warming). Ultimately, routinely employing models such as Planetary Health or One Health in sport science research is of high importance as we seek to advance our understanding of how to balance the effects of sport on individual and planetary health.

Conclusion

Our analysis suggests that recreational sport and league play is a significant source of GHG emissions. Our analysis also reveals that significant savings in GHG emissions may be achieved by employing computer generated scheduling solutions and by testing alternative game formats that go beyond a simple double-round-robin.
