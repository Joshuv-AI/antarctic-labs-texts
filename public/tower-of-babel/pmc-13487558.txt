ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat mediates fatigue 24-months after breast cancer (BC) treatment?FindingsIn this cohort study of 381 patients with BC 2 years after receiving potentially cardiotoxic chemotherapy for BC, 34.6% of women experienced increased fatigue relative to their precancer treatment fatigue level. Independent of cardiovascular risk factors, including pretreatment and posttreatment left ventricular ejection fraction, only depressive symptoms and perceived stress were associated with and mediated the 24-month post-BC treatment fatigue measures.MeaningThese findings suggest clinically relevant fatigue 2 years after initiating BC treatment is mediated partly by depressive symptoms and perceived stress irrespective of left ventricular ejection fraction.

Introduction

Post–breast cancer (BC) treatment prevalence estimates of fatigue range from 20% to 62% depending on the population, type of treatment, time since treatment, and method of assessment.1,2,3,4 It is unknown whether the interrelationship of preexisting or dynamic cancer therapy–associated change in cardiovascular (CV), physiologic, psychological, or metabolic factors mediate BC treatment–related fatigue.

We hypothesized that cancer chemotherapy associated with changes in left ventricular (LV) function (known to occur early during cancer treatment) were associated and mediated 2-year post–BC treatment fatigue. To address this hypothesis, we conducted a multisite prospective cohort study of women treated with adjuvant chemotherapy and/or an aromatase inhibitor for BC—including a noncancer comparison cohort5,6— to determine the pretreatment to 24-month posttreatment prevalence of fatigue accounting for age, race and ethnicity, cancer therapy, LV function, and sociodemographic, psychological, physiologic, and CV risk factors.

Methods

Understanding and Predicting Breast Cancer Events after Treatment (UPBEAT, WF-97415, funded via R01CA199167) is a multisite prospective cohort study of women diagnosed with stage I to III BC7 and noncancer comparators operating through the Wake Forest National Cancer Institute Community Oncology Research Program Research Base (UG1CA189824) and the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group-American College of Radiology Imaging Network Cancer Research Group (UG1CA189828) under a National Cancer Institute Central institutional review board–approved protocol and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02791581). All participants provided witnessed written informed consent. Participants were enrolled across 66 community hospitals, where up to 85% of women receive BC treatment.8,9 Participants were registered between August 2017 and July 2021 and followed up for 24 months after their baseline or pretreatment assessment. The study design and rationale along with detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria have been described.2,10 This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies (eTable 8 in Supplement 1).

Study Population

All participants, including noncancer comparators, were at least 18 years old. Eligibility included inflammatory, newly diagnosed, or locally recurrent BC being treated with curative intent scheduled to receive chemotherapy and/or aromatase inhibitor (AI) with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status between 0 and 2.11 Exclusion criteria included a contraindication to CV magnetic resonance imaging, an LV ejection fraction of less than 50% if assessed prior to treatment, current pregnancy or lactation, uncontrolled hypertension, or a preexisting inflammatory disease (eg, systemic lupus). A convenience sample of female family and friends without a history of cancer, breast surgery, or chemotherapy served as a comparator group.

Persons were categorized as (1) receiving chemotherapy inclusive of doxorubicin along with other chemotherapy agents or an AI (chemotherapy inclusive of doxorubicin), (2) receiving chemotherapy possibly with an AI but without doxorubicin (eg, paclitaxel, cyclophosphamide, capecitabine, gemcitabine) (chemotherapy without doxorubicin), or (3) receiving an AI only (anastrozole, letrozole, or exemestane) and no doxorubicin or other chemotherapy (AI, no chemotherapy) (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). For each participant, receipt of radiation treatment for breast cancer was recorded. Participant’s age of less than or at least 52 years was used respectively to characterize premenopausal or postmenopausal status.12,13

Participant characteristics including age, race, height, weight, body mass index, brachial artery systolic and diastolic blood pressure (collected according to American Heart Association criteria),14 heart rate, and receipt of cardioactive medications (beta blockers, angiotensin receptor antagonists, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, calcium antagonists, and diuretics) were collected at each study visit. Race and ethnicity were self-reported at enrollment using fixed case report form categories consistent with the National Institutes of Health categories.

Study Procedures

In participants with BC, complete data inclusive of magnetic resonance imaging were collected pretreatment (postdiagnosis, before postsurgical or nonsurgical treatment) and then 3 and 24 months later. Comparators contributed magnetic resonance imaging and laboratory measures at enrollment and 24 months, and other data at enrollment and at 3 and 24 months.

Outcome

Fatigue was measured by the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy fatigue scale15 across all study visits.16 Scores range from 0 to 52, with higher scores indicating lower fatigue levels.16 A fatigue score of less than or equal to 3017 and a change in score greater than or equal to 5 points was regarded as clinically significant.18

Risk Factors

Factors of fatigue included sociodemographics, CV risk factors, cancer-related factors, laboratory and imaging biomarkers of subclinical CV disease, and psychological factors.7 Medical treatment was retrieved from participants’ medical records. The comorbidities diabetes, hypertension, and a history of coronary artery disease were self-reported (yes, no, or do not know to ever receiving a diagnosis) as was smoking history (never, former, or current). Laboratory data pertaining to glucose, serum creatinine, C-reactive protein, and cholesterol status were collected.

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance assessments of LV volumes, mass, ejection fraction, and global circumferential strain were performed with a standard set of acquisition parameters across a range of 1.5 and 3.0 Tesla scanners from General Electric, Philips, and Siemens according to previously published methods.19,20 Circumferential strain and other LV measures were obtained using cine white blood steady state free precession techniques with a 256 × 128 matrix, a 40-cm field of view, a 10-millisecond repetition time, a 4-millisecond echo time, a 20-degree flip angle, an 8-mm–thick slice with a 2-mm gap, and a 40-millisecond temporal resolution.19,20 All images were analyzed (Medis Medical Imaging, Leiden, Netherlands) by readers in a central core laboratory blinded to all identifiers as an unpaired, blinded read. Submaximal exercise capacity was measured by the 6-minute walk distance (6MWD), an independent factor of CV mortality.21 Psychological factors included depressive symptoms (measured by the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression scale [CESD] in which scores range from 0 to 30 with a score of 10 or greater indicating significant depression),22 and perceived stress (measured by the Cohen 4-item Perceived Stress Scale [PSS]).23

Statistical Analysis

Expanded statistical methods are provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1. All analyses were conducted between October 2024 through April 2025 using R version 4.1.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing).24 Continuous variables were summarized as mean (SD), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Analyses followed an intention-to-treat framework based on treatment group at enrollment. Missing longitudinal data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations, with estimates via Rubin rules.25 Sensitivity analyses compared multiply imputed and complete-case results.

To evaluate differences in fatigue at 24 months between treatment groups and comparators, linear regression models were fit with and without adjustment for pretreatment fatigue (ie, baseline-adjusted analysis of covariance), which provides greater power and precision than change-score or mixed-effects analyses for pre-post data.26,27 Associations between candidate factors and 24-month fatigue were explored using separate pretreatment fatigue adjusted regression models. To identify variables independently associated with fatigue, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used for variable selection from prespecified demographic, clinical, imaging, and psychological factors28,29 and selected variables were then included in multivariable models.

Potential mediating pathways linking treatment, depressive symptoms, perceived stress, and fatigue over time were evaluated using structural equation models with latent change scores.30,31,32,33 All statistical tests were 2-sided and tested at a P = .05 level of significance.

Results

This cohort of 381 encompassed patients with BC (236) and noncancer comparators (145) who had a mean (SD) age of 54 (12) years, and reported 6 Asian (1.6%), 24 (16.6%) Black, and 110 (75.9%) White individuals (Table 1; eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Although 403 participants enrolled in the study (258 participants with BC and 145 comparators), 22 participants withdrew due to feeling overwhelmed prior to any participation. Sixty-one of 381 participants (16%) dropped out (Figure 1) before 24-month; 35 (57.4%) were comparators, and dropouts were younger (50.7 vs 54.4 years, t test P = .03). Overall, there was not a significant difference in pretreatment fatigue between those who dropped out vs those who remained in the study.

Univariable Associations

Table 1 summarizes the prevalence of clinically significant fatigue and changes in fatigue over 24 months. Figure 2 displays the association between cancer treatment groups relative to comparators in pretreatment and 24-month fatigue. Relative to comparators, participants in all 3 BC groups reported worse (ie, lower) pretreatment and 24-month posttreatment fatigue. Compared with those without cancer, participants receiving chemotherapy inclusive of doxorubicin and chemotherapy without doxorubicin continued to report worse fatigue scores over time (mean differences, −3.93 [95% CI, −6.08 to −1.79]; P < .001; and −4.37 [95% CI, −6.81 to −1.94; P < .001], respectively). However, fatigue scores for the AI, no chemotherapy group were not different from the comparators (mean difference, −2.46 [95% CI, −5.65 to 0.73; P = .13]). Notably, pretreatment fatigue was associated with 24-month fatigue (β, 0.35 [95% CI, 0.25 to 0.46]; P < .001), indicating that worse fatigue at pretreatment was associated with worse fatigue 24 months later.

Table 2 also presents results from a multivariable model for pre- to 24-month fatigue change over time. Pretreatment fatigue and worsening in fatigue from pretreatment to 3 months were associated with clinically significant change in fatigue at 24 months. Each 10-meter improvement in 6MWD from 3 to 24 months was associated with greater reduction in fatigue over time (β, 0.07; 95% CI, 0.05 to 0.10; P < .001). Larger reductions in depressive symptoms from pretreatment to 24 months were associated with less of an increase in fatigue over 24 months (β, −0.10; 95% CI, −0.16 to −0.03; P = .003). Change in PSS was not associated with the 24-month increase or decrease in fatigue scores in this multivariable model. A sensitivity analysis restricting the cohort to participants without prior chemotherapy, radiation, or hormonal therapy yielded materially unchanged results (eTable 7 in Supplement 1).

Mediation Analyses

From bivariate analyses (Figure 3; eTable 5 in Supplement 1) the only 2 significant candidate factors formally assessed as mediators were PSS and depressive symptoms. In a latent change score mediation model, treatment group assignment was associated with changes in both CESD and PSS, which in turn were associated with changes in participant-reported fatigue. Relative to the comparators, both chemotherapy groups experienced increased fatigue at 3 months, partially mediated by worsening CESD (indirect effect −1.16; 95% CI, −2.44 to 0.13 [14.5% of the total effect]; and −1.91; 95% CI, −3.33 to −0.49 [19.1%]). At 24 months, both groups reported significant improvements in fatigue from 3 to 24 months, substantially mediated by CESD improvement (indirect effect, 2.14; 95% CI, 0.77 to 3.51 [25.0%]; and 2.04; 95% CI, 0.67 to 3.41 [21.4%]). An additional 2% to 3% was mediated through joint CESD through PSS pathways.

In an assessment of direct associations, changes in CESD and PSS were each independently associated with fatigue change at both time points, with worsening depression (3 months: β, −1.08; 95% CI, −1.33 to −0.83 and 24 months: β, −0.88; 95% CI, −1.12 to −0.64) and greater perceived psychosocial stress (3 months: β, −0.36; 95% CI, −0.68 to −0.05 and 24 months: β, −0.32; 95% CI, −0.62 to −0.01) significantly associated with greater fatigue burden (P < .05 for all paths). The significant perceived-stress pathway in the structural model contrasts with the LASSO change model (Table 2), where change in perceived stress was not retained. Because LASSO selects among collinear factors, the perceived-stress contribution is absorbed by the correlated change in depressive symptoms, whereas the latent change score model estimates it explicitly. Perceived stress is therefore best interpreted as a secondary contributor—smaller than depressive symptoms and not retained under penalized selection, although significant when modeled directly.

Discussion

The findings of this longitudinal prospective cohort study of 236 women receiving potentially cardiotoxic chemotherapy for stage I to III BC and 145 comparators without cancer recruited from community and academic health systems in the US showed first, that 2 years after receiving treatment for BC and after accounting for age, BC stage, and the presence of CV risk factors, 18.2% of women receiving potentially cardiotoxic chemotherapy experienced clinically relevant fatigue regardless of receipt of anthracycline or nonanthracycline based chemotherapy regimens. Also, 34.6% of patients receiving chemotherapy experienced clinically relevant increases in fatigue manifested by at least 5 points from baseline declines in the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy Fatigue Scale.18 These percentages are consistent with other studies that have measured fatigue over a similar length of time.34,35 Second, that relative to women receiving nonanthracycline based chemotherapy or aromatase inhibitors no chemotherapy, women receiving anthracycline-based chemotherapy experienced baseline adjusted declines in 6MWD, LVEF, and LV circumferential strain, however none of these were associated with 24-month post–BC treatment fatigue. Additionally, that after accounting for the presence of pretreatment and posttreatment LVEF, diabetes, hypertension, tobacco use, hematocrit, age, body mass index, and receipt of potentially cardio-protective medications, depressive symptoms were the principal mediator of the association between BC treatment group on 24-month fatigue, with perceived stress contributing secondarily.

Participants in this study reflected similar demographics, risk factor profiles, anticancer and antihypertensive treatment regimens, and resting values of cardiac parameters to other populations reported across other studies involving women receiving treatment for BC (Table 1; eTable 2 in Supplement 1).36,37,38,39 Seventeen percent (and nearly 20% of those receiving doxorubicin) identified as Black, which is slightly greater relative to Black-identifying women enrolled in prior studies.40,41

As shown in Figure 2, 3 months into treatment for BC, fatigue worsened on average for women receiving anthracycline and nonanthracycline-based chemotherapeutic regimens, but not in women receiving AIs with no chemotherapy or those without BC. For women receiving chemotherapy at 3 months, there were declines in hematocrit, LV ejection fraction, LV circumferential strain, 6MWD, and systolic blood pressure, and increases in perceived stress and depressive symptoms. These changes occurred regardless of race, age, cancer stage, or receipt of cardioprotective medications, and many of these measures recovered at the 24-month post–BC initiation of treatment (eTable 3 in Supplement 1), and with the exception of perceived stress and depressive symptoms, none were associated with fatigue at 24 months (eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Using various metrics, other studies have also shown a relationship between depressive symptoms or stress and fatigue.34,35,42,43,44,45,46,47,48 Previous research has also shown that stress is related to depression.49,50,51,52 However, none of these studies have included measures of cardiovascular function, fatigue, depression, and perceived stress altogether. Furthermore, to our knowledge, few studies have measured the extensive range of clinical parameters related to fatigue as in the present study. One exception is a cross-sectional study that examined 1787 participants with cancer and found that participants with higher levels of depressive symptoms, pain, and lower hemoglobin levels had significantly greater fatigue.43 In our analyses, depressive symptoms dominated, carrying most of the mediated contribution with associations roughly 3 times those for perceived stress. Interventions targeting depression may therefore reduce posttreatment fatigue, with stress a secondary target.

Surprisingly, there was a lack of an association of clinical CV risk factors (eg, hypertension, diabetes, smoking) or objective measures of cardiac function including LV ejection fraction or strain parameters with 24-month post initiation of BC treatment fatigue. Future studies may determine if immune, inflammatory, neuroendocrine, and neural processes including activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis are associated with cancer-related fatigue.53,54 Since the American Society of Clinical Oncology has suggested physical activity, and psychosocial interventions, may associate with improvements in both depressive symptoms and perceived stress,55,56 our results support consideration of screening for depressive symptoms and the presence of stress. Depressive symptoms are common (ranging from 12% to 25%) among participants with BC,57,58,59,60 are associated with poorer quality of life and may contribute to increased mortality.61,62,63,64,65

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, and some participants had missed or delayed scheduled visits. A total of 61 of 381 participants dropped out before 24 months. However, 84% of the participants in the study had data across baseline, 3, and 24 months (Figure 1), and there was no significant difference in pretreatment fatigue between dropouts or those who remained at 24 months. Missing visit data were addressed with multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption; the imputation models included 34 demographic and risk-factor covariates, likely capturing factors of missingness. Complete-case and multiply imputed analyses yielded similar results. Second, although the study included mostly White participants, the percentage of Black participants exceeded that of many studies (66 [17.3% overall]) including those recruited from community hospitals where greater than 80% of women with BC receive treatment.5,6 Third, some potential mediators considered failed to show statistical significance. Importantly, the observed mediated proportions for the nonsignificant mediators were all less than 5%; even with a larger sample to detect them, such small associations would likely be clinically irrelevant. Fourth, as an observational cohort, our mediation estimates are interpretable as causal under assumptions of no unmeasured confounding and correct temporal ordering; we therefore report all findings as associations. Finally, although the noncancer comparator group does not represent a random sample, we believe this comparator sample controls for environmental and/or geographic exposures and to an extent may share some genetic characteristics of the patients with BC (if they are relatives). Of note, the primary results and conclusions of this study focus on the comparisons longitudinally within BC groups rather than across the BC vs comparator group. Therefore, the method of selection of the comparators likely has minimal impact on the overall conclusions.

Conclusions

In summary, the results of this study indicate that 34% of women receiving adjuvant and neoadjuvant chemotherapy for breast cancer experienced a clinically significant increase in fatigue 24 months after initiation of their treatment regardless of age, CV risk factors, or change in hematocrit or LV ejection fraction and/or circumferential strain. Importantly this BC treatment–related fatigue was partially mediated by symptoms of depression and the presence of stress irrespective of cardiovascular function. Thus, post–BC treatment of fatigue may require strategies targeting depression and stress independent of cardiovascular function.
