Catheter-assisted evacuation through limited laminectomy for traumatic long-segment spinal epidural hematoma in ankylosing spondylitis: a technical note and two case reports
Authors: Jie Shang, Bao Jia, MUSTAFA ABBAS FARHOOD SULTANI, Lei Liu, Jianwei Zhang, Jie Chen, Xianyi Zhang, Liang Guo, Peipei Li, Meng Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) are prone to unstable spinal fractures with long-segment spinal epidural hematoma (LSEH). Conventional multilevel laminectomy can achieve complete decompression but may disrupt the posterior tension complex. In this article, we describe a catheter-assisted technique for evacuating LSEH through a limited laminectomy window.MethodsTwo AS patients with traumatic cervicothoracic LSEH underwent emergency posterior stabilization followed by limited laminectomy. A 15 Fr silicone catheter with a distal side-hole was advanced epidurally along the hematoma through the laminectomy window. We applied alternating negative-pressure suction and intermittent saline irrigation until free backflow was achieved and dural decompression was confirmed.ResultsComplete radiographic decompression was obtained in both cases and their neurological recovery was substantial. Case 1 improved from Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score 3/17 and American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) classification B preoperatively to JOA 16/17 and ASIA E at 6 months post operation, regaining independent ambulation. Case 2, presenting with JOA 0/17 and ASIA A pre-operation, improved to JOA 9/17 and ASIA C at final follow-up, with a recovery of deep sensation and partial motor function.ConclusionCatheter-assisted evacuation via limited laminectomy represents a feasible approach for LSEH in AS patients. This technique facilitates effective decompression while minimizing damage to posterior spinal structures.Keywords: Spinal epidural hematoma, Ankylosing spondylitis, Hematoma evacuation, Minimally invasive

Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic immune-mediated spondyloarthritis, characterized by persistent axial inflammation and pathologic new bone formation [1]. This process often leads to a rigid spine, making patients susceptible to unstable fractures and neurological injury, even following low-energy trauma. Complication and mortality rates among these patients are also higher than those in the general trauma population [2–3]. Spinal epidural hematoma (SEH) has been repeatedly reported in AS, sometimes spanning multiple vertebral levels [4–6].

SEH is a time-critical condition that causes spinal cord compression. It typically presents with acute pain and rapidly progressing neurological deficits. Delayed diagnosis and treatment raise the likelihood of permanent neurological dysfunction [7–8]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the preferred choice for confirming the presence of SEH and assessing its distribution [9]. Standard treatment has conventionally involved emergency decompressive laminectomy and hematoma evacuation. Multiple studies have reported that earlier intervention was closely related to improved neurological recovery [8, 10].

When SEH spans multiple segments, performing a multilevel laminectomy may lead to more blood loss, significant soft-tissue damage, and an increased risk of postoperative deformity or instability. These complications are particularly pertinent in patients with AS, who usually exhibit preexisting structural ankylosis, osteoporosis, and altered spinal biomechanics [2–3, 11]. Given these risks, alternative approaches, such as skip hemilaminectomy and minimally invasive catheter-assisted evacuation were developed, which allow for clot irrigation and aspiration through a limited surgical exposure, thereby avoiding extensive bone resection [11–14].

In this report, we describe two AS patients who developed long-segment spinal epidural hematoma (LSEH) following spinal fracture. Both were treated with a minimally invasive approach involving hematoma evacuation via irrigation and aspiration. A soft tube was introduced through a limited laminotomy to achieve decompression. This technique may help reduce operative trauma while maintaining spinal integrity and achieving adequate decompression in this vulnerable patient group.

Indications and selection criteria

Catheter-assisted evacuation was considered in patients with long-segment spinal epidural hematoma when preoperative imaging demonstrated a predominantly posterior or posterolateral epidural collection spanning multiple levels and producing clear dural sac compression. Selection was based on: (1) extent and location of hematoma, preferably posterior or posterolateral rather than primarily anterior, (2) maximal canal compromise, defined as ≥ 50% compromise at the most compressed level or intramedullary T2 hyperintensity, and (3) neurological status, including progressive deficits prompting urgent decompression within an early time window.

The technique was not intended as a universal alternative to multilevel laminectomy but as an option for carefully selected cases in which extensive posterior element disruption is undesirable. Contraindications or relative exclusions included imaging features suggesting a markedly organized or solid clot, predominantly anterior hematoma distribution, or situations in which safe catheter passage was unlikely.

Standardized technical details

Catheter length and advancement depth

The planned catheter advancement depth was determined from preoperative MRI/CT by measuring the distance from the decompression window to the endpoint of the hematoma along the dorsal epidural space. The catheter was trimmed accordingly, and depth marks can be placed at 1-cm intervals. Advancement was performed gently under direct tactile feedback, or fluoroscopic confirmation when needed. The intended endpoint was reaching the planned depth with free backflow and visible reduction in dural sac tension.

Irrigation–aspiration protocol

Irrigation was performed using intermittent, small-volume saline instillation followed by suction aspiration. Typical total irrigation volume ranged from 5 to 10 mL in our cases. Forceful injection was strictly avoided. Irrigation was continued until aspirate became largely clear and no further clot fragments were retrieved.

Prevention of irrigation-induced epidural pressure rise

To avoid secondary cord compression, irrigation was only performed when an outflow pathway was patent, and injection was stopped immediately if resistance increased, outflow diminished, or dural tension subjectively increased. When necessary, an additional intermediate decompression window was created to facilitate egress and pressure relief across long segments. Any sign of ineffective decompression or safety concern triggered early conversion to wider decompression according to the predefined criteria above.

Case 1

A 62-year-old woman presented three hours after a ground-level fall with occipital impact. One hour later, she developed progressive limb numbness and weakness. She described a sensation of head-body separation and needed to support her head while walking. She had a 40-year history of AS and had undergone posterior corrective fixation for a thoracic Andersson lesion three years earlier.

On admission, superficial and deep sensation were absent below the sternal angle. Ulnar-sided paresthesia was present in both forearms. Grip strength was grade II, biceps and triceps were grade III, Lower-limb strength was grade 0, but perianal sensation was preserved. Based on the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scoring system, the score was 3/17, and according to American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) classification, the patient was classified as B.

MRI demonstrated C6/7 fracture-dislocation with spinal cord injury, accompanied by an extensive LSEH extending from C5 to T10, occupying approximately 70%-80% of the spinal canal cross-sectional area (Fig. 1A-F).

MRI examination showed fracture and dislocation of the C6/7 with spinal cord injury, accompanied by an LSEH at C5 to T10

Emergency surgery was performed 8 h after injury, and the surgery mainly consisted of two phases. The first phase involved posterior laminectomy and decompression from C4 to T2, along with hematoma evacuation and instrumented fixation. A posterior midline approach was performed to expose C4 to T2. Lateral mass screws were inserted at C4-C6, and pedicle screws were placed at T1-T2. Pre-contoured rods were placed and locked. Then a C5-T1 laminectomy was conducted for focal evacuation and decompression.

The second phase focused on decompression and hematoma removal from T2 to T10. A 15-Fr silicone catheter was trimmed according to the measured T1-T10 distance, and a side hole was created 1 cm from the distal end. Using a 20 ml syringe or a negative pressure aspirator, suction was applied from the proximal to the distal end of the tube, while normal saline was intermittently injected to irrigate and flush out the clot. These steps were alternated until the tube was fully advanced and the hematoma was completely evacuated (Fig. 2A-D). Care was taken to avoid excessive advancement of the tube or saline injection into the clot, which could elevate local epidural pressure and potentially compress the spinal cord.

Using a 15-Fr silicone catheter for hematoma aspiration and irrigation

On the second postoperative day, the patient exhibited marked improvement in limb sensation and muscle strength compared to pre-operation. Follow-up MRI was performed on postoperative day 7, demonstrating a significant reduction in hematoma volume and complete compression (Fig. 3A-F). One month post operation, the patient was able to walk with a walker. Three months post operation, independent indoor ambulation unaided was achieved. At six months postoperatively, the patient was able to walk freely outdoors, with a JOA score of 16/17 and an ASIA classification improved to Grade E.

7 days post operation, X-ray and MRI examinations were performed. The MRI showed that the epidural hematoma was significantly reduced

Case 2

A 56-year-old man presented five hours after a backward fall with occipital-cervical impact. He had immediate quadriplegia without loss of consciousness. The patient had a history of ankylosing spondylitis for over 30 years.

Neurological examination revealed that superficial and deep sensation were absent below the cervical level. Perianal sensation was lost. All limb muscle groups were grade 0. Imaging examination demonstrated C4/5 fracture-dislocation with associated spinal cord injury, T12 vertebral fracture, and a LSEH extending from C3 to T7 within the spinal canal (Fig. 4A-F). Based on the JOA scoring system, the patient scored 0/17, and the ASIA grade was A.

MRI examination showed fracture dislocation with spinal cord injury at C4/5, vertebral fracture at T12, and a LSEH in the spinal canal at C3-T7

The patient underwent surgical treatment 11 h post-injury. Phase 1, C2-C7 were exposed through a posterior approach. Pedicle screws were placed at C2 and C7, and lateral mass screws were placed at C4-C6, then the pre-bending rods were placed and locked. For T12 stabilization, a T9-L3 pedicle screw-rod construct was applied. A laminectomy was performed at C3-C7 and T8 level to achieve localized decompression. Phase 2, a 15-Fr catheter, trimmed to the C7-T7 length, was used for alternating aspiration and saline irrigation through the C7 and T8 windows, aiming to remove the cervicothoracic LSEH.

Due to preoperative diaphragmatic dysfunction, the patient received tracheostomy placement and was managed in the ICU. Three days post operation, deep sensory perception in the neck and limbs began to return. 7 days post operation, the muscle strength of the left upper limb had recovered to Grade 1, and the patient achieved spontaneous breathing. One month postoperatively, MRI demonstrated a marked reduction in the epidural hematoma volume and complete decompression of the dural sac (Fig. 5A-F). One month post operation, superficial and deep sensations were fully restored and left upper-limb strength reached grade 2. At the final follow-up, the JOA score was 9/17, and the ASIA Grade was C.

MRI at 1 month after surgery showed a significant reduction in intraspinal hematoma

Perioperative complications

In both cases, no intraoperative complications such as dural tear, catheter misplacement–related neural injury, or uncontrolled bleeding were observed in these two cases. No transient postoperative neurological deterioration occurred. There were no wound complications or infections during follow-up. Nevertheless, given the limited sample size, the absence of complications in this report should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of safety.

Discussion

It was reported that traumatic SEH accounts for approximately 0.5% to 1.7% of all spinal injuries. However, in patients with AS, the incidence is significantly higher, reaching up to 10.4% [2, 15–16]. This increased risk can be attributed to several pathophysiological factors. Firstly, the ankylosed spine acts like an extended lever arm with reduced energy-absorbing capacity. As a result, it becomes prone to three-column fractures with displacement, even after low-energy trauma [17]. Once there is a displacement of the fracture, the sharp fracture edges may lacerate epidural veins and fragile neovessels. Secondly, chronic inflammation in AS drives persistent overexpression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-17, which stimulate abnormal angiogenesis [18–21]. These newly formed vessels often prone to rupture under shear stress during fracture events. Additionally, chronic inflammation induces fibrosis and atrophy of the epidural fat pad. This process enlarges the epidural space and diminishes resistance to blood flow, facilitating the longitudinal spread of hematoma and ultimately leading to LSEH [22–23].

AS patients with LSEH usually showed rapidly progressive incomplete neurological deficits. Prompt surgical decompression is crucial for optimizing neurological recovery. Studies indicate that surgery within 12–24 h after motor deficit onset leads to the best outcomes. For mildly affected patients, the surgical window may extend to 48 h [24]. Other evidence suggests that incomplete spinal cord injuries should be managed within 48 h, while complete injuries require intervention within 36 h [25]. Preoperative neurological status is also a strong predictor of recovery. Patients with higher ASIA scores before surgery generally experience better neurological improvement [24–26]. For example, Rajz et al. reported that among 8 patients with preoperative ASIA Grade A, 5 showed no improvement and 3 recovered only to Grade C. In contrast, of 9 patients with preoperative Grades C or D, 8 improved to Grade E and 1 reached Grade D [26]. Thus, close monitoring for new or worsening neurological deficits is essential. Prompt imaging, particularly MRI, should be used to confirm the presence and extent of the hematoma and assess spinal cord compression.

The main aims of surgery in LSEH are adequate decompression, complete hematoma evacuation, and preservation of spinal stability [24, 27]. For long-segment spinal epidural hematoma, conventional multilevel laminectomy provides direct visualization and facilitates complete clot removal, particularly when the hematoma is dense or organized. However, the trade-off is a wider exposure with more extensive posterior element disruption, which may increase blood loss, wound morbidity, and the risk of postoperative mechanical instability. In contrast, catheter-assisted evacuation through limited decompression windows aims to reduce the extent of bony and soft-tissue dissection while still enabling longitudinal evacuation via alternating aspiration and low-pressure irrigation. From a practical standpoint, this approach is best viewed as a selective strategy that prioritizes minimizing exposure, with the understanding that incomplete evacuation or failure to achieve adequate decompression should prompt early conversion to wider decompression.

Although our cases involved AS, it is not a prerequisite for catheter-assisted evacuation. The feasibility of catheter-assisted evacuation is likely determined more by anatomical distribution and clot characteristics than by etiology alone. For primarily anterior or circumferential hematomas, or when imaging suggests a solid and organized clot, conventional decompression remains more reliable. Importantly, our current experience is limited to the two AS-associated cases reported here, and generalizability to the broader spectrum of SEH remains uncertain and requires validation in broader cohorts.

Additionally, several limitations of this technique must be noted. First, hematoma characteristics such as location, length, and consistency can influence its feasibility. Preoperative MRI should be conducted to carefully evaluate hematoma morphology. Secondly, as emphasized in prior studies, earlier intervention is consistently associated with better recovery, particularly in incomplete injuries. Therefore, the favorable neurological outcomes observed in our two cases cannot be attributed to the catheter technique alone and may be confounded by early surgical timing. Larger series are required to define failure rates and comparative outcomes. Thirdly, intraoperative irrigation speed and pressure should be regulated to avoid excessive epidural perfusion, which could worsen spinal cord compression. Additionally, with ongoing advances in image guidance, evacuation with endoscopy could further improve its utility.

Conclusion

In summary, AS complicated by LSEH is a serious complication of spinal trauma with a multifactorial pathogenesis. While traditional laminectomy can achieve complete decompression, it increases the risks of significant tissue damage and instability, especially in AS patients. This study illustrates the use of soft tube aspiration and irrigation in two cases, highlighting its feasibility and efficacy. Larger, prospective studies are still needed for further investigation.
