Transcriptomic Landscape Reveals Immunity Related Trade‐Offs in an Invertebrate‐Fungal Host–Parasite System
Authors: Sofia Paraskevopoulou, Sabrina Gattis, Frida Ben‐Ami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHost–parasite interactions represent an evolutionary arms race, with parasites evolving strategies to exploit host resources, and hosts deploying mechanisms to resist or tolerate infection. Unlike vertebrates, which possess a sophisticated immune system, invertebrates rely entirely on innate immunity to combat pathogens. Yet, the molecular underpinnings and life‐history consequences of immune activation remain little understood in non‐insect invertebrates. Here, we combine life‐history trait analysis with transcriptomics to investigate immune responses in an aquatic invertebrate exposed to a natural yeast parasite. By comparing infected, exposed‐uninfected, and unexposed individuals, we identified candidate genes potentially involved in haemocyte recruitment and the reinforcement of the gut epithelium. These patterns highlight candidate mechanisms that may contribute to resistance following pathogen exposure. Exposed‐uninfected individuals also showed delayed reproductive maturation, though lifetime reproductive output was ultimately maintained. Therefore, this suggests that successful exposure and immunity changes may be associated with a transient shift in reproductive allocation rather than a persistent reduction in reproductive output. In contrast, infected hosts exhibited stronger changes in metabolic and protein biosynthesis processes, consistent with a resource depletion hypothesis. These metabolic shifts align with reduced investment in reproduction, as infection appears to affect the number of offspring produced in the first brood. Our findings provide novel insights into the molecular and life‐history responses associated with pathogen exposure and established infection and suggest candidate mechanisms through which host responses may shape life‐history strategies in non‐insect invertebrates, with broad implications for understanding host–parasite coevolution.

Introduction

Parasites (used here interchangeably with pathogens) impose significant fitness costs to their hosts by exploiting host resources and reducing host growth, reproduction and survival (Marzal et al. 2004; Hall et al. 2009; Pigeault et al. 2018; Eleftheriou 2020), thereby affecting host population dynamics. Hosts, in turn, employ a series of mechanisms to resist or tolerate parasitic infections. Unlike vertebrates, whose immune system is adaptive, invertebrates rely solely on their innate immune system to combat infections (Litman and Cooper 2007; Litman et al. 2010). This immune system consists of a diverse set of cellular components, such as receptors that recognize pathogen associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), regulators that modulate signalling pathways, and effectors that are directly engaged in the inhibition of pathogen proliferation and survival (Coates et al. 2022; Schmid‐Hempel 2009; Rathinam et al. 2024). Yet, the molecular mechanisms central to these defences remain relatively unexplored in non‐insect invertebrates.

The Daphnia‐microparasites system is ideal for investigating the innate immune response in invertebrates. First, natural populations of Daphnia are often infected by a variety of parasites, such as bacterial, fungal, microsporidian and viral pathogens (Ebert 2005, 2022; Goren and Ben‐Ami 2013). Additionally, Daphnia species play a crucial role in freshwater ecosystems as keystone species in aquatic trophic webs and in nutrient cycling through decomposition of organic matter (Ebert 2022; Gorzelnik et al. 2023). Lastly, Daphnia are essential in aquaculture since they are used as a food source for many fish larvae (Das et al. 2012; Miura et al. 2021). Given their ecological and economic importance, parasite infections in Daphnia can have cascading effects, disrupting food web stability, altering ecosystem dynamics, and potentially impacting aquaculture industries (Ebert et al. 1997; Decaestecker et al. 2005; Hall et al. 2010, 2011).

Over the past decades, substantial advancements have been made in understanding the ecological and evolutionary impacts of infections in Daphnia (Crustacea, Arthropoda). Recent studies suggest that infection outcome and parasite virulence are species‐ and genotype‐dependent as well as host age‐dependent (Duneau et al. 2011; Hall and Ebert 2012; Izhar and Ben‐Ami 2015; Izhar et al. 2015; Ben‐Ami 2019; Izhar et al. 2020; Shaw et al. 2021; Gattis and Ben‐Ami 2025). In coinfections, the sequence of parasite exposure (i.e., priority effect) has also been found to alter the outcome of these interactions, affecting both survival and reproduction (Ben‐Ami et al. 2011; Clay et al. 2019, 2023; Manzi et al. 2021; Gurung et al. 2024; Halle et al. 2026). Despite these achievements, the molecular mechanisms underlying Daphnia immunity remain quite unexplored, with most molecular studies focusing on infections caused by the bacterium Pasteuria ramosa . More precisely, candidate immune genes (e.g., Phenoloxidase) have been identified in the Daphnia genome (Labbé and Little 2009). Later on, McTaggart et al. (2015) identified putative immune genes related to the challenge of Daphnia magna hosts with infective and non‐infective strains of P. ramosa . Recently, it has been shown that distinct resistance loci control infection routes in Pasteuria, revealing a complex genetic architecture that underlies host–parasite interactions (Mathieu‐Bégné et al. 2024). Further transcriptomic analysis in another Daphnia species, Daphnia galeata, challenged with a microsporidian parasite, suggests immunity‐metabolism trade‐offs (Lu et al. 2018).

When it comes to fungal pathogens, the molecular basis of the immune response of Daphnia is even more understudied, despite their broad distribution in natural populations and the important impacts they have on host fitness (Ebert 2005; Shaw et al. 2021; Paraskevopoulou et al. 2022). Among these pathogens, Metschnikowia bicuspidata is one of the most prevalent fungal parasites of Daphnia (Ebert 2005, 2022; Hall et al. 2010, 2011). This yeast parasite forms needle‐shaped ascospores that are horizontally transmitted through ingestion (Ebert et al. 1997; Stewart Merrill and Cáceres 2018). After ingestion, spores penetrate the gut epithelium and start proliferating in the haemolymph of the animal, ultimately leading to host death (Ebert 2005; Stewart Merrill and Cáceres 2018; Figure 1a). So far, only one transcriptomic study has examined early‐stage immune response (within 24 h of exposure) in D. dentifera challenged with M. bicuspidata . This study revealed a number of upregulated genes related to cuticle development and defence responses (Terrill Sondag et al. 2023). However, we still lack information on whether these immune responses are conserved across Daphnia species or how hosts respond beyond early contact with the parasite. Here, we examined gene expression changes across unexposed, exposed‐infected, and exposed‐uninfected individuals to capture differences between immune system activation and metabolic shifts due to infection. Then, we associated these shifts with observed trade‐offs in other phenotypic traits, such as host survival and reproduction.

Material & Methods

Experimental Design

Daphnia magna (NO‐V‐7 clone, Norway) were acclimated under standardized lab conditions (20°C± 1°C, 16:8 Light: Dark) for two generations. The experimental generation was isolated from the third brood of the last acclimated generation and fed daily with Scenedesmus sp. ad libitum (Izhar and Ben‐Ami 2015). Five days post‐birth, 720 juveniles were placed individually into separate jars containing 20 mL of Artificial Daphnia Medium (ADaM; Ebert et al. 1998; Klüttgen et al. 1994) and randomly assigned to one of two treatments: an exposed treatment, in which individuals were inoculated with 1500 M. bicuspidata spores/mL for 5 days, and (C) an unexposed treatment, in which individuals received crushed uninfected D. magna as a placebo. Five days post‐exposure, all Daphnia were transferred to jars filled with 80 mL of fresh ADaM to terminate the exposure period and thereafter medium was replaced three times per week (Figure 1b).

RNA Isolation and Library Preparation

On Day 12 post‐exposure (17 days post‐birth), infection status was visually determined under a stereomicroscope (Leica DFC 295, Germany) based on Daphnia's external phenotype, with infected individuals developing an opaque colour due to the accumulation of fungal spores within the haemolymph (Figure 1c). Day 12 post‐exposure was chosen because this time point corresponds to the onset of widespread, externally visible infection while preceding substantial host mortality. The parasite treatment was further divided into either infected (I) or exposed‐uninfected (E) groups. Individuals classified as exposed‐uninfected showed no visible signs of infection at this time point. Four biological replicates were selected from each treatment (C, E and I), with each biological replicate consisting of a single individual Daphnia. Each Daphnia was homogenized with a sterile plastic pestle in 500 μL of Trizol, and RNA was extracted using a method that combines extraction through Trizol and column precipitation (RNeasy Mini Kit, QIAGEN), with a double final elution step (20 μL each time). RNA quality and quantity were assessed with Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the 2200 TapeStation (Agilent), respectively. For library preparation, mRNA was enriched from 300 ng total RNA using the NEBNext Poly (A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs), followed by stranded library preparation with the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs). Libraries were sequenced (single‐end, 100 bp) on a NovaSeq6000 Illumina platform at the Crown Genomics Institute, Weizmann Institute of Science (Rehovot, Israel).

Differential Gene Expression Analysis

We trimmed raw reads with Trimmomatic v0.40 and assessed their quality using FastQC (Andrews 2010). Trimmed reads were mapped to the D. magna genome (daphmag2.4; GCA_001632505.1) with STAR (Dobin et al. 2013) and gene‐level quantification estimates were produced by RSEM (Li and Dewey 2011) (Table S1). To assess potential cross‐mapping of parasite‐derived reads, all libraries were aligned against a chimeric host–parasite genome ( Daphnia magna : GCA_001632505.1; Metschnikowia bicuspidata : GCF_001664035.1). We compared host unique mapping rates between the original host‐only alignment and the chimeric alignment, and quantified the proportion of reads uniquely assigned to the parasite genome (Table S2).

Count data were imported into R/Bioconductor with the Tximport R package (v1.38.2; Soneson et al. 2015) and gene expression was estimated using the DESeq2 R package, which performs a size‐factor normalization to account for differences in sequencing depth among libraries (v1.50.2; Love et al. 2014). To improve model fit and increase the reliability of FDR inference, we filtered out genes with zero counts across all samples and those with low counts (< 100) in more than 75% of the samples, resulting in 10,223 genes retained for differential expression analysis and hereafter referred to as ‘background genes’. Before conducting differential expression analysis, we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) to assess sample clustering (Figure S1). PC1 (47% of variance explained) separated infected from control and exposed‐uninfected samples. One sample originally classified as exposed‐uninfected (library E383) clustered with infected individuals. Given the limited sensitivity of microscopy for detecting early‐stage infections, this discrepancy likely reflects a misclassified infected individual and was therefore excluded from further analyses. In contrast, one infected individual (library I521) exhibited greater dispersion along PC2 (25% of the variance explained), however, its infection status was clearly confirmed. Therefore, this sample was retained as true biological variation (Figure S1). Differential gene expression analysis was performed in three pairwise comparisons: E versus C, I versus C, and I versus E. Statistical power was estimated using RNASeqPower (v1.50.0; Hart et al. 2013), with dispersion and sequencing depth parameters derived from the DESeq2 model. For the smallest group size (n = 3, exposed‐uninfected), log2FC = 0.585 was detectable at 81.5% power (α = 0.05), exceeding the commonly used 80% threshold (Figure S2). Differential expression was therefore assessed using DESeq2 with lfcThreshold = 0.585, which corresponds to the composite null hypothesis H0: |log2FC| ≤ 0.585 (McCarthy et al. 2012; Love et al. 2014). p‐values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction, and genes with an adjusted p‐value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed (DEGs) (Love et al. 2014). A Venn diagram (VennDiagram v1.8.2; Chen and Boutros 2011) was used to depict the overlap of significant DEGs for each pairwise comparison, while their expression profiles were visualized in heat maps using normalized counts scaled per gene (pheatmap v1.0.13; Kolde 2018). To identify enriched biological functions in DEGs, we annotated all D. magna coding sequences (CDS) using eggNOG (Huerta‐Cepas et al. 2019) with an e‐value cut‐off of 1 × 10−10 (Table S3).

Over‐Representation Enrichment Analysis (ORA)

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was assessed by over‐representation analysis (ORA). GO‐ORA was performed using TopGO (v2.62.0; Alexa and Rahnenfuhrer 2024) with the ‘weight01’ algorithm. Enrichment was tested across biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC) ontologies using a Fisher's Exact Test (p‐value < 0.05). For both analyses the ‘background genes’ were used as the universe.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

We used clusterProfiler (v4.18.4; Wu et al. 2021) to perform a GSEA, testing both GO terms (BP, MF and CC ontologies) and KEGG Ortholog (KO) gene sets. All 10,223 genes passing the DESeq2 expression prefiltering were ranked by their DESeq2 Wald statistic (H0: LFC = 0) rather than the Wald statistic (H0: |LFC| ≤ 0.585) used for differential expression calling, as the truncated null distribution of the alternative hypothesis statistic distorts the score distribution and invalidates GSEA's ranking assumptions. Significance was assessed using a permutation‐based FDR (Benjamini–Hochberg, adjusted p < 0.001), a more stringent threshold than the p < 0.05 applied in ORA, reflecting the higher statistical power of GSEA, which uses the full ranked gene list rather than a binary DEG classification. Redundant GO terms were removed using semantic similarity (Wang method, GOSemSim v2.36.0; Yu et al. 2010), retaining the most significant representative from each cluster of terms with pairwise similarity ≥ 0.7.

Generally Applicable Gene Set Enrichment (GAGE) Analysis

To assess pathway‐level transcriptional responses, we performed a gene set based enrichment analysis using the GAGE R package (v2.60.0; Luo et al. 2009), which identifies co‐ordinately regulated KEGG pathways by comparing expression changes across entire gene sets, rather than single DEGs (Paul et al. 2022). DESeq2 size‐factor‐normalized counts were aggregated to the KO term level using the mol.sum function and log2(x + 1) transformed prior to analysis. Significant pathways were identified at a q‐value < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg FDR), with the additional requirement that at least two of the sample replicates individually showed a consistent directional shift (per‐sample p < 0.05), providing a sample‐level consistency filter against pathway calls driven by a single outlier sample. Redundancy was reduced by prioritizing pathways with the most distinct and functionally relevant gene contributions using the esset.grp function in the GAGE R package. The R package Pathview (v1.50.0; Luo and Brouwer 2013) was used for visualization.

Phenotypic Data Analysis

Survival and reproduction were recorded daily up until the release of the first brood. Thereafter, survival was recorded daily, while reproduction was monitored three times per week until host death. Life‐history traits, that is, age at first reproduction (AFR), brood size at first reproduction (BSFR), offspring production and survival were recorded as resistance‐related fitness components. AFR was defined as the age of releasing the first brood from the brood pouch. Animals that had died before the first infected individual died were excluded, as this death was likely due to non‐natural causes. Animals that were sampled for the RNA‐sequencing experiment and animals that died because they were mishandled in the lab were also excluded (Table S4).

BSFR and total offspring production were analysed with generalized linear models (GLMs) using a negative binomial distribution and implemented in R using the glm.nb function of the MASS package (v7.3.65; Venables and Ripley 2002), with treatment as a fixed factor. Survival and AFR were analyzed using GLMs with a gamma distribution. For each trait, a null model and a treatment model were fitted, and the most parsimonious model, that is, the one with the lowest corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc), was selected using the dredge function of the MuMIn R package (v1.48.11; Barton 2009). Statistical significance of treatment effects was assessed using Type II Wald tests. Post hoc comparisons were computed using estimated marginal means with Tukey adjustment, as implemented in the emmeans R package (v2.0.1; Lenth 2016). To test whether BSFR predicts lifetime offspring reproduction, we fitted a negative binomial GLM with total offspring as the response and BSFR, treatment, and their interaction as predictors. All statistical analyses were performed using R v4.5.2, while for visualization we used the R package ggplot2 (v4.0.1; Wickham 2016).

Results

Effects of Parasite Exposure on Life‐History Traits

Survival differed significantly among treatments (χ 2 = 3459.3, df = 2, p < 0.001). Exposed‐uninfected (E) and unexposed (C) animals lived longer than infected (I) individuals (mean ratio E/I = 3.47, p < 0.0001; mean ratio C/I = 3.36, p < 0.0001; Figure 2a), while there was no difference in survival between those two groups (mean ratio C/E = 0.97, p = 0.5371; Figure 2a). AFR differed among treatments (χ 2 = 80.37, df = 2, p < 0.001). Exposed‐uninfected individuals started reproducing significantly later than unexposed (mean ratio E/C = 1.22, p < 0.0001) and infected individuals (mean ratio E/I = 1.25, p < 0.0001), while unexposed and infected individuals did not differ (mean ratio C/I = 1.02, p = 0.6143; Figure 2b). Brood size at first reproduction (BSFR) was significantly affected by infection (χ 2 = 85.12, df = 2, p < 0.001), with infected individuals producing less offspring than both exposed‐uninfected (mean ratio I/E = 0.54, p < 0.0001) and unexposed animals (mean ratio I/C = 0.63, p < 0.0001; Figure 2c). However, there was no significant difference between unexposed and exposed‐uninfected animals (mean ratio C/E = 0.86, p = 0.0685; Figure 2c), indicating that exposure without infection does not affect BSFR. Finally, total offspring production differed significantly among treatments (χ 2 = 1491.0, df = 2, p < 0.001). Infected individuals produced substantially fewer offspring than both unexposed (C) and exposed‐uninfected individuals (mean ratio C/I = 23.72, p < 0.0001; mean ratio E/I = 21.63, p < 0.0001; Figure 2d). However, there was no significant difference between unexposed and exposed‐uninfected animals (mean ratio C/E = 1.10, p = 0.6862; Figure 2d). The relationship between BSFR and total offspring differed among treatments (BSFR × treatment interaction: χ 2 = 131.07, df = 2, p < 0.001). BSFR significantly predicted lifetime reproduction in infected individuals (slope ratio = 1.43, 95% CI = 1.35–1.51), but not in unexposed (slope ratio = 1.02, 95% CI = 0.99–1.05) or exposed‐uninfected individuals (slope ratio = 1.01, 95% CI = 0.98–1.03).

(d) Total number of offspring across lifetime. Points represent individual values; boxplots show medians and interquartile range (IQR). White circles indicate group means, and bars indicate 95% CI. Effects of Parasite Exposure and Infection on Gene Expression of Individual Genes

We identified significant shifts in the transcriptomic landscape associated with parasite exposure and infection (Venn Diagram: Figure S3). In exposed‐uninfected individuals compared to unexposed controls (E vs. C), we identified 17 differentially expressed genes (12 upregulated, five downregulated; Figure 3a, Figure S4a, Table S5). GO‐ORA did not yield any significant enrichment results.

(a) Heat maps showing differentially up‐ and downregulated genes across three pairwise comparisons (E vs. C, I vs. C, and I vs. E). For visualization, the normalized gene counts are plotted, with coloured bar representing z‐scores. Red indicates upregulation and blue indicates downregulation in the first condition relative to the second in each pairwise comparison. (b) GO‐ORA enrichment analysis for biological process (BP) ontology. Circle size represents the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with each GO term, while colour indicates the p‐value.

In infected individuals compared to unexposed controls (I vs. C), we identified 313 differentially expressed genes (99 upregulated, 214 downregulated; Figure 3a, Figure S4b, Table S6). GO‐ORA analysis revealed enrichment of ontologies involved in triglyceride homeostasis (GO: 0070328; p = 0.001) and leukocyte activation (GO: 0045321; p = 0.005) among genes upregulated in infected animals. In contrast, several key metabolic and physiological processes were enriched among downregulated genes. Terms related to lipid utilization, including fatty acid oxidation (GO: 0019395; p = 0.024) and lipid catabolic process (GO: 0016042; p < 0.001), as well as sterol metabolic process (GO: 0016125; p = 0.042), were significantly enriched. Terms related to response to bacterium (GO: 0009617; p = 0.024), response to toxic substance (GO: 0009636; p = 0.028), and xenobiotic metabolic process (GO: 0006805; p = 0.012) were also significantly enriched, suggesting reduced detoxification and certain defence processes in infected individuals (Figure 3b, Figure S5).

In infected individuals compared to exposed‐uninfected ones (I vs. E), we captured the most extensive gene expression changes. Specifically, we identified 415 differentially expressed genes (185 upregulated, 230 downregulated; Figure 3a, Figure S4c, Table S7). GO‐ORA analysis revealed pronounced differences in barrier, immune and metabolic processes. Among genes upregulated in infected individuals, enriched categories were dominated by lipid remodelling processes, i.e., ether lipid metabolic process (GO: 0046485; p < 0.001), triglyceride homeostasis (GO: 0070328; p = 0.001), triglyceride catabolic process (GO: 0019433; p = 0.001), fatty‐acyl‐CoA biosynthetic process (GO: 0046949; p = 0.003) and long‐chain fatty acid metabolic process (GO: 0001676; p = 0.018) as well as peroxisome‐related GO terms, that is, peroxisomal transport (GO: 0006625; p = 0.007) and protein localization to peroxisome (GO: 0072662; p = 0.006). Additional enrichment of leukocyte activation (GO: 0045321; p = 0.009) suggests a simultaneous activation of immune components. In contrast, genes downregulated in infected individuals (i.e., relatively upregulated in exposed‐uninfected animals) were strongly enriched for cuticle‐ and chitin‐associated processes, including chitin‐based cuticle development (GO: 0040003; p < 0.001), moulting cycle chitin‐based cuticle (GO: 0007591; p = 0.013), moulting cycle process (GO: 0022404; p = 0.032) and chitin catabolic process (GO: 0006032; p = 0.001). Immune signalling categories such as response to fungus (GO: 0009620; p < 0.001), cytokine production (GO: 0001816; p = 0.027), and response to cytokine (GO: 0034097; p = 0.049) were also enriched among downregulated genes (Figure 3b, Figure S5). Taken together, these results indicate that exposed‐uninfected individuals exhibit stronger activation of cuticle‐associated and immune signalling pathways, whereas infected individuals display a shift towards lipid metabolic remodelling and peroxisomal activity.

Effects of Parasite Exposure and Infection on Coordinated Shifts in Gene Expression

In the exposed‐uninfected versus control comparison (E vs. C; 118 enriched GO terms in BP), GSEA showed strong positive enrichment of cuticle‐ and chitin‐related processes (GO: 0040003: NES = 2.36, p < 0.001) and pathways associated with moulting (GO: 0022404: NES = 2.01, p < 0.001). Several positively enriched GO terms, such as JNK signalling (GO: 0046330: NES = 2.14; p < 0.001) and regulation of small GTPase mediated signal transduction (GO: 0051056: NES = 2.02; p < 0.001), are consistent with activation of innate cellular immune processes (Table S8). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using GAGE revealed coordinated positive enrichment of immune‐associated (T cell receptor signalling: stat.mean = 1.99, q = 0.010; Chemokine signalling: stat.mean = 1.87, q = 0.015; Leukocyte transendothelial migration: stat.mean = 1.98, q = 0.010) and regulatory signalling pathways (Hippo signalling pathway: stat.mean = 2.42, q = 0.005; PI3K‐Akt signalling pathway: stat.mean = 2.17, q = 0.006; Wnt signalling pathway: stat.mean = 1.86, q = 0.015) in exposed‐uninfected individuals (Figure 4a,b; Table S9). KEGG pathways such as Ribosome (stat.mean = −5.13, q < 0.001) and oxidative phosphorylation (stat.mean = −2.26, q = 0.008) were negatively enriched in exposed‐uninfected individuals, indicating suppression of protein synthesis and mitochondrial respiration (27 enriched KEGG pathways; Figure 4a,b; Table S9).

(b) Heat maps showing KEGG pathways uniquely enriched within a single pairwise comparison. KEGG functional categories are indicated by coloured squares adjacent to each pathway name. Heat map colours represent the stat.mean value from GAGE, defined as the mean of per‐sample two‐sided t‐statistics (i.e., fold change z‐scores) across all samples within each comparison group. Positive values indicate relative upregulation in the first condition of the comparison, whereas negative values indicate relative downregulation.

In the infected versus control comparison (I vs. C; 44 enriched GO terms in BP), GSEA showed a negative enrichment of translational and mitochondrial pathways alongside upregulation of adhesion, morphogenetic and signalling processes, indicating systemic metabolic suppression coupled with structural remodelling (Table S10). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using GAGE revealed coordinated negative enrichment of core translational and mitochondrial functions (Oxidative phosphorylation: stat.mean = −3.11, q < 0.001; Ribosome: stat.mean = −2.90, q < 0.001; Ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes: stat.mean = −2.72, q < 0.001), RNA‐processing pathways (Spliceosome: stat.mean = −2.80, q < 0.001), as well as metabolism (Porphyrin metabolism: stat.mean = −1.73, q = 0.015). In contrast, infection‐associated shifts were reflected by positive enrichment of the endocytosis pathway (stat.mean = 2.06, q = 0.005; 17 enriched KEGG pathways; Figure 4a,b; Table S11).

In the infected versus exposed‐uninfected comparison (I vs. E; 21 enriched GO terms in BP), GSEA showed a suppression of ribosome biogenesis and RNA‐related pathways in infected individuals, indicating downscaling of transcriptional and translational activity relative to exposed‐uninfected animals (Table S12). In contrast, sphingolipid metabolic process (GO: 0006665: NES = 2.18, p < 0.001) and trafficking processes (GO: 0006888: endoplasmic reticulum to Golgi vesicle‐mediated transport, NES = 1.97, p < 0.001) were positively enriched in infected individuals, consistent with infection‐associated membrane remodelling (Table S12). GSEA in KO terms revealed a significant negative enrichment of chitinase in infected individuals (positive in exposed‐uninfected; NES = −2.01, p < 0.001; Figure S6). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using GAGE revealed coordinated positive enrichment of infection‐associated pathways, including Lysosome (stat.mean = 2.71, q < 0.001) and Phagosome (stat.mean = 2.18, q = 0.001) in infected individuals. In contrast, RNA‐processing pathways were negatively enriched in infected individuals relative to exposed‐uninfected animals, consistent with the pattern observed in the infected versus control comparison (15 enriched KEGG pathways; Figure 4a,b; Table S13).

Taken together, these results indicate coordinated transcriptional shifts along the C → E → I gradient. Exposure is characterized by activation of immune‐related signalling and structural reinforcement processes, whereas infection is associated with a transition towards metabolic suppression and cellular remodelling.

Discussion

The Immune Landscape of Exposed‐Uninfected Hosts

To interpret the life‐history and transcriptional responses of the exposed‐uninfected individuals (E), it is necessary to consider their trajectories during the infection process. Individuals classified as exposed‐uninfected showed no visible signs of infection. Clearance of fully established infections has not been reported in the Daphnia–Metschnikowia system, suggesting that infection outcomes are likely determined during early stages following gut penetration, via haemocyte recruitment around parasite entry sites (Stewart Merrill et al. 2019, 2021). Thus, exposed‐uninfected individuals likely either prevented parasite establishment or eliminated infection prior to parasite proliferation. Although our experimental design cannot distinguish between these scenarios, the distinct transcriptional profile of exposed‐uninfected animals indicates that their trajectory had already diverged substantially from that of infected hosts.

In exposed‐uninfected individuals (E vs. C), parasite exposure was associated with positive enrichment of cuticle‐ and chitin‐related processes (NES = 2.3) and moulting‐associated pathways (NES = 2.0). This enrichment may reflect structural remodelling or reinforcement of epithelial barriers rather than an increase in moulting frequency per se. Although moulting has been proposed as a defence mechanism in crustaceans (Shields 2012; Zuo et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2020), including Daphnia (Duneau and Ebert 2012), it is unlikely to directly prevent Metschnikowia infection because the parasite invades via the gut rather than attaching externally. We therefore interpret the enrichment of cuticle remodelling and moulting‐associated pathways as an indication of coordinated changes in structural processes that might be linked to epithelial maintenance or invertebrate immunity (Xu et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022). Because chitin is a key structural component of the peritrophic matrix and gut epithelium in arthropods, enrichment of chitin‐binding and cuticle‐development processes may reflect epithelial turnover or barrier reinforcement. A similar pattern was reported in the D. dentifera–M. bicuspidata system, in which cuticle‐development genes were upregulated shortly after exposure (Terrill Sondag et al. 2023). In contrast, comparable responses were not detected in systems involving pathogens such as Pasteuria ramosa or microsporidia (McTaggart et al. 2015; Lu et al. 2018). This difference likely reflects distinct infection strategies. Pasteuria ramosa attaches to the host's oesophagus before penetrating the body cavity, whereas microsporidia infect epithelial cells intracellularly without requiring extensive mechanical disruption of the gut barrier (Ebert 2005). Given that Metschnikowia is a fungal parasite with chitin‐containing cell walls, enrichment of chitin‐binding processes may additionally reflect a role in pathogen recognition. Genomic analyses of Daphnia pulex have identified candidate pattern‐recognition genes (McTaggart et al. 2009), suggesting that Daphnia possesses molecular components capable of innate immune detection. However, the specific ligand recognized by these receptors, including whether they directly recognize fungal components, remains poorly characterized in Daphnia.

Beyond cuticle‐associated responses, exposed‐uninfected individuals showed coordinated enrichment of immune‐associated and regulatory signalling pathways. GO and KEGG analyses revealed positive enrichment of JNK signalling, small GTPase mediated signal transduction, PI3K‐Akt, Wnt, Hippo, and pathways annotated as T cell receptor signalling, chemokine signalling, and leukocyte transendothelial migration. Although these latter pathways are associated with vertebrate immunity (Magor and Magor 2001; Buchmann 2014; Wang and Knaut 2014; Shah et al. 2021), their enrichment in Daphnia should not be interpreted as evidence that the corresponding vertebrate immune processes occur in this species. Instead, the results likely reflect coordinated expression changes among intracellular signalling, adhesion, and cytoskeletal components that are evolutionarily conserved between vertebrates and invertebrates.

In vertebrates, chemokine signalling directs leukocytes towards the endothelium, and leukocyte transendothelial migration enables their passage into tissues. However, invertebrates lack leukocytes. Instead, they employ haemocytes as their primary immune cells, and it is their mobilization that plays a key role in pathogen defence (Lavine and Strand 2002; Coates et al. 2022). Previous observations in the Daphnia–Metschnikowia system reported haemocyte aggregation within the hemolymph at sites where Metschnikowia spores have penetrated the gut (Stewart Merrill and Cáceres 2018). Thus, the enrichment of chemokine signalling and leukocyte transendothelial migration pathways may reflect coordinated changes in conserved molecular processes potentially associated with haemocyte mobilization, adhesion, or migration. These processes may be functionally analogous to vertebrate immune cell migration. Similarly, simultaneous coordinated expression shifts within the PI3K‐Akt and JNK signalling pathways may be compatible with changes in cell mobilization or cytoskeletal rearrangements which could potentially facilitate haemocyte migration and adherence to infection sites.

This enrichment of structural and immune‐associated processes occurred alongside negative enrichment of several metabolic pathways. Exposed‐uninfected individuals exhibited significant negative enrichment of cytoplasmic translation, ribosomal components, and oxidative phosphorylation, which may reflect a reduction in protein biosynthesis and aerobic energy metabolism during parasite exposure.

Immune responses can impose energetic costs, and the simultaneous positive enrichment of immune‐associated processes and negative enrichment of ribosomal and mitochondrial pathways suggests that resources were reallocated away from growth towards defence and tissue maintenance. This interpretation remains tentative because resource allocation and metabolic flux were not measured directly. Nevertheless, it is also consistent with the delayed reproductive maturation observed in exposed‐uninfected individuals. Immune‐reproduction trade‐offs have been observed in other invertebrates. For example, increased haemocyte investment has been associated with reproduction and survival trade‐offs in snails (Rigby and Jokela 2000), while resistant individuals exhibited delayed maturation (Langand et al. 1998). In scallops, reduced haemocyte function following spawning has similarly been interpreted as an indication of an energy‐mediated trade‐off between reproduction and immunity (Brokordt et al. 2019). Because RNA‐sequencing was conducted before the onset of reproduction, our transcriptional data may have captured this early physiological response associated with parasite exposure and delayed maturation. The subsequent recovery of reproduction suggests that the initial delay was transient, although the mechanism underlying this recovery cannot be determined from our data. Such compensation may have involved adjustments in the number of produced clutches or in brood size, traits that are highly responsive to environmental conditions in Daphnia (Ebert 2005). In other systems, fecundity compensation has also been associated with immune activation (Pike et al. 2019), suggesting that such responses may arise as part of a broader physiological reaction to parasite exposure. Our findings likely suggest that exposure‐related immune or structural physiological responses temporarily postponed reproduction rather than imposed a permanent fitness cost. This form of compensation should be distinguished from the terminal investment hypothesis (Clutton‐Brock 1984; Duffield et al. 2017), in which infected individuals increase early reproductive effort because future reproduction is threatened by mortality (Chadwick and Little 2005) or sterilization (Vale and Little 2012). In other Daphnia‐microparasite systems, infection by sterilizing pathogens can induce shifts towards increased early reproduction, thereby improving host fitness without directly reducing parasite loads (Vale and Little 2012).

The Immune Landscape of Infected Hosts

Infection with Metschnikowia was associated with pronounced and coordinated expression changes in metabolic pathways in Daphnia. In infected hosts (I vs. C), GAGE analysis identified significant downregulation of gene sets associated with ribosomal biogenesis, rRNA processing, cytoplasmic translation and spliceosomal activity, together with gene sets related to oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial ATP synthesis. These processes represent the core anabolic and energetic machinery of the cell, and their downregulation may indicate a systemic reduction in biosynthetic capacity. Because ribosome biogenesis and mitochondrial respiration are among the most energetically demanding cellular processes (Shore and Albert 2022), their downregulation is consistent with a possible large‐scale shift in energy allocation.

Enrichment of porphyrin and heme metabolism in downregulated DEGs is also consistent with this interpretation. Porphyrins are precursors of heme (Dailey 1997), which is a crucial component of cytochromes, including Cytochrome P450 enzymes (Fisher et al. 1973). Reduced expression of genes associated with porphyrin metabolism may therefore be consistent with reduced cytochrome‐dependent respiration and electron transport chain activity, complementing the downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation observed at the pathway level. Because heme‐containing enzymes such as peroxidases and cytochrome P450s also contribute to detoxification and oxidative stress responses (Raven and Dunford 2015; Nappi and Ottaviani 2000; Snyder 2000), reduced heme biosynthesis could additionally constrain immune‐associated metabolic functions. Taken together, these results are consistent with a broad transcriptional reduction in metabolic and biosynthetic processes in infected hosts and may reflect that Daphnia suffered from metabolic exhaustion.

In infected hosts, downregulated DEGs were also enriched for lipid catabolic processes and fatty acid oxidation. Because fatty acids serve as substrates for mitochondrial β‐oxidation, this pattern is concordant with a simultaneous downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation pathways. Compared to healthy individuals, infected animals appear to reduce lipid utilization for energy production, suggesting a shift away from core metabolic processes during infection. A similar suppression of lipid metabolism has been reported in Daphnia exposed to microparasites (Lu et al. 2018). According to the metabolic cost hypothesis, many organisms shift energy allocation away from reproduction towards survival or the maintenance of homeostasis under stress conditions (Rokitta et al. 2014; Lu et al. 2016). Consistent with this interpretation, infected individuals produced fewer offspring than both unexposed control (I vs. C) and exposed‐uninfected individuals (I vs. E). Such reduced fecundity aligns with infection‐induced life‐history trade‐offs documented in invertebrates, including insects and Daphnia, where energy allocation shifts away from reproduction under pathogenic stress (Ebert et al. 2005; Lohr et al. 2010; Schwenke et al. 2016; Paraskevopoulou et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2023). The lower offspring number at first reproduction therefore suggests that infection‐associated metabolic reprogramming likely limits early reproductive investment.

Divergent Gene Regulation Between Exposed‐Uninfected and Infected Individuals

Differential regulation between exposed‐uninfected and infected individuals (I vs. E) reveals the transcriptional patterns that distinguish established infection from exposure. Processes associated with cuticle development, chitin metabolism, and moulting were enriched in exposed‐uninfected individuals, but were significantly downregulated in infected hosts. This reversal suggests that the epithelial reinforcement and immune‐related structural programs associated with exposure are not maintained once infection is established. Accordingly, infected individuals exhibited negative enrichment of rRNA processing, ribosome biogenesis, spliceosomal components and nucleocytoplasmic transport, consistent with a broader suppression of processes associated with the gene expression machinery beyond that observed during exposure alone.

In contrast, pathways related to vesicular trafficking, lysosome and phagosome function, endocytosis, fatty acid metabolism and peroxisomal activity were upregulated in infected individuals, suggesting coordinated changes in membrane dynamics and lipid metabolism during established infection. Such lipid‐associated changes may contribute to immune signalling processes and the production of lipid‐derived mediators involved in inflammatory and stress responses during infection. In insects like Drosophila melanogaster , infection triggers the synthesis and secretion of effector proteins, usually antimicrobial peptides that are produced in the fat body (Martínez et al. 2020). Consistent with this, immune activation can shift lipid metabolism away from energy storage towards pathways supporting immune signalling and cellular defence processes (Wrońska et al. 2023). Because Daphnia relies heavily on lipids as a primary internal energy reserve (Tessier et al. 1983), infection‐associated lipid remodelling may reflect a reallocation of these reserves towards immune and cellular defence mechanisms.

Taken together, these patterns suggest that established infection is not simply an amplification of the responses observed following exposure. Instead, it is characterized by a reduced representation of early barrier‐associated processes coupled with broader transcriptional changes affecting biosynthetic and metabolic functions.

Conclusions and Limitations

Our results indicate that host responses to Metschnikowia follow function‐specific trajectories rather than a single linear gradient of stress (Figure 5). Pathogen exposure without established infection (E) was associated with coordinated expression shifts in immune‐ and cuticle‐related processes, accompanied by negative enrichment of core metabolic and translational pathways, a pattern consistent with a possible reallocation of resources towards defence‐related processes. These exposure‐associated transcriptional changes coincided with delayed reproductive maturation, followed by recovery of total reproductive output, suggesting that the initial life‐history cost was transient. In contrast, established infection (I) was characterized by more pronounced transcriptional changes in gene expression machinery and an increased representation of vesicle‐mediated and lysosomal processes. Cuticle‐associated processes that were enriched during exposure were downregulated once infection was established, whereas core biosynthetic functions showed progressively stronger transcriptional reductions from control to exposure to infection.

Schematic overview of life‐history and transcriptomic trajectories following pathogen exposure to and infection by the yeast Metschnikowia bicuspidata in Daphnia magna .

These patterns support a model in which pathogen exposure initiates an immune‐ and structure‐associated state that may be reversible but entails a temporary life‐history cost, whereas established infection represents a more constrained physiological state characterized by a broader transcriptional suppression and metabolic reorganization. Importantly, infection does not simply amplify exposure responses, but instead, distinct functional modules follow different trajectories across control, exposure, and infection states. Distinguishing pathogen exposure from established infection is therefore essential for accurately interpreting the potential costs of immune responses, resistance outcomes, and the temporal dynamics of host physiology and resource allocation under pathogenic challenge.

RNA‐seq‐based transcriptomic analyses are influenced by sequencing depth and biological replication, and therefore involve inherent practical trade‐offs. Although our experimental design and quality control procedures were sufficient to detect broad transcriptional patterns, the limited biological replication reduces confidence in subtle expression differences and in the functional interpretation of modest pathway‐level changes. Further validation with functional assays will be necessary if the transcriptomic signals identified here are to be developed into markers for epidemic forecasting or monitoring in freshwater systems.
