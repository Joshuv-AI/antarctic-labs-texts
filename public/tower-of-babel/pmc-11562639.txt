Shivering Hopes: A Qualitative Inquiry into the Experiences of Family Caregivers of Critically Ill Patients Reliant on Health Care Technology
Authors: Olaolorunpo Olorunfemi, Chinomso Ugochukwu Nwozichi, Christiana Olarewaju Sowunmi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimThe aim of the study was to assess the lived experience of family caregivers of critically ill patients sustained by healthcare technologies in Benin-City, Nigeria.MethodsThis study employed a hermeneutic phenomenological design. Data were collected through in-depth individual interviews with ten participants, observations, and field notes. The analysis was conducted using Paul Ricoeur’s Theory of Interpretation and managed with Nvivo data management software. The study was conducted at the University of Benin Teaching Hospital (UBTH) from August 2022 to December 2023, with a sample size of 10 participants.ResultsThe study’s findings revealed that the majority (70%) of family caregivers were at least 30 years old, Christian, and had spent a minimum of two weeks in the intensive care unit (ICU). Ninety percent had received post-secondary education. Three themes emerged from their experiences: “Journeying into the unknown,” “Technological care is an ecosystem,” and “Exhibiting emotional intelligence.” In addition to that, the findings also showed that upon learning of their loved one’s’’’ transfer to the ICU, caregivers experienced mixed emotions, including palpable fear of death. This fear was linked to the high costs of management, a tense hospital environment, lack of involvement in care, institutional shortcomings, and diminished motivation due to frequent deaths in the ICU.ConclusionFamily caregivers must be engaged in formulating and implementing care plans to provide holistic care that meets the needs of both patients and their caregivers. Hospital administrators should take proactive steps to reduce treatment costs, making care more affordable for families. Furthermore, comprehensive programs aimed at addressing the institutional flaws identified in this study must be implemented urgently.

Introduction

The advancement of healthcare technology has revolutionized the management of critically ill patients, improving survival rates and enhancing patient outcomes. However, as healthcare technology becomes more integral to the treatment process, it presents unique challenges for family caregivers, who must navigate the emotional and psychological complexities of caring for a loved one reliant on life-sustaining devices. Family caregivers are often overwhelmed by the sight of their loved ones connected to machines, leading to emotional distress, anxiety, and a sense of helplessness (Pepito & Locsin, 2019). These challenges are particularly significant in Nigerian cultural contexts, where caregiving responsibilities are traditionally borne by close relatives, even within formal healthcare settings (Olorunfemi et al., 2024). Existing literature highlights the benefits of healthcare technology in improving life expectancy and patient quality of life (Lakdawalla et al., 2018), but there is a gap in understanding the lived experiences of family caregivers who witness and support their loved ones in such high-tech environments. Although studies like Washington et al. (2018) have explored caregiver stress, there remains a need to examine how caregivers from diverse cultural backgrounds, especially in African settings, cope with these challenges. Furthermore, there is limited research on the emotional and psychological impact of witnessing the intersection of life-saving technology and critical illness from the caregivers’ perspective. Bridging this gap, the current study aims to provide a deeper understanding of the experiences, struggles, and resilience of family caregivers caring for critically ill patients reliant on healthcare technology. Through this research, we explored the emotional journey of these caregivers and how technology impacts their caregiving roles. Highlighting their voices, this study concludes by recommending policies and practices that can better support family caregivers in high-tech healthcare environments. This study was conducted at the University of Benin Teaching Hospital (UBTH) from August 2022 to December 2023. This qualitative study involved a sample of 10 family caregivers actively involved in caring for critically ill patients reliant on healthcare technology. The in-depth exploration of their experiences provides valuable insights into the complexities and emotional dimensions of caregiving in a technologically advanced healthcare setting. Concentrating on this under-researched area, this study sheds light on the significant emotional and psychological burdens faced by family caregivers, offering recommendations for support strategies that can improve their well-being and resilience.

Research Question

1. What is the lived experience of family caregivers when caring for critically ill patients reliant on healthcare technology?

Methods

Study Design

The study was conducted at the University of Benin Teaching Hospital (UBTH) from August 2022 to December 2023, with a sample size of 10 participants. This study used a qualitative, hermeneutic phenomenological design that included in-depth interviews. In this regards, SRQRChecklist of qualitative study was used.

Sample

The study population was made up of family caregivers who were actively involved in caring for critically sick patients supported by health technologies in the intensive care unit (ICU) of the University of Benin Teaching Hospital, Benin-City, Edo state, Nigeria. The data saturation approach was used to calculate the sample size, and the researcher recruited 10 participants by purposive sampling. This strategy ensured that volunteers had adequate experience caring for critically ill patients in an ICU. Caregivers with less than a week of ICU experience were excluded, as well as those who refused to be audio recorded.

Data Collection

Tape recording was used for in-depth interview, individual interviews were conducted to explore and describe the phenomenon, with the researcher using bracketing to avoid bias. Recruiting participants involved writing a letter outlining the study and providing contact information. Before the interview process, the researchers met with participants to discuss the study’s content, address questions, provide clarifications, and they were advised prior to each interview that they could request verbatim transcripts of their interviews if desired. Data was gathered through in-depth interviews, analyzed, and interpreted through Paul Ricoeur’’’s theory.

Data Collection Tools

Tape Recording: Recording the interviews to capture detailed responses from participants.

In-depth Interviews: Conducting detailed, one-on-one interviews to explore participants’ experiences and perspectives.

Observations: Noting non-verbal cues, behaviors, and environmental context during the interviews or interactions.

Field Notes: The researcher’s written records of their observations, thoughts, and reflections during the research process

Ethical Considerations

Ethics committee approval was received for this study from the ethics committee of University of Benin Teaching Hospital (Approval no: ADM/E22/A/VOLV11/1411735165, Date: August 11, 2022). Additionally, approval was received from the Research and Ethical Committee of Babcock University (Approval no: ADM/E22/A/VOLV11/1411735165, Date: August 11, 2022). After receiving the study’s eligible participants’ signed agreements, the study was allowed to continue. Respondents were informed about the purpose and benefits of the study. They were also informed of their right to withdraw at any time without any consequences.

Results

The study’s participants were family caregivers who cared for critically ill patients who were being sustained by technology and were interviewed in this study. Table 1 summarizes the demographics of the participants, while Table 2 summarizes the themes and sub-themes that emerged from the collected data.

Theme 1: Journeying Toward the Unknown

This theme captures the experiences of patient relatives during the early days following their loved one’s transfer to the ICU. As they come to terms with the severity of the situation, they are faced with a deep sense of uncertainty about their loved one’s prognosis and the path ahead.

Anxious yet Hopeful

A jumbled feeling mixed with anxiety was this relative’s description of his feelings about the transfer of his wife to the ICU.

”It was a jumbled feeling of acute anxiety about a disastrous outcome and flimsy, shaky hopes and expectations of survival, backed up by fervent, fervent prayer to God.” (Patient relative 1)

Another relative was more terrified than anxious, especially upon seeing her aunt on various gadgets.

“……I was terrified. But that is the only available option to help her, now that she is here for week.” (Patient relative 5)

Worried about Unpredictable Outcome

For most of the patient relatives, the ICU is a place of unknown fate. In the thoughts of one of the relatives, the uncertainty about the outcome of care was palpable, but all she could do was hope for a better outcome.

”It’s not a pleasant situation, and I’m sorry because we all know the intensive care unit is a 50/50 place.” (Patient relative 2)

Belief in Healthcare Technology

The thought of devices in the ICU alone contributed to either a positive or negative experience with the care of their loved one. A relative stated that she believes that is the only way for their loved one to survive the current health challenges.

”To me, without these machines, my aunt’s life cannot be saved; removing these devices means they will simply tell me that my aunty is no longer alive.” (Patient relative 5)

Death in the Corner

The horror of witnessing the deaths of other patients gave this relative a feeling of death lurking around his wife.

”…..My experience has been a bad one because I am always afraid when the nurses come out of the ward, as if they are about to announce my wife’s death. After all, my wife had been unconscious for about a week after being transferred here.” (Patient relative 1)

Another relative considers the sound of flatlining coming from the device a bad sign for his own relative.

”The only thing I can hear coming from the ward is the sound of various alarms; I get scared every time one goes off. It appears to be a bad omen.” (Patient relative 3)

Technological Care is an Ecosystem

This theme describes the view of patient relatives about technological care in relation to interactions involved, the financial implication, emotions, and outcomes. This view of technological care as an ecosystem implies that the experience of the patient relatives has been shaped by the various circumstances surrounding the care delivered or received by their loved one.

Built on Interaction

For some of the patients’ relatives, their experience with technological care was built on their interaction with the custodians of care, especially the nurses. They usually have bitter-and-sweet stories to tell about such interactions. This has also mostly reflected how they judge the competencies and work ethics of the nurses.

One of the patient’s relatives was not too impressed with the way some healthcare professionals handled the situation when asked some questions.

”When we first arrived, I’m not sure; I think they’re nurses or doctors, but I’m not sure what their specialization is. They were both men. They just rushed in and took my aunty to the intensive care unit; we tried to follow them in, but they wouldn’t let anyone in. So we went outside to pray. Personally, I was concerned and dissatisfied with their decision.” (Patient relative 5)

Some other relatives did not share this sentiment, especially regarding the nurses who cared for their loved one.

”Based on my personal experience with the nurses in the intensive care unit, I can say that they are trying, that they provide feedback, and that they also show sympathy and care. I like the nurses in the ICU, as opposed to the nurses on the other wards, who are rude and arrogant.” (Patient relative 7)

Supported by Money

The participants had a major experience with the cost of care. They described the enormity of the financial implications of receiving care and being supported by technological devices. For some, the cost is nothing if and only if their loved one survives, while for others, the cost of care has affected every aspect of their lives. From one relative’s experience, the bills in a unit that cares for patients using technology were as high as expected, but as long as her loved one recovers, she does not mind.

”Since I was aware of the high expense of housing patients in that facility, I did not feel horrible when I received the bill. Thus, I wasn’t surprised when I got the bill. Whenever you pay or receive whatever it is you are paying for in a hospital and you see the patient recover, you will be paying it with delight, as was my case, regardless of how large the bill they offer you is.” (Patient relative 9)

Conditioned by Emotions

In describing this subtheme, the patient’s relatives recalled how the sound of technological devices made them feel. Interestingly, they somehow connected the gadgets to their daily emotions while waiting for their loved one outside the ICU.

”For some relatives like this one, the sound was unsettling coupled with sadness and tears. It was upsetting for everyone to witness. So that’s one of the major things I’ve seen here, and it’s been one death after another since then.” (Patient relative 5)

However, another participant demonstrated that their emotions were fear-based.

”The only thing I can hear coming from the ward is the sound of various alarms; I get scared every time one goes off. It appears to be a bad omen…. It’s terrifying, but we just have to keep praying and hoping.” (Patient relative 3)

Filled With Unmet Relatives’ Basic Needs

In their experience, sacrificing personal comfort for the sake of their loved one who is receiving technological care was common. The sacrifice is necessary but facilities for that can help meet basic needs are not available. Therefore, their experience with technological support in care was riddled with unmet needs, which were mostly blamed on the hospital management.

”….I didn’t expect to have to stay outside the unit’s corridor on the first day, in a cold environment with a lot of mosquito bites, it was not a pleasant experience at all.” (Patient relative 6)

Evaluated by Outcomes

Some participants attributed the relevance of technological care to the expected outcome of the treatment. The ecosystem they believe is not worth it when recovery does not happen, especially at their imagined speed.

”With all of these different gadgets and machines used on him, I expect my husband to recover in a matter of weeks. His condition does not appear to be improving, in my opinion...” (Patient relative 4)

Hope Reinforced

At this point, some participants whose relatives have been successfully discharged from the ICU, where they needed technological care, to the regular ward described how renewed their hopes have been and the relief they have experienced during that time. Gratitude to the healthcare providers became part of their experience. One patient’s relative recounted how grateful he is each time he remembers his experience with nurses at the ICU and said,

“In fact, I always say ‘thank you’ to the nurses. I always arrive at the intensive care unit with joy to express my gratitude to the doctors and nurses there. Given how seriously ill she was, it surprises me that my mother is suddenly having fun with the nurses. Every day she was getting better, which made me glad.” (Patient relative 9)

Discussion

Journeying Toward the Unknown

In this study, everyone’s experiences were distinct, ranging from being quite delighted with the knowledge to seeing the entire affair as a journey into the unknown. Hopelessness can frequently significantly impact people’s behavior because it reflects how they feel about the future (Kredentser et al., 2018; Olorunfemi & Nwozichi, 2022).

When participants feel hopeless, they frequently lose interest in significant objects, activities, events, or people. When participants become hopeless, they lose value for things that were once critical to caring for their loved ones. This sense of helplessness, abandonment, captivity, oppression, and isolation is frequently accompanied by a lack of motivation and similar feelings of helplessness, abandonment, captivity, oppression, and isolation in relation to the situation of their loved ones sustained by health technology (Ali, 2024; Olorunfemi & Nwozichi, 2022). This finding is congruent with the results of Camponogara et al. (2013), who investigated the views and demands of relatives of patients hospitalized in an ICU. They discovered that patient relatives experienced the following emotions: anxiety, worry, anguish, sadness, powerlessness, pain, sorrow, loss, fear, panic, stress, trust, security, uncertainty, faith, hope, and unexplained feelings. They concluded that all of these feelings were related to the unusual surroundings, the various procedures, the anxiety about what would happen tomorrow, and their families’ expectations that things would improve. Although many people enter an ICU with high expectations and significant costs, most of the time the results are not what they were hoping for (Olorunfemi et al., 2024). According to a study conducted in the Enugu State University of Science and Technology Teaching Hospital: A Three-Year Retrospective Study, the overall mortality rate in the ICU during the study period was found to be 34.1%. Patients receiving intensive care in Nigeria have a high mortality rate. This is further supported by a study conducted at the University Teaching Hospital in Ilorin, Nigeria, where it was discovered that the mortality rate in the ICU was 37.3% (Omolola Adams et al., 2021). The costs of this care are also quite high, leading to psychological and emotional breakdown. The current state of Nigeria’s economy makes the ICU stressful and burdensome for the patient’s family. Family members of ICU patients have a higher risk of depression, anxiety, and stress-related disorders. These complications may impair the relatives’ ability to perform the role of caregiver, as well as their ability to function on a daily basis.

In Figure 1: The emotional responses of patients’ family members to technological care vary. Because persons with critically illnesses are unable to make their own decisions due to their illness, caregivers are essential in intensive care units (ICUs). According to Gray-Renfrew in 2020, the emotional responses of patients’ family members to technological care are connected to a psychological effect known as “condemning emotion” that family caregivers experience (Gray-Renfrew, 2020). These feelings reflect the unfavorable emotions that patients’ families or close friends go through in ICUs when they are required to participate in moral dilemmas involving the technological care provided by nurses and other medical professionals. According to this present study, this type of emotional response is more pronounced when the family members’ opinions were not taken into consideration in the care of their loved ones; the patient’s relative may have expressed these feelings out of emotions. They felt that hospital management did not permit them to participate in the planning of care and saw this as unmet needs. This finding is consistent with the findings of a study conducted by Salameh et al. (2020), which discovered that family members of ICU patients have certain unmet needs that require attention from healthcare professionals and administration in Palestinian hospitals. They concluded that understanding the needs of hospital patients, particularly those in ICUs, is essential. These family members are prone to stress, anxiety, and various other difficulties and concerns (Girdhar et al., 2020; Korkmaz et al., 2020). The source of this stress could be the high mortality rate of patients receiving intensive care, but it could also be a lack of knowledge about the course of their loved one’s illness. On the other hand, some ICU patients’ family members are involved in the care process because they are frequently required to speak on behalf of unconscious patients; therefore, their needs must be taken into account. This finding corroborates with a study carried out in 2023, which discovered that mental health issues such as caregivers’ fear, anxiety, stress, frustration, and burnout still exist in the ICU, particularly among family caregivers (Najafi Ghezeljeh et al., 2023).

Furthermore, we found that not giving family caregivers enough recognition demoralizes their emotions and this is the root cause of various violent incidents reported in the unit. As a result, the hospital management is required to provide basic facilities to meet the needs of family caregivers. Similarly, the healthcare team, particularly nurses, should keep good interpersonal relationships by focusing on shared interests and potential solutions.

In addition, in this study, the authors found that the cost of care in the ICU was a significant experience that the participants identified as a significant source of pain or financial strain. While for some respondents, the cost of care has no impact whatsoever unless and until their loved one passes away, for others, it is a financial burden that has had a profound impact on every facet of their lives. Financial strain raises participants’ self-reported stress levels and lowers their levels of risk aversion for a loved one. As a result of this, ICU nurses must demonstrate compassion, empathy, sympathy, and care for patients’ relatives in order to lessen their financial burden. This recommendation is in line with a 2013 study by Moghaddasian et al., entitled “Nurses’ Empathy and Family Needs in the Intensive Care Units,” which discovered that the patients’ families in ICUs undergo excessive stress, which may impair their ability to function in daily life and recommend empathy from nurses.

Nursing empathy is the capacity to comprehend and relate to patients while also recognizing and acknowledging their feelings (Fernandez & Zahavi, 2020). The ability to effectively communicate with patients and their families while they are receiving technological care in an ICU is a crucial component of empathy and emotional control.

Self-Conscious Emotion or Conditioned by Emotion

The patient’s relatives described this subtheme by recalling how the sound of technological devices made them feel. It’s interesting to see how they managed to link the technology to their daily emotions while waiting for their loved one outside the ICU. Negative emotions like fear, guilt, or embarrassment are triggered when people are emotionally conditioned.

Many of the family members described how frequently people died in the ICU despite medical advances. The terror was tangible to them. As they watched the nurses and doctors leave the unit, they started to wonder whose relative would be next. The horror of seeing other patients die gave this relative the impression that his or her loved ones are in danger of dying.

This finding is in line with the study of Reuvers in 2018; despite the fact that infants who are premature or sickly are admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) for specialized nursing and medical attention, their prognosis and expected outcomes can still change negatively, even while in the hospital. He concluded that there are many types of losses that parents who have a child in the NICU suffer, and these losses can generate a grief reaction as well as emotional horror, as seeing other patients die gave these mothers the sense that their children were in danger of dying (Reuvers, 2018).

Most frequently, family members ask, “Is my loved one going to die?” This may be a mirror of other unsaid hopes and anxieties. It can convey fear of pain and physical suffering, anticipatory mourning over the loss of loved ones, and existential and spiritual anxiety in confronting both the unknown and the entirety of one’s life (Harmuth, 2019, Naidoo, 2011).

Furthermore, in this study, the researcher discovered that patient relatives are not well educated about what is expected of them, which may be the primary cause of anxiety and concern reported by them. For example, one of the patients stated that he was unhappy because he was not allowed to stay with his relative receiving technological care in the unit, which caused him a great deal of concern and fear. Intensive care unit attendance has been limited, mostly due to septic concerns, which is in the best interests of patients. This frequently resulted in violent confrontations between nurses and family caregivers, endangering the nurses’ lives. As a result, there is a need to support and protect nurse practitioners within the legal framework. These legal criteria will include the protections provided to nurses while they work, as well as any gaps in the regulatory framework and the legal and regulatory safeguards now established in the Code of Conduct. It is hoped that as a result of this insight, patient relatives will address their excesses when their loved one receiving technological care passes away, minimizing violence in Nigeria’s ICU. Without the rule of law, criminal and political violence will remain a concern (Davies et al., 2023). An inadequate justice system will allow petty crime, severe crime, crimes motivated by politics or race, domestic and sexual violence, and organized crime to flourish. Publicizing the regulations and legislation governing critical care units will ensure that all parties are aware of their boundaries, fostering peace and calm within the facility. This will protect nurses, enabling them to work without fear from patient relatives when their loved ones in the ICU die, ultimately enhancing the quality of care provided.

In Nigeria, cultural norms dictate that sick family members rely heavily on other household members for care and sustenance, even when they are being treated in a healthcare setting. As a result, these family caregivers often experience significant stress from their caregiving responsibilities, compounded by the emotional distress of seeing their loved ones connected to various medical technologies. If not well managed, this can become overwhelming and stressful within clinical practice.

Study Limitations

One of the study’s limitations is that the demographic and ethnic composition of the participants does not adequately reflect the diversity of family caregivers in various hospitals. The experiences of caregiving may differ for family caregivers from diverse ethnic origins.

Conclusion and Recommendations

The study highlights the multifaceted experiences of family caregivers in the ICU, emphasizing the complex emotional and logistical challenges they face. These challenges include intense fear of death, associated stressors, high management costs, a tense hospital environment, limited involvement in care, institutional shortcomings, and frequent ICU deaths. These factors collectively underscore the profound emotional toll on caregivers. The insights from this study call for targeted support strategies to alleviate the burden on family caregivers, enhance their involvement in care, and address systemic issues within ICU settings. Implementing such strategies can improve the overall experience for caregivers and contribute to better patient outcomes.

Based on the issues encountered by family caregivers in the ICU, it is critical to establish comprehensive support systems. Hospitals should implement specific caregiver support programs that include emotional counseling, financial advice, and practical assistance. Training staff to effectively communicate with caregivers and involve them in the care process can help alleviate some of the stress and fear associated with their loved one’s critical condition. Additionally, creating a more welcoming and less tense hospital environment through improved facilities and clear information dissemination can significantly enhance caregivers’ experiences. Regular feedback from caregivers should be sought to continually refine and improve these support systems, ensuring they remain responsive to the evolving needs of caregivers.
