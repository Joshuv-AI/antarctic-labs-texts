Distributionally Robust Optimization over Wasserstein Balls with i.i.d. Structure


Abstract
. We consider distributionally robust optimization problems where the uncertainty is modeled via a structured Wasserstein ambiguity set. Specifically, the ambiguity is restricted to product measures P ⊗ N P^{\otimes N} , where P P lies within a Wasserstein ball centered at a distribution P ^ \widehat{P} . This structure reflects the assumption of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) uncertainty components and yields a non-convex ambiguity set that is strictly contained in its unstructured counterpart, thereby reducing conservatism. The resulting optimization problem is generally intractable due to the loss of convexity. We address this by introducing a sequence of tractable convex relaxations, each admitting strong duality, and prove that this sequence converges to the original problem value under suitable conditions. Numerical examples are provided to illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach. As a byproduct of our proofs, we establish a novel formula, of independent interest, relating the Wasserstein distance of a mixture of product distributions to the Wasserstein distance between its constituent measures.

1.
Introduction

In stochastic optimization a common goal is to minimize an objective
\Psi
over a set of feasible decisions
\Theta
, where the objective
\Psi
is defined as an average of a family of individual uncertainty-affected loss functions
\ell:\Theta\times X\to\mathbb{R}
, with
X
being a random vector of uncertain parameters defined on a probability space
(X,\Sigma,P)
.
In mathematical terms, a stochastic optimization method evaluates

(1)

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\Psi(\theta)\text{\ \ with\ \ }\Psi(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\ell(\theta,x)\,.

To avoid trivialities, we assume throughout that the feasible set
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the support set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
are non-empty and closed.

Problem (
1
) is ubiquitous in the areas of machine learning, operation research, economics, and automatic control
[
37
]
.
Unfortunately, the practical deployment of (
1
) is complicated by the fact that the precise form of the underlying distribution
P
is often unknown and can only be inferred indirectly from past data in the form of a finite number of samples
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X

[
9
]
. In this case, one can employ statistical methods to infer an estimated (parametric or non-parametric) distribution
\widehat{P}
from the available data. However, solving (
1
) with the estimated
P=\widehat{P}
may yield solutions that display poor out-of-sample performance due to the unavoidable mismatch between the true underlying distribution and
\widehat{P}
.

In distributionally robust (stochastic) optimization (DRO), the decision-maker hedges against this mismatch by minimizing the worst-case expected loss
\Psi_{\operatorname{WC}}
with respect to all distributions in a neighborhood of
\widehat{P}
; namely the (unstructured) DRO problem is formulated as

(2)

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\Psi_{\operatorname{WC}}(\theta)\text{\ \ with\ \ }\Psi_{\operatorname{WC}}(\theta)=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\ell(\theta,x)\,,

where
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
is a set of distributions, called ambiguity set, on the space
\mathcal{P}(X)
of all distributions supported on
X
.
If appropriately chosen, the set
\mathcal{W}
contains the true underlying distribution, which implies that any solution
\theta_{*}
to (
2
) will provide an objective value of
\Psi(\theta_{*})
lower or equal to
\Psi_{\operatorname{WC}}(\theta_{*})

[
27
]
.
Consequently, the quality of this robust approach heavily depends on the form of the ambiguity set
\mathcal{W}
: while it should be “large enough” to include the true distribution (with high confidence), it should not contain superfluous distributions that cannot realistically appear in the problem at hand as this would lead to unnecessarily conservative solutions.
Typical ambiguity sets that appear in the literature include support-, moment-, or distance-based sets of distributions or mixtures thereof
[
32
]
.
While the first two types of ambiguity sets contain all distributions complying with a specified support and moment information (generally, first and second moments)
[
16
,
30
]
, the distance-based sets include all distributions that are within a certain given “distance” of a fixed nominal distribution.
In the latter setting, the nominal distribution is often obtained through statistical techniques from available empirical data, while the distance, commonly expressed in terms of e.g. the
\phi-
divergence
[
3
,
4
]
, the total variation norm
[
38
]
, the kernel mean embedding
[
21
,
8
]
, or optimal transport based-distances
[
36
]
including the celebrated Wasserstein distance
[
20
,
27
]
, signifies the “trust” in the statistical methods used as well as the obtained data at hand.
Due to the favorable properties of Wasserstein distance in terms of expressivity and statistical properties allowing for finite-sample guarantees
[
18
]
, a significant proportion of the recent literature has focused on so-called Wasserstein balls
\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(\widehat{P})
, i.e., sets containing all distributions that are within some Wasserstein-distance
\rho>0
from a nominal distribution
\widehat{P}
.
For finitely supported nominal distributions
\widehat{P}
, the optimization of an expectation over a Wasserstein ball, which is convex, can often be reformulated into a finite-dimensional optimization program by means of Lagrange duality
[
20
,
36
]
, and solved efficiently via off-the-shelf solvers.

However, in several applications, the distributional uncertainty does not enter the problem at hand in an arbitrary fashion, but rather in a
structured
manner.
One common structure consists of a set
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
of identically and independently distributed (i.i.d.) random variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\sim P
, where
P
is again assumed to belong to a Wasserstein ball
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(\widehat{P})
.
This results in the
structured Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization
problem of the form

(3)

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\Psi_{\operatorname{S}}(\theta)\text{\ \ with\ \ }\Psi_{\operatorname{S}}(\theta)=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(\theta,x)\,,

where

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\{P^{\otimes N}\mid P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(\widehat{P})\}

is a
structured Wasserstein ambiguity set
containing product distributions of the form
\overline{P}=P^{\otimes N}=P\otimes\cdots\otimes P
only.
Problem (
3
) arises across several domains, such as (i) control of uncertain dynamical systems, which has traditionally assumed stationarity and independence of the additive noise affecting the system dynamics; (ii) strategically robust game theory, where irrationality in the opponents’ actions is captured via a distributionally robust “best response map” that, in case of disjoint agents action sets, takes the form of (
3
); (iii) supply chain optimization and/or inventory management of goods with uniform popularity across buyers and shared demand drivers may be approximated as (
3
).

Despite the popularity of this structure in stochastic optimization, handling Problem (
3
) is computationally challenging. In fact, contrary to the problem of evaluating
\Psi_{\operatorname{WC}}(\theta)
, the evaluation of
\Psi_{\operatorname{S}}(\theta)
now involves optimizing over the non-convex set
\mathcal{W}
(the non-convexity arises from the nonlinearity in the expectation operator due to the product structure). In turn, this prohibits the use of standard (strong) duality tools to reformulate (
3
) as an equivalent finite-dimensional optimization program.

1.1.
Related work

While the literature concerning the (unstructured) Wasserstein DRO problem in (
2
) is abundant, little attention has been devoted to the case of its structured counterpart. The closest works to ours are
[
13
,
14
]
, where non-convex ambiguity sets of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\operatorname{rect}}=\{P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{l}\mid P_{i}\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho_{i}}(\widehat{P}_{i})\text{\ for all\ }i=1,\ldots,l\}\,.

are considered.
For losses
\ell
that are either additively or multiplicatively separable, a strong duality result is proven, which allows, as in the unstructured case, to compute the worst-case expectation by solving a finite-dimensional convex program.
Moreover,
[
13
,
14
]
also proposes convex overestimations of the above ambiguity set for which strong duality can be shown without requiring the restrictive separability condition.
We will compare our proposed approach in more detail to the latter in Section
3.7
.
On a different direction,
[
19
]
considers the problem of maximizing an expectation functional over a Wasserstein ambiguity set under an additional moment constraints of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\operatorname{mom}}=\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(\widehat{P})\cap\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^{\top}\leq\Sigma\}\,,

for a-priori given mean vector
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}\succeq 0
.
By choosing
\Sigma
to be diagonal, independence among the lower-dimensional components of the uncertain vector can be enforced.
The resulting ambiguity set
\mathcal{W}
retains convexity as it is described as the intersection of convex sets, allowing again to compute the worst-case expectation by means of convex programming for certain classes of loss functions, such as e.g. quadratic or piecewise affine
\ell
.

1.2.
Outline and contributions of the paper

We consider data-driven DRO problems with Wasserstein ambiguity sets, where the
uncertainty affects the problem in an i.i.d. fashion, as in (
3
).
Specifically, we first examine the problem of evaluating the inner supremum of (
3
) for a fixed, given
\theta\in\Theta
, i.e. computing the so-called
(primal) uncertainty quantification (UQ) problem

(5)

\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)\,,

where
\mathcal{W}
is defined in (
4
).
After introducing the necessary background in Section
2
, we establish conditions under which (
5
) is finite and attains its optimum in Section
3.1
.
Moreover, there we also show that (
5
) can be upper-bounded by a standard Wasserstein uncertainty quantification problem.
Next, in Section
3.2
we show that the latter upper bound is in general conservative and propose a potentially tighter upper bound based on symmetrization of the corresponding loss
\ell
that allows for strong duality.
Further, in Section
3.4
, using the concept of lifting and based on the previous bound, we introduce a nonincreasing sequence of upper bounds (relaxations) on (
5
) that admit strong duality.
We investigate the properties of this sequence of relaxations in Section
3.5
.
As a main result, in Section
3.6
, we show in Theorem
10
that the gap between the lower bound of the relaxation sequence and (
5
) vanishes if
\ell
is concave.
Additionally, in Section
3.7
we show that for sets of the form
\mathcal{W}
our upper bound established in Section
3.2
is not more conservative than an upper bound provided in
[
14
]
.
Furthermore, in Section
4
we turn back to Problem
3
and show that if
\ell=\ell(\theta,x)
is convex-concave, then the minimizers of the sequence of relaxed problems, if existent, converge to the set of minimizers of Problem
3
.
Finally, in Section
5
we formulate the strong duality for the relaxation sequence as a second order cone program whenever
\ell
is a polyhedral loss function and provide some numerical examples.

Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization

Consider now an uncertainty quantification problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{c}(X)

\displaystyle=\{\ell:X\to\mathbb{R}\mid\exists C>0\,,x_{0}\in X\,:\;\ell\leq C(1+c(x_{0},\cdot))\}\,,

3.
Structured Uncertainty Quantification

In this section, we are interested in the inner optimization problems of (
3
), namely

(8)

\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)=\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}\int\ell(x)\,\text{d}P^{\otimes N}(x)=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\int\ell(x)\,\text{d}\overline{P}(x)\,,

for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, Borel measurable
\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
and

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\{P^{\otimes N}\mid P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})\}\,.

In its stated form problem (
8
) is non-convex, since the set
\mathcal{W}
is non-convex.
The following subsections investigate different aspects of this problem in more detail.
We confine the proofs of the subsequent results to the appendix for expositional clarity.
Unless stated otherwise, we assume that the transportation cost
c
is proper and
\widehat{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
throughout.

3.1.
Finiteness and existence of optimizers

First we delve into when (
8
) is well-defined, finite and attains its solution on
\mathcal{W}
.
For
N\in\mathbb{N}
we write

for the

c
.
In some cases we will omit the superscript
N
and write again

for brevity.

To start, we note that for
N\in\mathbb{N}
the set
\mathcal{W}
can be overestimated by an ordinary Wasserstein ball w.r.t. the lifted transportation cost.

Lemma 1
.

For any transportation cost
c:X\times X\to[0,\infty]

\widehat{P}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
,
\rho\geq 0
and

Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{B}_{N\rho}^{c^{N}}(\widehat{P}^{\otimes N})\,.

and it always holds that

(11)

\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)\leq\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathbb{B}_{N\rho}^{c^{N}}(\widehat{P}^{\otimes N})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)\,,

and we can use a Theorem

\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{c^{N}}(X^{N})

(12)

\displaystyle\ell(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}c(x_{0},x_{k})\right)\text{\ \ for all\ \ }x\in X^{N}\text{\ for some\ }C>0\,,\;x_{0}\in X\,.

However, because of the product structure of

For this purpose define the following function growth classes

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{c,N}(X^{N})

\displaystyle\quad=\left\{\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\mid\exists C>0\,,x_{0}\in X\,,\forall x\in X^{N}:\;\ell(x)\leq C\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1+c(x_{0},x_{k}))\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{c,N}^{<}(X^{N})

\displaystyle\quad=\left\{\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\mid\exists\delta\in\mathcal{D}\,,x_{0}\in X\,,\forall x\in X^{N}:\;\ell(x)\leq C\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1+\delta(c(x_{0},x_{k})))\right\}\,.

\mathcal{G}_{c^{N}}(X^{N})\subsetneq\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{c,N}(X^{N})
and
\mathcal{G}_{c^{N}}^{<}(X^{N})\subsetneq\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{c,N}^{<}(X^{N})
.
Then we have the following analogon of Theorem

Theorem 3
.

For any Borel
\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
the value of (
7
) is finite if
c
satisfies the weak triangle inequality,
\widehat{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
and
\ell\in\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{c,N}(X^{N})
.
Moreover, if
c
is proper,
\ell
is upper semi-continuous and
\ell\in\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{c,N}^{<}(X^{N})
, then the optimal value of (
7

3.2.
A convex upper bound

In the previous section we have seen that (
11

8

In general this bound will be conservative as the following example shows.

Example 1
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}

P=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-\sqrt{\rho}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\sqrt{\rho}}
showing that in fact the latter bound is attained.
On the other hand, the value of the right hand side of (
11
) can be computed using Theorem
2
and is given by

\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathbb{B}_{2\rho}^{c^{2}}(\delta_{0}^{\otimes 2})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)=\inf_{\mu\geq 0}\left[2\rho\mu+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}-\mu\lVert x\rVert^{2}\right)\right]=2\rho\,.

The next example shows that the conservatism of this bound can be arbitrary large in a relative sense.

Example 2
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}
,
N=2
,
c(x_{1},x_{2})=\lvert x_{1}-x_{2}\rvert^{2}
,
\widehat{P}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1}
and
\ell(x_{1},x_{2})=-x_{1}x_{2}
.
Then the value of the structured problem is

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P^{\otimes 2}}\ell(x)=\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}-(\mathbb{E}P)^{2}=0\,.

The value of the right hand side of (
11
) is, after some elementary calculations, given by

\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathbb{B}_{2\rho}^{c^{2}}(\widehat{P}^{\otimes 2})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mu\geq 0}\left[2\rho\mu+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\widehat{\xi}\in\{\pm 1\}^{2}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(-x_{1}x_{2}-\mu\lVert x-\widehat{\xi}\rVert^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mu>\frac{1}{2}}\left[2\rho\mu-\frac{2\mu}{1-4\mu^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\rho\,.

Hence, while the value of the structured problem is constant, the value of the latter bound grows asymptotically linearly as the ball radius increases.

In view of the last two examples we turn to the question whether we can obtain better bounds on (
8

).
This corresponds to obtaining tighter convex overestimations of the set
\mathcal{W}
than the one given by (

Clearly, the best such overestimation is the convex hull

and indeed even more is true.

Lemma 2
.

If
\ell\in L^{1}(X^{N},P^{\otimes N})
for all
P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\operatorname{conv}\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\overline{\operatorname{conv}}^{\operatorname{w}}\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)\,,

where
\overline{\operatorname{conv}}^{\operatorname{w}}\mathcal{W}
is the closure of
\operatorname{conv}\mathcal{W}
in the weak topology of
\mathcal{P}(X)

The nontrivial part is the second equality, since the first equality is simply a consequence of the expectation being linear in the distribution.
Note that by Theorem

the condition
\ell\in L^{1}(X^{N},P^{\otimes N})
for all

holds if e.g.

While this shows that our original non-convex UQ problem can be reformulated as a convex problem, it is very difficult to exhibit an explicit description of the sets
\operatorname{conv}\mathcal{W}
or
\overline{\operatorname{conv}}^{\operatorname{w}}\mathcal{W}

Hence we proceed to search for convex sets that overestimate

For this we explore (

(13)

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)\,,\quad\operatorname{U}(\ell)=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathbb{B}_{N\rho}^{c^{N}}(\widehat{P}^{\otimes N})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)\,.

Then, (

\operatorname{S}(\ell)\leq\operatorname{U}(\ell)
for any Borel
\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}

Note that

is a UQ problem of the type (

3.3.
A tighter convex upper bound

While the bound (
11
) can be quite conservative, we can establish tighter bounds by using the following, general, principle:
Suppose that there exists some class
\mathcal{F}
of transformations
F:\mathbb{R}^{X^{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{X^{N}}
such that
\operatorname{id}\in\mathcal{F}
,
F(\ell)
is Borel measurable for any Borel measurable
\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
and

(14)

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(F(\ell))=\operatorname{S}(\ell)\text{\ \ for all\ \ }F\in\mathcal{F}\text{\ and Borel\ }\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\,.

Then, for any Borel
\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)=\operatorname{S}(F(\ell))\leq\operatorname{U}(F(\ell))

and hence

(15)

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)\leq\inf_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\operatorname{U}(F(\ell))

is a potentially tighter upper bound on
\operatorname{S}(\ell)
than
\operatorname{U}(\ell)
.
Similar arguments have been used in
[
28
]
to obtain convex upper bounds on the structured singular value in the domain of control theory.
To obtain such a class
\mathcal{F}
for our problem, we make the following, crucial observation: If
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
is a permutation, then the transformation
F_{\pi}:\ell\mapsto\ell_{\pi}
with
\ell_{\pi}(x)=\ell(\pi(x))
, where
\pi(x)=(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(N)})
for
x\in X^{N}
, satisfies

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(F_{\pi}(\ell))=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell_{\pi}(x)=\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P^{\otimes N}}\ell_{\pi}(x)=\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P^{\otimes N}}\ell(x)=\operatorname{S}(\ell)\,,

since
\pi\#P^{\otimes N}=P^{\otimes N}
for any permutation
\pi
.
Moreover, since
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell
is linear in
\ell
, it follows that (
14
) holds for

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\operatorname{conv}\{F_{\pi}\mid\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}\}=\left\{F_{\alpha}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\alpha_{\pi}F_{\pi}\mid\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}_{N}}\right\}\,,

where
\Delta^{\mathcal{S}_{N}}=\{\alpha=(\alpha_{\pi})_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\in[0,1]^{\mathcal{S}_{N}}\mid\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\alpha_{\pi}=1\}
is the standard simplex in the
N!
-dimensional Euclidean space.
This implies that (
15
) holds for the above class
\mathcal{F}
of transformations.
In this specific case we can actually establish the following

Lemma 3
.

For any Borel measurable
\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
it holds that

\displaystyle\inf_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\operatorname{U}(F(\ell))=\min_{\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}_{N}}}\operatorname{U}(F_{\alpha}(\ell))=\operatorname{U}(\ell_{\operatorname{sym}})\,,

where the
symmetrization
of a function is defined by

(17)

\displaystyle\ell_{\operatorname{sym}}(x)=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\ell(\pi(x))\,,

i.e. the infimum in (
15
) is attained in the uniform coefficient
\alpha=(1/N!,\ldots,1/N!)\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}_{N}}
.

Thus instead of optimizing over the class
\mathcal{F}
as in (
15
), we can replace the latter by the single upper bound

(18)

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)\leq\operatorname{U}(\ell_{\operatorname{sym}})\,.

Example 3
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}
,

,

,

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)=\operatorname{S}(\ell_{\operatorname{sym}})=\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}-2(\mathbb{E}(P^{2})+(\mathbb{E}P)^{2})=-2(1-\min(\sqrt{\rho},1))^{2}\leq 0\,.

while the value of the symmetrized bound is

\displaystyle\operatorname{U}(\ell_{\operatorname{sym}})=2(2\sqrt{2\min(\rho,1/2)}-2\min(\rho,1/2)-1)\leq 0\,.

3.4.
Sequence of convex relaxations

It is interesting to ask now whether we can find even tighter overestimations of

than
\mathbb{B}_{N\rho}^{c^{N}}(P^{\otimes N})_{\operatorname{sym}}
.
In view of (

) the latter question is equivalent to determining a property that distinguishes powers of measures in the set of all symmetric measures.
The crucial observation is that powers of powers of measures are again powers of measures and hence symmetric, while the same does not have to be true for general symmetric measures.
This latter viewpoint hints us to lift our problem and consider instead of (

) the problem

(23)

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}\int\ell\circ\operatorname{pr}_{1:N}^{M}(x)\,\text{d}P^{\otimes M}(x)\,,

is a “lifting parameter” and
\operatorname{pr}_{1:N}^{M}:X^{M}\to X^{N}
is the projection onto the first
N
components, i.e. in (

) we see the function
\ell

as a function
\widehat{\ell}=\ell\circ\operatorname{pr}_{1:N}^{M}

Analogously we define the quantities

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}_{M}(\widehat{\ell}):=\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P^{\otimes M}}\widehat{\ell}(x)\leq\operatorname{U}_{M}(\widehat{\ell}):=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathbb{B}_{M\rho}^{c^{M}}(\widehat{P}^{\otimes M})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\widehat{\ell}(x)\,.

\operatorname{S}_{N}(\ell)=\operatorname{S}_{M}(\ell\circ\operatorname{pr}_{1:N}^{M})

In view of (

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)=\operatorname{S}_{N}(\ell)=\operatorname{S}_{M}(\widehat{\ell})\leq\operatorname{U}_{M}(\widehat{\ell}_{\operatorname{sym}})\text{\ \ for\ \ }N\leq M<\infty\,.

Note that here
\widehat{\ell}_{\operatorname{sym}}
denotes the symmetrization of

and thus itself depends on

.
To summarize, for each

we obtain an upper bound

(24)

\displaystyle\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell):=\operatorname{U}_{M}((\ell\circ\operatorname{pr}_{1:N}^{M})_{\operatorname{sym}})

on
\operatorname{S}(\ell)
.
It then holds that

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)\leq\operatorname{U}_{\infty}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell):=\inf_{M\geq N}\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)\,.

We stress again that, contrary to the original problem

For notational brevity we also set

Theorem 5
.

The value
\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)
is finite if

Additionally, if

Let

After some elementary calculations (see Appendix) we obtain

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mu>\frac{1}{M(M-1)}}M(\rho-1)\mu+\frac{(M-1)M^{2}\mu^{2}}{(M-1)M\mu-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{(M-1)(1+M\mu)}\right)

3.5.
Relaxation gap

To understand the gap between
\operatorname{S}(\ell)
and
\operatorname{U}_{\infty}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)

\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)
in terms of overestimations of
\overline{\operatorname{conv}}^{\operatorname{w}}\mathcal{W}
.
For this purpose we unfold the definitions to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathbb{B}_{M\rho}^{c^{M}}(\widehat{P}^{\otimes M})}\int(\ell\circ\operatorname{pr}_{1:N}^{M})_{\operatorname{sym}}\,\text{d}\overline{P}=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathbb{B}_{M\rho}^{c^{M}}(\widehat{P}^{\otimes M})}\int\ell\circ\operatorname{pr}_{1:N}^{M}\,\text{d}\overline{P}_{\operatorname{sym}}\\

.

For each

(ii)

(iii)

As an immediate corollary, we obtain that the sequence of upper bounds

Corollary 1

For all

Theorem 6

If

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{U}}_{\infty}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)=\operatorname{U}_{\infty}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)\,.

It holds that

Let us look at Theorem
7
from the angle of the so-called de Finetti’s theorem
[
11
, Theorem 10.10.19]
:

Theorem 8

(De Finetti)
.

Let
X
be a Polish space.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{sym}}(X^{\infty})=\left\{\int P^{\otimes\infty}\,\text{d}\nu(P)\mid\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(X))\right\}\,.

Moreover, for each
\overline{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{sym}}(X^{\infty})
the representing mixture
\nu
is unique.

This theorem shows that the set of all symmetric measures on the infinite product space
X^{\infty}
is precisely the set of mixtures of product distributions.
In other words, any sequence of random variables that is
exchangeable
, i.e. its distribution is unchanged under permutations of its elements, is the mixture of i.i.d. sequences of random variables.
Equipped with Theorem
8
we can rewrite the statement of Theorem
7
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{sym}}(X^{\infty})\mid W_{c}(\widehat{P},\cdot)\leq\rho\text{\ $\nu_{\overline{P}}$-almost-surely}\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{sym}}(X^{\infty})\mid\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\nu_{\overline{P}}}W_{c}(\widehat{P},P)\leq\rho\right\}\,,

where
\nu_{\overline{P}}
denotes the unique mixture distribution of the symmetric measure
\overline{P}
.

Now, (
28
) and Theorem
7
together imply:

(29)

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)\text{\ \ and\ \ }\widehat{\operatorname{U}}_{\infty}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)=\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(x)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\int P^{\otimes N}\,\text{d}\nu(P)\mid\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(X))\,,\;W_{c}(\widehat{P},\cdot)\leq\rho\text{\ $\nu$-almost-surely}\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\{\int P^{\otimes N}\,\text{d}\nu(P)\mid\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(X))\,,\;\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\nu}W_{c}(\widehat{P},P)\leq\rho\right\}\,.

Having obtained a good understanding the nature on the limit of the sequence of relaxations we can ask now which conditions on
\ell
would suffice to imply that there is no relaxation gap, i.e. that
\operatorname{S}(f)=\widehat{\operatorname{U}}_{\infty}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)
.
Let us one last time rewrite (
29
), this time in terms of the mixing measures
\nu
directly.
We have

(30)

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(X))\\
W_{c}(\widehat{P},\cdot)\leq\rho\text{\ $\nu$-a.s.}\end{subarray}}\int F_{\ell}(P)\,\text{d}\nu(P)\,,\quad\widehat{\operatorname{U}}_{\infty}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(X))\\
\mathbb{E}_{P\sim\nu}W_{c}(\widehat{P},P)\leq\rho\end{subarray}}\int F_{\ell}(P)\,\text{d}\nu(P)\,,

where
F_{\ell}(P)=\int\ell\,\text{d}P^{\otimes N}
.
Thus, the question on the absence of a relaxation gap is equivalent to asking when a constraint can be replaced by its expectation.
The following example shows that in general the relaxation gap is not zero.

Example 5
.

Let us consider
X=\mathbb{R}
and
N=2
with
\widehat{P}=\delta_{0}
and
\ell:X^{2}\to\mathbb{R}:(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto x_{1}x_{2}^{2}
and
\rho>0
.
Then we have for the structured ambiguity set
\mathcal{W}=\{P^{\otimes 2}\mid P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})\}
that

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(f)=\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}\int\ell(x_{1},x_{2})\,\text{d}P^{\otimes 2}(x_{1},x_{2})=\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}(\mathbb{E}P)(\mathbb{E}(P^{2}))=\rho^{3/2}\,,

where we have exploited Theorem
2
to obtain that

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}(\mathbb{E}P)=\rho^{1/2}\text{\ \ and\ \ }\sup_{P\in\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})}\mathbb{E}(P^{2})=\rho

and noted that both attained at
P=\delta_{\sqrt{\rho}}
.
We claim that
\widehat{\operatorname{U}}_{\infty}^{\operatorname{sym}}(f)=\infty
and thus
\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(f)=\infty
for all
M\geq 2
.
Indeed, the sequence of distributions
\overline{P}_{n}=(1-\frac{\rho}{n^{2}})\delta_{0}^{\otimes\infty}+\frac{\rho}{n^{2}}\delta_{n}^{\otimes\infty}
satisfies
\operatorname{pr}_{1:M}\#\overline{P}_{n}=(1-\frac{\rho}{n^{2}})\delta_{0}^{\otimes M}+\frac{\rho}{n^{2}}\delta_{n}^{\otimes M}\in\mathbb{B}_{M\rho}^{c^{M}}(P^{\otimes M})
for all
M\geq 1
, since

\displaystyle\int\int c^{M}(x,y)\,\text{d}\delta_{0}^{\otimes M}(y)\,\text{d}\operatorname{pr}_{1:M}\#\overline{P}_{n}(x)=\int\sum_{i=1}^{M}x_{i}^{2}\,\text{d}\operatorname{pr}_{1:M}\#\overline{P}_{n}(x)=\frac{\rho}{n^{2}}Mn^{2}=M\rho\,.

Moreover, it holds that

\displaystyle\int\widehat{\ell}_{\operatorname{sym}}(x)\,\text{d}\overline{P}_{n}(x)=\frac{\rho}{n^{2}}\ell(n,n)=\rho n\to\infty\text{\ for\ }n\to\infty\,.

Thus
\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)=\infty
for all
M\geq N=2
and the relaxation gap is infinite.

Note that in this example
\ell
does not satisfy the growth assumption
\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{c^{2}}(X^{2})
, which is sufficient for the finiteness of the unstructured UQ problem (
7
), but not necessary for the finiteness of the structured version (
8
).
The question of whether there exists an example with a non-zero relaxation gap such that (
7
) is finite, remains open.

3.6.
Tightness of the relaxation sequence

Intuitively, replacing a constraint by its average will not increase the optimal value if any “average feasible” point can be replaced by a single point that satisfies the original constraint without decreasing its objective value.
If the latter “replacement” is done by taking the average, i.e. taking the mean
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}x
of
\nu
itself, then Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle F(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}x)\geq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}F(x)\text{\ \ for all concave\ }F\,,

shows that concavity of the objective function
F
is sufficient for the absence of a relaxation gap.
The following theorem gives a sufficient condition for the function
F_{\ell}(P)=\int\ell\,\text{d}P^{\otimes N}
to be convex in the sense of the usual linear structure on
\mathcal{P}(X)
.
For this we need to introduce the following notion
[
41
, Definition 8.1]
: A function
k:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be conditionally negative definite (c.n.d.) if for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
x\in X^{n}
the matrix
(k(x_{i},x_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{n}
is negative semi-definite on the subspace
\mathbbm{1}^{\top}\gamma=0
, i.e. if

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}=0\Longrightarrow\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}k(x_{i},x_{j})\leq 0\,.

The result reads now as follows.

Theorem 9
.

Let
N\geq 2
and let
\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
\lvert\ell\rvert\in\mathcal{G}_{c^{N}}(X^{N})
and the symmetrization
\ell_{\operatorname{sym}}
is c.n.d. in the first two (and hence any two) of its variables, i.e. for all
\overline{x}\in X^{N-2}
the map
X\times X\to\mathbb{R}:(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\ell_{\operatorname{sym}}(x_{1},x_{2},\overline{x})
is c.n.d.
Then
F_{\ell}
is concave on
\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
, i.e.

\displaystyle F_{\ell}(\alpha P_{1}+(1-\alpha)P_{2})\geq\alpha F_{\ell}(P_{1})+(1-\alpha)F_{\ell}(P_{2})\text{\ \ for\ }P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)\,,\;\alpha\in[0,1]\,.

Moreover, in this case

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)=\widehat{\operatorname{U}}_{\infty}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)\,,

and if
\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{c^{N}}^{<}(X^{N})
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)=\lim_{M\to\infty}\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)\,.

Example 6
.

Let
X
,
N
,
c
and
\ell
be as in Example
4
,
\ell(x_{1},x_{2})=-x_{1}x_{2}
.
Then
\ell
is (conditionally) negative definite, since if
x\in X^{n}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are such that
\mathbbm{1}^{\top}\gamma=0
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}\ell(x_{i},x_{j})=-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}x_{i}\right)^{2}\leq 0\,.

Thus, the conclusion of Theorem
9
is consistent with Example
4
.

Unfortunately, verifying conditional negative definiteness in practice can be challenging.
It turns out, however, that if
X
is additionally a vector space and
\ell:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is concave, then
F_{\ell}
is
geodesically concave
in the sense of the Wasserstein space on
\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
.
We will not introduce the concepts of geodesic convexity or concavity here, since they will not be needed any further in this paper, but just note that the so-called
Wasserstein geodesics

[
2
]
provide a way of interpolating between two distributions
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
in
\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
that is
different
from
\alpha P_{1}+(1-\alpha)P_{2}
.
Thus, if the average w.r.t.
\nu
is understood in a different, namely
geodesic
sense, then we can indeed show the relaxation gap again to be zero.
The price to pay, however, is that the underlying feasible set
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(\widehat{P})
is convex w.r.t. this new notion of convexity as well, which requires an additional assumption on the transportation cost
c
.
The following theorem summarizes these observations and is the main result of this section.

Theorem 10
.

Suppose that
X
is a convex subset of a vector space and
c
is proper such that
c(x,\cdot):X\to[0,\infty)
is convex for all
x\in X
.
If
\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{c^{N}}(X^{N})
is upper semi-continuous and concave, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)=\widehat{\operatorname{U}}_{\infty}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)\,.

If additionally
\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{c^{N}}^{<}(X^{N})
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}(\ell)=\lim_{M\to\infty}\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)\,.

Note that the convexity assumption on the transportation cost is naturally satisfied if e.g.
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
c=d^{p}
with
d(x,y)=\lVert x-y\rVert
for some
p\in[1,\infty)
and norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
on
X
.

Remark 1
.

Note that Theorem
9
and Theorem
10
are not subcases of each other and rely on different notions of convex interpolation.
The problem of determining the largest class of functions
\ell
with a zero relaxation gap remains open.

4.
Relaxations of the distributionally robust optimization problem

In this section we return to the problem (
3
) and examine the implications of replacing its inner uncertainty quantification problem by its relaxed version, i.e. replacing (
3
), where
\Psi_{\operatorname{S}}(\theta)=\operatorname{S}(\ell(\theta,\cdot))
in the notation of the previous section, by the problem

(33)

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\Psi_{\operatorname{U}}^{M}(\theta)\text{\ \ with\ \ }\Psi_{\operatorname{U}}^{M}(\theta)=\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell(\theta,\cdot))\,,

where this time we explicitly see
\ell:\Theta\times X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
as a function of two arguments
(\theta,x)\in\Theta\times X^{N}
.
The following result relates the solutions of the relaxed problem (
33
) to solutions of (
3
).

Theorem 13
.

Suppose that
X
is a convex subset of a vector space,
c:X\times X\to[0,\infty)
is proper and
c(x,\cdot)
is convex for all
x\in X
.
Further, let
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\ell:\Theta\times X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
.
If
\ell(\theta,\cdot)\in\mathcal{G}_{c^{N}}^{<}(X^{N})
is concave and upper semi-continuous for all
\theta\in\Theta
, then it holds

(34)

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\Psi_{\operatorname{U}}^{M}(\theta)=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\Psi_{\operatorname{S}}(\theta)\,.

Suppose additionally that
\Theta
is compact, convex with
\operatorname{int}(\Theta)\neq\emptyset
and that
\ell(\cdot,x)
is convex and lower semi-continuous on
\Theta
for all
x\in X^{N}
and that the family
\{-\min\{\ell(\theta,\cdot),0\}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
is
\overline{P}
-integrable
uniformly
in
\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}
.
Then the infimum on the right hand side of (
34
) is attained and given any sequence
(\epsilon_{M})_{M=N}^{\infty}
with
0<\epsilon_{M}\searrow 0
for
M\to\infty
and arbitrary
\epsilon_{M}
-approximate minimizers

\displaystyle\theta_{M}^{*}\in\epsilon_{M}\text{-}\operatorname{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\Psi_{\operatorname{U}}^{M}(\theta):=\left\{\theta\in\Theta\mid\Psi_{\operatorname{U}}^{M}(\theta)<\inf_{\Theta}\Psi_{\operatorname{U}}^{M}+\epsilon_{M}\right\}\,,

it holds that

\displaystyle\theta^{*}\in\operatorname{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\Psi_{\operatorname{S}}(\theta)

for any limit point
\theta^{*}
of
(\theta_{M}^{*})_{M=N}^{\infty}
.

Remark 2
.

The uniform integrability assumption on
\{-\min\{\ell(\theta,\cdot),0\}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
is used to establish the lower semi-continuity of
\Psi_{\operatorname{S}}
on
\Theta
only and is satisfied if e.g. there exists a constant
C>0
and
x_{0}\in X^{N}
such that

\displaystyle\ell(\theta,x)\geq-C(1+c^{N}(x,x_{0}))\text{\ \ for all\ }x\in X^{N}\,,\;\theta\in\Theta\,,

i.e.
-\ell(\theta,\cdot)
“belong uniformly in
\theta\in\Theta
” to the class
\mathcal{G}_{c^{N}}(X^{N})
.

Thus Theorem
13
provides a guarantee that (possibly suboptimal) solutions of the relaxed problem (
33
) will approximate the true minimizers of (
3
) if
\ell
is a saddle (i.e. convex-concave) function with certain integrability and continuity properties.
Note, however, that even without any convexity or concavity assumptions on
\ell
, it can be beneficial to solve (
33
) as a substitute to (
3
).
Indeed, suppose that we want to find a decision vector
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
that satisfies a robustness margin given by a threshold
R\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(\theta^{*},x)\leq R\,.

If we now solve instead (
33
) for some fixed
M\geq N
to (sub)optimality and obtain a decision vector
\theta_{M}^{*}\in\Theta
with an objective value
\Psi_{\operatorname{U}}^{M}(\theta_{M}^{*})\leq R
, then after implementing
\theta_{M}^{*}
in our original problem (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\overline{P}}\ell(\theta_{M}^{*},x)=\Psi_{\operatorname{S}}(\theta_{M}^{*})\leq\Psi_{\operatorname{U}}^{M}(\theta_{M}^{*})\leq R\,,

i.e. we can take
\theta^{*}=\theta_{M}^{*}
to meet the desired specifications.

Remark 3
.

While Theorem
13
implies that
\lim_{M\to\infty}\Psi_{\operatorname{S}}(\theta_{M}^{*})=\inf_{\Theta}\Psi_{\operatorname{S}}
whenever
\ell
is concave in the second argument, this convergence is, in general, not monotone (see Section
5.2.2
for a numerical example).
As such, after having obtained
\theta_{M}^{*}\in\epsilon_{M}\text{-}\operatorname{argmin}_{\Theta}\Psi_{\operatorname{U}}^{M}
for
M\in\{N,\ldots,M_{\max}\}
with some maximal
M_{\max}\geq N
, it is advisable to take
\theta_{M^{*}}^{*}
with
M^{*}=\operatorname{argmin}_{M\in\{N,\ldots,M_{\max}\}}\Psi_{\operatorname{U}}^{M}(\theta_{M}^{*})
as the final decision vector.

5.
Tractable reformulations and numerical examples

In this section we reformulate each uncertainty quantification problem in the proposed sequence of relaxations into an explicit finite-dimensional convex program and provide some numerical examples.

5.1.
Finite-dimensional reformulations using Lagrange duality

When the nominal distribution
\widehat{P}
is discrete, i.e.

(35)

\displaystyle\widehat{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\widehat{P}}}\widehat{p}_{i}\delta_{\widehat{\xi}_{i}}\,,

We first note that

\displaystyle\operatorname{U}_{M}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mu\geq 0}M\rho\mu+\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\boldsymbol{\widehat{I}}}\widehat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sup_{x\in X^{M}}\left((\ell\circ\operatorname{pr}_{1:N}^{M})_{\operatorname{sym}}(x)-\mu c^{M}(x,\widehat{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{i}})\right)\,,

where

,
\widehat{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=(\widehat{\xi}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\widehat{\xi}_{i_{M}})
when

and the last equality holds due to Theorem

\displaystyle\widehat{P}^{\otimes M}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\boldsymbol{\widehat{I}}}\widehat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\delta_{\widehat{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{i}}}

is also discrete.
By introducing slack variables

6.
Conclusions and Outlook

In this paper, we focused on Wasserstein DRO formulations where the uncertain vector exhibits an i.i.d. structure.
By exploiting this structure, we construct a structured ambiguity set that only contains product distributions.
To solve the resulting non-convex program, we devise a sequence of convex relaxations that, under mild conditions on the loss function, converge to the optimal solution of the original non-convex problem.
Our numerical results certify how structured ambiguity sets can capture uncertainty in a more effective manner than unstructured ambiguity sets, ultimately improving the overall decision-making.
Future work includes (i) the analysis of the relaxation gap for convex loss functions, (ii) addressing the computational tractability issues of data-driven problems where the product of empirical distributions is supported on a exponential amount of points and finally (iii) extending the results to exploit invariance w.r.t. groups
G
other than the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{N}
.

Appendix B
Background on measures in Polish spaces

Here we collect some results on measures in Polish spaces.
First we note that if
X
is a Polish space, then so is
\mathcal{P}(X)
with the topology of weak convergence of measures (i.e. convergence in distribution)
[
1
, 15.15 Theorem]
.
While
\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
is in general not closed as a subset of
\mathcal{P}(X)
, it can be shown that, under the assumptions on the cost
c
made in Section
2
, for
\widehat{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)
the function
\mathcal{P}(X)\to[0,\infty]:P\mapsto W_{c}(P,\widehat{P})
is lower-semicontinuous in the weak topology and hence Borel measurable
[
29
, Paragraph right after Corollary 2.2.2]
.
This implies that for each
\rho>0
and
x_{0}\in X
the set
\mathcal{P}_{c}(X,\rho)=\{P\mid W_{c}(\delta_{x_{0}},P)\leq\rho\}
is closed as a subset of
\mathcal{P}(X)
(and independent of
x_{0}
because of the weak triangle inequality for
c
) and hence
\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)=\bigcup_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{P}_{c}(X,\rho)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
is Borel measurable as a countable union of closed sets.
Next, if
c
is proper (i.e. metric sublevel sets are compact), then, for any
\rho>0
, the set
\mathcal{P}_{c}(X,\rho)
is compact in the weak topology
[
43
,
29
]
as is the corresponding Wasserstein ball
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{c}(P)
whenever
P\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(X)

[
43
]
.

Now we turn to mixtures of measures: Since
\mathcal{P}(X)
is Polish, we can define
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(X))
as the set of Borel measures (the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{P}(X)
is induced by the weak topology) on
\mathcal{P}(X)
.
Now, given any Polish space
Y
and a Borel subset

the map
\mathcal{P}(Y)\to[0,1]:P\mapsto P(A)=\int\mathbbm{1}_{A}\,\text{d}P
is Borel measurable by
[
1
, Theorem 15.13]
.
Hence for any Borel measurable

\mathcal{P}(X)\to[0,1]:P\mapsto F(P)(A)

Then for any

\overline{P}

\sigma

[

is a Borel measure and we write

.
Furthermore, we often need the following identity that relates the integration w.r.t. a mixture distribution to the integration w.r.t. the mixture measure itself.

For

can be written as the composition

Lemma 8
.

, then
a\in h(X)
.
But if
\int h\,\text{d}\mu=a\in\partial I
, then either
h(X)\subseteq[a,\infty)
or
h(X)\subseteq(-\infty,a]
.
If, say,
h(x)>a
for all
x\in X
, then
0<\int h-a\,\text{d}\mu=\int h\,\text{d}\mu-a
, a contradiction.
Now assume that the claim is true for some
n-1\geq 1
.
Let
y=\int h\,\text{d}\mu
.
If
y\notin\operatorname{conv}h(X)
, then there exists a separating hyperplane
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\left\langle z,y\right\rangle\leq\left\langle z,h(x)\right\rangle
for all
x\in X
.
Now the set
E_{+}=\{x\in X\mid\left\langle z,y\right\rangle<\left\langle z,h(x)\right\rangle\}
is a
\mu
-nullset, since if not, then
0>\int_{E}\left\langle z,y\right\rangle-\left\langle z,h(x)\right\rangle\,\text{d}\mu(x)=0
, a contradiction.
Similarly
E_{-}=\{x\in X\mid\left\langle z,y\right\rangle>\left\langle z,h(x)\right\rangle\}
is
\mu
-nullset.
Pick an arbitrary
x_{0}\in X
and let
\widetilde{h}(x)=h(x)
for
x\notin E_{+}\cup E_{-}
and
\widetilde{h}(x)=h(x_{0})
if
x\in E_{+}\cup E_{-}
.
We obtain that
\int\widetilde{h}\,\text{d}\mu=y
, and
\widetilde{h}(X)\subseteq h(X)
as well as
\widetilde{h}(X)\subseteq U=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\langle w-y,z\right\rangle=0\}
.
Let
T:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
be an invertible linear transformation and set
\widehat{h}=T\circ\widetilde{h}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
Since
\operatorname{dim}U=n-1
, such a transformation exists.
By induction hypothesis
Ty=\int\widehat{h}\,\text{d}\mu\in\operatorname{conv}\widehat{h}(X)=T\operatorname{conv}\widetilde{h}(X)\subseteq T\operatorname{conv}h(X)
and thus
y\in\operatorname{conv}h(X)
, a contradiction.
Hence
y\in\operatorname{conv}h(X)
in the first place, which finishes the induction step.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
X
be a measurable space and let
f,g:X\to\mathbb{R}
be Borel measurable.
If there exists some Borel probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
such that
f,g\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
, then the set

\displaystyle\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)\;\middle|\;f,g\in L^{1}(X,\nu)\text{\ and\ }\int g\,\text{d}\nu=\int g\,\text{d}\mu\,,\;\int f\,\text{d}\nu=\int f\,\text{d}\mu\right\}

contains a finitely supported measure.

Proof.

Let
h:X\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{2}:x\mapsto(f(x),g(x))
.
Then clearly
\int h\,\text{d}\mu\in\overline{\operatorname{conv}}h(X)
, while
\operatorname{conv}h(X)=\{\int h\,\text{d}\nu\mid\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)\,,\;\lvert\operatorname{supp}\nu\rvert<\infty\}
.
Thus the task is to show that actually
\int h\,\text{d}\mu\in\operatorname{conv}h(X)
, which follows from Lemma
14
.
∎

Lemma 16
.

Let
X
be a Polish space and
\ell:X\to\mathbb{R}
be Borel measurable.
Define
F:\mathcal{P}(X)\mapsto\overline{\mathbb{R}}:\overline{P}\mapsto\int\ell\,\text{d}\overline{P}
, where we set
F(\overline{P})=\infty
if
\ell
is not
\overline{P}
-integrable.
Then
F
is Borel measurable.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(X)=\{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{P}(X)\mid\ell\in L^{1}(X,\overline{P})\}=\operatorname{dom}(F)
and observe that
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(X)
is convex and
F
satisfies
F(\alpha P_{1}+(1-\alpha)P_{2})=\alpha F(P_{1})+(1-\alpha)F(P_{2})
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(X)
.
The set
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(X)
is a Borel set w.r.t. the weak topology.
This follows from the fact that
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(X)=\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{M\in\mathbb{N}}\{\overline{P}\in\mathcal{P}(X)\mid\int\lvert\ell\rvert_{M}\,\text{d}\overline{P}\leq N\}
, where
\lvert\ell\rvert_{M}=\min\{\lvert\ell\rvert,M\}
, and that
\overline{P}\mapsto\int\lvert\ell\rvert_{M}\,\text{d}\overline{P}
is Borel measurable for each
M
by
[
1
, Theorem 15.13]
.
Moreover, we also note that

\displaystyle F(\overline{P})=\lim_{M\to\infty}\int{\ell\mathbbm{1}_{|\ell|\leq M}}{\overline{P}}\text{\ \ for all\ }\overline{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(X)\,,

where each
\overline{P}\mapsto\int{\ell\mathbbm{1}_{|\ell|\leq M}}{\overline{P}}
is again Borel measurable by
[
1
, Theorem 15.13]
.
The Borel measurability of
F
follows now from the fact that pointwise limits of Borel measurable functions are again Borel measurable
[
11
]
.
∎

Lemma 17
.

Let
Y,Z
be Polish spaces and let
\mathscr{Z}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(Z)
be a weakly closed, convex set with its extreme points denoted by
\operatorname{ext}\mathscr{Z}
.
Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
and
F:Y\to\mathcal{P}(Z)
be measurable w.r.t. Borel-
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{P}(Z)
such that
F(y)\in\mathscr{Z}
for
\nu
-almost all
y\in Y
.
If

\displaystyle\int_{Y}F(y)\,\text{d}\nu(y)\in\operatorname{ext}\mathscr{Z}\,,

then there exists
\zeta\in\operatorname{ext}\mathscr{Z}
such that
F(y)=\zeta
for
\nu
-almost all
y\in Y
.

Proof.

If there exists a single
\zeta\in\mathcal{P}(Z)
such that
F(y)=\zeta
for
\nu
-almost all
y\in Y
, then the claim is obvious.
Suppose now that
F
is not constant
\nu
-almost everywhere.
Then there exist Borel subsets
Y_{i}\subseteq Y
such that
\nu(Y_{i})>0
for
i=1,2
and

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{1}}F(y)\,\text{d}\nu(y)\neq\int_{Y_{2}}F(y)\,\text{d}\nu(y)\,.

Then we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{Y}F(y)\,\text{d}\nu(y)=\nu(Y_{1})\underbrace{\int_{Y_{1}}F(y)\,\text{d}\nu_{1}(y)}_{\in\mathscr{Z}}+\nu(Y_{2})\underbrace{\int_{Y_{2}}F(y)\,\text{d}\nu_{2}(y)}_{\in\mathscr{Z}}

with
\nu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(Y_{i})
defined by
\nu_{i}(A)=\frac{\nu(A)}{\nu(Y_{i})}
for
A\subseteq Y_{i}
Borel and
i=1,2
.
Since
\nu(Y_{1})+\nu(Y_{2})=\nu(Y)=1
, this contradicts
\int_{Y}F(y)\,\text{d}\nu(y)\in\operatorname{ext}\mathscr{Z}
and hence finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 18
.

For any transportation cost
c:X\times X\to[0,\infty]
,
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that
W_{c^{N}}(P_{1}^{\otimes N},P_{2}^{\otimes N})=NW_{c}(P_{1},P_{2})
.

Proof.

First we show
\leq
.
Let
\epsilon>0
be given.
Then we can find a coupling
\widetilde{\Lambda}\in\Gamma(P_{1},P_{2})
such that
\int c\,\text{d}\widetilde{\Lambda}\leq W_{c}(P_{1},P_{2})+\epsilon
.
Define the distribution
\Lambda\in\mathcal{P}(X^{N}\times X^{N})
by
\Lambda=\widetilde{\Lambda}^{\otimes N}
, where
(X\times X)^{N}
is identified with
X^{N}\times X^{N}
.
Clearly
\Lambda\in\Gamma(P_{1}^{\otimes N},P_{2}^{\otimes N})
and it holds that

\displaystyle\int c^{N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\,\text{d}\Lambda(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int c(x_{i},y_{i})\,\text{d}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{\otimes N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int c(x_{i},y_{i})\,\text{d}\widetilde{\Lambda}(x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle\quad\leq N(W_{c}(P_{1},P_{2})+\epsilon)\,.

Since
\epsilon
was arbitrary,
\leq
follows.
Now we show
\geq
.
Let again
\epsilon>0
and
\Lambda\in\Gamma(P_{1}^{\otimes N},P_{2}^{\otimes N})
be a coupling such that
\int c^{N}\,\text{d}\Lambda\leq W_{c^{N}}(P_{1}^{\otimes N},P_{2}^{\otimes N})+\epsilon
.
Then it is easy to see that
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}:=\operatorname{pr}_{i,i}\#\Lambda\in\Gamma(P_{1},P_{2})
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
.
Moreover, for
\widetilde{\Lambda}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}
it holds that
\widetilde{\Lambda}\in\Gamma(P_{1},P_{2})
, since averages of couplings are again couplings.
Then

\displaystyle W_{c}(P_{1},P_{2})\leq\int c\,\text{d}\widetilde{\Lambda}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int c\,\text{d}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\int c^{N}\,\text{d}\Lambda\leq\frac{1}{N}(W_{c^{N}}(P_{1}^{\otimes N},P_{2}^{\otimes N})+\epsilon)\,.

Since
\epsilon
was again arbitrary, this finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 19
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a polyhedral function given by

\displaystyle f(x)=\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}h^{\top}\left(\begin{matrix}x\\
1\end{matrix}\right)\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,

where
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is a polytope.
Then the Legendre-Fenchel conjugate is given by

\displaystyle f^{*}(z)=-\max\left\{b\mid\left(\begin{matrix}z\\
b\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathcal{H}\right\}\,,\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle f^{*}(z)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}x^{\top}z-\left(\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}h^{\top}\left(\begin{matrix}x\\
1\end{matrix}\right)\right)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\begin{matrix}z\\
0\end{matrix}\right)^{\top}\left(\begin{matrix}x\\
1\end{matrix}\right)+\left(\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}-h^{\top}\left(\begin{matrix}x\\
1\end{matrix}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left(\left(\begin{matrix}z\\
0\end{matrix}\right)-h\right)^{\top}\left(\begin{matrix}x\\
1\end{matrix}\right)=\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\left(\begin{matrix}z\\
0\end{matrix}\right)-h\right)^{\top}\left(\begin{matrix}x\\
1\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{-b\mid\left(\begin{matrix}z\\
b\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathcal{H}\right\}=-\max\left\{b\mid\left(\begin{matrix}z\\
b\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathcal{H}\right\}

where in the fourth equality we have used the Sions minimax theorem and in the fifth the fact that if
h=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a\\
b\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{H}
, then the inner supremum is finite if and only if
a=z
.
∎
