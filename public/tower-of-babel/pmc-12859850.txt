The mediating role of self-regulatory fatigue on the relationship between insomnia and sleep-related worry among shift nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Qingfen Zeng, Yumeng Zhang, Heting Liang, Yunting Li, Xia Zhang, Chaoping Wang, Xiaoli Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShift work frequently disrupts circadian rhythms in nurses, posing threats not only to their physical and mental health but also potentially impacting the quality of care and patient safety. Nurses are a high-risk group for sleep-related worry, yet the intrinsic mechanisms linking insomnia, self-regulatory fatigue, and sleep-related worry among shift nurses remain unclear. This study aimed to explore the mediating role of self-regulatory fatigue in the relationship between insomnia and sleep-related worry.MethodsThis cross-sectional study employed a convenience sampling method to survey shift nurses from a tertiary Grade A general hospital in Zunyi City between February and April 2025. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS), the Self-Regulatory Fatigue Scale (SRF-S), and the Anxiety and Preoccupation about Sleep Questionnaire (APSQ). Univariate analysis, Spearman correlation analysis, and hierarchical regression analysis were conducted to investigate factors influencing sleep-related worry. Structural equation modeling was used to test the mediating effect of self-regulatory fatigue.ResultsThe final sample comprised 988 participants, predominantly female (92.21%), aged 31–40 years (53.14%), and married (74.09%). Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations between insomnia and self-regulatory fatigue (r = 0.557), between insomnia and sleep-related worry (r = 0.679), and between self-regulatory fatigue and sleep-related worry (r = 0.565) (all P < 0.01). Structural equation modeling indicated that self-regulatory fatigue played a partial mediating role in the relationship between insomnia and sleep-related worry, with a mediation effect value of 0.226 (95% CI: 0.177, 0.277), accounting for 32.56% of the total effect.ConclusionShift nurses experience a relatively high level of sleep-related worry. Self-regulatory fatigue partially mediates the relationship between insomnia and sleep-related worry in this population. This suggests that nursing managers should adopt comprehensive measures, focusing on both improving nurses’ insomnia and reducing their levels of self-regulatory fatigue to effectively alleviate sleep-related worry.Keywords: Nurses, Shift work, Sleep-related worry, Insomnia, Self-regulatory fatigue, Mediating effect

Introduction

Against the backdrop of sustained pressure on global healthcare systems, a profound contradiction has emerged between the expansion of public health demands and the structural shortage of medical resources. Compounded by an aging population and a growing burden of chronic diseases, healthcare institutions have become increasingly reliant on nursing services. Consequently, the workload intensity, technical complexity, and psychological burden on the nursing workforce have escalated significantly. According to a World Health Organization (WHO) report, a global shortage of nurses persists [1]. As a conventional strategy to address workforce shortages, “shift work” (e.g., “three-shift rotation,” “consecutive night shifts”) has become the norm in the nursing profession. However, unlike regular daytime work, shift schedules directly disrupt the body’s inherent circadian rhythm and constitute a core risk factor for sleep disturbances among nurses [1, 2]. Data indicates that the prevalence of sleep disorders among shift nurses in China is as high as 90.1% [3]. These disturbances encompass not only symptoms such as insomnia and daytime sleepiness [4] but also give rise to excessive worry concerning sleep quality, its consequences, and perceived loss of control over sleep [5]. For nurses engaged in long-term shift work, the schedule forces them to remain awake during biologically programmed sleep periods. The frequent switching between day and night shifts leads to irregular sleep timing and reduced sleep duration, making nurses prone to excessive preoccupation with sleep issues. This preoccupation is defined as “sleep-related worry” [6, 7], characterized by apprehensions about the potential repercussions of insufficient sleep (e.g., work errors, health deterioration) and doubts regarding one’s own ability to control sleep. Multiple studies suggest that the current state of sleep-related worry among shift nurses is concerning [8, 9]. Severe sleep-related worry not only elevates nurses’ risk of developing emotional disorders such as depression and anxiety [5, 10] but also impairs cognitive function [11, 12], leading to decreased work efficiency [13] and an increased rate of work-related errors [14]. A multicenter cross-sectional study involving 1,831 nurses revealed a negative correlation between sleep-related worry and both job well-being and retention intention. Sleep-related worry was found to directly diminish job well-being and indirectly weaken retention intention through this pathway, posing a threat to the stability of the nursing workforce [15]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying sleep-related worry and exploring effective mitigation strategies represent an urgent need for ensuring both the quality of care and the resilience of the nursing workforce.

Among the various factors contributing to sleep-related worry, insomnia is recognized as a central antecedent. Characterized by insufficient sleep duration or quality leading to impaired daytime functioning, insomnia triggers symptoms such as fatigue and difficulty concentrating [16], which can directly foster sleep-related worry. This worry, in turn, may exacerbate the initial insomnia, establishing a persistent vicious cycle [7]. While existing evidence confirms a positive correlation between insomnia and sleep-related worry [9, 17], the specific pathways underlying this relationship remain unclear, leaving key questions unanswered: Through what mechanism does insomnia influence sleep-related worry? Could a pivotal psychological variable act as a mediating bridge?

Given the demanding and high-stress nature of nursing work, we postulate that insomnia may intensify sleep-related worry by depleting nurses’ psychological resources, consequently undermining their self-regulatory capacity and leading to self-regulatory fatigue. “Self-regulatory fatigue,” a central concept within self-regulation theory, refers to the diminished willpower and impaired regulatory capacity resulting from the depletion of psychological resources after prolonged engagement in self-control efforts, such as emotion management, cognitive focus, and behavioral restraint [18]. On one hand, nursing is characterized by high intensity and frequent shift rotations [19]. Under sustained exposure to such work-related stress, nurses’ finite self-regulatory resources are continuously consumed with limited opportunity for adequate recovery [20]. Furthermore, insomnia induced by shift work can exacerbate this drain on psychological resources, thereby promoting the onset of self-regulatory fatigue [21]. On the other hand, existing research has confirmed a close association between the severity of nurses’ self-regulatory fatigue and their level of sleep-related worry [22]. This collective evidence positions self-regulatory fatigue as a potential critical bridge linking sleep disturbances to subsequent adverse psychological outcomes, such as sleep-related worry.

In summary, while associations among insomnia, self-regulatory fatigue, and sleep-related worry have been observed, the underlying pathway connecting these variables remains unclear within the shift nurse population. To address this gap, the present study proposes a hypothesized mediation model (Fig. 1). We posit that self-regulatory fatigue mediates the relationship between insomnia and sleep-related worry among shift nurses. This study aims to employ structural equation modeling to test this hypothesis, thereby elucidating the pathway through which insomnia influences sleep-related worry via self-regulatory fatigue in this cohort. The findings are anticipated to provide a theoretical foundation for developing targeted interventions aimed at alleviating sleep-related worry among nurses.

Method

Study design and participants

Research design

This cross-sectional study employed a convenience sampling method to recruit shift nurses from the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University between February and April 2025. Data collection adhered to the principles of anonymity and informed consent.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Based on the literature review [23, 24], we established the following inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Possession of a valid nursing practice certificate; (2) Engagement in shift work for ≥ 6 months; (3) Informed consent and voluntary participation in the survey.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Nurses not formally employed by the hospital, such as interns, trainees, or residents (the shift arrangements, workload, and sense of professional belonging among such personnel may differ systematically from those of the hospital’s regular, on-duty shift nurses. Excluding them helps reduce the introduction of confounding variables); (2) Absence during the survey period due to reasons such as further studies, sick leave, or personal leave.

Sample size

According to Kendall’ s sample size calculation principle, the sample size should be 5 to 10 times the number of independent variable [25]. This study involved 17 variables, and considering a 20% rate of invalid responses, the required sample size ranged from 102 to 204. Additionally, based on the requirements for constructing structural equation models, the sample size should not be less than 200 [26].Therefore, to ensure data representativeness and meet the analytical needs of the structural equation model, the study ultimately expanded the sample size and included 1,076 shift nurses.

Measures

Demographic and occupational characteristics

This questionnaire was designed by the researchers based on a literature review and focus group discussions. It includes 11 variables: gender, age, marital status, number of children, educational level, professional title, department, years of service, monthly income, monthly night shift frequency, and self-rated health status.

Anxiety and preoccupation about sleep questionnaire (APSQ)

The Chinese version of the APSQ, developed by Jansson-Fröjmark [6]and revised by Shi Xuliang [5], was used to assess the level of sleep-related worry among shift nurses. This scale consists of two dimensions: worry about sleep consequences (6 items) and inability to control sleep (4 items), totaling 10 items. A Likert 5-point scoring method (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) was adopted, with a total score ranging from 10 to 50. Higher scores indicate a more severe level of sleep-related worry among shift nurses. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.953. Example items for the “worry about sleep consequences” dimension include: “I worry that the amount of sleep I get will affect my mood.” Example items for the “Inability to control sleep” dimension include: “I worry that I cannot control my sleep.”

Athens insomnia scale (AIS)

This scale [27] is a self-assessment tool for sleep quality developed based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), and consists of 8 items. It employs a Likert 4-point scoring method (0 = no problem, 3 = severe delay or no sleep), with a total score ranging from 0 to 24. A score of < 4 indicates no sleep disorder, 4–6 suggests suspected insomnia, and ≥ 7 indicates insomnia. Higher scores reflect more severe insomnia. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.910. Example item: “Time to fall asleep (time from turning off the lights to falling asleep).” Participants rate this item on a scale from 0 points (no issues) to 3 points (severe delay or inability to fall asleep).

Self-regulatory fatigue scale (SRF-S)

This scale was developed by Nes [28] and translated and revised by Ligang W [29]. It consists of 16 items across three dimensions: cognitive control (6 items), emotional control (5 items), and behavioral control (5 items). A Likert 5-point scoring method (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) is used, with total scores ranging from 16 to 80. Higher scores indicate more severe self-regulatory fatigue. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.830. Example items include: “I feel energetic” (cognitive control), “I get frustrated easily” (emotional control), and “I’ve had the urge to destroy something” (behavioral control).

Data collection

Data were collected via an online survey platform. To ensure completeness, all questionnaire items were set as mandatory. Technical restrictions were implemented, allowing only one submission per registered account and device. Following approval from the head of the Nursing Department and with the assistance of head nurses from respective units, the survey link (generated via Wenjuanxing, a widely used Chinese online survey platform) and QR code were distributed within departmental communication groups. Potential participants were informed of the study’s purpose, content, and significance. Participation was entirely voluntary, and respondents could withdraw at any time. After data collection, two investigators independently verified each response. The criteria for exclusion included completion times that were significant outliers (i.e., below one-third of the mean) and responses that demonstrated systematic patterns. A total of 1,076 surveys were distributed, with 988 valid responses collected, yielding a valid response rate of 91.82%.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 and R version 4.4.3. Categorical data were described using frequencies (n) and percentages (%). The normality of continuous data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (‾X ± SD), while non-normally distributed data are presented as the median with interquartile range [M (P25, P75)]. Differences in sleep-related worry scores across demographic characteristics were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis H test, as appropriate. If the data were normally distributed, Pearson correlation analysis was employed; otherwise, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used. Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. To further identify factors influencing sleep-related worry, hierarchical linear regression analysis was conducted. Demographic and occupational variables that showed significant differences in the univariate analyses were entered as control variables in the first block. Subsequently, insomnia and self-regulatory fatigue scores were added as independent variables in the following blocks to assess their unique contribution to sleep-related worry. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was examined to check for multicollinearity. AMOS 28.0 was used to construct a structural equation model to examine the mediating role of self-regulatory fatigue in the relationship between insomnia and sleep-related worry. Goodness-of-fit indices, including CMIN/df < 5, GFI > 0.90, CFI > 0.90, TLI > 0.90, and RMSEA < 0.08, indicated an adequate model fit. The Bootstrap method was applied to test the mediating effect, and the 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated. A 95% CI that did not include zero indicated a statistically significant mediating effect. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Based on modification indices, the model was adjusted by adding two paths: “behavioral control + inability to control sleep” and “cognitive control + inability to control sleep”. Discussion

Current status of insomnia, self-regulatory fatigue, and sleep-related worry among shift nurses

The results of this study indicate that shift nurses exhibit significant levels of insomnia, self-regulatory fatigue, and sleep-related worry. Specifically, the median insomnia score among shift nurses was 11.00 (8.00, 15.00). According to the AIS scoring criteria (a score of ≥ 7 indicates insomnia), this suggests that insomnia is prevalent within this group. This result is slightly higher than that reported in the study by Fenfen [9], which may be related to the demographic composition of the participants, among whom 92.21% were female and 74.09% were married. After shifts, these nurses often undertake household responsibilities such as cooking, childcare, and caring for elderly family members [31, 32], making it difficult for them to compensate for sleep loss, thereby further compromising sleep quality. Meanwhile, the median self-regulatory fatigue score among shift nurses was 49.00 (43.00, 54.00), indicating a moderate to high level of self-regulatory fatigue. This result is slightly higher than that reported by Yuhuan [21] in their survey of 942 clinical nurses. The discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that all participants in this study were shift nurses. Furthermore, shift nurses often face heavy workloads and frequent night shifts, which can lead to irregular sleep patterns. This sustained high-pressure environment and disrupted circadian rhythms, combined with insufficient recovery, collectively exacerbate the depletion of psychological resources and contribute to self-regulatory fatigue [33].

The sleep-related worry score among shift nurses was 39.00 (32.00, 42.00), indicating a relatively high level, which is consistent with previous studies [8, 9].This suggests that the current state of sleep-related worry is alarming. Notably, the score for the “worry about sleep consequences” dimension was higher than that for the “inability to control sleep” dimension, indicating that shift nurses are more concerned about the negative impacts of poor sleep on their work, daily life, and physical and mental health. Several factors may explain this finding. On one hand, sleep is crucial for an individual’s quality of life and health. Shift work may increase nurses’ risk of various health issues, such as obesity, cardiovascular diseases, or cancer [34–36]. On the other hand, the unique nature of nursing work requires high levels of concentration and leaves no room for error. Frequent night shifts exacerbate the long-term high-stress state of nurses, making them prone to anxiety and tension, which further affects sleep quality [37, 38]. This, in turn, intensifies shift nurses’ concerns about the consequences of poor sleep. This study also found that sleep-related worry levels were higher among female nurses, those aged 31–40, those with higher professional titles, those working in ICU, those with less than 20 years of work experience, those with a monthly night shift frequency of ≥ 5 times, and those with poor self-rated health. Previous studies [8, 22, 39, 40] have also identified associations between gender, age, work experience, monthly night shift frequency, self-rated health, and sleep-related worry among nurses. Therefore, nursing administrators should pay attention to the sleep-related worry levels of shift nurses, with a particular focus on female staff and controlling the frequency of night shifts. It should be ensured that nurses have at least two consecutive days of rest after shifts, free from hospital-related disruptions or interruptions [41]. Additionally, timely psychological support and humanistic care should be provided to shift nurses experiencing severe sleep-related worry [8], thereby alleviating their level of sleep-related worry.

Correlations among insomnia, self-regulatory fatigue, and sleep-related worry

Spearman correlation analysis confirmed significant positive correlations among the three variables (r = 0.557–0.679, all P < 0.01), which is consistent with previous research [9]. Insomnia, as a core stressor, directly impairs nurses’ physical and mental recovery, leading to daytime fatigue and cognitive impairment [16]. These symptoms not only immediately trigger concerns about sleep quality but also persistently deplete self-regulatory resources, such as the demands for maintaining emotional stability during patient interactions and focusing on complex care tasks, thereby exacerbating self-regulatory fatigue [42]. Conversely, self-regulatory fatigue weakens nurses’ ability to cope with sleep-related negative emotions, further amplifying sleep-related worry and forming a vicious cycle of “insomnia → self-regulatory fatigue → sleep-related worry” [22]. This pattern of correlation underscores the interconnectedness of the three variables and provides a foundation for the subsequent mediation analysis.

Self-regulatory fatigue partially mediates the relationship between insomnia and sleep-related worry in shift nurses

The mediation effect analysis results indicate that self-regulatory fatigue plays a partial mediating role in the relationship between insomnia and sleep-related worry among shift nurses. The mediation effect value was 0.226, accounting for 32.56% of the total effect. This suggests that insomnia not only directly triggers sleep-related worry but also indirectly exacerbates the degree of sleep-related worry by depleting nurses’ self-regulatory resources.

Self-regulatory fatigue plays a partial mediating role between insomnia and sleep-related worry, indicating that this mechanism does not operate in isolation but is likely moderated by various individual and contextual factors. Firstly, individual psychological characteristics, such as resilience, coping styles, and emotion regulation strategies, may moderate the impact of insomnia on self-regulatory fatigue. Nurses with higher resilience may be more resistant to the depletion of psychological resources caused by sleep deprivation, thereby attenuating the indirect effect of insomnia on sleep-related worry via self-regulatory fatigue [43, 44]. Data from this study show that nurses with “good” self-rated health had significantly lower sleep-related worry scores (35.00) compared to those with “General” (39.00) and “poor” (43.00) ratings. This finding indirectly suggests that positive mental health and adaptability may serve as important protective factors [23]. Secondly, work-related factors such as night shift frequency, workload, and unit culture may also influence this mediating pathway. In this study, nurses with ≥ 8 night shifts per month reported the highest sleep-related worry scores (40.00), and scores among nurses in ICU (40.00) were also significantly higher than those in general wards (39.00). This corroborates the notion that high-pressure, high-frequency shift work environments may exacerbate psychological resource depletion, making insomnia more likely to trigger worry through the pathway of self-regulatory fatigue. For instance, nurses in high-stress units such as the ICU, who are chronically exposed to heightened stress, may experience more rapid depletion of their self-regulatory resources, potentially making the pathway from insomnia to sleep-related worry via self-regulatory fatigue more pronounced [45, 46]. Furthermore, the adequacy of organizational and social support systems is also a significant factor influencing the level of self-regulatory fatigue [44]. A supportive team atmosphere and leadership may buffer the psychological resource depletion caused by insomnia, potentially weakening the bridging role of self-regulatory fatigue between insomnia and sleep-related worry [9]. Research on moderating factors of nurses’ self-regulatory fatigue is still developing. Future studies could further investigate the specific moderating effects of the aforementioned variables (e.g., resilience, unit support culture) to gain a more comprehensive understanding of this mediating mechanism and to provide a basis for developing more targeted, stratified intervention measures.

Practical implications

The findings of this study carry clear implications for nursing management and clinical practice. Firstly, the mediating role of self-regulatory fatigue between insomnia and sleep-related worry suggests that focusing solely on sleep improvement may be insufficient to fully alleviate nurses’ sleep-related worry, necessitating concurrent interventions targeting psychological resource depletion. Clinical administrators could consider implementing a dual-track intervention strategy: on the one hand, improving nurses’ sleep quality through schedule optimization, sleep hygiene education, and environmental adjustments; on the other hand, introducing psychological resource recovery training, such as mindfulness-based stress reduction, emotion regulation workshops, and cognitive restructuring training, to reduce levels of self-regulatory fatigue. Secondly, the high-risk groups identified by the study—such as female nurses, those with high night shift frequency, and those with poor self-rated health—should be prioritized for focused attention. It is recommended that nursing management departments establish screening mechanisms for sleep and psychological well-being, conducting regular assessments and providing early support for these groups to prevent sleep-related worry from evolving into chronic psychological burdens. Furthermore, the results support the integration of organizational support and team climate building into nurses’ health promotion systems. By fostering a supportive work environment, providing flexible rest arrangements, and enhancing psychological support from leaders and colleagues, the resource depletion experienced by nurses during shift work can be mitigated, indirectly reducing sleep-related worry. Finally, it is advisable to incorporate courses on sleep science and psychological self-regulation into nurses’ continuing education. This would enhance nurses’ awareness and self-management capabilities regarding their own sleep issues, promoting a shift from passive endurance to active regulation, thereby improving overall occupational health and nursing safety.

Limitations and future directions

First, the cross-sectional study design cannot establish causal relationships between variables. Future research could build upon our model by conducting longitudinal studies or intervention experiments to more effectively validate the causal pathways among insomnia, self-regulatory fatigue, and sleep-related worry. Second, the participants in this study were solely recruited from a tertiary Grade A hospital in the same region, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should consider multi-regional and multi-center collaborations to obtain more representative samples. Additionally, all data were collected through self-reports from nurses, which may introduce common method bias. Future research should further validate the causal pathways through longitudinal or experimental designs, expand the diversity and representativeness of samples, and combine both subjective and objective measures (e.g., actigraphy for sleep monitoring) to enhance precision. Furthermore, exploring moderating variables that influence these pathways and developing targeted intervention strategies to translate findings into practical applications represent important directions for future investigation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates significant correlations among insomnia, self-regulatory fatigue, and sleep-related worry in shift nurses, and for the first time, validates the partial mediating role of self-regulatory fatigue between insomnia and sleep-related worry. This finding indicates that sleep-related worry is not only directly triggered by insomnia but are also exacerbated by the depletion of psychological resources. Therefore, to effectively alleviate sleep-related worry among shift nurses, nursing managers should adopt comprehensive intervention strategies. While directly improving nurses’ sleep quality, it is equally crucial to prioritize and implement measures to reduce their self-regulatory fatigue, such as optimizing organizational support and enhancing psychological resilience training. This multi-faceted approach will promote the physical and mental well-being of nurses and maintain the stability of the nursing workforce.
