Knowledge, attitude, and self-medication practices among healthcare students from the metropolitan City of South India
Authors: Vigneshwaran Easwaran, Sreeja Padinjare Anthoor, Khalid Orayj, Mohammad Jaffar Sadiq Mantargi, Monisha Dinakaran, Avanthi Nagarajan, Villivakkam Saravanan Harini, Krishnaraju Venkatesan, Noohu Abdulla Khan, Sirajudeen Shaik Alavudeen, Narayana Goruntla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-medication is common in many developing and developed countries. The young population and students are highly influenced and opt for self-medication. Healthcare students often practice self-medication despite their knowledge of both the positive and negative consequences. Thus, the goals of the present study included estimating the difference in knowledge, attitude, and practice regarding self-medication between healthcare students and identifying the influence of demographic characteristics on the knowledge, attitude, and practice among healthcare students. A cross-sectional study was conducted among undergraduate healthcare students at a private university in South India. The non-probabilistic convenience sampling technique was employed in this study. The pre-validated questionnaire was used to collect data. Binary logistic regression and chi-square tests were used to analyze the data. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A total of 466 healthcare students participated in the current survey, 234 of whom were males. The most current study participants were pharmacy students (44.8%), followed by nursing students (21.5%). More than 85.6% and 84.8% of the study participants had adequate knowledge and a positive attitude regarding self-medication usage. However, good practice was low (15.9%). The male participants tend to have a more positive attitude than females (p < 0.001). The nursing students had a higher positive attitude towards self-medication (p = 0.002). The practice of self-medication differs significantly among medicine and allied health students compared to pharmacy students (p = 0.002 and p = 0.006). Most students obtained information about self-medication from community pharmacists (41.2%), followed by social media networks or the internet (24.5%). Most of the current participants agree that self-medication is not safe in all age groups. The results highlight the urgent need for structured educational interventions and public health awareness to promote responsible self-medication, including better curriculum content, interprofessional collaborative education, and restricted access to health information. Keywords: Self-medication, Healthcare students, KAP, South india, Responsible self-medicationSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Background

Being healthy is an essential component, and medications play a significant role. The World Health Organization considers medicines and health technologies to be one of the six building blocks of the healthcare system1. As one of the most essential pillars of the healthcare system, patient safety is enhanced and improved by the appropriate use of pharmaceuticals2–4. The inappropriate use and misuse of medications is a common issue worldwide, with approximately 68% of European countries and 42% of American nationals5. Easier access to various medicines worldwide might be an additional contributor to the inappropriate use of medications6. The medicines are being used inappropriately on so many occasions in different forms, including self-medication7–9.

As per the definition provided by the World Health Organization, self-medication is the “use of medicinal products by the consumer to treat self-recognized disorders or symptoms, or the intermittent or continued use of a medication10. In addition, it included using leftover medicines at home, purchasing medications without a prescription, using an expired prescription, or obtaining medicines from friends or relatives11. Self-medication can treat minor ailments and acute medical conditions in the right circumstances12. However, it has a negative aspect that could cause significant issues, including adverse drug reactions, treatment failure, antimicrobial resistance, etc13. However, self-medication is common in many developing and developed countries, with a prevalence rate of 25.6% to 73.6%13. People in developing countries assume that self-medication is a reasonable option to reduce healthcare costs, without fully understanding its negative consequences14. In India, self-medication is more common, and the prevalence ranges from 8.3 to 92%15. Easy availability of medications, conjoined with inadequate health services, brings about the high proportion of self-medication in India, and it is more common among younger people from both urban and rural communities16,17. The previous reports suggested that the prevalence of self-medication is frequent among university students around the world5,13. Multiple factors, including social, economic, and cultural influences, have been shaping the practice of self-medication. Preferably, young adults are highly influenced by pharmaceutical product marketing strategies through social media and the internet, leading to self-medication13,15,18. The study among the student population is critical because they are the community’s future.

Healthcare students differ from other students in that they possess a high level of knowledge about medicines and diseases, which enables them to practice self-medication more easily. In addition, future healthcare providers must understand their role in the responsible use of self-medications19. As healthcare students, they have an additional responsibility to educate the public on the responsible use of self-medications20. As reported from other studies around the world, the prevalence of self-medication is high among healthcare students, despite their knowledge of the positive and negative consequences21. However, research studies among healthcare students from South India are scarce. It is also unclear about the differences in KAP among various healthcare students, which could help target future interventions. Most studies in this area among Indian students are conducted among non-healthcare students or do not evaluate the factors associated with self-medications. Therefore, the present research was designed to assess the current levels of knowledge, attitude, and practice regarding self-medication among students from various healthcare disciplines. Additionally, the current study also aimed to identify the influence of demographic characteristics on these knowledge, attitude, and practice levels.

Materials and methods

Study design and area

This is a cross-sectional survey conducted over 4 months from September 2024 to December 2024. This survey was conducted among healthcare students enrolled at ACS Medical College and Hospital, affiliated to Dr. M.G.R. Educational and Research Institute, a private deemed university located in Chennai, the fourth-largest metropolitan city in southern India22. It is also the capital of one of the southern states of India23. We invited all undergraduate healthcare students at the current study site using a non-probabilistic convenience sampling technique. Students who were unwilling to participate or refused to provide informed consent were excluded from the study. In addition, the fifth-year medical students were excluded from the study due to their inaccessibility because of internship postings.

Sample size

SurveyMonkey, available online, was used to estimate the number of students to be included in the study. With a 95% confidence interval and a 5% margin of error, the required sample size was 376. By assuming a 5% non-response rate, the final required sample size was calculated to be 395.

Study tool

A self-administered questionnaire was adapted from previously published literature and modified to suit the current study’s settings24–26. The questionnaire was originally prepared and distributed in English. The face validity of the questionnaire was ensured by an expert panel comprised of various healthcare professionals. Including physicians, pharmacists, nurses, dentists, and allied health care professionals. The estimated face validity was found to be acceptable. The questionnaire’s reliability was assessed through a pilot study involving 10 undergraduate students. The necessary modifications to the questionnaire, based on the pilot study findings, were not included in the final analysis. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The estimated Cronbach’s alpha for the whole scale was found to be 0.809. The Cronbach alpha value for the individual domains, such as knowledge, attitude, practice, and reasons, was found to be 0.760, 0.849, 0.668, and 0.672, respectively. To verify the internal consistency, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for the whole questionnaire and found to be 0.68. The study questionnaire comprises questions on demographic characteristics (8 items), knowledge (10 items), Attitude (7 items), Practice (5 items), source of information for self-medication (1 item), and reasons for self-medication use (5 items).

Data collection

The research assistants collected data by meeting the healthcare students in person at the university and administering the printed questionnaire. The research assistants explained the purpose and method of the research to the study participants to obtain consent. The study participants received assistance from the research assistants if they had any questions or encountered difficulties filling out the questionnaire.

Scorings

The knowledge associated with self-medication was measured using a 10-point scale. Each correct answer was given a point of 1 and a wrong answer a point of 0. In addition, don’t know’ responses were considered incorrect responses. The total knowledge score was calculated by summing the scores of all knowledge items, which ranged from 0 to 10. The percentages were calculated for the knowledge scores of all study participants. Based on Bloom’s cutoff points, the valid responses to the knowledge domain were categorized as adequate (61–100%) and inadequate (less than or equal to 60%).

The attitude and practice scores were derived using Likert scale-based statements. The total scores for both the attitude and practice domains were calculated by summing the scores of all items. The responses ranged from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” and the scores ranged from 1 to 5, respectively. The scores for the negatively phrased items were reversed. A score of 21 or higher was considered a positive attitude, with a sum score ranging from 7 to 35. The practice domain score ranges from 5 to 25, with scores greater than 15 considered positive practice.

The items related to reasons for self-medication were administered on a Likert scale. The responses were converted into a binary variable. The scores ranged from 1 to 5, and the negatively phrased items were reversed. Scores of 1 to 3 were considered negative for the relevant reason, and scores of 4 & 5 were considered positive.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS 22.0) (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Both descriptive and inferential statistics were used for statistical analysis. The binary logistic regression analysis analyzed the relationship between socio-demographic characteristics and knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Additionally, a chi-square test was conducted to determine the frequency distribution of knowledge, attitude, practice, and reasons associated with self-medication among various healthcare students. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of ACS Medical College and Hospital, with approval number #1409/2024/IEC/ACSMCH. An informed consent form, including the research protocol and objectives, was provided at the beginning of the survey questionnaire. The survey was conducted only with the study participants who gave informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants and adhered to the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies.

Results

The most current study participants were pharmacy students (44.8%), followed by nursing students (21.5%). The fewest number of students from the medicine specialization participated. The parents of all the study participants held non-medical occupations. Family income was between 20,000 and 50,000 Indian rupees (approximately $225 to $560) for 88.2% of the study participants (Table 1).

Discussion

The current study aimed to explore and compare knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding responsible self-medication among healthcare students in South India. The study highlights essential insights into the self-medication behaviour of health science students, which have far-reaching implications for public health and professional practice.

In developing countries where healthcare accessibility is limited and challenging, the practice of self-medication is consistently seen in various cultures27. The results obtained in the current study clearly outline the extent of self-medication among young healthcare students, with similarities to the findings published in earlier studies6,14,25,26,28. The prevalence of self-medication in the current study is significant; however, the nature and quality of self-medication should be taken into account, rather than prevalence alone, while dictating its health impact28.

The study reveals a disproportionate gap between knowledge and practice, despite most healthcare students demonstrating adequate knowledge (85.6%) about self-medication and its implications; however, only a few exhibited good practices. This gap between knowledge and practice indicates that only information regarding self-medication and its impacts is not enough; the sense of responsibility must be inculcated among the students to practice responsible self-medication29. Contrary to expectations, the knowledge, attitude, and practice scores do not correlate with demographic variables, except for gender and specialization. Family income was the most non-contributing factor identified. These findings contradict other studies, where family background and educational level were found to be significant30.

Knowledge about self-medication

The positive impact of the students’ educational background was evident with an adequate level of knowledge among students regarding self-medication, where most of the participants responded that the medications must be continued as per the regimen, even after symptom relief, and further recognized potential risks associated with inappropriate self-medication. These findings align with research published that showed a relatively adequate knowledge and awareness among pharmacy and medical students in India and other developing countries31.

However, even with adequate knowledge, specific gaps in medication handling were identified. A higher proportion of students lacked knowledge about proper storage conditions, highlighting an implementation gap in drug-related knowledge. Inadequate drug storage practices can compromise drug efficacy and stability, leading to the development of adverse drug reactions. This scenario suggests that the educational curricula may have adequately covered pharmacological effects, but practice aspects, such as drug storage and expiry awareness, may be inadequately addressed32.

A reasonably uniform distribution of knowledge was identified across several healthcare disciplines in the current study settings. This might be due to shared foundational courses, and or institutional policies on curriculum integration33.

Attitude towards self-medication

Demographic characteristics strongly influenced attitudes toward self-medication, particularly by gender, discipline, and year of study. Male students expressed a more positive attitude towards responsible self-medication than females. A similar gender based difference was identified in various studies regarding drug utilization34. However, such outcomes require further investigation to understand if this attitude is based on sound judgment or overconfidence.

Attitudes differed by participants’ educational discipline. The pharmacy students showed a professional attitude, which might be attributed to their pharmacological training and education. On the other hand, nursing and allied health science students showed a lack of concern regarding the duration of usage and monitoring symptoms. Students who are from the discipline of medicine expressed balanced views and cautioned against self-medication and the use of drugs during pregnancy and potential drug interactions. These deviations highlight the influence of academic exposure and professional orientation on student attitude.

Furthermore, the higher the participants’ education, the better their responsible attitude towards drug use, indicating a cumulative effect of education. These findings are similar to the literature, suggesting that with advances in education, better risk assessment capabilities and decision-making skills will be developed in the students34.

The year of study is significantly influencing attitudes toward self-medication, a result similar to that reported by Daanish et al.35. It is concerning that 83% of students expressed that self-medication is better than visiting a clinician. This highlights overconfidence in personal knowledge, convenience-seeking behaviour, or dissatisfaction with healthcare accessibility. Regardless of these findings, this attitude weakens the importance of clinical diagnosis and professional regulation in all cases.

Practice of self-medication

A greater number of concerning facts were identified through the practice data. While knowledge and attitude were generally positive, real self-medication is extremely risky and inappropriate. Most of the students opt for self-medication without reading the package leaflets, even continuing the usage of medications without proper medical advice. These practices expose individuals to the risk of drug toxicity, drug resistance, and treatment failure36.

Remarkably, pharmacy and medical students reported a lower self-medication rate than allied health and nursing students. This suggests that thorough comprehension could lead to caution and self-control, highlighting the importance of pharmacological knowledge and education in fostering professional conduct. These results are consistent with earlier research showing greater understanding and practice alignment among medical and pharmacy students37.

Allied health students expressed riskier behavior, including more extended periods of unsupervised medication usage and a greater tendency for self-medication. These issues highlight the deficiency in their education, necessitating curricular changes that prioritize pharmacological safety and pharmacovigilance. Prescription sharing is relatively positive, indicating a certain degree of accountability. It is concerning because over half of the participants disregarded drug information leaflets and turned to unofficial sources like peers and social media, which can lead to misinformation and inappropriate drug usage38. The middle- and lower family income groups tend to practice self-medication than higher-income groups39,40. These results contradict our findings, indicating that family income does not influence self-medication practice.

Sources of information for self-medication

It is encouraging that community pharmacists are identified as the primary source of information, which supports their training and the trustworthy advice they provide. However, the internet and social media were identified as the second most searched source for obtaining drug information. Such trends are also reported worldwide in various research publications highlighting the same fact41. The ease of accessibility of unsupported, irrational drug information poses a threat to rational drug use42. These findings advocate for educational interventions and for reshaping governmental regulations to regulate the availability of drug information online. Massive reliance on pharmacists also emphasizes the need to empower them through education, established procedures, and professional licensing to provide reliable, consistent drug-related advice. Additionally, it highlights the importance of pharmacists as primary drug counsellors, particularly in resource-limited settings42.

Reasons for practicing self-medication

According to the participants, the primary reason for self-medication was to save money and time. Such reasons can be justified in a fast-paced, resource-limited setting. However, this undervalues the professional consultation and increases the chance of developing adverse drug reactions and other negative consequences43. Pharmacy students prefer fast relief and ease of accessibility over professional care and pharmaceutical knowledge. If this attitude continues, self-medication will be considered normal even if it is not. Hence, it becomes imperative to educate the students about the limitations of pharmacological knowledge without clinical diagnosis and patient-specific factors44.

Implications for public health and professional education

The current study identifies the dual nature of the self-medication, i.e., if carried out professionally, it adds advantage to the primary care by reducing the strain on the healthcare system and encouraging patients’ autonomy; if practiced unethically under a lack of proper guidance it can lead to health risks such as conceal underlying ailments, unpleasant responses, antibiotic resistance and chronic adverse drug events45. Since these students will become healthcare providers in the future, their attitudes and practices will shape their conduct in patient counselling and treatment; in this regard, early and focused intervention is therefore essential. The gap between knowledge and practice can be closed by introducing practice modules on medication safety, rational drug use, and the critical evaluation of drug information sources across all healthcare settings14.

Additionally, workshops, simulations, and awareness campaigns can emphasize the importance of appropriate professional self-care. All institutions should promote interprofessional learning, where learners from various disciplines can interact and enhance their understanding of drug management through collaboration46.

Limitations

The current study stands out due to its fewer limitations, despite providing insightful information. Convenient sampling limits the generalizability of the study’s results. Recall bias is inherent to self-reported data, which was the preferred approach in the current study. Moreover, the cross-sectional approach is unable to account for behavioural changes over time or to establish causality. Despite the face validity of the current study tool, which was estimated by experts, we did not include students in the face validity assessment. Although the internal consistency of the study tool was acceptable, Cronbach’s alpha value was slightly lower for the practice and reasons domains. The study is confined to students from a single institution; thus, the influence of various factors, such as institutional culture and curriculum, may limit the external validity of the study’s outcomes. Longitudinal or mixed-model designs in future studies may be employed to understand how KAP has evolved over time.

Conclusion

According to the current study, students are well knowledgeable about self-medication and related aspects; however, there is a notable difference in their attitudes and practices by academic discipline and gender. In some instances, self-medication is considered a time saver and economical. Still, various students show risky behaviour like not reading drug labels, prolonged usage, and undertaking self-medication without professional advice. Pharmacy and medical students demonstrated more responsible practices regarding self-medication than allied health and nursing professionals.

The results identify the urgent need for structured educational interventions and public health awareness to promote responsible self-medication. The educational interventions should include better curriculum content, interprofessional collaborative education, and restricted access to health information. Healthcare students have a moral responsibility to create a safer medical culture, as they will be future healthcare practitioners. The health and safety of the community they serve will be enhanced through well-prepared educational programs and proper training.
