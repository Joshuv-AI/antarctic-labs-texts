Association between red cell distribution width-to-lymphocyte ratio and 30-day mortality in patients with ischemic stroke: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Liqiang Chen, Jianchao Li, Shuang Wang, Lizhen Zhao, Shuai Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIschaemic stroke (IS) has become a major health problem globally as it is one of the leading causes of long-term disability and death. This study aimed to evaluate the association between red cell distribution width (RDW) to lymphocyte (LYM) ratio (RLR) and 30-day mortality risk in patients with IS.MethodsThe present study employed a retrospectively cohort study design with the adult data extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV) databases between 2001 and 2019. The RLR was measured using RDW and LYM. Confounders were adjusted in Cox proportional hazards model. The outcome was 30-day mortality. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were establised. A further analysis was conducted on the basis of subgroup stratification by heart failure (HF) (yes or no), atrial fibrillation or flutter (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), dyslipidemia (yes or no), sepsis (yes or no), and age (≥ 65 years and < 65 years).ResultsIn this study, 1,127 adult patients with IS were finally identified. Among them,818 patients survived (the survival group) and 309 patients died (the death group). The mean age was older in individuals from the death group than those from the survival group (70.19 years vs. 64.56 years). The elevated levels of RLR were linked to an increased risk of mortality within 30 days in patients with IS, with an HR of 1.70 (95% CI: 1.34–2.17). Subgroup analyses showed that high RLR levels was a significant risk factor for mortality at 30 days particularly in IS patients aged ≥ 65 years, HF, no atrial fibrillation or flutter, no hypertension, no dyslipidemia, and no sepsis.ConclusionOur study shows that high levels of RLR were associated with an increased risk of 30-day mortality in patients with IS, providing additional prognostic information for the treatment and supportive care of these patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12959-024-00671-y.Keywords: RLR, Ischemic stroke, 30-day mortality, Retrospective study

Introduction

Globally, stroke has emerged as a major contributor to disability and the second leading cause of mortality with an annual mortality rate of about 5.5 million [1]. Ischemic stroke (IS) represents the most prevalent subtype of stroke, accounting for 60–80% of all stroke cases [2]. IS is caused by a reduction in the blood supply to an area of the brain due to vascular obstruction [3], which is characterized by high incidence of disease, disability, mortality, and recurrence [2]. Despite the improvement in prevention, the burden of IS continues to increase due to global population growth and demographic changes, as well as increased life expectancy [4–8]. Therefore, it is very important to find simple indicators to predict the prognosis of IS patients.

Red cell distribution width (RDW), a relatively inexpensive and widely available index, is widely used in the differential diagnosis of anemia [9]. It has been previously reported that there is an association between elevated levels of RDW and increased inflammatory responses and oxidative stress [10, 11], and RDW has been used as a prognostic biomarker for hypertension [12], pulmonary hypertension [13], acute kidney injury [14], and stroke [15]. In addition, lymphocytes (LYM), a marker of inflammatory state, was associated with stroke prognosis [16, 17]. RLR, which is defined as the proportion of RDW to LYM count, is a novel and simple inflammatory marker, and a significantly elevated RLR level may indicate a strong inflammatory response [18]. Studies have confirmed that RLR is a more powerful diagnostic tool than using RDW and LYM alone [19]. Huang et al. have reported that RLR can serve as a potential biomarker for colorectal cancer and predict its progression [18]. Ma et al. has indicated that preoperative RLR may be employed as a prospective biomarker to predict the prognosis of postoperative renal cell carcinoma patients [20]. Although RLR has been studied in various inflammatory disorders, however, to date, the effect of RLR on the mortality of IS patients has not been investigated.

The present study explored the association between RLR and 30-day mortality in patients with IS. To further explore the association, subgroup stratification by heart failure (HF) (yes or no), atrial fibrillation or flutter (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), dyslipidemia (yes or no), sepsis (yes or no), and age (≥ 65 years and < 65 years) were performed.

Methods

Study design and population

The present cohort study extracted adult data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care [MIMIC-III (https://mimic.mit.edu/docs/iii/) and MIMIC-IV (https://mimic.mit.edu/docs/iv/) ] databases, which includes data on de-identification of hospitalized patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston and Tertiary Academic Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts, USA between 2001 and 2012 and 2008–2019. Overall information includes demographics, vital sign measurements made at the bedside, laboratory test results, procedures, medications, caregiver notes, imaging reports, and mortality (in-hospital and out-of-hospital). Because the data are anonymized, the requirement for informed consent was waived. The following criteria were used to determine which participants were eligible for the study: (1) patients aged 18 years and older; (2) patients with a diagnosis of IS. Exclusion criteria: (1) missing survival information; (2) patient’s length of stay (LOS) in ICU less than 24 h; (3) abnormal RLR.

IS cases were identified according to ICD-9 code: 433.x1, 434.x1 and ICD-10 code: I63 [21]. The outcome event was death within 30 days following admission to ICU. The follow-up period began when patients first admitted to the ICU and ended 30 days following admission or at the time of death. Patient inclusion process is shown in Fig. 1.

Data extraction

The following variables were extracted: (1) demographic characteristics including gender, age, and race; (2) various scores and index, including Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPSII), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Glasgow coma scale (GCS), and Charlson comorbidity index (CCI); (3) comorbidities including acute myocardial infarction, HF, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, dementia, liver disease, renal failure, malignancy, sepsis; (4) vital signs including heart rate, systolic, diastolic, respiratory rate, temperature, ventilation, vasopressor; (5) medication use and therapy including anticoagulation, thrombolysis, removal thrombolysis; and (6) hematological and biochemical parameters including red cell distribution width (RDW), platelet (PLT), white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin, hematocrit, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), anion gap, bicarbonate, sodium, potassium, chloride, international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), oxygen saturation (SpO2), glucose, LYMs. All the data were collected using the first measurement within 24 h after admission to ICU.

Main variable and outcome

RDW-to-LYM ratio (RLR) was the main variable. RLR were classified according to the cut-off value of 13.12 based on X-tile. An RLR value < 13.12 indicates low RLR level, and an RLR value ≥ 13.12 or above indicates high RLR level. The 30-day mortality in IS patients was the outcome in our study.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were described as the mean ± standard deviation [Mean (± SD)], and Student-t test was employed to evaluate the discrepancies between the groups. Continuous variables exhibiting non-normal distributions were reported as medians along with the quartiles [M (Q1, Q3)], and compared using Mann-Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were represented as cases and percentages [n (%)], and Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test were employed for comparison between groups.

Random forests were used to impute missing values, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to compare the data prior to and subsequent to imputation (Supplementary Table 1). RLR on mortality within 30 days in patients with IS was analyzed using both univariable and multivariable Cox models, and hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated. Variables with P < 0.05 were screened by univariate COX proportional hazards model, and further screened by two-way stepwise regression method. Variables were selected as covariates according to the principle of minimizing Akaike information criterion, which was adjusted in the multivariate model. The multivariable model finally adjusted SAPSII, race, anion gap, CCI, anticoagulation, respiratory rate, age, and heart rate. A further analysis was conducted on the basis of subgroup stratification by heart failure (HF) (yes or no), atrial fibrillation or flutter (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), dyslipidemia (yes or no), sepsis (yes or no), and age (≥ 65 years and < 65 years), and heterogeneity across subgroups was assessed by Cox proportional hazards model.

Missing value statistics and missing value interpolation were performed using Python 3.9.12. Sensitivity analysis, difference comparison, univariable/multivariable Cox proportional risk modeling, and subgroup analysis were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves were completed by R version 4.2.1 (2022-06-23 ucrt). A P-value less than 0.05 was deemed to be statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographic and biochemical information of IS patients

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 1237 adult patients were included. We excluded 2 patients younger than 18 years, 104 deaths within 24 h after admission to ICU, and 4 patients with RLR outliers, and the final cohort included 1127 IS patients. Patients were stratified by 30-day mortality, and among them, 818 patients survived and 309 died. Table 1 shows the demographics and biochemical test results of patients with IS. We observed that the death group was older and had other comorbidities compared with the survival group. In addition, the SOFA score, SAPS II, and CCI were higher in the death group than the survival group. GCS of the death group was lower than the survival group. We similarly found significant differences for anticoagulation, thrombolysis, heart rate, systolic, respiratory rate, WBC, hemoglobin, hematocrit, creatinine, BUN, anion gap, bicarbonate, INR, PT, SpO2, glucose, RLR, ventilation and vasopressor in death group than the survival group. Notably, the death group had higher levels of RDW (15.31% vs. 14.66%, P < 0.001), lower levels of LYM (0.80 K/uL vs. 0.96 K/uL, P = 0.031), and higher levels of RLR (18.99 vs. 15.05, P = 0.007) compared to the survival group. For RLR, death group had a significantly higher mortality within 30 days than survival group (69.90% vs. 55.13%, P < 0.001).

Association of RLR and 30-day mortality in IS patients

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression model were performed, respectively. All covariates were adjusted in the multivariable model based on the statistically significant variables in Table 2 by step-based regression. The multivariable model finally adjusted SAPS II, race, anion gap, CCI, anticoagulation, respiratory rate, age, and heart rate were adjusted. The results are shown in Table 3, when using the low RLR levels (RLR < 13.12) as reference, the high RLR levels (RLR ≥ 13.12) presented a HR of 1.70 (95% CI: 1.34–2.17) and 1.38 (95% CI: 1.08–1.77) in the univariable and multivariable models, respectively, suggesting an association between high levels of RLR and mortality within 30 days in patients with IS. The KM curve revealed lower 30-day survival at high RLR levels than at low RLR levels, and the Log-rank results of the two RLR levels showed P < 0.0001 (Fig. 2). The C-index of RLR combined with SAPSII and RLR combined with CCI was improved compared to SAPSII and CCI alone (Supplementary Table 2).

RLR on 30-day mortality in patients with IS in different subgroups

A further analysis was conducted on the basis of subgroup stratification. The results confirmed that the high RLR levels was a significant risk factor for IS patients aged 65 years and older, with HF, without atrial fibrillation or flutter, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and sepsis. Further analysis indicated that high RLR was correlated with elevated risk of 30-day mortality in patients with IS aged > 65 with HF (HR = 1.93, 95%CI: 1.05–3.55) or without HF (HR = 1.78, 95%CI: 1.21–2.61) (Supplementary Table 3). The interaction effect of RLR with subgroup variables on the risk of 30-day mortality in patients with IS was also analyzed, which indicated that RLR have interaction effect with age on the risk of 30-day mortality in patients with IS (P = 0.034). Discussion

This study investigated the association between RLR and mortality within 30 days in patients with IS, finding that high levels of RLR were associated with an increased risk of death within 30 days after controlling for confounders. Subgroup analyses showed that high RLR levels was a significant risk factor in IS patients in age ≥ 65 years, with HF, without atrial fibrillation or flutter, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and sepsis.

Insufficient cerebrovascular blood and oxygen supply leads to massive death of nerve cells in ischemic areas and neurological function defects within a few hours, which is the main cause of IS [22]. In addition, IS can cause changes in routine hematological parameters and continuous inflammatory responses, leading to severe cardiac comorbidities such as HF [23]. Therefore, early identification of high-risk groups with poor prognosis using routine hematological parameters is crucial to improve patients’ adverse outcomes.

In the past research, new research advances have been made regarding routine hematologic parameters for IS risk assessment. Routine hematologic parameters, including neutrophil count/LYM count (NLR), eosinophils, and RDW, have been reported as useful and independent prognostic factors for IS [24]. Retrospective analysis of large clinical databases found that higher RDW was linked to an elevated risk of mortality from all causes in individuals with short- and long-term acute stroke [25]. Previous research has shown that RDW reflects the level of inflammation. For example, RDW was positively associated with inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) [26]. Several recent studies have also indicated that elevated RDW is linked to a higher risk or poorer prognosis for certain inflammation-related diseases, such as stroke [27] and HF [28]. On the other hand, LYMs are an important component of white blood cells that participate in pathophysiological processes through infiltration into ischemic tissue [29]. Previous research has shown that high levels of LYMs play an anti-inflammatory role by mediating the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin (IL) -10 and inhibiting inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α and IL-6 [30]. Based on the above discussion, it is known that increased levels of inflammation correspond to increased number of RDW and/or lower LYM levels. Consistent with this view, we found that the death group had higher RDW levels than the survival group, and the levels of LYM were reversed in IS patients stratified by 30-day mortality.

According to previous studies, changes in RDW and LYM count are associated with the inflammatory response of organs or tissues, which can reflect the prognosis of critically ill patients. Recent studies have confirmed that RLR, the ratio of these two parameters, has emerged as a novel inflammatory related predictor and independent risk factor. Katipoglu et al. [31] found that the preoperative RLR can be used as a biomarker for prolonged ICU stay among older patients undergoing cardiac surgery. In addition, RLR might be a promising biomarker in acute appendicitis, which help predict the progression [32]. Similarly, in our study, we found that high levels of RLR were associated with mortality within 30 days in IS patients after adjustment for confounders such as SAPS II, race, anion gap, CCI, anticoagulation, respiratory rate, age, and heart rate. Further subgroup analysis found that high RLR levels was a significant risk factor for the mortality within 30 days in patients with IS aged 65 years and above and HF.

Our study possesses certain strengths. Firstly, RLR can comprehensively reflect inflammation degree in organs and tissues, which can be evaluated quickly and easily. Secondly, we adjusted for other confounders to improve the reliability of the results. Thirdly, two parameters of RLR, RDW and LYMs, are readily available, additionally, RLR adds additional value to SAPSII and CCI, providing a convenient tool for early warning of mortality risk in IS patients. It should be noted that the study is subject to certain limitations. Firstly, this was retrospective study without long-term follow-up. Therefore, there might be some bias. Secondly, the database did not collect relevant lifestyle variables influencing patient outcome as well as the difference ofequipments used to determine the analyses LYM and RDW and therefore these factors could not be adjusted. Thirdly, this study analyzed the data of a ICU population with stroke diagnosis, and the generalization of findings is hampered to some extent to other population. More future studies were required to verify the findings in our study.

Conclusion

This study indicated that high levels of RLR were associated with an increased risk of mortality within 30 days in patients with IS. Subgroup analyses showed that high RLR levels was a significant risk factor in IS patients in age ≥ 65 years, with HF, without atrial fibrillation or flutter, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and sepsis. The findings might provide additional prognostic information for the treatment and supportive care of such patients.
