On the ability of Deep Neural Networks to Learn Granger Causality in Multi-Variate Time Series Data.
Malik Shahid Sultan, Hernando Ombao

Abstract
Granger Causality (GC) offers an elegant statistical framework to study the association between multivariate time series data. Linear Vector Autoregressive models (VAR) though have nice interpretation properties but have limited practical application due to underlying assumptions on the kind of associations that can be captured by these models. Numerous attempts have already been made in the literature that exploit the functional approximation power of Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) for the task of GC estimation. These methods however treat GC as a variable selection problem. We present a novel paradigm for approaching GC. We present this idea that GC is essentially linked with prediction and if a deep learning model is used to model the time series collectively or jointly, a well regularized model may learn the true granger causal structure from the data, given that there is enough training data. We propose to uncover the learned GC structure by comparing the model uncertainty or distribution of the residuals when the past of everything is used as compared to the one where a specific time series component is dropped from the model. We also compare the effect of input layer dropout on the ability of a neural network to learn granger causality from the data. We show that a well regularized model infact can learn the true GC structure from the data without explicitly adding terms in the loss function that guide the model to select variables or perform sparse regression.



1
Introduction

Neural Networks are power machine learning models that learn data dependent transformations. Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) excel at predictive modeling tasks, recent advances in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Computer Vision (CV) demonstrate tremendous predictive and representation learning power of these models. Time series data comprise of discrete measurements of stochastic processes. While analyzing multi-variate time series data one maybe interested in predictive modeling or understanding the dependence structure in the data. Understanding the structure in the data is important for various reasons one of which is explainability. Estimation and analysis of dependence structure is of great importance especially in healthcare data where the objective of the study can be to discover a bio-marker that differentiates between groups and subgroups. There are existing tools that take into account specific information while quantifying the dependence structure including cross correlation, mutual information, coherence, and spectral transfer entropy to name a few.

Granger Causality (GC) is another measure of dependence in multivariate time series data. GC essentially measures the ability of the past of a time series
i
to predict or forecast the future of another time series
j
. This framework proposed by Granger
[
4
]
relies on two main assumptions that cause happens prior to its effect and cause has a unique information about the future of its effect. If X(t) is a P dimensional real valued time series, then to test if time series
i
is a granger cause of time series
j
, two nested models are built. One model where the past of everything is used to predict the future of time series
j
, this is referred to as full model. A second model where the past of everything is used except that of time series
i
, this is referred to as reduced model. Model fit or residuals are computed under the full and reduced models. A test statistic based on the model fits is computed and compared with a reference distribution under null hypothesis of no granger causality from
i
to
j
.

Linear and non-linear models have been proposed in literature to estimate GC from observational data. Among linear models Vector Auto regressive (VAR) models are most widely employed for the purpose of GC. Deep Learning (DL) has also been extensively explored for GC estimation where the problem has been essentially dealt as a sparse regression problem. The famous framework introduced in
[
8
]
builds a case for the use of separate neural networks for modeling individual time series components. Sparsity inducing penalties are applied on the input layer of these component wise models to learn the dependence structure from the data. The weights of the input layer are gradually shrink to zero using proximal gradient descent. GC is then estimated from the weights of the input layer of these component wise models. Let X(t) be the recording of a P dimensional multivariate time series data at time instance t. A component wise NN
f_{p,\theta}
with
g_{p,\omega}
as the associated input layer, models the dynamics of this time series using the past
K
lags of the multivariate time series data.

X(p,t)=f_{p,\theta}(g_{p,\omega}(X(t-K)))+e(p,t)

(1)

Group Lasso in applied on
g_{p,\omega}
to sparsify the input layer weights and GC is estimated from the weights of
g_{p,\omega}
. As one may think this can become quickly computationally expensive if the number of time series grows or if the number of datasets grow like in healthcare one maybe interested in learning the GC between EEG recording of each subject separately for the purpose of personalized medicine or group level analysis.

Authors in
[
8
]
argue that inference on the weights of the neural network can not lead to meaningful conclusion for estimation of GC when a multivariate time series data is modeled using a single auto-regressive model. We however present a different point of view for GC in our work. We agree on the aforementioned statement if a joint model is trained it is not straight forward to estimate GC from the weights of the network. However GC is linked to prediction, if a joint model is trained to predict the future dynamics of a multivariate time series data, it may learn some Granger causal structure which has to be uncovered. This leads towards the motivation of the present work, where we show how to uncover the GC structure learned by the joint model. We in our work show that even if a joint model is being used to model the multivariate time series data, model explanations about GC can still be performed, by comparing the distribution of the residuals from the fit of the joint model under specific input layer configurations. In this work we ask simple questions.

1.

Can DNNs jointly model the time series data and learn the GC in the data.

2.

How can a trained NN be used to uncover the learned GC from the time series data with a method different from sparse regression, without adding extra tuneable hyper-parameters in the model. The trained model shall be such that explanations can be generated to comment on GC.

3.

What is the effect of input layer dropout on the ability of the neural networks to learn granger causal associations in time series context.

4.

What shall be the mechanism to quantify the granger causality from a neural network when time series are modeled jointly.

The answers to these questions are the novelty of the present work. We propose a method to jointly model the time series using a single neural network, and comparing the errors in the predictions of the model under general and reduced models. We compare the effect of input layer dropout on the model’s ability to figure out GC from the data without a need for component-wise modeling or sparse regression. To the best of our knowledge this is the first work in time series GC that explores the effect of input layer dropout during training and estimation of GC by comparing distribution of residuals.

Related Work

The problem of studying the dependence structure with GC has been extensively explored in statistics, ML and DL community. Traditionally Vector Auto regressive Models are used to infer GC from the multi-variate time series data. One of the limitation of these methods is their ability to model the non-linear associations in the data. Data transformations like kernel based methods have been studied and proposed in literature to model the non-linear dynamics however the choice of these kernel functions has to be driven by domain knowledge. ML and DL community has dealt with the problem essentially as a sparse regression problem. Neural networks are becoming more and more popular because of their ability to learn data dependent transformations. Because of their ability to model complex associations in the data these models are an excellent candidate for modeling time series data. Method

Let X(t)
\in

R^{P}

P\times P

P[i,j]=1

represent the neural network used to model the dynamics of the system.

\theta

1.

No input layer dropout, dropout is just incorporated in the hidden layers.

2.

Input layer and hidden layer dropout, but two forward passes are made through the model, one with the input layer dropout and one without input layer dropout.

3.

Input layer and hidden layer dropout, but just one forward pass is made through the model, with the input layer dropout being active.

Let
m
denote a P dimensional multi hot encoded mask. The positions where 1 is present indicates that the past of these time series are being used by the model to predict the future. In the present setting all past lags of the time series
i
are dropped if it has 0 in the mask at the index
i
. A general and reduced model where no and few time series are dropped at random can be written as given in equations
2
and
3
respectively.

X_{f}(t)=f_{\theta}(X_{t-K})+e_{f}(t)

(2)

X_{r}(t)=f_{\theta}(m*X_{t-K})+e_{r}(t)

(3)

X(t-K) refers to the past K lags of time series X. If
f_{\theta}
, is trained in a fashion that it can be used for uncertainty quantification in the predictions, predictions under full and reduced models with specific masking structure incorporated in
m
can be made along with uncertainty quantification in these predictions. Multiple passes can be made to create distributions of residuals under full and reduced models. The task at hand is then to compare the distributions under the full and reduced models for specific components of time series to check if one time series is a granger cause for another one. This will uncover the model dependent GC, which can be used to create explanations for the predictions.

3.1
Training with Monte Carlo Dropout

MC Dropout proposed in
[
3
]
provides a computationally economical way for model uncertainty quantification in neural networks. Dropout is a famous regularization technique used in the training of neural networks. With MC dropout, dropout is set to active even in the prediction phase and multiple forward passes are made through the neural network while keeping the data constant. This leads towards accumulating multiple predictions. The variance from the predictions gives uncertainty estimates in the prediction of the neural network. The dropout probability can be tuned using cross validation on multiple folds or with just one validation set. The loss functions for the models trained with MC dropout is given in equations
4
,
5
, and
6
for three scenarios.

\mathcal{L}(\theta,\alpha)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(X(t)-\hat{X}_{f}(t)\right)^{2}+\lambda g(\theta).

(4)

\mathcal{L}(\theta,\alpha)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(X(t)-\hat{X}_{f}(t)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(X(t)-\hat{X}_{r}(t)\right)^{2}+\lambda g(\theta).

(5)

\mathcal{L}(\theta,\alpha)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(X(t)-\hat{X}_{r}(t)\right)^{2}+\lambda g(\theta).

(6)

Where
\alpha
is the dropout probability in the hidden layers which is a hyper-parameter and has to be tuned.
Equation
4
is a regular loss function for regression problems, in the present scenario it refers to the case where predictions are made from a model with just hidden layer dropout. In equation
5
, the overall loss has two components, the discrepancy in the prediction under no dropout on the input time series components and with random dropout. The dropout which we perform on the input layer is random, at any instance randomly at max
P-1
time series can be dropped. This ensures that in the worst case scenario there is at least the past of one time series to predict the future of everything when the dropout is active during the training.
g(\theta)
is a regularization term for the parameters
\theta
of the neural network, it can be lasso, ridge or elastic net, in the present case
g(\theta)
correspond to weight decay penalty.

3.2
Obtaining Distribution of Residuals

Once
f_{\theta}
has been trained, the model can be used to make Q forward passes to make Q predictions while keeping the data constant. To check if time series
i
granger causes times series
j
, Q predictions for time series
j
for the test set shall be made with and without the past of time series
i
, but under the same dropout structure in the hidden layers.
E_{j,f}=[e_{1,f},e_{2,f}\cdots e_{Q,f}]
and
E_{j,r}=[e_{1,r},e_{2,r}\cdots e_{Q,r}]
will correspond to the distributions of the residuals under the full and reduced models.

3.3
Comparing Distributions of Residuals for GC

After obtaining
E_{j,f}
and
E_{j,r}
, the next step is to decide if
i\rightarrow j
. This can be done by comparing these distributions. One strategy is to look at the overlap of these distributions. If the distributions do not overlap and if the mean of
E_{j,r}
is greater then
E_{j,f}
then
i
is a granger cause of
j
. However if the distributions overlap significantly or if the mean of
E_{j,r}
is less then
E_{j,f}
then
i
is not a granger cause of
j
. The scenario where it happens that the mean of
E_{j,r}
is less than mean of
E_{j,f}
, is an indication that removing the time series
i
leads towards a decrease in the model prediction error, which leads towards non-granger causation. This is a plausible case in case of highly overly parameterized models like neural networks.

To measure the overlap between the two distributions a binary classifier can be used. To check if two distributions are similar or overlap we can create a labeled data set
D=\{e_{m},y_{m}\}_{m=1}^{Q}
for Q training examples, where
y_{m}
is binary label which is 1 if
e_{m}\in E_{j,f}
else 0. A binary classifier can be trained on this dataset D using cross-validation. Suppose
\hat{g}
is the classifier trained using cross validation, the extent to which two distributions overlap can be obtained by using
\hat{g}
to predict the
P(y_{m}=1|e_{m})

\forall

e_{m}\in E_{i,r}
. This essentially gives the probability of a sample coming from reduced model residual distribution to be labeled as coming from full model. The average of this probability for all samples in
E_{m,r}
can be used to quantify the degree of overlap.

P(y_{m}=1|e_{m,r})=\hat{g}_{p,q}(e_{m,r})

(7)

Average of this probability can be used to summarize the information. If both distributions can be clearly classified or separated by the classifier and if the mean of the distribution under the full model is less then that of the reduced model then we can conclude that
i\rightarrow j
.
We can now quantify the probability of
i

\rightarrow

j
as mean of
1-P(y_{m}=1|e_{m,r})

\forall

e_{m,r}\in E_{r}
as indicated in equation
8

P(i\rightarrow j)=\sum_{m=1}^{Q}1-P(y_{m}=1|e_{m,r})

4.1
Chain, Fork and Collider

Chain fork and collider are the three main structures that appear in causal inference. If A, B, C are three real valued time series we can say that the granger causal structure is that of a chain if the past of A is predictive of the future of B, and the past of B is predictive of the future of C. Fork is the second causal structure which appears most frequently in causal inference literature. Fork arises when there is a common cause of two effects. In the absence of the cause the effects may appears to be related to each other. In terms of granger causation in time series data, the fork structure will appear is the past of time series A granger causes the future of both time series B and C. The third important structure is that of collider. Collider structure appears when there is a common effect of two separate causes. By controlling for the effect the causes may appear to be dependent. In terms of granger causation for time series data it will appear when the past of time series A and B granger cause time series C. To include self loops we can make the past of each time series predictive of its own future.

Multivariate time series data is generated using autoregressive (AR) processes with non-linear and linear granger causal components.
