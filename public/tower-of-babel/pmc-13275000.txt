Postflood Leptospirosis Outbreak Response Due to Leptospira interrogans and Leptospira kirschneri in Central Greece, 2023: Comparison of Laboratory and Clinical Manifestations
Authors: Zacharoula Bogogiannidou, Ioanna Voulgaridi, Maria Tseroni, Georgios Dougas, Evangelia D Apostolidi, Evangelia Angelouli, Theologia Sideroglou, Katerina Maria Kontouli, Sotirios Vasileiadis, Christos Kontos, Athanasios G Lianos, Thalia Panagiotopoulou, Dimitrios Babalis, Dora Konstantoula, Konstantinos Krapis, George Karras, Alexandra Manika, Chrisovalanti Koukouletsou, Ilias Karametos, Meropi Zigra, George Βatziogiorgos, Sotiria Dimou, Nikolaos Papageorgiou, Dimitrios Georgiou, Dimitrios Paraskevis, Dimitrios Hatzigeorgiou, Stamatia Kokkali, Fani Kalala, Varvara A Mouchtouri, Matthaios Speletas, Efthymia Petinaki, Christos Hadjichristodoulou

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn September 2023, catastrophic flooding from storms Daniel and Elias struck Thessaly, Greece. We present findings from the postflood leptospirosis outbreak investigation and key lessons for future preparedness.MethodsAd hoc active surveillance was set up to complement national notifications. We used the national case definition for leptospirosis probable and confirmed cases. Diagnostic protocols involved clinical manifestation, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), targeted sequencing (blood/urine,) and serology. Exposure history was collected via a structured questionnaire. We performed case-series analyses, logistic regression, and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis.ResultsDuring the 3-month postflood period, 285 probable and 45 confirmed leptospirosis cases were identified. The postflood notification rate was 6.5/100 000 population. Leptospira kirschneri and Leptospira interrogans were identified in 33 (87%) and 5 (13%) of the 38 PCR-confirmed cases, respectively. Two deaths were attributed to L interrogans. Patients with L interrogans had higher odds of hospitalization (odds ratio, 16 [95% confidence interval, 1.6–159.2]), elevated creatinine (P = .03) and urea (P = .03), and lower platelet counts (P = .02). Urine PCR had the highest diagnostic yield (84%). Patients with leptospirosis reported exposure to floodwater (89%), lack of strict adherence to personal protective equipment (100%), and limited awareness of protective measures (25%).ConclusionsThe 2023 Thessaly floods led to a marked rise in leptospirosis incidence, with L interrogans linked to greater clinical severity. The outbreak highlights the need for public and healthcare workers’ awareness, early diagnosis, species-specific active surveillance, and One Health strategies. Strengthening multisectoral preparedness is critical as climate change increases the risk of similar events in Europe.

Leptospirosis is a zoonotic disease, caused by an infection with the spirochete bacterium Leptospira, comprising several pathogenic species [1]. Disease severity varies among species, with Leptospira interrogans regarded as the most virulent in humans [2]. Species differ in immune response and, subsequently, in the feasibility of serodiagnosis [3]. Most mammalian species are susceptible to infection by Leptospira spp. Rats, mice, cattle, sheep, and dogs act as maintenance hosts, as they can establish long-term kidney colonization and shedding Leptospira in urine, infecting animals and humans [1]. Although the density of stray dogs in the region of Thessaly is low, animals that are identified are routinely vaccinated against leptospirosis through municipal vaccination programs. Susceptible individuals can become infected through various routes, including contact with contaminated water or soil, especially after hurricanes, flooding, or heavy rainfall [3]. Humans are dead-end hosts and person-to-person transmission is rare. Leptospirosis has a wide range of nonspecific symptoms, with the majority of infected individuals remaining asymptomatic or presenting with mild clinical symptoms [3, 4], while only 10% experience severe disease [4].

Accurate diagnosis of leptospirosis depends on both the type of laboratory assay and the timing of sample collection. Antibodies specific to Leptospira typically become detectable 3–10 days after symptom onset. As a result, serological tests performed during the first week may yield false-negative results and should be repeated on a convalescent sample collected 7–14 days later. To increase the diagnostic yield, it is recommended to collect multiple specimen types—including serum, whole blood, and urine—at appropriate stages throughout the illness [5].

The annual incidence of leptospirosis is approximately 1 000 000 cases globally [6]. In Greece, the mean annual number of cases during 2004–2022 was 23.3, corresponding to an annual incidence of 0.22 cases per 100 000 population [7]. Thessaly has historically been among the 5 regions with the highest incidence (0.36/100 000) [7]. The incubation period is usually 5–14 days (range, 2–30 days) [3]. In Greece, human leptospirosis surveillance is limited mainly to serology and molecular detection, with species and serovar identification in human cases being rarely available. Most information on circulating Leptospira therefore derives from animal serosurveys [8].

On 5 September 2023, the region of Thessaly in central Greece was hit by the Mediterranean storm “Daniel,” resulting in extensive floods. Approximately 20 days later, a second extreme weather event, the storm “Elias,” affected the same area. The Thessaly region, covering 14 051 km2, was designated as an “affected area.” The Greek government declared a state of emergency for parts of 5 regional units: Larissa, Magnesia, Trikala, Karditsa, and Sporades. The affected population was estimated to include the entire resident population of 666 525 [9]. The flooded area was estimated at 1150 km2, with the most severely affected zones located along the Pineios River of the Thessalian plain (approximately 820 km2) [10]. Major roads were flooded, and rainwater and floodwater entered residential buildings, resulting in direct exposure of residents to contaminated water, particularly during cleaning activities [11]. Children were also part of the affected population. Schools were temporarily closed until conditions were deemed safe. The floodwaters swept away many livestock carcasses. Flooding led to more than 100 000 livestock and 130 000 poultry deaths [12].

To monitor and identify possible public health threats related to the floods and coordinate response activities, a multidisciplinary coordination center was established at the Laboratory of Hygiene and Epidemiology of the Medical School of the University of Thessaly. The National Public Health Organization (NPHO) and the coordination center immediately conducted a rapid risk assessment to detect possible flood-related infectious diseases threats, using the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) methodology. The rapid risk assessment identified leptospirosis as one of the infectious diseases with potential increased risk. Αn ad hoc active syndromic surveillance system was established to complement passive surveillance and case investigations in the context of mandatory notifications.

Following the 2023 floods in Thessaly, Greece, we aimed to describe leptospirosis cases, and identify species-specific clinical and laboratory differences, along with risk behaviors associated with infection, to inform future mitigation efforts.

METHODS

Case Definitions

We used the NPHO case definitions for leptospirosis, which are the standard definitions used in Greece and are consistent with the ECDC criteria. The clinical criteria include the presence of fever or ≥2 of the following: chill, headache, myalgia, subconjunctival hemorrhage, bleeding on the skin and mucous membranes, rash, jaundice, myocarditis, meningitis, kidney damage, and respiratory symptoms, such as hemoptysis. The laboratory criteria include ≥1 of the following: (1) isolation of Leptospira interrogans or other pathogenic Leptospira species from clinical specimens, (2) detection of nucleic acid of Leptospira interrogans or other pathogenic Leptospira species in a clinical specimen, (3) detection of Leptospira interrogans or other pathogenic Leptospira species by immunofluorescence in a clinical specimen, (4) and detection of specific antibodies against Leptospira interrogans or other pathogenic Leptospira species. Finally, the epidemiological criteria include ≥1 of the following: (1) animal-to-human transmission, (2) environmental exposure, and (3) exposure to a common source [13]. A case was classified as probable if both clinical and epidemiological criteria were met and as confirmed if both clinical and laboratory criteria were fulfilled. The treatment of both probable and confirmed cases was based on the physician's assessment, in accordance with the official NPHO guidelines.

Data Collection

Ad Hoc Active and Passive Surveillance

All public healthcare facilities in the affected region, including the Military General Hospital of Larissa, and 1 private medical clinic reported cases and participated in the ad hoc active surveillance. The ad hoc surveillance system refers to a temporary, rapidly implemented surveillance mechanism that was specifically established in response to the flooding event and the emerging public health risk. A web-based platform was developed, and data were actively imported in this. We defined a standard diagnostic approach with specific biological specimens and sampling timing to enhance diagnostic accuracy [5]. Specifically, during a probable case patient's first visit to any participating medical unit, whole blood and urine specimens were collected, the former for the detection of antibodies and both for the detection of Leptospira spp nucleic acid. In the event of a negative initial test result, a second antibody test was conducted 15 days later. When Leptospira spp nucleic acid was detected, sequencing analysis was performed to identify the Leptospira species.

The clinical specimens for all probable cases were accompanied by an ad hoc referral form completed with demographics, epidemiological, and clinical data. In the event of laboratory confirmation, the NPHO notification form was completed by physicians and sent to the NPHO within 1 week in the context of the mandatory notification system. The case investigation was then conducted by the NPHO zoonoses department.

To ensure that all participants involved in the ad hoc active surveillance were adequately informed about the procedure, we conducted an educational webinar to explain this approach. During the webinar, case management and treatment strategies were reviewed and discussed by physicians and public health professionals.

Cases Series

A questionnaire including medical history, underlying conditions, exposure history, and infection risk factors for leptospirosis was developed and completed by flood-related confirmed cases. It was administered via telephone or on site, supported by the members of the ad hoc active surveillance team. Laboratory results, including complete blood cell count, biochemistry and coagulation profiles, were also collected from confirmed leptospirosis cases, when available, to facilitate comparisons across different Leptospira species.

Laboratory Analysis

Immunoglobulin (Ig) G and IgM antibodies were evaluated using the commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit, SERION ELISA classic Leptospira IgG/IgM (Institut Virion\Serion), according to the manufacturer's recommendations (sensitivity, 94.7%; specificity, >99%).

DNA extraction from blood and urine specimens was performed using an automated system (PSS magLEAD 12gC; Precision System Science). Subsequently, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher) and specific primers (Lepto 1, 5′-GGC GGC GCG TCT TAA ACA TG-3′; Lepto 2, 5′-TTC CCC CCA TTG AGC AAG ATT-3′) by Metabion to detect pathogenic Leptospira DNA [14]. To detect the LipL32 gene we used specific primers (Supplementary Table 1) [15]. The PCR-positive specimens were further analyzed by means of targeted Sanger sequencing at an external laboratory. The obtained chromatograms were quality checked and analyzed using the BLAST base and the National Center for Biotechnology Information GenBank database. Species identification was determined based on the highest sequence identity (>97%) with reference strains.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized demographic, clinical, and microbiological characteristics. Depending on the distribution, we summarized continuous variables as mean (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]). We calculated frequencies (no.) and percentages for categorical variables. We assessed group differences using appropriate statistical tests based on data distribution. We applied independent samples t tests for normally distributed continuous variables, and nonparametric tests (Mann-Whitney U), when normality was not met. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. We set the significance level at P < .05.

To examine associations between variables (age group and Leptospira species) and clinical outcomes (hospitalization and death), we used Firth's penalized logistic regression to address small sample size and separation issues, reducing bias in parameter estimates. Based on this, we calculated odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P values. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis assessed the ability of age to predict hospitalization. The optimal age cutoff was identified using Youden's index, balancing sensitivity and specificity, and was used to classify individuals into age-based risk groups. We performed the statistical analyses using R software (version 4.5.0) [16].

Ethical Considerations

The current study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Thessaly (no. 07/17.01.2024), and informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study. Participation was voluntary. It was not possible to identify participants at the analysis stage or during the dissemination of results.

RESULTS

Descriptive Analysis

Between 5 September and 31 December 2023, a total of 330 probable leptospirosis case patients were tested for Leptospira spp infection; 45 cases (14%) were laboratory confirmed. The patients with confirmed leptospirosis had a median age (IQR) of 48 years (30–58) years, with the youngest aged 17 years; their male-female ratio was 1.14 (53% male [n = 24]), with no difference in sex-related odds among case patients (P > .05). Clinical Manifestations and Severity in Confirmed Leptospirosis Cases

Among the 45 patients with confirmed leptospirosis, the most frequently reported clinical symptoms were fever (n = 25 [55.6%]), rash (n = 10 [22.2%]), myalgia (n = 4 [8.9%]), chills (n = 4 [8.9%]), diarrhea (n = 3 [6.7%]), headache (n = 3 [6.7%]), abdominal pain (n = 2 [4.4%]), and vomiting (n = 2 [4.4%]). Clinical signs included jaundice (n = 4 [9.1%]). Severe complications were observed in a subset of patients and comprised hepatic failure (n = 8 [17.8%]), acute kidney injury (n = 6 [13.3%]), myocarditis (n = 1 [2.2%]), and meningitis (n = 1 [2.2%]). None of the patients with acute kidney injury underwent renal replacement therapy. No cases of conjunctival suffusion, skin or mucous membrane hemorrhage, or severe respiratory manifestations were reported. Of the 45 patients with confirmed cases, 29 (64.4%) were hospitalized and 2 (4.4%) died. Both deaths were attributed to severe leptospirosis complicated by multiorgan failure. The patients who died were both male, 56 and 57 years of age, without underlying diseases. The ROC curve identified 34 years as the optimal cutoff age. The patient group aged <34 years had a median age of 25 years (range, 17–32 years), and the group aged ≥34 years had a median age of 55 years (36–87 years). The odds of hospitalization was 9 times higher in patients aged ≥34 years, compared with the younger group (OR, 9.19 [95% CI, 2–58.9]).

Laboratory Results

Diagnostic Testing and Sequencing Results

Overall, 359 serum specimens were analyzed for IgM antibodies against Leptospira spp, including 121 convalescent samples. In addition, 344 serum samples were examined for IgG antibodies, including 117 convalescent samples. Moreover, 253 urine and 260 blood samples were tested for the presence of Leptospira nucleic acid. PCR in urine samples had the highest diagnostic capacity (38 cases [84%]), followed by IgM detection in blood serum (20 cases [44%]), whereas PCR in blood samples did not yield any positive results. Among cases with a molecular diagnosis, Leptospira kirschneri was identified in 33 (87%), and L interrogans in 5 (13%).

Among seropositive patients, 5 (11%) exhibited only IgM antibodies. Four of them demonstrated an increase in antibody levels on follow-up testing. In the single case without follow-up testing, the initial IgM antibody titer was extremely high (428.14 U/mL). The median IgM titer (range) was 232.21 (18.36–273.44) U/mL) for L interrogans and 27.995 (17.7 = 53.13) U/mL for L kirschneri. Positive IgG antibody levels were detected in 3 patients (6.7%) at the initial test, combined in all of them with a positive IgM titer. The IgG titers ranged from 26.08 to 102.44 U/mL. Comparison Between Leptospira Species

Both patients who died were infected with L interrogans (100%). The odds of death in cases with L interrogans was 44 (OR, 44 [95% CI, 1.6–1,215) times higher compared with L kirschneri cases. The odds of hospitalization among patients infected with L interrogans was 16 times higher than among those infected with L kirschneri (OR, 16 [95% CI, 1.6–159.2]).

On the first day of hospital admission for patients with confirmed cases, creatinine and urea levels were significantly higher in L interrogans than in L kirschneri cases (P = .03), while platelet counts were lower in L interrogans cases (P = .02) (Figure 3). Among patients with acute kidney injury, 2 were infected with L interrogans (1 of whom died before initiation of renal replacement therapy) and 2 with L kirschneri. For the patients with repeated laboratory measurements (4 with L interrogans [54 measures] and 30 with L kirschneri [142 measures]), the levels of creatinine and urea remained significantly different between the 2 species (P = .02 for creatinine and P = .03 for urea) (Figure 4). None of the 9 IgM-negative L kirschneri–infected patients who were followed up seroconverted, and only 1 of the 2 IgM-negative L interrogans–infected patients was positive at follow-up.

Case Series

Of the 45 patients with confirmed cases, 43 excluding the 2 who died, 28 agreed to be interviewed, for a response rate of 65.1%. Among the respondents, 15 (54%) were female, and the median age (IQR) was 50.5 (31.5–59) years. Only 1 respondent (3.6%) was infected with L interrogans, and 20 (71%) were infected with L kirschneri. Occupation varied among respondents: 8 (29%) were employed in professions with potential environmental exposure to flooded water, 5 (18%) were members of emergency response teams (eg, firefighters), and the remaining 15 (5%) reported a diverse range of occupations.

Of all respondents, 18 (64%) had been diagnosed with ≥1 underlying disease, including 13 (46%) with cardiovascular disease and 6 (21%) with metabolic or endocrinological disorders. Among patients with underlying diseases, 16 (57%) received systematic treatment. In addition, 15 participants (54%) reported alcohol consumption, and 6 (21.0%) were smokers.

Of the 28 respondents, 19 (68%) reported a hospital visit within 5 days of symptom onset, with 5 of them seeking medical assessment on the first day. In contrast, 4 (14%) presented to the hospital ≥10 days after symptoms onset. Overall, 19 patients (68%) required hospitalization, with a median duration (IQR) of 6.5 (4.3–11) days. Presentation to the hospital ≥5 days after symptom onset was not significantly associated with an increased risk of hospitalization (P = .38). All participants except 1 (96%) reported receiving antibiotic treatment, of whom 19 (70%) reported full adherence to the treatment.

Regarding information on Leptospira spp exposure, 25 participants (89%) reported exposure to floodwaters, including flood site cleanup activities and engagement in manual labor in muddy or/and stagnant water. Ten (36%) reported having open wounds on their hands or feet during exposure to flooded water. None of them reported strict adherence to implementation of personal protective equipment (PPE) during flooded water exposure. Only 7 (25%) reported that they had been informed about the prevention measures for Leptospira spp infection, with all of them mentioning a source of information other than the NPHO.

DISCUSSION

Storms Daniel and Elias caused several public health threats in Thessaly, Greece. Precipitation events have been associated with increased human exposure to contaminated water, particularly in combination with poor sanitation, insufficient healthcare, or crowded conditions [12], and consequently higher risk of infectious diseases outbreak, including leptospirosis [17, 18]. Using active and passive surveillance, we identified a leptospirosis outbreak in the flood-affected area. The 3-month postflood notification rate was 6.5/100 000 population while the mean incidence for the same 3-month period of the previous 19 years was 0.08/100 000 in the same region [7]. The regions with the highest notification rates did not completely overlap with the main flooded zones, since individuals were exposed not only at their residences but also at properties owned by relatives.

The relationship between rainfall and leptospirosis incidence has been well documented worldwide [19, 20]. In a study in Brazil, a weekly cumulative rainfall anomaly of 20 mm increased the risk of leptospirosis by 12% compared with typical seasonal patterns, with a 10.3-fold increase in incidence rate, from 0.8 to 8.5 per 100 000 inhabitants in the 3-month period [21]. In Mumbai, India, an 8-fold increase in disease incidence was noted after severe flooding in 2005 [22]. In Manila, Philippines, a large outbreak of leptospirosis was reported after tropical storms and severe flooding in October 2009; the number of leptospirosis-related hospital admissions soared from 140 to 1027 in just 3 days, with a case-fatality rate of 8.6% [23]. Ιn the Czech Republic, a 3 times higher incidence rate per 100 000 was observed after floods (increasing from 0.3 to 0.9 per 100 000 population) [24]. A higher seroprevalence of infection has also been associated with heavy rainfall and flooding in China, France, and Brazil, indicating that leptospirosis outbreaks can affect both developing and industrialized nations alike and occur in a wide variety of settings, including large urban centers, rural areas, and small island states [25–27].

Many leptospirosis cases remain undiagnosed, as infected individuals may be asymptomatic or experience only mild symptoms [3, 4]. Globally, only about 1 in 5–10 leptospirosis infections are accurately diagnosed, primarily due to the broad spectrum of clinical manifestations in symptomatic individuals and the significant overlap with symptoms of other diseases [22, 28]. In the current study, fever was present in 1 in 2 confirmed cases and rash in almost 1 in 4 cases. The absence of pathognomonic clinical signs and symptoms underscores the critical importance of implementing a comprehensive and widely adopted diagnostic approach. The ROC curve analysis identified 34 years as the optimal age cutoff. Individuals aged >34 years had approximately 9-fold higher odds of hospitalization compared with those aged ≤34 years. This observation may reflect the more severe clinical course of leptospirosis with increasing age, potentially related to age-associated comorbid conditions.

In the postflood period, leptospirosis cases were diagnosed using either serological or molecular methods. Although the microscopic agglutination test is considered the reference standard serological assay, it was not available in our setting. Therefore, we used a commercially available ELISA kit, which is considered an acceptable alternative for the serological diagnosis of leptospirosis [29, 30]. Previously available local data also support the use of ELISAs in diagnosing leptospirosis [31].

In our study, 1 in 2 cases were confirmed solely through positive PCR results in urine specimens, whereas positive serological findings in negative PCR results accounted for the diagnosis of <20% of cases. PCR is a reliable tool for the early detection of leptospirosis, with its sensitivity being highest when samples are obtained within the first 6 days following symptom onset [32]. Emerging evidence indicates that urine PCR may be positive as early as 4–6 days after onset and may be more sensitive than blood PCR beyond the initial bacteremic phase [33]. Negative blood PCR results in the presence of positive urine PCR findings may reflect the natural decline in bacteremia, differences in bacterial load between specimen types (blood vs urine), and the inherent sensitivity limits of the assay [33]. In addition, in our study the median time interval between the onset of symptoms and the collection of urine specimens for PCR testing was 4.5 days. This sampling time frame may have contributed to the high diagnostic role of PCR, which is consistent with previous findings [34].

Our findings suggest that laboratory confirmation of non-interrogans leptospirosis cases remains a challenge, highlighting the need for species-specific diagnostic capacity and broader molecular testing as part of preparedness planning. Although both L interrogans and L kirschneri are classified as pathogenic Leptospira species, statistically significant differences between them were observed for virulence and laboratory hematological and biochemical parameters. L interrogans was associated with a higher risk of severe disease (higher odds of hospitalization and death) than L kirschneri. This finding is also supported by another study conducted in Mayotte between January 2018 and April 2020, in which 2 of 4 (50%) L interrogans cases were severe and required admission to intensive care units but none of the 8 L kirschneri cases involved severe clinical manifestations [35]. L interrogans and L kirschneri, the major pathogenic species in human leptospirosis [36, 37], exhibit significant genetic diversity. L interrogans demonstrates enhanced virulence through Toll-like receptor 2–dependent recognition of lipoproteins and inflammasome-related responses [38] as well as regulatory flexibility, dynamically modulating genes involved in motility, oxidative stress resistance, and outer membrane composition, thereby promoting both persistence and vascular injury [39].

Based on our experience, early detection can be challenging in cases without specific symptoms, underscoring the importance of clinical awareness and risk-based screening following flood exposure. Following the floods caused by Storm Daniel in Thessaly, educational campaigns targeting both the general public and healthcare workers focused on improving risk perception, promoting the use of PPE, encouraging prompt medical consultation for illnesses, and enhancing the clinical suspicion of leptospirosis among frontline health professionals. After identification of the most affected areas, targeted door-to-door public health campaigns were conducted, including dissemination of PPE and informative material. The campaigns may have played a role in limiting the health impact of leptospirosis. World Health Organization outbreak guidance emphasizes informing the public about leptospirosis symptoms, transmission risks, and protective measures, while training healthcare providers to recognize the disease early and treat it appropriately [40].

The use of antibiotic chemoprophylaxis for leptospirosis during floods remains debated, and public health authorities in Greece decided to avoid mass prophylaxis. The decision was based on the results from large-scale campaigns in Guyana (2005) and Mumbai (2017) showing limited and inconclusive effectiveness [41, 42], as well as on a 2024 systematic review reporting uncertainty regarding its impact on mortality rates [43]. Moreover, the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the ECDC similarly highlight unclear benefits and instead emphasize targeted use and preventive measures, such as environmental control, protective equipment, and surveillance [29, 44, 45].

The coordination center was informed on 25 September 2023 about 3 Leptospira spp infections in stray dogs in the area of Larissa. Although stray dogs are present in Thessaly, their population size and leptospirosis vaccination coverage are not systematically documented, while pet dogs and other susceptible domestic animals are generally vaccinated. A One Health approach is needed when addressing this disease, because environmental drivers play a crucial role in leptospirosis transmission. This approach emphasizes the necessity for a multidisciplinary and intersectoral plan involving public health, civil defense, agricultural sectors, and veterinarians. In a 2025 article on leptospirosis in the Netherlands, an increase over the past decades occurred and could continue [46]. Authors believe that this increase was due to climate change and the temperature increase, which favors Leptospira survival. The combination of climate change, flooding, population growth, and urbanization, alongside the growing overlap of habitats for both disease reservoirs and human populations, will almost certainly lead to an escalation in the global burden of disease from leptospirosis.

Α comprehensive strategy is needed, to address not only immediate public health needs but also long-term recovery and prevention of future flood-related outbreaks. The importance of effective collaboration among governments, health authorities, policy makers, and local communities to mitigate the disaster's impact and enhance recovery efforts must be emphasized. Addressing leptospirosis must be prioritized in both national and international public health agendas during similar extreme weather events.

Our study has some limitations. Due to the wide spectrum of clinical symptoms associated with leptospirosis, many cases, particularly those with no or mild symptoms, may have not been diagnosed before the floods through passive surveillance, leading to underdiagnosis. However, active surveillance and the enhanced awareness of frontline healthcare workers after the floods led to the laboratory investigation of cases with mild symptoms. Another limitation is the lack of microscopic agglutination tests, cultures, and double-phase microscopy, due to their unavailability. Moreover, as no data on species-level surveillance were available for the preflood period, it was not possible to make preflood versus postflood comparisons. Regarding the timing of specimen collection, due to reliance on self-reported symptom onset, combined with the possibility that mild symptoms may have been overlooked or underestimated by patients, the estimation of the interval since symptom onset may not have been entirely accurate. Finally, the low number of L interrogans cases may have resulted in no statistically significant differences.

Despite these limitations, our study has important strengths. It documents a leptospirosis outbreak following extreme weather events in an area endemic for the disease, providing valuable epidemiological insight into the impact of climate-related events on transmission dynamics. Moreover, the novelty of our study lies in its integrated analysis of the clinical course and laboratory findings of confirmed leptospirosis cases, combined with serotyping of Leptospira species. This comprehensive approach enabled comparisons between different serotypes with respect to clinical manifestations and laboratory parameters, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of disease presentation and pathogen diversity. Although microscopic agglutination tests, double-phase microscopy, and culture were not performed, we used ELISAs, which have been recommended as an alternative method for the serological diagnosis of leptospirosis [29, 30]. ELISAs provide results more rapidly than culture, thereby facilitating the timely initiation of appropriate treatment. Moreover, they has been widely used in Europe, including Greece, for more than a decade.

In conclusion, active surveillance identified a surge of leptospirosis cases following the 2023 catastrophic floods in Thessaly, Greece. L interrogans caused more severe disease than L kirshneeri. Climate change and shifting rainfall patterns are likely to contribute to a greater disease impact in Greece and Europe. The increasing frequency of extreme weather events and floods poses a particularly high risk of rising leptospirosis infections in the future. To safeguard public health, a comprehensive national multisectoral emergency preparedness and response plan for infectious disease outbreaks, including leptospirosis, is critical.
