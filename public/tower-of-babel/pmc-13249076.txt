Quality of hospital medicine care in Veterans Affairs hospitals versus non‐Veterans Affairs hospitals: A systematic review of comparative studies
Authors: Paul G Shekelle, Robert E Burke, Eric A Apaydin, Meron M Begashaw, Charlie M Wray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Veterans Health Administration (VA) cares for more than one million hospitalized Veterans each year. There is limited knowledge on how hospital medicine care in VA facilities compares with non‐VA hospitals in terms of the Institute of Medicine domains of healthcare access, patient experience, quality, clinical outcomes, and cost.ObjectivesTo perform a systematic review that compares hospital‐based care in VA and non‐VA settings with a focus on general medicine conditions typically cared for by hospitalists.MethodsWe identified relevant articles across four databases from January 2015 to April 2025. Eligible studies were required to assess outcomes in any Institute of Medicine healthcare domain among Veterans receiving care in the VA for acute medical conditions. Relevant comparisons included outcomes of (1) Veterans receiving care in the community, or (2) members of the general population receiving care in the community.ResultsWe identified 13 studies. VA care generally exhibited equal or better outcomes compared with non‐VA care, especially in risk‐adjusted mortality rates. Most process‐of‐care studies showed VA care to be as good as or better than non‐VA care. Findings on readmissions and patient experience were mixed, with some early findings showing VA care lagging behind but improving significantly by 2021. However, few studies were completed after the passage of the 2018 MISSION Act, which significantly shifted hospitalizations to non‐VA care.ConclusionsMost published studies of comparisons of quality‐of‐care show that Veterans receiving hospital medicine care from VA receive the same or better quality care than Veterans getting community care.

INTRODUCTION

The Veterans Health Administration (VA) provides acute care services to more than one million Veterans annually, making it the largest integrated health system in the United States. 1 Prior work has shown that across multiple outpatient, specialty, and patient experience domains, VA compares favorably with non‐VA settings. 2 , 3 , 4 However, far less is known about how general inpatient care—most often delivered by hospitalists—compares between VA and non‐VA hospitals.

The 2018 VA Maintaining Internal Systems and Strengthening Integrated Outside Networks (MISSION) Act substantially expanded Veterans' eligibility to receive VA‐paid hospital care in community settings, with the potential to affect inpatient quality through multiple pathways. By shifting patient volume away from VA hospitals, the MISSION Act could alter case mix, staffing stability, and clinical volume at VA facilities—factors known to influence hospitalist experience, team‐based care, and outcomes. At the same time, increased reliance on community hospitals exposes Veterans to care delivered in settings with different care structures, coordination practices, and continuity with VA‐based outpatient teams, with uncertain implications for quality, safety, and transitions of care.

To address these questions, we conducted a systematic review to (1) compare hospital‐based care in VA and non‐VA settings, and (2) assess evidence related to the impact of the 2018 VA MISSION Act on inpatient care delivery and outcomes. We focused on conditions that are typically managed on general medicine wards (e.g., heart failure, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, and pneumonia) and on hospital‐wide measures, reflecting the central role of hospitalists in care for most medical inpatients. 2 Because the 2018 MISSION Act could impact access and quality of care to VA hospitals, we also identified studies that assessed changes in care related to MISSION Act implementation.

METHODS

This systematic review was commissioned by the VA's national Hospital Medicine Program Office. 3 A preregistered protocol for this review can be found on the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42022314154). VA leadership participated in setting the scope of the review and had the opportunity to comment on the draft report, but did not participate in the synthesis of the data nor the decision to publish the results.

Literature search and screening

To identify relevant articles, a research librarian conducted broad searches using terms relating to Veterans health and community health services or private sector in the PubMed, American Psychological Association (APA) PsycINFO, and Web of Science databases (1/1/2015–4/20/2025). Complete search strategies are provided in Supporting Information S1: Appendix Table 1. The start date was chosen to match the end date of the most recent review of VA and non‐VA care by O'Hanlon. 4 Additional citations were identified from hand‐searching reference lists and consultation with content experts.

Titles, abstracts, and full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility by at least two team members working in duplicate. Disagreements were reconciled through group discussion. Eligible studies were required to assess outcomes in any Institute of Medicine health care domain (clinical quality, safety, efficiency, access, patient experience, or equity) 5 among Veterans receiving care in VA for acute medical conditions, or be hospital‐wide measures of care. Comparisons needed to be made to outcomes of (1) Veterans receiving care in the community (either VA‐paid or not‐ VA‐paid) or (2) members of the general population receiving care in the community. Studies were permitted to use any research design, but must have been conducted in the United States (i.e., compared VA care with another US health care provider/setting).

Data abstraction and risk of bias assessment

Data abstracted from studies included the medical condition, type of outcome reported, populations under comparison, years that data were collected, whether study years were contemporaneous, the sampling approach, geographic representativeness (national/representative/regional), similarity of outcomes between the comparison groups, sample size, control variables, actual reported outcomes, findings, and statistical methods. All data abstraction and internal validity ratings were first completed by one reviewer and then checked by another; disagreements were resolved by consensus or discussion with an additional reviewer. We disaggregated reported composite outcome measures when possible and included those most relevant to hospitalist care (e.g., 30‐day mortality for pneumonia or heart failure) and excluded measures not relevant to hospitalist care (e.g., postoperative sepsis and postoperative wound dehiscence).

The risk of bias for studies eligible for this review was evaluated using the same methods as a prior review of VA versus non‐VA care (not limited to hospital medicine). 6 Studies were assessed on whether or not the time frames for measurement were contemporaneous, whether the sample was national or representative for both groups, whether the measures of quality were identical or nearly identical, and whether the sample size and statistical methods were appropriate to test the hypothesis. Studies could fully meet a criterion, partially meet a criterion, or fail a criterion. Studies fully meeting all of these criteria were considered to be “good” quality and given greater weight than studies not meeting all the criteria, which were considered to be “fair” quality. Studies failing 1 or more criteria were not included in the analysis (Supporting Information S2: Appendix Table 2).

Synthesis

We narratively synthesized available evidence because studies differed too substantially in comparison groups, outcome domains, and/or procedure types or health conditions to allow for meta‐analysis. Studies were first classified by the domain(s) of reported outcomes (quality and safety, access, patient experience, efficiency/cost, and equity). Within domains, studies were grouped by clinical condition (cardiovascular, diabetes, etc.). If multiple cost outcomes were reported, the total cost was abstracted. Studies were grouped into two categories based on their quality assessment: those that had no obvious flaws limiting their internal validity (risk of bias) or external validity (generalizability), and those that had some flaws limiting internal or external validity. Studies with serious internal validity flaws were not included in the synthesis. Certainty of evidence judgments were not made as the existing certainty of evidence framework is not suited to this type of evidence.

Evidence map interpretation

For each of the Evidence Maps, studies are listed by domains of care of the outcomes they report by shape: circles for clinical quality/safety, diamonds for access, squares for patient experience, triangles for cost/efficiency, and octagon for equity, with larger shapes being studies of better quality and representativeness than studies depicted by smaller shapes. The color of the shape indicates the type of comparison: blue for studies comparing Veterans getting care from VA to Veterans getting VA‐paid care in the community; orange for studies comparing Veterans getting care from VA and non‐Veterans, or a general population, getting care in the community; and yellow for studies comparing Veterans getting care from VA to Veterans getting community care not paid by VA. Next to each shape is a brief thumbnail of what the study was about, and inside the shape is the year of publication ('18 = 2018, '19 = 2019, etc).

RESULTS

Search results and synthesis overview

We identified 13 studies that met the inclusion criteria, after successively screening 3133 titles (after deduplication), 241 abstracts, and 145 full‐text articles. The literature flow is presented in Figure 1 and a list of all included studies are provided in Supporting Information S3: Appendix Table 3. Our overall results are presented in bubble plots in Figures 2 and 3. Figure 2 presents results for studies with a single or global measure of quality, Figure 3 includes results for studies with composites that we disaggregated and then only included those most relevant to hospitalist care. Six of the 13 studies were about hospital‐wide measures, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 two studies were about groups of conditions (one study about acute myocardial infarction (AMI), heart failure, and pneumonia 13 ; and the other about AMI, heart failure, gastrointestinal bleed, pneumonia, and stroke 14 ), and five studies were about specific conditions (two studies about chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, 15 , 16 one study about heart failure, 17 one study about AMI, 18 and one study about end‐of‐life care 19 ).

Risk of bias/quality

Eleven of the included studies 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 15 , 16 , 18 , 19 met all our risk of bias criteria. See Supporting Information S4: Appendix Table 4 for risk of bias ratings. These good‐quality studies were given more weight in our narrative synthesis than the two studies that did not meet one or more criteria.

Studies with hospital‐wide measures

Outcome measures used in the six studies with hospital‐wide measures included quality measures included in Hospital Compare, patient experience (via Hospital Consumer Assessment of Health Care Providers and Systems [HCAHPS] and the VA's identically worded Survey of Healthcare Experience of Patients [SHEP]), and hospital readmission rates.

In the first study, Blay and colleagues used data in Hospital Compare to compare care at 129 VA hospitals and 4010 non‐VA hospitals on 9 patient safety measures, 4 mortality measures, 4 readmission measures, 10 patient experience measures, and 9 behavioral health measures between 2012 and 2015. 11 For three of four patient safety measures relevant to hospitalist care, VA hospitals had significantly better performance than non‐VA hospitals (lower rates of pressure ulcer, iatrogenic pneumothorax, and catheter‐associated blood stream infection rate). On all measures of 30‐day mortality and 30‐day readmission rate for patients with AMI, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or heart failure, VA hospitals had significantly lower readmission rates than non‐VA hospitals. For patient experience measures, non‐VA hospitals had significantly better performance on 6 of 10 measures (nursing communication, physician communication, responsiveness, pain management, quietness, and recommendation of the hospital to others).

Similarly, Bagshaw and colleagues used 2023 Hospital Compare data (which includes measures relevant to all hospitalizations and additional measures specific to AMI, pneumonia, COPD, and heart failure) to compare VA versus non‐VA hospitals, this time using aggregated “star ratings” which compile individual measures like those above (5 stars = highest quality, 1 star = lowest quality). 8 Across more than 100 hospitals (sample varies across star rating domains), VA facilities were more likely to be rated four stars and five stars and less likely to be rated two stars or three stars than 2962 non‐VA hospitals. Among the domains in the star ratings, VA hospitals scored better than non‐VA hospitals in mortality and patient experience, but worse in timely and effective care and readmissions.

Anhang Price and colleagues 12 used outcome data from the Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS) and patient experience data (Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems [HCAHPS] outside the VA, Survey of Healthcare Experience by Patients [SHEP] inside the VA) to compare 135 VA hospitals and 402 matched non‐VA hospitals using data from 2014. Compared with non‐VA hospitals, VA hospital care scored the same or significantly better on all measures of inpatient safety, inpatient mortality, and inpatient effectiveness but significantly worse on measures of readmission. Results on individual patient experience measures were mixed.

Axon and colleagues used data from VA and Medicare between 2016 and 2021 to investigate changing patterns of Veteran hospitalization (any inpatient stay of 90 days or less) during implementation of the MISSION Act. 9 While the primary focus was about shifting sites of hospitalization toward VA‐paid community care and away from VA‐delivered care and Medicare‐paid care, the analyses included numerous comparisons of 7‐day and 30‐day readmission rates (unadjusted and adjusted) over time up to 2022. Depending on which outcome and which time point, readmissions for Veterans with community care hospitalizations were 19%–47% higher than for Veterans with VA hospital care (e.g., 30‐day readmission had an adjusted rate ratio = 1.2, 95% confidence interval: 1.19, 1.22, in May 2019). Veterans getting care from non‐VA sources, paid by Medicare, had about 30%–50% higher readmission rates than either of the other two groups.

Elliott and colleagues used patient experience data from 2017 to 2021 to compare VA (n = 127 hospitals) and non‐VA (n = 4500 hospitals) from national surveys of patient experience using HCAHPS. 10 Data are presented over the time period 2017–2021, spanning the MISSION Act implementation period, and focus on results for certain patient groups. We focused primarily on data from 2021 and the overall population. Whereas in 2017, VA HCAHPS summary scores (from 127 VA hospitals) were significantly worse than the scores for non‐VA hospitals (N = 4500 hospitals), by 2021, the VA HCAHPS summary score was significantly better for VA (N = 122 hospitals) than non‐VA (N = 4429 hospitals). This result was consistent regardless of patient subgroups by baseline health status and race.

Lastly, a study by Eid and colleagues, 7 assessed factors contributing to patient experience. Using data from 2018 (pre‐MISSION Act), the authors reported a comparison of the VA HCAHPS “would you recommend” rating between 133 VA hospitals and 1116 non‐VA hospitals. There was no significant difference found.

Studies with groups of clinical conditions

We identified two published assessments of clinical quality for patients with a group of conditions comparing Veterans receiving VA care to non‐Veterans getting care in the community. Nuti and colleagues 13 used Medicare fee‐for‐service data and VA administrative data to compare mortality and readmission rates for men aged ≥65 who received care in an urban VA hospital versus receiving care in a geographically nearby non‐VA hospital. Patients had to have been hospitalized for AMI, heart failure, or pneumonia between July 2010 and June 2013. The number of hospitals and patients included varied based on hospital discharge diagnosis and outcome assessed, but in general consisted of 100 + VA hospitals and approximately 1500 non‐VA hospitals, and between 10,000 and 200,000 patients. The risk‐standardized 30‐day mortality rate was slightly but significantly lower in VA hospitals than in non‐VA hospitals for AMI and heart failure, but slightly worse in pneumonia. 30‐day readmission rates were consistently slightly higher in VA hospitals compared with non‐VA hospitals.

Yoon and colleagues performed a similar assessment of VA care to non‐VA care, 14 but only included Veterans. Patients were both men (98% of the sample) and women, and patients under age 65 were included. The study population consisted of 593,578 hospitalizations experienced by 414,861 Veterans. Outcomes examined included mortality, readmissions, length of stay, and costs. There were no significant differences between groups for patients with AMI, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and pneumonia, but significantly better outcomes for VA‐treated patients with heart failure and stroke. 30‐day readmission rates were lower in VA‐treated patients in 7 of 10 comparisons. Length of stay was higher in VA hospitals compared with care in non‐VA hospitals for 9 of 10 comparisons (by between 0.5 and 2.3 days), as were costs, which may in part be due to longer length of stay.

Studies of specific conditions

We identified five published studies focusing on specific conditions commonly treated by hospitalists. These conditions include heart failure (the first leading cause of hospitalization in the VA), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (sixth leading cause), AMI (seventh leading cause), as well as end‐of‐life care.

Heart failure

Axon and colleagues compared readmission rates during 2007–2011 for Veterans discharged with a heart failure diagnosis from a South Carolina VA hospital to those who were discharged from non‐VA facilities in South Carolina. 17 Sample sizes are not listed for patients with hospitalizations, but for hospitalizations and Emergency Department visits combined, there were 2242 VA users and 8825 non‐VA users. Restricting just to patients with a heart failure hospitalization, adjusted analyses showed that both all‐cause and heart failure‐specific 30‐day readmission rates were about 50% lower (adjusted rate ratio = 0.51, 95% confidence interval: 0.46, 0.57) in Veterans getting care at non‐VA hospitals.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease

We identified two published studies that compared care for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in VA and non‐VA hospitals. In the first study, LaBedz and colleagues 16 used national Hospital Compare data to assess readmission and mortality rates following a VA or non‐VA discharge for COPD between 2015 and 2018. Data came from 126 VA hospitals and 3523 non‐VA hospitals. Both the 30‐day risk‐adjusted admission rate and the 30‐day risk‐adjusted mortality favored VA hospital care. In the second study, Vercammen‐Grandjean and colleagues 15 used national data from VA and from Medicare to assess care for patients discharged with a qualifying diagnosis of COPD during 2007‐–2011 at VA hospitals (N = 32,856 patients) or non‐VA hospitals (N = 158,137 patients). The primary outcome was the receipt of at least one pulmonary rehabilitation session. Use of pulmonary rehabilitation was very low in each group—1.5% in VA care and 2.0% in non‐VA care—though this difference was statistically significant.

AMI

Weeda and colleagues used national VA and Medicare data to compare outpatient medication prescribing among Veterans treated at VA hospitals and Veterans treated at non‐VA hospitals treated for AMI between 2013 and 2018. 18 The primary outcome was omissions of outpatient medications in patients with compelling indications filled within 30 days of discharge. As most of these patients could have, or should have, had these outpatient medications written by the inpatient team at the time of hospital discharge, we judged this sufficiently relevant to include in an assessment of hospitalist care. There were a total of 118,456 Veterans hospitalized for AMI, of which 102,209 were treated in non‐VA hospitals, and 16,247 were treated at VA hospitals. Relevant medications included angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blockers, beta‐blockers, statins, high‐intensity statins, and P2Y12 drugs such as clopidogrel. Across all classes of medications, Veterans treated in VA hospitals were statistically significantly more likely to have received the medications than were Veterans treated at non‐VA hospitals.

End‐of‐life costs of care

Gidwani and colleagues used national VA and Medicare data to compare costs of end‐of‐life care between Veterans predominantly ( > 75% of visits) cared for in VA and Veterans predominantly receiving care outside VA, between 2010 and 2014 19 (pre‐MISSION Act). Although the study assessed inpatient, outpatient, and drug costs, we focused on inpatient costs. Over the course of 12 months before death, there were similar trajectories of inpatient hospital spending for both VA and non‐VA treated Veterans.

DISCUSSION

In this systematic review of studies comparing VA versus non‐VA care for medical conditions that would be delivered by hospitalists, we find VA care usually has better or at least similar outcomes to non‐VA care; few studies report that non‐VA care is significantly better. This pattern is most evident in studies reporting risk‐adjusted mortality rates, where multiple studies consistently found lower (better) risk‐adjusted mortality in VA care. Only one study reported the opposite, with higher (worse) 30‐day risk‐adjusted mortality in patients hospitalized for pneumonia. Other studies of 30‐day risk‐adjusted mortality rates for patients with pneumonia found either no difference or lower (better) risk in VA hospitals. Studies of processes of care (e.g., did a patient get an indicated medication, were discharge instructions given) also universally showed VA care to be as good as or better than non‐VA care, with one exception: a study demonstrating patients with COPD admissions rarely were referred for pulmonary rehabilitation.

Studies of readmissions and patient experience report mixed outcomes. Studies were evenly mixed on the findings of VA care being better than, worse than, or about the same as non‐VA care in terms of readmissions for various conditions. Likewise, patient experience studies reported mixed results. Some of these differences may be due to timing: in one study with longitudinal data, VA care was worse than non‐VA care in terms of patient experience in 2017, but by 2021 was better than non‐VA care. Length of stay and costs were rarely reported, but where they were, they favored non‐VA care.

Nearly all of the studies used data from time periods before the MISSION Act implementation. Two studies that reported longitudinal data during the time before and after MISSION Act implementation showed some changes in performance over time, and thus, extrapolation of results of both VA and community performance from a decade or more ago may not be valid today.

These findings have implications for clinicians, health system leaders, policymakers, and patients both within and outside the VA. For VA hospitalists and leaders, the generally favorable mortality and process‐of‐care outcomes provide reassurance that inpatient care delivered within an integrated, publicly financed system can achieve outcomes comparable to or better than community hospitals, even while serving a medically and socially complex population. At the same time, mixed findings in readmissions, patient experience, and cost highlight areas for continued improvement, particularly as referral patterns potentially shift under the MISSION Act. For non‐VA providers and policymakers, these results suggest studies into the “how and why” VA achieves the results it does, such studies may focus on the role of integrated care models, robust quality measurement infrastructure, and alignment between inpatient and outpatient systems. Finally, for Veterans‐navigating decisions about where to receive care, these findings suggest that VA hospital care performs at least as well as community care across many clinical outcomes.

Limitations

In addition to the usual limitation of any systematic review, namely the quantity and quality of the original studies, we add the possibility of publication bias or subconscious investigator bias, in that most of the published studies are by VA authors. We scrutinized each study for objective evidence of bias and diminished the degree to which studies with such bias contributed to our overall conclusions, both by excluding studies that failed one or more risk of bias criteria and by giving less weight in our narrative synthesis to studies of “fair” quality than studies of “good” quality. Nevertheless, we cannot assess the degree to which unmeasurable bias or the decision to undertake a comparative study and what topics to focus on are influenced by VA investigators.

Beyond this, the most important limitation to any of these comparisons is the possibility of confounding by choice of care delivery site—in other words, the comparability of the patients getting VA care to the patients getting care outside VA, whether they be Veterans or non‐Veterans getting care in the community. Studies attempted to control for this by using multivariable methods to adjust for baseline differences between groups, but these methods are limited by the availability of baseline variables and the degree to which those variables are captured. Thus, one study of outcomes of heart failure care was able to adjust for the presence or absence of a large number of comorbidities, but was not able to adjust for differences in the severity of heart failure. Providers in fee‐for‐service health care have a financial incentive to code for comorbidities that VA providers do not have; thus, there may be differential capture of this between patients in VA and non‐VA care. Likewise, most studies were not able to adjust for differences in social determinants of health, which may affect length of stay, readmission, and outcomes of chronic illness. VA patients are known to bear a heavier burden of social determinants of health than patients outside VA care. 20 , 21 To the extent these burdens are uncaptured and unadjusted for, this discrepancy places VA care at a disadvantage compared with patients outside VA care for such outcomes. The bias introduced by this heavier burden makes the findings that VA care was equivalent to or better than non‐VA care notable.

An additional limitation in arriving at overarching conclusions is that the conditions and procedures for which such comparisons have been published are only a small fraction of the care Veterans receive; their results cannot be generalized to all kinds of care, and no conclusions about comparisons of patient access or equity are possible because no studies were identified reporting these outcomes.

Conclusions

In general, most published studies of comparisons of quality of care show that Veterans getting care from VA for hospital medicine get the same or better quality care than Veterans getting community care or the general public getting non‐VA care. However, almost all published studies are before the implementation of the MISSION Act, and thus generalizing these conclusions to today's care may not be valid. Newer studies are needed.
