On the depth of depth-weighted trees
Lyuben Lichev, Amitai Linker, Bas Lodewijks, Dieter Mitsche

Abstract
. The depth-weighted tree DWT( f f ) with weight function f : { 0 , 1 , 2 , … , } → ( 0 , ∞ ) f:\{0,1,2,\ldots,\}\to(0,\infty) is a dynamic random tree grown from a root r r where vertices arrive consecutively and every new vertex attaches to a parent u u with probability proportional to f f (distance between u u and r r ). This work is dedicated to a systematic analysis of the depth of DWT( f f ). Namely, we provide precise analytic expressions of the typical depth of DWT( f f ) for convergent, periodic, slowly growing, and (super-)exponentially growing weight functions. Furthermore, for bounded or exponentially growing f f , we determine the typical depth up to a multiplicative constant, thus confirming and strengthening a conjecture of Leckey, Mitsche and Wormald.

1.
Introduction

Random graphs have been a central topic in probability theory and network science for decades.
Their applicability as models for real-world networks and the intrinsic mathematical interest of many classic models has led to the development of a general theoretical framework, see e.g. the books of Van der Hofstad
[
15
,
17
]
.
Some of the most intriguing mathematical questions in the field concern the evolution of growing random graphs and networks; here, we restrict our attention to the most widely studied setting of dynamic random (rooted) trees.

The variety of growth mechanisms for random trees which have been proposed and studied in the literature can be classified into two categories according to the power of a fixed vertex to attract new neighbours.
The first category consists of models where this power evolves with the dynamic construction of the tree.
One classic example is given by
preferential attachment
models where the probability that a vertex receives a new child at a given step is proportional to a growing function of its current degree; see the books
[
14
,
28
,
17
]
for a summary of the vast literature on the topic. We do mention a very recent paper on preferential attachment trees that studies monotonicity of the depth constant
[
26
]
, which relates to the statistics of interest in this paper. Another classic model is the
online nearest-neighbour tree
where vertices are embedded consecutively and randomly in a compact metric space and every new vertex sends an edge to the nearest vertex already embedded
[
1
,
4
,
8
,
9
,
24
,
30
,
35
,
37
]
.

In the second category of models, the power of a vertex to attract neighbours is determined by a
weight
attributed at birth: namely, the probability that a vertex receives a child at a given step is always proportional to its weight.
The most famous example here is the
random recursive tree
studied in a long line of research
[
5
,
6
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
25
]
where all vertices receive equal weights.
Another natural connection rule giving rise to the
depth-weighted tree
where the weight attributed to a vertex is a function of its distance to the root.
Here, the choice of a function may serve to privilege connections closer to the root (e.g. when it is the primary source of a signal) or to the leaves instead (e.g. in the mathematics genealogy tree), or also adapt to a more complex hierarchical structure over different generations.
Perhaps surprisingly, unlike the remaining models described above, depth-weighted trees were introduced by Leckey, Mitsche and Wormald
[
23
]
only a few years ago (see also
[
3
]
for a very recent and somewhat related model).

Definition 1.1

(Depth-weighted tree (DWT))
.

Fix a function
f:\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\to(0,\infty)
. An (inclusion-wise) increasing sequence of labelled rooted trees
(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(T_{n}(f))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with depth functions
(d_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
d_{n}:\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
is constructed as follows:

•

The tree
T_{1}
contains a single vertex labelled
1
(the root) on depth
d_{1}(1)=0
.

•

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the tree
T_{n+1}
is constructed conditionally on
T_{n}
by adding a vertex labelled
n+1
to
T_{n}
and connecting it to a single vertex, which is
i\in[n]
with probability

(1.1)

\frac{f(d_{n}(i))}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(d_{n}(j))}.

On this event, we say that
n+1

attaches
to vertex
i
and to depth
d_{n}(i)
(and thus vertex
n+1
has depth
d_{n+1}(n+1)=d_{n}(i)+1
).

In the described context,
f
is called a
weight function
and
T_{n}=T_{n}(f)
is called the
depth-weighted tree on
n
vertices with weight function
f
.
Moreover, we define
T_{\infty}
to be the infinite tree obtained as union of the trees
(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

For a rooted tree
T
, the
depth of the tree
is denoted
d(T)
and denotes the maximum over the depths of the vertices in
T
.
A description of the depth of DWTs was the main focus of
[
23
]
where the following bounds on
\mathbb{E}[d(T_{n})]
for different weight functions were provided.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem 2.3 in
[
23
]
)
.

Fix a weight function
f:\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\to(0,\infty)
.

(a)

If
0<\inf f\leq\sup f<\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}[d(T_{n})]=\Theta(\log n)
.

(b)

If
f:k\mapsto(k+1)^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\geq 0
, then
\mathbb{E}[d(T_{n})]=\Theta(\log n)
.

(c)

If
f:k\mapsto\exp(k^{\beta})
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
, then
\mathbb{E}[d(T_{n})]=O((\log n)^{1/(1-\beta)})
.

(d)

If
f:k\mapsto c^{k}
for some
c>2
, then
\mathbb{E}[d(T_{n})]=\Omega(n/\log n)
.

(e)

If
f:k\mapsto\exp(ak\log k)
for some
a>1
, then
\mathbb{E}[d(T_{n})]=\Theta(n)
.

We extend the analysis of DWTs and significantly strengthen parts (a), (d) and (e) of Theorem
1.2
by determining the typical growth of
d(T_{n})
(sometimes up to lower order terms) for bounded, slowly growing, exponential, and super-exponential weight functions.
The next subsection is dedicated to a discussion of our main results and open problems for further study followed by an outline of the proof ideas.

1.1.
Our results

We separately analyse
d(T_{n})
in each of the four previously described regimes.

Bounded weight functions.

We first study the depth of DWTs with weight functions
f
bounded away from 0 and infinity, that is,
0<\inf f\leq\sup f<\infty
.
This regime is fundamental as it consists of models which may be considered as perturbations of the random recursive tree. Indeed, beyond the apparent similarity, our first result shows that, in this setting, the depth of DWTs behaves similarly to that of the random recursive tree.

Proposition 1.3
.

For every weight function
f
bounded away from
0
and infinity, there exist constants
\ell=\ell(\inf f,\sup f),L=L(\inf f,\sup f)>0
such that almost surely

(1.2)

\ell\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(T_{n})}{\log n}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(T_{n})}{\log n}\leq L.

Under additional regularity assumptions on the weight function
f
, it is possible to strengthen Proposition
1.3
to a strong law of large numbers, mirroring precisely the behaviour of
d(T_{n})
for random recursive trees.

Theorem 1.4
.

For every convergent or periodic weight function bounded away from
0
,

(1.3)

\frac{d(T_{n})}{\log n}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{a.s.}}}{\mathrm{e}}.

While our proof of
Theorem

1.4
uses the assumed regularity of the weight function in an essential way, we believe that the conclusion of (
1.3
) may hold more generally.

Question 1.5
.

Does (
1.3
) hold for every weight function
f
bounded away from
0
and infinity?

Slowly growing weight functions.

A weight function
f
is called
slowly growing
if it is non-decreasing, tends to infinity and satisfies

(WSV)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{f(k/f(k))}{f(k)}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{f(kf(k))}{f(k)}=1.

Simple examples of slowly growing functions include
n\mapsto\log(n+1)
or
n\mapsto\exp((\log(n+1))^{\alpha})
with
\alpha<1/2
.
Such weight functions introduce a weak bias towards larger depths, and it is a priori unclear if this bias makes the tree significantly deeper.
Our next theorem shows that this is not the case and that (
1.3
) also holds for slowly growing functions.

Theorem 1.6
.

For every slowly growing weight function,

(1.4)

\frac{d(T_{n})}{\log n}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{a.s.}}}{\mathrm{e}}.

Remark 1.7
.

We note that the lower bound in
Theorem

1.6
can be derived by combining
[
23
, Proposition 2.7]
showing that the depth of
T_{n}(f)
for any non-decreasing weight function
f
stochastically dominates the depth of an
n
-vertex random recursive tree, and
[
31
, Theorem
1
]
showing that the depth of the latter rescaled by
\log n
almost surely converges to
{\mathrm{e}}
. Thus, our contribution is the proof of the upper bound.

Exponential weight functions.

Next, we focus on functions for which there is
c\geq 1
with

(EG)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{f(k+1)}{f(k)}=c.

When
c>1
, these are exactly the functions exhibiting exponential growth.
Unlike previous regimes, the strong bias towards lower depths ensures that vertices typically attach to a parent at (nearly) maximal distance from the root, causing the depth of
T_{n}
to grow much faster. Our next result formalises this intuition.

Theorem 1.8
.

Fix a weight function satisfying assumption (
EG
) for some
c\geq 1
.

(i)

If
c>1
, there is
\gamma=\gamma(c)>0
such that, with high probability
1
1

1

That is, with probability tending to 1 as
n\to\infty
.
,
d(T_{n})\geq\gamma n
.

(ii)

If
c=1
, for every
\varepsilon>0
,
d(T_{n})\leq\varepsilon n
with probability
1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-\omega(n)}
. Thus,
d(T_{n})/n\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{a.s.}}}0
.

Remark 1.9
.

For every
\eta>0
, at the cost of allowing
\gamma
in
Theorem

1.8
(i) to depend on
\eta
, our proof shows that
Theorem

1.8
(i) holds with probability at least
1-n^{-\eta}
.

The result in Theorem
1.8
(i) is a significant strengthening compared to part (d) in Theorem
1.2
in
[

]
, as we provide the correct asymptotic order of the depth for all constants

and for a wider range of functions

, not just the purely exponential case
k\mapsto c^{k}
. The second part of Conjecture
2.4
from
[

]
(suggesting that the expected depth of

is linear under the assumptions of part (d) of

(i).

Remark 1.10
.

The techniques from the proof of Theorem

can be extended to functions satisfying a slightly weaker condition compared to assumption (

).
For example, consider the weight function
k\mapsto{\mathrm{e}}^{k/(\log(k+2))^{\alpha}}
with
0<\alpha<1
.
For this slightly sub-exponential function, the methods in

can be adapted to show that there is

such that, with high probability,

(1.5)

d(T_{n})\geq n{\mathrm{e}}^{-\nu(\log n)^{\alpha}(\log\log n)^{2}}.

Notice that this lower bound is sublinear but still asymptotically larger than any bound of the form

Super-exponential weight functions.

Finally, we present several results concerning certain weight functions
f
growing
faster
than exponentially. Namely, we assume that

(D)

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{f(j+1)}{f(j)}=\infty

and define the quantity

(1.7)

I_{n}\coloneq\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{f(j)}{f(j+1)}.

Note that, by the first part of Assumption (
D
), it follows that
I_{n}=o(n)
. As implied by the following theorem, when
I_{n}
is large, it determines precisely the typical number of vertices outside a longest path starting from the root of
T_{n}
.

Our main theorem for super-exponentially growing functions is then the following:

Theorem 1.13
.

Fix a weight function satisfying Assumption (
D
).

(i)

If
I_{n}
converges, then there is an integer-valued random variable
K
such that

n-d(T_{n})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{a.s.}}}K.

(ii)

If
I_{n}=\omega(1)
, then

\frac{n-d(T_{n})}{I_{n}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathbb{P}}}1.

Observe that, when
I_{n}
converges, part (i) of Theorem
1.13
shows that all but a finite (random) number of vertices belong to a unique infinite path from the root of the tree.

Theorem
1.13
significantly strengthens part (e) in Theorem
1.2
in
[
23
]
. That result concerns a special case of (ii), and its proof is based on the fact that the probability of the event that
T_{n}
is a path has a non-zero limit, so that the expected value of the depth of
T_{n}
is linear. We extend this to more general functions, also including case (ii), and obtain much stronger convergence results.

1.2.
Outline of the proof ideas.

Important prerequisites for our proofs are two continuous-time processes presented in
Sections

2.2.1
and
2.2.2
.
The first of them is a continuous-time branching process version of the DWT which was also used in
[
23
]
. Here, each vertex in the process independently produces offspring according to a Poisson process with intensity depending on its depth in the tree.
The second process simplifies the structure of the previously described branching process by recursively tracking only the profile of the process (the number of vertices on each depth).
It presents a convenient framework used in each of the proofs of our main results and is among the main technical novelties of this work.

The proofs of
Proposition

1.3
,
Theorems

1.4
and
1.6
follow a similar theme.
The main point is to estimate the stopping times
\tau_{n}
(when the
n
-th vertex is born) and
\tau_{1,k}
(when the first vertex on depth
k
is born) in the continuous-time version sufficiently precisely: then, any function
k(n)
such that
\tau_{n}\approx\tau_{1,k(n)}
actually asymptotically characterises the typical depth of
T_{n}
.
The short proof of
Proposition

1.3
makes use of the second-moment method to show rough (although simple and sufficient) estimates of
\tau_{n}
and
\tau_{1,k}
.
Achieving the more precise conclusions of
Theorems

1.4
and
1.6
requires a refined understanding of the growth of the DWT.
Estimating
\tau_{1,k}
is the comparatively simpler part: this requires standard first moment computations (for convergent and slowly growing functions), a subadditivity argument (for convergent functions) and an old result of Biggins
[
7
]
(for periodic functions).
The main complication in each of these proofs is the estimation of
\tau_{n}
, which requires finding a convenient way to circumvent the potential irregularities among the small values of
f
. Note that, ince vertices produce offspring at different rates (depending on their depths), these results cannot be obtained directly from the well-known theory on Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes. To estimate
\tau_{n}
, we analyse the second continuous-time process over an interval
[0,t]
in two phases.
During the short first phase taking place over the interval
[0,\varepsilon t]
with
\varepsilon
small, the process typically produces a vertex on a sufficiently large depth where
f
starts to behave regularly.
The second phase analyses the process over the interval
[\varepsilon t,t]
and uses the regularity of
f
to show convenient lower bounds for the process
N(\cdot)
which keeps track of the total number of vertices.

We turn to the proof of
Theorem

1.8
. Part (ii) relies on a short renewal-process-type of argument: it uses that the time needed to increase the depth of the tree from
k
to
k+1
is stochastically minimised when all unsuccessful attempts result in a new vertex on depth
k
.
The proof of
Theorem

1.8
(i) is much harder and uses the framework defined in
Section

2.2.2
in a different and novel way.
In a nutshell, our approach estimates the profile of the depths in
T_{n}
in two steps.
First, we provide an exponential concentration estimate on the event that some depth is a sort of non-strict local maximum (in a suitably chosen constant-size window) containing exactly
s
vertices.
This step is the most technical part of the proof: it consists in a recursive analysis of the continuous-time trajectories of blocks of consecutive depths, and relies on several concentration bounds, stochastic comparisons between processes and a concise case analysis.
The second step analyses a deterministic algorithm showing that every depth
r
is within a small distance from a local maximum
r^{\prime}
, and one can reach
r^{\prime}
from
r
by making jumps of constant length towards depths containing (strictly) more vertices.
Finally, to show
Theorem

1.8
(i), we show that typically the interval
[0,cn]
of depths contains fewer than
n
vertices of
T_{n}
: here, the first step serves to control the number of vertices on depths which are local maxima while the second step controls the vertices on all remaining depths.

Finally, the proof of
Theorem

1.13
relies on a simple but crucial coupling result and a rather delicate first-second moment argument.
The coupling result states that, in the framework of
Section

2.2.2
, there exists a sequence of independent exponential random variables
(E_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
with suitable rates satisfying the following two properties:
first,
E_{k}
almost surely dominates the difference between the birth times of the first vertices on depths
k
and
k+1
and, second, for every
k
, the family
E_{k},E_{k+1},\ldots
is independent of the process on depths
0,\ldots,k
.
The sequence
(E_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
is important for two related reasons: the first property above allows us to suitably approximate the explosion time of the continuous-time process which,
combined with the independence encoded in the sequence, makes it possible to precisely bound the joint moments of the number of vertices on different depths at the explosion time.
The latter task is achieved via delicate recursive computations.

, we gather notation and present preliminary results.
Then, Section

In Section

Preliminaries

General notation and concentration results

We start with some standard notation. We write

\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

Moreover, for a positive integer

Concentration inequalities

We begin with the well-known Paley-Zygmund inequality for concentration of non-negative random variables with bounded second moment.

Lemma 2.1

(Paley-Zygmund inequality, see
[

]

For every

\mathbb{P}(Z\geq\theta\mathbb{E}[Z])\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\mathbb{E}[Z]^{2}/\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]

Next, we state standard Chernoff bounds for binomial and Poisson random variables, see e.g.

Lemma 2.2

(Chernoff bounds)

For every binomial or Poisson random variable

\mathbb{P}(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\leq-t)\leq\exp(-t^{2}/2\mathbb{E}[X])

\mathbb{P}(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\geq t)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{t^{2}}{2(\mathbb{E}[X]+t/3)}\bigg).

Lemma 2.3

(see Corollary 2.9.2 in

A

2.2.
Continuous embeddings

In this subsection we describe two continuous-time interpretations of the DWT model.

2.2.1.
First interpretation: via a continuous-time branching process

This continuous-time version of the model appears in the original paper of Leckey, Mitsche and Wormald
[
23
]
. It has the advantage that the discrete model can be completely recovered from it (which is not the case with the second interpretation presented in
Section

2.2.2
).

A central object in this construction is the
Ulam-Harris tree
with vertex set

(2.1)

\mathrm{UH}\coloneq\{\varnothing\}\cup\bigg(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{N}^{n}\bigg).

This tree grows away from a root
\varnothing
and the children of each vertex
v\in\mathrm{UH}
are
v1,v2,\ldots
.
Moreover, we denote by
d_{\mathrm{UH}}\colon\mathrm{UH}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
the depth function for the Ulam-Harris tree; namely,
d_{\mathrm{UH}}(\varnothing)=0
and, for every vertex
v=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{k}
with
k,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
,
d_{\mathrm{UH}}(v)=k
.
We also define a
ray
to be an infinite sequence of positive integers and denote by
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
the set of rays.

To every
v\in\mathrm{UH}
, we then assign a Poisson process
\xi^{v}
of rate
f(d_{\mathrm{UH}}(v))
where
f
is the weight function associated with the depth-weighted tree.
The points
\sigma^{v}_{1},\sigma^{v}_{2},\ldots
in the Poisson process
\xi^{v}
correspond to the difference between the birth-times of the children
v1,v2,\ldots
of
v
and the birth-time of
v
itself.
We can thus recursively construct the
absolute
birth times
(\mathfrak{b}(v))_{v\in\mathrm{UH}}
as

(2.2)

\mathfrak{b}(\varnothing)\coloneq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ \mathfrak{b}(vj)\coloneq\mathfrak{b}(v)+\sigma^{v}_{j}\qquad\text{for every }v\in\mathrm{UH}\text{ and }j\in\mathbb{N}.

Finally, we construct the continuous-time branching process
(\mathcal{T}(t))_{t\geq 0}
by defining

(2.3)

\mathcal{T}(t)\coloneq\{v\in\mathrm{UH}:\mathfrak{b}(v)\leq t\}\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

In particular,
d(\mathcal{T}(t))
stands for the depth of the branching process
\mathcal{T}
at time
t
. To justify the correspondence between the continuous-time branching process
\mathcal{T}
and the depth-weighted tree process, we introduce the stopping times
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
where

(2.4)

\tau_{n}\coloneq\inf\{t\geq 0:|\mathcal{T}(t)|=n\}\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

It is then not hard to observe (and follows from
[
23
, Lemma 3.1]
) that

(2.5)

\{T_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\overset{d}{=}\{\mathcal{T}(\tau_{n}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

2.2.2.
Second interpretation: via a Cox processes

Sometimes the exact structure of the tree
T_{n}
is irrelevant and only the number of vertices at different depths matters.
The continuous-time construction from
Section

2.2.1
carries more information than needed in such a case, which can complicate the analysis.
To this end, we introduce the following simplified interpretation.

•

First, we define a Poisson point process
N_{1}(t)
with intensity
\Lambda_{1}(\mathrm{d}t)=f(0)\mathrm{d}t
(counting the vertices on depth 1).

•

Recursively, for every
r\geq 2
, we define a Cox process
N_{r}(t)
with intensity
\Lambda_{r}(\mathrm{d}t)=f(r-1)N_{r-1}(t)\mathrm{d}t
(counting the vertices on depth
r
).

For every
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we call
\Lambda_{r}
the
directing measure
of
N_{r}
and additionally denote
N_{0}(t)=1
for all
t\geq 0
to account for the root on depth zero. It will be useful to further define the stopping times

(2.6)

\tau_{j,r}=\inf\{t\geq 0:N_{r}(t)\geq j\}.

Note that
\tau_{j,r}
can be interpreted as the first time when the tree reaches
j
vertices on depth
r
.
Further, for every
t\geq 0
, we define

(2.7)

N(t)\coloneq\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}N_{r}(t)=1+\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}N_{r}(t),

interpreted as the total number of individuals at time
t
, that is,
N(t)=|\mathcal{T}(t)|
. This motivates the alternative definition of the stopping times
\tau_{n}
as

(2.8)

\tau_{n}\coloneq\inf\{t\geq 0:N(t)=n\}.

The following result formalises the connection with the original model.

Lemma 2.5
.

For every weight function
f
, the distribution of the sequences
(N_{r}(\tau_{n}))_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(|\{v\in T_{n}:d_{n}(v)=r\}|)_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
coincide.

Proof.

We proceed by recursively constructing a coupling of the two sequences that identifies them almost surely.
The coupling of the roots is immediate.
Suppose that, for some
n\geq 2
, a coupling with the above description has been constructed so that
N_{r}(\tau_{n-1})=|\{v\in T_{n-1}:d(v)=r\}|
for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and define
s_{r}=s_{r}(n)\coloneq N_{r}(\tau_{n-1})+1
.
Then, for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
(\tau_{s_{r},r}-\tau_{n-1})_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
is a family of independent exponential random variables with rates
f(r-1)N_{r-1}(\tau_{n-1})
, respectively. Thus, since
N_{r}(\tau_{n})=s_{r}
holds only for the index
r
of the minimum among
(\tau_{s_{r},r}-\tau_{n-1})_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
,

\mathbb{P}(N_{r}(\tau_{n})=s_{r})=\frac{f(r-1)N_{r-1}(\tau_{n-1})}{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(i-1)N_{i-1}(\tau_{n-1})}.

However, this is precisely the probability that the
n
-th vertex attaches to a vertex on depth
r-1
in the discrete model, which concludes the coupling until time
\tau_{n}
and the proof.
∎

(Non)-explosion of DWTs via infinite paths

We end with a minor result concerning the embedding of DWTs in the continuous-time branching process discussed in Section

(2.9)

\tau_{\infty}\coloneq\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{n},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ \tau_{1,\infty}\coloneq\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{1,k}

Almost surely

.

3.
Bounded and slowly-varying weight functions

This section is dedicated to the proofs of
Proposition

1.3
and Theorem
1.4
regarding bounded weight functions, and Theorem
1.6
regarding slowly-varying weight functions.
These two settings are handled within a common framework using the continuous-time formulation described in Section
2.2.2
.
We recall that the number of vertices at each depth
k
is represented by a continuous-time jump process
N_{k}(t)
, and
\tau_{1,n}
and
\tau_{n}
correspond to the first time the tree attains depth and size
n
, respectively.
Our proofs relate to the analysis of the
\mathrm{DWT}
with constant weight function
f\equiv 1
which, when interpreted through the continuous-time formulation described in Section
2.2.2
, becomes a Yule process, where individuals produce children in an i.i.d. fashion according to a rate-one Poisson point process. This is a special case of a Crump–Mode–Jagers (CMJ) branching processes. Both Kingman
[
19
]
and Biggins
[
7
]
study the growth rate of
\tau_{1,n}
for such processes, and the growth rate of
\tau_{n}
follows from general work of Nerman on CMJ branching processes
[
27
]
.

In the case of the Yule process, the following properties, described in our terminology, hold almost surely:

•

\log(N(t))/t\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{a.s.}}}1
(exponential growth of the size, see
[
29
, Section
2.5
]
), and

•

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{1,n}/n=1/{\mathrm{e}}
(linear growth of the depth, see
[
19
, Theorem
5
]
).

Our proofs rely on extending these properties to the more general class of functions
f
that are bounded but not constant.
We end this preamble by giving a closed expression for
\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(t)\right]
, which plays a central role in our computations. Recall the convention that an empty product equals one.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
t\geq 0
,

(3.1)

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(t)\right]=\frac{(t\ell_{k})^{k}}{k!}

with

(3.2)

\ell_{k}\coloneq\bigg(\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}f(j)\bigg)^{1/k}.

Proof.

We prove the result by induction over
k
. The result holds for
k=0
as
N_{0}(t)=1
for all
t\geq 0
. Assume (
3.1
) for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k+1}(t)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k+1}(t)\,\big|\,(N_{k}(s))_{s\in[0,t]}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}f(k)N_{k}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right].

After switching expectation and integration by Fubini’s theorem, the induction hypothesis gives

(3.3)

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k+1}(t)\right]=\int_{0}^{t}f(k)\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(s)\right]\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{k!}\prod_{i=0}^{k}f(i)\int_{0}^{t}s^{k}\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{(t\ell_{k})^{k+1}}{(k+1)!},

as desired.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

By combining Markov’s inequality, (
3.1
) and the bound
k!\geq(k/{\mathrm{e}})^{k}
for all
k\geq 0
, we obtain the useful bound

(3.4)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tau_{1,k}\leq t\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(N_{k}(t)\geq 1\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(t)\right]=\frac{(t\ell_{k})^{k}}{k!}\leq\bigg(\frac{{\mathrm{e}}t\ell_{k}}{k}\bigg)^{k}.

3.1.
Weight functions bounded away from
0
and infinity

In this section, we prove
Proposition

1.3
for functions
f
for which
0<\inf f<\sup f<\infty
; observe that then
0<\inf\ell_{k}<\sup\ell_{k}<\infty
for
\ell_{k}
as defined in Lemma
3.1
.

To prove Proposition
1.3
, we make use of the continuous-time formulation of the model from
Section

2.2.2
.
More precisely, Proposition
1.3
holds if there are constants
\lambda=\lambda(\sup f)>0
and
\Lambda=\Lambda(\inf f)>0
such that almost surely

(3.5)

\lambda\leq\min\bigg\{\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{k^{3}}}{\log k},\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{1,k}}{k}\bigg\}\leq\max\bigg\{\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{k^{3}}}{\log k},\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{1,k}}{k}\bigg\}\leq\Lambda.

Indeed, if (
3.5
) holds, then there is almost surely some
k_{0}
such that, for all
k\geq k_{0}
,

\tau_{1,\,\lambda(\log k)/2\Lambda}<\tau_{k^{3}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\tau_{k^{3}}<\tau_{1,\,2\Lambda(\log k)/\lambda}.

As a result, for every large
m\geq 1
and integer
n
with
(n-1)^{3}\leq m<n^{3}
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\lambda}{7\Lambda}\leq\frac{\lambda\log(n-1)}{2\Lambda\cdot 3\log n}&\leq\frac{\max\{k:\tau_{1,k}\leq\tau_{(n-1)^{3}}\}}{3\log n}\\
&=\frac{d(\mathcal{T}(\tau_{(n-1)^{3}}))}{3\log n}\leq\frac{d(\mathcal{T}(\tau_{m}))}{\log m}\leq\frac{d(\mathcal{T}(\tau_{n^{3}}))}{3\log(n-1)}\\
&\hskip 130.50023pt\leq\frac{\min\{k:\tau_{1,k}\geq\tau_{n^{3}}\}}{3\log(n-1)}\leq\frac{2\Lambda\log n}{\lambda\cdot 3\log(n-1)}\leq\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda},\end{aligned}

thus implying Proposition
1.3
.
The rest of the proof consists in finding the upper and lower bounds in (
3.5
). Our proof is based on the second moment method for
N_{k}(t)
.

Proof of
Proposition

1.3
.

Denote
c=\inf f
and
C=\sup f
.

Lower bounds in (
3.5
).

First, by using (
3.4
) with
t=k/2{\mathrm{e}}\ell_{k}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1,k}\leq k/2{\mathrm{e}}\ell_{k})\leq 2^{-k}
. Thus, by Borel-Cantelli’s lemma, almost surely

(3.6)

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{1,k}}{k/\ell_{k}}\geq\frac{1}{2{\mathrm{e}}}\Rightarrow\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{1,k}}{k}\geq\frac{1}{2{\mathrm{e}}C}.

On the other hand, using Lemma
3.1
,

(3.7)

\mathbb{E}[N(t)]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(t\ell_{k})^{k}}{k!}\leq\exp(Ct),

implying that

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tau_{n^{3}}\leq t\right)=\mathbb{P}(N(t)\geq n^{3})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[N(t)\right]}{n^{3}}\leq\frac{\exp(Ct)}{n^{3}}.

Thus, for all
n\geq 1
,
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n^{3}}\leq(\log n)/C)\leq n^{-2}
. By Borel-Cantelli’s lemma, almost surely

(3.8)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{n^{3}}}{\log n}\geq\frac{1}{C}.

Upper bounds in (
3.5
).

We continue by inductively estimating the second moment of
N_{k}(t)
.
Using that a Poisson random variable with parameter
\lambda>0
has second moment
\lambda^{2}+\lambda
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{k+1}(t)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k+1}(t)^{2}\,\big|\,(N_{k}(s))_{s\in[0,t]}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}f(k)N_{k}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}f(k)N_{k}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}f(k)N_{k}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}[N_{k+1}(t)].

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the first summand above is dominated by

f(k)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(s)\right]\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{N_{k}(s)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(s)\right]}\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\right]=f(k)^{2}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(s)\right]\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(s)^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(s)\right]}\mathrm{d}s\bigg).

Then, by combining the latter bound with the first equality in (
3.3
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}[N_{k+1}(t)^{2}]\leq f(k)\mathbb{E}[N_{k+1}(t)]\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(s)^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(s)\right]}\mathrm{d}s+\mathbb{E}[N_{k+1}(t)].

Dividing both sides by
\mathbb{E}[N_{k+1}(t)]\prod_{i=0}^{k}f(i)
implies that

\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{k+1}(t)^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[N_{k+1}(t)]\prod_{i=0}^{k}f(i)}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(s)^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(s)\right]\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}f(i)}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{k}f(i)}.

By iterating the last bound and using that
N_{0}\equiv 1
, we arrive at

\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(t)^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(t)]\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}f(i)}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{t^{j}}{j!\prod_{i=0}^{k-j-1}f(i)}.

Moreover, by dividing by
t^{k}/k!
and using Lemma
3.1
finally yields

\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(t)^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(t)]^{2}}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{k!}{j!\prod_{i=0}^{k-j-1}tf(i)}=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\binom{k}{r}\frac{r!}{(t\ell_{r})^{r}},

where the latter equality follows from the change of variable
r=k-j
. Thus, by setting

t_{k}\coloneq\frac{2k}{c}\geq 2\max_{r\in[k]}\bigg(\binom{k}{r}\frac{r!}{\ell_{r}^{r}}\bigg)^{1/r},

we obtain that
\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(t_{k})^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(t_{k})]^{2}\sum_{r=0}^{k}2^{-r}\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(t_{k})\right]^{2}
.
Thus, by the Paley-Zygmund inequality (
Lemma

2.1
), for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.9)

\mathbb{P}(N_{k}(t_{k})\geq\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(t_{k})]/2)\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(t_{k})]^{2}}{4\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(t_{k})^{2}]}\geq\frac{1}{8}.

Further, let
\mathcal{E}_{1}\coloneq\{N_{1}(t_{k})<t_{k}f(0)/2\}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}\coloneq\{N_{2}(2t_{k})<t_{k}^{2}f(0)f(1)/4\}
.
By Chernoff’s bound for Poisson random variables (
Lemma

2.2
),

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1})=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Poi}(t_{k}f(0))\leq t_{k}f(0)/2)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{t_{k}f(0)}{8}\bigg)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-k/8}.

On the other hand, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}
, there are at least
t_{k}f(0)/2
vertices on depth
1
at time
t_{k}
.
Hence, analysing the number of vertices born on depth
2
throughout
[t_{k},2t_{k}]
gives

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Poi}(t_{k}^{2}f(0)f(1)/2)\leq t_{k}^{2}f(0)f(1)/4)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{t_{k}^{2}f(0)f(1)}{32}\bigg)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-k/8}.

Furthermore, note that, conditionally on the event
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c}
, there are at least
t_{k}^{2}f(0)f(1)/4\geq k^{2}
vertices on depth
2
at time
2t_{k}
, and the subtrees rooted at these vertices evolve independently during the time interval
[2t_{k},3t_{k}]
.
Call
\widetilde{N}^{i}_{k}(3t_{k})
the number of vertices in the
i
-th subtree born on depth
k
before time
3t_{k}
(which are at distance
k-2
from the root of the subtree). Note that each
\widetilde{N}^{i}_{k}(3t_{k})
stochastically dominates the random variable
\widetilde{N}_{k}(3t_{k})
given by the number of descendants of a vertex born at time
2t_{k}
on depth 2, which themselves are born in the interval
[2t_{k},3t_{k}]
on depth
k
.
Moreover, since a subtree rooted on depth
2
is a shifted version of the branching process
\mathcal{T}
and
t_{k}\geq t_{k-2}
,
\widetilde{N}_{k}(3t_{k})
also satisfies (
3.9
). Thus,

(3.10)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(N_{k}(3t_{k})<\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{N}_{k}&(3t_{k})]/2)\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c})+\mathbb{P}\big(N_{k}(3t_{k})<\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{N}_{k}(3t_{k})]/2\mid\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c}\big)\\[4.0pt]
&\leq 2{\mathrm{e}}^{-k/8}+\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{N}_{k}(3t_{k})<\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{N}_{k}(3t_{k})]/2\,\text{ for all }\,i\leq t_{k}^{2}f(0)f(1)/4\big)\\
&\leq 2{\mathrm{e}}^{-k/8}+(7/8)^{t_{k}^{2}f(0)f(1)/4}\leq 3{\mathrm{e}}^{-k/8}.\end{split}

As a consequence,
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1,k}>3t_{k})=\mathbb{P}(N_{k}(3t_{k})=0)\leq 3{\mathrm{e}}^{-k/8}
.
Next, we apply (
3.10
) to obtain a bound for
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n^{3}}>3t_{k_{0}})
for some
k_{0}=k_{0}(n)
to be determined.
Indeed, by applying (
3.1
) to
\widetilde{N}_{k}(3t_{k})
and the shifted weight function
k\mapsto f(k+2)
, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{N}_{k}(3t_{k})]=\frac{t_{k}^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}\prod_{j=2}^{k-1}f(j)\geq\frac{(2k)^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}\geq 2^{k-2}.

Define
k_{0}=k_{0}(n)\coloneq 16\log n
so that
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{N}_{k_{0}}(3t_{k_{0}})]\geq 2n^{3}
and note that the event
\tau_{n^{3}}>3t_{k_{0}}
implies that there are fewer than
n^{3}
vertices on depth
k_{0}
at time
3t_{k_{0}}
. Then, (
3.10
) yields

(3.11)

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n^{3}}>3t_{k_{0}})\leq\mathbb{P}(N_{k_{0}}(3t_{k_{0}})\leq\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{N}_{k_{0}}(3t_{k_{0}})]/2)\leq 3{\mathrm{e}}^{-k_{0}/8}\leq 3n^{-2}.

Two applications of Borel-Cantelli’s lemma imply that, almost surely,

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{1,k}}{3t_{k}}\leq 1\Rightarrow\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{1,k}}{k}\leq\frac{6}{c}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{n^{3}}}{3t_{k_{0}(n)}}\leq 1\Rightarrow\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{n^{3}}}{\log n}\leq\frac{96}{c},

which together with (
3.6
) and (
3.8
) implies that (
3.5
) holds almost surely, finishing the proof.
∎

We now focus on the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
We begin with three simple but important observations. First, for convergent functions
f
bounded away from 0,
(\ell_{k})_{k\geq 1}
converges to the limit
\ell\coloneq\lim_{k\to\infty}f(k)
.
Second, as rescaling the weight function by a constant factor does not modify the distribution of the resulting depth-weighted tree, we henceforth assume without loss of generality that
\ell=1
.
Third, for the case of
r-
periodic functions
f
, the same rescaling argument allows us to assume without loss of generality that
\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}f(i)=1
.

We next show that, in the setting of converging weight functions, the quantity
\tau_{1,k}/k
converges almost surely, thus improving on the bounds in (
3.5
).
Our lower bound on
\tau_{1,k}/k
comes from a first moment argument while the upper bound is more involved and adapts ideas of Kingman
[
19
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For every weight function
f(k)\to 1
as
k\to\infty
,

(3.12)

\frac{\tau_{1,k}}{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{a.s.}}}\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}.

Remark 3.4
.

For periodic weight functions, (
3.12
) is implied by Theorem 2 in
[
7
]
.
This more general result concerns Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes satisfying the following assumptions:

•

supercriticality: with positive probability,
\tau_{1,k}<\infty
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

finite number of types: the type of a vertex

is given by the depth of

modulo
r

•

, a vertex of type
i
has a descendant of type

with positive probability.

Under these assumptions (which are clearly satisfied in our case), Theorem 2 in
[
7
]
shows that the sequence
(\tau_{1,k}/k)_{k}
almost surely converges to a limit
\gamma
.
The fact that
\gamma=1/{\mathrm{e}}
in our case requires a combination of displays (2.5), (3.2), the equality for

after display (3.3) and Lemma 3.1 in
[

]
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.3
.

For the lower bound on
\tau_{1,k}/k
, fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in(0,1)

As

Proof of
Proposition

3.6
.

To prove the result, it is enough to show that

(3.19)

\frac{\log(N(t))}{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathrm{a.s.}}}1.

Indeed, as
\tau_{n}\to\infty
with
n
, and
N(\tau_{n})=n
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, (
3.19
) implies the desired result.

Upper bound.

Recall that as
f(k)\to 1
, we also have
\ell_{k}\to 1
with
\ell_{k}
defined in (
3.2
). Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,
\ell_{k}\leq 1+\varepsilon
for all but finitely many
k
.
Combining this with Lemma
3.1
, we deduce that, for every suitably large
t
, we have that

(3.20)

\mathbb{E}[N(t)]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(t\ell_{k})^{k}}{k!}\leq t^{\max\{i:\ell_{i}>1+\varepsilon\}+1}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{((1+\varepsilon)t)^{k}}{k!}\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{(1+2\varepsilon)t}.

As a consequence, for every such
t
, Markov’s inequality implies that

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{\log(N(t))}{t}\geq 1+3\varepsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}(N(t)\geq{\mathrm{e}}^{(1+3\varepsilon)t})\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-\varepsilon t}.

By the monotonicity of
(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
, a union bound and the last inequality, for every large enough
t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{u\geq t}\frac{\log(N(u))}{u}\geq 1+4\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{k\geq 1}\sup_{u\in[t+\varepsilon(k-1),t+\varepsilon k]}\frac{\log(N(u))}{u}\geq 1+4\varepsilon\right)

(3.21)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{k\geq 1}\frac{\log(N(t+\varepsilon k))}{t+\varepsilon(k-1)}\geq 1+4\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{k\geq 1}\frac{\log(N(t+\varepsilon k))}{t+\varepsilon k}\geq 1+3\varepsilon\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\varepsilon t-\varepsilon^{2}k}\leq\frac{\exp(-\varepsilon t)}{1-\exp(-\varepsilon^{2})}.

Thus, by Borel-Cantelli’s lemma for the events in (
3.21
) with
t\in\mathbb{N}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, almost surely

(3.22)

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log(N(t))}{t}\leq 1+4\varepsilon.

Lower bound.
To obtain lower bounds for
N(t)
with large
t
, our approach consists in finding a depth
r_{0}
such that, from that depth onwards, the weight function
f
is sufficiently close to
1
.
We then follow the growth of
(N(s))_{s\in[0,\varepsilon t]}
for some small
\varepsilon>0
and show that, by time
\varepsilon t
, there is a large number of vertices on depth
r_{0}
.
From this time onwards, we use concentration bounds to show that the evolution of the tree (from depth
r_{0}
onwards) is close to that of a tree with weight function
f\equiv 1
. To formalise this idea, fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
small and let
r_{0}=r_{0}(\varepsilon)
be the smallest integer in the interval
[8,\infty)
such that
f(r)\geq 1-\varepsilon/4
for every
r\geq r_{0}
. We then choose
t>0
large enough so that

(3.23)

\frac{\varepsilon t\min_{k\leq r_{0}}f(k)}{r_{0}}\geq 2t^{1-\varepsilon}.

We split the interval
[0,t]
into
[0,\varepsilon t]
and
[\varepsilon t,t]
and analyse the process on these time intervals separately.

For the growth of
N
throughout
[0,\varepsilon t]
, define a sequence of times of the form
s_{i}=i\varepsilon t/r_{0}
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,r_{0}\}
. Notice that
s_{r_{0}}=\varepsilon t
and that, by our assumption on
t
,
s_{i}-s_{i-1}
is large.
Then, we define the events
\mathcal{D}_{i}\coloneq\{N_{i}(s_{i})\geq t^{(1-\varepsilon)i}\}
; observe that, at each time
s_{i}
,
\mathcal{D}_{i}
zooms on a different depth
i
.
Since
N_{1}(s_{1})
is a Poisson random variable with mean
\varepsilon tf(0)/r_{0}\geq 2t^{1-\varepsilon}
by (
3.23
), Chernoff’s bound for Poisson random variables (
Lemma

2.2
) yields

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{D}_{1}^{c}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(N_{1}(s_{1})\leq\mathbb{E}\left[N_{1}(s_{1})\right]/2\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon t\min_{k\leq r_{0}}f(k)}{8r_{0}}\right).

Furthermore, on the event
\mathcal{D}_{j}
, there are at least
t^{(1-\varepsilon)j}
vertices on depth
j
at time
s_{j}
.
The offspring produced by these vertices throughout
[s_{j},s_{j+1}]
is a Poisson random variable with mean
(\varepsilon tf(j)/r_{0})t^{(1-\varepsilon)j}\geq 2t^{(1-\varepsilon)(j+1)}
.
Thus, by repeating the latter argument, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{c}\,\right|\mathcal{D}_{j}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon t\min_{k\leq r_{0}}f(k)}{8r_{0}}\right),

and hence conclude that

(3.24)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D}_{r_{0}}^{c})\leq\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{r_{0}-1}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{c}\mid\mathcal{D}_{i})\bigg)+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D}_{1}^{c})\leq r_{0}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\varepsilon t\min_{k\leq r_{0}}f(k)}{8r_{0}}\bigg).

For the growth of
N
throughout
[\varepsilon t,t]
define a new sequence of times of the form
s^{\prime}_{i}=\varepsilon t+i\alpha
where
0\leq i\leq L\coloneq\lceil(1-\varepsilon)t/\varepsilon^{2}\rceil
and
\alpha\coloneq(1-\varepsilon)t/L\approx\varepsilon^{2}
. In particular, observe that
s_{0}^{\prime}=\varepsilon t
and
s_{L}^{\prime}=t
. Let
N_{r_{0}}^{+}(u)
denote the number of vertices on depth at least
r_{0}
at time
u
and, for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,L\}
, define the event

\mathcal{E}_{i}\coloneq\{N_{r_{0}}^{+}(s_{i}^{\prime})\geq{\mathrm{e}}^{i(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}t^{4}\}.

Two key observations about these events are:

•

\mathcal{D}_{r_{0}}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{0}
, since
t^{(1-\varepsilon)r_{0}}\geq t^{4}
, from our assumptions that
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and
r_{0}\geq 8
.

•

\mathcal{E}_{L}
implies
\log(N(t))\geq(1-2\varepsilon)t
: indeed, by definition of
\alpha
,
{\mathrm{e}}^{L(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}t^{4}\geq{\mathrm{e}}^{(1-2\varepsilon)t}
.

In words, the event
\mathcal{E}_{i}
means that there are at least
{\mathrm{e}}^{i(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}t^{4}
vertices on depth at least
r_{0}
at time
s_{i}^{\prime}
. Call
\mathcal{O}_{i}
the number of children on depth at least
r_{0}
at time
s_{i}^{\prime}
produced in the interval
[s_{i}^{\prime},s^{\prime}_{i+1}]
. Conditionally on the event
\mathcal{E}_{i}
, this is a Poisson random variable with mean
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{O}_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]\geq\alpha(1-\varepsilon/4){\mathrm{e}}^{i(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}t^{4}
, where we used that
f(i)\geq 1-\varepsilon/4
for all
i\geq r_{0}
.
Since the vertices at time
s_{i}^{\prime}
are also counted at time
s_{i+1}^{\prime}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{E}_{i+1}^{c}\,\right|\mathcal{E}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{O}_{i}\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{(i+1)(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}t^{4}-{\mathrm{e}}^{i(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}t^{4}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{O}_{i}\leq\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}-1}{(1-\varepsilon/4)\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{O}_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}_{i}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{O}_{i}\leq(1-\varepsilon/2)\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{O}_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]\,|\,\mathcal{E}_{i}\right).

By Chernoff’s bound for Poisson random variables (
Lemma

2.2
), we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{E}_{i+1}^{c}\,\right|\mathcal{E}_{i}\right)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{O}_{i}\right]/8}\leq\exp\big(-(\varepsilon^{2}/8)\alpha(1-\varepsilon/4){\mathrm{e}}^{i(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}t^{4}\big)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-(\varepsilon t/2)^{4}}.

By the last two displays, the inclusion
\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{r_{0}}^{c}
and (
3.24
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{\log(N(t))}{t}\leq 1-2\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{L}^{c})\leq\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{i+1}^{c}\mid\mathcal{E}_{i})\bigg)+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq L{\mathrm{e}}^{-(\varepsilon t/2)^{4}}+r_{0}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\varepsilon t\min_{k\leq r_{0}}f(k)}{8r_{0}}\bigg).

Similar argument to (
3.21
) allows to strengthen this bound to
\mathbb{P}\big(\inf_{u\geq t}\frac{\log(N(u))}{u}\leq 1-3\varepsilon\big)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}t}
for some
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon^{\prime}(\varepsilon,f)>0
. By Borel-Cantelli’s lemma for
t\in\mathbb{N}
, for every sufficiently small
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
, almost surely

(3.25)

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log(N(t))}{t}\geq 1-3\varepsilon.

The fact that each of (
3.22
) and (
3.25
) holds for every
\varepsilon>0
completes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

3.7
.

Recall our assumption that
f
is
r
-periodic with
\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}f(i)=1
and extend

periodically to the entire

.
Our proof of

is very similar to that of

: we show that

by obtaining upper and lower bounds.
For the upper bound, the argument remains unchanged except that (

) is replaced by

\mathbb{E}[N(t)]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(t\ell_{k})^{k}}{k!}\leq\bigg(\max_{i\in[r]}\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}f(j)\bigg)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}=\bigg(\max_{i\in[r]}\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}f(j)\bigg){\mathrm{e}}^{t}.

For the lower bound, we again analyse separately the growth of
N
on two intervals,
[0,\varepsilon t]
and
[\varepsilon t,t]
, where
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
is a fixed small value and

is large enough to satisfy (
3.23
) with

in place of

.
For the growth of the process on
[0,\varepsilon t]
and for every

s_{i}=\frac{i\varepsilon t}{r+8}
and the event
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\{N_{i}(s_{i})\geq t^{(1-\varepsilon)i}\}

Observe that, for each

and that, for each

t^{(1-\varepsilon)i}\geq t^{4}
. Setting

(which neither depends on

),
we can repeat verbatim the argument from the previous proof to dominate each

as in (
3.24
), and thus obtain

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\min_{i\in[9,r+8]}N_{i}(\varepsilon t)\geq t^{4}\right)\geq 1-(r+8){\mathrm{e}}^{-c_{1}\varepsilon t}.

Next, we study the growth of
N
over the interval
[\varepsilon t,t]
by again considering the sequence of times
s_{i}^{\prime}=\varepsilon t+i\alpha
where
0\leq i\leq L\coloneq\lceil(1-\varepsilon)t/\varepsilon^{2}\rceil
and
\alpha\coloneq(1-\varepsilon)t/L\approx\varepsilon^{2}
. For every
j\in[r]
, let
\widehat{N}_{j}(u)
denote the number of vertices on depths congruent to
j
modulo

at time

, that is,

\widehat{N}_{j}(u)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}N_{j+kr}(u).

Using this notation, for every

and

, we define the events

\mathcal{E}_{i,j}=\bigg\{\widehat{N}_{j}(s_{i}^{\prime})\geq Q_{i,j}t^{4}\bigg\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{E}_{i}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{r}\mathcal{E}_{i,j}

where the quantities

(recall the convention that empty products are equal to

).
Three key observations about these quantities are:

•

For all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the identity
\binom{i-1}{k-1}+\binom{i-1}{k}=\binom{i}{k}
and a simple change of variable give

Q_{i,j}=Q_{i-1,j}+(1-\varepsilon)\alpha f(j-1)Q_{i-1,j-1}.

•

For all

, we have
Q_{0,j}=1
. Combined with (
3.27
), thus yields
Q_{i,j}\geq 1
for all

.

•

Since
\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}f(i)=1
, there is some
\hat{c}>0
such that
\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}f(j-1-\ell)\geq\hat{c}
for all
k\geq 0
and
j\in[r]
. Consequently, for all
i\geq 0
and
j\in[r]
,

(3.28)

Q_{i,j}\geq\hat{c}\sum_{k=0}^{i}\binom{i}{k}((1-\varepsilon)\alpha)^{k}=\hat{c}(1+(1-\varepsilon)\alpha)^{i}\geq\hat{c}e^{(1-2\varepsilon)\alpha i}

provided
\varepsilon
(and thus also
\alpha
) is sufficiently small.
In particular, using the definition of
\alpha
and
L
, we obtain
Q_{L,j}\geq\hat{c}{\mathrm{e}}^{(1-2\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon)t}.

As in the previous proof, we observe that the event in (
3.26
) already implies
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, and our strategy again focusses on bounding
\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{E}_{i+1}^{c}\,\right|\mathcal{E}_{i}\right)
.
To do so, fix some
j\in[r]
and observe that, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{i}
, there are at least
Q_{i,j-1}t^{4}
vertices born by time
s_{i}^{\prime}
on levels congruent to

modulo
r
.
Thus, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{i}
, the number of children of such vertices throughout the interval
[s^{\prime}_{i},s^{\prime}_{i+1}]
is stochastically dominates a Poisson random variable
\mathcal{O}_{i,j-1}
with mean
\alpha f(j-1)Q_{i,j-1}t^{4}
.
Observing that
\widehat{N}_{j}(s^{\prime}_{i+1})\geq\widehat{N}_{j}(s^{\prime}_{i})+\mathcal{O}_{i,j-1}
and using (
3.27
) and Chernoff’s bound (
Lemma

2.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\widehat{N}_{j}(s^{\prime}_{i+1})<Q_{i+1,j}t^{4}\,\right|\mathcal{E}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\widehat{N}_{j}(s^{\prime}_{i})+\mathcal{O}_{i,j-1}<Q_{i,j}t^{4}+(1-\varepsilon)\alpha f(j-1)Q_{i,j-1}t^{4}\,\right|\mathcal{E}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-50.00008pt\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{O}_{i,j-1}<(1-\varepsilon)\alpha f(j-1)Q_{i,j-1}t^{4}\,\right|\mathcal{E}_{i}\right)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-\varepsilon^{2}\alpha f(j-1)Q_{i,j-1}t^{4}/8}\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-\varepsilon^{5}t^{4}}.

It follows as before that

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}^{c}_{L}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}^{c}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{j\in[r]}\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{E}_{i,j}^{c}\,\right|\mathcal{E}_{i-1}\right)\leq(r+8){\mathrm{e}}^{-c_{1}\varepsilon t}+rL{\mathrm{e}}^{-\varepsilon^{5}t^{4}}.

From our observation (
3.28
) applied to

, we observe that

implies

provided

is sufficiently large. This implies that, for every

small,

which is enough to conclude the proof of the lower bound as in the proof of

3.6
.
∎

, it suffices to show that

(3.29)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(T_{n})}{\log n}\leq{\mathrm{e}}

almost surely. As in the case of bounded weight functions, our strategy consists in controlling the growth of
\tau_{1,k}
and
\tau_{n}
, from which we draw the desired conclusion.

Lemma 3.8
.

For every weight function
f
satisfying (
WSV
), almost surely

(3.30)

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{1,k}}{k/f(k)}\geq\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
t_{k}\coloneq(1-\varepsilon)k/({\mathrm{e}}f(k))
.
Using the monotonicity of
f
and (
3.4
), we obtain

(3.31)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tau_{1,k}\leq t_{k}\right)\leq\Big(\frac{{\mathrm{e}}t_{k}\ell_{k}}{k}\Big)^{k}\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{k}.

Hence, by Borel-Cantelli’s lemma, almost surely
\liminf_{k\to\infty}\tau_{1,k}/(k/f(k))\geq(1-\varepsilon)/{\mathrm{e}}
. As the latter holds for every
\varepsilon>0
, the desired conclusion follows.
∎

Next, we state a one-sided analogue of
Propositions

3.6
and
3.7
regarding the growth rate of
\tau_{n}
for slowly growing weight functions.
For the purposes of the following proposition, we extend
f
to the interval
[0,\infty)
by interpolating linearly between consecutive integers.
We slightly abuse notation and keep denoting the extension
f
.

Proposition 3.9
.

For every weight function
f
satisfying (
WSV
),

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{n}f(\tau_{n})}{\log n}\leq 1.

We postpone the proof of
Proposition

3.9
and first derive the upper bound in Theorem
1.6
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
assuming
Proposition

3.9
.

The lower bound of Theorem
1.6
already follows from Remark
1.7
.
We focus on the upper bound. Fix
\varepsilon>0
small. By (
WSV
), for all sufficiently large
n
, we have

\frac{1+\varepsilon/2}{1+\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{f(\log n)}f\bigg(\frac{\log n}{f(\log n)}\bigg).

By rearranging this inequality and using the monotonicity of
f
, we obtain

(1+\varepsilon/2)\log n\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{f(\log n)}f\bigg(\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{f(\log n)}\bigg).

Now, from
Proposition

3.9
we also know that, for all
n
large,
\tau_{n}f(\tau_{n})\leq(1+\varepsilon/2)\log n
, and thus

\tau_{n}f(\tau_{n})\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{f(\log n)}f\bigg(\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{f(\log n)}\bigg).

Using that the function
xf(x)
is strictly increasing, we conclude that, for all such
n
, we have

\tau_{n}\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{f(\log n)}.

On the other hand, taking
k=(1+3\varepsilon){\mathrm{e}}\log n
in
Lemma

3.8
gives

\tau_{1,(1+3\varepsilon){\mathrm{e}}\log n}\geq\frac{1+2\varepsilon}{1+3\varepsilon}\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}}\frac{(1+3\varepsilon){\mathrm{e}}\log n}{f((1+3\varepsilon){\mathrm{e}}\log n)}=\frac{(1+2\varepsilon)\log n}{f((1+3\varepsilon){\mathrm{e}}\log n)}.

As

satisfies (
WSV
),
f(\log n)/f((1+3\varepsilon){\mathrm{e}}\log n)\to 1
. Thus, almost surely, for all large

,

\tau_{n}\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\log n}{f(\log n)}\leq\frac{(1+2\varepsilon)\log n}{f((1+3\varepsilon){\mathrm{e}}\log n)}\leq\tau_{1,(1+3\varepsilon){\mathrm{e}}\log n}.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrarily small and
\mathcal{T}(\tau_{n})
and

have the same distribution by (

), the latter display proves the upper bound in
Theorem

1.6
.
∎

We conclude the section by proving
Proposition

3.9
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.9
.

As before, we extend the function
f
to
[0,\infty)
by linear interpolation between consecutive integers but keep denoting the extension
f
. Instead of proving bounds on
\tau_{n}
, it will be enough to show that almost surely

(3.32)

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log(N(t))}{tf(t)}\geq 1.

Indeed, as
N(\tau_{n})=n
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, (
3.32
) implies the desired result.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
small and
t
large so that
\varepsilon t
is also large. Set also
K=K(t)\coloneq\varepsilon t/4
.
We assume without loss of generality that
f(0)=1
so that
f(k)\geq 1
for all
k\geq 0
(recall that
f
is increasing).
We begin by showing that with high probability the tree attains depth
K
quickly.
To do so, we observe that, for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau_{1,j}-\tau_{1,j-1}
is stochastically dominated by an exponential random variable
E_{j}
with rate
f(j-1)\geq 1
, which is the time it takes for the first vertex on depth
j-1
to produce its first child.
Even further, the variables
\{E_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
are independent, so each
\tau_{j}
stochastically dominates the sum of
j
independent exponential variables with rate
1
. Taking
j=K
and using Bernstein’s inequality (
Lemma

2.3
) gives

(3.33)

\mathbb{P}(N_{K}(2K)=0)=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1,K}>2K)\leq\mathbb{P}(|\tau_{1,K}-K|>K)\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\min\bigg\{\frac{K^{2}}{c_{1}K},\frac{K}{c_{2}}\bigg\}\bigg).

Next, denote by
v_{K}
the vertex born on depth
k
at time
\tau_{1,K}
.
From this time onwards, we focus on the subtree rooted at
v_{K}
.
Recalling that
\min_{i\geq K}f(i)=f(K)
by monotonicity of
f
, we can (and do) couple this tree with the branching process
\overline{\mathcal{T}}(\cdot)
given by the DWT with constant weight function equal to

so that
\overline{\mathcal{T}}(s)\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\tau_{1,K}+s)
for all

, with
\mathcal{T}
being the original DWT.
Call
Y(s)=|\overline{\mathcal{T}}(s)|
and observe that, since the weight function of
\overline{\mathcal{T}}
is constant,
Y(s)
is a Yule process with rate
f(K)
.
By
[
16
, (4.3.4)]
and simple rescaling,

is a geometric random variable with parameter
p=p(s)\coloneq\exp(-sf(K))
.
Therefore, setting
s=t-2K=(1-\varepsilon/2)t
and using (
WSV
), we obtain that

\mathbb{P}(Y(s)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{(1-\varepsilon)sf(K)})=1-(1-p)^{\exp((1-\varepsilon)sf(K))}\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-\varepsilon sf(K)}\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{-\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon/2)tf(t)/2}.

By combining the latter with (

) and (
3.33
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N(t)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{(1-3\varepsilon)tf(t)})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(N(t)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{(1-2\varepsilon)tf(K)})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(N_{K}(2K)=0)+\mathbb{P}(\{N(t)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{(1-\varepsilon)sf(K)}\}\cap\{N_{K}(2K)\geq 1\})

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathrm{e}}^{-K/\max\{c_{1},c_{2}\}}+\mathbb{P}(Y(s)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{(1-\varepsilon)sf(K)})\leq 3{\mathrm{e}}^{-K/\max\{c_{1},c_{2}\}},

where the third inequality uses that
N_{K}(2K)\geq 1
implies
\tau_{1,K}<2K
and hence our coupling gives

, and the last inequality uses that

is large.
Combined with the monotonicity of each of
N(\cdot)
and
x\mapsto xf(x)
and Borel-Cantelli’s lemma for

, this is enough to conclude the proof of (
3.32
) as in the proof of
Proposition

3.6
.
∎

4.
Exponential weight functions

We turn to Theorem
1.8
.
First, we prove part (ii) for subexponential functions.

Proof of
Theorem

1.8
(ii).

Recall the discrete model from Definition
1.1
and define the function

g:n\in\mathbb{N}\mapsto\frac{f(n)}{\sum_{i=0}^{n}f(i)}.

Then, our assumption on the weight function implies that
g(n)=o(1)
.
Set
\zeta_{0}=0
and, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, denote by
\zeta_{k}
the smallest
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
T_{n}
has depth
k
; observe that
\zeta_{k}
is a discrete analogue of
\tau_{1,k}
from previous sections.
Recalling the convention that irrelevant rounding is ignored, our task comes down to bounding from above the probability of the event
\zeta_{1,\varepsilon n}\leq n
.

To begin with, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the probability that the vertex born at step
\zeta_{1,k}+i
has depth
k+1
is bounded from above by
p_{k,i}\coloneq\min(ig(k),1)
.
Indeed, at this step, there is at least one vertex on each of the depths
0,1,\ldots,k
and at most
i
vertices on depth
k
.
For all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
, define the family of independent Bernoulli random variables
(I_{k,i})
with respective parameters
(p_{k,i})
.
Then, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\zeta_{k+1}-\zeta_{k}
stochastically dominates
\min\{i:I_{k,i}=1\}
.
Let
\mathcal{I}=\{\varepsilon n/2,\ldots,\varepsilon n-1\}
and
\mathcal{S}
be the family of subsets of
\mathcal{I}
of size
\varepsilon n/4
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\varepsilon n}\leq n)\leq\mathbb{P}(\exists S\in\mathcal{S}:\forall k\in S,\,\zeta_{k+1}-\zeta_{k}\leq 5/\varepsilon)\leq\binom{\varepsilon n/2}{\varepsilon n/4}\max_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\bigg\{\prod_{k\in S}\mathbb{P}(\exists i\leq 5/\varepsilon:I_{k,i}=1)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\varepsilon n/2}\bigg(\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{5/\varepsilon}p_{k,i}\bigg\}\bigg)^{\varepsilon n/4}\leq 2^{\varepsilon n/2}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{5/\varepsilon}i\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}}g(k)\bigg)^{\varepsilon n/4}\leq\bigg(\frac{100}{\varepsilon^{2}}\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}}g(k)\bigg)^{\varepsilon n/4}={\mathrm{e}}^{-\omega(n)},

where the last inequality used that
\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}}g(k)=o(1)
.
∎

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.8
(i) for
c>1
.
Even though the results presented here apply to all functions satisfying assumption (
EG
), the reader might benefit from keeping in mind the case of pure exponential functions
k\mapsto c^{k}
for some
c>1
, for which some of the definitions and proofs become cleaner.

The proof consists of several steps. The first one is Proposition
4.3
below, which is arguably the most technical component.
Before stating the proposition formally, we first give an intuitive explanation of its meaning.
Imagine that a large mass of vertices in the tree
T_{n}
accumulates at a certain depth
k
away from the deepest vertices.
Then, this accumulation is likely to result in a rapid gain of mass on depths
k+1,k+2,\ldots
.
This ‘wave’ of vertices eventually reaches and overtakes the vertices at maximal depth and significantly delays the growth of
d(T_{n})
.
Our analysis of exponentially growing functions shows that the above phenomenon is atypical to happen on a large scale: accumulations of mass are rare and well-spaced, which results in a regularly paced growth of the height of the tree.
To properly state this intuition, define the function

(4.1)

\displaystyle\Psi:k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\mapsto\min\left\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}f(k+j-1)}{(2f(k-1))^{\ell}(\ell+1)!}\geq 8\right\},

with the convention
\Psi(k)=\infty
if there is no such
\ell
. This function controls the accumulation on depth
k
: in fact,
k+\Psi(k)
can be interpreted as a depth which, once reached, the vertices on depth
k
lose their impact with probability close to 1.
Note that, for the particular choice of
f(k)=c^{k}
,

\Psi(k)=\min\left\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{c^{\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}}}{2^{\ell}(\ell+1)!}\geq 8\right\}.

While generally
\Psi
depends on
k
, we show
\Psi
is always bounded thanks to assumption (
EG
).

Lemma 4.1
.

For every weight function
f
satisfying assumption (
EG
),
\Psi
is a bounded function.

Proof.

Denote by
k_{0}
the smallest integer such that, for every
k\geq k_{0}
, each of
f(k)\geq\tfrac{c+1}{2}f(k-1)
and
f(k-1)\geq\max\{f(i):i\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}\}
holds.
Then, for every
k\geq k_{0}
, we have that
\Psi(k)
is bounded from above by the minimum integer
\ell
such that

\bigg(\frac{c+1}{2}\bigg)^{\ell(\ell+1)/2}\geq 8\cdot 2^{\ell}(\ell+1)!,

where we used that
n!=2^{o(n^{2})}
.
For
k\in[k_{0}-1]
, by distinguishing the cases
k+j-1<k_{0}
and
k+j-1\geq k_{0}
, the inequality in (
4.1
) is satisfied when

\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k_{0}-k}\frac{f(k+j-1)}{f(k-1)}\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\frac{f(k_{0}-1)}{f(k-1)}\bigg)^{\ell-k_{0}+k}\bigg(\prod_{j=k_{0}-k+1}^{\ell}\frac{f(k+j-1)}{f(k_{0}-1)}\bigg)\geq 8\cdot 2^{\ell}(\ell+1)!.

Since the second term above is at least 1 and the third term dominates
c^{(\ell-k_{0}+k)(\ell-k_{0}+k+1)/2}
, the last inequality holds for every large enough
\ell
. Thus,
\Psi(k)<\infty
for all
k<k_{0}
, as desired.
∎

Next, we define a family of important events appearing in Proposition
4.3
.
To this end, we work in the continuous-time setting introduced in Section
2.2.2
.
Recall that
N_{k}(t)
stands for the number of vertices on depth
k
at time
t
,
\tau_{s,k}
stands for the first time at which there are
s
vertices on depth
k
, and
\tau_{n}
stands for the first time when the tree attains
n
vertices.

Definition 4.2
.

For integers
r,s,n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the events

\mathcal{E}_{s,r,n}\coloneq\{N_{r-1}(\tau_{n})\leq s\}\cap\{N_{r}(\tau_{n})\geq s\}\cap\bigg(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\Psi(r)}\{N_{r+i}(\tau_{n})\leq s\}\bigg)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}_{s,r}\coloneq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_{s,r,n}.

In words, the event
\mathcal{E}_{s,r,n}
states that depth
r
contains at least
s
vertices of the tree
T_{n}
while each of the depths
r-1,r+1,r+2,\ldots,r+\Psi(r)
contains at most
s
vertices, see
Figure

6
.
Next, we show that the union
\mathcal{E}_{s,r}
is a rather improbable event when
s
is large: the reason is that a large mass of vertices on depth
r
typically results in a faster growth on depths
r+1,r+2,\ldots
whilst the upper bound on depth
r-1
limits the growth on depth
r
itself.
The following proposition formalises this intuition by providing an upper bound on
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{s,r})
which is uniform over
r
and decreases exponentially with
s
.

Figure 6
.
An illustration of the event
\mathcal{E}_{s,r,n}
.
In blue, the load of vertices on depth
r
, which is above the threshold
s
. In green, the previous depth and the subsequent
\Psi(r)
depths remaining below the threshold
s
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Fix a weight function
f
satisfying assumption (
EG
). Then, there is
\delta=\delta(f)>0
such that, for all
r,s\in\mathbb{N}
with
s\geq 2
,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{s,r})\leq 2(\max\Psi+2){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}\leq 6(\max\Psi){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}.

Proof.

Recall the stopping times
\tau_{j,\ell}
from (
2.6
),
\tau_{n}
from (
2.8
), and
\tau_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{n}
from (
2.9
).
For every
r\geq 1
and
s\geq 2
, define the event

\mathcal{G}_{s,r}\coloneq\{\tau_{s,r}<\min\{\tau_{s+1,r-1},\tau_{s+1,r+1},\ldots,\tau_{s+1,r+\Psi(r)}\}\}.

Note that
\mathcal{E}_{s,r}=\mathcal{G}_{s,r}\cap\{\tau_{s,r}<\tau_{\infty}\}
, as it could happen that depth
r
never reaches
s
vertices before the completion of the DWT. Thus, it is enough to provide an upper bound for
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{s,r})
.

For the purpose of the analysis, we introduce an auxiliary process
(M_{\ell}(\cdot))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
defined in the same way as
(N_{\ell}(\cdot))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
in Section
2.2.2
,
except for the fact that
M_{r-1}(\cdot)
is stopped once it reaches size
s
, that is, it satisfies
M_{r-1}(\cdot)=\min\{N_{r-1}(\cdot),s\}
.
Observe that the restriction on
M_{r-1}
influences the distribution of all subsequent depths. This auxiliary process is interpreted similarly to
(N_{\ell}(\cdot))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
but the uniformly bounded load on depth
r-1
is convenient for consequent computations.
Note that, by restricting the number of vertices on depth
r-1
, the probability of the event
\mathcal{G}_{s,r}
(with the stopping times
\tau_{j,\ell}
redefined for the process
(M_{\ell}(\cdot))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
) can only increase since we exclude the possibility of having
\tau_{s+1,r-1}\leq\tau_{s,r}
.
From now on, we work with
(M_{\ell}(\cdot))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
instead of
(N_{\ell}(\cdot))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, that is, we adapt the definition of the events
\mathcal{G}_{s,r}
, the directing measures
(\Lambda_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
from
Section

2.2.2
and the random variables
\tau_{j,\ell}
and
\tau_{j}
to the process
(M_{\ell}(\cdot))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
For every
r\in\mathbb{N}
, define
\mathcal{F}_{r-2}
as the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
(M_{\ell}(\cdot))_{\ell=0}^{r-2}
. (Observe that
\mathcal{F}_{-1}
is defined as the trivial
\sigma
-algebra.)
We show the more general result that, for some
\delta=\delta(f,\max\Psi)>0
and every
r,s\in\mathbb{N}
with
s\geq 2
, almost surely

(4.2)

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{G}_{s,r}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{r-2}\right)\leq 2(\max\Psi+2){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}.

To begin with, we note that the distribution of the processes
(M_{\ell}(\cdot))_{\ell\in[r,\infty)}
is independent of
(M_{\ell}(\cdot))_{\ell\in[0,r-2]}
conditionally on a realisation of
(M_{r-1}(t))_{t\geq 0}
.
In particular,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{G}_{s,r}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{r-2}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{G}_{s,r}\,\right|(M_{r-1}(t))_{t\geq 0}\right)\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{r-2}\right].

Thus, our goal is to obtain a suitable upper bound (which will turn out to be uniform in
r
) on the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{G}_{s,r}\,\right|(M_{r-1}(t))_{t\geq 0}\right)
. A heuristic version of our argument is as follows: given any trajectory of
M_{r-1}
(outside of a null probability set), the trajectories of the stochastic processes
M_{r}(\cdot),\,M_{r+1}(\cdot),\ldots,\,M_{r+\Psi(r)}(\cdot)
are typically concentrated around some deterministic functions
F_{r}(\cdot)
,
F_{r+1}(\cdot),\ldots,F_{r+\Psi(r)}(\cdot)
which are constructed recursively from
M_{r-1}(\cdot)
. Using
F_{r}
(which approximates
M_{r}
), we can identify the time
T
when
F_{r}
is close to the target value
s
.

To complete the proof, it suffices to show that at least one of the functions
F_{r+1},\ldots,F_{r+\Psi(r)}
attains a value significantly larger than
s
before time
T
.
In this case, the event
\mathcal{G}_{s,r}
can only occur if at least one of the stochastic processes deviates substantially from its deterministic counterpart, an event which will turn out to have an exponentially small probability.
Note that the exponential growth of
f
in the interval
[r-1,r+\Psi(r)]
is essential to prove that this strategy works irrespectively of the chosen trajectory of
M_{r-1}(\cdot)
.

As a first step, we address the trajectory of
M_{r}
. Fix a realisation of
(M_{r-1}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and call it
F_{r-1}(t)
.
Observe that, by the construction of the process, all of the
M_{r}(\cdot),\,M_{r+1}(\cdot),\ldots,\,M_{\Psi(r)}(\cdot)
must be equal to 0 before time
\tau_{1,r-1}
when generation
r-1
receives its first vertex.
To simplify subsequent computations, we (somewhat abusively) identify
\tau_{1,r-1}
with 0 so that
F_{r-1}(0)=1
. By construction,

(4.3)

\Lambda_{r}[0,t]\coloneq\int_{0}^{t}f(r-1)F_{r-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u

is deterministic and therefore
M_{r}
is a Poisson process whose expected trajectory follows
\Lambda_{r}[0,\cdot]
.
To unify notation, we call
F_{r}(t)=\Lambda_{r}[0,t]
: this is a continuous and increasing function since
F_{r-1}
is the trajectory of a positive jump process.
Keeping in mind that our aim is to control the probability of
\mathcal{G}_{s,r}
, we define
T=T(F_{r-1},\varepsilon)
as the unique value such that

(4.4)

F_{r}(T)=(1-2\varepsilon)s,

for some
\varepsilon\in(0,0.1)
depending on
f
to be fixed later.
The key idea behind this choice is that
[0,T]
is not long enough for
M_{r}
to reach the value
s
, whereas at least one of the subsequent depths contains at least
s
vertices before time
T
.
To show that the process
M_{r}
stays close to
F_{r}
, we make use of Theorem
2.4
with
M_{r}
and
\mathrm{d}F_{r}
taking the role of the Poisson process and its directing measure, respectively, and with
x=\varepsilon s
, thus obtaining

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{u\in[0,T]}\left|M_{r}(u)-F_{r}(u)\right|\geq\varepsilon s\,\bigg|\,M_{r-1}=F_{r-1}\bigg)\,\leq\,2\exp\bigg(-F_{r}(T)\cdot I\bigg(\frac{\varepsilon s}{F_{r}(T)}\bigg)\bigg),

where
\varepsilon
is taken the same as in the definition of
T
and
I(z)=(1+z)\log(1+z)-z
.
Using the definition of
T
in (
4.4
), we thus arrive at

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{u\in[0,T]}\left|M_{r}(u)-F_{r}(u)\right|\geq\varepsilon s\,\bigg|\,M_{r-1}=F_{r-1}\bigg)\,\leq\,2{\mathrm{e}}^{-I_{1}s},

where
I_{1}\coloneq(1-2\varepsilon)I(\varepsilon/(1-2\varepsilon))
can be checked to be bounded from below by
\delta\coloneq\varepsilon^{2}/8
from our choice of
\varepsilon\in(0,0.1)
.
Now, we define the event
\mathcal{B}_{r}
that
M_{r}
concentrates around the deterministic trajectory
F_{r}
, that is,

\mathcal{B}_{r}\coloneq\left\{\sup_{u\in[0,T]}\left|M_{r}(u)-F_{r}(u)\right|\leq\varepsilon s\right\}.

Then, on
\mathcal{B}_{r}
, we necessarily have
M_{r}(T)\leq F_{r}(T)+\varepsilon s\leq(1-\varepsilon)s
, which means that by time
T
the tree has not yet produced
s
vertices on depth
r
.
We can therefore bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{s,r})
by the probability of the slightly more general event that none of the subsequent depths has reached
s+1
vertices by time
T
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{G}_{s,r}\,\right|M_{r-1}=F_{r-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{G}_{s,r}\cap\mathcal{B}_{r}\,\right|M_{r-1}=F_{r-1}\right)+2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\{T<\min\{\tau_{s+1,r+1},\ldots,\tau_{s+1,r+\Psi(r)}\}\}\cap\mathcal{B}_{r}\,\right|M_{r-1}=F_{r-1}\right)+2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}.

Now, define the event
\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\coloneq\{T<\min\{\tau_{s+1,r+1},\ldots,\tau_{s+1,r+\Psi(r)}\}\}
and observe that once
M_{r-1}
and
\varepsilon
(and thus
T
) are fixed,

\mathcal{C}_{r+1}
depends solely on the trajectories of
M_{r+1},M_{r+2},\ldots,M_{r+\Psi(r)}
on
[0,T]
, which can be constructed recursively if
\Lambda_{r+1}
is given on this interval.
Moreover, an immediate coupling argument shows that
\mathcal{C}_{r+1}
is monotone on
\Lambda_{r+1}
, meaning that for every pair of increasing non-negative functions
G_{1},G_{2}:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
satisfying
G_{1}\leq G_{2}
on
[0,T]
, we have

(4.5)

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=G_{2}(\cdot)\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=G_{1}(\cdot)\right).

Indeed, if
G_{1}\leq G_{2}
, then the trajectory of
M_{r+1}
constructed using
\mathrm{d}G_{1}
as directing measure is stochastically bounded from above by the trajectory of the same process using
\mathrm{d}G_{2}
.
Since the directing measure of
M_{r+i+1}
is given by an integral over
M_{r+i}
for every
i\geq 1
, repeating the argument allows to show that the collection of trajectories
M_{r+1},\ldots,M_{r+\Psi(r)}
constructed based on
\mathrm{d}G_{1}
is stochastically bounded from above by the same collection using
\mathrm{d}G_{2}
, and thus the event
\mathcal{C}_{r+1}
is less likely for the latter.

Recall that
\Lambda_{r+1}
is constructed from
M_{r}
as

\Lambda_{r+1}[0,t]=\int_{0}^{t}f(r)M_{r}(u)\mathrm{d}u,

where
M_{r}
is random.
Nevertheless, conditionally on
\mathcal{B}_{r}
, the trajectory of
M_{r}
is at distance at most
\varepsilon s
from
F_{r}
, and hence

\Lambda_{r+1}[0,t]\geq F_{r+1}(t)\coloneq f(r)\int_{0}^{t}\max\{F_{r}(u)-\varepsilon s,0\}\mathrm{d}u

for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Using the stochastic domination stated in (
4.5
) with
G_{2}=\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]
and
G_{1}=F_{r+1}
, and the independence between
M_{r+1}
and
M_{r-1}
conditionally on
\Lambda_{r+1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{s,r}\mid M_{r-1}=F_{r-1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{r}\,\right|M_{r-1}=F_{r-1}\right)+2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+1}(\cdot)\right)+2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}.

To bound the probability term on the right-hand side of (
4.6
), we consider two possible scenarios for
F_{r+1}(T)
.

Case 1:
F_{r+1}(T)\geq 2s
.

In this case, since the trajectory of
M_{r+1}
typically dominates
F_{r+1}
over the entire interval
[0,T]
,
M_{r+1}
reaches values significantly larger than
s
before time
T
with high probability.
To formalise this statement, we use Theorem
2.4
again to obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\sup_{u\in[0,T]}\left|M_{r+1}(u)-F_{r+1}(u)\right|\geq\varepsilon F_{r+1}(T)\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+1}(\cdot)\right)\,\leq\,2{\mathrm{e}}^{-F_{r+1}(T)I(\varepsilon)}\leq 2{\mathrm{e}}^{-I_{2}s},

where
I_{2}\coloneq 2I(\varepsilon)
can be checked to be bounded from below by
\delta=\varepsilon^{2}/8
(again from our choice
\varepsilon\in(0,0.1)
). Notice however that
\mathcal{C}_{r+1}
must be contained in the event inside the probability above, since if both
\sup_{u\in[0,T]}|M_{r+1}(u)-F_{r+1}(u)|\leq\varepsilon F_{r+1}(T)
and
F_{r+1}(T)\geq 2s
hold, then for every
s\geq 2
,

M_{r+1}(T)\geq(1-\varepsilon)F_{r+1}(T)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot 2s>s+1.

It follows that if
F_{r+1}(T)\geq 2s
holds, then

(4.7)

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+1}(\cdot)\right)\leq 2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}.

Case 2:
F_{r+1}(T)<2s
.
In this case, the deterministic trajectory
F_{r+1}
does not reach values large enough to infer that
M_{r+1}
reaches
s
vertices before time
T
.
However, concentration of
M_{r+1}
around
F_{r+1}
still holds with high probability, which we use to derive an upper bound for
\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+1}(\cdot)\right)
which is identical in structure but only takes into account the trajectories of
M_{r+2},\ldots,M_{r+\Psi(r)}
.
Analogously to
\mathcal{B}_{r}
, we define

\mathcal{B}_{r+1}\coloneq\left\{\sup_{u\in[0,T]}\left|M_{r+1}(u)-F_{r+1}(u)\right|\leq\varepsilon s\right\}

and use Theorem
2.4
again to obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{B}_{r+1}^{c}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+1}(\cdot)\right)\,\leq\,2{\mathrm{e}}^{-F_{r+1}(T)I(\varepsilon s/F_{r+1}(T))}.

To analyse the bound obtained on the right-hand side, we observe that, for every
\alpha>0
, the function
x\in(0,\infty)\mapsto xI(\alpha/x)
has derivative
\log(1+\alpha/x)-\alpha/x<0
, so it is decreasing. In our case
\alpha=\varepsilon s
and
x=F_{r+1}(T)<2s
. Using monotonicity to replace
F_{r+1}(T)
by
2s
in the exponent, we arrive at

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{B}_{r+1}^{c}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+1}(\cdot)\right)\,\leq\,2{\mathrm{e}}^{-I_{3}s}

where
I_{3}=2I(\varepsilon/2)
is bounded from below by
\delta=\varepsilon^{2}/8
from our assumptions on
\varepsilon
. It follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+1}(\cdot)\right)\,\leq\,\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{r+1}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+1}(\cdot)\right)+2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}.

As for
\mathcal{B}_{r}
, on the event
\mathcal{B}_{r+1}
, the trajectory of
M_{r+1}
is within distance
\varepsilon s
from
F_{r+1}
, and hence

\Lambda_{r+2}[0,t]\geq F_{r+2}(t)\coloneq f(r+1)\int_{0}^{t}\max\{F_{r+1}(u)-\varepsilon s,0\}\mathrm{d}u,

for all
t\in[0,T]
. We can thus repeat the argument used to derive (
4.6
) and obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+1}(\cdot)\right)\,\leq\,\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+2}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+2}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+2}(\cdot)\right)+2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s},

where
\mathcal{C}_{r+2}
is defined analogously to
\mathcal{C}_{r+1}
as
\mathcal{C}_{r+2}\coloneq\{T<\min\{\tau_{s+1,r+2},\ldots,\tau_{s+1,r+\Psi(r)}\}\}
.

Thus far, we know
\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+1}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+1}(\cdot)\right)
is bounded either by
2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}
, if
F_{r+1}(T)\geq 2s
, or by

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+2}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+2}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+2}(\cdot)\right)+2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s},

if
F_{r+1}(T)<2s
.
For every
i\in[\Psi(r)]
, defining
\mathcal{C}_{r+i}\coloneq\{T<\min\{\tau_{s+1,r+i},\ldots,\tau_{s+1,r+\Psi(r)}\}\}
and

(4.8)

F_{r+i}(t)\coloneq f(r+i-1)\int_{0}^{t}\max\{F_{r+i-1}(u)-\varepsilon s,0\}\mathrm{d}u\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,T]

allows us to iterate the argument to show that, for any
i<\Psi(r)
,
\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+i}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+i}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+i}(\cdot)\right)
is bounded either by
2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}
, if
F_{r+i}(T)\geq 2s
, or by

\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+i+1}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+i+1}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+i+1}(\cdot)\right)+2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}

if
F_{r+i}(T)<2s
.
Iterating this reasoning at most
\Psi(r)
times and using (
4.6
), we deduce the bound

(4.9)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{s,r}\mid M_{r-1}=F_{r-1})\leq 2(\Psi(r)+1){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}+\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\mathcal{C}_{r+\Psi(r)}\,\right|\Lambda_{r+\Psi(r)}[0,\cdot]=F_{r+\Psi(r)}(\cdot)\right).

This bound almost matches the one in the statement, except for the probability term on the right.
Notice, however, that from our previous analysis this term would be bounded by
2{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}
as soon as
F_{r+\Psi(r)}(T)\geq 2s
, thus giving the result.
The rest of the proof consists in showing that this is indeed the case.

In fact, we will prove something stronger: namely, that we can find
\varepsilon>0
small, depending on
f
, such that, for all
r
, any choice of
F_{r-1}
, and any
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,\Psi(r)\}
, we have

(4.10)

F_{r+\ell}(T)\geq\frac{s}{2}\bigg(\prod_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{f(r+j-1)}{f(r-1)}\bigg)\frac{(1-2\varepsilon)^{\ell+1}}{(\ell+1)!}.

If this inequality holds, then the proof of the proposition follows since, in particular,

F_{r+\Psi(r)}(T)\geq\frac{s}{2}\bigg(\prod_{j=0}^{\Psi(r)}\frac{f(r+j-1)}{f(r-1)}\bigg)\frac{(1-2\varepsilon)^{\Psi(r)+1}}{(\Psi(r)+1)!}\geq s2^{\Psi(r)+2}(1-2\varepsilon)^{\Psi(r)+1}\geq 2s,

where for the second inequality we used the definition of
\Psi(r)
, and for the last one we used the fact that
2-4\varepsilon>1
.

To prove (
4.10
), we define
T_{0}=T-\frac{1-2\varepsilon}{f(r-1)}
and the function
L_{r}(t)
on
[0,T]
as

L_{r}(t)=\begin{cases}0,&\mbox{ if }t\in[0,T_{0}],\\[2.0pt]
sf(r-1)\cdot(t-T_{0}),&\mbox{ if }t\in(T_{0},T].\end{cases}

We argue that
L_{r}(t)\leq F_{r}(t)
on
[0,T]
. This statement is trivial on the interval
[0,T_{0}]
whereas on the interval
(T_{0},T]
,
L_{r}
is a straight line whose slope
f(r-1)s
dominates the derivative of
F_{r}
(recall its definition (
4.3
) and that by construction of
M(\cdot)
, the process
F_{r-1}
is at most
f(r-1)s
) and
L_{r}(T)=F_{r}(T)=(1-2\varepsilon)s
, see Figure
7
. Since
L_{r}(T)=F_{r}(T)=(1-2\varepsilon)s
, it must be the case that
L_{r}\leq F_{r}
also on the interval
(T_{0},T]
.

Figure 7
.
A plot of the functions
F_{r}(t)
(solid red), and
L_{r}(t)
(dashed green). The blue circles represent the times at which
F_{r-1}
jumps, and hence
F_{r}
increases its slope by
f(r-1)
, attaining a maximum slope of
f(r-1)s
.
L_{r}
, on the other hand, is constructed with one line segment with slope
0
, and another with slope
f(r-1)s
.

Given
L_{r}
, for each
\ell\in[\Psi(r)]
, we recursively define a function
L_{r+\ell}
on
[0,T]
by setting

L_{r+\ell}(t)=\begin{cases}0,&\mbox{ if }t\in[0,T_{0}],\\[4.0pt]
f(r+\ell-1)\int_{T_{0}}^{t}(L_{r+\ell-1}(u)-\varepsilon s)\mathrm{d}u,&\mbox{ if }t\in(T_{0},T].\end{cases}

The purpose of
L_{r+\ell}
is to serve as a simple and convenient lower bound for
F_{r+\ell}
: indeed, from the inequality
L_{r}\leq F_{r}
and the definition (
4.8
), it can be checked by immediate induction that, for each
\ell
, we have
L_{r+\ell}\leq F_{r+\ell}
on
[0,T]
.
Hence, it is enough to prove that
L_{r+\Psi(r)}(T)\geq 2s
. To do so, observe that, for every
t\in(T_{0},T]
and
\ell\in[\Psi(r)]
, we can explicitly compute

L_{r+\ell}(t)\;=\;s\bigg(\prod_{j=0}^{\ell}f(r+j-1)\bigg)\frac{(t-T_{0})^{\ell+1}}{(\ell+1)!}-\varepsilon s\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bigg(\prod_{j=i}^{\ell}f(r+j-1)\bigg)\frac{(t-T_{0})^{\ell-i+1}}{(\ell-i+1)!}.

Taking
t=T
, and using the definition of
T_{0}
we arrive at

L_{r+\ell}(T)\;=\;s\bigg(\prod_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{f(r+j-1)}{f(r-1)}\bigg)\frac{(1-2\varepsilon)^{\ell+1}}{(\ell+1)!}-\varepsilon s\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bigg(\prod_{j=i}^{\ell}\frac{f(r+j-1)}{f(r-1)}\bigg)\frac{(1-2\varepsilon)^{\ell-i+1}}{(\ell-i+1)!}.

Moreover, the second term above is at most

\varepsilon s\max_{i\in[\ell]}\bigg\{\prod_{j=i}^{\ell}\frac{f(r+j-1)}{f(r-1)}\bigg\}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{(1-2\varepsilon)^{\ell-i+1}}{(\ell-i+1)!}\leq 3\varepsilon s\max_{i\in[\ell]}\bigg\{\prod_{j=i}^{\ell}\frac{f(r+j-1)}{f(r-1)}\bigg\}.

Hence, (
4.10
) follows if we show that there exists
\varepsilon
small such that, for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\ell\in[\Psi(r)]
,

(4.11)

3\varepsilon\max_{i\in[\ell]}\bigg\{\prod_{j=i}^{\ell}\frac{f(r+j-1)}{f(r-1)}\bigg\}\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\prod_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{f(r+j-1)}{f(r-1)}\bigg)\frac{(1-2\varepsilon)^{\ell+1}}{(\ell+1)!}.

To prove (
4.11
), we observe that, from assumption (
EG
), there are constants
m
and
M
(depending on
f
) such that, for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
m\leq f(r)/f(r-1)\leq M
, and hence

m^{j}\leq\frac{f(r+j-1)}{f(r-1)}\leq M^{j}.

It follows that the left-hand side of (
4.11
) is bounded from above by
3\varepsilon M^{\ell(\ell+1)/2}
, while the right-hand side is bounded from below by
\frac{m^{\ell(\ell+1)/2}}{2^{\ell+2}(\ell+1)!}
. Noticing that
\ell\leq\Psi(r)\leq\max\Psi
, we can find
\varepsilon
small enough (depending on
m
,
M
, and
\max\Psi
, all of which depend solely on
f
) so that (
4.11
) holds, and the result then follows.
∎

Recall that, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{s,r,n}
, there are at least
s
vertices in
T_{n}
on depth
r
while each of the depths
r-1
,
r+1,\ldots,r+\Psi(r)
contains at most
s
vertices.
In that case, we say that there is an
unusual accumulation of size
s
on depth
r
. Moreover, we say that the unusual accumulation has size
exactly
s
if
N_{r}(\tau_{n})=s
.
Noticing that the global maxima of the function
r\mapsto N_{r}(\tau_{n})
are depths with unusual accumulation leads to the following result.

Corollary 4.4
.

Fix a weight function
f
satisfying assumption (
EG
), and
\delta=\delta(f)>0
defined as in Proposition
4.3
.
Then, with high probability, there are no more than
(2\log n)/\delta
vertices on any depth in
T_{n}
.

Proof.

For all
r\in[n]
and
s\geq 2
, conditionally on the event
N_{r}(\tau_{n})=\max_{k\in[n]}N_{k}(\tau_{n})=s
, the event
\mathcal{E}_{s,r,n}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{s,r}
is necessarily satisfied.
Hence, by Proposition
4.3
and a union bound,

(4.12)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{k\in[n]}N_{k}(\tau_{n})=s\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\exists r\in[n]:\mathcal{E}_{s,r}\right)\leq 6(\max\Psi){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}n.

Define
s_{0}\coloneq(2\log n)/\delta
. Summing over all
s\geq s_{0}
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\max_{k\in[n]}N_{k}(\tau_{n})\geq s_{0}\bigg)\leq 6(\max\Psi)\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s_{0}}n}{1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta}}\leq\frac{6(\max\Psi)}{(1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta})n},

as desired.
∎

Corollary
4.4
shows that the largest number of vertices found on a single depth of
T_{n}
is typically of order
\log n
, thus showing that the height of
T_{n}
is of order at least
n/\log n
.
To strengthen this weaker lower bound to a linear one, we need to show that depths containing many vertices are rare.
Our strategy is to use Proposition
4.3
to control the number of depths
r^{\prime}\in[n]
on which the function
r\mapsto N_{r}(\tau_{n})
attains a sort of ‘local maximum’, and then to show that
N_{r}(\tau_{n})
reaches comparable values only within a small neighbourhood of each such depth.
We begin with the former. Fix integers
n,m,s\in\mathbb{N}
with
s\geq 2
and
m\leq n
and, for each
r\in[m]
, define

\mathcal{H}_{s,r,n}\coloneq\{N_{r}(\tau_{n})=s\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{s,r,n}\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{n}(m,s)\coloneq|\{r\in[m]:\mathcal{H}_{s,r,n}\text{ holds}\}|.

In words,
\mathcal{H}_{s,r,n}
is the event that there is an unusual accumulation of size exactly
s
on depth
r
, and
Y_{n}(m,s)
corresponds to the number of depths with unusual accumulation of size exactly
s
among the first
m
depths of
T_{n}
. Notice that a direct application of Proposition
4.3
gives

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}(m,s)\right]\leq\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{s,r,n}\right)\leq 6m(\max\Psi){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s},

which is a small fraction of the considered
m
depths when
s
is large. The next lemma estimates the probability that
Y_{n}(m,s)
exceeds this bound.

Lemma 4.5
.

Fix a weight function
f
satisfying assumption (
EG
), and
\delta
as in Proposition
4.3
.
Then, there is
s_{0}=s_{0}(f)
such that, for every
s\geq s_{0}
,
x>0
and
n\geq m\geq 4s
, we have

(4.13)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(Y_{n}(m,s)-6m(\max\Psi){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}\geq x\right)\leq\widetilde{\Psi}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\widetilde{\Psi}^{2}(6m{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}+x/3\widetilde{\Psi})}\right),

where
\widetilde{\Psi}:=\max\Psi+3.

Proof.

Partition the set
\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
into
\tilde{\Psi}
subsets
\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\tilde{\Psi}}
of the form

S_{i}=\{i,i+\widetilde{\Psi},i+2\widetilde{\Psi},i+3\widetilde{\Psi},\ldots\}.

Define
Z_{n}^{i}(m,s)
as the number of integers
r\in S_{i}
such that
\mathcal{H}_{s,r,n}
holds. It follows that
Y_{n}(m,s)
is the sum of
(Z_{n}^{i}(m,s))_{i=1}^{\widetilde{\Psi}}
, and hence

(4.14)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(Y_{n}(m,s)-6m(\max\Psi){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}\geq x\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{\Psi}}\mathbb{P}\big(Z^{i}_{n}(m,s)-6m(\max\Psi){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}/\widetilde{\Psi}\geq x/\widetilde{\Psi}\big).

Figure 8
.
In this figure, we see the profile of vertices on each depth of
T_{n}
. The depths used to construct
Z^{i}_{n}(m,s)
are in blue. For each of them, one needs to check if the number of vertices on this depth is exactly
s
, and if
\mathcal{E}_{s,r,n}
is satisfied. In this example, only the third blue depth (from left to right) satisfies both requirements.

Now, fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,\widetilde{\Psi}\}
and
r\in S_{i}
with
r>\tilde{\Psi}
. For every other
r^{\prime}\in S_{i}
with
r^{\prime}<r
, we have
r^{\prime}+\tilde{\Psi}\leq r
and hence the event
\mathcal{H}_{s,r^{\prime},n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{r^{\prime}+\widetilde{\Psi}-2}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{r-2}
-measurable (recall that
\mathcal{F}_{r^{\prime}+\widetilde{\Psi}-2}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the depths
0,1,\ldots,r^{\prime}+\widetilde{\Psi}-2
). It then follows from (
4.2
) that, for any
S^{\prime}\subseteq S_{i}\cap\{1,2,\ldots,r-1\}
, we must have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{s,r,n}\,\bigg|\bigcap_{r^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{s,r^{\prime},n}\right)\leq 2(\max\Psi+2){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}\leq 6(\max\Psi){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}.

In particular, a repeated application of the latter inequality gives that, for every
S^{\prime}\subseteq S_{i}
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\bigcap_{r\in S^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{s,r,n}\right)\leq(6(\max\Psi){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s})^{|S^{\prime}|}.

As a result,
Z_{i}
is stochastically dominated by a binomial random variable
Z_{i}^{\prime}
with parameters
m/\widetilde{\Psi}
and
\min\{6(\max\Psi){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s},1\}
.
Hence, for every
s
such that
6(\max\Psi){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}<1
, by Chernoff’s bound (
Lemma

2.2
), each of the probabilities on the right-hand side of (
4.14
) is at most

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(-\frac{(x/\widetilde{\Psi})^{2}}{2(6m{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}+x/3\widetilde{\Psi})}\bigg).

The lemma follows by using (
4.14
).
∎

Lemma
4.5
provides the control needed on the number of unusual accumulations of
T_{n}
.
To control the effect of these unusual accumulations on other depths, we provide a simple algorithm which, given a realisation of
T_{n}
and a starting value
r
, outputs a close position
r^{\prime}
in
T_{n}
with an unusual accumulation.

(1)

Place a walker at position
\textbf{r}=r
and set
\textbf{s}=N_{\textbf{r}}(\tau_{n})
.

(2)

The walker checks if
\mathcal{E}_{\textbf{s},\textbf{r},n}
holds; if it does, we terminate the algorithm and return the position
r
of the walker.

(3)

If
\mathcal{E}_{\textbf{s},\textbf{r},n}
does not hold, then there is some depth among
\textbf{r}-1
,
\textbf{r}+1,\ldots,\textbf{r}+\Psi(\textbf{r})
with at least
\textbf{s}+1
vertices.
Find the depth
r^{\prime}\in\{\textbf{r}-1,\textbf{r}+1,\ldots,\textbf{r}+\Psi(\textbf{r})\}
which maximises
N_{(\cdot)}(\tau_{n})
(if there is a tie, choose the smallest possible
r^{\prime}
). Then, update
\textbf{r}=r^{\prime}
and
\textbf{s}=N_{r^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})
.

(4)

Repeat steps (2)–(3) until the algorithm terminates.

A key property of the above algorithm is presented in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

For every
r\in\mathbb{N}
and every realisation of
T_{n}
, the algorithm starting from depth
r
terminates and its final output
r^{\prime}
satisfies
|r^{\prime}-r|\leq\max\Psi\cdot(N_{r^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})-N_{r}(\tau_{n}))
.

Proof.

The algorithm clearly terminates since
T_{n}
is finite and every step increases the value of
N_{\textbf{r}}(\tau_{n})
by at least
1
.
Moreover, at every step, the position of the walker changes by at most
\max\Psi
, which implies the inequality.
∎

We say that a depth
r
with
s\in\mathbb{N}
vertices is
covered
by
r^{\prime}
if, when initiated at
\textbf{r}=r
, the previous algorithm outputs
\textbf{r}=r^{\prime}
upon termination. From the previous lemma, we deduce the following conclusions:

•

Since the algorithm always terminates, every depth
r\in[n]
is covered by some
r^{\prime}\in[n]
(which may be itself).

•

If
r
is covered by
r^{\prime}
, then
N_{r}(\tau_{n})\leq N_{r^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})
.

•

An unusual accumulation of size
s
on depth
r
covers itself, at most
(\max\Psi)s
depths before it, and at most
(\max\Psi)s
depths after it, so in total it covers at most
2(\max\Psi)s+1
depths.

.
A realisation of
T_{n}
with

, where all depths with an unusual accumulation are shown in blue. Each such depth defines a covering region (dashed in grey), and bounded by red vertical lines.

Notice that Lemma
4.5
controls the number and size of unusual accumulations, while Lemma
4.6
allows to control the amount of vertices at other depths. With these results in hand, we are ready to prove Theorem
1.8
.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Set
m=c_{1}n
for a small constant
c_{1}>0
to be fixed later.
We construct an event which holds with high probability and implies
d(T_{n})>m
. To this end, define

\mathcal{C}(m)\coloneq\{r\in[n]:\exists r^{\prime}\in[m],r\text{ is covered by }r^{\prime}\},

the set of depths covered by unusual accumulations located within
[m]
. Define also
C(m)\coloneq\sum_{r\in\mathcal{C}(m)}N_{r}(\tau_{n})
, the total number of vertices within this set.
Observe that the event
C(m)<n
means that there is a non-empty depth
r
covered by some
r^{\prime}\notin[m]
, thus implying
d(T_{n})>m
.
Recall from the discussion following Lemma
4.6
that each unusual accumulation of size exactly
s
covers at most
2(\max\Psi)s+1
depths (including itself), each having at most
s
vertices. Summing over all unusual accumulations within
[m]
of different sizes gives the upper bound

C(m)\leq\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}Y_{n}(m,s)s(2(\max\Psi)s+1)\leq 3\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}s^{2}(\max\Psi)\cdot Y_{n}(m,s),

where
Y_{n}(m,s)
is as in Lemma
4.5
. We can further improve this bound by observing that Corollary
4.4
states that, with high probability,
no depth contains more than
(2\log n)/\delta
vertices at time
n
. Thus, with high probability,

(4.15)

C(m)\leq\ 3\sum_{s=1}^{(2\log n)/\delta}s^{2}(\max\Psi)\cdot Y_{n}(m,s).

We now bound the values of
Y_{n}(m,s)
by using Lemma
4.5
.
To this end, recall that there is
s_{0}=s_{0}(f)
such that, for any
s\geq s_{0}
, the inequality in (
4.13
) holds for all
x>0
. For
s\geq s_{0}
fixed, we choose
x=14\widetilde{\Psi}\max\{m{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s},\log(n)\}
so that the probability on the right-hand side of (

) can be bounded as

\widetilde{\Psi}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\widetilde{\Psi}^{2}(6m{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}+x/3\widetilde{\Psi})}\right)\leq\widetilde{\Psi}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{x}{2\widetilde{\Psi}}\right)=o(n^{-2}),

where the first inequality used that
2\widetilde{\Psi}^{2}(6m{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}+x/3\widetilde{\Psi})\leq 2\widetilde{\Psi}x
.
Taking a union bound over all

, we obtain that, with probability
1-o(n^{-1})
, for all such

, we have

Y_{n}(m,s)\leq\begin{cases}20\widetilde{\Psi}m{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s},&\mbox{ if }s_{0}\leq s\leq\delta^{-1}\log\left(m/\log n\right),\\[2.0pt]
20\widetilde{\Psi}\log(n),&\mbox{ if }\delta^{-1}\log\left(m/\log n\right)\leq s\leq 2\delta^{-1}\log n.\end{cases}

Calling
s_{1}\coloneq\delta^{-1}\log\left(m/\log n\right)
and
s_{2}\coloneq 2\delta^{-1}\log n
and observing that

is always bounded by

, we arrive at

\displaystyle C(m)

\displaystyle\leq\ 3\widetilde{\Psi}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{s_{0}}s^{2}m+\sum_{s=s_{0}+1}^{s_{1}}s^{2}\cdot 20\widetilde{\Psi}m{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}+\sum_{s=s_{1}+1}^{s_{2}}s^{2}\cdot 20\widetilde{\Psi}\log(n)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3\widetilde{\Psi}\left[s_{0}^{3}m+20\widetilde{\Psi}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}s^{2}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta s}\right)m+20\widetilde{\Psi}\left(\frac{2\log n}{\delta}\right)^{3}\log(n)\right]=C_{1}m+C_{2}(\log n)^{4},

where the constants
C_{1}
and

are absolute and do not depend on

or

.
Hence, taking

with

gives

for all large enough

∎

We conclude this section with a brief discussion of Remark
1.10
on how to extend our results to certain growth functions
f
that do not satisfy Assumption (
EG
). Notice that these functions only play a role in the proof of Proposition
4.3
and are not used afterwards, so that one can try to weaken Assumption (
EG
) such that Proposition
4.3
still holds. It turns out that for this proposition to work, we only need the function
\Psi(r)
to be finite for all
r
, even if it is not bounded. In this case, the structure of the proof still applies, although it becomes heavier notation-wise, and more care is needed at certain points. The resulting bound is of the form

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{r,s}\right)\leq 5\Psi(r){\mathrm{e}}^{-\delta(r)s},

where
\delta
is no longer a constant depending on
f
, but rather an explicit function of
r
(and
f
). All intermediate results following Proposition
4.3
as well as the structure of the proof of Theorem
1.8
can be adapted to include the new dependency of
\delta
on
r
, although the statements become increasingly more obscure at each step, until concluding the main theorem, which in its adapted version states that with probability
1-n^{-\Omega(1)}
it holds that

d(T_{n})\geq m(n),

for some function
m(n)
satisfying an intricate set of conditions. As pointed out in (
1.5
), for the choice
f(k)={\mathrm{e}}^{k(\log(k+2))^{-\alpha}}
we have that one can choose
\nu>0
such that
m(n)=n{\mathrm{e}}^{-\nu\log^{\alpha}(n)\log^{2}\log(n)}
satisfies these conditions. In particular showing that for
0<\alpha<1
the growth of the depth is at least close to linear. The particular choice of
m(n)
in this case is optimal for the set of conditions, and we also believe that our techniques cannot be improved to obtain much better lower bounds, thus suggesting some possibly interesting behavior at
\alpha=1
.

5.
Super-exponential weight functions

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.13
(i)–(ii). The proof of both parts uses a coupling result (
5.1
) and a delicate moment computation presented in
Lemma

5.2
and
Proposition

5.5
. We start with a simple but very useful claim that allows us to relate the number of vertices at a given level with exponential random variables of suitable rates:

Claim 5.1
.

There is a coupling of the processes
(N_{n}(\cdot))_{n=1}^{\infty}
and a sequence of independent exponential random variables
(E_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
with rates
(f(n))_{n=0}^{\infty}
, respectively, such that each of the following holds:

(i)

almost surely, for every
n\geq 0
,
E_{n}\geq\tau_{1,n+1}-\tau_{1,n}
,

(ii)

for every
n\geq 1
,
(N_{j}(\cdot))_{j\leq n}
is independent from the collection
(E_{i})_{i=n}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Denote by
E_{n}
the time needed by the vertex born on depth
n
at time
\tau_{1,n}
to produce its first child. We justify that the random variables
(E_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
satisfy the properties required in the claim.
They clearly have the desired distributions, and their independence comes from the fact that different vertices in the DWT tree produce offspring independently of each other.
The latter argument also justifies (ii).
Finally, as the child of every vertex on depth
n
is a vertex on depth
n+1
, (i) also holds.
∎

Next, we work with
(E_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
as defined in
5.1
.
For every
n\geq 0
, we define
F_{n}\coloneq\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}E_{i}
. Observe that Claim
5.1
yields the inequality
F_{n}\geq\tau_{1,\infty}-\tau_{1,n}
almost surely for every
n
.
It then follows that these random variables can be used to control the number of vertices at each level of the tree at time
\tau_{\infty}
(which equals
\tau_{1,\infty}
by Lemma
2.6
).
Moreover, since each
E_{k}
follows an exponential distribution with an explicit rate, expressions involving the collection of random variables
(F_{i})_{i\geq 0}
become tractable from a computational standpoint.
This can be seen in the next lemma where we derive bounds for the joint moments of
(F_{i})_{i\geq 0}
. The proof involves lengthy combinatorial arguments and is therefore deferred to
Section

5.1
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix a weight function
f
satisfying Assumption (
D
), and recall the random variables
(E_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
and
(F_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
from the last paragraph.
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
G_{n}\coloneq f(n-1)F_{n}
.

(a)

There are
k_{0}=k_{0}(f)\geq 1
and
C=C(f)>0
such that each of the following holds:

(a1)

For all
k\leq k_{0}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
k<\ell
,

\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k}G_{\ell}\right]\leq C\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}.

(a2)

For all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
k_{0}<k<\ell
,

\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k}G_{\ell}\right]\leq\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\left(1+C\frac{f(k)}{f(k+1)}+C\frac{f(\ell)}{f(\ell+1)}\right).

(b)

For every integer
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C_{d}=C_{d}(f)>0
such that, for every finite integer set
B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
|B|\geq d
, every sequence
(a_{j})_{j\in B}
with values in
\{1,2\}
, and every subset
I\subseteq B
with
|I|=d
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j\in B}G_{j}^{a_{j}}\right]\leq C_{d}2^{-|B|}\prod_{j\in I}\left(\frac{f(j-1)}{f(j)}\right)^{a_{j}}.

Remark 5.3
.

Part (b) of
Lemma

5.2
allows to find tractable upper bounds for arbitrary (but finite) products of variables of the form
G_{k}
and
G_{k}^{2}
. By ignoring correlations, a naive approach would be to assume an approximation of the form

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j\in B}G_{j}^{a_{j}}\right]\leq C\prod_{j\in B}\mathbb{E}\left[G_{j}^{a_{j}}\right],

for some constant
C
independent of the set
B
. Unfortunately, the correlations between the random variables do not allow for such an inequality, giving instead an inequality of the form

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j\in B}G_{j}^{a_{j}}\right]\leq M(B)\prod_{j\in B}\mathbb{E}\left[G_{j}^{a_{j}}\right],

where
M
is some unbounded function. The key idea of the lemma is to find a bound that holds uniformly over
B
by keeping only a given number of factors on the right-hand side, which is enough for our applications.

We finally state the following minor technical lemma regarding the asymptotic growth of partial sums, whose proof we leave to the reader.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(c_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\varepsilon_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be two sequences of non-negative real numbers and let
M_{1},M_{2}=M_{1}(m),M_{2}(m)\in\mathbb{N}
with
M_{1}\leq M_{2}
for all
m
large be such that

(5.1)

\lim_{j\to\infty}\varepsilon_{j}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{j=M_{1}(m)}^{M_{2}(m)}c_{j}=\infty.

Then, as
m\to\infty
,

(5.2)

\sum_{j=M_{1}(m)}^{M_{2}(m)}c_{j}\varepsilon_{j}=o\Bigg(\sum_{j=M_{1}(m)}^{M_{2}(m)}c_{j}\Bigg).

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

1.13
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.13
assuming
Lemma

5.2
.

We start with part (i). Define
Z_{n}=n-d(T_{n})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and observe that the sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is (deterministically) non-decreasing.
In particular,
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges almost surely to
Z_{\infty}\coloneq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}Z_{n}
, which can be written as

Z_{\infty}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(N_{k}(\tau_{\infty})-1).

Observe that
Theorem

1.13
(i) follows as long as
Z_{\infty}
is a.s. finite.
We use the continuous-time interpretation of the DWT from
Section

2.2.2
to show that
Z_{\infty}
is an integrable random variable, i.e. that the sum of
(N_{k}(\tau_{\infty})-1)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
has finite mean. As almost surely
\tau_{\infty}\leq\tau_{1,k}+F_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(5.3)

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(\tau_{\infty})-1\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(\tau_{\infty})-N_{k}{(\tau_{1,k})}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(\tau_{1,k}+F_{k})-N_{k}{(\tau_{1,k})}\right].

The above expression on the right-hand side has the advantage that, thanks to
5.1
,
F_{k}
is independent of
N_{k}
and
\tau_{1,k}
. Hence, we are interested in studying

(5.4)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.N_{k}(\tau_{1,k}+F_{k})-N_{k}{(\tau_{1,k})}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]

where
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the random variables
(E_{i})_{i=k}^{\infty}
.
For convenience of notation, we introduce the variables

(5.5)

H_{k}\coloneq N_{k}(\tau_{1,k}+F_{k})-N_{k}(\tau_{1,k})=N_{k}(\tau_{1,k}+F_{k})-1

with the convention
H_{0}=0
, so that the expression in (
5.4
) becomes
\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]
.
We begin by observing that, given the trajectory of
N_{k-1}
,
N_{k}
is a Poisson point process with directing measure
N_{k-1}(s)\mathrm{d}s
, so a direct use of the strong Markov property yields

(5.6)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\int_{\tau_{1,k}}^{\tau_{1,k}+F_{k}}f(k-1)N_{k-1}(s)\mathrm{d}s\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right].

Recall that almost surely
\tau_{1,k}\leq\tau_{1,k-1}+E_{k-1}
by Lemma
5.1
, and also that
F_{k}+E_{k-1}=F_{k-1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}
-measurable. Since
N_{k-1}
is increasing, shifting the integration interval in (
5.6
) to the right on the real line can only increase the value of the integral. Hence,

(5.7)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\int_{\tau_{1,k-1}+E_{k-1}}^{\tau_{1,k-1}+E_{k-1}+F_{k}}f(k-1)N_{k-1}(s)\mathrm{d}s\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}\right]\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right].

Using that
N_{k-1}
is increasing, we have that (
5.7
) is at most

(5.8)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[\left.f(k-1)F_{k}(H_{k-1}+1)\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}\right]\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]\\
&\hskip 100.00015pt\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left.f(k-1)F_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k-1}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}\right]\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]+f(k-1)F_{k}.\end{split}

At this point, we recall the notation
G_{k}=f(k-1)F_{k}
from
Lemma

5.2
, and introduce

(5.9)

\Pi_{i,k}\coloneq f(k-1)F_{k}f(k-2)F_{k-1}\cdots f(i-1)F_{i}=G_{k}G_{k-1}\cdots G_{i},

so that iterating (
5.6
)–(
5.8
) until reaching
H_{0}=0
and taking expected values leads to

(5.10)

\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}\right].

An application of
Lemma

5.2
(b) with
d=1
,
B=\{i,\ldots,k\}
,
I=\{k\}
, and
a_{i}=\cdots=a_{k}=1
yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}\right]\leq C_{1}2^{i-k-1}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}.

Combined with (
5.10
), we thus get

\mathbb{E}[H_{k}]\leq C_{1}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}2^{i-k-1}\leq C_{1}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}.

Since
\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(\tau_{\infty})-1\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}\right]
by (
5.3
), the assumption that
I_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{f(j)}{f(j+1)}
converges shows that the sum of
(N_{k}(\tau_{\infty})-1)_{k=1}^{\infty}
has finite mean, as desired.

We now move to the proof of
Theorem

1.13
(ii), that is, we want to show that if
I_{n}=\omega(1)
, then
\frac{n-d(T_{n})}{I_{n}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\mathbb{P}}}1
. Our proof relies on obtaining lower and upper bounds on
d(T_{n})
using concentration and second moment arguments.
The lower and the upper bounds are derived separately.

Upper bound.
We show that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability
d(T_{n})<m_{n}\coloneq n-(1-\varepsilon)I_{n}
. Note that
m_{n}=(1-o(1))n
, by applying Lemma
5.4
to the sum
I_{n}
, with
c_{j}=1,\varepsilon_{j}=f(j-1)/f(j)
, and
M_{1}=1,M_{2}=n+1
. We work with the Cox process interpretation of the model introduced in Section
2.2.2
, and define

d(t)\coloneq\sup\{k\in\mathbb{N},\,N_{k}(t)>0\},

which in this context corresponds to the depth of the tree, that is,
d(\tau_{n})\overset{d}{=}d(T_{n})
. Then,

(5.11)

n-d(\tau_{n})=1+\sum_{k=1}^{d(\tau_{n})}(N_{k}(\tau_{n})-1).

To control the sum on the right-hand side, for each
k\geq 1
, we introduce the random variable
J_{k}
defined as the indicator of the event
{\tau_{2,k}<\tau_{1,k+1}}
.
Since
N_{k}(\cdot)
is increasing, for each
k\leq d(\tau_{n})-1
such that
J_{k}=1
, it must hold that
N_{k}(\tau_{n})\geq 2
, so in particular

(5.12)

\sum_{k=1}^{d(\tau_{n})}(N_{k}(\tau_{n})-1)\,\geq\,\sum_{k=1}^{d(\tau_{n})-1}J_{k}\geq\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{d(\tau_{n})}J_{k}\bigg)-1.

Now, fix any
\varepsilon>0
. By combining (
5.11
) and (
5.12
) and recalling
m_{n}=n-(1-\varepsilon)I_{n}
,
we obtain

(5.13)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(d(\tau_{n})\geq n-(1-\varepsilon)I_{n}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}J_{k}\leq(1-\varepsilon)I_{n}\right).

We now address the probability on the right-hand side by making two observations.
First, given any realisation of the processes
N_{1},\ldots,N_{k-1}
until time
\tau_{1,k}
, within the time interval
[\tau_{1,k},\min\{\tau_{2,k},\tau_{1,k+1}\}]
, it holds that
N_{k-1}\geq 1
and
N_{k}\equiv 1
. Thus, it directly follows that, almost surely,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left.J_{k}=1\,\right|(N_{1}(t),\ldots,N_{k-1}(t))_{t=0}^{\tau_{1,k}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k-1)+f(k)},

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{P}\left(\left.J_{k}=1\,\right|J_{1},\ldots,J_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k-1)+f(k)},

where we used that
J_{1},\ldots,J_{k-1}
are measurable in terms of the mentioned trajectories. As a result, the sequence
(J_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
stochastically dominates a sequence
(J^{\prime}_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
of independent Bernoulli random variables with
\mathbb{P}\!\left(J_{k}^{\prime}=1\right)=\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k-1)+f(k)}
for each
k
.
In particular, the latter sequence satisfies

(5.14)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}J^{\prime}_{k}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k-1)+f(k)}=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}-\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k-1)+f(k)}.

The first sum on the right-hand side equals
I_{m_{n}-1}
. By Assumption (
D
), we can apply Lemma
5.4
to the second sum on the right-hand side, with
c_{j}=f(j-1)/f(j)
,
\varepsilon_{j}=f(j-1)/(f(j-1)+f(j))
, and
M_{1}=1
and
M_{2}=m_{n}
. This yields

(5.15)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}J^{\prime}_{k}\right]=(1-o(1))I_{m_{n}-1}.

We then write

(5.16)

I_{m_{n}-1}=I_{n}-\sum_{k=m_{n}+1}^{n+1}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)},

and we again use Lemma
5.4
, this time with
c_{j}=1
,
\varepsilon_{j}=f(j-1)/f(j)
, and
M_{1}=m_{n}+1
and
M_{2}=n+1
(where we note that
M_{1}\leq M_{2}
for all
n
large, since
M_{2}-M_{1}=n-m_{n}=(1-\varepsilon)I_{n}=\omega(1)
), to finally obtain

(5.17)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}J^{\prime}_{k}\right]=(1-o(1))I_{n}.

Indeed, by Assumption (
D
), there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f(k-1)\leq\varepsilon f(k)/4
for all
k\geq k_{0}
, implying that

\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\leq\bigg(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\bigg)\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k-1)+f(k)}\qquad\text{for all}\qquad k\geq k_{0}.

Moreover, by Assumption (
D
) we also have
m_{n}=\omega(1)
and thus
I_{m_{n}}=\omega(1)
. Hence, for large
n
,

\displaystyle I_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=m_{n}+1}^{n+1}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}+\bigg(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\bigg)\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k-1)+f(k)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(n+1-m_{n})\varepsilon}{4}+\bigg(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\bigg)I_{m_{n}-1}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}I_{n}+\bigg(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\bigg)I_{m_{n}-1}.

Furthermore, using again that
m_{n}=\omega(1)
and the latter chain of inequalities,

\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k-1)+f(k)}\geq\bigg(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\bigg)^{-1}I_{m_{n}-1}\geq\bigg(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\bigg)I_{n}\geq\bigg(1-\frac{6\varepsilon}{7}\bigg)I_{n}.

Thus, by Chernoff’s bound,

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}J_{k}\leq(1-\varepsilon)I_{n}\bigg)\leq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{m_{n}}J^{\prime}_{k}\leq(1-\varepsilon)I_{n}\bigg)=\exp(-\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}I_{n}))=o(1).

As the latter holds for every
\varepsilon>0
, this ensures the desired upper bound on
d(T_{n})
.

Lower bound.
We show that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability
d(T_{n})>n-(1+\varepsilon)I_{n}
. We work again with the Cox process interpretation of the model introduced in Section
2.2.2
, and begin the proof with a computation similar to the one in (
5.13
), namely

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(d(\tau_{n})\leq n-(1+\varepsilon)I_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{d(\tau_{n})}(N_{k}(\tau_{n})-1)\geq(1+\varepsilon)I_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(N_{k}(\tau_{1,k}+F_{k})-1)\geq(1+\varepsilon)I_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}\geq(1+\varepsilon)I_{n}-1\right),

where the first equality follows form (
5.11
), the inequality follows from the almost sure bounds
d(\tau_{n})\leq n
and
\tau_{n}\leq\tau_{\infty}\leq\tau_{1,k}+F_{k}
, and the second equality uses the definition of the variables
H_{k}
in (
5.5
).
The result will follow from Chebyshev’s inequality together with the following proposition.

Proposition 5.5
.

Assuming assumption (
D
) and
I_{n}=\omega(1)
, each of the following holds:

(i)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}\right]\geq(1+o(1))I_{n}
.

(ii)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}\bigg)^{2}\right]\leq(1+o(1))I_{n}^{2}
.

We delay the proof of Proposition
5.5
and use it to conclude the proof of
Theorem

1.13
(ii) first.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, Proposition
5.5
(ii) implies that
(1+\varepsilon/2)I_{n}-1\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}\right]
for all large
n
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(d(\tau_{n})\leq n-(1+\varepsilon)I_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}\geq(1+\varepsilon)I_{n}-1\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}\right]+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{n}\right),

and the probability on the right-hand side converges to zero by combining Chebyshev’s inequality and the two parts of Proposition
5.5
. As the latter holds for every
\varepsilon>0
, this ensures the desired lower bound on
d(\tau_{n})
, or equivalently on
d(T_{n})
.
∎

We conclude this section with the proof of Proposition
5.5
.

Proof of Proposition
5.5
assuming
Lemma

5.2
.

Recall the variables
J_{k}
from the proof of the upper bound on
d(T_{n})
, for which we also have

\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}\,\geq\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}J_{k}.

Using the previous analysis of the
J_{k}
variables we then arrive at

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}\bigg]\geq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{n}J_{k}\bigg]\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k-1)+f(k)}=(1+o(1))I_{n},

where the last equality uses Assumption (
D
), Lemma
5.4
(as applied to (
5.14
), but with
M_{2}=n
), and that
I_{n}=\omega(1)
. This gives the bound in (i). We now move to the proof of (ii), that is, we want to show that the expression

(5.18)

\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}\bigg)^{2}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}^{2}\right]\,+\,2\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}H_{\ell}\right]

is at most
(1+o(1))I_{n}^{2}
.
We first bound
\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}^{2}\right]
for a fixed value of
k
, similarly to what was done for
\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}\right]
in (
5.8
)–(
5.10
). Similarly to that case, we analyse
\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]
, where again
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the random variables
(E_{i})_{i=k}^{\infty}
. As in
Theorem

1.13
(i), conditioning on
N_{k-1}
(and on
F_{k}
) and using the strong Markov property gives that
H_{k}
is Poisson distributed with parameter
\lambda\coloneq\int_{\tau_{1,k}}^{\tau_{1,k}+F_{k}}f(k-1)N_{k-1}(s)\mathrm{d}s
, so that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\lambda^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\lambda\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right].

For
\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\lambda^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]
, we use that
\tau_{1,k}\leq\tau_{1,k-1}+E_{k-1}
by Lemma
5.1
and the fact that
N_{k-1}
is increasing in the same way as in the analysis of
\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\lambda^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\left(\int_{\tau_{1,k-1}+E_{k-1}}^{\tau_{1,k-1}+E_{k-1}+F_{k}}f(k-1)N_{k-1}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}\right]\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[\left.(f(k-1)F_{k}N_{k-1}(\tau_{1,k-1}+F_{k-1}))^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}\right]\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right].

Using the identity
x^{2}=(x-1)^{2}+2(x-1)+1
with
x=N_{k-1}(\tau_{1,k-1}+F_{k-1})
and the fact that
N_{k-1}(\tau_{1,k-1})=1
shows that

N_{k-1}(\tau_{1,k-1}+F_{k-1})^{2}=H_{k-1}^{2}+2H_{k-1}+1,

which further yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\lambda^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[\left.(f(k-1)F_{k})^{2}(H_{k-1}^{2}+2H_{k-1}+1)\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}\right]\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right].

Further, since
\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\lambda\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]
and
F_{k}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}\supseteq\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}
,

(5.19)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left.(f(k-1)F_{k})^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k-1}^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}\right]\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[\left.b_{k}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}\right]\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]

where

b_{k}\coloneq(f(k-1)F_{k})^{2}(2H_{k-1}+1)+H_{k}.

Iterating (
5.19
) and recalling the inclusion
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\infty}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{0}^{\infty}
, the fact that
H_{0}\equiv 0
and the notation
\Pi_{i,k}=f(k-1)F_{k}f(k-2)F_{k-1}\cdots f(i-1)F_{i}
(with
\Pi_{k+1,k}=1
by convention),
we obtain

(5.20)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{{\infty}}\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.b_{i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{{\infty}}\right],

and taking expectations on both sides yields

\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[b_{i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[2H_{i-1}\Pi_{i,k}^{2}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}^{2}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}\right].

Further, using (
5.7
)–(
5.8
) and that
\Pi_{i+1,k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\infty}
-measurable, we obtain that

(5.21)

\begin{split}\hskip-5.0pt\mathbb{E}[H_{i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[H_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\infty}]\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}]&\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[H_{i-1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}^{\infty}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\infty}]\Pi_{i,i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{i,i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}[H_{i-1}\Pi_{i,i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{i,i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}].\end{split}

Iterating the latter argument, and using that
H_{0}\equiv 0
and
\Pi_{j,i}\Pi_{i+1,k}=\Pi_{j,k}
for all
j\leq i<k
gives

(5.22)

\mathbb{E}[H_{i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{j,i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}].

Substituting the previous inequality into (
5.20
) shows that

(5.23)

\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}^{2}\right]\leq 2\sum_{i=2}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{j,i-1}\Pi_{i,k}^{2}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}^{2}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{j,i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}\right].

Recalling the notation
G_{k}=f(k-1)F_{k}
introduced in
Lemma

5.2
, we observe that all these terms correspond to expressions of the form

\prod_{j=i}^{k}G_{j}^{a_{j}}
, with
a_{j}\in\{1,2\}
. Moreover, the last factor in each term is
G_{k}^{2}
, the sole exception being the last sum in (
5.23
) which,
in the case
i=k
, yields a term of the form

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{j,k}\right]
, whose last factor is
G_{k}
. Hence, applying
Lemma

5.2
(b) with
d=1
and
I=\{k\}
, gives

\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}^{2}\right]\leq C_{1}\left(\left(\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\right)\right)\left(2\sum_{i=2}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}2^{j-k-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}2^{i-k-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{i}2^{j-k-1}\right).

As each of the three sums on the right is uniformly bounded as a function of
k
, it follows that there is an absolute constant
C>0

such that

\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}^{2}\right]\leq C\left[\left(\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\right)\right]

and, in particular,

(5.24)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}^{2}\right]=O\left(\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{f(k)}{f(k+1)}\right)^{2}\right)+I_{n}\right)=O(I_{n})=o\left(I_{n}^{2}\right),

where we use Lemma
5.4
with
c_{j}=\varepsilon_{j}=f(j)/f(j+1)
, and
M_{1}=1
and
M_{2}=n
to bound the sum from above.

We now turn to estimating the terms
\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}H_{\ell}\right]
with
1\leq k<\ell
appearing in (
5.18
).
We first analyse
\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}H_{\ell}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\right]
by following arguments already used in the study of
\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]
.
More precisely, conditioning on
N_{k},N_{k+1},\ldots,N_{\ell-1}
, we use the strong Markov property as well as the fact that
N_{\ell-1}
is increasing as in (
5.6
)–(
5.7
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}H_{\ell}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}\int_{\tau_{1,\ell}}^{\tau_{1,\ell}+F_{\ell}}f(\ell-1)N_{\ell-1}(s)ds\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}f(\ell-1)F_{\ell}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}f(\ell-1)F_{\ell}H_{\ell-1}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\right].

By iterating this procedure, we arrive at the upper bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}H_{\ell}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}\Pi_{j,\ell}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\right]\bigg)+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}^{2}\Pi_{k+1,\ell}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\right]

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]\Pi_{j,\ell}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\right]\bigg)+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{k}^{2}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}\right]{\Pi_{k+1,\ell}}\,\right|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\right],

where the last equality used that
(\Pi_{j,\ell})_{j=k+1}^{\ell}
are all
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}
-measurable and
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\infty}
as
k<\ell
.
Now, repeating the argument from (
5.6
)–(
5.8
) for the first term, the argument from (
5.19
)–(
5.23
) for the second term and taking expectations on both sides leads to

(5.25)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}H_{\ell}\right]\leq{}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}\Pi_{j,\ell}\right]+2\sum_{i=2}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{j,i-1}\Pi_{i,k}^{2}\Pi_{k+1,\ell}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}^{2}\Pi_{k+1,\ell}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{j,i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}\Pi_{k+1,\ell}\right].

Taking the sum over
\ell
from
2
to
n
and then over

from
1
to
\ell-1
gives

(5.26)

\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}H_{\ell}\right]\leq\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}\left(\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}+\delta_{k,\ell}\right),

where
\delta_{k,\ell}
is defined as

\displaystyle\delta_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle\coloneq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k}G_{\ell}\right]-\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}}_{\coloneq\delta^{(1)}_{k,\ell}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}G_{\ell}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}\Pi_{j,\ell}\right]+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{j,\ell}\right]}_{\coloneq\delta^{(2)}_{k,\ell}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\underbrace{2\sum_{i=2}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{j,i-1}\Pi_{i,k}^{2}\Pi_{k+1,\ell}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}^{2}\Pi_{k+1,\ell}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{j,i}\Pi_{i+1,k}^{2}\Pi_{k+1,\ell}\right]}_{\coloneq\delta^{(3)}_{k,\ell}}.

Note that the term
\delta^{(3)}_{k,\ell}
represents the last three sums in (

) except for the part for

and

of the last double sum, which appears as the last term in
\delta^{(2)}_{k,\ell}

\delta^{(1)}_{k,\ell}+\delta^{(2)}_{k,\ell}
corresponds to a decomposition of the first double sum. We observe that all terms in the definition of

can be expressed as products of the type

for suitable choices of

taking values in
\{1,2\}
. The remainder of the proof then consists in applying

5.2
(b) to show that

(5.27)

\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}\delta_{k,\ell}=o(I_{n}^{2}).

Note that this is enough to conclude since

\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}=\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\bigg)I_{n}^{2},

which together with (

2\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}H_{\ell}\right]\leq(1+o(1))I_{n}^{2}.

Combined with (

) and (

5.5
(b).

To show (

As a next step, we show that
\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\delta_{\ell-1,\ell}=o(I_{n}^{2})
since the bounds for
\delta_{k,\ell}
for
k=\ell-1
have to be treated slightly differently from the general case.
In the case

, we observe that each of the products
\prod_{j\in B}G_{j}^{a_{j}}

and

,

5.2
(b) with

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell-1,\ell}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\frac{f(\ell-2)}{f(\ell-1)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-2}2^{i-\ell-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}2^{j-\ell-1}\right]\leq C_{2}^{\prime}\frac{f(\ell-2)}{f(\ell-1)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)},

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell-1,\ell}^{(3)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\bigg(\frac{f(\ell-2)}{f(\ell-1)}\bigg)^{2}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\left[2\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}2^{j-\ell-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}2^{i-\ell-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-2}\sum_{j=1}^{i}2^{j-\ell-1}\right]\leq C_{2}^{\prime}\frac{f(\ell-2)}{f(\ell-1)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)},

.

Next, we assume that

\displaystyle=O(I_{n})+o\bigg(\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-2}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\bigg),

and the right-hand side is

).

For the contribution coming from
\delta_{k,\ell}^{(2)}
, we observe first that the first sum

\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}G_{\ell}\right]

is either zero (if
k=1
), or otherwise contains the factor
G_{k-1}G_{k}G_{\ell}
. Applying
Lemma

5.2
(b) with
d=3
and
I=\{k-1,k,\ell\}
gives

\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{i,k}G_{\ell}\right]\leq C_{3}\frac{f(k-2)}{f(k-1)}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}2^{i-k-2}\leq C_{3}^{\prime}\frac{f(k-2)}{f(k-1)}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}.

Similarly, as
k\leq\ell-2
, each of the products appearing in the other sums in
\delta_{k,\ell}^{(2)}
contains the factor
G_{k}G_{\ell-1}G_{\ell}
. By
Lemma

5.2
(b) with
d=3
and
I=\{k,\ell-1,\ell\}
, the remainder of
\delta_{k,\ell}^{(2)}
is at most

C_{3}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-2)}{f(\ell-1)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell-1}2^{i-k+j-\ell-2}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}2^{j-\ell-1}\bigg]\leq C_{3}^{\prime}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-2)}{f(\ell-1)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}.

By summing these bounds over
k,\ell
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-2}\delta_{k,\ell}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}^{\prime}\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-2}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\bigg(\frac{f(k-2)}{f(k-1)}+\frac{f(\ell-2)}{f(\ell-1)}\bigg)

\displaystyle=o\left(\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-2}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\right),

and the final expression is again of the order
o(I_{n}^{2})
.

Lastly, for
\delta_{k,\ell}^{(3)}
, using that
k\leq\ell-2
, each of the products in
\delta_{k,\ell}^{(3)}
contains the factor
G_{k}^{2}G_{\ell-1}G_{\ell}
(if
k=1
, the first and the third sums are zero). By
Lemma

5.2
(b) with
d=3
and
I=\{k,\ell-1,\ell\}
,

\displaystyle\delta_{k,\ell}^{(3)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\left(\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\right)^{2}\frac{f(\ell-2)}{f(\ell-1)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\left[2\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}2^{j-\ell-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}2^{i-\ell-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}2^{j-\ell-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}^{\prime}\left(\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\right)^{2}\frac{f(\ell-2)}{f(\ell-1)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}.

Once again, by summing these bounds over
k,\ell
, we obtain

\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-2}\delta_{k,\ell}^{(3)}\leq C_{3}^{\prime}\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-2}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\Big(\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-2)}{f(\ell-1)}\Big)=o\bigg(\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-2}\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\frac{f(\ell-1)}{f(\ell)}\bigg),

which again is
o(I_{n}^{2})
by (
5.28
).
This shows (
5.27
) and completes the proof.
∎

5.1.
Proof of Lemma
5.2

We conclude the section by proving Lemma
5.2
.
Recall the variables
G_{k}=f(k-1)F_{k}
and the fact that the exponential variable
E_{k}
with rate
f(k)
has moments
\mathbb{E}\left[E_{k}^{n}\right]=n!/f(k)^{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We proceed by establishing a couple of intermediate estimates which allow us to control the expectations in the statement of the lemma.

Proposition 5.6
.

For every weight function
f
satisfying Assumption (
D
), there is
k_{0}=k_{0}(f)\geq 1
and
C=C(f)>0
such that, for every
k\geq k_{0}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, as well as for every
k<k_{0}
and
\ell\leq 2(k_{0}+1)
,

(5.30)

\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k}^{\ell}\right]\leq\ell!\left(\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\right)^{\ell}\left(1+C\frac{f(k)}{f(k+1)}\right).

Proof.

Fix
k_{0}
such that
f(k-1)\leq 0.1f(k)
for all
k\geq k_{0}
(such
k_{0}
is ensured by Assumption (
D
)).
For positive integers
k,\ell
, define
(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell}\coloneq\{(y_{i})_{i=k}^{\infty}\colon\sum_{i=k}^{\infty}y_{i}=\ell\}
.
As
F_{k}=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}E_{j}
, we have

F_{k}^{\ell}=\left(\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}E_{j}\right)^{\ell}=\sum_{(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell}}\frac{\ell!}{y_{k}!y_{k+1}!\cdots}\prod_{j=k}^{\infty}E_{j}^{y_{j}}.

Multiplying by
f(k-1)^{\ell}
and taking expectations yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k}^{\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell}}\frac{\ell!f(k-1)^{\ell}}{y_{k}!y_{k+1}!\cdots}\prod_{j=k}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[E_{j}^{y_{j}}\right]=\sum_{(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell}}\frac{\ell!f(k-1)^{\ell}}{y_{k}!y_{k+1}!\cdots}\prod_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{y_{j}!}{f(j)^{y_{j}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell}}\ell!f(k-1)^{\ell}\prod_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(j)^{y_{j}}}=\ell!\left(\frac{f(k-1)}{f(k)}\right)^{\ell}\sum_{(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell}}\prod_{j=k}^{\infty}\left(\frac{f(k)}{f(j)}\right)^{y_{j}},

where in the last equality uses that
\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}y_{j}=\ell
. Further, set

A_{k,\ell}\coloneq\sum_{(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell}}\prod_{j=k}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{f(k)}{f(j)}\bigg)^{y_{j}}.

Our goal is to prove that there exists a large
C=C(f)>0
such that, for all
k\geq k_{0}
and
\ell\geq 0
, as well as for all
k<k_{0}
and all
\ell\leq 2(k_{0}+1)
,

(5.31)

A_{k,\ell}\leq 1+C\frac{f(k)}{f(k+1)}.

To this end, we first show that

(5.32)

A_{k,\ell}=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{f(k)}{f(j)}\bigg)^{\ell}A_{j,\ell-1}.

Indeed, for any sequence
(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell}
, denote by
r
the index of the first non-zero entry.
That is,
y_{k}=y_{k+1}=\cdots=y_{r-1}=0
and
y_{r}\geq 1
: we denote this family of sequences by
(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell,r}
.
By splitting the sum over all sequences
(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell}
into sums over
(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell,r}
with
r\geq k
, we obtain

A_{k,\ell}=\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}\sum_{(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell,r}}\prod_{j=k}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{f(k)}{f(j)}\bigg)^{y_{j}}=\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}\sum_{(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell,r}}\prod_{j=r}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{f(k)}{f(j)}\bigg)^{y_{j}}.

Now, for fixed
r
and any sequence in
(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell,r}
, we define a new sequence
y^{\prime}_{r},y^{\prime}_{r+1},\ldots
by setting
y^{\prime}_{r}=y_{r}-1
and

y^{\prime}_{j}=y_{j}
for all
j\neq r
. Observe that these sequences are precisely those counted in
(y^{\prime}_{r},\ldots)_{\ell-1}
.
Using this observation and the fact that
\sum_{j=r}^{\infty}y_{j}=\ell
, we deduce that

A_{k,\ell}=\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}\sum_{(y_{k},\ldots)_{\ell,r}}\left(\frac{f(k)}{f(r)}\right)^{\ell}\prod_{j=r}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{f(r)}{f(j)}\bigg)^{y_{j}}=\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}\sum_{(y_{r}^{\prime},\ldots)_{\ell-1}}\left(\frac{f(k)}{f(r)}\right)^{\ell}\prod_{j=r}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{f(r)}{f(j)}\bigg)^{y_{j}^{\prime}}=\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}\left(\frac{f(k)}{f(r)}\right)^{\ell}A_{r,\ell-1},

which shows (
5.32
).
We now move to the proof of (
5.31
) when
k\geq k_{0}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We observe that, by our choice of

, for all
\ell\geq 1
and

, it holds that

(5.33)

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\bigg(\frac{f(k)}{f(k+1)}\bigg)^{j}\leq\frac{10}{9}\frac{f(k)}{f(k+1)}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{f(k)}{f(j)}\bigg)^{\ell}\leq\frac{10}{9}\bigg(\frac{f(k)}{f(k+1)}\bigg)^{\ell}.

We prove (

For

for every

.
Then, fix

and assume that (

with

and

) and (

so using (

which yields (

and

We can thus choose

sufficiently large such that the inequality in (

and

∎

j_{i}
with
i\geq R
in the set
B_{R-1}
are all at least
k_{0}
, so that we do not have to worry about the fact that the exponents of the random variables
G_{j}
can become arbitrarily large, as we can still apply Proposition
5.7
. We can thus continue the iteration to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j\in B}G_{j}^{a_{j}}\right]\leq(2A)^{r-1}\prod_{k=1}^{r-1}\left(\frac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})}\right)^{a_{j_{k}}}\sum_{(t_{2},\ldots,t_{r})}\hskip-2.55005pt\bigg[\prod_{k=2}^{r-1}\binom{a_{k}+t_{k}}{t_{k}}\bigg(D\frac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})}\bigg)^{t_{k}}\bigg]\frac{D^{t_{r}}}{t_{r}!}\mathbb{E}\left[G_{j_{r}}^{t_{r}+a_{j_{r}}}\right],

where again the sum ranges over all non-negative integer tuples
(t_{2},\ldots,t_{r})
such that
0\leq t_{2}\leq a_{j_{1}}
and,
t_{k}
satisfies (
5.40
) for all
3\leq k\leq r
. Applying
Proposition

5.6
to the last expectation gives

\frac{D^{t_{r}}}{t_{r}!}\mathbb{E}\left[G_{j_{r}}^{t_{r}+a_{j_{r}}}\right]\leq\frac{D^{t_{r}}}{t_{r}!}(t_{r}+a_{j_{r}})!\left(\frac{f(j_{r}-1)}{f(j_{r})}\right)^{t_{r}+a_{j_{r}}}A,

which, inserted in the previous bounds, finally yields

(5.41)

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j\in B}G_{j}^{a_{j}}\right]\leq(2A)^{r}\prod_{k=1}^{r}\left(\frac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})}\right)^{a_{j_{k}}}\sum_{(t_{2},\ldots,t_{r})}\hskip-3.00003pt\bigg[\prod_{k=2}^{r}\binom{a_{k}+t_{k}}{t_{k}}\bigg(D\frac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})}\bigg)^{t_{k}}\bigg].

Our goal is to further bound the expression

(5.42)

\sum_{(t_{2},\ldots,t_{r})}\hskip-3.00003pt\bigg[\prod_{k=2}^{r}\binom{a_{k}+t_{k}}{t_{k}}\bigg(D\frac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})}\bigg)^{t_{k}}\bigg]

in (
5.41
).
To do so, note that Assumption (
D
) implies the existence of some
m_{0}=m_{0}(f)\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
j\geq m_{0}
,
D\frac{f(j-1)}{f(j)}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Suppose that
|B\cap[m_{0}]\}|=\ell\leq m_{0}
. Then, for each
k\leq\ell
, the corresponding index
t_{k}
appearing in the sum satisfies

t_{k}\leq a_{j_{k-1}}+t_{k-1}\leq a_{j_{k-1}}+a_{j_{k-2}}+t_{k-2}\leq\cdots\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{j_{i-1}}\leq 2m_{0},

and hence, for each such
k
,

\binom{a_{k}+t_{k}}{t_{k}}\bigg(D\frac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})}\bigg)^{t_{k}}\leq\binom{2m_{0}+2}{2m_{0}}\bigg(\max\bigg\{D\frac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})},1\bigg\}\bigg)^{2m_{0}}.

Moreover, since
j_{k}\leq m_{0}
, the whole term can be bounded by some constant
D^{\prime}=D^{\prime}(f)
.
Thus, each index in
[m_{0}]
contributes a uniformly bounded factor to (
5.42
).
As there are at most
m_{0}
such indices, their total contribution can be absorbed into the constant
C_{d}
in (
5.37
) by multiplying it by
D^{\prime m_{0}}
.
Therefore, without loss of generality, we assume henceforth that
B
has no elements in
[m_{0}]
. Under this assumption, we further bound (
5.42
) by allowing each
t_{k}
to range over
\mathbb{N}_{0}
; note that this allows us to change order of the sum and the product, and it suffices to bound

\prod_{k=2}^{r}\sum_{t_{k}=0}^{\infty}\binom{a_{j_{k}}+t_{k}}{t_{k}}\bigg(D\frac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})}\bigg)^{t_{k}}.

To this end, we use the identity

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\binom{b+m}{m}x^{m}=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{b+1}}\qquad\text{for all}\qquad b\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,x\in[0,1)

which follows from the probability density function of a negative binomial distribution with
b+1
successful trials and success probability
1-x
. Since
D\tfrac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
,

\sum_{(t_{2},\ldots,t_{r})}\hskip-3.00003pt\bigg[\prod_{k=2}^{r}\binom{a_{k}+t_{k}}{t_{k}}\bigg(D\frac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})}\bigg)^{t_{k}}\bigg]\leq\prod_{k=2}^{r}\left(1-D\frac{f(j_{k}-1)}{f(j_{k})}\right)^{-a_{j_{k}}-1}\leq\prod_{k=2}^{r}2^{a_{j_{k}}+1}\leq 8^{r},

where we used that
a_{j}\leq 2
for the last inequality.
Inserting this back into (
5.41
) gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j\in B}G_{j}^{a_{j}}\right]\leq(16A)^{|B|}\prod_{j\in B}\left(\frac{f(j-1)}{f(j)}\right)^{a_{j}}.

Fix now
I\subseteq B
with
|I|=d
, and write the right-hand side as

2^{-|B|}\prod_{j\in I}\left(\frac{f(j-1)}{f(j)}\right)^{a_{j}}\cdot\left[(32A)^{d}\prod_{j\in B\setminus I}32A\left(\frac{f(j-1)}{f(j)}\right)^{a_{j}}\right].

To conclude the proof, we only need to find a bound for the expression within the square brackets which is independent of
B,I
and the sequence
a_{j}
.
To do so, note that
\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{f(j-1)}{f(j)}=0
, so there is
j_{0}=j_{0}(f)
such that
32Af(j-1)/f(j)\leq 1
for all
j\geq j_{0}
. We therefore have

\prod_{j\in B\setminus I}32A\left(\frac{f(j-1)}{f(j)}\right)^{a_{j}}\leq\prod_{j\in(B\setminus I)\cap[j_{0}]}32A\left(\frac{f(j-1)}{f(j)}\right)^{a_{j}}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{j_{0}}\max\bigg\{32A\bigg(\frac{f(j-1)}{f(j)}\bigg)^{2},1\bigg\},

which depends on
f
alone.
Multiplying this last bound by
(32A)^{d}
and
D_{3}^{m_{0}}
gives the required constant
C_{d}
in the statement of the lemma and finishes the proof.
