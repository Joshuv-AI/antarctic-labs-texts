Young Carers' Identification: Comparing Adolescents' and Parents' Perspectives
Authors: Jade Pilato, Géraldine Dorard, Clizia Cincidda, Aurélie Untas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundYoung carers are children or adolescents who provide significant care to another family member who has an illness or disability. This situation impacts their physical and mental health, and their social and school life. Nonetheless, this vulnerable population is often covert, which makes YCs' identification a major challenge. One of the methods in the literature to identify them consists of obtaining the adolescent's report of a relative's illness or disability, coupled with the standardized psychometric evaluation of their level of care. To date, no research had compared adolescents' and parents' perspectives using this method, while this would enable to gain insights on YCs' identification. The objective of this study was to compare parents' and adolescents' reports on the identification of an ill or disabled relative within the household, and adolescents' levels of caring activities.MethodsSurvey data were collected from 776 adolescents (mean age = 12.76 years; 55.50% of females) and one of their parents (mean age = 44.31 years; 83.76% of females). They completed questionnaires evaluating their sociodemographic characteristics, illnesses or disabilities within their household, and adolescents' levels of caring activities (MACA–YC 18). Cohen's κ for interrater agreement, Student's t‐tests and chi‐square tests of independence were performed for child–parent responses comparisons.ResultsFewer adolescents reported that there was an ill or disabled relative within the household compared with their parents. Only 27.56% of the dyads agreed both on the person who was ill or disabled and the illness or disability. The dyads were more often in agreement when the ill or disabled relative had a serious somatic illness and was the mother. On the other hand, adolescents reported engaging in higher levels of caregiving activities than indicated by their parents' assessments.ConclusionThis study highlights the difference of perception between parents and adolescents both on illness and disability reports, and on adolescents' levels of caring activities. These two points of view seem complementary, suggesting that combining adolescents' and parents' perspectives can aid in better identifying young carers.

Young carers (YCs) are children and adolescents under 18 years old who provide or intend to provide significant or substantial care, assistance or support to another family member who has an illness or disability (Becker 2000). This situation impacts their physical and mental health (Lacey, Xue, and McMunn 2022), as well as their social and school life (Moore et al. 2006). Therefore, these youths need to be identified to receive appropriate support. Nonetheless, the identification of YCs is a major challenge, as caring is often covert (Banks et al. 2002). These youths do not always perceive themselves as carers, and their parents are not always aware that their child provide help because caring is part of their daily life and is often seen as normal (Bou 2023; Cass et al. 2009; Clay et al. 2016). Additionally, some YCs are reluctant to disclose their situation because they think it is not socially acceptable (Dearden, Becker, and Aldridge 1995; Jarrige, Dorard, and Untas 2020; Pakenham et al. 2006) or they fear being bullied by their peers (Clay et al. 2016) or stigmatized because of their relative's illness or disability (Pakenham et al. 2006; Stamatopoulos 2015). Parents are also reluctant to disclose the caring situation out of guilt, pride (Dearden, Becker, and Aldridge 1995), or shame (Metzing et al. 2020). YCs and their parents also fear adverse consequences, such as social service interventions (Clay et al. 2016; Dearden, Becker, and Aldridge 1995).

Several studies in the literature have focused on YCs' identification and established prevalences. The figures have varied across studies depending on the methodology used and the sample's characteristics. Specifically, major discrepancies have been found between studies that surveyed the youths themselves and those that surveyed their parents. Studies that surveyed parents showed proportions of YCs between 1.4% (Office for National Statistics 2021) and 3.2% (Hunt, Levine, and Naiditch 2005), whereas research conducted with adolescents found figures mostly ranging from 4.5% (Nagl‐Cupal et al. 2014) to 14.3% (Untas et al. 2022). This suggests that parents identify fewer YCs than the youths. Nonetheless, no study had surveyed both parents and adolescents regarding YCs' identification.

Most of the studies focusing on YCs' identification have relied on adolescents' or parents' disclosure of adolescents' caring status and might not have captured the caring situation because of the different barriers of identification presented above. Hence, Untas et al. (2022) proposed the method of obtaining the adolescent's report of a relative's illness or disability, coupled with the standardized psychometric evaluation of the level of care using a validated tool, the MACA‐YC18. This questionnaire can be used in the general population as a screening tool to evaluate children and adolescents' levels of caring activities (Joseph et al. 2009, 2019). According to this method, YCs are those both facing the illness of a relative and performing high levels of caring activities. However, no study had compared adolescents' and parents' perspectives on their reports of an illness or disability within the household, and adolescents' levels of caring activities. This would enable to better understand the differences in prevalences reported in the literature, and to better understand how adolescents and their parents identify YCs.

The aims of this population‐based study were to compare adolescents' and parents' reports of an illness or disability within the household, and to compare their evaluation of adolescents' levels of caring activities.

Methods

Participants

Adolescents from 13 French secondary schools were included. Pupils needed to sign a consent to participate form, return the parental consent form, and not attend a class dedicated to non‐French‐speaking pupils. In total, 1983 pupils participated in this study. Results for this sample are presented in a previous article (Pilato, Dorard, and Untas 2024). Parents could also participate in this study; one parent was invited to participate but both could take part in the study. Adolescents could participate even if their parent did not participate, and the parent could participate even if their adolescent did not participate. Regarding parents' inclusion criteria, they had to have a level of French proficiency that was sufficient to complete the questionnaire and sign the informed consent document. Initially, 1061 parents were included. Those who had incomplete questionnaires were excluded. For six of the adolescents, both parents participated, and only the answers of the first respondent were considered. The adolescents whose parent did not participate, and parents whose adolescent did not participate, were excluded from the analyses, resulting in a subsample of 776 adolescent–parent dyads. The analyses presented below focus on these 776 adolescent–parent dyads.

Procedure

The study was introduced to adolescents during class; they were given information notes and consent forms. The following week, adolescents who had returned the signed parental consent form and agreed to participate completed the questionnaire during class under the supervision of a researcher on electronic tablets, which took approximately 35 min. For parents, a link to an online questionnaire was sent via email to those who agreed to participate, and their questionnaire took approximately 30 min to complete.

Materials

Adolescents' Self‐Report Questionnaires

Sociodemographic Characteristics

A sociodemographic questionnaire was used to collect information about adolescents' age, gender, school grade, family living arrangement, siblings, another language spoken at home apart from French, and the perceived financial status of the family.

Illness or Disability in the Family

Adolescents answered questions about the health of relatives within the household. They had to indicate whether they had any relative at home with a severe or chronic somatic illness (e.g., cancer, diabetes, multiple sclerosis), a psychological problem (e.g., depression, schizophrenia), drug or alcohol abuse, a disability (e.g., blindness, paraplegy), or any other health issue. The illnesses or disabilities were then recoded manually into five categories: severe somatic illness (e.g., cancer, multiple sclerosis), chronic somatic illness (e.g., diabetes, high blood pressure), psychological problem (e.g., depression, substance abuse), physical or sensorial disability (e.g., paraplegy, blindness), and any other health issue (not specified). The ‘severe somatic illness’ category included illnesses that were either life‐threatening, required invasive treatments, and/or had major impacts on daily life, while the ‘chronic somatic illnesses’ category included more indolent or manageable conditions.

Adolescents' Illness or Disability

Adolescents had to indicate whether they had a severe or chronic somatic illness (e.g., cancer, diabetes, multiple sclerosis), a psychological problem (e.g., depression, schizophrenia), drug or alcohol abuse, a disability (e.g., blindness, paraplegy), or any other health issue. The illnesses or disabilities were then recoded manually into five categories: severe somatic illness (e.g., cancer, multiple sclerosis), chronic somatic illness (e.g., diabetes, high blood pressure), psychological problem (e.g., depression, substance abuse), physical or sensorial disability (e.g., paraplegy, blindness) and any other health issue (not specified).

Caregiving Activities

The Multidimensional Assessment of Caring Activities (MACA–YC 18) is an 18‐item validated questionnaire used to assess the amount of caring activities undertaken by children and adolescents between eight and 21 years old (Joseph et al. 2009). It uses three‐point Likert‐type scales ranging from never to always. It provides a total score ranging from 0 to 36 and six sub‐scores ranging from 0 to 6 evaluating six domains: domestic tasks, household management, financial and practical help, personal care, emotional support and sibling care. This questionnaire has been used in a previous study; a score equal to or above 15 on the scale was considered high (Pilato, Dorard, and Untas 2024). The questionnaire has been validated in French (Chevrier et al. 2022), and it showed fairly good internal consistency in our sample (Cronbach's α = 0.76).

Parents' Self‐Report Questionnaires

Sociodemographic Characteristics

A sociodemographic questionnaire was used to collect information about parents' age, gender, level of education, occupational status and job. The jobs were recoded manually based on their proximity to a medical environment, as this might have biased the illness or disability reports. The jobs we therefore classified into three categories: medical job, medically oriented job, or another type of job.

Illness or Disability in the Family

Parents answered questions about the health of relatives within the household. They had to indicate whether they had any relative at home with a severe or chronic somatic illness (e.g., cancer, diabetes, multiple sclerosis), a psychological problem (e.g., depression, schizophrenia), drug or alcohol abuse, a disability (e.g., blindness, paraplegy), or any other health issue. The illnesses or disabilities were then recoded manually into five categories: severe somatic illness (e.g., cancer, multiple sclerosis), chronic somatic illness (e.g., diabetes, high blood pressure), psychological problem (e.g., depression, substance abuse), physical or sensorial disability (e.g., paraplegy, blindness) and any other health issue (not specified).

Parents' Illness or Disability

Parents had to indicate whether they had a severe or chronic somatic illness (e.g., cancer, diabetes, multiple sclerosis), a psychological problem (e.g., depression, schizophrenia), drug or alcohol abuse, a disability (e.g., blindness, paraplegy), or any other health issue. The illnesses or disabilities were then recoded manually into five categories: severe somatic illness (e.g., cancer, multiple sclerosis), chronic somatic illness (e.g., diabetes, high blood pressure), psychological problem (e.g., depression, substance abuse), physical or sensorial disability (e.g., paraplegy, blindness) and any other health issue (not specified).

Caregiving Activities

The MACA–YC 18 was adapted for proxy use. The proxy version showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.80), and the six‐factor model (i.e., domestic tasks, household management, financial and practical help, personal care, emotional support and sibling care) yielded a good fit to the data according to conventional criteria (Hooper, Coughlan, and Mullen 2008): χ2 (120) = 141.941, p = 0.084, root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.019 [0.000–0.030], comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.998, Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) = 0.998, and weighted root mean square residual (WRMR) = 0.869.

Analyses

Descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were performed on the adolescent and parent samples. To compare adolescents who identified an ill or disabled relative within the household to those who did not, Student's t‐tests and chi squared tests of independence were performed on their sociodemographic characteristics (adolescent's age, adolescent's gender, the language spoken at home, school grade, family living arrangement, siblings, adolescent's perceived financial status of the family), adolescent's health status and adolescents' levels of caring activities. To compare parents who identified an ill or disabled relative within the household to those who did not, again, Student's t‐tests and chi squared tests of independence were performed on their sociodemographic characteristics (parent's age, parent's gender, parent's occupational status, job type, parent's level of education) and adolescents' level of caring activities.

To assess adolescents' and parents' agreement on their report of illnesses or disabilities within the household, Cohen's κ, enabling interrater agreement for categorical items, was used.

Specific analyses were performed on the dyads who identified an ill or disabled relative within the household, either identified by the adolescent, the parent, or both. Dyads were recoded into two categories: dyads who agreed and dyads who disagreed. We considered that adolescents and their parents were in agreement when they both agreed on who was ill or disabled and what the illness or disability was. When more than one ill or disabled relative was identified, they were considered in agreement when they at least agreed on one relative. Student's t‐tests and chi squared tests of independence were conducted to compare the dyads who agreed with the dyads who disagreed. These tests were performed according to adolescents' sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, language spoken at home, school grade, family living arrangement, siblings, perceived financial status of the family), adolescents' health status, parents' sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, occupational status, type of job, level of education), the person who was ill or disabled, and the type of illness or disability. Student's t‐tests were then conducted to compare parents' and adolescents' evaluations of adolescents' levels of caring activities.

Chi squared tests were only conducted if the expected count in each cell was above 5 (Sharpe 2015). Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 28.0; SPSS Inc.) and the lavaan package of R 4.1.2.

Results

The Samples' Characteristics

Regarding adolescents' characteristics, the mean age was 12.76 years (SD = 1.08); 11.21% were in Year 7, 31.32% were in Year 8, 31.44% were in Year 9, and 26.03% were in Year 10. The gender ratio was balanced; slightly more than half of the participants were females (55.50%). Most participants had siblings (92.40%), and most of them lived with both their parents (69.20%). Concerning their health status, 16.11% of adolescents reported having a health issue.

Regarding parents' characteristics, the mean age was 44.31 years (SD = 5.48). Mostly mothers took part in the study (83.76%). In terms of their occupation, 78.53% reported having a full‐time job. More adolescents' and parents' characteristics are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

Reports of Illnesses or Disabilities Within the Household

Adolescents' Reports

Out of the 776 adolescents surveyed, 20.49% (N = 159) reported that there was an ill or disabled relative within the household. The ill or disabled relative was mainly the mother (N = 78; 49.06%), followed by the father (N = 61; 38.36%), a sibling (N = 41; 25.79%) and a grandparent (N = 13; 8.18%). Concerning the type of illness, 18.87% (N = 30) reported a serious somatic illness, 48.43% (N = 77) reported a chronic somatic illness, 27.67% (N = 44) reported a psychological problem, 5.03% (N = 8) reported a physical or sensorial disability, and 21.38% (N = 34) reported another health issue. These results are presented in Table 1.

In comparison with the group that did not identify an ill or disabled relative within the household, the adolescents that did were older (p = 0.019), more likely to speak another language at home (p = 0.048), had more siblings (p = 0.025), were more likely to have an illness or disability themselves (p < 0.001), and perceived their family's financial status as worse than their peers (p = 0.043). No differences were found in gender, school grade and family living arrangement (see Table 1).

Parents' Reports

Out of the 776 parents surveyed, 33.76% (N = 262) reported that there was someone ill or disabled within the household, including themselves. The ill or disabled relative was mainly the mother (N = 143; 54.58%), followed by the father (N = 94; 35.88%), a sibling (N = 73; 27.86%) and a grandparent (N = 9; 3.44%). In terms of the type of illness, 25.19% (N = 66) reported a serious somatic illness, 47.71% (N = 125) reported a chronic somatic illness, 29.39% (N = 77) reported a psychological problem, 4.96% (N = 13) reported a physical or sensorial disability and 12.60% (N = 33) reported another health issue. These results are presented in Table 2.

In comparison with the group that did not identify an ill or disabled relative within the household, the parents that did were older (p = 0.025), more likely to have a part‐time job (p = 0.007) and more likely to be unemployed (p = 0.002). No differences were found in parents' gender or level of education (see Table 2).

Discussion

This is the first population‐based study to compare adolescents' and parents' perspectives on the identification of an illness or disability within the household and the help provided by adolescents. The results highlight the difference in reports between adolescents and parents. While fewer adolescents reported the presence of an ill or disabled relative at home compared with their parents, they nonetheless reported engaging in higher levels of caregiving activities when contrasted with their parents' assessments. This research brings new insights into YCs' identification and highlights the relevance of combining adolescents' and parents' views to better capture the caring situations.

Even if no study in the literature has compared adolescents' and parents' perspectives to investigate the presence of an ill or disabled relative within the household, some literature has explored children's exposure to life stressors and trauma and their exposure to severe illness through a short evaluation that did not specify the type of illness or the person who was ill. A study focusing on exposure to potentially traumatic events showed that youth aged 7–16 years reported less exposure to serious illnesses than their parents (Oransky, Hahn, and Stover 2013), whereas another study on 6‐ to 18‐year‐olds showed that youth reported more exposure to illnesses than their parents (Skar, Jensen, and Harpviken 2021). A study focusing on 12‐ to 17‐year‐old adolescents' experiences with adverse life events assessed adolescents' confrontation to family mental health problems and substance abuse, and found that youth reported fewer mental health problems and substance abuse in the family than their parents, whereas there was good concordance in their reports on other life events, such as financial problems, parental discord, or divorce (Kushner and Tackett 2017).

Few dyads agreed both on the ill or disabled relative and the illness or disability, with adolescents reporting less often the presence of an ill or disabled relative at home. This low agreement rate might be attributed to poor communication. The literature on communication about serious somatic illnesses in parents (e.g., cancer, HIV) has shown that parents are often reluctant to disclose their illness to their minor children. A study among 18 patients with cancer showed that only four disclosed their diagnosis to their minor children (Wang et al. 2020). Research on patients with HIV showed that 77% of participants had not disclosed their HIV status to their children by the time of the study (Zhou et al. 2013), and a study on parental multiple sclerosis showed that only 53.6% had disclosed their diagnosis and its implications to their children (Paliokosta et al. 2009). Regarding siblings, the literature has mainly focused on genetic syndromes or cancer and showed that diagnoses are sometimes only disclosed several years later (Sulmonte et al. 2021) or gradually disclosed (Okashah et al. 2015; Read et al. 2011). Moreover, some topics are sometimes left unspoken (Lövgren et al. 2020) resulting in children failing to grasp the severity or implications of their sibling's illness (Okashah et al. 2015; Read et al. 2011). Therefore, children might not be fully informed about the illness or disability, which might explain the low agreement rate. Concerning the reasons not to disclose a diagnosis, the literature has shown that parents want to protect their children. They are afraid of causing them psychological burdens (Zhou et al. 2013). Nonetheless, the literature has shown that even when parents do not disclose their health status, their children are often aware that something is wrong through their own observations, and the absence of information leads to misinterpretations and causal inferences that result in guilt, feelings of regret, and worries (Dalton et al. 2019). Stigma associated with the illness is another reason not to disclose it. Parents fear that either they or their child might be stigmatized (Zhou et al. 2013). Similarly, some adolescents might be aware of but reluctant to disclose their family health problems because of stigma (Bou 2023; Pakenham et al. 2006), especially in a classroom setting. This might explain the low agreement rate for more observable illnesses or disabilities in our study, especially physical and sensorial disabilities that adolescents would be expected to be aware of.

Comparison analyses showed differences between the dyads who agreed and those who did not. Dyads agreed more when the adolescent was older. This might be due to the fact that older adolescents are more able to identify and label illnesses or disabilities, and their parents more willing to communicate with them about the illness or disability. Indeed, adolescents in our study identified more illnesses when they were older. This is consistent with the literature showing that one of the main reasons for parents not disclosing their own or a sibling's diagnosis is that their healthy child is not old enough (Boyer et al. 2023; Dalton et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2020).

Comparison analyses also showed that dyads agreed more when the relative had a serious illness. This is in line with the literature on illness communication showing that the severity of an illness is a reason to disclose it (Dalton et al. 2019). That is, when an illness is serious, one cannot avoid talking about it because the illness is life threatening, is more visible, or disrupts the family routines. Our results can also be explained by the fact that when an illness is not severe, it might be considered common, so adolescents might not have thought to mention it in their questionnaire. Conversely, this pattern was not observed for psychological problems. Here, the literature on children facing a parental mental illness has shown that children perceive that their parent's illness is a secret, so they conceal it to protect the parent or because they think their peers will not understand (Dam and Hall 2016). They also do not disclose mental illness because of the stigma associated with it (Dam and Hall 2016). Additionally, mental illness is often less observable, so adolescents might not be aware of it if the diagnosis is not disclosed and explained. In this regard, the literature has shown that adolescents often lack information about parental mental illness (Mordoch 2010). In another study, the 8–15‐year‐old children of parents with psychosis were found to have a poor understanding of their parent's illness and were unable to distinguish the symptoms from their parent's personality (Boström and Strand 2021).

It was also found that the adolescent–parent dyads agreed more when the ill or disabled relative was the mother. This is because mothers might be more willing to talk about their health problems. This gender difference was highlighted in a previous study focusing on chronic illness disclosure at work (Munir et al. 2006). Furthermore, a large proportion of participants were mothers, and the parents who participated might have been more interested in this topic and more willing to communicate about their health problems with their children.

Interestingly, our results showed that the dyads agreed more when the adolescents reported that they themselves had an illness or disability, especially a chronic somatic illness. Adolescents might be more aware of health problems when they themselves have an illness, and communication about these topics at home might be more open. Our results showed that the dyads were more likely to agree when the adolescent spoke another language at home apart from French, suggesting that the identification or disclosure of illness and disability differs across cultures. This could be attributed to differences in communication on these topics. Cultural aspects related to the identification and communication on illness or disability need to be further explored. Surprisingly, parents' type of job did not influence the dyads' agreement, although one might have expected that when the parent was a healthcare professional, the dyads would agree more since communication about illness and disability in the family would be more open.

Regarding adolescents' caring activities among households with an ill or disabled relative, adolescents reported higher levels than their parents. This is the first study to compare adolescents' and parents' perspectives on adolescents' levels of caring activities. As expected, adolescents who identified an illness or disability within the household reported higher levels of caring activities compared to those who did not. Interestingly, parents who identified an illness or disability within the household did not report higher levels of adolescent caring activities. This may have been because parents might not notice the help their adolescent provides or might be reluctant to disclose it. In this regard, the literature has shown that parents are not always aware that their children provide help and regard their responsibilities as normal (Clay et al. 2016). They can also be reluctant to disclose it out of guilt, pride (Dearden, Becker, and Aldridge 1995), or shame (Metzing et al. 2020) or because they fear adverse consequences, such as the intervention of social services (Clay et al. 2016). Adolescents might also have overreported their caring activities at home. It would be interesting to conduct interviews with the dyads to better understand these differences.

Limitations

The first limitation of this study is that the setting might have influenced adolescents' reports. Adolescents completed the questionnaire in class with their peers. Although participants had to complete the questionnaire on their own and confidentially, they might have underreported on illnesses or disabilities because they were reluctant to disclose information about their family's health in this context. The second limitation is the low participation rate of fathers. Indeed, one of the parents was invited to participate, and it was mostly the mothers who took part in our study, which is often the case in research involving their children. Future research should increase fathers' participation by inviting both parents to participate or by specifically targeting fathers. Nonetheless, these strategies should avoid being stigmatizing for single‐parent families or same‐gender families. The third limitation is that the parents who participated were either the ill or disabled relative or the healthy parent, which might have influenced their disclosure. The fourth limitation is related to our classification of illnesses and disabilities. We distinguished between ‘severe somatic illness’ and ‘chronic somatic illness’. In the first category, we classified illnesses such as cancer or Alzheimer disease, whereas we placed diabetes or high blood pressure in the second. The ‘severe illness’ category referred to illnesses that were either life‐threatening, required invasive treatments, and/or had major impacts on daily life, while the ‘chronic illness’ category included more indolent or controllable diseases. Nonetheless, the label ‘severe’ is questionable, as well as the classification. For instance, a cancer can be indolent and a type 1 diabetes difficult to control and very impactful. Further studies should use an evaluation of the functional impacts of the illness/disability, to create more objective categories.

Conclusion

The results of this study underline the differences between adolescents and parents in their perceptions on the identification of ill or disabled household members and adolescents' levels of caring activities. Fewer adolescents reported the presence of an ill or disabled relative at home in comparison with their parents. However, they reported engaging in higher levels of caring activities than suggested by their parents' assessments. Parents and adolescents were more often in agreement when the ill or disabled relative had a serious illness and was the mother. This research brings new insights into the barriers to YCs' identification. The identification of YCs based only on either adolescents' or parents' disclosure is not satisfactory. Therefore, school professionals and educators should not only rely on families' spontaneous disclosure. They should be trained to identify weak signals of YC situations, bearing in mind that adolescents have difficulties in identifying or reporting illnesses and disabilities. Future studies should more thoroughly investigate the differences in illness or disability reports using a qualitative approach. This research could focus on aspects such as illness communication and the disclosure of diagnoses within the family, alongside examining adolescents' perceptions and understanding of the illness or disability. As this is an exploratory study, our results should be interpreted with caution and further confirmatory studies are needed.
