Filter Quotient Model Structures
Nima Rasekh

Abstract
. The filter quotient construction is a particular instance of a filtered colimit of categories. It has primarily been considered in the context of categorical logic, where it has been used effectively to construct non-trivial models, for example new models of set theory. In this work we prove that given a model category and a suitable notion of filter of subterminal objects, the filter quotient construction will preserve the model structure. We also show that this new model structure inherits certain important properties (such as being simplicial or proper), but not all (such as being cofibrantly generated). Finally, we show it is compatible with the construction of filter quotient ∞ \infty -categories [ Ras21 ] .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Model Categories in Homotopy Theory

Homotopy theory arises whenever we study mathematical objects that exhibit a non-trivial notion of equivalence, or “sameness”. This first manifested in the study of topological spaces, with the notion of (weak) homotopy equivalence of topological spaces, which in general is weaker than a homeomorphism. From there it generalized to quasi-isomorphisms of chain complexes, and then to many other mathematical objects in algebra and geometry.

Applying a mathematical lens to this situation suggests the need for a formal structure that can capture such mathematical objects along with their equivalences. This was first achieved by Quillen. In
[
Qui67
]
, he defined
model categories
as an abstract category along with a chosen class of morphisms that are “weak equivalences”, along with further data that permits an effective handling of this data.

Since then, many mathematicians have used the model categorical framework to study equivalences of interest. Examples include the study of stable homotopy theory and spectra
[
EKMM95
]
, the study of motivic homotopy theory
[
Voe98
]
, the study of
\infty
-category theory
[
Rez01
]
, and the study of type theories
[
KL21
]
. In all of these cases the structure of a model category has non-trivially contributed towards a better understanding of the mathematical objects and the equivalences in question.

1.2.
New Model Categories from Old

Unfortunately, the model categorical framework comes at a price. A model category involves significant amount of data and numerous conditions, making it difficult to construct new model structures just out of a category and a desired class of equivalences. This has resulted in a variety of methods that permit obtaining new model structures from existing ones.

(1)

Induced (transferred) model categories:
Given a suitable model category and a left (or right) adjoint functor
F\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathscr{C}
, if
\mathcal{M},F,\mathscr{C}
satisfy certain conditions, we can construct a model structure on
\mathscr{C}
, that is “induced” or “transferred” from
\mathcal{M}
, meaning that the weak equivalences and cofibrations (or fibrations) of
\mathscr{C}
are determined via the functor
F
. Induced model structures have been used for decades
[
Cra95
,
GS07
]
, however, recently we have seen various refinements generalizing previous results
[
BHK+15
,
HKRS17
,
GKR20
]
.

(2)

Functor model categories:
Given a suitable model category
\mathcal{M}
and an arbitrary category
\mathscr{C}
, we can use a variation of inducing model structures to construct two model structures on the functor category
\mathrm{Fun}(\mathscr{C},\mathcal{M})
, known as
projective model structures
and
injective model structures

[
Hel82
]
. In recent years we have seen generalizations of these methods to a variety of settings, such as enriched model structures
[
Mos19
]
.

(3)

Reedy model categories:
Given a general model category
\mathcal{M}
and a
Reedy category

\mathcal{R}
, we can construct the
Reedy model structure
on the functor category
\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{M})

[
Ree74
]
. This approach has since been generalized to the enriched setting
[
Ang08
,
GK19
]
, and also to more general diagram categories, known as
generalized Reedy categories

[
Cis06
,
BM11
]

(4)

Bousfield localizations:
Given a suitable model category
\mathcal{M}
and a choice of cofibrations (or fibrations) one can define a
left Bousfield localization
(or
right Bousfield localization
) on the same category
[
Bou75
]
. This approach has since been generalized to the enriched case
[
Bar10
]
, and the monoidal case
[
Whi22
]
.

Except for the very particular case of functor categories on Reedy categories, all the other examples have one thing in common: the underlying category is locally presentable (or at least accessible), and the model structure is cofibrantly generated. This leaves the situation for other, non-accessible, model structures largely unexplored. We can witness these challenges in specific instances, where the authors aim to directly construct model structures on finite categories (finite set of objects, finite set of morphisms)
[
BOOR23
]
, or induce it via a chosen functor to posets
[
DZ15
]
. In this paper we introduce new methods to construct model structures without any presentability or accessibility requirement.

1.3.
Model Categories and Presentable
\infty
-Categories

Before proceeding to a new method to construct model structures, it is instructive to better comprehend the historical focus on cofibrantly generated model structures. Initially this focus might appear restrictive. However, the advent of
\infty
-categorical methods provided a proper justification for the current state of affairs.
\infty
-category theory is an abstract concept that has been made precise via
models of
\infty
-categories
, all of which are equivalent in a suitable way and generalize classical categories. This includes categorical structures with a weak notion of composition, such as quasi-categories
[
Joy08
]
, as well as categories with an abstract notion of weak equivalences
[
BK12
]
. In recent decades we have seen significant effort developing category theory in the context of
\infty
-categories as a suitable setting for homotopy theory, paralleling and often surpassing analogous developments in model category theory
[
Lur09
,
RV22
]
.

Every model category comes with an
underlying
\infty
-category
. Not every
\infty
-category can be recovered as the underlying
\infty
-category of a model category, indeed any such
\infty
-category necessarily has some (co)limits, however many examples of interest, such as the
\infty
-category of homotopy types, can be constructed this way, and it is natural to wonder which
\infty
-categories can be obtained this way.

Among all
\infty
-categories, a special role is played by
presentable
\infty
-categories
, which are cocomplete
\infty
-categories that are generated by a small set of objects, and include many relevant examples of interest, such as algebras for an operad, spectra, even
\infty
-categories themselves
[
Lur09
,
Lur17
]
. It is a crucial observation, due to Dugger
[
Dug01
]
, that every presentable
\infty
-category can in fact be obtained as the underlying
\infty
-category of a cofibrantly generated model category. Hence, if the aim is to study presentable
\infty
-categories, then, without loss of generality, we can focus exclusively on cofibrantly generated model categories. Moreover, non-cofibrantly generated model categories, which capture non-presentable
\infty
-categories, cannot hope to recover all remaining
\infty
-categories, and hence cannot assume a similarly central role.

1.4.
Model Categories and Type Theories

Given the innate connection between cofibrantly generated model categories and presentable
\infty
-categories, introducing new methods to construct model structures that are not necessarily cofibrantly generated necessitates a proper justification. Here it is instructive to revisit the connection between model categories and type theories.
Type theory
is a foundation for mathematics in which mathematical objects and statements about them are both represented by types and terms therein. This property has made type theory a particularly useful foundation for proof assistants, which are programming languages that can be used to write and verify mathematical proofs. As a result, many prominent proof assistants, such as Rocq or Lean, use a certain type theoretical foundation in its formalization
[
CH86
]
.

While the syntactical nature of type theories are a powerful framework in the context of computers, human users often benefit from a more traditional, i.e. semantic, understanding of type theoretical statements. This has resulted in various soundness and completeness results and more generally a syntax-semantics duality, in which for a given type theory one introduces and studies a precise definition of a
model of a type theory
, as a suitably structured category. The existence of such a model gives us a soundness result and guarantees that the type theoretical statements permit a meaningful interpretation. Completeness results demarcate what results can or cannot be proven in the type theory. There are now many examples of such soundness and completeness results, relating type theories (such as dependent type theories with
\Pi
-types) and categorical models (such as locally Cartesian closed categories)
[
See83
,
See84
,
LS88
,
Hof95
,
CGH14
,
CD14
]
.

In recent decades we have seen the rise of
homotopy type theory
, as a type theory with a richer notion of identity, that is particularly suitable for the formalization of homotopical statements
[
Uni13
]
. One early realization in this realm is that suitable model categories can in fact provide models for homotopy type theory
[
KL21
]
, hence giving us soundness results in this setting. This has resulted in a series of papers constructing more and more intricate models for homotopy type theory
[
AK11
,
Shu15
,
LLS20
,
Shu19
]
.

While this development has been a tremendous success, all models of homotopy type theory constructed this way have one thing in common: they are cofibrantly generated
1
1

1

In fact a
type-theoretic model topos
, which is a model categorical model of homotopy type theory due to Shulman includes cofibrant generation as part of its definition
[
Shu19
]
.
. However, the analogy with other (non-homotopical) type theories suggest there should indeed be models that do not satisfy such conditions. Hence, to tackle such a topic directly requires developing suitable model categorical tools that permit the construction of new model structures that do not rely on any presentability or cofibrant generation assumption. This is the aim of this paper.

1.5.
Filter Quotient Model Structures

The aim of this paper is to introduce a novel technique for constructing new model structures from a given model structure without any presentability or accessibility requirement. The approach relies on the
filter quotient
construction, which we review first.

Given a category
\mathscr{C}
with finite products and a suitable set of subterminal objects
\Phi
2
2

2

An object
C
is subterminal if the unique morphism to the terminal object is a mono.
, we can construct a new category
\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
, known as the

, which has the same objects as
\mathscr{C}
, and where morphisms are given by equivalence classes of morphisms in
\mathscr{C}
, via an equivalence relation generated by
\Phi
. By construction it comes with a projection functor

, which maps a morphism to its equivalence class. See

2.2
for a detailed review.

In this paper we show that if the original category is a model category
\mathcal{M}
, then under suitable conditions on
\mathcal{M}
and
\Phi
, which does not include any accessibility or cofibrant generation, the filter quotient category
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
also admits a model structure (
Theorem

3.16
). Moreover, the projection functor
P_{\Phi}
preserves many important model categorical properties. This includes weak equivalences, (co)fibrations, finite (co)limits (
Section

4.1
), Cartesian closure (
Section

4.2
), properness (
Section

4.4
), and being simplicial or enriched (
Section

4.5
).

Having constructed filter quotient model categories, we also show compatibility by proving that the underlying
\infty
-category of the filter quotient model category recovers the already studied
filter quotient
\infty
-category

[
Ras21
]
(
Section

5
). We culminate the paper with a careful analysis of examples, both classes of examples, namely
filter products
(
Section

6
), as well as specific examples (
Section

7
).

In light of the previous subsection, having constructed filter quotient model categories, we might wonder if they indeed provide us with new type-theoretical models. In two follow-up papers, we indeed prove that the filter quotient construction can be used to construct new and non-trivial models of various homotopy type theories of interest
[
Ras25a
,
Ras25b
]
.

2.1.
Model Categories à la Quillen

Model category theory has been studied very extensively in the past decades, resulting in many detailed references
[
Qui67
,
Hov99
,
Hir03
,
Bal21
]
. However, various definitions of model categories presented in these citations have subtle differences, which will be of utmost importance to us. In this section we will hence explicate the relevant definitions that exhibit such nuances.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a category. A
model structure
on
\mathcal{M}
is a triple
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{W})
, called the
fibrations
,
cofibrations
and
weak equivalences
(where
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{W}
are named trivial fibrations and
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{W}
are named trivial cofibrations), respectively, such that the following conditions hold:

(1)

Lifting:
Any commutative square with the left hand map a cofibration, and right hand map fibration, at least one is also a weak equivalence, admits a lift.

(2)

Factorization:
Every morphism admits two factorizations: a cofibration followed by a trivial fibration and a trivial cofibration followed by a fibration.

(3)

Retract:
The three classes are closed under retracts.

(4)

2
-out-of-
3
:
For two composable morphisms
f,g
, if
2
out of
f,g,g\circ f
are in
\mathcal{W}
then so is the third.

Definition 2.2
.

A
model category
is a category
\mathcal{M}
with finite limits and colimits and a model structure
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{W})
.

Warning 2.3
.

This definition coincides with
[
Qui67
]
, but differs with
[
Hov99
,
Hir03
,
Bal21
]
and many other modern sources, where model categories are assumed to be (co)complete.

While the definition of model category differs from many other sources, most common model categorical constructions (properness, Cartesian closure) still coincide. One major difference in the definition of model categories à la Quillen manifests in the context of enriched (and particularly simplicial) model categories, which we hence review here.

In this next definition
V^{cmpt}
denotes the full subcategory of compact objects in
\mathscr{V}
, where a
compact object
is an object
K
such that the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathscr{V}}(K,-)
commutes with filtered colimits.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(\mathscr{V},I_{\mathscr{V}},\otimes_{\mathscr{V}})
be a monoidal model category and
\mathcal{M}
a model category. A
compactly enriched structure
on
\mathcal{M}
consists of the following data:

(1)

\mathcal{M}
is enriched over
\mathscr{V}
, meaning for two objects

, we have an object in
\mathscr{V}
, denoted
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(X,Y)
, called the

, such that

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathscr{V}}(I_{\mathscr{V}},\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(X,Y))=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{M}}(X,Y)

along with unital and associative composition maps

\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(X,Y)\otimes_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(Y,Z)\to\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(X,Z)

in
\mathscr{V}
, for three objects
X,Y,Z
in
\mathcal{M}
.

(2)

Two functors:

•

Tensor:

-\otimes-\colon\mathcal{M}\times\mathscr{V}^{cmpt}\to\mathcal{M}

•

Cotensor:

((-))^{(-)}\colon(\mathscr{V}^{cmpt})^{op}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}

along with three isomorphisms, natural in
X,Y
in
\mathcal{M}
and
K
in
\mathscr{V}^{cmpt}

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{M}}(X\otimes K,Z)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{M}}(X,Y^{K})\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathscr{V}}(K,\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(X,Y))

(3)

For every cofibration
i\colon A\to B
and fibration
p\colon Y\to X
, the induced map

(i^{*},p_{*})\colon\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(B,Y)\to\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(B,X)\times_{\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(A,X)}\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}}(A,Y)

is a fibration, and is trivial if either
i
or
p
is trivial.

We can in particular consider this definition for
\mathscr{V}=s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
with the Kan model structure.

Definition 2.5
.

A
simplicial model category
is a compactly enriched model category
\mathcal{M}
over
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
with the Kan model structure.

More explicitly, the compact objects in

are the

, meaning finite colimits of representables

. Hence in the case of simplicial sets, simplicial model structures coincide with the original definition of Quillen
[
Qui67
]
and differ from more modern references, which would assume (co)tensor for all objects
[
Hov99
,
Hir03
]
.

2.2.
Filter Quotients

Filter quotient categories have been studied extensively in the context of topos theory
[
Joh02
,
MLM94
]
. Here we review basic definitions and results.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
(I,\leq)
be a poset. A
filter

\Phi
is a subset of
I
that satisfies the following three properties:

•

Non-emptiness:

\Phi
is non-empty.

•

Upward closure:
For every

and

x\leq y
, then
y\in\Phi
.

•

Finite intersection:
For every
x,y\in\Phi
, there exists
z\in\Phi
such that
z\leq x
and
z\leq y
.

We now use filters to construct categories. Recall that in a category
\mathscr{C}
with terminal object, an object
U
is
subterminal
if the unique morphism to the terminal object is a mono.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a category with finite products. A
filter of subterminal objects
on
\mathscr{C}
is a filter on the poset of subterminal objects of
\mathscr{C}
.

Given a filter of subterminal objects, we can construct new categories.

.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a category with finite products and
\Phi
be a filter of subterminal objects. The

•

Objects:
The objects of
\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
are the objects of

.

•

Morphisms:
The morphisms between two objects

and

are given by equivalence classes of morphisms from

to

, where

is in
\Phi
. Here two morphisms

and

are equivalent if there exists

in
\Phi
, such that the following diagram commutes:

The filter quotient category

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a category with finite products and
\Phi

.

(2)

.

\mathscr{C}

.

3.
Filter Quotient Model Categories

We now prove that in suitable situations, filter quotients preserve model structures.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category. An object
U
is
homotopically subterminal
if it is fibrant and the diagonal map
\Delta\colon U\to U\times U
is a weak equivalence.

Example 3.2
.

The terminal object
1
is always homotopically subterminal.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category. A homotopically subterminal object
U
is called
discrete
if it is also subterminal in
\mathcal{M}
.

Example 3.4
.

Let
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Kan}
be the Kan model structure on simplicial sets. Then a fibrant simplicial set
K
(a Kan complex) is homotopically subterminal if it is empty or contractible. Among those the discrete ones are
\Delta^{0}
and
\emptyset
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a simplicial model category and
U
be a fibrant object. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

U
is homotopically subterminal.

(2)

For every cofibrant object
X
, the Kan complex
\mathrm{Map}(X,U)
is homotopically subterminal.

Proof.

Direct implication of the fact that a morphism in
\mathcal{M}
is a weak equivalence if and only if for every cofibrant object
X
, the functor
\mathrm{Map}(X,-)
takes it to a Kan equivalence.
∎

Definition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category. Define
\mathscr{S}\mathrm{ub}^{\mathrm{fib}}(\mathcal{M})
as the poset with elements discrete homotopically subterminal objects and

if
\mathrm{Hom}(U,V)
is non-empty.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category. A

is a filter in the poset
\mathscr{S}\mathrm{ub}^{\mathrm{fib}}(\mathcal{M})

Remark 3.8
.

The non-emptiness and

.

Given a model category
\mathcal{M}
and any filter of subterminal object

\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category and
\Phi
be a filter of discrete homotopical subterminal objects. A class of morphisms

is called
\Phi
-product stable
, if for every

in
S
and
U
in
\Phi
,
f\times U
is in
S
.

Notation 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category and
\Phi
a filter of discrete homotopical subterminal objects. Let
S
be a class of morphisms that is
\Phi
-product stable. Let
S_{\Phi}
denote the class of morphisms
f
in
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
with the property that there exists a
U
in
\Phi
, such that
f\times U
is in
S
.

Remark 3.11
.

Let us make some observations about
3.10
.

(1)

If
f
is in
S_{\Phi}
with the assumption that
f\times U
is in
S
, then for all

,

is also in
S
, as
S
is
\Phi
-product stable.

(2)

The characterization is independent of a representative, as for
f,f^{\prime}\in[f]
,
f\times U=f^{\prime}\times U
for sufficiently large
U
in
\Phi
.

(3)

The map

takes a morphism
f
in
S
to a morphism in
S_{\Phi}
, as
f\times 1
is in
S
.

\Phi
-product stable.

g
are weak equivalences, then so is
gf
, the other two cases are analogous. Pick
W_{1}
in
\Phi
, such that
f\times W_{1}
is a weak equivalence and
W_{2}
, such that
g\times W_{2}
is a weak equivalence in
\mathcal{M}
. Now let
W\leq W_{1},W_{2}
in
\Phi
. Then
gf\times W
is a weak equivalence in
\mathcal{M}
, and so
gf
is a weak equivalence in
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
.

∎

Remark 3.17
.

The functor
P_{\Phi}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
preserves (co)fibrations and weak equivalences, however is not left or right Quillen, as it is not a left or right adjoint.

4.
From the original to the filter quotient

In this section we aim to discuss which classical model categorical properties are preserved via the filter quotient construction. For every case that fails we present a counter-example.

4.1.
Limits and Colimits

We have already established that
P_{\Phi}
preserves finite (co)limit diagrams and hence preserves finite (co)completeness (
Theorem

3.16
). However, this does not generalize to infinite (co)completeness. For an explicit counter-example see
Example

7.1
.

4.2.
Cartesian Closure

We will prove
P_{\Phi}
preserves internal mapping objects, and hence
P_{\Phi}
preserves the property of being Cartesian closed.

Notation 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category. For two morphisms
i\colon A\to B,j\colon C\to D
in
\mathcal{M}
, we denote the pushout product of
i
and
j
,
A\times D\coprod_{A\times C}B\times C\to B\times D
by
i\square j
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category and
\Phi
a model filter. Then
P_{\Phi}
preserves pushout products and pullback-exponentials. Hence, if
\mathcal{M}
is a Cartesian closed model category, then so is
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Cartesian closed model category. By
Theorem

2.10
, the underlying category of
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
is Cartesian closed and
P_{\Phi}
preserves Cartesian closure. Let
i\colon A\to B
and
j\colon C\to D
be two cofibrations in
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
. We need to prove that the pushout product
i\square j
is a cofibration and is a trivial cofibration if one of
i
or
j
are a trivial cofibration.

Pick
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
in
\Phi
, such that
i\times W_{1}
and
j\times W_{2}
are cofibrations in
\mathcal{M}
and let
W\leq W_{1},W_{2}
. By direct computation
(i\square j)\times W\cong(i\times W)\square(j\times W)
, using the fact that
-\times W
commutes with products and pushouts. As
\mathcal{M}
is Cartesian closed,
(i\times W)\square(j\times W)
is also a cofibration, and so
i\square j
is a cofibration in
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
. The case where
i
or
j
is a trivial cofibration follows analogously. Hence,
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
is a Cartesian closed model category.
∎

4.3.
Local Presentability and Cofibrant Generation

There are examples where
\mathcal{M}
is a locally presentable category with a cofibrantly generated model structure, but
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
is neither. See
Example

7.1
for an explicit counter-example.

4.4.
Properness

Let us prove that
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
is (right or left) proper if
\mathcal{M}
is (right or left) proper.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category and
\Phi
a model filter. If
\mathcal{M}
is right proper, then
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
is right proper.

Proof.

Let
p\colon Y\to X
be a fibration and
f\colon Z\to X
be a weak equivalence in
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
. Pick
W_{1}
in
\Phi
such that
p\times W_{1}
is a fibration in
\mathcal{M}
and
W_{2}
in
\Phi
, such that
f\times W_{2}
is a weak equivalence in
\mathcal{M}
, and let
W\leq W_{1},W_{2}
in
\Phi
. As
\mathcal{M}
is right proper, the pullback
(p\times W)^{*}(f\times W)\cong p^{*}f\times W
is a weak equivalence in
\mathcal{M}
, meaning
p^{*}f
is a weak equivalence in
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
. Finally, as
P_{\Phi}
preserves pullbacks,
p^{*}f\times W
is also the pullback in
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
, hence we are done.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category and
\Phi
a model filter. Assume that for every
U
in
\Phi
,
U\times-
preserves pushouts. If
\mathcal{M}
is left proper, then
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
is left proper.

Proof.

We follow the same steps as in
Proposition

4.3
, using the fact that
P_{\Phi}
and
-\times U
preserve pushouts.
∎

We now have the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category and
\Phi
a model filter. Assume that for every
U
in
\Phi
,
U\times-
preserves pushouts. If
\mathcal{M}
is proper, then
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
is proper.

4.5.
Enriched Model Structures

We now want to study the interaction between filter quotients and enriched model structures. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a compactly enriched model category and
\Phi
a model filter. We want to construct a compactly enriched model structure on the model category
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
. Constructing the enriched mapping object on
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
is straightforward.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
(\mathscr{V},\otimes_{\mathscr{V}},I_{\mathscr{V}})
be a monoidal model category with filtered colimits. Let
\mathscr{C}
be a
\mathscr{V}
-enriched category and
\Phi
a filter of subterminal objects. We define the
induced enriched mapping object
,

\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}(-,-)\colon\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}^{op}\times\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}\to\mathscr{V},

by
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}(X,Y)=\mathrm{colim}_{U\in\Phi}\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}}(X\times U,Y\times U)
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
(\mathscr{V},\otimes_{\mathscr{V}},I_{\mathscr{V}})
be a monoidal model category with filtered colimits, such that the functor
-\otimes_{\mathscr{V}}-
commutes with filtered colimits. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a
\mathscr{V}
-enriched category and
\Phi
a filter of subterminal objects. The induced enriched mapping object
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}}(X,Y)
makes
\mathscr{C}
a
\mathscr{V}
-enriched category.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\mathrm{comp}_{X,Y,Z}\colon\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}}(X,Y)\times\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}}(Y,Z)\to\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}}(X,Z)
the composition map of
\mathscr{C}
. Now, for three objects
X,Y,Z
in
\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
, we have the following map

\displaystyle\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}(X,Y)\otimes_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}(Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\underset{U\in\Phi}{\mathrm{colim}}\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}(X\times U,Y\times U)\otimes_{\mathscr{V}}\underset{U\in\Phi}{\mathrm{colim}}\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}(Y\times U,Z\times U)

\displaystyle\cong\underset{U\in\Phi}{\mathrm{colim}}\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}(X\times U,Y\times U)\otimes_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}(Y\times U,Z\times U)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\underset{U\in\Phi}{\mathrm{colim}}\ \mathrm{comp}_{X\times U,Y\times U,Z\times U}}\underset{U\in\Phi}{\mathrm{colim}}\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}(X\times U,Z\times U)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}}(X,Z).

Here the bijection follows from the fact that filtered colimits commute with the monoidal structure. As each
\mathrm{comp}_{X\times U,Y\times U,Z\times U}
is associative and unital, it follows that this composition map is associative and unital, as well.
∎

Of course we need to make sure that this enriched mapping object is compatible with the hom set in the filter quotient, which is the aim of the next lemma, the proof of which directly follows from the fact that compact objects commute with filtered colimits.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
(\mathscr{V},\otimes_{\mathscr{V}},I_{\mathscr{V}})
be a monoidal model category with filtered colimits, such that the unit
I_{\mathscr{V}}
is compact. Then

\mathrm{Hom}(I_{\mathscr{V}},\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}}(X,Y))=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}}(X,Y).

We now want to construct an enriched tensor and cotensor on
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
. This requires additional conditions on the enrichment category
\mathscr{V}
and the filter
\Phi
.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
(\mathscr{V},\otimes_{\mathscr{V}},I_{\mathscr{V}})
be a monoidal model category. We say
\mathscr{V}
is
quotient compatible
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\mathscr{V}
has filtered colimits.

(2)

-\otimes_{\mathscr{V}}-
commutes with filtered colimits.

(3)

The unit
I_{\mathscr{V}}
is compact.

(4)

(Trivial) fibrations are closed under filtered colimits.

The last condition might appear restrictive, however, it is satisfied in many relevant cases, as the following lemma demonstrates.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\mathscr{V}
be a monoidal model category with filtered colimits. If there is a set of generating (trivial) cofibrations with (co)domain compact objects, then (trivial) fibrations are closed under filtered colimits. Hence, if
\mathscr{V}
also has a compact unit and
-\otimes_{\mathscr{V}}-
commutes with filtered colimits, then
\mathscr{V}
is quotient compatible.

Proof.

We prove the case for fibrations, the case for trivial fibrations is analogous. Let
I
be a filtered category and
p_{i}\colon Y_{i}\to X_{i}
be a filtered diagram of fibrations in
\mathscr{V}
. We need to prove that
\mathrm{colim}p_{i}
lifts against a generating trivial cofibration

in

Lemma 4.12

Let

-compactly enriched model category and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}}(X\otimes K,Y)

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{colim}_{U\in\Phi}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{M}}((X\otimes K)\times U,Y)

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{colim}_{U\in\Phi}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{M}}((X\times U)\otimes K,Y)

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}}(X,Y^{K})

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}}(X\otimes K,Y)

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{colim}_{U\in\Phi}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{M}}((X\otimes K)\times U,Y)

K is compact

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
(\mathscr{V},\otimes_{\mathscr{V}},I_{\mathscr{V}})
be a quotient compatible monoidal model category. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a
\mathscr{V}
-compactly enriched model category and
\Phi

\mathscr{V}
-enriched model filter. Then
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
is a
\mathscr{V}
-compactly enriched model category. Moreover,

Proof.

We have already shown that

is enriched and (co)tensored, and the (co)tensor is preserved by

(

4.12
). We also proved that the mapping object is compatible with the underlying hom set (

). So, we only need to verify the third axiom of

2.4
. Let

be a cofibration and

be a fibration in

. Choose a

in
\Phi
, such that

is a cofibration in

and

is a fibration in
\mathcal{M}
. By assumption, the induced map

is a fibration, which is trivial if either

or

As filtered colimits commute with finite limits,
([i]^{*},[p]_{*})=\mathrm{colim}_{V\in\Phi}((i\times V)^{*},(p\times V)_{*})

\Phi
a model filter with all elements cofibrant. Then we have an equivalence of categories
\mathrm{Ho}(\mathcal{M}_{\Phi})\simeq\mathrm{Ho}(\mathcal{M})_{\Phi}
.

Proof.

By construction, we have the identity
\mathrm{Ho}(\mathcal{M})=\mathrm{Ho}(\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}(\mathcal{M}))
. By
[
Ras21
, Theorem 2.22]
, the filter quotient
\infty
-category
\mathrm{Ho}_{\infty}(\mathcal{M})_{\Phi}
is defined via a filtered colimit. So, the result follows from the fact that
\mathrm{Ho}\colon\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}_{\infty}\to\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}
is the left adjoint to the inclusion and hence commutes with colimits.
∎

6.
The Case of Filter Products

We now focus on a specific class of filter quotients, which provide many examples of interest: filter products.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
I
be a set. A
filter of subsets of
I
is a filter on the poset
PI
, the power set of
I
.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a category with an initial object
\emptyset
and terminal object
1
,
I
be a set, and
\Phi
be a filter of subsets of
I
. Let
\mathscr{F}\mathrm{il}(\Phi)
be the filter of subterminal objects in
\prod_{I}\mathscr{C}
, defined as

\mathscr{F}\mathrm{il}(\Phi)=\{(1)_{i\in J}\colon J\in\Phi\}

If
\emptyset
and
1
are isomorphic, this map is trivial. We will hence assume
\emptyset\not\cong 1
, in which case the assignment
\mathscr{F}\mathrm{il}
is evidently injective. We will abuse notation and denote the filter of subterminal objects in
\prod_{I}\mathscr{C}
by
\Phi
again.

For the filter to be homotopically relevant the initial object needs to be fibrant. Here we will focus on the case of
strict initial objects
, i.e. initial objects with no non-trivial morphism into them. This will guarantee fibrancy (it lifts against all trivial cofibrations) and not being isomorphic to the terminal object (except in the trivial category
[0]
).

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category with strict initial object,
I
be a set and
\Phi
a filter of subsets of
I
. Then the induced filter on
\prod_{I}\mathcal{M}
is a model filter.

Proof.

By
Example

3.2
, the filter is homotopical. Moreover, it is discrete by construction. The cofibrations and weak equivalences are
\Phi
-product stable, as the product with
J\in\Phi
, simply corresponds to projection
\prod_{I}\mathcal{M}\to\prod_{J}\mathcal{M}
.
∎

Definition 6.4
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category with strict initial object,
I
be a set, and
\Phi
a filter of subsets of
I
. Then the
filter product model category
is the model category
(\prod_{I}\mathcal{M})_{\Phi}
, where
\Phi
is the model filter in
\prod_{I}\mathcal{M}
.

Notation 6.5
.

The filter product is commonly denoted by
\prod_{\Phi}\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category with strict initial object,
I
a set, and
\Phi
a filter of subsets on
I
. Let
\pi_{\Phi}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\prod_{\Phi}\mathcal{M}
be defined as the composition

\mathcal{M}\xrightarrow{\ \Delta\ }\prod_{I}\mathcal{M}\xrightarrow{\ P_{\Phi}\ }\prod_{\Phi}\mathcal{M},

where
\Delta
is the diagonal functor.

We have the following fact about
\pi_{\Phi}
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category with strict initial object,
I
a set, and
\Phi
a filter of subsets on
I
. Then the functor
\pi_{\Phi}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\prod_{\Phi}\mathcal{M}
preserves weak equivalences, (co)fibrations and finite (co)limits.

Notice, the filter product construction will in fact preserve relevant properties of the original model structure.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model category with strict initial object,
I
a set, and
\Phi
a filter of subsets on
I
. If
\mathcal{M}
is (right or left) proper then so is
\prod_{\Phi}\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Let us assume
\mathcal{M}
is (right or left) proper. We want to show that
\prod_{\Phi}\mathcal{M}
is (right or left) proper. The case for right properness is immediate (
Proposition

4.3
). The case for left properness follows from the fact that
U\times-
corresponds to the projection functor
\prod_{I}\mathcal{M}\to\prod_{U}\mathcal{M}
, which evidently preserves pushouts, and
Proposition

4.4
. The case for properness follows from combining the last two steps.
∎

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a
\mathscr{V}
-compactly enriched model category with strict initial object,
I
a set, and
\Phi
a filter of subsets on
I
. If
\mathscr{V}
is quotient compatible, then
\Phi
is a
\mathscr{V}
-enriched model filter, and hence
\prod_{\Phi}\mathcal{M}
is a
\mathscr{V}
-compactly enriched model category.

Proof.

We need to check that
\Phi
is a
\mathscr{V}
-enriched model filter, the result will then follow from
Theorem

4.14
. Let
K
be a compact object in
\mathscr{V}
and
U
an object in
\Phi
. Then
-\times U\colon\prod_{I}\mathcal{M}\to\prod_{U}\mathcal{M}
is simply the projection map, which evidently commutes with
-\otimes K\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
. Hence, the filter quotient, in this case the filter product
\prod_{\Phi}\mathcal{M}
, is
\mathscr{V}
-enriched.
∎

We can in particular apply this to simplicial enrichment.

Corollary 6.10
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a simplicial model category with strict initial object,
I
a set, and
\Phi
a filter on
I
. Then
\Phi
is a simplicial model filter and
\prod_{\Phi}\mathcal{M}
is a simplicial model category.

Finally, we can apply the result to Quillen adjunctions and equivalences.

7.
Examples

In this final section, we focus on examples of filter quotients. For the first example we look at the most crucial example, which also provides relevant counter-examples.

Example 7.1
.

Let
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Kan}
denote the category of simplicial sets with the Kan model structure. Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of natural numbers and
\mathcal{F}
the Fréchet filter given by cofinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
. In this case the initial object in
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
is indeed strict. Hence, the filter product
\prod_{\mathcal{F}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
is a model category with the following properties: it is proper (
Proposition

6.8
), simplicial (
Corollary

6.10
), and Cartesian closed (
Proposition

4.2
).

On the other hand, the category
\prod_{\mathcal{F}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
does not have countable coproducts
[
Joh02
, Example D.5.1.7]
. Hence, the category is also not locally presentable or accessible. This means the model structure is not cofibrantly generated or combinatorial.

For the next example, recall that an
ultrafilter
of subsets of
I
is a filter that is maximal as a subset of
PI
with respect to inclusion. For a given element
i
in
I
, the subset
\{S\subseteq I|i\in S\}\subseteq PI
is in particular an ultrafilter, called
principal
. All other ultrafilters are called
non-principal
.

Example 7.2
.

Let
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
denote the category of simplicial sets with the Kan model structure. Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of natural numbers and let
\mathscr{U}
be a non-principal ultrafilter on
\mathbb{N}
. We then obtain a filter product model category
\prod_{\mathscr{U}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
, which is simplicial. Notice, the global section functor
\mathrm{Map}((\Delta[0])_{\mathbb{N}},-)\colon\prod_{\mathscr{U}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}\to s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
is faithful, without being an equivalence, meaning the terminal object is a
generator
. Indeed, it suffices to observe that the induced functor on homotopy categories

\mathrm{Ho}(\prod_{\mathscr{U}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et})\cong\prod_{\mathscr{U}}\mathrm{Ho}(s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et})\xrightarrow{\pi_{0}\mathrm{Map}((\Delta[0])_{\mathbb{N}},-)}\mathrm{Ho}(s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et})

is faithful. The first functor is just an equivalence, by
Proposition

5.7
, and the second is faithful, by
[
Joh77
, Example 9.45]
.

Let’s generalize these examples in two directions.

Definition 7.3

(
[
Rez10
]
)
.

A
model topos
is a left-exact Bousfield localization of the projective model structure on
\mathrm{Fun}(\mathscr{C}^{op},s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et})
, where
\mathscr{C}
is a small category and
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
has the Kan model structure.

The underlying category is a presheaf category, meaning the initial object is strict.

Example 7.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a
model topos
,
I
a set and
\Phi
a filter of subsets of
I
. Based on
Definition

7.3
, we obtain a filter product model category
\prod_{\Phi}\mathcal{G}
.

Example 7.5
.

Let
X
be a topological space and
x
a point in
X
. Let
\Phi_{x}
be the filter of open subsets of
X
containing
x
. Let
\mathscr{S}\mathrm{hv}(X)
be the model topos given as category of simplicial sheaves on the category
Open_{X}
. Then
\mathscr{S}\mathrm{hv}(X)_{\Phi}
has an induced model structure.

Let us move on from examples of model categories modeling homotopy types (such as Kan complexes), to examples of model categories modeling
\infty
-categories.

Example 7.6
.

Let
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Joy}
denote the category of simplicial sets with the Joyal model structure,
I
a set and
\mathcal{F}
a filter of subsets of
I
. Then, by
Corollary

4.18
, the filter product
\prod_{\Phi}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Joy}
is enriched over the Joyal model structure with all objects cofibrant. This means the full subcategory of fibrant objects is an
\infty
-cosmos
[
RV17
, Lemma 2.2.1]
. This in particular means there is a well-defined notion of
Cartesian fibration

[
RV17
, Theorem 4.1.10]
that satisfies an internal version of the Yoneda lemma
[
RV17
, Theorem 6.0.1]
.

If, in addition,
\mathcal{F}
is a non-principal ultrafilter, then we can repeat the same argument used in
Example

7.2
to deduce that
\mathrm{Hom}((\Delta^{0})_{I},-)\colon\prod_{\mathcal{F}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}\to s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
is faithful, meaning the terminal object is a generator. Hence, we have an example of an
\infty
-cosmos that is not locally presentable or accessible, in the sense of
[
BL23
]
, that is still generated by the terminal object. This means we are generating a whole new class of
\infty
-cosmoi, not yet considered in the literature.

Remark 7.7
.

Let
I
be a set and
\mathcal{F}
a filter of subsets of
I
. The identity functor
\mathrm{id}\colon s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Kan}\to s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Joy}
is right Quillen and homotopically fully faithful, and evidently preserves the initial object. Moreover, the left adjoint, which is also the identity, preserves the terminal object. This means, by
Corollary

6.11
, we have an induced homotopically fully faithful right adjoint
\mathrm{id}\colon\prod_{\mathcal{F}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Kan}\to\prod_{\mathcal{F}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{Joy}
. Intuitively, the image consists of the “discrete
\infty
-categories” or “
\infty
-groupoids”.

In these examples we focused on quasi-categories, however, the filter product construction is quite stable under change of models of
(\infty,1)
-categories.

Example 7.8
.

Let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{{\mathbbe{\Delta}}^{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{1}^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bot}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{1}^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces t_{!}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bot}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces t^{!}}

denote the Quillen equivalences between the Joyal model structure on
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
and the Rezk model structure on
s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{{\mathbbe{\Delta}}^{op}}

[
JT07
]
. Notice, both
p_{1}^{*}
and
t_{!}
preserve the terminal object and
i_{1}^{*}
and
t^{!}
preserves the initial object.

Now, let
I
be a set and
\mathcal{F}
be a filter of subsets. Then
Corollary

6.11
implies that we have a diagram of Quillen equivalences

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{\mathcal{F}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{\mathcal{F}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}^{{\mathbbe{\Delta}}^{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{\mathcal{F}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{1}^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bot}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{1}^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces t_{!}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bot}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces t^{!}}

which we can think of as the filter product analogue of a change of model of
(\infty,1)
-categories between quasi-categories and complete Segal spaces.

We already observed above that
\prod_{\mathcal{F}}s\mathscr{S}\mathrm{et}
is an
\infty
-cosmos (
Example

7.6
). We can similarly use the fact that the Rezk model structure gives us an
\infty
-cosmos
[
RV22
, Example 1.2.24]
. Now, by
[
RV22
, Corollary E.1.2]
this Quillen equivalence gives us a cosmological biequivalence between these
\infty
-cosmoi.

Let us finally observe a situation where filter quotients do not result in new examples.

Example 7.9
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a pointed model category, meaning the unique map from the initial object to the terminal object is a weak equivalence. Then, every subterminal object is equivalent to the terminal object. Hence, every filter quotient is trivial.
