Outcomes of Patients with RET Fusion-Positive Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer Treated with First-Line Non-Molecularly Targeted Therapies: A Real-World Benchmark in Europe from the RECALIB-RET Study
Authors: Luis León-Mateos, Maximillian J Hochmair, Amparo Sánchez-Gastaldo, Audrey Rabeau, Jean-Bernard Auliac, Teresa Morán, Damian T Rieke, Patricia Iranzo Gomez, Tarun Puri, Ana Sofia Afonso, Manoj Khanal, Xiaohong Ivy Li, Grace Segall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe single-arm phase 1/2 LIBRETTO-001 trial demonstrated durable efficacy and a favorable safety profile with the selective RET inhibitor (RETi) selpercatinib in patients with RET fusion-positive non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), regardless of prior non-RET-selective treatment. The aim of this study (RECALIB-RET), comparison of outcomes with first-line (1L) non-RET-selective therapy in a real-world external control arm to the LIBRETTO-001 selpercatinib-treated population, was precluded by a small real-world sample size. Therefore, we report European benchmark data, reflecting physician’s choice of 1L therapy and treatment availability in routine clinical practice.MethodsThe study population comprised data from adult patients with RET fusion-positive, locally advanced/metastatic NSCLC who received ≥ 1 prior line of systemic non-RET-selective therapy, pooled across chart reviews from four European countries. Patient characteristics, biomarker testing and treatment patterns, clinical outcomes, and safety events during 1L therapy are reported.ResultsPatients in the RW benchmark population (n = 24) had a median age at 1L therapy initiation of 61.0 years (interquartile range 56.0–71.5); 58.3% were male and 41.7% were female, 95.8% had adenocarcinoma, and 91.7% had advanced-stage disease at diagnosis. Overall response and disease-control rates to 1L non-RET-selective therapy were 37.5% (95% confidence interval [CI] 18.8–59.4) and 58.3% (95% CI 36.6–77.9), respectively. Median progression-free survival was 5.4 months (95% CI 2.1–8.0). Median time between advanced disease diagnosis and biomarker testing (n = 21, 87.5%) was 17 days, with a median turnaround time for the results of 25 days; 70.8% (n = 17) initiated 1L treatment before receiving test results. Of 125 adverse events reported, 20 (16.0%) were grade ≥ 3.ConclusionOutcomes observed in the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population reflect the limitations of non-selective RETi therapies in RET fusion-positive NSCLC, and reinforce the rationale for early use of targeted agents such as selpercatinib. Optimizing biomarker testing practices may enable earlier access to these therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03629-w.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, RET fusion, Selpercatinib, Non-RET-selective therapy, LIBRETTO-001, Real-world external control arm

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Where head-to-head trials are not feasible, use of a real-world (RW) external control arm or descriptive benchmark population to a single-arm clinical trial may enable generation of comparative or contextual evidence that is acceptable to regulatory agencies.Selpercatinib has shown efficacy and a manageable safety profile in patients with RET fusion-positive non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC; any line) in the phase 1/2 single-arm LIBRETTO-001 trial, and significantly longer progression-free survival versus placebo in the first-line (1L) setting in the phase 3 LIBRETTO-431 trial.In RECALIB-RET, a descriptive benchmark population treated with standard of care therapies was utilized toward evaluating whether the characteristics and outcomes of patients reported in clinical trials are representative of those observed in the RW.What was learned from the study?The baseline characteristics of the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population appeared consistent with those of LIBRETTO-001 and LIBRETTO-431 study populations, and outcomes appeared similar to those for the LIBRETTO-431 control arm.1L treatment was generally initiated before receiving the results of RET biomarker testing, suggesting that optimizing biomarker testing practices could enable earlier use of targeted therapies.The findings support the use of genomic testing at the time of NSCLC diagnosis to identify actionable biomarkers and highlight the need to reduce biomarker test turnaround times prior to 1L therapy to enable patients with RET fusion-positive NSCLC to benefit from expedited targeted therapy.

Introduction

The global burden of lung cancer remains high, in 2022 ranking highest by number of new cases annually (approx. 2.5 million), with cancer-related mortality accounting for almost 19% of all cancer-related deaths annually [1, 2]. The vast majority (85%) of newly diagnosed cases are non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [3]. Over the past 2 decades, identification of oncogenic drivers and subsequent development of corresponding targeted therapies with reduced toxicity have improved outcomes for patients with biomarker-driven NSCLC [4–6]. Around 60% of patients with NSCLC in the Western world have targetable oncogenic alterations, most commonly KRAS (approx. 30%), EGFR (approx. 15%), and ALK (approx. 4%) [4, 5]. Of these, approximately 10% correspond to non-G12C KRAS mutations [5], for which there is no targeted treatment in routine clinical practice [7]. The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines recommend genomic testing for a variety of driver alterations in NSCLC, and a wide range of approved targeted therapies [4, 8].

Fusions in the gene encoding rearranged during transfection protein (RET), which lead to activated oncogenic alterations, occur in approximately 1–2% of NSCLC cases globally, and are associated with aggressive disease and increased risk of metastases [9–11]. Almost half of patients with RET fusion-positive NSCLC will develop central nervous system (CNS) metastases over the course of their lifetime [10]. Outcomes of patients with RET fusion-positive NSCLC treated with platinum-based chemotherapy (PBC) with or without immunotherapy—until recently the only recommended treatment for advanced or metastatic NSCLC [12]—have been studied in a real-world (RW) setting [13–18]. However, these previously published studies predominantly included fully- or majority-pretreated patients [13–16]; there remains a gap in understanding outcomes in the first-line (1L) treatment setting.

Selpercatinib, a first-in-class, highly selective and potent RET inhibitor (RETi), has shown durable efficacy and a favorable safety profile in advanced solid tumors, including RET fusion-positive NSCLC regardless of prior systemic treatments, in the phase 1/2 single-arm LIBRETTO-001 trial (NCT03157128) [19–22]. The subsequent phase 3 randomized controlled LIBRETTO-431 trial (NCT04194944) demonstrated significantly longer median progression-free survival (PFS) with selpercatinib versus a standard-of-care (SoC) treatment of PBC with immunotherapy (n = 83) or PBC alone (n = 19) in the 1L setting. In addition, CNS-protective activity and improved intracranial efficacy were reported following 1L treatment with selpercatinib in patients with previously untreated RET fusion-positive NSCLC [23, 24]. Selpercatinib is currently approved in several regions worldwide for the treatment of RET fusion-positive NSCLC [22, 25–27], and is recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and ESMO Clinical Practice Guidelines as a preferred 1L treatment option for RET-rearranged NSCLC. It is also recommended by the NCCN as a subsequent option in those patients not treated in 1L with selpercatinib or pralsetinib (a RETi without global approval) [8, 28].

Using a RW external control arm (ECA) to a single-arm trial that mimics the characteristics of a randomized trial control arm is one way of increasing the sample size and generating comparative or contextual evidence in the absence of a head-to-head trial and that may be accepted by regulatory authorities and payers [29, 30]. In the observational, retrospective RECALIB-RET study, a RW cohort of patients with RET fusion-positive NSCLC treated with a non-RET-selective 1L therapy (physician’s choice) was generated and planned as an external control to the selpercatinib-treated patients from LIBRETTO-001 (enrolment commenced in May 2017; January 2023 data cut [19]). This protocol-planned comparison of a RW ECA and LIBRETTO-001 arms was ultimately precluded by the limited sample size of RECALIB-RET. Therefore, we report here European data for a RW benchmark population, which, other than exclusion of patients with selective RETi agents as part of their 1L therapy, was unselected with regard to particular treatments, thus reflecting physician’s choice of 1L therapy and treatment availability in routine clinical practice for RET fusion-positive NSCLC. The findings provide insights into how the outcomes observed in clinical trial control arms (e.g., control arm of the phase 3 LIBRETTO-431 trial of selpercatinib vs. PBC with or without pembrolizumab) may represent outcomes in RW clinical practice, and into patterns of RW RET biomarker testing.

Methods

Data Sources and Patient Selection

The RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population utilized pseudonymized patient-level data collected via chart reviews across four countries in Europe (Austria, France, Germany, and Spain). Data were entered into a study-specific case report form by physicians who regularly treat patients with RET fusion-positive advanced NSCLC at these locations.

Patient Eligibility Criteria for RW Benchmark Population

RW patient data were abstracted between June 2023 and April 2024 from medical records for patients with a diagnosis of RET fusion-positive, locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC who were aged ≥ 18 years at initiation of 1L systemic therapy. Patients must also have met all the following criteria: (1) received ≥ 1 line of systemic therapy (LoT) to treat NSCLC and initiated a treatment between June 1, 2013, and 9 months before the date of data abstraction (latest data abstraction April 2024); (2) had the following minimum information available: sex, age, date of diagnosis of advanced disease, details of RET alteration; (3) had never received any selective RETi either within or outside of a clinical trial as a 1L therapy or for metastatic cancer, or participated in LIBRETTO-001.

Patients were observed from the date of initial diagnosis until death or the date of data abstraction (whichever occurred first). The index LoT for analysis was defined as the 1L of therapy for all patients and was physician’s choice of 1L systemic therapy.

Ethical Approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles originating in the Declaration of Helsinki, and consistent with Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices and applicable laws and regulations of the countries where the study was conducted. Since this was an observational study with no protocol-mandated intervention, the assessment and treatment of the patients were based solely on the investigator’s routine or usual practice in the provision of care to patients with advanced stages of cancer. Informed consent waivers were requested during ethical review processes in each site and country: a full waiver was obtained for Austria (Ethikkommission der Stadt Wien; approval no. EK 23-037-VK), and partial waivers for Germany (Ethikkommission – Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin; approval no. EA2/002/23) and Spain (Comité de Etica de la Investigación con Medicamentos de Galicia; approval no. 2022/495). Signed informed consent from patients was obtained in the event that a waiver was not obtained. In France, since this was a noninterventional health study not involving human participants that used existing data for a public-health purpose, the study was conducted under Commission Nationale de L’informatique et des Libertés Reference Methodology 004 (registration no. 2219942) [31]. In accordance with this framework, ethics committee approval was not required. Instead, patients received prior written information about the study with the option to opt out, and a formal declaration of compliance was released by the sponsor.

Study Objectives

Details of the planned study objectives are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The revised objectives reported here in patients with advanced RET fusion-positive NSCLC treated with non-RET-selective therapies include descriptions of (1) the patient and disease characteristics; (2) treatment patterns by LoT; (3) clinical effectiveness outcomes (PFS, objective response rate [ORR], disease control rate [DCR], duration of response [DoR], overall survival [OS]) and treatment-related outcomes (duration of 1L treatment, time to next treatment); and (4) the safety profile during the first LoT.

Statistical Analysis

Outcomes Analyses

A full list of study outcomes and their definitions is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Demographic, clinical, and molecular characteristics of patients who received systemic treatment were summarized using descriptive measures. Treatment patterns were described for each treatment line up to third line (3L), including a description of the treatment received, including systemic treatment (regimen, drugs, duration, number of administrations, adverse event [AE]-related dose reductions, and discontinuations).

Tumor response to 1L treatment (ORR and DCR) was determined in the RW benchmark population by the treating physician either radiologically or clinically, with no mandated adherence to National Cancer Institute Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) [32]. ORR and DCR were calculated descriptively. Time-to-event analyses, including PFS and OS, were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, providing median and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Safety analyses included number and proportion of AEs or serious AEs (SAEs) documented in patient clinical charts and notes from the start date of therapy to 30 days after the last dose or patient death, whichever occurred first. Analyses were descriptive, and included the number of AEs and SAEs, number and percentage of patients with each AE and SAE, and the distribution of AEs by grade and by treatment relatedness. Drug-toxicity-related discontinuations and dose reductions were also reported. Safety summaries are reported at the AE level (i.e., number of AEs as a proportion of the total number of AEs), while listings of AEs and data on discontinuations or dose reductions are reported at the patient level (i.e., number of patients experiencing particular AEs as a proportion of the total number of patients).

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 or later, and R statistical software version 3.6.1 or later; the threshold for statistical significance was set at a two-sided alpha of 0.05.

Changes to Analysis Plan

Analyses were ultimately limited to descriptive data on 1L treatment. The planned comparative analyses of the pretreated (second line [2L] and beyond [≥ 2L]) cohort, and 1L-treated patients with NSCLC between the RW benchmark and LIBRETTO-001 (selpercatinib) arms were not possible because of limited sample sizes (hence insufficient power to identify differences). Some missingness in key covariates, such as smoking status, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance score, and RET fusion partner, may have decreased the sample size, further limiting comparative analysis. Moreover, balancing baseline covariates using propensity score matching may have further reduced the sample size, and weighting methods such as entropy balancing or inverse probability of treatment weighting would have resulted in a small effective sample size. Only the descriptive objectives for the RET fusion-positive NSCLC 1L non-RET-selective treatment cohort are presented.

Results

Among the 66 sites invited to participate in this study, eight ultimately agreed and were able to provide data on patients with RET fusion-positive NSCLC. A diagram depicting the flow of study site involvement and attrition is provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Patients Patient and 1L Treatment Characteristics

A summary of the baseline patient demographic, clinical, and tumor molecular characteristics for the RW benchmark population (n = 24) is provided in Table 1. Patients had a median (interquartile range) age at initiation of 1L therapy of 61.0 years (56.0–71.5); 58.3% (n = 14) were male and 41.7% (n = 10) were female. The most common country of treatment was Spain (n = 12, 50.0%), followed by France (n = 6, 25.0%). The majority had a diagnosis of adenocarcinoma (n = 23, 95.8%) and advanced-stage disease at diagnosis (n = 22, 91.7%), and harbored KIF5B as the RET fusion partner (n = 20, 83.3%). During the 7-year 1L treatment-initiation period (2016–2022), 50.0% (n = 12) of patients initiated 1L therapy during the latter 2-year period (2021, n = 5 [20.8%]; 2022, n = 7 [29.2%]) (Table 1).

The pattern of treatments from 1L and beyond is shown in Supplementary Fig. S2 and detailed in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. The most common 1L treatments in the RW benchmark population were chemoimmunotherapy (n = 9, 37.5%), including carboplatin with pembrolizumab and pemetrexed (n = 8, 33.3%), and chemotherapy (n = 8, 33.3%) (Supplementary Table S3). One (4.2%) patient received cetuximab.

At the data abstraction date, eight (33.3%) patients had not initiated a subsequent line of treatment after discontinuation of 1L therapy (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Table S3). Sixteen (66.7%) patients had received 2L therapy, of whom nine (56.3%) received a selective RETi (most commonly selpercatinib, n = 5/16 [31.3%]), and five (31.3%) received immunotherapy alone. The remainder (n = 2, 12.5%) received chemotherapy-based regimens. Ten of these 16 (62.5%) patients had received 3L treatment (six [37.5%] had received no further therapy after 2L), including five (50.0%) who received a selective RETi and one (4.2%) who received a multikinase inhibitor (MKI) (Supplementary Table S3). Selective RETi therapy was received in any subsequent line (≥ 2L) by 15 of the total cohort (62.5%), most frequently selpercatinib (n = 9, 37.5%) and pralsetinib (n = 5, 20.8%) (Supplementary Table S4).

Median (interquartile range) follow-up time from initiation of index LoT was 19.3 months (12.5–36.1).

The ORR to 1L treatment was 37.5% (95% CI 18.8–59.4), and included nine partial responses (no complete responses); five patients achieved stable disease, yielding a DCR of 58.3% (95% CI 36.6–77.9). Median PFS was 5.4 months (95% CI 2.1–8.0) after a median follow-up duration of 8.2 months (95% CI 3.1–not evaluable [NE]). In this RW setting, the most frequently used method of assessing disease progression was radiographic (n = 14, 58.3%), followed by a combination of radiographic and clinical (n = 2, 8.3%); the method was unknown for almost one-third of patients (Supplementary Table S5). After a median follow-up of almost 2 years, median OS was 49.5 months (95% CI 40.0–NE); the 36-month OS rate was 83% (95% CI 53–94) (Table 2).

Safety

During 1L treatment, a total of 125 AEs were reported in 19 of 24 (79.2%) patients (Table 3). Assessed at the AE level, AEs were predominantly mild-to-moderate in severity (n = 90/125 [72.0%] at grade 1 or 2); 35/125 (28.0%) grade ≥ 3 AEs were reported, including 11/125 (8.8%) that were life threatening (Supplementary Table S6). A total of 44/125 (35.2%) AEs were considered by the treating physician to be related to the 1L treatment received (Table 3).

At the patient level (n = 24), the most commonly reported AEs were asthenia (n = 8, 33.3%), dyspnea (n = 5, 20.8%), anemia (n = 4, 16.7%), and fever (n = 4, 16.7%) (Table 3). Grade ≥ 3 AEs occurring in ≥ 1 patient were anemia, dyspnea, acute renal failure, pneumonia, and pulmonary embolism (n = 2, 8.3% for each). SAEs were experienced by eight (33.3%) patients. Individual SAEs that occurred in ≥ 1 patient were acute renal failure (n = 2, 8.3%), pneumonia (n = 2, 8.3%), and pulmonary embolism (n = 2, 8.3%).

Also at the patient level, 1L treatment was discontinued for unacceptable toxicity in two (8.3%) patients. In addition, three (12.5%) patients had their treatment dose reduced, due to patient and physician decision (n = 2, 8.3%) and AE (n = 1, 4.2%). AEs led to death in four (16.7%) patients during the study period: death due to unknown cause (n = 2/24, 8.3%), bilateral pleurisy (n = 1/24, 4.2%), and intestinal occlusion due to progressive disease (n = 1/24, 4.2%). None of the deaths was considered to be related to the treatment received.

Biomarker Testing Practices

In the RW setting, specimen sampling for biomarker testing to identify the presence of RET fusions was conducted predominantly on the primary tumor specimen (n = 16, 66.7%) and using a tissue biopsy procedure (n = 17, 70.8%). The genomic testing method was next-generation sequencing for all 24 (100%) patients (Table 4). Most patients were tested after they had received a diagnosis of advanced disease (n = 21, 87.5%); however, most had initiated 1L treatment before receiving the test result (n = 17, 70.8%), with a median time from initiation of 1L therapy to test result of 56 days (Table 4). Overall, the median times from diagnosis of advanced disease and requesting a RET test, and from requesting the test to receiving the result were 17 and 25 days, respectively. Over half of patients received the result of their biomarker test during 2021–2023 (n = 14, 58.3%); the remainder (n = 10, 41.7%) received them during 2017–2020 (respective dates are proxies for post- and pre-selpercatinib marketing authorization in Europe) (Table 4).

Discussion

In the RECALIB-RET study, we compiled a contextual RW benchmark population reflective of RW clinical practice, initially planned as a control to the LIBRETTO-001 study, the latter of which resulted in approval of selpercatinib in 1L in patients with RET fusion-positive NSCLC [33]. The follow-up duration was slightly longer than for the LIBRETTO-431 control (SoC) arm (median 19.3 months vs. 16.5 months) [23]. Although the limited sample size ultimately precluded comparative analysis between selpercatinib-treated patients in LIBRETTO-001 and the SoC-treated patients in the RW benchmark population, it was possible to address descriptive objectives for the latter, which provided insights into how outcomes in clinical trial control arms may translate to RW outcomes. In addition, it provided insights into biomarker testing in routine clinical practice and subsequent treatment patterns within the study period.

The baseline characteristics of the 1L RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population (predominantly male, never smokers with adenocarcinoma and a RET KIF5B fusion partner) are consistent with those of other published studies that enrolled patients with previously untreated, RET fusion-positive NSCLC [16–18, 34–37], including LIBRETTO-001 [19, 38]. They are also in line with those of the LIBRETTO-431 control arm, although with a slightly higher proportion of patients with programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1)-positive disease [23]. The high proportion of missing/not tested PD-L1 data in both the LIBRETTO-431 control arm [23] and the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population may have contributed to this difference. During the 7-year duration of the present study, half of the patients received their 1L therapy in the 2 years after European Medicines Agency (EMA) approval of selpercatinib (2021–2022).

As expected, given the recommended SoC treatment for treatment-naïve advanced NSCLC [12], PBC plus immunotherapy (pembrolizumab or atezolizumab) was the predominant 1L therapy in the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population (Supplementary Table S3). Two-thirds of patients received an immunotherapy-containing regimen, consistent with the proportion with confirmed PD-L1-positive tumors (66.7%). One-third of patients received the regimen administered in KEYNOTE-189 (carboplatin or carboplatin with pembrolizumab and pemetrexed) [39] (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. S2). The 1L treatments received by the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population were more heterogeneous than for the prespecified LIBRETTO-431 control arm (PBC with or without pembrolizumab), reflecting routine clinical practice [15, 16, 23, 40, 41] (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Table S3). The only patients in the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population who received an MKI did so in the 3L setting (docetaxel plus nintedanib) (Supplementary Table S3), consistent with the finding from a global multicenter RET registry study reporting that MKIs confer limited benefit in patients with RET-altered NSCLC [18]. This combination is SoC for later lines of therapy in patients with advanced NSCLC in many European countries, irrespective of molecular biomarker status [42]. In the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population, the uptake of selective RETi agents in post-1L treatment lines was common (62.5% of patients), as in the LIBRETTO-431 control arm (75%) [23].

Post-1L disease progression was most often assessed radiographically in the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population, and rarely by clinical assessment only. Median PFS (5.4 months in a population where 66.7% received an immunotherapy-containing regimen) was generally consistent with other published estimates for either chemotherapy- or immunotherapy-treated RET fusion-positive NSCLC populations: 4.2–6 months when 100% of the study populations received an immunotherapy-containing regimen [15, 16]; 6.7 months when 71.2% of the population received an immunotherapy-containing regimen [36]; and 6.6 months when patients received chemotherapy only [18]. Median PFS in the present study was also more favorable than the 2.1 months reported for patients with RET fusion-positive NSCLC in a study using data from patients treated with immunotherapy in the IMMUNOTARGET registry [41], or 3.1 months reported in the RET-MAP study [13]. However, the IMMUNOTARGET study population was 94.6% pretreated, and that in RET-MAP included patients with 1–3 prior LoTs [13, 41]. The most favorable PFS outcomes reported in RW studies of RET fusion-positive NSCLC include 9.6 months with 1L chemoimmunotherapy regimens [13] and 7.6 months with immunotherapy in the pretreated setting [40].

ORR and DCR were slightly less favorable in the RECALIB-RET 1L RW benchmark population (37.5% and 58.3%, respectively) than in other published RW estimates in the 1L setting: 52% and 75%, respectively, for chemotherapy [18]; and 41–67% and 69–82%, respectively, for immunotherapy [15, 16, 36]. Median PFS (5.4 months, 95% CI 2.1–8.0) and ORR (37.5%, 95% CI 18.8–59.4) were numerically lower in the RECALIB-RET 1L RW benchmark population than for the LIBRETTO-431 control arm (11.2 months and 63%, respectively) [23]. Several factors may account for this difference, including the more heterogeneous RW population and treatment regimens received in the present study, enrolment of healthier patients into clinical trials, and differences in the mode of response and progression assessments between the two studies.

Median OS (49.5 months, 95% CI 40.0–NE) was much more favorable in the RECALIB-RET 1L RW benchmark population than reported in previous RW studies (18–31 months) [14–18, 41]. While OS data were immature in the LIBRETTO-431 trial, after a median follow-up duration of 21 months, 76.4% of patients in the SoC arm were still alive [23]. OS in RECALIB-RET and LIBRETTO-431 are not comparable to estimates from prior RW studies in similar populations due to high rates of crossover to selpercatinib or receipt of other post-1L selective RETi therapies (62.5% of the RECALIB-RET 1L RW benchmark population and 75.0% of the LIBRETTO-431 control arm, with 37.5% and 60%, respectively, receiving post-1L selpercatinib [23]). This should be considered when interpreting these findings.

While the majority of the 125 AEs reported in the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population were considered mild, 35 (28.0%) were grade ≥ 3 and four (3.2%) resulted in death. Over one-third of AEs were considered to be related to the 1L treatment received. Rates of AEs in this study, which are similar to those for selpercatinib in LIBRETTO-001 [19], should be interpreted cautiously given uncertainty in rates of reporting in routine clinical practice [43]. Relatively lower rates of AEs and SAEs were reported in the observational, retrospective, RW SPRINT-RET study of selpercatinib in patients with advanced RET fusion-positive NSCLC or thyroid cancer compared with those in selpercatinib-treated patients with RET fusion-positive NSCLC in LIBRETTO-001 and LIBRETTO-431 [19, 23, 44]. Nevertheless, no new safety signals were observed.

Biomarker testing was conducted using next-generation sequencing in all patients in the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population. In compliance with ESMO guideline recommendations [8], the vast majority of patients were tested after receiving a diagnosis of advanced disease (87.5%). Although the median time between diagnosis and request for biomarker testing of 17 days was relatively timely [45], the median time between requesting the test and receiving the result was 25 days, and most patients (70.8%) initiated 1L treatment before receiving the results. Lack of access to a selective RETi (through clinical trials, early access programs, or commercially) at the time of biomarker testing may have been a contributing factor (41.7% of test results were received prior to the EMA approval of selpercatinib in February 2021 [46]). Most patients in this study received their RET test results from 2021 onwards, during which time reimbursement may not have been fully established across European countries. Furthermore, the initial EMA approval was only in the pretreated setting (the label was extended to the 1L setting in April 2022) [47]. Selpercatinib may thus have been withheld to later lines of treatment despite a positive RET test result. In addition, median turnaround time from RET test to result was 25 days, which, although within the range reported in other studies (recommended 10 working days to over 1 month [48]), is a long time in the context of waiting for treatment. When faced with long test turnaround times, physicians may prescribe conventional chemotherapy rather than wait for the results [48, 49], potentially to the detriment of patient outcomes since responses to 1L RETi were found to be higher than with non-RETi therapies (LIBRETTO-001 exploratory analysis) [50]. Implementing RET testing immediately upon diagnosis and reducing test turnaround times may encourage healthcare providers to wait for results and enable their patients to receive and benefit from targeted therapy earlier in their treatment algorithm [48, 51].

This study has some limitations. The small sample size precluded the planned comparison (including balancing of baseline characteristics) between the LIBRETTO-001 selpercatinib arm and 1L RECALIB-RET RW population; analyses were thus limited to descriptive data for a RW benchmark population receiving 1L for RET fusion-positive NSCLC. The small sample size may also limit the statistical robustness of the benchmark efficacy and safety findings. However, while study in larger populations would provide greater validity, the present findings provide promising evidence supporting these benchmarks, which are generally aligned with those of previous RW studies and relevant clinical trial control arms. The limited sample size also precluded analysis of the findings according to sex. The sex distribution in our cohort, which was predominantly male (58.3%), is highly consistent with two other studies of patients with RET fusion-positive NSCLC (55.9–56.8% male) [37, 52]. However, published studies in this patient population more typically report a predominance of female patients (51.6–80.0%) [15, 17, 18, 44, 53–55]. Although this discrepancy may limit the sex-specific generalizability of our findings, we did not identify any published literature to date that has explored the influence of sex as a prognostic factor for outcomes in RET fusion-positive NSCLC. While 66 centers were considered for inclusion in this study, final participation was limited to those sites with the resources and willingness to participate. In addition, data were obtained only for individuals from four European countries, thus excluding diverse geographic regions and restricting the external validity of the findings for global clinical practice. In the RECALIB-RET RW cohort, RW outcomes were determined by the treating physician and were assessed according to routine clinical practice, with no protocol-driven assessment of tumor response or progression, including no requirement for adherence to RECIST. Therefore, tumor response was not assessed using the same criteria as in the LIBRETTO-001 trial, introducing bias in both the response assessment itself and time-to-event outcomes, which could be neither measured nor adjusted for. The RW safety and efficacy outcomes of the benchmark population in this study were not validated against results from controlled clinical trials. Integrating prospective RW data collection in parallel with the clinical trial would have improved the quality of the RW data, enabling a more thorough assessment of long-term follow-up. Unfortunately, this was not feasible at the time of study conception. It was not possible to assess OS because of the potential for confounding introduced by subsequent treatment with a selective RETi in the majority of patients. Finally, the median follow-up duration in the RECALIB-RET NSCLC 1L RW benchmark population was relatively short at less than 2 years.

Strengths of this study include the similarity of baseline characteristics to other published populations, suggesting generalizability of the outcomes, moderated by a small sample size. The data add to the body of evidence around the clinical benefit of selpercatinib relative to treatments that do not target RET, and fill gaps in European evidence specifically focused on 1L chemoimmunotherapy outcomes in this population. Moreover, we have provided clinically rich data including RET fusion partners, which are generally lacking in previous studies [16, 36]. The data also provide insights into biomarker testing practices, including timing of tests relative to diagnosis, and test results relative to 1L treatment, which may inform optimization of testing practices.

Conclusions

The outcomes observed in the RECALIB-RET RW benchmark population are similar to those observed in the LIBRETTO-431 control arm; they are congruent with a clinical benefit for selpercatinib over non-RETi treatments as demonstrated in LIBRETTO-431, and reveal no new safety concerns. They also further support the NCCN and ESMO guidelines recommendation of selpercatinib to treat patients with documented RET fusion-positive NSCLC in the 1L setting [8, 28]. These findings support the use of comprehensive genomic testing at the time of diagnosis to identify actionable biomarkers and inform the 1L therapy that will most benefit patients.
