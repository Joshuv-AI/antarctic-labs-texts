1.
Introduction

The study of
p
-adic vertex operator algebras was initiated by Franc-Mason
[
FM23
]
in part as a means to further incorporate the
p
-adic study of modular forms into VOA theory. In particular, the normalized character map (i.e. 1-point correlation function or graded trace) on these new structures was shown to have image inside Serre’s space of
p
-adic modular forms
[
Ser73
]
. In the algebraic rather than the
p
-adic case, it is known that the normalized character map
F_{S}
on the Heisenberg VOA
S
gives a surjection of graded linear spaces
S\to M^{\prime}
where
M^{\prime}
denotes the space of quasi-modular forms — see
[
MT10
]
for a proof. Whether or not the
p
-adic character map surjects onto the ring of
p
-adic modular forms is currently an open question.

For the
p
-adic completion of
S
, which we denote as
S_{1}
, it was shown in
[
BF24
]
that the image of the character map contains families of
p
-adic modular forms, including every
p
-adic Eisenstein series
G_{k}^{\star}(q)
. In fact, a similar result was proven for a
p
-adic completion of a lattice vertex operator algebra, though in this manuscript we focus only on
S_{1}
. Then in
[
FM25
]
, the authors used the description of the space of
2
-adic modular forms as the Tate ring
\mathcal{M}_{2}=\mathbb{Q}_{2}\langle j^{-1}\rangle
in the variable
j^{-1}
, where
j
is the classical
j
-invariant, to prove that the space
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(7/4)
of overconvergent modular forms of weight zero lies in the image of the character map on
S_{1}
. See Section
2.3
for a brief introduction to
p
-adic and overconvergent modular forms, and in particular for a definition of the space
M^{\dagger}_{2}(r)
. In Remark 6.8 of
[
FM25
]
, it was proposed that instead of working with the description
\mathcal{M}_{2}=\mathbb{Q}_{2}\langle j^{-1}\rangle
, the alternative description
\mathcal{M}_{2}=\mathbb{Q}_{2}\langle\lambda\rangle
, where
\lambda
is a genus-zero Hauptmodul on
\Gamma_{0}(2)
, might yield better
p
-adic properties. This would be in analogy with similar computations in number theory, cf.
[
BC05
,
BK05
]
. Indeed, our first main theorem is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

The space
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(1/2)
of
2
-adic overconvergent modular forms of level one and weight zero lies in the image of the character map on
S_{1}
.

Since there are containments
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(r)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(s)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}
when
s\leq r
, Theorem
1.1
represents an improvement over the analogous result of
[
FM25
]
.

Though our focus will be when
p=2
, the hauptmodul
\lambda\in M_{0}\left(\Gamma_{0}(p)\right)
over any prime
p\in\{2,3,5,7,13\}
is given by the expression

\displaystyle\lambda=\left(\frac{\eta(q^{p})}{\eta(q)}\right)^{\frac{24}{p-1}}.

This is a meromorphic function on the compact modular curve
X_{0}(p)
possessing a simple zero at the cusp and a pole at zero. Here,
\eta(q)
is the Dedekind-eta function of weight
1/2
satisfying
\eta(q)^{24}=\Delta(q)
where

\displaystyle\Delta(q)=\frac{E_{4}(q)^{3}-E_{6}(q)^{2}}{1728}

is the modular discriminant of weight 12, and

\displaystyle E_{k}(q)=1-\frac{2k}{B_{k}}\sum_{n\geq 1}\sigma_{k-1}(n)q^{n}

denotes the normalized Eisenstein series of level one of weight
k
on
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Much of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is dedicated to showing that the powers
\lambda^{n}
can be realized as the characters of specific states in
S_{1}
. Hence, if we ignore the convergence conditions underlying the definition of the overconvergent spaces, then Theorem
1.1
immediately implies the following simpler statement:

Theorem 1.2
.

The space
\mathbb{Q}_{2}[\lambda]
lies in the image of the character map on
S_{1}
where
\lambda=\Delta(q^{2})/\Delta(q)
.

Now in all of
[
FM22
,
BF24
,
FM25
]
, the character on
S_{1}
is defined via
p
-adic completion, and it is in particular the linear map
\widehat{F}_{S}
making the following diagram commute:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{M}_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Q}_{2}[E_{2},E_{4},E_{6}]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widehat{F}_{S}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{S}}

Hence, we summarize the process of showing that specific
p
-adic modular forms arise as characters here using this diagram: First we find a sequence of modular forms of level one
(\lambda_{n,m})\in M
whose
p
-adic limit is
\lambda^{n}
for each
n\geq 1
. Then we find a sequence
(v_{n,m})\in S
of states satisfying that
F_{S}(v_{n,m},q)=\lambda_{n,m}
. There is some choice for these pre-images, but we show that a rather natural choice produces the sequence
(v_{n,m})
that converges
2
-adically in
S_{1}
with respect to the sup-norm. Finally, we assess the
2
-adic properties of these

along with the description of
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(r)
to show that indeed
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(1/2)
is contained in the image of the
p
-adic character map.

We say a bit more about the first step, realizing
\lambda^{n}
as a sequence of modular functions of level one: In his 1973 paper
[
Ser73
]
, Serre proved that modular forms
f
on
\Gamma_{0}(p)
are
p
-adic by constructing an explicit sequence
(f_{m})
of forms of level one converging
p
-adically to
f
; these are obtained by “tracing” over representatives of
\Gamma_{0}(p)/\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. We apply this method directly to
\lambda^{n}
in order to obtain our sequence
(\lambda_{n,m})
with
\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{n,m}=\lambda^{n}
.

The next step is to pass to the Heisenberg
S
using eq. (
2.14
) of Mason-Tuite in
[
MT10
]
, that is, we find pre-images of the modular forms
\lambda_{n,m}
discussed above. To use the Mason-Tuite formula, it is necessary to describe the modular forms
\lambda_{n,m}
in terms of the Eisenstein basis of
M
. It is not immediately obvious how to obtain such expressions from Serre’s trace procedure that underlies the definition of
\lambda_{n,m}
. We resolve this issue via the following striking fact which serves as our second main theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

When
p=2
, the Serre sequence
(\lambda_{n,m})
satisfying that
\lambda^{n}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{n,m}
for all
n\geq 1
satisfies a third-degree modular linear differential equation for all
m\geq 1
.

The exact equation is of generalized hypergeometric
{}_{3}F_{2}
-type, and it is given in the statement of Proposition
3.4
below. Of course, it is known that all modular forms satisfy differential equations, but the degree of these equations generally increases with the weight of the form. What we find striking in this result is that, by construction, the weights of the forms
\lambda_{n,m}
in general grow with
n
and
m
, yet the degree of the corresponding MLDE is always three.

With Theorem
1.3
in hand, and knowing that the corresponding differential equation is generalized hypergeometric, we solve the MLDE using methods found in
[
FM16
,
KT01
]
, and this solution allows us to write our modular forms in the Eisenstein basis
\lambda_{n,m}\in\mathbb{Z}[E_{4},E_{6}]
for all
n,m\geq 1
. With such explicit descriptions in hand, we can then use the formula of Mason-Tuite to lift
\lambda_{n,m}
to the Heisenberg algebra. We then conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
by studying the
p
-adic properties of these explicit formulas for the pre-images.

When
p\in\{3,5,7,13\}
, we find analogous sequence terms
\lambda_{n,m}\in M
which converge to their respective hauptmoduln on
\Gamma_{0}(p)
respectively, however, we are currently unable to find an MLDE such as the one referred to in Theorem
1.3
which is satisfied by these
\lambda_{n,m}
for the odd genus zero primes. At the very least, computational evidence using SAGE suggests that there is no low-degree (monic or non-monic) MLDE which has
\lambda_{n,m}
as a solution for these primes. Besides providing general formulas for the
\lambda_{n,m}
in Subsection
3.1
below that work for all genus zero primes, we leave the search for analogous MLDEs — or an alternative method for studying the image of the
p
-adic character map — in these cases to the interested reader.

2.2.
Modular Differential Operators

Recall that the Ramanujan-Serre derivative
\mathcal{D}_{r}:M_{r}(\Gamma)\to M_{r+2}(\Gamma)
acts on modular forms
f\in M_{r}(\Gamma)
by the rule

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{r}(f)=\theta(f)-\frac{r}{12}E_{2}(q)f=\theta(f)+2rG_{2}(q)f

where
\theta=q\frac{d}{dq}
. Define also the iterates

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{r}^{n}=\mathcal{D}_{r+2(n-1)}\circ\mathcal{D}_{r+2(n-2)}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{D}_{r},

keeping in mind that
\mathcal{D}_{r}^{2}\neq\mathcal{D}_{r}\circ\mathcal{D}_{r}
. The modular derivative satisfies the Leibniz rule in the following way: For
fg\in M_{r}(\Gamma)
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{r}(fg)=\mathcal{D}_{a}(f)\cdot g+f\cdot\mathcal{D}_{b}(g)
where
r=a+b
. Hence we will often omit writing the subscript in the notation for the modular derivative when the weight is clear. The operator
\theta
will also play an important role, and its interaction with Eisenstein series is given by the well-known Ramanujan identities

(2.2)

\displaystyle\theta(E_{2}(q))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\left(E_{2}(q)^{2}-E_{4}(q)\right)

(2.3)

\displaystyle\theta(E_{4}(q))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left(E_{2}(q)E_{4}(q)-E_{6}(q)\right)

(2.4)

\displaystyle\theta(E_{6}(q))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{2}(q)E_{6}(q)-E_{4}(q)^{2}\right).

A monic degree-
t
modular linear differential equation (MLDE) over
M^{\prime}
is then an expression of the form

\displaystyle c_{t}\mathcal{D}^{t}(f(q))+c_{t-1}\mathcal{D}^{t-1}(f(q))g_{t-1}(q)+\cdots+c_{0}g_{0}(q)=0

where
g_{i}(q)\in M^{\prime}_{2t-2i}
for some coefficients
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
, and where
f(q)
is called a solution of the MLDE. As a modular linear differential operator or “MLDO”, it takes modular forms of weight
n
to those of weight
n+2t
.

2.3.
p
-adic modular forms

Our description of
p
-adic modular forms follows the classical one of Serre in
[
Ser73
]
. That is, a
p
-adic modular form is a formal power series

(2.5)

\displaystyle f=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}q^{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}[[q]]

where
f
is the limit of a sequence of modular forms
f_{m}\in M
having weight
k_{m}
. Note the limit here is taken over the sup-norm; for
g=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}q^{j}\in\mathbb{Q}[[q]]
, this is

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(g)=\inf_{j}\nu_{p}(c_{j}).

The space
\mathcal{M}_{p}
is then defined as the completion of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}[E_{4},E_{6}]
with respect to the sup-norm above. We note also that the weight of
f
in (
2.5
) is an element of the space

\displaystyle X=\varprojlim_{m}\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}(p-1)\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}/(p-1)\mathbb{Z}

where
X\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
when
p=2
. Important examples of
p
-adic modular forms are the
p
-adic Eisenstein series, which we will be repeatedly expressing in the form

\displaystyle E_{k}^{\star}(q)=E_{k}(q)-p^{k-1}E_{k}(q^{p}),\hskip 10.0ptk\geq 2.

Again our focus will be when
p=2
, and we showcase some important identities in this case. We have
M(\Gamma_{0}(2))=\mathbb{C}[E_{2}^{\star},E_{4}]
(see pg. 3 of
[
Got20
]
), so the following are easy to check:

(2.6)

\displaystyle E_{4}^{\star}(q)

\displaystyle=-10E_{2}^{\star}(q)^{2}+3E_{4}(q)

(2.7)

\displaystyle E_{6}^{\star}(q)

\displaystyle=40E_{2}^{\star}(q)^{3}-9E_{2}^{\star}(q)E_{4}(q)

Along with the Ramanujan identities (
2.2
)-(
2.4
) we may compute their modular derivatives:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(E_{2}^{\star}(q)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}E_{2}^{\star}(q)^{2}-\frac{1}{6}E_{4}(q)

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(E_{4}^{\star}(q)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{8}{3}E_{2}^{\star}(q)^{3}+\frac{1}{3}E_{2}^{\star}(q)E_{4}(q).

These will be used later in Subsection
3.2
when deriving the MLDE in Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem
1.1
is concerned with the subspaces
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\dagger}(r)\subset\mathcal{M}_{p}
of
r
-overconvergent forms. Here we outline relevant facts from Section 3 of
[
Von21
]
as well as
[
BC05
]
, in particular when
p=2
. The space of
2
-adic modular forms has the description

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}\cong\mathbb{Q}_{2}\langle j^{-1}\rangle=\Set{a_{0}+a_{1}j^{-1}+a_{2}j^{-2}+\cdots}{|a_{n}|_{2}\to 0}

where
|\cdot|_{2}
denotes the
2
-adic absolute value. For
r\geq 0
, the spaces
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(r)
are Banach spaces inside
\mathcal{M}_{2}
which have additional growth conditions on the coefficients, hence the name “overconvergent”. Alternatively, and motivated by number theoretical methods (cf.
[
BC05
,
BK05
]
), we have the description
\mathcal{M}_{2}\cong\mathbb{Q}_{2}\langle\lambda\rangle
where
\lambda=\Delta(q^{2})/\Delta(q)
, and the space of
r
-overconvergent forms becomes

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(r)=\mathbb{Q}_{2}\langle 2^{12r}\lambda\rangle=\Set{a_{0}+a_{1}\lambda+a_{2}\lambda^{2}+\cdots}{|a_{n}|_{2}\cdot 2^{12nr}\to 0}.

The functions
j^{-1}
and
\lambda
are related via the expression

\displaystyle j^{-1}=\frac{\lambda}{(1+2^{8}\lambda)^{3}},

and we note that
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(r)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(s)
whenever

. In Subsection
4.1
, we construct a sequence

and in Subsection

, we prove that its

-adic limit

, which satisfies

, lies in

for all

. The proof of Theorem

is found in Subsection

where we show that for all

,

. From the linearity of

, this will show that the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}^{\dagger}(1/2)=\mathbb{Q}_{2}\langle 2^{6}\lambda\rangle=\Set{a_{0}+a_{1}\widehat{F}_{S}(v_{1},q)+a_{2}\widehat{F}_{S}(v_{2},q)+\cdots}{|a_{n}|_{2}\cdot 2^{6n}\to 0}

lies in the image of

.

2.4.
The Heisenberg VOA

For the purposes of this manuscript, we only require a few basic facts about the Heisenberg VOA
S
, though we refer the reader to
[
LL04
]
for an excellent overview of its construction. We also assume
S
has central charge
c_{V}=1
throughout. The VOA
S
can be interpreted as the symmetric algebra in the variables
h(-n)
where
n\geq 1
, along with a distinguished element
1
acting as the identity. The terms
h(n)
for
n\geq 1
act as scalar partial derivatives
n\partial_{h(-n)}
, and
h(0)
acts as zero. Each monomial vector in
S
, or “state”, is assigned a weight given by

(2.11)

\displaystyle\text{wt}(h(-n_{1})h(-n_{2})\cdots h(-n_{r})\textbf{1})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}

so that
S
possesses a
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
grading. A key feature of
S
and in fact of any VOA is its “square-bracket” counterpart introduced in
[
Zhu96
]
. This is a VOA isomorphic to
S
however now with endomorphisms
h[n]
where for
n\leq-1
these act again by multiplication and as scalar partial derivatives for
n\geq 1
. Explicitly, these are given in terms of the round-bracket variables by expanding

\displaystyle h[n]=\text{Res}_{z}Y(h(-1)\textbf{1},e^{z}-1)(e^{z})(z^{n})

where
Y
denote the vertex operator on
S
. We not refer to this description for the remainder of the discussion, and in fact, we will only require the following expressions which can be easily computed from above:

(2.12)

\displaystyle h[-2]=h(-2)+h(-1)-\frac{1}{240}h(2)+\frac{1}{240}h(3)-\cdots

(2.13)

\displaystyle h[-3]=h(-3)+\frac{3}{2}h(-2)+\frac{1}{2}h(-1)+\frac{1}{240}h(1)-\frac{1}{480}h(2)+\frac{1}{945}h(3)-\cdots

For the square-bracket Heisenberg VOA, we then have another grading analogous to (
2.11
) defined in the obvious way. We note, however that for a VOA
V
we only have the equality

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{n\leq N}V_{(n)}=\bigoplus_{n\leq N}V_{[n]}\hskip 15.0ptN\in\mathbb{Z}

where
V_{(n)}
denotes the homogeneous degree-
n
subspace of
V
, and
V_{[n]}
is the same in the square-bracket grading. This follows from the expansion of the square-bracket Virasoro modes as

\displaystyle L[0]=L(0)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k(k+1)}L(k)

as well as the fact that the grading on a VOA coincides with its
L(0)
-eigenspace decomposition. The point is that states of
S
of square-bracket weight
k
are modular forms of level one and weight
k
. To be more precise, let
F_{S}
denote the normalized character of
v\in V
:

\displaystyle F_{S}(v,q)=\eta(q)Z_{S}(v,q)=q^{-1/24}\eta(q)\sum_{n\geq 0}\text{Tr}_{S_{(n)}}o(v)q^{n}

where
o(v)
is the unique endomorphism associated to
v
which preserves the round-bracket grading of
S
. Note above that the trace is taken over the
round
-bracket grading. We use the following re-formulation of equation (44) of
[
MT10
]
in order to compute
F_{S}(v,q)
for a state
v=h[-k_{1}]h[-k_{2}]\cdots h[-k_{r}]\textbf{1}
for
k_{i}\geq 1
not necessarily distinct:

(2.14)

\displaystyle F_{S}(v,q)=\sum_{(\ldots\{s,t\}\ldots)\in\mathcal{P}(\Phi,2)}\prod_{\{s,t\}}\frac{2(-1)^{s+1}}{(s-1)!(t-1)!}G_{s+t}(q).

Here,
\mathcal{P}(\Phi,2)
denotes all partitions of the set
\Phi=\{k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{r}\}
into parts
\{s,t\}
of size two. Then for each such partition, the product is taken over each part. With this, one can show that
F_{S}:S\to M^{\prime}
is a surjection of graded linear spaces. We note however that
F_{S}
has large kernel, as for example, any state of odd weight is mapped to zero.

A major technical aspect of the process of computing characters in
S_{1}
is that the
p
-adic convergence of pre-images needs to be assessed in the round-bracket formalism and not in the square-bracket one, despite the fact that the description of the pre-images via eq. (
2.14
) is given in the latter. This technicality will be addressed in Subsections
4.2
and
4.1
by constructing an isomorphism of vector spaces between a certain subspace
\Sigma\subset S
and
M^{\prime}
, then using the formulas (
2.12
) and (
2.13
) above to obtain a lower bound on the
2
-adic valuation of the pre-images
v_{n,m}
, rather than an explicit formula in the round-bracket formalism.

2.5.
The
p
-adic Heisenberg VOA

We briefly recall some notions on
p
-adic VOAs also stated in
[
BF24
]
, however the inclined reader should consult
[
FM23
]
for all of the necessary details. In general, given an algebraic VOA
U
over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, one can define the
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module

\displaystyle\widehat{U}=\varprojlim_{m}U/p^{m}U

and set
\widehat{V}=\widehat{U}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\mathbb{Q}_{p}
which carries the structure of a
p
-adic VOA. Equivalently one can define
\widehat{V}
in terms of a completion with respect to a sup-norm arising from an integral basis of
U
. Now when
V=S
which we view as algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, we define the space
S_{r}
for
r>0
as the completion of
S
with respect to the sup-norm

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{I}a_{I}h^{I}\right\rvert_{r}=\sup_{I}|a_{I}|r^{I}

for a finite multi-subset
I
of
\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
with
|I|=-\sum_{i\in I}i
and where
h^{I}=\prod_{i\in I}h(i)
. Elements of
S_{r}
are then of the form
\sum_{I}a_{I}h^{I}
where

\displaystyle\lim_{|I|\to\infty}|a_{I}|r^{|I|}=0.

The space
S_{1}
is a
p
-adic VOA, and it will be our focus for the remainder of the manuscript. Note for different values of

, one has a filtration
S_{1}\supseteq S_{2}\supseteq S_{3}\supseteq\cdots
.

3.
Modular Differential Equations

3.1.
Serre’s Sequence

Let
p\in\{2,3,5,7,13\}
. Following Théorème 10 of
[
Ser73
]
and for any
n\geq 1
, we construct a sequence
\lambda_{n,m}
of modular forms of level one satisfying
\lambda^{n}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{n,m}
by tracing over the representatives of
\Gamma_{0}(p)/\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. We will see shortly that the
\lambda_{n,m}
possess a pole of order
n
at the zero cusp, hence we will be considering the sequence
\Delta(q)^{n}\lambda_{n,m}\in M_{12\cdot 2^{m}+12n}
satisfying that
\Delta(q)^{n}\lambda^{n}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\Delta(q)^{n}\lambda_{n,m}
. Only after realizing each as the solution of an MLDE in Proposition
3.4
and getting explicit expressions in Proposition
3.7
will we see that the
\lambda_{n,m}
are modular of level one as expected, and we will divide by
\Delta(q)^{n}
. Let us set

(3.1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n,m}=\lambda^{n}g^{3\cdot p^{m}}+p^{1-6\cdot p^{m}}\left.\left(\left.\lambda^{n}g^{3\cdot p^{m}}\right\rvert_{12\cdot p^{m}}\hskip 2.0ptW\right)\hskip 2.0pt\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{p}.

Note that
\lambda_{n,m}
has weight
12\cdot p^{m}
, the form
g\in M_{4}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
is given by

\displaystyle g=E_{4}(q)-p^{4}\cdot E_{4}(q^{p}),

and
f\mid_{r}W
denotes application of the slash operator for a modular form
f
of weight
r
with

\displaystyle W=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
p&0\end{pmatrix},

and
U_{p}
is the linear operator acting on
q
-series, given by the rule

\displaystyle f\mid\hskip 1.0ptU_{p}=\left.\left(\sum_{i}a_{i}q^{i}\right)\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{p}=\sum_{i}a_{pi}q^{i}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p
be prime. For formal Laurent series
f(q),g(q)\in\mathbb{C}[[q]]
, we have the identities

(1)

\left.\left(f(q)g(q^{p})\right)\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{p}=g(q)\left.\left(f(q)\right)\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{p}

(2)

\left.\theta(f(q))\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{p}=p\cdot\theta\left(\left.f(q)\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{p}\right)
where
\theta=q\frac{d}{dq}

(3)

For all
k\geq 2
even,
\left.E_{k}^{\star}(q)\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{p}=E_{k}^{\star}(q)
.

Proof.

Parts (1) and (2) are easily proven from the definitions. For part (3), see pg. 210 of
[
Ser73
]
.
∎

It is easy to compute

\displaystyle\left.\lambda^{n}g^{3\cdot p^{m}}\right\rvert_{12\cdot p^{m}}\hskip 2.0ptW=p^{6\cdot p^{m}}\left(p\tau\right)^{-12\cdot p^{m}}\left(\frac{\eta(-1/\tau)}{\eta(-1/p\tau)}\right)^{\frac{24n}{p-1}}\left(E_{4}(-1/p\tau)-p^{4}E_{4}(-1/\tau)\right)^{3\cdot p^{m}},

and with modular transformation laws

\displaystyle\eta(-1/\tau)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-i\tau}\cdot\eta(\tau)

\displaystyle E_{4}(-1/\tau)

\displaystyle=\tau^{4}E_{k}(\tau)

and

\displaystyle\eta(-1/p\tau)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-ip\tau}\cdot\eta(p\tau)

\displaystyle E_{4}(-1/p\tau)

\displaystyle=(p\tau)^{4}E_{4}(p\tau)

obtained by setting
\tau\mapsto p\tau
, we have

\displaystyle\left.\lambda^{n}g^{3\cdot p^{m}}\right\rvert_{12\cdot p^{m}}\hskip 2.0ptW=p^{6\cdot p^{m}-\frac{12n}{p-1}}\cdot\lambda^{-n}\cdot\left(E_{4}(p\tau)-E_{4}(\tau)\right)^{3\cdot p^{m}}

so that we may re-write eq. (
3.1
) as

(3.2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n,m}=\lambda^{n}\left(E_{4}(q)-p^{4}E_{4}(q^{p})\right)^{3\cdot p^{m}}+p^{1-\frac{12n}{p-1}}\left.\left(\lambda^{-n}\left(E_{4}(q^{p})-E_{4}(q)\right)^{3\cdot p^{m}}\right)\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{p}.

and
\lambda^{n}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{n,m}
as desired.

3.2.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

. For

(3.3)

Proof.

Note first that for all

\lambda^{n}
satisfies the MLDE in the claim with the modular derivative acting with weight zero.

Since
\mathcal{D}(\Delta(q))=0
we have

and so

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\Delta(q^{2}))=2E_{2}(q^{2})\Delta(q^{2})-E_{2}(q)\Delta(q^{2}).

Also since
\mathcal{D}(\lambda^{n})=\mathcal{D}(\Delta(q^{2})^{n})\cdot\Delta(q)^{-n}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\lambda^{n})=-n\lambda^{n}E_{2}^{\star}(q).

The identities (

)-(

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{2}\left(\lambda^{n}\right)=\left(n^{2}-\frac{n}{3}\right)\lambda^{n}E_{2}^{\star}(q)^{2}+\frac{n}{6}\lambda^{n}E_{4}(q),

and from this we get

\mathcal{D}^{3}\left(\lambda^{n}\right)=\left(-n\left(n^{2}-\frac{n}{3}\right)+\frac{2}{3}\left(n^{2}-\frac{n}{3}\right)\right)\lambda^{n}E_{2}^{\star}(q)^{3}\\
+\left(-\frac{1}{3}\left(n^{2}-\frac{n}{3}\right)-\frac{n^{2}}{6}\right)\lambda^{n}E_{2}^{\star}(q)E_{4}(q)-\frac{n}{18}\lambda^{n}E_{6}(q),

so using the identity
E_{6}(q)=-4E_{2}^{\star}(q)^{3}+3E_{2}^{\star}(q)E_{4}(q)
this simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{3}\left(\lambda^{n}\right)=\left(\left(n^{2}-n\right)E_{2}^{\star}(q)^{2}+\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{18}\right)E_{4}(q)\right)\mathcal{D}(\lambda^{n}),

and the result follows from eq. (
3.4

∎

We wish to show the
\Delta(q)^{n}\lambda_{n,m}

operator and the modular derivative, we wish to simplify the second term of (

\displaystyle\lambda^{-n}\mathcal{E}_{4}(q)^{3\cdot 2^{m}}=\Delta(q^{2})^{2^{m+1}-n}\Delta(q)^{n-2^{m}}.

Using Lemma

\displaystyle\left.\left(\lambda^{-n}\mathcal{E}_{4}(q)^{3\cdot 2^{m}}\right)\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{2}=\Delta(q)^{2^{m+1}-n}\left.\left(\Delta(q)^{n-2^{m}}\right)\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{2}.

Putting everything together and using identities derived in Lemma
3.2
, eq. (

(3.5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n,m}=\lambda^{n}\left(5E_{4}(q)-20E_{2}^{\star}(q)^{2}\right)^{3\cdot 2^{m}}+2^{1-12n}(240)^{3\cdot 2^{m}}\Delta(q)^{2^{m+1}-n}\left.\left(\Delta(q)^{n-2^{m}}\right)\right\rvert\hskip 1.0ptU_{2}.

This is done using (
2.14
). Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
and consider
F_{S}(h[-2]^{2r}\textbf{1},q)
. In this case,

\displaystyle\Phi=\{\underbrace{2,2,\ldots,2}_{\text{$2r$ times}}\}

and there are
(2r-1)!!
ways of partitioning
\Phi
into
r
parts
\{2,2\}
. So,

\displaystyle F_{S}(h[-2]^{2r}\textbf{1},q)=(2r-1)!!\left(\frac{2(-1)^{3}}{(2-1)!(2-1)!}G_{4}(q)\right)^{r}=(-1)^{r}\cdot 2^{r}(2r-1)!!G_{4}(q)^{r}

which shows that
F_{S}(\alpha_{r},q)=G_{4}(q)^{r}
. The proof of
F_{S}(\beta_{s},q)=G_{6}(q)^{s}
for
s\in\mathbb{N}
is similar. For
r,s\in\mathbb{N}
, consider now
F_{S}(h[-2]^{2r}h[-3]^{2s}\textbf{1},q)
. We now have that

\displaystyle\Phi=\{\underbrace{2,2,\ldots,2}_{\text{$2r$ times}},\underbrace{3,3,\ldots,3}_{\text{$2s$ times}}\}.

Notice that any partition of
\Phi
above that involves a part of the form
\{2,3\}
produces a factor of
G_{5}(q)=0
. Hence the only partitions yielding non-zero terms are those where the
2
’s and
3
’s are paired among themselves, and there are
(2r-1)!!(2s-1)!!
such partitions. Thus

\displaystyle F_{S}(h[-2]^{2r}h[-3]^{2s}\textbf{1},q)

\displaystyle=(2r-1)!!(2s-1)!!\left(\frac{2(-1)^{3}}{(2-1)!(2-1)!}G_{4}(q)\right)^{r}\left(\frac{2(-1)^{4}}{(3-1)!(3-1)!}G_{6}(q)\right)^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{r}(-1)^{r}(2r-1)!!(2s-1)!!}{2^{s}}G_{4}(q)^{r}G_{6}(q)^{s}

which proves (
4.2
). From this, it is clear that
F_{S}
restricted to
\Sigma
is injective. Let
f\in M_{k}
for some
k\geq 4
even. So we can write

\displaystyle f=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/12\rfloor\pm 1}c_{i}G_{4}(q)^{k/4-3i}G_{6}(q)^{2i}

for some
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. By letting

\displaystyle v=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/12\rfloor\pm 1}c_{i}\alpha_{k/4-3i}\beta_{2i}\in\Sigma,

from the linearity of the character map we see that
F_{S}(v,q)=f
, establishing that
F_{S}
gives the required isomorphism.
∎

Proposition
4.1
alongside the linearity of the character map show that for all
n,m\geq 1
with
a=3
, the states

(4.3)

\displaystyle v_{n,m}:=\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m}+1}c_{n,m,i}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}-3i}\beta_{2i}

where the coefficients

are given in (

4.2.
Convergence of Pre-Images

The purpose of this subsection is to develop results necessary for the proof of Proposition
4.6
, namely that for all
n,m\geq 1
, the pre-images
v_{n,m}
satisfy
\nu_{2}(v_{n,m})\geq-6n
. These results involve a close examination of the
2
-adic properties of the coefficients
c_{n,m,i}
. In contrast to
[
BF24
,
FM25
,
FM23
]
, we avoid obtaining explicit expressions for the
v_{n,m}
in the round-bracket formalism. In fact, in Lemma
4.3
, we prove a useful tool for assessing the valuation of the pre-images
\alpha_{r}\beta_{s}
(cf. (
4.1
)) for any
r,s\geq 2
. This will be used repeatedly towards the proof of Proposition
4.6
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
n,m\geq 1
. When
0\leq i\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{2}(c_{n,m,i})\geq 12\cdot 2^{m}-6n-6i,

and when
n+1\leq i\leq 2^{m}-n
we have

\displaystyle\nu_{2}(c_{n,m,i})\geq 12\cdot 2^{m}-8n-4i.

Proof.

From (
3.13
),

\nu_{2}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m}-n}\Psi_{n,m,i,j}\right)=\nu_{2}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m}-n}\binom{n+j}{i}\binom{3\cdot 2^{m}-3n-2j}{j}\frac{3\cdot 2^{m}-3n}{3\cdot 2^{m}-3n-2j}\cdot(-1)^{j}\cdot 2^{2j}\cdot 3^{-3j}\right)\\
+12\cdot 2^{m}-6n-6i

Suppose
0\leq i\leq n
. Since each term in the sum above has non-negative valuation, we can bound

\displaystyle\nu_{2}(c_{n,m,i})\geq 12\cdot 2^{m}-6n-6i.

Likewise when
n+1\leq i\leq 2^{m}+1
, we have
i-n\leq j\leq 2^{m}-n
. So it is not hard to see that we can write

\displaystyle\nu_{2}(c_{n,m,i})\geq 12\cdot 2^{m}-8n-4i.

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
r,s
be integers with
r,s\geq 2
. Then

\displaystyle\nu_{2}\left(\alpha_{r}\beta_{s}\right)=-4r-3s.

Proof.

We write (
2.12
) and (
2.13
) as the following commuting operators:

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=h(-2)+h(-1)-\frac{1}{120}\partial_{h(-2)}+\frac{1}{120}\partial_{h(-3)}

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=h(-3)+\frac{3}{2}h(-2)+\frac{1}{2}h(-1)+\frac{1}{240}\partial_{h(-1)}-\frac{1}{240}\partial_{h(-2)}+\frac{1}{315}\partial_{h(-3)}.

The fact that these commute is direct from the property that
h[-3]
and
h[-2]
commute. We see that for
r,s\in\mathbb{N}
,
\nu_{2}(X^{r}Y^{s}\textbf{1})=-3r-4s
since
\nu_{2}(120)=3
and
\nu_{2}(240)=4
. So from the definitions of
\alpha_{r}
and
\beta_{s}
in (
4.1
), we have
\nu_{2}(\alpha_{r}\beta_{s})=-4r-3s
as desired.
∎

We now establish that the sequence
\{v_{n,m}\}_{m\geq 1}
for fixed
n\geq 1
is Cauchy. Write

\displaystyle\nu_{2}

\displaystyle(v_{n,m+1}-v_{n,m})

\displaystyle=\nu_{2}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m+1}}c_{n,m+1,i}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m+1}-3i}\beta_{2i}-\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m}}c_{n,m,j}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}-3j}\beta_{2j}\right)

(4.4)

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{\nu_{2}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m}}c_{n,m+1,i}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m+1}-3i}\beta_{2i}-c_{n,m,i}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}-3i}\beta_{2i}\right),\nu_{2}\left(\sum_{j=2^{m}+1}^{2^{m+1}}c_{n,m+1,j}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m+1}-3j}\beta_{2j}\right)\right\}.

Note that
\nu_{2}(\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m+1}-3j}\beta_{2j})=-12\cdot 2^{m+1}+6j
from Lemma
4.3
. Since we have assumed
n<2^{m}
, from Lemma
4.2
, whenever
2^{m}+1\leq j\leq 2^{m+1}
we get

\displaystyle\nu_{2}(c_{n,m+1,j}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m+1}-3j}\beta_{2j})\geq 2j-8n.

Thus the second term in (
4.4
) tends to infinity as
m\to\infty
. We can write

\displaystyle\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m+1}-3i}=\frac{(6\cdot 2^{m}-6i-1)!!(6\cdot 2^{m}-1)!!}{(6\cdot 2^{m+1}-6i-1)!!}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}-3i}

where the coefficient above has
2
-adic valuation of zero. With this, the first term in (
4.4
) becomes

\displaystyle\nu_{2}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m}}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}-3i}\beta_{2i}\left(c_{n,m+1,i}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}-c_{n,m,i}\right)\right).

Using Lemma
4.3
once again, it remains to prove that

\displaystyle\nu_{2}\left(c_{n,m+1,i}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}-c_{n,m,i}\right)-12\cdot 2^{m}+6i\to\infty

for all
0\leq i\leq 2^{m}
as
m\to\infty
. We require a small lemma first:

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
r\geq 1
. Then

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{2^{r}(2r-1)!!}\sum_{l=0}^{r}\binom{2r}{2l}\frac{(2l)!}{(-240)^{l}l!}\left(h(-2)+h(-1)\right)^{2(r-l)}\textbf{1}.

Proof.

We obtain a formula for
h[-2]^{2r}\textbf{1}
. It was shown in Section 3.2 of
[
BF24
]
that the Rodrigue’s formula for the generalized Hermite polynomials

(4.5)

\displaystyle\text{He}_{r}^{\alpha}(x)=(x-\gamma\partial_{x})^{r}\cdot 1=\sum_{l\geq 0}\binom{r}{2l}\frac{(-\gamma)^{l}(2l)!}{2^{l}(l)!}x^{r-2l}

with

and
\gamma>0
can be used to prove that

\displaystyle h[-1]^{r}\textbf{1}=\sum_{l\geq 0}\binom{r}{2l}\frac{(2l)!}{l!(-24)^{l}}h(-1)^{r-2l}\textbf{1}

by setting

and

. By setting

\displaystyle W=h(-2)+h(-1)-\frac{1}{120}\partial_{h(-2)}

(cf. Lemma
4.3

h[-2]^{2r}\textbf{1}=W^{2r}\textbf{1}
. So by letting

and

in (

) and using the definition of

in (

) we get the desired expression.
∎

From above, as

, the valuation of the coefficient of

is decreasing, and reaches a minimum at the constant term when

(cf. Lemma

). In particular, this shows that

\nu_{2}\left(c_{n,m+1,i}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}-c_{n,m,i}\right)\\
=\min\left\{\nu_{2}\left(c_{n,m+1,i}\text{Coeff}_{h(-1)^{2}\textbf{1}}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}\right),\nu_{2}\left(c_{n,m+1,i}\left(\text{Coeff}_{\textbf{1}}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}\right)-c_{n,m,i}\right)\right\}.

Proposition 4.5
.

Fix
n\geq 1
. Then for all
0\leq i\leq 2^{m}+1
, and as

we have

\displaystyle\nu_{2}\left(c_{n,m+1,i}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}-c_{n,m,i}\right)-12\cdot 2^{m}+6i\to\infty

Proof.

From Lemma
4.4
,

\displaystyle\text{Coeff}_{\textbf{1}}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(6\cdot 2^{m})!}{(480)^{3\cdot 2^{m}}(3\cdot 2^{m})!(6\cdot 2^{m}-1)!!}

\displaystyle=2^{-12\cdot 2^{m}}\cdot 3^{-3\cdot 2^{m}}\cdot 5^{-3\cdot 2^{m}},

and

\displaystyle\text{Coeff}_{h(-1)^{2}\textbf{1}}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}

\displaystyle=-45\cdot 2^{m+3}\left(\frac{(6\cdot 2^{m})!}{(480)^{3\cdot 2^{m}}(3\cdot 2^{m})!(6\cdot 2^{m}-1)!!}\right)

\displaystyle=-2^{-12\cdot 2^{m}+m+3}\cdot 3^{-3\cdot 2^{m}+2}\cdot 5^{-3\cdot 2^{m}+1}.

Suppose that
0\leq i\leq n
. It is not difficult to compute using Lemma

that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\nu_{2}\left(c_{n,m+1,i}\text{Coeff}_{h(-1)^{2}\textbf{1}}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}\right)\geq 12\cdot 2^{m}-6n-6i+m+3.

Likewise, when

(4.7)

\displaystyle\nu_{2}\left(c_{n,m+1,i}\text{Coeff}_{h(-1)^{2}\textbf{1}}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}\right)\geq 12\cdot 2^{m}-8n-4i+m+3.

Now for the constant terms:

\displaystyle c_{n,m+1,i}\text{Coeff}_{\textbf{1}}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}-c_{n,m,i}

\displaystyle=2^{-12\cdot 2^{m}}\cdot 3^{-3\cdot 2^{m}}\cdot 5^{-3\cdot 2^{m}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m+1}-n}\Psi_{n,m+1,i,j}-\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m}-n}\Psi_{n,m,i,j}.

which have valuation equal to

\nu_{2}\left(3^{3\cdot 2^{m}}\cdot 5^{3\cdot 2^{m}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m+1}-n}\binom{n+j}{i}\binom{6\cdot 2^{m}-3n-2j}{j}\frac{6\cdot 2^{m}-3n}{6\cdot 2^{m}-3n-2j}(-1)^{j}\cdot 2^{2j}\cdot 3^{-3j}\right.\\
\left.-\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m}-n}\binom{n+j}{i}\binom{3\cdot 2^{m}-3n-2j}{j}\frac{3\cdot 2^{m}-3n}{3\cdot 2^{m}-3n-2j}(-1)^{j}\cdot 2^{2j}\cdot 3^{-3j}\right)+12\cdot 2^{m}-6n-6i.

Consider the valuation of the difference of sums above. When

, the minimum is reached when

so that

\displaystyle\nu_{2}\left(c_{n,m+1,i}\text{Coeff}_{\textbf{1}}\alpha_{3\cdot 2^{m}}-c_{n,m,i}\right)

\displaystyle=12\cdot 2^{m}-6n-6i+\nu_{2}\left(\binom{n}{i}\left((1-2^{4})^{3\cdot 2^{m}}-1\right)\right)

\displaystyle=12\cdot 2^{m}-6n-6i+m+4+\nu_{2}\left(\binom{n}{i}\right).

\displaystyle\geq 12\cdot 2^{m}-8n-4i+m+2-2i+2n

\displaystyle=12\cdot 2^{m}-6n-6i+m+2.

Looking at all four cases (
