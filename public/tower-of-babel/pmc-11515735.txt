Influence of sonic activation of epoxy-resin and calcium silicate sealer on postoperative pain: a patient-blinded, parallel group, randomized clinical trial
Authors: Koray Yılmaz, Merve Sarı
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis clinical study aims to compare postoperative pain after single-visit root canal treatment of teeth with asymptomatic apical periodontitis using epoxy-resin-based AH Plus and calcium silicate-based Endosequence BC sealers with or without sonic activation.MethodsThis study included 72 individuals with one first or second mandibular premolar tooth with asymptomatic apical periodontitis. They were randomly divided into four groups according to the root canal sealer (AH Plus or Endosequence BC) and activation protocol (sonic activation or non-activation) (n = 18). The participants were ask to rate their postoperative pain intensity on a NRS scale as none, minimal, moderate, or severe after 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 7 days following treatment. Patients were also asked to record the number of prescribed analgesic medication tablets (400 mg of ibuprofen) taken. Statistical analysis were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, the Friedman test, the Spearman’s correlation test, the Chi-square test. Significance level was established at 0.05.ResultsThere was no statistically significant difference in postoperative pain scores or analgesic intake between AH Plus and Endosequence BC sealers regardless of the activation protocol (p > 0.05). There was no statistically significant difference in postoperative pain scores between the sonic activation and non-activation groups (p > 0.05). A weak positive correlation was detected between Endoactivator sonic activation and sealer extrusion (r = 0.36).ConclusionsAH Plus and EndoSequence BC root canal sealers showed similar results in terms of prevalance and intensity of postoperative pain. Sonic activation and non-activation groups had similar postoperative pain scores.Clinical trial registrationThe study was retrospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06403293). Date of Registration: 07/05/24.

Introduction

Root filling quality is an essential factor that affects the success of root canal treatment (RCT). Root filling has serves purposes: it prevents the proliferation of residual microorganisms after chemomechanical disinfection procedures, prevents apical or coronal microleakage, and promotes periapical tissue healing with appropriate materials and techniques [1]. The complicated morphology of the root canal system, including dentinal tubules, irregular areas, and ramifications, poses a challenge for achieving ideal root filling. Root filling is usually performed using a combination of a root canal sealer and a core material. Root canal sealers play crucial roles in sealing gaps and canal irregularities between the core material and the canal walls [2]. AH Plus (Dentsply, Germany) is an epoxy-resin-based sealer known for its favorable physical properties including low solubility and adequate dimensional stability. It is often used as a reference material for evaluating other sealers [3]. Hydrophilic calcium silicate sealers (CSS) exhibit similar or superior physico-chemical and biological properties compared to epoxy-resin based sealers [4]. Owing to their ability to induce mineralized tissue formation, small particle size, and hydrophilic nature these sealers show good adaptation to canal walls allowing for hermetic filling [5]. EndoSequence BC (Brasseler, USA) is a CSS sealer available in a pre-mixed ready-to-use injectable form.

Previous studies have indicated that various activation systems can improve the adaptation of sealers to dentinal walls and enhance the penetration of the sealer into dentinal tubules and lateral canals [6]. Endoactivator (EA) (Dentsply, OK) is a sonic activation system with plastic tips that operates at a frequency of 2000–6000 Hz [7].

The prevalence of pain after RCT varies between 3 and 58% and is associated with multiple mechanical, chemical, and microbial factors [8]. The extrusion of infected debris into apical tissues during chemomechanical preparation, or contact with irrigation solutions, intra-canal medications, filling materials, and their constituents with periradicular tissues can lead to increased release of inflammatory mediators such as histamine, prostaglandins, and neuropeptides; consequently triggering postoperative pain and tenderness [9]. Most studies have investigated the effect of different root canal sealers on pain [10–12]; however, the impact of sealer activation methods on postoperative pain has not yet been explored. Therefore, the aim of this study is to comparatively evaluate the effect of EndoActivator activation of AH Plus and EndoSequence BC sealers on postoperative pain in teeth with asymptomatic apical periodontitis. The null hypothesis is that there will be no statistically significant difference in postoperative pain intensity between root canal sealers, and also no statistically significant difference in postoperative pain intensity between activation regimens.

Materials and methods

Hatay Mustafa Kemal University, Clinical Research Ethics Committee approved this single blind, randomized trial with parallel experimental group (Protocol Number: 14/12/2022-08). The trial design was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov. This randomized clinical trial has been written in accordance with CONSORT reporting guidelines [13].

The sample size was calculated using the G*Power v.3.1.9.2 (Heinrich Heine, Dusseldorf University, Dusseldorf, Germany), on the basis of a previous study [10]. An alpha-type error of 0.05, a beta power of 0.80, and an effect size of 0.65 were specified. The sample size for each group was determined to be as 16 (allocation ratio 1:1:1:1). Taking into account for potential patient dropout, a total of a 72 patients were enrolled.

Patient selection

Inclusion Criteria: Patients aged 18–60, classified as medically healthy (ASA I or II), who were referred to our clinic for root canal treatment (RCT) between January 2023 and November 2023. Included patients presented with mandibular first and second premolars diagnosed with asymptomatic apical periodontitis. The diagnosis was confirmed during initial visits through radiographic examination (periapical radiograph) and clinical tests (no response to electric pulp test or cold test (Endo Ice; Coltene, Altstätten, Switzerland), and no pain on percussion or palpation). Only single-rooted teeth with a single canal and periapical index score is from 2 to 4 were included [14]. Teeth without prosthetic crown preparation were chosen. Teeth eligible for coronal restorations that would enable occlusal contact in the same session were selected.

Exclusion Criteria: Patients were excluded if they had a history of previous RCT, had taken analgesics or antibiotics within the last 7 days, presented with symptomatic teeth, sinus tracts, or periodontal disease (probing depth > 4 mm). Additionally, teeth with an initial file size larger than ISO #20 K-file were excluded. Each patient was informed about the trial, and written consent was taken from the volunteers. All treatment procedures were performed by a single endodontist.

Root canal treatment

After administering inferior alveolar nervus block anesthesia with 4% Articaine with 1:100.000 adrenaline, the tooth was isolated with a rubber dam. A traditional access cavity was prepared with a high-speed diamond round bur with water cooling. The WL was set at 0.5 mm shorter than the 0.0 reading on apex locator (Morita Root ZX, Japan) and confirmed with radiographs. The root canals were prepared using a Reciproc (VDW, Munich, Germany) R25 and R40 files, respectively, at the WL using the crown down technique. The root canals were irrigated with 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) after every three pecking motions. During the shaping process, a total of 20 mL of NaOCl was delivered using a 30-gauge Max-i-Probe needle (Dentsply Sirona). Next, 5 mL of distilled water was left in the canal for 1 min to neutralize the effect of NaOCl, and then the root canals were irrigated with 3 ml of 17% ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA). EDTA was activated for 3 × 20 s with the EA 25/04 tip, which was 2 mm shorter than the WL. The EA tip was used with 2–4 mm vertical strokes. The root canals were subsequently irrigated with 5 mL distilled water and dried with paper points.

The subjects were divided into four groups for root filling. Randomization was performed using online software (www.randomizer.org) with a four-block-sized block randomization technique to ensure even distribution between the groups (allocation ratio 1:1:1:1). A co-investigator managed the allocation and reported the allocated treatment procedure to the operator. The operator could not be blinded because of the different processes involved in the experimental protocols. However, the patients were blinded to the chosen root canal sealer and activation protocol.

Experimental groups

AH Plus-NA; Master cone was tested for tug-back before the root filling. AH Plus was mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Before placing the master cone into the canal, the sealer was carried into the canal with a gutta-percha cone. The sealer was not activated. Then, the R40 gutta-percha (VDW), coated with sealer, was inserted into the root canals. AH Plus-EA; Before placing the master cone into the canal, the sealer was carried into the canal with a gutta-percha cone. An EA 25/04 tip was coated with AH Plus and activated in the root canals for 10 s. Then, R40 gutta-percha was coated with the sealer and inserted into the canal. BC-NA; The ready-to-use EndoSequence BC was injected into the root canals and no activation was performed. Then, the R40 gutta-percha was inserted into the canal. BC-EA; EndoSequence BC was injected into the root canals. An EA 25/04 tip was inserted 2 mm beyond the WL and activated for 10 s. Then, the R40 gutta-percha was inserted into the canal.

All root canals were filled using the single cone technique and teeth were restored with composite fillings in the same session. All endodontic procedures were performed by a single endodontist. Periapical radiographs were taken after root filling and the radiographic evaluations were conducted by two independent researchers.

All patients were prescribed 400 mg of ibuprofen and instructed to take it as needed. Each participant was provided with a questionnaire based on a 0–10 numeric rating scale (NRS), where 0 indicated no pain and 10 indicated the worst pain. Patients recorded their pain levels on days 1, 2, 3, and 7 after treatment. Pain intensity was categorized using the NRS. None: (NRS 0); mild (NRS 1–3); moderate (NRS 4–7); severe (NRS 8–10). Pain scores were recorded by an independent researcher who was blinded to the experimental groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that the data were not normally distributed. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the postoperative pain scores with and without EA for the same sealer; and to compare the postoperative pain scores between different sealers. The Friedman test was used to evaluate the changes in pain scores over time. Additionally, the Chi-square test used to assess differences in the intake of analgesic medication and gender between groups. The correlation between sonic activation, gender, age factors and postoperative pain were analyzed with Spearman’s correlation test. The correlation between sealer extrusion and sonic activation was also analyzed with Spearman’s correlation test. Statistical significance level was established at 0.05.

Results

In this study, 120 patients were assessed for eligibility. Forty-eight patients were excluded from the study due to the following reasons: inability to achieve occlusal contact in a single visit (due to the presence of a prosthetic crown or extensive crown damage) (n = 28), teeth with multiple canals (n = 5), medical condition (n = 4), initial apical file size greater than an ISO #20 K-file (n = 7), and declined to participate (n = 4). There were no missing patients during the follow-up period, and the data from 72 patients were analyzed (Fig. 1). Of these 34 were female and 38 were male patients. The mean ages in the AH Plus-EA, AH Plus-NA, BC-EA, and BC-NA groups were 38.7 ± 14.5, 35.7 ± 10.2, 34.4 ± 10.7, and 32.9 ± 12.9, respectively. Discussion

The pain intensity after RCT influenced by both treatment-related factors, such as the chosen instrument, preparation technique, irrigation solution, and activation method, and patient-related factors, including age, gender, pulpal and periapical diagnosis, presence of pre-operative pain, and tooth type [15, 16]. A positive correlation between preoperative pain and severe postoperative pain has been previously shown [17]; which is why only clinically asymptomatic patients were included in this study. It has been shown that periapical lesions and occlusal contact are predictive factors for postoperative pain [18]. Therefore, teeth with periapical index scores of 2, 3, and 4 were included in this study, and teeth that could receive a coronal restoration allowing occlusal contact in the same session were selected. Considering the difficulty of chemomechanical shaping and root filling in teeth with complex anatomy and their impact on postoperative pain single-rooted and single canal mandibular premolars were selected. All patients underwent a single-visit RCT to minimize the potential pain-related factors associated with multiple visits [19]. Consistent with the literature [10], this study recorded none or mild postoperative pain scores, with pain being substantially relieved within the first 48 h. However, no significant difference was found in pain scores between the groups, and the null hypothesis was accepted.

Hermetic, three-dimensional obturation is one of the main goals of endodontic treatment. Importantly, that the filling materials remain within the confines of the canal space and do not extend into periapical tissues [20]. Extrusion of the filling material into the periapical tissues may provoke a local inflammatory reaction potentially causing-pain [21]. In this study, while a significant correlation was found between sonic activation and sealer extrusion, no correlation was established between sealer extrusion and postoperative pain. Fonseca et al. reported that CSSs exhibited a higher incidence of extrusion compared to resin-based sealers; however, sealer extrusion was not associated with postoperative pain in maxillary anterior teeth with necrotic pulp [10].

Zamparini et al. [22] reported that CSS used with the warm carrier-based gutta-percha technique exhibited a lower rate of extrusion compared to resin-based sealers. Additionally, in cases where CSS extrusion was observed, the extruded sealer resorbed in half of the cases, whereas the extruded resin sealer remained stable. However, after a 24-month follow-up, both sealers demonstrated similar success rates. Spinelli et al. [23] observed sealer extrusion in 47% of teeth filled with AH Plus bioceramic sealer and the warm carrier-based gutta-percha technique. However, they reported that the extruded sealer could undergo partial or total resorption after a 12-month follow-up. They also stated that sealer extrusion and the size of the extrusion did not influence the treatment outcome. A meta-analysis showed that the risk of sealer extrusion contributing to non-healing outcomes was 32% higher compared to cases without extrusion. However, successful healing outcomes were achieved in many cases where a “sealer puff” was observed [24]. A literature review revealed that most cases of altered sensation following extrusion of filling materials fully recovered over time or at least showed partial improvement. It was noted that cases involving mandibular molars, paraformaldehyde-containing sealers, or those that did not receive immediate treatment were less likely to achieve full recovery [25].

Chemical by-products released from root sealers during polymerization can cause local inflammation in periapical tissue, potentially triggerring postoperative pain [26]. The cytotoxicity of AH Plus has been attributed primarily to its amine and epoxy-resin components. Epoxy resin is mutagenic and causes DNA chain breaks, leading to cell death [27]. Additionally, a slight release of formaldehyde (3.9 ppm) was detected during AH Plus polymerization [28]. In contrast, Endosequence BC known for its good biocompatibility and lack of cytotoxicity [5]. Studies have reported that EndoSequence BC is more biocompatible and has greater cell viability compared to AH Plus [27]. Therefore, it might be expected that EndoSequence BC would cause significantly less postoperative pain compared to the AH Plus, but no significant difference was found between the participants in this study. The fact that AH Plus exhibits an acceptable level of biological properties (ISO-6876/2012) for a root canal sealer [29] might explain this result. Similarly, Aslan et al. [11] reported no significant difference between Endosequence BC and AH Plus in teeth with asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis. Additionally, another clinical study involving teeth with asymptomatic apical periodontitis reported no significant difference between AH Plus and TotalFill BC [12]. Drumond et al. [29] compared the intensity of postoperative pain in teeth with asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis using different root canal sealers (AH Plus, EndoSequence BC Sealer, and Bio-C Sealer). They reported no significant differences between the sealers, and the extrusion rate was similar for all (~ 12%). It was noted that small amounts of sealer extrusion are considered acceptable as long as vitally important structures are not affected.

In this study, there was no statistically significant difference in postoperative pain scores and analgesic intake between the groups with and without EA sonic activation. In the literature, there is no study evaluating the effect of sealer activation on postoperative pain that can be directly compared with our results. Within the limitations of this study, sonic activation does not increase the prevalence of postoperative pain and could be considered a safe technique.

Sonic activation has been shown to positively affect the sealing ability by enhancing the bond between the sealer and dentin [30]. Carneiro et al. [31] demonstrated through confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) evaluation that ultrasonic activation of CSSs and resin-based sealers improved the marginal adaptation of the sealer compared to non-activated groups. De Bem et al. [32] showed that ultrasonic activation was ineffective with resin-based sealers, whereas CSSs increased tubule penetration. However, some studies have reported that sonic or ultrasonic activation does not significantly enhance sealer penetration into dentinal tubules. This inconsistency between studies may be related to the evaluation of different parameters when assessing dentinal tubule penetration with CLSM or the use of different root canal sealers.

Wiesse et al. [6] reported that ultrasonic activation for 20 s improved the adaptation and bond strength of the root canal sealer to root dentin and increased tubule penetration, but these results were not observed for sonic activation. The authors attributed this result to the high amplitude of the sonic activation device. High amplitude may cause rapid displacements in the sealer, leading to air entrainment in the filling material and the formation of voids in the root filling. To avoid this negative effect, the activation time in our study was limited to 10 s. However, it should be kept in mind that activation can change rheological properties such as flowability and viscosity, setting time and chemical behavior [33]. Lopes et al. showed that sonic activation induces minor changes in temperature compared to ultrasonics and therefore has a more subtle effect on the physicochemical properties of sealers [34].

Removing the smear layer is a key factor in enhancing the penetration of root canal sealer into dentinal tubules [35]. In this study, the smear layer was removed using NaOCl and EDTA. We used 2.5% NaOCl without activation, aiming to maximize its effectiveness by increasing the volume to 20 ml to ensure continuous delivery of fresh solution into the canal. The European Society of Endodontology (ESE) S3-level clinical practice guidelines do not recommend the use of adjunctive therapy alongside syringe-needle-based delivery of irrigants, though the strength of this recommendation is weak [36]. A recent review suggests that activation can be implemented [37]. While improper use of EDTA may lead to dentinal erosion [38], we chose to perform activation to enhance the solution’s efficacy. To avoid adverse effects, the activation time was limited to a total of 60 s.

In this study, no significant correlation was found between gender and postoperative pain (p > 0.05). Although Bamini et al. [39] showed that gender was not a significant factor in the incidence of pain, there are also studies showing that pain perception is gender dependent, with higher pain levels in women compared to men [40]. Similar to Ng et al. [15], the present study found no significant correlation between age and postoperative pain (p > 0.05).

After the treatment, patients were given the NRS scale, which can be used to indicate the pain levels they feel, is easy to interpret, and is widely used in similar studies [41]. Pain has a complex structure that is modulated by physiologic, psychological, environmental and genetic factors, making pain perception personalized [42]. A limitation of postoperative pain studies is the difficulty in clearly identifying pain, which is a subjective phenomenon. Another limitation of the study is that radiographic evaluations were performed on periapical radiographs that allow only two-dimensional analysis. Therefore, sealer extrusion may have been underestimated. Although only teeth with periapical index scores between 2 and 4 were included, standardization of periapical lesion size was not achieved in this study. Further comprehensive studies with standardized clinical procedures are needed to better understand the impact of sealer activation on postoperative pain and to establish evidence-based management strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, AH Plus and EndoSequence BC root canal sealers showed similar results in terms of prevalance and intensity of postoperative pain when potential pain-related factors were minimized in teeth with asymptomatic apical periodontitis. Sonic activation of sealers does not influence postoperative pain. Although sonic activation of root canal sealers appeared to increase apical extrusion, no significant association was found between apical extrusion and postoperative pain.
