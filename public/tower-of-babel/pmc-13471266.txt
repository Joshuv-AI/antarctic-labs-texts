Polarimetric entropy mapping of retinal pigment epithelium in full-thickness macular hole by polarization-sensitive OCT
Authors: Ryo Terao, Kazuki Yashiro, Takuya Kuriyama, Kosei Babaguchi, Gen Mihara, Kosuke Nakajima, Shuichiro Aoki, Takahiro Arai, Kohdai Kitamoto, Keiko Azuma, Takashi Ueta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study is aimed to evaluate retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) entropy in eyes with full-thickness macular hole (FTMH) using polarization-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PS-OCT).MethodsPatients diagnosed with treatment-naïve FTMH who underwent pars plana vitrectomy with internal limiting membrane peeling were included. Ophthalmologic examinations, including visual acuity (BCVA), swept-source OCT (SS-OCT), and PS-OCT, were performed. Polarimetric entropy (PE) of RPE was assessed using PS-OCT and compared with affected and fellow eyes and before and after surgery.ResultsFourteen eyes of 14 patients were included (64.5 ± 5.84 years). All eyes achieved complete FTMH closure following the surgery. PE value within the central foveal area (< 200 μm from the foveal center) was significantly lower than that in fellow eyes (p = 0.013), whereas PE in the area surrounding FTMH was significantly higher (p = 0.0402). The interocular difference in foveal PE (affected eye minus fellow eye) was significantly and negatively correlated with baseline BCVA (p = 0.0445). Postoperatively, PE in the surrounding area significantly decreased, whereas foveal PE did not change. Eyes without EZ recovery exhibited significantly greater baseline foveal PE reduction than those with EZ recovery (p = 0.0053).ConclusionsThese findings suggest two spatially distinct RPE responses in FTMH: decreased entropy in the foveal area and increased entropy in the surrounding region. Foveal RPE-PE quantified by PS-OCT is associated with EZ recovery and may reflect postoperative structural outcomes.Keywords: Macular hole, Polarization sensitive optical coherence tomography, Vitrectomy, Retinal pigment epithelium

Background

Full-thickness macular hole (FTMH) is a retinal disorder that impairs visual function and leads to decreased visual acuity [1]. It is characterized by a full-thickness defect of neurosensory retina at the fovea, resulting primarily from the tangential traction exerted by the vitreous cortex on the retinal inner surface. The current standard treatment consists of pars plana vitrectomy with removal of the posterior vitreous cortex to relieve vitreomacular traction, combined with peeling of the internal limiting membrane (ILM). For larger FTMHs, the ILM flap technique—placing the peeled ILM over the macular hole—has been shown to improve anatomical closure rates and visual outcomes [2, 3].

Although surgical techniques have been well established, functional recovery remains suboptimal, as postoperative visual improvement is often limited. Moreover, accurately predicting postoperative visual outcomes in FTMH remains challenging because reliable predictive factors and biomarkers have not been fully established. Preoperative best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and the symptom duration are known to correlate with BCVA after successful hole closure [4]. As an anatomical biomarker, macular hole diameter measured by optical coherence tomography (OCT) is widely recognized as a predictor of postoperative visual outcomes [5]. In addition, the integrity of ellipsoid zone (EZ) zone—a hyperreflective band located beneath the external limiting membrane, surrounding FTMH has been shown to contribute to visual recovery after the surgery [6].

Given that restoration of EZ integrity is associated with visual improvement [7], and that the outer retina plays a critical role in visual function, recovery of photoreceptors is essential for postoperative BCVA improvement. Furthermore, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) is indispensable for maintaining photoreceptor outer segments (PROS), and previous studies have suggested that the RPE is affected during macular hole formation [8, 9]. Therefore, mechanistic studies focusing on photoreceptor-RPE interactions are essential to elucidate additional aspects of FTMH pathogenesis.

Polarization-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PS-OCT) is a functional extension of OCT that measures polarization entropy [10]. PS-OCT quantifies polarimetric entropy (PE), a metric that reflects the degree of randomness in the polarization state of backscattered light. By providing tissue-specific contrast based on PE, PS-OCT enables visualization of distribution across retinal layers using cross-sectional, en face, and three-dimensional imaging. Under physiological conditions, PS-OCT detects melanin-rich tissues, such as the RPE and choroid, as high-entropy structures [11, 12]. Our previous studies have demonstrated the utility of PS-OCT in detecting entropic alterations associated with various retinal disorders, revealing that PE can increase or decrease by various conditions including cellular stress, inflammation, and structural alterations [13–16].

Based on these observations, we hypothesized that alterations in RPE entropy, along with changes in other retinal cells, may be involved in visual impairment associated with FTMH. In this study, we aimed to quantitatively assess RPE entropy in the eye with FTMH using PS-OCT and to evaluate the potential of RPE entropy as a predictive factor for postoperative visual outcomes following surgical treatment.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study is approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Tokyo (protocol no. 11822) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients diagnosed with treatment-naïve FTMH at the University of Tokyo Hospital between May 2025 and October 2025 were included. Clinical evaluations included BCVA (converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution [logMAR] from Snellen acuity), color fundus photography, and swept-source (SS)-OCT were used for the diagnosis. Macular hole diameter was defined as the minimum linear diameter measured on a horizontal foveal SS-OCT B-scan image. Macular hole size was classified according to the International Vitreomacular Traction Study (IVTS) classification [5].

Eyes with media opacity — including cataract Emery-Little grade > 3, which could potentially affect baseline visual acuity or PS-OCT image quality were excluded. Given the study design using the fellow eye as an intra-individual control, patients with bilateral vitreomacular traction syndrome or FTMH were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included a history of other retinochoroidal diseases or prior intraocular surgery (except for cataract surgery) in either eye. Eyes requiring reoperation due to incomplete closure of macular hole were also excluded from the analysis.

Surgical procedure

All patients underwent pars plana vitrectomy, which was combined with cataract surgery when necessary. Surgical procedures were performed by experienced vitreoretinal surgeons (R.T., G.M., K.N., T.A., and T.U.) under local anesthesia. Small-incision cataract surgery was performed immediately before vitrectomy in eyes with Emery-Little grade 2 cataract. The choice of intraocular lens, vitrectomy instruments (25-gauge or 27-gauge), and intraocular air tamponade (air or sulfur hexafluoride; SF6) varied according to the surgeons’ preferences.

All surgeries were performed using transconjunctival microincision vitrectomy with Constellation®; Vision System (Alcon Laboratories, Inc., TX, USA). Standard core vitrectomy was followed by complete induction of posterior vitreous detachment. In all cases, the internal limiting membrane (ILM) was stained with indocyanine green and peeled using forceps across the entire macular area. ILM flap technique was applied depending on the macular hole stage and surgeon preferences. The vitreous cavity was then filled with either air or 20% SF6 gas. Postoperatively, patients were instructed to maintain a facedown position until the confirmation of macular hole closure. PS-OCT imaging and ophthalmic examinations were performed preoperatively and 3 months after surgery.

PS-OCT and imaging analysis

PS-OCT (ROCTIA®; TOMEY, Nagoya, Japan) was used in this study. The mechanical and technical principle of PS-OCT system has been described in our previous reports [10, 14]., 17]ROCTIA®; simultaneously acquired swept source (SS)-OCT images covering the identical scan area. Scanning was performed over a 6 mm × 6 mm macula area using a raster scan pattern consisting of 512 A-lines and 512 B-scans.

Automated segmentation of the PROS and RPE layer was performed using the built-in software on SS-OCT images. Manual correction was applied when the automated segmentation line clearly deviated from the actual anatomical layer. PE values, ranging from 0 (completely uniform polarization) to 1 (completely random polarization), were calculated for the segmented RPE layer with a depth of 3-pixels. Kernel size for entropy calculation was 11 × 7 pixels. Each pixel corresponded to 11.7 × 4.4 μm² (lateral × axial). The mean PE value of the RPE line was calculated and visualized as two-dimensional en face images. The area surrounding the macular hole was defined as a circular region extending from 100 μm to 500 μm from the hole margin. For distance-dependent analysis of PE, concentric annular regions were defined with respect to the foveal center. The macular hole diameter was designated as zero, and mean PE values were calculated within annular areas according to their distance from the macular hole edge. To account for inherent spatial heterogeneity of PE relative to the foveal center, identical distance-based annular regions were applied to fellow eyes, using the macular hole diameter of the corresponding affected eye.

In the evaluation of postoperative changes, distance-based PE measurements were consistently performed using the preoperative macular hole diameter as the reference to ensure spatial comparability across time points. The mean PE values of the area of interest were used for statistical analysis. The schematic figure of the analysis is shown in Fig. 1. Postoperative EZ recovery was defined as the presence of a continuous EZ within a 400-µm foveal region on SS-OCT B-scan images. EZ recovery was determined by two independent graders (R.T. and K.Y.).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using JMP Pro software (version 18) (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, NC, USA). The Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to compare parameters between FTMH and fellow eyes within identical individuals. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for the comparison between two unpaired groups. Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Steel-Dwass post hoc method was used for comparisons among independent multiple groups. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used for correlation analysis. Distance-dependent differences in PE between two groups were evaluated using a generalized linear mixed model. PE was modeled as the dependent variable, with eye status (affected vs. fellow; preoperative vs. postoperative), distance from the macular hole margin (50-µm increments), and their interaction as fixed effects. Subject identity was included as a random effect to account for inter-eye correlation and repeated measurements within individuals. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Participants

PS-OCT images were obtained from 14 eyes of 14 patients with FTMH. The mean age was 64.5 ± 5.84 years (range, 55–71 years). Five patients (36%) were female and nine (64%) were male. Four cases (29%) were classified as Small (≤ 250 μm) FTMH and seven (50%) as medium, and three (21%) as large macular hole (> 400 μm). The baseline BCVA was 0.41 ± 0.30 logMAR. Ten eyes (72%) underwent combined cataract surgery. Nine eyes (64%) were treated using ILM flap technique, and SF6 gas tamponade was used in 13 eyes (93%).

A summary of baseline demographic and surgical characteristics is presented in Table 1. All eyes (100%) achieved successful macular hole closure after the surgery. Postoperative BCVA improved significantly to 0.16 ± 0.22 (p = 0.0051).

Entropic profiles of RPE in eyes with FTMH and their association with disease severity

PE values of the RPE layer were assessed using PS-OCT in all participants. Fellow eyes were analyzed as internal healthy controls and compared with affected eye. Representative images are shown in Fig. 2. There was no significant difference in the mean PE value across the entire 6 × 6 mm macular area between FTMH eyes and fellow eyes (Fig. 3A; Table 2). In contrast, the RPE entropy map of FTMH eyes demonstrated a localized decrease in PE within the macular hole area, where the neurosensory retina was absent. PE value within the central foveal area (< 200 μm from the foveal center) was significantly lower in FTMH eyes than in fellow eyes (Δ = -0.04436; 95% CI, -0.06847 – -0.02024; p = 0.0013; Fig. 3B). Medium and large macular holes exhibited significantly lower PE in central foveal area compared to small macular holes (Fig. 3C). Conversely, the area surrounding the macular hole exhibited significantly increased PE values compared to fellow eyes (Δ = 0.03386; 95% CI, 0.002683–0.06503; p = 0.0402; Fig. 3D). Specifically, PE values were significantly elevated in concentric annular regions located 150 μm to 600 μm from the macular hole margin, in addition to the significant reduction within the macular hole area (Fig. 3E). These regions corresponded to areas showing PROS disorganization and intraretinal fluid accumulation on SS-OCT (Fig. 2). However, no significant correlation was observed between baseline retinal thickness differences and PE differences between affected and fellow eyes (Fig. 3F).

We next examined the relationship between PE values and visual function in eyes with FTMH. PE-derived independent variables were calculated as the interocular difference (PE in the affected eye minus PE in the fellow eye), because substantial inter-individual variability in baseline PE values was observed even among fellow eyes, as shown in Fig. 3A–C. This normalization approach was employed to reduce inter-subject variability and to more specifically capture disease-related PE alterations. Although multivariable analysis did not reach statistical significance due to the exploratory nature of this study and the small sample size, correlation analysis revealed that the interocular difference in foveal PE (< 200 μm from the foveal center) was significantly correlated with baseline BCVA (p = 0.0445; Fig. 3G), whereas neither PE value of the surrounding area nor the macular hole diameter showed a significant association (Fig. 3H).

Postoperative changes of RPE entropy and prognostic factors of visual outcomes

We next evaluated postoperative changes in RPE entropy in FTMH eyes during the recovery phase. No significant differences were observed in PE value within the entire macula area at baseline and 3 months after the surgery (Fig. 4A). The postoperative central macular hole region did not show significant difference (Fig. 4B). Although the potential influence of overlying retinal structures and intraretinal fluid on PE measurements cannot be excluded, our findings suggest that PE may reflect intrinsic RPE properties. In contrast, the PE value in the area surrounding the macular hole significantly decreased at 3 months postoperatively. In particular, the region located 400–700 μm from the macular hole margin exhibited a significant reduction in PE at 3 months (Fig. 4C). Moreover, postoperative changes in retinal thickness were significantly correlated with changes in PE (p = 0.0413; Fig. 4D). We further investigated prognostic factors associated with postoperative visual and morphological recovery. No baseline parameters, including foveal PE values (affected eye minus fellow eye), were significantly correlated with BCVA at 3 months postoperatively. Notably, compared with eyes without EZ recovery, eyes that exhibited recovery of EZ at 3 months postoperatively showed significantly smaller decrease in preoperative foveal PE values relative to fellow eyes (Δ = 0.06150; 95% CI, 0.01344–0.1096; p = 0.0053; Fig. 4E).

Discussion

FTMH is a vitreoretinal disorder caused by vitreomacular adhesion and traction, particularly at the fovea, resulting in significant visual impairment [17]. Based on its pathophysiology, FTMH has traditionally been considered as a disease primarily affecting inner retina. In this study, we investigated whether RPE is involved in the disease phase of FTMH using PS-OCT-derived polarimetric entropy as a structural optical biomarker. Compared with healthy fellow eyes, RPE entropy decreases within the macular hole itself and increases in the surrounding retina, suggesting two spatially distinct RPE responses: degenerative changes in the foveal area and stress-related activation in the surrounding region. Importantly, the reduction of PE in the foveal area was significantly correlated with preoperative visual impairment in FTMH. Postoperatively, in the present cohort consisting of eyes with successful macular hole closure, the elevated PE in the surrounding area significantly decreased, and baseline foveal PE was associated with short-term EZ recovery, suggesting that baseline RPE entropy may be associated with photoreceptor structural recovery. However, as only eyes with successful closure were included, the predictive value of PE for broader surgical outcomes, including closure failure, could not be evaluated, and the generalizability of these findings is therefore limited. Although this study is exploratory in nature with a small sample size and these interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive biological conclusions, our findings suggest that RPE may be involved in visual impairment in FTMH and that RPE entropy may provide complementary information regarding postoperative structural recovery.

PE represents the degree of randomness or disorganization in the polarization state of light. High PE values are thought to reflect increased complexity of backscattered polarization signals, which may be influenced by melanin-containing structures, lipid accumulation, and inflammatory changes [14, 18, 19]. Physiologically, RPE and choroid are visualized as high-entropy structures. In contrast, under pathological conditions, dysfunctional or structurally altered RPE cells exhibit reduced PE values [15]. Therefore, reduced PE observed within the macular hole area likely reflects RPE alteration or dysfunction induced by FTMH development. Notably, RPE entropy within the foveal area (< 200 μm) did not show significant changes after surgical closure of the macular hole. This finding suggests that the presence or absence of the overlying neurosensory retina may have limited influence on the measured PE values. Therefore, the reduced PE observed within the macular hole area is more likely to reflect intrinsic alterations of the RPE rather than a measurement artifact, although this interpretation should be considered with caution. Moreover, the absence of correlation between PE changes and retinal thickening at baseline suggests that PE alterations are not solely determined by retinal thickness or fluid-related changes. However, the observed significant association between postoperative changes in retinal thickness and PE indicates that intraretinal fluid or structural alterations may partially influence PE measurements over time. These findings suggest that fluid-related changes may contribute to dynamic variations in PE, but do not fully explain baseline PE alterations. In addition, given that PE values exhibit spatial variation relative to the foveal center, potential mismatch between fluid distribution and the predefined analysis regions may have influenced the observed results.

Several mechanisms may explain RPE alteration in FTMH. First, the absence of neurosensory retina overlying RPE may disrupt RPE–photoreceptor coupling, leading to attenuation of RPE function [20]. Second, direct exposure of RPE to the vitreous cavity may induce cellular stress, inflammation, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [21, 22]. Third, as the macular hole enlarges and the neurosensory retina is displaced toward surrounding area, horizontal mechanical shear stress exerted by the retina on RPE may trigger the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and growth factors [21, 23]. Although the present study could not directly elucidate the underlying mechanisms and multivariable analysis was limited by the exploratory design, the significant correlation between baseline BCVA and reduced foveal PE (< 200 μm from the foveal center) suggests that decreased RPE entropy reflects local RPE alteration and which may contribute to visual impairment.

Another key finding of this study was the increased RPE entropy in the area surrounding the macular hole. Regions with elevated PE corresponded to areas exhibiting EZ disruption and intraretinal fluid, indicating a close interaction between RPE and outer retinal pathology. Increased PE is known to reflect inflammatory states and subsequent lipid accumulation, consistent with cellular stress with the RPE [14, 24]. Therefore, PE may serve as a surrogate marker of RPE stress or integrity. Intraretinal cyst formation and impaired visual cycling due to disrupted photoreceptor-RPE alignment may induce RPE stress. Alternatively, mechanical shear stress exerted by the displaced neurosensory retina may primarily injure the RPE, secondarily affecting the outer retina. Indeed, EZ disruption reflects disorganization of photoreceptor inner and outer segments and impaired disc shedding [25]. Furthermore, intraretinal cysts in FTMH are thought to originate from the loss of contact between photoreceptors and the RPE, reflecting reduced RPE pump function and impaired fluid transport from the subretinal space to choriocapillaris [26]. Although we could not determine causality mechanistically, these findings suggest that RPE stress in the surrounding area is closely associated with outer retinal impairment.

In addition to baseline alterations, we demonstrated that RPE entropy changes were partially reversible following surgical closure of FTMH. Specifically, the elevated PE in the surrounding area significantly decreased after the surgery, indicating that the RPE stress is alleviated by anatomical restoration. Given that intraretinal cysts resolved postoperatively, increased baseline PE in the surrounding area may be associated with intraretinal cyst formation. In contrast, reduced PE within the macular hole did not recover, suggesting that RPE alteration in foveal region may be persistent despite anatomical closure.

We further explored the prognostic value of PS-OCT-derived parameters. Eyes that achieved EZ recovery at 3 months exhibited significantly higher baseline foveal PE, suggesting that RPE plays a role in photoreceptor restoration. Recovery of EZ integrity, along with photoreceptor layer thickness, is a key determinant of postoperative visual acuity [9, 27]. EZ restoration is preceded by recovery of external limiting membrane and outer nuclear layer [28]. These findings support the notion that RPE integrity influences morphological photoreceptor recovery, consistent with our previous report demonstrating an positive association between RPE entropy and retinal function [16]. Although the present study was exploratory and limited by the small sample size, baseline foveal PE may provide complementary information regarding postoperative photoreceptor recovery beyond conventional structural assessments.

This study has several limitations. First, this study was retrospective in nature and included a relatively small sample size, which limits the robustness of statistical analyses. Future studies with larger cohorts and appropriately controlled analyses are required to validate our findings. Second, retinal sensitivity assessed by microperimetry was not evaluated; this parameter could provide complementary functional information, particularly in perifoveal regions with increased PE. Third, this study lacked dense longitudinal sampling, which would better characterize temporal changes in RPE entropy after surgery. Fourth, surgical factors such as surgeon’s preference and ILM flap technique may have introduced potential confounding effects that were not fully controlled in this study. Fifth, although patients with vitreoretinal disorders in the fellow eye were excluded, the use of the fellow eye as an internal control may still involve subclinical alterations. In addition, the study population included patients with relatively long axial length and myopic refractive status, which may represent a mixture of idiopathic and myopic macular holes. Given that RPE function may already be altered in highly myopic eyes, myopia could have acted as a potential confounding factor influencing PE measurements. Furthermore, the use of the fellow eye as an internal control may be affected by inter-eye asymmetry commonly observed in myopic eyes, which could have influenced interocular comparisons. Nevertheless, given the substantial inter-individual variability in RPE PE values, the fellow eye represents the most appropriate intra-individual control. Finally, PS-OCT cannot directly identify histological alterations of RPE, and further experimental and histopathological studies are required to elucidate the cellular basis of RPE dysfunction.

Conclusions

In summary, we quantitatively evaluated RPE entropy in eyes with FTMH and assessed its short-term postoperative morphological changes using PS-OCT. This non-invasive imaging modality revealed previously unrecognized RPE alterations associated with FTMH and visual dysfunction. The ability of PS-OCT to visualize PE highlights its potential value not only in FTMH but also in a broad range of retinal disorders for diagnosis, prognostication, and postoperative monitoring.
