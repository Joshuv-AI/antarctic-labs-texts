A solvable generative model with a linear, one-step denoiser
Indranil Halder

Abstract
We develop an analytically tractable single-step diffusion model based on a linear denoiser and present explicit formula for the Kullback-Leibler divergence between generated and sampling distribution, taken to be isotropic Gaussian, showing the effect of finite diffusion time and noise scale. Our study further reveals that the monotonic fall phase of Kullback-Leibler divergence begins when the training dataset size reaches the dimension of the data points. Along the way, we provide a mathematically precise definition of memorization to non-memorization transition when only finite number of data points are available. It is shown that the simplified model also features this transition during the monotonic fall phase of the aforementioned Kullback-Leibler divergence. For large-scale practical diffusion models, we explain why higher number of diffusion steps enhance production quality based on the theoretical arguments presented before. In addition, we show that higher diffusion steps does not necessarily help in reducing memorization. These two facts combined suggests existence of an optimal number of diffusion steps for finite number of training samples.

1.1
Our contributions

Our main contributions to this paper are as follows:

1.

We define a linear denoiser based generative model. Within the framework of the model, we present explicit formula for the Kullback-Leibler divergence between generated and sampling distribution, taken to be isotropic Gaussian, showing the effect of finite diffusion time and noise scale. In particular, our formula shows we can recover the sampling distribution from the generative model only if the noise scale is small enough compared to certain function of diffusion time.

2.

We establish that aforementioned Kullback-Leibler divergence starts to decrease monotonically with addition of new training data when the size of the training set reaches the dimension of the data points as opposed to an exponential scale indicated by the curse of dimensionality.

3.

Given a finite number of samples from a probability distribution, the true distribution can be approximated by a L
2
-distance optimal Gaussian kernel. We observe that in the context of higher dimensional statistics, the variance of the kernel coincides with the mixing time for the samples under Ornstein-Uhlenbeck forward diffusion process.

4.

Using this observation, we formulate a mathematically precise metric for calculating pairwise distance between generated, sampling and tain distribution when only finite amount of data is available and give a definition of the memorization to non-memorization transition.

5.

We show that the linear denoiser based model features a transition from memorization to non-memorization during monotonic decrease of Kullback-Leibler divergence between generated and sampling distribution.

6.

Our metric allows us to systematically study the generalization properties of a realistic diffusion model. Using this on Gaussian mixture model training set, we quantify the fact that larger diffusion steps leads to better production quality. On the other hand, we argue that the theorem that we proved before gives us theoretical explanation of this fact. This also tells us how to scale noise introduced in the diffusion process with diffusion time for good production quality.

7.

Motivated by the results of linear denoiser based model, we hypothesize that memorization-to-non-memorization transition is caused by the decay of generalization error for generic diffusion models. For realistic diffusion models we show that this result holds. Furthermore we show that higher diffusion steps does not necessarily reduce memorization of diffusion models. Combining this with the point mentioned above, it tells us that given finite amount of training data, there exists an optimal number of diffusion steps.

Foundations of diffusion-driven generative models

In the domain of image generation, we don’t know the exact functional form of the sampling distribution
\displaystyle\rho(x)
. However we have access to a finite number of samples from it and the goal is to generate more data points from the unknown probability density
\displaystyle\rho(x)
. Traditional likelihood maximization techniques would assume a trial density function
\displaystyle\rho_{\theta}
and try to adjust
\displaystyle\theta
so that likelihood for obtaining known samples is maximized. In this process determination of the normalization of
\displaystyle\rho_{\theta}
is computationally expensive as it requires multi-dimensional integration (typically it is required for each step of the optimization procedure for
\displaystyle\theta
). Diffusion based generative models are an alternative
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. In this section, we review basic notions of these stochastic differential equation based models. In particular, we examine an exactly solvable stochastic differential equation (SDE).
The Itô SDE under consideration is known as the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Langevin dynamics and is expressed by:

dX^{F}_{t}=-X^{F}_{t}dt+\sqrt{2}dW_{t},\quad X^{F}_{t}\sim\rho(t).

(2.1)

The score function associated with the stochastic process will be denoted as (see Appendix
A
for details of notation and conventions)

s(t,x)=\nabla_{x}\log\rho(t,x)=\frac{1}{\rho(t,x)}\nabla_{x}\rho(t,x)

(2.2)

The probability density
\displaystyle\rho
satisfies the transport equation (see (
A.3
) in Appendix
A
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left((x+s(t,x))\rho(t,x)\right)

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\rho(t,x)+x.\nabla\rho(t,x)+d\rho(t,x).

The dimension of the data is defined to be given by
\displaystyle d=\text{dim}(x)
. The time evolution of the probability distribution is exactly solvable and given by

\rho(t,X^{F}_{t})=\int dX^{F}_{0}\ \rho(0,X^{F}_{0})\ \mathcal{N}(X^{F}_{t}|X^{F}_{0}e^{-t},1-e^{-2t}).

(2.4)

Suppose we know the probability density
\displaystyle\rho(0,x)
exactly. One way to sample from it would be to use the knowledge of the exact score function
\displaystyle s(t,x)
in the reverse diffusion process (see (
A.10
) in Appendix
A
), i.e,

\displaystyle dX^{B}_{t}=(-X_{t}^{B}-2s(t,X_{t}^{B}))dt-\sqrt{2}\,dW_{1-t}

(2.5)

starting from a late time distribution
\displaystyle\rho(T,x)
(it is assumed that we know how to sample from
\displaystyle\rho(T,x)
).

This method requires estimating the score function
\displaystyle s
from known samples - it can be obtained by minimizing Fisher divergence
[
23
]
(see (
A.8
) in Appendix
A
for more details) using techniques such as the kernel method
[
24
]
or denoising score matching
[
25
]
.

3
A generative model based on a linear denoiser

In this section, we define and study a linear denoiser based generative model which is analogous to a one step diffusion model. Before explaining the model, we note certain basic facts about the diffusion process based on a finite number of samples. Given
\displaystyle n
samples
\displaystyle\rho(x)
can be approximated by the Dirac delta distribution
\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(0,x)\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta(x-x_{k}),x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The time evolution of the probability distribution under Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion process can be obtained from (
2.4
)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t,x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ \mathcal{N}(x|x_{k}e^{-t},1-e^{-2t})

(3.1)

This motivates us to sample
\displaystyle Y_{k},k=1,2,..,n
from the underlying distribution
\displaystyle\rho(x)
and add noise
\displaystyle Z_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{I}}_{d})
to it to obtain noisy samples
\displaystyle X_{k}=e^{-T}Y_{k}+\sqrt{\Delta_{T}}Z_{k},\Delta_{T}=\lambda\delta_{T}=\lambda(1-e^{-2T})
. Here
\displaystyle T
is the diffusion time cut-off
and
\displaystyle\lambda
is a free hyperparameter that controls the amount of noise added
2
2

2

This corresponds to scaling the noise term in (
2.1
) by a factor of
\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda}
.
. For simplicity, we add the entire noise in one step in contrast to the multi-step process of realistic diffusion models.

The denoiser based
model, trained on the data above, as input takes a noisy sample
\displaystyle X
and generates a clean sample
\displaystyle Y
. In this paper, we consider a linear model
\displaystyle Y=\hat{\theta}_{0}+\hat{\theta}_{1}X
as prototype denoiser for analytical tractability. The parameters
\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{0},\hat{\theta}_{1}
are solution to the linear regression problem of predicting
\displaystyle\{Y_{k}\}
given
\displaystyle\{X_{k}\}
and given by
3
3

3

A natural generalization of this is to feature kernel regression instead of linear regression.

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{1}^{T}=(x^{T}x)^{-1}x^{T}y,\quad\hat{\theta}_{0}=\hat{Y}-\theta_{1}\hat{X}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\hat{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k},\quad\hat{Y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}

Here
\displaystyle x,y
are
\displaystyle n\times d
dimensional matrices
whose
\displaystyle k
-th row is
\displaystyle(X_{k}-\hat{X})^{T},(Y_{k}-\hat{Y})^{T}
respectively.

To generate samples from the trained diffusion model we first draw
\displaystyle X
from
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mu_{X},\sigma_{X}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d})
, motivated by the fact that late time distribution can be reliably approximated by a Gaussian distribution
with

\mu_{X}=e^{-T}\hat{Y},\quad\sigma_{X}^{2}=e^{-2T}\frac{1}{nd}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|Y_{k}-\hat{Y}|^{2}+\Delta_{T}

(3.3)

and then use the diffusion model to predict corresponding
\displaystyle Y=\hat{\theta}_{0}+\hat{\theta}_{1}X
. The generated probability distribution for a given set
\displaystyle\{(X_{k},Y_{k}),k=1,2,..,n\}
is

\displaystyle\rho_{G}(Y|\{(X_{k},Y_{k})\})=\mathcal{N}(Y|\hat{\theta}_{0}+\hat{\theta}_{1}\mu_{X},\sigma_{X}^{2}\hat{\theta}_{1}^{T}\hat{\theta}_{1})

(3.4)

The generated probability distribution as defined above is a random variable conditioned on
\displaystyle\{(X_{k},Y_{k}),k=1,2,..,n\}
which itself is a random variable. We consider its expectation value
\displaystyle\rho_{G}(Y)
by further sampling
\displaystyle Y_{k}\sim\rho
and
\displaystyle X_{k}=e^{-T}Y_{k}+\sqrt{\Delta}_{T}Z_{k}
as mentioned above.

3.1
Effect of finite diffusion time

In this subsection, we study the effect of finite diffusion time
\displaystyle T
and noise scale
\displaystyle\lambda
on generalization error for the linear diffusion model defined above. We restrict our discussion to sampling from isotropic Gaussian distribution
\displaystyle\rho=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d})
. The distance between the underlying distribution
\displaystyle\rho
and the generated
\displaystyle\rho_{G}
distribution from the diffusion model as given in (

\displaystyle\text{KL}(\rho||\rho_{G})=\text{KL}_{\text{mean}}+\text{KL}_{\text{var}}\geq\text{KL}_{\text{var}}
. Where the contributions
\displaystyle\text{KL}_{\text{mean}},\text{KL}_{\text{var}}
are related to the difference between generated and the underlying distribution in mean and variance

\displaystyle\text{KL}_{\text{mean}}(\rho_{G}|\rho)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(\mu-\hat{\mu}_{G})^{T}(\mu-\hat{\mu}_{G})

(3.5)

\displaystyle\text{KL}_{\text{var}}(\rho_{G}|\rho)=\frac{1}{4}{\rm Tr\,}\bigg(\bigg(\frac{\hat{\Sigma}_{G}}{\sigma^{2}}-I\bigg)^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{G}=\hat{\theta}_{0}+\hat{\theta}_{1}\mu_{X},\quad\hat{\Sigma}_{G}=\sigma_{X}^{2}\hat{\theta}_{1}^{T}\hat{\theta}_{1}

The inequality follows because
\displaystyle\text{KL}_{\text{mean}}

\displaystyle\text{KL}_{\text{mean}}\ll\text{KL}_{\text{var}}

3.2
Memorization to non-memorization transition

In this subsection, we define and analyze a precise metric for memorization to non-memorization transition of the linear denoiser based generative model when only finite number of samples are available from the sampling distribution
\displaystyle\rho
. This collection of samples will be called the original dataset and we will train the diffusion model on a subset of it. To this end we need to precisely define pairwise distance between training, generated and original dataset. This is achieved by observing that mixing time-scale is optimal, we explain this point next and later study memorization to non-memorization transition.

3.2.1
Mixing time-scale is optimal

In this subsection, we present our observation: given
\displaystyle n
samples from a distribution
\displaystyle\rho(x)
, the mixing time
\displaystyle t_{M}
—defined as the minimum diffusion time after which two points exert significant mutual influence—corresponds to the optimal variance of a non-parametric Gaussian density estimator. We now proceed to explain this point in detail.

To formalize the definition of
\displaystyle t_{M}
we consider
\displaystyle x=x_{1}e^{-t}+\sqrt{\delta_{t}}Z
for some
\displaystyle Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{I}}_{d})
and decompose
\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t,x)
into two parts
\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t,x)=Z_{1}+Z_{1^{c}}
, with

\displaystyle Z_{1}=\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{N}(x|x_{1}e^{-t},1-e^{-2t})

(3.9)

\displaystyle Z_{1^{c}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\ \mathcal{N}(x|x_{k}e^{-t},1-e^{-2t})

It can be shown that in the limit
\displaystyle n\to\infty
with
\displaystyle\log n/d
fixed,
\displaystyle Z_{1^{c}}
is an increasing function of
\displaystyle t
such that for
\displaystyle t<t_{M}
,
\displaystyle Z_{1^{c}}<Z_{1}
and at mixing time
\displaystyle t=t_{M}
,
\displaystyle Z_{1}=Z_{1^{c}}
. When
\displaystyle\rho(0,x)=\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d})
, one can explicitly calculate
\displaystyle t_{M}
using ideas from random energy model
[
26
]
to be given by
[
27
]

t_{M}=\frac{1}{2}\log\bigg(1+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{\frac{2}{d}}-1}\bigg)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\sigma}{n^{\frac{1}{d}}}\bigg)^{2}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{4}}{n^{\frac{4}{d}}}\bigg)

(3.10)

In the second equality, we have further expanded the expression above in the limit
\displaystyle\log n/d\gg\log(\sigma)
and kept only the leading order term.

On a separate line of work,
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
has minimized the expectation value of
\displaystyle\int dx(\rho(x)-\rho_{E}(x))^{2}
,
\displaystyle\rho_{E}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}(x|x_{k},\epsilon^{2})
over
\displaystyle\epsilon
for large
\displaystyle n
at fixed
\displaystyle d
to obtain the following formula for the optimal
\displaystyle\epsilon

\epsilon=\bigg(\frac{4}{d+2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{d+4}}\frac{\sigma}{n^{\frac{1}{d+4}}}=\frac{\sigma}{n^{\frac{1}{d}}}+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{d^{2}}\bigg)

(3.11)

To go to the second equality we have taken
\displaystyle d
large. The error term above suggests leading order term is a good approximation only when
\displaystyle\log n/d\ll d
. On the other since the calculation is done first keeping
\displaystyle d
fixed and then taking
\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\log n/d\gg 1
.

We observe that the first term in (
3.11
) matches precisely with the expression of
\displaystyle\sqrt{2t_{M}}
as calculated above in (
3.10
). This shows that the region when both the analytical formula for mixing time of diffusion process
\displaystyle t_{M}
and that of the optimal standard deviation of the Gaussian kernel
\displaystyle\epsilon
is valid, they coincide with each other and
\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t_{M},x)
coincides with
\displaystyle\rho_{E}(x)
to the leading order in small
\displaystyle\epsilon
.

The discussion above is valid for large

. However it serves as motivation for the following definition valid for generic dataset.

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\rho_{O}(x)\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{N}\bigg(x|x_{k},\frac{\hat{\Sigma}_{O}}{N^{\frac{2}{d}}}\bigg)

3.2.2
Non-memorization is caused by the monotonic decay of generalization error

Armed with the definitions (

Appendix B
Principle of deterministic equivalence

In this appendix we review the theory of large random matrices leading to the principle of deterministic equivalence. A
\displaystyle d\times d
Hermitian random matrix
\displaystyle A
with measure
\displaystyle d\mu_{A}
is called an invariant random matrix if the measure satisfies

d\mu_{A}(A)=d\mu_{A}(U^{\dagger}AU)

(B.1)

for any unitary matrix
\displaystyle U
. In the limit of
\displaystyle d\to\infty
, the theory is conveniently described in terms of the single eigenvalue density
\displaystyle\rho_{A}
(normalized to unity) that can be obtained from the resolvent or the Stieltjes transform

G_{A}(z)=\langle\frac{1}{d}{\rm Tr\,}\bigg(\frac{1}{z-A}\bigg)\rangle=\int\frac{\rho_{A}(\lambda)d\lambda}{z-\lambda}\implies\rho_{A}(\lambda)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}\Im(G_{A}(\lambda+i\epsilon))

(B.2)

The moment generating function is given by

M_{A}(z)=\frac{1}{z}G_{A}\bigg(\frac{1}{z}\bigg)-1=\langle\frac{1}{d}{\rm Tr\,}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}A^{i}z^{i}\rangle

(B.3)

R, S transformation of the eigenvalue density are defined by

\displaystyle R_{A}(z)=G_{A}^{inv}(z)-\frac{1}{z},\quad G_{A}^{inv}(G_{A}(z))=z

(B.4)

\displaystyle S_{A}(z)=\frac{z+1}{z}M_{A}^{inv}(z),\quad M_{A}^{inv}(M_{A}(z))=z

R, S transformations are useful when we study the matrix model to the leading order in large
\displaystyle d
limit as we explain next. Two invariant random matrices
\displaystyle A,B
are called free to the leading order in large
\displaystyle d
limit if they are independent. Free sum and free product of
\displaystyle A,B
are defined as follows

\displaystyle A\boxplus B=U^{\dagger}AU+V^{\dagger}BV

(B.5)

\displaystyle A\star B=\sqrt{A}B\sqrt{A}

Here
\displaystyle U,V
are are sampled independently from uniform measure on the unitary group, i.e., Haar random unitary.
It can be shown that for two invariant, independent random matrices
\displaystyle A,B
the moment generating function of
\displaystyle A\boxplus B
and
\displaystyle A+B
coincides, similarly moment generating function of
\displaystyle A\star B
and
\displaystyle AB
coincides (to the leading order in large
\displaystyle d
, i.e., when they are free). Furthermore following identity holds to the leading order in large
\displaystyle d
limit for two free matrices
\displaystyle A,B

R_{A\boxplus B}(z)=R_{A}(z)+R_{B}(z),\quad S_{A\star B}(z)=S_{A}(z)S_{B}(z)

(B.6)

Now we turn to application of these ideas. Consider
\displaystyle d\times d
matrix
\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}=x^{T}x/n
where each row of
\displaystyle x
(there are
\displaystyle n
rows) is drawn from
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
. Then it can be written as a free product of
\displaystyle\Sigma
and a white Wishart matrix
\displaystyle W
(corresponds to
\displaystyle x^{T}x/n
where each row of
\displaystyle x
is drawn from
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{I}}_{d})
):
\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}=\Sigma\star W
. From the definition of S transformation it follows that

\displaystyle M_{\hat{\Sigma}}(z)=\frac{1}{\frac{S_{\hat{\Sigma}}(M_{\hat{\Sigma}}(z))}{z}-1}=\frac{1}{\frac{S_{\Sigma}(M_{\hat{\Sigma}}(z))S_{W}(M_{\hat{\Sigma}}(z))}{z}-1}=M_{\Sigma}\bigg(\frac{z}{S_{W}(M_{\hat{\Sigma}}(z))}\bigg)

(B.7)

To obtain the final equality we used self-consistency of the equation itself. To recast this equation in a compact way we define
\displaystyle d\text{f}^{1}_{A}(z)=-M_{A}(-1/z)=\langle{\rm Tr\,}\hat{\Sigma}(\hat{\Sigma}+\hat{R})^{-1}\rangle/d
. In terms of this new quantity we have

\displaystyle d\text{f}^{1}_{\hat{\Sigma}}(\hat{R})=d\text{f}^{1}_{\Sigma}(R),\quad\hat{R}=R(1-\alpha\ d\text{f}^{1}_{\Sigma}(R))

(B.8)

To obtain this equation we used knowledge of S transformation of white Wishart matrices
\displaystyle S_{W}(z)=1/(1+\alpha z),\alpha=d/n
. This equation is valid only leading order in large
\displaystyle d,n
limit with fixed
\displaystyle\alpha
. It is known as the principle of deterministic equivalence. See
[
38
]
and references therein for a recent discussion of it.
