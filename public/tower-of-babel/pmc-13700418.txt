Artificial intelligence generated virtual patients in health professions education: a scoping review
Authors: Eric Kin Meng Fang, Yuru Boon, Jin Yang Ho, Natasha Tey, Matthew Jian Wen Low
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenerative artificial intelligence (AI) can enable large scale virtual patient production. However, the health professions education (HPE) community lacks a synthesized overview of how AI generated virtual patients can be best utilized to achieve intended learning outcomes. We sought to synthesize the range of use cases, limitations and approaches to fulfilling these use cases using AI generated virtual patients, including beneficial design features and strategies addressing limitations.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review following the Arksey and O’Malley framework, and reported according to PRISMA-ScR guidelines. MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and CINAHL databases were searched using terms related to AI and virtual patients. After removal of duplicates, titles and abstracts were screened, followed by full-text review of eligible articles. Original research using AI to generate an interactive virtual patient for HPE was included. Data were charted on study characteristics, educational use cases, outcomes evaluated, limitations, beneficial features and development approaches, and qualitatively synthesized.ResultsNinety-seven studies were included. Outcomes were mostly evaluated at Kirkpatrick level one. Use cases included learning and assessing communication skills, diagnostic reasoning, and management reasoning. Limitations included inauthentic conversational flow, excessive agreeableness and clinical inaccuracy. Strategies addressing these challenges included iterative cycles of prompt engineering and review by clinical experts and learners, confining AI platforms to readily available valid information sources, specifying behavioral rules such as adherence to the patient role and limiting reply comprehensiveness, few shot prompting, and building conversational memory. Beneficial features included diverse cases capturing authentic variation in clinical conditions and patient personalities, and feedback that was immediate, individualized, specific, actionable, and transparent.ConclusionEducators seeking to locally harness the potential of AI generated virtual patients can utilize the range of use cases and design features identified in this review. They should also be cognizant of limitations in clinical accuracy and inauthentic conversational flow, and consider development strategies targeting such limitations. Future research should explore other potentially beneficial design features from the wider educational literature and evaluate outcomes beyond participant reaction and include behavioral transfer to the workplace, cost, and organizational impact of AI generated virtual patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-10028-w.Keywords: Virtual patients, Artificial intelligence, Simulation training, Professional education, Scoping review

Background

Rationale

Virtual patients are “a specific type of computer-based program that simulates real-life clinical scenarios” [1]. The mismatch between large demand to generate new healthcare professionals and limited training capacity is a strong impetus to develop scalable solutions such as virtual patients, especially in resource-limited contexts [2]. Virtual patients offer effective learning experiences [3] with purported scalability across large numbers of students [4] and can consistently deliver a casemix optimized for learning [5, 6] in contrast to opportunistic clinical environments.

Virtual patients were traditionally limited by high upfront production cost [1] reducing global accessibility. Compromises include linear cases with limited learner autonomy in actions [7], but this runs counter to fundamental principles of deep, active and constructive learning [6, 8] and deliberate practice aligned to authentic clinical practice [5, 9]. Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence (AI) have now made low cost and large scale virtual patient production more accessible globally [10], including open ended branching designs [7] that facilitate active learning.

However, barriers remain in fully and thoughtfully harnessing the potential of AI in education. These include technical barriers such as technological complexity and technical expertise, organizational barriers such as access to relevant hardware and software, and pedagogical barriers such as educators’ lack of knowledge, trust, or guidance in incorporating AI as a tool in curricula [11], with trust in AI influenced by prior workplace exposure to AI [12] and rooted in valid concerns about its limitations [13]. A synthesized understanding of how AI generated virtual patients have been utilized and developed thus far would increase the awareness of the health professions education (HPE) community about the range of possibilities and best practices in achieving them. Such an overview would also highlight under-explored areas for future research.

Most recent reviews of AI generated virtual patients focus on single or narrow use cases rather than exploring the breadth of use cases [14–17]. One scoping review [18] that aimed to determine the educational tasks for which AI generated virtual patients have been used found no studies in the category of communication skills in explaining or planning, despite the existence of studies directly targeting these skills [19–21]. One systematic review of AI driven simulations for non-technical skills in medical education defined non-technical skills broadly, encompassing clinical reasoning, decision making and communication [22]. Despite this broad definition, only 11 studies were included, suggesting a narrower than expected conceptualization of non-technical skills or a narrow search strategy.

Beyond knowing the breadth of use cases, educators would benefit from a synthesis of how AI generated virtual patients can be best designed to fulfil these use cases. In particular, the simulation literature has repeatedly called for a focus on design features that benefit learning and getting technology to serve educational principles rather than vice versa [3, 5, 23, 24]. However, existing reviews of AI generated virtual patients have not addressed such beneficial features thoroughly.

Additionally, a synthesis of the limitations of AI generated virtual patients and strategies to address them would help educators anticipate and mitigate limitations proactively. One systematic review attempted to synthesize such limitations and strategies, but for history taking only, with a highly technical focus in mitigation strategies [14]. Such technical complexity is a known barrier that limits accessibility to the wider pool of users that lack in-depth technical expertise and impedes diffusion of this innovation to harness its benefits [25].

Lastly, prior literature has also called for more systematic classification of study outcomes for AI generated virtual patients in HPE using Kirkpatrick levels for evaluation [26] to determine the current state of the literature and directions for future research [18]. However, existing reviews lack such systematic classification of outcomes [15, 16, 22, 27–30]. Evaluation of a meaningfully broad range of outcomes, beyond learner satisfaction and including cost and organizational impact, is important evidence for claims regarding the scalability of AI generated virtual patients.

Study reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [32] with the associated checklist provided in Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Original peer-reviewed research published in English that used AI to generate part or all of an interactive virtual patient simulation of real-life clinical scenarios for HPE were included. The HPE context was defined broadly and included undergraduate and postgraduate education across a wide range of health professions including nursing, pharmacy, medicine, dentistry and allied health. Other personal characteristics of participants were not part of the inclusion criteria. Operational definitions of virtual patients and AI for HPE simulation were aligned with those in the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s Healthcare Simulation Dictionary [33]. To be interactive, we required that the learner actively provide inputs to the virtual patient platform aligned to use case objectives (e.g. provide next questions to ask the patient if the use case was learning history taking), with subsequent output from the virtual patient platform based on this input. Eligible study designs included both quantitative and qualitative designs.

The exclusion criteria were the absence of any one of the following: the use of AI, an interactive virtual patient simulation, and an HPE context. Additionally, conference abstracts, commentaries, review articles and papers not published in English were excluded. Papers were not excluded based on year of publication.

Information sources

A comprehensive literature search was performed once on 23 September 2025 and updated on 18 March 2026 using the MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate), and CINAHL (via EBSCOhost) databases. Gray literature and clinical trial registries were not searched.

Search

The complete search strategy is presented in Table 2. The core search string combined terms related to AI and virtual patients: (“artificial intelligence” OR “machine learning” OR “neural networks, computer” OR “deep learning” OR “natural language processing”) AND (“virtual patient*” OR “patient simulat*”). Search terms were adapted using database-specific syntax and subject headings such as Medical Subject Heading (MESH) terms in the MEDLINE database. No date limits were applied. Reference lists of relevant narrative reviews, scoping reviews, and systematic reviews were hand-searched to identify additional eligible articles.

Complete search strategy

Selection of sources of evidence

After removing duplicates, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts using the predefined criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible articles were then independently assessed for inclusion. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer available for adjudication. Cohen’s kappa was calculated for inter-rater agreement regarding study inclusion or exclusion. The PRISMA flow diagram summarizing the selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection. A total of 1412 records were identified from database searches (MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL), with 687 duplicates removed, leaving 725 records for title and abstract screening. Of these, 155 reports were assessed for full-text eligibility, with 85 studies meeting inclusion criteria. Twelve additional studies were identified through reference list screening, yielding a total of 97 included studies

Data charting process and data items

A structured data-charting template was used to extract study characteristics (geographical origin, year of publication), AI platform architecture, educational use case, reported outcomes, highest Kirkpatrick level of reported outcomes, beneficial design features, described limitations, and development strategies including those targeting limitations. Beneficial design features were informed by those previously identified by Cook et al. [34], while not being solely restricted to this pool and allowing for context specific nuance.

Extraction was performed by one reviewer and checked by a second reviewer for accuracy. Kirkpatrick levels were classified according to established definitions in the Kirkpatrick evaluation model [26] where level 1 refers to participant reaction, level 2 refers to learning (changes in knowledge, skills or attitudes), level 3 refers to transfer of learning to behaviors in the workplace, and level 4 refers to results in improved patient outcomes or wider organizational impact including finances.

Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence

As the aim of this study was not to appraise the methodological quality of the literature, we did not perform this appraisal.

Synthesis of results

Extracted data were qualitatively synthesized using an iterative, inductive approach appropriate for scoping reviews, while quantitative data on geographic origin and Kirkpatrick levels were descriptively synthesized and presented in tables and diagrams. Findings were organized according to our research questions.

Ethics

Ethical approval was not required for this review.

Results

Selection of sources of evidence

The database search yielded 1412 records. After removal of 687 duplicates, 725 articles remained for screening for eligibility. 155 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Twelve additional records were identified through reference list screening. In total, 97 articles were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Among 725 records screened independently by two reviewers, observed agreement was 83.4% and Cohen’s kappa was 0.51, suggesting moderate agreement for inter-rater agreement regarding study inclusion [35].

Characteristics of sources of evidence

Most studies originated from Europe, North America, and East Asia. Smaller numbers were contributed by the Middle East, Oceania, Southeast Asia, Africa, Central America and South America (Fig. 2). Research activity was sparse before 2023, with isolated publications across the years 2000–2022. A notable increase occurred in 2023, and publications rose sharply from 2024 onwards (Fig. 3). Synthesis of results

RQ 1 and 2: use cases and outcomes

We report use cases and outcomes together here, given their intertwined nature. Use cases included learning communication skills (e.g. history taking and patient counselling), diagnostic reasoning and management reasoning. Fewer studies used AI generated virtual patients for learning physical examination, fostering positive learner attitudes towards patients, or assessment. Of 97 studies, 85 (87.6%) focused primarily on teaching and learning, 8 (8.2%) focused primarily on assessment, and 4 (4.1%) combined learning and assessment.

Most of the included studies evaluated outcomes at Kirkpatrick level 1 and some at level 2 (Table 4). No studies reported outcomes corresponding to Kirkpatrick level 3 or 4. Another four studies reported outcomes that did not fall within the Kirkpatrick framework; these predominantly examined technical performance metrics (e.g. speech-to-text transcription accuracy) without assessing educational impact.

Communication skills

The included studies encompassed a range of communication skills [4, 10, 16, 19, 21, 36–98], including history taking, interprofessional communication [77], and patient counselling about diagnoses, management and motivational interviewing [19–21, 51, 55, 57, 60, 81, 98–104], and cultural competency in communication [105, 106].

At Kirkpatrick level 1, learner self-reported outcomes included increased competence [74, 87, 99], self efficacy [60, 78, 88], confidence [20, 61, 67, 71, 72], engagement [90], high usability of the virtual patient platform [19, 56, 62, 74, 86, 90, 102, 104], and high likelihood of recommending AI generated virtual patients to other learners [94, 107]. Virtual patients were reported by learners to be less daunting [38, 66, 74, 84, 98], and provided greater access outside of fixed schedules [84].

At Kirkpatrick level 2, measured outcomes included improved history taking skills [47, 64, 66, 84, 91, 93], and improved patient counselling about diagnosis, with larger gains in students with lower baseline empathy [81]. Conversely, in three studies, learners trained using AI generated virtual patients scored no better than pre-training observed performance or lower compared to a concurrent or previous cohort without such training despite self-reported improvements [51, 78, 89].

Clinical reasoning

Clinical reasoning includes both diagnostic reasoning (e.g. reaching a diagnosis or list of plausible diagnoses) and management reasoning (e.g. choosing subsequent treatments) [108–110]. Some studies targeted diagnostic reasoning alone [41, 46, 49, 50, 52, 68, 80, 84, 88, 89, 111–118], while others included management reasoning [10, 20, 37, 42–45, 53, 59, 70, 72, 96, 100, 107, 119–123].

At Kirkpatrick level 1, learner self-reported outcomes included realism or authenticity of virtual patients [44, 45, 121, 122], perceived effectiveness of simulation [41, 107], confidence [20, 116], anxiety [50], engagement [42, 59], and overall learner satisfaction [81, 97], which was lower with AI generated virtual patients than with human standardized patients in one study [97].

At Kirkpatrick level 2, knowledge scores measured by multiple choice questions increased after learning using AI generated virtual patients [54]. In one study, there was no significant improvement in observed clinical reasoning, problem solving and crisis management skills despite learners self-reporting improvements [119]. Diagnostic reasoning measured by human experts observing learner performance improved in two studies of learners using AI generated virtual patients [46, 84].

Skills assessment

Several studies used AI to generate virtual patients and assessments of learners interacting with virtual patients, with assessment being the primary goal [66, 113, 115, 124–128].

Validity of AI generated assessment would be supported by expected relationships with other variables [129], such as high agreement with human generated assessments and detecting expected changes before and after an educational intervention. Dupont et al. provide evidence in support of the latter [127], while there is conflicting evidence for the former with both high agreement for assessing history taking [66, 94, 124–126] and low agreement for assessing knowledge [54] and communication and professional attitudes [128] in the included studies.

AI generated virtual patients were also used in the objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) context. In two studies, human examiners assessed medical students interacting with AI generated virtual patients [95, 100]. Familiarization with the OSCE environment through virtual reality simulations of OSCEs with AI generated virtual patients prior to actual OSCEs was associated with reduced self reported anxiety and increased perceived preparedness [130]. After encountering AI generated virtual patients in an OSCE station within a summative examination, learners reported that they were largely unsure or not supportive of AI generated virtual patients being used in OSCEs [112].

Other educational use cases

A few studies reported AI generated virtual patients for learning physical examination skills [41, 56, 131]. One such study of screen based AI generated virtual patients for learning breast examination found poorer performance and higher anxiety compared to learning with human standardized patients, despite higher student satisfaction [131]. Lack of realism of computer screen based and virtual reality based virtual patients for physical examination training limited its utility for this educational use case [41].

Attitude was studied as a target outcome in nursing students who interacted with an AI generated geriatric virtual patient [132]. There was no statistically significant difference before and after this intervention on the Geriatric Attitude Scale, despite nurses qualitatively reporting greater understanding [132].

Cost as an outcome

Only several studies in this review evaluated the cost of developing AI generated virtual patients [37, 59, 97, 115, 124] or indirectly considered it through broad descriptions of steps obviated [65, 70]. Dimensions of cost that were directly studied included financial cost per conversation using different AI models [37], financial cost per student to complete a curriculum comprising multiple cases [97], aggregate financial cost per conversation across an arbitrary number of learners considered within a study [59], aggregate financial cost for an arbitrary number of learners considered in a study to complete a curriculum comprising multiple cases [115], and time cost of faculty in delivering a curriculum [115] or scoring learner performance [124]. Development costs were sparsely described [70] or explicitly excluded [97]. Where cost was compared to existing teaching methods, cost savings were found from obviating costs from time and labour of faculty members and human standardized patients [97, 124]. Notably, such cost calculations and savings were reported in studies from the modern large language model (LLM) era rather than rule-based virtual patient designs before the LLM era.

Despite considering cost, none of these studies reached Kirkpatrick level 4 in terms of measuring organizational impact such as improved patient outcomes or institutional cost savings through more judicious ordering of diagnostic investigations in the workplace.

RQ 3: beneficial features

Beneficial design features of AI generated virtual patients included those that created alignment between learning conditions and authentic clinical practice. This went beyond audio and visual input and output modalities for authentic cues in communication [44, 45, 62, 75, 76, 79, 82, 103, 122] and included diverse variation in cases across various dimensions such as clinical condition encountered [20, 60, 61, 66, 75, 84, 98], patients’ geographical and cultural backgrounds [64, 95, 101], patients’ preferences (e.g. preference for testing or patient financial concerns) [37], patients’ trust in clinicians [106], and patient personalities [59, 66, 75], which aligned with realistic variation and challenge in authentic clinical practice. Such challenges were best tailored to each learner or specific learner groups, with progressively increasing complexity [43, 63, 74, 85, 115].

The presence of feedback, from humans or from the virtual patient platform, was a beneficial feature [4, 10, 37, 41, 49, 73, 75, 94, 119, 120, 130]. Key features of beneficial feedback were that it was immediate, personalized and actionable with sufficiently elaborate and specific explanations beyond nonspecific overall scores or binary checklists [52, 53, 59, 64, 66, 81, 84, 85, 116, 120]. Immediate feedback was especially powerful when paired with opportunity for reflection [105] and repeated practice [4, 43, 50, 71, 81, 98, 116]. Feedback actionability was enhanced by addressing both inner loop feedback related to the current learning activity and outer loop feedback related to optimal next steps in learning [113]. In some cases, feedback and scoring were aligned with prescribed rubrics [52, 54, 116], which could be borrowed from existing OSCE rubrics [46, 66, 106, 131]. Feedback transparency was aided by linking feedback to quotations from transcripts of learner interactions with virtual patients [94].

Despite feedback being a beneficial feature, only 52.6% (n = 51) of studies reported incorporating feedback as a feature in the AI generated virtual patients that were developed. To explore whether lack of feedback reflected earlier design maturity, we compared feedback reporting by publication era. Feedback was reported in 56.3% of pre-2023 studies and 51.9% of studies published from 2023 onward, with no evidence of a temporal association (Fisher exact p = 0.79). Thus, absence of feedback was not confined to earlier virtual patient systems and persisted in recent literature.

RQ 4: limitations of AI generated virtual patients

Limitations included a lack of natural conversational flow [48], a lack of emotional depth [96, 125], being excessively agreeable [10, 37, 98], giving overly polished and complete answers [37, 59, 74, 85, 98, 101, 103, 120, 125, 126], repetitive answers with little variation [103, 112], being deficient in clinical accuracy [58, 125] and being prone to inappropriately departing from its intended patient role, such as prematurely giving away the diagnosis like a tutor [10, 21, 58, 126]. These limitations led to learners adopting unnatural conversational styles [36, 46, 59, 74]. AI generated feedback could be excessively positive with failure to mention important weaknesses [37].

When analyzing the subgroup of studies published before 2023, prior to the LLM era, limitations centered around unrealistic dialog, especially due to virtual patients having a limited range of responses and related difficulty in adapting to the context of the conversation [4, 48, 77, 99, 112], with rule-based dialog systems using scripted responses laboriously crafted by humans. In contrast, role departure was a less prominent concern.

Learners reported a lack of nonverbal cues as a limitation of virtual patients with only text based inputs and outputs [52, 80]. However, limitations in clinical accuracy and unnatural conversational flow also afflicted virtual patients that went beyond text inputs and outputs and included other modalities such as learner voice, patient voice, and patient body language [19, 20, 60, 62, 73, 107, 130]. These additional modalities also introduced new challenges. Excessive latency between user input and audiovisual outputs caused unnatural conversational flow [56, 60, 62, 119]. Body language and tone of voice expressed by patient avatars had a limited range, in part due to training datasets for synthetic voice being largely from young healthy populations unrepresentative of patient populations [43, 56, 62, 64, 67, 78, 106]. Other issues included cybersickness and steep learning curves for virtual reality headsets and controllers [41], inaccurate speech to text conversion especially for non-native English speakers with local accents [62, 64, 69, 77, 119], and inaccurate interpretation of learner emotions based on body language [82]. Physical examination with virtual reality was still perceived as unrealistic [41]. When speech-to-text technology was employed, learners were concerned about privacy [57] and in one study preferred text rather than voice inputs for greater opportunity for reflection between conversational turns [106].

Because of these limitations, learners suggested that AI generated virtual patients would be best used as an adjunct in the curriculum without replacing real life history taking experiences [74].

RQ 5: development approaches including those addressing limitations

Wang et al. developed a useful conceptual model for educators that synthesized common issues in developing AI generated virtual patients, with three major domains: clinical accuracy, degree of anthropomorphism, and adaptability [125]. These domains contribute towards constructive alignment as a core educational principle, where tasks performed by the learner during learning activities (e.g. navigating authentic variation in patient personalities during history taking for internally consistent cases) align with tasks expected in the workplace after graduation. Given that these domains also align well with the limitations found in the prior section, we organize strategies based on these domains.

Virtual patient output improved in clinical accuracy when case content that the platform was trained on or drew from was reviewed and verified by clinical experts [19, 38, 40, 70, 90, 92, 112, 131]. Such validated content could be efficiently taken from existing sources, such as textbooks and academic papers [53, 113], existing cases for problem based learning or training with human simulated patients [36, 66, 77, 130], existing clinical cases from real world clinical records [53, 75, 104] or case banks for summative assessments [48]. Clinical accuracy was aided by case content providing detailed information across multiple clinical domains relevant to the intended use case of the virtual patient platform [20, 58, 72, 90, 126], and desired level of difficulty [21]. Prompts instructing the virtual patient platform to strictly adhere to a limited set of valid information in generating responses aided clinical accuracy [20]. Review of platform outputs by clinical experts and learners also contributed to clinical accuracy when paired with iterative changes to the platform, such as changes in its underlying prompts (i.e. prompt engineering), especially when conducted over multiple cycles [38, 40, 48, 50, 52, 58, 79, 80, 88, 89, 96, 104, 118, 119]. “Red-teaming” is a model for such review processes, where sub-teams within the development team engage in adversarial review of prompts and outputs from other sub-teams [120].

The degree of anthropomorphism was improved by the abovementioned processes to review platform outputs and case content that served as platform inputs. Additionally, anthropomorphism was fostered by behavioral rules that specified the style of communication and boundaries for playing its role [20, 21, 52, 74, 103, 126], such as the virtual patient’s literacy level [55, 86], confining answers to what a patient would know rather than what a clinician would know [96, 103, 119], limiting the comprehensiveness of its answers [58, 125, 126], and disclosing information gradually [86, 92]. Another useful strategy for anthropomorphism was few-shot prompting, where desired examples of communication styles were provided as part of instructions to the virtual patient platform [52, 88, 94, 120]. For non-English speaking contexts, options included translating from source languages to English and using LLMs largely trained on an English corpus, or using LLMs trained on a non-English corpus [66, 83, 94].

Adaptability was fostered by providing examples of fallback responses for incomprehensible or outrightly irrelevant learner input [20, 56]. Adherence to prescribed case content was in tension with adaptability, anthropomorphism and clinical accuracy in terms of diversity of clinical conditions and patient personalities aligned to authentic clinical practice [61]. One strategy to address this tension was to customize temperature settings for different roles of the virtual patient, with lower temperature settings for the tutor role generating feedback, and experimenting with temperature settings that were not too high or low for the patient role [59, 74, 89, 94, 103, 115], since higher temperature settings allowed more variation by the virtual patient platform. Chain of thought prompting enabled adaptability across different roles of the virtual patient platform by breaking down the complex process of diagnostic reasoning into smaller steps, allowing analysis of both product and process in learners’ diagnostic reasoning [120].

Overall output realism also improved by building up the virtual patient’s memory of unfolding conversations and using this memory to generate subsequent responses [62, 120], which could be achieved by platform coding that sent both current and initial conversational text with each new entry within the same conversation.

Discussion

Summary of evidence

Our main findings relate to our research questions in the range of use cases, outcomes, limitations, beneficial design features, and useful development strategies. The range of use cases spanned across learning and assessment of diagnostic reasoning, management reasoning, attitudes towards patients, and communication skills including history taking, patient counselling, and interprofessional communication, with significant limitations for learning physical examination. Outcomes were mostly measured at Kirkpatrick level 1. Limitations included unnatural conversational flow, excessive agreeableness, and role departure. Attempts to integrate nonverbal cues for realism added new challenges in inaccurate input recognition and unnatural response latency.

Beneficial features reported by included studies were diversity in clinical conditions and patient personality aligned with authentic clinical practice, feedback that was immediate, personalized, specific, actionable, and transparent, and repeated practice. These features were highlighted in both LLM-based virtual patients and rule-based systems, and are consistent with prior literature in simulation [133], virtual patients [30], and more generally in education as components of well established frameworks such as deliberate practice [9, 134, 135]. These features were also inconsistently incorporated, as exemplified by only 52.6% of studies in this review incorporating feedback as a feature. We echo prior calls to pay due attention to these features amidst enthusiasm about adopting technology [3]. Future research and educational practice could tap on the wellspring of instructional design features already known from other domains of HPE [34] to unlock the educational value of AI generated virtual patients. For example, mastery learning is a beneficial feature known from prior simulation literature [136] that remains under-explored in AI generated virtual patients, representing low hanging fruit for research and educational implementation.

Strategies in developing AI generated virtual patients have evolved in tandem with recognition of their limitations, but are scattered across studies, and in some cases, behind barriers of prohibitive complexity and in-depth technical expertise. This limits performance expectancy and in turn, effective adoption that benefits stakeholders [137]. To aid the wider academic and educator community in this regard, we suggest a concise framework synthesizing the above findings in developing AI generated virtual patients (Fig. 4). Because this scoping review is an exploratory study, we emphasize that this framework is a conceptual synthesis of exploratory findings, rather than representing definitive directives. Given this framework’s emphasis on optimizing prompts and how most studies in this review were published from 2023 onwards in the LLM era, these strategies mostly apply to LLM-based virtual patients, with more limited generalizability to rule-based dialog systems.

Limitations of existing literature and future directions

When outcomes were evaluated, Kirkpatrick level 1 was the most commonly studied, with optimistic findings at Kirkpatrick level 1 sometimes discordant with higher levels [51, 78, 119]. This is consistent with other more narrowly scoped reviews of AI generated virtual patients where most outcomes evaluated were learner self reports of usability and perceived learning [138, 139]. Though feasible to measure, Kirkpatrick level 1 does not evaluate learning or transfer of learning to actual clinical practice and downstream benefits to patients and health systems. The dearth of studies evaluating these more impactful outcomes is a recurring pattern in simulation [140] and AI in HPE [16] and perhaps reflects the relatively immature state of literature on AI generated virtual patients in HPE. Behavioral transfer to clinical practice is challenging to measure in HPE due to patient data confidentiality, feasibility challenges in achieving adequate sample size for statistical power, and intervening factors between learning interventions and behavior in clinical environments which disrupt causal chains and muddy causal inferences [141]. Despite these challenges, prior examples in simulation demonstrate that such research is possible as a field matures [142, 143].

Only several studies in this review evaluated the cost of developing AI generated virtual patients [37, 59, 97, 115, 124] and conceptualized cost in different ways with different methods of calculation which were sometimes sparsely described or excluded important components such as development cost. Costs go beyond financial cost and include the time cost of content and platform development and maintenance accrued by faculty members and other staff [144, 145] which are key concerns for educators who need to determine whether such upfront and recurrent costs are justified [75, 138]. This overall cost has important implications for global accessibility of AI generated virtual patients [146] and their appeal in resource constrained settings [17]. There remains a lack of rigorous cost evaluation in AI generated virtual patients, despite its importance and prior calls to close the gap in cost evaluation in simulation in HPE [147]. Additionally, data on cost and cost savings in review came from studies of LLM-based virtual patients, with limitations in generalizing findings to rule-based dialog systems.

Despite the prior assertion that AI makes virtual patients more accessible globally [10], most of the literature in this review still originated from traditionally high resource geographical locations such as North America and Europe, followed by East Asia. Potential reasons include barriers to accessing AI infrastructure in low resource regions, lack of AI literacy among faculty members, and disparities in languages included in AI training datasets [148, 149]. The dominance of English followed by Mandarin in AI training datasets mirrors the geographic distribution of studies in this review, suggesting how disparities in AI training corpora could drive differences in AI research output and educational innovation [148]. The transferability of largely English dominant AI platforms outside of English speaking and Western cultural contexts and challenges in deploying them remain under-reported, consistent with overrepresentation of these contexts across other academic domains that may perpetuate disparities [150]. Another contextual consideration is differing levels of uncertainty inherent in each specialty (e.g. emergency medicine compared to other specialties) contributing to differing risks of hallucinations [13].

In light of the above limitations and findings regarding beneficial features from this study, we suggest the following future directions for research and educational practice:

Explore mastery learning and other instructional design features in the context of AI generated virtual patients.

Limitations of this review

In the framework by Arksey and O’Malley [31], consultation with experts in the field is an optional last step. This step was not performed in this review due to the research team’s time and resource constraints, and thus represents a limitation [151].

This study did not include gray literature and was limited to English language citations due to challenges in accessing, translating and understanding scientific terminology in non-English literature, which may have resulted in a limited understanding of the non-English literature in this area [152].

The methodologic quality of included studies was not assessed. Thus, recommendations within the synthesized framework for developing and utilizing AI generated virtual patients are based directly on what was reported within individual studies without weighting by methodologic strength. Future research could refine this framework by appraising study quality and prioritizing findings from studies with greatest methodological rigor. Beyond that, relevant quantitative findings (e.g. frequency of role departure by AI generated virtual patients) could also be synthesized and analyzed over time as technology advances, to aid educators in determining when they have reached acceptable thresholds of performance.

AI in HPE has a rapidly expanding literature base in tandem with rapidly evolving capabilities of AI models. Though we repeated our search in March 2026, we recognize that publication lag may result in this study’s findings not fully representing the most current state of literature as it moves towards agentic AI and other capabilities. Future research may need to adopt additional search terms as the field evolves and develops new concepts and terminology.

The Kirkpatrick framework fits well with teaching and learning interventions [26] but less so for assessment oriented use cases. This fits well with teaching and learning being the most common and prominent use case in included studies in this review, with assessment being the primary use case in only a minority. The Kirkpatrick framework therefore provided a pragmatic structure for classifying educational outcomes across most included studies. However, we acknowledge that the Kirkpatrick framework has limitations in framing assessment validity. Future research focusing on assessment oriented use cases for AI generated virtual patients could use other frameworks more suitable for assessment, such as Messick’s sources of validity evidence [129].

Conclusions

Educators seeking to locally harness the potential of AI generated virtual patients can utilize the range of use cases and design features identified in this review. They should also be cognizant of limitations in clinical accuracy and inauthentic conversational flow, and consider development strategies targeting such limitations. Future research should explore other potentially beneficial design features from the wider educational literature and evaluate outcomes beyond participant reaction, including behavioral transfer to the workplace, cost, and organizational impact of AI generated virtual patients.
