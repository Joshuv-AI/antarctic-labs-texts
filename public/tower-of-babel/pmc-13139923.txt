Serotype distribution and coverage by pneumococcal vaccines in hospitalised adults with pneumococcal pneumonia in Germany

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding the prevalence of pneumococcal serotypes in hospitalised patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is critical for vaccine development and implementation of future public health policies. Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) have been implemented to decrease the burden of pneumococcal disease. This study aims to identify the serotype distribution of pneumococcal CAP in Germany.MethodsThis interim analysis of a multicentre, prospective, active observational study includes patients aged ≥18 years hospitalised with CAP in seven German hospitals. Patients were enrolled between August 2022 and September 2024. To determine CAP aetiology, microbiological tests were performed according to local standard of care. Urinary antigen testing for Streptococcus pneumoniae (BinaxNOW) was performed in all patients, and two serotype-specific urinary antigen detection (SSUAD) assays were used to detect serotypes in pneumococcal vaccines PCV15 and PCV20.ResultsS. pneumoniae was detected in 16.5% (85/514) of participants by all diagnostic tests. SSUAD assays detected S. pneumoniae as pathogen in 13.2% (68/514) of participants. In six patients, two pneumococcal serotypes were detected. The seven most detected serotypes were: 3, 11A, 8, 23B, 9N, 18C and 22F. Among 74 serotype detections, 64 (86.9%) are included in V116 compared with 56 (75.8%) in PCV20. The difference in coverage between PCV20 and V116 was 10.8% (95% CI −3.2–24.8%).ConclusionAmong adults hospitalised with CAP in Germany, S. pneumoniae was detected in 13% of cases by SSUAD assays. While the findings suggest that V116 may offer broader serotype coverage compared with PCV20, these differences were not statistically significant.

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains a significant contributor to morbidity and mortality among adults globally [1, 2]. CAP can be caused by an extensive variety of pathogens [1]. Populations most susceptible to CAP include young children, older adults and individuals with chronic comorbidities or immunodeficiency [3]. Furthermore, CAP is responsible for substantial healthcare utilisation and costs [4, 5]. With a growing population, including a greater proportion of older adults, this burden is anticipated to increase. Adequate CAP prevention is therefore a public health priority.

Due to the widespread implementation of effective pneumococcal vaccines, the incidence of invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) has decreased significantly [6]. Vaccines such as the 23-valent polysaccharide pneumococcal vaccine (PPV23) and the 7-, 10- and 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV7, PCV10 and PCV13) have contributed significantly to a reduced IPD burden [6]. However, over time non-IPD and the incidence of all-cause CAP have increased despite pneumococcal vaccination. Serotype replacement by non-vaccine serotypes, the persistent prevalence of serotype 3 and other trends, such as ageing and the increase of comorbidities in the population, affect the prevalence of all-cause CAP [7–9]. PCVs with additional pneumococcal serotypes included (PCV15 and PCV20) are currently available for the prevention of pneumococcal disease in adults. Nonetheless, Streptococcus pneumoniae remains the most often detected bacterial pathogen in CAP worldwide [2, 10], with varying vaccine effectiveness per geographical area and per country-specific vaccination programme, due to herd effects induced by vaccination of children and varying circulation of respiratory pathogens [5, 11].

In Germany, PCVs have been part of routine vaccination among children aged <2 years since 2006 (and since 2001 in at-risk children), consequently lowering the incidence of pneumococcal disease across all age groups through direct protection as well as indirect protection through herd immunity [12, 13]. Increases in IPD caused by serotypes not included in currently recommended vaccines have been reported [14]. Another recurring concern is the ongoing evidence that all PCVs have very limited effectiveness against the highly pathogenic serotype 3 [15–17].

For adults aged ≥60 years, the German Standing Committee on Immunisation (STIKO) has recommended PCV20 as part of routine vaccination since September 2023, replacing PPV23 [18]. The German Network for Community Acquired Pneumonia (CAPNETZ), a medical competence network for CAP in Germany, performed a longitudinal analysis of serotype distribution in Germany between 2013 and 2019 and aligned with previously described epidemiology of pneumococcal CAP after the introduction of PCV13 [19]. Serotype distribution showed that PCV20 covered 67% of adult IPD serotypes between 2015 and 2018 [14]. A 21-valent PCV (V116) was most recently developed and has received marketing authorisation, to specifically address serotypes responsible for adult pneumococcal disease, including 11 unique serotypes (9N, 15A, 15C, 16F, 17F, 20A, 23A, 23B, 24F, 31 and 35B) compared with PCV20 [20–22].

With the changing landscape of pneumococcal CAP epidemiology, due to influences such as vaccines and the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic, it is critical to continue monitoring the distribution of disease-causing pneumococcal serotypes to inform the development of new vaccines [23]. However, accurately establishing the aetiology of CAP is difficult due to the inherent limitations of standard methods. In 50% of pneumonia cases no microbial aetiology is found [1, 23]. Implementation of diagnostics such as nucleic acid amplification tests and urine antigen detection tests has facilitated detection of pathogens associated with CAP [24]. Serotype-specific urinary antigen detection (SSUAD) assays have been further developed to detect and identify pneumococcal serotypes likely causing non-IPD [25, 26].

Various studies have used urinary antigen assays to describe the pneumococcal serotype distribution after changes in Germany's vaccination programme. Previous studies have primarily focused on defining the serotype distribution of IPD and bacteraemic CAP, as the reporting obligation for IPD has been effective since 2020 in Germany. To assess the most recent developments in the distribution of pneumococcal serotypes among adults with CAP, this study incorporates both local standard-of-care microbiological methods (including blood cultures, pleural fluid cultures and respiratory samples, and immunological and molecular testing) and study-specific SSUAD assays. This study aims to provide an overview of the aetiology of CAP and describe the pneumococcal serotype distribution of pneumococcal CAP in Germany during respiratory seasons 2022–23 and 2023–24 to inform vaccine development and public health policies regarding vaccination.

Methods

Study design

The Pneumococcal Pneumonia Epidemiology, Urine Serotyping and Mental Outcomes (PNEUMO Europe) study is a multicentre, prospective, population-based, active observational study of hospitalised adults with CAP. Currently ongoing, this study is enrolling patients from more than 50 hospitals in Spain, France, Italy, Germany, the UK and the Netherlands. This interim analysis includes patients aged ≥18 years and enrolled with CAP in seven hospitals in Germany between August 2022 and September 2024. A detailed overview of enrolment times per hospital is provided in supplementary appendix S1.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with local German regulatory laws and regulations, the Declaration of Helsinki, and the International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study was approved by the research ethics committee of each participating hospital. Participants, or a surrogate in case of severe illness, provided written informed consent before the collection of data commenced.

Participants

Hospitalised adult patients aged ≥18 years with suspected pneumonia were prospectively screened for eligibility by the local study team when admitted through the emergency room, intensive care unit (ICU) or pulmonary department. Patients were eligible if they met all the following criteria: hospitalisation (including both in-patient and observation status stays); clinical signs and/or symptoms of an acute respiratory illness; and clinical signs of an acute infection and radiological evidence of pneumonia on chest radiography, computed tomography or echography. Exclusion criteria included: enrolment in this study within the previous 30 days; development of pneumonia >48 h after hospital admission (considered to be hospital acquired-pneumonia); inability or unwillingness of the patient to provide a urine sample within 72 h of hospital admission; or if the patient's acute symptoms could be completely explained by a non-pneumonia illness. The eligibility criteria are described further in supplementary appendix S2 and the study protocol [27].

Data collection

Clinical and questionnaire data were collected by reviewing medical charts and by a patient interview, or interview with a proxy when the patient was too unwell to be interviewed. All data were collected using a standardised electronic case report form (eCRF). Pneumonia severity at the time of hospital admission was determined using the Pneumonia Severity Index [28], the CURB-65 (confusion, urea >7 mmol·L−1, respiratory rate ≥30 breaths·min−1, blood pressure <90 mmHg (systolic) or ≤60 mmHg (diastolic), age ≥65 years) score [29] and the Infectious Diseases Society of America/American Thoracic Society criteria [30]. It was recommended to document radiological features following readily specified definitions [31]. In-hospital clinical outcomes, gathered from the participant's medical record, included hospital length of stay, ICU admission, ICU length of stay, use of invasive mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, new renal replacement therapy and in-hospital mortality. All data were collected until hospital discharge or death. The eCRF used for data collection was initially Research Online 2 (University Medical Center Utrecht (UMCU), Utrecht, The Netherlands), with data completion in Research Online 2 on 1 November 2023. The electronic data capture system Castor EDC (Amsterdam, The Netherlands) [32] was implemented starting 1 March 2023 according to UMCU regulations. All data from both Research Online and Castor were transferred using secured data communication protocols.

Risk factors for CAP

Relevant comorbidities were defined based on pre-existent chronic medical conditions classified by STIKO as at risk for CAP [18]. Risk factors included chronic diseases of the cardiovascular or respiratory systems, metabolic and neurological diseases, and immunocompromising conditions (supplementary appendix S3).

Microbiological testing

Microbiological samples were collected following local standard of care at each site. Respiratory cultures (sputum, endotracheal and bronchoalveolar) were collected if possible. Cultures from normally sterile sites, including blood and pleural fluid, were considered evidence of pneumococcal aetiology when positive for S. pneumoniae. In case of a positive culture from any of the collected samples during standard-of-care microbiological testing, a subculture was sent to PPD Global Central Labs (Zaventem, Belgium) for sample management, and tested by JMI Laboratories (North Liberty, IA, USA) for confirmation and additional serotyping by whole-genome sequencing.

Urine samples

Urine samples were collected, as part of study procedures, from all enrolled patients within 72 h of hospital admission. All urine samples were tested onsite for S. pneumoniae using the BinaxNOW antigen test (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA). The BinaxNOW test detects a C-polysaccharide cell wall antigen common to all pneumococcal serotypes [33]. Therefore, this test is unable to distinguish among serotypes. Results of urinary Legionella pneumophila or S. pneumoniae antigen tests were additionally collected at study sites where these were performed as part of standard care. A subaliquot of each urine sample was additionally sent to PPD for sample management and tested by Nexelis (Q2 Solutions Company, Laval, ON, Canada) using MSD's SSUAD assays [25, 26, 34]. The first SSUAD assay detects the pneumococcal serotypes in the PCV15 vaccine: 1, 3, 4, 5, 6A/6C, 6B, 7F, 9V, 14, 18C, 19A, 19F, 22F, 23F, 33F. The second detects pneumococcal serotypes included in V116: 3, 6A, 7F, 8, 9N, 10A, 11A, 12F, 15A, 15C, 16F, 17F, 19A, 20A, 22F, 23A, 23B, 24F, 31, 33F, 35B. These two assays combined include 30 serotypes that are in V116, PCV15 and PCV20, except serotype 15B (which is included in PCV20).

Statistical analysis

Participants with an International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision discharge diagnosis inconsistent with CAP were excluded from the analysis. Only patients with an available SSUAD test were included. Missing data were accepted after maximal efforts to retrieve data. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean and standard deviation, while non-normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are reported as number and percentage. Baseline characteristics were compared between patients with pneumococcal and non-pneumococcal CAP using either the Chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, and the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables.

To evaluate the distribution of aetiologies across different age groups and risk conditions (yes/no), Chi-squared or Fisher's exact tests were applied as appropriate. The frequency of pneumococcal serotypes was reported for the overall cohort and stratified by age group and vaccine categories. Pneumococcal serotype distributions were compared descriptively across age groups to explore potential differences in vaccine coverage profiles. This analysis was exploratory in nature and aimed to provide context for the observed serotype epidemiology, rather than to test specific hypotheses. Comparisons of vaccine coverage were performed between V116 and PCV20. PCV15 was not included in this comparative analysis, as PCV20 contains all serotypes in PCV15 plus five additional serotypes, which would inherently favour PCV20 over PCV15 due to its coverage. To compare serotype coverage between PCV20 and V116, McNemar's test without continuity correction was performed. The difference in coverage proportions was estimated with a 95% confidence interval.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 or later [35].

Results

Participants

For this interim analysis, 548 patients hospitalised with all-cause CAP were recruited between August 2022 and September 2024. After enrolment, 34 patients were excluded from this analysis because there was no SSUAD result available before October 2024, resulting in an analytical population of 514 patients. The median (IQR) age was 67 (53–77) years and 234 (46%) were female. Overall, the baseline characteristics of patients with pneumococcal and non-pneumococcal CAP were similar. Significant differences were seen in interaction with children aged <5 years, outpatient antibiotic use and chronic liver disease. CAP aetiology

S. pneumoniae was detected in 85 (16.5%) participants with all-cause CAP. Other pathogens were detected in 144 (28%) participants, including SARS-CoV-2 (n=36 (7%)), influenza (n=19 (3.7%)), Enterobacteriaceae (n=11 (2.2%)), Mycoplasma pneumoniae (n=9 (1.8%)), respiratory syncytial virus (n=9 (1.8%)), Staphylococcus aureus (n=5 (1%)), non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria (n=4 (0.8%)) and Haemophilus influenzae (n=3 (0.6%)). Other bacteria were detected in 40 (7.8%) patients.

Microbiological detection of S. pneumoniae

Urine samples were collected from all participants and underwent SSUAD and BinaxNOW S. pneumoniae urinary antigen testing. Blood cultures were obtained in 336 (65%) patients and lower respiratory tract cultures were taken in 45 (8.7%) patients. A total of 109 tests from 85 unique patients were positive for S. pneumoniae (figure 1). Most positive tests were detected by SSUAD assays (n=68 (62.4%)), followed by urinary antigen tests (n=23 (21.1%)), blood cultures (n=16 (14.7%)) and respiratory cultures (n=2 (1.8%)). Serotype results from isolates (including blood and respiratory samples) were not yet available.

Pneumococcal serotype detections by SSUAD

SSUAD assays were positive for one pneumococcal serotype in 62 (12.1%) patients hospitalised with CAP and two serotypes were detected in six (1.2%) patients, resulting in a total of 74 serotypes detected. The seven most frequently detected serotypes were: 3, 11A, 8, 23B, 9N, 18C and 22F (figure 2). Among 74 serotype detections, 64 (86.9%) were included in V116 compared with 56 (75.8%) in PCV20 and 39 (52.8%) in PCV15 (table 3). Among all detected serotypes, 18/74 (24.6%) serotypes were contained only in V116, not PCV20. In total, 46/74 (62.1%) serotypes were covered by both vaccines, 10/74 (13.5%) were covered only by PCV20 and 18/74 (24.3%) were covered only by V116. McNemar's test did not indicate a statistically significant difference in serotype coverage between V116 and PCV20 (χ2=2.29, df=1, p=0.13). The estimated difference in coverage proportion was 10.8% (95% CI –3.2–24.8%), suggesting a trend toward broader coverage by V116, although not statistically significant. Among adults aged 50–59 years hospitalised with CAP, 13.2% had at least one pneumococcal serotype detected included in V116 compared with 7.9% and 5.3% of patients with pneumococcal serotypes contained in PCV20 and PCV15, respectively (figure 3). Among adults ≥60 years hospitalised with CAP, the percentage of patients with at least one detected serotype contained in V116 was 13.5% compared with 12.6% for PCV20 and 9.4% for PCV15 (figure 3).

Discussion

S. pneumoniae was the most frequently detected pathogen in patients hospitalised with all-cause CAP in seven hospitals in Germany. Approximately 16% of all patients were identified as having pneumococcal pneumonia.

Approximately a quarter of patients with pneumococcal CAP had a serotype unique to V116. Of all detected serotypes, a higher proportion was included in V116 compared with PCV20. Interestingly, among adults aged 50–60 years the difference in detected serotypes contained in V116 compared with PCV20 and PCV15 was the highest. In line with previous data, including studies relating to only IPD, serotype 3 was the most prevalent serotype, followed by serotypes 11A and 8 [14–17, 19]. Importantly, serotype 3 is associated with carriage, a high invasiveness and lower effectiveness of vaccines against this serotype [11]. This highlights that a broader coverage of serotypes by a vaccine does not necessarily translate to better clinical outcomes as effectiveness of the vaccines may be different and serotypes additionally covered may not be associated with the same disease burden. Serotypes unique for V116 (9N and 23B) are among the 10 most reported serotypes in IPD by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control [36]. Notably, only 15% of participants were sure about having been vaccinated with any pneumococcal vaccine prior to admission. Unfortunately, 49% of participants were not sure whether they received any pneumococcal vaccine prior to admission.

The prevalence of pneumococcal aetiology in this study (16%) was consistent with the earlier observed prevalence of pneumococcal CAP using SSUAD assays in patients included in the CAPNETZ study (15%) [19]. Both results differ from the previously (2002–2008) detected prevalence of ∼10% by CAPNETZ using standard-of-care microbiological testing (including blood and respiratory cultures, and conventional urine antigen detection) [37]. The difference in reported prevalence might be due to increased sensitivity of SSUAD assays compared with standard-of-care microbiological tests. Using SSUAD assays as a diagnostic test could lead to identifying more cases of pneumococcal CAP, with the potential drawback of false-positive results. Therefore, it is less likely that the assays detect nasopharyngeal carriage, since the cut-offs of these tests were established to favour specificity to label non-symptomatic pneumococcal carriers or recent vaccine recipients as negative [26]. Some cases of bacteraemic pneumococcal CAP were not SSUAD positive. A potential explanation is that these cases represent serotypes not covered by these assays. Also, for serotypes included in the SSUAD assay, false-negative results may have arisen due to imperfect sensitivity. This study showed a relatively low in-hospital mortality rate of 3.9% compared with population-based data of in-hospital mortality of 17–19% in patients hospitalised with CAP in Germany [38, 39]. However, the in-hospital mortality rate in this study is identical to the mortality observed in the CAPNETZ study [40].

One strength of this study is the use of SSUAD assays with detection of 30 serotypes in total, including nearly all serotypes in V116, PCV20 and PCV15. This is more comprehensive than serotype-specific tests in prior studies.

This study has a number of limitations. 1) Imperfect clinical sensitivity of SSUAD assays (86.4%) for pneumococcal CAP by serotypes included in the assays could lead to false-negative results [26]. This could result in misclassification of pneumococcal CAP cases as non-pneumococcal, resulting in an underestimation of pneumococcal CAP prevalence. This could also lead to overestimation of the vaccine coverage as serotypes not included in any of the vaccines are not included in the SSUAD assays. 2) Since no gold standard exists for determining CAP aetiology, it remains unclear whether the higher yield of SSUAD assays includes false positives. This could inflate prevalence estimates. 3) Serotype 15B (contained in PCV20) was not tested for in this study, potentially resulting in underestimation of PCV20 serotype prevalence [17]. However, the prevalence of this serotype remained relatively low (∼1%). 4) Serotype distribution varies geographically and temporally. By including patients in seven hospitals with a wide geographical distribution throughout Germany, the serotype distribution in our study is most likely a good representation. However, serotype distribution fluctuates temporally as well. Due to the relatively short time of inclusions of this preliminary analysis, the temporal variation of serotype prevalence could not be addressed. 5) Patients in the non-pneumococcal CAP group had a significantly higher percentage (28%) of outpatient antibiotic use before admission compared with the pneumococcal CAP group (16%). Antibiotic use prior to admission could have led to unreliability of the results of diagnostic tests. 6) Due to local guidelines, most hospitals could not use surrogate consent. Therefore, inclusion of patients with mental impairment or a higher severity of disease is limited. Participating hospitals have a study team available only on weekdays. However, the effect of this on inclusion numbers is minimal due the inclusion window. This could have led to selection bias, considering mainly patients with a lower severity of disease have been included in this study and a relatively low mortality was observed in this study. 7) Not all methods are standardised between sites, including decisions during admission that might impact in-hospital outcomes. Therefore, due to differences in local standard of care, blood and respiratory cultures for microbiological tests were not taken from all patients.

Conclusions

In this study of adults hospitalised for all-cause CAP in Germany during the 2022–23 and 2023–24 respiratory seasons, ∼16% of patients had pneumococcal CAP. While the findings suggest that V116 may offer broader serotype coverage compared with PCV20, these differences were not statistically significant. Nevertheless, the inclusion of additional serotypes in future pneumococcal vaccines could potentially contribute to improved disease prevention and reduced healthcare burden, warranting further investigation.
