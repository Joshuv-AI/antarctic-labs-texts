Turnpike Properties in Linear Quadratic Gaussian N N -Player Differential Games
Asaf Cohen, Jiamin Jian

Abstract
. We consider the long-time behavior of equilibrium strategies and state trajectories in a linear quadratic N N -player game with Gaussian initial data. By analyzing convergence toward the corresponding ergodic game, we establish exponential convergence estimates between the solutions of the finite-horizon Riccati system and the associated algebraic Riccati system arising in the ergodic setting. Building on these results, we prove the convergence of the time-averaged value function and derive a turnpike property for the equilibrium pairs of each player. Importantly, our approach avoids reliance on the mean field game limiting model, allowing for a fully uniform analysis with respect to the number of players N N . As a result, we further establish a uniform turnpike property for the equilibrium pairs between the finite-horizon and ergodic games with N N players. Keywords: Stochastic differential games, turnpike property, Riccati equations, mean field games, ergodic games. AMS subject classification: Primary: 91A15, 49N10, 91A23; Secondary: 49N70, 34H05.



1.
Introduction

This paper investigates turnpike properties in linear quadratic Gaussian (LQG)
N
-player differential games, where the dynamics of players are modeled by a system of linear stochastic differential equations (SDEs). The primary objective is to examine the long-time behavior of equilibrium strategies and state trajectories in the finite-horizon game by analyzing their convergence toward those of the associated ergodic game. In the finite-horizon game, each player seeks to minimize a quadratic cost functional, involving both control effort and deviation from a reference state, over a given time interval. As the time horizon tends to infinity, the problem naturally evolves into an ergodic game, where players aim to minimize the long-time-average cost.

A central focus of this work is the turnpike property, which characterize the convergence of equilibrium pairs from the finite-horizon game to those of the corresponding ergodic game. Our main result shows that the deviation between the equilibrium pairs of the finite-horizon game and those of the ergodic game decays exponentially fast in time. Specifically, we prove that there exist some positive constants
K^{(N)}
and
\lambda^{(N)}
, independent of
t
and
T
, such that the following estimate holds for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
:

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|X_{T}^{i}(t)-X^{i}(t)\big|^{2}+\big|\alpha_{T}^{i}(t)-\alpha^{i}(t)\big|^{2}\Big]\leqslant K^{(N)}\big(e^{-\lambda^{(N)}t}+e^{-\lambda^{(N)}(T-t)}\big)

for all
T
and
t\in[0,T]
, where
(X_{T}^{i},\alpha_{T}^{i})
denotes the equilibrium state-strategy pair for Player
i
in the finite-horizon game, and
(X^{i},\alpha^{i})
denotes the corresponding equilibrium pair for Player
i
in the ergodic game.
Furthermore, under stronger structural assumptions–specifically, by imposing uniformity of the data in the problem with respect to the number of players
N
–we establish a uniform turnpike property: the convergence is not only exponential but also uniform with respect to the number of players. That is, there exist positive constants
K
and
\lambda
, independent of
t,T
, and
N
, such that

\sup_{N}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\bm{X}_{T}(t)-\bm{X}(t)\big|^{2}+\big|\bm{\alpha}_{T}(t)-\bm{\alpha}(t)\big|^{2}\Big]\leqslant K\big(e^{-\lambda t}+e^{-\lambda(T-t)}\big)

for all
T
and
t\in[0,T]
, where
\bm{X}_{T}(t):=(X_{T}^{1}(t),\dots,X_{T}^{N}(t))
,
\bm{X}(t):=(X^{1}(t),\dots,X^{N}(t))
, and
\bm{\alpha}_{T}(t):=(\alpha^{1}_{T}(t),\dots,\alpha_{T}^{N}(t))
,
\bm{\alpha}(t):=(\alpha^{1}(t),\dots,\alpha^{N}(t))
are all valued in
\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
.
These results highlight the persistence of the ergodic equilibrium as a stable attractor for the finite-horizon game, providing new insights into the structure and stability of multi-player stochastic differential games.

1.1.
Relevant works

To contextualize our analysis, we begin by reviewing relevant literature in three key areas. First, we frame the problem within the theory of stochastic differential games, as we consider Nash equilibria for
N
-player systems governed by stochastic dynamics. Second, we discuss large population games and their limiting models, specifically mean field games. Although our results do not rely on a mean field game limit, the coupled structure of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman and Fokker-Planck (HJB-FP) equations in the
N
-player setting is similar to that of mean field game systems, and several techniques used in our analysis are inspired by this literature. In this context, we also position our work within the growing body of research on large population games analyzed directly in the finite-
N
regime, without relying on the limiting mean field game model. Finally, since one of our main contributions is the establishment of a (uniform) turnpike property for the equilibrium pairs, we review existing results on turnpike phenomena in optimal control and game-theoretic contexts, which provide both conceptual and technical foundations for our work.

1.1.1.
Stochastic differential games

Stochastic differential games provide a fundamental framework for modeling and analyzing dynamic strategic interactions under uncertainty among multiple rational players. These models have found broad applications in diverse areas such as economics, finance, engineering, and biology. In multi-player stochastic differential games, a finite number of players–each governed by controlled stochastic dynamics–seek to optimize individual objective functionals that typically depend on the states and controls of all players. The interplay between stochastic effects and strategic competition introduces significant analytical challenges and rich mathematical structures. Both finite-horizon and infinite-horizon formulations of such games have been extensively investigated in the literature; see, for example,
[
37
,
7
,
9
,
4
,
59
]
.

1.1.2.
Large population games

Mean field game theory, independently introduced by Lasry and Lions
[
50
]
, and Huang, Malhamé, and Caines
[
44
]
, provides a tractable framework for asymptotically analyzing
N
-player stochastic differential games. It approximates Nash equilibria in large-population games by replacing individual interactions with a representative agent interacting with the aggregate population effect, the mean field. As
N
grows large, the Nash equilibrium of the
N
-player system converges to the mean field game equilibrium, which is characterized by a coupled system of differential equations: a backward HJB equation for the equilibrium’s cost, and a forward FP equation for the evolution of the state process distribution, capturing the flow of measures under equilibrium.
As the time horizon tends to infinity, ergodic mean field games arise naturally in the study of long-time behavior. In continuous state spaces, ergodic/stationary mean field games with standard controls have been studied in
[
34
,
2
]
, as well as with singular control and regime switching
[
11
,
31
,
1
,
35
,
26
]
. The finite-state counterparts were explored in
[
39
,
27
,
28
]
. In the ergodic settings, the HJB and FP equations take a stationary form, capturing the long-term behavior of the system.
For a comprehensive overview of recent advancements and relevant applications of
mean field game theory, we refer the reader to the monographs
[
19
,
20
,
13
]
, and the references therein.

Current research efforts have investigated high-dimensional stochastic control problems and large-population games without relying on the mean field limiting framework, enabling the study of non-symmetric settings or heterogeneous interactions. For instance, Jackson and Lacker
[
45
]
derived sharp non-asymptotic bounds between full-information and distributed formulations of stochastic control problems, while Lacker, Mukherjee, and Yeung
[
48
]
developed quantitative mean field approximations for high-dimensional systems. Cirant, Jackson, and Redaelli
[
24
]
provided a non-asymptotic framework for analyzing multi-player stochastic differential games. Our work also follows the philosophy of avoiding the mean field game limit, offering a uniform analysis of
N
-player games, and establishing a uniform turnpike property for
N
-player games.

1.1.3.
Turnpike property

Recent works have increasingly focused on the long-term behavior of multi-player differential games and mean field games. A particularly notable phenomenon in this context is the turnpike property–the tendency of optimal trajectories and controls in dynamic optimization problems to remain close, over most of the time horizon, to those of a corresponding steady-state or static optimization problem. The turnpike phenomenon has a rich history that traces back to economic theory. Ramsey
[
57
]
was the first to observe such a tendency while studying optimal savings in economic growth problems over infinite horizon. Later, von Neumann
[
53
]
developed foundational mathematical frameworks that formalized aspects of the phenomenon in growth models. The term
turnpike property
was formally introduced by Dorfman, Samuelson, and Solow
[
32
]
, drawing an analogy with U.S. highway systems to describe the structure of optimal trajectories in long-time dynamic optimization problems. Since then, the turnpike property has been extensively studied in both finite- and infinite-dimensional deterministic control systems, encompassing both discrete-time and continuous-time formulations. For further background and developments, we refer to
[
56
,
30
,
64
,
40
,
51
,
10
,
33
]
, as well as the monographs
[
67
]
and
[
18
]
. More recently, Gugat, Herty, and Segala
[
41
]
extended the turnpike property to optimal control problems governed by mean field dynamics, which arise as the limit of systems involving a large number of interacting ordinary differential equations. A comprehensive and up-to-date survey on turnpike theory and its various applications is presented in Trélat and Zuazua
[
65
]
.

It is worth noting that the study of turnpike properties in stochastic optimal control problems remained largely unexplored for a long time. A significant breakthrough was achieved in the recent work of Sun, Wang, and Yong
[
60
]
, which was the first to rigorously establish the turnpike property for stochastic linear quadratic optimal control problems. This initiated a wave of systematic investigations into turnpike behavior in continuous-time stochastic control systems, including further developments in
[
22
,
29
,
63
,
58
,
46
,
52
]
.

Recently, attention has also turned to turnpike properties in mean field control problems. In particular, Sun and Yong
[
62
]
extended their earlier results
[
60
]
to mean field linear stochastic systems, establishing a turnpike property under the stabilizability assumption on the homogeneous state equation. In a related development, Bayraktar and Jian
[
6
]
derived the turnpike property for finite-horizon linear quadratic mean field control problems by introducing and analyzing the corresponding ergodic control problem in the mean field setting. Additionally, long-term average impulse control problems with specific mean field interactions have been studied in
[
42
]
.

On the other hand, driven by the growing interest in understanding long-term equilibrium behavior in large interacting systems, the study of turnpike properties in multi-player differential games and mean field games has emerged as a rapidly advancing field. The long-time average behavior of mean field game systems with local couplings was first studied by Cardaliaguet, Lasry, Lions, and Porretta
[
14
]
, who subsequently extended their results to nonlocal coupling settings in
[
15
]
. Further investigations into the long-time behavior of mean field game systems were carried out in
[
38
,
12
,
25
]
, addressing various structural settings. Notably, Cardaliaguet and Porretta
[
16
]
also analyzed the long-time behavior of the master equation, which characterizes the evolution of equilibria in mean field games with common noise.

1.2.
Contributions

In this work, we establish turnpike properties for the equilibrium strategies and state trajectories in the
N
-player game defined on a finite horizon by analyzing their convergence toward those of the associated ergodic game.

In Section
2
, we formulate the problem setup for both the finite-horizon game and its corresponding ergodic game, and present the main results of the paper. We begin with the finite-horizon game in Section
2.1
, where the definition of admissible strategies and Nash equilibrium are introduced in Definition
2.1
and
2.2
, respectively. Under the assumption that each player only observes their own dynamics, we adjust the cost functional of each player to depend on their private initial state and the distribution of the initial states of the other players. This formulation leads to a coupled system of HJB-FP equations, which can (alternatively) be interpreted as a finite-horizon mean field game involving
N
-types of players. For related studies on multi-population mean field games, we refer the reader to
[
44
,
47
,
5
,
8
]
. By using the standard techniques from linear-quadratic
N
-player differential games with Gaussian initial data, we apply an ansatz to the HJB-FP system and derive a system of Riccati ordinary differential equations (ODEs). The main results for the finite-horizon game are then presented in Proposition
2.1
. In this proposition, we establish the unique solvability to the Riccati system, which is a non-trivial result as the system includes a subsystem of fully coupled forward-backward ODEs. Moreover, the equilibrium strategies, state trajectories, and the value functions for each player in the finite-horizon game are characterized in Proposition
2.1
. To derive results that are uniform with respect to the number of players
N
, we impose additional structural assumptions on the problem data and establish a uniform version of Proposition
2.1
in Corollary
2.1
. Building on the finite-horizon setup, we then present the ergodic game formulation and its corresponding results in Section
2.2
. We note that this ergodic game was previously studied in
[
4
]
. The main results for the ergodic game are summarized in Proposition
2.2
.

In Section
2.3
, we present the main result of this work. To establish the desired turnpike properties for the pairs of equilibrium strategies and equilibrium state trajectories arising from the finite-horizon and ergodic formulations of the
N
-player differential game, we first analyze the convergence between the solutions to the respective system of Riccati equations. These convergence results, which form the core technical component of our analysis, are established in Proposition
2.3
. Both non-uniform and uniform versions of the estimates are provided therein. Then, based on these estimates, we provide the ergodicity for the value function
\mathcal{V}_{T}^{i}
associated with the finite-horizon game to the value
c^{i}
arising in the ergodic game in Corollary
2.2
. Finally, in Theorem
2.1
, we establish the turnpike property and its uniform counterpart that characterizes the relationship between the pairs of equilibrium strategies and equilibrium states of the finite-horizon game and those of the ergodic game. To demonstrate the applicability and verification of the assumptions imposed throughout the paper, we provide two illustrative examples in Section
2.4
.

Section
3
collects the proofs of several preliminary results used in the derivation of the main results. In Section
4
, we provide a detailed and step-by-step proof of Proposition
2.1
. We begin in Section
4.1

by establishing the unique solvability of the Riccati system associated with the finite-horizon game. For the subsystem of fully coupled forward-backward ODEs, we adopt the Leray-Schauder fixed point theorem to prove the existence of solutions. A central challenge in this analysis is to demonstrate the boundedness of solutions to this subsystem, for which we utilize techniques inspired by
[
16
]
and
[
25
]
. This approach builds on a standard duality argument frequently used in the study of HJB-FP system arising in mean field games. We then prove the unique solvability of the finite-horizon HJB-FP system under the ansatz formulation in Section
4.2
. Moreover, the equilibrium strategy and the corresponding equilibrium state trajectory for each player are also characterized in this section. Building on these results, in Section
4.3
, we apply the stochastic verification theorem to derive the value function for each player, which solves the associated HJB equations over the finite horizon.

In Section
5
, we provide the detailed proof of Proposition
2.3
, which establishes exponential convergence estimates between the solution to the finite-horizon Riccati system and the solution to the algebraic Riccati system arising in the ergodic game. The most technical part lies in deriving estimates for the difference between the solution of fully coupled forward-backward ODE system and their ergodic counterpart. Here, we again draw on analytical tools developed in
[
16
]
and
[
25
]
, which was used in studying the turnpike properties for mean field games.

Section
6
presents the proofs of Corollary
2.2
and Theorem
2.1
, which address, respectively, the convergence of the time-averaged value function as the time horizon tends to infinity, and the turnpike property for the equilibrium pairs. Both non-uniform and uniform versions of these results are proved in this section. Finally, in Section
7
, we summarize the work in this paper and outline some possible directions for future research.

2.
Problem setup and main results

In this section, we study two types of open-loop games, where each player’s strategy depends only on their own filtration. We start with the finite-horizon game in Subsection
2.1
, where players minimize their expected total cost over a fixed time interval. We establish existence, uniqueness, and related properties for the corresponding
N
-player game via coupled Riccati systems. Alongside, we summarize known results for the ergodic game in Subsection
2.2
, where players minimize long-run average costs, as presented in
[
4
]
. The centerpiece of our contribution–turnpike properties for the finite-horizon games–are detailed in Subsection
2.3
, and Section
2.4
offers examples of models that fit within our framework.

We start with the common ground for both games, which is the probability space and the dynamics of the players.
Consider a complete filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, which satisfies the usual conditions and supports
N
independent
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motions
\{W^{1}(t),\ldots,W^{N}(t)\}_{t\geqslant 0}
. We denote the expectation on this space by
\mathbb{E}
. For each
i\in[N]
, denote by
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{i})_{t\geqslant 0}
the augmentation of the filtration generated by
W^{i}
. For technical reasons, which will become clearer later, we fix an arbitrary initial time
t_{0}\geqslant 0
. We consider the following system of SDEs:

(1)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle dX^{i}(t)=(A^{i}X^{i}(t)-\alpha^{i}(t))dt+\sigma^{i}dW^{i}(t),\quad t\geqslant t_{0},\\
\displaystyle X^{i}(t_{0})=x^{i}_{0},\end{cases}

for
i\in[N]
,
where
A^{i},\sigma^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
\sigma^{i}
assumed to be invertible, and
x^{i}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the system of SDEs (
1
), the process
X^{i}=(X^{i}(t))_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
represents the state of Player
i
and the process
\alpha^{i}=(\alpha^{i}(t))_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
denotes the corresponding control process, also referred to as the strategy of Player
i
.

2.1.
The finite-horizon game

Beyond the obvious difference in cost criteria, a key distinction between the two games lies in the definitions of the admissible controls. We denote the time horizon of the game by
T\geqslant t_{0}
.

Definition 2.1

(Admissible strategies in the finite-horizon game)
.

A strategy
\alpha^{i}
for Player
i\in[N]
is called
admissible in the finite-horizon game
if it belongs to the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{i}[t_{0},T]:=\Big\{\alpha:\Omega\times[t_{0},T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}:\alpha\hbox{ is $(\mathcal{F}^{i}_{t})_{t\geqslant t_{0}}$-progressively measurable, and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}|\alpha(t)|^{2}dt\Big]<\infty\Big\}.

The cost functional is quadratic in both the control and state processes. Given the initial states
\bm{x}_{0}=(x^{1}_{0},\dots,x^{N}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
at time
t_{0}
from all the players, the objective of Player
i
is to choose a strategy
\alpha^{i}
that minimizes the following expected total cost

(2)

J^{i}_{T}(t_{0},\bm{x}_{0};\bm{\alpha}):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{i}(t)^{\top}R^{i}\alpha^{i}(t)+(\bm{X}(t)-\bar{\bm{x}}_{i})^{\top}\bm{Q}^{i}(\bm{X}(t)-\bar{\bm{x}}_{i})\Big)dt\Big],

where
(\bm{X}(t))_{t\geqslant t_{0}}:=(X^{1}(t),\dots,X^{N}(t))_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
is the state-dynamics of all the players, given in (
1
), associated with the strategy profile
(\bm{\alpha}(t))_{t\geqslant t_{0}}:=(\alpha^{1}(t),\dots,\alpha^{N}(t))_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
. In the above,
R^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are positive definite symmetric matrices,
\bm{Q}^{i}
are symmetric
{Nd\times Nd}
matrices, and
\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}=(\bar{x}_{i}^{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{i}^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
are given reference positions.

Note that we assume players only observe their own dynamics, hence, we adjust the cost to depend on their private initial state and the distribution of the initial states of the other players. For this, we need a few more notations. Denote by
\eta^{j}
,
j\in[N]
a density of a distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as well as a vector of densities for all the players except for
i
:

\bm{\eta}^{-i}:=(\eta^{1},\ldots,\eta^{i-1},\eta^{i+1},\ldots,\eta^{N}),

and set the cost function for Player
i
, given the initial state to Player
i
,
x^{i}_{0}
, and the initial distribution vector for the rest of the players
\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0}=(\mathbb{P}\circ(X^{j}(t_{0})^{-1}:j\in[N]\setminus\{i\})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{T}^{i}(t_{0},x^{i}_{0};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0},\bm{\alpha}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}}J_{T}^{i}\big(t_{0},\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{i-1},x_{0}^{i},\xi^{i+1},\dots,\xi^{N};\bm{\alpha}\big)\prod_{j\neq i}d\eta^{j}\big(\xi^{j}\big).

For the simplicity of notation, we define the state-dependent cost function

\displaystyle F^{i}(\bm{X}(t))

\displaystyle:=(\bm{X}(t)-\bar{\bm{x}}_{i})^{\top}\bm{Q}^{i}(\bm{X}(t)-\bar{\bm{x}}_{i})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(X^{j}(t)-\bar{x}_{i}^{j})^{\top}Q_{jk}^{i}(X^{k}(t)-\bar{x}_{i}^{k}),

for all
i\in[N]
, where the matrices
\{Q_{jk}^{i}:j,k\in[N]\}
are
d\times d
blocks of
\bm{Q}^{i}
. Note that, each block
Q_{jk}^{i}
captures the sensitivity of Player
i
’s cost to the joint displacement of players
j
and
k
from their respective reference positions
\bar{x}_{i}^{j}
and
\bar{x}_{i}^{k}
as perceived by Player
i
. In the following, we assume that
Q_{ii}^{i}
is positive definite for all
i\in[N]
, which reflects the assumption that Player
i
incurs a strictly positive cost for deviating from their own preferred location
\bar{x}_{i}^{i}
.

By further setting the function
f^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{N-1}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle f^{i}(x;\bm{\eta}^{-i}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}}F^{i}\big(\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{i-1},x,\xi^{i+1},\dots,\xi^{N}\big)\prod_{j\neq i}d\eta^{j}\big(\xi^{j}\big),

we can rewrite the cost as

\mathcal{J}_{T}^{i}(t_{0},x_{0}^{i};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0},\bm{\alpha})=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{i}(t)^{\top}R^{i}\alpha^{i}(t)+f^{i}\big(X^{i}(t);(\mathbb{P}\circ(X^{j}(t))^{-1}:j\in[N]\setminus\{i\})\big)\Big)dt\Big].

The optimality in the game is defined via Nash equilibria. For this, we need further notation. Given an admissible strategy profile
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha^{1},\ldots,\alpha^{N})\in\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{A}^{j}[t_{0},T]
and an individual admissible strategy
\widehat{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}^{i}[t_{0},T]
, we denote by
[\bm{\alpha}^{-i};\widehat{\alpha}]:=(\alpha^{1},\ldots,\alpha^{i-1},\widehat{\alpha},\alpha^{i+1},\ldots,\alpha^{N})
the strategy profile obtained from
\bm{\alpha}
by replacing
\alpha^{i}
with
\widehat{\alpha}
.

Definition 2.2

(Nash equilibrium in the finite-horizon game)
.

Fix an initial time
t_{0}
, an
initial distribution vector
\bm{\eta}_{0}=(\eta^{1}_{0},\ldots,\eta^{N}_{0})
, and
initial state vector of private realizations
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
, where each
x^{i}_{0}
is sampled from
\eta^{i}_{0}
, possibly in a dependent manner across players. We say that a strategy profile
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha^{1},\ldots,\alpha^{N})\in\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{A}^{j}[t_{0},T]
is a
Nash equilibrium in the finite-horizon game
if for every
i\in[N]
and every
\widehat{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}^{i}[t_{0},T]
,

\mathcal{J}^{i}_{T}(t_{0},x^{i}_{0};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0},[\bm{\alpha}^{-i};\widehat{\alpha}])\geqslant\mathcal{J}^{i}_{T}(t_{0},x^{i}_{0};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0},\bm{\alpha}).

For any Nash equilibrium
\bm{\alpha}
, we refer to the cost for Player
i
as the
value function of the finite-horizon game
, associated with the initial time
t_{0}
, the initial private state
x^{i}_{0}
of Player
i
, the initial distribution vector
\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0}

2.2.
The ergodic game

In this section, we build on the setting of the finite-horizon game and present the setup and the equivalent results for the ergodic game, which was studied in
[
4
]
. In this setting, each player
i\in[N]
aims to minimize a long-time average cost functional over the infinite time horizon
[0,\infty)
. Before introducing it, we set up the set of admissible strategies in the ergodic game.

Definition 2.3

(Admissible strategies in the ergodic game)
.

A strategy
\alpha^{i}
is called an
admissible strategy in the ergodic game
if:

•

It is adapted to
(\mathcal{F}^{i}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
;

•

For any
t\geqslant 0
, the expectations
\mathbb{E}[|\alpha^{i}(t)|^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}[|X^{i}(t)|^{2}]
are finite, where
(X^{i}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is the corresponding state dynamics in (
1
);

•

(X^{i}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is ergodic in the following sense: there exists a probability measure
\pi^{i}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}d\pi^{i}(x)<\infty

and

(24)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}h(X_{t}^{i})dt\Big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)d\pi^{i}(x),

locally uniformly with respect to the initial state
x_{0}^{i}
for all polynomial functions
h
of degree at most 2.

We denote by
\mathcal{A}^{i}[0,\infty)
the set of all admissible strategies for Player
i
in the ergodic game.

In the ergodic case, the initial state is irrelevant. However, for consistency with the finite-horizon formulation, we present only the cost version that depends on the initial private state and the initial distributions of the other players. The cost function for Player
i\in[N]
is given by:

(25)

\mathcal{J}^{i}_{\infty}(x^{i}_{0};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0},\bm{\alpha}):=\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{i}(t)^{\top}R^{i}\alpha^{i}(t)+(\bm{X}(t)-\bar{\bm{x}}_{i})^{\top}\bm{Q}^{i}(\bm{X}(t)-\bar{\bm{x}}_{i})\Big)dt\Big].

The associated
ergodic value (function) of the ergodic game
is defined as

(26)

\mathcal{V}_{\infty}^{i}(x^{i}_{0};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0}):=\mathcal{J}^{i}_{\infty}(x^{i}_{0};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0},\bm{\alpha}):=\inf_{\widehat{\alpha}^{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{i}[0,\infty)}\mathcal{J}^{i}_{\infty}(x^{i}_{0};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0},[\bm{\alpha}^{-i};\widehat{\alpha}^{i}]).

The definition of Nash equilibrium in the ergodic game is equivalent to the one in the finite-horizon case, yet for the completeness of presentation, we provide it here.

Definition 2.4

(Nash equilibrium in the ergodic game)
.

Fix an initial distribution vector
\bm{\eta}_{0}=(\eta^{1}_{0},\ldots,\eta^{N}_{0})
and
initial state vector of private realizations
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
, where each
x^{i}_{0}
is sampled from
\eta^{i}_{0}
, possibly in a dependent manner across players. We say that a strategy profile
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha^{1},\ldots,\alpha^{N})\in\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{A}^{j}[0,\infty)
is a
Nash equilibrium in the ergodic game
if for every
i\in[N]
and every
\widehat{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}^{i}[0,\infty)
,

\mathcal{J}^{i}_{\infty}(x^{i}_{0};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0},[\bm{\alpha}^{-i};\widehat{\alpha}])\geqslant\mathcal{J}^{i}_{\infty}(x^{i}_{0};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0},\bm{\alpha}).

The
stationary HJB–FP equations
are given by, for
i\in[N]
,

(27)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\text{tr}(\varsigma^{i}D^{2}v^{i}(x))+H^{i}(x,\nabla v^{i}(x))+f^{i}(x;\bm{m}^{-i})=c^{i},\qquad&\text{(HJB)}\\
\displaystyle\text{tr}(\varsigma^{i}D^{2}m^{i})-\text{div}\Big(m^{i}\frac{\partial H^{i}}{\partial p}(x,\nabla v^{i}(x))\Big)=0.&\text{(FP)}\end{cases}

A solution to this system is a triplet
\bm{c}=(c^{1},\ldots,c^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\bm{v}=(v^{1},\ldots,v^{N}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and
\bm{m}=(m^{1},\ldots,m^{N})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For each
i
, the two equations are coupled through a strategy profile
(\bm{\alpha}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, where the strategy of Player
i
is defined by

\alpha^{i}(t):=a^{i,*}\big(\nabla v^{i}(t,X^{i}(t))\big),

with
X^{i}
denoting the state process of Player
i
under the strategy profile
\bm{\alpha}
.
The first equation is the ergodic HJB equation, derived from a dynamic programming principle. Its solution is the couple
c^{i}
and
v^{i}(x)
, where the former stands for the
value
for Player
i
under the strategy profile
\bm{\alpha}
and is equal to
\mathcal{V}_{\infty}^{i}(x^{i}_{0};\bm{\eta}^{-i}_{0})
and, as expected in ergodic problems, it is independent of the initial positions of the players.
The
relative value

v^{i}
encodes the “cost-to-go” from each state, reflecting both immediate costs and the asymptotic behavior of the system. While it plays a role similar to the value function in the finite-horizon case, it is more accurately characterized as the limit of value function differences in the discounted game as the discount rate approaches zero. The solution for the second equation
m^{i}
is the stationary distribution of Player
i
’s state under the strategy profile
\bm{\alpha}
.

Similarly to the finite-horizon case, we consider the system of stationary HJB-FP equations (
27
), which admits solutions with
\{(v^{i},m^{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
of the following explicit form

(28)

\displaystyle v^{i}(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}\Lambda^{i}x+(\rho^{i})^{\top}x,

\displaystyle m^{i}=\mathcal{N}\big(\mu^{i},(\Sigma^{i})^{-1}\big),

where
\Lambda^{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
,
\Sigma^{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++}
, and
\mu^{i},\rho^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{i}
for all
i\in[N]
. Consequently, the system of HJB-FP equations (
27
) is linked to Riccati equations, which, in this case, are algebraic:

(29)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\Lambda^{i}A^{i}+(A^{i})^{\top}\Lambda^{i}-\Lambda^{i}(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}+2Q^{i}_{ii}=0,\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\Sigma^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}+\Sigma^{i}\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}\right)=0,\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}\right)\mu^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\rho^{i}=0,\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})^{\top}\rho^{i}+2F_{1}^{i}(\bm{\mu}^{-i})=0,\\
\displaystyle\text{tr}(\varsigma^{i}\Lambda^{i})-\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{i})^{\top}(R^{i})^{-1}\rho^{i}+F_{0}^{i}(\bm{\mu}^{-i},\Sigma^{i})=c^{i}.\end{cases}

The solution to the above system is
\{\Lambda^{i},\Sigma^{i},\mu^{i},\rho^{i},c^{i}:i\in[N]\}
with

c^{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i\in[N]
. In the system of equations (
29
), the coefficients
\{F_{1}^{i},F_{0}^{i}:i\in[N]\}
are also derived from the running cost functional and are explicitly given in (
10
).

Remark 2.5
.

It is worth noting that the system of algebraic Riccati equations (
29
) is equivalent to the one obtained in the work of Bardi and Priuli
[
4
]
, namely:

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\Lambda^{i}=R^{i}(\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}+A^{i}),\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\rho^{i}=-R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}\mu^{i},\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\Sigma^{i}\varsigma^{i}R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}-(A^{i})^{\top}R^{i}A^{i}=2Q^{i}_{ii},\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle-\Sigma^{i}\varsigma^{i}R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}\mu^{i}=2F_{1}^{i}(\bm{\mu}^{-i}),\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mu^{i})^{\top}\Sigma^{i}\varsigma^{i}R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}\mu^{i}-\text{tr}(\varsigma^{i}R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}+\varsigma^{i}R^{i}A^{i})+c^{i}=F_{0}^{i}(\bm{\mu}^{-i},\Sigma^{i}).\end{cases}

We begin with the equation for
\Sigma^{i}
in (
29
). Since
\Sigma^{i}
is positive definite, we readily obtain
\Lambda^{i}=R^{i}(\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}+A^{i})
. Next, from the third equation for
\mu^{i}
in (
29
) and using the identity

A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}=-\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i},

we derive
\rho^{i}=-R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}\mu^{i}
.
Substituting the identity
\Lambda^{i}=R^{i}(\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}+A^{i})
into the first equation for
\Lambda^{i}
in (
29
), and applying the symmetry relation

R^{i}(\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}+A^{i})=(\Sigma^{i}\varsigma^{i}+(A^{i})^{\top})R^{i},

we obtain the equation

\Sigma^{i}\varsigma^{i}R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}-(A^{i})^{\top}R^{i}A^{i}=2Q^{i}_{ii}.

Similarly, by plugging
\rho^{i}=-R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}\mu^{i}
into the fourth equation of (
29
) and using the identity
(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})^{\top}=-\Sigma^{i}\varsigma^{i}
again, we deduce

-\Sigma^{i}\varsigma^{i}R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}\mu^{i}=2F_{1}^{i}(\bm{\mu}^{-i}).

Finally, using the previously established expressions for
\Lambda^{i}
and
\rho^{i}
, it is immediate that the last equation in (
29
) is equivalent to

\frac{1}{2}(\mu^{i})^{\top}\Sigma^{i}\varsigma^{i}R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}\mu^{i}-\text{tr}(\varsigma^{i}R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}+\varsigma^{i}R^{i}A^{i})+c^{i}=F_{0}^{i}(\bm{\mu}^{-i},\Sigma^{i}).

In order to obtain the unique solvability of the algebraic Riccati system (
29
) and to derive the corresponding results for the ergodic game in
N
-player games, we introduce the following assumption. To proceed, we define the matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd\times Nd}
as

(30)

\bm{M}=(M_{ij})_{i,j\in[N]},\quad M_{ij}=Q_{ij}^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}(A^{i})^{\top}R^{i}A^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta.

Assumption 2.6
.

For each
i\in[N]
, every symmetric and positive definite solution
\Sigma
of the algebraic Riccati equation

\Sigma\varsigma^{i}R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma-(A^{i})^{\top}R^{i}A^{i}=2Q^{i}_{ii}

is also a solution to the Sylvester equation

\Sigma\varsigma^{i}R^{i}-R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma=R^{i}A^{i}-(A^{i})^{\top}R^{i}.

Moreover, the block matrix
\bm{M}
in (
30
) is invertible.

Remark 2.6
.

Assumption
2.6
is adopted from
[
4
]
to guarantee the solvability of the algebraic Riccati system (
29
). The condition on the solution to the algebraic Riccati equation for
\Sigma

is imposed to ensure that
\Lambda^{i}
is symmetric for each
i\in[N]
. In addition, the condition on the matrix
\bm{M}
is required to guarantee the unique solvability of
\{\mu^{i}:i\in[N]\}
.

Based on the assumptions introduced above, we now establish the following results concerning the ergodic game in the context of
N
-player interactions. These results were originally proved in
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that the
N
-player game with dynamics given by (

in the form of (

(31)

\displaystyle\alpha^{i}(t)

\displaystyle=a^{i,*}(\partial_{x}v^{i}(X^{i}(t)))=(R^{i})^{-1}\partial_{x}v^{i}(X^{i}(t))

\displaystyle=(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}X^{i}(t)+(R^{i})^{-1}\rho^{i},\quad t\geqslant 0,\,i\in[N],

is a Nash equilibrium with open-loop feedback strategies in the ergodic game, where

is given in (

), and

(32)

This SDE admits an explicit solution:

\displaystyle X^{i}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})t}x_{0}^{i}-\int_{0}^{t}e^{(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})(t-s)}(R^{i})^{-1}\rho^{i}ds

where

coincides with the measure
m^{i}
.

(iii)

(Value and relative value) For each

and the value of Player

\mathcal{V}_{\infty}^{i}(x;\bm{m}_{0}^{-i})=c^{i}=\text{tr}(\varsigma^{i}\Lambda^{i})-\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{i})^{\top}(R^{i})^{-1}\rho^{i}+F_{0}^{i}(\bm{\mu}^{-i},\Sigma^{i}),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where

The solvability of the algebraic Riccati system (

2.3.
The turnpike results

This section includes the main results of this work. To establish the desired turnpike properties for the pairs of equilibrium strategies and equilibrium state trajectories arising from the finite-horizon and ergodic formulations of the
N
-player differential game, it is crucial to analyze the convergence of the solutions to the Riccati systems (
9
) and (
29
).

To this end, we begin by establishing the convergence results between the solutions to the system of Riccati equations (
9
) and (
29
) in the following proposition. Specifically, we provide essential estimates for the parameter functions
\Lambda^{i}_{T},\Sigma^{i}_{T},\mu^{i}_{T}
and
\rho^{i}_{T}
encountered in the finite-horizon game (
3
), as well as the corresponding constant matrices and vectors
\Lambda^{i},\Sigma^{i},\mu^{i}
and
\rho^{i}
arising in the ergodic game (
26
). We anticipate a natural convergence between these quantities. Moreover, these estimates constitute the principal technical challenge for proving the turnpike properties. The detailed and rigorous proof is deferred to Section
5
.

To proceed, we denote vectors
\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
and
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}

\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t):=\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 4.0pt\rho_{T}^{1}(t)-\rho^{1}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\rho_{T}^{2}(t)-\rho^{2}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\vdots\\
\vskip 4.0pt\rho_{T}^{N}(t)-\rho^{N}\end{bmatrix},\quad\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t):=\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 4.0pt\mu_{T}^{1}(t)-\mu^{1}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\mu_{T}^{2}(t)-\mu^{2}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\vdots\\
\vskip 4.0pt\mu_{T}^{N}(t)-\mu^{N}\end{bmatrix}

for all
t\in[0,T]
, and introduce the following assumption, which is required for uniform-in-
N
results.

Assumption 2.7
.

There exists a constant
\beta_{3}>0
such that for all
i\in[N]
and
N\geqslant 2
, the quantities

\max_{j\in[N]}|\bar{x}_{j}^{i}|,\quad\|\varsigma^{i}\|,\quad\|(\Sigma_{0}^{i})^{-1}\|,\quad|\mu^{i}_{0}|,\quad\|(\Sigma^{i})^{-1}\|,\quad|\mu^{i}|,\quad|\rho^{i}|

and

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sup_{j\neq i}\|Q_{ij}^{i}\|,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\sup_{k\neq j,i}\|Q_{jk}^{i}\|,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq i}\sup_{j\neq k,i}\|Q_{jk}^{i}\|,\quad\sum_{j\neq i}\|Q_{jj}^{i}\|,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sup_{j\neq i}\|Q_{jj}^{i}\|

are all bounded above by
\beta_{3}
, where
\{\Sigma^{i},\mu^{i},\rho^{i}:i\in[N]\}
is the solution to the corresponding subsystem in the algebraic Riccati equations (

).

Proposition 2.3
.

Fix
N\geqslant 2
, and suppose that Assumptions
2.2
,
2.3
, and
2.6
hold. Let
\{\Lambda^{i}_{T},\Sigma^{i}_{T},\mu^{i}_{T},\rho^{i}_{T}:i\in[N]\}
denote the solution to the system of Riccati equations (
9
), and let
\{\Lambda^{i},\Sigma^{i},\mu^{i},\rho^{i}:i\in[N]\}
be the solution to the algebraic Riccati system (

). Then, there exist some constants
\widehat{K}^{(N)}>0
and
\widehat{\lambda}^{(N)}>0
, independent of
t
and
T
, such that the following exponential convergence estimates hold:

(33)

\sup_{i\in[N]}\|\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)-\Lambda^{i}\|\leqslant\widehat{K}^{(N)}e^{-\widehat{\lambda}^{(N)}(T-t)},

(34)

\sup_{i\in[N]}\|(\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t))^{-1}-(\Sigma^{i})^{-1}\|\leqslant\widehat{K}^{(N)}\big(e^{-\widehat{\lambda}^{(N)}t}+e^{-\widehat{\lambda}^{(N)}(T-t)}\big),

and

(35)

\sup_{i\in[N]}\big(|\mu^{i}_{T}(t)-\mu^{i}|+|\rho^{i}_{T}(t)-\rho^{i}|\big)\leqslant\widehat{K}^{(N)}\big(e^{-\widehat{\lambda}^{(N)}t}+e^{-\widehat{\lambda}^{(N)}(T-t)}\big)

for all

and

Moreover, if in addition, Assumptions
2.4
,
2.5
, and
2.7
hold, then we get the existence of constants
\widehat{K}>0
and
\widehat{\lambda}>0
, independent of

, and

, such that the following exponential convergence estimates hold uniformly in

(36)

\sup_{N}\sup_{i\in[N]}\|\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)-\Lambda^{i}\|\leqslant\widehat{K}e^{-\widehat{\lambda}(T-t)},

(37)

\sup_{N}\sup_{i\in[N]}\|(\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t))^{-1}-(\Sigma^{i})^{-1}\|\leqslant\widehat{K}\big(e^{-\widehat{\lambda}t}+e^{-\widehat{\lambda}(T-t)}\big),

and

(38)

\sup_{N}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\big(|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|+|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|\big)\leqslant\widehat{K}\big(e^{-\widehat{\lambda}t}+e^{-\widehat{\lambda}(T-t)}\big)

for all

and

.

Remark 2.8
.

The most technical part of the proof of Proposition

involves deriving estimates between the solution to subsystems
\{\mu^{i}_{T},\rho^{i}_{T}:i\in[N]\}
and
\{\mu^{i},\rho^{i}:i\in[N]\}

and

The convergence results established in Proposition
2.3
reveal the following interesting connection between the value function

Corollary 2.2

Fix

,
2.2
,
2.3
, and

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathcal{V}_{T}^{i}(0,x;\bm{m}^{-i}_{0})=c^{i},

Moreover, if in addition, Assumptions

(40)

3

Theorem 2.1

Fix
N\geqslant 2
and suppose that Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
,
2.3
, and
2.6
hold. For each

, let

denote the equilibrium pair for the finite-horizon game given in (

be the equilibrium pair for the ergodic game defined in (

)–(

are defined on the same probability space and are driven by the same Brownian motion. Then, there exist some constants

and

(41)

\sup_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|X_{T}^{i}(t)-X^{i}(t)\big|^{2}+\big|\alpha_{T}^{i}(t)-\alpha^{i}(t)\big|^{2}\Big]\leqslant\widetilde{K}^{(N)}\big(e^{-\widetilde{\lambda}^{(N)}t}+e^{-\widetilde{\lambda}^{(N)}(T-t)}\big)

for all

Moreover, if in addition, Assumptions
2.4
,
2.5
, and
2.7

and
\widetilde{\lambda}>0

for all

,
\bm{X}(t):=(X^{1}(t),\dots,X^{N}(t))

2.4.1.
Example 1: Symmetric system

Consider an
N
-player game with dynamics (
1
) and cost functional (
2
) and suppose that Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold. Moreover, we assume the players are
nearly identical
in the sense of
[
4
, Definition 4.2]
. Specifically, for all
i\in[N]
, the following conditions are satisfied:

•

A^{i}=A\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
,
\sigma^{i}=\sigma=a_{1}I_{d}
with
a_{1}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
;

•

R^{i}=R=a_{2}I_{d}
with
a_{2}>0
;

•

\bm{Q}^{i}
is given as following:
Q_{ii}^{i}=Q>0
, and

\displaystyle Q_{ij}^{i}=B\geqslant 0,\quad Q_{jj}^{i}=C_{i},\quad\forall j\neq i,

\displaystyle Q_{jk}^{i}=D_{i},\quad j,k\neq i,j\neq k.

The players are not fully identical, as the secondary displacement cost terms
C_{i}
and
D_{i}
may differ across individuals.

Under this setting, we can verify that Assumption
2.6
is satisfied. In particular, the algebraic Riccati equation

\Sigma\varsigma^{i}R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma-(A^{i})^{\top}R^{i}A^{i}=2Q^{i}_{ii}

can be reduced to

\Sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{a_{1}^{4}a_{2}}(2Q+a_{2}A^{2}),

and it is straightforward to verify that the Sylvester equation

\Sigma\varsigma^{i}R^{i}-R^{i}\varsigma^{i}\Sigma=R^{i}A^{i}-(A^{i})^{\top}R^{i}

is always satisfied. Moreover, from the definition of
\bm{M}
and
M_{ij}
in (
30
), we have

M_{ij}=\begin{cases}B,&\text{if }j\neq i,\\
Q+\frac{a_{2}}{2}A^{2},&\text{if }j=i.\end{cases}

which implies that the block matrix
\bm{M}
is invertible.

To verify the condition
c_{*}I_{d}\leqslant\Lambda^{i}\leqslant c^{*}I_{d}
in Assumption
2.4
, it suffices to require that

\frac{(c_{*})^{2}}{a_{2}}I_{d}-2c_{*}A\leqslant 2Q\leqslant\frac{(c^{*})^{2}}{a_{2}}I_{d}-2c^{*}A

as shown in Lemma
2.2
.

We now check Assumptions
2.3
and
2.5
. Under the structure specified above, and using the definition of
\bm{Q}
in (
17
), we obtain that

\bm{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 4.0pt0&B&\cdots&B\\
B&0&\cdots&B\\
\vskip 4.0pt\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
B&B&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 4.0pt0&I_{d}&\cdots&I_{d}\\
I_{d}&0&\cdots&I_{d}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
I_{d}&I_{d}&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}\otimes B=\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 4.0pt0&1&\cdots&1\\
1&0&\cdots&1\\
\vskip 4.0pt\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
1&1&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}\otimes I_{d}\otimes B,

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. Therefore, by assuming that the largest eigenvalue of the matrix
B
is sufficiently small and of order
O(N^{-1})
, we ensure that Assumptions
2.3
and
2.5
are satisfied.

To further ensure that the condition on
\bm{Q}^{i}
in Assumption
2.7
holds, we require that

N\|B\|,\quad(N-1)\|C_{i}\|,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|C_{i}\|,\quad(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|D_{i}\|

are all uniformly bounded with respect to
N
for all
i\in[N]
. This implies that the largest eigenvalues of the matrices
B
and
\{C_{i}:i\in[N]\}
must be of order
O(N^{-1})
.

2.4.2.
Example 2: Consensus models

As in Example 1, we assume that Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
are satisfied. In this section, we consider a class of simple consensus models in multi-player systems, where each player aims to minimize their deviation from the rest of the group. Specifically, the cost function
F^{i}
is defined as

F^{i}(\bm{X}(t))=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}(X^{i}(t)-X^{j}(t))^{\top}Q(X^{i}(t)-X^{j}(t)),\quad\forall i\in[N],

where
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++}
. Under this formulation, each player seeks a position as close as possible to the positions of the other players. In this case, the corresponding matrix
\bm{Q}^{i}
for each
i\in[N]
is specified by
Q_{ii}^{i}=Q
and

\displaystyle Q_{ij}^{i}=-\frac{1}{N-1}Q,\quad Q_{jj}^{i}=\frac{1}{N-1}Q,\quad\forall j\neq i,

\displaystyle Q_{jk}^{i}=0,\quad j,k\neq i,j\neq k.

Moreover, the reference states are set to zero, i.e.,
\bar{x}_{i}^{j}=0
for all
i,j\in[N]
.

We also assume that the dynamics and cost structure are identical for all players, so that the players are identical. For simplicity, for all
i\in[N]
, we still consider the following conditions are satisfied:

•

A^{i}=A\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
,
\sigma^{i}=\sigma=a_{1}I_{d}
with
a_{1}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
;

•

R^{i}=R=a_{2}I_{d}
with
a_{2}>0
.

Under this setting, Assumption
2.6
can be verified in the same manner. By the definition of
\bm{M}
and
M_{ij}
in (
30
), we obtain

M_{ij}=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{N-1}Q,&\text{if }j\neq i,\\
Q+\frac{a_{2}}{2}A^{2},&\text{if }j=i,\end{cases}

thus
\bm{M}
is a strictly diagonally dominant block matrix, which implies that
\bm{M}
is invertible. Consequently, by the result of Remark
2.5
, the equation for
\bm{\mu}=(\mu^{1},\dots,\mu^{N})
in system (
29
) admits a unique solution, which satisfies
\mu^{i}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
i\in[N]
.

Similarly to Example 1, by the result in Lemma
2.2
, to verify the condition
c_{*}I_{d}\leqslant\Lambda^{i}\leqslant c^{*}I_{d}
in Assumption
2.4
, it suffices to require that

\frac{(c_{*})^{2}}{a_{2}}I_{d}-2c_{*}A\leqslant 2Q\leqslant\frac{(c^{*})^{2}}{a_{2}}I_{d}-2c^{*}A.

Moreover, in this consensus setting, the block matrix
\bm{Q}
in (
17
) can be simplified to

\bm{Q}=-\frac{1}{N-1}\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 4.0pt0&1&\cdots&1\\
1&0&\cdots&1\\
\vskip 4.0pt\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
1&1&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}\otimes I_{d}\otimes Q.

Hence, by assuming that the eigenvalues of
Q
are sufficiently small, Assumptions
2.3
and
2.5
can be ensured.

Finally, based on the definition of
\bm{Q}^{i}
in the consensus models, it is straightforward to verify that the following quantities

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sup_{j\neq i}\|Q_{ij}^{i}\|,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\sup_{k\neq j,i}\|Q_{jk}^{i}\|,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq i}\sup_{j\neq k,i}\|Q_{jk}^{i}\|,\quad\sum_{j\neq i}\|Q_{jj}^{i}\|,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sup_{j\neq i}\|Q_{jj}^{i}\|

are all uniform bounded with respect to
N
, which is required in Assumption
2.7
.

First, we prove Lemma

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

(1)

Since

and

are symmetric and positive definite, Conditions (L1), (L2), and (4.23) in Chapter 6 of

11

\Lambda_{T}^{i}\in C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+})
.

(2)

The system (
13

\Theta^{i}=A^{i}+I_{d}
so that all eigenvalues of
A^{i}-\Theta^{i}=-I_{d}
are strictly negative. Under Assumption
2.2
, the matrices

and

satisfy conditions (A1) and (A2) in
[
62
]
. Hence, by
[
62

, the algebraic Riccati equation (
12
) admits a unique positive definite solution
\Lambda^{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++}

(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}
is a stabilizer. The estimate (
14

(3)

Since the ODE (
13
) is stabilizable and
R^{i}
,

are symmetric positive definite matrices, the exponential convergence estimate (
15
) follows directly from
[
62
, Lemma 2.3]
.

∎

Next, we present the proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

We begin by considering the candidate
\Lambda_{*}=c_{*}I_{d}
, and evaluate it through the algebraic Riccati equation (
12
). A straightforward computation yields

\Lambda_{*}A^{i}+(A^{i})^{\top}\Lambda_{*}-\Lambda_{*}(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda_{*}+2Q^{i}_{ii}=2Q^{i}_{ii}+c_{*}(A^{i}+(A^{i})^{\top})-(c_{*})^{2}(R^{i})^{-1}\geqslant 0,

which implies that
\Lambda_{*}=c_{*}I_{d}
serves as a subsolution to the equation (
12
).
By the comparison theorem for algebraic Riccati equations, see
[
49
, Theorem 9.1.1]
, we deduce that

c_{*}I_{d}=\Lambda_{*}\leqslant\Lambda^{i},\quad\forall i\in[N]\text{ and }N\geqslant 2.

Analogously, we examine the upper bound candidate
\Lambda^{*}=c^{*}I_{d}
, for which

\Lambda^{*}A^{i}+(A^{i})^{\top}\Lambda^{*}-\Lambda^{*}(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{*}+2Q^{i}_{ii}=2Q^{i}_{ii}+c^{*}(A^{i}+(A^{i})^{\top})-(c^{*})^{2}(R^{i})^{-1}\leqslant 0,

establishing that
\Lambda^{*}=c^{*}I_{d}
is a supersolution to the equation (
12
).
By the comparison theorem for algebraic Riccati equations again, we derive that

\Lambda^{i}\leqslant\Lambda=c^{*}I_{d},\quad\forall i\in[N]\text{ and }N\geqslant 2.

Therefore, the solution
\Lambda^{i}
to (
12
) satisfies the uniform bounds

c_{*}I_{d}\leqslant\Lambda^{i}\leqslant c^{*}I_{d},\quad\forall i\in[N]\text{ and }N\geqslant 2.

∎

We now prove Lemma
2.3
, which provides uniform-in-
N
estimates for the norm of the matrix exponential
e^{(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})t}
and the deviation
\Lambda_{T}^{i}(t)-\Lambda^{i}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

First, we establish the uniform estimate for the norm of the matrix exponential
e^{(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})t}
under Assumption
2.2
and Assumption
2.4
(i). Since
R^{i}
and
\Lambda^{i}
are positive definite matrices, by Assumption
2.4
(i), the matrix
A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}
is uniform Hurwitz, i.e., the real part of every eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
of
A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}
satisfies
\Re(\lambda_{i})\leqslant-\bar{c}
. Let
\lambda^{*}=\bar{c}
. It follows that there exists
K_{1}^{*}>0
, independent of
t,T
and
N
, such that

(43)

\sup_{N}\sup_{i\in[N]}\big\|e^{(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})t}\big\|\leqslant K_{1}^{*}e^{-\lambda^{*}t},\quad\forall t\geqslant 0.

Next, we recover the uniform estimate (
43
), with potentially different constants
K_{1}^{*}
and
\lambda^{*}
, under Assumption
2.2
and Assumption
2.4
(ii). From equation (
12
), we have

\Lambda^{i}(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})+(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})^{\top}\Lambda^{i}=-\Lambda^{i}(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}-2Q^{i}_{ii}\leqslant-\beta_{1}I_{d}

for all
i\in[N]
and
N\geqslant 2
, since
R^{i}
is positive definite and
2Q^{i}_{ii}\geqslant\beta_{1}I_{d}
by Assumption
2.2
and Assumption
2.4
(ii). Consider the following ODE which is valued in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\frac{d}{dt}x^{i}(t)=(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})x^{i}(t).

Then, the derivative of the quadratic form
\langle\Lambda^{i}x^{i}(t),x^{i}(t)\rangle
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\Lambda^{i}x^{i}(t),x^{i}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Lambda^{i}(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})x^{i}(t),x^{i}(t)\rangle+\langle\Lambda^{i}x^{i}(t),(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})x^{i}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle-\beta_{1}|x^{i}(t)|^{2},\quad\forall t\geqslant 0,

for all
i\in[N]
and
N\geqslant 2
. From Assumption
2.4
(ii), it is clear that

c_{*}|x^{i}(t)|^{2}\leqslant(x^{i}(t))^{\top}\Lambda^{i}x^{i}(t)\leqslant c^{*}|x^{i}(t)|^{2},\quad\forall t\geqslant 0.

Combining these inequalities, we obtain

-\beta_{1}|x^{i}(t)|^{2}\leqslant-\frac{\beta_{1}}{c^{*}}(x^{i}(t))^{\top}\Lambda^{i}x^{i}(t),

which implies that

\frac{d}{dt}\left((x^{i}(t))^{\top}\Lambda^{i}x^{i}(t)\right)\leqslant-\frac{\beta_{1}}{c^{*}}(x^{i}(t))^{\top}\Lambda^{i}x^{i}(t).

Hence, we derive the following inequalities

c_{*}|x^{i}(t)|^{2}\leqslant(x^{i}(t))^{\top}\Lambda^{i}x^{i}(t)\leqslant(x^{i}(0))^{\top}\Lambda^{i}x^{i}(0)e^{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{c^{*}}t},\quad\forall t\geqslant 0.

Let
\Phi^{i}
denote the fundamental solution to the matrix ODE

(44)

\frac{d}{dt}\Phi^{i}(t)=(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})\Phi^{i}(t),\quad\Phi^{i}(0)=I_{d}.

Then we obtain the uniform exponential estimate

\|\Phi^{i}(t)\|=\big\|e^{(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})t}\big\|\leqslant\frac{1}{c_{*}}\|\Lambda^{i}\|e^{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{c^{*}}t}\leqslant\frac{c^{*}}{c_{*}}e^{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{c^{*}}t},\quad\forall t\geqslant 0,

for all
i\in[N]
and
N\geqslant 2
. By choosing
K_{1}^{*}=\frac{c^{*}}{c_{*}}
and
\lambda^{*}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{c^{*}}
, we arrive at the uniform estimate (
43
).

Then, following the approach of
[
60
]
, we establish a uniform estimate for the norm of
\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)-\Lambda^{i}
by using the exponential estimate (
43
). Define the deviation

\widehat{\Lambda}^{i}(t)=\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)-\Lambda^{i},\quad\forall t\in[0,T],\ i\in[N].

Then, from the equations (
11
) and (
12
), we deduce that

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\Lambda}^{i}(t)+\widehat{\Lambda}^{i}(t)(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})+(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})^{\top}\widehat{\Lambda}^{i}(t)-\widehat{\Lambda}^{i}(t)(R^{i})^{-1}\widehat{\Lambda}^{i}(t)=0,\\
\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}^{i}(T)=-\Lambda^{i}.\end{cases}

Let
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t)=\widehat{\Lambda}^{i}(T-t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
and
i\in[N]
. It follows that

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t)=\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t)(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})+(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})^{\top}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t)-\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t)(R^{i})^{-1}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t),\\

Then, using the chain rule and the ODE (

\frac{d}{ds}\big((\Phi^{i}(s))^{\top}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t-s)\Phi^{i}(s)\big)=(\Phi^{i}(s))^{\top}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t-s)(R^{i})^{-1}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t-s)\Phi^{i}(s)

for all

. Consequently, integrating the above identity from

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t-s)

\displaystyle=\big(\Phi^{i}(t)(\Phi^{i}(s))^{-1}\big)^{\top}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(0)\Phi^{i}(t)(\Phi^{i}(s))^{-1}

\displaystyle-\int_{s}^{t}\big(\Phi^{i}(r)(\Phi^{i}(s))^{-1}\big)^{\top}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t-r)(R^{i})^{-1}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t-r)\Phi^{i}(r)(\Phi^{i}(s))^{-1}dr.

as

\displaystyle\sup_{N}\sup_{i\in[N]}\|\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(t)\|\leqslant\sup_{N}\sup_{i\in[N]}\big(K_{1}^{*}e^{-\lambda^{*}t}\big)^{2}\|\widetilde{\Lambda}^{i}(0)\|\leqslant c^{*}\big(K_{1}^{*}\big)^{2}e^{-2\lambda^{*}t}\leqslant K_{2}^{*}e^{-2\lambda^{*}t}

, we define the block-diagonal matrix

for all

and

, with

being the solutions to (
11
). Next, we provide an estimate for the norm of the matrix exponential

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix

2.2

:

(47)

\big\|e^{\int_{t}^{s}\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(r)dr}\big\|\leqslant K^{(N)}e^{-\lambda^{(N)}(s-t)},\quad\forall 0\leqslant t\leqslant s\leqslant T,

where
K^{(N)}
and
\lambda^{(N)}
are defined in (
16
). As a consequence, we obtain the corresponding estimate for the block-diagonal matrix exponential:

(48)

\big\|e^{\int_{t}^{s}\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(r)dr}\big\|\leqslant K^{(N)}e^{-\lambda^{(N)}(s-t)},\quad\forall 0\leqslant t\leqslant s\leqslant T.

If in addition, Assumption
2.4
holds, then there exists a positive constant
K_{3}^{*}
, independent of
t,s,T
, and
N
, such that

(49)

\sup_{N}\sup_{i\in[N]}\big\|e^{\int_{t}^{s}\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(r)dr}\big\|\leqslant K_{3}^{*}e^{-\lambda^{*}(s-t)},\quad\forall 0\leqslant t\leqslant s\leqslant T,

where
\lambda^{*}
is from the estimate (
43
). Consequently, the corresponding estimate for the block-diagonal matrix exponential holds:

(50)

\big\|e^{\int_{t}^{s}\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(r)dr}\big\|\leqslant K_{3}^{*}e^{-\lambda^{*}(s-t)},\quad\forall 0\leqslant t\leqslant s\leqslant T

for all
N\geqslant 2
.

Proof.

From the estimate (
15
) in Lemma
2.1
, for each
i\in[N]
, there exist positive constants
K_{\Lambda^{i}}
and
\lambda_{\Lambda^{i}}
, independent of
t
and
T
, such that

\big\|\Lambda_{T}^{i}(t)-\Lambda^{i}\big\|\leqslant K_{\Lambda^{i}}e^{-\lambda_{\Lambda^{i}}(T-t)},\quad\forall 0\leqslant t\leqslant T.

Thus, applying the estimate (
14
),
for each
i\in[N]
, we derive that

\displaystyle\big\|e^{\int_{t}^{s}(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(r))dr}\big\|

\displaystyle=\big\|e^{\int_{t}^{s}(R^{i})^{-1}(\Lambda^{i}-\Lambda_{T}^{i}(r))dr}e^{(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i})(s-t)}\big\|

\displaystyle\leqslant K_{i}e^{-\lambda_{i}(s-t)}\big\|e^{\int_{t}^{s}(R^{i})^{-1}(\Lambda^{i}-\Lambda_{T}^{i}(r))dr}\big\|

\displaystyle\leqslant K_{i}e^{-\lambda_{i}(s-t)}e^{\|(R^{i})^{-1}\|\int_{t}^{s}K_{\Lambda^{i}}e^{-\lambda_{\Lambda^{i}}(T-r)}dr}

\displaystyle\leqslant K_{i}e^{\frac{K_{\Lambda^{i}}}{\lambda_{\Lambda^{i}}}\|(R^{i})^{-1}\|}e^{-\lambda_{i}(s-t)}

for all
0\leqslant t\leqslant s\leqslant T
. By the definition of
K^{(N)}
and
\lambda^{(N)}
in (
16
),
we conclude the estimate (
47
).
Recalling the definition of
\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}
in (
46
) and
\bar{A}^{i}_{T}(t):=(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t))^{\top}
for all
i\in[N]
and
t\in[0,T]
, the proof of the desired estimate (
48
) is thus complete.

Using a similar argument as above, together with the results from Lemma
2.3
, and noting that
R^{i}\geqslant\beta_{*}I_{d}
for all
i\in[N]
and
N\geqslant 2
, we may choose the constant
K_{3}^{*}=:K_{1}^{*}e^{\frac{K_{2}^{*}}{2\beta_{*}\lambda^{*}}}
such that

\sup_{N}\sup_{i\in[N]}\big\|e^{\int_{t}^{s}(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(r))dr}\big\|\leqslant K_{3}^{*}e^{-\lambda^{*}(s-t)}

for all
0\leqslant t\leqslant s\leqslant T
. This completes the proof of estimate (
49
). Then, the estimate (
50
) follows directly from the definition of
\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}
in (
46
) and
\bar{A}^{i}_{T}(t):=(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t))^{\top}
for all
i\in[N]
and
t\in[0,T]
.
∎

4.
Proof of Proposition
2.1
: Solvability of the finite-horizon game

In this section, we provide a step-by-step proof of Proposition
2.1
. We begin in Section
4.1
by establishing the unique solvability of the Riccati system (
9
), thereby proving part (i) of the proposition. Building on this result, Section
4.2
addresses the unique solvability of the finite-horizon HJB-FP system (
6
) in the form of (
7
). We then derive the equilibrium strategy and the corresponding equilibrium state trajectory for each player, thus completing the proof of part (ii). Finally, in Section
4.3
, we characterize the value function for each player in the finite-horizon game, which concludes the proof of part (iii).

4.1.
Proof of part (i) of Proposition
2.1

We now establish the solvability of the Riccati system of equations (
9
), which arises in the analysis of the finite-horizon game (
3
), under Assumptions
2.2
and
2.3
. Our analysis follows the approach outlined in Remark
2.4
.

Throughout the following analysis in this section, we use
K
and
\lambda
to denote two generic positive constants that are independent of
t
and
T
, and may vary from line to line. When necessary, we write
K(N)
to emphasize the dependence of the constant
K
on the number of players
N
.

Recall that we proved the unique solvability of
\Lambda^{i}_{T}
in (
11
) for each
i\in[N]
in Lemma
2.1
. Next, given that
\Lambda^{i}_{T}
is the solution to (
11
) for each
i\in[N]
, we proceed to establish the unique solvability of
\Sigma^{i}_{T}
in the Riccati system of ODEs (
9
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let Assumption
2.2
hold and
\Lambda^{i}_{T}
be the solution to (
11
). Then, for each
i\in[N]
, the ODE

(51)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)+2\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)+2\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)\right)=0,\\
\displaystyle\Sigma^{i}_{T}(0)=\Sigma^{i}_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++}\end{cases}

admits a unique positive definition
\Sigma^{i}_{T}\in C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++})
.

Proof.

Taking the transpose of on both sides of (
51
), we obtain the following equation:

\frac{d}{dt}\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)+2\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)+2\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)\right)^{\top}\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)=0,

which implies the symmetry condition

\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)\right)=\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)\right)^{\top}\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t).

Recall that
\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(t):=(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t))^{\top}
for all
i\in[N]
and
t\in[0,T]
, the ODE for
\Sigma^{i}_{T}
can be rewritten as

\frac{d}{dt}\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)+2\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)\varsigma^{i}\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)+\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)(\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(t))^{\top}+\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(t)\Sigma^{i}_{T}(t)=0,\quad\Sigma^{i}_{T}(0)=\Sigma^{i}_{0}.

Define
\bar{\Sigma}^{i}_{T}(t)=\Sigma^{i}_{T}(T-t)
. We derive the backward form of the equation satisfied by
\bar{\Sigma}^{i}_{T}
as follows:

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bar{\Sigma}^{i}_{T}(t)-\bar{\Sigma}^{i}_{T}(t)(\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(t))^{\top}-\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(t)\bar{\Sigma}^{i}_{T}(t)-2\bar{\Sigma}^{i}_{T}(t)\varsigma^{i}\bar{\Sigma}^{i}_{T}(t)=0,\\
\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}^{i}_{T}(T)=\Sigma^{i}_{0}.\end{cases}

Since
\varsigma^{i}>0
and
\Sigma_{0}^{i}>0
for each
i\in[N]
, it follows from Theorem 7.2 in Chapter 6 of
[
66
]
that the backward equation for
\bar{\Sigma}^{i}_{T}
admits a unique solution in
C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++})
. Consequently, the original forward equation (
51
) also admits a unique solution in
C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++})
.
∎

In what follows, we focus on the proof of the unique solvability of the subsystem in (
9
) governing
\{\rho^{i}_{T},\mu^{i}_{T}:i\in[N]\}
. This system corresponds to the third and fourth lines in (
9
). It is important to note that this subsystem forms a fully coupled forward-backward system of ODEs, whose solvability is inherently nontrivial and requires a careful and rigorous analysis.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let Assumptions
2.2
and
2.3
hold, and let
\Lambda^{i}_{T}
be the solution to (
11
) for each
i\in[N]
. Then, there exists a unique solution
\{\rho^{i}_{T},\mu^{i}_{T}:i\in[N]\}
to the following fully coupled forward backward system of ODEs

(52)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho^{i}_{T}(t)+\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)\right)^{\top}\rho^{i}_{T}(t)+2F_{1}^{i}(\bm{\mu}_{T}^{-i}(t))=0,\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mu^{i}_{T}(t)-\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)\right)\mu^{i}_{T}(t)+(R^{i})^{-1}\rho^{i}_{T}(t)=0,\\
\displaystyle\rho_{T}^{i}(T)=0\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\mu_{T}^{i}(0)=\mu_{0}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

where

F_{1}^{i}(\bm{\mu}_{T}^{-i}(t))=-Q_{ii}^{i}\bar{x}_{i}^{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}Q_{ij}^{i}\big(\mu^{j}_{T}(t)-\bar{x}_{i}^{j}\big).

Proof.

The proof consists of two parts: the existence and uniqueness of the solution.

Existence.

To obtain the solvability of the system of equations (
52
), we apply the Leray–Schauder fixed point theorem: Let
\Psi
be a continuous and compact mapping from a Banach space
\mathbb{X}
to itself such that the set

\mathcal{S}=\{\check{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathbb{X}:\check{\bm{\mu}}=\ell\Psi(\check{\bm{\mu}})\hbox{ for some }0\leqslant\ell\leqslant 1\}

is bounded, then
\Psi
has a fixed point. For simplicity of notation, we denote the following vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
:

\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t):=\begin{bmatrix}\check{\rho}_{T}^{1}(t)\\
\vskip 4.0pt\vdots\\
\vskip 4.0pt\check{\rho}_{T}^{N}(t)\end{bmatrix},\quad\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t):=\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 4.0pt\check{\mu}_{T}^{1}(t)\\
\vskip 4.0pt\vdots\\
\vskip 4.0pt\check{\mu}_{T}^{N}(t)\end{bmatrix},

and we define a mapping
\bm{\Psi}:C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{Nd})\to C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{Nd})
as follows:

\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{T}=\bm{\Psi}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}):=\bm{\Psi}_{2}\circ\bm{\Psi}_{1}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}).

In the above,

(i)

\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}=\bm{\Psi}_{1}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T})
, where
\check{\rho}_{T}^{i}=\Psi_{1}^{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T})
is the solution to

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\check{\rho}^{i}_{T}(t)+\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)\right)^{\top}\check{\rho}^{i}_{T}(t)+2F_{1}^{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}^{-i}(t))=0,\\
\check{\rho}_{T}^{i}(T)=0,\end{cases}

with a given
\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}=(\check{\mu}_{T}^{1},\dots,\check{\mu}_{T}^{N})\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{Nd})
for all
i\in[N]
;

(ii)

\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{T}=\bm{\Psi}_{2}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T})
, where
\widetilde{\mu}_{T}^{i}=\Psi_{2}^{i}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T})
is the solution to

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{\mu}_{T}^{i}(t)-\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)\right)\widetilde{\mu}_{T}^{i}(t)+(R^{i})^{-1}\check{\rho}^{i}_{T}(t)=0,\\
\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{T}^{i}(0)=\mu_{0}^{i},\end{cases}

with a given
\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}=(\check{\rho}_{T}^{1},\dots,\check{\rho}_{T}^{N})\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{Nd})
for all
i\in[N]
.

Recall that
\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(t):=(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t))^{\top}
for all
i\in[N]
and
t\in[0,T]
. Then, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\check{\rho}^{i}_{T}(t)+\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(t)\check{\rho}^{i}_{T}(t)+2F_{1}^{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}^{-i}(t))=0,\quad\check{\rho}^{i}_{T}(T)=0,

which yields the explicit representation

(53)

\displaystyle\check{\rho}^{i}_{T}(t)=\Psi_{1}^{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t))=2\int_{t}^{T}e^{\int_{t}^{s}\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(r)dr}F_{1}^{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}^{-i}(s))ds,\quad\forall t\in[0,T]

for all
i\in[N]
. Thus,
\check{\rho}_{T}^{i}\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
for a given
\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{Nd})
. Similarly, for a given
\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{Nd})
, the function
\widetilde{\mu}_{T}^{i}
satisfies

\widetilde{\mu}_{T}^{i}(t)=\Psi_{2}^{i}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t))=e^{\int_{0}^{t}(\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(s))^{\top}ds}\mu_{0}^{i}-\int_{0}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}(\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(r))^{\top}dr}(R^{i})^{-1}\check{\rho}_{T}^{i}(s)ds,\quad\forall t\in[0,T]

for all
i\in[N]
, which implies that
\widetilde{\mu}_{T}^{i}\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

We now outline our approach to establishing the existence of a solution in two steps as follows.

Step 1
: First, we show that
\bm{\Psi}
is a continuous and compact mapping from
\mathbb{X}:=C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{Nd})
to
itself.
Let
\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}=(\check{\mu}_{T}^{1},\dots,\check{\mu}_{T}^{N})
and
\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}=(\check{\nu}_{T}^{1},\dots,\check{\nu}_{T}^{N})
be two elements in
\mathbb{X}
. Then, by (
53
), for each
i\in[N]
,

\Psi_{1}^{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t))-\Psi_{1}^{i}(\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}(t))=2\int_{t}^{T}e^{\int_{t}^{s}\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(r)dr}\big(F^{i}_{1}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}^{-i}(s))-F^{i}_{1}(\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}^{-i}(s))\big)ds,

where

F^{i}_{1}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}^{-i}(s))-F^{i}_{1}(\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}^{-i}(s))=\sum_{j\neq i}Q_{ij}^{i}\left(\check{\mu}_{T}^{j}(s)-\check{\nu}_{T}^{j}(s)\right).

From the estimate (
47
) in Lemma
3.1
, there exist some positive constants
K
and
\lambda
, independent of
t,s
, and
T
, such that

\big\|e^{\int_{t}^{s}\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(r)dr}\big\|\leqslant Ke^{-\lambda(s-t)},\quad\forall 0\leqslant t\leqslant s\leqslant T.

Hence, we obtain the following inequalities

\displaystyle\big|\Psi_{1}^{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t))-\Psi_{1}^{i}(\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}(t))\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant K\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\lambda(s-t)}\sqrt{N}\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big|\check{\mu}_{T}^{j}(s)-\check{\nu}_{T}^{j}(s)\big|^{2}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}ds

\displaystyle\leqslant K(N)\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(s)-\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}(s)|\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\lambda(s-t)}ds

\displaystyle\leqslant K(N)d(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T},\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}),

where
K(N)
is used to emphasize the dependence of the constant
K
on the number of players
N
, and
d(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T},\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}):=\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(s)-\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}(s)|
denotes the uniform metric on the space
\mathbb{X}
. Therefore, it is cleat that

d(\bm{\Psi}_{1}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}),\bm{\Psi}_{1}(\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}))=\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big|\Psi_{1}^{j}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t))-\Psi_{1}^{j}(\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}(t))\big|^{2}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant K(N)d(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T},\check{\bm{\nu}}_{T}),

which yields that
\bm{\Psi}_{1}
is a Lipschitz continuous mapping from
\mathbb{X}
to
C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{Nd})\subset\mathbb{X}
.

Next, with the given of
\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{j}=(\check{\rho}^{j1}_{T},\dots,\check{\rho}_{T}^{jN})
in
\mathbb{X}
for
j=1,2
, we have

\widetilde{\mu}_{T}^{1i}(t)-\widetilde{\mu}_{T}^{2i}(t):=\Psi_{2}^{i}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{1}(t))-\Psi_{2}^{i}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{2}(t))=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}(\bar{A}_{T}^{i}(r))^{\top}dr}(R^{i})^{-1}\big(\check{\rho}_{T}^{2i}(s)-\check{\rho}_{T}^{1i}(s)\big)ds.

Using the estimate (
47
) again, we obtain

\displaystyle\big|\Psi_{2}^{i}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{1}(t))-\Psi_{2}^{i}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{2}(t))\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant K\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\big|\check{\rho}_{T}^{2i}(s)-\check{\rho}_{T}^{1i}(s)\big|ds

\displaystyle\leqslant K\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\big|\check{\rho}_{T}^{2i}(s)-\check{\rho}_{T}^{1i}(s)\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant Kd(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{1},\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{2}).

for some positive
K
and
\lambda
. Thus, by a similar argument, we derive

\displaystyle d\big(\bm{\Psi}_{2}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{1}),\bm{\Psi}_{2}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{2})\big)

\displaystyle=\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big|\Psi_{2}^{j}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{1}(t))-\Psi_{2}^{j}(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{2}(t))\big|^{2}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant K(N)d\big(\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{1},\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}^{2}\big),

which shows that
\bm{\Psi}_{2}
is a Lipschitz continuous mapping from
\mathbb{X}
to
C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{Nd})\subset\mathbb{X}
. Therefore, combining the results above, we conclude that
\bm{\Psi}=\bm{\Psi}_{2}\circ\bm{\Psi}_{1}
is a Lipschitz mapping from
\mathbb{X}
to itself, which yields that
\bm{\Psi}
is a continuous and compact mapping from
\mathbb{X}
to
\mathbb{X}
.

Step 2:
Next, we prove that the set

\mathcal{S}=\{\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}\in\mathbb{X}:\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}=\ell\bm{\Psi}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T})\hbox{ for some }0\leqslant\ell\leqslant 1\}

is bounded. Fix
\ell\in[0,1]
, the identity
\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}=\ell\bm{\Psi}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T})=\ell\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{T}
implies that
(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T},\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T})
satisfies

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\check{\rho}^{i}_{T}(t)+\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)\right)^{\top}\check{\rho}^{i}_{T}(t)+2F_{1}^{i}(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}^{-i}(t))=0,\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\check{\mu}^{i}_{T}(t)-\left(A^{i}-(R^{i})^{-1}\Lambda^{i}_{T}(t)\right)\check{\mu}^{i}_{T}(t)+\ell(R^{i})^{-1}\check{\rho}^{i}_{T}(t)=0,\\
\displaystyle\check{\rho}_{T}^{i}(T)=0,\,\check{\mu}_{T}^{i}(0)=\ell\mu_{0}^{i}.\end{cases}

We then introduce the vector
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
, defined as follows:

(54)

\bm{q}:=\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 4.0pt\sum_{j=1}^{N}Q_{1j}^{1}\bar{x}_{1}^{j}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\sum_{j=1}^{N}Q_{2j}^{2}\bar{x}_{2}^{j}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\vdots\\
\vskip 4.0pt\sum_{j=1}^{N}Q_{Nj}^{N}\bar{x}_{N}^{j}\end{bmatrix}.

Then, the above system of equations can be written as

(55)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)=-\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(t)\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)-2\bm{Q}\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)+2\bm{q},\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)=(\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(t))^{\top}\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)-\ell\bm{R}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t),\\
\displaystyle\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(T)=0,\,\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)=\ell\bm{\mu}_{0},\end{cases}

where
\bm{\mu}_{0}=(\mu_{0}^{1},\dots,\mu_{0}^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
,
\bm{Q}
and
\bm{R}
are defined in (
17
), and
\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(t)
is given in (
46
). If
\ell=0
, the boundedness of
(\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T},\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T})
to (
55
) follows directly from the estimate (
48
) in Lemma
3.1
. So, in the following, we consider the case
\ell\in(0,1]
. In addition, to establish the boundedness of
\mathcal{S}
, it suffices to consider the regime where
|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}|
is sufficiently large. From the system of equations (
55
), it is clear that

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dt}\langle\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t),\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\{\langle(\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(t))^{\top}\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)-\ell\bm{R}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t),\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)\rangle+\langle\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t),-\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(t)\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)-2\bm{Q}\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)+2\bm{q}\rangle\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\ell\bm{R}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t),\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)\rangle+\langle 2\bm{Q}\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t),\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)\rangle-\langle 2\bm{q},\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)\rangle.

Under Assumption
2.3
, it is evident that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(56)

\langle 2\bm{Q}v,v\rangle-\langle 2\bm{q},v\rangle\geqslant-\gamma|v|^{2},\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}\text{ with }|v|>C

for some constant
\gamma\in(0,\frac{(\lambda^{(N)})^{2}}{2(K^{(N)})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}\lambda_{\bm{R},min})
. Thus, applying the inequality (
56
), and integrating both sides of the above identity over the interval
[0,T]
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\ell\langle\bm{R}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t),\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)\rangle dt\leqslant\langle\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0),\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(0)\rangle-\langle\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(T),\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(T)\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}\gamma|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)|^{2}dt.

From the second equation in the system (
55
), the semi-explicit representation of
\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}
in terms of
\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}
is given by

\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)=e^{\int_{0}^{t}(\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(s))^{\top}ds}\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)-\int_{0}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}(\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(r))^{\top}dr}\ell\bm{R}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(s)ds.

It follows from the estimate (
48
) that

(57)

\displaystyle|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\Big\|e^{\int_{0}^{t}(\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(s))^{\top}ds}\Big\|^{2}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|^{2}+2\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}(\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(r))^{\top}dr}\ell\bm{R}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(s)ds\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\big(K^{(N)}\big)^{2}e^{-2\lambda^{(N)}t}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|^{2}+2\big(K^{(N)}\big)^{2}\ell^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda^{(N)}(t-s)}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(s)ds\Big|^{2}

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Integrating both sides over
[0,T]
, we deduce

\int_{0}^{T}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)|^{2}dt\leqslant\frac{\big(K^{(N)}\big)^{2}}{\lambda^{(N)}}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|^{2}+2\big(K^{(N)}\big)^{2}\ell^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda^{(N)}(t-s)}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(s)ds\Big|^{2}dt.

Thus, as
\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(T)=0
and
0<\ell\leqslant 1
, we derive the following inequality

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{R}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t),\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)\rangle dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\ell}\langle\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0),\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(0)\rangle+\frac{\gamma\big(K^{(N)}\big)^{2}}{\ell\lambda^{(N)}}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|^{2}

\displaystyle+2\gamma\big(K^{(N)}\big)^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda^{(N)}(t-s)}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(s)ds\Big|^{2}dt.

Next, for any fixed
\lambda>0
, we denote

\bm{g}_{\lambda}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(s)ds=e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(s)ds

and

\|\bm{g}_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}([0,T])}:=\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|\bm{g}_{\lambda}(t)|^{2}dt\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Since

\bm{g}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)=-\lambda\bm{g}_{\lambda}(t)+\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)
,
multiplying both sides by
(\bm{g}_{\lambda}(t))^{\top}
and integrating over
[0,T]
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}(\bm{g}_{\lambda}(t))^{\top}\bm{g}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)dt+\lambda\int_{0}^{T}|\bm{g}_{\lambda}(t)|^{2}dt=\int_{0}^{T}(\bm{g}_{\lambda}(t))^{\top}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)dt.

It is clear that

\int_{0}^{T}(\bm{g}_{\lambda}(t))^{\top}\bm{g}_{\lambda}^{\prime}(t)dt=\frac{1}{2}|\bm{g}_{\lambda}(T)|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\bm{g}_{\lambda}(0)|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}|\bm{g}_{\lambda}(T)|^{2}

since
\bm{g}_{\lambda}(0)=0
. Thus, we have

\lambda\|\bm{g}_{\lambda}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T])}=\lambda\int_{0}^{T}|\bm{g}_{\lambda}(t)|^{2}dt\leqslant\int_{0}^{T}(\bm{g}_{\lambda}(t))^{\top}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)dt\leqslant\|\bm{g}_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}([0,T])}\|\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}\|_{L^{2}([0,T])}

by Hölder’s inequality, which yields that
\|\bm{g}_{\lambda}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T])}\leqslant\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\|\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T])}
, i.e.,

(58)

\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(s)ds\Big|^{2}dt\leqslant\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}|\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)|^{2}dt.

By using the inequality (
58
), we derive

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{R}\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t),\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)\rangle dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\ell}\langle\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0),\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(0)\rangle+\frac{\gamma\big(K^{(N)}\big)^{2}}{\ell\lambda^{(N)}}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2\gamma\big(K^{(N)}\big)^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}{(\lambda^{(N)})^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}|\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)|^{2}dt.

Under Assumption
2.3
, we have
\gamma<\frac{(\lambda^{(N)})^{2}}{2(K^{(N)})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}\lambda_{\bm{R},min}
, so that the last term on the right-hand side of the above inequality can be absorbed by the left-hand side, which follows that

\int_{0}^{T}|\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)|^{2}dt\leqslant K\big(|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)||\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(0)|+|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|^{2}\big)

for some positive constant
K
. Returning to the estimate (
57
) and applying Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)|^{2}\leqslant Ke^{-2\lambda t}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|^{2}+K(1-e^{-2\lambda t})\int_{0}^{t}|\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(s)|^{2}ds,\quad\forall t\in[0,T]

for some
K,\lambda>0
. Taking the supremum over
t\in[0,T]
, we conclude

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)|^{2}\leqslant K\big(|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)||\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(0)|+|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|^{2}\big).

Next, from the first equation in the system (
55
) along with the terminal condition
\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(T)=0
, the semi-explicit form of
\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}
in terms of
\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}
is

\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)=\int_{t}^{T}e^{\int_{t}^{s}\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(r)dr}(2\bm{Q}\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(s)-2\bm{q})ds,

which gives that

|\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(0)|\leqslant\int_{0}^{T}\Big\|e^{\int_{0}^{s}\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(r)dr}\Big\|\big(2\|\bm{Q}\||\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(s)|+2|\bm{q}|\big)ds\leqslant K\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)|+1\Big)

by using the exponential decay estimate in (
48
) from Lemma
3.1
.
Hence, by Young’s inequality, we obtain the uniform boundedness of
\check{\bm{\mu}}
on
[0,T]
:

(59)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)|^{2}\leqslant K\big(|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|+|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|^{2}\big),

which concludes that
\mathcal{S}
is a bounded set. Therefore, by the Leray–Schauder fixed point theorem,
\bm{\Psi}
has a fixed point, i.e., the system (
52
) admits a solution. Moreover, using the semi-explicit representation of
\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}
once again, for all
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\displaystyle|\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}(t)|

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\Big\|e^{\int_{t}^{s}\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(r)dr}\Big\|\big(2\|\bm{Q}\||\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(s)|+2|\bm{q}|\big)ds

\displaystyle\leqslant K\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(t)|+1\Big)\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\lambda(s-t)}ds

\displaystyle\leqslant K\big(1+|\check{\bm{\mu}}_{T}(0)|\big),

which show that
\check{\bm{\rho}}_{T}
is also uniformly bounded on
[0,T]
.

Uniqueness.
Finally, we prove the uniqueness of the solution to the system of equations (
52
). Note that, for a given collection
\{\Lambda^{i}_{T}:i\in[N]\}
, the equations for
\{\mu^{i}_{T},\rho^{i}_{T}:i\in[N]\}
form a system of linear ODEs. Suppose there exist two solutions
(\bm{\rho}_{T}^{1},\bm{\mu}_{T}^{1})
and
(\bm{\rho}_{T}^{2},\bm{\mu}_{T}^{2})
to (

). Then, their difference satisfies the system:

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big(\bm{\rho}_{T}^{1}(t)-\bm{\rho}_{T}^{2}(t)\big)=-\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(t)\big(\bm{\rho}_{T}^{1}(t)-\bm{\rho}_{T}^{2}(t)\big)-2\bm{Q}\big(\bm{\mu}_{T}^{1}(t)-\bm{\mu}_{T}^{2}(t)\big),\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big(\bm{\mu}_{T}^{1}(t)-\bm{\mu}_{T}^{2}(t)\big)=(\bar{\bm{A}}_{T}(t))^{\top}\big(\bm{\mu}_{T}^{1}(t)-\bm{\mu}_{T}^{2}(t)\big)-\bm{R}\big(\bm{\rho}_{T}^{1}(t)-\bm{\rho}_{T}^{2}(t)\big),\\
\displaystyle\bm{\rho}_{T}^{1}(T)-\bm{\rho}_{T}^{2}(T)=0,\ \bm{\mu}_{T}^{1}(0)-\bm{\mu}_{T}^{2}(0)=0.\end{cases}

By the similar argument for the boundedness of
\mathcal{S}
in the above and the result in (

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big|\bm{\mu}_{T}^{1}(t)-\bm{\mu}_{T}^{2}(t)\big|^{2}\leqslant K\big(\big|\bm{\mu}_{T}^{1}(0)-\bm{\mu}_{T}^{2}(0)\big|+\big|\bm{\mu}_{T}^{1}(0)-\bm{\mu}_{T}^{2}(0)\big|^{2}\big)=0,

which yields
\bm{\mu}_{T}^{1}(t)=\bm{\mu}_{T}^{2}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
. From the first equation in the above system of equations, we also obtain
\bm{\rho}_{T}^{1}(t)=\bm{\rho}_{T}^{2}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Thus, the proof of uniqueness of solution to (

First, we establish an estimate for the solution to the homogeneous system (
66
) in Lemma
5.2
.

•

Next, by incorporating an additional linear term
\bm{B}^{N}(t)
in the ODE satisfied by
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}
, we derive an estimate for the perturbed system (
78
) in Lemma
5.3
.

•

Then, using the results of Lemmas
5.2
and
5.3
, we obtain a uniform estimate for the full system (

) in Lemma

.

•

Finally, combining the above results and applying Assumption

Lemma 5.2
.

Let Assumptions
2.2
,
2.4
, and
2.5
hold. There exist some positive constants
K
and
\lambda
, independent of
t,T
, and
N
, such that if
(\widehat{\bm{\rho}},\widehat{\bm{\mu}})
is a solution to the homogeneous system of equations

(66)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)=-\bar{\bm{A}}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)-2\bm{Q}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t),\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)=(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)-\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t),\\
\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)=-\bm{\rho},\,\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)=\bm{\mu}_{0}-\bm{\mu},\end{cases}

then the following estimate holds for all
N\geqslant 2
:

|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|+|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|\big)\big(e^{-\lambda t}+e^{-\lambda(T-t)}\big),\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Proof.

From the system of equations (
66
), we observe that

(67)

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dt}\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\big\{\langle(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)-\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle+\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t),-\bar{\bm{A}}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)-2\bm{Q}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)\rangle\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle+\langle 2\bm{Q}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)\rangle,

Under Assumption
2.5
, for the specified constant
\gamma\in(0,\bar{\gamma})
, it is straightforward that

(68)

\langle 2\bm{Q}v,v\rangle\geqslant-\gamma|v|^{2},\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}.

Taking integration from
0
to
T
on both sides of (
67
) and using the inequality (
68
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle dt\leqslant\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(0)\rangle-\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(T),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)\rangle+\gamma\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}dt.

By the integrating factor method and from the second equation in (
66
),

\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)=e^{(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}t}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)-\int_{0}^{t}e^{(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}(t-s)}\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds.

It follows from the estimation (
65
) that, for all
t\in[0,T]
,

(69)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\Big\|e^{(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}t}\Big\|^{2}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}+2\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}(t-s)}\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}e^{-2\lambda^{*}t}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}+2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda^{*}(t-s)}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}.

By using the inequality (
58
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle dt

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(0)\rangle-\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(T),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)\rangle+\frac{\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}}{\lambda^{*}}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}+2\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda^{*}(t-s)}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(0)\rangle-\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(T),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)\rangle+\frac{\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}}{\lambda^{*}}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}+\frac{2\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}{(\lambda^{*})^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}dt.

Recall that
\lambda_{\bm{R},min}
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of
\bm{R}
.
Then, by Assumption
2.5
and the proof of Lemma
2.3
, as
\bm{R}
is positive definite and
\gamma<\bar{\gamma}<\frac{(\lambda^{*})^{2}}{2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}\lambda_{\bm{R},min}
, we know that

\frac{2\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}{(\lambda^{*})^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}dt<\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle dt,

and it gives

(70)

\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}dt\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)||\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(0)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(T)||\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}\big)

for some constant
K>0
. From the estimate in (
69
), and by the Hölder’s inequality, there exist some positive
K
and
\lambda
such that

\displaystyle|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant Ke^{-2\lambda t}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}+\frac{K}{\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})\int_{0}^{t}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)||\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(0)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(T)||\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}\big)

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Taking supremum over
t
on
[0,T]
and applying Young’s inequality, and modifying
K
if necessary, we derive that

(71)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)||\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(0)|+|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}\big).

Next, from the first equation in the system (
66
), the semi-explicit form of
\widehat{\bm{\rho}}
in terms of
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}
is given by

(72)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)=e^{\bar{\bm{A}}(T-t)}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)+\int_{t}^{T}2e^{\bar{\bm{A}}(s-t)}\bm{Q}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(s)ds.

Plugging
t=0
, by estimate (
65
) again and since
\|\bm{Q}\|
is uniformly bounded above from Assumption
2.5
, we have the following inequalities

\displaystyle|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(0)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\big\|e^{\bar{\bm{A}}T}\big\||\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|+\int_{0}^{T}2\big\|e^{\bar{\bm{A}}s}\big\|\|\bm{Q}\||\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(s)|ds

\displaystyle\leqslant Ke^{-\lambda T}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|+K\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda s}ds

\displaystyle\leqslant Ke^{-\lambda T}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|+K\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|

for some positive constants
K
and
\lambda
, independent of
t,T
, and
N
. Then, by using Young’s inequality again for (
71
), we conclude that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}\big).

With the similar argument as above, it is clear from (
72
) that

\displaystyle|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}\leqslant 2\big\|e^{\bar{\bm{A}}(T-t)}\big\|^{2}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|^{2}+2\Big|\int_{t}^{T}2e^{\bar{\bm{A}}(s-t)}\bm{Q}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}\leqslant K|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|^{2}+K\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}

for all
t\in[0,T]
, which yields that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big(|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}\big)\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}\big).

By using the inequalities (
69
), (
70
), and (
58
),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant K|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2\lambda t}dt+K\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant K|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}+\frac{K}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}\big).

The same bound (with potentially different
K
) holds for
\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}dt
, thus

(73)

\int_{0}^{T}\big(|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}\big)dt\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}\big).

Next, we show that there exists a
\tau>0
such that for all
T>2\tau
,

(74)

|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\big(|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}\big),\quad\forall t\in[\tau,T-\tau].

Fix a
\tau\in(0,\frac{T}{2})
. Using the inequality (
73
), by the mean value theorem for the integral, we obtain that there exist some
\xi_{\tau}\in(0,\frac{\tau}{2})
and
\eta_{\tau}\in(T-\frac{\tau}{2},T)
such that

|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(\xi_{\tau})|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau})|^{2}\leqslant\frac{K}{\tau}M^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(\eta_{\tau})|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\eta_{\tau})|^{2}\leqslant\frac{K}{\tau}M^{2},

where
M^{2}:=|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|^{2}
. The constant
K
appearing here and throughout the remainder of the proof is independent of
\tau
, as well as of
t
,
T
, and
N
. Recall that the semi-explicit form of
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}
in terms of
\widehat{\bm{\rho}}
is given by

\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)=e^{(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}(t-\xi_{\tau})}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau})-\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{t}e^{(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}(t-s)}\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds

for
t\geqslant\xi_{\tau}
, thus

(75)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}\leqslant 2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}e^{-2\lambda^{*}(t-\xi_{\tau})}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau})|^{2}+2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}\Big|\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{t}e^{-\lambda^{*}(t-s)}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}.

Taking integration from
\xi_{\tau}
to
\eta_{\tau}
, and using the inequality (
58
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{\eta_{\tau}}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau})|^{2}\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{\eta_{\tau}}e^{-2\lambda^{*}(t-\xi_{\tau})}dt+2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{\eta_{\tau}}\Big|\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{t}e^{-\lambda^{*}(t-s)}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(K_{1}^{*})^{2}}{\lambda^{*}}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau})|^{2}+\frac{2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}{(\lambda^{*})^{2}}\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{\eta_{\tau}}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}dt.

Moreover, from (
67
) and (
68
), we derive

\displaystyle\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{\eta_{\tau}}\langle\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau}),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(\xi_{\tau})\rangle-\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\eta_{\tau}),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(\eta_{\tau})\rangle+\gamma\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{\eta_{\tau}}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau}),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(\xi_{\tau})\rangle-\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\eta_{\tau}),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(\eta_{\tau})\rangle+\frac{\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}}{\lambda^{*}}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau})|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}{(\lambda^{*})^{2}}\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{\eta_{\tau}}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}dt.

Since
\bm{R}
is positive definite and
\gamma<\bar{\gamma}<\frac{(\lambda^{*})^{2}}{2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}\lambda_{\bm{R},min}
, we establish the following estimate

\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{\eta_{\tau}}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}dt\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau})|^{2}+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau})||\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(\xi_{\tau})|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\eta_{\tau})||\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(\eta_{\tau})|\big)\leqslant\frac{K}{\tau}M^{2}

for some
K>0
. Thus, by (
75
) and using the Hölder’s inequality, since
\bm{R}
is uniformly bounded from Assumption
2.4
, there exist
K
and
\lambda>0
such that

(76)

|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}\leqslant Ke^{-2\lambda(t-\xi_{\tau})}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\xi_{\tau})|^{2}+K\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{t}e^{-2\lambda(t-s)}ds\int_{\xi_{\tau}}^{t}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant\frac{K}{\tau}M^{2}

for
t\in[\xi_{\tau},\eta_{\tau}]
. From the semi-explicit form of
\widehat{\bm{\rho}}
in terms of
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}
in (
72
) and the assumption that
\|\bm{Q}\|
is uniformly bounded above in Assumption
2.5
, for
t\in[\xi_{\tau},\eta_{\tau}]
, we also derive that

(77)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\big\|e^{\bar{\bm{A}}(\eta_{\tau}-t)}\big\||\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(\eta_{\tau})|+\int_{t}^{\eta_{\tau}}2\big\|e^{\bar{\bm{A}}(s-t)}\big\|\|\bm{Q}\||\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(s)|ds

\displaystyle\leqslant Ke^{-\lambda(\eta_{\tau}-t)}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(\eta_{\tau})|+K\sup_{t\in[\xi_{\tau},\eta_{\tau}]}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|\int_{t}^{\eta_{\tau}}e^{-\lambda(s-t)}ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{K}{\sqrt{\tau}}M.

The above inequality (
77
), together with (
76
), imply (
74
) for a convenient choice of
\tau
. Finally, by iteration of the inequality (
74
), we conclude that there exist some positive constants
K
and
\lambda
, independent of
t,T
, and
N
, such that

|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|+|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|\big)\big(e^{-\lambda t}+e^{-\lambda(T-t)}\big),\quad\forall t\in(0,T).

Moreover, by the uniformly boundedness result of
\widehat{\bm{\rho}}
and
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}
, we complete the proof of the desired result that the following estimate holds uniformly for all
N\geqslant 2
:

|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)|+|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)|\big)\big(e^{-\lambda t}+e^{-\lambda(T-t)}\big),\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Lemma 5.3
.

Let Assumptions
2.2
,
2.4
, and
2.5
hold. Consider a collection of functions
\bm{B}^{N}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
for
N\geqslant 2
. In addition, assume that there exist constants
K_{\bm{B}^{N}}>0
(possibly depending on
N
, but independent of
t
and
T
), and
\check{\lambda}>0
(independent of
t
,
T
, and
N
), such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and all
N\geqslant 2
,

|\bm{B}^{N}(t)|\leqslant K_{\bm{B}^{N}}e^{-\check{\lambda}(T-t)}.

Then, there exist positive constants
K
and
\lambda
, independent of
t,T
, and
N
, such that any solution
(\widehat{\bm{\rho}},\widehat{\bm{\mu}})
for the system of equations

(78)

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)=-\bar{\bm{A}}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)-2\bm{Q}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t),\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)=(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)-\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)-\bm{B}^{N}(t),\\
\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)=0,\,\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)=0,\end{cases}

satisfies the following estimate for all
N\geqslant 2
:

|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|\leqslant KK_{\bm{B^{N}}}\big((1+t)e^{-\lambda t}+(1+T-t)e^{-\lambda(T-t)}\big),\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Proof.

To avoid cumbersome notation throughout the proof, we remove the superscript
N
from
\bm{B}^{N}
. We first prove that the solution
(\widehat{\bm{\rho}},\widehat{\bm{\mu}})
to (
78
) is uniformly bounded. This result can be obtained by a similar argument as Lemma
5.2
. From the system of equations (
78
), it is clear that

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dt}\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\big\{\langle(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)-\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)-\bm{B}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle+\langle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t),-\bar{\bm{A}}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)-2\bm{Q}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)\rangle\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle+\langle 2\bm{Q}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)\rangle+\langle\bm{B}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle,

which gives the following inequality

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle dt\leqslant\gamma\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}dt-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{B}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle dt

by the facts that
\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)=0
and
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)=0
, and the inequality for
\bm{Q}
in (
68
) obtained by Assumption
2.5
. Applying the integrating factor method and from the second equation in (
78
), as
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(0)=0
, we derive

\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}e^{(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}(t-s)}\big(\bm{B}(s)+\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)\big)ds.

It yields the following estimates

(79)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}(t-s)}\bm{B}(s)ds\Big|^{2}+2\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{(\bar{\bm{A}})^{\top}(t-s)}\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}K_{\bm{B}}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\lambda^{*}(t-s)}ds\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\check{\lambda}(T-s)}ds

\displaystyle+2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda^{*}(t-s)}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(K_{1}^{*})^{2}}{2\lambda^{*}\check{\lambda}}K_{\bm{B}}^{2}e^{-2\check{\lambda}(T-t)}+2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda^{*}(t-s)}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}

for all
t\in[0,T]
by applying the estimate (
65
). Using the inequality (
58
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}}{4\lambda^{*}\check{\lambda}^{2}}K_{\bm{B}}^{2}+2\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda^{*}(t-s)}\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)ds\Big|^{2}dt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{B}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle dt

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}}{4\lambda^{*}\check{\lambda}^{2}}K_{\bm{B}}^{2}+\frac{2\gamma(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}{(\lambda^{*})^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}dt-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\bm{B}(t),\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)\rangle dt.

Since
\gamma<\bar{\gamma}<\frac{(\lambda^{*})^{2}}{2(K_{1}^{*})^{2}\|\bm{R}\|^{2}}\lambda_{\bm{R},min}
from Assumption
2.5
,
|\bm{B}(t)|\leqslant K_{\bm{B}}e^{-\check{\lambda}(T-t)}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and
\bm{R}
is positive definite, by Young’s inequality, one can derive that there exists a positive constant
K
such that

\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|^{2}dt\leqslant KK_{\bm{B}}^{2}

for all
N\geqslant 2
. Thus, from the inequality (
79
), and by Hölder’s inequality,

|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}\leqslant KK_{\bm{B}}^{2}e^{-2\check{\lambda}(T-t)}+\frac{K}{2\lambda}\big(1-e^{-2\lambda t}\big)\int_{0}^{t}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)|^{2}ds

for some positive
K

\lambda
. Taking supremum over
t

[0,T]
, we obtain the uniformly upper bound for
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|^{2}\leqslant KK_{\bm{B}}^{2}.

Similarly, from the first equation in system (
78
), the semi-explicit representation of
\widehat{\bm{\rho}}
in terms of
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}
is given by

\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)=\int_{t}^{T}2e^{\bar{\bm{A}}(s-t)}\bm{Q}\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(s)ds

since
\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T)=0
. By the uniformly boundedness of

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|\leqslant K\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|\leqslant KK_{\bm{B}}.

Therefore, we complete the proof that
(\widehat{\bm{\rho}},\widehat{\bm{\mu}})
is uniformly bounded, i.e.,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big(|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|\big)\leqslant KK_{\bm{B}}

for all
N\geqslant 2
.

Next, for
\tau>0
, we set

\zeta(\tau)=\sup_{t,T}\big(|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|\big),

where the supremum is taken over any

. Thus,
\zeta(\tau)

for any
\tau
. Note that the restriction
(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}})
of
(\widehat{\bm{\rho}},\widehat{\bm{\mu}})

can be written as

(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}})=(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{1},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{1})+(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{2},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{2}),

where
(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{1},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{1})
solves the equation (
66
) with the boundary condition
\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{1}(\tau)=\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\tau)
and
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{1}(T-\tau)=\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T-\tau)
, while
(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{2},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{2})

) on the time interval
[\tau,T-\tau]
with homogeneous conditions. From the results in Lemma

, we have, for any

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{1}(t)|+|\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{1}(t)|

\displaystyle\leqslant K\big(|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(T-\tau)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\tau)|\big)\big(e^{-\lambda(t-\tau)}+e^{-\lambda(T-\tau-t)}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant KK_{\bm{B}}\big(e^{-\lambda(t-\tau)}+e^{-\lambda(T-\tau-t)}\big).

Moreover, the restriction of
\bm{B}
to
[\tau,T-\tau]
satisfies

|\bm{B}(t)|\leqslant K_{\bm{B}}e^{-\lambda\tau}e^{-\lambda(T-t-\tau)}
for some positive
\lambda
. So, by the linearity and invariance in time of the equation, we get

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{2}(t)|+|\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{2}(t)|\leqslant e^{-\lambda\tau}\zeta(t-\tau),\quad\forall t\in[\tau,T-\tau],

as we may set
\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{2}(t)=\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t-\tau)
and
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{2}(t)=\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t-\tau)
on
[\tau,T-\tau]
. Plugging together the estimates of
(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{1},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{1})
and
(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{2},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{2})
, we obtain, for any

,

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[t+\tau,T-\tau-t]}\big(|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(s)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(s)|\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[t+\tau,T-\tau-t]}\big\{KK_{\bm{B}}\big(e^{-\lambda(s-\tau)}+e^{-\lambda(T-\tau-s)}\big)+e^{-\lambda\tau}\zeta(s-\tau)\big\}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle KK_{\bm{B}}e^{-\lambda t}+e^{-\lambda\tau}\zeta(t).

Taking supremum over
(\widehat{\bm{\rho}},\widehat{\bm{\mu}})
and multiplying by
e^{\lambda(t+\tau)}
to the above inequality gives

e^{\lambda(t+\tau)}\zeta(t+\tau)\leqslant KK_{\bm{B}}e^{\lambda\tau}+e^{\lambda t}\zeta(t),

from which we infer that
\zeta(t)\leqslant KK_{\bm{B}}(1+t)e^{-\lambda t}
.
By the definition of
\zeta
, this implies the conclusion that

|\widehat{\bm{\rho}}(t)|+|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(t)|\leqslant KK_{\bm{B}}\big((1+t)e^{-\lambda t}+(1+T-t)e^{-\lambda(T-t)}\big)

for all
t\in[0,T]
when choosing
\tau=t
if
t\in[0,T/2]
and
\tau=T-t
otherwise.
∎

Proof.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}(T)=0.\end{cases}

7.
Summary and future outlook

This paper investigates the long-time behavior of equilibrium strategies and state trajectories in a linear quadratic
N
-player stochastic differential game with Gaussian initial data. We analyze the convergence of the finite-horizon game toward its associated ergodic counterpart, with particular emphasis on the turnpike property–a phenomenon where equilibrium strategies and trajectories remain close to their steady-state counterparts over most of the time horizon. We formulate both of the finite-horizon and ergodic problems, derive the corresponding systems HJB-FP equations, and characterize their solutions via systems of Riccati equations. The solvability of these Riccati systems and the resulting characterization of equilibrium strategies, state processes, and value functions are established in both settings, including uniform results under additional structural assumptions.

The main technical contribution lies in proving the unique solvability of the Riccati system of ODEs arising from the finite-horizon game, and establishing exponential convergence estimates between the solutions to the finite-horizon and ergodic Riccati systems. These convergence estimates are then used to demonstrate the convergence of the time-averaged value function and to establish the turnpike properties for the equilibrium pairs of each player. It is important to note that our analysis does not rely on the mean field game limiting framework, which enables a fully uniform treatment with respect to the number of players. As a result, we also derive a uniform turnpike property for the
N
-player game.

A natural direction for future research is to extend the present analysis beyond the linear quadratic framework and investigate the uniform turnpike property for
N
-player games in more general settings. This would require constructing tractable models for the associated ergodic games. Then, one could adapt the analytical tools developed in
[
16
,
25
]
to obtain uniform estimates for the difference between solutions to the finite-horizon and ergodic HJB-FP systems. Another promising extension is to consider high-dimensional stochastic optimal control problems and their associated mean field control formulations. In this context, the analysis may become more tractable, as it avoids the complexities of fully coupled forward-backward systems and allows for more direct methods to establish convergence and turnpike-type properties.
