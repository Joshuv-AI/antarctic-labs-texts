Comprehensive arthroscopic management procedure and autologous microfragmented adipose tissue infiltration in mild-to-moderate glenohumeral osteoarthritis: two-year follow-up outcomes
Authors: Andrea Giorgini, Hajhar Addala, Lisa De Pasquale, Gian Mario Micheloni, Umberto Fregni, Giuseppe Porcellini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate clinical outcomes in patients with mild-to-moderate concentric glenohumeral osteoarthritis (GHOA) treated with arthroscopic surgery according to the Comprehensive Arthroscopic Management (CAM) procedure combined with intra-articular injection of autologous microfragmented adipose tissue (mFAT) (Lipogems). Since there was no CAM-only control group, the study does not assess the independent contribution of mFAT.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 19 patients (mean age 52 years; 17 males, 2 females) with Samilson–Prieto grade 1–3 GHOA were included. All patients underwent arthroscopic CAM surgery followed by intra-articular injection of autologous mFAT. Primary outcomes were assessed using the visual analog scale for pain and the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score at baseline (T0) and at 1 (T1), 3 (T3), 6 (T6), 12 (T12), and 24 months (T24) post-operatively.ResultsSignificant improvements in both visual analog scale and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons scores were observed at 1 month and maintained up to 24 months (P < .05). Patients aged ≤50 years or with grade 1–2 GHOA tended to experience greater clinical improvements. The observed improvements in pain and function exceeded published thresholds for the Minimal Clinically Important Difference and fell below the Patient Acceptable Symptom State, indicating that changes were not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful. No major adverse events occurred, and no patients required conversion to shoulder arthroplasty. The combination of CAM and mFAT was safe and feasible, providing sustained pain relief and functional improvement over a 2-year follow-up.ConclusionThe CAM procedure combined with intra-articular mFAT injection represents a safe and minimally invasive approach associated with durable improvements in pain and shoulder function in patients with mild-to-moderate GHOA. However, the study design does not allow conclusions regarding the specific contribution of mFAT beyond the CAM procedure alone. Further controlled studies are needed to clarify potential additive benefits.

Glenohumeral osteoarthritis (GHOA) affects an estimated 4–26% of the general population.40 Age is the main risk factor, with a prevalence of roughly 20% in people over 60.13,18,19 Radiographic classification systems commonly used to assess GHOA severity include Kellgren-Lawrence, Samilson–Prieto, and Walch.1,15,16,34

Nonoperative treatments typically include physical therapy, oral anti-inflammatory medications, and intra-articular injections of corticosteroids or hyaluronic acid.8 When conservative management fails, surgical intervention should be considered.7 Shoulder arthroplasty remains the treatment of choice for severe GHOA.4,22 However, young patients with mild-to-moderate GHOA and high functional demands present a clinical challenge, as they are at higher risk for complications such as premature implant failure, component dislocation, and periprosthetic fractures.

Arthroscopic débridement has been reported to provide pain relief and functional improvement in selected patients,9,11,17,21,24,31,42,44 particularly those who are poor candidates for joint replacement due to age or activity level. Indications for arthroscopic treatment generally include symptomatic GHOA unresponsive to conservative measures, while severe joint incongruity, large osteophytes, or osteochondral lesions >2 cm are considered contraindications.2,5,9 Patients with advanced osteoarthritis or delayed treatment tend to experience poorer clinical outcomes.2,8,42

The Comprehensive Arthroscopic Management (CAM) procedure, described by Millett et al,24 combines traditional glenohumeral débridement and capsular release with inferior humeral osteoplasty and axillary nerve neurolysis. Additional interventions may include biceps tenotomy or tenodesis, subacromial decompression, and microfracture of focal chondral lesions. Clinical studies have shown that CAM can provide durable pain relief and functional improvement in patients with mild-to-severe GHOA.24,25

Regenerative approaches, particularly the use of autologous microfragmented adipose tissue (mFAT), have shown promise in the treatment of osteoarthritis of other joints, such as the knee. mFAT has been applied either alone or in combination with surgical interventions (eg, arthroscopic débridement, microfractures, osteotomy) and has been suggested to potentially support pain relief and joint function over time.3,6,10,12,14,20,23,28, 29, 30,32,33,35, 36, 37,41,45,46 However, studies investigating mFAT in the context of shoulder osteoarthritis are lacking.

The present study aims to report the 24-month clinical outcomes of patients with mild-to-moderate concentric GHOA treated with the combination of CAM and intra-articular mFAT injection (Lipogems). The primary outcome measures were patient-reported pain and shoulder function, assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS) and the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score. While the study design does not allow determination of the additive effect of mFAT beyond CAM alone, this work provides real-world data on a joint-preserving strategy in a challenging patient population.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective, observational, single-center study included 19 patients (aged 18–80 years) diagnosed with symptomatic concentric GHOA graded 1–3 according to Samilson–Prieto, all with an intact rotator cuff and having failed one or more conservative treatment strategies. Exclusion criteria were age <18 or >80 years, pregnancy, active neoplasm, rotator cuff insufficiency, and severe (grade IV) osteoarthritis.

Eligible patients underwent arthroscopic débridement according to the CAM procedure, followed by intra-articular injection of autologous mFAT harvested from the abdomen and processed using a mechanical microfragmentation device (Lipogems Ortho Kit, Lipogems International SpA, Milan, Italy) in the same operating session. All procedures were performed by a single experienced shoulder arthroscopist.

Imaging assessment

All patients underwent standard pre-operative radiographs (including true anteroposterior, axillary, and scapular Y views) to grade GHOA according to the Samilson–Prieto classification. To evaluate rotator cuff integrity and rule out additional joint pathology, patients also underwent either shoulder ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging, depending on the clinical judgment of the treating surgeon. Post-operatively, radiographs were obtained for all patients immediately after surgery and at 12-month follow-up.

Surgical procedure

All surgeries were performed under general anesthesia combined with interscalene brachial plexus block. Patients were positioned in lateral decubitus with a 30° posterior tilt, the operative limb in 5 kg traction, 70° abduction, and 10° anteposition. A dual sterile field was prepared at the beginning of the procedure, encompassing both the operative shoulder and the abdominal donor site. Standard arthroscopic portals (2 posterior, 1 anterosuperior) were used. Intra-articular evaluation included removal of any free bodies (largest 3 × 5 cm). Débridement included capsular synovitis and joint surface cleaning, inferior osteophyte removal, and axillary nerve neurolysis with a motorized shaver. Anterior and posteroinferior capsular release was performed according to pre-operative planning and intraoperative findings. Long head of the biceps tenotomy was performed in 4 patients; tenodesis was not required. Subacromial decompression with acromioplasty and bursectomy was performed in all patients. Microfractures were performed in 1 patient with grade IV chondropathy (Outerbridge classification).

Adipose tissue harvesting was performed from the abdomen through a small peri-umbilical incision. After subcutaneous infiltration of Klein's solution (250 mL saline, 1 mg adrenaline, and 50 mL 2% lidocaine) using a 17G blunt-tipped, 185-mm cannula, a waiting period of 15 minutes allowed adequate local anesthesia and tissue turgor. Lipoaspiration was then performed using a 13G × 185-mm cannula connected to a 30-mL VacLok syringe under low-pressure suction, yielding approximately 60–80 mL of adipose tissue. The lipoaspirate was processed intraoperatively using the Lipogems device, which mechanically washed, filtered, and microfragmented the tissue, resulting in 5–14 mL (mean 7 mL) of autologous mFAT.

The mFAT was injected slowly into the glenohumeral joint under direct arthroscopic visualization in dry conditions at the end of the CAM procedure, without subsequent placement of a drain. After completion of the surgery, a compressive bandage was applied over the abdominal donor site. No major adverse events were observed. No infections or persistent donor-site morbidities were reported.

Rehabilitation protocol

All patients followed a standardized post-operative rehabilitation protocol, based on discharge instructions and routine clinical practice. The main components were:1.Immobilization (0–15 days): Patients wore a shoulder immobilizer immediately after surgery. A compressive bandage was applied to the abdominal donor site for 7 days.2.Passive mobilization (15–30 days): Passive range-of-motion exercises were initiated from the 15th post-operative day to prevent stiffness.3.Active mobilization (from day 30): Active range-of-motion exercises were started at approximately 1 month post-operatively and progressed according to individual tolerance.4.Strengthening and functional recovery (from 4 to 6 weeks): Progressive strengthening exercises for the rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers were initiated. Gradual return to daily activities and recreational sports was guided by clinical assessment.

Patient adherence was monitored during follow-up visits through clinical assessment and patient-reported compliance. All patients were reported to have adequately followed the protocol.

Variables and patient-reported outcome measures

The primary outcome measures were patient-reported pain and shoulder function, assessed using the VAS and the ASES score at baseline (T0) and at 1 (T1), 3 (T3), 6 (T6), 12 (T12), and 24 months (T24) post-operatively.

Patients were considered for conversion to shoulder arthroplasty in the event of persistent pain and functional limitation unresponsive to conservative or joint-preserving interventions, associated with radiographic progression to severe osteoarthritis (Samilson–Prieto grade 4).

Outcomes were stratified by age (≤50 years, >50 years) and Samilson–Prieto grade (1, 2, 3). The age cutoff of 50 years was selected because it approximated the mean age of the study cohort and allowed comparison between 2 clinically relevant age groups.

For descriptive purposes, VAS scores were categorized as no pain (0), mild (1–3), moderate (4–6), and severe (≥7); ASES scores were categorized as low function (<40), sufficient (40–59), good (60–79), and excellent (≥80).

For secondary outcomes, patients were assessed regarding return to sports, pre-operative expectations, and post-operative satisfaction, all evaluated using 5-level Likert scales. Return to sports was scored as 1 = no, 2 = no for other reasons, 3 = yes with changed sport, 4 = yes with lower performance, and 5 = yes with the same performance. Pre-operative expectations and post-operative satisfaction were rated as 1 = strongly agree, 2 = agree, 3 = neutral, 4 = disagree, and 5 = strongly disagree. These questions may be considered anchor questions, providing contextual reference to interpret changes in the primary outcomes (VAS and ASES scores).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R (version 4.2.3). Categorical variables were summarized by counts and percentages; continuous variables by mean ± standard deviation. Pearson’s chi-square test was used for categorical comparisons. Mixed models for repeated measures were used to analyze longitudinal changes in VAS and ASES, stratified by age and OA grade. Mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney tests were used for comparisons at individual follow-up points. A P value ≤.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen's d for paired samples, comparing baseline (T0) and 24-month (T24) scores. A conservative correlation coefficient (r = 0.5) between repeated measures was assumed, in line with recommendations from the literature for approximating paired effect sizes. Hedges' g was applied to correct for the small sample size, and 95% CIs were calculated using the standard error based on d and n.

Using this approach, VAS decreased from 6.3 ± 2.8 at baseline to 0.8 ± 1.7 at 24 months, corresponding to a very large effect size (Cohen's d ≈ −2.25; Hedges' g ≈ −2.15; 95% CI: -3.00 to −1.31). Similarly, ASES scores increased from 42.8 ± 20.9 to 87.4 ± 14.2, with a very large effect size (Cohen's d ≈ 2.41; Hedges' g ≈ 2.30; 95% CI: 1.41 to 3.19).

Age-related outcomes

Patients were stratified according to age (≤50 years and >50 years). Eleven patients (47.4%) were aged 50 years or younger, whereas 10 patients (52.6%) were older than 50 years. Older patients had worse baseline scores (VAS 7.7 ± 1.57 vs. 4.67 ± 3.12; ASES 32.99 ± 18.45 vs. 53.7 ± 18.76; P = .023 and .027, respectively).

During follow-up, both groups demonstrated progressive improvement in VAS and ASES scores (Fig. 3a and b). Younger patients reached near pain-free values at 12 months (VAS 0.56 ± 1.67). Discussion

In our retrospective study, 19 patients diagnosed with Samilson–Prieto grade 1–3 concentric glenohumeral arthritis underwent arthroscopic débridement according to the CAM procedure and subsequent infiltration of autologous mFAT. All patients reported substantial improvements in both VAS and ASES scores.

A transient increase in pain and functional limitation was observed at 1 month post-operatively, likely reflecting the early post-operative phase common to arthroscopic shoulder surgery. Similar short-term deterioration has been described previously and typically resolves as recovery progresses.

Specifically, from T0 to T24, mean VAS decreased by 85% and ASES increased by 104% (P < .05), with no major adverse events or additional shoulder surgeries required. These improvements exceeded the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) reported for chronic rotator cuff disease in nonoperative patient populations for VAS (1.1–1.4 cm)26,39 and for ASES score (12–17 points).38 VAS scores also fell below the Patient Acceptable Symptom State ( ≈ 3 cm)39 indicating clinically meaningful outcomes. In our cohort of arthroscopically treated patients, ASES scores increased from 42.8 ± 20.9 to 87.4 ± 14.2, corresponding to a very large effect size (Cohen's d ≈ 2.41; Hedges' g ≈ 2.30; 95% CI: 1.41–3.19), far exceeding the published MCID. While these MCID and Patient Acceptable Symptom State thresholds were derived from nonoperative populations, they provide a useful benchmark for interpreting the clinical relevance of outcomes in surgically treated patients.

Although our study lacked a CAM-only control group, improvements in VAS and ASES scores at 24 months in our cohort are comparable to those reported by Millett et al25 for standard CAM arthroscopy in patients with more advanced GHOA (grades 3–4). Specifically, our mean ASES improved from 42.8 at baseline to 87.4 at 24 months, whereas Millett et al reported an increase from 46.7 to 90 points. VAS values decreased by 85% in our cohort, similar to the pain relief reported in their study. In contrast to Millett et al, all patients in our study survived without conversion to arthroplasty at 1 and 2 years, and our cohort predominantly included patients with lower-grade arthritis (Samilson–Prieto grades 1–2 versus 3–4 in Millett's study). These findings suggest that the combination of CAM and mFAT is safe and feasible and yields outcomes comparable to CAM alone; however, no causal inference regarding the additive effect of mFAT can be made.

Comparing the use of mFAT alone or in combination with CAM, Vinet-Jones and Darr43 reported promising results at 1-year follow-up, with VAS improvements around 50% and Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand improvements of 50% (grade II K-L) and 40% (grade III K-L). Considering these studies, CAM alone appears to provide better results than mFAT infiltration, and the combination of the 2 techniques does not seem to add a significant improvement.

Age and arthritis grade influenced outcomes in our cohort. Younger patients (≤50 years) and those with lower-grade arthrosis (grades 1–2) generally had better functional outcomes,22,28 although no significant post-operative differences according to age or OA grade were found (P > .05). Younger patients achieved mean VAS values close to pain free at 1 year (0.56 ± 1.67), further improving at 2 years (0.22 ± 0.67). Statistically significant differences in VAS were observed between age groups at T0 and T12, whereas ASES improvements were significant in both groups with no differences between them (−5.09, 95% CI: -20 to 9.8; P = .52).

When asked about satisfaction and expectations, older patients reported higher satisfaction, while younger patients had greater expectations. Dividing the sample according to the Samilson-Prieto GHOA classification, the most frequent grades were 1 in younger patients and 2 in patients over 50, with the distribution reaching statistical significance (P < .05). Mean VAS values for grades 1 and 2 closely followed the overall sample trend, with lower arthrosis consistently associated with lower VAS scores at all follow-ups. With regard to the mean ASES score values, the statistically significant distribution at T0 is very different between the 3 groups: 61 ± 11.86 for grade 1, 39.62 ± 18.74 for grade 2, and 30.32 ± 22.43 for grade 3. Again, at subsequent follow-ups, patients with grade 3 had lower mean ASES score values. For all patients categorized by age and degree of arthritis, there is a continuous improvement in both VAS and ASES score at the various timings with no statistically significant differences between the groups considered. Patients with the lowest degree of arthritis (1 and 2) and patients aged ≤ to 50 years see a reduction in pain earlier (at T3) and the achievement of shoulder function values that are considered excellent already 12 months after surgery (ASES ≥80).

Although age >50 years and grade 3 arthritis have not been shown to be contraindicating factors, they are likely to be associated with worse, albeit better outcomes than at T0, due to the greater likelihood of the onset of the chronic pain condition. These results partially confirm what already assessed by Mitchell et al27 who identified age >50 years and grade III-IV K-L arthritis as a negative predictive factor after a CAM technique.

The strengths of the study are that (1) it is the first study applying autologous mFAT in combination with CAM for the treatment of GHOA; (2) it included in its constitution a long follow-up of 24 months; and (3) there were no dropouts from enrolled patients. The study has, however, some limitations: (1) a small sample size; (2) the lack of a control group; and (3) the lack of a comparison of pre- and post-operative magnetic resonance imaging images.

Conclusions

All patients demonstrated substantial improvements in VAS and ASES scores at both 1 and 2 years post-operatively (P < .05). Therefore, the results allow us to conclude that the antalgic and functional benefits of the treatment are not visible in the short post-operative period but from the third month of follow-up onwards. ASES score and VAS results at T24 are independent of the pain category and ASES score category found at T0, that is, at 24 months the treatment is effective regardless of the initial pain and extent of shoulder dysfunction. Our results show that the treatment appears to be more effective in achieving painlessness and excellent shoulder function in younger patients with arthritis grades 1 and 2 but relieves symptoms and improves function in both patient categories. The results show that improvements not only persist 1 year after surgery but also continue up to 24 months. Although it is conceivable that the use of Lipogems in combination with the CAM procedure may prolong its beneficial effects, future studies with larger samples and longer follow-up times are needed to assess its long-term effects of GHOA treatment. Importantly, the procedure was well tolerated, with no major complications or persistent donor-site morbidity observed in our cohort.
