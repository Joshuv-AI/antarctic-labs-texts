Sleep quality and physical activity profiles associated with quality of life, self-rated health, and happiness: A cross-sectional population study in Western Iran
Authors: Ali Zakiei, Neda Sarabi, Giti Zangeneh-Mehr, Nader Rajabi-Gilan, Mehdi Khezeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAlthough the benefits of a healthy lifestyle are well recognized, little is known about how sleep and physical activity patterns jointly relate to multiple well-being outcomes – namely quality of life, self-rated health, and happiness. This study was designed to address that gap.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted across five western provinces of Iran (in october 2024), involving 1187 residents aged 16 years and older. A person-centered cluster analysis was applied to explore the combined associations of sleep quality and physical activity behaviors with the three outcome measures.ResultsSignificant differences emerged among clusters for all outcomes (p < 0.01). Cluster 3 consistently reported the highest mean scores for quality of life (3.57 ± 0.88), self-rated health (4.05 ± 0.76), and happiness (2.38 ± 0.82). Effect sizes were 0.15, 0.19, and 0.10, respectively – large for quality of life and self-rated health, and medium-to-large for happiness.ConclusionOverall, the findings indicate that specific configurations of sleep quality and physical activity are meaningfully linked to well-being indicators. However, given the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn. The results nonetheless offer valuable insights for future research and for tailoring health interventions to population subgroups.

Introduction

Self-rated health (SRH) is a reliable measure of an individual's perception of their own health and serves as a valuable tool for monitoring health status (Caramenti and Castiglioni, 2022). Concurrently, societal development and rising living standards have underscored the importance of quality of life (QOL) as a key outcome in social and medical research (Zhao et al., 2009). Over the past three decades, improving QOL has become a central goal in the social development and health policies of many nations (Pourtaheri et al., 2011). Happiness is another crucial concept, linked to ideals ranging from physical and mental health to interpersonal relationships and occupational success (Cohn et al., 2009). However, reports indicate that nearly one-third of people are dissatisfied with their lives, a proportion that has been increasing over the past decade (Jiang et al., 2022).

Among health-related behaviors, sleep quality (SQ) and physical activity (PA) are critical determinants of well-being (Watson et al., 2015). Evidence suggests that good sleep quality is associated with higher levels of happiness (Okamura et al., 2020), while poor sleep negatively impacts various dimensions of QOL (Rezaei et al., 2020) and can lead to declines in both physical and psychological health (Marques et al., 2017). This is a significant public health concern, as chronic sleep deprivation is linked to an increased risk of hypertension (Han et al., 2020) and cardiovascular events (Aggarwal et al., 2013). Modifiable factors such as inadequate sleep quality and low levels of physical activity also affect energy balance (Atiyah, 2024). Furthermore, sleep quality is influenced by socioeconomic and demographic factors, including social class and income (Badri et al., 2023).

While the literature reports extensive evidence on the impact of SQ and PA on various outcomes, most studies utilized traditional variable-oriented methods to explore their independent, linear associations and interactions. Nevertheless, applying conventional regression models or comparing group means inherently assumes that the examined associations are consistent across all levels of analysis. In contrast to these model-based, often overly simplified, assumptions, real-world behavior typically exhibits complex patterns that go beyond the description of an isolated average. Specifically, the unique value of cluster analyses lies in the researchers' ability to identify subgroups that share similar patterns of response without a priori assumptions regarding the nature of the distribution (Howard and Hoffman, 2018). Thus, taking a person-centered perspective, as opposed to the traditional variable-oriented method, allows the researchers to unveil meaningful differences between groups and highlight specific associations between sleep and PA domains and individual domains of well-being. This study's critical value stems from the fact that there is a limited number of studies, if any that explore the combined associations of SQ and PA with other variables in the population of western Iran, which is currently undergoing rapid lifestyle and socio-cultural changes. Therefore, addressing this critical gap in the literature may benefit the local population in terms of precision health and individualized prevention.

To the best of us knowledge, no previous study has attempted to identify SQ and PA profiles and examine their associations with perceived QOL, SRH, and happiness in the population of western Iran. Individual profiles of SQ and PA are highly relevant because they serve as precursors to developing chronic diseases and unfavorable health consequences (Hoseini et al., 2025). Simultaneously, identifying subgroups of individuals with distinct behavioral patterns enables personalized medicine and more effective health promotion interventions (Kanai et al., 2025). From a broader perspective, recognizing specific behavioral clusters helps distinguish “vulnerable and high-risk” groups from the general population, advancing our ability to implement targeted screening and prevention campaigns (Esmaeili et al., 2025). This study adopts a person-centered approach rather than focusing on average associations. It will: (Caramenti and Castiglioni, 2022) identify distinct profiles via cluster analysis, and (Zhao et al., 2009) compare these clusters on QOL, SRH, and happiness. Two hypotheses are tested: at least two clusters will emerge, and the more favorable cluster will show significantly better outcomes with medium-to-large effects. The findings aim to advance theoretical understanding and provide practical evidence for policymakers to prioritize regional behavioral interventions.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted across five urban areas in western Iran (Kermanshah, Hamedan, Khorramabad, Sanandaj, and Ilam). The target population comprised men and women aged 16 to 75 years. The sample size was calculated using the correlation formula, with a design effect of 1.2, resulting in an estimated requirement of 1268 individuals. Quotas for each city were allocated proportionally to population size: Kermanshah (483), Hamedan (283), Sanandaj (215), Khorramabad (187), and Ilam (100). Ultimately, 1187 valid questionnaires were analyzed.

A two-stage mixed sampling strategy was used: municipal districts and neighborhoods were randomly selected, followed by convenience sampling to meet quotas. Although this approach may not have been fully representative, we attempted to minimize bias by contacting households at different times and days. All participants provided informed consent. Ethics approval was obtained (IR.KUMS.REC.1403.534).

Measures

The first items of the questionnaire included the socio-demographic variables (age, sex, level of education, marital status, and occupation), which were measured using single items already validated in the literature.

Subjective Socioeconomic Status (SSS): The second section assessed subjective socioeconomic status using a single-item measure (Odéen et al., 2013). Respondents indicated their perceived position on a 10-rung ladder representing the social structure, with a higher score reflecting a better self-perceived status. Although it is a single item, it demonstrates good convergent validity with objective measures of SES (Zhao et al., 2023).

Quality of Life (QOL) and Self-Rated Health (SRH): The third section incorporated the first two general items from the WHOQOL-BREF-26 instrument. These items evaluated participants' overall perception of their QOL and SRH. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very bad to 5 = very good), with higher scores indicating better QOL and SRH (Organization WH, 1996). Acceptable test-retest reliability and cross-cultural validity for these items (Skevington et al., 2004).

Happiness: single-item measure assessed current happiness (Chalabi and Musavi, 2009). Participants selected the statement that best described their feelings: 1 (I feel extremely happy), 2 (I am very happy), 3 (I am somewhat happy), or 4 (I am not happy at all). These items were previously validated with longer measures of happiness (Abdel-Khalek, 2006). In the final analysis, we recoded the happiness variable so that a higher score indicated greater happiness.

Physical Activity (PA): PA level was measured by asking, “How often do you participate in sports and physical activities over the past week?” Responses were categorized and scored as: 1 (never), 2 (1–3 times per month), 3 (1–2 times per week), and 4 (almost every day) (Ueshima et al., 2010) which correlates moderately with accelerometer data (Milton et al., 2011).

Sleep Quality (SQ): The sixth section employed the Single-Item Sleep Quality Scale. Respondents rated their overall sleep quality over the past 7 days on a discretized visual analog scale from 0 to 10 (0 = terrible; 10 = excellent). The instruction prompted consideration of key aspects, including sleep duration, ease of falling asleep, nighttime awakenings, early morning awakenings, and sleep refreshment (Riegel and Weaver, 2009). The Sleep Quality Scale is well-correlated with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and morning questionnaire-insomnia (MQI) (Snyder et al., 2018).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS-27. Continuous variables are expressed as means (SD), and categorical data as frequencies (%). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and effect sizes were reported alongside p-values to assess clinical relevance.

To identify homogeneous subgroups, a person-centered hierarchical agglomerative clustering procedure was applied to the SQ scores and PA scores, using squared Euclidean distances and Ward's linkage. The optimal number of clusters was determined by examining the elbow plot, the agglomeration schedule, and interpretability. After testing 2- to 6-cluster solutions, a three-cluster model was chosen, yielding favorable, intermediate, and adverse functioning profiles – a balance between theoretical meaning and statistical parsimony.

All Normality assumptions were checked and confirmed. Homogeneity of variance–covariance matrices was verified using Box's M test, and Levene's test confirmed equal variances. Pillai's trace was used for multivariate significance. Multicollinearity was assessed among predictor variables using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Tolerance; all VIF values were well below 5 (ranging from 1.26 to 1.69) and Tolerance values were above 0.2, confirming no problematic collinearity. No outliers were detected (standardized residuals within ±3.29), and linearity between the covariate and dependent variables was confirmed via scatter plots.

A one-way MANOVA first tested overall differences among the three clusters on all dependent variables. Then, a series of one-way ANCOVAs were performed with SSS as a covariate, to control for SSS. Scheffé post-hoc tests were applied in both analyses. Finally, Pearson correlations among all independent and dependent variables were computed for the full sample.

Results

Based on the scores of sleep quality (SQ) and physical activity (PA), a cluster analysis identified three groups of subjects with similar sleep and PA patterns. Rather than statistical significance, we highlight what these group differences tell us about the relationship between SQ and PA. It is this cluster who are referred to as having an ‘Unfavorable cluster: Poor sleepers & inactive’. Cluster 2 (n = 546; 46%) demonstrated moderate levels of both SQ (M = 6.35; SD = 1.08) and PA (M = 2.27; SD = 1.00). Approximately one-third of those taking part in this cluster were physically active. We labeled this cluster ‘Moderate cluster: Intermediate.’ Finally, cluster 3 (n = 422; 35.6%) consisted of those who had high SQ (M = 9.19; SD = 0.85) and moderate PA (M = 2.87; SD = 0.99); nearly one-third of participants in this cluster were active almost every day. Discussion

The present study aimed to determine the association between SQ and PA and to explore their relationship with the QOL, SRH, and happiness of the residents in western Iran. Three behavioral profiles with a range of health outcomes were identified, and their association with the indicators was confirmed. First, the observed differences in the outcome variables were substantial, with the largest effects noted for the QOL and SRH. Second, behavioral profiles were largely independent of the SSS with which they interact. The present results highlight the observed associations of SQ and PA with well-being and suggest that these domains could be considered as potential targets for future intervention studies in precision public health.

The higher levels of functionality in Cluster 3 may be associated with the interaction between SQ and PA. PA lowers stress and normalizes circadian rhythms and inflammatory responses, thus improving SQ (Saadh et al., 2026; Chennaoui et al., 2015). At the same time, many interventions focus on diet and exercise without adequately addressing sleep health, a crucial modifiable risk factor (Rahelić et al., 2024). It is plausible that the two variables may act synergistically to create a self-perpetuating dynamic, whereby beneficial habits may enhance health benefits and poor routines may compound the harm. The large effect sizes found for the QOL and SRH (ƞ2 = 0.15, 0.19) suggest that the differences in health-related aspects may be considerable. Similar physiological mechanisms appear to underlie the associations with happiness, although the effects were smaller (ƞ2 = 0.10). Recent studies have examined that the relationship between different levels of PA (moderate and vigorous activities) and happiness (Castellanos-García et al., 2023). Physical activity has been shown to promote healthier sleep patterns while simultaneously alleviating symptoms of diverse psychiatric disorders. Moreover, exercise is broadly linked to elevated mood and a superior quality of life (Mahindru et al., 2023). It is possible that individuals in Cluster 3 may consequently benefit from these pathways, as reflected in their higher reports of general well-being compared to Cluster 1.

The identified differences in well-being outcomes further may reflect variations in SQ and PA that appear to operate independently of an individual's SSS. Although controlling for SSS reduced most of the effects, the reduction was largely modest and did not negate the significant effects on well-being outcomes. This result is inconsistent with many studies that emphasize the effects of socioeconomic status on health and well-being outcomes (Adler and Ostrove, 1999). A possible interpretation stems from the fact that many low-SSS individuals failed to engage in healthy daytime and nighttime routines. However, the fact thatn Controlling for SSS only accounts for a small part of the variance in health outcomes, suggests that other factors contribute substantially to the differences in the outcomes. This might be related to the interaction between PA and SQ discussed above, particularly the combined impact on mood and physiology. This study highlights the importance of adopting a holistic approach in precision public health and considering adjustments to multiple behaviors rather than focusing on discrete issues. The observed associations suggest that addressing both PA and SQ may be associated with substantial improvements in health-related quality of life and general well-being, although this interpretation should be tested in future longitudinal or interventional studies.

The present findings provide potential implications for public health policy and practice. In particular, the observed associations suggest that multilevel, person-centered interventions may be beneficial in targeting the individuals in Cluster 1, with separate targeted actions developed for the group in Cluster 2. The principal dimensions of lifestyle medicine encompass whole foods, plant-based dietary patterns, regular physical activity, restorative sleep, stress management, avoidance of risky substances, and fostering positive social connections, all of which have been identified as fundamental determinants, or root causes, of non-communicable chronic diseases (Rahelić et al., 2024). Accordingly, PA and SQ appear to play a critical role within this paradigm in being associated with improved health outcomes across the general population and among at-risk subgroups, with potential implications for future preventive strategies. Furthermore, accumulating research suggests that intensive lifestyle-based interventions are capable of arresting, and in some instances reversing, the natural course of chronic disease progression (Sadiq, 2023). Accordingly, behavioral interventions could be further reinforced through macro-level policy measures designed to increase the availability of safe exercise environments. Crucially, our findings highlight that sleep disruptions and inconsistent physical activity levels warrant particular clinical attention, as they may serve as potential leverage points that could be associated with broader health outcomes in future interventional studies.

A strengths-based perspective, applied in the present research, may help identify behavioral patterns characteristic of different segments of the population. Understanding the ways in which these patterns intersect with other variables, such as the SSS, is arguably critical in precision public health, where interventions are designed to specifically target unique needs of particular groups. A few limitations of the present study should also be noted. As a cross-sectional study, it was unable to establish the causal relationships between the variables examined. In particular, the mechanisms underlying the observed associations between SQ and PA and health-related outcomes and well-being are unclear, as are the ways in which these variables may relate to each other. The SRH measure used in the study is susceptible to response and desirability biases. Finally, the study relied on a convenience sample representing urban western Iran; thus, the findings may not be generalized to rural areas or other populations. Future research should employ a longitudinal design to explore the bidirectional associations between behavioral profiles and health outcomes. Additionally, researchers should pursue interventional studies attempting to test the efficacy of profile-specific treatments, potentially using randomized controlled trials designs with comparison groups. Finally, objective measures of the key variables, including accelerometers and actigraphy, should be employed to improve validity and reliability.

Conclusion

The differences between clusters remained stable across levels of subjective SES, suggesting that the patterns may be independent of socioeconomic background. The results highlight the observed associations between sleep quality and physical activity and well-being outcomes, and suggest that addressing multiple behavioral domains together could be a promising strategy in intervention design. Although preliminary, the findings may carry potential implications for health promotion that warrant further investigation. Future longitudinal and interventional research is needed to establish causality and develop specific recommendations for each subgroup. A person-centered approach that accounts for varied responses to health challenges appears valuable in precision public health, and may help to inform policies and practices that better respond to the diverse needs of the population.
