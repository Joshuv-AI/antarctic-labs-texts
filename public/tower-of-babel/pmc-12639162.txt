Synergistic effects of graphene oxide loading and particle size in ice-templated carboxymethyl cellulose–based aerogels on dye removal
Authors: Soon Wan Kweon, Dae-Hyun Jang, Tai Ju Lee, Heoug-Yoon Kweon, Heon-Sang Kim, Hyoung Jin Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSustainable carboxymethyl cellulose/graphene oxide (GO) aerogels were fabricated via epichlorohydrin cross-linking and ice-templating for effective methylene blue (MB) removal from aqueous solutions. The synergistic effects of GO loading (1, 3, and 5 wt%) and particle size (500, 10, and 1 nm) on the structural, mechanical, and adsorption properties of aerogels were systematically explored. The optimal dye adsorption capacity (555.6 mg/g) was obtained at 3 wt% GO loading, owing to enhanced π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions between GO and MB. Conversely, excessive GO loading caused nanosheet aggregation, decreasing adsorption efficiency. Reducing GO particle size to 1 nm notably improved the compressive strength (546 kPa) of aerogels because of uniform dispersion but deteriorated adsorption performance by disrupting interlayer interactions (π–π stacking). The 10 nm–GO composite achieved the optimal balance, demonstrating high adsorption capacity (416.7 mg/g) and adsorbate affinity (KL = 0.387 L/mg). Spectroscopic and morphological analyses confirmed successful cross-linking, improved pore structure, and interfacial compatibility. Adsorption kinetics followed the pseudo-second-order model, whereas the isotherm data fitted well to the Langmuir model, indicating monolayer chemisorption. The aerogel retained > 93% of its initial efficiency after five reuse cycles, demonstrating excellent reusability and potential for sustainable dye-laden wastewater treatment.Keywords: Biocomposite, Nanocomposite, Epichlorohydrin, Dye adsorption, Adsorption isotherm, Adsorption kineticsSubject terms: Graphene, Environmental biotechnology, Biotechnology

Introduction

Industrial wastewater contamination, particularly by synthetic dyes, is a considerable environmental concern owing to its adverse effects on aquatic ecosystems and human health1. Dyes are extensively used in industries, such as textiles, paper, and pharmaceuticals, and their complex aromatic structures and chemical stability render their degradation using conventional treatment methods difficult2–4. Methylene blue (MB; C16H18N3SCl), which is a widely utilized cationic dye, is a persistent contaminant that presents substantial environmental hazards owing to its high solubility, toxicity, and biodegradation resistance5. Therefore, the development of efficient and sustainable dye removal technologies is of paramount importance for mitigating the environmental impact of dye effluents.

Numerous dye removal technologies, including chemical precipitation, membrane separation, solvent extraction, coagulation, catalytic decomposition, and reverse osmosis, have been investigated to address dye pollution6. However, several of these methods exhibit inherent limitations, such as high operational costs, secondary pollutant generation, and complex infrastructure requirements7.Conversely, adsorption has gained considerable attention as a promising dye removal technique because of its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, reusability, and high removal efficiency. Adsorbent materials, particularly aerogels, are characterized by their large surface area, high porosity, and tunable surface chemistry, rendering them suitable candidates for dye adsorption applications8–10.

Natural polymers, such as polysaccharides, have garnered considerable interest for wastewater treatment because of their environmental compatibility and cost-effectiveness11. Polysaccharides, including starch, chitosan, alginate, and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), are extensively employed as effective adsorbents in wastewater treatment owing to their abundance, biodegradability, and adsorption-facilitating functional groups12. Among them, CMC has received particular attention as an anionic linear polysaccharide. It is derived from cellulose via etherification with chloroacetic acid, which results in the introduction of –CH₂–COOH groups on the glucopyranose backbone. This structural modification yields a water-soluble polymer featuring abundant hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, which function as active sites for dye adsorption. Additionally, CMC is economically viable, biocompatible, and nontoxic, which enhance its applicability in environmental remediation13–15. Despite these beneficial properties, pure CMC aerogels typically possess low mechanical strength and limited adsorption capacity owing to their small surface area and fragile network structure. To address these limitations, various modification strategies, such as composite formation and grafting, have been explored to enhance the structural integrity and adsorption capacity of CMC-based aerogels16–18.

Graphene oxide (GO) is a two-dimensional carbonaceous nanomaterial that is a functionalized derivative of graphene. GO can be synthesized via two primary approaches: top–down and bottom–up pathways19. Mechanical exfoliation, such as the peeling of graphite layers using adhesive tape or atomic force microscopy tips, was the earliest top–down method employed for GO synthesis, yielding high-purity monolayer graphene. However, this method was hindered by its limited scalability20. The oxidative exfoliation of graphite via chemical oxidation and ultrasonication is a commonly used method to synthesize GO enriched with hydroxyl, epoxy, and carboxyl groups21. These oxygenated functionalities endow GO with excellent hydrophilicity, enabling its stable dispersion in aqueous media and rendering it highly suitable for surface modification and adsorption applications. Bottom–up techniques, such as chemical vapor deposition, involve the thermal decomposition of hydrocarbon gases on metal substrates, forming high-crystallinity graphene films featuring precisely controlled thickness22. These differences in synthesis pathways result in distinct physicochemical properties, which ultimately affect the performance of GO in environmental applications, such as dye adsorption.

The unique structural and chemical properties of GO originate from its extensive π-conjugated aromatic domains and hydrophilic functional groups, which enable it to effectively interact with various organic and inorganic molecules. The hydrophilic characteristics of GO facilitate its dispersion in aqueous media, whereas the π–π conjugated networks provide active sites for adsorption via π–π interactions with aromatic compounds. Additionally, the carboxyl and hydroxyl groups on the GO surface facilitate hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, enhancing its affinity for cationic dyes, such as MB23–26. The incorporation of GO into polymeric matrices, such as CMC, can considerably improve the mechanical strength and adsorption capacity of the resulting composite aerogels11,27. However, the extent of these enhancements is dependent on factors such as GO particle size, surface chemistry, and dispersion uniformity, rendering the systematic investigation of the effects of GO loading and particle size on the structural and functional properties of GO-based aerogels essential. Although numerous studies have reported the fabrication of CMC–GO aerogels, few have systematically examined the synergistic effects of both GO loading level and particle size on their structural, mechanical, and adsorption behaviors. This study uniquely addresses this gap by providing a comparative and quantitative evaluation across multiple GO sizes and concentrations, enabling the establishment of structure–property relationships critical for rational adsorbent design.

Therefore, this study systematically investigate the effects of GO loading (1, 3, and 5 wt%) and particle size (1, 10, and 500 nm) on the structural, mechanical, and adsorption properties of bio-based CMC aerogels. This study evaluates the rheological behavior, morphological characteristics, mechanical integrity, surface chemistry, and dye adsorption mechanisms for a range of GO loadings and particle sizes and elucidates the underlying structure–function relations governing the performance of CMC–GO composite aerogels. The findings of this study will contribute to the rational design and optimization of sustainable aerogel adsorbents for the effective removal of cationic dyes, such as MB, which will advance green technologies for wastewater treatment applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

CMC (average molecular weight ≈ 700,000 g/mol; degree of substitution 0.9; CAS No. 9004-32-4) and GO (particle size > 500 nm; CAS No. 796034-1G) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Epichlorohydrin (ECH; 99%; CAS No. 106-89-8) was obtained from Daejung Chemicals and Metals Co., Ltd. (Siheung, Korea). NaOH (50%, CAS No. 1310-73-2) was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. GO samples featuring the particle sizes of < 10 and < 1 nm were obtained from NGF Korea (Ansan, Korea). MB (0.1%; CAS No. 7220-79-3) used for cationic dye adsorption experiments was purchased from Daejung Chemical and Metals (Siheung, Korea). The HCl (10%; CAS No. 7647-01-0) solution for dye desorption experiments was procured from Deoksan Pure Chemical (Ansan, Korea). Synthesis of CMC–GO composite aerogels

Effects of GO loading

The CMC–GO composite aerogels were prepared via the sol–gel process (Fig. 2). The conditions for evaluating the effects of GO loading in the CMC–GO composite aerogels are specified in Table 2. First, CMC (0.2 g) was dispersed in distilled water (10 mL) under constant stirring at ambient temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, 500 nm–GO was added at the mass ratios of 1%, 3%, and 5% based on the dry mass of CMC. The mixture was homogenized at a rotational speed of > 30,000 rpm to ensure uniform dispersion. Thereafter, the pH was adjusted to 10 using 1 M NaOH, and subsequently, ECH (6 µL; 3 wt%) was added as a cross-linker. The solution was continuously stirred at 40 °C for 12 h until gel formation was stabilized. Afterward, the obtained material was gelatinized at 25 °C for 24 h. Finally, the gel was frozen at − 80 °C for 2 h and subsequently freeze dried at − 80 °C and 2.5 Pa for 72 h using a freeze dryer (FDL-1000, Tokyo Rikakikai, Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Reusability of the CMC–GO composite aerogels

To evaluate the reusability of the CMC–GO composite aerogel, the aerogel (0.02 g) was immersed in a 50 ppm MB dye solution (50 mL) at pH 6. The mixture was stirred at 150 rpm for 2 h at 25 °C. After adsorption, the dye-loaded aerogel was thoroughly rinsed with distilled water to remove the unadsorbed dye. The desorption process was conducted by immersing the dye-loaded aerogel in a 0.1 M HCl solution (50 mL) to release the adsorbed dye molecules. Subsequently, the aerogel was regenerated by soaking it in a 0.1 M NaOH solution for 1 h, followed by extensive washing with distilled water until neutral pH was achieved. The washed aerogel was subsequently reused under the same aforementioned conditions. The regenerated aerogel was reused for several adsorption cycles under the same experimental conditions. Results and discussion

Rheological properties of hydrogels

The rheological characteristics of the CMC–GO composite hydrogels featuring different contents and particle sizes of GO were systematically investigated to assess their effects on shear viscosity, storage modulus (G’), loss modulus (G”), and tan δ values (Fig. 3). In the low shear region, viscosity increases with the GO particle size and loading, exhibiting shear-thinning behavior with increasing shear rate, as depicted in Fig. 3a and d. This increase in viscosity at low shear rates can be attributed to the increase in the effective volume fraction due to the surface replacement effect35, which gains prominence with increasing GO particle size and loading. Consequently, enhanced interactions between CMC and GO and the obstruction of the CMC flow by GO particles contribute to the increase in viscosity. Conversely, under high-shear conditions, the alignment of GO particles along the shear direction, together with the CMC chains, improves dispersion and reduces flow resistance36. Figure 3b and e present the G’ and G” that reflect the elastic and viscous components of the hydrogels, respectively. G’ represents the elastic, energy-storing behavior, whereas G” reflects the viscous, energy-dissipating behavior37. With increasing GO content, the G’ and G’’ values decrease, exhibiting a more prominent trend for smaller particle sizes. This behavior is attributed to the local disruption of the CMC flow caused by the addition of plate-like GO particles, which interfere with the entanglement of CMC chains. Furthermore, GO particles consume potential cross-linking sites between the CMC molecules, which reduces the cross-linking density and consequently decreases G’. The values of tan δ, which is defined as the ratio of G” to G’, are presented in Fig. 3c and f. A tan δ value of < 1 indicates a more elastic (solid-like) behavior, whereas a tan δ value of > 1 signifies a more viscous (liquid-like) behavior. Considering GO loading, GO500-3 displays lower tan δ values within the tested frequency range, indicating stronger cross-linking and higher structural integrity. Conversely, GO500-5 depicts higher tan δ values, which indicates a stronger viscous behavior plausibly caused by steric hindrance and the lack of network connectivity due to excessive GO loading. Considering the particle size, GO10-3 demonstrates a more stable formation than GO500-3 and GO1-3. The uniform dispersion and moderate aspect ratio of the 10 nm–GO sample plausibly promote optimal filler–matrix interactions in the network. Conversely, the 1 nm–GO sample may exhibit a lower cross-linking efficiency because of its extremely larger specific surface area, which limits the probability of intersheet contact and hinders the formation of a robust network.

Polymer network of the CMC–GO composites

The polymer network structure of the ECH-cross-linked CMC–GO composite is illustrated in Fig. 4. Under alkaline conditions, the hydroxyl groups of CMC and the oxygen-containing functional groups (hydroxyl and carboxyl groups) of GO are nucleophilic, facilitating nucleophilic attacks on the epoxide rings of ECH38. This reaction primarily forms ether linkages between CMC and GO, creating a three-dimensional cross-linked network. Additionally, residual epoxide groups react with other hydroxyl groups to form secondary ether bonds, stabilizing the structure. In contrast to cellulose, CMC contains carboxymethyl groups that provide additional active sites for ECH binding, potentially enhancing the cross-linking efficiency and structural stability of the polymer network.

Chemical and structural characteristics of the CMC–GO composite aerogels

The ATR-FTIR spectra of CMC, GO500-0, GO500-3, and MB-adsorbed composite (Comp-MB) aerogels are depicted in Fig. 5a. The CMC spectrum exhibits characteristic peaks within 3500–3300 cm⁻¹, corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations. The peak at 2900 cm⁻¹ is attributed to C–H stretching vibrations5. The peaks at approximately 1600 and 1420 cm⁻¹ are assigned to –COO stretching vibrations and O–H bending vibrations, respectively. The distinct peak at approximately 1320 cm⁻¹ is attributed to C–H bending vibrations, whereas the absorption peak at approximately 1055 cm⁻¹ corresponds to C–O stretching vibrations39. These spectral characteristics are consistent with the molecular structure of CMC. For the GO-containing samples (GO500-3), additional absorption bands are observed. The peaks at 1734, 1227, and 1067 cm− 1 correspond to the stretching vibrations of the C = O bond in carboxylic acids and ketones, the C–O bond in carboxylic acids, and the C–O–C bond, respectively5,39. These observations indicate that the GO surface contains a considerable number of oxygenated functional groups34,40,41. Furthermore, the intensity of the O–H stretching band increases after GO incorporation, which suggests the optimization of the dispersion of GO and its interactions with CMC chains, resulting in minimal defect formation compared with that under other conditions.

Structural and chemical analyses of the CMC–GO composite aerogels: (a) ATR-FTIR spectra; (b) XRD patterns; (c) Raman spectra; (d–f) C 1s XPS spectra.

The XRD patterns of ECH15 and the CMC–GO composite aerogels featuring different GO particle sizes (GO500-3, GO10-3, and GO1-3) are depicted in Fig. 5b. Upon ECH cross-linking, a diffraction peak appeared at 2θ = 21.7°, corresponding to a d-spacing of 0.409 nm. For the GO-containing aerogels, the diffraction peaks shifted toward smaller angles, indicating further structural rearrangement due to GO incorporation. Specifically, the d-spacing values of ECH15, GO500-3, GO10-3, and GO1-3 were 0.409, 0.413, 0.414, and 0.419 nm, respectively. These results corroborated the structural optimization indicated by the rheological analysis (Fig. 3). GO500-3 and GO10-3 possessed comparable d-spacing values; the smallest 1 nm–GO sample exhibited the largest d-spacing owing to the enhanced steric repulsion and disrupted stacking regularity. This expansion alleviated local stress concentrations. Overall, the reduction in tan δ (Fig. 3f) could be attributed to the optimized structural balance and efficient energy dissipation mechanism of the nanocomposite system42. The XRD results demonstrated that GO incorporation affected the crystallinity and molecular packing of the aerogel matrix11. Furthermore, the gradual increase in d-spacing with decreasing GO particle size may facilitate improved diffusion of MB molecules and enhance their accessibility to internal adsorption sites. Nevertheless, excessive interlayer expansion, as observed in GO1-3, could disrupt the π–π stacking alignment between GO sheets, thereby reducing the number of effective adsorption sites.

The Raman spectra of GO500-1, GO500-3, and GO500-5 are presented in Fig. 5c. All the spectra display two prominent peaks corresponding to the D (~ 1350 cm− 1) and G (~ 1580 cm− 1) bands, which are characteristic of the disordered and graphitic structures of GO, respectively43. The D-to-G band intensity (ID/IG) ratio provides insights into the degree of structural defects and disorder within the carbon framework5. Among the analyzed samples, GO500-1 exhibits the highest ID/IG ratio (0.83), suggesting a higher proportion of defects and disrupted sp2 domains, whereas GO500-5 demonstrates the lowest ratio (0.74), indicating a higher degree of graphitic ordering. This trend implies that the increase in GO loading leads to the partial restoration of sp2-hybridized networks via enhanced π–π stacking interactions between GO sheets. Additionally, the overall increase in the Raman peak intensities with increasing GO loading can be attributed to the presence of larger amounts of GO in the matrix and the improved interfacial contact between GO and CMC, which collectively enhance the Raman scattering efficiency of the composite structure.

Furthermore, to investigate the interfacial chemical interactions between CMC and GO in the aerogel matrix, XPS measurements were performed. The high-resolution C 1s spectra (Fig. 5d–f) exhibit systematic changes in the carbon bonding environment with the incorporation of ECH and GO. The pristine CMC spectrum exhibits three main peaks at 282.8, 283.8, and 286.3 eV, corresponding to C–C/C = C, C–O, and C = O species, respectively, which are typical of the polysaccharide backbone and carboxymethyl functionalities44. After ECH cross-linking (GO500-0), the C–O peak intensity increases, whereas the C–C peak intensity decreases, suggesting the conversion of non-oxidized carbon atoms into ether linkages, which reduces the relative proportion of C–C bonds in the structure45. In the GO500-3 spectrum, a distinct peak is observed at 282.8 eV (C–C/C = C), indicating the incorporation of GO-derived oxygen functionalities46. The appearance and shift of these peaks confirm the strong chemical interactions between CMC and GO, plausibly involving hydrogen bonding and/or covalent cross-linking, which contribute to the modified surface chemistry and improved functional performance of the resulting aerogels.

Morphology of the CMC–GO composite aerogels

The visual images of the freeze dried aerogels are presented in Fig. 6. During freeze drying, as water molecules crystallize into ice, the polymer matrix is displaced into the interstitial spaces surrounding the growing ice crystals (Fig. 7a). Upon sublimation, these ice crystals create a template for the formation of a porous structure featuring a lamellar architecture47. As depicted in Fig. 6, the appearance of the aerogels turns darker with increasing GO content, indicating a higher degree of GO dispersion throughout the matrix. The layered structure gains prominence with the addition of GO, indicating that plate-like GO serves as nucleation sites within the CMC network for the formation of well-defined lamellae. The color of GO1-3 is relatively lighter, plausibly because of the smaller particle size, which may have limited the extent of GO aggregation and resulted in a visually lighter appearance. Structurally, the aerogels exhibit a unidirectional lamellar alignment, which results from the directional freezing using open-top silicone molds. The absence of a lid at the top of the mold causes a temperature gradient, resulting in rapid heat transfer from the exposed surface to the bottom (Fig. 7b). This thermal gradient promotes the vertical growth of ice crystals, resulting in the formation of a vertically aligned porous structure upon sublimation27. This structural configuration is advantageous for maintaining mechanical integrity and provides interconnected pore networks suitable for adsorption48, as the unidirectional porous structure can enhance the rigid alignment of the CMC–GO composite aerogels along the sublimation direction and increase the capillary forces.

Figure 8 presents the SEM images illustrating the effects of GO loading and particle size on the morphological characteristics of the CMC–GO composite aerogels. After GO loading (Fig. 8a), distinct striated structures were observed along the radial direction, and the clarity of these striations gained more prominence upon GO incorporation. Specifically, GO500-1 and GO500-3 exhibited well-defined lamellar boundaries and more homogeneous pore structures compared with the pristine CMC aerogels (CMC and GO500-0). This phenomenon could be attributed to the functioning of GO sheets as nucleation sites, promoting the alignment of polymer chains during freezing. However, in GO500-5, excessive GO loading led to the partial obstruction of pore channels, resulting in a dense, less-porous structure. This structural densification plausibly originated from excessive GO aggregation, hindering the formation of interconnected pore networks. Furthermore, the formation of uniform, lamellar, and porous structures were observed regardless of GO particle size (Fig. 8b). However, GO1-3 exhibited more compact striations than GO500-3 and GO10-3. This could be ascribed to the smaller particle size of GO1-3, which facilitated more uniform dispersion and compact packing within the aerogel matrix. Additionally, the smaller GO particles might have effectively filled the void spaces between the larger CMC chains, increasing the contact points and enhancing the overall structural integrity of the aerogel. The distance between the layers in the unidirectional lamellar structure was 50–200 μm, which was consistent with the previously reported values for lamellar aerogels synthesized via unidirectional freeze casting27. This consistency emphasized the effectiveness of freezing in directing the alignment of GO sheets and polymer chains, contributing to the formation of well-defined layered architectures.

Physical properties of the CMC–GO composite aerogels

The apparent density and porosity of the CMC–GO composite aerogels were analyzed to assess the effects of GO loading and particle size on the structural properties of the aerogels (Fig. 9a and b). The addition of GO at different concentrations resulted in distinct trends in density and porosity. GO500-0 exhibited the highest density (0.032 g/cm³) and the lowest porosity (97.9%), indicating the formation of a relatively compact structure by the CMC matrix in the absence of GO. After the incorporation of 1 wt% GO (GO500-1), the density of the resulting material decreased to 0.026 g/cm³, whereas its porosity increased to 98.3%. The incorporation of GO enhanced the porosity by increasing the interlayer spacing and generating micropores owing to its planar structure and abundant oxygen-containing functional groups, which facilitated hydrogen bonding and physical interactions, expanding the pore network within the polymer matrix. However, with increasing GO content, the density of the aerogels gradually increased, whereas their porosity decreased. The decrease in porosity with increasing GO content could be attributed to the progressive filling of interstitial voids by GO sheets, leading to a highly compact structure and low pore volume because of densification. Conversely, when GO particle size was decreased to 10 nm (GO10-3), the density of the aerogel increased, whereas the porosity decreased. This indicated that smaller GO particles facilitated better packing and structural reinforcement. A further decrease in the GO particle size to 1 nm (GO1-3) led to a notable increase in density and a corresponding decrease in porosity, suggesting that the 1 nm–GO particles effectively filled the interstitial voids, leading to a dense network structure. From Fig. 9c, when the GO content increases from 0 (GO500-0) to 3 wt% (GO500-3), the compressive strength considerably improves from 344 to 431 kPa, representing an increase of 25%. This enhancement could be attributed to the effective dispersion of GO sheets within the CMC matrix, leading to a highly interconnected network structure that effectively distributed the applied stress. However, at 5 wt% GO loading (GO500-5), the compressive strength slightly decreased to 405 kPa, which was ascribed to the excessive GO content, resulting in structural defects and localized stress concentrations that deteriorated the network integrity. The effects of GO particle size on compressive strength were examined at a fixed GO content of 3 wt% (Fig. 9d). The compressive strength increased with decreasing GO particle size, reaching the maximum of 546 kPa for GO1-3, which represented an increase of 48% compared with that of ECH15. This substantial improvement was plausibly due to the larger surface area of smaller GO particles, which promoted their stronger interfacial interactions with the CMC matrix, leading to a denser and more robust composite structure49.

Effects of the CMC–GO composite aerogels on dye adsorption

Adsorption mechanism

Figure 10 presents the proposed mechanism for the adsorption of MB in aqueous media, which involves multiple interactions between the CMC–GO composite aerogel matrix and MB dye molecules. The visual observation images in Fig. 10 demonstrate the rapid adsorption of MB by GO500-3, indicating the high affinity of the sample for cationic dyes. Adsorption occurs almost instantaneously, emphasizing the high porosity and well-developed pore structure of the aerogel, which facilitate rapid dye uptake. This rapid response can be attributed to the extensive pore network and large surface area of the GO sheets, effectively increasing the number of accessible adsorption sites. Additionally, the layered structure of GO promotes interfacial interactions, contributing to rapid adsorption kinetics and high adsorption capacity5. In the ATR-FTIR spectrum (Fig. 5a) of the aerogel obtained after dye adsorption (Comp-MB), the O–H stretching vibration peak is slightly shifted, and the peak at approximately 1622 cm⁻¹ is more pronounced, which can be attributed to the aromatic C = C stretching54 of MB. The overall reduction in intensity and the broadening of peaks suggest the successful adsorption of MB by the aerogel, which is accompanied by π–π stacking and electrostatic interactions at the aerogel–liquid interface. These spectral changes collectively indicate that both GO and CMC contribute functional groups for dye adsorption, and their hybrid network facilitates the immobilization of dye molecules.

Dye removal efficiency

Figure 11 illustrates the dye removal efficiency of the CMC–GO composite aerogels. Among the samples comprising different GO loadings (Fig. 11a), GO500-3 consistently exhibited the highest dye removal efficiency, indicating its optimal loading for facilitating adsorption. The more porous network and uniform dispersion of GO plausibly provided a larger surface area and more accessible interfaces for MB molecules. Conversely, GO500-5 exhibited a noticeably low dye removal efficiency, suggesting that the excessive GO content might have led to pore blockage, reducing the number of effective sites for MB adsorption. Among the analyzed samples, GO500-0 exhibited the lowest dye removal capacity, owing to the absence of GO inducing π–π and electrostatic interactions. Additionally, the adsorption affinity of CMC for MB was minimal, which decreased the adsorption capability of the composite55–57. However, CMC might enhance GO dispersion, indirectly increasing the effective adsorption surface area despite the overall low GO content. Figure 11b demonstrates the effects of GO particle size on the dye adsorption performance. The aerogels prepared using GO10-3 exhibited the highest dye removal percentage, indicating the formation of a highly interconnected porous network that facilitated dye diffusion and adsorption. This finding was consistent with the results of the rheological analysis (Fig. 3f), in which GO10-3 exhibited the lowest tan δ value, indicating enhanced elastic behavior and efficient stress distribution within the aerogel matrix. The higher network integrity and mechanical stability plausibly contributed to the maintenance of open pore structures and prevention of pore collapse during adsorption, providing more accessible adsorption sites for MB molecules. Conversely, GO1-3 demonstrated a lower dye removal capacity, which could be attributed to the denser structure formed by the smaller GO particles (Fig. 9b). The low porosity of GO1-3 might have restricted dye penetration and adsorption, as the close packing of GO sheets would limit the available adsorption sites. Additionally, the 1 nm–GO sample, possessing a monolayer structure, plausibly lacked the π–π stacking interactions that could have facilitated dye adsorption via interlayer interactions. Figure 11c presents the adsorption kinetics of the CMC–GO composite aerogels under different GO loading conditions. GO500-1 and GO500-3 exhibited rapid dye uptake in the initial 30 min, reaching equilibrium within 60 min. This phenomenon could be attributed to the presence of numerous active sites on the adsorbent, which could promote dye adsorption at low initial dye concentrations. When the dye concentration was increased, these vacant sites were saturated with MB, losing their ability to absorb additional dye molecules58–61.

Dye removal efficiency of the aerogels featuring different GO (a and c) loadings and (b) particle sizes.

As illustrated in Fig. 12, the removal rate of MB increased progressively with rising pH values below 6, reaching a maximum of 100% at pH 6. Under acidic conditions (pH < 6), H⁺ ions likely adsorb onto the aerogel surface, leading to a positively charged surface that repels cationic MB molecules and suppresses dye uptake. When the pH exceeded 6, a slight decline in removal efficiency was observed, which may be attributed to the presence of OH⁻ ions interfering with the electrostatic attraction between –COOH groups on the CMC–GO surface and MB molecules, thereby lowering adsorption capacity. These observations suggest that maintaining a pH close to 6 provides optimal conditions for effective MB adsorption by the CMC–GO composite aerogels.

Analysis of MB adsorption isotherms and kinetics

MB adsorption isotherms

The adsorption isotherms of MB on the CMC–GO composite aerogels were analyzed using Langmuir and Freundlich models to evaluate the distribution of adsorbate molecules between the liquid and solid phases at equilibrium (Fig. 13a–d)62,63, focusing on the effects of GO loading (Table 4) and particle size (Table 5). The high R2 values obtained for the Langmuir model for all the samples indicated that MB adsorption onto the CMC–GO composite aerogels predominantly followed monolayer adsorption on homogeneous surface sites64, emphasizing the importance of specific interactions, such as π–π and electrostatic interactions, in the adsorption mechanism64–66. The adsorption capacity (qmax) notably varied with the GO content and particle size. GO500-1 and GO500-3 exhibited the highest qmax of 555.6 mg/g, indicating their optimal porous structure that facilitated dye adsorption, which suggested that moderate GO contents effectively maintained a well-developed porous network for adsorption. Conversely, GO500-5 depicted a considerably reduced qmax of 357.1 mg/g, owing to the pore blockage caused by excessive GO loading. Considering the samples comprising different GO particle sizes, GO500-3 and GO10-3 achieved a higher qmax of 416.7 mg/g, whereas GO1-3 exhibited a lower qmax of 384.6 mg/g. This reduction can be attributed to the monolayer structure of the 1 nm–GO sample, which limited π–π stacking interactions and reduced the number of available adsorption sites. Additionally, the compact and dense structure of GO1-3 might have restricted dye diffusion, decreasing the overall adsorption capacity. A higher Langmuir constant (KL) indicates a higher affinity between the adsorbent and adsorbate. Considering the samples featuring different GO loadings, despite the identical maximum adsorption capacity (qmax) of 555.6 mg/g observed for both GO500-1 and GO500-3, the KL value of GO500-3 (0.295 L/mg) was notably higher than that of GO500-1 (0.133 L/mg). The higher KL value of GO500-3 indicated its higher affinity for MB molecules, suggesting that the optimal GO loading of 3 wt% effectively resulted in a more accessible and well-connected adsorption network. Among the samples featuring different GO particle sizes, GO10-3 exhibited the highest KL value of 0.387 L/mg, suggesting strong interactions between GO10-3 and MB dye molecules, owing to the optimal surface area and well-distributed GO sheets that provided abundant adsorption sites. Conversely, GO1-3 possessed a lower KL value of 0.255 L/mg, indicating weaker interactions between the adsorbent and dye molecules. This reduction in KL could be attributed to the absence of π–π stacking interactions in the monolayer GO structure, which limited the specific interactions that would otherwise enhance the adsorption affinity. Moreover, the 1/n values ​​obtained via Freundlich adsorption were smaller than 0.5 for all the samples, except for GO500-0 and GO500-5, indicating substantially easy MB adsorption67–69.

MB adsorption kinetics

Kinetic studies on the adsorption of various pollutants, such as synthetic dyes and heavy metal ions, are essential because they provide information on the time required to reach adsorption equilibrium, the rate of adsorption, and the concentration of adsorbate in each phase after reaching equilibrium70. To gain an in-depth understanding of the adsorption of MB on the CMC–GO composite aerogels, kinetic analysis was conducted using PFO and PSO models to evaluate the MB adsorption rate and mechanism62, and the results are presented in Fig. 13e and f; Table 6. The R² values of the PSO model were consistently higher than those of the PFO model for all the samples, suggesting that the adsorption of MB on the CMC–GO composite aerogels could be better described using the PSO model than the PFO model71,72. Moreover, the calculated equilibrium capacity values (qe) form the Pseudo-second order were found to be more closer to the corresponding experimental values (qe−exp) than those of Pseudo-First order model (qe). This indicated that adsorption was primarily governed by chemisorption, involving electron sharing or exchange between MB molecules and active sites on the aerogel surface, such as via π–π, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions50,73.

Adsorbent reusability

The main objective of the regeneration process is to facilitate the reuse of the adsorbent for multiple cycles. Adsorption–desorption experiments were conducted under identical conditions using 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH solutions to evaluate the regeneration performance of GO10-3, and the results are depicted in Fig. 14a. The MB removal efficiency of the aerogel gradually decreased over successive cycles. However, after five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles, the adsorbent retained 93% of its initial removal efficiency. The CMC–GO composite aerogel lost only 7% of its adsorption capacity after five cycles, confirming its reusability and stability. Moreover, the SEM image after the fifth cycle (Fig. 14b) shows that the porous network structure remained largely intact, further supporting the structural stability of the aerogel during repeated use.

Conclusions

In this study, bio-based CMC–GO aerogels cross-linked with ECH were developed as efficient adsorbents for MB removal from aqueous solutions. The effects of GO loading and particle size on structural, mechanical, and adsorption properties were systematically investigated. Optimal adsorption performance was observed for GO500-3 (3 wt% GO), achieving a maximum capacity of 555.6 mg/g through π–π stacking, n–π interactions, and hydrogen bonding, whereas excessive loading (5 wt%) caused nanosheet aggregation and reduced performance. The smallest GO (1 nm) enhanced compressive strength (546 kPa) but slightly decreased adsorption capacity due to disruption of π–π interactions. GO10-3 exhibited the most balanced properties, combining high adsorption capacity (416.7 mg/g) and strong adsorbate affinity (KL = 0.387 L/mg). Spectroscopic and structural analyses (ATR-FTIR, XRD, Raman, XPS, SEM) confirmed successful cross-linking, optimized pore structure, and improved mechanical integrity. Adsorption followed Langmuir isotherm and PSO kinetics, and > 93% of the removal efficiency was maintained after five cycles, highlighting excellent reusability. This study uniquely integrates a systematic evaluation of both GO loading and particle size, establishing direct structure–property–performance relationships for CMC–GO aerogels. By correlating interlayer spacing, interfacial bonding, and rheological behavior with adsorption capacity and reusability, this work provides design guidelines for achieving both high efficiency and mechanical robustness. These findings highlight the potential of CMC–GO aerogels as a sustainable platform for advanced wastewater treatment applications.
