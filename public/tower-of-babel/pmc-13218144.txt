Short-term intravenous fluids for prevention of post-ERCP pancreatitis (the STRIPE study): protocol for a five-arm randomised controlled trial
Authors: Nauzer Forbes, Howard Guo, Ashlee Kruk, Sara Ficaccio, Shane Cartwright, Megan Howarth, Getanshu Malik, Paul J Nietert, Zachary L Smith, Suqing Li, Yen-I Chen, Natalia Causada Calo, Frances Tse, Jennifer J Telford, Deborah J Cook, Michael D Hill, B Joseph Elmunzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPost-endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) pancreatitis (PEP) is a significant procedural adverse event (AE), occurring in 5–15% of cases and leading to substantial morbidity and mortality. Aggressive prolonged intravenous (IV) fluid regimens have demonstrated efficacy in reducing PEP in clinical trials. However, these regimens typically involve continuous infusion of IV fluids over 8–24 hours following ERCP, making them impractical for outpatient settings. Data on shorter hydration protocols are lacking. The STRIPE study aims to address this gap by evaluating short-term peri-procedural IV fluid regimens as a practical alternative for mitigating PEP.Methods and analysisThis proof-of-concept, parallel-arm, randomised controlled trial will evaluate the impact of various short-term IV fluid regimens on post-ERCP serum amylase levels, a surrogate marker for PEP. Participants undergoing ERCP will be randomised into five groups, receiving 500 mL, 1000 mL, 1500 mL, 2000 mL or 2500 mL of IV Ringer’s lactate during the peri-procedural period. Patients, endoscopists and outcome assessors will be blinded to treatment allocation during the peri-procedural period. The primary outcome is the serum amylase level 24 hours post-ERCP. Secondary outcomes include PEP, 30-day AEs and unplanned healthcare encounters including those related to volume overload or cardiovascular AEs, duration of hospitalisation (for inpatients), death within 30 days and other relevant laboratory markers at 24 hours. A total of 505 participants (101 in each arm) will be enrolled to ensure adequate power after accounting for attrition and/or sample loss.Ethics and disseminationThis trial was registered on clinicaltrials.gov on 7 February 2024. The study was approved by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB23-0625). On study completion, data will be made available on reasonable request to the corresponding author after completion of the study. Study dissemination and knowledge translation is planned via presentations at scholarly meetings, publications in peer-reviewed journals and, ideally, via adoption of results into clinical practice guidelines.Trial registration numberNCT06260878.Keywords: Clinical Trial, Endoscopy, Fluid Therapy

Introduction

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is a widely used procedure for diagnosing and managing biliary and pancreatic diseases, with a stable high usage rate in the past two decades despite the rise of alternate techniques.1 2 Despite its critical and ubiquitous role, ERCP is associated with among the highest adverse event (AE) rates among commonly performed endoscopic procedures, with post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP) being the most frequent and serious complication.3,6 Affecting 5–15% of cases, PEP results in significant patient morbidity, prolonged hospitalisations and substantial healthcare costs.7 Severe cases of PEP can lead to intensive care unit admission, additional endoscopic and surgical interventions and a mortality rate of approximately 1 in 500 patients.6 8

Currently, three evidence-based methods are widely available and recommended to prevent PEP: pharmacologic prophylaxis via rectal non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), intra-procedural pancreatic duct stenting and aggressive prolonged post-procedural intravenous (IV) hydration.9 10 However, each approach has limitations. Rectal NSAIDs have been consistently shown to be underused, with usage rates below 50% even among high-risk patients, due to multiple factors including cost variability, endoscopist usual care and perceived and actual contraindications and AEs.11,14 Pancreatic duct stenting is equally or more efficacious in reducing PEP compared with NSAIDs, but has been demonstrated to be performed in less than 7% of cases due to technical challenges, prolonged procedure times and follow-up requirements.14 15 Aggressive IV hydration has also demonstrated efficacy in reducing PEP, but all efficacious regimens demonstrated to date rely on continuous infusion of fluids over 8–24 hours following ERCP, which is impractical for outpatient settings.16 Short-term peri-procedural IV hydration protocols offer a potentially effective, safe and economical alternative to mitigate PEP risk, improve outcomes and reduce healthcare costs. Even though short-term IV fluid boluses around the time of ERCP are commonly administered across multiple practice settings, evidence to support their use is completely lacking to date.

Given the clinical and societal impacts of PEP, several organisations and investigators continue to prioritise the design and conduct of studies aiming to reduce its risk. However, conducting studies on PEP prevention presents unique challenges, especially given the very large sample sizes required to detect meaningful reductions in PEP incidence within the confines of a well-designed randomised controlled trial (RCT).17 18 However, a recent framework proposes the use of surrogate endpoints such as post-ERCP serum amylase levels, which correlate with PEP risk, to enable smaller proof-of-concept trials.19 The main premise of the framework is that post-ERCP amylase levels act as an intermediate biomarker that can predict PEP and serve as a surrogate endpoint in such studies. To develop the framework, a meta-analysis of 45 RCTs was performed, and it was determined that the relative risk of PEP was significantly correlated with the post-procedural amylase effect size.19 This observation is mechanistically plausible as hyperamylasemia is a relatively consistent reflection of pancreatic injury and is part of the definition of acute pancreatitis, but is not specific enough to be, in itself, diagnostic of the condition.

Therefore, relying on this framework, the short-term randomised intravenous peri-procedural hydration in ERCP (STRIPE) investigators seek to evaluate the relative efficacies of five short-term peri-procedural IV fluid regimens in reducing post-ERCP amylase elevation. The secondary aims are to evaluate the safety associated with these fluid regimens by assessing both immediate and delayed AEs up to 30 days following ERCP. We hypothesise that higher short-term volumes of IV fluid in the peri-endoscopic period will demonstrate protective effects against PEP up to an optimal threshold. The goal of the proposed pilot RCT is to identify the most promising regimens for further evaluation in a fully powered RCT focused on PEP as the primary outcome. If no clear benefits are observed, the findings may indicate the potential futility and/or risks of this widely employed yet currently unsupported practice.

Methods and analysis

Study design, setting, and intervention

This RCT protocol is reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2025 Statement.20 Trial funders have provided input on study design to date but will have no input on its conduct, analysis or reporting going forward. The STRIPE study is a parallel-arm RCT that will compare various peri-procedural IV fluid volumes for patients undergoing ERCP. All patients will undergo ERCP as part of standard care, with interventional arms defined by the total peri-procedural IV Ringer’s lactate (RL) volumes: 500 mL, 1000 mL, 1500 mL, 2000 mL and 2500 mL. The 500 mL IV RL arm will be considered the control group. The interventional arms in our study were chosen to be representative of ‘real-world’ pragmatic regimens that could be applied in routine practice. RL was chosen over normal saline given its favourable physiologic and acid/base profiles, evidence of superiority in treating acute pancreatitis and preventing severe outcomes.21 All fluids will be administered in the immediate peri-procedural period, which will be defined as the window commencing 1 hour prior to the start of the ERCP procedure and terminating 1.5 hours after ERCP has ended. As of submission of this protocol, the trial is active at a single tertiary care centre in Calgary, AB, Canada, and expansion is underway to two additional tertiary care endoscopy centres in Montreal, QC, and Toronto, ON.

Study population

Patients aged 18–75 years with native major papillary anatomy, capable of providing informed consent and willing to obtain post-ERCP bloodwork will be eligible for inclusion. The upper age restriction was implemented to minimise the risk of undiagnosed cardiac or renal comorbidities in older individuals. Patients will be excluded if they meet any of the following criteria: prior ERCP with sphincterotomy or sphincteroplasty, planned additional endoscopic procedures alongside ERCP, confirmed or suspected cholangitis or sepsis, confirmed pancreatitis, hyperlipasemia or hyperamylasemia within the 7 days prior to enrolment, New York Heart Association Class II or higher heart failure, active pulmonary oedema, recent myocardial infarction or ischaemia within the preceding 3 months, renal insufficiency with creatinine clearance <40 mL/min, lack of recent available creatinine and electrolyte measurements, Child-Pugh B or C cirrhosis and/or end-stage liver disease, oxygen saturation <90% or a requirement for home oxygen, hypernatremia (≥150 mEq/L) or hyponatremia (<130 mEq/L), uncontrolled hypertension or hypotension or pregnancy.

Participant recruitment

Patients scheduled for ERCP will be referred to the endoscopy unit per standard care protocols. As part of routine research procedures, all referred patients will receive a ‘consent to approach’ form as part of their check-in package. This form will be administered by the admitting nurse during the patient’s admission into the endoscopy unit. In parallel, a research assistant (RA) will have reviewed the patient’s records to determine eligibility for the study based on eligibility criteria. If a patient consents for our research team to approach them and their corresponding signed form is received, the RA will provide detailed information about the study, including its objectives, risks, benefits, and additional responsibilities associated with enrolment. All questions posed by the patient will be addressed thoroughly during this process. If the patient agrees to participate, a written informed consent form (online supplemental material) will be reviewed, signed, and dated by the patient before any study-related procedures are initiated. The original signed consent form will be securely retained at the study centre, and a copy will be provided to the patient. Should a patient choose not to participate, the ERCP procedure will proceed as per standard care, with no further study-related interventions applied and no impact on their care. As of the date of this revised submission, 150 patients have been enrolled into the study and completed 30-day follow-up.

Randomisation and allocation

Patients meeting eligibility criteria and providing informed consent will be randomised in a 1:1:1:1:1 ratio to one of five interventional arms: (1) 500 mL of IV RL, (2) 1000 mL of IV RL, (3) 1500 mL of IV RL, (4) 2000 mL of IV RL or (5) 2500 mL of IV RL. Confidential random number allocation will be accessed via a pre-populated randomisation list that is housed securely in all participating hospital-based investigational pharmacy units. Permuted block randomisation will be used with blocks of 10, ensuring equal distribution among the five treatment arms. No stratification is planned in order to favour randomness of allocation and prevent small bin sizes within strata.

Study period and sequence, blinding and potential unblinding

For enrolled patients, the RA will collect pre-procedural data through a combination of direct patient interviews and medical record reviews. Once enrolment has taken place, the corresponding pre-randomised study kit will be retrieved. Each centre’s investigational pharmacy will be responsible for preparing and disseminating kits, with centralised oversight from the coordinating centre. An unblinded nurse will open the kit, read the instructions and set up the patient’s IV line as would be part of standard pre-ERCP care. The nurse will take special note of the assigned IV RL volume provided in the kit and will hang the IV bag (or the first of the IV bags) provided. The bag(s), once hung, will then be concealed with an opaque black bag so that patients and endoscopists are blinded, as shown in figure 1.

Once a patient is transported into the ERCP suite, intra-procedural nurses will be made aware of study allocation and will immediately transfer the running IV bag onto a setup designed to maintain endoscopist blinding throughout the procedure. This setup will make use of pre-existing procedural monitors that have been adapted with additional opaque barriers, to avoid any additional space requirements, as shown in figure 2. In addition, there will be an intra-procedural alarm that will ring every 15 min. When the alarm rings, the sedating nurse will stand up behind the barrier for a minimum of 1 min and either perform an IV bag change, if required, or do nothing. Thus, this alarm will mitigate the risk of endoscopists being made aware of allocation based on whether intra-procedural IV changes are needed. Once the procedure is complete, the opaque black bag will be placed back onto the IV pole as the patient is transported back into the recovery room. At the end of the procedure, the endoscopist’s blinding will be assessed using James’ Blinding Index and recorded.22 Other aspects of peri-procedural ERCP care and PEP mitigation, including the choice to administer NSAIDs (and timing thereof) and/or the choice to place a prophylactic pancreatic stent, will be left to the discretion of the performing endoscopist.

Outpatients will have 24-hour post-ERCP bloodwork (±8 hours) arranged through a home visit by a private partner, while inpatients will have bloodwork ordered within their electronic medical records. Follow-up will involve a 30-day telephone call by the RA to assess symptoms, AEs, and any unplanned healthcare encounters, which will be corroborated by reviewing the patient’s medical records for emergency department visits or admissions. Participants may withdraw from the study at any time without prejudice, in line with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. Investigators may also withdraw participants to prioritise safety or if regulatory authorities terminate the study. Withdrawal reasons will be documented, and data collected up to the point of withdrawal may be retained if permitted by the Informed Consent Form. Lost-to-follow-up participants will receive three documented contact attempts before being considered withdrawn. The primary outcome is complete when 24-hour bloodwork is obtained, and the study concludes after the final participant’s 30-day follow-up is completed.

For most enrolled patients, RL will already be running at a ‘wide open’ rate through the intravenous line for the duration of ERCP. However, in the uncommon event of intra-procedural or post-procedural hypotension or other haemodynamic instability, endoscopists and/or anaesthetists can break protocol (and will accordingly be unblinded) to administer resuscitative efforts as required, which may include additional fluids and/or aborting ERCP. The same applies to post-procedural pain, haemodynamic or respiratory instability, and other concerns. Per-protocol analyses will be performed to address any potential crossovers. Endoscopists who performed the index ERCP will not be permitted to participate in patient care after ERCP, ensuring equal post-procedure care and co-interventions between study groups. In these cases, clinical care will be handled by establishing an ‘on-call’ rotation of treating physicians for study patients, as was successfully employed for a recent ERCP trial.18 This also mitigates the risk of introducing bias from unblinding due to receiving results on electronic medical records. Outcome assessors will be blinded to treatment allocation.

Data management

The study will leverage the Calgary Registry for Advanced and Therapeutic Endoscopy (CReATE), a prospective high-fidelity data collective designed to support prospective ERCP outcomes research, including trials. In addition to existing operationality at the coordinating centre, CReATE is also active at the two additional sites currently being prepared for expansion. Data entry is performed by RAs in real-time peri- and intra-procedurally and stored on a secure encrypted web-based platform specialising in healthcare data management.

A comprehensive range of variables will be routinely collected as part of this RCT. Patient-related variables will include age, sex, comorbidities, relevant medications, surgical history and social history. Endoscopist-related variables will include procedural volumes, experience, trainee involvement and the degree of procedural supervision. Procedure-related variables will include the procedural indication, relevant anatomy, procedural details (eg, papilla morphology, relevant findings, cannulation attempts and procedural time), instruments and devices used, stent placement, procedural success and any intraprocedural AEs. Post-procedural variables will include patient pain and satisfaction scores.23 24

Outcomes

The primary outcome for this pilot RCT is a serum amylase level greater than 3× the upper limit of normal, measured 24 hours after the ERCP procedure. Secondary outcomes include the occurrence of overall and specific AEs within 30 days, including PEP, cardiorespiratory events (including symptomatic fluid overload or congestive heart failure), bleeding and cholangitis or sepsis. Additional secondary outcomes will include any unplanned healthcare encounters (ie, emergency department visits or hospital admissions) within 30 days, along with their causes, length of hospital admission (if applicable) and death. In addition, other 24-hour laboratory parameters will constitute the remaining secondary outcomes, including lipase, electrolytes, creatinine and brain natriuretic peptide. In the case of lipase and amylase, these outcomes will be assessed as continuous variables in secondary analyses (in addition to as a binary outcome in the primary analysis). Post-ERCP AEs will be classified according to the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Lexicon and assigned causality ratings using a Delphi-based framework.25 26 Bloodwork for the primary and some secondary outcomes will be considered valid if collected at 24±8 hours from the time of completion of ERCP. Patient and public involvement

During the development phase of STRIPE, patient partnership was actively sought to ensure that its design and execution are aligned with patient needs and perspectives. A focus group was conducted between the research team and two patient partners, identifying priorities in study design from the patient perspective. Considerations included acceptability of the interventions, the enrolment and follow-up process and preferences regarding potential outcomes (all described in various sections of this proposal). These efforts demonstrate a strong commitment to incorporate patient voices.

Sample size calculation and statistical analyses

As discussed, our estimated sample sizes rely on a recently published framework based on a meta-analysis of 45 RCTs.19 According to this framework, the required sample size is 91 patients per arm to demonstrate an effect size in amylase of −0.50, which corresponds to a relative risk of PEP of 0.51. Accounting for attrition and/or sample missingness of 10% results in 505 patients needing to be enrolled (101 per arm) if one assumes a Bonferroni-adjusted alpha level of 0.0125, to account for four hypothesis tests. Proportions will be compared using two-tailed Fisher’s exact tests where five or fewer patients have a particular outcome in any group, whereas chi-squared tests will be used to compare proportions where each group has over five patients.

The primary hypothesis is based on superiority of one regimen over the other(s). Pairwise comparisons between groups will be performed based on the proportions of patients experiencing the binary outcome of an amylase level 3× the upper limit of normal or greater at 24 hours following ERCP. The primary comparator will be the lowest dose tier, and the primary contrast will be the highest to lowest dose, with pairwise comparisons between all other arms being performed as well. The primary analysis will be conducted on the intention-to-treat (as randomised) population and will be adjusted for key baseline variables (age, sex, site and procedural indication). Following this, a supportive analysis will adjust for additional variables including disposition, procedural difficulty,27 site, NSAID use and other key intraprocedural variables.6 Comparisons will be reported using risk ratios and numbers needed to treat. In a secondary analysis, differences in mean amylase levels among treatment groups will be estimated using analysis of covariance, treating the amylase level as a continuous (rather than a binary) outcome. Though not powered as a dose-response study, this analysis will enable exploration of a dose-response relationship, as previously described.28 All laboratory values will be standardised according to local upper limits of normal.

Outcomes will also be compared in a per-protocol analysis including all patients randomised without protocol deviations, and in a safety analysis including patients receiving any dose. Subgroup analyses will be performed to assess heterogeneity in treatment effects, as described. A priori subgroups will include: age, sex, disposition, procedural difficulty,27 site, weight/body mass index, Charlson comorbidity index, pancreatic vs biliary indications and choledocholithiasis vs other indications, in addition to other established risk factors for PEP.6 Post hoc subgroup analyses will be considered exploratory and not planned for multiplicity adjustment. Specific analyses of key prognostic variables (subgroups) including site will be assessed for treatment effect modification by including a multiplicative interaction term (variable-by-treatment) within the multivariable model. All hypotheses will be assessed at alpha=0.05 and all tests will be two-sided. No formal interim efficacy or futility analyses are planned, but safety analyses will be performed after 25%, 50% and 75% of the sample size has been followed up, with stopping criteria.

We will make best efforts to collect complete information on covariates. The missingness of any covariate is not expected to exceed 10%. In cases of missing data, if missingness is deemed at random or completely at random, we will use multiple imputation with chained equations for imputing the values. If missingness is not at random, we will estimate bias and adjust analyses. We will conduct sensitivity analyses of complete case analysis and those with imputed cases. Any enrolled patients who do not complete 24-hour laboratory measurements will be excluded from final analyses, with a 10% attrition rate built into sample size calculations, as explained above.

Data safety and monitoring

The trial will be guided by a Steering Committee charged with design, execution, analysis and interpretation, composed of the primary investigator, co-investigators and study site leads. The committee has had meetings leading up to this submission to refine methodology and discuss site-specific considerations. During execution, the Committee will continue to gather input from site leads, staff and the central research coordinator, and meet by teleconference every 90 days. They will also review input from the Data Safety and Monitoring Board (DSMB). An independent DSMB will monitor the trial and convene after 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of planned enrolment has occurred. Safety data will be reviewed at each DSMB meeting. The DSMB consists of three individuals independent of the investigators. Goals of the DSMB include (1) identifying protocol violations possibly requiring changes, (2) ensuring validity of results and (3) protecting participant safety. If any irregularities are identified, the DSMB can propose protocol changes or study termination.

Discussion

There is an urgent and unmet need to determine whether short-term peri-procedural IV fluids impact the rate of PEP, which is a significant source of patient morbidity and mortality as well as substantial healthcare-related costs. The STRIPE study seeks to establish a foundation for a transformative RCT which could have significant implications, regardless of its outcome. Its primary objective is to provide evidence on pragmatic peri-procedural IV fluid regimens in ERCP, aiming to reduce patient morbidity and mortality while minimising futility and waste. We fully acknowledge that reliance on a surrogate outcome is not ideal. However, given the challenges outlined in adequately powering trials for PEP as an outcome, and the relatively unexplored area we are studying, we still feel that this is the best solution by which to move forward in this line of inquiry. Similarly, while the inclusion of five study arms could be considered overly ambitious and could result in the dilution of power, we again feel this is appropriate given the exploratory dose-finding nature of our question. If one or more IV fluid regimens in STRIPE are shown to significantly reduce post-ERCP amylase levels, the next step would be to evaluate these regimens via a fully powered, multi-centre RCT powered for PEP as the primary outcome. Such a study would be supported by the justification of biological plausibility and the demonstrated positive effects on surrogate markers of PEP. Equally importantly, if no IV fluid regimen demonstrates a clear impact on post-ERCP amylase levels, the findings would encourage clinicians to take pause regarding this commonly employed but unsupported practice. This could instead help redirect focus toward increasing the adoption of other proven PEP mitigation strategies.

Ethics and dissemination

All research described herein is being/will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. The STRIPE study was approved by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB23-0625) and local ethics approval will be required prior to expanding to secondary sites. Data are stored on a secure encrypted web-based platform specialising in healthcare data management. On study completion, data will be made available on reasonable request to the corresponding author after completion of the study. Study dissemination and knowledge translation is planned via presentations at scholarly meetings, publications in peer-reviewed journals and, ideally, via adoption of results into clinical practice guidelines.
