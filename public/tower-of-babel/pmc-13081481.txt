Proportions of patients meeting definitions for progressive disease or improvement should be included in clinical trials for systemic sclerosis interstitial lung disease
Authors: Kathleen Morrisroe, Donald Tashkin, Murray Baron
Published: 2026

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) affects around 50–80% of patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc) and is associated with significant morbidity, mortality and healthcare utilisation [1–3]. In approximately 10–15% of SSc-ILD patients, a continuous and progressive decline in lung function occurs and is associated with substantial mortality [1, 2]. The FDA has accepted a decline in FVC as a surrogate for SSc-ILD progression [4] and mortality in fibrotic lung disease including SSc-ILD [5] to be used in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) (Table 1).

In this editorial, we will propose that trials should report on both the mean change in spirometric parameters of lung function over the treatment period and on the proportion of patients in the treatment and control groups who experienced a statistically significant and clinically meaningful decline, improvement or stability in pulmonary function. We highlight the importance of such binary outcome data as necessary for the calculation of the number needed to treat (NNT), thereby supporting physicians in the clinic in evaluating the suitability of trial drugs to individual patients. This will enhance shared decision-making, and facilitate real-world translation of clinical trial findings. Although the prevention of spirometric decline, as measured by the differences of mean changes in pulmonary function measures, may for now continue to be the primary endpoint of clinical trials, we suggest that an assessment of binary outcome data be included in all such trials as key secondary outcomes.

Immunosuppressive or anti-fibrotic medications are now commonly recommended for the treatment of SSc-ILD based on RCT evidence of efficacy [5]. However, not all patients will experience a decrease in FVC during the trials and therefore the question that requires addressing is what are the chances that any particular patient will obtain any benefit. Although our ability to predict decline in individual patients is very limited [16], from RCT data it is possible to derive an expected progression rate from the control group that would apply to any patient that meets the entry criteria for the study. Although this data is not perfect, it is the best we have and could be used to develop NNT data.

To contextualize our editorial, we have summarized the SSc-ILD landmark trials to date in Table 1. The majority of these studies included SSc patients early in their disease course (< 7years from disease onset) and included a change in continuous measures of FVC, either as a %predicted FVC or mean decline in FVC (ml), as their primary endpoint over a 24- or 52-week time period. To understand the chances of progression or improvement in an individual patient requires the addition of a binary definition of progression or improvement. Incorporation of binary outcomes of lung function decline or improvement in clinical trials would require the inclusion of at least one definition of progression or improvement. At present there is no consensus on what is considered clinically significant disease progression in SSc-ILD. Until there is consensus, clinical trialists might choose to assess a number of binary outcomes as secondary endpoints and leave the decision to the clinicians to decide on how to present that data to the patient. Some examples of definitions of progression from the literature, summarized in Table 2, include the ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT [17] definition, INBUILD trial definition [18], and the SENSCIS trial definition [8].which include various percent changes in spirometric parameters over time (predominantly FVC) with or without worsening extent of fibrosis on high-resolution CT lung and /or worsening respiratory symptoms.

No matter which definition of progression is used, such information will allow the clinician to advise the patient who meets the entry criteria that, if untreated, they have an X% chance of progressing or improving in the next number of months (the duration of the trial) by comparison with those on an active drug who had a Y% chance. This information would allow us to assess the NNT to prevent disease progression or result in improvement. This information could be used to inform the clinician and patient regarding their shared decision whether the patient is willing to be prescribed treatment in the expectation of a specific chance of reduced risk of disease progression or an increased chance of improvement.

Binary outcome research is nicely illustrated in the osteoporotic literature, where the primary outcome is the incidence of new fractures over a certain time frame [19, 20]. Studies in osteoporosis have determined that use of the most active drug will reduce the incidence of new osteoporotic fractures during the trial period by about 40% [21, 22]. Epidemiologic studies have looked at fracture risk factors in the general population and it is now possible to estimate the 10-year risk of an osteoporotic fracture in individual patients using calculators such as the fracture risk assessment tool (FRAX) [23]. This concept has also supported recommendations by various national osteoporosis societies about whom to treat based on the individual’s fracture risk, the reduction in risk provided by medication and health economic assessments [19, 20, 24, 25].

To highlight the importance of incorporating binary data in SSc-ILD RCTs, we will show the clinical utility of providing binary data based on information from four landmark SSc-ILD trials. It is important to note that the data provided below is an exploratory analysis rather than a confirmatory analysis as we are interpreting post hoc data. First, in a post hoc retrospective analysis of the DESIRES trial [26], the proportion of patients who experienced an absolute decrease in FVC% of ≥ 5% at 24 weeks was lower in the rituximab group versus the placebo group (4% [1/25] vs. 39% [9/23]; P= 0.0038) [26]. This use of binary analysis indicates that rituximab would reduce the likelihood of significant worsening of FVC% by approximately 35%, equivalent to a NNT of 3. Second, using data from the Scleroderma Lung Study (SLS) trials I and II [6, 15] (Fig. 1A), we determined that 36% of those on placebo had a decline over 1 year of ≥ 5% in FVC versus 28.8%% of those on cyclophosphamide. This result was not significant by chi-square assessment so that further calculation of NNT to prevent disease progression is not warranted. Similarly, there was no significant difference between drug and placebo for the proportions who improved by > 5%. Third, Fig. 1B summarizes data to assess the benefit of mycophenolate (MMF) versus placebo from a study that incorporated data from both SLS I and SLS II [7]. We estimated that 33% of those on placebo had a decline over 1 year of > 5% in FVC versus 10% of those on MMF, a difference that was significant with a p-value of 0.001. The calculated NNT to treat in this population to prevent that degree of decline in FVC is 4.4, indicating that for every 100 patients treated, 23 will benefit. The same analysis can be made for improvement of FVC > 5%. 32% of those on MMF had an improvement over 1 year of > 5% in FVC versus 13.5% of those on placebo, a difference that was significant at p = 0.002. The calculated NNT in this population to promote that degree of improvement in FVC is about 5.4, indicating that for every 100 patients treated, 18.5 will benefit. Finally, in the SENSCIS trial [8], 29% of the control group and 20.6% of the nintedanib arm experienced a decline of ≥ 5% in FVC. Translating this to the clinical setting, if you can predict that only 29% of your SSc-ILD patients will experience a ≥ 5% decline in FVC, then of all patients you treat you will prevent ≥ 5% decline in 8.4% of patients with a NNT of 11. Notably this trial found no difference between nintedanib and placebo in the proportions who declined by ≥ 10% in FVC.

Categorization of data into binary outcomes is not without limitations. One limitation is that for any binary outcome based on a cutoff of a continuous variable, there will be patients who fall very close to the cutoff value. For example, with a binary outcome of ≥ 5%, a decline of 4.9% in FVC would not be considered progression whereas a decline in FVC of 5.1% would be. Furthermore, we note that starting in our early twenties there is an average decline of 20–26 ml per year in FVC [7] further highlighting the difficulties in determining exact binary cut offs in FVC when analyzed as a relative decline. On the other hand, it is noteworthy that a 20–26 ml age-related decline in FVC (ml)/yr is quite small in relation to a disease-related decline in FVC over 12 months of as much as 5 or 10% from the baseline FVC (ml). Additionally, since current definitions of ILD progression are determined at 12-month or 24-month time points, using a binary outcome measure for an outcome during a 24-week trial may underestimate the number defined as progressors who may become a progressor over the following 6-months.

This editorial is not advocating for the sole use of binary outcomes data in clinical trials and practice, but rather proposing that its inclusion in trials would be helpful in interpreting trial outcome data from a clinical standpoint. A difference in the mean change of FVC between treated and placebo groups tells us that the drug is biologically active but does not tell us how useful the drug will be for individual patients. Furthermore, although we are not suggesting that a decline or improvement in FVC % predicted of ≥ 5% is the only significant definition of progression that should be used in trials, the concept of the importance of binary outcomes is clinically relevant no matter what percent change is used when communicating with other specialists involved in SSc-ILD treatment, especially when discussing a specific treatments chances of benefits and risks with a patient.

In summary, incorporating binary outcome data into SSc-ILD clinical trials will facilitate the translation of trial findings into real-world decision-making. Such data will allow the estimation of an individual patient’s likelihood of benefiting from an active therapy through the calculation of NNT values. In the future, these binary outcomes may also enable researchers to perform more robust health-economic evaluations - similar to approaches used in osteoporosis research - and thereby support more informed and rational recommendations regarding the use of these medications.
