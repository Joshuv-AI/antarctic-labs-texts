Quantum Advantage via Solving Multivariate Polynomials
Pierre Briaud, Itai Dinur, Riddhi Ghosal, Aayush Jain, Paul Lou, Amit Sahai

Abstract
In this work, we propose a new way to (non-interactively, verifiably) demonstrate quantum advantage by solving the average-case 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} search problem of finding a solution to a system of (underdetermined) constant degree multivariate equations over the finite field 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} drawn from a specified distribution. In particular, for any d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , we design a distribution of degree up to d d polynomials { p i ​ ( x 1 , … , x n ) } i ∈ [ m ] \{p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\}_{i\in[m]} for m < n m<n over 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} for which we show that there is a expected polynomial-time quantum algorithm that provably simultaneously solves { p i ( x 1 , … , x n ) = y i } i ∈ [ m ] \{p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=y_{i}\}_{i\in[m]} for a random vector ( y 1 , … , y m ) (y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}) . On the other hand, while solutions exist with high probability, we conjecture that for constant d > 2 d>2 , it is classically hard to find one based on a thorough review of existing classical cryptanalysis. Our work thus posits that degree three functions are enough to instantiate the random oracle to obtain non-relativized quantum advantage. Our approach begins with the breakthrough Yamakawa-Zhandry (FOCS 2022) quantum algorithmic framework. In our work, we demonstrate that this quantum algorithmic framework extends to the setting of multivariate polynomial systems. Our key technical contribution is a new analysis on the Fourier spectra of distributions induced by a general family of distributions over 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} multivariate polynomials—those that satisfy 2 2 -wise independence and shift-invariance. This family of distributions includes the distribution of uniform random degree at most d d polynomials for any constant d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . Our analysis opens up potentially new directions for quantum cryptanalysis of other multivariate systems.

Since Shor’s seminal result

Outside the context of

In our work, we will crucially build upon this algorithmic framework, albeit in a very different setting.

We study (conjectured) classically hard average-case NP search problems in the area of solving systems of multivariate polynomial equations.

Multivariate cryptography has been widely accepted by the cryptographic community as a post-quantum form of cryptography, positing that even quantum algorithms cannot efficiently solve the underlying equations. This belief, however, can be more accurately attributed to the general lack of suitable quantum algorithmic techniques. Indeed, our work provides the first evidence challenging this general belief that classically hard multivariate systems are also quantumly hard, by demonstrating quantum advantage for a family of random multivariate equations with degree at least 3.

In this work, we give an efficient polynomial-time quantum algorithm that provably solves certain families of multivariate polynomial systems, which we will shortly describe. The solutions to these equations generally do not have any nice periodic structure to exploit, nor do we discover any such periodic structure to aid our quantum algorithm. As such, our problem stands apart from previous non-oracle-based quantum advantage results for average-case
\mathsf{NP}
search problems. We conjecture that this problem is hard to solve using classical methods by analyzing standard solvers for multivariate equations and identifying several features of the equations that could be leveraged for cryptanalysis.

Note that, due to the differences between our equations and those typically considered in multivariate cryptography, we do not expect a direct impact of our work in this particular area. However, our work still offers a fresh perspective on the quantum solving of structured multivariate equations, which may still be of cryptanalytic interest. Indeed, multivariate systems arise in various cryptanalytic contexts, such as solving the Learning With Errors (LWE) problem via the Arora-Ge approach
[

[

On Classical Hardness.

We argue that quantum advantage for this problem is achievable when
d\geq 3
, based on our analysis of classical solvers.

First, we explain why the problem is easy when the first set of constraints contains polynomials of degree
d=2
instead of
d\geq 3
by giving a specific polynomial-time algorithm that operates through clever specialization, emphasizing that we are only interested in one of the exponentially many solutions to the multivariate system. We then demonstrate that this algorithm, as well as more general variants reminiscent of algorithms used for the solving of underdetermined multivariate quadratic (MQ) systems
[
TW12
,
FNT21
,
Has23
]
, become ineffective when
d\geq 3
.

We complete our analysis by examining standard classical solvers. Among the main algorithms used in cryptanalysis for solving Boolean systems – specifically, optimized variants of exhaustive search
[
BCC+10
]
, the polynomial method
[
LPT+17
,
BKW19
,
Din21b
,
Din21a
]
, and Gröbner bases
[
Fau99
,
Fau02
,
BFP09
,
BFSS13
,
BDT22
]
– we focus on the latter. Indeed, Gröbner basis methods are arguably the most challenging to analyze when applied to structured equations, and thus potentially the most threatening. Since our system is highly underdetermined, it is standard to reduce the number of solutions from exponential to approximately constant before applying these methods. If the initial system were unstructured, or if the resulting overdetermined system had lost some of its initial structure through specialization, we would not be concerned about the cost of Gröbner basis algorithms, as they would then also apply to an unstructured degree-
d
system with
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
equations and
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
variables. However, one might worry that the structure of our equations may contradict this observation or even be leveraged. We provide experimental results and analysis indicating that, to the best of our knowledge, our structure does not enable classical polynomial-time algorithms. By that, we mean that (1) these standard solvers are not expected to run in polynomial time due to the structure of Reed-Solomon code, and (2) it does not seem easy to leverage the efficient decoding procedure to construct other types of efficient solvers.

On Cryptanalytic Impact.

In terms of existing quantum algorithms for solving multivariate polynomial systems that differ from Grover-based exhaustive search
[
SW16
,
Pri18
]
, the work of Faugère et al.
[
FHK+17
]
gives a faster exponential-time quantum algorithm for solving Boolean quadratic equations, an algorithm that requires on average the evaluation of
\mathcal{O}(2^{0.462n})
quantum gates. Towards the same goal, Chen and Gao
[
CG17
]
proposed a solver that leverages the Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) algorithm
[
HHL09
]
as a subroutine, with a cost that is polynomial in
n
and in the condition number of the linear system to which HHL is applied. However, a more recent work
[
DGG+23
]
has shown that this condition number is typically extremely high and that the algorithm of
[
CG17
]
does not outperform a Grover-based exhaustive search. Note that all these algorithms apply to general multivariate systems. In particular, to the best of our knowledge, no quantum polynomial-time algorithms have been found for possibly more structured ones that are hard to solve classically. Our system provides the first counterexample.

The structure of our equations does not resemble the systems typically encountered in the cryptanalysis of multivariate schemes. They are neither all quadratic, nor similar to the higher-degree equations used in MinRank-based attacks, nor to other types of systems commonly considered in this area. In addition, in the current literature, the equation systems are either overdetermined or mildly underdetermined (e.g. by a constant factor), whereas ours is even more underdetermined (by a polynomial factor). For these reasons, we do not claim any advancement in the quantum cryptanalysis of multivariate cryptography. Nevertheless, our work introduces a new non-generic method of quantumly attacking conjecturably classically hard multivariate systems of equations, offering a fresh perspective and motivation for further work on quantum solving algorithms for structured algebraic systems.

Another Perspective: The Complexity of Instantiating the Yamakawa-Zhandry Random Oracle.

Another perspective on our work is to think about the problem of un-relativizing Yamakawa-Zhandry: that is, to propose a concrete distribution of simple functions that can be used as a substitute for the random oracle to establish quantum advantage. In particular, our proposal gives a way to replace the Yamakawa-Zhandry constraints that call a random oracle with cubic constraints over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. This is tight, in that substituting the random oracle-based constraint with linear constraints would yield systems of equations that are trivial to solve classically, and we also show non-trivial attacks when the random oracle is replaced with certain family of quadratic constraints.

1.1
Technical Overview

The major technical goal of our paper is to give a quantum algorithm that solves a system of multivariate equations sampled from a distribution satisfying certain properties that we conjecture to be classically hard. For simpler exposition, we will describe our algorithm for a concrete multivariate system. Our result can be generalized to a large family of polynomials. We will briefly mention it at the end of this section.

As described above, we have a polynomial system
\mathcal{P}
with
n^{2}
many multivariate degree
d>2
(constant) equations

\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n^{2}}\}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{n^{2},n}],

and with
(1-\alpha)n^{3}
many linear constraints over the
n^{3}
variables given by
\overline{\mathbf{H}}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{0}
, where
\overline{\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq(x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{n^{2},n})
. We often replace
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
with simply
\mathbf{x}
; throughout this paper we will overload notation and interchangeably use
\mathbf{x}
to denote an element of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
when it is clear from context.

The linear constraints ensure that if some
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n^{3}}
satisfies the entire system, then
\mathbf{y}
interpreted as
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}
must also satisfy a set of linear constraints
\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
.
Our choice of
\overline{\mathbf{H}}
is such that the corresponding
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{((1-\alpha)n^{2})\times n^{2}}
is a parity-check matrix for a dual Reed-Solomon code
C
over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
with length
n^{2}
and rank
\alpha n^{2}
.

The breakthrough work of Yamakawa and Zhandry
[
YZ22
]
deals with a related problem in the random oracle model, and crucially uses the properties of random oracles to argue that their quantum algorithm will succeed. Despite these differences from our setting, we (successfully) attempt to carry out their algorithmic framework, albeit with a very different analysis utilizing structural properties of solutions to multivariate polynomials in place of properties of random oracles exploited by Yamakawa and Zhandry.

We start with Regev’s overall quantum algorithmic paradigm
[
Reg09
]
(as did
[
YZ22
]
) for constructing our quantum algorithm.
Our use of a dual Reed-Solomon code constraint is directly lifted from Yamakawa and Zhandry, which fits perfectly with our setting because a dual Reed-Solomon code can be implemented through a system of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear constraints, as already noted earlier. This fact was not important to Yamakawa-Zhandry but is crucial for us.

The actual analysis of the algorithm proceeds by, roughly speaking, showing that unique decoding of the Reed-Solomon code will work in quantum superposition. For
[
YZ22
]
, this analysis involved careful and clever analysis of the random oracle.
Our analysis is completely different and based on analyzing properties of degree
d>2
multivariate polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
(or other prime fields), as we will outline below.

Quantum Algorithm Overview.

Given the explicit description of the generating matrix for
C
and the system of multivariate degree

\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n^{2}}\}
in
n^{3}

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}V(\mathbf{x})\ket{\mat{x}}\propto\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in C}\ket{\mat{x}},

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}W^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mat{e}}\propto\sum_{\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{e}_{1}\ldots\mathbf{e}_{n^{2}}~:~\forall i\in[n^{2}],~p_{i}(\mathbf{e}_{i})=0}\ket{\mat{e}},

where the coefficient functions are defined as

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|C|}}\qquad\mathbf{x}\in C\\
0\qquad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle W^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})=\begin{cases}2^{-R/2}&\text{if }\forall i\in[n^{2}],p_{i}(\mathbf{e}_{i})=0\\
0&\text{o.w.}\end{cases}

such that
R=\prod_{i\in[n^{2}]}R_{i}
where
R_{i}
is the number of roots of polynomial
p_{i}
.
At a high level, the quantum algorithm aims to take the quantum states
\ket{\Psi}
and
\ket{\Phi}
and prepare the pointwise product

\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}(V\cdot W^{\mathcal{P}})(\mathbf{\mathbf{z}})\ket{\mat{z}}.

Observe that measuring this pointwise product state is guaranteed to yield a state
\ket{\mat{z}}
that simultaneously is a codeword of
C

\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}
, and is a solution for the degree-
d

p_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{i})=0
.

To produce this pointwise product state, the algorithm first computes the Quantum Fourier Transformation (QFT) of each of
\ket{\Phi}
and
\ket{\Psi}
over the extension field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.
The QFT of a uniform superposition of codewords is identical to the uniform superposition over the
dual codewords
. This will yield the state

\ket{\hat{\Psi}}\otimes\ket{\hat{\Phi}}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}},\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mat{x}}\ket{\mat{e}},

where
\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})=\frac{1}{2^{n^{3}/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}W^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{y})\cdot(-1)^{\mathsf{Tr}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{y})}

\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|C^{\perp}|}}\text{ when }\mathbf{x}\in C^{\perp},\text{ and }0\text{ otherwise}
.
Then, we compute a unitary addition to obtain
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in C^{\perp},\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mat{x}}\ket{\mat{x}+\mat{e}}
.
Here, we can view
\mathbf{e}
as an error term added to
\mathbf{x}\in C^{\perp}
.

We can observe at this point that if we were able to surgically remove the first register, the expression
\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mat{x} + \mat{e}}

\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}\left(\hat{V}*\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}\right)(\mathbf{z})\ket{\mat{z}}=\mathsf{QFT}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}\left(V\cdot W^{\mathcal{P}}\right)(\mathbf{z})\ket{\mat{z}},

where the right hand side is exactly our desired state up to a QFT.
In other words, the critical step is showing that we can uncompute the first register!
To uncompute the first register, the algorithm will apply a decoding algorithm for the Reed-Solomon Code
C^{\perp}
and subtract the output of the decoding algorithm from the first register, i.e.

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}},\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mat{x}-\decode(\mat{x}+\mat{e})}\ket{\mat{x}+\mat{e}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}},\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mat{0}}\ket{\mat{x}+\mat{e}}

(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}(\hat{V}*\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}})(\mathbf{z})\ket{\mat{0}}\ket{\mat{z}}.

where the last line follows by the Convolution Theorem (Lemma
2.12

Applying an inverse QFT to the second register yields our desired state
\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}}\left(V\cdot W^{\mathcal{P}}\right)(\mathbf{z})\ket{\mat{z}}.

The Technical Heart of Our Work.

We see now that the key task for proving the algorithm’s correctness is proving that there exists a decoding algorithm that succeeds with high probability at uniquely recovering
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
where the probability is taken over the distribution of
\mathbf{e}
defined by a probability mass function related to the Fourier coefficient function
\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}
.
This begs two questions.

1.

For what error distributions can we expect to have average-case unique decoding of Reed-Solomon codes?

2.

What is the the error distribution induced by the solutions of degree
d
multivaiate equations?

The work of Yamakawa-Zhandry
[
YZ22
]
address the first question for an error distribution that is naturally induced by their use of a random oracle.
They proved that if the error distribution is a product distribution over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
of the form
\bar{\mathcal{D}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
where
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is the distribution
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
that has
\frac{1}{2}
probability mass on
\mathbf{0}
and is otherwise uniformly distributed over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, then there exists a simple decoding algorithm
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
for any small constant
\varepsilon>0
that succeeds with high probability over
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
:

1.

List-decode the noisy codeword
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
to obtain a list
L
of candidate codewords in
C^{\perp}
.

2.

If there is a unique codeword
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
in
L
such that
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
has
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-Hamming weight bounded above by
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)n^{2}
, then output
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
. Otherwise, if there are multiple such candidates, the algorithm fails and returns
\bot
.

This decoding algorithm is natural for this error distribution.
Namely, the first step must use list-decoding because worst-case unique decoding is impossible as there is a significant chance that the relative
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-Hamming weight of the error is at least
\frac{1}{2}
.
The second step of the algorithm pattern matches the average case error.
Unfortunately, we should not expect such a perfect looking generic distribution
\bar{\mathcal{D}}
to capture an error distribution induced by a structured object such as degree
d
multivariate system of equations.
Nevertheless, after characterizing the error distribution induced by the degree
d
multivariate system, we will show that we can generalize the class of average-case uniquely decodable distributions to capture it, and moreover the same decoding algorithm
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
will succeed with overwhelming probability for this larger class of error distributions.

The error distribution induced by multivariate degree
d
polynomials.

The error distribution we will consider is the error distribution defined by the probability mass function
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{\cP}(\cdot) }^{2}\right]:\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}\to[0,1]
.
The reason why we focus on this mass function is that if we can show that for this probability mass function an all but negligible amount of the mass is on errors
\mathbf{e}
for which for any
\mathbf{x}\in C^{\perp}
,
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
is uniquely decodable to
\mathbf{x}
, a set of errors that we term

\mathcal{G}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n^{2}}\colon\forall\mathbf{x}\in C^{\perp},\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e})=\mathbf{x}\},

then a simple Markov argument implies that with all but negligible probability over the choice of
\mathcal{P}
, an all but negligible amount of the mass of the probability mass function
\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{\cP}(\cdot) }^{2}
is on
\mathcal{G}
.

This error distribution with probability mass function
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{\cP}(\cdot) }^{2}\right]

.
In particular, it suffices to understand the function

In particular the mass function is as follows: For all
\mathbf{e}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}

\hat{W}^{p}(\mathbf{e}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+(-1)^{p(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{R_{p}}},

where

), we can immediately see that for a single polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}

\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{\cP}(\mat{0}) }^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\frac{R_{p}}{2^{n}}\right]=\frac{1}{2},

so that this distribution, like the distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
previously defined, has
1/2
probability mass on
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.

For the remaining mass on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, we need to do some more work. In fact, we will not be able to show that the mass function is uniformly distributed over all
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
. So, we first need to analyze what is a
sufficient
property that this mass function needs to satisfy in order for us to successfully decode with an overwhelming probability. Intuitively, for
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
to succeed, we need to ensure that the probability mass cannot be concentrated on some large hamming weight strings that would break the uniqueness condition required by Step 2 of
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
. Therefore it would be sufficient to ensure that there does not exist any
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{\cP}(\mat{0}) }^{2}\right]
is large. In particular, we show that

\forall\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\},\E_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{p}(\mat{e})}^{2}\right]\leq 2^{-\Omega(n)}.

To prove this result, the crucial result that we need is that a uniform random degree
d\geq 2
multivariate polynomial is
2
-wise independent.

Lemma 1.2

(
2
-wise Independence of Non-linear Polynomials)
.

Let
d\geq 2
be any integer. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for any two distinct vectors
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, for any
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
over the choice of a uniform random inhomogeneous degree
d
polynomial
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
,

\Pr_{p}\left[p(\mathbf{x})=a_{1}\land p(\mathbf{y})=a_{2}\right]=\frac{1}{4}.

Putting Things Together.

From this analysis, we can conclude that the error distribution is defined by the probability mass function
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{\cP}(\cdot) }^{2}\right]
that is a product distribution of
n^{2}
-many distributions
\mathcal{D}_{i}
that each have
\frac{1}{2}
probability mass on
\mathbf{0}
and has a mass of no more than
2^{-\Omega(n)}
at any other point. We show that we will be able to extend the decoder of Yamakawa-Zhandry to work for such distributions.
This concludes the high-level intuition for how we adapt this algorithmic approach to efficiently solving our family of degree
d
bounded multivariate systems using a quantum algorithm.

Some Technical Details.

To formally prove correctness, however, our analysis will run into technical issues that were previously avoided by the use of the random oracle.
These are non-trivial obstacles for which we will need to use certain symmetries over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. In particular, we also need to rely on the observation that a random multivariate polynomial is
shift-invariant
(see Definition
4.1
). Specifically we show that for any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, the map
\Pi_{z}
that maps a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
to
p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
such that for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,
p^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})
is a permutation. We refer readers to Section
4.3
, which provides detailed intuition on top of the formal proof.

Our result holds for a larger family of polynomials. We show that the correctness of our algorithm holds when polynomials
\mathcal{P}
are sampled from
any
distribution that satisfies (1)
2
-wise independence, and (2) shift invariance.

Lemma 2.22

(Boolean Parseval’s Theorem)
.

Let
p:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
and define
P:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
such that
P(\mathbf{x})=(-1)^{p(\mathbf{x})}
. Define
\tilde{P}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\tilde{P}(\mathbf{a})\coloneqq\E_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[P(\mathbf{z})\cdot(-1)^{\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}\right]
where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
denotes
\mathbb{F}_{2}
dot product.
Then,

\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\tilde{P}(\mathbf{a})^{2}=1.

Proof.

For any
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, define the
0/1
indicator random variable

\mathbb{I}_{\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}\\
0&\text{o.w.}\end{cases}=\E_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[(-1)^{\langle\mathbf{a}\oplus\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\rangle_{2}}\right].

Then define
\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{b})\coloneqq(-1)^{\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle_{2}}=\chi_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a})
.

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\tilde{P}(\mathbf{a})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\tilde{P}(\mathbf{a})\tilde{P}(\mathbf{b})\E_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})\chi_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\tilde{P}(\mathbf{a})\tilde{P}(\mathbf{b})\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})\chi_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[\left(\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\tilde{P}(\mathbf{a})\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[\left(\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\E_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[P(\mathbf{y})\cdot\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{y})\right]\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[\left(\E_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[P(\mathbf{y})\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\chi_{\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{a})\right]\right)^{2}\right]

If
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, then for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,
\chi_{\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{a})=1
so that
\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\chi_{\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{a})=2^{n}
.
Otherwise,
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}
and the function
\chi_{\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y}}
is a balanced function, so the sum
\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\chi_{\mathbf{x}\oplus\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{a})=0
.
Therefore, by expanding the last line above we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\tilde{P}(\mathbf{a})^{2}=\E_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[P(\mathbf{x})^{2}\right]=1

where we observe that
P^{2}
is identically
1
.
∎

Final Polynomial System.

Combining the two ingredients defined above, the final polynomial system

1.

m

-degree bounded polynomials

2.

(1-\alpha)m

Remark 3.2
.

The above constraints can be equivalently interpreted as
m

The Conjectured Classically Hard Problem.

Given the system of random polynomials
\mathcal{P}
and
\overline{\mathbf{H}}
, the task is to find
\mathbf{y}\coloneqq(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{m})
where for all

\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

p_{i}(\mathbf{y_{i}})=0

and

\overline{\mathbf{H}}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{0}
.

Remark 3.3

(Interpreting
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
elements as
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
elements)
.

Note that while the polynomials are evaluated over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, the code
C
is defined over the extension field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. This will be crucial for the quantum algorithm as we will see later. Observe that every binary string
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
can be interpreted in the usual way as a single element over the extension field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.

That is,
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
is a dimension
n
vectorspace over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
because
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/q(x)
for indeterminate
x
and some irreducible polynomial

of degree
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Therefore, every element

can be expressed as a degree

polynomial in

where

Therefore

can be represented by a coefficient vector
(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n-1})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

Throughout this paper, we abuse notation so that a symbol denoting the extension field element
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
will simultaneously refer to a canonical representation of this element as a vector over the prime field
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

Let

be the security parameter. Our polynomial system

over

is parameterized by

•

•

An example of parameter settings with respect to a security parameter

Our quantum algorithm will in fact succeed even when the polynomials

that satisfies shift-invariance and standard

Therefore, we present a correctness theorem for our algorithm for all distributions

We will prove in Section

Definition 4.1

(Shift-invariant Polynomial Distributions)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a distribution
\mathscr{P}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
is said to be shift-invariant if for all
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, the distribution
\mathscr{P}_{\mathbf{s}}
with a sampling procedure defined by

1.

Sample
p\leftarrow\mathscr{P}
.

2.

Output
p^{\prime}
where
p^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{s})
.

is identically distributed as
\mathscr{P}
.

Definition 4.2

(2-wise Independent Polynomial Distributions)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a distribution
\mathscr{P}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
is 2-wise independent if
for any distinct vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1}\neq\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, for any
a_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
a_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
,

\Pr_{p\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[p(\mathbf{x}_{1})=a_{1}\land p(\mathbf{x}_{2})=a_{2}\right]=\frac{1}{4}.

Example 1. Random High Degree Sparse Polynomials

An example of an interesting shift-invariant

-wise independent polynomial distribution is the following distribution on
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
for

and for any

2.

Output

Note here that the sparsity ensures that the representation of this polynomial has a description size bounded by

Algorithm 1
.

The algorithm is parameterized by a constant decoding parameter
\varepsilon>0

The inputs to the algorithm are the description of system of polynomials
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{i,1},\ldots,X_{i,n}]\}

1.

For each

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of
0
under

\ket{\phi_i}\propto\sum_{\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}~:~p_{i}(\mathbf{e}_{i})=0}\ket{\mbf{e}_i}

Define the state

\ket{\Phi}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\ket{\phi_i}\propto\sum_{\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{m})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{mn}~:~\forall~i\in[m],p_{i}(\mathbf{e}_{i})=0}\ket{\mbf{e}}.

2.

Prepare a uniform superposition over all codewords
3
3

3

Both primal and dual code is over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. See Remark
3.3
.
for the primal code
C

\ket{\Psi}\propto\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in C}\ket{\mbf{x}},

In more detail, starting from the uniform superposition

3.

Compute

\ket{\hat{\Phi}}=\mathsf{QFT}_{2^{n}}\ket{\Phi}

4.

Perform the unitary operation

\ket{\mbf{x}}\ket{\mbf{e}}\xrightarrow{U_{\mathsf{add}}}\ket{\mbf{x}}\ket{\mbf{e} + \mbf{x}}

Perform the unitary operation

\ket{\mbf{x}}\ket{\mbf{e} + \mbf{x}}\xrightarrow{U_{\mathsf{decode}}}\ket{\mbf{x}-\decode(\mat{e}+\mat{x})}\ket{\mbf{e} + \mbf{x}}.

For a constant

\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z})

(a)

On input

(b)

If there is a unique

Finally, measure the second register, and output the outcome

Next, define the set

\mathsf{R}\coloneqq\mathsf{R}_{1}\times\mathsf{R}_{2}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{R}_{m}.

Thus, for every

\mathbf{e}\in\mathsf{R}
if
\forall i\in[m],\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathsf{R}_{i}

2.

Weights used in quantum superposition.
Let

,
W^{p_{i}}:\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
,
\forall i\in[m]
, and
W^{\mathcal{P}}:\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be functions defined as follows:

V(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|C|}}\qquad\mathbf{x}\in C\\
0\qquad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

W^{p_{i}}(\mathbf{e}_{i})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathsf{R}_{i}|}}&\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathsf{R}_{i}\\

Note here that if
p_{i}
is identically

, i.e.

has no roots, then for all
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, we have

because

describes the post-partial measurement state of observing

Remark 4.4

(Expected Runtime of the Algorithm)
.

To observe that the algorithm runs in expected polynomial time, first observe that the QFT can be performed efficiently and the Reed-Solomon list-decoding algorithm for our parameter setting will return a polynomial-sized list.
The only step that is not clearly efficient is Step
1
, namely the state preparation of the uniform superposition over the roots of a polynomial
p_{i}
.

In more detail, in Step
1
, the algorithm prepares a register that is the uniform superposition over all vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, it evaluates
p_{i}
on the uniform superposition in an auxiliary register, and it measures the auxiliary register.
If the measurement reads
0
, then the algorithm discards the measured register and the remaining post-measurement state is set to be
\ket{\phi_i}
. If the measurement outcome is not
0
, the algorithm starts this process afresh.

We now formally show that this process terminates in expected constant time. Since
\mathscr{P}
is
2
-wise independent, we apply Lemma
4.12
to see that the number of roots of
p_{i}\sim\mathscr{P}_{i}
, denoted
\absolutevalue{\sR_i}
, is tightly concentrated on
2^{n-1}
with variance
2^{n-2}
.
Therefore, the variance of
\frac{\absolutevalue{\sR_i}}{2^{n}}
is
\frac{1}{2^{n+2}}
.
Then by applying Chebyshev’s inequality, we obtain that for all
i\in[m]
, for any constant
\varepsilon>0
,

\Pr_{p_{i}\sim\mathscr{P}_{i}}\left[\absolutevalue{\frac{\abs{\sR_i}}{2^n} - \frac{1}{2}}\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot 2^{n+2}}.

This implies that the state preparation in Step
1
can be done in expected constant time.

\delta:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1]
such that for all sufficiently large
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left\lVert\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})\in\mathsf{BAD}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mbf{x+e}}\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{\delta(\lambda)}

which implies

\displaystyle\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}\times\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mat{x} + \mat{e}}\approx_{\sqrt{\delta}}\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})\in\mathsf{GOOD}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mat{x} + \mat{e}}.

(3)

Equation
2
and Equation
3
allow us to observe the following sequence of relations:

\displaystyle\left(U_{\mathsf{decode}}U_{\mathsf{add}}\right)\left(\ket{\hat{\Psi}}\otimes\ket{\hat{\Phi}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(U_{\mathsf{decode}}U_{\mathsf{add}}\right)\left(\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}\times\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mbf{x}}\ket{\mbf{e}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx_{\sqrt{\mu}}\left(U_{\mathsf{decode}}U_{\mathsf{add}}\right)\left(\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})\in\mathsf{GOOD}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mbf{x}}\ket{\mbf{e}}\right)

(By Equation
2
.)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})\in\mathsf{GOOD}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mat{0}}\ket{\mbf{x} + \mbf{e}}

(By definition of
\mathsf{GOOD}
.)

\displaystyle\approx_{\sqrt{\delta}}\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}\times\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\ket{\mat{0}}\ket{\mbf{x} + \mbf{e}}

(By Equation
3
.)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}(\hat{V}*\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}})(\mathbf{z})\ket{\mat{0}}\ket{\mat{z}}

(By definition of convolution.)

\displaystyle=2^{mn/2}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}(\widehat{V\cdot W^{\mathcal{P}}})(\mathbf{z})\ket{\mat{0}}\ket{\mat{z}}

(By Lemma
2.12
.)

\displaystyle=(I\otimes\mathsf{QFT}_{2^{n}})2^{mn/2}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}(V\cdot W^{\mathcal{P}})(\mathbf{z})\ket{\mbf{0}}\ket{\mbf{z}}.

(By definition of QFT.)

Therefore in Step
6
, the algorithm obtains the following state:

\displaystyle(I\otimes\mathsf{QFT}_{2^{n}}^{-1})\left(U_{\mathsf{decode}}U_{\mathsf{add}}\right)\left(\ket{\hat{\Psi}}\otimes\ket{\hat{\Phi}}\right)\approx_{\sqrt{\mu}+\sqrt{\delta}}2^{mn/2}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}(V\cdot W^{\mathcal{P}})(\mathbf{z})\ket{\mbf{0}}\ket{\mbf{z}}.

By the definition of the
V
and
W^{\mathcal{P}}
, a measurement of the second register on the right hand side is guaranteed to observe a value
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}_{1}\mathbf{z}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{z}_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
that simultaneously satisfies that
\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{0}
and for all

,

.
By the indistinguishability of the left and right hand side, with at least

This concludes our proof of correctness.

4.3
Proof of Secondary Technical Lemma: Lemma
4.6

We now prove the secondary technical lemma, Lemma
4.6
, by assuming the main technical lemma, Lemma
4.5
.
A reader may choose to skip the following exposition on the intuition behind the proof and instead jump directly to the
Proof of Secondary Technical Lemma
.

For intuition, recall that Lemma
4.5
bounds a sum of norm values squared given by

\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})\in\mathsf{BAD}}\absolutevalue{\hat{V}(\mbf{x}) \hat{W}^{\cP}( \mat{e}) }^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{B}}\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{\cP}(\mat{e})}^{2}.

The obvious idea to relate the two lemmas is to attempt to apply a triangle inequality on the expression in Lemma
4.6
,

\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})\in\mathsf{BAD}:\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{z}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\right|^{2}.

Unfortunately, the triangle inequality is false for upper bounding the norm squared of a sum by the sum of norms squared.
Therefore, to semantically match the statements of the two lemmas, we aim to remove the inner summation.

A natural way to remove the summation is to introduce a convolution operator, which by its definition would absorb the inner summation.
Namely, observe that our starting expression can be equivalently rewritten as

\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\in\mathsf{BAD}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}.

The inner expression already resembles a convolution!
To equivalently rewrite the inner expression as a convolution, however, we require the inner sum to range over all
\mathbf{x}
values in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
.
Towards this end, observe that since
\hat{V}
takes the value
0
on all vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}\setminus C^{\perp}
, we can equivalently rewrite the above as

\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}\times\mathcal{B}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}.

The inner summation now nearly looks like a convolution with the exception of the required condition on
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}
.
To address this, we apply the exactly same idea as we did with
\hat{V}
, namely we introduce an indicator function
\hat{B}:\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}
that is exactly
1
if its input is in the set of values
\mathcal{B}
defined above to be the set of “bad” error values and
0
otherwise, i.e.

\hat{B}(\mathbf{e})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{B}$.}\\
0&\text{o.w.}\end{cases}.

Finally, we can equivalently rewrite the above expression as

\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\cdot(\hat{B}\cdot\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}})(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\left(\hat{V}*(\hat{B}\cdot\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}})\right)(\mathbf{z})\right|^{2}.

How do we relate this above expression to that expression in Lemma
4.5
?

Recall that the main expression in Lemma
4.5
can be written equivalently as
\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathcal{B}}\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{\cP}(\mat{e})}^{2}
, an expression that notably has no dependence on
\hat{V}
.
The above convolution, however, tangles
\hat{V}
in a non-separable way.
If the inner expression
\hat{V}*(\hat{B}\cdot\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}})
were instead a pointwise product, we could have multiplicatively factored
\hat{V}
out of the summation since the value of
\hat{V}
is an indicator function whose value is completely known to us.
Fortunately, transforming a convolution into a pointwise product is exactly what the Convolution Theorem (see Lemma
2.12
) does.
Namely, applying the Convolution Theorem twice gives us

\hat{V}*(\hat{B}\cdot\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}})=\widehat{V\cdot(B*W^{\mathcal{P}})}.

We still cannot separate out
V
since there is a hat sitting on the entire expression.
Fortunately the summation over
\mathbf{z}
is over the entirety of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
, so we can appeal to Parseval’s Theorem (see Lemma
2.10
) to remove the hat and obtain the following equivalent expression to the original statement of Lemma
4.6
,

\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\left(V\cdot(B*W^{\mathcal{P}})(\mathbf{z})\right)\right|^{2},

allowing us to finally pull out
V
from the expression to obtain

\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{C}}\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in C}\left|(B*W^{\mathcal{P}})(\mathbf{z})\right|^{2}.

Unfortunately, the function
B
, which is the dual function to our indicator function
\hat{B}
, is not readily interpretable, nor is its convolution with
W^{\mathcal{P}}
.
It seems that we have hit the end of the road as we cannot apply Parseval’s with the summation on
\mathbf{z}
being only over the code
C
.
If the summation were instead over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
, Parseval’s Theorem would be our ticket back to
\hat{B}\cdot\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}
.
Can we find a ticket back?

What allows us to return to the desired expression is the following symmetry on the set of all
n
-variate
\mathbb{F}_{2}
polynomials.

Lemma 4.7
.

For any
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
, for any
m
-many polynomials
\mathcal{P}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})
sampled such that
\forall i\in[m],~p_{i}\sim\mathscr{P}_{i}
where
\mathscr{P}_{i}
is shift-invariant (Def.
4.1
), we have that

\E_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\absolutevalue{\left( B * W^\cP\right)(\mbf{z})}^{2}\right]=\E_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\absolutevalue{\left( B * W^\cP\right)(\mbf{z'})}^{2}\right].

Proof.

It suffices to show that the left hand side expression is independent of
\mathbf{z}
. Here we will use the fact that the distribution
\mathscr{P}_{i}
is shift-invariant and therefore shift-invariant.

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{P}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathscr{P}_{i}}\left[\absolutevalue{\left( B * W^{\cP}\right)(\mbf{z})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{P}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathscr{P}_{i}}\left[\absolutevalue{\sum_{\mat{x} \in\mextension} B(\mat{x}) W^\cP(\mbf{z}-\mat{x})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{P}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathscr{P}_{i,\mathbf{z}_{i}}}\left[\absolutevalue{\sum_{\mat{x} \in\mextension} B(\mat{x}) W^{\cP}(\mat{x})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{P}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathscr{P}_{i}}\left[\absolutevalue{\sum_{\mat{x} \in\mextension} B(\mat{x}) W^{\cP}(\mat{x})}^{2}\right].

(By the shift invariance property.)

∎

Lemma
4.7
shows us that taking an expectation over the expression in Lemma
4.6
allows the observed symmetry to introduce a summation over the entirety of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
, enabling us to apply Parseval’s Theorem and return from
B*W^{\mathcal{P}}
to the known quantity of
\hat{B}\cdot\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}
.
Namely, for any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
,

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\absolutevalue{\left( B * W^\cP\right)(\mbf{z})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{nm}}\cdot\E_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{z^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\absolutevalue{\left( B * W^\cP\right)(\mbf{z'})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{z^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\absolutevalue{\left( \hat{B} \cdot\hat{W}^\cP\right)(\mbf{z'})}^{2}\right]

(By the Convolution Theorem.)

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{z^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}\absolutevalue{ \hat{W}^\cP(\mbf{z'})}^{2}\right].

Observe that
\sum_{\mathbf{z^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}}\absolutevalue{ \hat{W}^\cP(\mbf{z'})}^{2}
is exactly what Lemma
4.5
upper bounds, so we have established a relation between the two lemmas.
We now provide the formal proof.

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

By Markov’s inequality, to prove Lemma
4.6
it suffices to prove the existence of a negligible function
\delta:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\in\mathsf{BAD}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}\right]\leq\delta(\lambda).

We now rewrite the expression inside the expectation in a sequence of steps.
Recalling the definition of the indicator function

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e})\in\mathsf{BAD}:\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{z}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{e})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\in\mathsf{BAD}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}\times\mathcal{B}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{x})\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}

\hat{B}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}}\left|\left(\hat{V}*(\hat{B}\cdot\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}})\right)(\mathbf{z})\right|^{2}

4.4
Main Technical Lemma Proof: Lemma
4.5

The proof of Lemma
4.5
is our main technical contribution and the supporting lemmas used and proven in the process may be of independent interest for how to apply quantum Fourier transformations to solve multivariate polynomial systems.

Interpretation of Lemma
4.5
.

The use of Lemma
4.5
in the proof of correctness is to show that applying
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
in superposition to uncompute the first register will succeed on an overwhelming measure of the terms in the superposition.
Therefore,
Lemma
4.5
is best framed by viewing the function
\hat{W}^{\mathcal{P}}:\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}
as inducing a probability distribution defined by the probability mass function
\E_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{\cP}(\cdot)}^{2}\right]:\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}\to[0,1]
over the error values
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
.
This is a distribution that notably does not depend on any specific choice of
\mathcal{P}
.
Lemma
4.5
states exactly that
random errors sampled from this
distribution are, with all but negligible probability,
uniquely
decodable under the classical decoding algorithm
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
.
Recall that our decoding algorithm
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
proceeds in two natural steps:

1.

List-decode the noisy codeword
\mathbf{z}\coloneqq\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
to obtain a list
L
of candidate codewords in
C^{\perp}
.

2.

If there is a unique codeword
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
in
L
such that
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
looks like a “reasonable” error, i.e. the
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-Hamming weight is bounded above by
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m
, then output
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
. Otherwise, if there are multiple such candidates, the algorithm fails and returns
\bot
.

List-decoding, rather than unique decoding, must be performed as the worst case
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-hamming weight is well beyond the unique decoding radius.
For any generator
\gamma
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, one can uniquely decode noisy
(m,\gamma,(1-\alpha)m)
-Reed-Solomon codewords with worst-case errors of up to
\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha m}{2}\right\rfloor

\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-Hamming weight.
However, we can only guarantee that this error distribution outputs errors that have at most
(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon)m

\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-Hamming weight with high probability, for some small constant
\varepsilon>0
.
Therefore, successfully running unique decoding instead of list decoding as the first step would impose the requirement that
\alpha\geq 1+2\varepsilon
, a nonsensical setting of the rate parameter
\alpha
.
Moreover, since the error’s relative
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-hamming weight may be well over
\frac{1}{2}
, we must perform this list-decoding process over the field extension, enlarging our alphabet size to avoid the Johnson bound.
Once we’ve obtained a list of candidates from the list decoding step, we can use a straightforward counting argument to argue that with high probability, there exists a unique candidate in the list that satisfies our Hamming weight constraint.
In other words, this argument gives average-case unique decoding for this type of error distribution.

4.4.1
Average-case Unique Decoding of Reed-Solomon Codes

We now generalize the proof of Item 2 in Lemma 4.2 in
[
YZ22
]
to characterize a more general distribution of error distribution for which
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
can successfully recover the original codeword.
This may be of interest for extending the application of the Yamakawa-Zhandry algorithm
[
YZ22
]
to other instantiations of the random oracle beyond an MQ polynomial system.

Previously, the condition in the analogous theorem in
[
YZ22
]
considers a distribution in which
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
has probability mass
\frac{1}{2}
and is otherwise uniform over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
We observe that we can relax this condition to stipulating that the remaining
\frac{1}{2}
probability mass is not concentrated on any particular element in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

Lemma 4.8

(Generalization of Item 2 in Lemma 4.2 in
[
YZ22
]
)
.

Let
n=\Theta(\lambda)
and
m=\omega(1)
.
Let
\mathcal{D}
by any error distribution described by a product distribution
\mathcal{D}=\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}
that satisfies the following property:

•

For
i\in[m]
, let
\mathcal{D}_{i}
be a distribution over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
that takes
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
and otherwise takes any element in the support with probability at most
2^{-c_{i}n}
for some constant
c_{i}>0
. Let
c_{\min}\coloneqq\min_{i\in[m]}c_{i}
.

For any arbitrarily small constant
\varepsilon>0
, for any
\alpha>\max\Bigg(\left(\frac{3}{4}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}\right),~\left(1-\frac{c_{\min}}{4}+\varepsilon\cdot\left(1-\frac{c_{\min}}{2}\right)\right)\Bigg)
, for any generator
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n-1}}
, let
\{C^{\perp}_{\lambda}\}
be a
(m,\gamma,(1-\alpha)m)
-Reed-Solomon code family
over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
.
Then, there exists an efficient unique decoding algorithm
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
for
C^{\perp}
for
\mathcal{D}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{e}_{1}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{m}~\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\forall~\mathbf{x}\in C^{\perp},\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e})=\mathbf{x}\right]\geq 1-2^{-\Omega(\lambda)}.

Proof.

First, since each distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is independent and takes on the value
\mathbf{0}
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
, a standard Chernoff argument gives that for any constant
\varepsilon>0
, the product distribution satisfies an
\varepsilon
-tight concentration on the relative
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-Hamming weight of
\frac{1}{2}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\cdot m\leq\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)\cdot m\right]\geq 1-2^{-\Omega(\lambda)}.

(4)

Recalling the construction of
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
, the first requirement for correctness of this algorithm is that, given a noisy codeword of the form
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
where
\mathbf{x}
is the original noisefree codeword in
C^{\perp}
, the list-decoding of
C^{\perp}
succeeds in returning a list
L
that contains
\mathbf{x}
.
This occurs as long as the list-decoding radius is sufficiently large enough to encompass the typical error, where the term typical is formally captured above in Equation
4
on the error distribution. Using the list-decoding bound found in
[
GS98
]
, this requirement is therefore,

m-\sqrt{(1-\alpha)m^{2}}>\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)\cdot m,

which holds whenever
\alpha>\frac{3}{4}-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}
.
This constraint will be subsumed by a similar constraint on
\alpha
that we introduce subsequently for technical reasons.
Observe that this constraint on
\alpha
is quite relaxed because
\varepsilon
can be any arbitrarily small positive constant.

The second requirement for correctness of this decoding algorithm is that among the codewords in the list
L
, there should only be one codeword
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in L
such that
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
satisfies the typicality condition in Equation
4
.
A sufficient condition for when this event occurs is when the error
\mathbf{e}
comes from the set

\displaystyle G^{\prime}\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}:\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m~\bigwedge~\forall~\mathbf{y}\in C^{\perp}\setminus\{0\},~\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{y})>\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m\right\}.

Observe that the condition
\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m
implies that
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}
satisfies the typicality constraint, while for all
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{x}\in L\subseteq C^{\perp}
, the vector
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
has relative
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-Hamming weight exceeding the typicality constraint.
Therefore, whenever a sampled
\mathbf{e}
is in
G^{\prime}
, then
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
will successfully uniquely decode the noisy codeword
\mathbf{z}
.

We now argue that the probability that an error
\mathbf{e}
sampled from
\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}
lands in the set
G^{\prime}
is at least
1-2^{-\Omega(\lambda)}
.
First, we define the set of typical errors:

\displaystyle T\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}:\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)m\leq\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m\right\}.

Observe that Equation
4
stipulates that
\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin T\right]\leq 2^{-\Omega(\lambda)}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}\land\mathbf{e}\in T\right]+\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}\land\mathbf{e}\notin T\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}\land\mathbf{e}\in T\right]+\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin T\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}\land\mathbf{e}\in T\right]+2^{-\Omega(\lambda)}.

(5)

We now upper bound the first term in the above sum in which we have the event
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
such that
\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}\land\mathbf{e}\in T
. To give an upper bound, we formulate a necessary event for this event, and upper bound the probability of the necessary event, which upper bounds the probability of this event.
Observe that

1.

If
\mathbf{e}\in T
, then there exists a subset of indices
S^{*}\subseteq[m]
on which
\mathbf{e}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
such that
\absolutevalue{S^*}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)m
.

2.

If
\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}
, then there exists a codeword
\mathbf{y}^{*}\in C^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{y}^{*})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m
.

We can rewrite the latter observation as,

\displaystyle\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{y}^{*})=\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e}_{S^{*}}-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{S^{*}})+\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e}_{\overline{S^{*}}}-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\overline{S^{*}}})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m.

(6)

where the subscript with a set denotes a restriction to the indices in the set, i.e.
\mathbf{e}_{S^{*}}=\mathbf{e}_{i_{1}}\mathbf{e}_{i_{2}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{i_{\absolutevalue{S^*}}}
for
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{\absolutevalue{S^*}}\in S^{*}
, and where
\overline{S^{*}}=[m]\setminus S^{*}
. Then using the structure of the Reed-Solomon code, we have that

\displaystyle\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e}_{S^{*}}-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{S^{*}})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{S^{*}})

\displaystyle\geq\absolutevalue{S^*}-(1-\alpha)m+1

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)m-(1-\alpha)m+1.

(7)

The first inequality is because the codeword
\mathbf{y}^{*}
is a vector of evaluations of a univariate polynomial of degree
(1-\alpha)m-1
, which can can have at most
(1-\alpha)m-1
many roots.
The second inequality is due to the fact that
\mathbf{e}\in T
as noted above.

Equation
6
and Equation
7
imply the following useful necessary event for when
\mathbf{e}
lands in the set
G^{\prime}
: Namely we will consider, for any fixed
S^{*}\subseteq[m]
such that
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)m\leq\absolutevalue{S^*}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m
, the event
\mathcal{E}_{S^{*}}
over
\mathbf{e}\sim\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}
that

\displaystyle\exists~\mathbf{y}^{*}\in C^{\perp}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\},\text{ s.t. }\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e}_{\overline{S^{*}}}-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\overline{S^{*}}})\leq\left(2\varepsilon-\alpha+1\right)m-1.

(8)

Let
S_{\mathbf{e}}\coloneqq\left\{i\in[m]:\mathbf{e}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\right\}
, then observe that Equation
6
and Equation
7
imply that for any
S^{*}\subseteq[m]
such that
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\leq\absolutevalue{S^*}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e\notin G^{\prime}\mid S_{\mathbf{e}}=S^{*}}\right]\leq\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{S^{*}}\right].

(9)

By a straightforward counting argument, we can show that for any
S^{*}\subseteq[m]
such that
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\leq\absolutevalue{S^*}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m
, we have

\displaystyle\lg\left(\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{S^{*}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\coloneqq\lg\left(\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\exists~\mathbf{y}^{*}\in C^{\perp}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\text{ s.t. }\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\overline{S^{*}}}-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\overline{S^{*}}}\right)\leq(2\varepsilon-\alpha+1)m-1\right]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq-2\left(\alpha-\left(1-\frac{c_{\min}}{4}+\varepsilon\left(1-\frac{c_{\min}}{2}\right)\right)\right)\cdot mn+O(m\log m).

(10)

The probability inside the
\lg
is therefore
2^{-\Omega(mn)}
whenever
\alpha>\left(1-\frac{c_{\min}}{4}+\varepsilon\left(1-\frac{c_{\min}}{2}\right)\right)
.
To prove Equation
10
, we first show that for any
S^{*}\subseteq[m]
such that
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\leq\absolutevalue{S^*}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m
, for any fixed
\mathbf{y}^{*}\in C^{\perp}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
,

\displaystyle\lg\left(\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\overline{S^{*}}}-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\overline{S^{*}}}\right)\leq(2\varepsilon-\alpha+1)m-1\right]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(2\varepsilon-\alpha+1-c_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)\right)mn+O(m\log m).

(11)

and then apply a union bound over all
2^{(1-\alpha)mn}
many codewords of
C^{\perp}
.
To show Equation
11
, for any
\mathbf{y}^{*}\in C^{\perp}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, the number of
\mathbf{e}_{\overline{S^{*}}}
that satisfy
\mathsf{hw}_{2^{n}}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\overline{S^{*}}}-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\overline{S^{*}}}\right)\leq(2\varepsilon-\alpha+1)m-1
is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{(2\varepsilon-\alpha+1)m-1}\binom{\absolutevalue{\overline{S^*}}}{i}\cdot(2^{n})^{i}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\left((2\varepsilon-\alpha+1)m-1\right)\cdot\binom{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m}{(2\varepsilon-\alpha+1)m-1}\cdot 2^{n\cdot((2\varepsilon-\alpha+1)m-1)}

where the inequality holds when
\alpha\geq\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon+\frac{3}{4}
, subsuming the previous constraint that
\alpha>\frac{3}{4}-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}
.
Each such
\mathbf{e}_{\overline{S^{*}}}
is sampled with probability
2^{-\sum_{i\in\overline{S^{*}}}c_{i}n}\geq 2^{-c_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)mn}
. Combining these two facts gives Equation
11
.
By applying a union bound over all
2^{(1-\alpha)mn}
many codewords of
C^{\perp}
on Equation
11
, we obtain Equation
10
.
Then observe that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}\land\mathbf{e}\in T\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\in T\right]\cdot\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{e}\in T\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 1\cdot\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{e}\in T\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{*}\subseteq[m]\\
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)m\leq\absolutevalue{S^*}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m\end{subarray}}\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[S_{\mathbf{e}}=S^{*}\mid\mathbf{e}\in T\right]\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\in G^{\prime}\mid S_{\mathbf{e}}=S^{*}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{*}\subseteq[m]\\
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)m\leq\absolutevalue{S^*}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m\end{subarray}}\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[S_{\mathbf{e}}=S^{*}\mid\mathbf{e}\in T\right]\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{S^{*}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2^{-\Omega(mn)}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{*}\subseteq[m]\\
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)m\leq\absolutevalue{S^*}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)m\end{subarray}}\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[S_{\mathbf{e}}=S^{*}\mid\mathbf{e}\in T\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=2^{-\Omega(mn)}.

(12)

Combining Equation
5
and Equation
12
gives

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathbf{e}\sim~\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}\notin G^{\prime}\right]\leq 2^{-\Omega(mn)}+2^{-\Omega(\lambda)}.

Since
\mathbf{e}\in G^{\prime}
is a sufficient condition for correctness of
\mathsf{Decode}_{C^{\perp},\varepsilon}
, this completes the proof of our desired lemma.
∎

4.4.2
Error Distribution Induced by Shift-Invariant and 2-wise Independent Distributions

Our goal is to characterize the distribution of errors induced by any shift-invariant and 2-wise independent polynomial distribution over multivariate
\mathbb{F}_{2}
polynomials.
We show that the induced distribution of errors satisfies the condition in Lemma
4.8
, i.e. they are uniquely decodable under a Reed-Solomon code.
Namely, we will prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
n=\Theta(\lambda)
,
m=\omega(\log(\lambda))
, and let
\{X_{i,j}\}_{i\in[m],j\in[n]}
be indeterminates.
Let
\mathcal{P}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})
be a random variable in which, for
i\in[m]
, where
p_{i}\sim\mathscr{P}_{i}
where
\mathscr{P}_{i}
is a 2-wise independent distribution over
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{i,1},\ldots,X_{i,n}]
,
For
i\in[m]
, let
\mathcal{D}_{i}
denote the distribution with probability mass function

\E_{p_{i}}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{p_i}(\cdot)}^{2}\right]

and let
\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathcal{D}_{i}
.
Then, for sufficiently large
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n(\lambda)\geq 10
,

•

For all
i\in[m]
,
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is a distribution over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
that takes
0
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
and otherwise takes any element in the support with probability at most
2^{-n/2}
.

Proof.

This is immediate by Lemma
4.10
.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\geq 10
, for any
2
-wise independent distribution
\mathscr{P}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
,

\displaystyle\E_{p\sim\mathscr{P}}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{p}(\mat{0})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}.

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\},\E_{p\sim\mathscr{P}}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{p}(\mat{e})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-n/2}.

Proof.

For any polynomial
p
, let
N_{p}
denote the number of roots.
Opening the definition of
\hat{W}^{p}
gives

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{p}(\mat{0})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}}W^{p}(\mathbf{z})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}\cdot\frac{N_{p}}{\sqrt{N_{p}}}\right)^{2}=\frac{N_{p}}{2^{n}}.

For any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, define the scaled indicator random variable
\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{\mathbf{x}}
as a function of
p
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{\mathbf{x}}(p)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2^{n}}&\text{if }p(\mathbf{x})=0\\
0&\text{o.w.}\end{cases}

and observe that for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, we have
\E_{p}\left[\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{\mathbf{x}}(p)\right]=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\E_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{p}(\mat{0})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{p}\left[\frac{N_{p}}{2^{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{p}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{\mathbf{x}}(p)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\E_{p}\left[\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{\mathbf{x}}(p)\right]=\frac{1}{2}.

Next we will prove that

\forall\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\},\E_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{p}(\mat{e})}^{2}\right]\leq 2^{-\Omega(n)}.

We abuse notation and a vector
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
will be occasionally considered in the isomorphic vector space
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
Expand the definition of
\hat{W}^{p}(\mathbf{e})
,

\hat{W}^{p}(\mathbf{e})=\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}}W^{p}(\mathbf{z})(-1)^{\mathsf{Tr}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{z})}.

Let
\sigma:\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be the bijective map whose existence is guaranteed by Lemma
2.3
.
Then,

\hat{W}^{p}(\mathbf{e})=\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}}W^{p}(\mathbf{z})(-1)^{\langle\sigma(\mathbf{e}),\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}

where we interpret
\mathbf{z}
in the exponent
\langle\sigma(\mathbf{e}),\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}
as a
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
vector via the canonical isomorphism between
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
Observe for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
by definition of
W^{p}(\mathbf{z})
,

W^{p}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N_{p}}}\cdot\left(1+(-1)^{p(\mathbf{z})}\right).

By substitution,

\displaystyle\hat{W}^{p}(\mathbf{e})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n/2+1}\sqrt{N_{p}}}\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left(1+(-1)^{p(\mathbf{z})}\right)\cdot(-1)^{\langle\sigma(\mathbf{e}),\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n/2+1}\sqrt{N_{p}}}\cdot\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\langle\sigma(\mathbf{e}),\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}+\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{p(\mathbf{z})+\langle\sigma(\mathbf{e}),\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n/2+1}\sqrt{N_{p}}}\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{p(\mathbf{z})+\langle\sigma(\mathbf{e}),\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}

where in the last equality we critically used the fact that
\mathbf{e}\neq\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
implies that
\sigma(\mathbf{e})\neq\mathbf{0}
by Lemma
2.3
, implying that

\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\langle\sigma(\mathbf{e}),\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}=0.

First let us define
P(\mathbf{z})=(-1)^{p(\mathbf{z})}
. Observe that
P:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{+1,-1\}
.
Therefore we can rewrite for
\mathbf{e}\neq\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
,

\displaystyle\hat{W}^{p}(\mathbf{e})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n/2+1}\sqrt{N_{p}}}\cdot\frac{2^{n/2}}{2^{n/2}}\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}P(\mathbf{z})\cdot(-1)^{\langle\sigma(\mathbf{e}),\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n/2}}{2\sqrt{N_{p}}}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}P(\mathbf{z})\cdot(-1)^{\langle\sigma(\mathbf{e}),\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n/2}}{2\sqrt{N_{p}}}\cdot\E_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[(-1)^{\langle\sigma(\mathbf{e}),\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}P(\mathbf{z})\right]=\frac{2^{n/2}}{2\sqrt{N_{p}}}\cdot\tilde{P}(\sigma(\mathbf{e})),

where we define the function
\tilde{P}
such that for any
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,

\tilde{P}(\mathbf{e})\coloneqq\E_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[(-1)^{\langle\mathbf{e},\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}\cdot P(\mathbf{z})\right]=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}}P(\mathbf{z})\cdot(-1)^{\langle\mathbf{e},\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}.

We begin by splitting the support of
\mathscr{P}
into two subsets
\mathsf{Central}\sqcup\mathsf{Tail}
where

\displaystyle\mathsf{Central}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{p\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathscr{P}):N_{p}\in[2^{n-1}-2^{0.9n-1},2^{n-1}+2^{0.9n-1}]\right\}

\displaystyle\mathsf{Tail}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{supp}(\mathscr{P})\setminus\mathsf{Central}

Define
\delta\coloneqq\Pr_{p}\left[p\in\mathsf{Tail}\right]
and observe by a standard Chebyshev’s inequality

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\Pr_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{N_p - 2^{n-1}}>2^{0.9n-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\E_{p}\left[(N_{p}-2^{n-1})^{2}\right]}{2^{1.8n-2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n-2}}{2^{1.8n-2}}=2^{-0.8n}.~

(By Lemma
4.12
.)

Recall that for all
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
we have
\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}}\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{p}(\mat{e})}^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}}\absolutevalue{W^{p}(\mat{e})}^{2}=N_{p}/N_{p}=1
where the first equality is due to Parseval’s Theorem (Lemma
2.10
). This implies that for all
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, for all
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
, we have
\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^{p}(\mat{e})}^{2}\leq 1
.
Therefore, for any
\mathbf{e}\neq\mathbf{0}
,

\displaystyle\E_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^p(\mat{e})}^{2}~\bigg|~p\in\mathsf{Tail}\right]\leq 1.

Then observe that for
\mathbf{e}\neq\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and for
n\geq 10
,

\displaystyle\E_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{ \hat{W}^p(\mat{e})}^{2}~\bigg|~p\in\mathsf{Central}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{p}\left[\frac{2^{n-2}}{N_{p}}\cdot\tilde{P}(\mathbf{e})^{2}~\bigg|~p\in\mathsf{Central}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{n-2}}{2^{n-1}-2^{0.9n-1}}\cdot\E_{p}\left[\tilde{P}(\mathbf{e})^{2}~\bigg|~p\in\mathsf{Central}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n-2}}{2^{n-1}-2^{0.9n-1}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\cdot\E_{p}\left[\tilde{P}(\mathbf{e})^{2}\right]-\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\E_{p}\left[\tilde{P}(\mathbf{e})^{2}~\big|~p\in\mathsf{Tail}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{n-2}}{2^{n-1}-2^{0.9n-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\delta}\cdot\E_{p}\left[\tilde{P}(\mathbf{e})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1\cdot 2\cdot\frac{1}{2^{n}}=2^{-n+1},

where the first inequality uses the conditioning on
p\in\mathsf{Central}
to imply that
N_{p}\geq 2^{n-1}-2^{0.9n-1}
, the next line (third line) uses the total law of probability, the fourth line uses the non-negativity of
\tilde{P}(\mathbf{e})^{2}
, and the fifth line uses Lemma
4.11
.
Finally, for
n\geq 10
,

\displaystyle\E_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^p(\mat{e})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\Pr_{p}\left[p\in\mathsf{Tail}\right]\cdot\E_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{\hat{W}^p(\mat{e})}^{2}~\bigg|~p\in\mathsf{Tail}\right]+\Pr_{p}\left[p\in\mathsf{Central}\right]\cdot\E_{p}\left[\absolutevalue{ \hat{W}^p(\mat{e})}^{2}~\bigg|~p\in\mathsf{Central}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2^{-0.8n}\cdot 1+1\cdot 2^{-n+1}\leq 2^{-n/2}.

∎

In the above lemma, we used the following fact.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
P:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
and
\tilde{P}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as above relative to a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
.
For all
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, for any 2-wise independent distribution
\mathscr{P}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
,

\E_{p\sim\mathscr{P}}[\tilde{P}(\mathbf{e})^{2}]=\frac{1}{2^{n}}.

Proof.

\displaystyle\E_{p}[\tilde{P}(\mathbf{e})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E_{p}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}P(\mathbf{z})\cdot(-1)^{\langle\mathbf{e},\mathbf{z}\rangle_{2}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{p}\left[\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}P(\mathbf{z})\cdot P(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\cdot(-1)^{\langle\mathbf{e},\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rangle_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\sum_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\E_{p}\left[P(\mathbf{z})\cdot P(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\right]\cdot(-1)^{\langle\mathbf{e},\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rangle_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\E_{p}\left[P(\mathbf{z})^{2}\right]+\sum_{\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\E_{p}\left[P(\mathbf{z})\cdot P(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\right]\cdot(-1)^{\langle\mathbf{e},\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rangle_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}

Where in the last equality, 2-wise independence directly implies that for
\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,

\E_{p}\left[P(\mathbf{z})\cdot P(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\right]=\E_{p}[P(\mathbf{z})]\cdot\E_{p}[P(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})]=0,

and where we use the fact that
\E_{p}\left[P(\mathbf{z})^{2}\right]=1
since the codomain of
P
is
\{\pm 1\}
.

∎

Lemma 4.12

(Moments of the Variety)
.

For any polynomial
p
, let
N_{p}
denote the number of roots of
p
.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for any
2
-wise independent distribution
\mathscr{P}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
,

\displaystyle\mu\coloneqq

5
Conjectured Classical Hardness

Although the proof of quantum easiness is independent of the degree
d
, we instantiate our construction only for
d\geq 3
. We make this choice because, in our regime, the system instantiated with quadratic polynomials can be efficiently solved (under a very plausible assumption). Indeed, one can exploit the reduction of quadratic forms to produce extra linear equations in the system by fixing relatively few variables. We describe this approach in Section
5.1
, where we note that extending it to polynomials of degree
d\geq 3
is unlikely to be feasible.

The classical solving algorithms we studied all have exponential complexity in
n^{2}
. In Section
5.2
, we examine algorithms that can be viewed as enhanced versions of exhaustive search. The most efficient of them has a cost of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{(1-\alpha)n^{2}})
, where the
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
notation suppresses polynomial factors. This exponent can be improved by using more efficient enumerative algorithms for solving polynomial systems (such as
[
Din21b
]
), but this improvement is limited and the complexity remains exponential in
n^{2}
. In Section
5.3
, we consider algebraic algorithms related to Gröbner bases
[
Buc65
,
Laz83
,
Fau99
,
Fau02
]
. Their behavior is provably exponential for generic degree-
d
systems where the number of equations is roughly equal to the number of variables
[
Bar04
,
BFS15
]
, but their cost remains uncertain for specific structured systems that do not fall into well-studied categories (see, for example,
[
FSEDS10
,
FSS11
]
). This partly explains why it is challenging to analyze Gröbner basis solvers as precisely as those in Section
5.2
. Still, we provide both experimental and theoretical evidence suggesting that they should not threaten our construction.

Finally, note that all the solving algorithms described so far do not take advantage of the efficient decoding procedure of the code
C
. However, this procedure is a key component of our quantum algorithm, raising the question of whether classical algorithms could also exploit it. Despite significant efforts, we failed to come up with potential out-of-the-box solvers that can leverage it, but we think it is an interesting direction for research. An informal discussion on this can be found in Appendix
B
.

Remark 5.1
.

In this section, and with a slight abuse of notation, we denote by
C
a binary image of the original dual Reed-Solomon code of length
m
and dimension
\alpha m
over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
that is obtained through basis extension by using a fixed basis of the
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-vector space
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. This code is an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear code of length

\alpha mn

5.1
Specialization approach for quadratics and higher degrees

Theorem 5.2
.

Assume that the system
\mathcal{P}
in Section
3

p_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,n}]

has a solution with high probability, there exists a classical algorithm that solves
\mathcal{F}
in polynomial time with high probability.

While the assumption of Theorem
5.2
is non-trivial to prove, it becomes easy to prove if the Reed-Solomon code were replaced by a random linear code of the same dimension (in which case, the system would have exponentially many solutions with very high probability). The algorithm is described in the proof of the theorem. The key observation is that every quadratic form can be efficiently written as a canonical normal form. Moreover, in our setting, the equations
p_{i}
involve distinct variable blocks
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, so that their normal forms will also be variable-disjoint.

Proposition 5.3

(Normal Form in Characteristic
2

[
LN97
]
)
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a quadratic form defined as
p(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}
, where
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times n}
is upper triangular. Let
r
be the rank of the skew-symmetric matrix
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{M}^{\perp}
, which is an even number. Then
p
is equivalent to one of the following types of quadratic forms:

1.

x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}+\cdots+x_{r-1}x_{r}

2.

x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}+\cdots+x_{r-1}x_{r}+x_{r-1}^{2}+x_{r}^{2}
.

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

The algorithm starts by applying an invertible linear change of variables
\mathbf{x}_{i}\mapsto\mathbf{y}_{i}=(y_{i,1},\dots,y_{i,n})
in each equation
p_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\mathcal{P}
, yielding a new equation
p^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{i})=q_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{i})+\ell_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{i})
, where
q_{i}
is the normal form of the quadratic part of

and
\deg{(\ell_{i})}\leq 1
. One can without loss of generality assume that
q_{i}
is of the form of Case 1 in Proposition
5.3
by merging potential squares into
\ell_{i}
. Unless additional conditions are imposed on the coefficients, note that this procedure does not apply to general MQ systems in which all equations share the same set of variables.

The rest of the algorithm exploits the sparsity of the
q_{i}
’s. Without loss of generality, let us assume that
n
is even and that the rank of
q_{i}
is maximal for
i\in[m]
. For each
i\in[m]
, the algorithm arbitrarily fixes the
\frac{n}{2}
variables

with even indices

. The structure of
q_{i}
ensures that, after specialization, the resulting equation in the remaining variables

with odd indices

is of degree at most one. As a result, this procedure yields an affine linear system of
m
equations in the
\frac{mn}{2}
variables

and odd

In order to solve
\mathcal{F}
, the algorithm finally combines this linear system with the

, applying the same change of variables and the same specialization to them before the merge. This gives an affine linear system of
m+(1-\alpha)mn
equations in the same
\frac{mn}{2}

. This system is largely underdetermined for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
. In case solutions exist (which would occur with very high probability in case the Reed-Solomon code is replaced by a random linear code of the same dimension), an arbitrary solution can be found in polynomial time and easily extended to a solution of
\mathcal{F}
.
∎

Generalization to higher-degree polynomials.

The above algorithm highly relies on the fact that each equation of
\mathcal{P}
involves distinct variables, which remains true in our construction where

. However, we show that even a generalized form of this algorithm fails when
d=3
, and similarly for higher degrees.

Before that, recall that the algorithm in the quadratic case selected a special set of
\frac{n}{2}
linear variable expressions (corresponding to
\frac{n}{2}
variables after a linear change of variables), such that assigning
any

\frac{n}{2}
Boolean values of these expressions converts each quadratic polynomial into an affine linear polynomial. This strategy can be related to techniques commonly employed for undetermined MQ systems
[
TW12
,
FNT21
,
Has23
]
. The key difference is that, for random systems, these algorithms select
specific
Boolean linear expressions to cancel out the quadratic terms, resulting in affine linear polynomials. Note also that no efficient generalization of the techniques in
[
TW12
,
FNT21
,
Has23
]
to higher-degree systems is known. In the following, we show that such a generalization also fails for our system with
d=3
.

Given a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and an affine linear polynomial
\ell=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}x_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that
\alpha_{i}=1
for some index

from

amounts to replacing this variable with
\alpha_{0}+\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}}^{n}\alpha_{j}x_{j}
in the algebraic normal form of
p
(as a sum of monomials). The degree of this result may (or may not) be smaller than the degree of
p
. Importantly, the degree of
p
after variable elimination is independent of which variable was eliminated using
\ell
, since an invertible affine change of variables relates two polynomials obtained by eliminating different variables. More generally, given a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
t
(consistent) affine linear polynomials
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
for
i\in[t]
, we can derive the polynomial
p
restricted to the corresponding affine subspace by performing the elimination process iteratively. Its degree is well-defined regardless of which variables were eliminated.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a uniformly chosen polynomial of degree 3.
Then, the probability that
there exists an affine subspace
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of co-dimension at most
t=n-n^{3/4}=n-o(n)
for which

restricted to
A

has degree at most 2 is at most
2^{-\Omega(n^{2})}
.

Proof.

Let
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a uniformly chosen polynomial of degree
3
, and fix an affine subpace
A
of co-dimension at most
t^{\prime}\leq t
together with
t^{\prime}
affine equations
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
that span this subspace. Eliminating
t^{\prime}
variables from

using
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{t^{\prime}}
, we remain with a uniform polynomial in
n-t^{\prime}

Such a polynomial has
\binom{n-t^{\prime}}{3}

2^{-\binom{n-t^{\prime}}{3}}\leq 2^{-\binom{n-t}{3}}
.

On the other hand, the number of possible choices for affine subspaces of dimension at most
t
(spanned by at most

2^{(n+1)t}

Taking a union bound over all affine subspaces of co-dimension at most

Based on Lemma

(linearly transformed) variables in each

5.2
Combinatorial algorithms

We describe algorithms akin to exhaustive search, which we refer to as “combinatorial”. The main idea of these algorithms is very simple. Recall our system consists of
n^{3}
variables,
n^{2}
equations of degree
d>2
over disjoint variable sets (each equation with
n
variables), and
(1-\alpha)n^{3}
linear equations induced by the Reed-Solomon code. This system is largely underdetermined; specifically, we have about
n^{3}-n^{2}-(1-\alpha)n^{3}\approx\alpha n^{3}
more variables than equations. Thus, we specialize about
\alpha n^{3}
variables that make up
\alpha n^{2}
degree-
d
equations in a way that these equations are all satisfied. We remain with about

(1-\alpha)n^{2}
degree-
d
equations. We then eliminate the
(1-\alpha)n^{3}
variables from the linear equation system using Gaussian elimination. Overall, we remain with a system of about
(1-\alpha)n^{2}
degree-
d
equations and variables. Assuming that a solution exists, it can be found by exhaustive search in time of about
2^{(1-\alpha)n^{2}}
. The assumption that a solution exists with high probability is non-trivial, but plausible (and easy to prove if the Reed-Solomon code were replaced with a random linear code of the same dimension).

Theorem
5.6
gives a precise algorithm that achieves the claimed complexity of
2^{(1-\alpha)n^{2}}
. This algorithm simply consists of sampling solutions to
\mathcal{P}
and verifying whether they belong to the code
C
, and its complexity relies on an assumption that we now describe.

The dual Reed-Solomon code admits a systematic generator matrix
\mathbf{G}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{\alpha m}&\mathbf{P}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{\alpha m\times m},~\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{\alpha m\times(1-\alpha)m}
, where
\mathsf{rk}{(\mathbf{P})}=\min{(\alpha m,(1-\alpha)m)}
since Reed-Solomon codes are MDS. By choosing the extension field basis appropriately, we can further make sure that the binary image
C
has a generator matrix
\overline{\mathbf{G}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{\alpha mn}&\mathbf{R}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\alpha mn\times mn},~\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\alpha mn\times(1-\alpha)mn}
, where
\mathsf{rk}{(\mathbf{R})}=n\mathsf{rk}{(\mathbf{P})}=n\min{(\alpha m,(1-\alpha)m)}
. Since in our setting
\alpha>\frac{7}{8}>\frac{1}{2}
, the linear map
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\alpha mn}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(1-\alpha)mn}
defined by the matrix
\mathbf{R}
is surjective. Now, let
Z_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathcal{P})
be the set of solutions to
\mathcal{P}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. We can express it as the Cartesian product
V\times W
, where
V\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\alpha mn}
and
W\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(1-\alpha)mn}
are the solution sets of
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{\alpha m}\}
and
\{p_{\alpha m+1},\dots,p_{m}\}
, respectively. Since the solutions of
\mathcal{F}
correspond to the dual Reed-Solomon codewords that satisfy the equations of
\mathcal{P}
, we obtain the final solution set

Z_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathcal{F})=\left\{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}\mathbf{R}),~\mathbf{a}\in V,~\mathbf{a}\mathbf{R}\in W\right\}.

In particular, we see that the right factor of each element of
Z_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathcal{F})
belongs to
V\mathbf{R}\cap W
. This makes it important to grasp this intersection, noting that
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\alpha mn\times(1-\alpha)mn}
depends on the Reed-Solomon code and the extension field basis, while
V\times W
is the set of solutions of
m
Boolean degree-
d
polynomials sampled independently from this code. As the linear map
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\alpha mn}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(1-\alpha)mn}
defined by
\mathbf{R}
is surjective and as
\absolutevalue{V}=2^{\alpha m(n-1)}
remains significantly larger than
\absolutevalue{\ff{2}^{(1-\alpha)mn}}=2^{(1-\alpha)mn}
, we adopt the following assumption, which can also be verified experimentally. Under this assumption, we have that
V\mathbf{R}\cap W=W
.

Assumption 5.5
.

Using the notation above, we assume that the linear map defined by
\mathbf{R}
, when restricted to
V
, remains surjective.

Theorem 5.6
.

Under Assumption
5.5
, there exists a classical algorithm that solves the system
\mathcal{F}
in time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{(1-\alpha)m})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{(1-\alpha)n^{2}})
.

Proof.

First, we can neglect the complexity of finding all solutions to
\mathcal{P}
since it is lower than the cost claimed in the theorem. Indeed, we can simply solve the
m
degree-
d
polynomials of
\mathcal{P}
separately, as each involves
n
distinct variables. Note again that the degree
d
is treated as a constant.

Then, using the notation above, the
\alpha mn
leftmost components of an arbitrary element in
Z_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathcal{P})
correspond to a vector
\mathbf{a}\in V
. This element is an element of
Z_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathcal{F})
if and only if the vector
\mathbf{a}\mathbf{R}
belongs to
W
. This happens with probability

\frac{\absolutevalue{V\mat{R} \cap W}}{\absolutevalue{V\mat{R}}}=\frac{\absolutevalue{W}}{\absolutevalue{\ff{2}^{(1-\alpha)mn}}}=\frac{2^{(1-\alpha)m(n-1)}}{2^{(1-\alpha)mn}}=2^{(\alpha-1)m},

where the first equality is under Assumption
5.5
. The cost follows by taking the inverse of this probability.
∎

Note that another naive algorithm is to sample elements of
C
and test if they satisfy the equations of
\mathcal{P}
, but with worse complexity

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}{\left(\frac{\absolutevalue{C}}{\absolutevalue{Z_{\ff{2}}(\mathcal{F})}}\right)}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{2^{\alpha mn}}{2^{\alpha mn-m}}\right)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{m})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(2^{n^{2}}).

Finally, even if the sets
V
and
W
can be assumed to be known, we have not found any clever collision-based methods for finding elements in
V\mathbf{R}\cap W
. In fact, under Assumption
5.5
, such methods do not exist.

5.3
Gröbner basis algorithms

Gröbner basis methods can often be more efficient than other types of solvers for certain structured systems, both empirically and in some provable cases. Before applying them, we restrict ourselves to the setting typically considered in cryptanalysis, namely, overdetermined systems. For that purpose, we fix variables in
\mathcal{F}
to reduce it to a smaller overdetermined degree-
d
system
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
with
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
equations and variables, as was done at the beginning of Section
5.2
. The goal is to determine whether Gröbner basis methods can offer a significant improvement over exhaustive search and enumerative methods for such an
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
.

It is known that solving a generic degree-
d
system with the same number of equations and variables as
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
would require time exponential in
n^{2}
, with precise estimates for the associated complexity exponent available (see, for instance,
[
Bar04
,
BFS15
]
). However, our system
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
is highly structured. As we have just said, this structure may be what makes Gröbner basis methods significantly more efficient, while also making their theoretical analysis more difficult. In our context, the structure of
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
depends on both the structure of the original system
\mathcal{F}
and the specific way in which the specialization is carried out.

It is extremely unclear how to approximate the complexity of solving
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
by that of a generic degree-
d
system, or by that of any other structured system whose solving complexity has been tackled in the literature. We provide some theoretical and experimental evidence in Appendix
A
that points to the exponential run time required by some common Gröbner basis algorithms. We report the timings of experiments considering one specialization strategy on
\mathcal{F}
, which seems to be the most relevant, examining the performance of Gröbner basis algorithms on the resulting specialization
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
. We refer readers to Appendix
A
for details.

Appendix A
Details on Gröbner Basis Algorithms

This appendix gives more details on solving strategies that involve specializing variables in the system
\mathcal{F}
, followed by the application of Gröbner basis techniques to the resulting mildly overdetermined system
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
. In Section
A.1
, we describe these techniques before applying them to such an
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
. Their cost depends on several factors, such as the number of equations, the number of variables, and their degrees, but it is ultimately governed by the system’s overall structure, whose influence is also the most difficult to characterize. In Section
A.2
, we detail the structure of
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
. As established in Section
5.1
, we have already ruled out the existence of clever specializations that would yield equations of degree lower than
d
, so that
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
only contains degree-
d
equations. However, the way specialization is carried out still influences the precise number of equations and variables beyond the
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
baseline, as well as the overall structure of the system. In Section
A.3
, we present the results of our experiments for one particular specialization strategy, which seems to yield the easiest system to solve.

A.1
Solving overdetermined systems via Gröbner bases

Broadly speaking, by applying Gröbner basis techniques, we mean either applying directly standard Gröbner basis algorithms commonly employed in cryptanalysis
[
Fau99
,
Fau02
]
or combining them with additional specializations that aim to make the system
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
more overdetermined, thereby facilitating their application. Algorithms from
[
BFP09
,
JV17
,
BDT22
]
fall into the second category, which we refer to as “hybrid methods”. Since we start from a system
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
having an equal number of equations and variables, the probability that a random specialization keeps this system consistent is exponentially small in the number of specialized variables. Therefore, the process must be repeated approximately as many times as the inverse of that probability, with consistency checked in each iteration by applying the Gröbner basis algorithm.

Both types of methods have exponential complexity in the MQ regime that is generally considered in cryptanalysis, and solving the same number of generic degree-
d
equations in the same number of variables for
d\geq 3
is even more costly than in the quadratic case
[
Bar04
,
BFS15
]
. Note also that an asymptotic comparison between these two methods has only been sketched for generic MQ systems (see
[
BFP09
]
). While the work in
[
BFP09
]
can, in principle, be extended to higher-degree generic systems, such a comparison has not yet been performed for more structured systems.

Our experiments in Section
A.3
include the simplest hybrid method of
[
BFP09
]
by fixing randomly
f\geq 1
variables in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
. Our goal there is to explore whether variable fixing can lead to empirical improvements rather than to provide a full asymptotic comparison. In fact, analyzing Gröbner basis algorithms applied directly to
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
, without fixing any variables, is already quite challenging (this first task can be seen both as a particular case and as a prerequisite for such a comparison).

A.2
Reduction to an overdetermined system
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}

In the following, we set
k=\alpha m
for the dimension of the dual Reed-Solomon code over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, and we reuse some notation of Section
5.2
. Specifically, let

\overline{\mathbf{G}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{kn}&\mathbf{R}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{kn\times mn},~\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{kn\times(m-k)n}

(14)

be a generator matrix for the binary image of this code, once again obtained by choosing an appropriate extension field basis. In Section
5.2
, we have seen that the matrix
\mathbf{R}
is of full rank in this case. Let us also recall that the system
\mathcal{F}
was obtained from the initial degree-
d
equations of
\mathcal{P}
by eliminating variables using the parity check equations. This process amounts to introducing a vector of
kn
new variables
\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k})
such that
\mathbf{y}\overline{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbf{x}
, where
\mathbf{x}
represents the original variables. The system
\mathcal{F}
is then reinterpreted as a system in these new variables
\mathbf{y}
. More precisely:

System 1

(Underdetermined system
\mathcal{F}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
denote the initial system, where
p_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[\mathbf{x}_{i}]
for
i\in[m]
, let
\overline{\mathbf{G}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{kn}&\mathbf{R}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{kn\times mn}
denote the generator matrix of Equation (
14
), and let
\mathbf{R}_{j}
denote the
j
-th block of size
kn\times n
in
\mathbf{R}
for
j\in[m-k]
. The equations of
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}[\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k}]
can be defined by:

•

for
i\in[k]
,

f_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{i})=p_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{i});

•

for
i\in[k+1..m]
,

f_{i}(\mathbf{y})=p_{i}(\mathbf{y}\mathbf{R}_{i-k}).

In other words,
k
equations of
\mathcal{F}
remain unchanged from those in
\mathcal{P}
, while the remaining
m-k
equations involve all
y_{i,j}
variables mixed in a way that depends on the dual Reed-Solomon code.

We now describe specialization strategies applied to
\mathcal{F}
that yield an overdetermined system
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
. The main point is that we are free to choose how to distribute the specializations across the
\mathbf{y}_{i}
blocks.

System 2

(Specialization of System
1
)
.

Let
J\subset[kn]
be the set of specialized positions in
\mathbf{y}
and let
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\absolutevalue{J}}
be the vector that contains the specialized values. In other words, each initial block
\mathbf{y}_{i}
is replaced with a block whose components can be reordered as
(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i})
, where
\mathbf{t}_{i}=\mathbf{t}_{j\in J\cap[n(i-1)+1..ni]}
contains binary elements and where
\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{j\in\bar{J}\cap[n(i-1)+1..ni]}
still contains variables. Note that both
\mathbf{z}_{i}
and
\mathbf{t}_{i}
can be empty if all and no positions are fixed within this block, respectively. We consider
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{k^{\prime}})
the set of non-empty blocks, where
k^{\prime}=\absolutevalue{\lbrace i \in[k],~\abs{J \cap[n(i-1)+1..ni]} < n \rbrace}
. The specialization of
\mathcal{F}
at the positions
J
to the values described by
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\absolutevalue{J}}
is then given by:

•

for
i\in[k^{\prime}]
,

h_{i}(\mathbf{z}_{i})=p_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{i})|_{\mathbf{y}_{j\in J\cap[n(i-1)+1..ni]}=\mathbf{t}_{i}},

(15)

•

for
i\in[k+1..m]
,

h_{i}(\mathbf{z})=p_{i}(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{S}_{i-k}+\underbrace{\mathbf{t}\mathbf{T}_{i-k}}_{{\in\mathbb{F}_{2}}}),

(16)

where
\mathbf{S}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(kn-\absolutevalue{J})\times n}
is the set of rows of the matrix
\mathbf{R}_{i}
defined in System
1
with indexes in
\bar{J}
and where
\mathbf{T}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\absolutevalue{J}\times n}
is the set of remaining rows.

Remark A.1
.

We note that our specialized systems share similarities with hard-to-solve algebraic systems considered in recent cryptanalyses
[
BØ23
,
JM25
]
. Indeed, they contain constraints involving specific blocks of variables
\mathbf{z}_{i}
, as well as independent constraints involving all variables
\mathbf{z}
, similar to the structure observed in those works. However, there are significant differences. First, the degree of our equations exceeds those in
[
BØ23
,
JM25
]
, where all equations are of degree 1 or 2. Moreover, the sizes of the variable blocks can vary in our case, unlike in
[
BØ23
,
JM25
]
. Most notably, the variables
\mathbf{z}
are compressed into length-
n
vectors via the
\mathbf{S}_{i}
matrices in Equation (
16
), which prevents us from directly treating the constraints involving all variables as generic degree-
d
constraints. This contrasts with the generic degree-1 (resp. degree-2) constraints considered in
[
BØ23
]
(resp.
[
JM25
]
).

Let
n_{i}\in[n]
be the size of
\mathbf{z}_{i}
from System
2
for
i\in[k^{\prime}]
. Specializations that yield a well-defined system correspond to the condition

\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}n_{i}=m-k+k^{\prime}.

Our
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
will be a system of the type described in System
2
that satisfies this criterion. Regardless of the choice of
J
, this implies that the number of equations and variables is in
[m-k..m]
, and thus is in
\Theta(n^{2})
.

We will mostly discuss
J
rather than the choice of the corresponding set of specialized values
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\absolutevalue{J}}
. Different fixing strategies, along with partial or full knowledge of the solutions to
\mathcal{P}
, can result in different success probabilities. However, as our main goal is to determine if polynomial-time algorithms exist for solving
\mathcal{F}_{\text{spec}}
, we will not explore this aspect in detail. In particular, we expect that different specializations over the same
J
will require roughly the same amount of time to solve. Among choices of
J
in which the final blocks
\mathbf{z}_{i}
all have the same length, one natural configuration is to fix variables evenly across the
k
blocks of
\mathbf{y}
, resulting in each block having
n_{i}=m/k=1/\alpha
unfixed variables for
i\in[k]
. Concretely, this implies that, on average, each
\mathbf{z}_{i}
contains one or two unfixed variables. Another configuration corresponds to fixing as many blocks as possible completely, leaving the remaining ones unchanged. In this case, the number of remaining (fully unfixed) blocks is given by
k^{\prime}=\frac{m-k}{n-1}
. Naturally, there are trade-offs, including approaches whose final blocks have different lengths. While some particular choices of
J
may offer advantages in terms of solving complexity, the way the solving algorithms of Section
A.1
operate suggests that the asymptotic behavior should remain roughly the same. In other words, we expect that a polynomial-time algorithm will either exist for all such specialized systems or none of them.

The experiments reported below focus on the second configuration with
\frac{(1-\alpha)m}{n-1}=\frac{m-k}{n-1}
blocks, each of size
n
. Recalling that
n\frac{m-k}{n-1}\approx(1-\alpha)n^{2}
, this specialized system has the size outlined at the beginning of Section
5.2
. In particular, it is smaller by a constant factor, specifically
1-\alpha\leq 1/8
, compared to the system arising from the first configuration in which the variables are evenly distributed into
k
blocks
\mathbf{z}_{i}
, each of size about
1/\alpha
. This motivated our decision to focus our experiments on the second configuration.

Appendix B
Leveraging the Code Structure in Classical Cryptanalysis

We have tried to exploit the efficient decoding procedure or, at least, the structural properties of the Reed-Solomon code for classical cryptanalysis. Before describing some unsuccessful attempts, note that, according to our analysis, the complexity of the best solving algorithms studied in Sections
5.2
and
5.3
depends only on the code rate
\alpha
.

Using Lemma
2.20
, our dual Reed-Solomon code is a particular Generalized Reed-Solomon code. Each of its codewords
\mathbf{c}\in C
can thus be written as

\mathbf{c}=(v^{\prime}_{1}g(\gamma_{1}),\dots,v^{\prime}_{m}g(\gamma_{m}))\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m},

where
(v^{\prime}_{1},\dots,v^{\prime}_{m})
and
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m})
are fixed and where
g
is a univariate polynomial of degree
<k
. Using a fixed
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-isomorphism between and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, it is also standard to associate a set of
n
quadratic Boolean polynomials
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\}
in
n
variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
with a univariate polynomial
F
over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
in a new variable
X
, such that this polynomial is quadratic in terms of
(X,\dots,X^{2^{n-1}})
. The same holds if the
f_{i}
’s have degree
d\geq 3
, and this time the polynomial
F
has degree
d
in
(X,\dots,X^{2^{n-1}})
. Doing this on
\{p_{i},0\dots,0\}
for
i\in[m]
from our construction yields a polynomial system
\{P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}[X_{1}]\times\dots\times\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}[X_{m}]
, whose solutions correspond to that of
\mathcal{P}
via the fixed isomorphism. Our problem can thus be restated as the one of finding a univariate polynomial
g
of degree
<k
such that
P_{i}(v^{\prime}_{i}g(\gamma_{i}))=0
for
i\in[m]
. By introducing
k
formal unknowns corresponding to the coefficients of
g
, this gives a polynomial system of
m
equations and
k
variables over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. A system of the same type can in fact be derived from an arbitrary
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-linear code with generator matrix
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{k\times n}
by introducing variables
y_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
for a codeword
(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\mathbf{G}
. Therefore, such an approach should not bring direct improvements.

More generally, we discuss the feasibility of using the efficient decoding procedure of any type of structured code, beyond just Reed-Solomon. First, it is not obvious to us how to formulate a relevant decoding instance – target point, metric – that yields a codeword whose components satisfy the equations of
\mathcal{P}
. However, we can still hope to use the structure of
C
to efficiently find such a codeword, perhaps more directly, as we have just tried to achieve in the case of a Generalized Reed-Solomon code. When the code is unstructured, decoding algorithms and algorithms to find low-weight codewords are fairly similar (the same happens for random lattices). Therefore, a first optimistic scenario for cryptanalysis might be that the polynomial constraints – which are independent of
C
– model a sort of low-weight feature or more generally a rather simple-to-state property. As far as we know, this does not seem to be the case. To see this, let us assume for now that
C
still comes from an
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-linear code
\subset\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{m}
. In this case, the set of solutions to
\{P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\}
is the product
Z=Z_{1}\times\dots\times Z_{m}
, where
Z_{i}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
are the roots of the univariate polynomial
P_{i}
and the
Z_{i}
’s are independent of each other. While one could try to exploit the non-random character of each
Z_{i}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, the coordinate-wise independence does not seem advantageous from a solving perspective. A second optimistic scenario is that there exist code families tailored to such a set
Z
, but obviously, the code chosen to instantiate the construction has no reason to belong to such a family. Overall, all these reasons make us think that an efficient classical algorithm that can exploit the structure of
C
should be a significant contribution to cryptanalysis.
