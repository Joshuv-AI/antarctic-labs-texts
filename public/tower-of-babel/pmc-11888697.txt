Various Methods for Measuring Gingival Thickness – A Comparative Analysis of Three Different Methodologies
Authors: Ankita Pati, Balaji Manohar, Awneet Kaur, Snigdha Sagar, Shruti Sharma, S. Bhuvaneshwari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Measuring gingival thickness (GT) is crucial in periodontal and implant dentistry as it impacts clinical decisions and outcomes.Materials and methods:Several methods are employed to assess GT, including conebeam computed tomography (CBCT), transgingival probing, and ultrasonography. Each technique has distinct advantages and limitations in terms of accuracy, invasiveness, and practical application.Results:This study has established that transgingival probing is the gold standard technique for measuring GT.

INTRODUCTION

The measurement of gingival thickness (GT) is a critical aspect in periodontal diagnostics, treatment planning, and outcome assessment.[1] Accurate determination of GT is essential for procedures such as dental implant placement, periodontal surgeries, and esthetic dental treatments.[2] Thin gingival biotypes are more likely to experience gingival recession, bone dehiscence, and alveolar ridge resorption following dental extraction, whereas thick gingival biotypes are more likely to experience periodontal pockets and fibrotic tissue response, as well as a more consistent response to regenerative treatments. Additionally, the esthetic appearance might be influenced by the gingival biotype, especially in the maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth. Consequently, one of the most important aspects of preoperative exams can be measuring GT before periodontal and implant surgeries as well as restorative procedures. Various techniques have been developed and employed to measure GT, each with its own advantages and limitations. Thin tissue biotype was characterised by Claffey and Shanley as GT less than 1.5 mm, and thick tissue biotype as a tissue thickness greater than 2 mm. A number of techniques have been put forth to gauge GT: 1) invasive procedures like using an endodontic file or transgingival probing (TRAN) and 2) noninvasive procedures like using an endodontic file, modifiable callipers, plaster models, ultrasound devices, and, most recently, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and the “puffed cheek” method (computed tomography scans with swollen cheeks).[3] Prior to orthodontic procedures requiring the installation of miniscrews in the buccal mucosa, gingival biotype assessment and GT measurement are essential in order to estimate the primary stability of the miniscrews. This comparative evaluation aims to critically assess these different methods, examining their precision, reliability, invasiveness, and practicality.[345] By evaluating the effectiveness and applicability of each technique in clinical settings, this study seeks to identify the most accurate and efficient methods for GT measurement, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and advancing periodontal research.[6]

METHODOLOGY

This is an experimental study in which data from routine outpatient department (OPD) patients’ records was considered. Three observers participated, and their interobserver reliability was documented. For the purposes of this investigation, patients between the ages of 20 and 30 were included. The research maintained a 95% confidence interval, a 5% alpha error, and a power of 80%. The data were imported into MS Excel (.xls) files, and IBM SPSS Statistical software version 25 was utilized for the study. This study involved 30 patients, with ten patients in each group. Every patient received an explanation of the operation prior to the commencement of the study and gave their informed consent. The maxillary central incisors were used to measure GT. Every participant in the research had normal dental health and no tooth crowding. Five groups of patients were created using various methods for measuring GT. RESULT

The reliability between the observers for each method was calculated to be 0.78 (good).

DISCUSSION

Comparing these methods using findings from clinical studies provides insight into their relative efficacy and appropriateness for different clinical scenarios. Research by Barriviera et al.[7] demonstrated that CBCT offers greater accuracy and reproducibility in measuring GT compared to transgingival probing. However, the study also highlighted the practicality and low cost of transgingival probing for routine use. A study by Eger et al.[8] found that ultrasonography provided more accurate and consistent measurements than trans gingival probing. The noninvasive nature of ultrasonography was particularly noted as a significant advantage over the discomfort associated with probing. Research by Muñoz et al.[9] compared CBCT and ultrasonography, finding both methods to be highly accurate. However, ultrasonography was preferred due to the absence of radiation exposure, despite the slightly lower detail compared to CBCT. The choice of method for measuring GT depends on the clinical context, available resources, and specific patient needs. CBCT provides highly detailed and accurate measurements but comes with concerns about radiation exposure and higher costs. Transgingival probing, while cost-effective and simple, may lack the accuracy and consistency of the other methods and is invasive. Ultrasonography strikes a balance, offering accurate, noninvasive measurements without radiation exposure, though it requires operator skill and appropriate equipment. Future advancements in these technologies and further comparative studies will continue to refine the best practices for measuring GT in clinical dentistry.
