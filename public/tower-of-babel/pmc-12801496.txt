SRP/dGATAb modulates the ecdysone response bidirectionally via locus-specific regulatory landscape
Authors: Aleksandra A Evdokimova, Anna A Tarakanova, Maksim Erokhin, Darya Chetverina, Ming-an Sun, Nadezhda E Vorobyeva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSteroid hormones drive the transcription of developmental genes by activating distinct sets of enhancers across tissues and developmental contexts, but the mechanisms that target specific enhancers remain incompletely understood, even in model organisms. It has been proposed that selective binding of nuclear receptors occurs at regulatory sites primed by other classes of DNA-binding transcription factors. However, direct studies of cooperation between nuclear receptors and different transcription factors remain limited. A previous study suggested that the GATA family factor SRP/dGATAb primes regulatory sites in S2 Schneider cells for activation by 20-hydroxyecdysone (20E), the principal steroid hormone in Drosophila development. Yet the genome-wide impact of SRP/dGATAb depletion on transcriptional responses to 20E has not been examined.ResultsWe investigated the role of SRP/dGATAb in the response of S2 Schneider cells to 20E using SRP/dGATAb depletion via RNA interference. Combined RNA-Seq and ChIP-Seq analyses identified primary targets of SRP/dGATAb that (i) are transcriptionally induced by 20E and (ii) contain binding sites for both EcR and SRP/dGATAb. SRP/dGATAb depletion altered the transcription of different 20E-induced genes in opposite ways. For one subset of 20E-activated genes whose expression decreased upon depletion, SRP/dGATAb regulated active regulatory sites marked by H3K27Ac and enriched with SRP/dGATAb motifs. In these loci, SRP/dGATAb depletion reduced EcR binding at co-bound sites, demonstrating a priming role for this GATA family protein. In contrast, for a second subset of 20E-activated but SRP/dGATAb-suppressed genes (i.e., genes whose expression increased upon SRP/dGATAb depletion), SRP/dGATAb and EcR co-bound sites exhibited undisturbed EcR binding. Notably, the overall level of H3K27 acetylation at these loci increased upon SRP/dGATAb depletion.ConclusionsOur data indicate that SRP/dGATAb positively regulates 20E-inducible transcription in S2 Schneider cells for some genes, functioning as a priming factor that facilitates EcR recruitment and chromatin acetylation. Background

Steroid hormones are small, lipophilic molecules that freely circulate in the body, readily enter cells, and modulate transcription. Selective gene induction by steroids at defined developmental stages and in specific tissues is achieved through the targeted activation of distinct sets of enhancers. The mechanisms that confer enhancer susceptibility to steroid action remain largely unexplored. One proposed component is the selective binding of nuclear receptors to specific regulatory regions, known as hormone response elements [1, 2]. In this context, DNA-binding proteins that facilitate the recruitment of nuclear receptors to hormone response elements are of particular interest.

The best-characterized mechanism of targeted nuclear receptor binding involves cooperation between the human estrogen receptor (ER) and pioneer transcription factors that influence chromatin accessibility and establish ER-competent sites [3]. Among these, GATA family transcription factors have been shown to prime regulatory regions for ER recruitment. For example, GATA3 significantly affects estrogen signaling in breast cancer cells by remodeling chromatin in collaboration with the SWI/SNF complex [4, 5] while GATA4 influence ER binding in mesodermal and endodermal derivatives [6, 7].

Drosophila melanogaster provides an excellent model for studying the diverse, tissue- and stage-specific responses to steroid hormones. Drosophila development is largely regulated by a single steroid hormone, ecdysone (specifically, its active form, 20-hydroxyecdysone; 20E) [8, 9]. Elevated 20E levels activate distinct gene sets depending on tissue type and developmental stage. These tissue- and stage-specific responses are critical for developmental progression. During metamorphosis, for example, high 20E titers trigger divergent programs, including (i) fat body remodeling (organ dissociation into individual cells), (ii) destruction of the midgut and salivary glands, and (iii) differentiation of imaginal discs [10–13]. All of these processes are 20E-dependent, yet involve different gene networks in different tissues.

It is widely assumed that the selective effects of 20E on gene expression operate through mechanisms analogous to estrogen signaling, in which the ecdysone receptor (EcR) binds distinct regulatory elements across tissues and developmental stages, primed by partner DNA-binding proteins [14]. However, genome-wide studies demonstrating partner-mediated targeting of EcR to specific regulatory regions remain scarce.

A previous study examined 20E-dependent enhancers in embryonic S2 Schneider cells and ovarian somatic cells (OSC) [15]. This work identified distinct regulatory regions induced by 20E in each cell type and, based on motif analysis, proposed SRP/dGATAb and Traffic Jam as putative EcR partners in S2 Schneider and OSC cells, respectively. Mutation experiments confirmed that 20E activation of these regulatory sites depended on the corresponding motifs. However, direct evidence for SRP/dGATAb or Traffic Jam recruitment to 20E-inducible regulatory regions and the impact of their depletion on the 20E response has been lacking.

Here, we used embryonic S2 Schneider cells—the simplest system for studying 20E responses—to investigate how SRP/dGATAb contributes to 20E-regulated transcription. SRP/dGATAb is one of five Drosophila GATA transcription factors, with tissue-specific functions in hematopoiesis, gut, and fat body development [16]. It is expressed as two isoforms containing one or two ZnF domains, referred to as SrpC and SrpNC, respectively. SrpC and SrpNC are functionally redundant during gut formation in embryogenesis [17]. Yet only SrpC, but not SrpNC, is required for the development of mature fat body cells in the embryo [17]. Targeted knockout of SrpNC is not lethal, but it reveals the specific involvement of this isoform in eggshell formation and regulation of plasmatocyte fate in the absence of infection [17]. SRP/dGATAb has been deeply implicated in hematopoiesis, controlling development of two major hemocyte lineages: plasmatocytes and crystal cells [18]. Drosophila plasmatocytes function as macrophages in the hemolymph, while crystal cells mediate melanization of pathogenic material. SRP/dGATAb regulates expression of Lz and Gcm, transcription factors essential for maintaining crystal cell and plasmatocyte lineages, respectively [19]. Notably, EcR influences plasmatocyte fate during metamorphosis by inducing a migratory phase, suggesting potential interaction between SRP/dGATAb and EcR in hemocyte regulation [20].

Mechanistically, co-regulation by SRP/dGATAb and EcR has so far only been described for a single model locus, fbp1 [21]. SRP/dGATAb binding to 20E-responsive fbp1 elements mediates fat body-specific transcription and counteracts repression by AEF1 [21].

Because little is known about the genome-wide role of SRP/dGATAb in 20E responses, we hypothesize that profiling SRP/dGATAb dynamics at regulatory regions after 20E treatment would yield valuable insights. We also examine SRP/dGATAb as a promising protein candidate that facilitates EcR binding to hormone-response elements and contributes to their activation.

Methods

Antibodies

Rabbit polyclonal antibodies against full-length EcR-A isoform NM_165461 were obtained in our lab and described previously (referred as EcR-full antibodies) [22].

Rat polyclonal antibodies against SRP/dGATAb were generated in-house and are described here for the first time. The antigen corresponded to aa 946–1264 of the SRP/dGATAb isoform NP_732100.2, a region shared across isoforms (Fig. S1A). Antibodies were produced according to the procedures outlined in the NIH (USA) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The protocol used was approved by the Committee on Bioethics of the Institute of Gene Biology of the Russian Academy of Sciences. All procedures were performed under conditions designed to minimize suffering.

The specificity of the SRP/dGATAb antibodies was assessed by Western blot analysis of nuclear extracts from Schneider S2 cells under SRP/dGATAb-depleted (RNAi) and control conditions (Fig. S1B). Both antibody-detected bands were effectively reduced by knockdown. We attribute these bands to the long NC and C isoforms of SRP/dGATAb (Fig. S1B). We presume that the upper band represents a previously undescribed post-translationally modified form of the long NC and C isoforms. Analysis of nuclear extracts from tissues with the SRP/dGATAb antibodies detected the long isoforms in fat body, whereas the modified form was observed in ovary (Fig. S1C). ChIP–seq analysis of SRP/dGATAb binding in Schneider S2 cells using these antibodies identified 2,429 peaks; SRP/dGATAb depletion by RNAi decreased binding at 2,398 peaks (Fig. S1D). As expected for a GATA-family protein, GATA motifs were enriched in SRP/dGATAb-bound peaks. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis placed SRP/dGATAb peaks near genes involved in developmental progression (Fig. S1E).

Antibodies against histone H3K27Ac (39133) were purchased from Active Motif. Anti-tubulin (E7) was obtained from the Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank (DSHB). Rabbit antibodies against Pnr (aa 365–506 of the Pnr A isoform) were a generous gift from Yulii Shidlovskii (Institute of Gene Biology RAS). Rabbit polyclonal antibodies against CP190 were obtained and described previously by our group [23].

Treatment of Drosophila S2 cells

Drosophila Schneider cell line 2 (S2) cells were maintained at 25 °C in ecdysone-free Schneider’s insect medium (Sigma) containing 10% FBS (HyClone). The treatment of S2 cells with 20-hydroxyecdysone (20E) (H5142, Sigma-Aldrich) was performed at a final concentration of 0.3 µM, as was described previously [24]. Our approach was aimed at identifying only direct targets of 20E by limiting the time of 20E treatment to 1 h. Prior to 20E treatment, RNA interference was performed—S2 Schneider cells were treated with SRP dsRNA (corresponding to 1020–1525 bp of mRNA-SRP-H NM_001382083) or sham SKi dsRNA (corresponding to a fragment of pBluescriptKS(−) plasmid) for 5 days (30 µg of dsRNA were taken per 2 million of cells) (Fig. S1A). We observed that control Schneider S2 cells express previously described fat body marker genes—Tig (log2FC SRPi vs. SKi = − 1.012, p = 0.0000065), Glt (log2FC SRPi vs. SKi = − 0.59, p = 0.00094)—as well as plasmatocyte markers—vkg (log2FC SRPi vs. SKi = − 0.6529, p = 0.00035), crq (log2FC SRPi vs. SKi = − 0.7041, p = 0.000077), and pxn (log2FC SRPi vs. SKi = − 0.8612, p = 0.000046). The expression of these marker genes, and their depletion upon SRP/dGATAb RNAi, indicates that Schneider S2 cells are a suitable system for investigating the role of SRP/dGATAb in the development of the fat body and plasmatocytes.

Immunoprecipitation and RNA interference-mediated knockdown of SRP/dGATAb in Schneider S2 cells were performed as described previously [25, 26]. IPs elution was performed by 2% SDS-containing loading buffer supplemented with 100mM DTT. Knockdown efficiency was evaluated by Western blot (Fig. S1B). RNAi targeting SRP/dGATAb effectively depleted SRP/dGATAb from nuclear extracts without affecting the protein level of GATA family transcription factor Pnr (Fig. S1B). Moreover, according to RNA-Seq data, the transcriptional level of other members of the GATA family also did not show a decrease upon SRPi-GATAd (log2FC SRPi vs. SKi =-0.1391, p = 0.4029) and pnr (log2FC SRPi vs. SKi = 0.7049, p = 0.0000988), with grn and GATAe not transcribing at a substantial level in S2 Schneider cells. Thus, demonstrating the specific targeting of SRP/dGATAb with SRPi dsRNA. Chemoluminescent signal was detected using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

RNA-Seq, ChIP-Seq and FAIRE-Seq data acquiring and analysis

All the data for RNA-Seq, ChIP-Seq and FAIRE-Seq were obtained in two independent biological replicates for each condition. Raw and processed files were deposited into the Gene Expression Omnibus - GSE304853. All the regions analyzed in the manuscript are provided as .bed files in the Supplementary tables.

Total RNA was extracted with the TRI reagent (MRC). PolyA comprising RNA fraction was isolated and prepared for sequencing with the NEBNext Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit. New generation sequencing was performed by BGI Genomics with the DNBSEQ-G400 sequencer. Adapter sequences were removed from the raw files by Cutadapt [27]. Mapping to Drosophila dm6 genome was performed by HISAT2; only reads with MAPQ score ≥ 10 were taken for further analysis [28]. Differential analysis of RNA-Seqs was employed by limma [29]. Lists of transcripts differentially expressed in 20E_SKi-DMSO_SKi and 20E_SRPi-DMSO_SRPi samples are provided in a Supplementary. 20E-dependent target genes (20E_SKi-DMSO_SKi) and 20E-dependent SRP protected target genes (20E_SRPi-DMSO_SRPi) in SKi and SRPi samples were selected as genes whose transcription changed with |log2 fold change| ≥1.0| adj. p value ≤ 0.05 (Upregulated = 289, Downregulated = 92 for SKi; Upregulated = 338, Downregulated = 289 for SRPi after removing significant 20E-dependent genes in SKi) after 20E treatment.

SRP-regulated, 20E-dependent primary targets under control conditions were selected as follows. Among the 20E-activated genes in Ski (N = 289), we classified SRP-suppressed genes (upregulated in 20E_SRPi-20E_SKi, N = 63) and SRP-activated genes (downregulated in 20E_SRPi-20E_SKi, N = 59). From these we selected “20E-activated, SRP suppressed primary targets” (N = 40; the “ecdysone cascade group”) and “20E-activated, SRP-activated primary targets” (N = 34; the “fat body group”), defined as genes with both EcR and SRP/dGATAb peaks within ± 10 kb of their loci.

SRP-susceptible, 20E-dependent primary targets that became 20E-dependent only under SRPi conditions were selected as follows: SRP suppressed (upregulated in 20E_SRPi-20E_SKi, N = 42) and SRP-activated (downregulated in 20E_SRPi-20E_SKi, N = 25) genes were selected from SRP dependent genes downregulated after 20E treatment in SRPi (N = 289) (only susceptible targets were chosen, genes downregulated upon 20E treatment in SKi were excluded). From those we selected ‘SRP suppressed primary targets’ (N = 27) and SRP activated primary targets’ (N = 30)—genes that had both EcR and SRP/dGATAb peaks in their loci with the flanking regions ± 10 kb.

To estimate genome-wide SRP/dGATAb and EcR binding, we performed ChIP-Seqs on SRPi and SKi samples in DMSO and 20E treatment conditions with antibodies against SRP/dGATAb and EcR-full. SRP/dGATAb ChIP-Seq peaks were detected with MACS2 callpeak tool with the shifting model option separately for SRPi and SKi samples in DMSO and 20E conditions. Then SRP peaks were selected as differentially bound peaks (|log2FC|≥0, FDR < 0.05) between DMSO_SRPi-DMSO_SKi (N = 2441) and 20E_SRPi-20ESKi (N = 2366) contrasts using DiffBind package DESeq2 method. Then only unique SRP peaks from both contrasts were taken (N = 2931). EcR ChIP-Seq peaks were detected by MACS2 callpeak separately for DMSO (SRPi and SKi) and 20E (SRPi and SKi) conditions. Only unique EcR peaks were selected (N = 4259).

To define SRP and EcR dependent regulatory regions we performed DiffBind analysis with selected SRP (N = 2931) and EcR (N = 4259) genome sites. We considered regulatory sites differentially bound if they changed binding level with |log2 fold change| ≥ 0.5, FDR < 0.05 in 20E_SRPi relatively to 20E_SKi samples. We detected 2326 and 676 differentially bound (DB) SRP and EcR sites, respectively. To associate DB sites with target genes’ transcription levels in 20E activated, SRP activated (N = 34) and 20E activated, SRP suppressed (N = 40) primary targets we intersected the intervals of DB SRP (N = 2326) and DB EcR (N = 676) ChIP-Seq peaks with the primary targets’ gene loci +−10 kb. For 20E activated, SRP activated genes (N = 34) we identified 32 SRP separate sites, 30 common for EcR and SRP sites, 32 EcR separate sites. For 20E activated, SRP suppressed genes (N = 40) we established 58 SRP separate sites, 95 common for EcR and SRP sites, 90 EcR separate sites.

The chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and FAIRE-seq were performed and analysed exactly as previously described [25, 30, 31]. FAIRE-Seq was performed using previously described protocol [32]. ChIP-Seq and FAIRE-Seq libraries were obtained using the NEBNext UltraTM II DNA library preparation kit (New England Biolabs). Next generation sequencing was performed by BGI Gnomics with the DNBSEQ-G400 sequencer. The paired-end reads in .fq format were trimmed by Cutadapt, mapped to the Drosophila genome assembly dm6 using HISAT2 [28] and filtered (with minimum MAPQ quality score = 10, SRP, in EcR and SRP samples duplicates were removed with MarkDuplicates). Deeptool2 package was used for the further analysis of the obtained data [33]. BigWig files were generated using bamCoverage 3.0.2 with scores representing number of reads normalized by the size of the library (the protein binding levels were normalized to the genome content – calculated as RPGC: number of reads per bin/(total number of mapped reads * fragment length/effective genome size) [33]. The final bigwig files (representing the protein binding profiles) were obtained using bigwigCompare tool by computing a ratio between the ChIP signal and the Input (the Input file was obtained previously and provided as a part of GSE156847). For the FAIRE-Seq samples the Input was subtracted from the signal. For the peak calling from the ChIP-Seq data we used MACS2 [34, 35]. All peaks were called using two independent biological replicates and appropriate Input files. We performed the correlation analysis at common EcR and SRP regulatory sites located in 20E activated, SRP activated (N = 30) and 20E activated, SRP suppressed (N = 95) target loci to show reproducibility between biological ChIP-seq and FAIRE-Seq replicates (using multibigwigSummary and plotCorrelation of deeptool2 package) [33]. The results of the correlation analysis are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2 as heatmaps with indicated Pearson correlation coefficients.

To quantitively analyse the differences in protein binding in ChIP-Seqs upon the SRP RNAi depletion in 20E treatment condition (20E_SRPi-20E_SKi), we utilized DiffBind from Bioconductor package with the DESeq2 algorithm [36]. We considered peaks statistically significant if they met the conditions |log2FC| ≥ 0.5; FDR ≤ 0.05.

Pile-up profiles for the average analysis of ChIP-Seqs were calculated as a median level of protein binding. Standard error is presented at the averaged profiles as a lighter area surrounding a graph. To compare the average ChIP-Seq signal at a given set of sites, we performed Wilcoxon rank-sum test using the normalized coverage scores for two independent biological replicates of each ChIP-Seq and FAIRE-Seq sample from multiBigwigSummary. Normalized coverage scores were calculated within 1000 bp around the summit peaks for SRP separate sites and 1000 bp around the narrow peaks for EcR separate and EcR/SRP common regulatory regions when estimating EcR, SRP binding and FAIRE-Seq level (and within 5000 b.p. for H3K27Ac binding level). The test results were presented on average profiles, where “*” means FDR ≤ 0.05, and “**” means FDR ≤ 0.01. In addition, we provided the calculated fold change [which was calculated as the ratio between the average ChIP-Seq signals (median)].

We did not use any figures or text from the previously published manuscripts – only data deposited in free access databases. The Galaxy-P platform was used for analysis of ChIP-Seq data [37].

All processed files (both .bigwig and .bed) were obtained with coordinates of Drosophila dm6 genome.

Results

SRP/dGATAb knockdown affects the 20E-induced response in S2 Schneider cells both positively and negatively

To investigate the role of SRP/dGATAb in the ecdysone response, we treated Drosophila S2 Schneider cells with 20E (0.3 µM) following SRP knockdown using RNA-interference (SRPi) (Fig. 1A). The same treatment was carried out with sham dsRNA (SKi). As expected, we found approximately three times more genes upregulated by 20E than genes downregulated (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S1, cut-off: |log2FC|≥1, adj.p.value ≤ 0.05; where FC is the fold change). Among the 20E-activated targets, several previously described genes were detected including E23, Hr4, Fatp3/cg3394, br, Eip74ef, Eip75B, Eip78C and EcR) (Supplementary Table S2) [24, 38]. To determine how SRP/dGATAb modulates the ecdysone response, we compared the transcriptional profiles of 20E-activated (N = 289) and 20E-downregulated (N = 92) targets between SRP/dGATAb-depleted (SRPi) and sham-treated (SKi) Schneider S2 cells following 20E induction (Fig. 1C, D). The 20E-activated targets were more strongly affected by SRP/dGATAb depletion: 20% (N = 59) showed decreased expression and 22% (N = 63) showed increased expression (Supplementary Table S3, cut-off: |log2FC|≥1, adj.p.value ≤ 0.05). In contrast, among 20E-downregulated targets, only 10% (N = 9) and 15% (N = 14) showed decreased or increased expression, respectively, following SRP/dGATAb knockdown (Supplementary Table S4, same cut-off). Because the 20E-activated targets were both more numerous and more strongly affected by the knockdown, we examined them in greater detail.

Our priority was to identify mechanisms enabling cooperative transcriptional regulation by SRP/dGATAb and EcR, presumably through direct binding of these proteins to regulatory sites at target loci. Therefore, among the 20E-activated genes affected by SRP/dGATAb depletion, we focused on “primary targets” defined as genes with loci (± 10 kb flanking regions) containing both SRP/dGATAb and EcR peaks, presumably overlapping regulatory regions.

To map genome-wide binding of EcR and SRP/dGATAb, we performed ChIP-Seq in S2 cells using antibodies against EcR (as previously described [22]) and against SRP/dGATAb (developed for this study) (Fig. S1). The SRP/dGATAb antibodies were raised against an antigen common to all isoforms; details are provided in Methods. In brief, on Western blots of Schneider S2 cell extracts, the antibodies recognized two major bands—corresponding to the long SRP/dGATAb isoform and a putatively post-translationally modified form—both of which were depleted by SRPi. Both species likely contribute to the ChIP–seq signal obtained with the SRP/dGATAb antibodies.

By intersecting SRP/dGATAb-dependent 20E-activated loci with EcR and SRP/dGATAb peaks identified under control (SKi) conditions, both with and without 20E treatment, we identified 34 SRP/dGATAb-activated (Supplementary Table S5) and 40 SRP/dGATAb-suppressed (Supplementary Table S6) primary targets, representing over half of each SRP/dGATAb-dependent group (Fig. 1E). Notably, all ”ecdysone cascade” genes previously reported as upregulated by 20E in various cell types were present in the SRP/dGATAb-suppressed cohort, indicating that SRP/dGATAb negatively affects their transcription.

We next examined the tissue specificity of these primary targets using FlyAtlas2 transcriptome data for L3 larvae, a developmental stage characterized by active ecdysone signaling (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Table S7) [39]. Both primary target groups showed strong tissue specificity, with most genes expressed predominantly in one (occasionally two) tissue (Fig. S3). The 34 SRP/dGATAb-activated primary targets were enriched for fat body expression. Given that SRP/dGATAb itself is specifically transcribed in the fat body, these data suggest that SRP/dGATAb acts as a tissue-specific EcR cofactor promoting ecdysone responses in this tissue. Accordingly, we designated these 34 genes as the “fat body group”. In contrast, the 40 SRP/dGATAb-suppressed primary targets were enriched for expression in the larval CNS and salivary glands, but rarely in the fat body. Since salivary glands undergo a robust ecdysone response at late L3 and several known cascade genes were included in this group, we designated it the “ecdysone cascade group”. Hereafter, we refer to these groups by these names.

SRP/dGATAb knockdown increases the susceptibility of genes in S2 Schneider cells to 20E treatment

The analysis above focused on genes responsive to 20E under normal (SKi) conditions. To fully assess SRP/dGATAb’s role in ecdysone signaling, we also examined genes that became responsive to 20E only after SRP/dGATAb depletion (i.e., genes unresponsive to 20E in SKi cells) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S8).

Surprisingly, SRP/dGATAb knockdown increased the susceptibility of numerous genes to 20E treatment. Specifically, we identified an additional 338 20E-activated and 289 20E-downregulated targets under SRP/dGATAb-depleted conditions (Supplementary Table S9) (cut-off: |log₂FC| ≥ 1, adj.p ≤ 0.05; genes already responsive under SKi conditions were excluded) (Fig. 2A). We then assessed SRP/dGATAb-dependent effects on this SRPi-susceptible cohort by comparing gene expression in SRPi versus SKi cells following 20E treatment (Figs. 2B, C). Genes downregulated by 20E under SRPi conditions were more affected by SRP/dGATAb depletion than activated genes: 28% (N = 81) showed increased expression and 27% (N = 79) showed further decreased expression in SRPi compared with SKi cells (Supplementary Table S10). Among 20E-activated genes, only 19% were affected.

Given the stronger effects on genes downregulated by 20E in SRPi conditions, we focused on this group for primary target analysis (Fig. 2D). By intersecting SRP/dGATAb-dependent 20E-downregulated under SRPi loci with EcR and SRP/dGATAb peaks identified under control (SKi) conditions, both with and without 20E treatment, we identified 27 of 81 SRP/dGATAb-suppressed (Supplementary Table S11) and 30 of 79 SRP/dGATAb-activated (Supplementary Table S12) primary targets.

Tissue-specificity analysis using FlyAtlas2 revealed that, unlike genes upregulated by 20E under normal conditions, these primary targets were consistently enriched for fat body expression (Fig. 2E and Fig. S4, Supplementary Table S13).

Overall, these results indicate that SRP/dGATAb may protect the expression of many fat body genes from 20E action. Because fewer than half of the SRP/dGATAb-dependent, 20E-downregulated genes were direct targets, the increased sensitivity of some genes to 20E after SRP/dGATAb depletion is likely an indirect effect, possibly mediated by changes in other transcriptional regulators. While this observation merits further investigation, it is beyond the scope of our main goal—elucidating the coordinated action of SRP/dGATAb and EcR at regulatory regions. Therefore, subsequent analyses focus on SRP/dGATAb–EcR-dependent primary targets that are activated by 20E under normal conditions.

SRP/dGATAb knockdown decreases EcR binding at the “fat body” group, but not at the “ecdysone cascade” group of 20E-activated genes

To investigate EcR–SRP/dGATAb cooperativity at regulatory regions of 20E-activated loci, we examined the landscape after 1 h of 20E treatment and in control (1 h DMSO-treated) cells, both before and after SRP/dGATAb depletion (Fig. 3). EcR- and SRP/dGATAb-bound peaks were defined as regions of interest in our ChIP-seq analysis, and their association with target genes was based on proximity. For this analysis, we collected all EcR- and SRP/dGATAb-bound peaks within gene bodies or within ± 10 kb of genes in the “fat body” and “ecdysone cascade” groups. We observed no change in the total number of EcR or SRP/dGATAb peaks in these loci after 1 h of 20E treatment under control SKi conditions (the “fat body” group contained 62 EcR peaks and 62 SRP/dGATAb peaks; the “ecdysone cascade” group contained 185 EcR peaks and 153 SRP/dGATAb peaks). For deeper analysis, peaks within the gene loci were partitioned into three categories: EcR-separate, SRP/dGATAb-separate, and co-bound peaks (details in Methods). We first assessed the effect of SRP/dGATAb depletion on the binding levels of both factors across these peak categories. Differential binding was quantified using DiffBind with the DESeq2 method (|log2FC| ≥ 0.5; FDR ≤ 0.05; Supplementary Table S14) [36]. Changes upon SRP/dGATAb depletion are shown as MD plots, with significantly decreased peaks in red and increased peaks in green (Fig. 3).

Previous work suggested that EcR and SRP/dGATAb coordinately regulate 20E-dependent transcription by acting at co-occupied regulatory regions [15]. Our ChIP-Seq data revealed a complex regulatory landscape within EcR/SRP co-dependent loci in Schneider S2 cells. Only a subset of regulatory regions was co-bound: 32% (30/94) of sites in the “fat body” group and 39% (95/243) of sites in the “ecdysone cascade” group were co-bound by EcR and SRP/dGATAb (Fig. 3; Supplementary Tables S15, 16). The remaining sites contained either EcR or SRP/dGATAb alone. Notably, SRP/dGATAb-only sites were more frequent in “fat body” loci than in “ecdysone cascade” loci relative to the total number of sites (34% vs. 24%, respectively; Supplementary Tables S17–20).

SRP/dGATAb depletion markedly reduced SRP/dGATAb occupancy at the loci of both the “fat body” and “ecdysone cascade” groups, as well as genome-wide (approximately three quarters of SRP/dGATAb sites showed a significant decrease upon SRPi in both gene groups) (Fig. 3). EcR binding was differentially affected by SRPi: in “ecdysone cascade” loci, only 6% (6/95) of co-bound sites showed reduced EcR binding after SRP/dGATAb depletion, whereas in “fat body” loci, EcR binding was reduced at 33% (10/30) of co-bound sites (values shown for the 20E-treated condition). EcR-only sites were unaffected in both groups in terms of EcR occupancy.

At EcR/SRP-dependent 20E-activated “fat body” loci, both separate and co-bound regulatory sites were identified. To determine which sites were active and functionally relevant, we examined changes in chromatin accessibility and H3K27 acetylation following SRP/dGATAb depletion, analyzing the SRP/EcR separate and co-occupied regulatory sites in the 1 h 20E-treated condition (Fig. 4A).

H3K27Ac modification is a mark of the actively working enhancers [40, 41]. Its absence may indicate either a ‘poised’ state (accessible but inactive) or a ‘silent’ state (low accessibility and inactive) of regulatory region. Based on these criteria, only separate SRP/dGATAb and co-bound regulatory sites at “fat body” loci that are enriched in H3K27Ac can be considered truly ‘active’ at high 20E levels and thus likely to positively regulate target gene activity (Fig. 4A).

SRP/dGATAb depletion by RNA interference strongly affected both chromatin accessibility and H3K27Ac levels at SRP-separate and EcR/SRP co-bound sites. Importantly, EcR binding at co-bound sites was also reduced, indicating that SRP/dGATAb plays a leading role in establishing these active regulatory regions, with EcR recruitment being dependent on SRP/dGATAb (Fig. 4A). Quantification of normalized reads around site centers (± 1000 bp for EcR, SRP and FAIRE, ± 5000 bp for H3K27Ac) confirmed significant reductions in chromatin accessibility, H3K27Ac, and occupancy of both EcR and SRP/dGATAb (Wilcoxon rank-sum test on biological replicates), presented as fold-change values beneath each heatmap in Fig. 4A.

A locus-specific example, Reg-2, a member of the “fat body” group, corroborated these global observations (Fig. 4B). At this locus, SRP/dGATAb depletion reduced EcR binding, H3K27Ac levels, and chromatin accessibility specifically at the co-bound site, whereas a separate EcR-only sites remained unaffected. Of note, the Reg-2 gene, which is rhythmically expressed, was recently shown to be involved in adult fat body formation, a process controlled by both SRP/dGATAb and ecdysone signaling [42].

These observations are summarized in a model (Fig. 4C) describing the positive role of SRP/dGATAb in 20E-inducible transcription of fat body-enriched genes. We propose that SRP/dGATAb establishes and maintains active regulatory regions that recruit EcR, thereby enabling enhancer activity and target gene induction in response to 20E.

SRP/dGATAb negatively regulates co-bound regulatory sites in SRP/dGATAb-suppressed, 20E-activated genes (“ecdysone cascade” group)

As shown above, the regulatory landscape of “ecdysone cascade” loci, whose 20E-induced transcription increases upon SRP/dGATAb depletion, differs from that of “fat body” targets (Fig. 3). “Ecdysone cascade” loci contained fewer SRP/dGATAb-separate sites, and EcR binding at EcR/SRP co-bound sites remained unaffected by SRP/dGATAb depletion.

Analysis of chromatin accessibility and acetylation in the 1 h 20E-treated condition further clarified properties of regulatory sites in the “ecdysone cascade” group (Fig. 5A). Separate SRP/dGATAb sites were generally in a “poised” state, characterized by high chromatin accessibility but low H3K27 acetylation (H3K27Ac). SRP/dGATAb depletion reduced accessibility at these sites. Given the overall negative effect of SRP/dGATAb on 20E-inducible transcription in the “ecdysone cascade” group, we infer that SRP/dGATAb-only sites with low H3K27Ac may function as silencer-like elements that restrain locus activity. Their destabilization upon SRP/dGATAb depletion may contribute to the repression relief.

Similarly, separate EcR-only sites were inactive at high 20E levels, showing high accessibility but low H3K27Ac. SRP/dGATAb depletion did not affect chromatin accessibility at EcR-only sites, confirming the specific role of SRP/dGATAb at sites where it is bound.

The most active regulatory sites in “ecdysone cascade” loci were the EcR/SRP co-bound sites. SRP/dGATAb RNAi efficiently depleted SRP/dGATAb protein at these sites but did not alter EcR binding. In contrast, H3K27Ac levels increased slightly at co-bound sites following SRP/dGATAb depletion (Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis confirmed that acetylation was significantly higher in depleted cells than in controls (results shown beneath each heatmap). This effect was particularly evident at individual loci in the “ecdysone cascade” group, such as hr4, whose transcription increased under high 20E in SRP/dGATAb-depleted cells (Fig. 5B). Hr4 is a well-known early-late component of the ecdysone cascade that controls larval feeding behavior and has been used as a model ecdysone-responsive gene in transcriptional studies [24, 25, 43]. The hr4 locus contained multiple EcR/SRP co-bound sites; SRP/dGATAb depletion removed SRP/dGATAb binding without affecting EcR occupancy, while acetylation increased both at the co-bound site and at a nearby EcR-separate site (marked with green and blue lines, respectively, in Fig. 5B). Notably, H3K27Ac rose slightly at the active regulatory regions but increased more markedly in surrounding chromatin. We interpret this as SRP/dGATAb restraining the spread of H3K27Ac across “ecdysone cascade” loci. To assess whether H3K27Ac spreading upon SRPi is a common feature in this group, we compared the number of normalized H3K27Ac reads within gene loci ± 10 kb in SRPi versus SKi (1 h 20E-treated conditions). A significant increase in H3K27Ac upon SRPi was observed for 16 of 40 “ecdysone cascade” loci with log2FC ≥ 1 and p-value ≤ 0.05.

Analysis of EcR/SRP co-bound sites in the “fat body” and “ecdysone cascade” groups indicated that co-bound sites in both groups are active (enriched for H3K27Ac) and likely promote gene expression. However, SRP/dGATAb depletion reduced EcR binding and H3K27Ac in the “fat body” group, whereas EcR binding was unchanged and acetylation increased in the “ecdysone cascade” group. These contrasting effects on co-bound sites state likely underlie the opposite transcriptional outcomes following SRP/dGATAb depletion. To explore potential mechanisms, we analyzed DNA-binding motif enrichment at co-bound sites. Co-bound sites in the “fat body” group were more frequently enriched for SRP/dGATAb motifs than those in the “ecdysone cascade” group (70% vs. 46%; FDR = 0.04). Conversely, co-bound sites in the “ecdysone cascade” group were more frequently enriched for EcR motifs than those in the “fat body” group (64% vs. 56.7%; FDR = 0.52). These findings suggest distinct recruitment hierarchies at co-bound sites: in the “fat body” group, SRP/dGATAb may bind first and recruit EcR, likely via protein-protein interactions, whereas in the “ecdysone-cascade” group, EcR may directly bind DNA and recruit SRP/dGATAb (Fig. 5C).

To test for a physical interaction between EcR and SRP/dGATAb, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) with antibodies against EcR and SRP/dGATAb using nuclear extracts from S2 cells treated with 20E for 1 h and 5 h, and from untreated controls (Fig. S5). EcR and SRP/dGATAb reciprocally co-precipitated, indicating a protein-protein interaction independent of 20E levels. Notably, both isoforms detected by SRP/dGATAb antibodies were precipitated by EcR antibodies, including a putatively modified isoform migrating ~ 50 kDa above the known 140 kDa long isoform.

Given the proposed indirect recruitment of EcR at co-bound sites in the “fat body” group and of SRP/dGATAb in the “ecdysone cascade” group, we tested whether EcR knockdown affects SRP/dGATAb occupancy at representative sites. We depleted EcR by RNAi and assessed EcR and SRP/dGATAb binding by ChIP under 1 h 20E-treated conditions (Fig. S6). Knockdown efficiency was high by Western blot (Fig. S1). Unexpectedly, EcR depletion reduced SRP/dGATAb binding not only at co-bound sites in the “ecdysone cascade” group, but also at co-bound sites in the “fat body” group. These results support indirect recruitment of SRP/dGATAb at “ecdysone cascade” loci and indicate that SRP/dGATAb recruitment at co-bound sites in the “fat body” group is at least partly EcR dependent, consistent with cooperative binding.

Discussion

SRP/dGATAb promotes transcriptional response to 20E for a subset of genes (fat body group)

A previous comprehensive analysis of 20E-responsive enhancers in S2 Schneider cells using STARR-Seq revealed their enrichment for GATA motifs [15]. Mutational analysis of several enhancers showed that their inducibility by 20E was strongly reduced in SRP/dGATAb-containing S2 cells but not in the OSC ovarian somatic cell line, suggesting that some 20E-responsive enhancers depend on a GATA family protein present in S2 Schneider cells.

In this study, we examined how depletion of SRP/dGATAb affects the genome-wide transcriptional response of S2 Schneider cells to 20E treatment. RNAi-mediated SRP/dGATAb depletion influenced 20E-inducible gene expression both positively and negatively. Genes normally expressed in the larval fat body (the SRP/dGATAb-activated 20E-activated group) were positively regulated by SRP/dGATAb. Based on H3K27 acetylation patterns, we propose that 20E-dependent transcription of these genes is promoted by both separate SRP/dGATAb-bound sites and EcR/SRP co-bound sites. SRP/dGATAb depletion significantly reduced H3K27 acetylation at both types of sites, which we attribute to decreased enhancer activity. Importantly, depletion also reduced EcR binding at co-bound sites. Because these sites were enriched for SRP/dGATAb motifs, we infer that SRP/dGATAb plays a leading role in establishing these regulatory regions and possibly in recruiting EcR, potentially via protein-protein interaction.

Dependence of EcR binding on pioneering partners has been suspected but not firmly established. For example, EcR depletion by RNAi in larval wing imaginal discs did not reduce chromatin accessibility, indicating that EcR alone may be insufficient to control it [44]. By contrast, our recent work showed that 20E depletion in larval salivary glands decreased chromatin accessibility at a subset of EcR-bound sites corresponding to tissue-active enhancers, while many EcR-bound poised enhancers remained accessible [22]. The decrease in accessibility upon 20E depletion suggests that EcR can contribute to pioneer-like activity, although additional factors are likely involved. Our current data identify SRP/dGATAb as one such factor that governs chromatin accessibility at a subset of sites and dictates EcR occupancy. Moreover, the presence of EcR and SRP/dGATAb in a common multiprotein complex, together with reduced SRP/dGATAb binding upon EcR RNAi at representative sites, indicates cooperative binding.

SRP/dGATAb negatively regulates the ecdysone cascade gene response to 20E

Unexpectedly, SRP/dGATAb depletion enhanced the response of ecdysone cascade genes to 20E in Schneider S2 cells. The SRP/dGATAb-suppressed 20E-activated group included most genes previously reported as short-term 20E targets in wandering larval salivary glands (Eip75b, Eip74EF, Eip78C, EcR, br, Hr39, Hr4) [24]. These genes had approximately threefold more SRP/dGATAb and EcR peaks at co-bound or separate sites than the “fat body” group, suggesting a more complex regulatory architecture. In contrast to “fat body” genes loci, SRP/dGATAb-only sites in the “ecdysone cascade” group lacked H3K27Ac and thus represented inactive (“poised”) elements. Given the negative effect of SRP/dGATAb on transcription in this group, we propose that SRP/dGATAb-only sites, which are disrupted by SRPi, correspond to silencer-like regulatory elements. Active regulatory sites in the “ecdysone cascade” group were EcR/SRP co-bound sites. Notably, SRP/dGATAb depletion slightly increased H3K27Ac at these co-bound sites and, at some loci, promoted propagation of the H3K27Ac signal within the regulatory region. Because co-bound sites were enriched for EcR motifs rather than SRP/dGATAb motifs, we infer that SRP/dGATAb is recruited indirectly via protein–protein interactions.

Indirect recruitment of GATA proteins is well documented. For example, GATA factors interact with Friend of GATA (FOG; represented in Drosophila by Ush), a conserved mechanism that stabilizes GATA–DNA interactions at suboptimal motifs [45, 46]. Interaction with Ush can convert SRP/dGATAb into a transcriptional repressor [47], because Ush forms complexes with CtBP and NuRD corepressors [48, 49]. Since Ush binds SRP/dGATAb through its N-terminal ZnF domain—normally responsible for DNA binding—its recruitment may be more efficient at indirectly bound sites than at directly bound ones. This may explain why SRP/dGATAb exerts a repressive effect at co-bound sites in the “ecdysone cascade” gene group, where it is likely recruited indirectly.

Another possible mechanism for SRP/dGATAb-mediated repression is competition with coactivators for protein-protein binding domains within EcR. Of particular interest is CBP/p300, a major acetyltransferase required for 20E-dependent transcriptional activation [22, 25, 50]. Although a direct interaction between SRP/dGATAb and CBP/p300 has not been demonstrated, mammalian GATA proteins are known to interact with CBP/p300, suggesting that a similar interaction might occur in Drosophila and compete with coactivator recruitment under limiting conditions [51, 52].

GATA proteins can also influence gene expression through chromatin looping. The crystal structure of mammalian GATA3 showed simultaneous DNA binding via its N- and C-terminal ZnF domains, supporting the potential of double ZnF GATA proteins to mediate chromatin interactions [53]. Differential looping abilities of GATA proteins are important for regulating developmental gene expression, where proteins with different ZnF numbers alternate at regulatory sites [54, 55]. Although this function has not yet been demonstrated for Drosophila GATA proteins, SRP/dGATAb might similarly influence enhancer–promoter communication, contributing to its opposing effects on 20E-regulated genes.

How might Drosophila GATA proteins modulate tissue-specific responses to 20E?

Our data indicate that, for certain 20E-inducible genes normally expressed in the larval fat body, SRP/dGATAb establishes active regulatory regions and positively regulates the transcriptional response to 20E. This function is consistent with its proposed pioneering activity in EcR recruitment and enhancer activation [15]. For this fat body gene group, SRP/dGATAb appears to act as an EcR cofactor promoting tissue-specific responses to 20E.

Conversely, SRP/dGATAb suppressed the expression of canonical ecdysone cascade genes. Tissue-specific transcriptional programs can involve both activators and repressors, and SRP/dGATAb-mediated repression may contribute to tissue-specificity by keeping ecdysone cascade genes inactive in the fat body until the end of the larval stage, despite rising 20E levels. These genes are known to activate in the fat body during the early prepupal stage, contributing to fat body remodeling [56, 57]. We speculate that during this developmental transition, 20E-dependent regulatory regions undergo a switch between different GATA factors, a mechanism described in mammals [58, 59]. SRP/dGATAb may be replaced by Pnr, a GATA factor expressed later in the fat body, which could promote activation of ecdysone cascade genes. Such coordinated roles of different Drosophila GATA proteins in hormone-mediated transcription represent an interesting avenue for future research.

Conclusion

20E-responsive genes involved in developmental programs are among the most complex genomic loci in Drosophila, containing multiple regulatory elements, including “active” and “poised” enhancers, insulators, and promoters [60]. Our data provide evidence that SRP/dGATAb contributes to EcR recruitment at a subset of 20E-activated loci, thereby facilitating activation of target genes. At the same time, we identified SRP/dGATAb-suppressed 20E-activated loci, where SRP/dGATAb depletion disrupts some regulatory regions and reduces chromatin accessibility but, unlike in the first group, results in increased gene transcription.

This observation illustrates that the fate of an individual regulatory site does not necessarily determine the overall transcriptional output of a locus. Rather, a gene’s transcriptional potential is governed by the combined activity of all its regulatory elements, which is not simply the sum of their individual contributions [61]. Consequently, conclusions drawn from the properties of single regulatory regions must be applied to whole loci with caution.
