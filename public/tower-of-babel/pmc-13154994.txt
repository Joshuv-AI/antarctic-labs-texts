Structure–function analysis of empasiprubart, a calcium- and pH-dependent clinical phase complement C2 blocking antibody
Authors: Laura Bracke, Heidi Gytz Olesen, Erwin Pannecoucke, Tim Delahaye, Emma K. Persson, Christophe Blanchetot, Hans de Haard, C. Erik Hack, Gregers Rom Andersen, Inge Van de Walle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEmpasiprubart (ARGX-117) is a humanized recycling antibody that prevents binding of C2 to C4b, blocking downstream classical and lectin pathways of complement activation. Empasiprubart binds to the CCP2 domain of complement component C2 in a calcium- and pH-dependent manner, leveraging physiological differences between blood and endosomal environments to facilitate the release and subsequent degradation of bound C2. The molecule incorporates Fc region mutations (H433K and N434F) that enhance its affinity for the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) under acidic endosomal conditions, thereby prolonging its in vivo half-life and supporting its recycling capacity. However, despite the earlier description of the complex structure, the molecular mechanism underlying these dependencies has remained elusive. Here, we further explored the crystal structure of the empasiprubart fragment antigen-binding (Fab) complexed to a C2 fragment, and provide a molecular rationale for its unique properties, while recognizing that not all contributing factors have been fully elucidated. Our observations indicate that the pH-dependent target release is rooted in a subtle intramolecular complementarity-determining region (CDR) destabilization, rather than direct modulation of the binding interface, and highlight the interplay between framework residues and CDRs. Collectively, our results not only lead to a better understanding of the mode of action of empasiprubart but also demonstrate the pivotal role of framework residues in the orchestration of antibody CDR function for non-trivial target binding.

Introduction

The complement system is a part of the innate immune system, and consists of more than 30 different proteins in plasma and on cell surfaces.1 The complement system can be activated via three major pathways: the classical, lectin, and alternative pathways (CP, LP, and AP, respectively). These converge at the level of complement component 3 (C3) to activate a common terminal pathway, which leads to the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) and the release of the anaphylatoxin C5a. During activation of CP and LP, proteolytic events lead to cleavage of complement component 4 (C4) into C4a and C4b, by either C1s (CP) or MASP-2 (LP). The larger C4b fragment, whether covalently bound to the target cell surface or not, subsequently interacts with the zymogen protease C2 to form the proconvertase C4b2 (Figure 1A). In a second step, C2 bound to C4b is cleaved by C1s or MASP-1/MASP-2, which releases the smaller C2a fragment to produce the complex C4b2b, a C3 convertase that cleaves C3 into C3a and C3b. Like C4b, nascent C3b may become covalently attached to the activator surface and can amplify the deposition of additional C3b through the interaction with factors B, D and P to generate the AP convertase C3bBb stabilized by factor P. Deposition of additional C3b on the C3 convertase switches its specificity into a C5 convertase that cleaves C5 (Figure 1A). The complement system plays a critical role in inflammation and in the body’s defense against infection by enhancing the ability of antibodies and phagocytes to clear microbes and damaged cells. However, in pathologic conditions inappropriate regulation of complement activation can contribute to clinical disease, providing a therapeutic rationale for blocking components of the complement system. Antibody-mediated inhibition of complement proteins in humans has previously been shown to be feasible.3 However, due to high concentrations of most complement factors in plasma as well as target-mediated clearance, high doses of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are required to fully block the complement cascade. In some situations, it may also be beneficial to remove the target complement molecule from the circulation, rather than just neutralize a specific functional epitope. To address both issues, a therapeutic mAb candidate can be endowed with recycling properties, through engineering of both the constant and variable regions of the mAb. Introducing His433Lys and Asn434Phe mutations (H433K and N434F) to the heavy chain constant region improves affinity for the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) at acidic pH, and mutagenesis of the variable region promotes accelerated complex dissociation at lower pH. Once such an engineered mAb binds its target in the circulation and is taken up by the endothelial cells to enter the endosome, the antibody-antigen complex is dissociated at the acidic conditions. The released antigen is then targeted for destruction in the lysosome, while the mAb undergoes FcRn-mediated rescue and is recycled back to the circulation, where it can bind another target antigen.4–8

Empasiprubart (ARGX-117) is a monoclonal, humanized, recycling antibody that binds to C2 and thereby blocks downstream complement activation of both CP and LP, while leaving the AP and its antimicrobial activity intact. The antibody binds specifically to the second complement control protein (CCP) domain (also known as Sushi-2) of native C2 in a calcium- and pH-dependent manner, which is essential for its recycling properties, and prevents C2 from binding to C4b.4

Recently, we described the crystal structure of the ARGX-117 fragment antigen-binding (Fab) in complex with an N-terminal fragment of C2 (C2 Nt) and identified a calcium-ion in the center of the interface of the Fab and CCP2 of C2a.9 Here, we further explore the molecular structure of ARGX-117 Fab complexed to CCP2 of C2a to rationalize the antibody’s mode of action and species cross-reactivity, and to better understand the crucial role of calcium, pH, complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) and framework residues in the binding of ARGX-117 with its epitope on CCP2 of C2.

Results

ARGX-117 competes with C4b for C2 binding

We previously reported the structure of ARGX-117 Fab in complex with C2 Nt (Figure 1(B)), demonstrating the molecular mode of action of complex formation.9 To obtain further insights into how ARGX-117 prevents assembly of the CP pro-C3 convertase, we superimposed that crystal structure with the 3D reconstruction of the proconvertase C4b2, determined by negative-stain electron microscopy (EM).10 Notwithstanding the low resolution, comparison of our Fab–C2 Nt structure with this 3D EM reconstruction of C4b2 readily explained how ARGX-117 prevents C4b from interacting with zymogen C2.9 Indeed, upon reconstitution of the hypothetical ternary complex between antibody, C4b, and C2, the heavy chain of the antibody and C4b would exert a substantial steric hindrance on each other (Figure 1(C)). As such, the antibody and C4b seem to compete for the same binding site on the C2 CCP2 domain (indicated as shared epitope in Figure 1(C)). Furthermore, comparison of our crystal structure with the AlphaFold 2–predicted model of human C211 suggested that no major conformational changes are induced in C2 upon binding of ARGX-117. This is also supported by the observation that the conformation of the CCP2–CCP3 domain tandem of C2 Nt in complex with the ARGX-117 Fab can be superimposed onto the structure of C2a (PDB ID: 3ERB) with a root mean square deviation of 0.38 Å over 115 Cα positions.

A coordinated Ca2+ ion is at the center of the ARGX-117 Fab–C2 interface

The center stage of the intermolecular ARGX-117 Fab–C2 Nt interface is occupied by a hexa-coordinated Ca2+ ion (Figure 1(B,D)), which explains the previously described calcium dependency of the ARGX-117–C2 interaction.4 Except for the light chain CDR 2 (LCDR2), all other five CDRs participate in epitope binding, Ca2+ ion coordination, or both (Supplementary Figure S1A). The centrally positioned Ca2+ ion is directly coordinated by the side chains of Asp99 and Glu95 of heavy chain CDR3 (HCDR3, Kabat numbering scheme), Tyr96 of LCDR3, and the main chain carbonyl of C2 Nt Arg103 (Figure 1(D)). Two water molecules complete the coordination sphere and bridge the Ca2+ ion with the side chains of HCDR1-Asp35 and HCDR2-Asp50 (Figure 1(D)).

The importance of Ca2+ and its coordinating residues was confirmed by C2 binding experiments using point-mutated ARGX-117 alanine variants, which demonstrated a complete loss of binding regardless of the presence of additional Ca2+ (Supplementary Figure S1B). Additionally, biolayer interferometry (BLI) experiments demonstrated that addition of ethylenediamine-tetraacetic acid (EDTA) did not accelerate dissociation of C2 at pH 7.4 and only modestly at pH 5.5 (Supplementary Figure S2B-E). This observation suggests that once a Ca2+ ion is captured in the paratope-epitope interface, it remains tightly coordinated and resistant to EDTA-mediated chelation, opening the possibility that Ca2+ plays a critical role in stabilizing the paratope architecture. Furthermore, C2 binding was assessed (Supplementary Figure S3E) by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in the presence and absence of EDTA and Ca2+ at different pH values (pH 7.4 and pH 5.5) for ARGX-117. At pH 7.4, EDTA markedly reduced binding, consistent with a stabilizing role for Ca2+. In contrast, at pH 5.5, EDTA did not reduce binding, and addition of Ca2+ did not enhance binding.

Five CDRs of ARGX-117 contribute to calcium and/or C2 binding

The Fab ARGX-117–C2 Nt crystal structure reveals that five of the six CDR loops participate in the interface with CCP2 of C2. In HCDR1, the main chain of Asp31 engages in a hydrogen bond with the side chain of C2 Nt Ser106 (Figure 2(A)), while the side chain of Tyr32 supports a double salt bridge formed between C2 Nt Arg88 and Asp97 in the Fab HCDR3 (Figure 2(B)). In addition, side chains of heavy chain Thr28 and C2 Nt Lys85 are in contact through a hydrogen bond (Figure 2(A)). In HCDR2, Asp50 forms a double salt bridge with the side chain of C2 Nt Arg103 (Figure 2(A)), which simultaneously coordinates the Ca2+ ion through its main chain (Figure 1(D)). The side chain of HCDR2 residue Asn52 forms a hydrogen bond with the main chain of C2 Nt Gly105 (Figure 2(C)). Nearby, the side chain of Tyr54 engages in hydrogen bonding with the main chain of C2 Nt Ala91 and packs with the side chain of C2 Nt Val93 (Figure 2(A)). The bulky side chain of this HCDR2 Tyr54 further protrudes into a hydrophobic pocket from C2 (Figures 1(E) and 2(D)), indicating a possible hotspot for binding. To assess the importance of HCDR2 Tyr54 for binding to C2, this residue was substituted with either an alanine, phenylalanine, or aspartic acid. All three substitutions resulted in a drastic impact on C2 binding (Figure 2(E)), therefore indicating that the amphipathic characteristics of Tyr54 play a pivotal role in the paratope-epitope interface that cannot readily be provided by any of the other amino acids.

In the light chain, Ser28 from LCDR1 and C2 Nt Ser114 interact through a hydrogen bond, while LCDR1-Tyr32 delineates a binding pocket for Thr101-Pro102 of C2 Nt (Figure 2(F)). In LCDR3, the key interaction is the direct coordination of the Ca2+ ion by the Tyr96 hydroxyl group (Figure 1(D)). The position of this tyrosine side chain is secured by the side chains of Trp47 from the Fab heavy chain framework and LCDR3 Leu94 (Supplementary Figure S1C). Another prominent interaction involving LCDR3 is the double salt bridge formed between Arg92 and C2 Nt Glu116 (Figure 2(F)).

In summary, the Fab–C2 Nt interaction occurs through a highly hydrophilic interface maintained primarily by hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions organized around the central Ca2+ ion. This is further supported by a quantitative analysis with the Proteins, Interfaces, Structures and Assemblies (PISA) program,12 according to which the protein–protein interface between Fab and C2 Nt has an area of 817 Å2 (Supplementary Table S1C). The interface area between C2 Nt and the Fab heavy chain is twice the size, compared with the C2 Nt–light chain interface as illustrated in Figure 2(G). The PISA analysis further indicates that the interface is rather hydrophilic and adding further to this hydrophilic nature is the Ca2+ ion as result of its crucial role in bridging the paratope and epitope residues. In agreement with such a hydrophilic interface, PISA lists 22 hydrogen bonds and 12 salt bridges formed between the Fab and C2 Nt, which are distinct from the nine polar interactions for both direct as well as indirect coordination of the Ca2+ ion (Figure 1(D)).

The light chain tyrosine 96 plays a key role in coordination of the Ca2+ ion, but not in pH dependency

The crystal structure highlights how LCDR3-Tyr96 contributes to the Ca2+ ion coordination, which could involve at least a partially deprotonated hydroxyl group of this residue. In an idealized environment, a solvent-exposed tyrosine hydroxyl group has a pKa value of 10.46.13 Therefore, the pKa value of Tyr96 must be strongly influenced by the environment, even if it is only partially deprotonated at neutral pH. Notably, the position of Tyr96 is secured by the side chain of Asp99 from HCDR3 (Supplementary Figure S1C), which, besides being a Ca2+ ion-coordinating residue, is also tightly positioned by the main chain of C2-Arg103 and a water molecule. To gain further insight into the importance of the tyrosine hydroxyl group with respect to the function of ARGX-117, LCDR3-Tyr96 was substituted by a phenylalanine, and the effect on C2 binding and functional complement blocking activity was evaluated (Figure 3(A–C), Supplementary Figure S3). Like tyrosine, phenylalanine contains a benzene ring but is incapable of contributing to Ca2+ ion coordination due to the lack of a hydroxyl group. The phenylalanine variant still demonstrated calcium-dependent C2 binding, as shown by the observed effect of calcium chelation versus calcium addition on C2 binding efficacy (Figure 3(A)). Indeed, the addition of EDTA resulted in a Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) signal decrease of 9.8 × 104 electrochemiluminescence (ECL) units for Tyr96Phe. Moreover, Tyr96Phe illustrated a 2-fold stronger calcium dependency (maximum MSD signal difference with/without added calcium of 39 × 104 ECL units, compared with 22 × 104 ECL units for ARGX-117). These results indicate that, although LCDR3-Tyr96 is not essential for calcium-dependent C2 binding, it does increase binding efficacy, arguably by contributing to the efficacy of Ca2+ ion coordination. Aside from the Ca2+ ion-dependent binding, ARGX-117 also binds C2 in a pH-dependent manner.4 Although our data demonstrated the pivotal role of LCDR3-Tyr96 in Ca2+ ion coordination, the crystal structure argues for its side-chain hydroxyl group being in an unfavorable fully or partially deprotonated state at neutral pH. It was therefore hypothesized that at a more acidic pH (such as 5.5), the energetic penalty of participating in Ca2+ ion coordination could become too high, resulting in protonation of the LCDR3-Tyr96 hydroxyl group, loss of Ca2+ ion coordination and, eventually, loss of C2 binding. This hypothesis would endow LCDR3-Tyr96 with a dualistic role in the calcium and pH dependency of C2 binding. On the other hand, the Tyr96Phe variant retained pH-dependent target dissociation upon washing with buffer at pH 5.5 (Figure 3(A)), indicating that while LCDR3-Tyr96 may contribute to pH sensitivity, it is not the sole determinant of the dissociation mechanism. In an attempt to eliminate pH dependency while maintaining Ca2+ dependency, LCDR3-Tyr96 was therefore also substituted by either a glutamic acid or a histidine and the effect on C2 binding and functional complement blocking activity was assessed (Figure 3A–C, supplementary Figure S3). The imidazole group of histidine resembles that of an aromatic amino acid and could support calcium binding by stabilizing a water molecule coordinating the Ca2+ ion, albeit possibly in a pH-dependent manner. Glutamate, on the other hand, is the only amino acid with the potential to replace tyrosine in coordinating the Ca2+ ion in a pH-independent fashion. Substitution of LCDR3-Tyr96 by a histidine resulted in 18% reduced C2 binding at neutral pH compared with ARGX-117 (maximum MSD signal of 95.7 × 104 and 116.2 × 104 ECL units, respectively; Figure 3(A)), and an almost complete loss of bound C2 after washing with buffer at pH 5.5 (maximum MSD signal of 1.80 × 104 and 57.50 × 104 ECL units, respectively; Figure 3(A)).

For both Tyr96Phe and Tyr96His, a water molecule is likely to take the position of the Tyr96 phenol group to retain efficient Ca2+ ion coordination. Interestingly, Tyr96Glu substitution resulted in a 90% reduction in C2 binding (maximum signal of 11.95 × 104 vs 116.17 × 104 ECL units; Figure 3(A)), suggesting either an impaired Ca2+ ion coordination capability or a major rearrangement of the Fab–C2 Nt interface, although in silico mutagenesis suggested that multiple rotamers of the glutamic acid are sterically compatible with the position of Tyr96. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) data of Tyr96Glu hinted at a clear impact on binding kinetics, but the association rate constant (kon) and dissociation rate constant (koff) could not be calculated because data could not be fitted properly (Supplementary Figure S3).

Alanine scanning of CDR histidines identified variable heavy (VH) His98 as a key player in pH dependency

Despite the clear role of LCDR3-Tyr96 in the calcium dependency of C2 binding, our data thus far failed to demonstrate whether its putative deprotonated state is key for the pH dependency. As histidine residues with a predicted pKa value ~6 often play a central role in pH-dependent mechanisms,14–16 we wondered whether the pH-dependent C2 binding could be attributed to one or more histidine residues. Therefore, each CDR histidine (HCDR3-His98, LCDR1-His34, LCDR3-His90; Figure 4(A)) of ARGX-117 was substituted with an alanine and their contribution to pH-sensitive C2 binding was measured. C2 binding data singled out HCDR3-His98, and to a lesser extent LCDR1-His34, as key players in pH-dependent target release, as measured by the maximum MSD signal when washing with buffer at pH 5.5 (signal of 103.1 × 104 ECL units for His98Ala, versus 53.1 × 104 ECL units for ARGX-117; Figure 4(C)). Further inspection of this residue in the crystallographic model of the ARGX-117 Fab–C2 Nt complex revealed that HCDR3-His98 is optimally positioned to engage in an interaction with framework residue variable light (VL) Tyr49 (Figure 4(A,B)). As it has been described that π-π stacking of a protonated imidazole ring with tyrosine would further stabilize the interaction by decreasing free energy,17 we envisioned that lowering the pH would result in a repositioning of HCDR3-His98 toward VL Tyr49. Such a conformational rearrangement could propagate to the HCDR3 loop that embeds Asp99 and Glu95, which would lead to the release of the coordinated Ca2+ ion and loss of target binding. To validate this hypothesis, VL Tyr49 was substituted by either a histidine, tryptophane, or alanine. Histidine was selected because its imidazole group resembles that of the aromatic Tyr49, resulting in minimal impact at physiological pH. At pH 5.5, however, both HCDR3-His98 and VL His49 could become protonated, inducing repulsion and potentially stronger pH-dependent target dissociation. Tryptophan was selected as this would allow a more favorable π-π stacking with a protonated histidine compared to a tyrosine,17 increasing the pH dependency in a similar manner to Tyr49His substitution. Alanine was selected to prevent the observed π-π stacking and potentially eliminate pH-dependent target dissociation.

For all three mutants, MSD binding experiments (Figure 4(D)) indicate minimal impact on C2 binding compared with ARGX-117 at neutral pH, confirming that the Fab–C2 architecture remained unaffected for all three variants. On the other hand, when washed with buffer at pH 5.5, both VL Tyr49His and Tyr49Trp substitutions resulted in 90% and 81% reductions, respectively, in C2 binding compared with ARGX-117 (maximum signal of response 2.1 × 104 and 4.1 × 104 vs 22.2 × 104 ECL units; Figure 4(D)). Interestingly, substituting Tyr49 with alanine also led to an 83% reduction in C2 binding at pH 5.5 (maximum signal of 3.7 × 104 vs 22.2 × 104 ECL units; Figure 4(D)).

Framework mutations impact some key features of ARGX-117

Often, pH-dependent target binding is mediated by a histidine residue that functions as a pH switch.15 Indeed, alanine scanning of all CDR histidine residues of ARGX-117 singled out HCDR3-His98 as a key player in pH-dependent target release (Figure 4(C)). However, the crystal structure indicates that HCDR3-His98 does not directly interact with the antigen and would rather provide pH dependency through an intramolecular interaction with framework residue VL Tyr49 (Figure 4(B)). Interestingly, the non-humanized, calcium-dependent pre-lead antibody (anti-C2 mAb 5F2.44) exhibited reduced target dissociation at pH 5.5 compared to the humanized ARGX-117, despite both antibodies containing HCDR3-His98 and VL Tyr49 (Figures 5(A,C)), suggesting that additional structural or sequence elements beyond HCDR3-His98 and VL-Tyr49 contribute to the enhanced pH-dependent dissociation observed in ARGX-117. To identify the framework residues that drive the difference in pH-dependent target dissociation, single-point mutations were generated in mAb 5F2.4, while the corresponding backmutations were introduced into ARGX-117 (Supplementary Figure S4). At each step, the pH dependency of each variant was assessed. These experiments revealed that 6 of 29 framework amino acids that were introduced upon humanization (VL Asn2, VH Arg38, VH Ala40, VH Gly44, VH Arg66, and VH Met69) seemed to be involved in pH-dependent target dissociation. Interestingly, as single-point mutations in 5F2.4, none of these residues were able to introduce the full level of pH-dependent target dissociation as observed in ARGX-117 (data not shown). Only when all of these substitutions were incorporated into one construct, the 5F2.4 variant (5F2.4_5mut_Ile2Asn) displayed pH dependency comparable to ARGX-117 (Figure 5(A)).

Remarkably, although the combination of all six substitutions was necessary to introduce the pH-dependent target dissociation in 5F2.4, only the five mouse-like heavy chain residues (ARGX-117 VH_5mut) were needed to decrease pH dependency of ARGX-117 to the level of mAb 5F2.4. Surprisingly, simply mutating VL Asn2 to isoleucine in ARGX-117 (ARGX-117 Asn2Ile), without altering the heavy chain residues, was also able to eliminate pH-dependent target dissociation to the levels of mAb 5F2.4 (Figure 5(A)). So, while six substitutions were needed to introduce a human-like pH dependency in the mouse antibody, a mouse-like pH dependency could be introduced to ARGX-117 with either a subset of five VH substitutions or the single substitution VL Asn2Ile. Structural analysis reveals that Asn2 is oriented toward a buried internal pocket in immediate proximity to His90 within the LCDR3 loop (Supplementary Figure S1D). The substitution of the polar asparagine with a hydrophobic isoleucine could increase the local conformational rigidity through optimized packing within this pocket, effectively ‘locking’ the LCDR3 loop and preventing the structural rearrangements required for the pH-induced release of C2.

The ARGX-117 Fab–C2 Nt structure demonstrates that the five VH residues are positioned underneath and in the loop forming the β-sheet that carries Asp35 and Asp50, which presumably are essential for Ca2+ ion binding (Figure 5B). It is plausible that a slight reorientation of Asp35 and Asp50 could alter calcium dependency and/or affinity of ARGX-117 for C2, thereby affecting the pH-dependent target release. On the other hand, VL Asn2 is positioned in framework 1, for which mutations have been described that impact antigen binding through an indirect effect.19 In summary, the data suggest that framework residues play an important role in driving the pH-dependent target dissociation of ARGX-117.

A structural rationale for ARGX-117 species selectivity

It was previously observed that ARGX-117 binds human C2 with a sub-nanomolar affinity (KD = 0.3 nM) and is cross-reactive against cynomolgus monkey C2.4 In contrast, ARGX-117 exhibits lower affinity for rat C2 (KD = 9.08 nM) and does not react with mouse, guinea pig, or rabbit C2. This lack of cross-reactivity complicates the usage of ARGX-117 in animal disease models for evaluation of in vivo efficacy. To gain further insights into the different reactivity across species, sequence alignments of the mammalian C2 molecules were compared with our C2 Nt–Fab structure, focusing on the hC2 Nt residues Arg88 to Glu116 (Figure 5(D)). Strikingly, the non-binding C2 (rabbit, mouse, guinea pig) differs from the binding C2 (human, cynomolgus monkey, rat) at two positions–Gly105 and Ser106 – that play key roles in the C2 Nt–Fab interface (Figure 2(A–C)).

The side chain of Ser106 engages in hydrogen bonds with the main chain of Fab HCDR1 (Figure 2(A)) and neighbors the side chain of C2-Arg88. Guinea pig C2 features a histidine instead of Arg88 and an alanine instead of Ser106. The absence of the arginine is a likely explanation for the observed lack of cross-reactivity, as in the structure of the human Fab–C2 Nt complex, the side chain of Arg88 forms a double salt bridge with HCDR3-Asp97 (Figure 2(B)). Likewise, we observed that C2 Nt Gly105 interacts through van der Waals interactions with HCDR1-Asn33 and through a main chain hydrogen bond with HCDR2-Asn52 (Figure 2(C)). In silico substitution of Gly105 with alanine (as in rabbit C2) or valine (as in murine C2) provides an explanation for the observed lack of reactivity with these two mammalian C2 molecules. Although the main chain conformation of Gly105 is on the edge of the allowed area for α-helix in the Ramachandran plot and might be compatible with an alanine or valine at position 105, it is clear that even the alanine methyl group will exert steric hindrance on the main chain of the Fab CDR1.

In conclusion, the structure of the ARGX-117 Fab–C2 Nt complex explains why the antibody is unable to bind mouse or rabbit C2. Steric hindrance is likely to interfere with correct Ca2+ ion coordination by the Glu95 side chain from the Fab HCDR3 and correct positioning by HCDR1-Asn33 of Ca2+ ion-coordinating water. In addition, an alanine at position 105 may also influence the ability of the main chain carbonyl of C2-Arg103 to act as a Ca2+ ion ligand (Figure 2(C)). The weaker affinity of rat C2 is possibly due to the presence of a threonine at the equivalent of Val93 from human C2. The more polar side chain in rat C2 is likely to be less efficient at stabilizing the position of the side chain of HCDR3-Tyr54 that interacts with the main chain of C2-Ala91 (Figure 2(A)).

Discussion

Our initial characterization of the ARGX-117 antibody showed that it binds to the CCP2 domain of complement C2 and, by doing so, prevents C2 from binding to C4b, blocking further downstream activation of both CP and LP.4 Though ARGX-117 was confirmed to bind to the C2a fragment released from C2 upon cleavage by C1s or MASP1/2 after binding to C4b, the mechanism of this inhibitory activity of ARGX-117 remained elusive. Furthermore, it was previously demonstrated that ARGX-117 binds C2 in a calcium- and pH-dependent manner.4 The latter dependencies are essential for its recycling properties, which accelerate clearance of C2 from the circulation and prolong the half-life of ARGX-117. However, the exact molecular mechanism behind the calcium and pH dependency of ARGX-117 on C2 binding remained unclear.4 In this study, we present a comprehensive analysis of the ARGX-117-C2 interaction based on the crystal structure of the ARGX-117 Fab–C2 Nt complex. This analysis shows how ARGX-117 blocks proconvertase C4b2 assembly, clarifies calcium’s role in complex formation, and offers insights into how pH affects the interaction.

Comparison between the ARGX-117 Fab–C2 Nt crystal structure and the EM-derived 3D reconstruction of C4b2 explains how ARGX-117 sterically prevents C4b docking on C2 by competing for the same binding site and, consequently, stops activation of the complement cascade. A similar mechanism of complement inhibition was recently described for a C4b-specific VHH that also prevents assembly of the CP proconvertase by binding with subnanomolar affinity to C4b.20 Noteworthy, a fourth complement activation pathway has recently been identified through Granzyme K.21 Given that ARGX-117 sterically prevents intact C2 from interacting with C4b, it is unlikely to inhibit the granzyme-K-driven complement pathway, in which C2 is cleaved independently of C4b association.

The most fascinating feature of our crystal structure is arguably the presence of a Ca2+ ion at the center of the intermolecular interface, where the light chain, heavy chain, and C2 congregate. The observation that target dissociation did not increase in the presence of the chelating agent EDTA neatly illustrates how tightly this Ca2+ ion is embedded within the ARGX-117–C2 complex. Indeed, of the five CDR loops observed to participate in antigen recognition, four directly support coordination of the Ca2+ ion. In these four loops, five amino acids are responsible for Ca2+ ion coordination. Substituting each of the five amino acid residues (HCDR1-Asp35, HCDR2-Asp50, HCDR3-Glu95, HCDR3-Asp99, and LCDR3-Tyr96), with an alanine severely impacted C2 binding. Admittedly, it cannot be excluded that at least some of these residues are also determinants of CDR stability, rather than only being responsible for Ca2+ ion coordination. A remarkable residue engaging in Ca2+ ion coordination is LCDR3-Tyr96, which possibly requires its sidechain hydroxyl group to be partially or fully deprotonated. Given that a tyrosine phenol group has a predicted pKa value of 10.46, being deprotonated at physiological pH is accompanied by a significant energetic penalty. Interestingly, substitution of LCDR3-Tyr96 by phenylalanine severely impacted C2 binding and blocking efficacy, but the interaction was nonetheless still calcium dependent. LCDR3-Tyr96 is thus not essential for calcium dependency, but increases the binding efficacy, arguably by contributing to Ca2+ ion coordination. Although tyrosine-coordinated calcium interactions can be observed in different crystallographic structures, a metadata analysis using the MetalPDB database failed to deliver any examples of Fab–antigen complexes where calcium coordination occurs so deeply embedded in the interface. Hironiwa and colleagues have shown a Fab–antigen complex with calcium dependency; however, in their case, the Ca2+ ion stabilized the HCDR3 loop and was not at the interface of the Fab–antigen complex.22

In addition to calcium-dependent binding,4 ARGX-117 features pH-dependent target release. This pH dependency was found for the humanized antibody, but not for the originating murine antibody, as is confirmed by the results of this study. Notably, humanization of the murine mAb did not involve changes in histidine residues. Therefore, substantial efforts were made to better understand the key players in this pH dependency, which appeared to be mediated by CDR histidines that were already present in the murine sequences, but unexpectedly were affected by mutations of framework residues. Although we initially considered Tyr96 deprotonation as a possible contributor to pH dependency, our mutational data demonstrated that Tyr96 does not function as a pH-switch. CDR alanine scanning revealed HCDR3-His98 and, to a lesser extent, LCDR1-His34 to be involved in the pH-dependent properties of ARGX-117. Although histidine residues at or near the binding interface are often key mediators of pH-dependent binding modes,15–17 this is not the case for His98 or His34. Therefore, it was interesting to observe that the pH dependency of C2 binding is likely rooted in an intramolecular interplay involving the VL framework residue Tyr49.

We initially considered whether protonated HCDR3-His98 might engage in an aromatic interaction (π-π stacking) with VL-Tyr49. The eminent conformational change would induce a rearrangement of the VH loop that embeds Asp99 and Glu95, which are both engaged in Ca2+ ion coordination. If true, such a rearrangement could potentially result in loss of or decrease in calcium coordination and subsequently target binding. Indeed, substitution of VL Tyr49 with histidine or tryptophan both resulted in less C2 binding compared with ARGX-117 when washed in buffer at pH 5.5, strengthening the hypothesis of π-π stacking. However, substitution of the VL Tyr49 with alanine also resulted in stronger pH dependency, whereas no π-π stacking should be possible to push HCDR3-His98 from its stable position. Interestingly, Wade et al. recently reported a similar increase in pH dependency when mutating Tyr49 to alanine.23 In that study, molecular dynamics simulations indicated that substitution of Tyr49 by an alanine increased both the protonation probability and solvation of His34 at pH 5.5. Thus, rather than invalidating the identification of π-π stacking as key contributor to pH-dependent target release, our data support a more nuanced model in which framework residue Tyr49 might play a role by modulating local solvent accessibility and protonation probability of neighboring histidine residues.23 Substituting VL Tyr49 with a bulky non-aromatic amino acid that does not interact with the protonated histidine, but is still compatible with the current conformation, could help elucidate the factors that are truly causative for target release.23

Alongside investigating the role of CDR histidines in the pH dependency of ARGX-117, we studied the importance of framework residues. As the non-humanized, calcium-dependent pre-lead antibody (anti-C2 mAb 5F2.4) illustrated less target dissociation at pH 5.5 compared with ARGX-117, pH dependency apparently had increased during humanization. Our analysis identified 6 of 29 humanization mutations (VL Ile2Asn, VH Lys38Arg, VH Ser40Ala, VH Ser44Gly, VH Lys66Arg, and VH Leu69Met) that were responsible for the introduction of pH-dependent target dissociation. However, only the combination of all six together allowed the same level of pH-dependent target dissociation as seen with ARGX-117. Remarkably, substituting either the VL Asn2 or the five heavy-chain residues in ARGX-117 with the original mouse residue could induce a complete loss of pH-dependent target dissociation. How those six positions behave differently when inducing or reducing pH dependency in the mouse clone and ARGX-117, respectively, remains a question. But taken together, the data suggest subtle structural rearrangements as being responsible for higher structural flexibility, resulting in stronger pH dependency. More specifically, we hypothesize that when the antibody framework adopts a more rigid binding-prone conformation (in 5F2.4), it is more resistant to small conformational changes introduced by changes in the protonation state of histidine residues and/or VL Tyr96, which would otherwise induce repulsion and target dissociation at pH 5.5 (as in ARGX-117).

Although this study focuses on pH-dependent target release, reversible pH-dependent binding is essential for the catch-and-release mechanism of recycling antibodies. While binding reversibility was not directly assessed here, the sustained pharmacodynamic effects reported in our first-in-human study of ARGX-117 imply repeated target capture, endosomal release, and antibody recycling in vivo.9 To further clarify the relationship between pH dependence and Ca2+ dependence, C2 binding experiments at acidic pH were performed in the presence or absence of EDTA or excess Ca2+. As described above, dissociation experiments at low pH showed that chelation of Ca2+ only slightly accelerated dissociation under acidic conditions as compared to the effect on the dissociation rate induced by the pH drop. These observations indicate that Ca2+ withdrawal is not the primary trigger for target release and that the drop in pH is the dominating initiating event. ELISA-based binding experiments revealed that at pH 7.4 Ca2+ chelation by EDTA markedly reduced C2 binding, consistent with a stabilizing role for Ca2+ at neutral pH. In contrast, at pH 5.5, EDTA did not reduce binding, and addition of excess Ca2+ did not enhance binding, indicating that Ca2+ is functionally irrelevant for complex formation under acidic conditions. Taken together, these observations suggest that at acidic pH, protonation of histidine residues in the CDRs induce structural rearrangements within the paratope that disrupt the Ca2+ binding pocket, leading to disruption of the Ca2+ coordination sphere simultaneously with target release. Overall, our results support a model in which calcium and pH dependencies in ARGX-117 are mechanistically linked, with low pH inducing structural changes that destabilize calcium coordination and impair binding. Moreover, the data suggests an intrinsic antibody reorganization as the starting point for pH-dependent target release, in contrast to the usually observed attraction/repulsion because of histidine protonation.14–16 This pH-dependent target release appears to be lost when six framework residues that do not directly contribute to target binding are substituted, highlighting an important connection between framework and the CDRs. Admittedly, despite extensive structural and functional analysis, the calcium and pH dependencies of ARGX-117 binding could not be fully disentangled, underscoring the complex interplay between CDRs and framework residues. This unresolved complexity therefore emphasizes the current challenges with engineering environmental triggers in antibody activity and highlights how subtle structure features shape antibody functions. Collectively, this study therefore not only increases our understanding of the mode of action of ARGX-117 but also illustrates the pivotal–albeit complex–role of the antibody framework in guiding CDR conformations during environment-dependent target binding.

Materials and methods

Crystal structure

The crystal structure of the ARGX-117 Fab fragment with an N-terminal human C2 fragment (C2 Nt; human C2 residues 1–217; Uniprot entry: P06681) was previously determined and described by Van de Walle et al.9 Molecular graphics figures were prepared using PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics system, Version 4.6.0 Schrodinger, LLC). Kabat numbering is used throughout for ARGX-117, and a comparison with the Fab fragment numbering used in the structure deposited at the protein data bank entry 8ACI can be found in Supplementary Figure S2A.

Serum samples

Complement-preserved human serum samples of 20 healthy volunteers were obtained from BioIVT. After evaluation of CP (using an in-house developed assay, described below) and LP (Wieslab Complement System MBL Pathway, Svar, cat. no. COMPLMP320) activity of all individual samples, serum samples were pooled. Four samples were excluded due to undetectable LP activity.

Generation and production of ARGX-117–derived mutants

DNA fragments of the VL chain of ARGX-117 containing desired mutations were obtained from ThermoFisher Scientific (GeneArt synthesis) and ligated into a vector encoding the DNA sequence of the constant light (CL) chain. Resulting cloned plasmids were transformed into chemically competent Escherichia coli TOP10 cells by heat shock. Sequencing of plasmid DNA was performed with a 31-nucleotide primer (5′ctttctctccacaggtgtccactcccagttc3′). Antibodies were produced following protocols described elsewhere.24 Briefly, HEK293 cells were transfected with 1:1 Heavy/Light chain plasmid ratio using 1:3 DNA/polyethyleneimine ratio for transient production, followed by protein A affinity purification.

Classical complement pathway activation assay

Microtiter wells (Greiner Bio-One, cat. no. 675,061) were coated with 50 µg/mL human immunoglobulin (Ig) G3 (Lonza, cat. no. 787-181120-01) in 50 µL carbonate buffer (sodium carbonate and sodium bicarbonate, 0.1 M, pH 9.6; ThermoFisher Scientific, cat. no. 28,382). After an incubation overnight at 4°C, wells were blocked by 1% (w/v) casein (Bio-Rad, cat. no. 1,610,783) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Bio-Rad commercial, cat. no. 1,610,783) for 1 h and washed four times with PBS containing 0.05% (w/v) Tween 20 (Merck, cat. no. 8.17072). After blocking, wells were incubated with 2.5% (w/v) pooled human sera diluted in Veronal Buffer (VB) (Lonza BioWhittaker; ThermoFisher Scientific, cat. no. 12–624E) supplemented with 90 mM NaCl (final concentration in the well) for 30 min standing at 37°C (activation step) in a thermoshaker (ThermoMixer F series model F2.0, Roth). CP-blocking activity of ARGX-117 or derived mutants was assessed by addition of the antibodies to the dilution buffer. As control, 20 mM EDTA (final concentration) was added to the dilution buffer (to completely block CP, LP, and AP). After washing, wells were incubated with 7.28 ng/µL anti-human C3-mIgG2a antibody (ImmunoPrecise Antibodies) in PBS containing 0.05% (w/v) Tween 20 for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Subsequently, plates were washed and 10.6 pg/µL peroxidase-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Europe Ltd, CAT no. 715–035-150) in PBS containing 0.05% (w/v) Tween 20 was added. After incubation for 1 h at RT, wells were washed and 50 µL per well of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine solution (Merck, cat. no. CL07-1000 ML) was added. The enzymatic coloring reaction was stopped after 10 min by addition of 50 µL sulfuric acid (Chem-Lab cat. no. CL05.2615.1000). Signal (optical density at 450/620 nm) was measured by a spectrophotometer (Infinite M Nano, Tecan).

Meso Scale Discovery experiments

Multi-array 96-well plates (Meso Scale Discovery, cat. no. L15XA-3) were coated overnight at 4°C with 1 ng/µL purified human C2 (Complement Technology Inc., cat. no. A112, lot#20) in PBS. After washing the plates three times with 150 µL wash buffer A (Supplementary Table S1A), wells were blocked with 1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma, cat. no. 05482) for 2 h at RT. Plates were then washed three times with wash buffer A-D (Supplementary Table S1A) and incubated for 1 h at RT with serial dilutions of antibody in dilution buffer A-D (Supplementary Table S1A) to a final volume of 25 µL. After washing three times with wash buffer A-D, plates were incubated with wash buffer A-D for 5 min. A fifth wash step with wash buffer A was performed before applying 12.5 ng sulfo-TAG labeled goat anti-human IgG antibody (Meso Scale Discovery, cat. no. R32AJ-1) in 25 µL dilution buffer A for 1 h at RT. After washing five times with wash buffer A, 150 µL MSD reading buffer (Meso Scale Discovery, cat. no. R92TC-2, diluted to 50% v/v in deionized water before use) was added to wells and signal was measured immediately using a Meso™ QuickPlex SQ 120 imager (Meso Scale Discovery, cat. no. MSDAI0AA-0).

Surface plasmon resonance experiments of ARGX-117 and derived variants

SPR measurements were performed on a Biacore 3000 instrument (Cytiva). Biotinylated recombinant human C2 (produced in HEK293E-253 cells, biotinylated in-house using EZ-link NHS-Biotin 10 mM [ThermoFisher Scientific, cat. no. 20,217]) was diluted to 1 µg/mL in 20 mM N-2-hydroxyethylpiperazine-N-2-ethane sulfonic acid (HEPES), 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 0.15 mM MgCl2, 0.05% (w/v) Tween20, pH 7.5 and injected and immobilized to 200 response units (RU) on the surface of a high-affinity streptavidin (SA) sensor chip (Sensor Chip SA; Cytiva, cat. no. BR100398) at a flow rate of 30 µL/min. Binding measurements of ARGX-117 and derived mutants were performed in 20 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 0.15 mM MgCl2, 0.05% (w/v) Tween20, pH 7.5 at 30 µL/min flow rate. After each measurement, the surface was regenerated by two injections of 5 µL 10 mM glycine-HCl pH 1.5 (Cytiva, cat. no. BR100354) at a flow rate of 30 µL/min without loss of C2 binding capacity. Sensorgrams were generated at concentrations of 333.3, 166.7, 83.3, 41.7, and 20.8 nM antibody. Kinetics of the antibodies were determined with BIAevaluation software version 4.1.1 (GE Healthcare) using simultaneous ka/kd fitting with 1:1 mass transfer.

Biolayer interferometry

A BLI assay was developed to determine the effect of 20 mM EDTA pre-treatment on the on- and off-rate kinetics of the ARGX-117–C2 binding, both with and without 1.25 mM CaCl2 added during the association phase. All experiments were performed at 25°C using an Octet RED96 instrument (Sartorius). For every experiment, pre-conditioned Anti-Human Fc Capture (AHC2) Biosensors (Satorius, cat. no. 18–5060) were functionalized for 90 s at a concentration of 10 µg/mL in buffer at pH 7.4 with ARGX-117, resulting in a response shift of ~1 nm. Functionalized sensors were equilibrated in a washing buffer containing PBS pH 7.4, 0.1% (w/v) BSA, 0.02% (w/v) Tween20. Where indicated, 1.25 mM CaCl2 was added to the washing buffer. Association with indicated concentrations of plasma-purified human C2 and dissociation were performed in PBS buffer pH 7.4 or pH 5.5, 0.1% (w/v) BSA, 0.02% (w/v) Tween20. Where indicated, buffers were supplemented with 1.25 mM CaCl2 or 20 mM EDTA (final concentration). Both association and dissociation were performed for 300 s in the indicated buffers. Biosensors not coated with ARGX-117 were used during the assay to verify that nonspecific binding was minimal. Sensor traces of zero concentration conditions and a column of non-functionalized sensors were used to enable double reference subtraction. A 1:1 ligand model was fitted to all data using the ForteBio Data Analysis 9.0.0.14 software (Molecular devices, Sartorius).

C2 binding ELISA

96-well Nunc Maxisorp ELISA plates (Merck, cat. no. M9410) were coated overnight at 4°C with 1 µg/mL purified human C2 in 1xPBS. After washing three times with wash buffer A-E, plates were incubated with wash buffer A-E for 5 min. A fifth wash step with wash buffer A was performed before applying goat anti-human IgG-horseradish peroxidase antibody (Jackson 109–035-008) in 100 µL dilution buffer A for 1 h at RT. After washing five times with wash buffer A, 100 µL 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine solution was added to wells. The enzymatic coloring reaction was stopped after 10 min by the addition of 100 µL sulfuric acid. Signal (optical density at 450/620 nm) was measured by a spectrophotometer (Infinite M Nano, Tecan).
