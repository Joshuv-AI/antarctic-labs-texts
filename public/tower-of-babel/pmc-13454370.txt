Endoscopic Management of a Rare Pediatric Fronto‐Ethmoidal Mucocele With Orbital Involvement: Navigating Surgical Challenges
Authors: Sushil Raj Shrestha, Sajjad Ahmed Khan, Shiva Bhusan Pandit, Kshitiz Parajuli, Abhisek Jha, Samit Kumar Gautam, Kuldeep Timilsina, Ramakant Dhawal, Sajan Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParanasal mucoceles are benign lesions causing bone erosion with orbital and intracranial extension. An 11‐year‐old male presented with right orbital proptosis with restricted movement. Computed tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging revealed a fronto‐ethmoidal mucocele involving orbit. Endoscopic marsupialization with drainage and orbital decompression achieved complete clinical and radiological resolution.Keywords: endoscopy, lamina papyracea, mucocele, orbit, paranasal sinuses, pediatric

Introduction

Mucoceles of the paranasal sinuses (PNS) are gradually enlarging cystic formations that result in sinus enlargement, bone erosion, and, eventually, growth into the orbit and cranial space. Mucoceles may represent enlarging orbital lesions, with the frontal and ethmoidal sinuses being the most frequently affected locations [1]. Langenbeck was the one who described them for the first time in the early 19th century, and the term itself was coined by Rollet in 1886 [2]. As it progresses, a mucocele may undergo expansion, exerting pressure on adjacent anatomical structures. A characteristic clinical manifestation includes thinning of the sinus wall, often resulting in observable swelling in the region superior to the medial canthus of the eye [3]. Though initially asymptomatic, fronto‐ethmoidal mucoceles (FEM) can present with a wide variety of ophthalmological symptoms such as proptosis, periorbital pain, restricted ocular movement, diplopia, lid swelling, chemosis, optic neuropathy, and decreased vision [4]. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) remain the gold standard imaging modalities for the diagnosis of mucoceles of the PNS due to their ability to better demonstrate the extent of the lesion as well as surrounding bone erosion. The primary treatment modality for mucocele remains surgery, ranging from endoscopic marsupialization to more extensive approaches such as craniofacial exposures [5].

Here, we present a rare case of fronto‐ethmoidal mucocele where the age of presentation is quite rare. Likewise, the symptoms that the patient presented were not typical of the disease condition. The patient had right periorbital swelling with restricted right eye motility, being treated with non‐invasive endoscopic marsupialization with right orbital decompression (OD).

Case Presentation

Case History/Examination

An 11‐year‐old boy presented in the otolaryngology outpatient department with a history of right periorbital swelling for one and a half months. Swelling was insidious in onset, and he had difficulty moving his right eye. However, the patient did not report any other ophthalmologic symptoms such as periorbital pain, bulging of the eyeball, double vision, or visual disturbances. The patient also denied symptoms such as nasal obstruction and headache, and symptoms suggestive of optic neuropathy, thus excluding neurological involvement. Past history and family history were unremarkable.

On clinical examination, approximately 1 × 1.5 cm swelling was present in the right medial canthus extending to the lacrimal sac region, which was slightly tender on palpation, adherent to the underlying structure with no mobility or visible pulsation. No symptom of epiphora was seen. Ophthalmology was consulted to measure the orbital pressure; however it was not measured. Vision along with color vision and extra ocular movements were full and free (Figure 1). Ears revealed normal pinnae and external auditory canals, and otoscopic findings were normal. Tuning fork tests were within normal limits. Facial nerve examination revealed intact status. On diagnostic nasal endoscopy, the middle turbinate was found to be medialized. A smooth bulge was noted in the right nasal cavity at the level of the right nasomaxillary line (Figure 2). Examination of the oral cavity, oropharynx, and neck was unremarkable. Other systemic examinations were within normal limits. HPE was not sent as intra op was typical of mucocele. Culture was sent but found to be negative. OD is not done in all cases but only in cases of proptosis for symptoms relief.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

To evaluate the extent and nature of the swelling, a non‐contrast CT (NCCT) scan of the PNS was performed. The imaging demonstrated a well‐defined lesion in the right fronto‐ethmoidal region, associated with medialization of the middle turbinate and protrusion into the nasal cavity. Dehiscence of the right lamina papyracea (LP) was also observed, indicating possible orbital extension. No evidence of bony destruction or intracranial extension was identified (Figures 3 and 4). However, these imaging and clinical findings were inconclusive and hence a Magnetic Resonance Imaging of Brain was ordered which showcased a smooth, regular sac‐like structure containing mucinous fluid—dark colored fluid in T1 weighted imaging, bright white fluid in T2 weighted and Diffusion Weighted Imaging—with septations in the right fronto‐ethmoidal region (Figures 5, 6, 7). These radiological findings were consistent with the provisional diagnosis of a right fronto‐ethmoidal mucocele, whereas other probable diagnoses, such as lacrimal gland tumors, benign and malignant sinonasal lesions, allergic fungal rhinosinusitis, and rarer cholesterol granuloma, were ruled out.

After the final diagnosis, the patient underwent endoscopic marsupialization with right OD under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, a smooth bulge was identified in the right nasal cavity at the right nasomaxillary region, consistent with the preoperative findings. Thick mucin was aspirated, and marsupialization of the mucocele wall was performed. A defect in the right LP was confirmed intraoperatively with gentle orbital compression. OD was done to address the extension and reduce orbital pressure (Figure 8).

Intraoperative image showing marsupialization of the mucocele with orbital decompression.

Results and Conclusion (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Postoperatively, no complications were observed, and the patient was symptom‐free. On follow‐up, the patient showed remarkable improvement in swelling along with improvement in eye movement (Figure 9). To assess the postoperative status of the disease, an NCCT of the PNS was performed, which demonstrated near‐complete resolution of the lesion with clear delineation of boundaries of fronto‐ethmoidal sinuses and an intact right orbital region (Figure 10).

Discussion

A PNS mucocele is a benign growth that develops gradually due to the obstruction of normal mucus drainage produced by the epithelial lining of the PNS at the sinus ostium, the opening that links the sinus to the nasal cavity. It can develop secondary to obstruction of the duct of the minor salivary gland located within the PNS. Etiologies of ostial blockage are varied and range from mere sinus infections, allergies, trauma, iatrogenic injury, and benign tumors such as osteoma or fibrous dysplasia to more dreadful malignancies [6, 7]. Although non‐threatening, they tend to grow by eroding the nearby bony walls, which displaces and damages structures through pressure and bone resorption [8].

Mucoceles most commonly arise from frontal and frontoethmoidal sinuses, with very little involvement of sphenoidal and maxillary sinuses [2]. This may be linked to the complexity of drainage of fronto‐ethmoidal sinuses in comparison to others [7]. Mucoceles are harmless growths that appear with comparable regularity in adults of either gender and infrequently in children under 10 years [8]. A study published by Vince et al. found that mucocele normally affects people in their 30s and 40s, whereas James et al. found that the highest incidence is in the 4th to 7th decade of life [9, 10]. In this case, the pediatric age of the patient renders the diagnosis of frontoethmoidal mucocele both uncommon and diagnostically challenging.

The clinical signs of mucoceles typically arise from the pressure‐induced destruction of nearby structures, along with contributing elements such as localized osteomyelitis and increased decalcification. As mucus continues to be secreted and accumulated, the rising pressure leads to atrophy or erosion of the sinus bone, enabling the mucus to expand in areas of least resistance, usually the orbit or cranial cavity. Clinical manifestations may be extensive, ranging from ophthalmological symptoms, facial deformity, proptosis, enophthalmos, loss of vision, and diplopia to rhinological symptoms, facial pain or discomfort, headache, and nasal obstruction [2, 8]. Here, the patient presented with right periorbital swelling with difficulty moving his right eye, without any gross symptoms, highlighting the early presentation of the patient in the clinic.

CT is the primary imaging modality for mucoceles involving PNS due to better demonstration of the expansion of lesions and their relationship with surrounding structures. In imaging, mucoceles appear as non‐enhancing lesions with soft tissue density, typically iso‐dense to brain parenchyma, usually expanding the sinuses, eroding nearby bones, and extending into the orbit or cranium. Likewise, MRI offers further insights during the assessment of the orbit and is preferred if other soft tissue tumors leading to proptosis cannot be ruled out. MRI offers the benefit of multiplanar imaging and is significantly more effective in distinguishing a mucocele from a tumor by analyzing MR signal intensity features [7, 8, 11]. In this context, a non‐contrast CT imaging of PNS along with MRI Brain showed a well‐defined mucin containing lesion involving the frontoethmoidal region associated with dehiscence of the LP and orbital extension. These findings were invaluable in differentiating the mucocele from other orbital/sinonasal masses such as lacrimal gland tumor, dermoid cysts, encephaloceles, and malignancies.

Surgery remains the mainstay of treatment of mucocele and primarily involves two approaches: external and endonasal. The external method has considerable morbidities, such as scarring, cosmetic deformities, and paresthesia, while the endonasal technique with marsupialization and drainage not only conserves the epithelium but also has negligible complications. This minimally invasive technique offers several advantages, including preservation of the bony framework of the affected sinus, reduced operative time, avoidance of external incisions, shorter hospitalization, and decreased overall surgical costs. Furthermore, it is associated with lower recurrence rates. These benefits have enhanced the safety and efficacy of intranasal marsupialization, even in cases with intracranial or intra‐orbital extension [2, 7]. In accordance with standard treatment protocols, the patient underwent minimally invasive surgery comprising endoscopic marsupialization and OD. Although this approach is considered optimal, it presents technical challenges, especially in pediatric patients, due to the smaller anatomical confines. The patient remains under follow‐up, with no recurrence of previous symptoms and no reported postoperative complications to date.

Conclusion

This report presents a rare case of fronto‐ethmoidal mucocele in a pediatric patient—a clinical entity that poses significant diagnostic challenges and demands prompt and adequate surgical intervention to avert potentially severe complications. The absence of characteristic symptoms, particularly in the initial stages, combined with distinctive imaging findings such as LP dehiscence by iso‐dense non‐enhancing lesions highlights the critical role of radiological‐clinical correlation in distinguishing mucoceles from other sinonasal lesions. Consistent with previous literature, this case report emphasizes the efficacy and safety of endoscopic marsupialization and drainage with Orbital Decompression, which resulted in a favorable outcome, minimal morbidity, and rapid clinical recovery compared to more invasive external approaches. Furthermore, this text also underscores the importance of considering paranasal sinus mucoceles in the differential diagnosis of periorbital swelling in the pediatric population and advocates for a non‐invasive endoscopic approach as the treatment modality of choice.
