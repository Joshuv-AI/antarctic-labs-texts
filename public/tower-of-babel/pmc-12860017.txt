Motives and deterrents of blood donation: perspectives from the post-COVID era in Northern Iran
Authors: Rayehe Rahimi, Enayatollah Homaie Rad, Mohammad Amin Ashoobi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlood donation is essential to healthcare systems, yet shortages persist, highlighting the need to understand factors influencing donation behavior. Motivations and deterrents, shaped by societal changes like the COVID-19 pandemic, significantly impact donation. This study explores the motives and deterrents of blood donation among Iranian donors in the post-COVID era, while also examining how the pandemic has affected donor attitudes, including their willingness to donate.MethodsA prospective study was conducted at Anzali Blood Donation Center, Iran, from December 2023 to January 2024. Data on demographics, motivations, deterrents, and pandemic effects were collected through interviews with donors. Background

Blood donation plays a critical role in supporting healthcare systems worldwide. Providing a vital resource for transfusions saves lives and improves health outcomes for those in need. However, blood shortages continue to pose a significant challenge in many countries [1–3], making it crucial to understand the factors that affect blood donation.

Motivation is an essential factor in encouraging people to donate blood. Historically, altruism and empathy have been cited as primary motives for blood donation [4]. Knowing that one act of donating blood can save lives and make a positive impact on society is a powerful motivator, bringing a sense of satisfaction to blood donors [5]. In addition, people may be motivated to donate blood through social responsibility and the influence of friends, family, or the media [6]. It is also important to examine how these traditional motives interact with contemporary factors and societal changes.

Conversely, various deterrents and barriers can impede individuals’ willingness to donate blood. These deterrents can be categorized into two dimensions, including individual-related and facility-related factors. Fear of needles, concerns about potential adverse effects, medical excuses, negative experiences with prior donations, lack of awareness, and inconvenience may serve as individual-related barriers [4, 6, 7]. Moreover, facility-related challenges, such as a lack of accessible donation centers, long queues, and difficulty finding time to donate, can also discourage potential donors [8, 9].

In recent years, the landscape of blood donation has evolved and been influenced by various social, cultural, and demographic factors. The pandemic caused by COVID-19 has greatly affected our society, leading to changes in people’s perceptions, behaviors, and priorities. Fear of infection, changes in social norms, and restrictions on movement and gatherings have created unique circumstances that may influence individuals’ motives and deterrents for blood donation [10–13]. Exploring these factors is imperative to identify the challenges faced by blood donation organizations and to design targeted interventions to encourage and sustain blood donation in the post-COVID era.

This study investigates the motives and deterrents of blood donation among Iranian donors in the post-COVID era. In addition, we intend to assess the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on donors’ attitudes. By analyzing this data, we can provide valuable insights to policymakers, healthcare professionals, and blood donation organizations. This will help them create effective strategies based on evidence to overcome obstacles, increase motivation, and ensure a stable blood supply for those who require it.

Methods

This prospective study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Guilan University of Medical Science(IR.GUMS.REC.1402.590). From December 1, 2023, to January 31, 2024, all consecutive blood donors at the blood donation center of Anzali (a city in a northern province of Iran) who met the inclusion criteria of the study were included in our analysis. The inclusion criteria were age > 18 and consenting to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were repeated donors who participated in this study in a prior donation. and any communication or verbal disorder that interfered with the procedure of interviews. All donations were voluntary, as the Iranian Blood Transfusion Organization does not have a replacement donation system. To calculate an adequate sample size, we conducted a pilot study with 20 donors. Altruism was the most prevalent variable among motives or deterrents, with 50%, while fear of needles was the least prevalent at 10%. Based on Cochran’s formula with a 95% confidence level and considering the highest proportion of 50%, the required sample size for proportion estimation was 384. For logistic regression with six independent variables and a minimum prevalence of 10%, the sample size was calculated to be 600 using the rule of 10 events per variable. We chose the larger sample size of 600 to ensure adequate power for both analyses.

Research data were collected through an interview with one of the researchers. The researchers collected demographic information of donors and asked open-ended questions about factors that encourage or prevent them from donating blood. In addition, the donors were interviewed regarding the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on their motivations or obstacles, and any recommendations they had for increasing blood donation were recorded. The research team specifically developed these questions for the study (Supplement 1). Five researchers in the fields of public health, medicine, and epidemiology and two blood transfusion specialists approved the validity of the questions used in the interview. Furthermore, to examine the reliability, 30 individuals were initially subjected to interviews, with the same participants reinterviewed after a three-month interval. The results demonstrated a notably high level of agreement between the two interviews. Additionally, a code system was developed collaboratively to categorize responses, which underwent pilot testing for refinement (Supplement 2). Two independent coders applied the predefined codes to each response, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Inter-coder reliability was assessed to ensure consistency. We used a two-sample proportion test to compare the prevalence of subgroups and logistic regression to evaluate potential relationships. The results were reported with a Standard Deviation (SD) or Standard Error (SE). We performed statistical analysis using Stata version 16 (StataCorp, 2019). The level of significance was P < 0.05.

Results

The total sample size comprised 675 blood donors. The mean age of donors was 41.6 ± 9.57 years. The majority of participants were male (95.3%), and 81.8% were married. Furthermore, 37.2% of participants had a university degree, 40% had a high school education, and 22.8% had a lower education. Only 11% of participants were first donors. Table 1 presents more detailed information about the demographic characteristics of participants. Among the repeated donors, 70.4% had received an invitation to donate by Short Message Service (SMS) within seven days before donation.

The most frequent motive for donation was altruism, with 60.6%, which was significantly more prevalent than other motives. The second most prevalent motive was well-being sensation after donation, with 24.1%, followed by believing to have a high Hemoglobin level (16.9%), accompanying a donor friend (3%), and blood screening tests (0.4%)(Fig. 1). All of these proportions were significantly different from each other (P-value < 0.05). Altruism was significantly higher in repeated donors and participants older than 40 years. In addition, the motive of well-being sensation was lower in donors with university degrees, while the belief of having high hemoglobin levels was significantly more prevalent among first-time donors and those below the age of 40. Accompanying and blood testing motives were both more frequent in first-time donors. The most prevalent deterrent of the donation was lack of time (38.4%), distance to blood donation center (6.5%), general health concerns (5.3%), fear of needles (4%), concerns about anemia (1.9%), fear of adverse reactions (1.3%), and fear of COVID-19 infection (1.2%), respectively(Fig. 1). The statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between distance concerns and general health concerns, as well as between general health concerns and needle fear. Additionally, the differences observed between anemia concerns, reaction concerns, and COVID-19 infection were statistically insignificant. As shown in Table 3, the logistic regression analysis indicated that time concern was significantly higher among married donors, repeated donors, and those below the age of 40. In addition, concern about distance was more prevalent in lower levels of education (below high school), while health concern was higher in ages over 40. Furthermore, fear of COVID-19 infection was more frequent in single donors and those over the age of 40.

Among the participants, 43.5% (N = 294) raised suggestions to increase blood donation. Within this group, 35.7% specifically suggested increasing knowledge, 17.7% suggested establishing more donation centers, 16.7% suggested performing routine blood tests, 13.6% suggested more frequent SMS invitations, 13.3% suggested more working hours in donation centers, and 4.5% suggested using a more convenient needle for donors.

Approximately 16.6% of donors believed the pandemic experience had made them more eager to donate blood. On the contrary, 10.2% of participants reported more reluctance to donate blood after the COVID-19 pandemic. Among the first group, 58.9% reported that they acquired more awareness about the blood transfusion system and the importance of blood donation during the pandemic. In addition, 27.7% reported their increased intention to altruistic activities, and 13.4% reported an enhancement in well-being motive for donation. Among the second group who reported decreased eagerness to donate blood after the pandemic, 53.6% expressed concerns about being infected with COVID-19 or other respiratory infections, and 46.4% believed that frequent blood donation may decrease the immunity of COVID-19 vaccines.

A logistic regression model showed no significant relationship between more intention to blood donation following the pandemic and sex, age over 40, marriage, education level, and being a first or repeated donor. On the other hand, the regression with the same independent variables revealed a significant relationship between people older than 40 years and those who are more reluctant to donate blood after the pandemic. Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the motives and deterrents of blood donation from the perspective of Iranian donors in the post-COVID era. Our findings, based on a sample size of 675 blood donors, revealed several important insights.

The mean age of the donors in our sample was 41.6 ± 9.57 years, indicating a relatively mature donor population. The majority of participants were males (95.3%), reflecting a gender imbalance in blood donation. Furthermore, a large proportion of donors were married (81.8%). These demographic characteristics align with previous pre-COVID studies in Iran that have shown a higher prevalence of male and married donors in blood donation [14, 15].

Altruism emerged as the most frequent motive for blood donation, with 60.6% of participants indicating it as their primary motivation. This finding underscores the selfless nature of blood donors in Iran, emphasizing their willingness to contribute to the well-being of others. Among the other motives, well-being sensation after donation (24.1%) and believed to have high hemoglobin levels (16.9%) were the second and third most prevalent motives, respectively. Interestingly, altruism was significantly higher among repeated donors and participants older than 40 years, suggesting a stronger sense of social responsibility and commitment to donation in these groups. On the other hand, the motive of well-being sensation was lower among donors with a university degree, while the belief in having high hemoglobin levels was more prevalent among first-time donors and those below the age of 40. These findings may be related to a local belief about the occurrence of “blood viscosity” in muscular young individuals and its relief after blood donation. Accompanying a donor friend and blood screening tests were less common motives, with minimal representation in the sample.

Lack of time was identified as the most prevalent deterrent to blood donation, reported by 38.4% of participants. Other deterrents included distance to the blood donation center (6.5%), health concerns (5.3%), fear of needles (4%), concerns about anemia (1.9%), fear of adverse reactions (1.3%), and fear of COVID-19 infection (1.2%). Our analysis revealed that the time concern was significantly higher among married donors, repeated donors, and those below the age of 40. These findings may be due to more time occupation in these groups. Concerns about distance were more prevalent among individuals with lower levels of education (below high school). There is a possibility that there was a coincidence between education and living in suburban areas in our sample. In addition, health concerns were higher among participants over the age of 40, while fear of COVID-19 infection was more frequent in single donors and those over the age of 40.

When interpreting our findings, it is important to consider blood donation characteristics in this region and in Iran before the COVID-19 pandemic. Between 2003 and 2017, a total of 27,442,124 blood donations were collected in Iran, and the donation rate increased from 22 to 26 per 1,000 individuals. The northern provinces, where the present study was conducted, were among the regions with donation rates higher than the national average, indicating a strong pre-pandemic donation culture [16]. Previous studies from this area identified empathy and altruism as major motivational drivers, followed by beliefs related to health maintenance, health screening, and religious factors [17]. In a large national study, the most common motivations were altruism (39.1%), perceived health benefits (38.6%), and religious beliefs (11.4%) [15]. Consistent with these findings, altruism remained the dominant motivation in our post-COVID sample. However, there is limited published data on pre-COVID deterrents to blood donation in our study area, making direct comparisons difficult. Nevertheless, the presence of barriers such as health concerns and fear of infection in our results suggests that the pandemic may have introduced or intensified new obstacles to blood donation.

The COVID-19 pandemic had both positive and negative effects on blood donation based on our findings. In our study, 16.6% of donors reported an increased eagerness to donate blood due to their pandemic experience. Factors such as increased awareness about the blood transfusion system, the importance of blood donation, and an enhanced sense of altruism contributed to this increased intention. However, 10.2% of participants reported a decreased eagerness to donate blood following the pandemic. Concerns about being infected with COVID-19 or other respiratory infections, as well as the belief that frequent blood donation may decrease the efficacy of COVID-19 vaccines, were the primary reasons for this reluctance.

A notable proportion (43.5%) of participants provided suggestions to enhance blood donation. Increasing knowledge about blood donation, establishing more donation centers, performing routine blood tests, sending more frequent SMS invitations, extending working hours at donation centers, and using more convenient needles for donors were among the suggested improvements.

Based on the findings of this study, several targeted strategies can be proposed to strengthen blood donation rates in the post-COVID era. Educational interventions emphasizing the lifesaving role of blood donation may further reinforce altruistic motivation, while workplace-based or mobile donation programs and extended operating hours may reduce time-related barriers. Increasing the number and geographical distribution of donation centers could address accessibility concerns. Providing transparent information about donor safety and infection control measures may help alleviate health-related fears. Together, these strategies can support blood donation organizations in designing more effective, context-specific donor recruitment and retention programs.

It is important to acknowledge that this study had certain limitations. The sample was limited to a group of Iranian blood donors, which may affect the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Additionally, blood donors’ opinions about deterrents of donation may not be applicable to evaluate obstacles to blood donation in the general population because such deterrents may be stronger from the non-donors’ point of view. Furthermore, the study relied on self-reported data, which may introduce response biases.

Despite these limitations, the findings provide valuable insights into the motives and deterrents of blood donation in the post-COVID era. The results emphasize the significance of altruism as the primary motivator for blood donation and highlight the importance of addressing practical barriers such as time constraints and distance to donation centers. These findings can inform targeted interventions and strategies to encourage and facilitate blood donation, ultimately contributing to the sustainability of the blood supply. Further research is warranted to explore these factors in more detail and develop evidence-based interventions to address the identified challenges.

Conclusions

This study highlighted the significance of altruism as the primary motive for blood donation among Iranian donors in the post-COVID era. Practical barriers such as time constraints and distance to donation centers were identified as significant deterrents. The study further indicated the impact of the pandemic on donation attitudes, with increased awareness and concerns about infection shaping donor behavior. Further research and suitable strategies are necessary to optimize blood donation practices in this context.
