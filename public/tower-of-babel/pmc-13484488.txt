A machine learning framework with a public synthetic data set for early student profiling and outcome prediction
Authors: Sanjay Agal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The accurate and early prediction of student academic outcomes is fundamental to enabling timely interventions and personalized support in higher education. However, progress in educational data mining is severely constrained by the scarcity of publicly available data sets, as student records are protected by stringent privacy regulations that prohibit their distribution. This paper introduces a comprehensive machine learning framework for early student profiling and outcome prediction, underpinned by a novel public synthetic data set of over 100,000 student records designed specifically for machine learning research in educational contexts. The data set encompasses 28 attributes spanning demographic characteristics, academic background, entrance examination scores, socio economic indicators, and behavioral metrics, incorporating realistic complexities including non linear relationships, systematically introduced missing value patterns, and controlled class imbalance. Two complementary prediction tasks are defined: ordinal classification of student academic level into Beginner, Intermediate, Advanced, and Exceptional categories, and nominal classification of division allotment into Remedial, Regular, Advanced, and Honors divisions. A modular machine learning framework is developed encompassing comprehensive preprocessing, feature engineering including composite indices and interaction terms, implementation of eleven distinct algorithms ranging from interpretable baselines to ensemble methods and neural networks, rigorous cross validation, and multi faceted evaluation incorporating accuracy, per class metrics, macro averaged F1 scores, and ordinal specific measures. Experimental results demonstrate that ensemble methods substantially outperform simpler approaches, with LightGBM achieving macro F1 scores of 0.842 for student level prediction and 0.826 for division allotment. An ordinal neural network incorporating the ordinal nature of the student level target achieves the highest overall performance with macro F1 score of 0.846 and quadratic weighted kappa of 0.892, confirming the value of explicit ordinal modeling. Feature importance analysis reveals that prior academic achievement, particularly class 12 percentage, dominates predictions, while socio economic and behavioral factors provide meaningful secondary contributions. SHAP analysis uncovers important interaction effects, including the moderating role of prior achievement on the impact of entrance examination scores, and provides local explanations that enable targeted intervention design. Cross validation confirms stability with standard deviations below 0.011 across folds, and computational efficiency analysis identifies LightGBM as offering optimal balance between predictive performance and resource requirements. The complete framework, including all preprocessing modules, model implementations, evaluation protocols, and interpretation tools, is released as open source software alongside the validated synthetic data set, providing the research community with foundational resources to advance reproducible educational data mining research without privacy constraints. This work establishes a comprehensive benchmark for student outcome prediction and provides actionable insights for developing equitable, data driven student support systems.

Introduction

The landscape of higher education is undergoing a profound transformation, driven by the increasing digitization of learning environments and the burgeoning field of learning analytics. Institutions now have an unprecedented capacity to collect large amounts of data on student engagement, performance, and demographics. This data deluge presents a pivotal opportunity: to harness computational methods to understand the multifaceted factors that contribute to student success and, more importantly, to predict and mitigate the risks of academic failure1. Early prediction of student outcomes such as course grades, dropout risk, and overall academic achievement is not merely an academic exercise; it is a practical imperative with the potential to enable proactive personalized interventions that can significantly improve retention rates and student well being2,3.

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful toolkit in this endeavor, offering sophisticated means to model the complex, non linear relationships inherent in educational data. During the past decade, a substantial body of research has demonstrated the efficacy of various ML algorithms, from traditional decision trees and regression models to advanced ensemble methods and deep learning architectures, in predicting student performance4,5. Studies have successfully used data from Virtual Learning Environments (VLEs)6,7, demographic information, entrance examination scores, and historical academic records to build predictive models with promising precision8,9. The ultimate goal is to create early warning systems that can identify students at risk long before traditional assessments signal a problem, allowing educators and support staff to implement timely and effective interventions10,11.

Despite these significant advances, the field faces a persistent and critical bottleneck: the availability of high quality, publicly accessible data sets. Student data is inherently sensitive, governed by strict privacy regulations such as FERPA in the United States and GDPR in Europe. Consequently, most research is conducted on private institutional data sets that cannot be shared. This practice leads to several profound limitations. First, it severely hampers the reproducibility of research findings, a cornerstone of the scientific method. Second, it impedes the benchmarking and fair comparison of different algorithms, as each model is evaluated on a unique, non-comparative data context12. Third, it creates a high barrier to entry for new researchers and institutions with limited resources, slowing the overall pace of innovation13,14. The need for standardized, realistic and privacy preserving benchmark data sets has never been more urgent15.

The university Intake synthetic data set

In response to this critical gap, we introduce the University Intake Synthetic data set, a comprehensive, large scale resource comprising over 100,000 student records designed specifically for machine learning research in higher education. The data set is meticulously constructed using probabilistic models and real world correlation patterns, capturing the multifaceted nature of student profiles at the point of university admission. Problem statement

The core problem addressed in this research is the dual challenge of data scarcity and methodological reproducibility in the domain of educational data mining for the prediction of student success. Current predictive models, while sophisticated, are often siloed and non transferable due to the proprietary nature of the data on which they are trained19. The field lacks a standardized, realistic and publicly available benchmark data set that can serve as a common ground for developing, testing, and comparing novel machine learning frameworks. Furthermore, the development of such a resource must navigate the ethical and legal complexities of student data, ensuring that no personally identifiable information is compromised20. This scarcity of open data directly inhibits the development of robust, generalizable models that could be reliably deployed in diverse educational contexts, ultimately limiting the real world impact of years of methodological research21,22. The challenge, therefore, is not only to build a better predictor, but to create the foundational resources, both data and a framework that allow the entire research community to collaboratively advance the state of the art. Specifically, there is a pressing need for: (1) a privacy preserving synthetic data set that captures the complex multivariate dependencies of real student populations, (2) a comprehensive machine learning framework that can be consistently applied across studies, and (3) transparent benchmarking results that establish baselines for future research23,24.

Literature review

The intersection of machine learning and education has emerged as a vibrant field of inquiry, driven by the dual promises of enhancing pedagogical effectiveness and improving student outcomes. This section provides a comprehensive review of the literature, tracing the evolution of educational data mining and learning analytics, examining the machine learning methodologies employed for student performance prediction, exploring the role of synthetic data in addressing privacy concerns, and evaluating existing public data sets. Through this review, we identify critical research gaps that motivate the present investigation.

Evolution of educational data mining and learning analytics

The digitization of educational environments has generated unprecedented volumes of data, giving rise to two interrelated disciplines: Educational Data Mining (EDM) and Learning Analytics (LA). Although EDM focuses on the development of computational methods to analyze educational data, LA emphasizes the measurement, collection, and analysis of learner data to optimize learning environments22. During the past decade, these fields have matured significantly, transitioning from descriptive analysis of student populations to predictive modeling that enables proactive interventions21.

Early work in this domain utilized primarily traditional statistical methods to identify factors associated with academic success. However, the increasing complexity and volume of educational data required more sophisticated analytical approaches. Researchers began exploring data mining techniques such as decision trees, association rule mining, and clustering to uncover hidden patterns in student behavior and performance1. The evolution from simple correlation analyzes to complex predictive models reflects a broader shift towards data driven decision making in higher education institutions worldwide4.

A significant milestone in this evolution was the integration of learning analytics into institutional early warning systems. These systems aim to identify students at risk of academic failure or dropout, enabling timely interventions2. The effectiveness of such systems depends critically on the accuracy and timeliness of the underlying predictive models, which has driven substantial research into algorithm development and feature engineering11.

Machine learning for student performance prediction

The prediction of student academic outcomes has become a central focus of EDM research, with applications ranging from grade prediction to dropout prevention. This subsection reviews the methodological landscape, highlighting key algorithms, feature types, and evaluation approaches.

Predictive algorithms and their applications

A diverse array of machine learning algorithms have been applied to predict student performance, each offering different advantages depending on the characteristics of the data and the prediction task. Traditional algorithms such as logistic regression and decision trees provide interpretable baselines and remain widely used due to their transparency and ease of implementation5. These models are particularly valuable in educational contexts where understanding the factors driving the predictions is as important as the predictions themselves.

Ensemble methods, including random forests and gradient boosting machines, have demonstrated superior predictive performance by combining multiple weak learners to capture complex patterns in educational data26. Studies by Mahawar et al. (2025)9 showed that ensemble approaches, particularly when combined with optimization techniques, can achieve significant improvements in the accuracy of early prediction. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2025)12 reported that gradient boosting consistently outperformed single classifiers in predicting student performance in online programming courses.

The advent of deep learning has introduced new possibilities for modeling sequential and high dimensional educational data. Neural networks, particularly recurrent architectures, have been successfully applied to predict student outcomes from clickstream data in virtual learning environments6. Xu et al. (2021)7 demonstrated that deep learning models could effectively capture temporal patterns in student engagement, leading to more accurate predictions of academic performance. However, the opacity of deep learning models raises interpretability concerns that are particularly salient in educational settings29.

Support vector machines (SVMs) have also found applications in the prediction of student performance, particularly for binary classification tasks such as identification of risk25. The ability of SVMs to handle high dimensional feature spaces makes them suitable for data sets incorporating numerous demographic, academic, and behavioral variables.

Feature engineering and selection

The quality of predictive models depends not only on the choice of algorithm, but also on the features used to represent students. Early investigations relied primarily on demographic and prior academic achievement data, such as age, gender, and previous grades8. However, contemporary research has expanded the feature space to include more nuanced indicators of student engagement and learning behaviors.

The data on the interaction of the virtual learning environment (VLE) has emerged as a powerful predictor of student outcomes. Metrics such as login frequency, time spent on learning materials, and participation in discussion forums provide real time indicators of student engagement that complement static demographic features16. Fahd et al. (2021)17 demonstrated that the features derived from the VLE clickstream data significantly improved the precision of the early warning systems in blended learning environments.

Socioeconomic and contextual factors have also received increasing attention. Variables such as family income, parental education, and access to learning resources capture structural inequalities that influence educational trajectories19. The inclusion of such features enables not only more accurate predictions, but also the identification of systemic barriers to student success.

Feature selection techniques have been used to identify the most informative predictors while reducing dimensionality and improving model interpretability. Adnan et al. (2022)10 compared multiple feature selection methods and found that recursive feature elimination combined with random forest classifiers yielded optimal performance for early identification of students at risk.

Early prediction and temporal dynamics

A critical requirement for effective intervention is the ability to predict student outcomes early in the academic term. Research has therefore focused on developing models that can generate reliable predictions using limited initial data. Baneres et al. (2019)3 proposed an early feedback prediction system that achieved reasonable accuracy after only four weeks of a first year course. Similarly, Howard et al. (2018)28 compared prediction methods for early warning systems at the undergraduate level, finding that models incorporating demographic and early engagement data outperformed those based solely on demographics.

The temporal dynamics of student engagement pose challenges and opportunities for early prediction. Engagement patterns evolve over time, and models must adapt to changing behaviors. Longitudinal analyzes, such as that conducted by Saqr et al. (2021)32, have revealed different trajectories of online engagement that correlate with academic outcomes, suggesting the potential for personalized interventions based on engagement patterns.

Synthetic data generation in educational contexts

The development of predictive models in education is constrained by the sensitive nature of student data. Privacy regulations, including the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) in the United States and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in Europe, restrict the sharing of student records, limiting the availability of public data sets for research20. Synthetic data generation has emerged as a promising approach to address this challenge.

Motivation for synthetic educational data

The primary motivation for the generation of synthetic data in education is to create realistic data sets that preserve the statistical properties of real student populations while eliminating privacy concerns. Synthetic data enables researchers to develop and benchmark algorithms without accessing sensitive information, democratizing access to educational data mining research13. In addition, synthetic data can be generated with specific characteristics, such as controlled levels of class imbalance or missing data that facilitate the systematic evaluation of methodological innovations14.

Methods for generating synthetic student data

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) have emerged as a powerful tool for creating synthetic data across various domains, including education. Farhood et al. (2024)18 demonstrated the application of GANs to generate realistic student performance data, showing that synthetic data could effectively replicate the complex multivariate relationships observed in real educational data sets. The authors evaluated the utility of synthetic data by comparing model performance on real and synthetic training sets, finding comparable predictive accuracy.

Alternative approaches include probabilistic graphical models and agent based simulations. These methods leverage domain knowledge to specify relationships between variables, generating synthetic populations that reflect theoretical understanding of educational processes. Hernandez et al. (2022)33 reviewed state of the art methods for learning analytics, highlighting the potential of hybrid approaches that combine data driven generation with expert informed constraints.

Evaluating fidelity and utility

The utility of synthetic data depends on its fidelity to real world populations. Evaluation typically involves comparing univariate distributions, correlation structures, and the performance of predictive models trained on synthetic versus real data. Cagliero et al. (2023)27 proposed a framework for evaluating synthetic educational data that includes both statistical similarity measures and task based utility assessment. The latter approach, which examines whether models trained on synthetic data achieve comparable performance when tested on real data, provides a direct measure of practical utility.

Existing public data sets and benchmarks

Despite the proliferation of research in educational data mining, the availability of public data sets remains limited. Some notable exceptions have contributed significantly to the field. The Open University Learning Analytics data set (OULAD) provides student demographics, clickstream data, and assessment results for approximately 32,000 students, supporting research on dropout prediction and performance modeling31. The Student Performance Data Set from the UCI Machine Learning Repository, which contains demographic and academic data for secondary school students, has been widely used to benchmark classification algorithms1.

However, existing data sets exhibit several limitations. First, they often lack the richness of features available in institutional data, omitting important socio economic and behavioral indicators. Second, sample sizes are often insufficient for training complex models, particularly deep learning architectures that require large data sets. Third, privacy concerns limit the inclusion of sensitive variables that may be important predictors of student success. Finally, the heterogeneity of the data formats and the definition of characteristics in the data sets impedes cross study comparisons and the development of generalizable insights15.

Research gap and contribution of this investigation

The literature review reveals a critical gap: the absence of a large scale, publicly available synthetic data set that comprehensively captures the multifaceted nature of student profiles at university admission. Existing data sets, while valuable, do not provide the combination of demographic breadth, academic depth, socio economic contextualization, and behavioral indicators necessary for developing and benchmarking advanced machine learning frameworks. Furthermore, no existing resource explicitly addresses the two interconnected tasks of student level profiling and academic division allotment, which reflect real world decision processes in higher education.

The University Intake Synthetic data set introduced in this investigation addresses these gaps through several innovations. First, with over 100,000 records, it provides the scale necessary for training complex models and conducting robust statistical analyses. Second, its comprehensive feature set encompassing 28 variables across demographic, academic, entrance assessment, socio economic, and behavioral domains enables nuanced modeling of student success determinants. Third, the inclusion of realistic complexities such as non linear relationships, missing value patterns, and class imbalance ensures that models developed on this data set will generalize to real world challenges. Fourth, the two target variables, predicted_student_level and division_allotted, provide complementary perspectives on student outcomes, supporting both profiling and placement applications.

The machine learning framework developed alongside the data set contributes a standardized methodology for benchmarking predictive models in educational contexts. By providing open source implementations of the full pipeline from data preprocessing to model evaluation and interpretation this work lowers barriers to entry for new researchers and enables fair comparisons across future studies. The modular design of the framework allows researchers to easily incorporate new algorithms, feature engineering techniques, or evaluation metrics, facilitating continuous methodological advancement.

In summary, this investigation bridges the gap between the demand for privacy preserving educational data and the need for comprehensive benchmark resources. By making both the data set and the accompanying framework publicly available, we aim to catalyze research in early student profile and outcome prediction, ultimately contributing to more effective and equitable educational practices.

The synthetic data generation process used probabilistic models informed by realworld correlation patterns observed in educational literature. Key relationships, such as the positive correlation between socioeconomic status and academic performance19, and the influence of previous achievement on entrance exam scores8, were encoded in the generation algorithm. Non linear dependencies were introduced using polynomial transformations and interaction terms, while missing values were injected following Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) and Missing At Random (MAR) mechanisms to mirror real world data imperfections18. Class imbalance was optionally introduced in the target variables to reflect the skewed distributions often encountered in practice, where, for example, “Exceptional” students may constitute a small minority26.

To rigorously justify how closely the synthetic data mimics real world educational records, we conducted a multifaceted fidelity assessment. First, univariate distributions of key academic variables (e.g., percentages of class 10 and class 12) were compared against published national education statistics, showing close alignment in means (72.4% vs. 71.8% reported) and standard deviations (12.3 vs. 11.9). Second, the correlation structure between prior achievement, entrance scores, and socioeconomic indicators was validated against findings from large scale observational studies (e.g., the correlation between class 12 percentage and entrance exam score was 0.58, consistent with the 0.55–0.65 range reported in the literature). Third, a utility based evaluation was performed: a logistic regression model trained on our synthetic data achieved predictive performance (AUC = 0.79) comparable to the same model trained on a real institutional data set of similar size (AUC = 0.81) when tested on real data, indicating high practical utility. These validation steps confirm that the synthetic data set preserves the essential statistical properties and analytical challenges of real student populations while containing no actual personally identifiable information.

Data preprocessing

Raw data often contains inconsistencies, missing values, and features measured on different scales that can adversely affect the performance of machine learning models. The preprocessing stage addresses these issues through a systematic sequence of operations.

Handling missing values

The synthetic data set includes missing values in several variables, generated under MCAR and MAR patterns. For numerical variables (e.g., math_score, aptitude_score), missing values are imputed using median imputation, which is robust to outliers. For categorical variables (e.g., stream, parent_education_level), mode imputation is applied. To preserve the information inherent in missingness patterns, binary indicator variables are created for features with substantial missing rates, allowing models to learn potential correlations between missingness and outcomes1. Alternative imputation methods, such as k nearest neighbors or multiple imputation, were considered but deemed unnecessary given the controlled missing data mechanisms and the large sample size5.

Encoding categorical variables

The data set contains several categorical variables with varying numbers of levels. Nominal variables (e.g., gender, category, state) are encoded using one hot encoding, creating binary indicator columns for each category. For variables with high cardinality (e.g., state with over 30 categories), frequency encoding is applied as an alternative to avoid excessive dimensionality12. Ordinal categorical variables (e.g., family_income_range with levels Low, Lower Middle, Upper Middle, High) are encoded using integer mapping that preserves the natural order, which is particularly important for tree based models that can leverage ordinal information16.

Feature scaling

Machine learning algorithms that rely on distance calculations or gradient based optimization are sensitive to the scale of input features. Continuous numerical variables, including percentages, scores, and indices, are standardized using z score normalization, transforming them to have zero mean and unit variance. This scaling is applied after splitting the train test to prevent data leakage, with the mean and standard deviation calculated exclusively from the training set10. Tree based algorithms, which are invariant to monotonic transformations, are trained on unscaled data to preserve interpretability.

Feature engineering

Beyond the raw attributes provided in the data set, additional features are engineered to capture complex interactions and domain specific constructs that may enhance predictive performance.

Composite academic indices

Drawing on educational research that emphasizes the multifaceted nature of academic ability, we construct composite indices that aggregate related variables. The academic_performance_index is computed as a weighted average of class_10_percentage, class_12_percentage, and subject specific scores, with weights derived from factor analysis of the training data. Similarly, an aptitude_composite is created by averaging logical_reasoning_score and language_proficiency_score, reflecting the common practice of combining cognitive subtests4. These composites reduce dimensionality while preserving information and often exhibit stronger predictive power than individual components due to reduced measurement error17.

Interaction terms

To capture synergistic effects between variables, interaction terms are generated for pairs of features identified as theoretically important. For example, the interaction between internet_access and study_resources_index may capture the amplified benefit of resources when combined with connectivity. Similarly, the product of motivation_score and attendance_likelihood reflects the combined influence of motivation and engagement on academic success7. These interaction terms are created selectively to avoid an explosion of dimensionality, guided by prior literature and exploratory analysis.

Behavioral risk indicators

Drawing on research into early warning systems2, we construct risk indicators that flag potentially concerning patterns. For instance, a binary flag low_engagement_risk is set to 1 if self_learning_index falls below the 25th percentile and attendance_likelihood is simultaneously low. Such flags encapsulate domain knowledge and provide interpretable signals for intervention systems.

Machine learning models

A diverse portfolio of machine learning algorithms is selected to comprehensively benchmark predictive performance on the data set. The selection encompasses models with varying levels of complexity, interpretability, and inductive bias, allowing a thorough evaluation of their suitability for student outcome prediction tasks.

Baseline models

Logistic regression serves as the primary baseline due to its simplicity, interpretability, and established performance in educational data mining25. For multiclass classification, we employ the one vs rest (OvR) strategy. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) is also included as a generative baseline that assumes Gaussian class conditional distributions, providing a contrast to discriminative approaches. In this work, “baseline models” refer to simpler, often linear, or low complexity algorithms that provide a performance floor against which more sophisticated methods can be compared. Their practical significance lies in benchmarking: if an advanced model cannot substantially outperform a simple baseline on a given task, the additional complexity may not be justified.

Tree based models

Decision trees (CART) offer inherent interpretability and are included to provide a baseline for rule based decision making. Random forests34 aggregate multiple trees to improve accuracy and robustness, and have been successfully applied in numerous educational studies9. Gradient boosting machines (GBM), specifically XGBoost35 and LightGBM36, represent state of the art ensemble methods that have consistently achieved top performance in predictive modeling competitions and educational applications26.

Support vector machines

Support vector machines (SVM) with radial basis function (RBF) kernels are included to capture non linear decision boundaries. SVMs have shown strong performance in high dimensional spaces and are particularly effective when the number of features is moderate relative to sample size27. However, their computational cost and sensitivity to hyperparameters necessitate careful tuning.

Neural networks

A multilayer perceptron (MLP) with two hidden layers is implemented to represent deep learning approaches. The architecture consists of 128 and 64 neurons in the first and second hidden layers, respectively, with ReLU activation and dropout regularization (rate 0.3) to prevent overfitting. Although deeper architectures were considered, the relatively modest set of features (after encoding) does not warrant excessive complexity, and the chosen architecture balances capacity with generalization6.

Experimental setup

Rigorous experimental design is essential for obtaining reliable and generalizable performance estimates. This subsection describes the train test split, cross validation strategy, hyperparameter optimization, and handling of class imbalance.

Train test split and cross validation

The data set is randomly partitioned into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets, stratified by the target variables to preserve class distributions. The validation set is used for hyperparameter tuning, while the test set is held out until final evaluation to provide an unbiased estimate of generalization performance. To further assess stability, 5 fold cross validation is performed on the training set during model development, with results averaged across folds38.

Hyperparameter optimization

Each algorithm exposes hyperparameters that control model complexity and behavior. For logistic regression, the regularization strength C is tuned over a logarithmic grid. For random forests, the number of trees (100–500), maximum depth (5–20), and minimum samples split (2–10) are optimized via random search with 50 iterations. XGBoost and LightGBM hyperparameters, including learning rate, number of estimators, max depth, and subsample ratio, are similarly tuned. SVM hyperparameters (C and γ) are optimized using grid search with a logarithmic scale. The MLP is trained using Adam optimization with learning rate tuned over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[10^{ 4}, 10^{ 2}]$$\end{document} and batch size selected from {32, 64, 128}. All hyperparameter tuning is conducted exclusively on the validation set to prevent test set leakage.

Handling class imbalance

The target variables may exhibit class imbalance, particularly for extreme categories like “Exceptional” or “Honors”. To mitigate potential bias toward majority classes, we employ two strategies. First, during training, class weights are computed inversely proportional to class frequencies and incorporated into the loss functions of algorithms that support weighting (e.g., logistic regression, SVM, neural networks). Second, for tree based models, we experiment with balanced subsampling, where each bootstrap sample is drawn to maintain balanced class distributions39. Synthetic Minority Over sampling (SMOTE) is considered but applied cautiously to avoid generating unrealistic synthetic instances within an already synthetic data set40.

Evaluation metrics

Appropriate evaluation metrics are crucial for assessing model performance and enabling fair comparisons. Given the multi class nature of both prediction tasks, we employ a suite of metrics that capture different aspects of classification quality.

Overall performance metrics

Overall accuracy provides an intuitive measure of correct predictions, but it can be misleading under class imbalance. Therefore, we also report macro averaged precision, recall, and F1 score, which treat all classes equally regardless of size. Weighted averages, which account for class support, are additionally computed to reflect performance on the actual data distribution41.

Per class metrics

Confusion matrices are generated to visualize misclassification patterns, revealing which classes are frequently confused. For ordinal targets, we also compute the mean absolute error (MAE) in terms of class ranks, which penalizes predictions that are far from the true ordinal position more heavily42. The quadratic weighted kappa is employed as an additional ordinal metric that measures agreement between the predicted and true ordinal categories.

Model interpretation metrics

Beyond predictive accuracy, we assess the interpretability of the model using feature importance scores. For tree based models, impurity based feature importance is extracted. For all models, the importance of permutation is computed by shuffling each feature and measuring the drop in performance, providing a model agnostic measure of feature influence43. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values are calculated for a subset of models to allow local and global interpretability, facilitating understanding of how individual features contribute to predictions for specific student profiles44.

Ethical considerations

Although the data set is fully synthetic and contains no real student information, we adhere to ethical principles for educational data mining research29. The synthetic data generation process was designed to avoid reproducing biases that could perpetuate educational inequalities; however, we acknowledge that any biases present in the underlying generation models may propagate. Therefore, we commit to transparent reporting of model performance across demographic subgroups and encourage users of the data set and framework to similarly examine fairness implications. The public release of the data set and code is accompanied by documentation emphasizing responsible use and the limitations of synthetic data.

Fairness analysis across demographic groups

To assess potential algorithmic bias, we evaluated the top performing ordinal neural network across sensitive demographic attributes: gender (male/female), social category (General/OBC/SC/ST/EWS), and urban/rural background. For student level prediction, macro F1 scores were comparable across groups: female (0.845), male (0.847); urban (0.846), rural (0.844); and social categories ranged from 0.840 (SC) to 0.849 (General). The maximum difference in F1 score between any two groups was 0.012, indicating that the model does not exhibit substantial performance disparities. However, we emphasize that fairness is not solely about equal performance; future work should also examine calibration and error patterns across groups.

Feature engineering framework

Beyond the raw attributes provided in the data set, an extensive feature engineering process is undertaken to construct derived variables that capture complex relationships and domain specific constructs relevant to student success prediction. This process is guided by educational research literature and exploratory data analysis conducted on the training portion of the data set.

Composite academic indices are constructed to aggregate related achievement measures into unified representations of student capability. The academic performance index combines the percentage of the 10th class, the percentage of the 12th class, the mathematics score, the science score and the English score through a weighted averaging scheme, with weights derived from the analysis of principal components applied to the training data. This composite measure captures overall academic achievement while reducing dimensionality and mitigating multicollinearity among highly correlated subject specific scores. Similarly, a composite of aptitude is created by averaging logical reasoning scores and language proficiency scores, reflecting the common practice in educational assessment of combining multiple cognitive measures into a single aptitude indicator.

Interaction terms are generated for pairs of features identified by theoretical reasoning and preliminary analysis as potentially exhibiting synergistic effects on student outcomes. The interaction between internet access and the study resources index captures the amplified benefit of educational resources when combined with connectivity, reflecting the considerations of the digital divide prevalent in educational equity research. The product of motivation score and attendance probability represents the combined influence of psychological participation and behavioral participation on academic success. Additional interaction terms are created for academic performance with socioeconomic indicators to model how the relationship between prior achievement and future success can vary between different contextual backgrounds.

Behavioral risk indicators are constructed to identify potentially concerning patterns that may warrant early intervention. A low engagement risk flag is set to one when the self learning index falls below the twenty fifth percentile and attendance likelihood simultaneously falls below the median value, representing students who exhibit both low independent learning capability and predicted poor attendance. An academic risk flag is created for students whose class 12 percentage falls substantially below their class 10 percentage, indicating a potential performance decline that may signal underlying challenges. These risk indicators encapsulate domain knowledge about early warning signs and provide interpretable signals for intervention systems while potentially improving predictive performance.

Temporal features are derived from variables that implicitly contain temporal information. Gap year duration is transformed into categorical indicators for no gap, one year gap, and multiple years gap, recognizing that the impact of gap years may not be linearly related to academic outcomes. Age at admission is used to create generational cohort indicators, acknowledging that students of different ages may have distinct life circumstances and support needs.

Machine learning model configuration

A diverse portfolio of machine learning algorithms is configured and implemented to comprehensively benchmark predictive performance on the student profiling and division allotment tasks. The selection encompasses models with varying inductive biases, complexity levels, and interpretability characteristics, enabling thorough evaluation of algorithm suitability for educational prediction contexts.

Logistic regression serves as the primary baseline model due to its widespread adoption in educational data mining research and its inherent interpretability. For multi class classification tasks, the one versus rest strategy is employed, wherein a separate binary classifier is trained for each class against all others. Regularization strength is controlled through the inverse regularization parameter C, with values explored across a logarithmic range from 0.001 to 1000. Both L1 and L2 regularization variants are considered, with L1 regularization potentially producing sparse solutions that select informative features.

Linear discriminant analysis is included as a generative baseline that models the class conditional distributions of features under Gaussian assumptions. This approach provides a contrast to discriminative methods and may perform well when the underlying distributional assumptions are approximately maintained. The covariance matrix estimation method is configured to use shrinkage to improve numerical stability and generalization, particularly given the moderate dimensionality of the feature space after encoding.

Decision trees are implemented using the CART algorithm, providing inherently interpretable models that generate rule based decision paths. The maximum tree depth is constrained between three and fifteen levels to balance model complexity against interpretability and overfitting risk. The minimum sample split and minimum sample leaf parameters control the granularity of the splits, with values explored from two to twenty for minimum sample split and from one to ten for minimum sample leaf.

Random forests aggregate multiple decision trees through bootstrap aggregation and random feature selection, providing improved accuracy and robustness compared to single trees. The number of trees in the ensemble is configured between one hundred and fifty, with the optimal value determined through the performance of the validation set. The maximum depth is constrained similarly to individual decision trees, while the number of features considered for each split is set to the square root of the total feature count, following standard practice for classification tasks.

Gradient boosting machines are implemented using both both XGBoost and LightGBM frameworks, representing state of the art ensemble methods that have demonstrated exceptional performance across diverse prediction tasks. XGBoost is configured with learning rate values explored from 0.01 to 0.3, number of estimators from one hundred to one thousand, and maximum depth from three to ten. LightGBM, which employs leaf wise tree growth, is configured with similar hyperparameter ranges and additional parameters controlling the minimum child samples and subsampling ratios to prevent overfitting. Both implementations support native handling of missing values and categorical features, which are leveraged where appropriate.

Support vector machines with radial basis function kernels are included to capture non linear decision boundaries that may exist in the feature space. The regularization parameter C and kernel coefficient gamma are jointly optimized through grid search over a logarithmic grid, with C ranging from 0.1 to 1000 and gamma ranging from 0.0001 to 10. Feature scaling is critical for SVM performance, and all models are trained on standardized features. Given the computational complexity of SVM training on the full data set, stratified sampling is employed during hyperparameter tuning to reduce computational burden while maintaining representative class distributions.

Multi layer perceptron neural networks are implemented to represent deep learning approaches to student outcome prediction. The network architecture comprises two hidden layers with 128 and 64 neurons respectively, with rectified linear unit activation functions applied after each hidden layer. Dropout regularization with a rate of 0.3 is applied after each hidden layer to mitigate overfitting, particularly important given the synthetic nature of the data and the potential for the network to memorize patterns rather than learn generalizable representations. The output layer employs softmax activation for multi class probability estimation. Networks are trained using the Adam optimizer with initial learning rates explored over 0.0001 to 0.01, batch sizes selected from 32, 64, and 128, and training conducted for up to 200 epochs with early stopping based on validation loss.

For ordinal target variable predicted student level, specialized models that explicitly incorporate ordinal information are additionally implemented. Ordinal logistic regression, also known as the proportional odds model, is configured to model cumulative probabilities rather than class probabilities directly. A custom ordinal neural network is implemented using a cumulative link architecture, wherein the output layer produces a single continuous score that is then mapped to ordinal categories through learned thresholds. These ordinal models are expected to outperform standard multi class approaches when the ordinal nature of the target is informative for the prediction task.

Experimental design and validation strategy

Rigorous experimental design is essential to obtain reliable and generalizable performance estimates that can inform both research conclusions and practical applications. The experimental framework incorporates multiple validation strategies, careful data partitioning, and systematic hyperparameter optimization.

The data set is partitioned into three disjoint subsets: the training set comprising seventy percent of the data, the validation set comprising fifteen percent, and the test set comprising fifteen percent. Stratified random sampling is used to preserve the original class distributions of both target variables across all partitions, ensuring that each subset maintains representative proportions of each level of students and division category. The training set is used for model development and parameter estimation, the validation set guides hyperparameter selection and model comparison during development, and the test set is held entirely separate until final model evaluation to provide unbiased estimates of generalization performance.

Five fold cross validation is applied to the training set during model development to assess stability and reduce variance in performance estimates. In each cross validation iteration, four folds are used for training and one fold for validation, with the process repeated five times such that each fold serves as validation exactly once. Results are aggregated across folds to provide mean performance estimates and standard deviations, enabling assessment of model stability and identification of potential overfitting. The cross validation procedure is stratified to maintain class distributions within each fold.

Hyperparameter optimization is conducted using random search for complex models with large hyperparameter spaces and grid search for simpler models with limited parameters. Random search is configured with fifty to one hundred iterations depending on model complexity, with hyperparameter distributions specified to reflect appropriate ranges based on prior literature and preliminary experimentation. For each hyperparameter configuration, model performance is evaluated on the validation set, and the configuration yielding the highest macro averaged F1 score is selected for final evaluation. All hyperparameter tuning is conducted exclusively on the validation set, with the test set remaining untouched until the final evaluation phase to prevent information leakage and optimistic bias.

The handling of class imbalances is addressed through multiple complementary strategies given the potential for unequal class distributions in the target variables. Class weights are computed inversely proportional to class frequencies and incorporated into loss functions of algorithms that support instance weighting, including logistic regression, support vector machines, and neural networks. For tree based ensembles, balanced subsampling is employed during bootstrap aggregation, wherein each bootstrap sample is drawn to maintain balanced class distributions regardless of original class proportions. The synthetic minority over sampling is considered but applied cautiously given the synthetic nature of the base data set, with experiments conducted to assess whether additional synthetic instances improve or degrade model generalization.

Evaluation metrics and statistical analysis

Comprehensive evaluation requires multiple metrics that capture different aspects of classification performance, particularly given the multi class nature of both prediction tasks and the potential for class imbalance. The evaluation framework encompasses overall performance metrics, per class metrics, ordinal specific metrics, and model interpretation measures.

Overall accuracy provides an intuitive measure of correct predictions, but may be misleading under class imbalance. Therefore, macro averaged precision, recall, and F1 score are computed as primary metrics, treating all classes equally regardless of their frequency in the data set. Macro averaging ensures that performance in minority classes contributes equally to the overall assessment, reflecting the practical importance of correctly identifying students at all levels, including exceptional students who may be rare but particularly important to recognize. Weighted averages are additionally computed to reflect performance on the actual data distribution, providing context for expected performance in operational settings.

Confusion matrices are generated for each model to visualize misclassification patterns and identify which classes are frequently confused. For the predicted student level task, confusion matrices reveal whether misclassifications tend to be adjacent in the ordinal scale or reflect more substantial errors. For division allocation, confusion matrices indicate which divisions are most frequently confused and whether errors follow interpretable patterns related to division characteristics.

For the ordinal target variable predicted student level, specialized metrics that incorporate ordinal information are employed. Mean absolute error in terms of class ranks is computed by mapping ordinal categories to integer values from one to four and calculating the average absolute difference between predicted and true ranks. This metric penalizes predictions that are far from the true ordinal position more heavily than adjacent errors, aligning with the practical reality that misclassifying an exceptional student as a beginner is more consequential than misclassifying them as advanced. The quadratic weighted kappa is computed as an additional ordinal agreement measure, representing the degree of agreement between predicted and true ordinal categories with quadratic weighting that emphasizes larger discrepancies.

Model interpretation metrics are computed to provide insights into feature importance and decision processes. For tree based models, impurity based feature importance is extracted based on the reduction in node impurity achieved by splits on each feature. For all models, the importance of permutation is computed by randomly shuffling each feature and measuring the associated drop in performance, providing a model agnostic measure of feature influence that accounts for both main effects and interactions. SHAP values are calculated for a representative subset of models to enable both global interpretation of feature importance and local interpretation of individual predictions, facilitating understanding of how specific feature combinations contribute to predictions for particular student profiles.

Statistical significance testing is conducted to compare model performance and assess whether observed differences are unlikely to occur by chance. For comparisons between two models, paired t tests are applied to cross validation fold results, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. For comparisons involving multiple models, analysis of variance followed by post hoc Tukey tests identifies which specific model pairs exhibit statistically significant differences. Effect sizes are reported alongside p values to provide context for the magnitude of observed differences.

Descriptive analysis and data set validation

Prior to presenting predictive modeling results, we first examine the statistical properties of the University Intake Synthetic data set to validate its fidelity to real world educational data characteristics. This validation addresses the first research objective and establishes confidence in the subsequent modeling results (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Multivariate Pearson correlation matrix among academic performance, subject wise scores, and entrance assessment variables in the University Intake Synthetic data set.

Multivariate Pearson correlation matrix among academic performance, subject wise scores, and entrance assessment variables in the University Intake Synthetic data set.

The distributional characteristics of continuous variables were examined to ensure realistic ranges and shapes. Class 10 and class 12 percentages exhibited approximately normal distributions with means of 72.4 and 68.7 percent respectively, and standard deviations of 12.3 and 14.1 percent. These values align with published statistics from educational boards and university admission reports. Subject specific scores demonstrated expected patterns, with mathematics scores showing slightly higher variability compared to language scores, consistent with the wider range of mathematical aptitude observed in student populations. Entrance examination scores exhibited a mean of 65.2 with standard deviation 15.8, and demonstrated moderate positive correlation with class 12 percentage (r = 0.58), reflecting the expected relationship between prior academic achievement and standardized test performance.

Missing value analysis confirmed that missing data patterns were successfully generated according to the specified mechanisms. Approximately 8.3 percent of records contained at least one missing value, with variable specific missing rates ranging from 2.1 percent for demographic variables to 12.4 percent for family income range. Analysis of missingness patterns confirmed the absence of systematic relationships between missing indicators and target variables that would indicate non ignorable missing data mechanisms.

Class distributions for the target variables were examined to assess balance and inform modeling decisions. For predicted student level, the distribution comprised 28.3 percent Beginner, 31.2 percent Intermediate, 24.8 percent Advanced, and 15.7 percent Exceptional, reflecting a realistic scenario where exceptional students constitute a minority while intermediate students represent the largest group. For division allotted, the distribution showed 22.1 percent Remedial, 29.4 percent Regular, 28.5 percent Advanced, and 20.0 percent Honors, with the regular and advanced divisions representing the majority categories. These distributions exhibit moderate class imbalance that warrants the application of imbalance handling techniques as implemented in the experimental setup.

Feature importance analysis

Understanding which features most strongly influence predictions addresses the interpretability research objective and provides actionable insights for educational practitioners. Feature importance analysis was conducted using multiple complementary methods to ensure robust conclusions (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Feature importance rankings for student level prediction, with academic measures dominating the top positions.

Feature importance rankings for student level prediction, with academic measures dominating the top positions.

For student level prediction, the five most important features across all models were class 12 percentage, entrance examination score, mathematics score, academic performance composite index, and logical reasoning score. Class 12 percentage consistently emerged as the single most predictive feature, with permutation importance approximately 30 percent higher than the second ranked feature across all ensemble models. This finding aligns with educational research emphasizing the strong relationship between prior academic achievement and future success.

Subject specific scores demonstrated differential importance, with mathematics score ranking substantially higher than science score and English score. This pattern may reflect the particular importance of quantitative skills for the academic programs represented in the data set, or may indicate that mathematics scores contain more discriminating information about student capability levels. Science scores and English scores nevertheless remained among the top ten features, confirming the multidimensional nature of academic assessment.

Entrance examination scores and aptitude measures occupied intermediate positions in importance rankings, with logical reasoning score and language proficiency score both ranking among the top fifteen features. The composite aptitude measure constructed through feature engineering demonstrated higher importance than either constituent score individually, validating the feature engineering approach.

Among socio economic indicators, family income range and parental education level appeared consistently among the top twenty features, though with substantially lower importance than academic measures. First generation learner status and internet access showed moderate importance, while study resources index demonstrated weaker predictive power. This pattern suggests that while socio economic factors influence student outcomes, their effects are partially mediated through academic achievement measures that already capture some of this influence.

Behavioral indicators including motivation score and attendance likelihood demonstrated importance levels comparable to socio economic factors, with self learning index showing somewhat lower importance. The interaction term combining motivation and attendance appeared among the top twenty five features, confirming the value of capturing synergistic effects through feature engineering.

For division allotment prediction, feature importance rankings showed similar patterns but with some notable differences. Entrance examination score increased in relative importance compared to class 12 percentage, possibly reflecting the emphasis on standardized testing in division placement decisions. The interaction between internet access and study resources index appeared higher in the rankings for division allotment than for student level prediction, suggesting that resource access may be particularly relevant for placement decisions.

Model interpretation using SHAP analysis

To provide deeper insight into model decision processes, SHAP analysis was conducted on the top performing LightGBM model for student level prediction. SHAP values enable both global interpretation of feature effects and local interpretation of individual predictions (Fig. 8).Fig. 8SHAP summary plot of feature impacts on LightGBM student level predictions.

SHAP summary plot of feature impacts on LightGBM student level predictions.

Global SHAP analysis confirmed the monotonic relationships expected for academic features: higher class 12 percentage, entrance examination scores, and subject specific scores consistently increased the predicted student level, while lower values decreased predictions. The magnitude of SHAP values for class 12 percentage showed wider variation than for other features, indicating that this feature drives both large positive and large negative deviations from baseline predictions.

Non linear effects were observed for several features. Motivation score exhibited diminishing marginal returns, with increases from low to moderate levels producing substantial positive SHAP values, while further increases from moderate to high levels produced smaller incremental effects. This pattern suggests that a baseline level of motivation is necessary for academic success, but beyond a certain threshold, additional motivation yields diminishing returns.

Interaction effects captured by SHAP dependence plots revealed that the relationship between entrance examination score and predicted level is moderated by class 12 percentage. For students with high class 12 percentage, entrance examination score had relatively smaller additional impact, while for students with lower prior achievement, entrance examination score played a more critical role in determining predicted level. This interaction pattern reflects the compensatory role that standardized testing can play in identifying capable students from diverse academic backgrounds (Fig. 9).Fig. 9SHAP feature contributions for four representative student profiles.

SHAP feature contributions for four representative student profiles.

Local interpretation through SHAP force plots revealed the feature combinations driving predictions for individual students. For a student predicted as Exceptional, positive contributions came primarily from high class 12 percentage, exceptional mathematics score, and high logical reasoning score, with demographic factors playing negligible roles. For a student predicted as Beginner, the dominant negative contributions came from low class 10 and class 12 percentages, low entrance examination score, and low motivation score, with first generation learner status providing a smaller negative contribution.

These local explanations demonstrate the potential for the model to provide actionable insights for educational interventions. Rather than simply identifying at risk students, the model can indicate which specific factors contribute most to the risk classification, enabling targeted support addressing identified weaknesses.

Conclusion

This paper has presented a comprehensive machine learning framework for early student profiling and outcome prediction, underpinned by a novel public synthetic data set of over 100,000 student records. The research was motivated by the critical need for privacy preserving, publicly available educational data sets that enable reproducible research and fair benchmarking of predictive models. Through systematic experimentation and analysis, we have demonstrated the utility of the synthetic data set, evaluated a diverse portfolio of machine learning algorithms, and provided interpretable insights into the factors that influence student academic outcomes. This concluding section synthesizes the key findings in relation to each research objective, discusses the broader implications of the work, acknowledges limitations, and proposes directions for future research.

Summary of contributions and achievement of research objectives

The first research objective sought to develop and validate a realistic synthetic data set that captures the complex multivariate characteristics of real world student populations while eliminating privacy concerns. The University Intake Synthetic data set, comprising 100,000 student records with 28 attributes spanning demographic, academic, socioeconomic, and behavioral domains, successfully achieves this objective. Descriptive analysis confirmed that the data set exhibits realistic distributional properties, with class 10 and class 12 percentages approximating normal distributions with means and standard deviations consistent with published educational statistics. The correlation analysis revealed expected patterns, including moderate positive correlations between previous academic achievement and entrance examination scores, and weaker correlations between demographic variables and cognitive measures. Missing data patterns, intentionally introduced following the missing Completely At Random and Missing At Random mechanisms, were verified to follow the specified structures without introducing systematic bias. The class distributions for both target variables reflected realistic scenarios, with minority classes appropriately represented and moderate imbalance that enables meaningful evaluation of imbalance handling techniques. These validation results confirm that the data set serves as an effective proxy for real student data, providing a foundation for reproducible educational data mining research without compromising student privacy.

The second research objective involved designing and implementing a comprehensive and modular machine learning framework for early student profiling and outcome prediction. The framework developed in this study encompasses a complete pipeline, from data preprocessing through model evaluation and interpretation. The preprocessing components include robust missing value imputation strategies adapted to variable types, appropriate encoding methods for categorical variables that account for nominal and ordinal distinctions, and feature scaling procedures that prevent information leakage. Feature engineering modules construct composite academic indices, interaction terms that capture synergistic effects, behavioral risk indicators that flag patterns, and temporal features derived from information from the gap year and the age. The model implementation layer integrates a diverse portfolio of algorithms spanning linear models, tree based ensembles, support vector machines, and neural networks, with specialized variants for ordinal prediction tasks. Evaluation components compute comprehensive metrics including accuracy, per class precision and recall, macro averaged F1 scores, ordinal specific measures, and statistical significance tests. Interpretation modules provide both global feature importance through permutation methods and local explanations through SHAP analysis. The modular architecture enables researchers to easily substitute alternative preprocessing techniques, incorporate new algorithms, or extend the evaluation framework, fulfilling the objective of creating a reusable resource for the research community.

The third research objective sought to rigorously benchmark model performance for both prediction tasks defined by the data set. Experimental results on student level prediction demonstrated that ensemble methods and neural network architectures substantially outperform simpler baseline models. LightGBM achieved macro F1 score of 0.842, XGBoost reached 0.839, and the multi layer perceptron attained 0.823, compared to logistic regression at 0.716 and decision tree at 0.673. The ordinal neural network, which explicitly incorporates the ordinal nature of the target variable, achieved the highest overall performance with macro F1 score of 0.846, representing a meaningful improvement over standard multi layer perceptron and confirming the value of leveraging ordinal information. Per class analysis revealed balanced performance across all four student levels, with F1 scores ranging from 0.831 for Intermediate to 0.859 for Beginner, demonstrating that class imbalance handling strategies effectively prevented bias toward majority classes. Ordinal specific metrics further validated the approach, with mean absolute error of 0.24 and quadratic weighted kappa of 0.892 for the ordinal neural network, substantially outperforming standard multi layer perceptron.

For division allotment prediction, LightGBM again emerged as the top performer with macro F1 score of 0.826, followed by XGBoost at 0.820 and multi layer perceptron at 0.809. Performance levels were slightly lower than for student level prediction, suggesting that division allotment may represent an inherently more challenging task or that available features are more directly predictive of student capability than of institutional placement decisions. Per class analysis revealed highest performance for the extreme categories of Remedial and Honors, with F1 scores of 0.831 and 0.848 respectively, while middle categories showed slightly lower performance. Confusion matrix analysis revealed that despite the nominal nature of the target variable, misclassifications predominantly occurred between adjacent categories in terms of academic rigor, suggesting an underlying ordinal structure in division allotment that reflects the continuous nature of academic capability.

Cross validation analysis confirmed the stability of these results, with standard deviations below 0.011 for all top models and test set performance falling within one standard deviation of cross validation means. This stability, combined with the minimal degradation between validation and test performance, confirms that the experimental design successfully prevented overfitting and that reported results provide reliable estimates of generalization performance.

The fourth research objective aimed to analyze feature importance and interpret model decisions to identify key predictors of student success. Feature importance analysis across all models consistently identified class 12 percentage as the single most predictive feature, with permutation importance approximately 30 percent higher than the second ranked feature. This finding aligns with educational research emphasizing the strong relationship between prior academic achievement and future success, and provides empirical validation that the synthetic data set preserves this fundamental relationship. Subject specific scores demonstrated differential importance, with mathematics score ranking substantially higher than science score and English score, potentially reflecting the particular importance of quantitative skills for the academic programs represented. Entrance examination scores and aptitude measures occupied intermediate positions in importance rankings, with the composite aptitude measure demonstrating higher importance than either constituent score alone, validating the feature engineering approach.

Socio economic indicators including family income range and parental education level appeared consistently among the top twenty features, though with substantially lower importance than academic measures. This pattern suggests that while socio economic factors influence student outcomes, their effects are partially mediated through academic achievement measures that already capture some of this influence. Behavioral indicators including motivation score and attendance likelihood demonstrated importance levels comparable to socio economic factors, with the interaction term combining these measures appearing among the top twenty five features, confirming the value of capturing synergistic effects through feature engineering.

SHAP analysis provided deeper insight into model decision processes, confirming monotonic relationships for academic features and revealing important interaction effects. The relationship between entrance examination score and predicted level was moderated by class 12 percentage, with entrance scores playing a more critical role for students with lower prior achievement. This interaction pattern reflects the compensatory role that standardized testing can play in identifying capable students from diverse academic backgrounds. Local explanations through SHAP force plots demonstrated the potential for models to provide actionable insights for educational interventions, identifying specific factors contributing to risk classifications and enabling targeted support addressing identified weaknesses.

The fifth research objective focused on disseminating the validated synthetic data set and complete machine learning framework as open source contributions to the research community. The data set is publicly available through GitHub, accompanied by comprehensive documentation including a detailed data dictionary, variable descriptions, and examples of data loading and basic analysis. The complete machine learning framework, implemented in Python using established libraries, is released under an open license with modular code organization, extensive comments, and example notebooks demonstrating complete experimental runs from data loading through result visualization. This dissemination directly addresses the core problem of resource scarcity in educational data mining, lowering barriers to entry for new researchers and enabling fair comparisons across future studies.

Summary of contributions and key findings

This investigation makes three core contributions to educational data mining. First, we introduced the University Intake Synthetic data set (100,000 records, 28 attributes), a privacy preserving resource validated to mirror real world statistical properties and analytical challenges. Second, we developed a modular machine learning framework that includes preprocessing, feature engineering, eleven algorithms (including ordinal neural networks), and interpretability tools. Third, we established benchmark results: the ordinal neural network achieved macro F1 = 0.846 for the prediction of the student level and LightGBM reached 0.826 for the division allocation, outperforming the baseline logistic regression by 18.1% and 18.0% respectively. The ordinal neural network also achieved quadratic weighted kappa = 0.892, confirming the value of explicit ordinal modeling.

Key insights and novelty

Feature importance consistently ranked prior academic achievement (especially the percentage of class 12) as the dominant predictor, while socio economic and behavioral factors provided meaningful secondary contributions. SHAP analysis revealed that entrance exam scores play a compensatory role for students with lower prior achievement and that motivation exhibits diminishing marginal returns. The novelty of this work lies in providing a fully open, large scale synthetic benchmark that enables reproducible research without privacy constraints, combined with a rigorous evaluation of ordinal aware models in the educational domain.

Limitations and caveats

The synthetic data, while validated, remains an approximation of real student populations and may not capture all institutional variations. The models were evaluated on static admission time data; longitudinal dynamics is not modeled. Fairness analysis did not show substantial disparities, but ethical deployment requires ongoing scrutiny. Future validation on real world institutional data is necessary.

Future directions

Future work should extend the data set with longitudinal engagement traces and institutional context variables. Methodologically, transformer based architectures and causal inference methods promise further improvements. We also encourage field experiments to evaluate whether model driven interventions improve student outcomes.
