Nasolacrimal balloon dacryoplasty versus nasolacrimal bi-canalicular intubation for the management of congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction with a previous failed probing or intubation: a retrospective study
Authors: Lamiaa Salem, Mohamed El Sada, Rawan Hosny, R A El-Essawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare the results and complications of nasolacrimal duct intubation and balloon dacryoplasty (BDCP) for the treatment of cases with recurrent epiphora following previous probing or intubation.MethodsA retrospective study including patients with recurrent nasolacrimal duct obstruction following previous probing or intubation whether retreated with balloon dacryoplasty or bi-canalicular intubation. All procedures were performed by the same surgeon. The results were evaluated and compared using a Modified Munk classification.ResultsThe study included 103 eyes of 77 patients; 53 eyes (51.5%) underwent BDCP and 50 eyes (48.5%) underwent bi-canalicular intubation. Mean age was 4.03 ± 2.71 years (range 9 months to 12 years). Most eyes (85.5%) had severe pre-operative disease (Modified Munk grades 4 and 5). Post-operative, all patients showed improvement in terms of grading according to MMC; the collective success rate of both procedures in both groups was 51.5%, 69.7%, 86.7% and 88.9% at 1 week, 1-, 3- and 6-months post-op. respectively. Subgroup analysis revealed earlier successful outcome in group B compared to group A (p-value = 0.002), yet this relationship is reversed with Group A (BDCP) having 96% success at 6 months follow-up compared to 80% in Group B (Bi-canalicular intubation) (p-value = 0.021). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences in age, sex distribution, type of previous intervention (p-value = 0.95), or pre-operative Modified Munk grades. Complications were minimal and insignificant in both groups.ConclusionBalloon dacryoplasty showed a 96% success rate compared to 80% success rate of intubation after 6 months follow up. Higher success rates of treatment of recurrent CNLDO epiphora is seen when the previous procedure was a simple probing.Keywords: Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction, Recurrent epiphora, Failed probing, Failed intubation, Balloon dacryoplasty, Bi-canalicular intubation

Introduction

Pediatric nasolacrimal duct obstruction is commonly congenital [1]. Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction (CNLDO) occurs in approximately 20% of normal newborn infants. The blockage usually occurs at the valve of Hasner at the distal end of the nasolacrimal duct (NLD) with no sex predilection and no genetic predisposition recorded. The rate of spontaneous resolution is estimated to be 90% within the first year of life [2].

Though the etiology of CNLDO is most commonly a membranous obstruction at the valve of Hasner, general stenosis of the duct is the second most common cause of duct obstruction. Congenital proximal lacrimal outflow dysgenesis involves maldevelopment of the punctum and canaliculus. Proximal outflow dysgenesis can occur concurrently with distal obstruction [3].

Treatment of CNLDO is initially conservative waiting for spontaneous resolution and consists of observation, lacrimal massage and treatment with topical antibiotics. Probing is performed in cases of persistent duct obstruction. Management options following probe failure include nasolacrimal duct stent insertion and Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) [4].

Studies of primary surgical management have found probing to be successful in 70%–97% of cases, with many reports around 90%. The success of simple probing declines slightly with the increasing age of the child and in cases of bilateral obstruction [5, 6]. Nasolacrimal duct stent insertion is used as a primary procedure or following failure of simple probing and the success rate is estimated to be 79 to 96% [7]. Balloon catheter dilation or dacryoplasty (BDCP) has been described for the treatment of adults with partial NLDO and for children with congenital NLDO with good results [8, 9].

We aim to compare the results and complication rates of nasolacrimal intubation and balloon dacryoplasty in CNLDO in the management of a failed initial procedure.

Patients and methods

This is a retrospective study including patients with recurrent epiphora following a failed previous probing or intubation presenting to the oculoplastic clinic of Cairo University Pediatric Ophthalmology and Strabismus Department. Patients were grouped according to procedure received into 2 groups: Group (A) included those who underwent balloon dacryoplasty (BDCP) and Group (B) including those who underwent a bi-canalicular nasolacrimal intubation. Treatment allocation was non-random and primarily determined by the availability of surgical instruments at the time of surgery, which depended on institutional supply and donation availability. The availability of balloon dacryoplasty catheters and intubation sets was not consistent throughout the study period, resulting in periods where only one modality was available. Allocation was therefore not based on clinical characteristics, anatomical considerations, or disease severity. This may have introduced potential selection and temporal bias. An Informed consent was obtained from patients’ guardians at the time of the procedures, and all tenets of Declaration of Helsinki were followed. The study protocol was approved by the Ophthalmology department Council at Cairo University.

Inclusion criteria

A history of CNLDO and recurrent epiphora after at least one previous failed procedure (probing or intubation) and subsequently underwent either BDCP or bi-canalicular intubation.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with positive regurgitation of pus, recurrent episodes of dacryocystitis, presence of an associated fistula, lacrimal sac swelling and any craniofacial anomalies.

Intervention

All procedures were performed by the same surgeon (R.A.E.) using the same technique described.

Balloon dacryoplasty (BDCP)

The procedure was carried out under general anesthesia. Nasal pack was inserted by the anesthesiologist. The lacrimal punctum was dilated using punctal dilator, and probing was carried in a standard fashion using Bowman probes (00, 0, and 1). The probe is used to navigate the superior puncta and canaliculus with traction of the eyelid temporally to stretch the canaliculus. Then the probe is advanced until a firm stop of the lacrimal bone is felt. The probe is then rotated in a superior and medial fashion along the brow to allow for navigation through the nasolacrimal sac and duct. Probing was done through the upper and lower puncti in all our cases. The probe, indicates the proper balloon catheter that should be used in each case whether the 2 mm or the 3 mm balloon catheter. Before insertion of the LacriCath balloon catheter (Atrion Medical, Birmingham, Alabama, Quest Medical, Inc.), the inflation device was filled with saline, and the device is locked and any air bubbles must get out before it was connected to the balloon catheter. The LacriCath balloon catheter was tested by increasing the pressure in the inflation device by rotating the piston clockwise to detect if there is any leak and that the manometer is functioning properly. Then the balloon catheter is deflated by decreasing the pressure by rotating the piston anticlockwise. The deflated balloon catheter is passed through the superior puncta and into the distal NLD with both markings are buried, where it is inflated to 8 atmospheric pressure (atm) for 90 s, deflated and again inflated to 8 atm again for another 60 s, then the balloon is completely deflated and extruded and re-introduced to repeat the procedure for the proximal NLD with only the upper marking buried (Fig. 1).

Bi-canalicular nasolacrimal duct intubation

The procedure was carried out under general anesthesia, with nasal packing. Probing is performed as described through the upper and lower puncti, then followed by the passage of the probes connected to the bi-canalicular stents (Eaglevision® Lacrimal Intubation Set). Each probe is received from the nose using a straight artery entered directly vertical under the inferior turbinate. Each probe was carefully retrieved from the nose using straight artery forceps. To minimize mucosal trauma, the forceps were passed vertically, ensuring the stent was grasped only once identified at the inferior meatus, thereby avoiding unnecessary manipulation or ‘blind’ searching within the nasal cavity. When both probes are pulled out of the nose, the probes are cut and the silicone tube ends are tied in a square knot over an artery to avoid too tight knot which will over stretch the puncti, and cause punctal cheese-wiring (Fig. 2).

Post-operative treatment

All patients received Tobramycin / Dexamethasone combination eye drops and nasal decongestants 3 times daily for 1 week.

Follow up: was done on the second day post-operative then at 1 week, 1month, 3months and 6 months guided by modified Munk’s classification. Silicone tubes were removed after 3–6 months in the intubation group.

Statistical methods

Data were coded and entered using the statistical package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data was summarized using mean, standard deviation, median, minimum and maximum in quantitative data and using frequency (count) and relative frequency (percentage) for categorical data. Comparison of numerical variables between the study groups was done using Mann Whitney U test for independent samples. For comparing categorical data, Chi square test was performed. Exact test was used instead when the expected frequency is less than 5. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Post-op., patients showed improvement in terms of grading according to modified Munk’s classification; the collective success rate of both procedures was 51.5%, 69.7%, 86.7% and 88.9% at 1 week, 1-, 3- and 6-months post-op. respectively. Early postoperative assessments represent outcomes during active device retention in the intubation group, whereas the 6-month assessment reflects definitive outcomes following tube removal. Sub-group analysis revealed earlier successful outcome in group B compared to group A (p-value = 0.002), yet this relationship was reversed with group A (BDCP) having 96% success at 6 months follow-up compared to 80% in group B (Bi-canalicular intubation) (p-value = 0.021). Table 2 describes characteristics of both groups.

In the whole study population, there was no correlation between the success rate and gender, age or pre-op. severity of disease (p-value = 0.77, 0.90 and 0.95 respectively). There were no statistically significant differences were identified between treatment groups in baseline demographic or clinical characteristics, including age, sex, prior surgical intervention, and pre-operative Modified Munk classification (Table 1), suggesting minimal baseline imbalance between groups.

Complications were minimal and insignificant in both groups; in group A, 5 cases reported mild nasal bleeding, and 7 cases reported blood-stained vomitus on the day of operation. There was a suspected intra-operative rupture of the balloon catheter as denoted by failure to raise the pressure in the manometer, which occurred in 2 cases during the procedure, it was insignificant to the patient, but we needed to use another catheter which rendered the cost of the procedure higher. In group B extrusion of the tube occurred in 6 cases, 10 cases reported mild nasal bleeding and blood-stained vomitus on the day of operation.

Out of 103 (100%) eyes included in the study, 8 cases (7.8%) experienced early tube extrusion, and 5 cases were lost in follow-up and were considered treatment failures in the intention-to-treat analysis. A total of 90 eyes (92.2%) completed follow-up and were included in the per-protocol analysis. At 6 months follow-up, 85 out of 90 eyes achieved successful outcomes (Munk grade ≤ 2), corresponding to a per-protocol total success rate of both groups 88.9%. When all 103 eyes were included in an intention-to-treat analysis, with missing data considered as treatment failure, the overall success rate of both groups was 82.5%.

Discussion

At our service as well as many places elsewhere, the two main options for the management of recurrent epiphora in a child who has had a previous attempt at probing or intubation are either a second attempt at intubation or a BDCP [4], with intubation being the preferred choice at many centers [11]. This work aims to compare both techniques.

The findings of the present study complement the work initiated by the Pediatric Eye Disease Investigator Group (PEDIG) in the NLD2 protocol, which was originally designed as a randomized controlled trial comparing balloon catheter dilation and nasolacrimal duct intubation for persistent congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction [8]. Due to poor recruitment and lack of equipoise among investigators, NLD2 was converted to a prospective observational study with inclusion of a third arm (repeat probing), and anticipated success rates for both balloon dilation and intubation in the 70–90% range.

Our study, although retrospective and single-centered, provides practical clinical data with contemporary surgical techniques and demonstrates high success rates for both balloon dacryoplasty and silicone intubation, with a higher success rate observed for balloon dacryoplasty at 6 months follow-up.

A recent review by the American Academy of opthalmology, which included 8 studies, discussed the efficacy and safety of BDCP in CNLDO. The success rates reported ranged from 75–100% [9]. The success rate in our study was 96% in the BDCP group at the final follow up.

Studies evaluating success rates and safety of BDCP as a secondary procedure also share a similar success rate. Becker et al. [12], which studied the efficacy of balloon dacryoplasty for treatment of congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction in children over 12 months of age, reported a success rate of 94% in cases who had done previous probing or silicone intubation. Alañón et al., also performed a prospective study on balloon dacryoplasty for treatment of congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction after failed primary probing with a success rate of 83.33% [10].

Interestingly, at 1 week post-operatively the success rate was significantly higher in cases of bi-canalicular intubation compared to that of balloon dacryoplasty; however, this likely reflects the temporary stenting effect during active tube retention rather than definitive anatomical resolution, but this was reversed afterwards at 3 months post-operative and maintained up to 6 months, where the success rate of balloon dacryoplasty was 96% and that of bi-canalicular intubation 80% (p-value = 0.02). We hypothesize that in the early post-operative period BDCP is associated with edema of the mucosal linings, leading to failure of resolution of pre-operative symptoms. On the other hand, the relatively late and sustained effect of BDCP may be due to tissue remodeling [9, 13] with histopathological studies showing that BDCP does not induce inflammation or crush injury [13]. These findings are in line with a study evaluating probing vs. BDCP as primary treatment for CNLDO, which revealed a higher long-term success rate for BDCP especially in narrow bony canals [14]. It is also worth noting that BDCP may achieve a true widening of narrowed segments in all directions, as opposed to stenting, which merely provides a patent passage that is subject to fibrosis following stent removal.

In our study, the success rate was significantly higher in cases with previous probing compared to those with previous intubation at 3 months follow-up, but when studying the correlation between the type of previous intervention and each group alone at 3 months post-operative, there was no statistically significant correlation, Group A (p-value = 0.23) and Group B (p-value = 1). This may be due to the small sample size in each group. To our knowledge, this is the first report of this finding. This may be related to complications caused by the previous intubation, such as mucosal laceration and fibrosis or false passage creation [15]. Intra-operative findings during the second procedure revealed that while some cases presented with simple membranous obstructions, a significant portion of eyes exhibited secondary mucosal stenosis or cicatricial changes, likely resulting from the trauma or inflammatory response of the initial failed intervention.

The authors also attempted to study the effect of the preoperative severity of symptoms on the overall success rate as well as on each type of intervention and developed a modification of Munk’s classification originally described by Alañón et al. [10], in order to be able to determine any statistically significant effect, yet there was no statistically significant correlation between the preoperative severity and the overall success rate.

In our study complications were minimal. The intra-operative rupture of the balloon catheter during the procedure which resulted in using another catheter indicates the importance of priming before starting the procedure to make sure that the balloon was inflating without leakage. In cases who had done bi-canalicular intubation, there was extrusion of the tube due to pulling by the child occurred in 5 cases, this is considered a major drawback for intubation. Other potential drawbacks of NLD stenting include corneal complications, the potential biofilm formation with subsequent increased risk of infection, and in extremely uncooperative children, re-exposure to anesthesia for stent removal [16, 17].

In literature, complications related to BDCP were rarely reported, with the main disadvantage of this technique being the higher equipment cost. This explains the unpopularity of this treatment in developing countries with a recent survey of oculoplastic surgeons in India showing that BDCP is their least preferable treatment option for CNLDO [11]. A recent study explored the use of readily available coronary angioplasty balloons in BDCP to minimize cost, they reported an 87% success rate with mean follow up of 6.17 months and no complications [18]. Another study suggested the reuse of conventional lacrimal balloon catheters with plasma sterilization to further cut costs [19]. Some other advantages of BDCP include the short learning curve and simplicity of the procedure. It is rather important to discuss with the parents in detail procedures available for managing of CNLDO. Parents may be reluctant to have a stent near a child’s eye and thus prefer BDCP [19].

Our study limitations include the relatively small number of patients in each group, the lack of long term follow up of the patients after many years of treatment, limiting the comparison with a single stent type although introducing all types of stents available would require a huge number of patients to be able to reach a statistically significant result. An additional limitation of this study is the non-random allocation of treatment based on device availability, which varied over time. This may have introduced temporal confounding, as patients treated during different periods may not be fully comparable. The consistency between intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses supports the robustness of the study findings. An additional limitation relates to the classification of early tube extrusion. In the present study, cases with early stent extrusion were considered treatment failures in the intention-to-treat analysis. However, it is important to note that early extrusion may occur either spontaneously as a procedure-related complication or due to external factors such as accidental or intentional removal by an unattended child, which is not uncommon in our patient population. This introduces a potential source of misclassification bias, as not all extrusion events necessarily reflect failure of the surgical intervention itself. To address this, a per-protocol analysis including only cases with maintained stent placement throughout the intended follow-up period was also performed, allowing for a more consistent comparison of procedural efficacy.

Also, the use of a nasal endoscope would have helped in the direct visualization of the whole procedure and detection of possible causes of failure or success, but unfortunately a nasal endoscope was just added recently to our OR.

Conclusion

Both balloon dacryoplasty and bi-canalicular nasolacrimal duct intubation are valid treatment options after a failed previous probing or intubation with comparable good results and minimal complications. In our cohort of patients, BDCP demonstrated a higher success rate at 6 months follow up compared to bi-canalicular intubation. The choice is best individualized based on surgeon expertise, anatomical considerations, and available resources.
