Health System Policies Following State Abortion Restrictions in the US
Authors: Lena Shadow, Astha Ray, Brooke Schroeder, Caitlin Bernard, Catherine Chen, Hayley B. Gershengorn, Deepshikha C. Ashana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This qualitative study examines the policies and practices of hospitals that provide exempted abortions in abortion-restrictive states.

Introduction

In 2022, the US Supreme Court issued a decision in Dobbs v Jackson Women’s Health Organization that overturned federal protection for abortion.1 As a result, states now have the authority to regulate access to abortion. One in 3 US women of reproductive age live in states that either ban abortion entirely or at 6 weeks’ gestation when most women are not yet aware of the pregnancy.2,3 Nearly all states with total abortion bans also impose criminal penalties as severe as life in prison for physicians who are judged to have violated the law.4

All states with abortion bans exempt cases where the health or life of the pregnant person is threatened by continued pregnancy, known as a maternal indication for abortion.3 However, maternal indication exemptions are variable and ambiguously defined.5 The level of medical risk that is sufficient to activate a maternal indication exemption can be uncertain, and has been perceived to contradict the federal Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act in some cases.5,6 In this context, there have been several highly publicized cases of maternal deaths following delayed abortions or other necessary pregnancy care.7

To reduce uncertainty about medical decision-making for pregnant people, health systems have been encouraged to develop institutional policies to establish a local standard of care that complies with state law.8,9,10 However, knowledge surrounding the scope and content of such health system policies is limited.11,12,13 Therefore, our objective was to describe policies for maternal indication abortions enacted by hospitals in abortion-restrictive states.

Methods

This is a qualitative descriptive study of hospital policies and practices for exempted abortion care in states with total or near-total abortion bans.14 The study was approved by the Duke Health institutional review board (IRB; protocol No. 00118174) and follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) reporting guideline.15

Sample Selection

We identified 16 US states with total or 6-week abortion bans that provided exempted abortion care as of June 2025.3 These included Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Oklahoma, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, and West Virginia. Using the 2020 American Hospital Association Annual Survey, we identified the top 5 hospitals by annual birth volume in each state. We focused on high birth–volume hospitals because their policies affect the largest number of pregnant people in their states, and they typically provide the majority of obstetric care for patients with serious complications.16,17,18 We used our professional networks to identify 1 study champion from 1 high-volume hospital in each state. All study champions were obstetrics or critical care physicians.

Study champions identified colleagues who were involved in developing abortion-related policies at their hospital for inclusion in interviews. Eligible participants could be family planning, maternal-fetal medicine, critical care, or emergency medicine clinicians or hospital administrators, legal counsel, or bioethicists.

Data Collection and Processing

After introductions from study champions, we recruited participants using standardized, IRB-approved email scripts detailing the study purpose. Participants provided verbal consent at the beginning of virtual, audio-recorded, semistructured interviews conducted using videoconferencing software and lasting 45 to 60 minutes. Two female investigators (L.S. and D.C.A.) conducted all interviews between June 18 and December 3, 2025. D.C.A. has extensive prior qualitative research experience.19,20,21,22 She ensured adequate training of the study team through directed readings, mock interviews, supervision of interviews, and oversight of data analysis.

Three investigators (L.S., C.B., and D.C.A.), a public policy graduate student, an obstetrician-gynecologist, and a critical care physician developed an interview guide (eAppendix in Supplement 1) based on an American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) practice management guideline.9 The guideline, “Questions to Help Hospital Systems Support and Empower Physicians in the Post-Roe Legal Landscape,” and the interview guide focused on the following domains: (1) staying apprised of current state laws; (2) supporting timely, evidence-based patient care within legal constraints; and (3) protecting clinicians. The interview guide was pilot tested in mock interviews with 2 nonparticipating physicians. Given the sensitivity of the study topic and the risks of retaliation should participants be identified, as endorsed by participants themselves, we took several steps to maintain participant anonymity. Audio recordings contain identifiable voiceprints, thus recordings were permanently deleted after deidentified transcripts were prepared. Participants reported their professional role but no sociodemographic information. After data analysis, we permanently deleted all materials that could be used to infer participant identity from transcripts, such as recruitment emails and contact logs. There was no compensation for participation, as monetary compensation requires maintaining a record of participant identifiers. Included states are not reported here to minimize the risk of loss of institutional confidentiality.

Unlike a probability sample, our goal was to recruit an informationally representative sample that described a broad range of reproductive care policies used by hospitals to balance the potentially competing goals of patient health, clinician autonomy, and protection from legal risk. We continued recruitment until we reached saturation, the point in data analysis where 2 successive sites did not yield new information.23 Saturation was reached after hospitals from 9 of 15 states were sequentially enrolled; 1 hospital from the sixteenth state declined participation and indicated that no other hospitals in the state provided abortion services. Beyond the point of data saturation, the potential personal and professional risks to study participants were judged to outweigh the marginal benefits of obtaining additional information.

Data Analysis

We began content analysis in parallel with data collection to allow modification of recruitment strategies (eg, identifying highest yield participant roles) and participant prompts to probe emerging findings more deeply.24 The codebook was developed iteratively by 2 investigators (A.R. and B.S.),25,26 with deductive codes based on the ACOG framework and inductive codes based on additional concepts identified on initial review of the data by the whole study team.27 The coders concurrently analyzed transcripts in NVivo version 14 (Lumivero) until they achieved greater than 80% agreement, after which they coded transcripts independently. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion during weekly all-team meetings. The Results section and quotes table were member-checked (ie, shared with all interview participants) to ensure that policies were faithfully represented and no identifying information was inadvertently retained.28 A few policies were noted by participants to be privileged and were thus withheld from this report. Additional aspects of qualitative research trustworthiness (ie, credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability) were achieved through weekly team meetings, peer critique, examination of prior research findings, and presentation of exemplar quotations.29 Additionally, to augment trustworthiness, we only report policies that were verified by multiple respondents within a hospital or by detailed descriptions of their application to the care of real patients. Quotations are presented herein without any identifying information to maintain anonymity of participating individuals and their institutions. Hospital and state characteristics were identified from American Hospital Association annual survey data and relevant state laws.

Results

The sample included 34 participants from 9 hospitals in 9 states. Participants represented the following professional roles: physicians (16 participants [47.0%]), hospital administrators (10 participants [29.4%]), legal counsel (5 participants [14.7%]), and bioethicists (3 participants [8.8%]). Included hospitals were private (5 hospitals [55.5%]) or public (4 hospitals [44.4%]) institutions with a mean (SD) of 5257 (3289) live births per year (Table 1).

All participants strongly endorsed fear of prosecution by bad-faith actors and variability in clinical practice as contributing to unnecessarily restrictive interpretations of abortion laws. One said, “Ensuring that your hospital’s policies are not more restrictive than state policies is key.”

Participants identified the following existing institutional policies and practices that most effectively supported evidence-based reproductive care: (1) providing criminal defense resources for physicians; (2) implementing employment, safety, and health care protections for employees; (3) explicitly aligning institutional definitions of abortion with the fullest extent of care permitted under state law to avoid unnecessarily restrictive clinical care; (4) formalizing decision-making support for maternal indication exemptions; (5) building intrastate hospital alliances to standardize interpretation of state laws banning abortion; and (6) engaging in legislative advocacy and strategic litigation to maximize reproductive care (Figure). Providing Criminal Defense Resources for Physicians

Malpractice insurance policies only cover civil charges while many states bring criminal charges for alleged violations of abortion laws (Table 2). Therefore, hospitals developed new criminal defense insurance policies and either trained hospital lawyers in abortion law or retained external criminal defense attorneys. One participant emphasized that defending physicians for providing evidence-based care is an unusual but growing niche of health law that requires specific training.

Some participants shared examples of needing to use legal representation when they were being investigated by state attorneys general or interacting with law enforcement. Physicians who had access to criminal defense resources appreciated these as tangible commitments from their employers that were crucial to retention and recruitment.

Implementing Employment, Safety, and Health Care Protections for Employees

In addition to the risk of criminal prosecution, participants worried about job security during unjustified investigations, physical safety, and their own reproductive health (Table 2). Therefore, some hospitals added indemnity clauses to labor contracts to guarantee pay and continued employment for physicians during investigations, so long as they used sound medical judgment and followed hospital protocols. Regarding physical safety, hospital security teams surveilled the web, including the dark web, for threats against the institution or its employees, in some cases finding credible threats of violence that required relocating employee events. Finally, for pregnant employees, health system insurance plans occasionally covered out-of-state abortion care, particularly when medically indicated. Employees again perceived these resources as important for retention and recruitment.

Aligning Institutional Definitions of Abortion With the Fullest Extent of Care Permitted Under State Law

Participants emphasized that it was important for hospital policy to clarify which procedures or actions were considered induced abortion, in light of often vaguely worded state laws. For example, some hospital policies clarified that management of inevitable pregnancy loss was not considered induced abortion; this category variably included inevitable miscarriage, previable preterm rupture of membranes, previable cervical dilation, ectopic pregnancy, and gestational trophoblastic disease (Table 2). Similarly, unintended fetal demise, for example from anesthesia provided for another procedure during pregnancy, also was not considered induced abortion. Some states explicitly excluded such conditions from their abortion bans, while hospitals in other states did so through internal hospital policy. There were no reports of legal challenges regarding abortion definitions. When clinicians mistakenly considered the previously mentioned circumstances as induced abortions, this led to unnecessary interhospital transfers, delayed medical care, and patient harm.

Formalizing Decision-Making Support for Maternal Indication Exemptions

State laws differ in when they permit abortions for maternal indications—either when the pregnant person’s health is endangered or only when their life is endangered (Table 2). The more restrictive life standard was perceived as inappropriately narrow because there is no objective medical threshold that differentiates patients who are at risk of dying, and dangerous, irrecoverable critical illness can evolve rapidly (Table 2). Variability in judgements about maternal health and life risks led to maternal and fetal harm, geographic disparities in care, increasing concentration of risk among fewer hospitals, and a perception of greater legal risk to physicians.

Hospitals implemented a range of processes to support clinician medical decision-making amidst uncertainty about maternal indication exemptions. These included multidisciplinary committees to review cases in real time, a requirement for 2-physician agreement before a maternal indication abortion, or support for individual physicians to exercise their independent medical judgement. Participants preferred different approaches; however, all agreed that hospital lawyers should not unilaterally interpret laws that affect patient care.

Building Hospital Alliances to Standardize Interpretation of State Laws Banning Abortion

To address variability in interpretation of maternal indication exemptions, administrative leaders or groups of physicians from different hospitals formed intrastate alliances to attempt to create consistent standards of care. Such efforts guarded against unnecessarily conservative interpretations of abortion laws, reduced legal risk to any individual clinician or institution, and attempted to standardize patient care (Table 2).

Engaging in Legislative Advocacy and Strategic Litigation to Maximize Reproductive Care

Despite working in abortion-restrictive states, many participants emphasized the need for continued engagement with state lawmakers to shape health care legislation (Table 2). Some leveraged their hospital’s role as an anchor institution in the state to advocate for access to evidence-based reproductive care. Finally, when collaborative efforts were not fruitful, a few hospitals also used the legal system to challenge their state’s abortion laws.

Discussion

In this qualitative descriptive study, we identified policies for maternal indication abortions and obstetric care in 9 high birth–volume hospitals across 9 abortion-restrictive US states. Policies and practices that supported evidence-based reproductive care within local legal constraints included the following: (1) providing criminal defense resources for physicians; (2) implementing employment, safety, and health care protections for employees; (3) explicitly aligning institutional definitions of abortion with the fullest extent of care permitted under state law to avoid unnecessarily restrictive clinical care; (4) formalizing decision-making support for maternal indication exemptions; (5) building intrastate hospital alliances to standardize interpretation of state laws banning abortion; and (6) engaging in legislative advocacy and strategic litigation to maximize reproductive care. Study findings corroborate and extend a prior similar study by Sabbath et al,13 which increases their external validity. Furthermore, identified hospital policies are aligned with recent American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommendations and may be instructive to other hospitals that provide legally contested abortion care or other medical care.9

Many of the policies described by participants could be implemented immediately and at little cost to hospitals. These include updating physician contracts, identifying obstetric procedures that are not restricted by abortion bans, creating processes to support decision-making, and allying with other hospitals in the state. Other policies, such as providing criminal defense resources, require a greater financial investment and have a longer time horizon for implementation. However, participants underscored that protection from criminal prosecution was crucial, both as a sign of a hospital’s commitment to its physicians and as practical support that reduced the significant legal and financial risks for the physicians willing to continue providing the full spectrum of reproductive care.

The results of this study highlight that hospitals and clinicians in abortion-restrictive states must think about risk in a unique and disturbing way. Whereas the function of malpractice law is to minimize health risks to patients by encouraging adherence to a standard of care, the effect of criminalized abortion care is to create a perverse scenario in which increasing health risks to pregnant people (eg, emergencies meeting the life risk standard) reduce legal risks for clinicians who perform medically indicated abortions.5 In this milieu, we found the primary function of hospital policies was to reduce the risks borne by clinicians, so they were unencumbered to make patient-centered decisions. Establishing a standard of care, distinguishing abortions from permitted management of inevitable pregnancy loss, and providing criminal defense protections were important means of reducing legal risks to clinicians while preserving access to timely, life-saving care.8 Such strategies are increasingly relevant given the recent imposition of sanctions against clinicians in Texas who adopted overly cautious interpretations of abortion laws, resulting in delayed or nonstandard care, and national evidence of nonstandard management of spontaneous abortion in abortion-restrictive states.30,31

Other effective hospital policies addressed nonlegal risks, including the risks of job or wage loss, cyber harassment, and restricted reproductive care for clinicians or their families. Participants noted a downstream effect of these policies was increased clinician retention and recruitment. In a time when states such as Idaho have lost more than one-third of their obstetrics physicians in 2 years alone, any interventions that stabilize the women’s health workforce should be considered seriously.32,33,34

The hospital policies described in this study operated on multiple levels—those relevant to patient care, clinicians, statewide hospital networks, and public policy (Figure). Multilevel policies align with the social-ecological model, which describes that multiple levels of influence, or policy in this case, are necessary to shape public health.35 Other hospitals that provide legally contested medical care may find this framework instructive as they consider the types of policies to implement.

Strengths and Limitations

One strength of this study is the use of a multistate sample of high birth–volume hospitals that collectively provide care for nearly 50 000 pregnant people per year. Another is the focus on hospital policies that could be scaled to meaningfully maximize reproductive care access. There are also several limitations. First, worries about confidentiality, retaliation, and reputational risk may have prevented participants from sharing the full range of policies and practices in their hospitals. However, we went to great lengths to build trust with participants (eg, identifying institutional study champions) and maintain confidentiality (eg, member checking, excluding any identifiers from this report). In several instances, participants shared privileged information that could not be publicly reported, demonstrating their substantial trust in the study team. Second, our sampling strategy likely identified policies that are not present in smaller out-of-sample hospitals. However, the goal of the study was to identify innovative strategies used by high birth–volume hospitals with the greatest exposure to legal risk. We hope that knowledge of these policies will stimulate practice change in other hospitals. Future research should focus on dissemination of policies across hospital settings, including lower birth–volume hospitals that provide care for vulnerable populations. Third, to address social desirability bias, we only reported policies that were verified by multiple respondents within a hospital or by detailed descriptions of their application to the care of real patients.

Conclusions

This qualitative study of high birth–volume hospitals in abortion-restrictive US states found that institutions implemented a range of policies to protect clinicians and staff and support the fullest extent of legally permissible, evidence-based reproductive care. These policies were aligned with American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommendations and may be instructive to other hospitals that provide legally contested medical care.9
