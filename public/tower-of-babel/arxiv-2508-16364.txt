A canonical Fano threefold has Fano index ≤ 66 \leq 66
Chen Jiang, Haidong Liu

Abstract
. We show that the ℚ \mathbb{Q} -Fano index of a canonical weak Fano 3 3 -fold is at most 66 66 . This upper bound is optimal and gives an affirmative answer to a conjecture of Chengxi Wang in dimension 3 3 . During the proof, we establish a new Riemmann–Roch formula for canonical 3 3 -folds and provide a detailed study of non-isolated singularities on canonical Fano 3 3 -folds, concerning both their local and global properties. Our proof also involves a Kawamata–Miyaoka type inequality and geometry of foliations of rank 2 2 on canonical Fano 3 3 -folds.

1.
Introduction

A normal projective variety is called
Fano
(resp.,
weak Fano
) if its anti-canonical divisor is ample (resp., nef and big). According to the minimal model program, Fano varieties with mild singularities serve as fundamental building blocks of algebraic varieties.

For a weak Fano variety
X
with mild singularities (e.g., terminal or canonical singularities), its
\mathbb{Q}
-Fano index
\text{\rm q}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X)
is defined to be

\displaystyle\text{\rm q}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X){}

\displaystyle\coloneq\max\{q\mid-K_{X}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}qA,\,A\in\Cl(X)\}.

The
\mathbb{Q}
-Fano index of a terminal Fano
3
-fold is quite well-understood, which belongs to the set
\{1,\dots,9,11,13,17,19\}
(see
[
34
,
30
]
) and it plays a crucial role in the classification of terminal Fano
3
-folds
[
3
,
4
,
29
,
31
,
16
,
24
,
25
]
. However, for canonical Fano
3
-folds, progress has been limited due to the absence of Riemann–Roch formula and the classification of canonical singularities,
see §
1.1
for a detailed explanation.
Even a good upper bound for
\mathbb{Q}
-Fano indices remains unknown. In literature, for a canonical Fano
3
-fold
X
, we only have a very rough upper bound
\text{\rm q}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X)\leq 840^{2}\cdot 324\approx 2.3\cdot 10^{8}
(cf.
[
1
]
*Lemma 2.3): we know that
rK_{X}
is Cartier for some positive integer
r\leq 840
by
[
7
]
*Proposition 2.4 and
(-K_{X})^{3}\leq 324
by
[
15
]
; if
-K_{X}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}qA
for some ample Weil divisor
A
, then

q\leq qA\cdot(-rK_{X})^{2}=r^{2}(-K_{X})^{3}\leq 840^{2}\cdot 324.

In this paper, we give the optimal upper bound for the
\mathbb{Q}
-Fano index of a canonical weak Fano
3
-fold, which gives an affirmative answer to a conjecture of Wang
[
35
]
*Conjecture 3.7 in dimension
3
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X
be a canonical weak Fano
3
-fold. Then
\text{\rm q}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X)\leq 66
.

Kasprzyk classified terminal or canonical toric Fano
3
-folds in
[

,
17
]
. The following are some motivating examples with large

1.1.
Difficulties and strategy

We briefly explain the main difficulty and the strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.1
. After a reduction step (see Proposition
2.8
), we may assume that
X
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial canonical Fano
3
-fold of Picard number
1
with torsion-free Weil class group
\Cl(X)
and
-K_{X}\sim qA
where
A
is the ample generator of
\Cl(X)
. The goal is to show that
q\leq 66
.

The main difficulty comes from the singularities. A common way to deal with a
3
-fold
X
with canonical singularities is to pass to a partial resolution
f\colon Y\to X
where
Y
has only terminal singularities and
f^{*}K_{X}=K_{Y}
, then deal with
Y
. The advantage of working on
Y
is that there is a complete classification of
3
-dimensional terminal singularities and a powerful tool: Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula for Weil divisors on terminal
3
-folds. But for the proof of Theorem
1.1
, taking such a partial resolution is not very helpful, as
f^{*}A
is no longer a Weil divisor and most of the information of
A
is lost on
Y
. So we usually have to deal with
X
directly. This is more difficult: firstly,
3
-dimensional canonical singularities are much more complicated than
3
-dimensional terminal singularities, we do not have a classification of such singularities and they can be non-isolated; secondly, Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula does not work directly for Weil divisors on canonical
3
-folds, it only works for Weil divisors satisfying Reid’s condition, namely, those divisors locally proportional to
K_{X}
.

Our first ingredient is to establish a new Riemann–Roch formula based on Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula. Our idea is to associate a Weil divisor
D
on
X
to a Weil divisor
f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)
on
Y
which is called the
Weil pullback
of
D
(see Definition
4.3
) which preserves cohomologies, and then apply Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula to
f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)
on
Y
.
As a consequence, we show that (see Theorem
4.9
) for any integer
0<s<q
,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))={}&-\frac{s^{2}}{2}A^{2}\cdot K_{X}+2+\sum_{C\subset\Sing(X)}(-K_{X}\cdot C)c_{C}(sA)\\
{}&-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{(\overline{\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(sA),Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}(\overline{-\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(sA),Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}}{2r_{Q}},\end{split}

where the first sum comes from the contribution of non-isolated singularities of
X
and the second sum comes from the contribution of a finite set of singular points of
X
. As long as we know everything about the singularities of
X
, we can use (
1.1
) to calculate
h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))
for
0<s<q
. But often we use (
1.1
) in another way: as
h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))
is always an integer, (
1.1
) gives strong restrictions (which we call integrality constraints) on the singularities of
X
, which can be used to determine the contributions of singularities of
X
to the formula although we do not have a full classification of those singularities.

The second ingredient is a Kawamata–Miyaoka type inequality established in our earlier joint work with Jie Liu
[
14
]
(see §
2.4
) which gives an inequality (see Theorem
6.2
)

(1.2)

\displaystyle r_{X}c_{2}(X)\cdot c_{1}(X)-\frac{q^{2}+2q-4}{4q^{2}}r_{X}c_{1}(X)^{3}\geq\sum_{C\subset\Sing(X)}\left(j_{C}-\frac{1}{j_{C}}\right)(-r_{X}K_{X}\cdot C),

where
r_{X}
is the Gorenstein index of
X
,
C
runs over curves in
\Sing(X)
, and
j_{C}
is a local invariant of
C
(see Definition
2.2
).
This formula gives another restriction on non-isolated singularities of
X
, as well as a restriction for other numerical data of
X
such as
r_{X}
,
q
,
c_{1}(X)^{3}
, and
c_{2}(X)\cdot c_{1}(X)
. Indeed, only this inequality itself already gives an upper bound for
q
, but not as good as we expect.

In the application of (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), a natural technical difficulty is to determine the value or the lower bound of the intersection number
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
where
C
is a curve in
\Sing(X)
. This actually depends on the global geometry of
X
and
C
rather than local singularities. Priorly, we only have a naive lower bound
(-K_{X}\cdot C)\geq\frac{1}{r_{X}}
which is not very helpful; actually, we observed in many examples that often
(-K_{X}\cdot C)\geq 1
holds (see Example
5.12
), which suggests that the lower bound could be greatly improved under good conditions.
So we establish a method to give a better lower bound for
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
in §
5
with an algorithm depending on the type of
C
and singularities of
Y
. The idea is to relate the intersection number
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
with Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula for exceptional divisors centered at
C
on
Y
, then we can get some integrality constraints which eventually show that in many cases the denominator of
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
is small, sometimes it is just
1
.

Putting all the above ideas together, we are able to give an algorithm to
find possible numerical data of
X
with
q>66
.
It turns out that any
X
with
q>66
belongs to a list of
36
numerical types (see Theorem
6.4
) with the help of a computer program (which took about 15–30 minutes to run on a laptop). As a comparison, the number of canonical Fano
3
-folds is very huge, for example, there are 674,688 toric canonical Fano
3
-folds
[
17
]
.

Then the final mission is to rule out those
36
numerical types. In all cases, we have a good understanding of curves
C\subset\Sing(X)
and
the intersection numbers
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
, so we can calculate
h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))
up to finitely many choices of the second sum. Then we can rule out
24
numerical types in the list as
h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))
fails to be an integer for some
s
(see §
7
). In order to deal with integrality constraints, often we need to treat congruence equations on square residues.

For the remaining
12
numerical types, it turns out that
h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))
can be computed explicitly. An interesting phenomenon is that in all these
12
cases,

h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))=h^{0}(\mathbb{P}(5,6,22,33),\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(5,6,22,33)}(s))

for
0<s<66
. So there is no contradiction from
h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))
.
In order to rule out the remaining cases, we shall study the geometric structure of
X
in more detail. In the end, we find a contradiction on the algebraically integrable foliation
\mathcal{F}
of rank
2
induced by a refinement of (
1.2
): it turns out that
(-K_{\mathcal{F}})^{2}\cdot L\leq 8
where
L
is a general leaf of
\mathcal{F}
; on the other hand, by the numerical behavior of
h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))
and the geometry of the foliation
\mathcal{F}
, we show that
L\sim sA
for some
s\geq 60
, which implies that
(-K_{\mathcal{F}})^{2}\cdot L\geq(-K_{\mathcal{F}})^{2}\cdot 60A>8
and gives a contradiction (see §
8
–§
10
).

.

.

2.1.
Singularities

Let
X
be a normal variety such that
K_{X}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier. The
Gorenstein index

r_{X}
of
X
is defined as the smallest positive integer
m
such that
mK_{X}
is Cartier.
A prime divisor
E

over

X
is a prime divisor on some model
Y
where
f\colon Y\to X
is a proper birational morphism. The image
f(E)
is called the
center
of
E
on
X
, and
E
is said to be
centered at

f(E)
.
Write

K_{Y}=f^{*}K_{X}+\sum_{E}a(E,X)E,

where
a(E,X)\in\mathbb{Q}
is called the
discrepancy
of
E
. We say that
X
has
terminal
(resp.,
canonical
) singularities if
a(E,X)>0
(resp.,
a(E,X)\geq 0
) for any exceptional divisor
E
over
X
. Often we just simply say that
X
is
terminal
or
canonical
, respectively.

A
crepant center
of
X
is the center of an exceptional divisor
E
over
X
with
a(E,X)=0
.
A
crepant curve
(resp.,
crepant point
) is a crepant center of dimension
1
(resp.,
0
).
Denote by
\Sing(X)
the singular locus of
X
and by
\Sing_{1}(X)
the
1
-dimensional part of
\Sing(X)
.
Here recall that if
X
is a canonical
3
-fold, then
\Sing_{1}(X)
consists of crepant curves as
3
-dimensional terminal singularities are isolated (
[
23
]
*Corollary 5.18).

If
X
is a canonical variety, then by the minimal model program, there exists a
terminalization

f\colon Y\to X
such that
Y
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial terminal variety with
f^{*}K_{X}=K_{Y}
and
f
is projective birational (see
[
2
]
*Corollary 1.4.3).
As
f^{*}K_{X}=K_{Y}
, for a prime exceptional divisor
E
on
Y
,
f(E)
is a crepant center; also
X
is Gorenstein in a neighborhood of
f(E)
if and only if
Y
is Gorenstein in a neighborhood of
E
.

2.2.
Fano indices

Let
X
be a canonical weak Fano variety. The
\mathbb{Q}
-Fano index
of
X
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{\rm q}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X){}

\displaystyle\coloneq\max\{q\mid-K_{X}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}qA,\,A\in\Cl(X)\}.

It is known that
\Cl(X)
is a finitely generated Abelian group, so
\text{\rm q}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X)
is a positive integer. For more details, see
[
12
]
*§ 2 or
[
30
]
.

Recall the following theorem which relates Fano index with anti-canonical degree and other invariants.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
14
]
*Theorem 4.2)
.

Let
X
be a canonical Fano
3
-fold. Let
A
be an ample Weil divisor on
X
such that
-K_{X}\sim qA
for some positive integer
q
. Let
J_{A}
be the smallest positive integer such that
J_{A}A
is Cartier in codimension
2
. Then
J_{A}r_{X}(-K_{X})^{3}
is an integer and the following divisibility constraints hold:

J_{A}\mid q\text{ and }q^{2}\mid J_{A}r_{X}(-K_{X})^{3}.

2.3.
Crepant curves on canonical
3
-folds

We recall basic properties of crepant curves on canonical
3
-folds; generically they behave the same as Du Val singularities which are well-understood.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
X
be a canonical
3
-fold and let
C\subset\Sing(X)
be a crepant curve. Then at a general point of
C
,
X
is analytically isomorphic to
\mathbb{A}^{1}\times S_{C}
where
S_{C}
is a Du Val singularity (see
[
32
]
*Corollary 1.14).

We say that
C\subset\Sing(X)
is
of type
\mathsf{T}
if
S_{C}
is a Du Val singularity of type
\mathsf{T}
, or equivalently, for a general hyperplane
H
on
X
,
H
has Du Val singularities of type
\mathsf{T}
in a neighborhood of any point of
H\cap C
. Here
\mathsf{T}\in\{\mathsf{A}_{n},\mathsf{D}_{m},\mathsf{E}_{k}\mid n\geq 1,m\geq 4,k=6,7,8\}.

We associate the following invariants to
C
:

(1)

e_{C}
is defined to be
1
plus the number of exceptional curves on the minimal resolution of
S_{C}
;

(2)

e^{\prime}_{C}
is defined to be
1
plus the length of the longest chain of exceptional curves on the minimal resolution of
S_{C}
;

(3)

g_{C}
is defined to be the order of the local fundamental group of
S_{C}
;

(4)

j_{C}
is defined to be the order of the Weil divisor class group of
S_{C}
(see
[
19
]
*Remark 4.2.9).

Namely,

\displaystyle(e_{C},e^{\prime}_{C},g_{C},j_{C})=\begin{cases}(n+1,n+1,n+1,n+1)&\text{ if }C\subset\Sing(X)\text{ is of type }\mathsf{A}_{n};\\
(m+1,m,4m-8,4)&\text{ if }C\subset\Sing(X)\text{ is of type }\mathsf{D}_{m};\\
(7,6,24,3)&\text{ if }C\subset\Sing(X)\text{ is of type }\mathsf{E}_{6};\\
(8,7,48,2)&\text{ if }C\subset\Sing(X)\text{ is of type }\mathsf{E}_{7};\\
(9,8,120,1)&\text{ if }C\subset\Sing(X)\text{ is of type }\mathsf{E}_{8}.\end{cases}

Furthermore, let
f\colon Y\to X
be a terminalization and let
S^{\prime}_{C}
be the minimal resolution of
S_{C}
, then any prime
f
-exceptional divisor
E
centered at
C
corresponds to an exceptional curve (i.e., a
(-2)
-curve) of
S^{\prime}_{C}
over
S_{C}
, and vice versa. We denote this correspondence by
E\longleftrightarrow\ell_{E}
.

The following lemma gives the relation between the intersection of a crepant curve with a divisor and the intersection of exceptional divisors centered at this curve with the pullback of the divisor.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
X
be a projective canonical
3
-fold and let
C\subset\Sing(X)
be a crepant curve. Let
f\colon Y\to X
be a terminalization. Keep the notation in Definition
2.2
. Then for any prime
f
-exceptional divisors
E,E^{\prime}
centered at
C
and any
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
H
on
X
,

(E\cdot E^{\prime}\cdot f^{*}H)=(\ell_{E}\cdot\ell_{E^{\prime}})\cdot(H\cdot C).

In particular,

(E\cdot E^{\prime}\cdot K_{Y})=(\ell_{E}\cdot\ell_{E^{\prime}})\cdot(K_{X}\cdot C).

Proof.

The assertion is linear on
H
, so we may assume that
H
is a general very ample divisor on
X
. Then
H
is a projective surface with Du Val singularities by Bertini’s theorem. Take
H^{\prime}\coloneq f^{*}H=f^{-1}H
on
Y
. Then
f|_{H^{\prime}}\colon H^{\prime}\to H
is the minimal resolution of
H
. By construction,
E|_{H^{\prime}}
(resp.,
E^{\prime}|_{H^{\prime}}
) is a disjoint union of
(-2)
-curves
C_{i}
(resp.,
C^{\prime}_{i}
) (
1\leq i\leq(H\cdot C)
) over the points in
H\cap C
. Here
(C_{i}\cdot C_{i}^{\prime})=(\ell_{E}\cdot\ell_{E^{\prime}})
for each
i
via the correspondence
E\longleftrightarrow\ell_{E}
, and
(C_{i}\cdot C^{\prime}_{j})=0
for
i\neq j
. Then it follows that

(E\cdot E^{\prime}\cdot f^{*}H)=(E|_{H^{\prime}}\cdot E^{\prime}|_{H^{\prime}})=(\ell_{E}\cdot\ell_{E^{\prime}})\cdot(H\cdot C).\qed

2.4.
Kawamata–Miyaoka type inequality for canonical Fano threefolds

The following theorem is a detailed version of the Kawamata–Miyaoka type inequality of
[
14
]
*Theorem 3.8. Here we refer to
[
9
]
or
[

]
*§ 3 for the definition of
generalized second Chern class
(or

)
\hat{c}_{2}(X)
of a

.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
14
]
*Theorem 3.8)
.

Let
X
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial canonical Fano
3
-fold of Picard number
1
and let
q\coloneq\text{\rm q}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X)
be the
\mathbb{Q}
-Fano index of
X
. Let

(2.1)

\displaystyle 0=\mathcal{E}_{0}\subsetneq\mathcal{E}_{1}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq\mathcal{E}_{l}=\mathcal{T}_{X}

be the Harder–Narasimhan filtration of the tangent sheaf
\mathcal{T}_{X}
with respect to
c_{1}(X)
, where
1\leq l\leq 3
. Denote by
r_{1}\coloneq\rank\mathcal{E}_{1}
and take
p
to be the integer such that
c_{1}(\mathcal{E}_{l-1})\equiv\frac{p}{q}c_{1}(X)
. Here
p<q
, and
p>\frac{l-1}{l}q
if
l>1
. Then we have

\frac{c_{1}(X)^{3}}{\hat{c}_{2}(X)\cdot c_{1}(X)}\leq\begin{cases}3&\text{if }(l,r_{1})=(1,3);\\
\frac{16}{5}&\text{if }(l,r_{1})=(2,1);\\
\frac{4q^{2}}{p(4q-3p)}\leq\frac{4q^{2}}{q^{2}+2q-3}&\text{if }(l,r_{1})=(2,2);\\
\frac{4q^{2}}{-4p^{2}+6pq-q^{2}}\leq\frac{4q^{2}}{q^{2}+2q-4}&\text{if }(l,r_{1})=(3,1).\end{cases}

Proof.

We refer the reader to
[
14
]
*Proof of Theorem 3.8 with some necessary explanations as the following. Recall that in
[
14
]
*Proof of Theorem 3.8, we denote
\mathcal{F}_{i}\coloneqq(\mathcal{E}_{i}/\mathcal{E}_{i-1})^{**}
and denote by
q_{i}
the unique positive integer such that
c_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\equiv\frac{q_{i}}{q}c_{1}(X)
. Then
p=\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}q_{i}
.

If
(l,r_{1})=(1,3)
, that is,
\mathcal{T}_{X}
is semistable with respect to
c_{1}(X)
, then the inequality follows directly from the
\mathbb{Q}
-Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality
[
22
]
*Lemma 6.5.

If
(l,r_{1})=(2,1)
, then the inequality follows from
[
14
]
*Proof of Theorem 3.8, Case 1.

If
(l,r_{1})=(2,2)
, then
p=q_{1}
and
\frac{2}{3}q<p\leq q-1
.
By
[
14
]
*Proof of Theorem 3.8, Case 2, we have

6\hat{c}_{2}(X)\cdot c_{1}(X)-2c_{1}(X)^{3}\geq-\frac{(3p-2q)^{2}}{2q^{2}}c_{1}(X)^{3},

which yields the desired inequality.

If
(l,r_{1})=(3,1)
, then from
[
14
]
*Proof of Theorem 3.8, Case 3, we have

The condition in Definition

Lemma 2.9
.

Let

be a Du Val singularity and let

be a Weil divisor on

(1)

\mathsf{A}

(2)

\mathsf{A}

Proof.

We may write

(2.2)

\displaystyle\pi^{*}D=D^{\prime}+\sum_{i}a_{i}E_{i}

is the strict transform of

on

are all exceptional curves on

for some

The statement is clear if

\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
,
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
,
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
,
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

If

3.
Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula for surfaces and threefolds

In this section, we recall Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula for Weil divisors on surfaces with Du Val singularities and for Weil divisors satisfying Reid’s condition on
3
-folds with canonical singularities.

3.1.
Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula for surfaces with Du Val singularities

Let
S
be a projective surface with Du Val singularities and let
D
be a Weil divisor on
S
. According to Reid
[
33
]
*(9.1), there is a formula

(3.1)

\displaystyle\chi(S,\mathcal{O}_{S}(D))=\chi(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})+\frac{1}{2}D\cdot(D-K_{S})+\sum_{Q\in\Sing(S)}c_{Q}(D).

Here for a singular point
Q\in\Sing(S)
,
c_{Q}(D)
is defined as the following:

(1)

If
(Q\in S,D)
is a cyclic quotient singularity of type

i(\frac{1}{r}(1,-1))
for some
0\leq i<r

(see
[
33
]
*(8.3)), then
c_{Q}(D)=\frac{-i(r-i)}{2r}
;

(2)

In general, we can locally deform
(Q\in S,D)
into a collection of cyclic quotient singularities
(Q_{\alpha}\in S_{\alpha},D_{\alpha})
of type

i_{\alpha}(\frac{1}{r_{\alpha}}(1,-1))
, and define

c_{Q}(D)=\sum_{\alpha}c_{Q_{\alpha}}(D_{\alpha})=-\sum_{\alpha}\frac{i_{\alpha}(r_{\alpha}-i_{\alpha})}{2r_{\alpha}}.

Note that
c_{Q}(D)
depends only on the local Weil divisor class of
D
at
Q
.

For a crepant curve on a canonical
3
-fold, we introduce the following invariant.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a canonical
3
-fold and let
D
be a Weil divisor on
X
. Let
C\subset\Sing(X)
be a crepant curve. Then at a general point
P\in C
,
X
is analytically isomorphic to a neighborhood of
(0,P_{0})\in\mathbb{A}^{1}\times S_{C}
, where
P_{0}\in S_{C}
is a Du Val singularity. By
[
10
]
*Proposition II.6.6, locally we have a linear equivalence
D\sim\mathbb{A}^{1}\times D_{0}
where
D_{0}
is a Weil divisor on
S_{C}
.
So we just define

c_{C}(D)\coloneq c_{P_{0}}(D_{0}).

Here the definition is well-defined as
c_{P_{0}}(D_{0})
is independent of the choice of the general point
P\in C
: take a general hyperplane section
H\subset X
which intersects
C
transversely at a point
P^{\prime}
in an analytic neighborhood of
P
, then
P^{\prime}\in H
is locally isomorphic to
P_{0}\in S_{C}
via the natural projection
\mathbb{A}^{1}\times S_{C}\to S_{C}
, which implies that
c_{P^{\prime}}(D|_{H})=c_{P_{0}}(D_{0})
. Then the independency follows by varying
P
and
P^{\prime}
along
C
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
(X,A)
be an unmodifiable canonical Fano
3
-fold and let
C\subset\Sing(X)
be a crepant curve of type
\mathsf{A}_{k}
for some
k\leq 3
. Then by Corollary
2.10
,

c_{C}(A)=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{4}&\text{if }C\text{ is of type }\mathsf{A}_{1};\\
-\frac{1}{3}&\text{if }C\text{ is of type }\mathsf{A}_{2};\\
-\frac{3}{8}&\text{if }C\text{ is of type }\mathsf{A}_{3}.\end{cases}

More generally,

c_{C}(sA)=\begin{cases}-\frac{(\overline{s})_{2}(\overline{-s})_{2}}{4}&\text{if }C\text{ is of type }\mathsf{A}_{1};\\
-\frac{(\overline{s})_{3}(\overline{-s})_{3}}{6}&\text{if }C\text{ is of type }\mathsf{A}_{2};\\
-\frac{(\overline{s})_{4}(\overline{-s})_{4}}{8}&\text{if }C\text{ is of type }\mathsf{A}_{3}.\end{cases}

Here for a positive integer
r
and an integer
a
, the symbol
(\overline{a})_{r}
means the smallest non-negative residue of
a
mod
r
.

3.2.
Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula for canonical
3
-folds

Let
X
be a projective canonical
3
-fold. According to Reid
[
33
]
*(10.2), there is a collection of pairs of integers (permitting weights)

B_{X}=\{(r_{i},b_{i})\mid i=1,\dots,s;0<b_{i}\leq\frac{r_{i}}{2};b_{i}\text{ is coprime to }r_{i}\}

associated to
X
, called
Reid’s basket
, where a pair
(r,b)
corresponds to an orbifold point of type
\frac{1}{r}(1,-1,b)
which comes from locally deforming non-Gorenstein singularities of a terminalization of
X
. In other words,
B_{X}
is identified with the collection of virtual orbifold points (a.k.a fictitious singularities) of
X
. For simplicity, we sometimes just call a pair in
B_{X}
an orbifold point of
X
and often formally write

B_{X}=\{Q=(r_{Q},b_{Q})\}.

By definition, if
Y
is a terminalization of
X
, then
B_{Y}=B_{X}
. Denote by
\mathcal{R}_{X}
the collection of
r_{i}
(permitting weights) appearing in
B_{X}
. Note that the Gorenstein index
r_{X}
of
X
is just
\lcm\{r\mid r\in\mathcal{R}_{X}\}
.

Let
D
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor on
X
. We say that
D
satisfies
Reid’s condition
if locally at any point
P\in X
,
D\sim iK_{X}
for some integer
i
(depending on
P
). Note that if
X
has only terminal singularities, then
D
always satisfies Reid’s condition by
[
21
]
*Corollary 5.2.

For
D
satisfying Reid’s condition, according to
[
33
]
*(10.2), there exists an orbifold Riemann–Roch formula, called
Reid’s formula
:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{}&\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))\\
={}&\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})+\frac{1}{12}D\cdot(D-K_{X})\cdot(2D-K_{X})+\frac{1}{12}c_{2}(X)\cdot D+\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}c_{Q}(D),\end{split}

where the last sum runs over Reid’s basket of orbifold points. The term
c_{2}(X)\cdot D
is defined to be
c_{2}(W)\cdot\pi^{*}D
for any resolution
\pi\colon W\to X
.

For an orbifold point
Q=(r_{Q},b_{Q})\in B_{X}
,
c_{Q}(D)
is defined as the following: the local index
\mathsf{i}_{D,Q}
of
D
at
Q
is defined to be
an integer
i\geq 0
such that
D\sim iK_{X}
around
Q
after taking terminalization and local deformation, where
\mathsf{i}_{D,Q}
is determined up to modulo
r_{Q}
and well-defined as
D
satisfies Reid’s condition; then

c_{Q}(D)\coloneq-\frac{\mathsf{i}_{D,Q}(r_{Q}^{2}-1)}{12r_{Q}}+\sum_{j=0}^{\mathsf{i}_{D,Q}-1}\frac{(\overline{jb_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}(\overline{-jb_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}}{2r_{Q}}.

Here for a positive integer
r
and an integer
a
, the symbol
(\overline{a})_{r}
means the smallest non-negative residue of
a
mod
r
; we set
\sum_{j=0}^{-1}\coloneq 0
.

In particular, by
[
33
]
*(10.3), we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle c_{2}(X)\cdot c_{1}(X)+\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{X}}\left(r-\frac{1}{r}\right)=24\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}),

and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c_{1}(X)^{3}+3\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{b_{Q}(r_{Q}-b_{Q})}{2r_{Q}}=\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(-K_{X}))\in\mathbb{Z}.

Remark 3.3
.

For a canonical weak Fano
3
-fold
X
,
\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})=1
by the Kawamata–Viehweg vanishing theorem
[
20
]
*Theorem 1.2.5 and
c_{2}(X)\cdot c_{1}(X)>0
by
[
11
]
*Corollary 7.3.

The following lemma is an easy and well-known consequence of (
3.2
).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a projective canonical
3
-fold and let
D
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor on
X
satisfying Reid’s condition. Then

\displaystyle\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))-\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D+K_{X}))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}\cdot K_{X}+2\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{(\overline{\mathsf{i}_{D,Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}(\overline{-\mathsf{i}_{D,Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}}{2r_{Q}}.

Proof.

By (
3.2
),

\displaystyle\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))-\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D+K_{X}))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}\cdot K_{X}-\frac{1}{12}c_{2}(X)\cdot K_{X}+\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}(c_{Q}(D)-c_{Q}(D+K_{X})).

For an orbifold point
Q\in B_{X}
, we have

\mathsf{i}_{D+K_{X},Q}\equiv\mathsf{i}_{D,Q}+\mathsf{i}_{K_{X},Q}\equiv\mathsf{i}_{D,Q}+1\bmod r_{Q}.

Hence

c_{Q}(D)-c_{Q}(D+K_{X})=\frac{r_{Q}^{2}-1}{12r_{Q}}-\frac{(\overline{\mathsf{i}_{D,Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}(\overline{-\mathsf{i}_{D,Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}}{2r_{Q}}.

Then the conclusion follows from (
3.3
).
∎

4.
A Riemann–Roch formula for canonical threefolds

In the study of Fano indices of canonical Fano
3
-folds, we have to consider Weil divisors which do not satisfy Reid’s condition (e.g., divisors that are not Cartier in codimension
2
), for which Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula is not applicable.

In this section, we establish a Riemann–Roch formula for canonical
3
-folds based on Reid’s formula. The idea is to take a special transform called the Weil pullback of the divisor on
X
to a terminalization, and then apply Reid’s formula on the terminalization.

4.1.
Sequential terminalizations and Weil pullbacks

As we mentioned in the introduction, for a terminalization
f\colon Y\to X
and a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor
D
on
X
, in general
f^{*}D
is not a Weil divisor. To make it a Weil divisor we might just consider the round down
\lfloor f^{*}D\rfloor
, but then the cohomologies of
\lfloor f^{*}D\rfloor
usually differ from those of
D
. So in order to get a good Weil divisor on
Y
from
D
, we consider a special kind of terminalization called sequential terminalization
which is decomposed into a sequence of birational morphisms and do the pullback and round down operations via this sequence.

Definition 4.1

(Sequential terminalization)
.

Let
X
be a canonical variety. A projective birational morphism
f\colon Y\to X
together with a sequence of projective birational morphisms
f_{k}\colon X_{k}\to X_{k-1}
for
1\leq k\leq m
is called a
sequential terminalization
if the following properties are satisfied:

(1)

Y
is
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial terminal;

(2)

X_{0}=X
,
X_{m}=Y
,
f=f_{1}\circ\dots\circ f_{m}
;

(3)

for any
1\leq k\leq m
,
f_{k}
is crepant, i.e.,
f_{k}^{*}K_{X_{k-1}}=K_{X_{k}}
;

(4)

for any
1\leq k\leq m
, denote by
E_{k}
the sum of exceptional divisors of
f_{k}
, then either
E_{k}=0
, or
E_{k}
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier prime divisor and
-E_{k}
is
f_{k}
-nef.

For simplicity, we just say that
f\colon Y\to X
is a sequential terminalization.

Remark 4.2
.

(1)

We can always construct a sequential terminalization by applying
[
2
]
*Corollary 1.4.3 to extract one prime divisor at a time. See also
[
18
]
*Corollary 2.6.

(2)

If
f\colon Y\to X
is a sequential terminalization and
U\subset X
is an open subset, then
f
naturally induces a sequential terminalization
f^{-1}(U)\to U
.

Definition 4.3

(Weil pullback)
.

Let
X
be a canonical variety and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a sequential terminalization. For any
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor
D
on
X
, the
Weil pullback

f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)
of
D
on
Y
is defined as the following: keep the notation in Definition
4.1
, take
D_{0}=D
, and define inductively
D_{k}=\lfloor f_{k}^{*}D_{k-1}\rfloor
for
1\leq k\leq m
, and finally set
f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)\coloneq D_{m}
. Here we can inductively show that
D_{k}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier since
f_{k}^{*}D_{k-1}
and
E_{k}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier. So
f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor on
Y
.

One key good property of Weil pullback is that it preserves cohomologies.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X
be a canonical variety and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a sequential terminalization. Then for any
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor
D
on
X
and any integer
i\geq 0
, we have

H^{i}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}(f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)))=H^{i}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D)).

Proof.

Keep the notation in Definition
4.1
and Definition
4.3
. It suffices to prove that

H^{i}(X_{k},\mathcal{O}_{X_{k}}(D_{k}))=H^{i}(X_{k-1},\mathcal{O}_{X_{k-1}}(D_{k-1}))

for every
1\leq k\leq m
and
i\geq 0
.

By construction, we have
D_{k}=\lfloor f_{k}^{*}D_{k-1}\rfloor=f_{k}^{*}D_{k-1}-a_{k}E_{k}
for some
0\leq a_{k}<1
. So
f_{k*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{k}}(D_{k})=\mathcal{O}_{X_{k-1}}(D_{k-1})
by
[
27
]
*Lemma II.2.11. Moreover,
D_{k}
is
f_{k}
-nef and
f_{k}
-big and
K_{X_{k}}
is
f_{k}
-trivial as
f_{k}
is crepant. Hence the Kawamata–Viehweg vanishing theorem
[
20
]
*Theorem 1.2.5 yields that

R^{j}f_{k*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{k}}(D_{k})=R^{j}f_{k*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{k}}(K_{X_{k}}+D_{k}-K_{X_{k}})=0

for
j>0
. By a spectral sequence argument, it follows that

H^{i}(X_{k},\mathcal{O}_{X_{k}}(D_{k}))=H^{i}(X_{k-1},f_{k*}\mathcal{O}_{X_{k}}(D_{k}))=H^{i}(X_{k-1},\mathcal{O}_{X_{k-1}}(D_{k-1}))

for every
i\geq 0
.
∎

The Weil pullback behaves not so well as it is not additive but only superadditive, namely, for
2
divisors
D
and
D^{\prime}
,
f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D+D^{\prime})\geq f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)+f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D^{\prime})
. We have additivity in the following case which is useful.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
X
be a canonical variety and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a sequential terminalization. Let
D
and
D^{\prime}
be
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisors on
X
. If the usual pullback
f^{*}D^{\prime}
is a Weil divisor on
Y
, then
f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D+D^{\prime})=f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)+f^{*}D^{\prime}
; in particular,
f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D^{\prime})=f^{*}D^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Keep the notation in Definition
4.1
and Definition
4.3
. Take
D^{\prime}_{0}=D^{\prime}
, and define inductively
D^{\prime}_{k}=f_{k}^{*}D^{\prime}_{k-1}
for
1\leq k\leq m
. Then as
f^{*}D^{\prime}
is a Weil divisor,
D^{\prime}_{k}
is a Weil divisor for
1\leq k\leq m
. This implies that

\lfloor f_{k}^{*}(D_{k-1}+D^{\prime}_{k-1})\rfloor=\lfloor f_{k}^{*}D_{k-1}+f_{k}^{*}D^{\prime}_{k-1}\rfloor=\lfloor f_{k}^{*}D_{k-1}\rfloor+f_{k}^{*}D^{\prime}_{k-1}=D_{k}+D^{\prime}_{k}

for
1\leq k\leq m
. So we get
f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D+D^{\prime})=f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)+f^{*}D^{\prime}
.
∎

To conclude this subsection, we prove the following proposition which determines the difference between certain intersection numbers of the Weil pullback and the original divisor. It is an application of Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula for surfaces.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
X
be a projective canonical
3
-fold and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a sequential terminalization. Let
D
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor on
X
. Then for any
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
H
on
X
,

(4.1)

\displaystyle(f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D))^{2}\cdot f^{*}H-D^{2}\cdot H=\sum_{C\subset\Sing(X)}2(H\cdot C)c_{C}(D).

Proof.

By linearity, it suffices to prove (
4.1
) for
H
very ample. Take a general very ample divisor
H
on
X
and denote
H^{\prime}\coloneq f^{*}H=f^{-1}_{*}H
on
Y
. Denote
S\coloneq f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)
. As
H
is Cartier, by Lemma
4.5
,

f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D-H)={S}-H^{\prime}.

So by Lemma
4.4
,

\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))-\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D-H))=\chi(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}(S))-\chi(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}(S-H^{\prime})),

which implies that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\chi(H,\mathcal{O}_{H}(D|_{H}))=\chi(H^{\prime},\mathcal{O}_{H^{\prime}}(S|_{H^{\prime}})).

As
H
is a surface with Du Val singularities and
H^{\prime}
is smooth by Bertini’s theorem, by (
3.1
) and the usual Riemann–Roch formula, we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle\chi(H,\mathcal{O}_{H}(D|_{H}))={}

\displaystyle\chi(H,\mathcal{O}_{H})+\frac{1}{2}D|_{H}\cdot(D|_{H}-K_{H})+\sum_{Q\in\Sing(H)}c_{Q}(D|_{H});

(4.4)

\displaystyle\chi(H^{\prime},\mathcal{O}_{H^{\prime}}(S|_{H^{\prime}}))={}

\displaystyle\chi(H^{\prime},\mathcal{O}_{H^{\prime}})+\frac{1}{2}S|_{H^{\prime}}\cdot(S|_{H^{\prime}}-K_{H^{\prime}}).

As
f_{*}S=D
, by the projection formula, we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle(D|_{H}\cdot K_{H})=(D\cdot(K_{X}+H)\cdot H)=(S\cdot(K_{Y}+H^{\prime})\cdot H^{\prime})=(S|_{H^{\prime}}\cdot K_{H^{\prime}}).

Also we have
\chi(H,\mathcal{O}_{H})=\chi(H^{\prime},\mathcal{O}_{H^{\prime}})
as
H^{\prime}\to H
is birational and
H
has only rational singularities. So combining (
4.2
)–(
4.5
), we have

S^{2}\cdot H^{\prime}-D^{2}\cdot H=\sum_{Q\in\Sing(H)}2c_{Q}(D|_{H}).

Note that by Bertini’s theorem, the singularities of
H
are exactly those points in
H\cap C
for all crepant curves
C\subset\Sing(X)
, so by Definition
3.1
,

\sum_{Q\in\Sing(H)}c_{Q}(D|_{H})=\sum_{C\subset\Sing(X)}\sum_{Q\in H\cap C}c_{Q}(D|_{H})=\sum_{C\subset\Sing(X)}(H\cdot C)c_{C}(D).

This proves (
4.1
).
∎

4.2.
A Riemann–Roch formula

After the preparation of §
4.1
, we are able to prove the main theorem of this section, which is a Riemann–Roch formula (in a weak form) for
Weil divisors on canonical
3
-folds.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
X
be a projective canonical
3
-fold and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a sequential terminalization. Let
D
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor on
X
. Then

\displaystyle\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))-\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D+K_{X}))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}\cdot K_{X}+2\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})+\sum_{C\subset\Sing(X)}(-K_{X}\cdot C)c_{C}(D)

\displaystyle-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{(\overline{\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D),Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}(\overline{-\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D),Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}}{2r_{Q}}.

Here recall that
\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D),Q}
makes sense for the Weil divisor
f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)
on
Y
as
B_{Y}=B_{X}
and
Y
is terminal.

Proof.

Denote
S\coloneq f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)
. As
f
is crepant, by Lemma
4.5
,

f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D+K_{X})={S}+K_{Y}.

So by Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
3.4
,

\displaystyle\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))-\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D+K_{X}))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\chi(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}(S))-\chi(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}(S+K_{Y}))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}S^{2}\cdot K_{Y}+2\chi(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y})-\sum_{Q\in B_{Y}}\frac{(\overline{\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D),Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}(\overline{-\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D),Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}}{2r_{Q}}.

The conclusion follows from Proposition
4.6
for
H=K_{X}
and the fact that
\chi(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y})=\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
as
X
has rational singularities.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

In Theorem
4.7
, by Lemma
4.4
and (
3.2
) we can get a more general form of Riemann–Roch formula:

\displaystyle\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))={}

\displaystyle\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})+\frac{1}{12}f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)\cdot(f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)-K_{Y})\cdot(2f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)-K_{Y})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{12}c_{2}(Y)\cdot f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)+\sum_{Q\in B_{Y}}c_{Q}(f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)).

But in order to make use of this formula, we need to understand
f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)
in more detail. For example, we need to calculate the intersection numbers
(f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D))^{3}
and
c_{2}(Y)\cdot f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D)
. This requires a better understanding of singular points of
X
rather than generic points of crepant curves. So in Theorem
4.7
we consider the form
\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))-\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D+K_{X}))
which cancels those terms that are difficult to compute.

One can compare this formula with
[
5
]
*Theorem 2.1 where a Riemann–Roch formula for
3
-folds with only quotient singularities (but not necessarily canonical singularities) is given.

As a corollary, we have the following theorem which can be effectively applied to problems on Fano indices of canonical Fano
3
-folds.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
X
be a canonical Fano
3
-fold and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a sequential terminalization. Suppose that
-K_{X}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}qA
for some positive rational number
q
and some ample Weil divisor
A
. Then for any integer
s
with
0<s<q
,

\displaystyle h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))={}

\displaystyle-\frac{s^{2}}{2}A^{2}\cdot K_{X}+2+\sum_{C\subset\Sing(X)}(-K_{X}\cdot C)c_{C}(sA)

\displaystyle-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{(\overline{\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(sA),Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}(\overline{-\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(sA),Q}b_{Q}})_{r_{Q}}}{2r_{Q}}.

Proof.

Since
-K_{X}
is ample, all higher cohomologies of
sA
and
sA+K_{X}
vanish
by the Kawamata–Viehweg vanishing theorem
[
20
]
*Theorem 1.2.5. Also
h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA+K_{X}))=0
as
sA+K_{X}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}(s-q)A
and
s<q
. So we have

\displaystyle\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})=h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})=1,

\displaystyle\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA))=h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA)),

\displaystyle\chi(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(sA+K_{X}))=0.

Then the conclusion follows directly from Theorem
4.7
for
D=sA
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

In the applications of Theorem
4.9
, the technical difficulty is to determine the contributions of singularities: we have to handle values of
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
,
c_{C}(sA)
, and
{\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(sA),Q}}
. We will treat the first two in §
5
and §
7.1
. On the other hand, determining
{\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(sA),Q}}
is much harder due to the complicated definition of Weil pullback; the problem is that
{\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(sA),Q}}
is not linear in
s
(see Lemma
4.5
) and we do not have a good way to calculate it due to the lack of classification of singularities. However, as we can usually determine
B_{X}
,
{\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(sA),Q}}
takes value in a finite set and often we can determine it from the integrality constraints discussed in §
4.3
.

4.3.
Integrality constraints

An important phenomenon for the Riemann–Roch formula is that the Euler characteristic is always an integer while the contribution from singularities is only a sum of rational numbers. This provides many integrality constraints which give strong restrictions on singularities.
In this subsection, we explore several integrality constraints that will be used later.

We will frequently use the fact that for a positive integer
r
and an integer
a
,

a(r-a)\equiv(\overline{a})_{r}(\overline{-a})_{r}\bmod 2r.

As a direct consequence of Theorem
4.7
, we have the following integrality constraint.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
X
be a projective canonical
3
-fold and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a sequential terminalization. Let
D
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor on
X
. Then for any positive integer
r^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle-\frac{r^{\prime}}{2}D^{2}\cdot K_{X}+\sum_{C\subset\Sing(X)}(-r^{\prime}K_{X}\cdot C)c_{C}(D)-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{r^{\prime}\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D),Q}b_{Q}(r_{Q}-\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D),Q}b_{Q})}{2r_{Q}}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Remark 4.12
.

In Corollary
4.11
, usually some terms are ignorable by the following facts:

Suppose that
C
is of type
\mathsf{A}_{j_{C}-1}
and
(-r^{\prime}K_{X}\cdot C)\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
(-r^{\prime}K_{X}\cdot C)c_{C}(D)\in\mathbb{Z}
holds under one of the following conditions:

(1)

2\nmid j_{C}
and
j_{C}\mid(-r^{\prime}K_{X}\cdot C)
;

(2)

2\mid j_{C}
and
2j_{C}\mid(-r^{\prime}K_{X}\cdot C)
.

For a positive integer
r
and an integer
a
,
\frac{r^{\prime}a(r-a)}{2r}\in\mathbb{Z}
holds under one of the following conditions:

(1)

2\nmid r
and
r\mid r^{\prime}
;

(2)

2\mid r
and
2r\mid r^{\prime}
.

The following two corollaries are special cases of Corollary
4.11
.

Corollary 4.13
.

Let
X
be a projective canonical
3
-fold and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a sequential terminalization. Let
D
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor on
X
which is Cartier in codimension
2
. Then

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}\cdot K_{X}-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{{\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D),Q}b_{Q}}({r_{Q}-\mathsf{i}_{f^{\lfloor*\rfloor}(D),Q}b_{Q}})}{2r_{Q}}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

As
D
is Cartier in codimension
2
,
c_{C}(D)=0
for any crepant curve
C
. So we get the conclusion from Corollary
4.11
for
r^{\prime}=1
.
∎

Corollary 4.14
.

Let
X
be a projective canonical
3
-fold and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a sequential terminalization. Let
D
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor on
X
. Then

-r_{X}D^{2}\cdot K_{X}+\sum_{C\subset\Sing(X)}2(-r_{X}K_{X}\cdot C)c_{C}(D)\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

This follows from Corollary
4.11
for
r^{\prime}=2r_{X}
and Remark
4.12
.
∎

Besides the integrality constraint provided by Theorem
4.7
, we also have integrality constraints provided by Reid’s Riemann–Roch formula for exceptional divisors on a (not necessarily sequential) terminalization. Those constraints will be essentially used to estimate the intersection number of a crepant curve with the anti-canonical divisor in §
5
.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
X
be a projective canonical
3
-fold and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a terminalization. Let
C\subset\Sing(X)
be a crepant curve and let
E,E^{\prime}
be prime
f
-exceptional divisors centered at
C
. Then we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle(K_{X}\cdot C)-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{{\mathsf{i}_{E,Q}b_{Q}}(r_{Q}-{\mathsf{i}_{E,Q}b_{Q}})}{2r_{Q}}\in\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle-(\ell_{E}\cdot\ell_{E^{\prime}})\cdot(K_{X}\cdot C)+\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{{\mathsf{i}_{E,Q}\mathsf{i}_{E^{\prime},Q}b_{Q}^{2}}}{r_{Q}}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
3.4
to
E
,
E^{\prime}
, and
E+E^{\prime}
on
Y
respectively, we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}E^{2}\cdot K_{Y}-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{{i_{Q}b_{Q}}(r_{Q}-i_{Q}b_{Q})}{2r_{Q}}\in\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}E^{\prime 2}\cdot K_{Y}-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{{i^{\prime}_{Q}b_{Q}}(r_{Q}-i^{\prime}_{Q}b_{Q})}{2r_{Q}}\in\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(E+E^{\prime})^{2}\cdot K_{Y}-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{{(i_{Q}b_{Q}+i^{\prime}_{Q}b_{Q})}(r_{Q}-i_{Q}b_{Q}-i^{\prime}_{Q}b_{Q})}{2r_{Q}}\in\mathbb{Z},

where
i_{Q}\coloneq\mathsf{i}_{E,Q}
and
i^{\prime}_{Q}\coloneq\mathsf{i}_{E^{\prime},Q}
. It follows that

(4.8)

\displaystyle-(E\cdot E^{\prime}\cdot K_{Y})+\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{{i_{Q}i^{\prime}_{Q}b_{Q}^{2}}}{r_{Q}}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Then we get the conclusion by (
4.7
), (
4.8
), and Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Similarly, for an exceptional divisor centered over a crepant point, we have the following integrality constraint, which is closely related to Kawakita’s result on crepant points
[
18
]
.

Proposition 4.16
.

Let
X
be a projective canonical
3
-fold and let
f\colon Y\to X
be a terminalization. Let
E
be a prime
f
-exceptional divisor centered at a crepant point. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{{\mathsf{i}_{E,Q}b_{Q}}(r_{Q}-{\mathsf{i}_{E,Q}b_{Q}})}{2r_{Q}}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
3.4
to
E
on
Y
, we have

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}E^{2}\cdot K_{Y}-\sum_{Q\in B_{X}}\frac{{\mathsf{i}_{E,Q}b_{Q}}(r_{Q}-{\mathsf{i}_{E,Q}b_{Q}})}{2r_{Q}}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Note that
f(E)
is a point, hence
E^{2}\cdot K_{Y}=E^{2}\cdot f^{*}K_{X}=0
by the projection formula.
∎

Example 4.17
.

In Proposition
4.16
, suppose that
f(E)=P
and the Gorenstein index
r_{P}
at
P
is
2
, then by
[
18
]
*Theorem 1.1, Table 2 (see also Remark
2.4
),
B_{X}
contains
4
orbifold points
\{4\times(2,1)\}
, which we denote by
Q_{i}

(1\leq i\leq 4)
. We assume further that
r_{Q}>2
for any orbifold point
Q\in B_{X}
such that
Q\neq Q_{i}

(1\leq i\leq 4)
. Under this assumption, for
Q\neq Q_{i}

(1\leq i\leq 4)
, we have
\mathsf{i}_{E,Q}=0
as
f(E)=P
and
r_{Q}>r_{P}
. Then by Proposition

, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{\mathsf{i}_{E,Q_{i}}(2-\mathsf{i}_{E,Q_{i}})}{4}\in\mathbb{Z},

which implies that

\mathsf{i}_{E,Q_{1}}\equiv\mathsf{i}_{E,Q_{2}}\equiv\mathsf{i}_{E,Q_{3}}\equiv\mathsf{i}_{E,Q_{4}}\bmod 2.

5.
Lower bounds of degrees of crepant curves

In order to apply Theorem
2.6
and Theorem
4.9
, one crucial technical problem is to determine the intersection number
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
for any crepant curve
C\subset\Sing(X)
. Sometimes we just call
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
the
degree
of the crepant curve
C
(with respect to
-K_{X}
).
There is a naive lower bound
(-K_{X}\cdot C)\geq\frac{1}{r_{X}}

as
-r_{X}K_{X}
is Cartier and ample, but this is far from being sufficient for our purpose. After calculating many examples, we found that often
(-K_{X}\cdot C)\geq 1
holds (see Example
5.12
), which suggests that the lower bound of
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
could be greatly improved.

The main goal of this section is to give a good estimate on the lower bound of
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
(see Theorem
5.11
). The key idea is to use the integrality constraint in Proposition
4.15
to control the denominator of
(-K_{X}\cdot C)
and the proof is elementary.

First we set up the notation for this section.

Setting 5.1
.

(1)

Let
X
be a canonical Fano
3
-fold. Denote Reid’s basket of
X
by

B_{X}=\{(r_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(r_{s},b_{s})\}

and

\mathcal{R}\coloneq\mathcal{R}_{X}=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{s}\}.

Recall that the Gorenstein index of
X
is
r_{X}=\lcm\{r_{i}\in\mathcal{R}\}
. Recall that we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(r_{j}-\frac{1}{r_{j}}\right)<24

by (
3.3
) and Remark
3.3
.

(2)

For a prime number
p
, denote by
\nu_{p}\colon\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\infty\}
the
p
-adic valuation on
\mathbb{Q}
. We use
n_{p^{e}}
to denote the number of
r_{i}
’s in
\mathcal{R}
with
\nu_{p}(r_{i})=e
. Note that by (
5.1
),
n_{p^{e}}=0
if

Remark 5.10
.

We give some easy properties of the function
\LB\colon\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
defined in Algorithm
5.9
.

(1)

\LB
depends only on
\mathcal{R}_{X}
, and
\LB
takes values in the divisors of
r_{X}
;

(2)

\LB
is non-decreasing; moreover, if
N\leq N^{\prime}
, then
\LB(N)
divides
\LB(N^{\prime})
;

(3)

\LB(2)=1
;

(4)

If elements in
\mathcal{R}_{X}
are pairwisely coprime and square free (i.e.,
\nu_{p}(r_{X})\leq 1
and
n_{p}\leq 1
for any prime number

), then
\LB(4)=r_{X}
; if moreover
3\nmid r_{X}
, then
\LB(3)=r_{X}
.

The following is the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
X
be a canonical Fano
3
-fold and let
C\subset\Sing(X)
be a crepant curve. Then
\LB(e^{\prime}_{C})
divides
(-r_{X}K_{X}\cdot C)
. In particular,

(-r_{X}K_{X}\cdot C)\geq\LB(e^{\prime}_{C}).

Proof.

Keep Setting
5.1
. Recall that
l=e^{\prime}_{C}-1
and
c=(K_{X}\cdot C)
.

For any prime number
p\leq 23
, by the construction of
f_{p}
, we have

\nu_{p}(c)\geq-\nu_{p}(r_{X})+\nu_{p}(f_{p}(l+1))=-\nu_{p}(r_{X})+\nu_{p}(f_{p}(e^{\prime}_{C}))

by Corollary
5.5
, Proposition
5.6
, and Proposition
5.8
(see Algorithm
5.9
for more precise references in the construction of
f_{p}
). So for any prime number
p\leq 23
,
\nu_{p}(r_{X}c)\geq\nu_{p}(\LB(e^{\prime}_{C}))
. By construction, all prime factors of
\LB(e^{\prime}_{C})
are at most
23
, so
\LB(e^{\prime}_{C})
divides
(-r_{X}K_{X}\cdot C)
.
∎

We conclude this section by computing a concrete example which motivated us to formulate Theorem

Example 5.12
.

Consider

in Example

. Then

and

contains

of types

where

and

are the unique elements. Here note that both

and

7.1.
Determining types and degrees of crepant curves

In order to apply Corollary
4.11
or Theorem

, it is crucial to determine
(-r_{X}K_{X}\cdot C)
and

for a crepant curve

. For the former one, we need to know its exact value rather than a lower bound given by Theorem
5.11
; for the latter one, we need to know the type of

. We will address these two issues in this subsection.

First we deal with crepant curves that are not of type
\mathsf{A}_{1}
. In most of the cases, we can determine the types and degrees of such curves by Theorem
6.2
. For example, if the degree of the curve

is strictly larger than the lower bound
\LB(j_{C})
in Table

, then
we will often get a contradiction by Theorem
6.2
as
\nabla_{X}
is already known.

Proposition 7.1
.

Keep Setting
6.1
. Suppose that
J_{A}>2
. We may write

J_{A}=p_{0}^{a_{0}}p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\dots p_{k}^{a_{k}}

where
p_{i}
are distinct prime numbers with
2=p_{0}<p_{1}<p_{2}<\dots<p_{k}
,
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
are positive integers and
a_{0}\geq 0
.

(1)

If
a_{0}\leq 1
and

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}<\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(p_{i}^{a_{i}}-\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}}}\right)\LB(p_{i}^{a_{i}})+\left(p_{1}-\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)\LB(p_{1}),

then
\Sing_{1}(X)
consists of crepant curves
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
where
C_{i}
is of type
\mathsf{A}_{p_{i}^{a_{i}}-1}
with

for

, and possibly several crepant curves of type
\mathsf{A}_{1}
; moreover, there is no crepant curve of type
\mathsf{A}_{1}
if
a_{0}=0
.

(2)

If
a_{0}\geq 2
and

\nabla_{X}<\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(p_{i}^{a_{i}}-\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}}}\right)\LB(p_{i}^{a_{i}})+\left(p^{\prime}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\right)\LB(p^{\prime}),

where
p^{\prime}=\min\{4,p_{1}\}
, then
\Sing_{1}(X)
consists of crepant curves
C_{0},\dots,C_{k}
where
C_{i}
is of type
\mathsf{A}_{p_{i}^{a_{i}}-1}
with
(-r_{X}K_{X}\cdot C_{i})=\LB(p_{i}^{a_{i}})
for
0\leq i\leq k
, and possibly several crepant curves of type
\mathsf{A}_{1}
.

Proof.

All crepant curves are of type
\mathsf{A}
by Corollary
2.10
; also there is no crepant curve of type
\mathsf{A}_{1}
if
a_{0}=0
as
2\nmid J_{A}
. Recall that each
p_{i}^{a_{i}}
divides at least one
j_{C}
for some crepant curve
C\subset\Sing(X)
by the definition of
J_{A}
. Conversely, by Corollary
2.10
,
j_{C}
divides
J_{A}
for any crepant curve
C\subset\Sing(X)
.

(1)
Step 1
. We show that there are distinct crepant curves
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\subset\Sing(X)
such that
p_{i}^{a_{i}}\mid j_{C_{i}}
for
1\leq i\leq k
; moreover,
j_{C_{i}}=p_{i}^{a_{i}}
for
1\leq i\leq k
.

Suppose that
p_{i_{0}}^{a_{i_{0}}}p_{i_{1}}^{a_{i_{1}}}\mid j_{C^{\prime}}
for some crepant curve
C^{\prime}\subset\Sing(X)
and
1\leq i_{0}<i_{1}\leq k
, then by Theorem
6.2
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}\geq{}

9.
Geometry of foliations of rank two

We gather some basic notions and facts regarding foliations on varieties. We refer the reader to
[
8
]
*§ 3 and the references therein for a more detailed explanation.

Definition 9.1
.

A
foliation
on a normal variety
X
is a non-zero coherent subsheaf
\mathcal{F}
of the tangent sheaf
\mathcal{T}_{X}
such that

(1)

\mathcal{T}_{X}/\mathcal{F}
is torsion-free, and

(2)

\mathcal{F}
is closed under the Lie bracket.

The
canonical divisor
of a foliation
\mathcal{F}
is any Weil divisor
K_{\mathcal{F}}
on
X
such that
\det(\mathcal{F})\cong\mathcal{O}_{X}(-K_{\mathcal{F}})
.

Let
X_{\circ}\subset X_{\reg}
be the largest open subset over which
\mathcal{T}_{X}/\mathcal{F}
is locally free. A
leaf
of
\mathcal{F}
is a maximal connected and immersed holomorphic submanifold
L\subset X_{\circ}
such that
\mathcal{T}_{L}=\mathcal{F}|_{L}
. A leaf is called
algebraic
if it is open in its Zariski closure and a foliation
\mathcal{F}
is said to be
algebraically integrable
if its leaves are algebraic.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an algebraically integrable foliation on a normal projective variety
X
. Then there exists a diagram, called the
family of leaves
, as follows (
[
8
]
*§ 3.6)

(9.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}

with the following properties:

(1)

U
and
T
are normal projective varieties;

(2)

e
is birational and
g
is an equidimensional fibration;

(3)

For a general
t\in T
,
e
is finite on
g^{-1}(t)
and the image
e(g^{-1}(t))
is the closure of a leaf of
\mathcal{F}
.

Assume in addition that
K_{\mathcal{F}}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier, then there exists a canonically defined effective

\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
\Delta_{U}
such that

(9.2)

\displaystyle K_{U}-g^{*}K_{T}-R(g)+\Delta_{U}\sim K_{e^{-1}\mathcal{F}}+\Delta_{U}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}e^{*}K_{\mathcal{F}},

where

(9.3)

\displaystyle R(g)\coloneq\sum_{P}(g^{*}P-(g^{*}P)_{\red})

is the

of

, where
P
runs through all prime divisors on

, see
[

*§ 3.6.

We are mainly interested in algebraically integrable foliations of rank

on a

whose general fibers are smooth.

Let
\mathcal{F}

The following theorem allows us to understand the general fibers under good numerical conditions.

Theorem 9.3
.

Let

be a
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial canonical Fano variety of dimension

and of Picard number

. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a foliation of rank

on

(1)

\mathcal{F}
is algebraically integrable;

(2)

Let
F
be a general fiber of

in (

-K_{\mathcal{F}}+\frac{5}{6}K_{X}
is ample, then there exists a birational morphism

for some

(1) As
\mu_{c_{1}(X),\min}(\mathcal{F})>0
, by
[

]
*Theorem 1.1 or

]
*Proposition 2.2,
\mathcal{F}
is algebraically integrable and a general fiber

of

in (

) is rationally connected.

(2) As

is of Picard number
1
, we may write
-K_{\mathcal{F}}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}-uK_{X}
where

by assumption.

As
V
has only terminal singularities,

is a smooth rationally connected surface as the natural map
\pi|_{F}:F\to G
between general fibers is birational. By Lemma

, there exists an effective
\mathbb{Q}

such that

-K_{F}-\Delta_{F}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}\mu^{*}(-K_{\mathcal{F}})|_{F}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}\mu^{*}(-uK_{X})|_{F}

is nef and big. On the other hand, as

has only canonical singularities, there exists an effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
\Delta^{\prime}
on

such that

. Hence there exists an effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor

such that

-K_{F}+\Delta^{\prime}_{F}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}\mu^{*}(-K_{X})|_{F}.

By a standard minimal model program, there exists a birational morphism

where

is either
\mathbb{P}^{2}
or a Hirzebruch surface
\mathbb{F}_{n}
for some

.

First, we claim that

is neither
\mathbb{P}^{2}
nor
\mathbb{F}_{0}
. Suppose that

is either
\mathbb{P}^{2}
or
\mathbb{F}_{0}
, then
\pi|_{F}\colon F\to G
is the identity morphism as

admits no non-trivial birational contraction. Hence the coefficients of

are at least

by

]
*Proposition 3.5. If

, then for a line

which is a contradiction.
If

, then there exists a ruling
\ell
on

such that
(\Delta_{F}\cdot\ell)\geq 1
and hence

\frac{5}{6}<u=\frac{\mu^{*}(-uK_{X})|_{F}\cdot\ell}{\mu^{*}(-K_{X})|_{F}\cdot\ell}=\frac{(-K_{F}-\Delta_{F})\cdot\ell}{(-K_{F}+\Delta^{\prime}_{F})\cdot\ell}\leq\frac{1}{2},

which is a contradiction.

is either
\mathbb{P}^{2}
or
\mathbb{F}_{0}
, then

, which dominates

. Hence we conclude that there exists a birational morphism

for some

.

. Let

be the strict transforms of

on

. Then
\sigma_{0}^{\prime 2}\leq\sigma_{0}^{2}=-n
and

.

so

. On the other hand, as

is nef, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq(-K_{F}-\Delta_{F})\cdot\sigma_{0}^{\prime}\leq(-K_{F}-a\sigma_{0}^{\prime})\cdot\sigma_{0}^{\prime}=2+(1-a)\sigma_{0}^{\prime 2}<2-\frac{2n}{3},

which implies that

.
∎
