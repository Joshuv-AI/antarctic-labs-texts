Predicting heavy metal accumulation in alfalfa (Medicago sativa) using soil properties of sewage sludge amendments
Authors: Mohamed T. Ahmed, Sulaiman A. Alrumman, Pankaj Kumar, Ivan Širić, Željko Andabaka, Ebrahem M. Eid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sustainable management of sewage sludge (SS) produced from municipal wastewater treatment facilities has become one of the major challenges around the world. Despite its utilization as a soil amendment, the issue of heavy metal contamination persists as a major concern, given the potential for accumulation in the edible parts of crops. Therefore, the present study aimed to develop predictive models to evaluate the uptake of nine heavy metals (Cd, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ni, Pb, and Zn) by the shoots and roots of alfalfa plants (Medicago sativa L.) grown in soil mixed with varying contents of SS (0, 10, and 20 g/kg). The results of the present study showed that the mixing of SS enhanced soil nutrients but also elevated heavy metal contents. Alfalfa shoots accumulated significantly higher levels of Pb (26.03 mg/kg) and Zn (83.67 mg/kg) compared to roots, with Pb showing the highest bioaccumulation factor (7.1) and Mn exhibiting the strongest translocation factor (> 1.8). Predictive regression models indicated that soil pH, organic matter, electrical conductivity, and heavy metal contents were the most influential determinants of uptake. The best-fitting models achieved R2 values up to 0.837, with efficiency indices and error parameters confirming their robustness. The findings of the current study showed that SS may improve soil fertility under alfalfa cultivation, while developed models can be used to mitigate risks of heavy metal contamination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24299-8.

Introduction

Urban sewage sludge (SS) is the semi-solid by-product generated during the treatment of municipal wastewater1,2. In recent decades, rapid urbanization and industrial establishments have resulted in the generation of significant volumes of SS globally3. According to estimates, urban centers worldwide produce approximately 75 million megagrams of SS annually4, which is projected to increase in response to population growth and urban expansion. In developing and developed nations alike, SS management has become a critical component of environmental sustainability efforts5–8. SS contains essential nutrients such as nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and organic matter (OM), which can enhance soil fertility when applied as an organic amendment. It also provides an effective method to recycle nutrients and OM back into agricultural systems, reducing the dependency on synthetic fertilizers9,10. However, the application of untreated or inadequately treated SS has raised concerns about the accumulation of heavy metals in agricultural soils and crops, posing risks to human and environmental health11,12.

Heavy metals are naturally occurring metallic elements with high atomic weights and densities, typically greater than 5 g/cm3, that are toxic to living organisms even at low concentrations13. Heavy metal contamination from SS application is a serious environmental challenge as it often contains cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), and nickel (Ni), originating from industrial discharges, household waste, and urban runoff4,14. When applied to soil, these heavy metals show high persistence and bioavailability, entering food chains through crop uptake15. The mechanisms governing heavy metal accumulation in plants involve soil-plant interactions, including heavy metal adsorption to soil particles, desorption into the soil solution, and plant root absorption16. On the other hand, soil properties such as pH, OM content, and cation exchange capacity (CEC) also affect heavy metal availability and plant uptake rates17. In this, plant-specific factors such as root structure, tolerance potential, and metal transport systems also determine the magnitude of bioaccumulation18.

Previous studies have reported the contamination of soil and crops due to SS application19. For instance, research on SS-amended soils has revealed elevated levels of Cd and Pb in various crops, exceeding permissible limits for human consumption20. The environmental consequences of heavy metal contamination are also significant, such as soil degradation, reduced microbial diversity, and altered ecosystem functions21. From a health perspective, the ingestion of heavy metal-contaminated food can lead to bioaccumulation in human tissues, causing severe conditions such as kidney dysfunction, neurological disorders, and carcinogenic effects22. Therefore, there is a need for more effective monitoring and management practices to mitigate the risks associated with SS applications. In order to address heavy metal risks, mathematical modeling has emerged as a useful tool. Mathematical models can predict the behavior of heavy metals in soil-plant systems, considering variables such as soil properties, SS application rates, and crop type23,24. Such models provide a scientific basis for determining safe application rates of SS to minimize heavy metal uptake by crops. On the other hand, dose optimization also helps to balance the benefits of nutrient addition with the risks of heavy metal contamination, ensuring sustainable agricultural practices25.

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a perennial legume, is widely cultivated for its nutritional and economic value around the globe26. It is an important forage crop, rich in protein, vitamins, and minerals, making it a vital component of livestock diets27. Alfalfa is classified within the Fabaceae family and exhibits remarkable adaptability to diverse climatic and soil conditions28. It also helps in soil fertility enhancement through nitrogen fixation and OM recycling29. Apart from its agricultural benefits, alfalfa is known for its ability to accumulate and tolerate heavy metals, making it a valuable candidate for studying soil-plant interactions in SS-amended systems30.

Although previous studies have reported heavy metal accumulation in crops cultivated on sewage sludge-amended soils, limited efforts have been made to develop predictive models linking soil properties with metal uptake in forage crops such as alfalfa. Most existing studies focus either on soil contamination or crop metal content, without providing an integrated predictive framework for risk assessment. Addressing this gap is essential for establishing safe sludge application practices and for reducing potential risks to food chains and consumer health. Therefore, the present work aimed to study heavy metal accumulation in alfalfa grown in SS-amended soils. The objectives of this research were: (1) to quantify the relationship between soil characteristics and heavy metal uptake by alfalfa and (2) to develop predictive models for heavy metal accumulation based on varying sludge application rates.

Materials and methods

The methodology of this study followed standard experimental and analytical protocols, with partial overlap with the procedures described by AL-Huqail et al.31. A complete description of the methodology is provided below, and a concise tabular summary of the main experimental steps is presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Experimental design

Verified alfalfa seeds (The Seed Vine, Melbourne, Victoria, Australia) were procured from a local market in Abha City, Saudi Arabia. The soil used for the experiment was a coarse sandy loam, classified as Typic Torriorthents, collected from a recently reclaimed area nearby (coordinates: 18°14′26.52″ N, 42°34′15.24″ E) (Fig. 1). The SS utilized in the study was obtained from the Abha City Municipal Wastewater Treatment Plant (coordinates: 18°13′59.16″ N, 42°31′16.32″ E). Both the soil and sludge samples were air-dried for two weeks, ground, and sieved through a 2 mm mesh. The experiment was conducted in the greenhouse of the Biology Department at King Khalid University. Prior to the main experiment, a preliminary trial was conducted to determine the appropriate mixing ratio of SS with the cultivated soil. The tested rates were 0 (control), 10, and 20 g/kg. Each treatment had ten (9 L) plastic pots, each filled with 4 kg of the specific treatment and 20 seeds, arranged in a completely randomized design. The plants were grown for 81 days, from December 11, 2022, in the greenhouse, following the natural day-night cycle. Irrigation was provided using tap water to maintain a water content of 40–50% in each pot. Manual weeding was performed as needed, and after 15 days, the plants were thinned to 5 per pot. The greenhouse maintained an average temperature of 24–28 °C during the day and 16–20 °C at night, with relative humidity of 55–65%. Natural day length (11–12 h) prevailed, and supplemental lighting was used on cloudy days to ensure uniform photoperiod. The sewage sludge was air-dried, homogenized, and stored at room temperature in sealed polyethylene bags until its use.

Sample analysis

Soil physicochemical properties were analyzed before experimentation. Soil texture was determined by the hydrometer method, while cation exchange capacity (CEC) was measured using ammonium acetate extraction. Available nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium were estimated by the Kjeldahl method, Olsen’s method, and flame photometry, respectively. The alfalfa plants were harvested on March 1, 2023. All plant materials underwent thorough washing under running water, followed by rinsing with deionized water. The materials were then divided into roots and shoots, which were dried at 60 °C until they reached a constant weight. Afterward, the dried samples were ground using a plastic mill and stored for future analyses. At the end of the experiment, soil samples were collected from each replicate. These samples were air-dried for two weeks, then ground and sieved through a 2 mm mesh. The soil samples from all treatments, as well as the SS and cultivated field soil, were analyzed to determine their OM content using the loss-on-ignition method at 550 °C for 2 h. The EC and pH were measured in 1:5 soil-water extracts.

To analyze the heavy metal contents, 0.5–1.0 g of each soil, SS, cultivated field soil, and plant sample was digested using a tri-acid mix (HNO3:H2SO4:HClO4; 5:1:1, v/v/v)32. A microwave sample preparation system (PerkinElmer Titan MPS) was used for the digestion. Blank samples were included to ensure the accuracy and precision of the digestion and subsequent analyses. The content of nine heavy metals (cadmium: Cd, chromium: Cr, cobalt: Co, copper: Cu, iron: Fe, manganese: Mn, nickel: Ni, lead: Pb, and zinc: Zn) was determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Thermo Scientific iCAP 7000 Plus Series). The detection limits for these elements (in µg/L) were: 6.0 for Ni; 2.0 for Co, Cr, Cu; 1.0 for Fe, Pb, Zn; 0.3 for Mn; and 0.1 for Cd. Standard solutions with known element contents were used to calibrate the ICP-OES, following the manufacturer’s specifications.

Quality assurance and quality control

To validate the accuracy of the heavy metal determinations, a certified reference material (SRM 1573a, tomato leaves) was used. This reference material underwent the same digestion and analysis methods as the alfalfa samples. The heavy metal digestions and measurements were conducted in triplicate. Accuracy was assessed by comparing the measured heavy metal content with the certified value, and the outcome was expressed as a percentage. The recovery rates of the heavy metals varied from 95.80% to 104.80%, indicating the high accuracy of the analysis.

The translocation factor (TF) was used to estimate the potential of the alfalfa plants to transfer heavy metals from the roots to the aerial shoot tissues. Student’s t-tests were performed to identify any significant differences between the predicted and measured metal contents for each plant tissue component. The Pearson simple linear correlation coefficient (r) was used to identify any correlations between the ME and other model quality parameters, such as the R2, MNAE, and MNB. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variance was confirmed by the Levene test. When the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were not met, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to identify any significant differences in the BAFs and TFs calculated for the studied heavy metals. Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test was then employed to determine which specific metal means differed from each other, using a significance level of p < 0.05. All data analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 23.0 (IBM SPSS 2015).

Results and discussion

Effect of sewage sludge on soil properties

In the present investigation, soil and SS were analyzed for selected physicochemical and heavy metal properties as shown in Table S2. In this, the soil showed a slightly higher pH than SS. Although EC and OM of SS were significantly higher than those of the soil. On the other hand, Cd, Co, Pb, and Zn contents in soil were lower than those observed in SS. However, the contents of Cr, Cu, Fe, Mn, and Ni were higher in soil than in SS. It was observed that the levels of several heavy metals in cultivated field soil and SS had higher values when compared to the normal limits of Kabata-Pendias38 and permissible limits of The Council of the European Communities (1986)39. Figure 2 shows the physicochemical characteristics of soil amended with SS after alfalfa cultivation. The results showed that the soil had an alkaline pH (mean 8.45), stable with low variability (1.24%) across experimental treatments. Also, the average value of OM was 2.27% with moderate variability (13.05%), whereas EC had a mean value of 0.27 mS/cm. In the case of heavy metals, Pb and Ni showed the highest, while Cd and Co showed the minimum variability coefficients. However, Fe and Mn showed the highest content in the experimental soil.

In previous studies, it has been reported that SS amendment affects heavy metal contents of soil under different cropping systems40 [Zhao H et al.,2025]. Soil particles may retain fractions of certain heavy metals through the adsorption process41 [Zhao X et al.,2025]. Also, clay minerals may react with metal elements to form complex compounds42. On the other hand, heavy metals may also get captured in soil through OM binding, ion exchange, and precipitation as insoluble compounds. In a recent study, Eid et al.43 found that applying 10 g/kg SS resulted in a 2-fold increase in soil OM content under cucumbers (Cucumis sativus L.) cultivation. Similarly, Kumar et al.44 also applied 5–10% SS and observed a proportional increase in soil OM and heavy metal fractions under okra cultivation (Abelmoschus esculentus (L.) Moench). Thus, SS is a nutrient-rich substance that contains several essential elements. These elements are crucial for maintaining optimal levels of nutrients in the soil, thereby ensuring the productivity and health of plants.

Heavy metal bioaccumulation in alfalfa

As depicted in Fig. 3, the content of nine heavy metals in the root and shoot parts of alfalfa did not vary significantly (p > 0.05) except for Cu, which showed significant (p < 0.05) differences. In particular, the mean contents of selected heavy metals in root parts were as follows: Cd (8.97), Co (6.51), Cr (0.19), Cu (6.03), Fe (59.74), Mn (132.30), Ni (11.75), Pb (20.08), and Zn (70.97). Whereas, the contents observed in shoots were as follows: Cd (10.14), Co (7.85), Cr (0.19), Cu (4.22), Fe (64.04), Mn (75.80), Ni (7.89), Pb (26.03), and Zn (83.67). Herein, the contents of Cd, Co, Cr, Fe, Pb, and Zn were recorded as highest in the shoot as compared to the root parts. BAF indicates the potential of a plant to accumulate heavy metals from the growing medium to its vegetative tissues45. The BAF studies indicated that Pb had the highest BAF value, approaching 7.1, followed by Ni and Co (Fig. 4). The lowest BAF values were observed for Fe and Zn. On the other hand, TF indicates the ability of plants to translocate heavy metals from below-ground tissues (roots) to above-ground tissues (shoots)46. TF analysis showed that Mn (> 1.8) showed the highest translocation ability, followed by Pb (> 1.3) in alfalfa plants (Fig. 5). Herein, the lowest TF was observed for Cu and Ni heavy metals, which might be associated with their restricted migration in plant tissues. The lowest and highest TF values for Cu, Ni, and Mn showed a significant (p < 0.05) difference according to one-way ANOVA comparisons.

Recent studies show that metal uptake by leguminous roots involves both apoplastic and symplastic pathways, with root excretion of organic acids (e.g., citric, malic) under lowered pH increasing solubilization of metals (Cd, Pb, Zn)47. Elevated cation exchange capacity (CEC) can sequester metals at exchange sites, thereby reducing their free ion activity in soil solution. Once inside the root, metals are either bound to cell wall components (e.g., pectins) or chelated by ligands such as phytochelatins and metallothioneins48. The higher translocation of Ni and Pb observed in our study can be associated with the expression of membrane transporters (ZIP, NRAMP) that facilitate loading into xylem sap49. Furthermore, vacuolar sequestration in shoots may reduce free intracellular metal concentrations, reducing toxicity but causing higher shoot concentrations. Soil pH affects metal speciation: at lower pH, proton competition for soil binding sites displaces metal cations, enhancing their mobility50. Organic matter can complex with metals, decreasing their bioavailability, but in sewage sludge applications, labile organic fractions may initially increase metal solubility51. Electrical conductivity reflects soluble salts, which may compete with heavy metals for binding sites, indirectly influencing uptake rates52. Heavy metals can interact antagonistically or synergistically: for example, Fe and Zn often compete for uptake via shared transporters, while an excess of one can suppress the uptake of the other53. Such interactions could explain why Fe accumulation was relatively lower in shoots despite high application, as Zn may have inhibited Fe translocation under our treatment regimes.

Moreover, Fig. 6 shows the Pearson correlation matrix between soil properties and heavy metal contents in the shoot (a) and root (b) tissues of cultivated alfalfa plants. In this case, positive correlations indicate that higher soil chemical property values correspond to increased heavy metal uptake, while negative correlations suggest an inverse relationship. In particular, all heavy metals in shoots and roots showed a strong negative correlation with soil pH, while positive for EC, OM, and soil heavy metals. Soil pH and OM content appear to significantly influence heavy metal bioavailability, which results in their localization in alfalfa tissues. Heavy metals accumulate in plants due to their ability to bind with root cell walls, be absorbed by transport proteins, and move through apoplastic or symplastic pathways54. Soil properties such as pH, OM, and metal bioavailability, along with plant species-specific uptake mechanisms, significantly influence this accumulation process55. Previously, Peralta et al.56 studied the uptake of nine heavy metals by alfalfa plants and found that it had significant effects on seed germination, plant growth, and productivity. Also, they found that the content of most heavy metals was higher in roots, which is consistent with some heavy metals studied in the current study. In another study, Mbarki et al.57 applied municipal compost and found increased heavy metal contents of alfalfa under higher loading doses in clay and sandy soils. Similarly, Khan et al.58 also observed that increased heavy metals in soil through wastewater irrigation could lead to higher uptake of heavy metals in alfalfa plants. Therefore, the results of the above studies are in line with those observed in the present experiment.

Prediction models and their validation

Multivariate regression analysis has been widely used to establish predictive relationships between soil properties and plant heavy metal uptake59. In the present study, selected soil properties such as pH, OM content, EC, and heavy metal content were used as predictive variables for net heavy metal uptake by alfalfa root and shoot tissues, separately. As shown in Table 1, the quality of regression models was acceptable based on selected validation indicators. In general, soil pH, OM, EC, and heavy metal contents showed significant effects on model strength as described by their coefficients. The highest interactive effects were observed for soil pH and EC. This is according to the fact that soil pH and EC affect heavy metal uptake by altering metal solubility, speciation, and ion exchange capacity, which influence their bioavailability for plant absorption60. The R2 values of models also ranged widely from 0.237 to 0.837, indicating that some models showed better fitness while some had lower predictive capabilities. For instance, Cr (root), Cu (root), Fe (root), and Co (shoot) showed lower R2 values. The ME index also corroborates with R2 values showing proportional values based on model fitness and ranging from 0.267 to 0.856. On the other hand, MNAE and MNB showed lower values for good-fitting models, while higher values for models with low predictability. This was further confirmed by the correlative relationship among ME, R2, MNAE, and MNB as given in Fig. 7. Specifically, R2 positively correlates with ME (r = 0.945), indicating better model performance with higher R2. In contrast, MNAE (r = − 0.950) and MNB (r = − 0.962) negatively correlate with ME, suggesting reduced error and bias improve efficiency. Thus, minimizing MNAE and MNB improves model performance, which is reflected in a higher ME.

Regression analysis has been a useful tool for assessing heavy metal contamination in edible crops61. Recently, Deng et al.62 developed linear regression models to assess heavy metal uptake by wheat crops while using soil-atmospheric variables as predictors. Similarly, Wang et al.63 also developed prediction models for assessing soil contamination levels in agricultural soil and compared them with a random forest approach. Both models were efficient for soil pollution prediction, thereby contributing to crop and consumer safety. Another study by Giri et al.64 found that pH, OM, and heavy metal content in soil were effective variables for assessing the heavy metal content in leafy vegetables grown in mining areas of Jharkhand in India. Therefore, it was evident that utilizing regression modeling for soil and crop pollution assessment is one of the cost and labor-intensive approaches to safeguard consumer safety.

Conclusion

This investigation demonstrated that SS significantly improves soil fertility but concurrently elevates heavy metal bioaccumulation in alfalfa. Shoots accumulated higher levels of Cd, Pb, Zn, and Co compared to roots, with Pb showing the highest bioaccumulation factor and Mn exhibiting the greatest translocation potential. These findings confirm that metal uptake is strongly influenced by soil pH, organic matter, and electrical conductivity, highlighting the mechanistic role of soil–plant interactions in determining bioavailability. The regression models developed in this study successfully predicted heavy metal uptake (R2 > 0.837), validating soil parameters as robust predictors of plant contamination risk. These models can serve as cost-effective decision-support tools for farmers and policymakers to evaluate safe application rates of SS in agriculture. Overall, the results suggest that while SS can be a valuable soil amendment, its use must be carefully regulated to avoid excessive transfer of toxic metals into forage crops. The predictive models developed in this study provide a practical framework for evaluating safe sewage sludge application in agriculture. Future research should consider (i) long-term monitoring of soil and crop contamination under repeated SS applications, (ii) evaluation of different crop species with varying uptake capacities, and (iii) integration of predictive models with risk assessment frameworks to ensure food and feed safety.
