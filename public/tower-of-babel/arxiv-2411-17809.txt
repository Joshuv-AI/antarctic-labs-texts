Transdimensional Defects
Elia de Sabbata, a , c {\!}{}^{a,c} Nadav Drukker, c {\!}{}^{c} Andreas Stergiou c eliadesabbata@gmail.com nadav.drukker@gmail.com andreas.stergiou@kcl.ac.uk

Abstract
This note introduces a novel paradigm for conformal defects with continuously adjustable dimensions. Just as the standard ε \varepsilon expansion interpolates between integer spacetime dimensions, a new parameter, δ \delta , is used to interpolate between different integer-dimensional defects. This framework is explored in detail for defects of dimension p = 2 + δ p=2+\delta in both free and interacting O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) bulk conformal field theories (CFTs) in d = 4 − ε d=4-\varepsilon . Comprehensive calculations are performed to first and second order in ε \varepsilon and to high or all orders in δ \delta . Additionally, in the large- N N limit, the interpolation between defects of dimensions p = 1 p=1 and p = 2 p=2 is analysed for spacetime dimensions 4 ⩽ d ⩽ 6 4\leqslant d\leqslant 6 . The new parameter δ \delta provides a natural enrichment of the space of defect CFTs and allows to find new integer dimension or co-dimension defects.

Introduction

The influence of the Wilson–Fisher dimensional continuation on the study of fixed points and the renormalisation group (RG)
[
1

cannot be overstated. Their versatile approach, which is recognised by the name “
\varepsilon

models in

, and also allows to formulate non-trivial RG flows and CFTs in dimension “
d=3.99
”
[
3
,
4
]
.

In recent years, there is an explosion of interest in conformal defects in CFTs (or dCFT), i.e. systems with conformal symmetry along a
p
-dimensional submanifold of space. This includes many examples of conformal defects in the
\varepsilon
expansion, mostly the critical
O(N)
model, but also systems with other symmetries. For a partial list of references see
[
5
,

,
7
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
. To the extent of our knowledge, all the many examples studied thus far have had either integer
p=1,2
or integer
q=d-p=1,2
. The former are line or surface defects and remain lines or become interfaces as
\varepsilon\to 1
. The latter are boundaries/interfaces or vortex-like configurations and retain those properties for all
\varepsilon
. The purpose of this paper is to relax this assumption and allow for arbitrary
p
with a focus on “the
p=2.01
defect in
d=3.99
dimensions.”

This idea immediately implies some variation on the already known examples of conformal defects. The additional parameter
\delta
in
p=2+\delta
allows for new perturbative expansions. It also allows for new interpolations, for example
p:2\to 3
as
d:4\to 3
, giving new bulk three-dimensional CFTs, or
p:2\to 3-\varepsilon
with fixed
d=4-\varepsilon
, giving possibly new interfaces. These are the focus of the next section, while in Section
3
we take
p:1\to 2
as
d:4\to 6
.

This summarises the main idea of the paper. The next sections illustrate it in the examples mentioned. We perform all-order (or high-order) calculations in
\delta
at fixed order in
\varepsilon
or at large
N
that enable real interpolation in the defect dimension. Each section is self-contained and the results of those calculations and multitude of possible generalisations are presented in the discussion section.

2.1
Free
O(N)
field theory

We begin with a simple soluble example, the free scalar
O(N)
theory in
d
dimensions. Its action is

S=\int d^{\hskip 0.5ptd}x\,\tfrac{1}{2}\hskip 0.5pt\partial^{\mu}\phi_{a}\hskip 0.5pt\partial_{\mu}\phi_{a}\,.

(2.1)

We deform the action by the defect interaction terms

S_{D}=\tfrac{1}{2}(h_{0})_{ab}\int d^{\hskip 0.5ptp}\tau\,(\delta_{ab}S+T_{ab})\,,

(2.2)

where
S
and
T_{ab}
are the two primary operators that are quadratic in the fields,

S\equiv\frac{1}{N}\phi_{a}\phi_{a}\,,\qquad T_{ab}\equiv\phi_{a}\phi_{b}-\frac{\delta_{ab}}{N}\phi_{c}\phi_{c}\,,

(2.3)

and
h_{0}
has mass dimension
p+2-d
. For simplicity we focus on the
O(N)
-invariant defect.

For
d=4-\varepsilon<4
and
p=2
, this deformation is weakly relevant and triggers a defect RG flow. This flow can be studied exactly in
\varepsilon
, and for
\varepsilon=1
it reaches an infrared (IR) fixed point that corresponds to the three-dimensional free scalar theory with an interface with Dirichlet boundary conditions
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. If we instead take
d=p=3
, the action just defines a free massive scalar theory with mass
m^{2}=h_{0}
. Here we explore a third possibility by setting
d=4-\varepsilon
and
p=2+\delta
. The deformation is again weakly relevant and the defect flows to an IR fixed point. This defect RG flow can be studied in perturbation theory, where it is possible to resum diagrams at all orders. The analysis is essentially the same as in
[
13
,
15
]
, except that defect integrals have now to be performed in
p=2+\delta
dimensions. A similar approach has been explored in the context of coupling theories across different dimensions
[
22
]

at the bulk fixed point in the presence of the defect, see (
A.17
,
A.18
). To compute it, we expand the result in powers of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
which allows us to guess the expression

\begin{split}\langle S(x_{\perp},x_{\parallel})\rangle&=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{S}\,a_{S}}{|x_{\perp}|^{2\Delta_{\phi}}}\,,\qquad\mathcal{N}_{S}=2N\hskip 0.5pt\mathcal{N}_{\phi}^{\,2}\,,\qquad a_{S}=-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma\!\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\delta\right)}{2^{2+\delta}\hskip 0.5pt\Gamma\!\left(\frac{3+\delta}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon+\delta}{2}\right)}\,.\end{split}

(2.10)

With these expressions in hand, we analyse the system for different values of
\delta
. For
\delta=1-\varepsilon
the defect is an interface and (
2.8
), (
2.10
) reproduce, for any
\varepsilon
, the values for the free scalar theory with Dirichlet boundary conditions
[
5
]
. The dimensions of both
\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}}
and
{\hat{\phi}}
are
2-\varepsilon/2
and further evidence that they are linearly dependent comes from the boundary operator product expansion (OPE) coefficients of these two operators
[
6
]
. At order
\varepsilon^{0}
,

\langle\phi(x){\hat{\phi}}(\tau)\rangle=\frac{{\mathcal{N}}_{\phi}\hskip 0.5pt{\mathcal{N}}_{\hat{\phi}}\hskip 0.5ptb_{\phi,{\hat{\phi}}}}{|x_{\perp}|^{\Delta_{\phi}-\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{\phi}}}|x_{\parallel}-\tau|^{2\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{\phi}}}}\,,\qquad b^{\hskip 0.5pt2}_{\phi,{\hat{\phi}}}=\frac{2^{\delta}\hskip 0.5pt\Gamma\!\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1+\delta}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\text{O}(\varepsilon)\,,

(2.11)

where
\langle{\hat{\phi}}(0){\hat{\phi}}(\tau)\rangle={\mathcal{N}}_{\hat{\phi}}^{\hskip 0.5pt2}/|\tau|^{2\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{\phi}}}
, and similarly

b^{\hskip 0.5pt2}_{\phi,\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}}}=\frac{2}{2-\delta}+\text{O}(\varepsilon)\,.

(2.12)

This means that for
\varepsilon=0
(and
\delta=1
), the boundary OPE coefficient for the unit normalised operator
\hat{\Phi}_{+}=({\hat{\phi}}+\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}})/\sqrt{2}
becomes
b^{\hskip 0.5pt2}_{\phi,\hat{\Phi}_{+}}=(b_{\phi,{\hat{\phi}}}+b_{\phi,\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}}})^{2}/2=4
, which is the correct value for the boundary operator in the Dirichlet interface theory in
d=4
. Similarly, we can see that the operator
\hat{\Phi}_{-}=({\hat{\phi}}-\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}})/\sqrt{2}
decouples. A similar statement should hold for
\varepsilon>0
.

As another example,
\delta\rightarrow-1
should lead to a line defect. To keep the quadratic deformation relevant, we must start with
\varepsilon+\delta>0
. For the case of
\varepsilon=1
, we get
\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{\phi}}=1/2
from (
2.8
), which is the dimension of the bulk field, so this is the trivial defect with
h_{*}=0
. A similar result was obtained for
N=1
in
[
23
]
.

Next, for
\varepsilon,\delta\rightarrow 1
we get a three-dimensional theory with
\hat{\Delta}_{{\hat{\phi}}}=5/2
, so a non-local theory of generalised free fields (GFF). More generally, leaving
0<\varepsilon<1
as a free parameter and looking only at defect correlators, one finds a continuous family of GFF theories with
2<\hat{\Delta}_{{\hat{\phi}}}<5/2
. This is similar in spirit to the defect description of the long-range Ising model
[
24
]
. Another generalisation, starting with any theory near the critical dimension
d_{c}
, i.e.
d=d_{c}-\varepsilon
and
p=d_{c}-2+\delta
for some positive integer
d_{c}\geqslant 2
, gives
\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{\phi}}=\frac{1}{2}(d_{c}+1)
when
\varepsilon,\delta\to 1
.

Finally, it is instructive to see what happens in the space-filling limit
p\to d
, i.e.
\delta\to 2-\varepsilon
. Since in this limit there are no orthogonal directions (
|x_{\perp}|\to 0
), the most one can do is push bulk operators to the defect by suitable rescalings. This procedure selects the lightest defect operator in the defect OPE. For example, in this limit the unit normalised operator
b^{-1}_{S,(\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}})^{2}}|x_{\perp}|^{\Delta_{S}-\hat{\Delta}_{(\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}})^{2}}}S(x)
reduces to the defect operator
(\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}})^{2}(x)
. Note that the dimensions of
{\hat{\phi}}
and
\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}}
cross at the interface value
\delta=1-\varepsilon
and for
\delta>1-\varepsilon
the latter becomes smaller. Crucially, for
\delta=2-\varepsilon
the bulk one-point function coefficient
a_{S}
remains finite, whereas the defect OPE coefficient

b_{S,(\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}})^{2}}=\frac{8N\hskip 0.5pt\pi^{\varepsilon}\hskip 0.5pt\Gamma\!\left(2-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{2}}{(2-\varepsilon-\delta)^{2}}\,,

(2.13)

is divergent. Therefore,
\lim_{p\to d}a_{S}/b_{S,(\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}})^{2}}=0
, so that there are no non-zero one-point functions.

4
Discussion

This note introduced transdimensional defects whose dimension is a free continuous parameter and demonstrated their utility in several examples. This includes defects with dimensions
2\leqslant p\leqslant 3
in Euclidean space of dimension
3\leqslant d\leqslant 4
, which are studied in the
\varepsilon
expansion, and defects of dimension
1\leqslant p\leqslant 2
in
4\leqslant d\leqslant 6
studied in the large-
N
expansion.

For the interacting
O(N)
model in
d=4-\varepsilon
we were able to perform very detailed calculations on a symmetry preserving defect of dimension
p=2+\delta
. Working at leading order in
\varepsilon
and to all orders in
\delta
, we were able to extrapolate to an interface with
p=3-\varepsilon
with low-lying spectrum presented in (
2.28
). This interface is different from those studied in
[
17
,
19
]
, which all break the global symmetry. In
[
31
]
it was conjectured that such a defect with
\varepsilon=1
and
\delta=0
is an impregnable interface with Dirichlet boundary conditions
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
. The fact that
{\hat{\phi}}
and
\partial_{\perp}{\hat{\phi}}
have equal dimensions is similar to that system, but it deviates from it since the dimensions of
\hat{S}
and
\hat{D}
are not equal, and neither can
\hat{S}
be interpreted as the product of defect primaries belonging to disjoint copies of a boundary theory, as in
[
47
]
. This is therefore a completely new interface in
d=4-\varepsilon
.

Another special value is
\delta=1
, giving a three-dimensional defect in
d=4-\varepsilon
. Focusing solely on defect operators, we treat this system as a non-local 3d CFT. We present the anomalous dimensions in (
2.29
,
2.30
) and those do not match any theory known to us. Setting
\varepsilon=0
returns us to the free theory in
d=4
and with the restriction of operators to
p=3
, this behaves like a theory of generalised free fields (GFF) with
\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{\phi}}=2
. Remaining in the free theory we can tune
\varepsilon
freely, as explained in Section
2.1
, and for
\varepsilon=\delta=1
the defect is space-filling and we find another three-dimensional theory, again GFF but now with
\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{\phi}}=5/2
. The defect theory at other values of
\varepsilon
interpolates between these two values. Note that this is markedly different from starting with a quadratic deformation of the free theory directly in
d=p=3
. In that case this is a mass term and the IR is the empty theory.

It would be very interesting to extend the results in the interacting theory to higher orders in
\varepsilon
beyond those presented in Sections
2.2
. As explained at the top of Section
2.2
, going to high order in
h
(or
\delta
) is relatively easy at a fixed order in
\varepsilon
, so this is not beyond the realm of possibilities.

While all our calculations can be immediately applied also to symmetry breaking defects, we explored them in less detail. In Appendix
Appendix B. Higher order results for surface defects
we extend the results of
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
for both the symmetry preserving and symmetry breaking surface defects to higher order. In particular we show how some degenerate solutions noted in
[
13
]
are lifted at order
\varepsilon^{2}
with square root branch cuts in the defect coupling
h
.

The second case we study is the
O(N)
model in
4\leqslant d\leqslant 6
, which we approach using large
N
techniques. It was shown in
[
13
,
15
]
that the properties of the symmetry preserving surface defect interpolate nicely across dimensions. On the other hand, the natural defects breaking
O(N)\to O(N-1)
are a line close to
d=4
and a surface close to
d=6
. As we show in Section
3
, this is a perfect setting for transdimensional defects and as long as
d=2p+2
there is a good symmetry breaking defect of dimension
p
. The dimensions and bulk one-point function coefficients we obtain at large
N
analytically in
d
match with the expected answers when expanded around
d=4
and
d=6
.

As demonstrated, transdimensional defects provide a practical tool to deal with non-weakly relevant defect deformations. This is an alternative method to massive approaches
[
48
,
30
]
and to finding solutions to the equations of motion with non-trivial defect profile
[
49
,
50
,
51
]
. The former involves complicated diagrammatic calculations, since the massless limit is taken only at the end, while the latter works particularly well in the co-dimension one case, but it is difficult to carry out for other values of the defect dimension.

This note is meant as a proof of principle and we leave many generalisations to the curious reader. One can further develop the examples studied here to higher orders in
\varepsilon
and/or
1/N
, and calculate more anomalous dimensions and look for structure constants or higher point functions. Natural further examples are deformations away from
p=1
lines or
p=3-\varepsilon
interfaces. Other observables involving transdimensional defects are defect four-point functions, bulk two-point functions, and so on. To those observables one could also apply analytic and numerical bootstrap techniques, as in
[
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
,
60
,
10
,
16
]
.

Another natural extension is to define transdimensional defects for other bulk fixed points beyond the
O(N)
model. The only requirement is that the model admit a perturbative defect that can be defined consistently in non-integer dimension. For example, scalar models with different bulk symmetries
[
12
]
and theories with fermionic degrees of freedom
[
9
,
12
,
61
]
.

We conclude with some comments and speculations about transdimensional defect conformal manifolds. In the free
O(N)
model in 4d the operator
\phi_{a}
is exactly marginal when integrated along a line,
\phi_{a}\phi_{b}
along a surface and likewise
\phi^{3}
operators along an interface, so the corresponding defect coupling
h
is arbitrary. When going to
4-\varepsilon
, the dimension of these operators changes even in the free theory and they are no longer marginal on an integer dimensional defect. Allowing for transdimensional defects, we can choose
p=2-\varepsilon
where
\phi_{a}\phi_{b}
is again exactly marginal.

The analogous story in the interacting theory is more subtle. To leading order in
\varepsilon
, the dimension of the scalar
\hat{S}
in (
2.3
) is given in (
2.23
). To make the operator exactly marginal we need higher corrections in
h
, where from (
2.22
)

\delta_{*}=-\frac{6}{N+8}\varepsilon+h-2h\varepsilon\frac{N+2}{N+8}\Big(1-h+\frac{6-\zeta(3)}{4}h^{2}+\text{O}(h^{3})\Big)\,.

(4.1)

Thus, we can view transdimensional defects as allowing nearly marginal operators like
S
to be exactly marginal by a choice of
\delta_{*}(h)
.

A similar statement for the bulk theory is to reverse (
2.16
) and define
\varepsilon_{*}(\lambda)
, so the quartic interaction term in (
2.14
) is exactly marginal when combined with the change of dimension. This is not a common (or particularly useful) viewpoint for the
\varepsilon
expansion, but it may be more pertinent in the case of transdimensional defects with symmetry breaking, generalising
[
62
]
. For example, in the case of the defect in Section
3
, the choice of
\phi_{1}
in (
3.2
) breaks
O(N)\to O(N-1)
giving a conformal manifold
S^{N-1}
. In the free theory in integer dimension,
h_{0}
is also a marginal parameter, extending the manifold to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. A similar structure may arise in the interacting theory for
p=\frac{d}{2}-1+\text{O}(N^{-1})
when
{\hat{\phi}}_{1}
, whose dimension is given by (
3.8
), is marginal. Similar considerations can apply for other symmetry-breaking transdimensional defects.

To evaluate the beta function of surface defect couplings
h_{ab}
at next-to-leading order, terms of order
\lambda h^{2}
can be found using results in the main text, specifically (

\lambda^{2}h

[

\beta_{ab}=-\varepsilon\hskip 0.5pth_{ab}+h_{ac}h_{bc}+\lambda[2\hskip 0.5pth_{ab}+h_{cc}\delta_{ab}-2(2\hskip 0.5pth_{ac}h_{bc}+h_{ab}h_{cc})]-\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{2}[(N+10)h_{ab}+4\hskip 0.5pth_{cc}\delta_{ab}]\,.

(B.1)

For the symmetry preserving case,
\beta_{ab}=\delta_{ab}h
, (
B.1
) becomes

\beta_{h}=-\varepsilon h+h^{2}+(N+2)\lambda h\big(1-2h-\tfrac{5}{6}\lambda\big)\,,

(B.2)

. With
\lambda_{*}=\frac{\varepsilon}{N+8}+\frac{3(3N+14)}{(N+8)^{3}}\varepsilon^{2}

[

, the dimension of
\hat{S}
at the non-trivial fixed point is

\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{S}}=2+\frac{\partial\beta_{h}}{\partial h}\Big|_{\lambda_{*},h_{*}}\!\!=2+\frac{6\hskip 0.5pt\varepsilon}{N+8}-\frac{(N+2)(13N+44)\,\varepsilon^{2}}{2(N+8)^{3}}+\text{O}(\varepsilon^{3})\,.

(B.3)

In the similar context of the
\phi^{2}
deformation integrated on a boundary, the extrapolation from the two-loop result
[
30
]
was already very close to the result of the numerical boundary bootstrap
[

]
. We can expect a similar behaviour here. Indeed, for example by setting

are already relatively close to each other

\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{S}}\hskip 0.5pt[2/0]\approx 2.55\,,\qquad\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{S}}\hskip 0.5pt[1/1]\approx 2.57\,.

However, a singlet primary operator with such dimension should be absent if the fixed point is an interface with Dirichlet boundary conditions, as proposed in

Moving on to the symmetry-breaking case, for
O(m)\times O(n)
symmetry with

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\hskip 0.5pth_{m}+h_{m}^{2}+\lambda[(m+2)h_{m}+nh_{n}-2(m+2)h_{m}^{2}-2nh_{m}h_{n}]

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt-\lambda^{2}[\tfrac{1}{2}h_{m}(5m+n+10)+2nh_{n}]\,,

(B.5)

\displaystyle\beta_{h_{n}}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\hskip 0.5pth_{n}+h_{n}^{2}+\lambda[(n+2)h_{n}+mh_{m}-2(n+2)h_{n}^{2}-2mh_{m}h_{n}]

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt-\lambda^{2}[\tfrac{1}{2}h_{n}(m+5n+10)+2mh_{m}]\,.

Zeroes of these beta functions at the
O(N)

\lambda=\frac{1}{N+8}\varepsilon+\frac{3(3N+14)}{(N+8)^{3}}\varepsilon^{2}

and similarly for

into (

h_{m,\pm}^{(1)}=\frac{m+3\pm\sqrt{9-mn}}{m+n+8}\,,\qquad h_{n,\pm}^{(1)}=\frac{n+3\mp\sqrt{9-mn}}{m+n+8}\,.

(B.7)

The expressions for
h_{m,\pm}^{(2)},h_{n,\pm}^{(2)}
and the analogue of (

Due to the square root
\sqrt{9-mn}
, these fixed points exist only for

. This inequality is saturated for integer
m,n
when
m=1,n=9
and when
m=n=3
where, without loss of generality, we have taken
m\leqslant n
. For these values of

the two fixed points collide and they move off to the complex plane for

. This collision point is corrected at next-to-leading order in
\varepsilon
, and a standard analysis following
[
4
]
shows that the critical
n
for which the two fixed points collide is given by

n_{c}=\frac{9}{m}-\frac{3(m^{2}-22\hskip 0.5ptm+9)}{2m(m^{2}+8\hskip 0.5ptm+9)}\hskip 0.5pt\varepsilon+\text{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

(B.8)

For
m=1
this becomes
9+\varepsilon
and for
m=3
it becomes
3+\frac{4}{7}\varepsilon
. In both cases the collision point lies above
9

3

m=1,n=9
and
m=n=3
unitary. Nevertheless, the
\varepsilon

involving half-integer powers of

h_{m=1,\pm}=\tfrac{2}{3}\hskip 0.5pt\varepsilon\pm\tfrac{1}{18}\hskip 0.5pt\varepsilon^{3/2}+\text{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\,,\qquad h_{n=9,\pm}=\tfrac{2}{9}\hskip 0.5pt\varepsilon\mp\tfrac{1}{18}\hskip 0.5pt\varepsilon^{3/2}+\text{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\,,

(B.9)

and for

Let us note that in the
