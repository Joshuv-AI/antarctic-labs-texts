Abdominal aortic tortuosity is not associated with other vascular peripheral pathologies or classical cardiovascular risk factors

ABSTRACT
Atherosclerosis is a chronic disease which can lead to peripheral vascular occlusion causing cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and peripheral artery diseases (PAD). Key contributors to PAD include ageing, classical cardiovascular risk factors, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension. For decades, these factors have also been related to arterial tortuosity, potentially indicating vascular fragility or arteriopathies. We aimed to evaluate the association between abdominal aortic tortuosity (AAT) and arteriopathies from other vascular beds and to assess its potential role as a subclinical marker of peripheral vascular disease. We included individuals aged ≥ 50 years and excluded those with dorsolumbar scoliosis. We built a multivariate log-linear regression model to identify factors associated with AAT. The model was adjusted by carotid calcification, stiffness, and stenosis as well as individual clinical characteristics and previously registered vascular diseases. A total of 490 individuals (mean age, 66.92 years; range, 50–98 years; 44.29% of women) from the Ageing Imageomics Study were included in this analysis. We observed that the AAT index was strongly dependent on age and also with diastolic blood pressure and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), but to a lesser extent. In contrast, other classical cardiovascular risk factors like diabetes mellitus, hypertension, smoking, aortic stiffness, and calcification did not play a significant role. Our results do not support the AAT index as a subclinical marker of cardiac, cerebrovascular, or peripheral vascular disease. Introduction

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterised by the accumulation of lipids, fibrous elements, and calcification within the large arteries. It is the main risk factor for cardiovascular disease and one of the main causes of worldwide mortality1,2. Ageing and cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs) such as smoking, diabetes mellitus (DM), and hypertension are established as the main determinants of atherosclerosis3,4. Moreover, this condition leads to peripheral vascular occlusion, which manifests chiefly as coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, and peripheral artery disease -including limb threatening ischemia (PAD)5. Atherosclerosis is usually more widespread in patients with symptomatic lower extremity artery disease (the most frequent presentation of PAD) than it is in those with coronary or cerebrovascular disease4,6,7.

The current worldwide increase in the PAD prevalence is a burden that requires attention from clinicians, who should focus on its prevention and management8. Peripheral artery disease usually affects the abdominal aorta, iliac arteries, and the lower limbs .People with PAD may present arterial ulceration, claudication, and ischaemia of the limbs, although some patients remain completely asymptomatic9. Importantly, even people with asymptomatic PAD are at high cardiovascular risk, and thus, could benefit from early diagnosis and preventive measures. In this regard, the ankle-brachial index (ABI) is one of the most appropriate tests to examine the vascular occlusion in the lower limbs; expose the general status of the arteries; and even detect asymptomatic cases3. However, the ABI could yield inaccurate results if the arteries are calcified. Therefore, additional parameters to explain the lesions in these or other arterial territories could be of interest10.

The abdominal arterial tortuosity (AAT) is defined as the presence of abdominal twists in the aorta and mid-sized arteries. These arterial curls are a common feature of certain genetic diseases and conditions and they also present specific clinical characteristics11,12. One of the multiple mechanisms that cause arterial tortuosity (AT) is the axial stress along the vessel. To reduce it, a gradual and abnormal lengthening of the arteries in a fixed space occurs (e.g., the abdomen). This leads to a forced bending that can take an S-shaped form11,13. Other contributing factors include aberrations of the elastic fibres or their abnormal distribution, along with distortions in the collagen alignment and turnover within the medial layer of the arterial wall. These factors stimulate abnormal arterial stiffness, which in turn leads to a progressive loss of structural stability14. Moreover, the AAT develop following an aneurysm, posing a risk of vessel rupture in the context of surgery15–17. Although this association is controversial, researchers have focused on improving the diagnosis and measurement of AT.

Recently, different imaging techniques, like abdominal magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), have been endorsed to examine the shape and condition of the aorta and its major branch vessels15,16,18,19, because abdominal tortuosity could be a useful indicator of vascular fragility or underlying pathologies11. Indeed, the aortic tortuosity index (ATI) has been identified as a potential marker of adverse long-term cardiovascular outcomes20. However, the geometry of the vascular system is significantly affected by ageing, demographics, gender, blood pressure, and other CVRFs21,22. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the potential association of abdominal aortic tortuosity with cardiovascular risk factors and the presence of atherosclerosis in several vascular territories, and assess its potential role as a subclinical marker of vascular fragility and underlying arteriopathies.

Methods

Study design, population and sample recruitment

Samples and data from participants included in this study were provided by the IDIBGI Horizontal Aging Program and the IDIBGI Biobank (B.0000872) from the Aging Imageomics Study. They were processed following standard operating procedures with the proper approval of the Ethics and Scientific Committees (2017.146). The Aging Imageomics Study is an observational study including participants living in the province of Girona (Northeast Catalonia, Spain). Detailed description of the cohort can be found elsewhere 23. In summary, this observational study encompassed 1030 participants from two independent cohort studies (with their own eligibility criteria) of individuals who lived in the province of Girona: the Maturity and Satisfactory Ageing in Girona study (MESGI50 study)24 and the Improving interMediAte RisK management study (MARK study)25. The MESGI50 study is a population-based cohort linked to the Survey of Health, Ageing, and Retirement in Europe project (SHARE), which included a representative sample of the population from the province of Girona aged ≥ 50 years26. The MARK study included a random sample of patients aged 35–74 years at intermediate cardiovascular risk recruited in public primary care centres in the province of Girona25. Common eligibility criteria included age ≥ 50 years, community dwelling (non-institutionalized), no history of infection within the previous 15 days, absence of contraindications for MRI (electronic cardiac implants, cochlear implants, incompatible prosthetic heart valves, incompatible vascular clips, metallic foreign bodies, or claustrophobia), and consent to be informed of potential incidental findings. After excluding 25 individuals with scoliosis, in whom tortuosity could not be reliably assessed, the final study population comprised 1005 participants meeting the inclusion criteria.

The data for the Ageing Imageomics study were collected between 14 November 2017 and 19 June 2019 and accessed for research purposes on 31 January 2023.

Participants underwent an MRI scan, interview, and physical examination. Clinical health records of the whole (Ageing Imageomics Study) cohort were extracted from the computerised clinical history program of the Catalan primary care system to assess the presence of diagnosed conditions at the time of study examination. Participants with dorsolumbar scoliosis were excluded because this deviation intrinsically affects AAT.

Abdominal aortic tortuosity index

The ATI for each participant was derived from MRI examinations performed using a mobile 1.5 T scanner (Vantage Elan, Toshiba Medical Systems)23. This index was defined as the difference between the geodesic distance (total measured length of the aortic track) and the Euclidean distance (minimum length between the two ends of the segment) in the analysed segments. A higher index reflects a higher level of tortuosity. To select this method, we considered all the published methods and discarded those that used relative measurements or required high computation or manual methods27.

Images were acquired using a non-contrast MRI protocol that, combined coronal T2-weighted and axial T1-weighted Spin Echo sequences, with Time-of-Flight (TOF) adjustments optimized for abdominal blood flow visualization. T Time-of-Flight settings were adapted to the velocity of flow in the infrarenal aorta and iliac arteries to improve lumen conspicuity without the need for gadolinium-based contrast agents. This was an essential aspect for a large-scale population study. The acquisition protocol provided high spatial resolution (in-plane pixel size approximately 0.6 × 0.6 mm, slice thickness 3 mm) and multiplanar coverage to ensure accurate reconstruction of vascular geometry. This protocol was performed on a mobile 1.5 T scanner (Vantage Elan, Toshiba Medical Systems) as part of a comprehensive whole-body MRI session that last approximately 50 min, included sequences for brain, cardiac, and musculoskeletal imaging. The calculation of this index was used in a previous study27 and is described in more detail in the Supplementary Information section.

Assessed cardiovascular risk factors and comorbidities

The included CVRFs and markers were age, gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, hyperlipidaemia, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), brachial–ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV), ABI, presence of carotid stenosis, presence of carotid plaque, and a clinical diagnosis of hypertension, DM, or previous atherosclerotic vascular disease (cardiac, cerebral, or PAD). Demographic and anthropometric characteristics as well as blood test results were collected as previously described23.

The BMI was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of the height in meters (Kg/m2). Participants were asked whether they smoked regularly at the time of the interview. Diastolic and systolic arterial pressure were measured using a VaSera VS-2000® device and expressed in millimetres of mercury (mmHg). Hyperlipidaemia was determined according to previously reported values28, and defined as the presence of at least one of the following: total cholesterol > 240 mg/dL, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels > 190 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels < 40 mg/dL or < 55 mg/dL in men and women respectively, triglycerides (TG) >200 mg/dL, or a self-reported prescription for antihyperlipidemic drugs. Fasting, total cholesterol, HDL-c, LDL-c, and TG levels were determined using basic biochemistry blood extraction analysis and expressed in milligrams per decilitre (mg/dL). The eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation, which employs an oscillometric method and estimates the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) to assess kidney function29.

The baPWV (which reflects aortic stiffness) was calculated with the VaSera VS-2000® device using the following equation: baPWV = (0.5934×height (cm) + 14.4724)/tba, where tba was the time interval between the arm and ankle waves30. The ABI was used as a dichotomous variable. Values ≤ 0.9 indicated low ABI, as in previous reports31 and defined lower extremity artery disease32.

Carotid ultrasound examinations, on a Siemens Acuson S2000 system (Mochida Siemens Medical System; Tokyo, Japan) with a 7.5 mHz linear array transducer on B-mode, measured the carotid diameter (common carotid artery and internal carotid artery)23. The presence of carotid stenosis was assessed by a radiologist with more than 20 years of experience. The presence of carotid plaque was defined as a localised protrusion of the vessel into the lumen ≥ 1.5 mm. Finally, the definition of each medical diagnosis was based on ICD1-10-CM codes that were considered up to the date of inclusion (baseline) and are reported in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were characterised using the mean (SD) and number (%), respectively. For descriptive purposes, we categorised the participants by tertiles of AAT index. The trends of the descriptive variables across AAT index tertiles were assessed using the Cochran-Armitage and the Jonckheere’s trend tests for categorical and numerical variables, respectively. Representative images of each tertile group were selected to illustrate their degree of tortuosity.

We performed univariate and multivariate log-linear regression analyses to assess the association of atherosclerosis in the considered arterial beds and cardiovascular risk factors with tortuosity, setting the tortuosity index as the dependent variable. Since the distribution of the tortuosity index was positively skewed, a log transformation was applied to ease the compliance with the linear regression assumptions. We checked these assumptions using diagnostic plots.The normality and homoscedasticity of the residuals were also checked using the Shapiro-Wilk and Breusch-Pagan tests, respectively. Finally, we assessed the potential influence of multicollinearity on the regression coefficients by computing the variation of the inflation factors. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. The statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.233 and the DescTools34, car35 and lmtest36 R packages.

Ethical aspects

The Ageing Imageomics Study protocol was approved by the ethics committee from Dr. Josep Trueta University Hospital (ref. CEIM: 2017.146 approved on 27 October 2017) and all participants gave written informed consent. The researchers were committed to respect the principles of human experimentation and good medical research from the Declaration of Helsinki (64th WMA General Assembly, Fortaleza, Brazil, October 2013). Informed consent was signed for all participants prior to the start of the study.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the patients

From 1005 participants meeting inclusion criteria, 434 participants were excluded from the analysis due to poor quality of the images, or data, namely: unavailable MRI (n = 140), failure to pass at least one of the two automatic system filters (n = 124), poor artery image quality (n = 59), incorrect automatic analysis (n = 49), ID errors (n = 5), and incomplete image reconstruction (n = 57). Moreover, 81 participants had missing information regarding clinical characteristics. After applying these criteria, 490 participants with complete information for all the assessed parameters remained for analysis (Fig. 1).

Associations between abdominal aortic tortuosity, cardiovascular risk factors, and atherosclerotic vascular diseases

The association of the considered factors with tortuosity was only significant for age, diastolic blood pressure, and eGFR (Table 3). Univariate analysis showed no relationship between eGFR and AAT index values. The opposite occurred regarding baPWV. It showed a significant association in the univariate analysis that was lost when adjusted. Of note, no significant association was observed between gender and AAT index in the univariate or multivariate regression (Table 3). Some of the classical CVRFs, such as tobacco consumption (smoking), BMI, and hyperlipidaemia were not associated with AAT index values either.

Discussion

We evaluated the use of the AAT as a potential marker of atherosclerotic coronary disease, cerebrovascular disease, and PAD in the general population older than 50 years. Using MRI, we assessed the AAT index and its association with classical CVRFs and atherosclerosis in other vascular territories. Previous studies were carried out in other arterial sections, populations with certain tortuosity syndromes, or patients undergoing blood vessel surgery11,12,15,16,20,37,38. Moreover, recent advances in medical technology have enabled the assessment of arterial tortuosity using 2D or 3D imaging of the vascular tree. Therefore, in this study, we were able to accurately calculate artery kinking15,16,19,37.

Our findings confirm the previously evidenced solid association of age with tortuosity index22,37. The aorta and large elastic arteries tend to become wider, longer, and more tortuous with age19,22. The kinking of the abdominal aorta, in particular, doubles by 80 years of age19 increasing at a slow but steady rate39. Other age-related changes, such as reduced height from vertebral deformities and osteoporosis, may decrease abdominal space and promote aortic bending, potentially leading to an increase in the tortuosity index39.The age-related burden on the AAT index might be explained by an increase in the mechanical tension within the abdominal aortic segment, which contributes to structural changes in the vessel wall and diameter as well as a decrease in the elastin fibres or its content19,22.

Previous studies showed a higher tortuosity index in women, explained by a reduction in the artery diameter and wall tension due to hormonal influences that may facilitate the progression of atherosclerosis in a gender-depending manner11,40. However, the examination of the abdominal segment in our analyses did not support this point.

Certain CVRFs previously linked to tortuosity such as DM, hypertension, or BMI19,40,41 did not appear to be associated with the AAT index in our results. Earlier studies reported that some of these CVRFs had a more direct impact on aortic geometry than on blood haemodynamics, due to calcification, stiffness, or atherosclerotic process19,37,42,43. Some authors have described an effect of diabetes on tortuosity, but only in small vessels (arterioles)11, whereas other studies have demonstrated lower aortic tortuosity values in patients with DM37. We did not observe correlation in participants previously diagnosed with hypertension either, or an association between AAT index and blood pressure, even though hypertension is a classical risk factor associated with PAD and with abdominal aortic tortuosity3,11,41. It is possible that our data might contain certain unmeasured demographic characteristics that obscured potential associations of the atherosclerotic processes with tortuosity in this specific artery segment.

The eGFR is an independent predictor of cardiovascular mortality, morbidity, arterial stiffness, and aortic calcification37. It is the principal global indicator of renal function, and its decline typically reflects arterial calcification44,45. Although we observed an association of eGFR with tortuosity, its direction contradicted our expectation. Finally, hyperlipidaemia did not show a clear contribution to the tortuosity burden either, although the interpretation of these results should consider that we could not specifically assess the lipid loading in the abdominal aortic section.

Previous studies have shown that the abdominal aortic segment is the first one to develop calcification19. However, the relation between aortic tortuosity and arterial calcification is controversial. Its impact has been deemed beneficial or detrimental depending on the specific artery section under consideration16,37,41,46.

The presence of atherosclerosis and calcification in the carotid artery have been described as factors contributing to aortic tortuosity, and also as outcomes resulting from it44. The absence of information on the percentage of calcification in the abdominal aorta or carotid artery in our study prevented the direct evaluation of this mechanism. However, we were able to examine the potential association between ATI and the presence of atherosclerotic plaques (in the carotid artery). The AAT has also been associated with calcification in the coronary arteries, contributing to increased vascular stiffness, hypertension, exacerbation of the atherosclerotic process41,47,48, and disturbance of the blood flow49. Therefore, it is plausible that the AAT could precede coronary atherosclerosis or calcification, similar to what might occur in the carotid arteries. In both cases, though -carotid and coronary arteries-, we observed no association.

The high prevalence of carotid stenosis (61%) and plaque (37%) in our cohort is consistent with the advanced age of the study population. It likely reflects the use of a sensitive diagnostic technique (ultrasound) combined with broad criteria that captured mild and subclinical lesions, which could overestimate these percentages of stenosis as previously reported50–53. These findings represent age-related atherosclerosis and shared risk factors, but showed no association with tortuosity, which appears to be an anatomical trait rather than a systemic marker.

The main feature of PAD is an insufficient arterial perfusion of the limbs to meet physiological demands, largely attributable to atherosclerosis54. This process is coupled with arterial elastic fibre calcification and a subsequent increase in blood pressure in the elastic arteries during ageing, which leads to altered blood haemodynamics, especially in the peripheral territories55. We assessed the presence of lower extremity artery disease with ABI ≤ 0.9 and baPWV values and observed no significant differences between AAT index tertile groups, nor any association with the AAT index in the regression analysis. The difference between the proportion of patients formally diagnosed with PAD (1.84%) and those diagnosed on the basis of ABI ≤ 0.9 (4.49%) suggest the presence of a certain number of asymptomatic, undiagnosed cases. In any case, our results do not support an association between tortuosity and atherosclerosis or arterial stiffness in the lower limbs, which partially differs from previous reports37. Finally, our multivariate analysis did not reveal any association between AAT index and previously diagnosed vascular diseases; however, in this case, the findings did agree with previous studies19,37. Unlike previous findings in the thoracic aorta or aortic arch segments12,52, our results showed that AAT should be considered an anatomical feature rather than a systemic disease marker, which underscores its relevance in surgical planning. Tortuosity directly influences device selection, access strategy, and procedural complexity in abdominal aortic interventions.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study to assess the tortuosity in the abdominal aorta and its potential association with classical CVRFs and related parameters in the general population. Previous studies in this regard were carried out in selected populations (such as patients with aortic aneurysm, diagnosed with tortuosity syndrome, or in the context of surgery). Moreover, we studied the AAT and its potential risk factors using non- or minimally invasive tests, including ABI and baPWV.

However, this study had some limitations. First, owing to the cross-sectional design, we could not establish a causal relationship between the assessed variables. Second, the sample size may have hindered our capacity to detect significant associations. The lack of availability of images and other data from some candidates additional to certain image quality filters lead to the exclusion of approximately half of the potentially eligible individuals. This might affect the generalizability of our results. For example, the number of participants with a diagnosis of vascular disease was quite modest. To assess the impact of this limitation, we compared the characteristics of the analysed cases and candidates excluded due to non-availability of MRI images or missing covariates (Table 4), which were similar. To a certain extent, however, the sample size could be considered substantial, because all participants underwent MRI.

Conclusions

Our analysis supports ageing as the most powerful factor associated with abdominal aortic tortuosity. Most of the rest of the analysed parameters were not associated with abdominal ATI, namely, the classical CVRFs, the presence of subclinical atherosclerosis, and the presence of diagnosed coronary heart disease or PAD. Although the controversy set in previous analyses remains, our results align with those that oppose the AAT index as a subclinical marker of peripheral vascular disease. Future studies in other arteries and sections would help elucidate the potential role of tortuosity as a subclinical vascular disease marker.
