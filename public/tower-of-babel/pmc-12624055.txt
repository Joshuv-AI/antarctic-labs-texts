Arousal, valence, and discrete categories in facial emotion
Authors: Jean-Yves Baudouin, Florenn Gallian, Jean-Michel Pinoit, Fabrice Damon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between the categorical recognition of emotional facial expressions and two affective dimensions, arousal and valence, as well as other facial characteristics, such as gender and age. To achieve this goal, participants were asked to evaluate expressive faces from two databases (the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces and FACES) along the dimensions of arousal and valence, in addition to completing an emotional categorization task. The results revealed variations in overall arousal and valence levels across different emotional categories. Most notably, they demonstrated that the three assessments were interdependent: the positive or negative valence of facial expressions was found to be a function of arousal level for all emotional category except surprise, whereas the accuracy of categorization into discrete categories was influenced by both arousal and valence level. Furthermore, the gender and age of the faces influenced evaluations across all three tasks – arousal ratings, valence ratings, and categorization. The implications of these findings for the study of facial emotion recognition mechanisms are discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24032-5.Keywords: Emotional facial expression recognition, Arousal, Valence, CategorySubject terms: Psychology, Emotion

Introduction

Recognizing emotions in others’ facial expressions is a fundamental ability for navigating social interactions and promoting social adaptation in humans1. As such, the cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying this ability have been a subject of scientific inquiry since at least the late nineteenth century2–4. Impairments in these mechanisms have been reported in numerous neurological and psychiatric disorders, as well as in many neurodevelopmental conditions, often resulting in compromised social interaction and integration capacities. This phenomenon has been particularly well documented in cases of amygdala lesions5, schizophrenia6,7, and autism spectrum disorders8 but also extends to a wide range of other pathologies [e.g.,9,10]. Accordingly, over the past few decades, extensive research has been conducted across multiple fields, including psychology, psychiatry, neuroscience and developmental sciences. In most of these fields, scientists have developed and employed emotional stimuli and tasks to assess the recognition of emotional facial expressions, whereby specific emotional categories are defined and participants are asked to identify them. To this end, several databases of expressive faces have been developed and made available to the scientific community (e.g., the NimStim:11; the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces or KDEF:12; FACES:13; the Radboud Faces Database or RaFD14) and are widely used in categorical recognition tasks in which participants must indicate the emotional categories to which the presented expressive stimuli belong. In line with Charles Darwin’s (or Guillaume-Benjamin Duchenne’s) original hypothesis, much of this research, either explicitly or implicitly, assumes that the perception and recognition of emotional facial expressions is grounded in preestablished discrete emotion categories. The cognitive and neural mechanisms involved are thought to function by mapping specific expressive patterns onto these preexisting categories. According to this “basic emotions” theory, a limited set of emotional categories (i.e., anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, and surprise) are expressed through stable and distinct facial action patterns, which are universally recognized as expressions of discrete emotions15–18.

Nonetheless, an almost equally longstanding assumption, originating from Williams James’ proposal, is that facial expressions are processed along affective dimensions, particularly positive–negative valence and arousal (19; see also20–23). According to this view, perceived emotion could be the result of combining valence and arousal with contextual information24,25. Importantly, the dimensional perspective and the discrete emotions framework may not be mutually exclusive; in fact, they may complement each other in advancing our understanding of facial emotion recognition26–28. Increasing evidence suggests that both types of process are involved in the processing of emotional facial expressions29–32 (see also32,33). In this context, several authors propose that inference of a discrete emotional category from a facial expression may be based on assessments of cognitive appraisal and action tendency (e.g.,34; see also35), suggesting that affective or cognitive dimensions could be integrated into the categorization processes of discrete emotions during facial emotion recognition. Similarly, the constructivist approach posits that concept knowledge guides emotion perception, enabling the separation of the affective properties of facial expressions into distinct categories36,37 (see also38).

In this context, considering both approaches could be instrumental in understanding the mechanisms underlying facial emotion recognition, as well as their development or impairment. For example, the difficulties experienced by certain patients, such as those with schizophrenia, have been linked to a reduction in the categorical perception of facial expressions39. From a developmental perspective, researchers argue that newborns and infants lack an innate categorization system and instead interpret facial expressions in terms of valence, arousal, and other contextual cues (e.g., gaze direction, vocalizations21,40; see also41). Over time, they gradually associate facial expression patterns with specific discrete categories through a process of progressive differentiation, which extends throughout childhood (see37,42). However, a significant limitation of adopting an approach that combines dimensional and categorical aspects is that most available emotional facial databases are currently evaluated on the basis of categorical distinctions, thus preventing the study or control of other dimensions, such as arousal and valence.

Another important issue is that, in the context of multidimensional facial emotion processing, evaluations of expressive faces on affective dimensions – as well as their categorization into discrete categories – appear to be influenced by visible social cues, such as model’s gender (i.e., the facial morphological features used to infer biological sex) and age (e.g.,43). Negative expressions displayed by male models and positive expressions displayed by female models tend to be rated as more intense, with decoding accuracy increasing linearly with the intensity of negative expressions44,45. The model’s sex also affects recognition accuracy, as well as ratings on affective dimensions such as arousal and valence41,46,47. Specifically, anger is more readily recognized in male faces, while happiness is more accurately identified in female faces43,48,49 (for an advantage in recognizing anger, fear, and sadness in female models, see44). Furthermore, both happiness and anger are perceived as more positive – or less negative – when displayed by female models43. As for age, emotional expressions are generally more difficult to recognize in older faces50. More specifically, it has been reported that recognition of anger, disgust, happiness, sadness and neutrality (but not fear) was less accurate when expressed by an older person than by a younger person13 (for similar observation for anger and neutrality, but not happiness, see also51). This effect was larger for male faces than female faces when expressing negative emotions (particularly disgust, fear, and sadness), but it was larger for female faces for happiness50. Furthermore, neutral older faces are more likely to be assigned a sad mood than younger faces, and less likely to be assigned a happy mood52. These phenomena are observed regardless of the age of the participants, although they are reduced for older participants in some studies (e.g.,50,52). These findings highlight the importance of accounting for sex and age when designing emotion recognition tasks.

In the present study, we addressed these issues by asking a group of 36 participants to evaluate faces from two widely used databases—the KDEF and FACES—not only in terms of categorical classification but also in terms of arousal and valence. These databases were selected because they offer a high level of control and are relatively comparable: the models were trained to display specific expressions prior to the photo sessions; body movements (apart from facial features) were restricted; and the photographs were taken from a standardized, frontal angle (although KDEF also includes non-frontal views). The models were dressed uniformly (gray T-shirts in both databases), and each expression was captured twice per model. Both databases are readily accessible and include a large number of young adult models of both sexes, with additional older models featured in the FACES database. We then analyzed the differences in arousal and valence across the emotional categories, as well as the potential relationships between these two dimensions and emotional categories, as well as the influence of other facial characteristics such as the gender and age of the faces. We hypothesized that (1) the three emotional components are interrelated, with higher levels of arousal and valence promoting the categorization of emotional expressions into discrete categories, and (2) sociodemographic factors such as gender and age modulate emotion perception across all three components, by increasing or decreasing perceived level of arousal and valence, and by facilitating or impairing emotion categorization. The investigation of potential effects of participant sex was not among the objectives of this study. However, several studies suggest that it may have an influence on the processing of facial emotions, although this influence is generally moderate to weak (e.g., see53). Thus, it was balanced among our participants and this factor was included in the statistical analyses to be controlled for.

Arousal, valence and categories in the KDEF

Arousal

Disgust was the expression rated highest in arousal (M = 4.9), followed by surprise (M = 4.7), fear (M = 4.4), anger (M = 4.4), happiness (M = 4.2), sadness (M = 3.4), and neutrality (M = 1.6) (main effect of Emotion: F(2.91,99.09) = 98.12, ε = 0.49, η2p = 0.74, p < 0.0001). Tukey’s post hoc tests (p < 0.05) revealed significantly lower ratings for neutral expressions than for emotional expressions and significantly lower ratings for sadness than for other emotional expressions. The ratings for disgust were also significantly higher than those for anger and happiness. While there was a trend toward higher arousal ratings for male faces than for female faces, the main effect of Face gender was not significant (F(1,34) = 3.82, p > 0.059). However, the interaction between Face Gender and Emotion was significant (F(3.90,132.45) = 14.00, ε = 0.65, η2p = 0.29, p < 0.0001). Linear contrasts indicated that male faces were rated with greater arousal than female faces for anger, fear, and surprise (lowest F value: F(1,34) = 6.15, p = 0.0183), with no significant difference for the other expressions (highest F value: F(1,34) = 3.04).

The factor of Participant sex had no significant main effect (F(1,34) = 0.48), but it did interact with Emotion (F(2.91,99.09) = 2.93, ε = 0.49, η2p = 0.08, p = 0.0389). Linear contrasts indicated that male participants rated neutrality with greater arousal than female participants did (F(1,34) = 5.64, p = 0.0234), whereas no significant difference in emotional expressions was found (highest F value: F(1,34) = 3.97). No other interactions were significant.

Valence

The valence ratings ranged from most negative to most positive as follows: disgust (M = − 1.9), anger (M = − 1.6), sadness (M = − 1.3), fear (M = − 1.3), surprise (M = − 0.4), neutrality (M = 0.0), and happiness (M = 2.1) (F(3.32,112.74) = 545.35, ε = 0.55, η2p = 0.94, p < 0.0001). All differences were significant after Tukey’s post hoc tests (p < 0.05), except between fear and sadness. A significant main effect of Face gender (F(1,34) = 62.33, η2p = 0.65, p < 0.0001) indicated more negative valence ratings for male faces (M = − 0.7) than for female faces (M = − 0.6). However, the significant interaction between Face Gender and Emotion (F(4.48,152.34) = 13.54, ε = 0.75, η2p = 0.28, p < 0.0001) demonstrated that this difference was significant for all emotional categories (lowest difference: F(1,34) = 12.61, p = 0.0011, on the basis of linear contrasts), except for sadness (F(1,34) = 0.02).

The main effect of Participant sex was not significant (F(1,34) = 1.67), but the interaction effect between Participant sex and Emotion was significant (F(3.32,112.73) = 7.43, ε = 0.55, η2p = 0.18, p < 0.0001). Linear contrasts revealed that female participants rated anger, disgust, and sadness significantly more negatively than male participants did (lowest F value: F(1,34) = 5.41, p = 0.0261), whereas they rated happiness more positively (F(1,34) = 13.46, p = 0.0008). No significant differences were found for fear, surprise, or neutrality (highest F value: F(1,34) = 2.48). No other interactions were significant.

Categories

The accuracy rates for emotion categorization varied by category, with performance being highest for happiness (M = 98.4%), neutrality (M = 94.1%), anger (M = 85.8%), surprise (M = 84.8%), disgust (M = 77.5%), sadness (M = 76.2%), and fear (M = 42.6) (F(3.83,130.15) = 147.54, ε = 0.64, η2p = 0.81, p < 0.0001). Tukey’s post hoc tests (p < 0.05) revealed significant differences between most categories, except for the following pairs: happiness and neutrality, anger and surprise, and disgust and sadness. Overall, accuracy was significantly better for male faces (M = 80.8%) than for female faces (M = 79.0%) (F(1,34) = 17.87, η2p = 0.34, p = 0.0002). However, there was a significant interaction effect between Face Gender and Emotion (F(4.34,147.46) = 12.41, ε = 0.72, η2p = 0.27, p < 0.0001), with greater accuracy for male faces in the categorization of anger and fear (lowest F value: F(1,34) = 26.00, p < 0.0001, on the basis of linear contrasts) and better accuracy for female faces in the categorization of sadness (F(1,34) = 11.99, p = 0.0015). No significant differences were found for the other emotional categories (highest F value: F(1,34) = 1.81).

The main effect of Participant sex was also significant (F(1,34) = 6.58, η2p = 0.16, p = 0.0210), with female participants demonstrating greater accuracy than male participants did (81.6% vs. 78.2%). Additionally, Participant sex interacted significantly with Emotion (F(3.83,130.15) = 3.05, ε = 0.64, η2p = 0.08, p = 0.0265). Linear contrasts revealed that female participants were more accurate in categorizing anger, fear, happiness, and sadness (lowest F value: F(1,34) = 4.62, p = 0.0388), but there were no significant sex differences for the other categories (highest F value: F(1,34) = 0.45). No other interactions were significant.

Arousal, valence and categories in FACES

Arousal

In the FACES dataset, fear received the highest arousal rating (M = 5.3), followed by disgust (M = 4.6), happiness (M = 4.4), anger (M = 4), sadness (M = 3.5), and neutrality (M = 1.5) (F(2.78,94.61) = 112.03, ε = 0.56, η2p = 0.77, p < 0.0001). Tukey’s post hoc tests (p < 0.05) revealed significant differences for fear or neutrality compared with all other emotions. Disgust was rated as significantly more arousing than anger and sadness, and happiness was rated as more arousing than sadness. Although the main effect of Face gender was not significant (F(1,34) = 2.03), there was a significant main effect of Age (F(1.08,36.71) = 6.56, ε = 0.54, η2p = 0.16, p = 0.0130), with lower arousal ratings for younger models (M = 3.8) than for middle-aged and older models (M = 3.9 for both; according to Tukey’s post hoc tests, p < 0.05).

Several interactions demonstrated that the effects of model gender and age interacted with each other and with emotion, including the significant three-way interaction between Emotion, Face gender and Age (F(6.75,229.44) = 5.94, ε = 0.67, η2p = 0.15, p < 0.0001). To further explore this interaction, linear contrasts were used to analyze (i) the simple effects of Face gender for each emotion and age group and (ii) the simple effects of Age for each emotion and face gender separately (Fig. 1). In summary, a significant effect of face gender was observed, with higher arousal ratings for male faces for anger across all ages and disgust in younger models. Conversely, higher arousal ratings were given to female faces for happiness across all ages and for sadness in the middle-aged and older models (lowest F value: F(1,34) = 7.03, p = 0.0121). The effect of age was significant for both male and female faces in terms of anger, disgust, fear, and neutrality but only for female faces in terms of sadness (lowest F value: F(2,68) = 7.50, p = 0.0011). Arousal ratings decreased with age for anger (for both female and male faces) and sadness (for female faces only) but increased with age for disgust, fear and neutrality across both genders. Additionally, the significant interaction between Face gender and Age for anger, fear, and sadness (lowest F value: F(2,68) = 3.80, p = 0.0272) showed that the gender effect decreased with age for anger and emerged for sadness. For fear, arousal ratings were higher in middle-aged models for female faces but higher in older models for male faces.

Although the main effect of Participant sex was not significant (F(1,34) = 0.82), it interacted significantly with Emotion (F(2.78,94.61) = 3.80, ε = 0.56, η2p = 0.10, p = 0.0147). Linear contrasts indicated that male participants rated neutrality with greater arousal than female participants did (F(1,34) = 9.85, p = 0.0035), whereas female participants rated happiness with greater arousal than male participants did (F(1,34) = 5.86, p = 0.0210). No significant differences were found for negative emotional expressions (highest F value: F(1,34) = 1.12). No other interactions were significant (all Fs < 1.18).

Valence

Disgust was rated with the most negative valence (M = − 0.1.9), followed by fear (M = − 1.5), sadness (M = − 1.4), anger (M = − 1.4), neutrality (M = 0), and happiness (M = + 2.3) (F(3.14,106.79) = 835.34, ε = 0.63, η2p = 0.96, p < 0.0001). Tukey’s post hoc tests (p < 0.05) revealed significant differences between each emotion except for fear, sadness, and anger. Compared with male faces, female faces were rated as expressing emotions with significantly less negative/more positive valence (female faces: M = − 0.62; male faces: M = − 0.66); F(1,34) = 16.94, η2p = 0.33, p = 0.0002). The main effect of Age was also significant (F(1.28,43.48) = 8.00, ε = 0.64, η2p = 0.19, p = 0.004), with younger models (M = − 0.6) being rated significantly less negatively/more positively than middle-aged and older models (M = − 0.7 for both; according to Tukey’s post hoc tests, p < 0.05).

Several interactions involved Emotion, Face gender and Age, including a significant three-way interaction between these factors (F(6.77,230.27) = 6.59, ε = 0.68, η2p = 0.16, p < 0.0001). Further investigation of the simple main effects of Face gender and Age (Fig. 1) revealed that female faces were rated with less negative/more positive valence ratings than male faces for anger at all ages, disgust in middle-aged models, happiness in younger models, sadness in middle-aged and older models, and neutrality in older models (lowest F value: F(1,34) = 9.88, p = 0.0035). The effect of age was significant for both male and female faces across all emotions (lowest F value: F(2,68) = 4.16, p = 0.0198), except for happy male faces. The valence ratings became less negative with age for angry models and sad male faces but more negative/less positive in other cases.

These gender and age effects were modulated by interactions between the two factors for anger, happiness, sadness, and neutrality (lowest F value: F(2,68) = 3.96, p = 0.0236). The gender effect diminished or vanished with age for anger and happiness but emerged for sadness and neutrality. Conversely, the age effect was more pronounced for female faces than for male happy faces, for male faces than for female angry and sad faces, and reversed between genders for neutral faces.

The main effect of Participant sex was not significant (F(1,34) = 2.01), but there was a significant interaction effect between Participant sex and Emotion (F(3.14,106.79) = 7.58, ε = 0.63, η2p = 0.18, p < 0.0001). No other interaction was significant (all Fs < 2.59). Overall, female participants were more polarized in their judgments, rating negative expressions as more negative and positive expressions as more positive. This was significant for anger (F(1,34) = 4.63, p = 0.0386), happiness (F(1,34) = 11.85, p = 0.0015) and sadness (F(1,34) = 6.54, p = 0.0152), with a trend for disgust (F(1,34) = 3.06, p = 0.0891).

Categories

Accuracy was highest for happiness (M = 99.2%), followed by neutrality (M = 93.0%), anger (M = 80.0%), disgust (M = 79.0%), sadness (M = 77.5%), and fear (M = 60.1%) (F(2.87,97.58) = 57.11, ε = 0.57, η2p = 0.63, p < 0.0001). Significant differences were observed between each emotion except between happiness and neutrality and between anger, disgust, and sadness (after Tukey’s post hoc tests, p < 0.05). The participants were more accurate with female faces (M = 82.6%) than with male faces (M = 81.3%) (F(1,34) = 20.26, η2p = 0.37, p < 0.0001). There was also a significant main effect of Age (F(1.33,45.06) = 259.12, ε = 0.66, η2p = 0.88, p < 0.0001), with accuracy decreasing significantly from young models (M = 86.9%) to middle-aged models (M = 83.1%) and from middle-aged to older models (M = 76.0%) (Tukey’s post hoc tests, p < 0.05).

Several interactions were found, including a three-way interaction between Emotion, Face gender, and Age (F(5.94,201.93) = 9.76, ε = 0.59, η2p = 0.22, p < 0.0001). Further investigation of the simple main effects of Face gender (Fig. 1) revealed greater accuracy for male faces with respect to anger (young and older models), fear (older models), and neutrality (young models). Female faces had higher accuracy in terms of disgust (middle-aged and older models), fear (middle-aged models), sadness (all age groups), and neutrality (older models) (lowest F value: F(1,34) = 6.51, p = 0.0154). The effect of age, i.e., decreased accuracy with the age of the models, was significant for both genders across all expressions (lowest F value: F(2,68) = 27.46, p = 0.0173), except for fear in male faces and happiness in both genders. The gender and age effects were modulated by an interaction between the two factors for anger, disgust, fear, and neutrality (lowest F value: F(2,68) = 8.77, p = 0.0004). For anger, the gender effect disappeared in middle-aged models, reversed with age for fear and neutrality, and emerged for disgust.

The main effect of Participant sex was significant (F(1,34) = 12.44, η2p = 0.27, p = 0.0001), with female participants (M = 84.1%) being more accurate than male participants (M = 79.8%). No other interaction was significant (all Fs < 1.93).

Interrelationships between arousal, valence and categorization: item-by-item analyses

The two-by-two scatterplots for the three tasks (arousal ratings, valence ratings, categorization) are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Scatterplots and Pearson correlation coefficients of the relationships between the three tasks (arousal ratings, valence ratings, categorization) illustrated in pairs. For surprise and neutrality, correlation coefficients involving valence are calculated from the absolute value of the mean valence ratings (i.e., deviation from 0).

Overall, there was a strong positive correlation between the arousal and valence ratings (R(3030) = 0.77, p < 0.0001). The relationships between these two dimensions and categorization accuracy were also significant, although much weaker (arousal: R(3030) = − 0.14, p < 0.0001; valence: R(3030) = 0.11, p < 0.0001), indicating a trend toward improved accuracy for lower arousal levels and more extreme valenced ratings (either positive or negative). However, these trends were moderated when emotional categories were considered, with different patterns according to emotion categories. Specifically, the correlation between arousal and valence was always strong for emotional categories with a negative or positive valence (lowest R value: R(480) = − 0.65, p < 0.0001), moderate for neutrality (R(480) = 0.49, p < 0.0001), and weak for surprise (R(138) = 0.25, p = 0.0029). Overall, the higher the arousal ratings were, the more extreme (positive or negative) the valence ratings. In terms of correct categorization, the correlation with arousal or valence was always significant but varied by emotion. For arousal in emotional expressions, higher arousal ratings correlated with better categorization (lowest R value: R(480) = 0.18, p < 0.0001; for surprise: R(138) = 0.23, p = 0.0069). In contrast, for neutrality, the correlation was negative and strong (R(480) = − 0.69, p < 0.0001). Similarly, for valence, in emotional categories with a distinct positive or a negative valence, the further the ratings deviated from 0, the better the categorization (lowest absolute R value: R(480) = − 0.20, p < 0.0001). However, for nonvalenced categories such as neutrality and surprise, the further the ratings deviated from 0, the worse the categorization (neutrality: R(480) = − 0.68, p < 0.0001; surprise: R(138) = − 0.39, p < 0.0001).

Conclusions

Our results demonstrate that different emotional categories exhibit varying levels of arousal and valence, which may act as confounding factors in participants’ response to tasks utilizing this type of material. Overall, sadness was associated with a lower level of arousal than other emotions and was also less negatively valenced. Disgust, in contrast, tended to have higher levels of both arousal and valence than anger across both databases, whereas fear showed greater variability. The models’ ability to generate discrete expressions (and/or the participants’ ability to categorization them) also varied by emotion, with near-perfect categorization for happiness and particular difficulties for fear. The former phenomenon may be due to the unique status of happiness as being the only positively valenced emotion. The latter phenomenon may be related to the relative similarity between fear and surprise, as well as the potential overlap between these two emotions at both the expressive and conceptual levels (see54–56), as suggested by the confusion patterns showing them to be the two most frequently confounded categories (see Supplementary Analyses 1). The existence of a discrete category for surprise distinct from fear or happiness remain debated, particularly due to the absence of clear valence for surprise (as indicated by our results, see also50) and the lack of well-defined categorical boundaries between surprise and other expressions49. Consequently, many studies have excluded surprise from their materials (e.g.,57–59), as did the authors of the FACES database13. Surprise may represent a “weak” example of either fear or happiness (see60), thereby introducing ambiguity in the mechanisms underlying the categorical perception of emotional facial expressions.

The confusion patterns further confirm that certain expressions are more likely to be confused with one another. This is particularly true for fear and surprise but also for anger and disgust, as well as sadness and neutrality. This point has important implications for the selection of emotional categories in experimental design: the absence of a category in a limited-choice task may bias responses toward another category. For example, the absence of “surprise” may lead to an increase in the recognition of “fear”, and the absence of “disgust” may enhance the recognition of “anger”, thereby creating the misleading impression of categorization accuracy for the latter expression. Notably, the overall percentage of correct categorization in our study is broadly comparable to the levels typically observed in validation studies for both databases (see Supplementary Analyses 2 and 3) or to validation studies of other databases (e.g., NimStim, see11; RaFD, see14). One exception is fear, which exhibited a lower categorization level in our study than that reported by Ebner et al.13. This discrepancy may be attributed to the absence of “surprise” and “other” response options in Ebner’s study, which were included in our design.

In line with the proposed relationship between the intensity of expressions and the precision of categorization (see41,42), covariation analyses revealed that the three components of an emotional expression – its arousal, valence and category – cannot be considered independent. On the contrary, the valence of an expression is proportional to its level of arousal, and its categorization becomes more accurate as the level of both arousal and valence increases. One exception is surprise, where high positive or negative valence decreases precision, likely because it shifts the perception of the expression into another category (happiness or fear), as suggested by the observed confusion pattern. In contrast, neutrality is better perceived when both arousal and valence are low. This finding underscores the need to control for these dimensions to ensure that any failure to recognize certain categories, compared with others, is in fact due to categorization issues rather than differences in arousal or valence. For example, a difference observed in the processing of anger and sadness (e.g., in brain responses) may reflect variations in arousal rather than a true differential response to distinct discrete emotion categories. Similarly, differences in cognitive or behavioral effects (e.g., attention capture) observed for anger, fear, or happiness may, in fact, be driven by differences in valence or even arousal levels of the expressions used.

Our study also demonstrated that both the gender and age of the models significantly impact emotion perception. Specifically, angry male faces were rated with higher arousal and more negative valence, and were more accurately categorized. In contrast, sad female faces were more accurately categorized as sad. Overall, emotions expressed by male models are perceived as more negative (or less positive) than those expressed by female models. These results are largely consistent with previous studies and offer further insight into the observed effects. Regarding arousal, Hess et al.42reported that negative expressions displayed by male models and positive expressions by female models were rated as more intense (for a replication of this finding for happiness but not anger, see43). For negative expressions, Hess et al. found a male model effect for anger, disgust and sadness. In our data, this male effect was also observed in arousal ratings for anger, disgust (but only for young models from the FACES database), fear (only in KDEF), and sadness (only for older models). This suggests that the male effect is most consistently observed for anger, but may extend to other negative emotions under specific conditions. Concerning valence, previous studies have described a general trend toward more positive (or less negative) evaluations of emotions expressed by female faces, particularly for anger and happiness (but not neutrality;43). We replicated these findings for both anger and happiness using both databases, but only with younger models. Interestingly, this female positivity effect persisted for anger in older models, while it diminished with age for happiness. We also observed this phenomenon for surprise in younger models, and noted that it may extend to other emotions such as disgust, fear, and sadness, although the pattern varied depending on the database and the age of the models. With respect to categorization accuracy, Kapitanovic et al.44 reported high accuracy for female models for the three emotions they tested (anger, fear, and sadness) using the KDEF database. We replicated this effect for sadness across both databases, but not for anger and fear. In fact, we observed higher accuracy for male models for these two emotions, particularly in the KDEF database. This discrepancy could be attributed to two key methodological differences: (1) our study included a larger set of possible response options, and (2) Kapitanovic et al. used facial stimuli presented from multiple angles. Additionally, as observed by Riediger et al.47, participants’ ability to accurately categorize emotional expressions decline as the models’ age increased, with older models’ expressions appearing more ambiguous, particularly for the younger participants in our study. Model age also influences perceived levels of arousal and valence, with these levels generally increasing with age across most emotions (including neutrality), except for anger, which decreases in both dimensions, especially for male faces. These findings are consistent with empirical evidence suggesting that perceptible social categories influence emotion perception (e.g.,40,61; see also62) and that contextual factors play a role in shaping inferences about facial emotions (e.g.,28). These factors should be carefully considered in the design of studies on facial emotion recognition.

Finally, even though our protocol did not aim to study the effects of participants’ sex and the sample size was too small to ensure sufficient statistical power, participant sex had a significant effect, with female participants being more accurate overall than male participants. This sex difference also manifested in the arousal and valence ratings. With respect to arousal, male participants rated neutral faces as higher in arousal than female participants did, whereas the opposite effect was observed for happiness, although this effect was evident in only one of the two databases (FACES). In contrast, female participants evaluated the valence of expressions in a more extreme manner, attributing a higher positive valence to happiness and a higher negative valence to negative expressions in general. Additionally, female participants were generally more accurate in categorizing emotions. However, while these findings align with previously reported sex differences in the literature (e.g.,53,63, they remain to be further corroborated, as other studies using these databases did not find similar results for FACES 13 or for KDEF (e.g.,53).

This study contributes significantly to the ongoing debate between basic emotion theory (BET) and affective dimension theory (ADT) by providing empirical evidence that supports the complementary nature of both frameworks. First, the findings demonstrate that emotion recognition is neither purely categorical, as BET suggests, nor entirely based on affective dimensions, such as arousal and valence, as proposed by ADT. Instead, both mechanisms interact, suggesting that they reflect different layers of emotional processing rather than mutually exclusive models. While BET highlights distinct emotional categories (e.g., happiness, fear, and disgust) as universally recognizable, the study also reveals that arousal and valence modulate the recognition process, especially for emotions such as fear and surprise, which are more susceptible to variability on the basis of these affective dimensions.

Another key finding is the interdependence between arousal, valence, and categorization accuracy, providing strong support for ADT. Emotions with high arousal and extreme valence, such as happiness and disgust, are categorized more accurately, whereas those with lower arousal or ambiguous valence, such as neutrality or surprise, are more prone to misclassification. This finding suggests that arousal and valence enhance the ability to categorize emotions, particularly when expressions are ambiguous or overlap, as reflected by the frequent confusion between fear and surprise. Moreover, the study challenges the universality of BET by showing that categorization accuracy varies depending on social and demographic factors, such as the gender/sex and age of both the models and the participants. Male faces, for instance, are perceived as more aggressive, whereas female faces are often associated with sadness. These findings indicate that emotion recognition is contextually influenced, aligning with ADT’s emphasis on continuous affective dimensions. This is also consistent with models that propose that the perception of faces is shaped by high-level social categorization processes (e.g., the dynamic interactive model,62).

Overall, the results support a hybrid approach, whereby BET provides a foundational understanding of core emotions, while ADT explains the variability in emotion perception influenced by arousal, valence, and social context. This integrative perspective highlights the importance of accounting for both categorical and dimensional factors in the study of emotion recognition, particularly in socially complex or ambiguous situations.

Overall, the images from KDEF and FACES yielded broadly comparable assessments, particularly for younger models across both databases: arousal and valence ratings, as well as categorization accuracy for the different expressions, were similar in most cases. However, a notable discrepancy emerged for fear, which was less accurately categorized and rated as lower in arousal in the KDEF compared to FACES. This occurred despite the fact that KDEF models were required to produce both fear and surprise expressions – which, as shown, are often similar and easily confused – whereas FACES models were only instructed to produce fear. It is plausible that the demand for KDEF models to clearly differentiate fear from surprise led them to downplay common features (e.g., wide eyes and open mouth) and emphasize distinctive ones, potentially resulting in fear expressions that were less intense and less recognizable. In addition, both databases allowed for the observation of model sex effects, though the specific patterns varied. Male faces were associated with higher arousal, more negative valence, and greater categorization accuracy for anger in both databases. A female face advantage was found for sadness categorization across both databases. However, some sex effects were not consistent across expressions or dimensions. For disgust, a male face advantage was found in valence ratings in KDEF and in arousal ratings in FACES. For fear, a male face superiority effect was evident across all three components in KDEF, but was absent in FACES. For happiness, a female face advantage in valence ratings was observed in both databases, but the arousal effect was only present in FACES.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, particularly with regard to the number of participants per sex (N = 18), limiting the generalizability of findings related to sex differences and underscoring the need for replication. Second, the study was conducted online, with young, relatively well-educated participants completing the task in their own homes, outside of a controlled laboratory setting. This may have increased inter-participant variability. Furthermore, the demographic characteristics of our sample may have contributed to relatively high recognition scores compared to the general population. The use of this material with other populations—such as children or older adults—could result in lower accuracy rates. The effect of facial age may also be smaller for older participants, as perceivers may be better at interpreting emotional cues from age peers, likely due to greater exposure and familiarity (see, for examples,50,52). Future research should also consider including measures of alexithymia or specific cognitive abilities that may influence emotion recognition performance. Another important limitation pertains to the stimuli themselves: the emotional expressions were portrayed by actors trained to produce prototypical facial expressions, following specific instructions based on established criteria (i.e.,64,65). While this approach ensures the presence of canonical facial action patterns for each emotion, it may reduce ecological validity, as spontaneous emotional expressions in everyday contexts tend to be more subtle and variable. The distribution of expressions on the dimensions of arousal and valence may likewise have been more spread out at the extremes than everyday expressions. The use of instructed expressions may also have influenced the quality and recognizability of the portrayed emotions. For instance, our results suggest that models who were instructed to produce both fear and surprise (as in KDEF) may have generated less intense and less recognizable expressions of fear, compared to models in FACES who were instructed to express only fear. These findings underscore that stimulus characteristics – including the instructions given to models and the conditions under which images are captured – can significantly influence both the properties of the stimuli and the effects observed in studies, such as sex differences in emotion recognition (see66).

In conclusion, by evaluating arousal and valence ratings alongside categorical recognition accuracy across two widely used databases, our study reinforces the importance of accounting for these dimensions in research on facial emotion recognition. Neglecting to control arousal and valence could lead to misleading conclusions about the cognitive and affective mechanisms underlying emotion perception, as well as about the factors contributing to their disruption or development, whether typical or atypical. The evaluations we provide for these two expressive face databases offer a valuable basis for controlling these variables or for investigating their roles across various domains.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-six participants (18 women) participated in the study. The average age of the participants was 25 years, with no difference between male and female participants (female participants: M = 24 years 6 months, SD = 2 years 6 months, range = 21–30 years; male participants: M = 25 years 7 months, SD = 3 years 6 months, range = 19–30 years; T(34) = 1.05, p = 0.3017). They all lived in France or Belgium. To be included in the study, participants had to have no vision problems (or problems corrected by eyeglasses, for example), no neurological or psychiatric problems, no developmental disorders, and be taking no somatic medications with a cerebral or psychic impact (neuroleptics or antipsychotics, antidepressants, mood regulators or normothymics, tranquilizers or anxiolytics, sleeping pills or hypnotics, or corticosteroids). The participants were recruited by advertisement at the university (N = 10, 8 women) or via the online participant recruitment site https://www.prolific.com/ (N = 26, 8 women) to take part in a study that involved 18 experimental sessions over the course of several days (average study duration in days from inclusion to the end of the study: M = 21, range 4–81). Twelve additional participants were recruited but not included in the analyses because they had not completed all the experimental conditions (N = 8) or had a high number of responses below 300 ms (from 25% to 85.9% of responses for one of the three tasks), indicating poor task performance (N = 4). The participants were paid the equivalent of 10 euros per hour for the various experimental conditions (estimated duration: approximately 10 h).

Facial expression stimuli

We used color photographs from 2 databases: the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces (KDEF) database12 and FACES13. In both databases, the images of the facial expressions were in color and captured from a frontal view, with the models looking directly at the camera and wearing uniform gray T-shirts, with two shots per expression. The models depicted in KDEF were amateur actors who received written instructions followed by an hour of training prior to the photo session, with the purpose of evoking the target emotion in a way that felt natural, ensuring that the expressions were both strong and clear. The models provided consent for their image to be used freely in research contexts (see https://kdef.se/home/using%20and%20publishing%20kdef%20and%20akdef.html). The modeled expressions were anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise, and neutrality. The database includes pictures from 70 models (35 females and 35 males) between 20 and 30 years. The expressions modeled in FACES were the same as those in KDEF, with the exception of surprise, which was not included in the database by the authors. Prior to the photo session, the models participated in a three-phase preparation process: (1) an emotion induction phase aimed at triggering a spontaneous emotion experience, (2) a personal experience phase to elicit the target emotion through the recall of personal events, and (3) a controlled expression phase providing guidance on how to activate and maintain specific facial muscle movements. This was followed by a training session during which an instructor explained the facial muscle actions involved in each expression – particularly around the eyes, nose, and mouth – based on the criteria described by Ekman and Friesen60. Models were instructed to express each emotion as intensively as possible, while maintaining a natural appearance. The models included 58 young (age range: 19–31; 29 women), 56 middle-aged (age range: 39–55; 27 women), and 57 older (age range: 69–80; 29 women) women and men (for more details about FACES, see https://faces.mpdl.mpg.de/imeji/). Thus, the total material comprised 3032 images (N = 980 for the KDEF, N = 2052 for FACES), with 241 models (120 women) of various ages, expressing 6–7 emotions twice.

Procedure

The participants completed the experimental conditions at home on their personal computers at their own pace. They were instructed not to complete more than two conditions in a row within a half-day period. Images were displayed either via Erun software (generated via E-Prime 3), which was installed by the experimenter on their computers, or online via the platform Pavlovia (https://pavlovia.org/, programmed via the free software PsychoPy v2023.2.3 on the Gitlab platform).

The images from the two databases were divided into six approximately equivalent blocks: two for the KDEF (one containing the first shot and another with the second shot; N = 490 per block) and four for FACES (two blocks with the first and second shots of Models 004 to 093; N = 516 per block, and two with the first and second shots of Models 094 to 182; N = 510 per block). The original images were unaltered, except for a few overexposed KDEF images (N = 44), for which exposure was corrected using the built-in filter of Adobe Photoshop CS6 software. Images were displayed sequentially, occupying 80% of the screen height, and remained on the screen until the participants responded. The instructions were presented above each image, and the possible responses presented below. The next image appeared immediately after the participant provided their response. The participants performed three tasks—arousal ratings, valence ratings and expression categorization—across all six image blocks, resulting in 18 experimental conditions (3 tasks × 6 blocks). The order of tasks, blocks within tasks, and images within blocks was randomized among the participants. Before each block, participants practiced on 20 pictures randomly selected from the pictures in the block to familiarize themselves with the task, with the option to skip practice if they had already completed the task in a previous block. The participants provided responses via the keyboard.

For the arousal rating task, the participants were instructed to use a 7-point scale to assess, for each image, the level of activation or deactivation evoked by the emotion displayed. The instructions, shown above each image, clarified that an activating emotion induces a high level of excitement, arousal, alertness, or energy, whereas a deactivating emotion evokes calm, relaxation, lethargy, serenity, or apathy. These terms were selected among those commonly used in the literature (e.g.,19), while avoiding those that trigger emotional categories defined in the categorization task (i.e., anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise, and neutrality). The responses were made by pressing keys 1 through 7 on the keyboard.

For the valence rating task, participants also used a 7-point scale, rating the emotional valence of each image as unpleasant or pleasant. The instructions above each image specified that an unpleasant emotion reflects negative feelings, whereas pleasant emotions indicate positive feelings. Response keys 1 through 7 corresponded to a valence range from − 3 (key 1) to + 3 (key 7), which was indicated below each image. The participants were explicitly instructed to ignore arousal when rating valence and vice versa, with reminders of the definitions provided for each dimension during the respective tasks. In other words, during their ratings on one dimension (arousal or valence), participants were given a description of the other dimension with explicit instructions not to take this second dimension into account in their rating on the first.

Finally, for the categorization task, the participants were asked to identify the emotion expressed by each person in the images. The possible responses—anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise, neutral, and “other”—were displayed at the bottom of the image and were the same for both databases. The participants pressed the first letter of the corresponding category on the keyboard (e.g., “D” for disgust), noting that the category labels were in French, as all the participants were French-speaking. The participants were not instructed to respond as quickly as possible and were encouraged to pause in the middle of the blocks, allowing an image to remain on screen if desired. However, they were advised not to spend excessive time on each image, given the large number of images to be processed throughout the study (N = 9096 over several days/weeks). The participants were informed of the total number of images they would need to evaluate and were provided with information on potential health risks from prolonged screen exposure with a link to relevant guidelines (https://www.inrs.fr/risques/travail-ecran/risques-sante.html). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Lyon University (Comité CER-UdL, notification no. 2023–09-21–011) and the study has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data analyses

All analyses were conducted using JASP 0.19.3 and Statistica 10.0 software. Responses (arousal ratings, valence ratings, and category classification) with response times shorter than 300 ms (representing 0.54% of all responses) were excluded from the analyses, as they were considered too rapid to reflect sufficient processing time to be valid.

To assess arousal, valence, and categorization accuracy for the KDEF database, we conducted three separate analyses of variance (ANOVAs) on arousal ratings, valence ratings, and categorization percentages. As the literature suggests that the sex of participants may have an effect on inter-individual variability, this factor was included in the analyses to control for its effect on the error terms. The effects involving this factor are reported for information purposes, but it should be noted that the respective sample sizes for each sex are insufficient to draw robust conclusions. Thus, the within-subjects factors were Emotion (anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise, and neutrality) and Face gender (female, male), whereas Participant sex (female vs. male) was a between-subjects factor. Similar ANOVAs were performed for the FACES database, with the within-subject factors being Emotion (anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, and neutrality), Face gender (female and male), and Age (young, middle-aged, and older), with Participant sex (female and male) as between-subject factor. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. For significant interactions, we tested the simple effects of face gender and age via linear contrasts. Bonferroni corrections were applied when interactions involved more than two factors (for the effect of Face gender at each age and expression: α = 0.05/(3 ages) = 0.0167; for the effect of Face age for each gender and expression: α = 0.05/(2 ages) = 0.025). Post hoc analyses for factors with more than two levels (i.e., emotion and age) were performed via Tukey’s post hoc test (p < 0.05) to identify significant pairwise differences between factor levels.

To assess the relationships between the three dimensions, Pearson correlation coefficients (R) were computed on the basis of item averages, first for all the pictures (N = 3032) and then for each category (N = 482; for surprise: N = 140). For emotions with clear positive or negative valence, mean valence ratings (either positive or negative) for each image were used to compute the correlation coefficient. For nonvalenced categories (surprise and neutrality) and for overall analyses across categories, the absolute values of the mean valence ratings (i.e., deviation from 0) were used. The rationale is that for these two non-valenced categories, the expected average valence is zero; so we would expect valence ratings to covary with arousal ratings and/or categorization accuracy when scores deviate from zero (positively or negatively), unlike valenced emotions, where these links are expected when valence ratings vary between the negative and positive poles.
