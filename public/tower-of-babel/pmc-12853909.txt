Advance biocatalyst for the green conversion of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural to a biopolymer precursor 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid
Authors: Lata Pawar, Pramod Kumar, Rishu Dhiman, Divya Mittal, Ashutosh Kumar, Rituraj Purohit, Sudesh Kumar Yadav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background2,5-Furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA) is a bio-derived compound which serves as a building block of polyethylene furandicarboxylate (PEF) for the synthesis of bio-plastics. 5-Hydroxymethylfurfural oxidase (HMFO) is a promising biocatalyst for the oxidation of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) to FDCA through three oxidation steps. The substrate could be fructose and other C6 sugars present in lignocellulosic biomass, providing an eco-friendly and sustainable route for the production of FDCA, an essential monomer for biopolymer development.ResultThe newly identified HMFO enzyme from Pseudomonas poae (PSPO) was heterologously expressed in E. coli, and the recombinant protein was purified and characterized. The catalytic function of PSPO was authenticated for the oxidation of HMF to FDCA. PSPO oxidized HMF, resulting in a 99% yield of FDCA within 24 h. The secondary structure composition of PSPO was analyzed. The optimal enzymatic activity of the recombinant protein was observed at pH 8.0 and temperature of 30℃. PSPO enzyme stability against H2O2 was evaluated, and the enzyme was observed to retain ˃90% of residual activity at lower concentrations of H2O2 (2 to 6 mM). The kinetic parameters of PSPO for the substrates HMF, FFCA, DFF, and vanillyl alcohol were also determined. The Km values for HMF, FFCA, DFF, and vanillyl alcohol were 2.252 mM, 5.551 mM, 2.503 mM, and 1.589 mM, respectively.ConclusionThe identified and characterized PSPO was found to have a potential for the accelerated enzymatic oxidation of HMF to FDCA. Through molecular dynamic (MD) simulations of the catalytic pathway and enzyme mechanisms, alongside assessing the impact of substrate concentration, PSPO turns out to be a potential candidate for enhancing reaction efficiency and maximizing yield.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13068-025-02705-7.

Introduction

Biopolymers could serve as a sustainable alternative to overcome plastic pollution and reduce greenhouse gas emissions. Biopolymers are the naturally occurring or synthetically driven polymers obtained from renewable sources such as plants, animals, and microorganisms, whereas polymers are obtained from fossil fuels. Polyethylene furandicarboxylate (PEF) is regarded as a promising substitute for polyethylene terephthalate (PET), exhibiting higher barrier properties, thermal stability, and inherent biodegradability [1]. PEF is a bio-derived polymer synthesized from FDCA, which is derived from abundant carbohydrate-based renewable materials. Carbohydrate-derived products such as 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) are widely used for FDCA production [2]. The oxidation of HMF to FDCA follows two different pathways (Fig. 1). The alcohol group of HMF is first oxidized to 2,5-diformylfuran (DFF), which then undergoes further oxidation to 5-formyl-2-furancarboxylic acid (FFCA) and is finally converted to FDCA. In the other route, the aldehyde group of HMF is oxidized to 5-hydroxymethyl-2-furancarboxylic acid (HMFCA) and continues with the oxidation of HMFCA to FFCA and finally yields FDCA [3]. Chemical synthesis of FDCA from HMF often requires organic solvents, metal catalysts, high temperatures, and pressure and high costs, making it less sustainable [4, 5]. Alternatively, biocatalysis using either whole cells or enzymatic processes offers a greener solution [6].Fig. 1Conversion of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) to 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA) via two different pathways. Conversion of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) to 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA) via two different pathways. The transformation of HMF proceeds via a stepwise oxidation mechanism, initially converting the hydroxyl and aldehyde moieties into 2,5-diformylfuran (DFF) and 5-hydroxymethyl-2-furancarboxylic acid (HMFCA). Subsequent oxidation of these intermediates results in the formation of 5-formyl-2-furancarboxylic acid (FFCA), which is then fully oxidized to FDCA

5-Hydroxymethylfurfural oxidase (HMFO, EC 1.1.3.47) is a member of the glucose–methanol–choline (GMC) family of oxidoreductases and has the ability to catalyze the oxidation of HMF to FDCA [7]. HMFO is a flavoprotein that employs flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) as a prosthetic group, catalyzing the oxidation of primary thiols, primary alcohols, and hydrated aldehydes [8]. The oxidation process involves the transfer of a proton from the hydroxyl group of the substrate to an active site histidine residue, followed by hydride abstraction from the substrate’s α-carbon by the oxidized flavin. The reduced flavin undergoes reoxidation by molecular oxygen, leading to the formation of hydrogen peroxide as a by-product [9]. HMFO has been well studied and characterized from Methylovorus sp. Strain MP688 [10]. The Cupriavidus basilensis strain HMF14, Pseudomonas nitroreducens, and Pseudomonas putida strain ALS1267 have been identified with HMF-oxidizing activity [11–13]. The ability of HMFO to catalyze furfural aldehydes and alcohols suggests its high potential as a biocatalyst for the conversion of HMF to FDCA. Other oxidases, such as glyoxal oxidase (GLOX) from Pycnoporus cinnabarinus and galactose oxidase from Dactylium dendroides, act either on aldehydes or alcoholic groups of furfural derivatives, thus restricting the complete oxidation of HMF to FDCA [14]. The formation of FFCA acts as the bottleneck in the oxidation pathway of HMF. Several mutants of Methylovorus HMFO were produced for faster oxidation of FFCA. These mutants V367R, W466F, and V367R/W466F act as improved biocatalysts compared to the native enzyme [15]. Although several enzymes have been studied, only a few have shown catalytic activity towards HMF and its oxidation intermediates, such as DFF, HMFCA, and FFCA. However, the conversion rate was either incomplete or slow, and therefore the yield of FDCA was quite low. Multienzyme-based reactions have been performed as most of these enzymes, upon their individual use, were incapable of full conversion of HMF to FDCA [16–18].

Taking this into consideration, HMFO being a highly potential biocatalyst for the biopolymer synthesis, present study was aimed to identify new HMFOs through comprehensive database screening. As a result, new HMFO enzyme from Pseudomonas poae strain MYb117 (PSPO) was expressed in E. coli, and the purified PSPO was characterized for oxidative activity on HMF. Further, the cofactor dependency, effect of pH and temperature on enzyme activity and stability, effect of metal ions on enzyme activity, and the inhibition by reaction by-product (H2O2) and kinetic parameters of PSPO were evaluated.

Materials and methods

5-(Hydroxymethyl)furfural (HMF), 5-formyl-2-furancarboxylic acid (FFCA), 2,5-diformylfuran (DFF), 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA), vanillin and vanillyl alcohol were procured from Sigma-Aldrich. Flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) was purchased from TCI. The gene HMFO encoding for 5-hydroxymethylfurfural oxidase from Pseudomonas poae was synthesized in pET-28 a ( +) vector from Gene Script, USA. Luria broth, Triton-X, methanol, isopropanol, bis-acrylamide, imidazole, sodium chloride (NaCl), β-mercaptoethanol, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and salts of Mg, K, Co, Cu, Ca, Li, Zn and Mn were obtained from Himedia. Isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), sodiumdodecyl sulfate (SDS), glycerol, formic acid, sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and acrylamide were procured from Sigma-Aldrich.

Bioinformatics study, computational modeling, and simulations

The well-characterized amino acid sequence of HMFO from Methylovorus sp. MP688 (mp688xHMFO) was BLAST against the nonredundant protein sequences in the database. The theoretical molecular weight of PSPO was calculated using Protparam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The molecular interactions between the 5-(hydroxymethyl)furfural (HMF) substrate and HMFO enzymes from Pseudomonas poae (PSPO) and Methylovorus sp. strain MP688 (mp688xHMFO) were modeled using Protenix (AlphaFold3) [19] and optimized with commercial Discovery Studio software [20] by adding some critical residues, including VAL105, HIS466, and ASN510 [21, 22]⁠. Docking poses of PSPO/HMF and mp688xHMFO/HMF complexes were subjected to molecular dynamic (MD) simulations using GROMACS 2020.5 [23]. Protein topology was generated using the CHARMM36 force field [24], and the HMF topology was prepared using the CGenFF server. The systems were solvated using the TIP3P explicit water model. The systems were neutralized by adding Na+ and Cl⁻ ions. Energy minimization was performed with the steepest descent algorithm (50,000 steps, convergence at 1000 kJ/mol). The Verlet cut-off scheme was used, with a 1.2 nm cutoff for Coulomb and van der Waals interactions, and Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) for long-range electrostatics⁠ [25]. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions. NVT and NPT equilibration runs (100 ps each) were conducted with the leap-frog integrator (2 fs time step). The temperature (300 K) was maintained using the V-rescale thermostat with separate coupling groups for the complex and solvent, while pressure stabilization (1 bar) during NPT was achieved with the Berendsen barostat. Final MD simulations (50 ns) were carried out with the leap-frog integrator (2 fs time step). System conditions were maintained at 300 K (V-rescale thermostat) and 1 bar (Parrinello–Rahman barostat), with LINCS constraints applied to all bonds. PME was used for electrostatics, and periodic boundary conditions and dispersion corrections were applied. Snapshots were saved at 30 ps intervals, and MM/PBSA analysis identified the lowest-energy conformations [26], which were further used for steered molecular dynamics (SMD) simulations.

SMD simulations, employing both constant velocity and constant force protocols, were performed for 500 ps using GROMACS 2020.5. The systems were prepared following the same methodology used in MD simulations, involving energy minimization and equilibration steps. The equilibrated coordinates and velocities were used as starting points for the SMD simulations. To simulate the pulling process, the protein and FAD cofactor were treated as a unified group, while HMF was pulled along the z-axis at a constant velocity of 0.01 nm/ps using a harmonic potential with a spring constant of 100 kJ/mol·nm2. The simulations were conducted under physiological conditions, maintaining a temperature of 310 K using the Nose–Hoover thermostat and a pressure of 1 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat.

Expression and purification of PSPO

The codon-optimized DNA sequence encoding HMFO from Pseudomonas poae strain MYb117 (PSPO) (NCBI accession number-105697938.1) was synthesized and cloned into the pET 28a( +) expression vector between the EcoR1 and Xho1 restriction sites by Gene Script. The recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells and selected on Luria agar plates supplemented with kanamycin. Positive transformants were screened via PCR amplification using T7 forward and reverse primers. Positive colonies were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells. E. coli cells harboring the expression vector were incubated overnight in Luria broth (LB) supplemented with 50 mg/ml kanamycin at 37℃ under continuous agitation. This preculture was used to inoculate 1L of LB supplemented with kanamycin, and the culture was allowed to grow until the A600 reached between 0.6 and 0.8. Further, the culture was induced with different concentrations of IPTG (0.2 mM to 1 mM) and grown for 36 h at 16℃ with shaking at 150 rpm. The bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was suspended in lysis buffer (Tris–Cl, EDTA and tritonX-100) and 5 µl of 50 mg/ml lysozyme. The mixture was incubated for 30–45 min and sonicated. The lysate was centrifuged, and the cell debris was eliminated. The supernatant was purified using Ni–NTA His tag column chromatography. The Ni–NTA column was first equilibrated using the equilibration buffer (20 mM Tris–Cl buffer [pH 8.0], 100 mM NaCl and 10 mM imidazole). The crude soluble His-tagged PSPO was passed through the pre-equilibrated column followed by washing buffer (20 mM Tris–Cl buffer [pH 8.0], 100 mM NaCl and 20 mM imidazole). Finally, PSPO was eluted using the elution buffer (20 mM Tris–Cl buffer [pH 8.0], 100 mM NaCl and 200 mM imidazole). A 30-kDa molecular weight cutoff (Millipore) was used for PSPO’s concentration. Bradford assay was performed for protein concentration, with BSA as a standard. Qualitative analysis of PSPO was conducted using 12% SDS-PAGE.

Circular dichroism analysis of PSPO

The secondary structure composition of PSPO was analyzed using a MOS-500 Circular Dichroism spectrophotometer (Biologic Science Instruments Private Limited, France) in the far UV region of 190–260 nm. The protein (PSPO) sample was dialyzed against 10 mM Tris–Cl, pH 8.0. The 7.5 µM of PSPO was prepared in 10 mM Tris–Cl, pH 8.0 and introduced in the CD spectrophotometer at 25℃ with a 1-mm path length in a nitrogen filled environment. Multiple scans were recorded and averaged to take the final spectrum. The spectra were analyzed with the online CD tool BeStSel (Beta Structure Selection)—Single spectrum analysis (https://bestsel.elte.hu/sexamin.php) to determine the secondary structure composition.

Enzymatic assay of PSPO

The enzymatic assay was performed in 20 mM Tris–Cl buffer (pH 8.0), containing 2 mM of HMF as a substrate, 2 µM of FAD as a cofactor treated with 7.5 µM of purified protein (PSPO) at 30℃, under continuous shaking (500 rpm). The reaction was terminated by boiling the reaction mixture at 100℃ for 5 min. The amount of FDCA produced after the catalytic activity of PSPO on HMF was measured by HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography, Agilent Technologies, USA).

Effect of pH and temperature on PSPO’s activity and stability

In order to determine the optimum pH for PSPO’s activity, standard enzymatic assays were performed in a wide pH range (pH 2.0–10.0) by using different buffers (20 mM). The pH stability was estimated by pre-incubating the enzyme in optimum buffer for 12, 24, 48, and 72 h at room temperature, followed by conducting standard enzymatic reactions. To assess the optimum temperature for PSPO’s activity, the enzyme reactions were carried out in the wide range of temperature (4–70 ℃). To examine the thermal stability, the enzyme was exposed to optimum temperature for 72 h, and residual activity was calculated.

Effect of metal ions on PSPO’s activity

To study the effect of different metals on PSPO, enzymatic activity was performed in the presence of 1 mM of metal ions such as Cu2+, Zn2+, Mn2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Ca2+, Na1+, Ag1+, K1+, and Fe2+ under standard assay conditions. Control reaction was having no metal ions.

Effect of H2O2 on PSPO’s stability

PSPO’s stability against H2O2 was estimated by incubating the enzyme (7.5 µM) in 20 mM Tris–Cl, pH 8.0 with different concentrations of H2O2 (2–10 mM) for 24, 48, and 72 h at room temperature. Residual activities were calculated by following the oxidation of saturating concentrations of HMF (2 mM) in 20 mM Tris–Cl, pH 8.0 at 30℃. The highest activity after mixing was taken as 100% and residual activity was calculated according to this maximal value.

Kinetic parameters of PSPO

The kinetic parameters Michaelis–Menten constant (Km), the turnover number (Kcat) and catalytic efficiency (Kcat/ Km) were evaluated for vanillyl alcohol, FFCA, DFF, and HMF using GraphPad Prism employing the Michaelis–Menten equation. Steady-state parameters for the oxidation of HMF (1–10 mM), FFCA (1–10 mM) and DFF (1–10 mM) to FDCA by PSPO were calculated. The oxidation reaction of vanillyl alcohol to vanillin by 4 µM of PSPO was carried out by using various concentrations of vanillyl alcohol (0.5–7 mM) in 20 mM Tris–Cl, pH 8.0 at 30 ℃ for 15 min, under continuous shaking (500 rpm). The amount of vanillin produced via oxidation of vanillyl alcohol was confirmed through UPLC (Ultra performance liquid chromatography, Acquity Waters Corporation, USA).

HPLC analysis

Reaction samples were filtered using 0.2 µm filters, followed by analysis of HMF and its derivatives using reverse-phase HPLC equipped with an Aminex HPX-87H column (300*7.8 mm, BIO-RAD). 5 mM sulfuric acid (H2SO4) mixed in 1 L Milli-Q (MQ) was degassed and used as mobile phase in reverse-phase HPLC. The chromatographic separation was conducted at a column temperature of 30℃, with mobile phase flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, while detection was performed at a wavelength of 280 nm.

UPLC analysis

Vanillyl alcohol and vanillin were analyzed using UPLC with Eclipse Plus C18, 5 µm column (4.6*100 mm, Agilent). The following gradient was used for the analysis: starting with 90% of A solvent (Milli-Q + 0.1% formic acid) and 10% B solvent (acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid), then gradually increasing B to 50–70%. The column temperature was 30 ℃, the flow rate was 0.5 mL/min, and the detector wavelength was 280 nm.

Results and discussion

Binding site-directed docking study on HMF oxidation enzymes

The blast of mp688xHMFO protein sequence against the nr protein sequences in the NCBI database revealed 53 sequences with percentage identities of ˃ 50% (Table S1). Most of the HMFOs were from the Pseudomonas, followed by Bradyrhizobium and Methylobacterium genera. From this list of enzymes, Pseudomonas poae (PSPO) was selected and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) for the production of recombinant protein. PSPO had conserved domains that are FAD binding sites (Pfam00732 and Pfam05199) present in GMC proteins [9]. The sequence alignment of PSPO and mp688xHMFO was analyzed further. Molecular docking of the HMF substrate with two key enzymes, PSPO and mp688xHMFO, was performed to elucidate their binding modes and interaction profiles. Utilizing high-accuracy docking server Protenix (AlphaFold3) [19], reliable docking poses were obtained with strong binding affinities, showcasing key interactions between the substrate and active site residues of both enzymes. Three residues were found to be common in both complexes including MET103, TRP368, and ASN510. However, the position of TRP and ASN residues are slightly downshifted in mp688xHMFO and it is showing TRP365 and ASN507. Figure 2 illustrates the protein sequence alignment of the PSPO and mp688xHMFO enzymes, revealing a sequence identity of 63.07%. Identical residues are highlighted in dark blue. Using insights from AlphaFold3, the binding pockets of the enzymes were optimized by including key residues VAL105, HIS466, and ASN510, based on literature studies [21, 22]. The CDOCKER interaction energies for the PSPO/HMF and mp688xHMFO/HMF complexes were calculated as −113.26 kJ/mol and −87.61 kJ/mol, respectively. These results indicate that PSPO exhibits a higher binding affinity toward the HMF substrate compared to mp688xHMFO. The stronger binding affinity of PSPO provides favorable conditions for efficient catalysis, enabling the 99% conversion of HMF to FDCA within 24 h, compared to 96 h for mp688xHMFO. Interaction diagram analysis of the two complexes revealed that the PSPO/HMF complex formed a total of seven non-covalent interactions, including two conventional hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) with ARG75 and ARG429, and two non-conventional carbon–hydrogen bonds (C··H–O bonds) with GLU78. Additionally, three π-electron-based interactions were observed, comprising two π–alkyl interactions with ALA130 and ILE518, and one π–donor interaction, as illustrated in Fig. 3. In contrast, the mp688xHMFO/HMF complex exhibited a total of six non-covalent interactions, including two hydrogen bonds with ASN507 and FAD, but no C··H–O bond was observed. This complex demonstrated four π-electron-based interactions, comprising two π–π stacking and T-shaped interactions with HIS330 and TRP365, one π–alkyl interaction with MET103, and one π–cation interaction with ARG329. The higher number of non-covalent interactions and stronger binding affinity of PSPO for HMF highlight its superior catalytic efficiency compared to mp688xHMFO. These computational findings align well with experimental results, further supporting the potential of PSPO as an effective biocatalyst for the conversion of HMF to FDCA. Structural and dynamic insights into the binding mechanisms of PSPO and mp688xHMFO with HMF

The structural and dynamic analysis of the PSPO and mp688xHMFO complexes with HMF highlights critical differences in their interactions, which can help us to explain the observed differences in catalytic efficiency. PSPO converted HMF to FDCA within 24 h, compared to the 96 h required by mp688xHMFO. This enhanced efficiency can be attributed to the stable and specific interactions between HMF and key residues in the PSPO binding cavity, as observed from the short MD simulations. At 5 ns, PSPO demonstrated stable non-covalent interactions, including π–alkyl bonds formed by LEU71, PRO428, and ARG432, as well as π–π stacking with PHE433, stabilizing HMF in the binding pocket. By 25 ns, the binding was further strengthened by forming one amide–π stacking with VAL130, two C··H–O bonds with GLY129, and π–alkyl bonds with ARG75, which suggests a dynamic and favorable binding environment for HMF. By 50 ns, the system revealed a network of non-covalent interactions involving PRO428 (C··H–O bond), ARG429 (H-bond), ARG75 (C··H–O bond and π–alkyl), LEU71 (π–alkyl), TYR133 (π–π T-shaped), GLY129 (amide–π stacked and C··H–O bond) and VAL130 (π–alkyl), collectively contributing to strong and consistent stabilization of HMF. These residues likely facilitate the proper orientation of HMF within the active site, promoting efficient catalytic conversion.

Conversely, in the mp688xHMFO/HMF complex, interactions were comparatively less diverse and stable. At 5 ns, critical residues such as MET103 and VAL104 formed H-bonds and π–alkyl bonds, as well as HIS463 forming one H-bond for stabilizing HMF. However, as the simulation progressed, fewer interactions were observed. At 25 ns, HIS351 formed one π–π and one C··H–O bond, and HIS330 formed one C··H–O bond, while at 50 ns, only one π–donor stabilizing interaction by HIS330 was observed. Notably, steric clashes due to donor–donor interactions involving ASN102 were identified at 50 ns. The details of these non-covalent interactions are shown in Table 1. Such clashes could hinder substrate stabilization and proper alignment, resulting in decreased catalytic efficiency compared to PSPO. Overall, the ability of PSPO to form and maintain a diverse non-covalent interaction involving key residues such as LEU71, ARG75, GLY129, VAL130, and PRO428 facilitates efficient substrate stabilization and proper orientation of HMF in the active site. In contrast, mp688xHMFO's relatively limited interaction and steric clashes contribute to its slower reaction rate. The MD-driven MM/PBSA analysis of PSPO/HMF and mp688xHMFO/HMF complexes reveals that van der Waals, electrostatic, and SASA energies contribute favorably to binding, while polar solvation energy opposes it (Table S2). Van der Waals interactions are the most substantial stabilizing factor, with PSPO/HMF (−61.01 kcal/mol) showing greater stability than mp688xHMFO/HMF (−58.19 kcal/mol). Electrostatic interactions are more pronounced in mp688xHMFO/HMF (−25.75 kJ/mol) than PSPO/HMF (−14.55 kJ/mol), indicating stronger charge-based interactions, while SASA energy remains comparable in both complexes. However, the high polar solvation energy in mp688xHMFO/HMF (94.36 kJ/mol) made unfavorable binding free energy (1.445 kcal/mol) compared to PSPO/HMF (−6.456 kcal/mol). The MM/PBSA results suggest that PSPO/HMF forms a more stable complex, primarily by stronger van der Waals interactions and lower polar solvation energy.

RMSF and RMSD insights into enzyme dynamics

The higher RMSF values observed in PSPO compared to mp688xHMFO provide enzyme flexibility to catalytic efficiency. Increased fluctuations in PSPO may indicate enhanced flexibility in regions critical for substrate binding, active site dynamics, or product release, facilitating faster conversion of HMF to FDCA. This flexibility might enable the enzyme to adopt multiple conformations essential for efficient catalysis, whereas the reduced fluctuations in mp688xHMFO could reflect a more rigid conformation that slows down the enzymatic reaction (HMF to FDCA conversion). RMSF analysis indicated enhanced flexibility in the 150–250 region of the PSPO enzyme. Notably, residues ARG209 and TYR216 in this region demonstrated significant interactions with HMF. The higher flexibility observed in the PSPO/HMF complex is further supported by RMSD analysis. The mp688xHMFO/HMF complex exhibited a stable RMSD below 0.25 nm throughout the simulation, whereas the PSPO/HMF complex displayed an average RMSD of 0.68 nm, stabilizing after 20 ns (Fig. 3). Despite this slight deviation, both complexes remained stable during the simulation. The increased RMSD in the PSPO/HMF complex can be attributed to the flexible regions within the PSPO enzyme.

Substrate stabilization and dissociation insights from SMD simulations

SMD simulations are widely utilized to investigate molecular dissociation processes by exerting a harmonic force on molecules and inducing displacement at a constant velocity. Constant force SMD simulations, in particular, reduce the activation barrier of dissociation events by applying a constant force, thereby facilitating dissociation and enabling the estimation of dissociation time under equilibrium conditions [27]. In this study, SMD simulations were employed to examine the unbinding mechanism of the HMF substrate from two enzymes, PSPO and mp688xHMFO, using the lowest binding energy (obtained from MM/PBSA) complexes of these enzymes, which showed −28.08 kJ/mol and −26.41 kJ/mol, respectively. These simulations provided insights into the unbinding force profiles, atomistic mechanisms, and transient interactions, aiding in identifying accessory binding sites. Such findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the unbinding process and the structural determinants underlying substrate–enzyme interactions.

The results from SMD simulations indicate that PSPO exhibits more non-covalent interactions with HMF than mp688xHMFO, which may contribute to its superior catalytic efficiency in converting HMF to FDCA. Key residues, including ARG209, ALA509, and THR511, initially stabilize the PSPO/HMF complex. As the pulling force is applied, the substrate establishes transient interactions with additional residues (ARG84, TYR216, and ARG429), suggesting a dynamic and stable binding environment that facilitates efficient catalysis. In contrast, the mp688xHMFO/HMF complex demonstrates a weaker interaction network, with only ARG60 and GLN434 playing a significant role in substrate stabilization. This limited interaction may reduce substrate retention and positioning, potentially hindering the reaction efficiency. The greater stability and prolonged interaction of HMF within the active site of PSPO enhances substrate accessibility and reaction turnover, leading to its faster conversion of HMF to FDCA as compared to mp688xHMFO. These findings highlight the structural advantages of PSPO in facilitating the catalytic process, supporting its potential as a more efficient biocatalyst for HMF oxidation.

Heterologous expression and HMF oxidation

The nucleotide sequence of PSPO was successfully cloned in pET-28a (+) and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) as soluble protein. Different concentrations of IPTG, i.e., 0.2–1 mM, were used in the culture medium for induction at 16 ℃ for 36 h. 0.4 mM of IPTG was optimized for the highest expression of protein. Recombinant PSPO was purified using Ni–NTA column chromatography, yielding 260 mg of protein per liter of culture and qualitatively analyzed using 12% SDS-PAGE. The molecular weight of the purified PSPO was~57 kDa (Fig. 4A). The theoretical molecular weight of PSPO was 57.18927 kDa, calculated using Protparam. The specific activity of the purified enzyme (PSPO) was 5.12 U / mg−1. The two recombinant HMFOs from Pseudomonas nitroreducens and Pseudomonas sp. strain 11/12A were expressed in E. coli C41 (DE3) and purified with a low yield of 10 mg and 47 mg of protein per liter of culture, respectively [21]. PSPO is a FAD-dependent enzyme, and 2 µM of FAD had a prominent effect on the activity of the enzyme while higher concentrations had an inhibitory effect (Fig. 4B). FAD prevents the inactivation of PSPO from cofactor dissociation, as it depends on FAD for catalysis [8]. The concentration of PSPO required to oxidize HMF and the duration of the reaction were optimized (Figs. S1A and S1B). The oxidation of 4 mM HMF with 15 µM of PSPO for 24 h resulted in 99% conversion to FDCA.Fig. 4SDS-PAGE of purified PSPO (A). Effect of varying concentration of FAD on the activity of PSPO (B). The intermediates DFF and FFCA were also obtained in the reaction mixture. HMF was completely oxidized to DFF after half an hour, and further DFF was oxidized to FFCA within 3 h. The standard enzyme reaction of PSPO with HMF resulted in the production of FDCA. The reactions were analyzed by HPLC, and the retention time of HMF and FDCA were 26.501 and 13.717 min, respectively (Fig. 4C). PseniHMFO and PsepHMFO oxidized 1.5 mM of HMF to FDCA with conversion efficiency of 99% and 65%, respectively, over 96 h, making the reaction less efficient [21]. HMF was converted to FFCA by an aryl-alcohol oxidase with a 98% yield after 4 h and 6% FDCA after 24 h [16]. A fungal laccase Tinea versicolor (CICC14001) with (2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-piperidin-1-yl)oxyl (TEMPO) as a mediator produced 90.2% of FDCA from HMF after 96 h of reaction time [28]. Besides, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural oxidase (HMFO) from Methylovorus sp. MP688 was capable of oxidizing HMF to 92% FFCA and 8% FDCA in 5 h, and at higher enzyme concentrations, 95% of FDCA was obtained after 24 h [10]. An unspecific peroxygenase (UPO) from Agrocybe aegerita oxidized HMF to 97% HMFCA and 10% FDCA after 24 h. Further oxidation of FFCA produced 90% FDCA after 96 h with the same enzyme concentration [14]. Multienzyme cascades were also developed for the conversion of HMF to FDCA, such as aryl-alcohol oxidase, unspecific peroxygenase, and galactose oxidase. In these cases, H2O2 was produced as a by-product that served as a co-substrate for peroxygenases and peroxidases. 100% FDCA was produced after 6 h with a combination of galactose oxidase, periplasmic aldehyde oxidase (PaoABC), horseradish peroxidase, and catalase [29]. Till date, only a limited number of HMFOs have been successfully expressed as functional protein. With its high-yield purification, the recombinant HMFO (PSPO) from Pseudomonas poae adds a new member with higher potential to the growing HMFO protein family.

Circular dichroism (CD) analysis of PSPO

The secondary structure of PSPO was evaluated by circular dichroism, which showed a positive peak around 195 nm, suggesting β-strands and the negative peak near 208–222 nm confirming intense α-helices [30]. The BeStSel computational analysis determined the secondary structure composition of PSPO, consisting of 28–31% α-helices, 18–20% β-strands, and 35–38% unordered structure (Fig. 5). The amino acid sequence of PSPO consists of 12.6% alanine, 11.1% leucine, 9.4% glycine, and 6.4% proline (Figure S2). The α-helix could be associated with methionine, alanine, glutamate, lysine, and leucine present in the amino acid sequences. Proline and glycine contribute to the β-strand structures of protein, as their unique side chains play a crucial role in altering the polypeptide chain’s direction and forming a loop structure [31]. The Circular Dichroism data of metspHMFOHis revealed 27% α-helices and 37% unordered structure [21]. Some studies indicated that the addition of His-tags had a minimal effect on the secondary structure of proteins. Effect of temperature and pH on PSPO’s activity and stability

The temperature and pH optima were investigated for the PSPO with HMF as the substrate. The catalytic activity of PSPO was observed in the temperature range of 10–80 ℃. The enzyme was highly active at lower temperatures, i.e., 10–30 ℃, exhibiting ˃85% enzyme activity as compared to a higher temperature range where activity decreased by 50%. The temperature optimum was obtained at 30 ℃ (Fig. 6A). To check the stability, the enzyme was incubated at 3 and 40 ℃ for 72 h. The enzyme was stable at 30℃ for 72 h, retaining more than 80% of residual activity. At 40 ℃, its residual activity decreased to 19%, suggesting lower thermostability (Fig. 6B). The enzyme PSPO was stable at 4 ℃ for a moderate period. The wild type HMFO from Methylovorus sp. strain MP688 has a temperature optima of 55 ℃ [10]. 7xHMFO, a variant of HMFO, had been engineered for thermostability. The 7xHMFO mutant incorporating 7 specific mutations (I73V, H74Y, G356H, V367L, T414K, A419Y and A435E) has been documented for Tm of 60.5 ℃ [1]. The optimal temperatures for HMFO enzymes range from 30 to 55 ℃. Since PSPO exhibits the highest catalytic efficiency at room temperature, it suggests a lower energy requirement for the reaction, thereby reducing the cost of industrial-scale conversion of HMF to FDCA.Fig. 6Effect of temperature and pH on the activity and stability of PSPO. Furthermore, pH optimum of PSPO was determined. The enzymatic activity of PSPO was determined in a wide range of pH (pH 2.0–pH 10.0). The enzyme remained catalytically active over a broad pH range (pH 7.0–pH 10.0), demonstrating ˃ 80% of its maximum activity (Fig. 6C). The pH optimum was obtained at pH 8.0. For pH stability, the enzyme was incubated in two different buffers (pH 7.0–pH 8.0) at 30 ℃ for 72 h. There was a 5–20% decrease in residual activity of PSPO at pH 8.0 and a 25% decrease at pH 7.0 (Fig. 6D). The optimum pH for HMFO enzyme from Methylovorus sp. strain MP688 was pH 8.0 [10]. During the bioconversion process of HMF to FDCA, pH decreased due to the accumulation of FDCA, requiring Na2CO3 as a stabilizing agent. Maintaining pH 6.0–7.0 enabled complete conversion of HMF to FDCA by Acinetobacter calcoaceticus NL14 [33]. The mutant of Methylovorus sp., 8BxHMFO, was stable in the broad pH range, exhibiting 70–80% of its initial activity. The optimal pH shift from pH 6.5 for HMFO WT to pH 8.0–9.0 for the HMFO variants has been observed [34]. HMFOs are mostly active in the alkaline pH range.

Effect of metal ions on PSPO’s activity

The enzyme assays were performed in the presence of different metal ions to assess their influence on the catalytic activity of PSPO. The activity of PSPO was increased by 5% in the presence of Mn2+ (Fig. 7A). The addition of Co2+ had no significant effect on the enzymatic activity. A detrimental effect on PSPO’s activity was observed in the presence of Cu2+, Zn2+, Ni2+, Ca2+, Na1+, Ag1+, K1+, and Fe2+ metal ions. Effect of H2O2 on PSPO’s stability

Since HMFO catalysis involves the reduction of O2, leading to the formation of H2O2, the effect of varying H2O2 concentrations (2–10 mM) was evaluated on the enzyme’s stability. After the addition of H2O2 in the reaction mixture, it was incubated for 24, 48, and 72 h. Initially, at 0 h, the FDCA production remained comparable regardless of the presence or absence of externally added H2O2. PSPO stability was decreased with the increase in H2O2 concentration and incubation period. The enzyme retained ˃90% of residual activity at lower concentrations of H2O2 (2 to 6 mM) and prolonged exposure to higher concentrations (8 to 10 mM) resulted in a significant decrease in the residual activity of PSPO (Fig. 7B). At the highest H2O2 concentration (10 mM), PSPO’s residual activity was found to be 83% after 72 h of incubation, suggesting that higher H2O2 concentration has a detrimental effect on the activity and stability of the enzyme. An earlier study has shown that HMFO retained 70% of its relative activity after 72 h of incubation in varying H2O2 concentrations [21]. Another study has reported that the addition of H2O2 had a positive effect on the conversion of HMF to FDCA. When 1 g/L of H2O2 was added, the conversion rate of FDCA increased up to 55.9% [33]. The catalytic activity of HMF/furfural oxidoreductase might be enhanced by H2O2, an essential enzyme involved in the metabolic conversion of HMF to FDCA [11]. Overall, PSPO maintains better stability at a lower amount of H2O2, and its presence in the reaction mixture did not hinder the conversion of HMF to FDCA.

Kinetic parameters of PSPO

The kinetics of PSPO was calculated for the substrates HMF, FFCA, DFF, and vanillyl alcohol. The Km, Kcat, and Kcat/Km for oxidation of HMF to FDCA were assessed to be 2.252 mM, 0.513 min−1, and 0.2278 mM−1 min−1. For DFF and FFCA, these values were calculated to be 2.503 mM, 0.5048 min−1, and 0.2016 mM−1 min−1, and 5.551 mM, 1.2347 min−1, and 0.2224 mM−1 min−1, respectively. In the case of vanillyl alcohol, these values were measured as 1.589 mM, 39.4 min−1, and 24.8 mM−1 min−1 (Table 2). The higher Km value in the case of HMF, as compared to vanillyl alcohol, confirms its moderate affinity towards HMF and higher affinity for vanillyl alcohol. The higher value of Kcat for HMF than for vanillyl alcohol confirms PSPO’s faster oxidation rate of HMF to FDCA. The catalytic efficiency (Kcat/Km) was found to be similar for both substrates, suggesting PSPO is effective in oxidizing HMF and vanillyl alcohol under standard assay conditions. In a study by Martin and co-workers, the wild type HMFO and its mutant 7xHMFO showed Km of 1.52 mM and 11.8 mM, and there was a twofold increase in the catalytic efficiency of 7xHMFO [1]. These kinetic parameters are comparable to those reported in the case of oxidation of HMF and vanillyl alcohol earlier [21]. Additionally, the enzymatic oxidation of HMF by fungal AAO (aryl-alcohol oxidase) from Pleurotus eryngii has been extensively studied. The study found that AAO oxidized HMF to DFF, but further oxidation to FDCA occurred at a lower rate [14]. The HMF-oxidizing enzyme from Apis mellifera (beeHMFO) belonging to the GMC oxidoreductase family, had a Km value of 3.6 ± 0.5 mM [35]. Conclusion

In this study, a new HMFO enzyme PSPO from Pseudomonas poae has been characterized. PSPO has been found to be a promising biocatalyst for the oxidation of HMF and the production of FDCA in a comparatively lesser time than that of the earlier known enzymes. In contrast to PSPO, previously characterized HMFOs exhibited the rate-limiting conversion at the FFCA oxidation step. PSPO can also withstand a higher amount of H2O2 (2–6 mM), suggesting its robustness to withstand harsh operational conditions and efficient FDCA production for further biopolymer generation. The PSPO enzyme also displayed the oxidation of vanillyl alcohol to vanillin, an important commercial molecule for flavor, fragrance, and pharmaceutical industries.
