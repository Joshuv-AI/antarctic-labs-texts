Q Cells in Wireless Networks
Martin Haenggi

Abstract
For a given set of transmitters such as cellular base stations or WiFi access points, is it possible to analytically characterize the set of locations that are “covered” in the sense that users at these locations experience a certain minimum quality of service? In this paper, we affirmatively answer this question, by providing explicit simple outer bounds and estimates for the coverage manifold. The key geometric elements of our analytical method are the Q cells , defined as the intersections of a small number of disks. The Q cell of a transmitter is an outer bound to the service region of the transmitter, and, in turn, the union of Q cells is an outer bound to the coverage manifold. In infinite networks, connections to the meta distribution of the signal-to-interference ratio allow for a scaling of the Q cells to obtain accurate estimates of the coverage manifold.



I
Introduction

I-A

Problem Formulation

Coverage is a key performance metric of wireless networks. It has been studied extensively in terms of
the fraction of the area of a region that is “covered” by wireless service, see,
e.g.
,
[
1
,
2
]
and references therein.
This paper addresses the more challenging
problem of analytically characterizing the
coverage manifold

\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
which is the subset of the plane that is covered in the sense that the probability that the signal-to-interference ratio
(SIR) exceeds a threshold
\theta
is at least
u
.
Specifically, for a set of transmitter locations, the problem is to find tight bounds and estimates for
\mathcal{C}
that
are described using only elementary geometric shapes—polygons and disk segments—and can be calculated
very efficiently.

I-B

Approach and Related Work

Our approach is based on the so-called
Q cells
, which consists of the locations in which a serving
transmitter provides enough power such that no single interferer can cause the location to be uncovered.
Conversely, for any location outside the Q cell of a transmitter, there exists an interferer that causes the
location to be uncovered.

Q cells generalize the standard Voronoi cells and provide an outer bound on the region that is covered by a
transmitter. They are related to the SIR cells introduced in
[
1
, Ch. 5]
,
defined as the set of locations where the SIR exceeds a certain threshold.
The SIR cells are random sets that depend on the instantaneous values of the fading coefficients
in the propagation model, which results in a much more complex and time-dependent structure. A
location may be inside an SIR cell at a given time but outside just a fraction of a second later, and the opposite
may hold for a location merely a wavelength apart. In contrast, the Q cells are deterministic (for a given set of
transmitters) and do not depend on time. As such, they correspond to the regions that are shown in coverage maps.

Simplified interference modeling using only the nearest interferer(s) dates back to at least
[
3
]

and
[
4
]
, whose model became known as the “Wyner model”.
More recently,

[
5
]
used the concept of
dominant nodes
to
bound the so-called transmission capacity of ad hoc
networks, where an interferer is said to be dominant if its interference alone can cause an outage.
In this terminology, our Q cells are defined as the locations for which there are no dominant interferers.
In
[
6
]
, the total interference is approximated using the nearest interferer only, and

[
7
]
uses a dominant-interferer approach for THz networks, with the additional
assumption that non-dominant interferers by themselves cannot causes outages.
Considering only the dominant interferer results can yield asymptotically exact or scaling results in terms of
the tail of the interference or in the low-outage regime, as established in
[
8
,
9
]
.

Another line of work models the additional interference from non-dominant interferers by
adding a deterministic term
[
10
]
or a Gaussian random variable
[
11
]
.
This is essential for path loss exponents near
2
, where the interference from distant transmitters is
significant
[
12
]
.
These articles
focus on a form of coverage probability or coverage area fraction, not estimates of the coverage manifold.

Related in their objective but rather different in their approach
are the works on using machine learning techniques for coverage estimation,
using convolutional neural networks
[
13
]
and generative adversarial networks
[
14
]
.
They require large data sets and extensive training, cannot be use to obtain bounds,
and do not lend themselves for simple interpretations,
in contrast to our direct geometric method.

The SIR meta distribution (MD)
[
15

16
]
is also relevant for our work since
it equals the fraction of the network area that is covered in infinite networks where the transmitters are modeled as a point process. We will take advantage of this connection to turn the coverage manifold bound that is
based on Q cells into an accurate estimate of
\mathcal{C}
. Conversely, if the MD is not available,
it can be upper bounded by the area fraction covered by the Q cells.
Of particular interest is the notion of

17

which asserts that the MD separates into the product of a function of

a certain set of values of

\theta
and

Lemma 2

(Equal distance ratio circle)

The set of locations

.

III-B

Coverage

Definition 1

(Coverage)

A location
\mathsf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is said to be covered if
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{SIR}_{\mathsf{y}}>\theta)>u

\theta\geq 0
and

The set of all covered locations is the coverage manifold

\mathcal{C}\triangleq\{\mathsf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{SIR}_{\mathsf{y}}>\theta)>u\}.

The set of locations covered when
\mathsf{x}
is the desired transmitter is denoted by

\mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{\mathsf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}C_{\mathsf{x}}
.

is “covered” if

, such a definition based on the instantaneous SIR would make coverage vary quickly over time (as the SIR depends on the
small-scale fading), and the covered locations would form a very complex, highly non-contiguous set,
inconsistent with how cellular operators draw coverage maps

Definition 3

The Q cell of transmitter

Q_{\mathsf{x}}
, is defined as

Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho)\triangleq\Big\{\mathsf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\frac{\|\mathcal{X}\setminus\{\mathsf{x}\}-\mathsf{y}\|}{\|\mathsf{x}-\mathsf{y}\|}>\rho\Big\},\quad\rho>0,

(8)

where
\|\mathcal{X}\setminus\{\mathsf{x}\}-\mathsf{y}\|
is the distance to the interferer closest to
\mathsf{y}
.

The next lemma shows that the Q cell can be analytically described using the Möbius transform put forth in Lemma
3
.

Lemma 5

(Complex Q cell characterization)

For
\mathsf{z}\in\mathcal{X}^{\sharp}
, let

Z_{\mathsf{z}}(\rho)\triangleq\mathsf{z}+\bigcap_{\mathsf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{\sharp}\setminus\{\mathsf{z}\}}f_{\rho}(H^{-})e^{j\arg(\mathsf{z}^{\prime}-z)},

(9)

with
f_{\rho}
given in (
1
). Then
Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho)\equiv Z_{\mathsf{x}^{\sharp}}^{\flat}(\rho)
.

Proof:

The right side of (
9
) consists of the locations at which the distance
ratio
|\mathsf{z}^{\prime}|/|\mathsf{z}|>\rho
for each interferer
\mathsf{z}^{\prime}
. This is equivalent to the condition in (
8
) since
if the condition is satisfied for the nearest interferer, it is satisfied for all interferers.
∎
Remarks.

•

For
\rho=1
, the Q cell
Q_{\mathsf{x}}
corresponds to the interior of the Voronoi cell
V_{\mathsf{x}}
,
which is defined as the intersection of the half planes
delineated by the perpendicular bisectors of
\mathsf{z}
and
\mathsf{z}^{\prime}
.

•

For
\rho\neq 1
, the Q cell is
the intersection of the regions whose boundaries are the equal distance ratio
circles in Lemma
2
.

–

For
\rho>1
, the half planes get morphed into disks per
f_{\rho}
, and the Q cell is the intersection of these

|\mathcal{X}|-1
disks.

–

For
\rho<1
, the Q cell consists of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(or
\mathbb{C}
) minus a union of exclusion disks for each interferer.

For a given stringency, increasing
\alpha
reduces
\rho
if
\rho>1
, resulting in larger Q cell.
If
\rho<1
, increasing
\alpha
increases
\rho
, which enlarges the exclusions disks.

•

Not every interferer contributes to the shaping of the Q cell. Those that do are termed
strong interferers
.

Definition 4

(Strong and dominant interferers)

\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
is a strong interferer to
\mathsf{x}
for a given
\rho
if the removal of
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
increases the Q cell.

A dominant interferer at location
\mathsf{y}
is an interferer that by itself is strong enough to cause
\mathsf{y}
to be uncovered.

Remarks.

•

For
\rho<1
, every interferer is strong.

•

For
\rho>1
, the intersection of the
|\mathcal{X}|-1
disks in the definition of
Q_{\mathsf{x}}
can be reduced to the
intersection of the disks of the strong interferers to
\mathsf{x}
. By Lemma
1
, their number determines the number of arcs
\breve{n}_{\mathsf{x}}
that define the boundary of
Q_{\mathsf{x}}
.
\breve{n}_{\mathsf{x}}
equals the number of Delaunay neighbors of
\mathsf{x}
.
The number of dominant interferers at
\mathsf{y}

equals the number of disks that
\mathsf{y}
is outside of. For
\mathsf{y}\in Q_{\mathsf{x}}
, there are no dominant interferers.

•

For
\rho\downarrow 1
, the number of strong interferers to
\mathsf{x}
tends to the number of sides
of the Voronoi cell of
\mathsf{x}
. As
\rho\to\infty
, the number may decrease to
1
.

•

Denote by
V_{\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}^{\prime}}
the second-order Voronoi cell of
\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
,
i.e.
, the set of locations that have

\mathsf{x}
and
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
as their two closest points in
\mathcal{X}

[
21
,
22
]
. If

\mathsf{y}\in(V_{\mathsf{x}}\cap V_{\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}^{\prime}})\setminus Q_{\mathsf{x}},

\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
is the dominant interferer at
\mathsf{y}
.

•

By construction, the corner points of the Q cell lie on an edge of the second-order Voronoi diagram.
These are the locations that have exactly two dominant interferers at equal distance. This property will be
exploited in Subsec.
IV-C
.

\rho=\sqrt{2}
with the dominant interferers and their equal distance ratio circles is shown in Fig.

which are shown in white.

IV-B

Coverage Outer Bounds

The key missing piece to bound the coverage manifold is the connection between

\theta
and
u
and the radius parameter
\rho
of the Q cells.
By the following theorem, the connection is established by equating
\rho^{\alpha}
with the stringency
\sigma
.

Theorem 1

(Outer bounds on coverage cells and manifold)

The coverage cell of transmitter
\mathsf{x}
is bounded as

C_{\mathsf{x}}\subseteq Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho(\theta,u)),

(10)

where
\rho(\theta,u)=\sigma(\theta,u)^{1/\alpha}
,
and the coverage manifold is bounded as

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{Q},

(11)

where

\mathcal{Q}\triangleq\bigcup_{\mathsf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho(\theta,u))

is the union of all Q cells.

Proof:

For a transmitter
\mathsf{x}
and a location
\mathsf{y}
, let
r_{1}=\|\mathsf{x}-\mathsf{y}|
and
r_{2}=\|\mathcal{X}\setminus\{\mathsf{x}\}-\mathsf{y}\|
be
the serving distance and nearest interferer distance, respectively.
With
v\triangleq r_{2}/r_{1}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{h_{1}r_{1}^{-\alpha}}{h_{2}r_{2}^{-\alpha}}>\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Hv^{\alpha}>\theta)

\displaystyle=1-F_{H}(\theta v^{-\alpha}).

(12)

1-F_{H}(\theta v^{-\alpha})>u
implies

\displaystyle v^{-\alpha}<\frac{F_{H}^{-1}(1-u)}{\theta}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v>\sigma(\theta,u)^{1/\alpha}.

Hence a distance ratio smaller than
\rho(\theta,u)
to the nearest interferer is sufficient
for
\mathsf{y}
to be uncovered. The bound on the coverage manifold is a consequence of the
bounds of the individual coverage cells.
∎
Remarks.

•

For
\rho>1
, to achieve a reliability
u
, a user needs to be located inside all circles with distance ratio
\rho

(Lemma
2
).

•

For Rayleigh fading, if coverage is expressed in terms of the SF threshold
t
,

\rho({t^{\ominus}},u)^{\alpha}={t^{\ominus}}{u^{\ominus}}
by (
4
), which shows a striking symmetry of
\rho
in the reliability
u
and the SF threshold
t
.
The stringent regime is simply
t+u>1
.

•

If only the nearest interferer for each location
\mathsf{y}
is considered, (
10
) is an equality.

•

In the lax regime
\rho<1
,
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
trivially holds since
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
For
\rho=1
,
\mathcal{Q}
is still essentially
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(minus the Voronoi edges).

•

Since the Q cells are disjoint in the
stringent regime, a user cannot be handed over from one transmitter to the next
without losing coverage. Only in the lax regime, seamless coverage is possible.

The Q cell
Q_{o}
in Fig.
3
is drawn for
\rho=\sqrt{2}
,
which means it is an outer bound of the coverage cell
C_{o}
for all combinations of
\theta
,
u
, and
\alpha

for which
\sigma(\theta,u)=2^{\alpha/2}
.
For Rayleigh fading and
\alpha=4
, all Q cells are the same if
u={(4/\theta)^{\oplus}}
.
The color map of the figure indicates
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{SIR}_{\mathsf{y}}>1)
for each
\mathsf{y}
in the square region, and the
corresponding reliability is
u={4^{\oplus}}=0.8
.
Hence outside the Q cell, in
V_{o}\setminus Q_{o}
, it is guaranteed that
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{SIR}_{\mathsf{y}}>1)<0.8
, which can be
confirmed from the colors—none of these locations reach a shade of yellow.

IV-C

Cutting Corners

Here we focus on the stringent regime
\rho>1
and show that is is possible to improve on the outer coverage bounds
by “cutting the corners” of the Q cells. The reason why the corner points of the Q cells are of special
interest is that they are equidistant to the two nearest interferers, while the definition of the Q cell
only considers a single nearest interferer. By taking into account the joint interference from the
two equidistant interferers, we can obtain a better outer bound of the coverage provided by each transmitter,

i.e.
, a
refined Q cell
.

First we define a version of a Q cell that always considers the two nearest interferers.

Definition 5

(Second-Order Q Cell)

For
|\mathcal{X}|>2
,
the second-order Q cell
Q_{\mathsf{x}}^{(2)}
is the set of location that are covered if the two nearest interferers to each location
are considered. Formally,

\displaystyle Q_{\mathsf{x}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\triangleq\Big\{\mathsf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{h_{1}r_{1}^{-\alpha}}{h_{2}r_{2}^{-\alpha}+h_{3}r_{3}^{-\alpha}}>\theta\right)>u\Big\}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\mathsf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{H_{2}v_{2}^{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{H_{3}v_{3}^{\alpha}}<\frac{1}{\theta}\right)>u\Big\},

(13)

where
(r_{i})_{i\in[3]}
are the ordered distances to
\mathsf{y}
and
v_{i}=r_{i}/r_{1}
and
H_{i}=h_{1}/h_{i}
, as before.

Remarks.

•

By construction,
C_{\mathsf{x}}\subseteq Q^{(2)}_{\mathsf{x}}\subseteq Q_{\mathsf{x}}
.

•

This definition could be extended easily to account for more than
2
interferers by adding more terms

1/(H_{i}\rho_{i})
to (
13
), but the higher-order Q cells
are increasingly intractable.

The key purpose of introducing the second-order cell is the definition of a refined version of the Q cell if
\breve{n}_{\mathsf{x}}>1
,
i.e.
,
if the Q cell has corner points. Before doing so, we need to introduce another variant of the Q cell.

Definition 6

(Interference-cloned Q Cell)

Let

\rho^{*}(\theta,u)\triangleq\left(\frac{\theta}{F_{H^{*}}^{-1}(1-u)}\right)^{1/\alpha},

(14)

where
F^{-1}_{H^{*}}
is the quantile function of
H^{*}\triangleq h_{1}/(h_{2}+h_{3})=H_{2}H_{3}/(H_{2}+H_{3})
.
The Q cell
Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho^{\ast})
is the interference-cloned version of the standard Q cell
Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho)
.

The interference-cloned Q cell is the Q cell obtained if every interferer is replaced by two interferers at the same distance, each with independent fading or, equivalently, if every interferer has two antennas, sufficiently spaced
for the fading to be independent and each transmitting at unit power. The ratio

\frac{\rho^{*}}{\rho}=\left(\frac{F_{H}^{-1}(1-u)}{F_{H^{*}}^{-1}(1-u)}\right)^{1/\alpha}

is larger than one since
F_{H^{*}}(x)>F_{H}(x)
and thus
F^{-1}_{H}(1-u)>F^{-1}_{H^{*}}(1-u)
.
For Nakagami-
(p,q
) fading,

F_{H^{*}}(x)=B_{p,2q}\left(\frac{px}{px+q}\right)=B_{p,2q}\big({(x\tfrac{p}{q})^{\oplus}}\big),

(15)

which is a slight modification of (
3
).
For
p=q=1
,
F_{H^{*}}(x)=x(x+2)/(x+1)^{2}
and thus
F^{-1}_{H^{*}}(u)=(1-u)^{-1/2}-1
.
It follows that
(\rho^{*})^{\alpha}=\theta{\sqrt{u}^{\ominus}}
and

\rho^{*}/\rho=\left(\frac{{\sqrt{u}^{\ominus}}}{{u^{\ominus}}}\right)^{1/\alpha}
.

The next lemma establishes a connection between
Q^{(2)}(\rho)
and
Q(\rho^{*})
.

Lemma 6

(Corner points of the interference-cloned cell)

The corner points of
Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho^{*})
(if any) lie on the boundary
\partial Q^{(2)}_{\mathsf{x}}
of the second-order cell.

Proof:

For the interference-cloned cell,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{h_{1}r_{1}^{-\alpha}}{(h_{2}+h_{3})r_{2}^{-\alpha}}>\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(H^{*}v^{\alpha}>\theta)

(16)

\displaystyle=1-F_{H^{*}}(\theta v^{-\alpha}),

as in the proof of Thm.
1
but with
H
replaced by
H^{\ast}
.
Hence a distance ratio of
(\theta/F^{-1}_{H^{*}}(1-u))^{1/\alpha}
demarcates the boundary of the interference-cloned cell
Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho^{*})
.

Now, at the corner points
Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho^{*})
, there are two actual equidistant interferers (without cloning), so
at these locations, cloning has the same effect as taking the two nearest interferers in account, hence
they lie on
\partial Q^{(2)}(\rho)
and have
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{SIR}_{\mathsf{y}}>\theta)\leq u
, with equality if there are only two
interferers.
∎

The remaining question is how to “interpolate” between
Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho)
and
Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho^{*})
to define a refined Q cell.

Definition 7

(Refined Q Cell)

Let
\rho^{*}(\theta,u)
be given as in (
14
)
and denote by
\mathsf{q}_{k}
,
k\in[\breve{n}_{\mathsf{x}}]
, the corner points of
Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho^{*})

and by
\varphi_{k}
and
\varphi^{\prime}_{k}
the angles of the tangents to the two arcs meeting at
\mathsf{q}_{k}
.
Letting
\bar{\varphi}_{k}=(\varphi_{k}+\varphi^{\prime}_{k})/2
, we define

\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{x}}\triangleq\bigcap_{k=1}^{\breve{n}_{\mathsf{x}}}\mathsf{q}_{k}^{\sharp}+H^{-}e^{\mathrm{i}(\bar{\varphi}_{k}-\pi/2)}

to be the intersection of the half-planes whose boundary is the line through
\mathsf{q}_{k}
at angle
\bar{\varphi}_{k}-\pi/2
,
such that
\mathsf{x}^{\sharp}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{x}}
. If
\breve{n}_{\mathsf{x}}=0
,
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}
, per the usual convention that the intersection
of
0
sets is the universal set.

The refined Q cell is

Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho)\triangleq Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho)\cap\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{x}}^{\flat}.

Q(\rho)

, (innermost cell, green) and refined Q cell

.

“cutting the corners” off of

. In this example, the refined Q cell is 8% smaller than the original Q cell.

, and

.

As before we set
\mathcal{Q}^{*}\triangleq\bigcup_{\mathsf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
.

Theorem 2

(Improved outer bound)

The refined Q cell
Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
is a tighter outer bound on
C_{\mathsf{x}}
than
Q_{\mathsf{x}}
,
i.e.,

C_{\mathsf{x}}\subseteq Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}\subseteq Q_{\mathsf{x}}\quad\forall\mathsf{x}\in\mathcal{X},

(17)

and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof:

First inclusion: Since
Q^{(2)}_{\mathsf{x}}
is convex, the intersection with a tangential half-plane at any point of its boundary
leaves it unchanged. So
Q^{(2)}_{\mathsf{x}}\subseteq Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
, which implies
C_{\mathsf{x}}\subseteq Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
.
Second inclusion: Since the corner points of
Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
lie inside
Q_{\mathsf{x}}
, the intersection with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{x}}^{\flat}

makes
Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
smaller than
Q_{\mathsf{x}}
—unless there are no corner points.
∎If there are more than two interferers, the first inclusion in (
17
) becomes strict, and
if
Q_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho^{*})
has at least two corner points, the second inclusion becomes strict.

V
Coverage Estimation in Infinite Networks

In this section, we focus on the stringent QoS regime and
assume that
\mathcal{X}
is countably infinite, in such as way that it forms a
proper point pattern

as per
[
23
, Def. 1]
. This means its convex hull spans
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and all Voronoi cells
are finite. If
\mathcal{X}
is random,
i.e.
, a point process, these properties are assumed to hold in an almost sure sense.

V-A

Covered Area Fraction

A key quantity in infinite networks is the covered area fraction.

Definition 8

(Area fraction covered by a set)

For
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\eta_{B}\triangleq\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{|B\cap b(o,r)|}{\pi r^{2}}\in[0,1]

is the area fraction covered by
B
.

An immediate consequence of Theorems
1
and
2
is
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}\geq\eta_{\mathcal{Q}^{*}}\geq\eta_{\mathcal{C}}
.
In the next two subsections, we quantify
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}
for different types of deployments
\mathcal{X}
.

For the asymptotic scaling of
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}
, we have the following lemma. We define
\delta\triangleq 2/\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Lemma 7

(Asymptotic coverage of
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}
)

For all stationary and ergodic point processes,
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}(\rho)=\Theta(\rho^{-2})=\Theta(\theta^{-\delta})
,
\rho\to\infty
.

Proof:

As
\rho\to\infty
, all equal distance ratio circles of
\mathsf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
become concentric at
\mathsf{x}
, and their radii are proportional
to the distance from
\mathsf{x}
to the interfering transmitters and decreasing with
\rho^{-1}
. Hence asymptotically
only transmitters with minimum distance, given by
\operatornamewithlimits{arg\ min}_{\mathsf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{\mathsf{x}\}}\|\mathsf{x}-\mathsf{x}^{\prime}\|
characterize the
Q cell. If there is a single interferer at the minimum distance, the Q cell is a disk with area proportional to

\rho^{2}/(\rho^{2}-1)^{2}=\Theta(\rho^{-2})
by Lemma
2
. If there are multiple interferers at the minimum
distance,
i.e.
, if
\mathcal{X}
forms a lattice, the Q cell is the intersection of a fixed number of
disks with equal radius, whose area also
scales with
\Theta(\rho^{-2})
. This behavior applies to all Q cells, hence the covered area fraction scales in the
same way.
Lastly, since
\rho^{\alpha}\propto\theta
by Thm.
1
,
\rho^{-2}\propto\theta^{-\delta}
.
∎

V-B

Lattices

In regular lattices, all Voronoi cells
V_{\mathsf{x}}
are congruent, and so are all Q cells
Q_{\mathsf{x}}
.
Hence their area fractions can be calculated by considering just a single cell.

Lemma 8

(Q cell area fraction in lattices)

For the square lattice,

\eta_{\mathcal{Q}^{\square}}(\rho)=\frac{4\rho^{2}\arctan\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\rho^{2}-1}-1}{\sqrt{2\rho^{2}-1}+1}\right)-2\sqrt{2\rho^{2}-1}+2}{(\rho^{2}-1)^{2}}.

(18)

For the triangular lattice,

\eta_{\mathcal{Q}^{\hexagon}}(\rho)=\frac{4\sqrt{3}\rho^{2}\arctan\left(\frac{r-\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}r+1}\right)-\sqrt{3}r+3}{(\rho^{2}-1)^{2}},

(19)

where
r=\sqrt{4\rho^{2}-1}
.
For both square and triangular lattices,

\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}(\rho)<\frac{4}{(1+\rho)^{2}}.

(20)

Proof:

(
18
) and (
19
) are obtained from straightforward calculation of the areas of the disk segments and the
inner square and hexagon, respectively.
For the square lattice, the tightest square outer bounding the Q cell
has side length
2/(1+\rho)
; for the triangular lattice,
the tightest hexagon outer bounding the Q cell has inner radius
a=\sqrt{2}/(3^{1/4}(1+\rho))
, hence its area
6a^{2}/\sqrt{3}=4/(1+\rho)^{2}
is the same.
∎Since the arctan in (
18
) tends to
\pi/4
,
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}^{\square}}\sim\pi\rho^{-2}
,
\rho\to\infty
.
For the triangular lattice, the arctan tends to
\pi/6
, so
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}^{\hexagon}}\sim(2\pi/\sqrt{3})\rho^{-2}
, which is
15\%
larger. The upper bound (
20
) is asymptotically larger by a factor
2\sqrt{3}/\pi\approx 1.10
than the Q cells for the triangular lattice, so the bound is at most
10\%
off.

V-C

Point Processes

If
\mathcal{X}
is a stationary and ergodic point process of density
\lambda
,

\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}=\lambda\mathbb{E}_{o}(|Q_{o}(\rho)|),

where
\mathbb{E}_{o}
is the expectation with respect to the Palm measure of
\mathcal{X}
at
o

[
24
, Ch. 8]
.
In words, the area fraction of
\mathcal{Q}
is the mean area of the typical Q cell divided by the mean area
1/\lambda

of the typical Voronoi cell.

for the Poisson point process (PPP).

(Q cell area fraction for PPP)

For the PPP,
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}=\rho^{-2}
.

Proof:

The area fraction only depends on
\rho
, so the result follows from re-writing the MD for the nearest interferer case
in
[

, Cor. 3]
in terms of
\rho
.
∎This lemma generalizes
[

to arbitrary fading, including asymmetric cases,
and it establishes which combinations of

,

, and

result in the same Q cell coverage.

Connection to the SIR Meta Distribution

We first state an important fact about the SIR meta distribution (MD) and the covered area fraction
\eta_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Fact 1

(Meta distribution and
\eta_{\mathcal{C}}
)

If
\mathcal{X}
is a stationary and ergodic point process,

\eta_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho(\theta,u))=\bar{F}_{[\mathsf{SIR}]}(\theta,u),

where
\bar{F}_{[\mathsf{SIR}]}(\theta,u)\triangleq\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{SIR}_{o}>\theta\mid\mathcal{X})>u)
is the meta distribution of the
SIR
[
15
,
16
]
.

Hence, the SIR MD can be either interpreted as
\eta_{\mathcal{C}}
or
as the fraction of users who achieve an SIR of
\theta

with probability at least
u

for a stationary and ergodic point process of users.

Consequently,
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}
and
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}^{*}}
are upper bounds on the meta distribution.
In particular, (
22
) is a universal upper bound for all point processes and all values of

\theta
and
u
, as stated in the next corollary.

Corollary 1

(Upper bounds on MD)

For any independent stationary and ergodic point processes of transmitters and users, the MD is bounded as

\bar{F}_{[\mathsf{SIR}]}(\theta,u)<\frac{4}{(1+\rho)^{2}}=\frac{4}{\left(1+\Big(\frac{\theta}{F_{H}^{-1}(1-u)}\Big)^{1/\alpha}\right)^{2}}.

(23)

For the PPP,

\bar{F}_{[\mathsf{SIR}]}(\theta,u)<(F_{H}^{-1}(1-u))^{\delta}\theta^{-\delta}.

(24)

Proof:

Follows from Thm.
3
and Fact
1
.
∎
Remarks.

•

For Rayleigh fading, the upper bound (
24
) simplifies to
(\theta{u^{\ominus}})^{-\delta}
.

•

If the MD exceeds the right side of (
24
), the transmitters form a more regular point process than the PPP.

•

If only the nearest interferer is considered in the MD and coverage definition,

\bar{F}_{[\mathsf{SIR}]}(\theta,u)=\eta_{\mathcal{C}}=\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}(\rho(\theta,u))
. Lemma
9
is a consequence
of this equality—the result follows from the nearest interferer-only version of the MD.

Separability in Poisson Networks

If
\mathcal{X}
is a stationary PPP,
the stringent regime corresponds to the
separable regime

where, for iid fading,
\bar{F}_{[\mathsf{SIR}]}(\theta,u)=g(u)\theta^{-\delta}

[
17
, Thm. 2]
. “Separable”
refers to the fact that the MD can be written as a product of a function of
u

and
\theta^{-\delta}
.
The bound (
24
) establishes that
g(u)<(F_{H}^{-1}(1-u))^{\delta}
.
For Nakagami-
m
fading,
the stringent/separable regime is

\{(\theta,u)\colon u>1-B_{m,m}({\theta^{\oplus}})\}
.

For Rayleigh fading,
\bar{F}_{[\mathsf{SIR}]}(\theta,u)\sim\operatorname{sinc}\delta(\theta{u^{\ominus}})^{-\delta}
,
u\to 1
,
[
17
, Cor. 5]
,
which suggests that

\eta_{\mathcal{C}}\approx\operatorname{sinc}\delta\,\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}.

(25)

The asymptotic proportionality of the MD to
\theta^{-\delta}
as
\theta\to\infty

holds for all stationary point processes with Rayleigh fading
[
17
, Thm. 3]
.
For
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}
, it holds for all types of fading.

Only for the PPP,
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}
is separable. For all point processes,

holds in the sense that

\eta_{\mathcal{Q}}(\rho)\sim\tilde{g}(u)\theta^{-\delta}

, where

V-F

Scaled Q Cells for Coverage Manifold Estimation

Q cells only account for one interferer, while refined Q cells account for two in specific locations.
Shrinking the Q cells can approximately incorporate the effect of the remaining interferers.
If the MD (
i.e.
,
\eta_{\mathcal{C}}
) is available, the Q cells can be shrunk to match the area fraction.
Applied to the refined cells,
letting
\nu=\eta_{\mathcal{C}}/\eta_{\mathcal{Q}^{*}}
, each cell is scaled as

\hat{Q}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}\triangleq\mathsf{x}+\sqrt{\nu}(Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}-\mathsf{x}),

and we let
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{*}\triangleq\bigcup_{\mathsf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\hat{Q}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
.
This way,
\eta_{\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{*}}=\eta_{\mathcal{C}}
.

To assess the accuracy of
Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
and
\hat{Q}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
as an outer bound and approximation of
C_{\mathsf{x}}
,
respectively, we use the
probabilities
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{SIR}>\theta)
averaged over the Q cell boundaries. Formally,

\displaystyle U_{\mathsf{x}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{|\partial Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}|}\int_{\mathsf{y}\in\partial Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}}\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{SIR}_{\mathsf{y}}>\theta)\mathrm{d}\mathsf{y},

(26)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\mathsf{x}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{|\partial\hat{Q}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}|}\int_{\mathsf{y}\in\partial\hat{Q}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}}\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{SIR}_{\mathsf{y}}>\theta)\mathrm{d}\mathsf{y}.

(27)

For random
\mathcal{X}
, the ccdfs of
\mathsf{SIR}_{\mathsf{y}}
are conditioned on
\mathcal{X}
, and
U
and
\hat{U}
are random
variables. Without subscript,
U
and
\hat{U}
refer to the average probabilities on the boundary of the typical
cells
Q^{*}_{o}
and
\hat{Q}^{*}_{o}
, respectively.

Along the boundary
\partial C_{\mathsf{x}}
, the reliability is
u
by definition, and,
since
C_{\mathsf{x}}\subset Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
,
U_{\mathsf{x}}<u
. Hence the proximity of
U_{\mathsf{x}}
to
u
and the deviation
of
\hat{U}_{\mathsf{x}}
from
u
are sensible indicators for the accuracy of the Q cells.

The effect of the scaling can be viewed as an increase in
\rho
and, in turn,
an increase of the reliabilities (or the threshold
\theta
) on the cell boundaries.
Using
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}^{*}}\approx\rho^{-2}
, which holds in general, we have
\hat{\rho}^{-2}=\nu\rho^{-2}\approx\eta_{\mathcal{C}}
,
so
Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}(\hat{\rho})\approx\hat{Q}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}(\rho)
for
\hat{\rho}=\rho/\sqrt{\nu}
.
Then, from (
12
),

\hat{u}\triangleq 1-F_{H}(\theta\hat{\rho}^{-\alpha})=1-F_{H}(\theta\nu^{1/\delta}\rho^{-\alpha})

(28)

is an estimate of the maximum reliability on the boundary of
\hat{Q}^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}
.
For Rayleigh fading,
\hat{u}=1-{(\theta\nu^{1/\delta}\rho^{-\alpha})^{\oplus}}={(\nu^{-1/\delta}{u^{\ominus}})^{\oplus}}
,
where
u
is the target reliability of the unscaled Q cells.

Area scaling without MD

If the MD is not available, the scaling factor
\nu
can be set
heuristically as

\nu(\delta,g)=(\operatorname{sinc}\delta)^{1-g(1-\delta)},

(30)

where
g
quantifies the regularity of the point process. For a lattice,
g=1
, while for the PPP,
g=0.3
.
For perturbed lattices with variance
v
,
g=\max\{0.3,1-3.5v\}
.

The rationale behind (
30
) is the following:

•

Since both
\eta_{\mathcal{C}}
and
\eta_{\mathcal{Q}^{*}}
scale similarly with
\rho
,
\rho
does not need to be included as an explicit
parameter in an estimate of
\nu
.

•

If
\delta\to 0
,
Q^{*}_{\mathsf{x}}\to C_{\mathsf{x}}
for all
\mathsf{x}
,
i.e.
,
\nu(0)=1
. If
\delta\to 1
, the interference becomes
overwhelming, and
|C_{\mathsf{x}}|\to 0
,
i.e.
,

\nu(1)=0
. For intermediate
\delta
,
\nu(\delta)
is monotonically decreasing.

•

The base
\operatorname{sinc}\delta
is taken from (
25
), which is raised to an exponent
that depends on the regularity
g
and
\delta
and takes into account that (
25
) is
the approximate scaling factor of the standard Q cells, not of the refined ones.
For higher regularity, the exponent is smaller since
the refined Q cell is more accurate.

•

For
\delta\ll 1
(
Q^{*}\approx C
in all cases) and
1-\delta\ll 1
(
|C|\approx 0
in all cases),
the regularity of the point process has little influence. To reflect this,
g
is multiplied by
1-\delta
.

Fig.
12
compares this simple heuristic with simulation results for the square lattice,
perturbed lattices, and the PPP.

In the example of
\delta=1/2
used in Fig.
10
,

(\operatorname{sinc}(1/2))^{0.85}\approx 0.68
, which is close to the true scaling of
0.676
(see the red line and
the red
\times
in Fig.

at
\delta=1/2
).

Recipe 1

(Scaled refined Q cells)

(1)

For given
\theta
and

, calculate the stringency
\sigma(\theta,u)
and set
\rho=\sigma^{1/\alpha}
.

(2)

Determine the refined Q cells

for each

.

If the MD is not available, set

VI
Conclusions

Q cell analysis (QCA)
is a powerful method to bound and approximate the coverage manifold in
wireless networks.
Standard Q cells constitute simple yet fairly accurate outer bounds on the coverage region of each transmitter.
They depend on the QoS parameters
\theta
and
u
only through the scalar stringency
\sigma
and can be
calculated very efficiently as the intersection of a modest number of disks whose boundaries are formed
by equal distance ratio circles with ratio
\rho=\sigma^{1/\alpha}
.
As a generalization of Voronoi cells, they are defined as the intersection of Möbius transformed half-planes
instead of just the intersection of half-planes. In the stringent regime, the complement of their union is
guaranteed to be uncovered. Cutting corner points to
account for equidistant interferers results in refined Q cells that tightly outer bound the coverage regions.

The stringency has a simple analytical expression for general Nakagami-
(p,q)
fading, which allows
for different fading types in the serving and interfering links.
It establishes that for all combinations of
\theta
,
u
, and
\alpha
that
have the same distance ratio
\rho=\sigma^{1/\alpha}
, the coverage performance will be similar.
Accordingly, it is unnecessary to explore many combinations of the three parameters—the coverage outer
bound only depends on the single scalar parameter
\rho
.

Applied to infinite networks,
Q cell coverage is much more tractable than the actual coverage (or SIR meta distribution) but closely related.
It allows for the derivation of simple universal bounds. For instance,
in any infinite network with
\rho=2\sqrt{2}-1
, at least 50% of the users are uncovered.
In Poisson networks, more than 50% are uncovered at
\rho=\sqrt{2}
.

We expect that QCA will be useful to quickly explore the effect of advanced
transmission schemes such as coordinated multipoint (CoMP) or non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA),
and the effects of resource allocation, scheduling and load balancing—and to facilitate their implementation.
For instance, users outside the Q cells are natural candidates for bandwidth aggregation, BS silencing or CoMP.
