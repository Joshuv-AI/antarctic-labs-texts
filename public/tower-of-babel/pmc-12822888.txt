Motivational interviewing as a tool for promoting self-care and prenatal care utilization in adolescent mothers: Evidence from a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Niloufar Heybati, Samira Khayat, Ali Navidian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Teenage pregnancy is a high-risk pregnancy. The complications of teenage pregnancy are partially caused by the low level of self-care and lower usage rates of prenatal care. This study was conducted with the aim of investigating the effect of training based on the principles of motivational interviewing on self-care and the use of prenatal services by teenage pregnant mothers.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This semi-experimental study was conducted in 2023 in Zahedan city. Sampling was done in a multistage manner. The trainings were based on motivational interviews, which were held in groups during four sessions. The participants completed the self-care questionnaire before the intervention as well as at 28–30 weeks of gestation, and a checklist of the number of cares was completed after termination of pregnancy. The final sample included 42 mothers in the intervention group and 40 mothers in the control group. Data analysis was done using SPSS software (version 26) by considering the significance level of P < 0.05. The data were analyzed using t-test, Mann-Whitney, and Chi-square tests.RESULTS:Before the intervention, there was no significant difference in mean self-care scores between the intervention group (89.23 ± 20.6) and control group (84.8 ± 15.0) (P = 0.26), while after the intervention, a significant difference was observed between the mean scores of self-care in the intervention group (103.4 ± 20.71) and control group (86.37 ± 15.13) (P < 0.001). A significant difference was found between the mean scores of number of prenatal care in the intervention group (7.33 ± 0.81) and the control group (6.2 ± 1.11) (P < 0.001).CONCLUSION:Training based on motivational interviewing increases self-care and the usage rate of prenatal care in pregnant adolescent mothers. It is recommended to take advantage of this method to improve the quality of care and create mother-centered and mother-friendly care for teenage pregnant women.

Introduction

Self-care refers to the decisions and activities of a pregnant mother to keep and promote good health, including actions that enable the mother to prevent pregnancy complications and maintain optimal health during pregnancy.[1,2] Receiving prenatal care is a dimension of self-care in pregnancy. Prenatal care is practiced to a lower extent in teenage pregnancy than in other women, which can play an important role in increasing the complications of teenage pregnancy.[3] A study from South Korea showed that the rate of prenatal care in teenage pregnancy was lower than that among women of other age groups. (6 vs. 9 care).[4] In addition, a majority of these women receive the first prenatal care after 12 weeks.[5]

The age range of 10–19 years is referred to as adolescence, which is a unique and vital stage in every person’s life.[6,7] One of the important events in the life of teenagers is pregnancy, which increases perinatal complications and has an adverse effect on the physical and social health of teenagers, family, and society.[1] WHO statistics show that 21,000,000 pregnancies occurred in 2019 in the 15–19-year-old age group from low- and middle-income countries.[6] At the international level, the age-specific fertility rate has been decreasing in the age range of 15–19 years (64.5 per 1000 women in 2000 compared to 42.5 in 2021). However, this trend is upward in Iran and has increased from 31.5 in 2005–2010 to 40.6 in 2015–2019.[8]

There are more risks for the mother, fetus, and newborn in teenage pregnancy, including fetal growth restriction, intrauterine fetal death, anomalies in babies, cesarean section, pre-eclampsia, and postpartum bleeding.[7] The increase in complications of these pregnancies is not only related to the age, growth, and development process but other factors such as unwanted pregnancy, low level of education and knowledge, risky behaviors, and poverty play a role in these complications. Another important factor is the low level of self-care and usage of prenatal services and care in teenage mothers.[1]

Social and family support, family income, self-confidence and self-efficacy, access to health services, and awareness of factors affects self-care in pregnancy. In teenage mothers, all these factors are at an inappropriate level. They have less education and knowledge, most of them are from poor and low-income families, their family support is insufficient, and low self-confidence is one of the crises of teenage pregnancy.[9]

Self-care during pregnancy improves the quality of life and the feeling of well-being in the mother. It also reduces pregnancy complications and healthcare costs. Low self-care is associated with pregnancy complications such as premature delivery, low birth weight, meconium, fetal heart rate deceleration, low Apgar score, and fetal distress.[10]

To improve self-care during pregnancy, pregnant women, especially teenagers, need to receive information and acquire skills. Education and counseling are among the approaches that increase self-care.[6] So far, various methods of counseling and training have been utilized to improve self-care of pregnant women. In their study on adolescent women, Khayat et al. (2022)[1] concluded that telemedicine and face-to-face training improve some but not all dimensions of self-care. In another study, a health literacy educational method has been associated with improving self-care in pregnant women.[11] Overall, various methods such as lectures, pamphlets, Q and A, booklets, and group discussions have been used to improve self-care, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. However, in most of these methods, the trainee is passive, there is low cooperation and correlation between the group, and individual differences are not considered.[12]

Motivational interviewing is an approach to involve a client in training; it is a new and motivating method meant to augment the effectiveness of training that leads to behavioral changes. Motivational interviewing is a reference-oriented guideline for strengthening and increasing internal motivation for change through discovery, identification and resolution of doubts and ambivalence.[13] The purpose of motivational interviewing is to encourage clients to change their behavior, and the responsibility for change is left to the client. The task of the counselor in this approach is to create a set of situations that boost the client’s motivation and commitment to change.[14] Reasons for the attractiveness of motivational interviewing compared to traditional and usual methods are as follows. (1) Motivational interviewing directly addresses resistance to change, which is recognized as a major problem in all therapeutic methods. (2) Due to the flexibility of this method, it has the ability to be used independently or integrated with other treatments, and motivational interviewing is learned in relatively few sessions and has significant therapeutic effects.[15]

The results of studies show that motivational interviewing has been effective in promoting self-care in people with diabetes[16] as well as in those with high blood pressure.[15] However, this method of counseling has not been used to promote women’s self-care and prenatal care.

Sistan and Baluchistan province has the highest rate of childbearing among teenagers in Iran. This province is also the poorest in Iran in terms of socioeconomic status, and it has the highest rate of early marriage.[17] According to what was described, considering the importance of teenage pregnancy and the main role of adolescence as a golden period in the life of an individual, family, and society, we decided to evaluate the effect of counseling based on principles of motivational interviewing on self-care and the usage rate of prenatal care among pregnant teenagers referred to Comprehensive Care Centers of Zahedan in 2023.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The present study was a two-group, semi-experimental study with pre-test and post-test design.

Study participants and sampling

The research population consisted of all teenage pregnant women who were referred to Comprehensive Care Centers of Zahedan in 2023. This research received the ethics code number from the ethics committee.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 10–19 years of age, gestational age of 14–20 weeks based on first-trimester ultrasound, first pregnancy, absence of any underlying disease and psychological disorder, lack of any complications during pregnancy, having at least elementary literacy, and no experience of a stressful event in the previous 3 months. Exclusion criteria were mother’s unwillingness to continue participation, failure to complete questionnaires, not participating in a meeting, the occurrence of any complications during pregnancy during the study period requiring hospitalization of the mother or change in her lifestyle, including hypertension, trauma, severe nausea and vomiting, threat of miscarriage, bleeding, and experience of stressful event during the study.

The mean and standard deviation of the self-care score of pregnant women was (=5.44, S1 = 1.93, = 6.69, S2 = 1.86) considering the confidence coefficient of 95% (Z1-α/2: 1.96) and test power of 80% (Z1-β = 0.85).[18]

Sampling in this study was of multistage type. First, the city of Zahedan was divided into four regions: North, South, East, and West. Comprehensive Care Centers were considered as clusters in each region. In each region, two centers were selected by draw; in other words, eight centers were selected in total. Subsequently, a lottery was drawn between the centers in each region, and one center was chosen for the intervention and one for the control group. In each center, eligible clients who were willing to participate in the study were invited, and a total of 11–12 people were selected in each center by a convenient sampling method.

Data collection tools and technique

Data-gathering tools

The data collection instruments in this research were the demographic and pregnancy information form, pregnancy self-care questionnaire, and prenatal care checklist.

The demographic and pregnancy information form included items related to age, level of education, employment status, spouse’s education and occupation, planned pregnancy, and gestation age.

The self-care questionnaire includes 46 items in five performance domains of nutrition (15 questions), exercise and physical activity (4 questions), smoking and drug use (4 questions), personal hygiene (23 questions), and routine pregnancy care (11 questions). The content validity and reliability of the questionnaire, which was created by Khayat et al. (2022),[1] was confirmed with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86. The answers were classified based on a 4-point Likert scale: always, most of the time, sometimes, and never. Each answer is assigned a score of 0–3, respectively; the higher the scores of the research units, the higher and better the level of self-care. The score range of the questionnaire is 0–138. In the present study, the reliability of the questionnaire was rechecked with the internal consistency method, and Cronbach’s alpha was calculated as 0.79.

The number of pregnancy cares was determined according to the number of cares registered in the Sib system, and the postpartum checklist was completed by the researcher. The adequacy of care was considered 8 cares based on Iran’s pregnancy care instructions.

Intervention

After obtaining the permits and code of ethics from Zahedan University of Medical Sciences, the researcher visited the selected Comprehensive Health Centers. After introducing herself and stating the goals and importance of the research, the researcher checked the inclusion criteria for those who were willing to participate.

A checklist was used to verify the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria. The eligible participants were selected, and written informed consent was taken from them. In the case of participants under the age of 16 years, written informed consent was obtained from the participants and their husbands or legal guardians. Afterward, the demographic and pregnancy information form and basic self-care questionnaires were completed by the research unit. The mode of participation in the study was explained to the subject, and her name along with contact information was inquired to coordinate the meetings.

In the intervention group, coordination was done for the training sessions, and the trainings were presented in four weekly 60–90-minute sessions in groups of 5–7 people. Training content in motivational interview sessions

In total, we used 20 worksheets. Of these, 18 were implemented during meetings, and two were assigned as homework. The first homework assignment involved determining the individual’s activities and behaviors of interest that could help modify the change. The second homework assignment required the prioritization of values, activities, and behaviors in accordance with those values.

The control group only received routine care with no intervention. To appreciate the participation of subjects, a baby care training package was presented to them after completion of the sampling. The self-care questionnaire was completed in both groups at 28–30 weeks of gestation. To check for the number of prenatal cares after delivery, the information was extracted from the Sib system and recorded by the researcher in the checklist.

Statistics

Data analysis was done using SPSS software (version 26) by considering the significance level of P < 0.05. After checking the normal distribution of data using the Shapiro-Wilk test, based on the results, the independent t-test and Mann-Whitney test were used for data with normal and non-normal distribution, respectively. For the analytical purpose of comparing the frequency of prenatal care adequacy in the two groups, the Chi-square test was used.

Ethics consideration

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Zahedan University of Medical Sciences (Ethical code: IR.ZAUMS.REC.1402.100).

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. A written informed consent was obtained from the participants before data collection. In the case of participants under the age of 16 years, written informed consent was obtained from the participants and their husbands or legal guardians. It was emphasized that the results will remain completely confidential, no information will be published with the person’s name, and the person’s response will not affect his treatment measures.

Discussion

In this study, the effect of training based on motivational interviewing on self-care and perinatal care was investigated.

The majority of our participants had a primary education level. The mean age in the intervention group was 17.29 years, while the mean age in the control group was 17.45 years. According to the results, training based on motivational interviewing improves self-care in teenage pregnant women and leads to more frequent use of prenatal services.

The results of the present study show that training based on motivational interviewing leads to improvement in nutrition performance. A study was conducted on pregnant women with type 2 diabetes regarding dietary compliance using motivational interviewing and its effect on macrosomic maternal and infant weight gain in Denmark. The findings showed that motivational interviewing training led to better weight gain and a decrease in the frequency of macrosomic babies.[19] A study was conducted in Iran to investigate the effect of motivational interviewing on dietary intake and weight changes in obese and overweight women before pregnancy, the results of which showed that motivational interviewing significantly reduced total energy consumption and weight gain relative to the control group.[20] Another study was performed in 2017 to reduce weight using a nutritional training program based on motivational interviewing. Unlike other studies, their findings indicated that weight management did not improve with motivational interviewing.[21] The reason for the ineffectiveness of intervention in this group was likely the use of volunteers who did not have enough motivation and held only two sessions, which was not enough to reach the effects of motivational interviews.

The next variable was physical activity, which, based on the results of the motivational interview, was significantly improved. In line with the findings of the present research, a study was conducted on 62 patients to improve physical activity in women with gestational diabetes in Oxford University Hospitals. Their results showed that motivational interviewing increased aerobic physical activity in these women.[22]

The results of the present study show that motivational interviewing leads to an increase in self-care with respect to smoking. Karatay et al. (2010)[23] conducted a study with the aim of evaluating the effects of a smoking cessation motivational interview program on smoking rates among pregnant women in Turkey. There were a total of eight home visits for each woman with intervals of 12–13 days between visits. The findings indicated that pregnant smokers significantly quit or reduced smoking compared to before the intervention and that the mean self-efficacy score increased significantly compared to before the intervention. A study by Mojahed (2018)[24] was performed to determine the effect of motivational interviewing on self-efficacy of hookah smoking in 140 pregnant women. The mean self-efficacy score of quitting hookahs was significantly different between the intervention group and control group. Considering the prevalence of hookah use among women in Zahedan (18.8%)[25] and its effects on pregnancy, it is of high importance to intervene in this regard.

In the present study, the personal health of teenage mothers improved through training based on motivational interviewing. The study by Saffari et al. (2020)[26] in pregnant women showed that the oral hygiene of pregnant women improved by holding two motivational interview sessions. Araban et al.[27] investigated the effect of training using motivational interviewing on self-care against air pollution in pregnancy, and their results indicated an increase in self-efficacy of pregnant women against air pollution.

The last domain of self-care was routine pregnancy care and receiving enough care during pregnancy. The results of our study showed that the rate of self-care in this domain as well as the number of cares in the intervention group was significantly different from the control group. A study in people with diabetes also showed that commitment to and satisfaction with treatment improves with motivational interviewing.[28] Today, motivational interviewing has been recognized as an effective factor in compliance with treatment. Motivational interviewing is an appropriate method and one of the most empathetic communication styles. In this method, the counselor confirms and supports the patient’s sense of self-efficacy. After the patients feel accepted by the doctor and realize that they are not compelled to do something, the patients are motivated to change, begin to participate in the treatment process, and take responsibility for following the doctor’s instructions. In this situation, cooperation and communication between doctor and patient improves, and compliance with treatment and care increases.[29]

Motivational interviewing’s effectiveness stems from its ability to mobilize clients’ inherent resources for motivation, learning, creativity, problem-solving, and purposeful activity. This method works by activating a patient’s own motivation to change and adhere to treatment. As a result, patients who are exposed to motivational interviewing are more likely to make positive changes. This method reduces resistance to change by employing a collaborative and non-confrontational style.[13,15,22]

In spite of progress in prenatal care, we still face problems related to prenatal care, including delayed care and treatment, inadequate care and medical advice, and poor adherence to medical advice, which can lead to the persistence of such adverse outcomes.[30] Dissatisfaction of mothers with the quality of services provided in the healthcare system and insufficient caregiver-client interaction have been reported as obstacles to receiving care in pregnant women.[31] Inadequate care in teenage pregnancy has been reported in several investigations. In Indonesia, 25% of teenage mothers received less than four antenatal cares.[32]

The role of timely and adequate prenatal care visits in ensuring the health of the mother and her baby cannot be ignored. Antenatal primary care provides an opportunity for early screening of complications, patient referral, and treatment. In addition, adequate prenatal care visits facilitate follow-up and monitoring of fetal growth and maternal health by physicians. Furthermore, the provision of prenatal care can create a friendly environment for care providers and mothers, which is a prerequisite for safe delivery.[33] Our searches did not identify any research reporting the number of pregnancy care visits among Iranian teenagers. In Zahedan, it is reported that 49.2% of women receive adequate pregnancy care.[34]

Prenatal care, particularly education, plays a crucial role. Pregnant women need proper counseling and training. Effective communication and interaction between midwives and pregnant women during prenatal education can foster strong social bonds, increasing the mother’s awareness of her capabilities and resources. Education also enhances expectant mothers’ self-confidence to navigate the challenges of pregnancy and childbirth, promoting self-care. However, many health professionals and caregivers are unable to provide comprehensive education due to their heavy workload.[35]

This research highlights several strengths: it focuses on teenage mothers, a group that has been less investigated, and it incorporates motivational interviewing principles, which have not been widely used in promoting self-care among pregnant women.

Limitation and recommendation

The first limitation was self-reporting in data collection. We attempted to explain the objectives and importance of studying the samples to be more accurate in their responses. The second limitation was the short follow-up period of the intervention; it is better to conduct studies with a longer follow-up period. The third limitation was sampling only from women who referred to comprehensive health centers, primigravida women, and were fluent in Farsi, which makes it possible to generalize the data only to these women.

It is recommended that future research investigate the effectiveness of motivational interviewing in promoting self-care and lifestyle changes in pregnant mothers across diverse societies and age groups. Comparisons with other intervention methods would be valuable for understanding the relative efficacy of motivational interviewing.

Conclusions

The findings of the present study reveal the positive effect of training based on motivational interviewing in promoting self-care and the use of prenatal care in teenage mothers. Nutritional behaviors, physical activity and exercise, smoking, personal hygiene, and prenatal care improved by using this training approach in adolescent mothers. Considering the fact that this method is one of the best approaches to communicate with clients that leads to behavior change, as well as its cost-effectiveness and feasibility, it is recommended to take advantage of this approach in improving the quality of prenatal services and creating friendly services for mothers.
