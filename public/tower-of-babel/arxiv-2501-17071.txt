On the sampling complexity of coherent superpositions
Beatriz Dias, Robert König

Abstract
We consider the problem of sampling from the distribution of measurement outcomes when applying a POVM to a superposition | Ψ ⟩ = ∑ j = 0 χ − 1 c j ​ | ψ j ⟩ \ket{\Psi}=\sum_{j=0}^{\chi-1}c_{j}\ket{\psi_{j}} of χ \chi pure states. We relate this problem to that of drawing samples from the outcome distribution when measuring a single state | ψ j ⟩ \ket{\psi_{j}} in the superposition. Here j j is drawn from the distribution p ⁡ ( j ) = | c j | 2 / ‖ c ‖ 2 2 p(j)=|c_{j}|^{2}/\|c\|^{2}_{2} of normalized amplitudes. We give an algorithm which – given O ⁡ ( χ ​ ‖ c ‖ 2 2 ​ log ⁡ 1 / δ ) O(\chi\|c\|_{2}^{2}\log 1/\delta) such samples and calls to oracles evaluating the involved probability density functions – outputs a sample from the target distribution except with probability at most δ \delta . In many cases of interest, the POVM and individual states in the superposition have efficient classical descriptions allowing to evaluate matrix elements of POVM elements and to draw samples from outcome distributions. In such a scenario, our algorithm gives a reduction from strong classical simulation (i.e., the problem of computing outcome probabilities) to weak simulation (i.e., the problem of sampling). In contrast to prior work focusing on finite-outcome POVMs, this reduction also applies to continuous-outcome POVMs. An example is homodyne or heterodyne measurements applied to a superposition of Gaussian states. Here we obtain a sampling algorithm with time complexity O ⁡ ( N 3 ​ χ 3 ​ ‖ c ‖ 2 2 ​ log ⁡ 1 / δ ) O(N^{3}\chi^{3}\|c\|_{2}^{2}\log 1/\delta) for a state of N N bosonic modes.

Coherent superpositions of states also figure prominently in all proposals towards demonstrating an algorithmic quantum advantage for computing: While the role of “quantum parallelism” as a source of quantum computational power is at times inflated in the popular science literature, both coherent application of function evaluation (e.g., in oracle calls in Grover’s algorithm

Given that coherent superpositions give rise to quantum computational power, it is natural to try to quantify the algorithmic complexity
associated with coherence. With this objective, we consider a particular algorithmic problem, namely that of sampling from the output distribution when measuring a state. Our main result is an algorithmic recipe which provides an upper bound on the resources required to draw such a sample.

Having established Theorem
1
, let us briefly comment on the broader context where it may find further applications. Depending on the measurement and the states considered, it may be computationally easy to evaluate the associated probability density functions, yet unclear how to (efficiently) sample from the corresponding distributions. In such a scenario, a quantum device allowing to prepare and measure individual states
\{\ket{\psi_{j}}\}_{j=0}^{\chi-1}
could be used to produce i.i.d. samples as required by Theorem
1
. Applying our algorithm, the quantum device could thus be used to sample from
\mu^{M}_{\Psi}
for a superposition
\ket{\Psi}=\sum_{j=0}^{\chi-1}c_{j}\ket{\psi_{j}}
even though it may not be possible to easily prepare
\ket{\Psi}
. This hybrid use of Theorem
1
– where a quantum device is combined with efficient classical processing – is made possible by the black-box formulation of our result: It makes no references to how the i.i.d. samples are produced.

We note that the ability to evaluate probabilities associated with measuring (often referred to as strong simulation) can often be leveraged to produce samples from the corresponding distribution (i.e., weak simulation). In particular, this is the case for finite-outcome measurements. A corresponding reduction from strong to weak simulation can be given along the lines sketched in the description of the generic routine
\mathsf{samp}_{p}
: it suffices to compute conditional probabilities of individual bits, which can then be sampled iteratively.
In the context of multiqubit systems and computational basis measurements,
such reductions from strong to weak simulation, as well as approximate versions thereof were previously considered e.g., in Ref.
[

[

]
(see also
[
14
]
) from strong to weak simulation. The key difference to the existing reductions mentioned above is that the latter only apply to finite-outcome POVMs. In contrast, Theorem
1
also covers cases with a continuous number of outcomes. In particular, it circumvents more naive approaches based on discretizing (binning), which generally lead to probabilities that are less tractable and/or cannot be computed efficiently.

Appendix A
On Radon-Nikodym theorems for POVMs

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma)
be a measure space.
A positive operator-valued measure (POVM) is
a function
M:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

assigning a non-negative operator
M(X)
to every element
X\in\Sigma

subject to the conditions
M(\emptyset)=0
,
M(\Omega)=I_{\mathcal{H}}

and satisfying
\sigma
-additivity, i.e.,
M\left(\bigcup_{j}X_{j}\right)=\sum_{j}M(X_{j})

for any countable family
\{X_{j}\}_{j}\subset\Sigma
of pairwise disjoint sets, with the sum converging
in the weak operator topology.

The Radon-Nikodym property concerns the representability of an additive function on a
\sigma
-algebra.
Following
[
15
]
, we call a POVM
M

decomposable

if there is a
\sigma
-finite measure
\lambda
on
(\Omega,\Sigma)

and a
weakly

\lambda
-measurable function
Q:X\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
Q(x)\geq 0
for every
x\in X
such that

\displaystyle M(X)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}Q(x)d\lambda(x)\quad\textrm{ for every }\quad X\in\Sigma\ .

(23)

In other words,
\Omega
can be covered by countably many measurable sets of finite measure, and
\langle Q(\cdot)\psi,\varphi\rangle
is
\lambda
-measurable for all
\psi,\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
. In the Ph.D. thesis
[
15
]
by Robinson, the following necessary and sufficient condition
for decomposability was established, see
[
15
, Theorem 3.3.2]
and the remarks following it.
The result of
[
15
]
generalizes an earlier result by
Berezanskii and
Kondratev
[
16
]
applicable only to the case where
M(\Omega)
is trace-class,
and the result of Chiribella et al.
[
17
]
which shows that decomposability is given if the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional.

Theorem 5
.

[
15
, Theorem 3.3.2, paraphrased]

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma)
be a measure space and
M:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
a POVM on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
\lambda
be a
\sigma
-finite measure on
(\Omega,\Sigma)
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\|M(X)\|\leq\mu(X)
for every
X\in\Sigma
.

(ii)

There is a weakly
\lambda
-measurable map
Q:\Omega^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
defined on a set
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega
of full
\lambda
-measure, with
Q(x)\geq 0

\mu
-almost-everywhere in
x
, such that

\displaystyle M(X)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}Q(x)d\lambda(x)\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad X\in\Omega\ .

(24)

Important special cases are when
\Omega=\mathbb{N}
: Here
\lambda
can be defined by

\lambda(\{j\})=M(\{j\})
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
On the other hand, if
\Omega=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}X_{j}
is a countable union of subsets
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\Sigma
with
M(X_{j})
trace-class for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\lambda
can be chosen as
\lambda(X)=\mathsf{tr}(M(X))
.

We refer to the more recent paper
[
18
]
for additional references and an in-depth review of the Radon-Nikodym property, including more general statements applicable to general Banach spaces (including non-separable Hilbert spaces, see e.g.,
[
18
, Theorem 2.7]
).

Gaussian measurements

Consider a Gaussian state
\rho=\rho(\Gamma,d)
on
N
bosonic modes with covariance matrix
\Gamma
and displacement vector
d
.
A general Gaussian measurement corresponds to projecting the system into a pure Gaussian state
\ket{\psi_{G}}=S(z)\ket{\alpha}
, with
z\in[0,+\infty)^{N}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, where
S=\diag(z_{1},1/z_{1},\ldots,z_{N},1/z_{N})
is the symplectic matrix associated with the squeezing operator
S(z)
. The state
\ket{\psi_{G}}
has covariance matrix
SS^{T}
and displacement vector
m=Sd(\alpha)
.
The probability density function defined by the measurement is
[
19
,
20
]
(see also e.g.,
[
21
]
)

\displaystyle f(m)=\frac{\exp\left(-(m-d)^{T}(\Gamma+SS^{T})^{-1}(m-d)\right)}{\pi^{N}\sqrt{\det((\Gamma+SS^{T})/2)}}\ .

(25)

Note that a heterodyne Gaussian measurement corresponds to having
z=(1,\ldots,1)
and
S=I
, while a homodyne measurement corresponds to taking the limit
z\rightarrow(0,\ldots,0)
. Notice that

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\left(\Gamma+SS^{T}\right)^{-1}=(X\Gamma X)^{MP}

(26)

where
X=\diag(1,0,1,0,\ldots)
and
MP
denotes the Moore-Penrose inverse.
The probability distribution for the outcome
m=(x_{1},0,\ldots,x_{N},0)
(where
x_{j}
is the outcome of a homodyne measurement on mode
j
) is

\displaystyle f(m)=\frac{\exp\left(-(m-d)^{T}(X\Gamma X)^{MP}(m-d)\right)}{\pi^{N}\sqrt{\det((X\Gamma X)/2)}}\ .

(27)

Appendix C
Relation to the Gaussian extent of a state

Consider a state
\ket{\Psi}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
of the form

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}c_{j}\ket{\omega_{j}}\ ,

(28)

where
r\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary,
\{\ket{\omega_{j}}\}_{j=0}^{r-1}
are Gaussian states, and
\{c_{j}\}_{j=0}^{r-1}
are complex coefficients such that
\ket{\Psi}
is normalized. The so-called “sparsification lemma” of Ref.
[
22
]
roughly states the following (when translated to our setting):
if
\|c\|_{1}^{2}
is small, then the state
\ket{\Psi}
has a sparse approximation, i.e., there is a vector
\ket{\Omega}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
(not necessarily normalized) which

(i)

is a linear combination of a small number of Gaussian states, and

(ii)

is also close to
\ket{\Psi}
.

We use the formulation given
in
[
23
, Lemma 6]
, which provides additional structural information on the form of
\ket{\Omega}
. More precisely,
[
23
, Lemma 6]
together with Markov’s inequality imply that
for
\varepsilon>0
and any integer

\displaystyle\chi\geq\frac{2\|c\|_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}

(29)

there is a (unnormalized) vector
\ket{\Omega}
of the form

\displaystyle\ket{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|c\|_{1}}{\chi}\sum_{j=0}^{\chi-1}\ket{\psi_{j}}\ ,

(30)

where each state
\ket{\psi_{j}}
for
j\in\{0,\ldots,\chi-1\}
is a Gaussian state (in fact, an element of
\{\ket{\omega_{k}}\}_{k=0}^{r-1}
up to a phase factor), and

\displaystyle\|\Psi-\Omega\|\leq\varepsilon\ .

(31)

(In
[
23
]
, it is shown that a randomly chosen element
\ket{\Omega}
from a certain ensemble of states satisfies these properties with probability at least
1/2
. In fact, an additional stronger concentration bound is shown (giving a higher probability), however, this requires additional information about
\ket{\Psi}
in the form of a quantity called the stabilizer fidelity.)

We note that (
31
) implies that

\displaystyle\left|1-\|\Omega\|\right|\leq\varepsilon\ .

(32)

Proof.

Because
\|\Psi\|=1
we have

\displaystyle\|\Psi-\Omega\|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-2\mathsf{Re}\langle\Psi,\Omega\rangle+\|\Omega\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\

(33)

and

\displaystyle\mathsf{Re}\langle\Psi,\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathsf{Re}\langle\Psi,\Omega\rangle\right|

(34)

\displaystyle\leq|\langle\Psi,\Omega\rangle|

(35)

\displaystyle\leq\|\Omega\|

(36)

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. This implies the claim.
∎

Consider the normalized state

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|\Omega\|}\ket{\Omega}\ .

(37)

By the triangle inequality as well as (
31
) and (
32
), we have

\displaystyle\|\Psi-\Psi^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Psi-\Omega\|+\|\Omega-\Psi^{\prime}\|

(38)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\left|\|\Omega\|-1\right|

(39)

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon\ .

(40)

By definition, we have

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\chi-1}c_{j}^{\prime}\ket{\psi_{j}}\ \textrm{ where }c_{j}^{\prime}=\frac{\|c\|_{1}}{\chi\|\Omega\|}\textrm{ for every }j\ .

(41)

With (
29
) and (
32
) it follows that

\displaystyle\|c^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\|c\|_{1}^{2}}{\chi\|\Omega\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\|\Omega\|^{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}\ .

(42)

In particular, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|c^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}\quad\textrm{ for }\quad\varepsilon<1/2\ .

(43)

For a state
\ket{\Psi}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, the Gaussian extent
\xi(\Psi)
is defined as the infimum of
\|c\|_{1}^{2}
over all decompositions
\ket{\Psi}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}c_{j}\ket{\omega_{j}}
into Gaussian states
\{\ket{\omega_{j}}\}_{j=0}^{r-1}
, where
r\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary (see e.g.,
[
6
,
14
]
).
Considering a sequence of decompositions
\ket{\Psi}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}c_{j}\ket{\omega_{j}}
approaching this infimum, the above can be summarized as follows:

Lemma 6
.

Let
\ket{\Psi}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Let
\varepsilon<1/2
. For any
\chi>2\xi(\Psi)/\varepsilon^{2}
(where the inequality is strict) there is a state

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\chi-1}c_{j}^{\prime}\ket{\psi_{j}}

(44)

where
\{\ket{\psi_{j}}\}_{j=0}^{\chi-1}
are Gaussian states such that

\displaystyle\|\Psi-\Psi^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon

(45)

and

\displaystyle\|c^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\ .

(46)

For concreteness, we define a state
\ket{\Psi^{\prime}}
as in Lemma
6
with
\chi=3\xi(\Psi)/\varepsilon^{2}
an
\varepsilon
-sparsification of
\Psi
.
Without loss of generality (by adjusting the phase of
c_{j}^{\prime}
), we may assume that
\ket{\psi_{j}}=\ket{\psi_{\Gamma_{j},d_{j}}}
is the unique
Gaussian state with covariance matrix
\Gamma_{j}
and displacement vector
d_{j}
satisfying
\langle\mathrm{\mathrm{vac}},\psi_{\Gamma_{j},d_{j}}\rangle>0
, for every
j\in\{0,\ldots,\chi-1\}
. Applying Theorem
1
we obtain the following in a scenario where an
\varepsilon
-sparsification of a state
\ket{\Psi}
is known, i.e., the parameters
\{(c^{\prime}_{j},\Gamma_{j},d_{j})\}_{j=0}^{\chi-1}
are given.

Corollary 7
.

Let
\ket{\Psi}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Let
\varepsilon<1/2
. There is a probabilistic algorithm which takes as input a tuple
\{(c^{\prime}_{j},\Gamma_{j},d_{j})\}_{j=0}^{\chi-1}

describing an
\varepsilon
-sparsification
\ket{\Psi^{\prime}}
of the state
\ket{\Psi}
, and outputs a sample
\zeta_{\Psi}^{M}
from a distribution
8\varepsilon
-close to
\mu_{\Psi}^{M}
in
L_{1}
-norm with probability at least
1-\delta
.
The algorithm has runtime upper bounded by
O(N^{3}\xi(\Psi)^{3}\varepsilon^{-4}\log 1/\delta)
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle|\mu_{\Psi}^{M}(X)-\mu_{\Psi^{\prime}}^{M}(X)|

(47)

\displaystyle=|\langle\Psi,M(X)\Psi\rangle-\langle\Psi^{\prime},M(X)\Psi^{\prime}\rangle|

(48)

\displaystyle\leq|\langle\Psi,M(X)\Psi\rangle-\langle\Psi,M(X)\Psi^{\prime}\rangle|

(49)

\displaystyle\qquad+|\langle\Psi,M(X)\Psi^{\prime}\rangle-\langle\Psi^{\prime},M(X)\Psi^{\prime}\rangle|

(50)

\displaystyle\leq|\langle\Psi,M(X)(\Psi-\Psi^{\prime})\rangle|+|\langle\Psi-\Psi^{\prime},M(X)\Psi^{\prime}\rangle

(51)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\Psi-\Psi^{\prime}\|

(52)

\displaystyle\leq 4\varepsilon

(53)

for any
X\in\Sigma
,
where we applied the triangle and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities, and where we used
M(X)\leq I
and
Eq.

40
.
As a consequence

\displaystyle\|\mu_{\Psi^{\prime}}^{M}-\mu_{\Psi}^{M}\|_{1}=2\sup_{X\in\Sigma}|\mu_{\Psi^{\prime}}^{M}(X)-\mu_{\Psi}^{M}(X)|\leq 8\varepsilon\ .

(54)

Therefore the algorithm in
Corollary

2
with input
\{(c^{\prime}_{j},\Gamma_{j},d_{j})\}_{j=0}^{\chi-1}
produces a sample from a distribution
8\varepsilon
-close to
\mu_{\Psi}^{M}
in
L_{1}
-norm with success probability at least
1-\delta
in time
O(N^{3}\chi^{3}\|c^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\log 1/\delta)
. From the definition of an
\varepsilon
-sparsification we have
\chi=3\xi(\Psi)/\varepsilon^{2}
. This together with
Eq.

43
gives the upper bound for the runtime in the claim.
∎

We consider a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma
denotes a
\sigma
-algebra on the set
\Omega
. Rejection sampling is a well-established technique, which can be succinctly summarized as follows:

Lemma 8
.

[
24
, Lemma 26, paraphrased]

Let
\mu,\nu
be two measures on
(\Omega,\Sigma)
and suppose that
X\sim\mu
. Suppose that
\mu,\nu
are such that
\left\|\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\right\|_{\infty}\leq M
for some
M<\infty
.
Let
\xi
be a binary-valued random variable such that conditioned on
X
, the probability that
\xi=1
is
\frac{1}{M}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)
. Then
\Pr(\xi=1)=\frac{1}{M}
and

\Pr(X\in A\mid\xi=1)=\nu(A)\qquad\textrm{ for any }A\in\Sigma\ .

In particular, if
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are sampled independently and identically with
X_{r}\sim\mu
, and
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n}
are constructed as above,
then at least one of
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
is equal to
1
with probability at least
1-e^{-\frac{n}{M}}
.

We refer to
[
24
]
for a proof of this statement.

It is worth noting that the average number of samples required to obtain one successful event (which corresponds to obtaining
\xi=1
) is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{number of samples until success}]

(55)

\displaystyle\qquad=\Pr(\xi=1)\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j\left(1-\Pr(\xi=1)\right)^{j-1}

(56)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{\Pr(\xi=1)}=M\ .

Appendix E
Proof of the pinching inequality

For completeness, we include a proof of Claim
4
, i.e., the pinching inequality established in
[
8
]
.

Proof.

Define
V_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{\chi-1}e^{2\pi ijk/\chi}P_{k}
.
For any operator
\rho\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\chi-1}V_{j}\rho V_{j}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\chi-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\chi-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\chi-1}e^{2\pi ij(k-\ell)/\chi}P_{k}\rho P_{\ell}

(58)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{\chi-1}\Bigg(\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{\chi-1}e^{2\pi ij(k-\ell)/\chi}}_{\chi\delta_{k,\ell}}\Bigg)P_{k}\rho P_{\ell}

(59)

\displaystyle=\chi\sum_{k=0}^{\chi-1}P_{k}\rho P_{k}\ .

(60)

For
\rho\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle V_{j}\rho V_{j}^{\dagger}\geq 0\qquad\textrm{ for all }j\in\{0,\ldots,\chi-1\}

(61)

hence

\displaystyle V_{0}\rho V_{0}^{\dagger}+\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{\chi-1}V_{j}\rho V_{j}^{\dagger}}_{\geq 0}=\chi\sum_{k=1}^{\chi-1}P_{k}\rho_{k}P_{k}\ .

(62)

It follows that

\displaystyle V_{0}\rho V_{0}^{\dagger}\leq\chi\sum_{k=0}^{\chi-1}P_{k}\rho P_{k}\ .

(63)

Take
\rho=|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|
. It remains to show that
|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|=V_{0}|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|V_{0}^{\dagger}
.
We have

\displaystyle V_{0}|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|V_{0}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,m=0}^{\chi-1}c_{\ell}\overline{c_{m}}V_{0}\left|\psi_{\ell}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{m}\right|V_{0}^{\dagger}\ .

(64)

By definition, we have

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\chi-1}P_{k}=P_{\ell}+Q_{\ell}

(65)

for every
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,\chi-1\}
, where
Q_{\ell}:=\sum_{k=0,k\neq\ell}^{\chi-1}P_{k}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle V_{0}|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|V_{0}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,m=0}^{\chi-1}c_{\ell}\overline{c_{m}}(P_{\ell}+Q_{\ell})\left|\psi_{\ell}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{m}\right|(P_{m}+Q_{m})

(66)

\displaystyle=\left(\ket{\Psi}+\ket{\Theta}\right)\left(\bra{\Psi}+\bra{\Theta}\right)

(67)

where we defined

\displaystyle\ket{\Theta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\chi-1}Q_{\ell}c_{\ell}\ket{\psi_{\ell}}\ .

(68)

The claim follows since
\ket{\Theta}=0
because the states
\ket{\psi_{\ell}}
are orthogonal.

∎
