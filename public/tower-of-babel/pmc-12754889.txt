Loading strategies for countermovement jump: an exploratory comparison of modality and load
Authors: Luyu Zhang, Haitao Zhu, Qiushi Wang, Dapeng Bao, Junhong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVertical jump ability is an important indicator for assessing lower-limb explosive power. Loaded jump training, using either peripheral (barbell) or centralized (weighted vest) loading, enhances jump performance through neuromuscular adaptation. However, it’s unclear how these modalities (barbell vs. weighted vest), particularly across different external load magnitudes (30%, 40%, 50% of body mass (BM)), affect jump mechanics, postural stability, and motor control. This pilot study aimed to examine how load type and magnitude influence performance, posture control, and kinetics during countermovement jumps (CMJ).MethodsSeventeen trained males (age 20.2 ± 3.2 yrs; body mass 72.3 ± 7.3 kg) performed CMJ under two loading strategies (weighted vest, barbell; randomized crossover), each at 30%, 40%, and 50% BM, with three trials per load. Dual force plates captured ground reaction force (GRF) and center of pressure (COP). Measured outcomes included jump height, reactive strength index-modified (RSI-mod), momentum, push-off displacement, bilateral strength asymmetry (BSA), postural sway, and kinetic variables. Data were analyzed using linear mixed model (LLM), generalized mixed model (GLMM), and two-way ANOVAs, and effect size was assessed via partial η2.ResultsCompared with a weighted vest, a barbell showed higher jump height, jump momentum, and push‑off distance (p-values ranged from 0.005 to 0.041; partial η2 ranged from 0.043 to 0.078), whereas increasing load from 30% to 50% body mass progressively reduced jump height and RSI_mod in both groups. For postural stability, at 30% body mass the weighted vest showed smaller landing ML COP displacement than the barbell (p < 0.019, partial η2 = 0.138). With increasing load from 30% to 50% BM, ML COP decreased with the barbell (p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.088) and moved opposite in weighted vest group. Kinetically, increasing load increased braking and landing demands (e.g., greater maximum yielding force, higher maximum landing force/acceleration, longer braking and shorter yielding durations (all p < 0.002, partial η2 = 0.121 ~ 0.423)), while group differences were limited to higher braking acceleration under the barbell condition (p = 0.029, partial η2 = 0.011). Overall, centralized loading conferred a low‑load stability advantage, while peripheral loading favored task‑level jump performance. Load increased braking/landing demands irrespective of centralized or peripheral loading.ConclusionWithin the tested load range (30–50% BM), load distribution strategy and magnitude significantly affect jump performance and postural stability during CMJ, with trade-offs for stability and power. Below approximately 30–40% BM, the weighted vest confers greater stability (lower ML displacement) compared to barbell, while at larger load (50%BM), barbell would be appropriate for force lead performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01437-3.Keywords: Barbell, Center of pressure trajectory, Jump performance, Load modality, Weighted vest

Introduction

Vertical jump performance is closely linked to explosive power [1, 2], which is critical for many sports (e.g., rugby [3]) that demand rapid force development during movement [4–7]. Therefore, to improve vertical jump performance in athletes, loaded jump training has been widely used in training practice [8–10], primarily augmenting the force component on the force-time curve [11]. The benefits of loaded jump training for jump performance are have been attributed primarily to neuromuscular adaptations, such as increased recruitment of high-threshold motor units, reduced antagonist coactivation, and improved inter‑muscular coordination, which collectively contribute to higher rate of force development (RFD) in the lower‑limb muscles [12]. Moreover, the magnitude and locus of these effects may depend on load level and jump phase, and, according to theories in biomechanical analyses of load distribution, the position where the loads are applied also influences key kinetic parameters in the movement pathway, including joint torques, ground reaction forces, and control of the center of mass [13]. Taken together, because coaches ultimately target task-level gains in jump height/power, it is essential to include jump performance outcomes to establish practical relevance; and because the above mechanisms concern how force is generated and distributed over time, kinetic outcomes are required to test these neuromechanical pathways.

Nowadays, two protocols for applying external load have been developed and are commonly used in training practice, that is, centralized loading and peripheral loading [14, 15]. Peripheral loading, typically implemented by placing a barbell on the shoulders, creates an external moment arm about the thoracolumbar spine and hips (i.e., the perpendicular distance from the line of action of the barbell load to the relevant joint centers), which increases the mechanical demands on the hip and knee extensors [16]. This loading strategy may also promote strength gains by placing increased mechanical tension on key muscle groups involved in jumping, particularly under heavier load conditions [17]. More recently, centralized loading, commonly implemented via a weighted vest, has become a widely used modality in loaded jump training. Evidence suggests that torso-borne loading strategies optimize force distribution by reducing focal pressure over the scapulothoracic complex compared with barbell loading, attributable to more uniform contact-interface dispersion [18]. Evenly distributed resistance across the torso minimizes localized joint stress (e.g., spinal compression) and reduces compensatory movements during takeoff [14]. In addition to load-dispersion benefits, the weighted vest allows athletes to perform movements without restrictions of upper limbs; while barbell placement restricts arm motion and thus constrain multiplanar movement [19]. Moreover, studies have shown the level of the external load may interact with the load type and the influences of increasing the load may thus be different between weighted vest and barbell [20, 21]. Yet, such interactive fluences on locomotor control of jump have not been directly compared [22]. These contrasted mechanical constraints imply that load strategies will influence not only the magnitude of force, but also its temporal deployment and whole-body equilibrium control thus a comprehensive outcome set spanning performance (what is achieved), kinetics (how it is produced over time), and stability/asymmetry (how well and safely it is controlled) is warranted.

Therefore, this pilot study aims to compare the effects of centralized (weighted vest) and peripheral loading (barbell) with different load levels (i.e., 30–50% BM) on kinematic and locomotor characteristics of jump (e.g., lower-extremity asymmetry [23], dynamic postural stability of landing after loaded jump [24, 25], and jump kinetics difference [1]). Practically, these findings can guide the selection of load placement and magnitude to balance power development and postural control and to structure progressions that align performance gains with movement quality and stability. We hypothesized that peripheral loading would increase concentric kinetics but reduce landing stability relative to a centralized weighted vest, with higher loads (30–50% BM) amplifying these differences and lowering jump height in both modalities.

Methods

Subjects

This pilot study recruited trained male athletes from Beijing Sports University through campus postings. Seventeen male subjects (age: 20.2 ± 3.2 years, height: 175.2 ± 9.2 cm, body mass (BM): 72.3 ± 7.3 kg) were recruited for this pilot study. All participants were undergraduate sport science students. As part of their curriculum, they engaged in the same structured, multidisciplinary training comprising fundamental track-and-field skills, gymnastics elements, ball games practice, and systematic jump/plyometric drills, typically 3 sessions per week (≈ 4.5 h/week).

Inclusion criteria: (1) All subjects had at least 1 year of resistance training experience; (2) the one-repetition maximum (1RM) for the basic back squat was at least 1.2 times BM; (3) Participants had prior experience with loaded CMJ (defined as ≥ 5 sessions in the past 6 months) and were assessed by two NSCA-CSCS certified trainers; Exclusion criteria: (1) All subjects were free from injury for at least 12 months prior to the start of the research (e.g., anterior cruciate ligament, hamstring, meniscus, ankle, or other lower-extremity injuries); (2) Subjects were instructed to refrain from any strenuous physical activity, alcohol, and caffeine intake for at least 24 h prior to each testing session, and to maintain consistent dietary and sleep habits during the study period.

Before the experiment, subjects were informed of the benefits and potential risks related to the study, and all signed the informed consent form. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Beijing Sport University (Approval No. 2025215 H). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. Clinical trial number not applicable.

Protocol

A within-subject crossover design was implemented, that is, all participants performed countermovement jumps (CMJ) under two experimental conditions: weighted vest (centralized load application) and barbell (peripheral load placement). The order of conditions was randomized to minimize order effects.

All participants attended a single within-subject, randomized crossover testing session at the Biomechanics Laboratory, Beijing Sport University. All testing sessions were conducted in the morning (between 8:00 and 12:00) to minimize circadian effects. Each participant’s session lasted approximately one hour. Participants were scheduled across multiple mornings within a two-week period, with each participant tested in a single visit. Upon arrival at the laboratory, participants’ height and BM were recorded, and participants were asked to wear the same pair of their own athletic shoes across the entire study to ensure within-subject footwear consistency. A standardized warm-up protocol was implemented, comprising: (1) 5 min of moderate-intensity cycling on a stationary ergometer (Watt Bike Pro, Wattbike Ltd, Nottingham, UK); (2) Dynamic lower-body stretches (hip flexion–extension, abduction–adduction); (3) 6–8 unloaded CMJ at progressive intensities (50–90% of maximal perceived effort). The loaded CMJ protocol was performed in two distinct conditions (barbell vs. weighted vest) within a single session. For each condition, 9 CMJ were executed: 3 trials at each of three loads at 30%, 40%, and 50% of BM. Within each condition, the three load magnitudes (30%, 40%, 50% BM) were administered in a randomized order. Between repeated trials at a given load, 15 s of passive rest were provided. This inter-trial rest is consistent with prior CMJ protocols [22]. A minimum of 3 min of rest was allowed between different load increments to minimize fatigue accumulation [26]. Upon completion of one loading condition, participants rested passively for 30 min before performing a second standardized dynamic warm-up, followed by the CMJ protocol under the alternate loading condition (Fig. 1).

Subjects were instructed to stand motionless for 2 s to provide a period of quiescence to determine the start of each jump using ground reaction force (GRF)-time data [27]. Once instructed, subjects began the unloading, yielding, and braking components of the CMJ to a self-selected depth, followed by maximal-effort concentric phases (verbal cue: “jump as fast and high as possible”) [28].

All jumps were performed under fixed‑arm conditions. Participants held a bar across the upper back with both hands throughout the countermovement and take‑off, eliminating arm swing. Barbell loads were applied via a standardized Olympic barbell (Eleiko, Halmstad, Sweden) positioned across the upper back; weighted vest loads were applied via Little Bloke Fitness (Reservoir, VIC, Australia), and a PVC bar held across the upper back to standardize arm position. Prior to the test, participants completed a familiarization session within 48–72 h before testing. During familiarization, they practiced the countermovement jump (CMJ) with the fixed‑arm position (hands holding a bar across the upper back) under each loading condition: barbell, weighted vest, and unloaded with a PVC bar. They performed 3 practice jumps per condition at self‑selected effort followed by 5 practice jumps at maximal intent, with standardized verbal instructions and feedback to ensure consistent depth, posture, and arm fixation. Session duration was approximately 40 min. No data from familiarization were included in analyses.

Data collection

GRF and COP data were collected using two force plates (Kistler 9281CA, KISTLER, Winterthur, Switzerland), sampling at 1000 Hz, with each foot placed on a separate plate. GRF outcomes, including vertical force, acceleration (computed from force and system mass), and net impulse (area under the force–time curve), as well as force-time curve characteristics morphology and timing (e.g., presence of multiple peaks, time to peak force, rate of force development, and braking/concentric phase durations), were derived from the GRF–time data using a forward dynamics approach as described in previous studies [29, 30]. COP outcomes, including total path length and directional excursions in both the anterior-posterior (AP) and medial-lateral (ML) axes, were analyzed to assess postural control and stability across different loading conditions.

The outcomes focused on (a) jump performance; (b) kinetic variables; and (c) postural stability variables. Jump performance including jump height, reactive strength index-modified (RSI-mod) and jump momentum [29]; Kinetic variables comprised maximum force, minimum force, maximum acceleration, minimum acceleration, and phase durations of CMJ phases (unloading, yielding, braking, concentric, landing); Postural stability variables including BSA (calculated from the peak vertical ground reaction force differences between the dominant and non-dominant legs during all phases [23, 31]) and COP; COP displacement was quantified using vector analysis in the global coordinate system along the anterior–posterior (AP) and medial–lateral (ML) axes; total displacement (TD) was computed as the vector sum of AP and ML excursions [25, 32, 33]. Data processing

Jump analysis for each trial was completed using a custom MATLAB script (R2024b; The Mathworks, Inc., Natick, MA) to understand differences in metrics and calculation strategies. Each trial was divided into five phases as follows: unloading, yielding, braking, concentric, landing [29, 34]. Derivatives of acceleration, velocity, and power were calculated from GRF-time data. All data were filtered using a fourth-order, bidirectional, low-pass Butterworth digital filtering with a 50-Hz cutoff frequency [35]. The final statistical analysis included the average of three trials for each incremental load condition (30%, 40%, 50% BM).

The quiescent period (0.5 s) before each trial was used to assess BM [34]. The unloading phase began when the stabilized vertical GRF (vGRF) fell below and ended when vGRF returned above that threshold. The yielding phase started immediately thereafter and terminated at the time of minimum vertical center-of-mass (COM) velocity (typically coinciding with peak knee flexion). The braking phase followed, concluding when COM velocity reached 0 m/s or the last negative value prior to upward motion reversal. The concentric phase began at the end of the braking phase and ended when vGRF dropped below BM − 5 × SD, marking the onset of the flight phase. Landing phase was defined as the first sample after flight at which vGRF exceeded 20 N, and stabilization as the force returning to BM + 5 × SD ≥ 100ms. Trials exhibiting multiple threshold crossings or oscillatory behavior around these thresholds were excluded.

The COP was partitioned into two primary phases: the take-off preparation phase (when the unloading phase starts to take off), and the landing stabilization phase (when the landing phase starts to stabilize). The assessment protocol and device have been validated according to those well-established and we have carefully checked the data obtained from loaded CMJ trials. If the COP outcomes in one or two trials were different from other trials, we excluded them and used the average of the remaining.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.12.0) using the statsmodels and pingouin packages. A repeated measures framework was employed, with load strategies (barbell vs. vest) and load intensity (30%, 40%, 50% body weight) modeled as fixed effects, while subject ID was included as a random intercept to account for within-subject variance.

This study employed Linear Mixed Models (LMM) to analyze repeated-measures data, addressing the limitations of traditional methods that fail to account for between-individual variability and temporal correlations [36]. The Shapiro-Wilk test assessed the normality of the residuals, and in cases of violation of normality assumptions, generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) were used as an alternative. A Bonferroni post-hoc comparison was conducted to determine the source of significance when a significant interaction was observed. Additionally, for all non-normally distributed data, we reported the median [IQR].

The changes in each outcome variable from 30% to 40% load and from 40% to 50% load were calculated to explore further whether different loading groups (barbell vs. weighted vest) were associated with differential changes in performance across load levels. These changes were analyzed using two-way ANOVA models with load interval (30–40% vs. 40–50%) and loading condition (barbell vs. weighted vest) as factors. When a significant interaction was observed, Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons were employed in the analysis of change scores between load intervals to improve sensitivity in detecting differences between loading conditions, given the exploratory intent of this analysis.

The level of significance was set at p < 0.05 for the comparisons of all outcomes. partial η2 was used to assess the effect size, was used to assess effect sizes where statistical significance was observed, with interpretation based on conventional thresholds: small η2 ≈ 0.01, medium ≈ 0.06, and large ≥ 0.14.

Results

All participants completed the assessment, and all the data were included in the analysis. The results of the Shapiro-Wilk normality test indicate that a subset of variables failed to meet the assumption of normality. To adequately address the skewed distributions of these non-normal variables, Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) were employed.

The effects of loads and group condition on jump performance

The LMM model demonstrated significant main effects of group on jump height (F (2, 32) = 7.14, p = 0.009, partial η2 = 0.069), jump momentum (F (2, 32) = 4.28, p = 0.041, partial η2 = 0.043) and push-off distance (F (2, 32) = 8.11, p = 0.005, partial η2 = 0.078) (Table 1), that is, the barbell induced a greater jump height, jump momentum, and push-off distance compared to the weighted vest condition. Meanwhile, significant main effects of load on jump height (F (2, 32) = 11.51, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.193) and RSI_mod (F (2, 32) = 4.30, p = 0.016, partial η2 = 0.082) were observed, that is, across groups, the increase of external load induced a progressive decrease in jump height and RSI_mod (i.e. 30%BM >40%BM >50%BM). No significant interaction (p-values ranged from 0.110 to 0.873) on all these outcomes of jump performance.

The effects of loads and group condition on postural sway in different periods

The GLMM model demonstrated a significant interaction (β = 0.055, p = <0.001, partial η2 = 0.097), main effects of group (β = −2.530, p = <0.001, partial η2 = 0.057), and main effects of load (β = −0.088, p = <0.001, partial η2 = 0.014), on ML COP displacement during the landing phase (Table 2). The post-hoc analyses showed that the weighted vest group at the 30% load had significantly lower ML displacement during the landing phase compared to the barbell group at the same load (p < 0.019, partial η2 = 0.138), and the barbell group exhibited a significant decrease in ML displacement at the 50% load compared to the 30% load (p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.088). As the load increases from 30% to 50% of BM, ML and total displacement in the barbell condition decrease overall, whereas the weighted vest condition remains relatively stable with a minimum at 40% (with ML at 50% slightly higher than the barbell); AP changes are small, and between-condition differences are mainly reflected in ML (Fig. 2).

The effects of loads and group condition on jump kinetics

The LMM model demonstrated significant main-effects of loads, but not interaction and main-effects of group (p-values ranged from 0.299 to 0.979), on maximum yielding force (F (2, 32) = 35.25, p <0.001, partial η2 = 0.423), maximum concentric acceleration (F (2, 32) = 15.99, p <0.001, partial η2 = 0.250), concentric phase duration (F (2, 32) = 6.62, p = 0.002, partial η2 = 0.121), and maximum landing acceleration (F (2, 32) = 7.47, p <0.001, partial η2 = 0.135) (Table 3). The GLMM model demonstrated significant main effects of loads and group, but not interaction (p = 0.516), on maximum braking acceleration (group: β = −0.669, p = 0.029, partial η2 = 0.011; load: β = −0.106, p< 0.001, partial η2 = 0.146). No significant main effects or interactions on other outcomes were observed (p-values ranged from 0.106 to 0.979).

# Statistically significant effects between loads

* Statistically significant effects between group

In the exploratory analysis, significant interactions, but not main effects of group and load (group: p = 0.257; load: p = 0.381), were observed in the changes in maximum braking force (F (2, 32) = 8.78, p = 0.004, partial η2 = 0.121) (Supplementary Table 3). The post-hoc analyses revealed that from 40% to 30% load, the increase in maximum force during the braking phase was significantly smaller in the weighted vest group than in the barbell group (p = 0.005, partial η2 = 0.116). Moreover, within the weighted vest group, maximum force during the braking phase increased significantly from 50% to 40% load (p = 0.008, partial η2 = 0.104).

Additionally, significant main effects of loads, but not interaction and main effects of group (interaction: p = 0.144; group: p = 0.970), were observed the changes in minimum unload acceleration (p = 0.036, partial η2 = 0.067), yielding phase duration (p = 0.036, partial η2 = 0.067), braking phase duration (p = 0.044, partial η2 = 0.062), maximum landing force (p = 0.004, partial η2 = 0.122), and maximum landing acceleration (p = 0.005, partial η2 = 0.117). Specifically, an elevated increase in minimum unload acceleration, maximum landing force, maximum landing acceleration, and braking phase duration, along with the increase of load levels, was observed; and at higher load levels, the yielding phase duration was shorter. No significant main effects or interactions on other outcomes were observed (p-values ranged from 0.055 to 0.859).

Discussion

This pilot study provides preliminary evidence that the load distribution strategy of resistance (weighted vest vs. barbell) is a critical factor determining the effects of loaded training on CMJ performance, postural control, and underlying kinetic responses, with these effects clearly modulated by the level of applied load. Overall, the findings indicate that different loading strategies and specific loads (within the range 30–50% BM), especially their interaction, may impose distinct neuromechanical demands and adaptations, which influence the body’s coordination, force production, and posture control during explosive tasks.

As the load increases, athletes may experience changes in RSI_mod and jump height that could affect their performance in CMJ, highlighting the importance of targeting optimal load conditions during training. Specifically, it is observed that the barbell consistently yielded significantly greater jump height, momentum, and push-off displacement across all load levels tested compared to the weighted vest. One possible explanation is that the centralized mass of the weighted vest alters the body’s center of mass by shifting it slightly forward. This change reduces the natural counter-movement of the trunk and mainly limits hip hinge capacity during the eccentric [22] and concentric phase. Under barbell loading, the higher peak acceleration during the braking phase suggests greater eccentric demands on the hip and trunk extensors, consistent with a more pronounced hip‑hinge strategy. By contrast, the weighted‑vest condition produces a more smoother movement pattern. This redistribution of load can blunt the stretch-shortening cycle by decreasing pre-stretch in the posterior chain [37], thereby impairing elastic energy storage and delaying force transmission. Furthermore, the increased torso rigidity required to stabilize the centralized load may result in reduced spinal and pelvic mobility, which compromises the sequential coordination of force from proximal (hip/trunk) to distal (knee/ankle) segments [38]. Bell et al. [39] also demonstrated that torso-borne loading conditions significantly reduced overall movement quality, with a majority of participants exhibiting compensatory movement patterns (such as trunk anterior tilt) during loaded squat. As a result, athletes are less able to generate effective triple extension, which is essential for explosive vertical propulsion [40]. In short, the load distribution may impose limitations on movement patterns, limiting the kinetic chain’s ability to function in a coordinated and elastic manner, thereby suppressing power output despite comparable external loads. In contrast, the peripheral placement of the barbell may facilitate a more natural counter-movement and enhance the utilization of the hip hinge during the eccentric phase. While this configuration can support the stretch-shortening cycle, it is essential to recognize that higher values for force-based variables do not necessarily indicate that barbell loading is the superior method of external resistance. Future research should also consider kinematic posture, alongside timing metrics, to determine whether the stretch-shortening cycle characteristics vary across different loading patterns. Furthermore, the analysis of BSA revealed no significant differences across the tested load conditions. This indicates that the loading form and load do not exert excessive asymmetrical effects on athletes, at least in terms of vertical force.

Regarding postural control, COP sway data revealed that the weighted vest resulted in significantly smaller ML displacement compared to the barbell, suggesting enhanced frontal plane stability, potentially due to its centralized and evenly distributed load [14]. The weighted vest may offer advantages for stability-oriented interventions, rehabilitation settings, or early-phase plyometric progressions where movement control and balance take priority over maximal force output. However, this stabilizing advantage for the weighted vest was load-dependent and contrasted sharply with the effects of the barbell. In the weighted vest group, ML displacement in the vest group showed a slight increasing trend with loading, AP displacement varied across phases and did not consistently change, requiring phase-specific analysis. In contrast, the barbell group shows a continuous decrease. Based on these observations, it seems the load at ≤ 30% BM may be within a stable range in body control, beyond which neuromuscular control starts to decline. It suggests that the body might prioritize stabilizing a heavier load closer to the torso’s center of mass for posture adjustment [41], resulting in greater reliance on deep stabilizing muscles and proprioceptive feedback systems, which may become saturated or fatigued at higher intensities. Another possible explanation is that heavier loads carried close to the body’s center (e.g., 40–50% BM) may increase the inertia of the trunk [42], may create challenges for postural control and movement coordination, especially during multi-joint explosive tasks like CMJ [43], as previous research mentioned [38]. These observations raise the possibility that an individual or task-specific threshold in load tolerance exists, beyond which the stabilizing benefits of centralized load reverse and begin to challenge balance and posture control. To preserve this stabilizing benefit without inducing kinetic or postural breakdown, it is advisable to keep the load under approximately 30% BM. However, factors such as core strength and task familiarity may shift this threshold, also offering a compelling avenue for future studies to explore.

Furthermore, load distribution-specific patterns were exhibited in the kinetic responses to increasing load. The weighted vest group showed abrupt and excessive changes in maximum braking force between 30% and 50% BM, also suggesting that a stability threshold may have been exceeded under this distributed loading condition at higher intensities. In contrast, the barbell group displayed consistent relative changes in COP, with both peak vGRF and braking impulse increasing with load. Although the barbell load placement may increase loading risks (e.g., spinal compression) [44], it appears to preserve postural control more effectively under higher loads, particularly in terms of COP sway and controlled force output.

Taken together, A practical load threshold of approximately 30–40% of BM is important for stability. This threshold is currently evident in postural stability metrics (e.g., ML displacement) but not yet observed in dynamic or performance metrics; further research is needed to verify its consistency and applicability. When the load is below this range, wearing vests reduces lateral movement (ML sway) during landing. However, when the load exceeds this threshold, the benefits of wearing vests decrease. Therefore, vests are preferable for lower weights when stability is a priority, whereas barbells are better for heavier weights to focus on performance, as long as proper movement quality is maintained.

Several limitations should be noted in this pilot study. First, the study only assessed the immediate effects of two types of external loads at different load levels on the jump performance and related kinetic characteristics without joint‑level kinematics. The impacts such influences of these load types and load levels on the repeated or longer-term exercise/training performance also need to be explored. Second, the sample size was relatively small, and all participants were of similar age and training background, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations, such as female athletes. Third, the range of external loading level was limited (30%–50% BM); the relationship between the changes in performance and a broader range of loading levels should be explored.
