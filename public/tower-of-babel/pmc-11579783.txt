Harnessing Cu2O-Doped 4-tert-Butylpyridine in Spiro-OMeTAD: Study on Improved Performance and Longevity of Perovskite Solar Cells
Authors: Thi Muoi Vo, Thi My Huyen Nguyen, Rui He, Chung Wung Bark
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Recently, spiro-OMeTAD has gained prominence as a hole-transporting material (HTM) in high-performance perovskite solar cells (PSCs) due to its superior properties, cutting-edge HOMO state of energy, and solution processability. To promote hole mobility and conductivity, hygroscopic dopants and additives such as lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li-TFSI) and 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP) are typically incorporated. However, these components can lead to aggregation and hydrolysis in ambient conditions, forming pinholes and voids in the films. In this study, we introduce Cu2O as a spiro-OMeTAD additive to reduce voids and improve the quality of films. Incorporating Cu2O alleviates TBP evaporation and provides a more consistent dispersion of Li-TFSI, decreasing the formation of pinholes and bubbles. As a result, additional anions are added to spiro-OMeTAD+ during the oxidation process, promoting the conductivity and hole mobility of the hole-transporting layer and thus boosting PSC performance. Thus, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) percentage of the Cu2O-modified HTM reaches 14.17%, surpassing that of the control HTM at 12.25%. Furthermore, the introduction of Cu2O protects the perovskite layer from water-induced deterioration, ensuring the stability of the PSCs. Our findings offer a promising approach to enhancing both the performance and stability of HTMs and PSCs.

Introduction

The widespread interest in incorporating sustainable and eco-friendly technologies that are both cutting-edge and budget-friendly has facilitated the revival of perovskite materials for solar applications.1,2 Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have garnered significant interest from researchers due to their remarkable efficiency, adjustable band gaps, excellent optical absorption, and cost-effective solution-based manufacturing methods.3−7 Recently, the performance of single-junction perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has successfully surpassed 25% following significant optimizations in device architecture, charge dynamics, interfacial passivation, and the engineering of functional materials.8−10 Typically, conventional perovskite solar cells (PSCs) feature a perovskite active layer as the light-absorbing component situated between the electron transport layer (ETL) and the hole transport layer (HTL), creating a sandwich-like structure.11 The perovskite layer absorbs photons from sunlight and generates electrons, and the holes are , which creates a photocurrent.12 The performance of a PSC is determined by its active layer properties, as well as other device configuration factors.13 As part of the different functional layers of devices, hole-transporting materials (HTMs) are regarded as key components owing to their vital contributions to device performance and durability.14−16 In planar PSCs, the HTM is positioned on top of the perovskite layer, which necessitates both high electrical conductivity for hole extraction and high barrier protection thickness to avoid having direct contact with the cathode (Au, Ag, or Al) or moisture and the perovskite, leading to improve the longevity and total photovoltaic performance of the device.16,17

Currently, spiro-OMeTAD, a small-molecule compound, is regarded as the most effective and widely utilized hole transport material (HTM) in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) areas.18,19 Despite this, pristine spiro-OMeTAD, an organic semiconductor, has well-known intrinsic disadvantages such as its low conductivity and high hole mobility, severely limiting PSC device progress.17,19

FK209, Li-TFSI, and TBP are the most commonly used additives in spiro-OMeTAD.11,15−17,19,20 It has been found that the inclusion of Li-TFSI enhances the reaction between spiro-OMeTAD and oxygen, and provides sufficient coordination of anions (TFSI–) to spiro-OMeTAD+, thereby enhancing its conductivity.16 Alternately, TBP helps to disperse Li-TFSI, which prevents accumulation, and it acts as a plasticizer and disrupts the crystalline structure of spiro-OMeTAD.22 This improves the morphology of the film and facilitates enhanced charge transport within the film. Although FK209 serves as an oxidizing agent, it further improves the reproducibility of the spiro-OMeTAD oxidation process.16,18,21 Unfortunately, owing to their complicated manufacturing processes and high costs, it is difficult to incorporate these chemical dopants into commercial PSCs.

Moreover, using Li-TFSI and TBP simultaneously can cause significant problems. Li-TFSI is hygroscopic; that is, it quickly absorbs water and exhibits enhanced agglomerative capabilities.23 Meanwhile, TBP is readily volatilized after electrode thermal evaporation, leaving bubbles inside the HTL, exacerbating the Li-TFSI buildup.24 When Li-TFSI is well hydrated, the bubbles in the HTL become voids, which limit charge transmission and collection.22,24 To address this, several additives with iodide salts, such as PbI2,25 RbI,26 and CsI,27 have been reported to inhibit Li-TFSI aggregation and suppress the perovskite layer from degradation by TBP. Nonetheless, these approaches mainly focus on enhancing the stability of Li-TFSI or TBP, without significantly impacting the overall performance of HTL or the perovskite layer.

Inspired by these approaches, we explore the incorporation of cuprous oxide (Cu2O) as a p-type semiconductor in an ensemble containing TBP. This compound improves hole extraction from the active layer and inhibits Li-TFSI aggregation, reducing the creation of voids inside the HTL. Cu2O, with its band gap of ∼2.1–2.5 eV, is an abundant and nontoxic material, making it an attractive candidate for photovoltaic applications, or n-i-p type PSCs as well.1−4 However, the intrinsic defects in Cu2O can introduce trap states that act as recombination centers, inhibiting minority carrier diffusion and potentially reducing effective photocurrent. These drawbacks underscore the importance of optimizing Cu2O incorporation to balance its advantages with the potential risks to device performance. Therefore, in this study, we utilized Cu2O as an additive dopant with TBP at an optimized concentration in spiro-OMeTAD. This method sought to improve hole extraction and minimize Li-TFSI aggregation while preventing the introduction of defects or recombination centers.

Compared with conventional spiro-OMeTAD, the morphology and conductivity of HTL after adding Cu2O were improved significantly with a smoother surface and free pinholes. This study aimed to harness the transition metal oxide Cu2O, which cannot be easily dispersed in the organic solvent of spiro-OMeTAD. However, it can be achieved by developing novel dopant compounds employing TPB. Despite Cu2O’s inherent challenges, we successfully leveraged its properties by developing novel dopant complexes with TBP. By optimizing the Cu2O concentration, the stable output performance of PSC was improved from 12.25% to 14.17% as compared to undoped Cu2O. Furthermore, after 350 h of keeping at room temperature, the device doped with Cu2O retained more than 50% of the initial power conversion efficiency (PCE). This strategy effectively mitigates the aggregation of Li-TFSI within the spiro-OMeTAD, reduces the water absorption of Li-TFSI, and substantially maintains PSC longevity by preventing deterioration.

Experimental Section

Material and Reagents

The PbI2, spiro-OMeTAD, Li-TFSI, TBP, CuSO4.5H2O, NaOH, C6H8O6, and solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. FAI, MACl, and MABr were obtained from the Great Cell Solar. The colloidal dispersion SnO2 (15%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar.

Synthesis of Cuprous Oxide Nanoparticles

First, 1 g of CuSO4.5H2O was dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water (DIW), stirred continuously for 30 min, and then stirred vigorously for 2 h with 100 mL of polyethylene glycol (PEG, 0.05 M). Subsequently, 150 mL of ascorbic acid (0.1 M) and 150 mL of sodium hydroxide (0.5 M) were mixed for 10 min before slowly adding to the CuSO4 solution. This reduction procedure was continued for 30 min at a constant stirring speed. The suspension was centrifuged and washed five times with deionized water and absolute ethanol to remove the remaining base, acid, and soluble byproducts. Further, they were dried at 65 °C in a vacuum heater for 12 h to get Cu2O nanoparticle powder.

Preparation of the HTM Precursor

The spiro-OMeTAD precursor solutions were obtained by dissolving the solution (72.3 mg/mL of spiro-OMeTAD in chlorobenzene) with Li-TFSI additive and Cu2O solution (28.8 μL) of different concentrations (0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 wt %) in TBP and dissolved in vials. Subsequently, these vials containing this solution sample were then stirred for at least 5 h before use, schematically demonstrated in Figure 1a.

(a) Schematic of TBP-Cu2O precursor preparation; (b) The fabrication of planar n-i-p PSCs based on HTL with or without added Cu2O.

Device Fabrication

The FTO glass was first washed with acetone, isopropanol, deionized water, and ethanol, respectively for 15 min. Then, the cleaned FTO glass was kept in a UVO bathing container for 30 min before use.

A 1 mg/mL SnO2 colloidal solution was then diluted to 2.98% in distilled water following established protocols.32 The cleaned FTO substrates were spin-coated with this SnO2 solution with the condition following: 3000 rpm for 30 s and annealing at 150 °C for 30 min. After UVO treatment for 20 min, perovskite films were fabricated in a nitrogen-filled glovebox. PbI2 precursor (600 mg of PbI2 mixed in 900 μL of DMF and 100 μL of DMSO solvents) was initially deposited onto SnO2 films at 2000 rpm for 20 s. Thereafter, the mixed perovskite precursor (60 mg FAI, 6 mg MACl, and 6 mg MABr into 1 mL IPA) was applied to the PbI2 film at 4000 rpm for 20 s, then heated at 150 °C for 20 min. The HTM and HTM/Cu2O solution were covered onto the perovskite layers at 4000 rpm for 30 s, allowing oxidation in air for 12 h. Lastly, a thermal evaporator was used to deposit Au electrodes (100 nm), shown in Figure 1b.

Characterizations

The SEM images were obtained using an ultrahigh-resolution scanning electron microscope (SEM, SU8600, Japan). The surface morphology images of thin films were attained by carrying out an atomic force microscope (AFM; Veeco, USA). The crystalline structures of all materials were examined by X-ray diffraction (Rigaku DMAX 2200, Japan). The UV–vis absorption spectra were tested using a spectrophotometer (Agilent 8453, USA). The Fourier transform infrared spectra were tested by a spectrometer (FTIR, Vertex 70, Bruker, Germany). The spectra of steady-state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) were determined with a Quanta Master TM 50 PTI (USA). The Nyquist plot (EIS spectra) was obtained using the SP-240 (Bio-Logic Science Instruments, France). The performance of PSCs was carried out using solar irradiation (Polaromix K201, Solar simulator LAB 50). The McScience K3100 measuring equipment was used to assess external quantum efficiency (EQE).

According to previous reports, Li-TFSI has an essential function in the oxidation of spiro-OMeTAD in an ion-doped spiro-OMeTAD structure.16 The effective and evenly spread distribution of Li-TFSI in the HTL improves the oxidation of spiro-OMeTAD, generating the spiro-OMeTAD+ oxidation state, which enhances hole mobility and device efficiency.22,37 Both theoretical and experimental evaluations assessed the reaction between TBP and Cu2O in the HTM solution. The UV–vis absorption spectra of pure liquid TBP and the Cu2O-dissolved TBP solution were obtained in Figure 2b. In contrast to the initial UV–vis absorption patterns of Cu2O and TBP, strong absorption was seen at 467 nm. For a more detailed comparison, the luminous TBP brightened in an ultraviolet (UV) lamp, but the emission vanished when Cu2O was added to the TBP, as shown in Figure 2c. This indicated that Cu2O and TBP interacted to generate an invention compound. Moreover, the FTIR spectra (Figure 2d) of the Cu2O-added TBP and pristine TBP were completely different. The highlight peak appeared at 621 cm–1, which confirms the introduction of Cu2O into TBP.38 Additionally, pyridine ring vibration (C = N bonds) was identified in the range of 1400–1500 cm–1, which typically were found in TBP, and at 1462 cm–1 in this work, transformed to a higher wavenumber of 1585 cm–1 in the TBP–Cu2O compound.39−41 Furthermore, the stretching vibrations of the C–H, C–N, and C–C groups have been identified for the major TBP peaks located at 1390, 1248, and 1065 cm–1, respectively, and were shifted to higher frequencies at 1478, 1393, and 1130 cm–1, respectively. This implied that Cu2O and TBP had a favorable interaction that reduced TBP vaporization and prevented Li-TFSI aggregation in the HTL.

To prevent the pinhole phenomena in the spiro-OMeTAD films, Cu2O was added as a supplement additive to Li-TFSI and TBP. This helped prevent Li-TFSI from aggregating and TBP from evaporating from the solution. To evaluate the effect of Cu2O on the morphological characteristics of the spiro-OMeTAD layer, we then investigated dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements for precursor solutions of HTM and HTM/Cu2O, as shown in Figure S3. As can be seen, the control HTM D displayed two Gaussian distributions for a small size of ∼170 nm and a large size of ∼4550 nm, respectively. In the HTM solution containing Cu2O, the small-size distribution evolved into a distinct single peak. This revealed that adding Cu2O to HTM inhibited the aggregation of components containing HTM precursor.

Figure 3a shows the molecular structure of HTM components, including spiro-OMeTAD, Li-TFSI, TBP, and Cu2O. The top-view SEM of HTLs illustrated in Figure 3b,d and Figure S4, clearly shows that the HTL without added Cu2O presents obvious undissolved residues and bright dots displayed in the purple square and yellow circle, respectively, because of the voids and accumulation and crystalline structure potential of Li-TFSI caused by TBP evaporation.19,26,34 In contrast, the HTL with added Cu2O shows a homogeneous film overlay on the perovskite material, without any pinholes or unsolved residues.

It was assumed that the chemical reaction between Cu2O and TBP produced an ensemble that delayed the dissolution of TBP, which facilitated a more homogeneous allocation of Li-TFSI and reduced the number of pinholes in the spiro-OMeTAD film. As observed from the AFM results in Figure 3c,e, and Figure S5, it is clear that voids are randomly distributed within the HTL without added Cu2O; alternately, the HTL with Cu2O modification exhibits an improvement in the morphology of the HTM surface, that is, a pinhole-free surface with evenly distributed HTM components. Moreover, the corresponding root-mean-square (RMS) of the Cu2O-added HTM also declined from 8.168 nm for the pristine HTM to 7.946 nm, indicating that introducing Cu2O enabled a smoother and flatter surface of the HTL thin film.

Figure 3f illustrates the effect of Cu2O in inhibiting the Li-TFSI aggregation and suppressing the TBP evaporation from the HTMs. Li-TFSI intrinsically has superhygroscopic properties and easily absorbs the water molecules in the form of LiOH.H2O, which accelerates perovskite decomposition.35−37 In contrast, TBP prevents moisture uptake by Li-TFSI under ambient conditions.19,34,37 However, TBP evaporates easily, especially after coating the electrode with the HTL thin film, and leaves voids and pinholes that have a negative impact on hole transport and extraction.19,37 Furthermore, the electrode could come into contact directly with the active layer, resulting in leakage currents that degrade the overall efficiency of the PSCs. Cu2O-added TBP was used to create a new complex to solve these issues and improve the HTL layer with a pinhole-free, dense, and uniform distribution of spiro-OMeTAD. Furthermore, uniform spatial dispersion of the elements present in HTM/Cu2O could be identified. Figure S6 shows the EDS of the HTL thin films. Spiro-OMeTAD has a uniform surface when 0.4 wt % Cu2O is introduced. This suggested that Cu and O were evenly distributed along the spiro-OMeTAD.

To further understand the role of Cu2O in the HTL, HTM solutions were produced with Cu2O doping percentages that changed from 0.2 to 1.0 wt % (Cu2O in TBP). Figure 4a demonstrates how the color of the modified HTM gradually deepened and converted from yellow to light orange as the Cu2O doping concentration in the HTM increased. The UV–vis spectra of the control HTM and HTM/Cu2O are exhibited in Figure 4b. The spiro-OMeTAD+ generation serves as a reflection that HTMs can improve hole mobility.11,23,42 In the UV–vis absorption spectra which range from 450 to 600 nm, HTM/Cu2O-0.4 exhibited the highest absorption at 520 nm, which confirmed the oxidizing ability of Cu2O. Therefore, the 0.4 wt % Cu2O doping concentration is likely optimized for HTM doping Cu2O samples. The contribution of Li-TFSI in the HTM was enhanced by introducing the Cu2O-TBP compound, which promoted the oxidation of spiro-OMeTAD or provided sufficient coordination anions to spiro+, which improved the hole mobility and overall efficiency of the PSCs devices as well.16,37

PSCs often encounter a significant reduction in their PCE % when exposed to humid atmospheres, which is a challenge widely recognized in the field. Li-TFSI is considered an essential additive that helps to improve charge mobility and conductivity by producing more TFSI– anions to spiro+ to achieve the desired performance of PSCs. Nevertheless, the intrinsically hygroscopic nature of Li-TFSI, which rapidly takes in molecules of water, accelerates the deterioration of the perovskite layer. In this study, we found the impact of a TBP-Cu2O compound in an HTM on humidity stability. The devices were then subjected to a high-humidity environment as depicted in Figure 7a. HTM and Cu2O/HTM were spin-coated onto the perovskite films and placed under humid conditions for 0–400 h. They were then encapsulated in aluminum foil to create darkness. As observed in Figure 7b, the film color of the original HTM-based device completely decomposed into light-yellow PbI2. In contrast, the HTM/Cu2O device changed color only slightly after 400 h at room temperature. Figure 7c demonstrates the XRD spectra of the perovskite covered with original HTM and HTM/Cu2O. The XRD analysis has shown these strong diffraction peaks at 13.99°, 19.86°, 24.4°, 26.8°, 28.26°, 31.7°, 33.7°, 37.8°, 40.34°, and 42.84°, which were indexed to the (110), (112), (200), (002), (220), (222), (310), (312), (224), and (314) diffraction of perovskite structure, or α-FAPbI3 in detailed.51−54 The XRD spectra of HTM/Cu2O in both initial and long-term devices exhibit similar findings; the peak of α-FAPbI3 (13.99°) was maintained with a sharp and strong diffraction. Conversely, the original HTM showed a significantly weakened perovskite peak and an apparent PbI2 peak at 12.7°, indicating the partial decomposition of FAPbI3 into PbI2.54 Furthermore, after exposure of 400 h, a peak at 11.8° also appeared in the HTM thin film without Cu2O doping, implying that the hydrated phases of δ-FAPbI3 were degraded of water molecules.53,54 This suggests that the Cu2O-TBP mixture not only enhances the dissolution of Li-TFSI but also forms a tightly bound complex to prevent water molecules from being attracted by the deliquescent and hygroscopic Li-TFSI. The HTM-Cu2O film exhibited a larger angle of 84.5° compared to the pristine HTM, which had a smaller angle of 75.6° in Figure 7d, indicating that the Cu2O-TBP combination formed an equally distributed hydrophobic layer on the surface of the HTM film.

We propose that incorporating Cu2O effectively restricts moisture entry into the perovskite layer, inhibits perovskite degradation, and ultimately enhances the PSC stability. We kept the PSCs in the dark in an air atmosphere at ∼30% RH and room temperature. The HTM/Cu2O-based device greatly delayed PSC decomposition and retained approximately 56.6% of initial PCE after 350 h, whereas the PCE of the control device decreased to 19.6%, according to the stability test findings shown in Figure S10. Therefore, doping the HTM with Cu2O improves the HTL stability and protects the device from moisture degradation.

Conclusions

In this study, the performance of PSCs was enhanced by modulating the Cu2O doping ratio. The interaction between Cu2O and TBP helped to prevent the agglomeration of Li-TFSI, leading to a smoother spiro-OMeTAD surface that effectively blocks water infiltration, protecting the perovskite layer. The optimally doped device showed enhanced conductivity, enhanced carrier separation, and significantly reduced recombination of charges at the perovskite/HTL encounters. By incorporating Cu2O into the HTM, the maximum PCE of the Cu2O/HTM-based device increased to 14.17%, outperforming the original HTM-based device, which had a PCE of 12.25%. Moreover, the PSC with the modified HTL retained 56.6% of its initial PCE value after 350 h, whereas the PCE of the control device decreased to 19.6%. This study presents a novel strategy for fabricating homogeneous hole transport layers (HTLs) with a reduction of pinholes to enhance charge carrier transport, which can greatly boost the performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs).
