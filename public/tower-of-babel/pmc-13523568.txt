Introduction

ST-segment elevation (STE) in the right precordial leads is pathognomonic of Brugada syndrome (BrS); moreover, spontaneous STE and quantitative burden of STE may correlate with arrhythmic risk in BrS.1, 2, 3

Quinidine and its derivatives, including hydroquinidine (HQ), are recommended for the treatment and prevention of ventricular arrhythmia (VA).4, 5, 6 However, the reported effect of these medications on STE has been highly variable with both attenuation and augmentation of STE reported.4,6, 7, 8 Diurnal fluctuations in ST segment may explain these conflicting findings, and 12-lead 24-hour Holter monitoring may overcome the confounding of diurnal variability.3,9 Elucidating the effect of these medications on STE may provide insights into their mechanism of action, as well as provide a potential tool for monitoring drug effects.

Hence, we designed a pilot study to quantify the in vivo effect of HQ on STE and arrhythmia burden using 12-lead 24-hour Holter monitoring.

Methods

Detailed methods are presented in the Supplement. In brief, patients with a confirmed diagnosis of BrS from our clinic were invited to participate if they had no previous exposure to quinidine or HQ. Patients were prescribed 2 weeks of treatment with HQ 300 mg twice daily specifically for the purposes of this pilot study rather than on clinical grounds. Serum HQ levels were determined, and the therapeutic range was defined as 3–6 μmol/L.6

Patients underwent 12-lead 24-hour Holter monitoring at “baseline” (pre-HQ) and “on-treatment” (at the end of the second week of treatment). Precordial leads were placed in the second to fourth intercostal spaces. STE was quantified manually as a continuous parameter at the J point using published criteria1 and averaged over 10-second periods at 5-minute intervals.

Metrics quantifying the burden of STE were computed across 3 domains.3 Temporal burden refers to the mean duration (minutes) demonstrating diagnostic STE (ie, ≥ 2 mm STE with type 1 morphology in ≥ 1 lead) per hour. Spatial burden refers to the number of precordial leads with diagnostic STE per hour, and the global burden is a product of the amplitude of STE (mm), the number of leads, and the duration of STE (minutes). These parameters were analyzed between baseline and on-treatment using a linear model accounting for the correlation within each patient.

Results

Cohort characteristics

10 patients were analyzed: 90% were male, the mean age was 47 ± 12 years, and the mean Shanghai score was 4.4 ± 1.4. 2 patients had an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, and 2 patients had a pathogenic SCN5A variant. Other baseline characteristics are presented in Supplemental Table 1.

Proarrhythmic potential of HQ

1 patient (BEU6) developed repetitive sustained salvos of monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT) on HQ therapy (Figure 4A) that were not observed during baseline monitoring. The VT exhibited left bundle branch block morphology with right inferior axis, suggestive of an origin from the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT). The serum HQ level was 2.87 μmol/L. Notably, this patient also exhibited a marked increase in the mean temporal burden of STE during HQ therapy compared with baseline (Figure 4B and 4C). Discussion

Although quinidine and its derivatives, such as HQ, are used to reduce the risk of VA in BrS, the basis for their clinical effects is not fully understood. In particular, the impact of these medications on STE has been variably reported. Indeed, investigators have reported that quinidine may attenuate, augment, or have no effect on the ST segment.4,6, 7, 8 It is possible that the reported variability in the effects of these medications on the ST segment may relate to the diurnal variability of STE.3,9

To minimize the confounding effect of diurnal variability, 24-hour Holter monitoring was used in this pilot study to quantify the effect of HQ therapy on STE. It showed that HQ was associated with an increased burden of STE compared with baseline. Moreover, the association was observed in patients with therapeutic HQ levels but not in those with subtherapeutic HQ levels. This suggests a degree of dose-responsiveness although more detailed pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiling would be required to confirm this observation.

The augmentation of STE with HQ was an unexpected finding based on existing literature. Using an experimental canine wedge preparation, Yan and Antzelevitch10 demonstrated that the loss of the action potential dome preferentially in the epicardium resulted in transmural dispersion of repolarization, resulting in STE. They also showed that inhibition of transient outward potassium current with quinidine restored the action potential dome and normalized the ST segment. In contrast, Hoogendijk and colleagues11 reported that STE may also result from subepicardial excitation failure or delayed activation by current-to-load mismatch in the RVOT. Nademanee and colleagues12 also showed that sodium-channel (INa) blockade with ajmaline exacerbated STE by compromising impulse conduction in areas of epicardial right ventricular fibrosis, resulting in conduction block, or excitation failure. Hence, it is plausible that HQ may exacerbate STE through a similar mechanism, that is, exacerbation of transmural conduction abnormality in the RVOT through inhibition of INa. However, quinidine has complex actions at the cellular level beyond its effect on transient outward potassium current and INa, with inhibition of the rapid component of the delayed rectifier potassium current, calcium current, and late sodium current also reported.13 Furthermore, quinidine’s complex multichannel effects are further modified by microanatomy, heart rate, and cellular drug concentration.14,15 Hence, the molecular mechanism underpinning the observation of increased STE during HQ therapy remains speculative.

Although spontaneous STE has been associated with increased arrhythmic events,2 the clinical significance of STE during HQ therapy is unclear and warrants further study. Interestingly, sustained VA was observed in 1 patient on HQ therapy. This patient had a previous cardiac arrest and a pathogenic frame-shift variant in SCN5A. Despite an HQ level that was just below the therapeutic range, this patient had a significant increase in temporal STE burden that was among the highest in the cohort. One may postulate that the underlying mechanism for VA was reentry owing to HQ-related conduction slowing in the diseased substrate; however, the exact mechanism could not be determined without invasive investigation. Nevertheless, this case illustrates that the effect of HQ may show significant individual variation with modulation by factors such as age, gender, microstructural disease, and/or genetic background. Moreover, beyond STE, Holter monitoring is useful for identifying or excluding proarrhythmia from HQ.

Limitations

The major limitation was the small sample size, which was because of the few quinidine-naïve patients who agreed to take a short course of HQ for this pilot study. This introduces the possibility of false-positive results. Participants in this study were also given a fixed dose of HQ based on previous clinical studies.4,6 A larger study cohort, with consideration of variable dosing, may shed light on the apparent dose-response relationship between HQ and STE.

Conclusion

HQ was associated with increased burden of STE on 12-lead 24-hour Holter monitoring in this exploratory pilot study. Larger cohorts will be required to confirm whether STE may be used as a marker of drug effect, or perhaps confer an increased risk of VA in some patients. Holter monitoring should also be performed after commencing HQ to exclude proarrhythmia from treatment.
