Neuropsychiatric adverse event reporting with GLP-1-based therapies: a post-marketing pharmacovigilance analysis using the WHO global individual case safety report database (VigiBase)
Authors: Jizhou Liang, Lulong Bo, Xiaoyong Miao, Xiaojing Guo, Jinfang Xu, Jia He, Xiaofei Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveConcerns regarding neuropsychiatric adverse events (NPAEs), including depression and suicidality, have emerged with the expanding use of GLP-1-based therapies across metabolic and weight-management indications. This study aimed to characterize neuropsychiatric adverse-event reporting patterns for GLP-1-based therapies in VigiBase using disproportionality analysis.MethodsIndividual case safety reports entered into the WHO global pharmacovigilance database (VigiBase) from inception through 31 January 2025 were examined. Disproportionality analysis using the information components and the reporting odds ratio was conducted to identify putative safety signals. The full VigiBase reporting background during the study period was used as the comparator. We described the drug-event pairs, mapped signal overlap across agents, evaluated the relationship between reporting frequency and signal magnitude, and conducted stratified analyses by sex and age.ResultsAmong 899 280 GLP-1RAs-related reports, 19 811 (2.2%) related NPAEs comprised 273 preferred terms and generated 69 positive signals. Semaglutide and liraglutide presented the broadest and most potent signal portfolios, driven chiefly by binge eating (semaglutide, IC₀₂₅ = 3.379; ROR₀₂₅ = 14.483), and aversion (liraglutide, IC₀₂₅ = 3.365; ROR₀₂₅ = 20.512). In contrast, albiglutide was essentially signal negative, and dulaglutide and tirzepatide generated narrower spectra. In addition, semaglutide was positively associated with two “suicide/self-injury” AEs: “depression suicidal” (ROR₀₂₅ = 4.822) and “suicidal ideation” (ROR₀₂₅ = 2.643).ConclusionsThis study identified several potential drug safety signals. However, the estimates were unadjusted and may be influenced by confounding by indication, channelling, notoriety bias, and event competition. Eating-related signals should be interpreted cautiously because they may overlap with the treated population and appetite-related pharmacology of these agents. Given the spontaneous reporting nature of VigiBase, these findings should be interpreted as potential reporting signals rather than evidence of causality. Further well-designed observational studies and prospective clinical investigations are needed to clarify whether GLP-1-based therapies are associated with increased neuropsychiatric risk and to identify patient-level risk modifiers.

Introduction

Propelled by population aging and lifestyle transitions, the global incidence of diabetes is increasing, burdening individuals and placing a formidable economic strain on health-care systems [1]. GLP-1RAs are being deployed with increasing frequency for glycemic control in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [2]. By mimicking the physiological activity of endogenous GLP-1, these agents engage its receptor to increase postprandial insulin secretion while curbing glucagon release, thereby mitigating hyperglycemia [3, 4]. In addition, GLP-1RAs reproduce the ancillary metabolic actions of hormones—most notably, they delay gastric emptying, increase satiety, and sustain weight reduction [5, 6]. The US FDA has authorized seven such agents (albiglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide, liraglutide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide) for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus [7, 8]. Compared with conventional antidiabetic therapies, this drug class not only delivers superior glycemic control but also induces clinically meaningful weight loss in many patients with obesity-related T2DM. In addition to their glycemic and weight-lowering properties, GLP-1RAs have been shown to reduce the incidence of major cardiovascular and renal events, thereby conferring cardioprotective and renoprotective benefits [4, 9, 10].

Although GLP-1RAs constitute a highly effective treatment modality for type 2 diabetes mellitus, their expanding clinical uptake has been paralleled by an increase in spontaneous adverse event reports, warranting intensified pharmacovigilance by researchers and prescribers alike. Available evidence indicates that the predominant safety signals involve gastrointestinal disturbances, most commonly nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea [11–13]. In particular, recent investigations have suggested the potential impact of GLP-1RAs on mental health [14, 15].

Neuropsychiatric adverse events have also attracted increasing attention in relation to GLP-1-based therapies. Reported concerns include mood-related symptoms, anxiety, sleep disturbances, eating-related events, and suicidality-related reports. However, the available evidence remains inconsistent, and regulatory reviews have not established a clear causal relationship between GLP-1-based therapies and suicidality. Because patients receiving these agents for diabetes or weight management may differ substantially in baseline psychiatric comorbidity, metabolic status, and reporting likelihood, neuropsychiatric signals from spontaneous-reporting systems require cautious interpretation.

Although regulatory reviews and prior pharmacovigilance studies using FAERS and EudraVigilance have examined psychiatric and suicidality-related reports associated with GLP-1-based therapies, evidence from VigiBase remains limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to describe global reporting patterns of neuropsychiatric adverse events associated with GLP-1-based therapies in VigiBase, with emphasis on hypothesis generation rather than causal inference.

Patients and methods

Data sources

Data for this retrospective pharmacovigilance study were extracted from VigiBase, one of the world’s largest adverse event repositories, curated by the Uppsala Monitoring Centre of the WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring [16]. VigiBase is supported by contributions from the 150 national pharmacovigilance centers engaged in the WHO Program for International Drug Monitoring [17], and has accumulated more than 28 million individual case safety reports since its establishment, covering more than 99% of the world’s population [18, 19]. This unmatched geographic scope and sample size provide a strong basis for identifying emergent adverse drug reaction signals.

Study design

Compared with previous studies that primarily focused on selected agents, selected adverse events, or individual spontaneous-reporting databases, the present study provides a broad descriptive assessment of neuropsychiatric adverse-event reporting with multiple GLP-1-based therapies using VigiBase, a global pharmacovigilance database. By examining signals at both the SOC and PT levels and describing reporting patterns across demographic strata, this study aims to generate hypotheses regarding potential neuropsychiatric reporting patterns that warrant further evaluation in controlled pharmacoepidemiologic studies.

We searched the whole VigiBase corpus, including every individual case safety report from the start of the database through January 2025 and this study was based on disproportionality analysis of all eligible reports available during the study period. Exposure to GLP-1RAs was ascertained by cross-referencing proprietary and international nonproprietary names, capturing the following products: Byetta (exenatide), Bydureon (exenatide), Victoza (liraglutide), Saxenda (liraglutide), Lyxumia (lixisenatide), Eperzan [EU]/Tanzeum [US] (albiglutide), Trulicity (dulaglutide), Ozempic (semaglutide), Rybelsus (semaglutide), Wegovy (semaglutide), and tirzepatide. Medicinal products and adverse events were harmonized via the WHO Drug Dictionary and MedDRA 27.0, respectively [20–22]. Within the MedDRA hierarchy, analyses concentrated on two tiers: System Organ Class (SOC) and Preferred Term (PT). Whereas the PT level provides finer granularity by capturing individual symptoms or diagnostic entities, the SOC level offers a macroscopic perspective of the anatomical or physiological distribution of reactions. Neuropsychiatric adverse events were identified at the MedDRA preferred-term level within the psychiatric disorders system organ class and through the suicide/self-injury SMQ. Because the analysis was exploratory, no pre-specified list of individual PTs was used for the primary screening. All eligible PTs meeting the case-count threshold were screened, and resulting signals were interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

To define the exposure set, we included reports in which a GLP-1-based therapy was recorded as the primary suspected, suspected, or interacting drug in VigiBase [23]. The classification of GLP-1RAs as primary/suspected drugs and the identification of preferred terms indicating underlying conditions were based on the structured information provided in the VigiBase dataset.

This disproportionality analysis was reported with consideration of key READUS-PV principles for transparent reporting of spontaneous-reporting database studies, including clarification of the data source, case definition, unit of analysis, comparator background, coding terminology, and signal-detection thresholds.

Data extraction and cleaning

Data extraction and cleaning were performed in a stepwise manner. First, all VigiBase individual case safety reports involving the selected GLP-1-based therapies during the study period were retrieved using generic and brand names. Second, reports in which the study drug was recorded as a suspected, primary suspected, or interacting drug were retained to define the exposure dataset. Third, adverse events were mapped to MedDRA system organ classes and preferred terms, and reports involving neuropsychiatric adverse events were identified according to the predefined MedDRA classification. Fourth, reports with incomplete key information required for disproportionality analysis were excluded where applicable. The final analytical dataset was then used for SOC-level and PT-level disproportionality analyses.

Disproportional analysis and subgroup analysis

VigiBase was then subjected to disproportionality analysis to identify putative safety signals. The full VigiBase reporting background during the study period was used as the comparator for disproportionality analysis. The unit of analysis was the individual case safety report unless otherwise specified. Total GLP-1-based therapy reports, neuropsychiatric adverse-event reports, and unique patients were reported separately to avoid conflating report-level and patient-level denominators.

Two complementary metrics approved by the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre were used: the reporting odds ratio (ROR) and the information component (IC) [24, 25]. Both statistics quantify whether a given drug‒event pair is reported more frequently than expected against the database background; an excess of reports yields an inflated ROR and a positive IC, thus flagging a potential signal [26]. We also conducted a descriptive analysis of the baseline data included in the reports covering demographic features (sex, age), adverse event characteristics (seriousness, clinical outcome), and report-level variables (reporting country, year). Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations; categorical variables were summed as frequencies and percentages.

Results

Clinical baseline characteristics

VigiBase maintained 899 280 individual case‒safety reports (ICSRs) with a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist listed as the main suspect drug as of 1 January 2025. Among these, 19 811 ICSRs (2.20%) related to psychiatric conditions, comprising 15,338 unique patients, were included. Seven GLP-1RAs: albiglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide, liraglutide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide, were linked to 273 different adverse events within these psychiatric ICSRs, generating 867 unique drug‒event combinations.

Table 1 summarizes the demographic features of the matched ICSRs. With just 146 and 20 cases, respectively, the reported volumes for lixisenatide and albiglutide were lower by orders of magnitude than those of the other five agents. With the exception of lixisenatide, all GLP-1RAs presented a clear female predominance, with female-to-male ratios ranging from approximately 2:1–3:1. For almost all the compounds, demographic profiling revealed that the 45–65 year age range accounted for the highest share of psychiatric ICSRs.

Disproportionality analysis

Screening with the ROR₀₂₅ threshold revealed 69 positive drug-adverse-event signals; application of the IC₀₂₅ criterion revealed 61 such signals (Fig. 2). Albiglutide produced the fewest signals; semaglutide accounted for the highest count. Semaglutide accounted for eight of the fifteen drug-adverse-reaction pairs with the most marked composite signal strength, followed by liraglutide with four. The drug–event pair “liraglutide-aversion” (ROR₀₂₅ = 20.512; IC₀₂₅ = 3.365) and drug–event pair “semaglutide-binge eating” pair (ROR₀₂₅ = 14.483; IC₀₂₅ = 3.379) were associated with the highest overall signal strength (Table 2).

Notably, visible and relatively strong disproportionality signals were observed for semaglutide in relation to depression, suicidal depression, near-death experience, intrusive thoughts, and self-injurious reaction.

We then extracted preferred terms nested under the SMQ “Suicide/self-injury” (version 27.0; SOC: Psychiatric disorders) to interrogate the psychiatric risk profile of GLP-1RAs. Five drug–event pairs met the predefined signal thresholds: liraglutide-depression suicidal (ROR₀₂₅ = 1.199; IC₀₂₅ = 0.015), liraglutide-suicidal ideation (ROR₀₂₅ = 1.039; IC₀₂₅ = 0.043), semaglutide-depression suicidal (ROR₀₂₅ = 4.822; IC₀₂₅ = 2.066), semaglutide-self injurious ideation (ROR₀₂₅ = 1.760; IC₀₂₅ = 0.635), and semaglutide-suicidal ideation (ROR₀₂₅ = 2.643; IC₀₂₅ = 1.385). Although spontaneous reports involving other GLP-1RAs were present in the database, none of the reports satisfied the criteria for a positive signal.

Discussion

This study provides a descriptive assessment of neuropsychiatric adverse-event reporting patterns associated with GLP-1-based therapies using the most recent data from the VigiBase database. Although GLP-1RAs have been widely studied for their metabolic benefits in diabetes and obesity, evidence regarding their neuropsychiatric safety profile remains limited, particularly from global pharmacovigilance databases.

In the demographic data of adverse event reports, we found two notable patterns: one is a minor peak in the number of reports in 2008, and the other is a continuous surge in new reports beginning in 2023. Exenatide was approved by the U.S. as the first GLP-1RA in 2008, and the FDA signaled the start of clinical use for this class of drugs. In the early years of drug market introduction, there was an increase in adverse event reports as a result of extensive clinical use among a larger patient population.

The rapid increase in the number of reports from 2023 greatly exceeded the variations observed in the previous decade, with semaglutide and tirzepatide mostly responsible for this trend. This trend may reflect the increasing acceptance of GLP-1RAs in recent years, as well as extensive advertising and social media exposure that have resulted in off-label use, greatly expanding the prescription base for GLP-1RAs and generating more related adverse event reports. Stefania Chiappini et al., in a study of the FAERS database, reported that semaglutide was associated with ‘drug abuse’, ‘drug withdrawal syndrome’ and ‘prescription drugs used without a prescription’ [27]. Rie Oyama et al. reported that approximately 83% of Japanese healthcare websites exaggerated the efficacy of GLP-1RA, which may lead consumers to overestimate the effects of the medication, especially in its use for weight loss [28]. Besides, the higher number of neuropsychiatric adverse event reports among females and individuals aged 45–65 years may partly reflect the greater background susceptibility to neuropsychiatric conditions in females, the common occurrence of diabetes in this age group, and prescribing preferences for GLP-1RAs in adult populations with more extensive randomized controlled trial evidence. Although more reports were submitted for female patients than for male patients, this descriptive predominance should not be interpreted as evidence of greater female susceptibility. No formal drug-by-sex interaction testing was performed, and subgroup analyses were descriptive and exploratory. Therefore, apparent sex-related differences in disproportionality estimates should be interpreted cautiously.

A total of 69 positive signals linked to psychiatric adverse events associated with GLP-1RAs were found in a disproportionate analysis of seven drugs. Because a large number of drug–event pairs were screened, multiplicity may have contributed to some positive findings, particularly for signals with lower confidence bounds only marginally above the null threshold. Therefore, weak signals, including suicidality-related signals with ROR₀₂₅ values close to 1, should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in further controlled studies [29]. Semaglutide, liraglutide, and exenatide were the drugs with the most signals in that sequence. Stress, eating disorders, and binge eating were the most often mentioned negative experiences. The FDA labels for several GLP-1RAs indicate that these drugs cause psychiatric AEs, including “insomnia”, “anxiety”, and “feeling jittery”. These signals were also found in our study, confirming the validity of our analytical approach.

One of the causes of increased anxiety about the risk of adverse psychiatric reactions associated with GLP-1RAs is the occurrence of negative events connected to suicide or self-harm. Notwithstanding the analysis of data on the risk of suicidal thoughts linked with GLP-1RAs for the treatment of type 2 diabetes and weight control by the Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee (PRAC) of the European Medicines Agency and the United States, the Food and Drug Administration concluded that “the information in these reports did not demonstrate a clear relationship with the use of GLP-1RAs” [30, 31]. Researchers continue to express concern regarding the risk of psychiatric adverse reactions related to GLP-1RAs, and a definite conclusion remains elusive. William Wang et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study in which the TriNetX Analytics Network contrasted with reports indicating an elevated risk of suicidal ideation associated with semaglutide. Roger S. McIntyre et al. [32] conducted a disproportionality analysis via the FAERS database and identified positive signals for “suicidal ideation” and “depression/suicidal” associated with semaglutide and liraglutide [33]. Conversely, a large community-based cohort study conducted by Edy Kornelius et al. challenged these findings, indicating an association between GLP-1RAs treatment and a 98% increase in the risk of any psychiatric disorder [15].

Consistent with existing research, we also identified reports of suicide-related adverse events associated with GLP-1RAs in the database. Only semaglutide and liraglutide showed positive signals for particular PTs classified under the “Suicide/self-injury” SMQ, according to further investigation; the other GLP-1RAs did not show such correlations. However, suicidality-related signals, particularly those involving semaglutide, should be interpreted with caution because they may be susceptible to notoriety bias. Regulatory reviews and extensive media or social-media attention during 2023–2024 may have increased the reporting of suicidality-related events, thereby influencing disproportionality estimates in spontaneous reporting data [34].

The adverse reaction profiles of GLP-1RAs show notable variability, as the IC₀₂₅ heatmap shows. The heatmap revealed that semaglutide and liraglutide presented both the greatest number and the strongest intensity of signal [34]. In contrast, albiglutide shows almost no signal, whereas dulaglutide, exenatide, and tirzepatide show rather smaller spectra of adverse effects. Within liraglutide-associated reports, “aversion” (ROR₀₂₅ = 20.512, IC₀₂₅ = 3.365) and “binge eating” (ROR₀₂₅ = 10.260, IC₀₂₅ = 2.873), were among the strongest disproportionality signals observed. In contrast, semaglutide exhibited notable signals for “fear of eating” (ROR₀₂₅ = 10.583, IC₀₂₅ = 2.094) and “bulimia nervosa” (ROR₀₂₅ = 7.255, IC₀₂₅ = 2.113).However, eating-related signals should be interpreted with caution because they may reflect confounding by indication, channelling to weight-loss populations, baseline disordered eating, and the appetite-suppressing pharmacology of GLP-1-based therapies, rather than independent evidence of drug-induced neuropsychiatric harm. Because diabetes and obesity are closely associated with depression and other neuropsychiatric conditions, including insomnia, anxiety, and eating disorders, and because withdrawal of GLP-1RAs may contribute to binge eating through loss of appetite-suppressing effects, these factors should be considered when interpreting neuropsychiatric adverse event signals in GLP-1RA-treated populations [35]. Although several biological mechanisms have been proposed to link GLP-1 signalling with central reward, mood, and appetite pathways, the present disproportionality analysis cannot determine whether these mechanisms explain the observed reporting signals. Therefore, mechanistic interpretations should be considered speculative and hypothesis-generating [36].

Consistent signals for suicide and binge eating have been noted in both the EudraVigilance and FAERS databases [14, 37], future studies should concentrate on evaluating the effects of GLP-1RAs on the nervous system and their possible consequences for psychiatric diseases [38]. Among the 15 strongest drug‒event signal pairs examined, 11 included semaglutide or liraglutide. For these two measures, liraglutide-aversion (ROR₀₂₅ = 20.512) and semaglutide-binge eating (IC₀₂₅ = 3.379) ranked highest.The primary psychiatric side effects of GLP-1RAs include stress or depression symptoms and eating-related problems. Pairwise overlap studies and UpSet plots revealed that GLP-1RAs share a common set of psychiatric adverse events. In particular, binge eating is the most common, with 83% of drugs reporting this adverse effect (ROR₀₂₅ range: 0.270–3.379). Following binge eating, “eating disorder” and “stress” were the most often mentioned problems; positive signals were found across the four different drugs. Among all the medications, semaglutide showed the best signal overlap with other similar drugs; albiglutide showed the least overlap [39].

We examined the disproportionality between the absolute number of adverse events recorded for several drugs. Our results revealed that a high frequency of AEs does not always equate to high risk: for instance, insomnia dominated the AE reports across all drugs but was not considered a positive signal (maximum ROR₀₂₅ ≈ 0.93). In contrast, exenatide stress and semaglutide-depressed mood/suicidal ideation showed strong disproportionality despite having moderate reporting counts. On the basis of the Japanese JADER and American FAERS databases, similar “frequency-signal decoupling” events were found in disproportionate analyses of drugs lowering blood glucose. This means that the number of AE reports is not necessarily related to the strength of the signal [40]. Therefore, we advise pharmacovigilance assessments and daily clinical decision-making to prioritize quantitative disproportionality indicators over raw counts when safety measures are prioritized since the number of AE reports is not always related to the strength of the signals. Furthermore, deserving particular attention are rare but significant events such as suicide.

Limitations

Focusing on the identification of particular psychiatric safety signals related to GLP-1RAs, this study used the VigiBase database for pharmacovigilance evaluation. Importantly, however, spontaneous reporting databases have certain natural limits. First, data completeness and repetitiveness raise questions. Safety reports for individual cases sometimes lack important factors, including exact age, latency, daily dosage, and psychiatric history; moreover, there may be cases of repeated report submission. The analysis was based on VigiBase data as provided by the Uppsala Monitoring Centre, in which duplicate detection and management are conducted using UMC procedures.Meanwhile, information on reporter type, including the proportions of reports submitted by physicians and patients, the onset time of neuropsychiatric adverse events, actions taken after event occurrence, and subsequent clinical outcomes was not available in the database used for the present analysis; therefore, these relevant aspects could not be further evaluated in this study. Baseline psychological status before initiation of GLP-1RA therapy may represent an important modifying factor for subsequent neuropsychiatric adverse events; however, this variable could not be evaluated in the present pharmacovigilance analysis. Second, the reports themselves carry reporting bias by nature. Variations in media coverage, under- or overreporting, inadequate reporting of minor events, and other factors can skew the actual characteristics of adverse events. Although these biases cannot be completely eliminated, our dual-indicator approach (IC and ROR) can help reduce them. There is also interference from indications and comedication. Obesity and diabetes are inherently prone to mood disorders, and concomitant treatments (e.g., antidepressants, opioids) may mask or exacerbate neuropsychiatric manifestations. We limited the analysis to records where GLP-1RAs were classified as “primary/suspected drug” and excluded preferred terms that clearly indicated underlying conditions to address this cause of error. The use of the full VigiBase database as the comparator may introduce competition or masking effects. Because GLP-1-based therapies are strongly associated with gastrointestinal adverse-event reporting, the predominance of gastrointestinal preferred terms may affect the relative reporting of less frequent neuropsychiatric events. No active-comparator analysis using other antidiabetic or anti-obesity agents and no masking-ratio or competition-bias sensitivity analysis was performed. Therefore, the observed neuropsychiatric disproportionality signals should be interpreted cautiously, the findings should be interpreted as potential safety signals rather than evidence of causality. The lack of active surveillance, incomplete clinical information, and possible underreporting or reporting bias should be considered when interpreting these results. Whereas establishing causal relationships, the proportional reporting ratio measures statistical correlations. The absence of thorough prescription data makes it difficult for us to estimate incidence rates precisely. Finally, although we restricted the exposure definition to reports in which the GLP-1-based therapy was recorded as a primary suspected, suspected, or interacting drug, co-reported medications and comorbidities may still have influenced the observed disproportionality estimates. Identification of the features of particular risks related to polypharmacy depends on future attempts to combine electronic medical records with prescription sequences. Postmarketing signal detection is crucial despite these restrictions for spotting rare or late-onset side effects. Given the spontaneous reporting nature of VigiBase, these findings should be interpreted as potential reporting signals rather than evidence of causality. Further well-designed observational studies and prospective clinical investigations are needed to clarify whether GLP-1-based therapies are associated with increased neuropsychiatric risk and to identify patient-level risk modifiers. We did not perform a time-to-onset analysis or Weibull shape-parameter estimation; therefore, this study cannot characterize whether neuropsychiatric adverse-event reports occurred predominantly as early- or late-onset events after treatment initiation. In addition, no formal sensitivity analyses were conducted to address notoriety bias, the Weber effect, reporter type, country-level reporting patterns, or competition/masking effects. In particular, we did not exclude reports from the 2023–2024 period or stratify the analysis before and after regulatory or media attention concerning suicidality. These limitations may have influenced the observed disproportionality estimates, especially for semaglutide-related suicidality reports.

Conclusions

Reports of neuropsychiatric adverse events are increasing as GLP-1RAs are increasingly accepted and used; moreover, there are growing worries about severe events, including depression and suicidality. Using the VigiBase database, we developed a safety profile of the neuropsychiatric effects of presently available GLP-1RAs in this postmarketing pharmacovigilance study.

This study should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating disproportionality analysis based on spontaneous reporting data. The detected signals reflect disproportionate reporting patterns and do not establish incidence, absolute risk, relative risk, or causal relationships between GLP-1RA exposure and neuropsychiatric adverse events.

Given the spontaneous reporting nature of VigiBase, these findings should be interpreted as potential reporting signals rather than evidence of causality. Further well-designed observational studies and prospective clinical investigations are needed to clarify whether GLP-1-based therapies are associated with increased neuropsychiatric risk and to identify patient-level risk modifiers.
