Congenital Heart Disease in Sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Miguel Vicente, Manuela Silva Lopes, António Pires
Published: 2026

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common congenital anomaly worldwide. Yet, in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), care is not defined by innovation, but by deep structural limitations. Most children with CHD in low- and middle-income countries lack access to adequate treatment.1,2 This disparity represents one of the most severe inequities in global cardiovascular medicine and demands immediate attention.

The problem begins with infrastructure. SSA accounts for only a minimal fraction of global cardiothoracic surgical capacity, with many countries lacking a comprehensive CHD center.3 Hemodynamic laboratories, advanced imaging, and pediatric intensive care units are inconsistent or nonexistent.4 In this setting, care is not merely suboptimal, but often simply inaccessible.

A profound shortage of professionals exacerbates the situation. Pediatric cardiologists and cardiothoracic surgeons are rare; some regions have fewer than 1 specialist per million inhabitants.5 Isolated incremental training cannot solve this. Instead, scalable solutions are needed: task sharing with nonmedical professionals, regional training centers, and integration of advanced practice providers into diagnostic pathways.

Diagnosis itself remains a critical failure point. In high-income countries, CHD is detected early through prenatal screening or postnatal evaluation. In SSA, diagnosis comes late, primarily dependent on clinical auscultation. Echocardiography is limited, and advanced modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging, are almost nonexistent.4 Consequently, many patients present outside the ideal therapeutic window.

The cost of care creates additional barriers. Surgery and follow-up frequently exceed family capacity, leading to catastrophic out-of-pocket expenses or a complete lack of treatment. Care often relies on temporary, governmental, or nongovernmental missions. Although these provide immediate relief, they are episodic, depend on external resources, and rarely empower local professionals. Sustainable systems require continuous projects integrated into the local health infrastructure.2

Reliable data are scarce. CHD epidemiology in SSA is based on small, fragmented cohorts.4 Without national or regional registries, the disease burden is underestimated, outcomes are poorly monitored, and policies remain reactive. Strategic planning without data is speculation. Furthermore, although maternal infections, malnutrition, and other determinants contribute to CHD, preventive strategies remain poorly integrated.2

Technology offers hope but also risks. Artificial intelligence and digital cardiology can fill diagnostic gaps, but in SSA, they are hampered by limited infrastructure and the absence of representative databases. Without contextualization, innovation may inadvertently exacerbate inequalities.

In addition, the growing population of adults with repaired or unrepaired CHD represents a silent burden of unmet needs. As more children survive, structured programs for adults remain scarce.3 Ignoring this transition risks replacing pediatric mortality with chronic morbidity.

The solution requires a radical shift: from episodic to systemic care. Regional centers of excellence must concentrate resources and knowledge. Workforce expansion must prioritize scalable models and regional training networks. Diagnostic pathways should leverage portable cost-effective technologies. Digital records will enable real-time data collection. Finally, sustainable public-private financing models are indispensable to reduce financial toxicity and ensure equitable access.

The global cardiovascular community now faces a critical decision: to treat CHD in SSA as a peripheral concern or to recognize it as a central test of equity and global responsibility. Ignoring this gap is not an option. How we respond will define the world's commitment to health justice.
