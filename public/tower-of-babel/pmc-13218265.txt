Molecularly self-assembled nanoplatforms for targeted ROS-scavenging and neuroinflammation alleviation in acute ischemic stroke
Authors: Xianru Bi, Meng He, Wenxuan Cao, Li Xiang, Dong Liu, Liangbo Sun, Jiqin Lian, Xia Li, Lu Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury (CIRI) poses a pivotal barrier to neuronal survival after successful recanalization in acute ischemic stroke. This phenomenon involves multiplexed pathophysiology where oxidative stress-neuroinflammation cascades critically drive neuronal death. Although antioxidative and anti-inflammatory agents hold therapeutic promise, their clinical translation is impeded by insufficient blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration and the challenge of simultaneously regulating multiple pathological pathways. To address these challenges, we developed a nano therapy integrating traditional Chinese and Western medicine via carrier-free self-assembly of the natural flavonoid baicalein (a Scutellaria baicalensis-derived radical scavenger) and the pharmaceutical rapamycin (an immunosuppressant). This binary nanodrug system achieved high co-loading efficiency via non-covalent interactions. It was further functionalized with DSPE-PEG2k-Angiopep-2 to enhance colloidal stability, significantly improve BBB penetration, and enhance neuron-targeting capability. The optimized nanoplatform demonstrated potent synergistic neuroprotection through dual-pathway modulation, simultaneously scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inhibiting inflammation. This approach facilitated targeted drug delivery to ischemic brain lesions, significantly reducing infarct area compared to monotherapies. By effectively integrating traditional Chinese medicine botanicals with Western medicine via molecular nano-engineering, this self-assembled nanoplatform presents a clinically viable strategy for precision intervention in CIRI, successfully bridging Eastern and Western therapeutic paradigms.Keywords: Cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, Self-assembled nanodrugs, Oxidative stress, microglia remodeling, Eastern-western fusion nanoplatform

Introduction

Globally, stroke is a primary contributor to long-term disability and the second most common cause of death [[1], [2], [3]]. Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is the most prevalent, constituting roughly 76 % of total stroke cases [4,5]. Currently, mechanical thrombectomy or intravenous thrombolytic medication administration are the two clinical treatments for AIS [6]. However, almost 40 % of stroke survivors are still disabled even after vascular recanalization, primarily as a result of ischemia-reperfusion injury during reperfusion. [7,8]. Although the mechanisms underlying cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury are not yet fully elucidated, preclinical research indicate that it constitutes a complex pathological process involving multiple pathways, ultimately leading to neuronal damage [[9], [10], [11]]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and develop innovative therapeutic approaches to address cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury (CIRI).

CIRI involves multiple mechanisms, including energy metabolism disruption, excitotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and apoptosis [12,13]. Among these, growing evidence indicates that excessive oxidative stress critically contributes to disease progression [14]. Specifically, due to the overgeneration of ROS and insufficient ROS clearance, oxidative stress exacerbates injury by directly inducing cellular damage and amplifying neuroinflammation cascades [15,16]. As a result, this process ultimately leads to apoptosis, resulting in irreversible neuronal loss and permanent neurological deficits [9]. To mitigate this damage and improve outcomes, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agents have demonstrated efficacy in stroke models and are now undergoing clinical evaluation [17,18]. Significantly, the key pathological processes can be directly targeted by a multitarget neuroprotective strategy combining antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, as exemplified by Edaravone and Borneol Sublingual Tablets [19]. However, conventional drug delivery systems struggle to overcome the BBB, achieve sufficient bioavailability at ischemic sites, and simultaneously regulate multiple pathological cascades in vivo [20,21]. Thus, it is crucial to develop effective strategies to overcome these obstacles and enhance the effectiveness of drug delivery for CIRI targeted therapy.

In recent years, nanoparticle-based co-delivery platforms have emerged as promising strategy for CIRI targeted therapy [22,23]. By encapsulating both anti-inflammatory compounds and ROS scavengers within functionalized nanocarriers, these systems can enhance lesion-specific accumulation and modulate the ROS-inflammation amplification loop [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. Nevertheless, the progression of the conventional nanocarriers toward clinical application is hampered by considerable hurdles including intricate multi-step fabrication processes requiring harsh solvents/surfactants [29] and suboptimal drug loading capacities (typically <20 wt%) [30]. To address these challenges, carrier-free self-assembled nanodrugs have recently gained prominence [31,32]. These carrier-free systems spontaneously self-assemble into stable nanostructures through non-covalent interactions, eliminating exogenous carriers while achieving ultrahigh drug loading (>70 wt%) with simplified one-step fabrication under aqueous conditions [33,34]. Critically, these intrinsic properties enable precise control over therapeutic agent delivery at ischemic lesions while minimizing systemic toxicity [34]. This unique combination of self-organization capability and high therapeutic payload makes carrier-free nanodrugs promising multi-target therapeutics for CIRI.

Addressing these limitations through an integrated traditional Chinese and western medicine approach, we innovatively designed carrier-free binary nanoparticles via molecular self-assembly. The platform synergistically combines baicalein (BLN), a Scutellaria baicalensis-derived radical scavenger, with rapamycin (RAP), a mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)-targeting immunosuppressant, thereby coordinating the modulation of both oxidative stress and neuroinflammation cascades (Scheme 1). To augment biostability, pharmacokinetics, and blood-brain barrier penetration, Angiopep-2-functionalized DSPE-PEG2k were anchored onto nanoparticle surfaces, yielding targeted nanocomposites (termed ABR NPs). These engineered particles achieved high drug loading (about 77.01 % w/w total; BLN: 16.50 %, RAP: 60.51 %) while exhibiting enhanced brain targeting and synergistic neuroprotection. The ABR NPs platform thus represents a novel traditional Chinese-West integrative strategy, where botanical and pharmaceutical chemical co-assemble into targeted nanotherapeutics that simultaneously quench free radicals and suppress inflammatory cascades. Collectively, this East-West convergent nanoplatform presents a clinically viable strategy for precision therapy against CIRI.

Nanoparticle synthesis and characterization

BLN/RAP self-assembled nanoparticles (BR NPs) were created using a nano-precipitation technique. Following dissolution in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (20 mg/mL, each), BLN and RAP were formulated into a series of mixtures at molar ratios of 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, and 1:3, then were added dropwise to deionized water and subjected to a 2-min probe sonication to promote optimal self-assembly. The optimal BR NPs (BLN: RAP = 1:1) were prepared by the nano-precipitation method. Next, under vigorous stirring (1500 rpm, 2 min), amphiphilic polymers containing DSPE-PEG2k-Angopep-2 (10 % by weight) and DSPE-PEG2k (15 % by weight) were added to BR NPs to obtain PEGylated targeted modified nanoparticles, named ABR NPs. To synthesis PEG NPs, only DSPE-PEG2k (25 % by weight) was added to the optimal BR NPs solution with vigorously stirring (1500 rpm, 2 min).

BR NPs, PEG NPs, and ABR NPs were characterized by Nano ZS90 (Malvern) for the hydrodynamic size, polydispersity index (PDI), along with zeta potential. Morphological evaluation of BR NPs and ABR NPs was conducted through transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL JEM-1400plus, Japan).

The EE and LE of BLN and RAP were measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an LC-20AB system (Shimadzu Co., Japan). Chromatographic separation was performed on a C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) with a mobile phase consisting of methanol and water at a volume ratio of 90:10, and the flow rate was 0.8 mL/min. Molecular dynamics simulations of nanoparticles formation

Materials Studio 2023 was applied to investigate the BLN and RAP molecular dynamics simulations. The PubChem compound database provided the three-dimensional molecular structure of BLN (CID: 5281605). GaussView software was used to draw the three-dimensional molecular structure of RAP (CID: 5284616), which was then optimized for molecular mechanics using Materials Studio. Both the molecular dynamics method and the quantum chemistry method can be used to determine the binding conformation. Finally, the molecular interactions between BLN and RAP were calculated using Materials Studio under the COMPASSIII forcefield.

Stability assay

The stability of BR NPs, PEG NPs, and ABR NPs (60 μg/mL BLN equivalent) was evaluated by 30-min incubation in water or phosphate buffer saline (PBS) (pH 7.4). Moreover, to further explore the stability of nanoparticles in a simulated physiological environment, ABR NPs were incubated in PBS (pH 7.4), PBS with 10 % FBS or 50% mouse serum for 12 h at 37 °C. Post-incubation, the size and PDI of ABR NPs were characterized by a Zetasizer.

The ROS scavenging property of ABR NPs

DPPH radicals scavenging property

Briefly, 0.2 mM DPPH was mixed with ABR NPs at a 1:1 vol ratio, resulting in final ABR NPs concentrations at 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL. After incubation at room temperature protected from light for a specified time, the mixture was subsequently analyzed for its absorbance at 517 nm on NanoDrop One.

ABTS radicals scavenging property

Briefly, the reaction system was generated by mixing 7.4 mM ABTS and 2.6 mM ammonium persulfate at a 1:1 vol ratio, incubated at 4 °C protected from light for 12 h, and then appropriately diluted to prepare the ABTS working solution, which was then mixed with ABR NPs at a 1:1 vol ratio to achieve final ABR NPs concentrations of 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature, subsequently analyzed for its absorbance at 734 nm on NanoDrop One.

H2O2 scavenging property

In brief, ABR NPs at different concentrations (0, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL) were incubated with 1 mM H2O2 in the dark for 24 h. Subsequently, an equal volume of 20 mM ammonium molybdate solution (prepared in 0.25 M sulfuric acid) was added to the above mixture, followed by incubation at room temperature for 10 min. Finally, the characteristic absorbance at 405 nm was measured via a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Hydroxyl radicals scavenging property

Briefly, hydroxyl radicals (•OH) were generated via Fenton reaction between 1.8 mM FeCl2 and 5 mM H2O2 for 10 min. The resulting •OH solution was then mixed with various ABR NPs concentrations at 0, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL for 30 min. TMB substrate was then added to the reaction mixtures and a further 10 min incubation. The mixture was subsequently analyzed for its absorbance at 652 nm on NanoDrop One.

Hemolysis assay

Mouse blood was collected using heparin sodium anticoagulant tubes and allowed to stand overnight at 4 °C. After centrifuging the samples for 5 min at 3000 rpm, the red blood cells were collected, washed and then diluted with saline to prepare a 2 % (v/v) RBC suspension. On the one hand, equal volumes of the red blood cells suspension were mixed with saline solutions of BLN, RAP, BR NPs, PEG NPs, and ABR NPs at equal concentrations. On the other hand, the red blood cell suspension was mixed at a 1:1 vol ratio with ABR NPs of the concentrations at 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL. The positive control was deionized water, while the negative control was saline. The samples were centrifuged and taken pictures after 2 h incubating at 37 °C. Finally, the absorbance at 540 nm was then analyzed after the supernatant was moved to a 96-well plate. The hemolysis rate of each group was then calculated.

Cell culture

PC12 cells, bEnd.3 cells, and BV2 murine microglia were bought from American Type Culture Collection (USA). The above cells were maintained in standard conditions (37 °C, 5 % CO2) in DMEM with 10 % FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 μg/mL).

Cytotoxicity assay

Cells (PC12, BV2, and bEnd.3) were cultured overnight in a 96-well plate. After 24 h of incubation with different concentrations of ABR NPs (0, 0.1, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 μM), the cells' viability was analyzed by the CCK-8 assay.

Oxygen glucose deprivation/re-oxygenation (OGD/R) model

The OGD/R model was created using slightly modified previously published protocols [34]. To put it briefly, cells were first incubated overnight under standard culture conditions. Subsequently, the medium was changed to serum-free, low-glucose DMEM, and cells were maintained in an anoxic chamber (5 % CO2, 95 % N2) at 37 °C for 12 h. The OGD/R model was finished after this hypoxic phase by substituting standard culture medium for 6 h. After hypoxia, a standard medium containing the target drug was substituted for the serum-free low-glucose medium in order to evaluate drug treatments in the OGD/R model.

Cellular uptake of ABR NPs

PC12 and bEnd.3 cells were inoculated into a 24-well glass-bottomed culture dish. After adhesion for 12 h, cells were treated for 2 or 4 h in 1 mL of standard medium containing FITC, FITC-labeled PEG NPs, or ABR NPs. The cells were then fixed and permeabilized, incubated with Rhodamine-phalloidin (Solarbio, CA1610) staining the cytoskeleton overnight at 4 °C. Finally, the cells were counterstained with Hoechst 33258 and fluorescence images were captured by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (Zeiss 900, Germany‌).

Flow cytometry (FCM) was used to quantitatively analyze cellular internalization. FITC, FITC-labeled PEG NPs, or ABR NPs were applied to cells (2 × 105/well) in 12-well plates once they reached optimal confluence for 2 or 4 h incubation. Finally, FCM (BD Biosciences, UK) was used to analyze the cells.

To investigate the internalization pathways of the nanoparticles, cells were incubated with FITC-labeled nanoparticles at 4 °C for 4 h. In parallel, cells were pretreated with an LRP1 inhibitor for 1 h, followed by co-incubation with FITC-labeled nanoparticles at 37 °C for 4 h. Finally, the samples were analyzed by CLSM and FCM.

Intracellular antioxidative evaluation

PC12 cells were plated in confocal dishes (5 × 105 cell/dish). At optimal confluence, cells were initiated the OGD/R as described. Upon the onset of reoxygenation, the cell medium was replaced with standard medium containing various drug formulations for 6 h. PC12 cells were then incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA (37 °C, 20 min), washed thrice, and finally imaged by CLSM (Leica TCS SP5, Germany).

For antioxidant capacity quantification, PC12 cells in 12-well plates were processed following the same protocol. After reoxygenation, cells were digested and finally analyzed by FCM.

Cell apoptosis assay

Briefly, PC12 cells were plated in a 12-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well) and cultured overnight, then initiated the OGD/R treatment as described. The cell medium was replaced with standard medium containing various drug formulations for 6 h at the beginning of the reoxygenation. Finally, cell apoptosis was quantitatively analyzed by an Annexin V-FITC/7-AAD as per the manufacturer's guidelines.

BBB penetration study

As previously reported [36], we established the BBB model for assessing the permeability ABR NPs across the barrier. Briefly, bEnd.3 cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were plated in a volume of 200 μL onto the upper layer of a 24-well transwell and cultured until the resistance of trans-endothelial electrical resistance (TEER) greater than 200 Ω cm2. PC12 cells were then cultured in the lower chambers and allowed to adhere overnight. The medium in the upper chambers was subsequently exchanged for one containing FITC, FITC-labeled PEG NPs or ABR NPs, with incubation times of 12 and 24 h. Subsequently, the PC12 cells in the lower chamber were then analyzed for fluorescence intensity using CLSM and FCM, respectively.

Investigation of microglia polarization

Briefly, BV2 cells were inoculated into 24-well plate containing glass coverslips or 6-well plates for overnight. LPS (1 μg/mL) was used to induce inflammatory environment for 24 h. The medium was then exchanged for standard medium containing various formulations at an equivalent BLN or RAP dose of 10 μM. The expression levels of CD86 and CD206 in BV2 cells was then examined using FCM and CLSM. Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin 6 (IL-6), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and interleukin 10 (IL-10) gene expression was identified via real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR).

Similarly, brain tissues were collected from mice in different treatment groups. The tissues were homogenized in normal saline and centrifuged. ELISA kits were used to measure the levels of IL-1β (Multi Sciences, EK201BHS) and IL-10 (Multi Sciences, EK210) in the brain tissue supernatants. Additionally, the mRNA expression levels of TNF-α, IL-6, TGF-β, and IL-10 in the brain tissues were also quantified by RT-qPCR.

RT-qPCR assay

A Roche LC96 Real-Time PCR system was used to conduct the RT-qPCR test, as previously mentioned [37]. The detailed primer sequences are available in Table S1. For normalization, GAPDH expression was utilized.

Western blot (WB) assay

WB was performed as previously described [38]. Briefly, proteins extracted from BV2 cells were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were then blocked at room temperature for 30 min using blocking buffer (Beyotime, P0252). Then the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-GAPDH, anti-mTOR, or anti-p-mTOR. Afterward, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (ZSGB-BIO, ZB-2305) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system (FUSION Solo S, Vilber GmbH).

Immunofluorescence (IF) assay

For cell samples, after fixation with 4 % paraformaldehyde, the cells were permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100 for 20 min, followed by blocking with QuickBlock™ immunostaining blocking solution (Beyotime, P0260) for 1 h at room temperature. After overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C, samples were treated with secondary antibodies for 1 h and finally fluorescence imaged after staining with Hoechst 33258. For brain paraffin sections, deparaffinized tissue slices underwent microwave antigen repair in Tris-EDTA (pH = 9.0) buffer. The remaining steps were the same as for cell samples.

Animals

All the animal studies in this research adhered strictly to the guidelines of China's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Army Medical University (Approval NO. AMUWEC20250035; Chongqing, China). Male C57BL/6J mice, aged 8-10 weeks, were obtained from the laboratory animal center of Army Medical University.

In vivo long-term toxicity tests

The healthy mice were randomly allocated into 2 groups (n = 6), and injected with saline or ABR NPs every 3 days via the tail vein at a dose of 1 mg/kg RAP. Post-administration, the body weights of mice was assessed every 3 days, along with the monitor of their general behaviors for any indications or symptoms of illness. To perform hematological analysis, we euthanized the experimental mice 30 days after administration and collected blood samples. Furthermore, major organs including heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys were collected and subsequently stained using the standard H&E protocol.

Mouse models

Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion (MCAO) model was performed based on an established method with minor adjustments [39]. In brief, the mice were anesthetized and the neck skin was cut. Following careful dissection of the internal, external, and common carotid arteries (ICA, ECA, CCA) with micro-tweezers, these vessels were ligated to block bloodstream. Subsequently, a micro-incision was created on the ECA, and a monofilament was introduced and advanced through the ICA until reaching the middle cerebral artery to establish occlusion. After 1 h, the monofilament was carefully removed to allow reperfusion. Sham-operated animals underwent the same surgical procedure but did not undergo monofilament insertion.

Neuroprotection evaluation in vivo

Following random allocation into six groups of the MCAO mice, treatments (saline, BLN, RAP, BR NPs, PEG NPs, or ABR NPs) were administered intravenously to the respective groups upon reperfusion, at a dose of 1 mg/kg RAP based on previously reported intravenous dosing ranges [18]. 24 h post-injection, neurological dysfunction in mice was assessed using the Zea-Longa scoring method as previously reported [40], and the mice were humanely sacrificed. Immediately after that, their brains were promptly excised, and sectioned into 5 slices along the coronal section, then stained using 4 % TTC for infarct visualization. The infarct area was measured through Image J software. Intracerebral ROS levels were assessed by DHE staining combined with Hoechst 33258 nuclear counterstain, and the sections were examined through fluorescence microscopy. And the nerve cells apoptosis was measured by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Solarbio, G1120), Nissl staining (Solarbio, G1430), as well as a TUNEL assay (Beyotime, C1090) with Hoechst 33258 counterstaining, all conducted in accordance with the manufacturers' guide.

Biodistribution

The in vivo distribution of ABR NPs were monitored with an IVIS Lumina Series III imaging system (PerkinElmer, United States). Following the establishment of the MCAO model as previously described, the mice received intravenous injections of free Cy7, Cy7-labeled PEG NPs, and Cy7-labeled ABR NPs. The fluorescence intensity in the brain was non-invasively monitored with the IVIS system, with monitoring intervals set at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 h. At the designated 24-h post-injection, the mice underwent euthanasia, heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney and brain were collected for fluorescence imaging. Subsequently, the brains sectioned into five pieces along the coronal section, then re-imaged using the IVIS system. Moreover, to elucidate the intracerebral distribution of the ABR NPs, brain tissue cryosections were prepared, counterstained with Hoechst 33258, and visualized by CLSM.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) (n ≥ 3). Statistical analyses were conducted using Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 8.0, with significance denoted as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ns (no significant). Each experiment was replicated at least three times independently; specific sample numbers (N) and technical replicates are indicated in the respective figure legends.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of ABR NPs

A one-step nano-precipitation method was successfully applied to synthesize self-assembled nanoparticles. The hydrodynamic size and PDI of the nanoparticles were methodically examined at BLN: RAP molar ratios of 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 in order to ascertain the ideal ratio of BLN to RAP. The results (Table S2) demonstrated that the average particle size of nanoparticles at all ratios was <180 nm and PDI ranged from 0.19 to 0.38, confirming the excellent self-assembly capabilities of BLN and RAP over across a broad range of molar ratios. It has been reported that smaller nanoparticles with low PDI improve blood-brain barrier penetration for efficient brain targeting [41]. A 1:1 M ratio of BLN to RAP was identified as optimal, yielding lowest PDI (0.19 ± 0.02) and the smallest particle size (134.00 ± 0.56 nm), as indicated in Table S2. Based on comprehensive characterization, this 1:1 ratio (named BR NPs) was ultimately selected for subsequent studies. Additionally, molecular dynamics simulations in Fig. 1A demonstrate that BLN and RAP can form stable nano-aggregates through hydrogen bonds as well as van der waals forces. Moreover, TEM results showed the BR NPs displayed consistent spherical shapes, as shown in Fig. 1B.

BR NPs were further engineered with DSPE-PEG2k-Angiopep-2 to form ABR NPs, aiming to enhance the pharmacokinetics for clinical application. TEM imaging revealed that ABR NPs maintained a spherical shape (Fig. 1C), with a minimal change in mean diameter from 134.00 ± 0.56 nm to 138.50 ± 0.79 nm after modification (Table S3). The zeta potential shifted to −40.53 mV for ABR NPs, compared to −32.77 mV (BR NPs) and −48.73 mV (PEG NPs) (Fig. 1D). This negatively charged surface is advantageous, as it enhances pharmacokinetics by utilizing electrostatic repulsion, which minimizes the formation of protein corona in the bloodstream [42]. Owing to the unique self-assembly mechanism for nanoparticle construction, the encapsulation efficiency of BLN and RAP was 73.84 ± 1.98 % and 81.24 ± 2.27 %, respectively, and their drug loading efficiency was 16.50 ± 0.27 % and 60.51 ± 0.52 %, as determined by HPLC. Additionally, the ABR NPs system presented the characteristic absorption peaks of BLN and RAP at 273 nm and 282 nm (Fig. 1E), immediately suggesting the successful incorporation of the two drug components thereof. Notably, the absorption intensities of BLN and RAP in the ABR NPs system were 4.04 and 1.26 times higher than their free counterparts at equivalent concentrations, which supports their supramolecular interaction.

Colloidal stability

To investigate the colloidal stability of the ABR NPs system, non-targeted nanoparticles (PEGylated-only, named PEG NPs) were prepared for comparative analysis. As illustrated in Fig. 1F, after incubation in PBS for 30 min, both PEG NPs and ABR NPs showed good colloidal stability with no significant size alteration, whereas non-PEGylated BR NPs aggregated rapidly under the same conditions. This implies that PEGylation is essential for maintaining drug co-assembly under biologically relevant conditions. Notably, after incubation of ABR NPs in PBS, PBS with 10 % FBS or 50 % mouse serum at 37 °C for 12 h, the particle size and PDI remained stable, with no significant alterations observed (Fig. 1G and H). These results indicated that the ABR NPs system had good colloidal stability in the biological environment, which is mainly attributed to the incorporation of PEG segment.

Cellular uptake

Firstly, the cytotoxicity of ABR NPs were tested on PC12, BV2, and bEnd.3 cells. Exhibiting typical neuroendocrine characteristics, the PC12 cell line originates from a rat adrenal medullary pheochromocytoma. Owing to these properties, it is widely employed as a model system for investigating the neuroprotection by the nanoparticles in the context of ischemic stroke [22]. The findings demonstrated that at therapeutic concentrations below 10 μM (equivalent to 10 μM BLN or RAP), ABR NPs did not affect the viability of BV2, PC12, or bEnd.3 cells (Fig. S1). However, when the concentration of ABR NPs reached up to 20 μM, the viability of PC12 cells decreased significantly. Therefore, we decided to set the concentration of ABR NPs at 10 μM (equivalent to 10 μM BLN or RAP) as the definitive drug treatment concentration in the remaining experiments.

Next, the uptake capacity of ABR NPs were studied in PC12 and bEnd.3 cells. Cells were treated with free FITC, FITC-labeled PEG NPs and ABR NPs, respectively, then CLSM was applied to study the cellular uptake. As depicted in Fig. 2A and B, nanoparticle accumulation within cells increased progressively with time. Fluorescence signals progressively intensified and spread from the cell periphery to the entire intracellular space in both PC12 and bEnd.3 cells, suggestive of active cellular internalization via endocytosis. Of note, cells treated with free FITC exhibited negligible fluorescence under CLSM. In contrast, under identical incubation conditions, the FITC fluorescence intensity was markedly stronger in the ABR NPs group compared to the PEG NPs group. This trend was corroborated by FCM analysis (Fig. 2C–H), which quantitatively confirmed the superior cellular uptake of ABR NPs. Collectively, these findings indicate that the Angiopep-2 modification significantly enhances the targeting efficacy of the self-assembled nanoparticles toward both PC12 and bEnd.3 cells.

To identify whether the Angiopep-2-modified nanoparticles in this study are internalized via an energy-dependent or energy-independent pathway, cellular uptake of FITC-labeled nanoparticles was examined at 4 °C and 37 °C using CLSM (Fig. S2). The results showed that uptake of FITC-labeled nanoparticles was markedly reduced at 4 °C in both PC12 and bEnd.3 cells relative to 37 °C, suggesting that ABR NPs are internalized through an energy-dependent process, similar to the unmodified PEG NPs. Next, to further verify whether the targeting ability of ABR NPs depends on the presence of the LRP1 receptor, PC12 cells and bEnd.3 cells were pretreated with LRP1 inhibitors for 1 h prior to incubation with FITC labeled nanoparticles. The results demonstrated that pretreatment with LRP1 inhibitors exerted no obvious influence on the internalization of free FITC and non-targeted PEG NPs in both cell lines (Fig. S3). In contrast, cellular uptake of Angiopep-2-modified ABR NPs was notably suppressed, with the fluorescence intensity reduced to a level similar to that observed in cells treated with non-targeted PEG NPs. Collectively, these findings indicated that the LRP1 receptors are crucial for the cellular uptake of Angiopep-2-modified nanoparticles, further demonstrating the targeting effect of the ABR NPs on nerve cells and endothelial cells with high LRP1 expression through LRP1-mediated endocytosis.

BBB penetration and biodistribution of the nanoparticles

Based on a published protocol, BBB model was established by culturing a confluent monolayer of bEnd.3 cells [36], for the dual purpose of evaluating nanoparticle BBB crossing and subsequent neuronal uptake. TEER values greater than 200 Ω cm2 confirmed the integrity of the BBB. The uptake of PC12 cells (lower chamber) via CLSM and FCM revealed a time-dependent accumulation of nanoparticles over 24 h (Fig. 3A and B and Fig. S4). Furthermore, when compared to free FITC and non-targeted FITC-labeled PEG NPs, FITC-labeled ABR NPs showed the strongest fluorescence intensity. In conclusion, the aforementioned findings demonstrated that surface modification with the Angiopep-2 improved the translocation of ABR NPs across the BBB and enhanced their uptake by PC12 cells.

Next, the In Vivo Image System was applied to further study the biodistribution of nanomaterials. To begin with, an ischemic stroke model was generated in mice through MCAO. Post administration of free Cy7, PEG NPs, or ABR NPs, the animals were anesthetized for in vivo imaging at scheduled intervals (3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 h) (Fig. 3C–D & Fig. S5A). Minimal fluorescence in the organs 24 h after injection supported the imaging results, which unmistakably showed that free Cy7 fluorophores were quickly removed from the systemic circulation in MCAO mice (Fig. 3E & Fig. S5B–C). In contrast, the fluorescence intensity displayed in the brains of mice injected with either PEG NPs or ABR NPs was substantially stronger than in the free Cy7 group. Notably, the signal intensity in the brains of the ABR NPs group significantly exceeded that of the PEG NPs group, demonstrating the enhanced ability of Angiopep-2 to facilitate drug transport across BBB in MCAO mice. The targeting propensity of the nanoparticles toward ischemic brain regions was further corroborated by ex vivo imaging of consecutive coronal brain sections at 24 h post-injection. The results, presented in Fig. 3F and G, showed a stark contrast: free Cy7 treatment resulted in almost no detectable fluorescence, whereas both ABR NPs and PEG NPs preferentially accumulated in the ischemic hemisphere, with the contralateral side showing negligible signals. Additionally, the fluorescence intensity of the ABR NPs group was markedly higher than that of the PEG NPs and free Cy7 groups, confirming enhanced accumulation of ABR NPs in cerebral ischemic foci.

To elucidate the nanoparticles’ cerebral penetration, brain tissue cryosections were analyzed for fluorescence distribution. As illustrated in Fig. 3H, the brains of MCAO mice treated with ABR NPs showed a noticeably stronger Cy7 signal than those receiving PEG NPs or free Cy7. This enhanced distribution is consistent with the role of Angiopep-2 in promoting BBB transport and cellular uptake. Collectively, the validations from both in vivo and ex vivo results support the enhanced ability of the designed carrier-free ABR NPs to cross the BBB and accumulate in ischemic brain regions.

ROS scavenging abilities of the nanoparticles

The antioxidant capacity of the ABR NPs, which utilize the polyphenolic compound BLN as their core, was systematically evaluated. ABR NPs exhibited potent, dose-dependent scavenging activities against DPPH, ABTS, and H2O2, as well as hydroxyl radicals (Fig. 4A–D). Maximum scavenging rates for DPPH, ABTS, H2O2, and hydroxyl radicals were achieved at concentrations of 25, 50, 100, and 100 μg/mL, reaching approximately 77.18 %, 92.04 %, 64.10 % and 60.20 %, respectively. The above results conclusively demonstrated the potent and broad-spectrum antioxidant capability of the carrier-free ABR NPs, a property highly relevant for mitigating ROS-induced pathology in cerebral ischemia [15].

Furthermore, in order to assess the effect of nanoparticles on scavenging intracellular ROS, PC12 cells were initiated the OGD/R treatment, and administered ABR NPs, PEG NPs, BR NPs or the individual components. FCM and CLSM were applied to detect the intracellular fluorescence via DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. FCM analysis indicated that the fluorescence intensity of OGD/R-treated PC12 cells (PBS group) was 18.69-fold that of the control group, verifying that the OGD/R model was successfully established (Fig. 4E and F). Moreover, ABR NPs demonstrated the most significant ROS scavenging effect (2.59-fold), outperforming both PEG NPs (4.18-fold) and BR NPs (6.76-fold). Concurrently, CLSM observations corroborated the FCM results, with ABR NPs showing superior ROS clearance compared to all other groups (Fig. 4G). These results confirmed that ABR NPs can efficiently transport medications to neural cells and scavenge intracellular ROS, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects.

Biosafety evaluation of ABR NPs

Biocompatibility represents a fundamental requirement for any biomaterial intended for biomedical use. To assess the in vivo safety of the nanoparticles, a plasma hemolysis test was performed. The results revealed that hemolysis rates in all experimental groups were considerably below the 5 % threshold accepted worldwide (Fig. S6A–B). It is noteworthy that ABR NPs maintained a low hemolysis percentage even when administered at a high concentration of 100 μg/mL (Fig. S6C–D), indicating their suitable blood compatibility for intravenous administration. Furthermore, mice receiving ABR NPs via intravenous injection showed no noticeable alterations in body weight or behavioral characteristics, which were similar to those found in the saline group (Fig. S7A). Hematological evaluation validated that the counts of white blood cells, red blood cells, platelets, as well as hemoglobin content, remained within normal ranges following tail vein administration of ABR NPs (Fig. S7B). Liver and kidney function assessments also indicated that ABR NPs caused no detectable impairment in these organs (Fig. S7C). Moreover, H&E-staining of major organs revealed no signs of significant structural damage or infiltration by inflammatory cells (Fig. S7D). In summary, these findings demonstrate that ABR NPs are highly biocompatible and suitable for in vivo use.

Neuroprotective potential of the nanoparticles

Originating from a rat adrenal medullary pheochromocytoma, the PC12 cell line displays neuroendocrine properties, making it a prevalent model in neurobiological studies [43]. In this study, an OGD/R model to PC12 cells was utilized to examine the protective effects of ABR NPs in the context of ischemic stroke injury. As depicted in Fig. S8, exposure to OGD/R led to markedly enhanced apoptosis in PC12 cells, with a pronounced effect on late-stage apoptosis (PBS group). However, this effect was suppressed by ABR NPs. Quantitatively, the late-stage apoptosis rate induced by OGD/R alone was 21.2 %, which was reduced to 13.1 % upon treatment with ABR NPs. These results suggest that ABR NPs could attenuate OGD/R-induced apoptotic cell death, potentially through their efficient free radical scavenging capacity, which counteracts the ROS-mediated DNA damage that triggers cell death.

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of the self-assembled nanoparticles, we employed a MCAO model in mice. The mice were allocated into seven experimental groups at random, including a sham-operated group. Following ischemia-reperfusion, the MCAO mice received intravenous injections of either saline, BLN, RAP, BR NPs, PEG NPs, or ABR NPs, while the sham group received saline alone (Fig. 5A). Cerebral infarction was assessed histopathologically at 24 h post-reperfusion using TTC staining (Fig. 5B and C). The saline-treated MCAO group exhibited an extensive infarct ratio of 37.88 %, confirming successful model establishment. Treatments with BLN, RAP, and BR NPs resulted in modest neuroprotection, reducing the infarct ratio to 32.24 %, 34.97 %, and 27.14 %, respectively. Most strikingly, ABR NPs conferred the most significant protection, drastically lowering the infarct ratio to 7.84 %, outperforming PEG NPs (16.87 %). To further assess neurological function in MCAO model mice, Zea-Longa neurological deficit scores was conducted after different therapeutic interventions. Mice administered with saline alone displayed significantly higher neurological deficit scores relative to the sham-operated group. However, this increase was significantly alleviated by ABR NPs administration, as presented in Fig. S9. These results strongly suggest that ABR NPs are highly effective in mitigating CIRI.

Further histopathological evaluation of cerebral tissues provided morphological corroboration for the neuroprotective potential of the nanoparticles. H&E staining revealed severe pathological features—including chromatin condensation, pyknosis, and necrosis—in the infarcted area of saline-treated groups (Fig. 5D). In stark contrast, ABR NPs treatment markedly preserved neuronal integrity, with chromatin structure appearing largely normal and necrosis significantly attenuated. Consistent with these findings, Nissl staining demonstrated a substantial loss of intact neurons, alongside disordered arrangement and aberrant morphology, in the saline group compared to the sham group. This neuronal damage was notably mitigated by ABR NPs administration. Next, neuronal apoptosis in the CIRI region was further assessed by TUNEL staining. As demonstrated in Fig. 5E, a marked increasement in apoptotic cells was observed in the saline-treated MCAO group relative to the sham controls, confirming that ischemic reperfusion induces substantial neuronal death. In contrast, this effect was significantly suppressed by ABR NPs treatment. Consistent with this finding, immunofluorescence for the neuronal marker NeuN revealed a higher density of preserved neurons in the ABR NPs group compared to both the PEG NPs and BR NPs groups (Fig. 5E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ABR NPs effectively mitigate cerebral injury by reducing both infarct size and apoptosis, which may be likely attributable to their enhanced targeting capability, combined with the ROS-scavenging and neuron-repairing activities.

Neuroprotective mechanism of the nanoparticles

Oxidative stress and the subsequent inflammatory cascade are the key driving factors in the pathogenesis of CIRI [44]. To study the potential neuroprotective mechanism of ABR NPs, we first assessed ROS generation at the Infarct site by DHE staining. And the results indicated that compared to sham groups, a substantial increase of ROS in the saline-treated MCAO mice, confirming a significant increase in ROS levels after CIRI. Administration of BLN, RAP, or BR NPs significantly reduced ROS levels at the site of cerebral infarction. Importantly, ABR NPs exhibited the most significant effect in eliminating ROS, outperforming PEG NPs (Fig. S10). The enhanced ROS scavenging effect achieved by ABR NPs may be due to the efficient enrichment at the site of cerebral infarction, which will directly provide protection for neurons.

Neuroinflammation is another key factor in the pathogenesis of ischemia-reperfusion injury [45]. Microglia, as the resident immune cells in the central nervous system, play a crucial role in regulating the neuro-immune balance [46]. Polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype enables microglia to promote protection within the ischemic stroke [18]. To investigate whether ABR NPs induce the transformation of microglia into anti-inflammatory type, we established an inflammatory microglial cell model by co incubating BV2 cells with LPS for 24 h, followed by treatment with different formulations. Since RAP exerts its anti-inflammatory effects mainly by inhibiting the mTOR signaling pathway [47], we examined the protein expression of total mTOR and p-mTOR in LPS-stimulated BV2 cells. As shown in Fig. S11A–B, LPS treatment markedly increased p-mTOR levels without affecting total mTOR expression. Following treatment with rapamycin-containing formulations, the LPS-induced elevation in p-mTOR was significantly reduced, while total mTOR showed no obvious change, confirming the specific inhibition of mTOR phosphorylation by RAP. Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining was performed to evaluate the microglial polarization. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, LPS treatment significantly increased the expression of the M1 marker CD86, indicating that BV2 cells had a pro-inflammatory transformation.

While RAP treatment decreased the expression of CD86 and enhanced the expression of CD206 in BV2 cells, which was consistent with earlier reports [48]. Furthermore, ABR NPs treatment significantly upregulated the expression of CD206, effectively converting BV2 cells into the M2 phenotype. Moreover, FCM analysis consistently corroborated CLSM results, with ABR NPs demonstrating reduced CD86 levels and enhanced CD206 levels superior to all the other groups (Fig. 6B and C). Next, we investigated the changes in the transcriptional levels of inflammatory factors in BV2 cells after various treatments, and obtained similar results. ABR NPs downregulated pro-inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-6) and upregulated anti-inflammatory markers (IL-10, TGF-β) in LPS treated BV2 cells (Fig. 6D–G). To further validate the anti-inflammatory effects of the nanoparticles in vivo, we also investigated the mRNA expression of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β in ischemic brain tissues by RT-qPCR, as well as the protein levels of IL-10 and IL-1β. Consistent with in vitro results, ABR NPs treatment significantly inhibited the transcription of pro-inflammatory TNF-α and IL-6, while increasing the expression of anti-inflammatory IL-10 and TGF-β in ischemic brain tissues (Fig. S11C–F). Accordingly, ELISA results confirmed that ABR NPs significantly reduced the protein level of IL-1β and increased IL-10 secretion compared with the saline group (Fig. S11G–H).

We further employed immunohistochemical double-staining of brain sections with CD86/IBA1 and CD206/IBA1 to assess inflammatory responses at the site of cerebral infarction (Fig. 6H). In contrast to the sham group, MCAO mice receiving saline showed greater microglia proliferation and activation within the ischemic region, whereas the MCAO mice injected with ABR NPs illustrated a decrease in CD86+/IBA1+ cells and an increase in CD206+/IBA1+ cells (Fig. S12). This suggests that the treatment with ABR NPs polarized M2 phenotype microglia in ischemic brain tissue for neuroprotection. Overall, these data support the conclusion that ABR NPs alleviate CIRI by combating oxidative stress and regulating inflammatory responses (Fig. 6I).

Discussion

In the present study, we developed a self-assembled nanoplatform for targeted therapy of CIRI by integrating ROS-scavenging and neuroinflammation alleviation. The nano-drug delivery system was formed by the self-assembly of BLN and RAP through non-covalent bonds, and the final targeted nanodrug ABR NPs were obtained through the modification of DSPE-PEG2k-Angiopep-2. Within this nanoplatform, BLN was primarily incorporated for its ROS-scavenging and antioxidant properties, with the aim of counteracting oxidative stress during reperfusion. Consistently, both in vitro and in vivo results revealed the potent antioxidant activity of BLN. In addition, previous studies have reported the anti-inflammatory effect of BLN [[49], [50], [51]], suggesting that it may also participate in the regulation of neuroinflammation in acute ischemic stroke. In the present study, BLN significantly reduced the mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory TNF-α and IL-6, and promoted TGF-β expression in LPS-stimulated BV2 cells, indicating its ability to suppress microglial overactivation. However, in MCAO mouse brain tissues, BLN alone significantly downregulated transcription of TNF-α and IL-6, but showed no significant influence on the mRNA levels of TGF-β and IL-10, nor on the protein levels of IL-10 and IL-1β, suggesting that its direct effect on microglial M2 polarization in vivo is limited. This discrepancy may be related, at least in part, to differences in dosing regimen and experimental design, since the BLN dose used here (0.3 mg/kg) was substantially lower than that reported in some previous studies (e.g., 300 mg/kg) [48]. Therefore, in our current formulation, the major role of BLN is interpreted as alleviating oxidative stress, whereas its direct immunomodulatory effect on microglial polarization is modest and complementary to that of rapamycin.

The other component, RAP, it is a well-established mTOR inhibitor that mainly exerts anti-inflammatory effects by regulate mTOR-related inflammatory signaling [52]. Our results further demonstrated that RAP could achieve marked anti-inflammatory effects and promote microglia-related phenotypic changes, which was supported by the observed modulation of the mTOR pathway. Nevertheless, the reported intravenous doses of RAP in ischemic stroke-related studies vary considerably, indicating that the effective dose may depend on the formulation, treatment regimen, and experimental setting [[53], [54], [55]]. Therefore, we do not intend to claim that 1 mg/kg represents a universally optimal dose. In the present study, RAP was administered at 1 mg/kg as a practical working dose based on previous reports [18]. Under this condition, the nanoplatform showed enhanced therapeutic efficacy against CIRI, and its safety was further confirmed by the 30-day biosafety evaluation.

It has been reported that the BBB restricts the brain delivery of many small-molecule drugs, thereby limiting the therapeutic efficacy of individual drugs in ischemic stroke [41,56]. Although nanocarriers have improved drug delivery to ischemic lesions, the BBB-penetrating efficiency of many nanomaterials remains limited [23]. To address this issue, various surface modification strategies have been developed to enhance the BBB penetration, including biomimetic membrane camouflage [57,58], and conjugation with peptides [18,59] or antibodies [21,60], with the aim of improving drug delivery to the ischemic site and therapeutic efficacy. Among these strategies, Angiopep-2 has attracted considerable attention because it acts as a ligand for the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1), which is highly expressed in brain endothelial cells and neuronal cells [61,62]. Through LRP1-mediated transcytosis, Angiopep-2 can facilitate nanoparticle transport across the BBB and enhance accumulation in ischemic brain tissue. Consistently, previous studies have shown that Angiopep-2-modified nanocarriers can improve targeted therapy for ischemic stroke [63,64]. In this study, DSPE-PEG2k-Angiopep-2 was introduced onto the surface of self-assembled BR NPs to improve colloidal stability, BBB transport and accumulation at the ischemic site.

Nevertheless, because DSPE-PEG2k-Angiopep-2 was incorporated onto the surface of the self-assembled nanoparticles through non-covalent interactions, the potential detachment of Angiopep-2 during blood circulation should be taken into consideration. In the current study, ABR NPs maintained good colloidal stability in PBS, PBS containing 10 % FBS, and PBS containing 50 % mouse serum over 12 h, whereas unmodified BR NPs exhibited aggregation in PBS, suggesting that surface modification with DSPE-PEG2k-Angiopep-2 contributed to the stability of the nanoplatform. These findings indirectly support the relative stability of the surface modification under the tested conditions, although they do not fully exclude the possibility of ligand dissociation during circulation, especially over longer periods. Notably, both in vitro and in vivo results demonstrated that Angiopep-2 modification enhanced the targeting ability of ABR NPs toward PC12 cells and bEnd.3 cells and increased the accumulation in ischemic brain regions. However, the in vivo stability of Angiopep-2 modification, particularly its possible detachment during prolonged circulation, remains to be further clarified.

Although ABR NPs showed encouraging therapeutic efficacy against ischemic stroke and preliminary in vivo biosafety, several issues remain to be addressed for future clinical translation. Previous studies have shown that nanomaterials are mainly cleared through the liver and kidneys [65,66], which is consistent with our biodistribution results showing predominant accumulation in these organs 24 h after administration. The current biosafety data, including hematological analysis, liver and kidney function tests, and histological examination of major organs, support the tolerability of ABR NPs at a dose of 1 mg/kg within 30 days. However, these findings mainly reflect short-to medium-term safety, and the long-term in vivo fate, clearance pathways, and potential chronic toxicity of ABR NPs remain to be systematically evaluated. Moreover, for this self-assembled nanoplatform, structural stability governed by non-covalent interactions, possible disassembly in complex biological environments, and potential shedding of the surface ligand Angiopep-2 may affect biodistribution, metabolic clearance, tissue accumulation, and long-term safety. In addition, as a multi-component and surface-functionalized nanoplatform integrating BLN and RAP, this “Eastern-Western fusion” strategy may face further translational challenges, including large-scale manufacturing, formulation reproducibility, batch-to-batch consistency, and regulatory evaluation. Therefore, more comprehensive studies on long-term safety, pharmacokinetics, clearance behavior, and manufacturability will be necessary to support future clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, we proposed a collaborative “Chinese and Western medicine integration” strategy to develop a carrier free binary nanoparticle for efficient treatment of acute ischemic stroke. In this study, we demonstrated that the Chinese herbal component BLN could self-assemble with RAP to form stable BR NPs. The surface modification of BR NPs with DSPE-PEG2k-Angiopep-2 yielded ABR NPs. This modification was found to enhance nanoparticle stability, as well as improve the blood-brain barrier permeability and targeting ability for nerve cells. Moreover, ABR NPs significantly reduced the ROS levels in nerve cells and decreased cell apoptosis, and polarized microglia at cerebral infarction site to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby exerting a neuroprotective effect. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments showed that ABR NPs can efficiently cross the BBB and precisely accumulate in the ischemia-reperfusion injury site, and then be internalized by nerve cells, achieving efficient therapeutic efficacy in CIRI. Overall, this Eastern-Western fusion nanoplatform provides a clinically applicable strategy for precise treatment of acute ischemic stroke.
