Spectral analysis of Grushin type operators on the quarter plane
Krzysztof Stempak

Abstract
. We investigate spectral properties of self-adjoint extensions of the operator G α , β = − ( ∂ 2 ∂ r 2 + 2 ​ α + 1 r ​ ∂ ∂ r ) − r 2 ​ ( ∂ 2 ∂ s 2 + 2 ​ β + 1 s ​ ∂ ∂ s ) , G_{\alpha,\beta}=-\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{2\alpha+1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Big)-r^{2}\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}+\frac{2\beta+1}{s}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Big), α , β ∈ ℝ \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R} , with domain Dom ​ G α , β = C ∞ ​ ( ℝ + 2 ) ⊂ L 2 ​ ( ℝ + 2 , r 2 ​ α + 1 ​ s 2 ​ β + 1 ​ d ​ r ​ d ​ s ) {\rm Dom}\,G_{\alpha,\beta}=C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+},r^{2\alpha+1}s^{2\beta+1}drds) , which for some specific values of α , β \alpha,\beta , is a bi-radial part of the Grushin operator. Alternatively, we investigate G α , β ∘ G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta} , the Liouville form of G α , β G_{\alpha,\beta} , which is a symmetric and nonnegative operator on L 2 ​ ( ℝ + 2 , d ​ r ​ d ​ s ) L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+},drds) . One of the main tools used is an integral transform which combines the Laguerre scaled transform and the Hankel transform. Self-adjoint extensions 𝔾 α , β ∘ \mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta} of G α , β ∘ G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta} are defined in terms of this transform, and the spectral decompositions of them are given. Another approach to construct self-adjoint extensions of G α , β ∘ G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta} , based on the technique of sesquilinear forms, is also presented and then the two approaches are compared. We also establish a closed form of the heat kernel corresponding to 𝔾 α , β ∘ \mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta} .



1.
Introduction

It is well known that the radial part of the (minus) Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the differential operator

B=-\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}-\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}

in the sense that

(-\Delta)F(x)=Bf(|x|),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
f=f(r)
,
r>0
, is the radial profile of a radial function
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.
F(x)=f(|x|)
. Moreover, for radial functions, the Fourier
transform reduces to the (modified) Hankel transform; this means that

\mathcal{F}_{d}F(x)=\mathcal{H}_{(d-2)/2}f(|x|),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Here, for the type parameter
\alpha>-1
,
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
is the integral transform given by

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}f(r)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u)\frac{J_{\alpha}(ru)}{(ru)^{\alpha}}\,u^{2\alpha+1}\,du,\qquad r\in(0,\infty),

where
J_{\alpha}
stands for the Bessel function of the first kind of order
\alpha
, see e.g.
[
6
, Section 5]
.

The
Grushin differential operator
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
,
d_{1},d_{2}\geq 1
, is given by the differential expression

G=-\Delta_{x^{\prime}}-|x^{\prime}|^{2}\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}},

where
x=(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
,
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
,
x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, and
\Delta_{x^{\prime}},\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}
are the Laplacians on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
,
respectively. The symbol
|\cdot|
stands for the Euclidean norm, e.g. in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
, or
\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, depending on the context.
It is easily seen that initially considered with domain
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
G
is symmetric and nonnegative. Moreover,

G
is essentially self-adjoint, hence it admits the unique self-adjoint extension on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, called the
Grushin operator
.
During the last years numerous papers
were devoted to different aspects of analysis of
G
. See, for instance, Dall’Ara and Martini
[
3
]
and the extensive literature therein,
and Jotsaroop et al.
[
5
]
, where, among others, the spectral decomposition of the Grushin operator was proposed. Also some generalizations
of
G
were considered, see, e.g. Almeida et al.
[
1
]
.

We now modify the concept of radiality and call a function
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}

bi-radial
provided
F(x)=f(|x^{\prime}|,|x^{\prime\prime}|)

for some
f\colon(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
. The structure of
G
immediately reveals that for sufficiently smooth bi-radial functions we have

GF(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})=G_{\frac{d_{1}-2}{2},\frac{d_{2}-2}{2}}f(r,s),\qquad r=|x^{\prime}|,\quad s=|x^{\prime\prime}|,

where for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
we set

G_{\alpha,\beta}=-\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{2\alpha+1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Big)-r^{2}\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}+\frac{2\beta+1}{s}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Big).

Thus, for the specific values
\alpha=(d_{1}-2)/2
,
\beta=(d_{2}-2)/2
, this differential operator can be seen as the bi-radial part of the Grushin differential operator on

\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.

The aim of this paper is to perform the spectral analysis of
G_{\alpha,\beta}
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, with domain
{\rm Dom}\,G_{\alpha,\beta}=C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)\subset L^{2}(r^{2\alpha+1}s^{2\beta+1}dr\,ds)
. In fact, we shall act simultaneously considering the unitarily equivalent version of
G_{\alpha,\beta}
, see Section
2.4
, namely

G_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}=-\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}-\frac{\alpha^{2}-1/4}{r^{2}}\Big)-r^{2}\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}-\frac{\beta^{2}-1/4}{s^{2}}\Big),

with domain
{\rm Dom}\,G_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}=C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)
; see Section
6.1
for additional comments. This version, being a symmetric and
nonnegative operator on
L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)
, is technically easier to work with because of the presence of Lebesgue measure.
One of the main tools we use is an integral transform which combines the Laguerre scaled transform and the Hankel transform. The idea of using the
Laguerre scaled transform is implicitly contained in the spectral decomposition of the Grushin operator presented in
[
5
]
. This decomposition
is based on applying scaled Hermite functions, forming orthonormal bases in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
, together with the Fourier transform in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
.
But, as it is well known, the radialization of the Hermite function setting leads to the Laguerre function framework; in addition, the radialization
of the Fourier transform results in using the Hankel transform. Therefore, combining these two facts found an outcome in the idea of construction
of the transform which is one of our main tools in the investigation of the Grushin-type operators. It is necessary to mention that the use of the
systems of scaled Hermite functions was initiated and intensively applied by Thangavelu in the harmonic analysis of the sub-Laplacian on the Heisenberg
group,
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
, and in an investigation of an analogue of Hardy’s theorem for the Heisenberg group,
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
(and also in
some other contexts). See also
[
16
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
contains preliminaries and includes a discussion of the Bessel operator and the Hankel transform,
the Laguerre operator and the Laguerre scaled transform, and the Grushin-type operators. In Section
3
we define the transform
\mathcal{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
,
which combines the Laguerre scaled transform and the Hankel transform, and in Theorem
3.1
we state its basic properties. Section
4

is devoted to a construction of self-adjoint extensions of the Grushin-type operator
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
given in terms of
\mathcal{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
. Then we
discuss the corresponding heat semigroup and deliver a compact form of the associated heat kernels, see Theorem
4.2
. In Section
5
another approach to construction of self-adjoint extensions is presented and then two approaches are compared. Here the main tools used are weak
\delta
-derivatives and
\delta
-Sobolev spaces, introduced and investigated in the
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
context. Finally, in Section
6
we gather comments, remainders and proofs of some technical results used earlier; we believe that putting all this stuff in the appendix will allow the reader to concentrate
on the main line of thoughts.

The final comment concerns the range of type parameters
\alpha,\beta
, used in the statements of results that follow. For the
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
-setting it is
obvious that we can limit the full range
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
to
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
. The same holds for the
G_{\alpha,\beta}
-setting, see Proposition
6.2
and the
remarks following it. Despite this possible limitation we keep the full range whenever we can. But in several statements, for technical reasons we must
limit the range to
\alpha,\beta>-1
. This comes from the fact that the
\mathcal{G}^{\circ}/\mathcal{G}
-transforms are defined only for
\alpha,\beta>-1
(which is caused by earlier
range limitations for the Hankel and Laguerre transforms). It must be, however, emphasized that considering the range
\alpha,\beta>-1
, larger than theoretically
necessary
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
, brings important advantages; one of them is exemplified by Theorem
4.1
.

Notation and terminology
. Throughout the paper we use fairly standard notions and symbols. An operator
T
on a Hilbert space
(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H})
, is said to be
nonnegative
provided
\langle Tf,f\rangle_{H}\geq 0
for all
f\in{\rm Dom}\,T\subset H
.
The operators considered below
with domains
C^{\infty}_{c}(0,\infty)
or
C^{\infty}_{c}\big((0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)\big)
in appropriate Hilbert spaces are densely defined; we shall not
repeat this fact later on. We use the calligraphic letters
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{G}
,
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{L}
, with subscripts and/or superscripts, to denote
transforms. For instance
\mathcal{F}_{d}
stands for the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by
\mathcal{F}_{d}F(x)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(y)\exp(-ix\cdot y)\,dy
,

F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Similarly, we use the
mathbb
font letters
\mathbb{B}
,
\mathbb{D}
,
\mathbb{G}
,
\mathbb{L}
,
\mathbb{M}
, with subscripts
and/or superscripts, to denote self-adjoint operators. For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
we set

d\mu_{\alpha}(r)=r^{2\alpha+1}\,dr,\qquad d\mu_{\alpha,\beta}(r,s)=r^{2\alpha+1}s^{2\beta+1}\,dr\,ds.

The canonical inner products in the Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}):=L^{2}\big((0,\infty),d\mu_{\alpha}\big)
and
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha,\beta}):=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+},d\mu_{\alpha,\beta}\big)
,
where
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:=(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)
, will be denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\alpha}
, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\alpha,\beta}
, respectively.
Relating two positive quantities
X
and
Y
we write
X\lesssim Y
, when
X\leq cY
for some
c>0
independent of possible significant quantities entering
X
and
Y
; the symbol
\gtrsim
is understood analogously. If
X\lesssim Y
and
Y\lesssim X
, then we write
X\simeq Y
.

We mention that we shall slightly abuse the notation using the same symbols to denote differential operators (without precisely specified domains) and corresponding to them unbounded operators on appropriate
L^{2}
spaces (with determined domains). We believe this will not lead to a confusion.

2.
Preliminaries

For the sake of clarity we decided to split this section into four subsections.

2.1.
Bessel operator

The
Bessel operator
given by the differential expression

B_{\alpha}=-\Big(\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{2\alpha+1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\Big)=\frac{1}{r^{2\alpha+1}}\Big(-\frac{d}{dr}\big(r^{2\alpha+1}\frac{d}{dr}\big)\Big),

and considered with domain
{\rm Dom}\,B_{\alpha}=C^{\infty}_{c}(0,\infty)\subset L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
, is symmetric and nonnegative; the latter divergence form of
B_{\alpha}
can be
used to verify this. It is also known that
B_{\alpha}
is essentially self-adjoint if and only if
|\alpha|\geq 1
. This follows from
[
4
, Section 11]
.
For
\alpha>-1
the canonical self-adjoint extension of
B_{\alpha}
is given in terms of the Hankel transform
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
.

1

\spadesuit
In the literature
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
is sometimes called the
modified
Hankel transform, in opposition to
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}^{\circ}
, see Section

2.4
, which is called the Hankel transform.

The kernel of this transform is built up from eigenfunctions of
B_{\alpha}
. More precisely, denoting

\tilde{J}_{\alpha,\tau}(u)=J_{\alpha}(\tau u)/(\tau u)^{\alpha},\qquad u>0,

so that
B_{\alpha}\tilde{J}_{\alpha,\tau}=\tau^{2}\tilde{J}_{\alpha,\tau}
(this follows from the basic differential equation satisfied by the Bessel function
J_{\alpha}
), we have

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}f(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u)\tilde{J}_{\alpha,\tau}(u)\,d\mu_{\alpha}(u),\qquad\tau>0.

Specifying the type parameter to
\alpha=-1/2
brings the cosine transform

\mathcal{H}_{-1/2}f(\tau)=\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u)\cos\tau u\,du,

and similarly, for
\alpha=1/2
we obtain the sine transform.

Recall that
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
, defined initially on
L^{1}(d\mu_{\alpha})
, extends (uniquely) to a unitary isomorphism onto
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
and we use the same
symbol
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
to denote this extension. See
[
2
, Section 4]
, where Plancherel’s identity and the inverse formula is proved for
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}

in the full range
\alpha>-1
. We also add that
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}^{-1}=\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
on
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
. In the same way as the Fourier transform intertwines
the Laplacian with the multiplication by
|\cdot|^{2}
, the Hankel transform
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
unitarily intertwines the Bessel operator
B_{\alpha}
with the
multiplication operator by
(\cdot)^{2}
, i.e.

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(B_{\alpha}f)=(\cdot)^{2}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}f,\qquad f\in{\rm Dom}\,B_{\alpha}=C^{\infty}_{c}(0,\infty).

(2.1)

Since the multiplication operator
\mathbb{M}_{(\cdot)^{2}}
(with maximal domain) is self-adjoint and nonnegative on
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
, see Lemma
6.1
,
the operator
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}
defined by

\displaystyle{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\{f\in L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})\colon(\cdot)^{2}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}f\in L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}\circ\mathbb{M}_{(\cdot)^{2}}\circ\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}

is also self-adjoint and nonnegative. It may easily be checked that
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}
indeed extends
B_{\alpha}
.

Finally, we mention that the Bessel differential operator
B_{\alpha}
is homogeneous of degree 2, which means that
B_{\alpha}(\rho_{\tau}f)=\tau^{2}\rho_{\tau}(B_{\alpha}f)
,
where
\rho_{\tau}
,
\tau>0
, is the dilation
\rho_{\tau}f(r)=\tau^{\alpha+1}f(\tau r)
,
r>0
, being a unitary automorphism of
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
.

2.2.
Laguerre operator

We shall also make use of the
Laguerre operator
with domain
{\rm Dom}\,L_{\alpha}=C^{\infty}_{c}(0,\infty)\subset L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
defined by the differential expression

L_{\alpha}=B_{\alpha}+r^{2}=\frac{1}{r^{2\alpha+1}}\Big(-\frac{d}{dr}\big(r^{2\alpha+1}\frac{d}{dr}\big)+r^{2\alpha+3}\Big).

The latter form, the divergence form of
L_{\alpha}
, immediately shows that
L_{\alpha}
is symmetric and nonnegative.
It is also known that
L_{\alpha}
is essentially self-adjoint if and only if
|\alpha|\geq 1
; basically, this follows from
[
4
, Sections 27, 28]
, cf. also

[
11
, Section 7.2]
. For
\alpha>-1
the Laguerre functions (of convolution type)

\ell_{n}^{\alpha}(r)=c_{n,\alpha}L^{\alpha}_{n}(r^{2})\exp(-r^{2}/2),\qquad r>0,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,\ldots\},

where
c_{n,\alpha}=\big(\frac{2\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}\big)^{1/2}
, form an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
and are eigenfunctions of
L_{\alpha}
,

L_{\alpha}\ell_{n}^{\alpha}=\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\ell_{n}^{\alpha},\qquad\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}=2(2n+\alpha+1),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Here
L^{\alpha}_{n}
stands for the
n
th Laguerre polynomial of order
\alpha
, see e.g. Lebedev
[
6
, 4.17]
.

The mapping
f\to\{\langle f,\ell_{n}^{\alpha}\rangle_{\alpha}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, denote it
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,1}
, unitarily identifies
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
with
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
,
and the inverse mapping
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,1}^{-1}
is
\{c_{n}\}_{0}^{\infty}\to\sum_{0}^{\infty}c_{n}\ell_{n}^{\alpha}
. Since the multiplication operator

\mathbb{M}_{\{\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\}}
is self-adjoint and nonnegative on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
, see Lemma
6.1
, the operator

\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}
given by

\displaystyle{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,1}^{-1}({\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}_{\{\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\}})=\{f\in L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})\colon\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\langle f,\ell^{\alpha}_{n}\rangle_{\alpha}|^{2}<\infty\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,1}^{-1}\circ\mathbb{M}_{\{\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\}}\circ\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,1}

is self-adjoint and nonnegative on
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
. A simple argument shows that indeed
\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}
is an extension of
L_{\alpha}
.

In fact we shall need a
scaled
Laguerre framework and by this we mean the following.
For any
\tau>0
the
scaled system

\ell_{n,\tau}^{\alpha}:=\rho_{\sqrt{\tau}}\ell_{n}^{\alpha},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
,
\alpha>-1
. Moreover, the scaled system consists of eigenfunctions of the
scaled Laguerre operator

L_{\alpha,\tau}=B_{\alpha}+\tau^{2}r^{2},

with eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\tau
, i.e.,

L_{\alpha,\tau}\ell_{n,\tau}^{\alpha}=\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\tau\,\ell_{n,\tau}^{\alpha}.

(2.2)

Indeed,
to obtain (
2.2
) we write

\displaystyle L_{\alpha,\tau}\ell_{n,\tau}^{\alpha}=(B_{\alpha}+\tau^{2}r^{2})(\rho_{\sqrt{\tau}}\ell_{n}^{\alpha})=\tau\rho_{\sqrt{\tau}}(B_{\alpha}\ell_{n}^{\alpha})+\tau^{2}r^{2}\rho_{\sqrt{\tau}}\ell_{n}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\tau\rho_{\sqrt{\tau}}\big((B_{\alpha}+(\sqrt{\tau}r)^{2})\ell_{n}^{\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\tau\rho_{\sqrt{\tau}}\big(L_{\alpha}\ell_{n}^{\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\tau\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\rho_{\sqrt{\tau}}\ell_{n}^{\alpha}.

In this scaled framework we consider the
Laguerre scaled transform
which, for every
\tau>0
, attaches to a function
f\in L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})

the sequence of coefficients from the expansion of
f
with respect to
\{\ell_{n,\tau}^{\alpha}\}_{0}^{\infty}
. Namely, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau>0
,

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,\tau}f(n):=\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}f(n,\tau)=\langle f,\ell_{n,\tau}^{\alpha}\rangle_{\alpha},\qquad f\in L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}).

By Parseval’s identity, for every
\tau>0
,
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,\tau}\colon L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
is a unitary isomorphism, and its inverse

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,\tau}^{-1}\colon\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})\to L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
is the mapping
\{c_{n}\}_{0}^{\infty}\to\sum_{0}^{\infty}c_{n}\ell_{n,\tau}^{\alpha}
.
Notice also that as a counterpart to (
2.1
), in the present framework we have for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau>0
,

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}(L_{\alpha,\tau}f)(n,\tau)=\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\tau\,\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}f(n,\tau),\qquad f\in{\rm Dom}\,L_{\alpha,\tau}=C^{\infty}_{c}(0,\infty).

Example 2.1
.

We apply the formula (see, for instance,
[
7
, 18.17.34]
)

\int_{0}^{\infty}L^{\alpha}_{n}(u)e^{-bu}u^{\alpha}\,du=\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(n+1)}\frac{(b-1)^{n}}{b^{n+\alpha+1}},\qquad\alpha>-1,\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad b>0,

(2.3)

(for
b=1
and
n=0
the convention
0^{0}=1
is used) to evaluate the Laguerre scaled transform of the gaussian function
g(r):=\exp(-r^{2}/2)
and to check,
by a direct calculation, Parseval’s identity for
g
, for every fixed
\tau>0
. We have

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}g(n,\tau)=\langle g,\ell_{n,\tau}^{\alpha}\rangle_{\alpha}=c_{n,\alpha}\tau^{(\alpha+1)/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}/2}L^{\alpha}_{n}(\tau r^{2})e^{-\tau r^{2}/2}r^{2\alpha+1}dr.

A change of variable with the aid
of (
2.3
) then leads to

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}g(n,\tau)=\frac{2^{\alpha+1}}{c_{n,\alpha}}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\tau}}{1+\tau}\Big)^{\alpha+1}\Big(\frac{1-\tau}{1+\tau}\Big)^{n}.

Next, recalling that
c_{n,\alpha}=\big(2\Gamma(n+1)/\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\big)^{1/2}
and applying

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+1)}q^{n}={\,}_{2}F_{1}(1,\alpha+1;1;q),\qquad\alpha>-1,\quad|q|<1,

gives

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}g(n,\tau)|^{2}=2^{2\alpha+1}\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\tau}}{1+\tau}\Big)^{2(\alpha+1)}{\,}_{2}F_{1}\Big(1,\alpha+1;1;\Big(\frac{1-\tau}{1+\tau}\Big)^{2}\Big);

here
{\,}{}_{2}F_{1}
is the hypergeometric function. But

{\,}{}_{2}F_{1}(1,\alpha+1;1;q)=\frac{1}{(1-q)^{\alpha+1}},

and finally, after a calculation,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}g(n,\tau)|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(\alpha+1)=\int_{0}^{\infty}|g(r)|^{2}r^{2\alpha+1}dr.

2.3.
Grushin-type operator, I

With notation introduced in Section
2.1
we see that

G_{\alpha,\beta}=B_{1,\alpha}+r^{2}B_{2,\beta},

where
j=1,2
, now indicates the variable,
r
(the 1
st
) or
s
(the 2
nd
), over which the differentiation in the Bessel operators
B_{\alpha}
and
B_{\beta}

is performed (more precisely, these operators are now assumed to be applied to functions of two variables). Recall that

{\rm Dom}\,G_{\alpha,\beta}=C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)
. An easy calculation then shows that
G_{\alpha,\beta}
is symmetric and nonnegative
in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha,\beta})
, hence it admits self-adjoint extensions. Notice also that the differential operator
G_{\alpha,\beta}
is
parabolically homogeneous
of degree 2,
in the sense that
G_{\alpha,\beta}(\check{\rho}_{\tau}f)=\tau^{2}\check{\rho}_{\tau}(G_{\alpha,\beta}f)
,
\tau>0
, where the family of ‘parabolic’ dilations

\{\check{\rho}_{\tau}\}_{\tau>0}
now acts on functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
by
\check{\rho}_{\tau}f(r,s)=f(\tau r,\tau^{2}s)
.

The appropriate scaling of the Laguerre functions is crucial in verification that the functions

\Psi_{n,\tau}(r,s):=\ell^{\alpha}_{n,\tau}(r)\tilde{J}_{\beta,\tau}(s),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\tau>0,

are eigenfunctions of the differential operator
G_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>-1
. Then for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\tau>0
,

G_{\alpha,\beta}\Psi_{n,\tau}(r,s)=\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\tau\,\Psi_{n,\tau}(r,s),\qquad r>0,\quad s>0.

Proof.

Using
B_{\beta}\tilde{J}_{\beta,\tau}=\tau^{2}\tilde{J}_{\beta,\tau}
and (
2.2
) gives

\displaystyle G_{\alpha,\beta}\Psi_{n,\tau}(r,s)

\displaystyle=\big(B_{1,\alpha}+r^{2}B_{2,\beta}\big)\big(\ell^{\alpha}_{n,\tau}(r)\tilde{J}_{\beta,\tau}(s)\big)

\displaystyle=\big(B_{\alpha}\ell^{\alpha}_{n,\tau}\big)(r)\tilde{J}_{\beta,\tau}(s)+r^{2}\ell^{\alpha}_{n,\tau}(r)(B_{\beta}\tilde{J}_{\beta,\tau})(s)

\displaystyle=\big(B_{\alpha}\ell^{\alpha}_{n,\tau}\big)(r)\tilde{J}_{\beta,\tau}(s)+\tau^{2}r^{2}\ell^{\alpha}_{n,\tau}(r)\tilde{J}_{\beta,\tau}(s)

\displaystyle=\big(L_{\alpha,\tau}\ell^{\alpha}_{n,\tau}\big)(r)\tilde{J}_{\beta,\tau}(s)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}\tau\,\Psi_{n,\tau}(r,s).

∎

For essential self-adjointness we have the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. If
|\alpha|\geq 1
, then
G_{\alpha,\beta}
is essentially self-adjoint.

Proof.

In the proof we modify appropriately an argument used in Dall’Ara and Martini
[
3
, Section 3.2]
.

Let
|\alpha|\geq 1
and assume that
\beta>-1
; this restriction is justified, see the remarks following Proposition
6.2
. By a basic criterion,
see e.g.
[
9
, Proposition 3.8]
, it suffices to check that
\mathcal{N}(G_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}-\overline{\lambda}I)=\{0\}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
;
here
G_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}
stands for the adjoint of
G_{\alpha,\beta}
. This amounts to verifying that the only
f\in L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha,\beta})
that satisfies

\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r,s)\overline{(G_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda I)\varphi(r,s)}\,d\mu_{\alpha,\beta}(r,s)=0,

must necessarily vanish a.e. Choosing
\varphi(r,s)=\varphi_{1}(r)\varphi_{2}(s)
,
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(0,\infty)
we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r,s)\overline{(B_{1,\alpha}+r^{2}B_{2,\beta}-\lambda I)\varphi_{1}(r)\varphi_{2}(s)}\,d\mu_{\alpha}(r)\Big)d\mu_{\beta}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r,s)\overline{B_{\alpha}\varphi_{1}(r)}\,d\mu_{\alpha}(r)\Big)\overline{\varphi_{2}(s)}\,d\mu_{\beta}(s)

\displaystyle\,\,\,+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r,s)\overline{(r^{2}-\lambda)\varphi_{1}(r)}\,d\mu_{\alpha}(r)\Big)\overline{B_{\beta}\varphi_{2}(s)}\,d\mu_{\beta}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{\beta}[f(r,\cdot)](\eta)\overline{(B_{\alpha}+\eta^{2}r^{2}-\eta^{2}\lambda)\varphi_{1}(r)}\,d\mu_{\alpha}(r)\Big)\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\varphi_{2}(\eta)}\,d\mu_{\beta}(\eta).

For the last equality we used an equivalent version of Plancherel’s identity for
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
and the fact that the Hankel transform
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
of the function

evaluated at
\eta>0
, with
r>0
fixed, is
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{\beta}[f(r,\cdot)](\eta)\overline{B_{\alpha}\varphi_{1}(r)}\,d\mu_{\alpha}(r)
, and similarly for

s\to\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r,s)\overline{(r^{2}-\lambda)\varphi_{1}(r)}\,d\mu_{\alpha}(r).

Since
\{\mathcal{H}_{\beta}\varphi_{2}\colon\varphi_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(0,\infty)\}
is dense in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\beta})
, we infer that for every
\varphi_{1}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(0,\infty)
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{\beta}[f(r,\cdot)](\eta)\overline{(B_{\alpha}+\eta^{2}r^{2}-\eta^{2}\lambda)\varphi_{1}(r)}\,d\mu_{\alpha}(r)=0,\qquad\eta\in(0,\infty)-{\rm a.e}.

But for every
\eta\in(0,\infty)
the operator
L_{\alpha,\eta}=B_{\alpha}+\eta^{2}r^{2}
with
{\rm Dom}\,L_{\alpha,\eta}=C^{\infty}_{c}(0,\infty)\subset L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
, is
essentially self-adjoint, see Lemma
6.3
, hence
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}[f(r,\cdot)](\eta)=0
for a.e.

. It follows that
f(r,\cdot)=0

for a.e.
r\in(0,\infty)
and finally
f=0
.
∎

It will follow from Theorem
4.1
that for every
(\alpha,\beta)\in(-1,1)\times(-1,1)\setminus\{(0,0)\}
,
G_{\alpha,\beta}
fails to be essentially
self-adjoint, because for any such
(\alpha,\beta)
at least two different self-adjoint extensions of
G_{\alpha,\beta}
are constructed. Currently, we are not
able to verify if for
|\alpha|<1
and
|\beta|\geq 1
,
G_{\alpha,\beta}
is or is not essentially self-adjoint and the same lack of answer concerns
G_{0,0}
.

3.
\mathcal{G}^{\circ}
-transform and its inverse

In this section we define a transform which will be crucial for the spectral analysis of the Grushin-type operators. Using the scaled Laguerre transform
in this definition was motivated by

Let
\alpha,\beta>-1

on

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f
as a function on

\Psi_{n,\tau}^{\circ}(r,s):=\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n,\tau}(r)\tilde{J}_{\beta,\tau}^{\circ}(s),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\tau>0,

for any

(3.2)

\mathcal{G}^{\circ}

Example 3.3
.

Self-adjoint extensions of
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}

For
\alpha>-1
consider the function

\Theta_{\alpha}\colon\mathbb{N}\times(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty),\qquad\Theta_{\alpha}(n,\tau)=\lambda^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}\tau,

and the corresponding multiplication operator
\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}:=\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\Theta_{\alpha}}
on
L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})
,

\displaystyle{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\big\{F\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})\colon\{\lambda^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}\tau F(n,\tau)\}_{(n,\tau)\in\mathbb{N}\times(0,\infty)}\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})\big\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}F(n,\tau)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}\tau F(n,\tau)\quad{\rm for}\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\,\tau>0,\qquad F\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}.

Then
\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}
is self-adjoint and nonnegative, and the spectrum of
\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}
coincides with
[0,\infty)
; see Lemma
6.1
.
Observe also, that as a subspace of
L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})
,
{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}
does not depend on
\alpha>-1
. This
is because the sequences
\{\lambda^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
are comparable for different
\alpha>-1
; we have

{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}=\{F\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})\colon\{(n+1)\tau F(n,\tau)\}_{(n,\tau)\in\mathbb{N}\times(0,\infty)}\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})\big\},\qquad\alpha>-1.

Now, for
\alpha,\beta>-1
, we define
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
, the transfer of
\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}
on

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;-1}\colon L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})\to L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)

\displaystyle{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;-1}({\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha})=\{f\in L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)\colon\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;-1}\circ\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}\circ\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ},

It follows that

\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
is

.

The following theorem is the main result of this section. In its proof we use Proposition

whose justification requires relatively technical

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>-1
. Then each of the operators,
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
,
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{-\alpha,\beta}
when
\alpha<1
,
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,-\beta}
when
\beta<1
, and

\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{-\alpha,-\beta}
when
\alpha<1
and
\beta<1

G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}

\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{-\alpha,\beta}
differ when

,
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,-\beta}
differ when
0<\beta<1
, and
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{-\alpha,-\beta}
differ when
0<\alpha<1
and
0<\beta<1
.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha,\beta>-1
. With the aid of (
3.2
) it follows (notice that
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
maps
C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)
into itself) that

C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)\subset{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
. For the main claim of the theorem it remains to show that

\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi=G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi,\qquad\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big),

(4.1)

because, as operators on
C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)
,
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
remain unchanged when
\alpha
is replaced by
-\alpha
, and/or
\beta
is replaced by
-\beta
.
Now, to check (

) it suffices to verify that

\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi)=\mathcal{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi),\qquad\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big),

which means checking that for every

and

, we have

\Theta_{\alpha}(n,\tau)\mathcal{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi(n,\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi(r,s)\Psi^{\circ}_{n,\tau}(r,s)\,dr\,ds.

But this holds because

\displaystyle\Theta_{\alpha}(n,\tau)\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(r,s)\Psi^{\circ}_{n,\tau}(r,s)\,dr\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(r,s)G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\Psi^{\circ}_{n,\tau}(r,s)\,dr\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi(r,s)\Psi^{\circ}_{n,\tau}(r,s)\,dr\,ds,

where in the last step an integration by parts was used. Thus (
4.1
) follows.

The differences between the pairs of operators described in the last sentence of the theorem are consequences of differences between the corresponding heat kernels; see Proposition
4.3
.
∎

The functional calculus for the self-adjoint operator
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
,
\alpha,\beta>-1
, will be the realization of the functional calculus for

\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}
mapped by
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
onto
L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)
. According to the remarks following Lemma
6.1
, in the particular
case of
L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})
and
\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\alpha}
as the multiplication operator by
\Theta_{\alpha}
, given a Borel function
\Phi\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
we set

\Phi(\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\Theta_{\alpha}})=M^{\circ}_{\Phi\circ\Theta_{\alpha}}
.

Consequently, the functional calculus for the self-adjoint operator
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
is the mapping

\Phi\to\Phi(\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}):=\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;-1}\circ M^{\circ}_{\Phi\circ\Theta_{\alpha}}\circ\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ},

so that the domain of
\Phi(\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})
is

{\rm Dom}\,\Phi(\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}):=\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;-1}({\rm Dom}\,M^{\circ}_{\Phi\circ\Theta_{\alpha}})=\{f\in L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)\colon\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f\in{\rm Dom}\,M^{\circ}_{\Phi\circ\Theta_{\alpha}}\}.

Obviously,
\Phi(\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})
inherits all the spectral properties of
\Phi(\mathbb{M}^{\circ}_{\Theta_{\alpha}})
.

We now apply the above comments to the bounded functions
\Phi_{t}(y)=e^{-ty}
,
y\geq 0
, where
t>0
is a parameter. The corresponding family

\{\exp(-t\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})\}_{t>0}
is the one-parameter semigroup of operators bounded on
L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\big)
, called the heat semigroup for
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
. We prove that these operators are integral operators; the family of the corresponding kernels
\{K^{\circ}_{t;\alpha,\beta}\}_{t>0}
is then called the heat kernel for
\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
.

In what follows
I_{\alpha}
stands for the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order
\alpha
, see
[
6
, Sec. 5.7]
. The statement and the proof
of Proposition
4.4
, which is an auxiliary technical result used in the proof of the following theorem, is postponed until the end of this section.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>-1
. For every
t>0
,
\exp(-t\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})
is an integral operator with kernel

K^{\circ}_{t;\alpha,\beta}\big((r,s),(u,v)\big)=\sqrt{rusv}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\beta}(\tau s)J_{\beta}(\tau v)\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\tau(r^{2}+u^{2})}{\tanh 2t\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau ru}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big)\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sinh 2t\tau}\,d\tau,

that is, for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
,

\exp(-t\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})f(r,s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}K^{\circ}_{t;\alpha,\beta}\big((r,s),(u,v)\big)f(u,v)\,du\,dv.

(4.2)

Proof.

Firstly, we notice that an easy computation with help of the asymptotics

J_{\beta}(y)\simeq y^{\beta}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad I_{\alpha}(y)\simeq y^{\alpha},\qquad y\to 0^{+},

(4.3)

the bounds

J_{\beta}(y)=O(y^{-1/2})\qquad{\rm and}\qquad I_{\alpha}(y)=O(e^{y}y^{-1/2}),\qquad y\to\infty,

(4.4)

and the limits
\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\frac{y}{\sinh y}=\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\frac{y}{\tanh y}=\lim_{y\to\infty}\tanh y=1
, shows that
the integral representing
K^{\circ}_{t;\alpha,\beta}\big((r,s),(u,v)\big)
indeed converges for every
(r,s),(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
.
Secondly, notice that by Proposition
4.4
and Schwarz‘ inequality, the integral in (
4.2
) converges for every
(r,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
.

Using the comments preceding the statement of Theorem
4.2
, we obtain for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
,

\exp(-t\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})f(r,s)=\mathcal{H}^{\circ}_{\beta}\Big[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-t\tau\lambda^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f(n,\tau)\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n,\tau}(r)\Big](s).

(4.5)

Note that by (
3.7
)
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f(n,\tau)\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})
, hence
for almost every
r>0
, the function of
\tau
inside the square brackets in (
4.5
)
is in
L^{2}(0,\infty)
so that
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}_{\beta}
can be applied.

From now on we temporarily assume that
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. For such
f
the sum in (
4.5
) becomes

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-t\tau\lambda^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f(n,\tau)\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n,\tau}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u,v)\tilde{J}^{\circ}_{\beta,\tau}(v)\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-t\tau\lambda^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}}\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n,\tau}(u)\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n,\tau}(r)\Big)\,du\,dv

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u,v)\tilde{J}^{\circ}_{\beta,\tau}(v)\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-2t\tau(2n+\alpha+1)}\tau^{1/4}\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}(\sqrt{\tau}u)\tau^{1/4}\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}(\sqrt{\tau}r)\Big)\,du\,dv

\displaystyle=\tau^{1/2}e^{-2t\tau(\alpha+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u,v)\tilde{J}^{\circ}_{\beta,\tau}(v)\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-4t\tau n}\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}(\sqrt{\tau}u)\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}(\sqrt{\tau}r)\Big)\,du\,dv.

Notice that exchanging summation with integration was possible since, assuming that
{\rm supp}\,f\subset[a,b]\times[a,b]
,
0<a<b<\infty
, and
for
t,\tau,r>0
fixed, we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-4t\tau n}\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}|\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}(\sqrt{\tau}u)\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}(\sqrt{\tau}r)|\,du\,dv\leq\frac{b-a}{\sqrt{\tau}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-4t\tau n}|\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}(\sqrt{\tau}r)|\|\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}\|_{L^{1}(0,\infty)}<\infty,

and this is enough for our purposes; we used the fact that the norms
\|\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}\|_{L^{1}(0,\infty)}
and
\|\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty)}
grow at most polynomially in
n\to\infty
; see e.g.
[
10
, (4.1)]
.

The closed form of the sum in (
4.5
) is now obtained by the formula (see
[
6
, 4.17.6]
),

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}L^{\alpha}_{n}(x)L^{\alpha}_{n}(y)w^{n}=\frac{1}{1-w}\exp\big(-\frac{w}{1-w}(x+y)\big)\frac{1}{(xyw)^{\alpha/2}}I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{2w^{1/2}}{1-w}(xy)^{1/2}\Big),

valid for
|w|<1
,
\alpha>-1
. Namely,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-4t\tau n}\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}(\sqrt{\tau}u)\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}(\sqrt{\tau}r)=\frac{e^{2t\tau(\alpha+1)}}{\sinh 2t\tau}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\tau(r^{2}+u^{2})}{\tanh 2t\tau}\Big)(\tau ru)^{1/2}I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau ru}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big),

and consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-t\tau\lambda^{\alpha,\circ}_{n}}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f(n,\tau)\ell^{\alpha,\circ}_{n,\tau}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau^{1/2}}{\sinh 2t\tau}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u,v)\tilde{J}^{\circ}_{\beta,\tau}(v)\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\tau(r^{2}+u^{2})}{\tanh 2t\tau}\Big)(\tau ru)^{1/2}I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau ru}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big)\,du\,dv.

Treating the above expression as a function of
\tau
and evaluating the Hankel transform
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}_{\beta}
of it at
s
finally shows that
\exp(-t\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})f(r,s)

equals

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{J}^{\circ}_{\beta,\tau}(s)\frac{\tau^{1/2}}{\sinh 2t\tau}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u,v)\tilde{J}^{\circ}_{\beta,\tau}(v)\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\tau(r^{2}+u^{2})}{\tanh 2t\tau}\Big)\sqrt{\tau ur}I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau ru}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big)\,du\,dv\,d\tau

\displaystyle\,\,\,=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}K^{\circ}_{t;\alpha,\beta}\big((r,s),(u,v)\big)f(u,v)\,du\,dv,

which proves (
4.2
) for
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
with
K^{\circ}_{t;\alpha,\beta}
as in the statement of Theorem
4.2
.
Notice that exchanging the order of integration was possible since, assuming again
f
to be supported in
[a,b]\times[a,b]
, and considering
for simplicity of notation the case
t=1/2
only (the proof in the general case
t>0
goes with minor changes), for
r,s>0
fixed, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}|J_{\beta}(\tau s)|\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sinh\tau}\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}(uv)^{1/2}|J_{\beta}(\tau v)|\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\tau(r^{2}+u^{2})}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau ru}{\sinh\tau}\Big)\,du\,dv\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau|J_{\beta}(\tau s)|\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{1+\alpha}\int_{a}^{b}|J_{\beta}(\tau v)|\,dv\,d\tau<\infty,

and this is enough for our purpose. We used the fact that
\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}<1
for
\tau\in(0,\infty)
, then we applied
(
4.3
) to bound
I_{\alpha}
and cancel integration with respect to the

variable. The concluding inequality easily follows by using (
4.3
)
and (
4.4
) to bound

.

For general
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
we now argue as follows. Let
\{f_{n}\}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
be such that
f_{n}\to f

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, hence also

\exp(-t\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})f_{n}\to\exp(-t\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})f

. With

,
\alpha,\beta
fixed, define

T^{\circ}g(r,s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}K^{\circ}_{t;\alpha,\beta}\big((r,s),(u,v)\big)g(u,v)\,du\,dv,\qquad g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}),\quad(r,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}.

By Proposition
4.4
this definition is correct. By what we have already proved, combined with Proposition

and Schwarz‘ inequality we obtain

\exp(-t\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})f_{n}(r,s)=T^{\circ}f_{n}(r,s)\to T^{\circ}f(r,s),\qquad n\to\infty,

for every

. It follows that
\exp(-t\mathbb{G}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})f(r,s)=T^{\circ}f(r,s)
,
(r,s)
-a.e.
∎

Observe that
K^{\circ}_{t;\alpha,\beta}\big((r,s),(u,v)\big)
is continuous jointly in

, and

(this may be verified by elementary means), symmetric in

(4.6)

In addition, using

{\cos}\end{matrix}\right\}(y)\quad{\rm and}\quad I_{\pm 1/2}(y)=\Big(\frac{2}{\pi y}\Big)^{1/2}\left\{\begin{matrix}{\sinh}\\

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,K^{\circ}_{t;-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}\big((r,s),(u,v)\big)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\tau s\cos\tau v\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\tau(r^{2}+u^{2})}{\tanh 2t\tau}\Big)\sinh\Big(\frac{\tau ru}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big)\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh 2t\tau}\Big)^{1/2}\,d\tau.

Proposition 4.3
.

The heat kernels
\{K^{\circ}_{t;\alpha,\beta}\}_{t>0}
differ from each other for each of the following two pairs of type parameters taken from the set

(-1,1)\times(-1,1)
: i)
(\alpha,\beta)
and
(\alpha,-\beta)
,
\beta\neq 0
; ii)
(\alpha,\beta)
and
(-\alpha,\beta)
,
\alpha\neq 0
; iii)
(\alpha,\beta)
and
(-\alpha,-\beta)
,
\alpha\neq 0
and
\beta\neq 0
.

Proof.

It suffices to show the difference for particular
t>0
in each of the three considered cases; we choose
t=1/2
for each of these cases.
Then, on the diagonal, when
u=r
and
v=s
, we obtain

K^{\circ}_{1/2;\,\alpha,\beta}\big((r,s),(r,s)\big)=rs\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\beta}(\tau s)^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau r^{2}}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau r^{2}}{\sinh\tau}\Big)\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sinh\tau}\,d\tau,

In case i), setting
r=1
in the above integral and denoting

F_{\alpha,\beta}^{(1)}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\beta}(\tau s)^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sinh\tau}\,d\tau,\qquad s>0,

we check that the functions
F_{\alpha,\beta}^{(1)}
and
F_{\alpha,-\beta}^{(1)}
differ. More precisely, we check that
F_{\alpha,\pm\beta}^{(1)}(s)
have different
behavior when
s\to 0^{+}
. This will be a consequence of different asymptotics of
J_{\pm\beta}
at zero, see (
4.3
) ((
4.3
) and (
4.4
)
are frequently used in this proof and we shall not recall them).
First, we verify that

T(A):=\int_{A}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\,d\tau

decays exponentially when
A\to\infty
. Indeed, for
\tau
large,

I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)\lesssim\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{\alpha}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau}{\tanh\tau}\Big)\leq\exp(-\tau),

hence

T(A)\lesssim\int_{A}^{\infty}\exp(-\tau)\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{\alpha+1}\,d\tau\lesssim\int_{A}^{\infty}\exp(-\tau)\,d\tau\lesssim\exp(-A).

Now, to fix the attention, assume
\beta>0
and estimate
F_{\alpha,\beta}^{(1)}(s)
on
0<s<1
from above using
J_{\beta}(\tau s)=O\big((\tau s)^{\beta}\big)

for
\tau<\frac{1}{s}
, and
J_{\beta}(\tau s)=O((\tau s)^{-1/2})
for
\tau\geq\frac{1}{s}
,

\displaystyle F_{\alpha,\beta}^{(1)}(s)

\displaystyle\lesssim s^{2\beta}\int_{0}^{1/s}\frac{\tau^{2+2\beta}}{\sinh\tau}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)\,d\tau

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+\frac{1}{s}\int_{1/s}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim s^{2\beta}+\frac{1}{s}\exp(-1/s)\lesssim s^{2\beta}.

On the other hand, using
J_{-\beta}(\tau s)\simeq(\tau s)^{-\beta}
for
\tau<\frac{1}{s}
, we estimate
F_{\alpha,-\beta}^{(1)}(s)
on
0<s<1
from below,

\displaystyle F_{\alpha,-\beta}^{(1)}(s)

\displaystyle\gtrsim s^{-2\beta}\int_{0}^{1/s}\frac{\tau^{2-\beta}}{\sinh\tau}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)\,d\tau\gtrsim s^{-2\beta},

simply by limiting the integration to the interval
(0,1)
.

In case ii), setting
s=1
in the relevant integral, and denoting

F_{\alpha,\beta}^{(2)}(r)=\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\beta}(\tau)^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau r^{2}}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau r^{2}}{\sinh\tau}\Big)\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sinh\tau}\,d\tau,\qquad r>0,

we verify that the functions
F_{\alpha,\beta}^{(2)}
and
F_{-\alpha,\beta}^{(2)}
differ by checking, similarly as in case i), that
F_{\pm\alpha,\beta}^{(2)}(r)
behave
differently when
r\to 0^{+}
. This will be a consequence of different asymptotics of
I_{\pm\alpha}
at zero.

To fix the attention assume that
\alpha>0
. For
0<r<1
and
0<\tau<\infty
we have
0<\frac{\tau r^{2}}{\sinh\tau}<1
.
Hence, estimating first
I_{\alpha}
, and then
F_{\alpha,\beta}^{(2)}(r)
on
0<r<1
from above gives

F_{\alpha,\beta}^{(2)}(r)\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\beta}(\tau)^{2}\Big(\frac{\tau r^{2}}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{\alpha}\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sinh\tau}\,d\tau=r^{2\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\beta}(\tau)^{2}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{1+\alpha}\tau\,d\tau\lesssim r^{2\alpha}.

Similarly, estimating first
I_{\alpha}
, then
J_{\beta}
and
F_{-\alpha,\beta}^{(2)}(r)
on
0<r<1
from below gives

\displaystyle F_{-\alpha,\beta}^{(2)}(r)\gtrsim r^{-2\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}J_{\beta}(\tau)^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau r^{2}}{\tanh\tau}\Big)\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{1-\alpha}\tau\,d\tau

\displaystyle\gtrsim r^{-2\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}\tau^{2\beta+1}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{1-\alpha}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\gtrsim r^{-2\alpha}.

Case iii) follows from case ii) since
F_{\alpha,\beta}^{(2)}
and
F_{-\alpha,-\beta}^{(2)}
differ.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>-1
. For every
t>0
and
(r,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
we have

K^{\circ}_{t;\alpha,\beta}\big((r,s),(\cdot,\cdot)\big)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}).

Proof.

For simplicity of notation we consider the case
t=1/2
only, the proof in the general case
t>0
goes with minor changes.
Now with
\alpha
and
\beta
fixed, we write
K
in place of
K^{\circ}_{1/2;\alpha,\beta}
, and setting

F_{(r,s);u}(\tau):=\sqrt{rsu}J_{\beta}(\tau s)\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\tau(r^{2}+u^{2})}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau ur}{\sinh\tau}\Big)\frac{\tau^{3/2}}{\sinh\tau},

we have

K\big((r,s),(u,v)\big)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\tau v)^{1/2}J_{\beta}(\tau v)F_{(r,s);u}(\tau)\,d\tau=\mathcal{H}_{\beta}F_{(r,s);u}(v).

Notice that
F_{(r,s);u}\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
. Then, by applying Plancherel‘s identity for
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|K\big((r,s),(u,v)\big)|^{2}\,dudv=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{H}_{\beta}F_{(r,s);u}(v)|^{2}\,dvdu=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|F_{(r,s);u}(\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau du.

It remains to check that the last double integral is finite. Again, to simplify the notation we consider the case
r=s=1
only; the proof for general
r,s>0

is analogous. Then we split the inner integration onto
(0,1)
and
(1,\infty)
, and use the bounds (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) for
J_{\beta}
, to be left with the integrals

A_{0}=\int_{0}^{\infty}u\int_{0}^{1}\tau^{2\beta}\frac{\tau^{3}}{\sinh^{2}\tau}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau(1+u^{2})}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau u}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{2}\,d\tau du

and

A_{\infty}=\int_{0}^{\infty}u\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{\tau^{3}}{\sinh^{2}\tau}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau(1+u^{2})}{\tanh\tau}\Big)I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau u}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{2}\,d\tau du

to bound. For
A_{0}
, using
\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}<1<\frac{\tau}{\tanh\tau}
on
(0,1)
and the asymptotic (
4.3
) for
I_{\alpha}
gives

A_{0}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2\alpha+1}\exp(-u^{2})\int_{0}^{1}\tau^{2\beta+1}\,d\tau du<\infty.

To estimate
A_{\infty}
, using first
1<\frac{\tau}{\tanh\tau}
on
(0,1)
, changing the order of integration and then splitting the inner integration appropriately to apply the bound (
4.4
) on
I_{\alpha}
, we write

\displaystyle A_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{1}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}u\exp(-u^{2})I_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\tau u}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{2}\,du\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{1}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{2\alpha+2}\int_{0}^{\frac{\sinh\tau}{\tau}}u^{2\alpha+1}\exp(-u^{2})\,du\,d\tau+\int_{1}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{\frac{3}{2}}\int_{\frac{\sinh\tau}{\tau}}^{\infty}u^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp(-u^{2})\,du\,d\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{1}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{2(\alpha+1)}\,d\tau+\int_{1}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\tau}{\sinh\tau}\Big)^{3/2}\,d\tau<\infty.

∎

5.
Sesquilinear forms and self-adjoint extensions of
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}

We begin with recalling a necessary part of a theory that links nonnegative sesquilinear forms and nonnegative self-adjoint operators on Hilbert spaces.
For details, see, e.g.,
[
9
, Chapter 10]
,
[
8
]
.

In the general setting, for a (sesquilinear) form
\mathfrak{s}
given on a Hilbert space
(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H})
with dense domain

{\rm Dom}\,\mathfrak{s}
, the associated operator
A_{\mathfrak{s}}
is defined by

\displaystyle{\rm Dom}\,A_{\mathfrak{s}}

\displaystyle=\{h\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathfrak{s}\colon\exists u_{h}\in H\,\,\,\forall h^{\prime}\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathfrak{s}\,\,\,\,\mathfrak{s}[h,h^{\prime}]=\langle u_{h},h^{\prime}\rangle_{H}\},

\displaystyle A_{\mathfrak{s}}h

\displaystyle=u_{h}\quad{\rm for}\quad h\in{\rm Dom}\,A_{\mathfrak{s}}.

Then, if
\mathfrak{s}
is Hermitian closed and nonnegative,
A_{\mathfrak{s}}
is self-adjoint and nonnegative. Recall that a nonnegative form
\mathfrak{s}

is called closed provided the norm
\|h\|_{\mathfrak{s}}=\big(\mathfrak{s}[h,h]^{2}+\langle h,h\rangle_{H}^{2}\big)^{1/2}
defined on
{\rm Dom}\,\mathfrak{s}
is complete.

On the other hand, if
A
is a self-adjoint nonnegative operator on
H
, then the associated form is
\mathfrak{s}_{A}[x,y]:=\langle A^{1/2}x,A^{1/2}y\rangle_{H}

with domain
{\rm Dom}\,\mathfrak{s}_{A}:={\rm Dom}\,A^{1/2}
;
\mathfrak{s}_{A}
is a densely defined Hermitian closed nonnegative form.

It is an important observation in this theory that the mappings
\mathfrak{s}\mapsto A_{\mathfrak{s}}
and
A\mapsto\mathfrak{s}_{A}

are mutually reciprocal bijections between the set of all densely defined Hermitian closed nonnegative forms on
H
and the set of all self-adjoint
nonnegative operators on
H
. Notably,
\mathfrak{s}_{A_{\mathfrak{s}}}=\mathfrak{s}
and
A_{\mathfrak{s}_{A}}=A
.

It is also well known that any densely defined symmetric and nonnegative (more generally, lower semibounded) operator
S
has a self-adjoint extension
which is also nonnegative (more generally, lower semibounded, which preserves the lower bound). The construction of this operator, denoted
S_{F}
and
nowadays called the Friedrichs extension of
S
, was given by Friedrichs in 1933. It goes as follows. Let
S
be as above and let
\mathfrak{s}_{S}
be
defined as
\mathfrak{s}_{S}[x,y]=\langle Sx,y\rangle_{H}
on
{\rm Dom}\,S
so that
\mathfrak{s}_{S}
is densely defined Hermitian and nonnegative.
But more importantly,
\mathfrak{s}_{S}
is closable, see
[
9
, Lemma 10.16]
. Let
\overline{\mathfrak{s}_{S}}
be the closure of
\mathfrak{s}_{S}
.
Although the completion procedure in the definition of
\overline{\mathfrak{s}_{S}}
is abstract from its nature it can be shown that
\overline{\mathfrak{s}_{S}}
may be realized in
H
, which means, in particular, that
{\rm Dom}\,\overline{\mathfrak{s}_{S}}\subset H
. Then,
S_{F}
is just
A_{\overline{\mathfrak{s}_{S}}}
,
the operator associated to
\overline{\mathfrak{s}_{S}}
. See, e.g.,
[
9
, Definition 10.6]
.

Coming back to the main line of our consideration, fix
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and define the operators

\mathfrak{d}_{1}^{\circ}:=\mathfrak{d}_{1;\alpha}^{\circ}=\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\frac{\alpha+1/2}{r},\qquad\mathfrak{d}_{1}^{\circ,\dagger}:=\mathfrak{d}_{1;\alpha}^{\circ,\dagger}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\frac{\alpha+1/2}{r},

and

\mathfrak{d}_{2}^{\circ}:=\mathfrak{d}_{2;\beta}^{\circ}=r\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial s}-\frac{\beta+1/2}{s}\Big),\qquad\mathfrak{d}_{2}^{\circ,\dagger}:=\mathfrak{d}_{2;\beta}^{\circ,\dagger}=r\Big(-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}-\frac{\beta+1/2}{s}\Big),

each with
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
as domain. It is easily seen that
\mathfrak{d}_{1}^{\circ,\dagger}
and
\mathfrak{d}_{2}^{\circ,\dagger}
,
are the restrictions of the adjoint operators
\mathfrak{d}_{1}^{\circ,*}
and
\mathfrak{d}_{2}^{\circ,*}
to
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
.
In particular, for
j=1,2
, it holds

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\mathfrak{d}_{j}^{\circ,\dagger}\varphi(r,s)\overline{\psi(r,s)}\,drds=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\varphi(r,s)\overline{\mathfrak{d}_{j}^{\circ}\psi(r,s)}\,drds,\qquad\varphi,\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}).

We call
\mathfrak{d}_{j}^{\circ}
,
j=1,2
,
the partial delta-derivatives
associated to
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Essential for further development is the decomposition

G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}=\mathfrak{d}_{1}^{\circ,\dagger}\mathfrak{d}_{1}^{\circ}+\mathfrak{d}_{2}^{\circ,\dagger}\mathfrak{d}_{2}^{\circ};

notice that
\mathfrak{d}_{j}^{\circ}(C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}))\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
,
j=1,2
. Observe that for fixed
\alpha,\beta,
the decomposition of

G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
of this type is not unique, since the choice of
\mathfrak{d}_{1;\pm\alpha}^{\circ}
and
\mathfrak{d}_{2;\pm\beta}^{\circ}
,
with the signs
+/-
taken independently, brings the same outcome.

Connected to the partial delta-derivatives is the concept of the weak partial derivatives.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
be given,
f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, and
j\in\{1,2\}
. We say that the weak (partial)
\mathfrak{d}_{j}^{\circ}
-derivative of
f
exists provided there is
g_{j}\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\mathfrak{d}_{j}^{\circ,\dagger}\varphi(r,s)\overline{f(r,s)}\,drds=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\varphi(r,s)\overline{g_{j}(r,s)}\,drds,\qquad\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}).

Then we set
\mathfrak{d}_{j,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f:=g_{j}
and call
g_{j}
the weak
\mathfrak{d}_{j}^{\circ}
-derivative of
f
.

Clearly, for
\alpha=-1/2
, the notion of weak
\mathfrak{d}_{1}^{\circ}
-derivative coincides with the notion of the usual weak partial derivative.
We write
\partial_{j,{\rm weak}}f
, to denote the usual weak partial derivatives of
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. For general
\alpha
we have the following.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. Then, for
j=1,2
,
\mathfrak{d}_{j,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f
exists if and only if
\partial_{j,{\rm weak}}f
exists; moreover

\mathfrak{d}_{1,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f=\partial_{1,{\rm weak}}f-\frac{\alpha+1/2}{r}f\quad and\quad\mathfrak{d}_{2,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f=r\Big(\partial_{2,{\rm weak}}f-\frac{\beta+1/2}{r}f\Big).

Proof.

The proof is straightforward. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
be given. For
j=1
we verify that the conditions

\exists g_{1}\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\,\,\,\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\,\,\,\quad\int\mathfrak{d}_{1}^{\circ,\dagger}\varphi\,\overline{f}=\int\varphi\,\overline{g_{1}}

(5.1)

(here and below, integration goes over
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
with respect to
drds
) and

\exists h_{1}\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\,\,\,\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\,\,\,\quad\int\partial_{1}\varphi\,\overline{f}=-\int\varphi\,\overline{h_{1}},

(5.2)

are equivalent and, moreover,
g_{1}=h_{1}-\frac{\alpha+1/2}{r}f
. Indeed, (
5.1
) asserts that there is
g_{1}
such that

\int\partial_{1}\varphi\,\overline{f}=-\int\varphi\,\overline{g_{1}}-\int\varphi\,\frac{\alpha+1/2}{r}\overline{f}=-\int\varphi\,\overline{\big(g_{1}-\frac{\alpha+1/2}{r}f\big)},

so that (
5.2
) follows with
h_{1}=g_{1}+\frac{\alpha+1/2}{r}f
. Conversely, (
5.2
) leads to

\int\mathfrak{d}_{1}^{\circ,\dagger}\varphi\,\overline{f}=\int\varphi\,\overline{h_{1}}-\int\varphi\,\frac{\alpha+1/2}{r}\overline{f}=\int\varphi\,\overline{\big(h_{1}-\frac{\alpha+1/2}{r}f\big)},

so that (
5.1
) follows with
g_{1}=h_{1}-\frac{\alpha+1/2}{r}f
. The case
j=2
is treated completely analogously.
∎

Despite of the above result, which equalizes the existence of
\partial_{j,{\rm weak}}f
with that of
\mathfrak{d}_{j,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f
, the concept
of
\mathfrak{d}_{j}^{\circ}
-derivatives is still convenient in the following definition of Sobolev-type spaces (of first order) connected to
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. The delta-Sobolev space
W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
is the space

W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})=\big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\colon\mathfrak{d}_{j,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f\,\,exist\,\,for\,\,j=1,2,\,\,and\,\,are\,\,in\,\,\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\big\},

equipped with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}=\langle f,g\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\langle\mathfrak{d}_{j,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f,\mathfrak{d}_{j,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}g\rangle.

The closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
in
W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}
generated by

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}
is denoted
W_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
.

The following proposition has a relatively standard proof hence we omit it.

Proposition 5.4
.

The delta-Sobolev space
W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
is a Hilbert space.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Define the form
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}=\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
with domain
W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
by

\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}[f,g]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\mathfrak{d}_{j,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f(r,s)\overline{\mathfrak{d}_{j,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}g(r,s)}\,drds.

The form
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}
restricted to
W_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
is denoted
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}
.
Thus
{\rm Dom}\,\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}=W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and
{\rm Dom}\,\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}=W_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. Obviously,
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}
and

\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}
are Hermitian nonnegative and densely defined on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
.

In what follows,
\mathbb{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
and
\mathbb{D}_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
denote the operators associated with forms
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}
and

\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}
, respectively.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>-1
. The operators
\mathbb{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
and
\mathbb{D}_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
are self-adjoint and nonnegative extensions of
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
.
Moreover,
\mathbb{D}_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
is the Friedrichs extension of
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Proof.

To complete the list of properties of
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}
we observe that
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}
is closed; this fact is just completeness of the norm

\|\cdot\|_{W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}
. Consequently,
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}
is closed. Thus, by the general theory,
\mathbb{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
and

\mathbb{D}_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
are self-adjoint and nonnegative. We now check that they extend
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
. From the general definition we have

{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{D}_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}=\{f\in W_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\colon\exists u_{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\,\,\,\forall g\in W_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\,\,\,\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}[f,g]=\langle u_{f},g\rangle\},

and
\mathbb{D}_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f=u_{f}
for
f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{D}_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
, and similarly for
\mathbb{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
.

We claim that
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\subset{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{D}_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
and
\mathbb{D}_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}\varphi=G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi
for

\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, and analogously for
\mathbb{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
. For this purpose it suffices to check (in both cases) that given

\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, for every
g\in W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
it holds

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\mathfrak{d}_{j}^{\circ}\varphi\,\overline{\mathfrak{d}_{j,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}g}=\langle G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi,g\rangle

(5.3)

(integration with respect to
drds
here and below). But expanding both sides of (
5.3
) with the aid of Lemma
5.2
, canceling common terms and abbreviating
\alpha^{*}:=\alpha+1/2
,
\beta^{*}:=\beta+1/2
,
\partial_{j}g:=\partial_{j,{\rm weak}}g
leads to
the equivalent version of (
5.3
), namely

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\big(\partial_{1}\varphi-\frac{\alpha^{*}}{r}\varphi\big)\overline{\partial_{1}g}+

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}r^{2}\big(\partial_{2}\varphi-\frac{\beta^{*}}{r}\varphi\big)\overline{\partial_{2}g}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\Big(\partial_{1}\big(\partial_{1}\varphi-\frac{\alpha^{*}}{r}\varphi\big)+\partial_{2}\big(r^{2}\big(\partial_{2}\varphi-\frac{\beta^{*}}{r}\varphi\big)\big)\Big)\overline{g}.

(5.4)

Now, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
be a bounded open subset containing
{\rm supp}\,\varphi
, separated from the boundary of
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, with
\partial\Omega
being
sufficiently smooth.
Equivalently, we prove (
5
) with integration over
\Omega
replacing
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. For this, abbreviating further

\psi_{1}:=\partial_{1}\varphi-\frac{\alpha^{*}}{r}\varphi
,
\psi_{2}:=r^{2}(\partial_{2}\varphi-\frac{\beta^{*}}{r}\varphi)
, so that
\psi_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, it suffices to check that

\int_{\Omega}\psi_{1}\overline{\partial_{1}g}=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{1}\psi_{1}\,\overline{g}\quad{\rm and}\quad\int_{\Omega}\psi_{2}\,\overline{\partial_{2}g}=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{2}\psi_{2}\,\overline{g}.

(5.5)

But
g\in W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
hence, with the aid of Lemma
5.2
, it follows that
g\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, where the
H^{1}(\Omega)

stands for the Euclidean Sobolev space. Thus, the assumptions needed in Gauss’ formula, cf.
[
9
, D.4, p. 408]
, are satisfied, hence (
5.5
) holds true.
This finishes verification of the claim and hence the proof of
the main part of the theorem.

It remains to prove that
\mathbb{D}_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}=\big(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\big)_{F}
. We take the form

\mathfrak{s}^{\circ}[f,g]=\langle G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}f,g\rangle

on the domain
{\rm Dom}\,\mathfrak{s}^{\circ}=C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and consider its closure
\overline{\mathfrak{s}^{\circ}}
. We claim that

\overline{\mathfrak{s}^{\circ}}=\mathfrak{t}_{0}^{\circ}.

(5.6)

This is enough for our purpose since then, with the previous notation, we have

{\rm Dom}\,(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})_{F}={\rm Dom}\,A_{\overline{\mathfrak{s}^{\circ}}}={\rm Dom}\,A_{\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}},

and as one immediately sees, the latter space coincides with
{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{D}^{\circ}_{0;\alpha,\beta}
. Moreover, it follows that

(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})_{F}f=\mathbb{D}^{\circ}_{0;\alpha,\beta}f
for
f
from these joint domains. Returning to (
5.6
), we note that it is a consequence of the fact
that
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
lies densely in
{\rm Dom}\,\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}=W_{0;\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}
is closed. Here are details. Clearly
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}
extends
\mathfrak{s}^{\circ}
and hence the inclusion
\subset
follows. For the opposite inclusion let

f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}_{0}=W_{0;\,\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and take
\{\varphi_{n}\}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
such that
\varphi_{n}\to f
in
W_{0;\,\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. In particular, this means that
\varphi_{n}\to f
and
\mathfrak{d}^{\circ}_{j}\varphi_{n}\to\mathfrak{d}^{\circ}_{j,{\rm weak}}f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
,
j=1,2
.
We want to show that
f\in{\rm Dom}\,\overline{\mathfrak{s}^{\circ}}
.

But for this purpose it suffices (see
[
9
, p.224]
) to ensure existence of
\{\psi_{n}\}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
convergent to
f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})

and such that
\mathfrak{s}^{\circ}[\psi_{n}-\psi_{m},\psi_{n}-\psi_{m}]\to 0
as
n,m\to\infty
. But

\mathfrak{s}^{\circ}[\psi_{n}-\psi_{m},\psi_{n}-\psi_{m}]=\langle G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}(\psi_{n}-\psi_{m}),\psi_{n}-\psi_{m}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\langle\mathfrak{d}^{\circ}_{j}(\psi_{n}-\psi_{m}),\mathfrak{d}^{\circ}_{j}(\psi_{n}-\psi_{m})\rangle

and the latter required convergence to 0 follows since
\mathfrak{d}^{\circ}_{j}\psi_{n}
,
j=1,2
, being convergent in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, are Cauchy sequences there.
∎

Finally, we define the minimal and the maximal operators related to
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
. These two operators are important because self-adjoint extensions of

G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
lie in between. We follow the well known pattern of construction of these two operators; see e.g.
[
9
, Sections 1.3.2 and 15.1]
.

First, we introduce the following concept. For
f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
we say that the weak
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
-
derivative
of
f
exist provided there is
g\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi(r,s)\overline{f(r,s)}\,drds=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\varphi(r,s)\overline{g(r,s)}\,drds,\qquad\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}).

Then we set
G_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f:=g
. Next, we define the
G^{\circ}
-Sobolev space (of order 2)

W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})=\big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\colon G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta,{\rm weak}}f\,\,{\rm exist}\,\,{\rm and}\,\,{\rm is}\,\,{\rm in}\,\,\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\big\}

Define
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}}:=\overline{G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}}
, the closure of
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
, and
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}
, the operator given by

G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}f:=G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}f\quad{\rm for}\quad f\in{\rm Dom}\,G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}:=W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}).

Notice that
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
is closable since
{\rm Dom}\,(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})^{*}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
; in addition

(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})^{*}=\big(\overline{G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}}\big)^{*}

Observe that
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}}\subset G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}
because
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}
is closed. To verify the last claim
assume that
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
are such that for some
f_{n}\in{\rm Dom}\,G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}
we have
f_{n}\to f
and
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}f_{n}\to g

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. This means that for every
n
,

\langle G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi,f_{n}\rangle=\langle\varphi,G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}f_{n}\rangle,

and passing with
n\to\infty
gives
\langle G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi,f\rangle=\langle\varphi,g\rangle
, which says that
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}f
exists and equals
g
.

Proposition 5.6
.

We have
(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}})^{*}=G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}
and
(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}})^{*}=G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}}
. Moreover,
every self-adjoint extension
S
of
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
satisfies
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}}\subset S\subset G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}
.

Proof.

We begin with the first equality and prove the inclusion
(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}})^{*}\supset G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}
. Let

f\in{\rm Dom}\,G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}
. This means that
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}f
exists and belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. In other words, it holds

\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\quad\langle G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi,f\rangle=\langle\varphi,G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}f\rangle,

which means that
f\in{\rm Dom}\,(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})^{*}={\rm Dom}\,\big(\overline{G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}}\big)^{*}
and
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}f=(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta})^{*}f=(\overline{G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}})^{*}f
.

To prove the opposite inclusion,
\subset
, let
f\in{\rm Dom}\,(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}})^{*}
. This means, in particular, that

\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\quad\langle G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi,f\rangle=\langle\varphi,(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}})^{*}f\rangle.

But this means that,
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}f
exists and equals
(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}})^{*}f
, so
f\in{\rm Dom}\,G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm max}}
.

Since
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}}
is closed, we have
(G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}})^{**}=G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm min}}
and thus the second equality is a consequence of the first one. The last claim of the proposition is obvious.
∎

An immediate but important consequence of Proposition
5.6
is that two self-adjoint extensions of
G^{\circ}_{\alpha,\beta}
differ if and only if their domains differ.

We are now prepared to state and prove the second main result of this section.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>-1
. Then
\mathbb{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}=\mathbb{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
.

Proof.

We shall prove the inclusion
\mathbb{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}\subset\mathbb{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
. This is enough due to the well-known maximality property of
self-adjoint operators: if
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are self-adjoint in
(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
and
S_{1}\subset S_{2}
, then
S_{1}=S_{2}
, see e.g.
[
9
, p. 42]
.
In fact, by Proposition
5.6
, it suffices to check that

{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}\subset{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}.

(5.7)

To do this we first reinterpret
{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
defined in terms of the
\mathcal{G}^{\circ}
transform. Recall that
{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}

consists of all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
such than
\Theta_{\alpha}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})
, see Section
4
. We claim that

{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}=W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}).

Indeed, for
\supset
take
f\in W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, so that
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
,
G_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f
exists and is in

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, i.e.,

\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\quad\langle G_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}\varphi,f\rangle=\langle\varphi,G_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f\rangle.

Now, the equivalent version of Plancherel’s identity (
3.7
) from Theorem
3.1
implies that also

\langle\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(G_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}\varphi),\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})}=\langle\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}\varphi,\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(G_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})}.

But, see (
3.2
),

\langle\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(G_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}\varphi),\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})}=\langle\Theta_{\alpha}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}\varphi,\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})}=\langle\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}\varphi,\Theta_{\alpha}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})},

hence
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(G_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f)=\Theta_{\alpha}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})
, so
f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
.
For
\subset
, let
f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
, so that
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and

\Theta_{\alpha}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}f\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\circ})
. Setting
h:=\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;-1}(\Theta_{\alpha}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ})f
, so that

h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, it is easily seen, simply by repeating the previous arguments but in the opposite order, that
G_{\alpha,\beta;{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f
exists and equals
h
, hence
f\in W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. This finishes proving our claim and thus (
5.7
) reduces to

{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}\subset W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ;2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}).

But this was essentially done in the proof of Theorem
5.5
. If fact,
f\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{D}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}
means that
f\in W_{\alpha,\beta}^{\circ}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})

and there is

we have
\mathfrak{t}^{\circ}[f,g]=\langle u_{f},g\rangle
.
In particular, taking for

\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\mathfrak{d}_{j}^{\circ}\varphi\overline{\mathfrak{d}_{j,{\rm weak}}^{\circ}f}=\langle\varphi,u_{f}\rangle.

∎

6.2.
A reminder on the spectral theory of multiplication operator

Let
X
be a set with a
\sigma
-algebra
\Xi
of subsets, and let
\mu
be a measure on
(X,\Xi)
.
For a measurable function
\Theta\colon X\to\mathbb{C}
consider the
multiplication operator
(with ‘maximal’ domain) on
L^{2}(X,\mu)
,

\displaystyle{\rm Dom}\,M_{\Theta}

\displaystyle=\{f\in L^{2}(X,\mu)\colon\Theta f\in L^{2}(X,\mu)\},

\displaystyle M_{\Theta}f

\displaystyle=\Theta f,\qquad f\in{\rm Dom}\,M_{\Theta}.

Then the following has a straightforward proof.

Lemma 6.1
.

The multiplication operator
M_{\Theta}
defined above is:

a) densely defined closed and normal (which means
M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}=M_{\Theta}^{*}M_{\Theta}
);

b) self-adjoint if and only if
\Theta
is real-valued;

c) nonnegative if and only if
\Theta
is nonnegative;

d) bounded if and only if
\Theta
is bounded.

Moreover the spectrum
\sigma(M_{\Theta})
coincides with the essential range of
\Theta
.

For a real-valued multiplier function we use the
\mathbb{M}
symbol to emphasize that the corresponding multiplier operator is self-adjoint.

One of versions of the spectral theorem states that every self-adjoint operator
S
on a Hilbert space
H
is unitarily
equivalent to a multiplication operator by a real-valued function. More precisely, given such
S
, there is a measure space
(X,\Xi,\mu)
, a real-valued measurable function
\Theta
on
X
and a unitary isomorphism
U\colon H\to L^{2}(X,\mu)
, such that
S=U^{-1}\circ\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}\circ U
, which in particular means

{\rm Dom}\,S={\rm Dom}(U^{-1}\circ\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}\circ U)=\{h\in H\colon Uh\in{\rm Dom}\,\mathbb{M}_{\Theta}\}
.

An important ingredient of the spectral theory of self-adjoint operators on Hilbert spaces is the functional calculus. For a multiplication operator
it has a simple description. Namely, with notation as above and assuming
\Theta
to be real-valued, the functional calculus is understood as the
mapping
\Phi\to M_{\Phi\circ\Theta}
assigning to any Borel function
\Phi\colon\sigma(\mathbb{M}_{\Theta})\to\mathbb{C}
the operator

\Phi(\mathbb{M}_{\Theta})=M_{\Phi\circ\Theta}
on
L^{2}(X,\mu)
, i.e. the multiplication operator by
\Phi\circ\Theta
.

Straighforward calculation.
