Revealing sassanid dyeing practices through synchrotron FTIR
Authors: Manijeh Hadian Dehkordi, Gihan Kamel, Amir Sayid Hassan Rozatian, Farah Sadat Madani, Sahar Noohi, Ahmed Refaat, Jebrael Nokandeh, Mohammadamin Emami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study employs synchrotron-based FTIR microspectroscopy and principal component analysis (PCA) to investigate natural dyes in wool textiles from the Chehrabad salt mine (Iran), dated to the Sassanid period (220–390 A.D.). We identified indigotin (blue), tannic acid (brown), quercetin and apigenin (yellow), and alizarin and carminic acid (red), with the absence of luteolin suggesting alternative native dye sources. Spectral analysis revealed dye-wool lipid interactions in the Amide and lipid regions, providing insights into Sassanid dyeing technologies and regional dye variations. Synchrotron FTIR proves a powerful non-destructive tool for archaeological textile analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25106-0.

Introduction

The discovery of naturally mummified remains in the Chehrabad salt mine, located in northwestern Iran (Fig. 1. A and B), marked a major milestone in the field of Iranian archaeology. Unearthed in the early 1990s, these mummies were remarkably well-preserved due to the high salinity of the environment1. Among the findings, Saltman No. 1—identified by his distinctive woolen clothing and leather boots (Fig. 1. C)—has become a central figure in studies of ancient textile technology and burial customs.

Archaeological- and geo-archaeological evidence comprising many remains around the mine approved that this place was also quarried and extracted in ancient times from the Achaemenid to the Islamic period1–3.

Subsequent radiocarbon analysis determined that Saltman No. 1 dates to the Sassanid period, specifically between 220 and 390 A.D1. The golden earrings, a fashionable leather boot, shorts, and clothing pieces of this mummy show that he should have been at a high level in society at that time. The textile artifacts associated with this individual, particularly the short woolen trousers featuring elaborate colored patterns, have attracted attention for their complexity and vibrancy. These items not only represent high craftsmanship but also provide a tangible link to ancient Persian dyeing practices (Fig. 2).

Textile production in the Iranian Plateau predates even ceramic traditions5, as evidenced by archaeological sites such as Ali Kosh and Zāgheh (dating back to the 8th and 7th millennia BCE, respectively), where spindle whorls have been discovered6. The importance of textile arts continued through the Achaemenid and Sassanid periods, with royal garments depicted in reliefs from Persepolis7 and elaborately dyed fabrics attributed to Persian nobility. Historical sources describe the Sassanid era as a golden age of textile innovation and trade5,8,9.

The dyeing techniques of these periods relied heavily on natural sources, including both indigenous and imported plants and insects. Common dye sources included madder (Rubia tinctorum) for red, indigo (Indigofera tinctoria) for blue, weld (Reseda luteola) for yellow, and walnut (Juglans regia) for brown (Tables 1 and 2). Despite extensive historical knowledge of these materials, identifying their molecular traces in ancient textiles presents a significant analytical challenge. Regional variations in plant species and environmental factors likely influenced dye composition, potentially leading to differences in chromophores such as luteolin or apigenin in yellow dyes10. While historical records describe Sassanid textile dyeing, molecular-level insights into regional dye sources and their interactions with wool fibers remain limited, particularly using non-destructive techniques.

Conventional methods such as HPLC-MS require destructive sampling and are often unsuitable for valuable archaeological specimens16,22. As an alternative, synchrotron-based FTIR microspectroscopy enables non-invasive, high-resolution analysis of fiber composition. When combined with multivariate statistical tools like Principal Component Analysis (PCA), this method allows researchers to identify specific dye compounds and observe their interactions with fiber structures. In this study, we utilize SR-FTIR microspectroscopy in combination with PCA to characterize the dyes in Saltman No. 1’s textiles. This study employs synchrotron-based FTIR microspectroscopy to characterize dye compounds in Saltman No. 1’s textiles, aiming to deepen our understanding of Sassanid dyeing practices and explore the impact of regional dye sources on molecular signatures.

Materials and methods

Materials and sampling strategy

Historical fiber samples were micro-extracted from the inner layers of Saltman No. 1’s half-trousers, curated at the National Museum of Iran. Sampling was conducted with minimal intervention, targeting colored regions to preserve artifact integrity. Five distinct fiber specimens-blue, brown, yellow, red, and undyed—were selected for molecular analysis.

To establish analytical benchmarks, reference wool samples were dyed using traditional Iranian techniques with natural sources including Rubia tinctorum (madder), Dactylopius coccus (cochineal), Indigofera spp. (indigo), Reseda luteola (weld), and Juglans regia (walnut), following methods described in prior studies23,24. These dyes represent major chemical classes such as anthraquinones, indigotins, flavonols, and tannins.

Madder and cochineal yield vibrant reds, indigo produce characteristic blues, and weld provides strong yellow tones. These dye plants have a well-documented historical presence across Europe and the Near East, extending from prehistoric to modern periods (LaBerge, 2018). For brown dyes, tannin-rich sources—especially Persian walnut—have been widely employed, with their active compounds (tannic acid and juglone) found in the tree’s husk, bark, and leaves21,25–28.

SR-FTIR microspectroscopic measurements

Synchrotron-based Fourier-Transform Infrared (SR-µFTIR) microspectroscopy was performed at the SESAME BM02-IR beamline (Allan, Jordan)29,30. Measurements were conducted using a Nicolet Continuum Infrared microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) coupled with an 8700 FTIR Spectrometer and a single-point MCT-A detector. Samples were mounted on a Prior motorized stage and analyzed using OMNIC v. 9.2.41 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The system was purged with dry air to minimize water vapor and carbon dioxide interference, with background spectra collected for correction.

Spectra were acquired in transmission mode using a diamond compression cell in the mid-infrared range (4000–650 cm⁻¹) at a 4 cm⁻¹ resolution, with 256 co-added scans, level 1 zero-filling, and Happ-Genzel apodization. Specac’s DC-3 Diamond Compression Cell (Specac©, UK) was used for the FTIR microscopic analysis. In principle, samples are compressed to allow a suitable/optimum thickness required for transmission mode experiments. The cell comprises two single flat, parallel crystal diamond windows of ~ 3.5 mm diameter and 0.5 mm thickness with a large clear aperture of 1.5 mm diameter.

IR mapping used Atlµs v. 9.1.24 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a 20 × 20 μm² aperture and a 32× Schwarzschild objective-condenser pair. Spectra were processed using OMNIC and Atlµs software, with Savitzky-Golay second derivatives and curve fitting performed in PeakFit v. 4.12 (Systat Software, Inc.).

Multivariate statistical analysis (MVA)

Multivariate statistical analysis in terms of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was executed using The Unscrambler© X v.10.4 software (CAMO Analytics, Oslo, Norway). The analysis was applied to the lipid region (3050 –2800 cm⁻¹) as a key spectral region. Spectra were baseline-corrected and smoothed based via Savitzky-Golay’s algorithm followed by unit-vector normalized.

Dyeing wool fibers with certain natural materials and various techniques can impact the structure of the fibers31–34. While keratin proteins form the structural backbone of wool and ultimately bind with dyes, it is the lipid composition that often controls how effectively dyes reach those binding sites.

Results

Results reveal some insights on the textile materials and techniques employed in the creation of Saltman’s colourful patterned trousers. The findings are presented in two major parts: (i) typical spectral assignment as a generic portrayal highlighting the mean FTIR spectra of the five ancient wool fibers under investigation [blue, red, brown, yellow, and undyed] compared to modern wool structural features, as well as reference dyed wool fibers [indigo, madder, cochineal, walnut, and weld]; (ii) curve fitting of particular regions, namely, the lipid spectral region (2800–3050 cm-1). Further insights were obtained from the PCA analysis.

FTIR spectral assignment

The molecular structure of wool is characterized by a sequence of absorption bands in the Mid-IR region (4000 cm-1-650 cm-1) detected as follows: (1) S-O vibrations (1700 cm-1-1000 cm-1), (2) protein amide bands (1700 cm-1-1500 cm-1), and (3) lipids structural components (3000 cm-1-2800 cm-1). Keratin, the primary protein in wool, influences wool’s interactions and characteristics, including dye uptake, and is considered fundamental in textiles’ manufacturing for its mechanical characteristics such as elasticity and strength. It is composed of disulfide bonds and α-helices cross-linking individual keratin strands and enabling wool fibers to endure mechanical and chemical processes33,35–37. Preserving the keratin integrity is a key factor in maintaining the quality and long-term durability of wool textiles38,39.

As per the archaeological context, keratin plays a key role in the preservation and analysis of ancient textiles. Marked as the main structural protein, keratin governs textiles durability over time as well as its resistance to potential environmental degradation. Its compact molecular structure together with disulfide bonds, it can protect the wool fibers, in this regard, from mechanical breakdown, moisture, or microbial activity40. There is a reasonable extent to which keratin remains intact in archaeological samples provides insights into burial conditions, preservation environments, and the original quality of the historically manufactured wool. Besides, the degradation patterns of keratin can reveal historical processing methods such as dyeing protocols, offering valuable clues and insights on textile technologies implemented in the past41. Therefore, keratin is not only important for understanding the physical state of ancient wool but also for reconstructing aspects of early textile production and use42.

In this study, keratin functional groups were identified within the characteristic spectral range of 1000–1150 cm⁻¹, as shown in the inset of Fig. 3. Cysteic acid features was detected at 1043, 1048, and 1042 cm⁻¹ in the blue, yellow, and red-dyed wool, respectively. The cysteine monoxide stretching vibrations were observed at 1080 cm⁻¹ in the blue-dyed sample and 1085 cm⁻¹ in the red-dyed sample, shifting to 1077 cm⁻¹ in the case of the yellow wool. Lastly, the cysteine dioxide (S = O) bands were predominantly detected in the yellow- and red-dyed wool samples at 1129 and 1124 cm⁻¹, respectively. In the case of the modern wool, a series of peaks were clearly identified in this spectral region marking the rich presence received at 1034 cm⁻¹, 1059 cm⁻¹, 1111 cm⁻¹, 1160 cm⁻¹. Whereas, for the ancient undyed, two major peaks were realized at 1043 cm⁻¹, and 1081 cm⁻¹.

In this study, we have explored the lipids region of the ancient dyed wool samples (Fig. 3B). Main findings comprised major lipids typical signatures; basically, the CH₃ asymmetric stretching band that was observed at ~ 2961 cm⁻¹ for the blue, the yellow, and the ancient undyed wool, whereas detected at 2960 cm⁻¹ and as a shoulder at ~ 2959 cm⁻¹ for the red and the brown-dyed wool, respectively.

Alternatively, the common CH₃ symmetric stretching peak was detected at 2877 cm⁻¹ for the ancient blue wool, 2876 cm⁻¹ in the yellow and in the red-dyed wool. The same vibration was identified at 2878 cm⁻¹ for the ancient undyed wool. The CH₂ asymmetric stretching was assigned at 2933 cm⁻¹, 2930 cm⁻¹, 2928 cm⁻¹ for the blue, red, yellow dyed wool, respectively. The same vibration was assigned to 2929 cm⁻¹ for the ancient undyed wool, with a shift towards low wavenumbers in the case of the brown-dyed wool (2924 cm⁻¹).

For the modern wool, lipids marked a distinctive structure featuring three main peaks identified at 2850 cm⁻¹, 2916 cm⁻¹, 2952 cm⁻¹.

FTIR spectral comparison and curve fitting

As demonstrated in previous studies, residual spectra those are calculated by subtracting the second derivative spectra of undyed wool from those of dyed samples can be effectively used to identify dyes, particularly when combined with spectral library matching43,44.

In this section, the residual second-derivative spectral curves resulting from the subtraction of the spectral pattern of the dyed (blue, brown, yellow, and red) and undyed wool in comparison with the spectra of the components of the proposed dyes (indigo, tannin, weld, cochineal and madder) are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Figs. 10, 11, 12 and 13a, and 13b, respectively).

Per instance, the residual second-derivative spectrum of the blue-dyed wool (labelled “Residual blue”) versus the second-derivative spectrum of the pure indigo dye (labelled “Indigo”) is reported in Fig. 10. This analysis shows that the spectral profiles of both curves in the lipids’ regions display several overlapping peaks, suggesting that the residual signal from the blue-dyed wool retains many of the same vibrational features as pure indigo. Despite the fact that the amplitudes (or peaks’ sharpness) may differ, major maxima and minima peak positions appear at similar wavenumbers. Minor shifts for peak positions can still be observed, likely due to the potential interactions between the indigo molecules and the wool fiber matrix, which can alter hydrogen bonding and/or the molecular environment. For the proteins broad region, certain peaks in the residual spectrum align well with those of indigo, confirming the dye’s presence, while other regions show differences. This is due to possible spectral contributions from other components of the dyed wool or caused by matrix effects.

Overall, such a close correspondence of key second-derivative peaks between the residual blue-dyed wool spectrum and pure indigo dye supports that the dye used in the wool is indigo. Nevertheless, amplitude differences, minor peak shifts, and additional features in the residual spectrum can still point to chemical interactions between the dye and the wool substrate, as well as possible contributions from other materials. This scenario was equally observed through the other dyed wool investigated samples.

Additionally, and as another axis of complementary examination, the comparative analysis of FTIR bands (Table 4) based on spectral database matching (IRUG Spectral Database) revealed spectral similarities between Indigotin and the blue-dyed ancient wool, indicating Indigotin as the likely source of the blue dye. Likewise, the red-dyed wool exhibited spectral features consistent with both Alizarin and carminic acid, suggesting the presence of both as red dye sources. For the brown-dyed sample, characteristic bands aligned with those of tannic acid, supporting its identification as the brown dye. Finally, the yellow dyed wool showed spectral similarities with both quercetin and apigenin, indicating these flavonoids as the likely coloring agents for that sample.

Furthermore, several studies have highlighted the importance of analyzing the protein secondary structure of wool using FTIR spectra to evaluate the impact of various chemical and physical treatments. Curve fitting of the amide I region in FTIR spectra has been widely used alongside microscopic analysis to investigate changes in wool and keratin structure under various treatments. This technique has helped identify functional groups involved in transamidation and transglutaminase activity during wool self-crosslinking45, and to assess secondary structural changes in keratin regenerated from wool using L-cysteine46. Changes in the positions and intensities of the amide I and II bands were jointly used to investigate structural transformations in wool fibers subjected to stretching and thinning, compared to the native (untreated) ones. Findings from these analyses showed that mechanical stretching may induce a shift in the secondary structure of wool proteins from an alpha-helical configuration to beta-pleated sheets47. A more detailed interpretation of the FTIR data through curve fitting procedure could provide deeper insight into the impact of dyes on the spectral features of the wool structure48,49.

In principle, the protein amide I band classically appears as a single broad peak, which confines the direct interpretation of structural details. To gain deeper insight, methods such as second derivative analysis or curve fitting are applied to deconvolute this band into its underlying individual peaks. These sub-peaks correspond to various protein secondary structures, allowing for the estimation of their relative contributions50–52. In this study, amide I curve fitting of the ancient blue, brown, yellow and red dyed wool, respectively, versus their respective proposed reference dyes was executed as illustrated in Figs. 4, 5, 6 and 7. It is worthy to note here that exact positions of peaks may shift with hydrogen bonding, solvent/water, side-chain environment and with keratin-specific structure. Well noted that dye molecules interact with the keratin protein through chemical bonding, electrostatic attraction, or hydrophobic interactions. These interactions together with the dyeing conditions (pH, temperature, mordants, etc.) can cause measurable changes in the protein’s secondary structure. Such impact of dyes on the secondary structure of wool can be detected through its secondary structure (α-helices, β-sheets, random coils, turns, etc.). Various changes in the secondary structure of the dyed wool can be identified through the disruption of the α-helices. Many dyes, especially acidic ones, bind to amino groups of lysine and arginine residues or to side chains containing hydroxyl groups. Binding can break hydrogen bonds that stabilize α-helices, leading to a decrease in α-helical content.

Equally, an increase in the β-sheet (or the random coil) content can be detected where the disruption of ordered α-helices leads to partial unfolding and rearrangement into β-sheets or amorphous regions. In addition, aggregations or crosslinking effects may occur. Such changes may be observed as peak positions’ shifts, increase or decrease in peak area or peak height. As well as disappearance or development of peaks. That is although there may be a similarity between the secondary structure evolvement of both the dyed wool and its corresponding dye, their peaks positions, areas, and heights can still differ. Overall, the curve fitting data suggest that natural dyes can influence the secondary structure of wool proteins. Herein, we detail the procedure that was followed to examine the correlation between the blue dyed wool and the indigo dye as an example. As a further examination, multivariate statistical analysis has been conducted, in particular, principal component analysis was executed over the whole set of samples including both the modern and the ancient undyed wool fibers.

Principal component analysis

As indicated, lipids exhibit a particular impact on wool dying procedures. In the wool dyeing process, lipids—though present in smaller quantities compared to proteins—play a disproportionately important role due to their influence on dye uptake and fiber accessibility. These lipids form hydrophobic barriers within and on the surface of the wool fiber, restricting the penetration of water-soluble dyes into the protein-rich keratin matrix. Unlike keratin, which is chemically stable and difficult to modify without damaging the fibers, lipids can be selectively altered and/or removed through mild treatments such as scouring, solvent extraction, or enzymatic processes. This modification may enhance the fiber’s surface energy and hydrophilicity, improving dye affinity and uniformity. Additionally, lipids interact with surfactants and dye carriers during processing, affecting dye distribution and fixation. Because these interactions significantly impact dyeing performance without compromising the structural integrity of the wool, lipid composition is often a more practical and effective target for optimization than the protein structure itself53. Different studies have articulated valuable insights into the chemical interactions between wool’s lipid components and the dye molecules, offering, in turn, a deeper understanding of how lipid composition influences the dyeing process and overall textile quality54,55. In an attempt to validate such findings, multivariate statistical analysis has been conducted, in particular, principal component analysis was executed over the whole set of samples including both the modern and the ancient undyed wool fibers.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied specifically to the lipid spectral region (3050–2800 cm⁻¹). The results, shown in Fig. 8, revealed clear clustering of the investigated samples based on their collective lipid profiles. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 98% of the total variance in the spectral data. The blue-dyed fibers displayed variability along both the positive axis of PC1 and across PC2. In contrast, the brown-dyed sample clustered entirely on the negative side of PC2, with minor variation along PC1. The ancient undyed wool sample was located near the center of the PCA plot, showing only slight variation in both directions. Notably, the red- and yellow-dyed samples overlapped completely in the negative region of PC1 and the positive region of PC2, reflecting their high similarity. The modern wool sample was distinctly separated, positioned in the negative regions of both PC1 and PC2. Based on these clustering patterns, it can be inferred that the categorization of the sample groups by PCA, particularly in the lipid region, is related to the dyeing methods used on the fibers.

This clustering trend is further supported by the comparison of the categorization of reference-dyed fiber samples with historical samples, as shown in Fig. 8. The results indicate that, with the exception of the brown color, the differentiation of historical samples aligns with the clusters of reference-dyed fibers based on their respective dyeing methods. Specifically, the upper-right region corresponds to vat dyeing (indigo blue), and the upper-left area corresponds to mordant dyeing (madder red, cochineal red, and weld yellow). Interestingly, the reference brown fibers are classified within the mordant-dyed cluster, whereas the historical brown fibers are positioned closer to the undyed historical fibers. This suggests that the tannin-based dye was directly fixed onto the fibers, resulting in minimal alteration of the fiber’s lipid structure. It is worth noting that tannin dyes do not require mordants for fixation; however, when used with a mordant, they produce a deeper color21. Additionally, the modern undyed wool fibers, which were prepared using a different method, are distinctly separated from the traditionally prepared ancient samples.

The PCA loadings plot is demonstrated in Fig. 9. PC1 loadings clearly separate saturated and unsaturated lipid moieties by highlighting two main responses. The first one represented by the positive peaks at 2933 cm− 1 (asymmetric CH2 stretching) and 2960 cm− 1 (asymmetric CH3 stretching), indicating enhanced vibrational modes in saturated acyl chains of wool. This suggests hydrophobic disruption of the epicuticle layer, likely due to dye intercalation. The second response is represented by the negative peaks at 3003 cm− 1 (= C–H stretching) and 2885 cm− 1 (symmetric CH3 stretching) which signify reduced unsaturation and methyl group disorder in wool samples. This may reflect oxidation of olefinic bonds or conformational constraints imposed by dye molecules. This suggests that PC1 primarily discriminates samples based on lipid saturation states, relevant to membrane’s lipid profiles. On the other hand, PC2 loadings show prominent negative contributions at 2850 cm− 1 (symmetric CH2 stretching) and 2918 cm− 1 (asymmetric CH2 stretching), indicating loss of lipid acyl chain order and packing (fluidization) in wool samples. This fluidization aligns with dye penetration disrupting van der Waals interactions in the lipid bilayer, potentially enhancing dye diffusion while compromising the fiber’s hydrophobic barrier. Collectively, these changes demonstrate that dyeing alters both lipid saturation (PC1) and chain packing (PC2), elucidating molecular-scale modifications to wool’s protective lipid architecture.

Discussion

The application of synchrotron-based FTIR microspectroscopy to the Saltman No. 1 textiles from the Chehrabad salt mine has provided significant insights into the chemical composition of dyes and their interactions with wool fibers, enhancing our understanding of Sassanid dyeing practices. The non-destructive nature of this technique ensures the preservation of these valuable archaeological artifacts, making it a critical tool for cultural heritage studies.

Spectral analysis revealed distinct vibrational bands associated with wool’s protein structures and dye components. The Amide I (1650–1660 cm⁻¹), Amide II (1525–1545 cm⁻¹), and Amide III (1230–1240 cm⁻¹) regions confirmed the presence of keratin, the primary structural protein in wool, which governs its mechanical properties and dye uptake. Additionally, lipid-related bands (2925–2960 cm⁻¹) and sulfur-containing groups (S = O stretching at 1080–1130 cm⁻¹) highlighted complex chemical interactions between dyes and wool fibers, influencing color fixation and textile durability.

To resolve overlapping bands and detect subtle spectral features, residual second derivative spectra were employed, enabling clearer identification of secondary vibrational components. In the amide I region, this revealed distinct α-helix and β-sheet contributions, highlighting protein structural modifications linked to different dyeing methods. In the lipid region, second derivative analysis exposed fine changes in CH₂ and CH₃ stretching modes that signaled alterations in lipid ordering and saturation.

The identification of specific dye compounds through spectral matching—supported by both residual second derivative and curve fitting analyses—further elucidated Sassanid dyeing techniques. Curve fitting of the amide I quantified these changes, showing, for example, that vat dyeing with indigotin increased β-sheet contributions, while mordant dyeing with madder, cochineal, or flavonoid-based yellows produced mixed effects on protein conformation. Lipid peak broadening and shifts in mordant-dyed samples indicated increased chain disorder, whereas tannin-dyed fibers retained profiles close to undyed wool. The blue dye, identified as indigotin and likely derived from Indigofera tinctoria, exhibited characteristic bands at ~ 1035 and 1075 cm⁻¹, indicative of vat dyeing processes involving reduction and reoxidation. The red dye, comprising alizarin (Rubia tinctorum) and carminic acid (Dactylopius coccus), showed distinct vibrational patterns at ~ 1265 and 1380 cm⁻¹, suggesting the use of mordant dyeing to achieve vibrant hues; the detection of carminic acid—rare in archaeological textiles from this region—points to extensive trade networks. The yellow dye, characterized by quercetin and apigenin, displayed bands at ~ 1155 and 1200 cm⁻¹; notably, luteolin was absent, suggesting the use of alternative native yellow dye sources and warranting further investigation into the dye source.

The brown dye, attributed to tannic acid (likely from Juglans regia), was confirmed by peaks at ~ 1085 and 1128 cm⁻¹; the absence of juglone indicates selective extraction of tannin-rich parts.

Multivariate analysis by PCA of the lipid region complemented these findings, clearly separating samples according to dyeing method and dye chemistry. Vat-dyed indigo fibers formed a distinct cluster due to greater lipid modifications; mordant-dyed red and yellow fibers clustered together, reflecting similarities in mordant–fiber–dye interactions; and tannin-dyed fibers were positioned close to undyed wool, consistent with their minimal lipid alteration. The PCA loadings further supported the second derivative and curve fitting observations, highlighting changes in lipid saturation and chain ordering.

By combining residual second derivative analysis, curve fitting, and PCA, this study provides a multi-dimensional perspective on ancient dyeing practices. Vat dyeing disrupts lipid order and alters protein secondary structure, mordant dyeing impacts both proteins and lipids, and tannin-based dyeing largely preserves the fiber’s original molecular architecture. When compared with Roman and Byzantine examples, these Sassanid textiles display both shared technological principles and unique chemical signatures—particularly the absence of luteolin in yellow fibers, the presence of apigenin, and the combined use of tannic acid and carminic acid—marking them as distinctive products of Persian craftsmanship.

Conclusions

FTIR analysis revealed clear spectral differences between undyed and dyed wool, both in the raw spectra and in residual spectra derived from second derivative comparisons. These differences enabled the identification of dyes through library matching. The dyes detected in the samples were: indigotin for blue (could be Indigo), tannic acid (likely from walnut bark because the Juglone color component has not been identified in it) for brown, quercetin and apigenin for yellow as one of the components Weld, and a mix of alizarin and carminic acid for red (Madder and Cochineal respectivly). Notably, this is the first identification of tannic acid in this context, and carminic acid was not previously found in other red textiles from the same site. Apigenin was identified in the yellow dye, but luteolin—commonly expected in weld (Reseda luteola)—was absent (Table 1), warranting further investigation into the dye source.

Multivariate analysis (PCA) of the lipid spectral region showed clustering patterns that may reflect different dyeing methods (vat, mordant, direct, or disperse), suggesting a link between dyeing techniques and changes in wool lipid composition. However, further studies are needed to confirm this correlation.

In summary, the FTIR spectral analysis not only confirmed the presence of various natural dyes used in Saltman’s textiles but also provided valuable insights into the chemical interactions between the wool fibers and the dyes. This information contributes significantly to our understanding of ancient dyeing techniques and the sophistication of the Sassanid textile industry, offering a glimpse into the advanced chemical knowledge and resource utilization of the period.

This research is ongoing to obtain more precise information about the yellow dye used in the Salt Man No. 1 and to identify native yellow dyes of Iran for dyeing historical wool textiles. Additional studies are needed to substantiate the hypothesis on relationship between the dyeing methods and the lipid regions of dyed wool fibers, Future studies should focus on broader sampling and comparative analyses to further illuminate the technological and cultural heritage of ancient Persia.
