Geometry of dyadic polygons I: The structure of dyadic triangles
A. Mućka 1, A.B. Romanowska 2

Abstract
. Dyadic rationals are rationals whose denominator is a power of 2 2 . A dyadic n n -dimensional convex set is defined as the intersection with n n -dimensional dyadic space of an n n -dimensional real convex set. Such a dyadic convex set is said to be a dyadic n n -dimensional polytope if the real convex set is a polytope whose vertices lie in the dyadic space. Dyadic convex sets are described as subalgebras of reducts of faithful affine spaces over the ring of dyadic numbers, or equivalently as commutative, entropic and idempotent groupoids (binars or magmas) under the binary operation of arithmetic mean. This paper investigates the structure of dyadic polygons (two-dimensional polytopes), in particular dyadic triangles, following earlier results of [ 14 ] and related papers. A new classification of dyadic triangles is provided. In addition, dyadic triangles with a pointed vertex are characterized.

1.
Introduction

This paper is devoted to the study of dyadic polygons: mathematical objects that belong to geometry as subsets of certain affine spaces and to algebra as sets with one binary idempotent, commutative and entropic operation. It may be considered as a continuation of earlier work from
[
14
]
.

Recall that dyadic rationals are rationals whose denominator is a power of
2
. They form the
principal ideal subdomain
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{Z}[1/2]
of the ring
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers. The affine
spaces of interest are idempotent reducts of faithful
\mathbb{D}
-modules (modules over

\mathbb{D}
). They are called
affine
\mathbb{D}
-spaces
and are subreducts (subalgebras of reducts) of affine
\mathbb{R}
-spaces, i.e. affine spaces over the ring
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers.

A subset
D
of
\mathbb{D}^{n}
is called a
geometric dyadic convex set

if it is the intersection of a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
. Such sets are also called
convex relative to
\mathbb{D}
(cf. for instance
[
1
]

Dyadic polytopes
are dyadic convex sets obtained as the intersection of dyadic spaces and real (convex) polytopes whose vertices are contained in the dyadic space.
Dyadic polygons
are the intersection of the dyadic
plane and real polygons with vertices in the dyadic plane. The one-dimensional analogs are

dyadic intervals
(considered as intervals with their ends).

Real convex sets are described algebraically as certain barycentric algebras, subsets of

\mathbb{R}^{n}
closed under the operations of weighted means with weights from the open unit interval

I^{\circ}=\ ]0,1[
. See e.g.
[
17
,
18

closed under weighted means with weights from the open dyadic unit interval

operations determined by

are all generated by the single arithmetic mean
operation

x\circ y:=xy\underline{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}(x+y).

The arithmetic mean is a commutative, idempotent and entropic operation.
This fact allows one to define dyadic convex sets equivalently as algebras with one basic binary operation (groupoids, binars or magmas) with the algebraic structure of so-called
commutative binary modes

(
\mathcal{CB}
-modes). Such algebras have a well developed theory. (See e.g.
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10

11

12

14

While all (closed) real intervals are isomorphic as barycentric algebras, and each is generated by
its ends, there are infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic dyadic intervals (considered as

\mathcal{CB}
-modes), classified first in
[

\mathbb{D}
is isomorphic to some dyadic interval

Dyadic triangles were characterised in
[
14
]
, with some
improvements given in
[
11

[

\mathbb{D}^{2}

This fact, and a mistake in Lemma 4.11 of
[
14

In this paper we provide a much simpler and clearer description of dyadic triangles with one pointed vertex. We show that each is isomorphic to a certain special dyadic triangle we call
representative
. In consequence, we get a classification of all dyadic triangles with a pointed vertex, and their characterization by means of certain triples of integers. These results essentially improve and correct the earlier results. A subsequent paper will deal with isomorphisms of pointed representative triangles. Because each dyadic polygon can be decomposed as a union of dyadic triangles whose interiors do not intersect, a good description of dyadic triangles is essential to an understanding of the structure of all dyadic polygons.
Since each dyadic polygon is isomorphic to a polygon with vertices having integral coordinates, its real convex hull is a
lattice polygon
(cf.
[
19
]
,
[
6

). Thus, a classification of dyadic convex polygons brings new insights to the study of lattice polygons.

This introductory work on triangles and polygons is intended as a preparation for a more extensive study of general dyadic polytopes.

2.1.

Recall that affine spaces over a unital subring

(2.1)

ab\,\underline{r}=a(1-r)+br\,

(

We are interested in affine

-spaces from the quasivariety

(the class of all algebras satisfying a given set of quasi-identities) of affine
\mathbb{D}
-spaces, generated by the affine
\mathbb{D}
-space

-space on

-space

Note that, unlike the case of the affine
\mathbb{R}
-space

-space

contains infinitely many pairwise distinct (though isomorphic) affine
\mathbb{D}
-subspaces.
Nontrivial subspaces of the affine
\mathbb{D}
-space

have the form

m\mathbb{D}=\{md\mid d\in\mathbb{D}\}

for some positive odd integer

For a finite-dimensional affine

-space

-hull

\mathbb{R}
-spaces
containing

. The intersection

\mathbb{D}
-hull

We are mainly interested in affine dyadic spaces with a certain density property, defined as follows. An
n
-dimensional subspace

precisely when it coincides with its affine
\mathbb{D}
-hull, i.e. when

For example, while the affine
\mathbb{D}
-space

is geometric, its subspace

Recall as well that automorphisms of the affine
\mathbb{D}
-space

form the

over the ring

and the group of translations of the space

2.3.
Dyadic polytopes

The class of dyadic convex sets we are interested in is the class of dyadic polytopes.

Definition 2.3
.

[
14
]

A
dyadic
n
-dimensional polytope
is the intersection with the dyadic space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
of
an
n
-dimensional real polytope whose vertices lie in the dyadic space.

Dyadic polytopes are obviously geometric, and for any dyadic

P=\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)\cap\mathbb{D}^{n}=\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{D}}(P).

The vertices of the (real) polytope

In this paper, we are interested in dyadic polygons, especially in dyadic intervals and dyadic triangles (all considered as
\mathcal{CB}
-modes). Recall that unlike the real case, there are infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic dyadic intervals, and infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic dyadic triangles.

A special role is played by dyadic simplices. Dyadic simplices may be defined similarly as real simplices. An
n
-dimensional

is an
n
-dimensional dyadic convex set with

vertices, generated by these vertices. Geometric simplices are polytopes. Among dyadic polygons, all dyadic intervals isomorphic to the dyadic unit interval are
1
-dimensional simplices, and all
2
-dimensional simplices are isomorphic to the dyadic triangle generated by the three elements
e_{0}=(0,0)
,
e_{1}=(1,0)
and
e_{2}=(0,1)
of
\mathbb{D}^{2}
.

Dyadic polytopes are considered as
\circ
-subreducts of their affine
\mathbb{D}
-hulls. After introducing coordinate axes in such a space, a given polytope will be located in the corresponding
\mathbb{D}
-module by providing the coordinates of its vertices. Affine
\mathbb{D}
-spaces of dimension

are considered as subreducts of the
n
-dimensional real affine space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, isomorphisms of dyadic polytopes are considered as restrictions of automorphisms of their affine
\mathbb{D}
-hulls. (See
[
12
]
for more explanations.)

We start with a necessary condition for two dyadic triangles to be
isomorphic. Then, a correction to the statement of Lemma
4.11
in
[
14
]
is presented.
Next, we show that each pointed dyadic triangle is isomorphic to a certain special triangle we call
representative
. As a conclusion we obtain a full classification of pointed dyadic triangles. This result is then used in the final section to characterize all representative dyadic triangles.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\iota:T\rightarrow T^{\prime}
be an isomorphism between two dyadic triangles
T
and
T^{\prime}
. Let

P
be the area of the convex
\mathbb{R}
-hull
\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}(T)
of
T
and
P^{\prime}
be the area of
the convex
\mathbb{R}
-hull
\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}(T^{\prime})
of
T^{\prime}
. Then
P/P^{\prime}=2^{k}
for some integer
k
.

Proof.

Let
A=(x_{a},y_{a})
,
B=(x_{b},y_{b})
,
C=(x_{c},y_{c})
be the vertices of
T
. The isomorphism

\iota
maps the set of vertices of
T
onto the set of vertices of
T^{\prime}
. Let
A^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{a},y^{\prime}_{a})
,
B^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{b},y^{\prime}_{b})
,
C^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{c},y^{\prime}_{c})
be the vertices of
T^{\prime}
, the respective
images of
A,B
and
C
under
\iota
.
Then

P=\frac{1}{2}\left|\rm{det}\begin{bmatrix}x_{b}-x_{a}&y_{b}-y_{a}\\
x_{c}-x_{a}&y_{c}-y_{a}\end{bmatrix}\right|

and

P^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left|\rm{det}\begin{bmatrix}x^{\prime}_{b}-x^{\prime}_{a}&y^{\prime}_{b}-y^{\prime}_{a}\\
x^{\prime}_{c}-x^{\prime}_{a}&y^{\prime}_{c}-y^{\prime}_{a}\end{bmatrix}\right|.

The isomorphism
\iota
is a composition of a translation and a linear mapping given by an
invertible dyadic matrix
M
. Note that translations do not change the area of a polygon. Then

\displaystyle P^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{vmatrix}\det\begin{bmatrix}x^{\prime}_{b}-x^{\prime}_{a}&y^{\prime}_{b}-y^{\prime}_{a}\\
x^{\prime}_{c}-x^{\prime}_{a}&y^{\prime}_{c}-y^{\prime}_{a}\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{vmatrix}\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}x^{\prime}_{b}&y^{\prime}_{b}\\
x^{\prime}_{c}&y^{\prime}_{c}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}x^{\prime}_{a}&y^{\prime}_{a}\\
x^{\prime}_{a}&y^{\prime}_{a}\end{bmatrix}\right)\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{vmatrix}\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}x_{b}&y_{b}\\
x_{c}&y_{c}\end{bmatrix}M-\begin{bmatrix}x_{a}&y_{a}\\
x_{a}&y_{a}\end{bmatrix}M\right)\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{vmatrix}\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}x_{b}-x_{a}&y_{b}-y_{a}\\
x_{c}-x_{a}&y_{c}-y_{a}\end{bmatrix}M\right)\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{vmatrix}\det\begin{bmatrix}x_{b}-x_{a}&y_{b}-y_{a}\\
x_{c}-x_{a}&y_{c}-y_{a}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\det M\end{vmatrix}=P\cdot 2^{l}

for some integer
l
.
∎

We will see later on that the converse of Proposition
4.1
does not hold.

Remark 4.2
.

By leaving the sign of the absolute value in the areas
P
and
P^{\prime}
, given as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
, one obtains so-called signed areas
P_{s}
and
P^{\prime}_{s}
, and one can easily see that
P^{\prime}_{s}=P_{s}\cdot\det M
. The orientation of the vertices of a triangle depends on the sign of its area. It follows that if
\det M>0
, then the orientation of the vertices of
T
and
T^{\prime}
are the same, and if
\det M<0
, then the isomorphism
\iota:T\rightarrow T^{\prime}
changes the orientation of the vertices.

Note that isomorphic dyadic triangles with the same orientation of vertices have the same
boundary type. However, next
Example
4.3
shows that two non-isomorphic dyadic triangles may also have the same
boundary type.

Example 4.3
.

In
[
14
, Prop. 4.7]
, it was shown that there are infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic
triangles of boundary type
(1,1,1)
. This observation may be extended to triangles of boundary
type
(m,m,m)
for any odd positive integer
m
.
Let
T=ABC
be the hat with
A=(0,0),B=(m,m)
and
C=(2m,0)
. Let
T_{k}=AB_{k}C
be a
hat with
B_{k}=(m,km)
, where
k
is also a positive odd integer. Note that
T_{1}=T
, and the
boundary type of each
T_{k}
is
(m,m,m)
. By Proposition
4.1
, if
k\neq l
,
then the triangles
T_{k}
and
T_{l}
are not isomorphic.

The following lemma provides a correction to the statement of Lemma

4.11
in
[
14
]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
P
be a point of the plane
\mathbb{D}^{2}
with integral coordinates, and not belonging to the
coordinate axes. There is a
\mathbb{D}
-module automorhism which takes the point
P
onto a point of one of the coordinate axes, again with integral coordinates.

Proof.

Let
P=(ma,mb)
with (positive)
m
and such that
\gcd\{a,b\}=1
. Let
M_{ax}
be the dyadic
matrix

(4.1)

M_{ax}=\begin{bmatrix}y&-b\\
-x&a\end{bmatrix}

with integers
x
and
y
, and such that
\det(M_{ax})=ay-bx=1
. (Note that the latter
equation has an integral solution.) Then
(ma,mb)M_{ax}=(may-mbx,-mab+mab)=(m,0)
.

In a similar way (using the matrix
M_{ax}
with changed order of the columns), one can map the point
P
to the point
(0,m)
of the
y
-axis.
∎

Note that using the reflexion
in the line
y=x
, one can always transform
(0,m)
to
(m,0)
and
(m,0)
to
(0,m)
.

Any dyadic triangle
T
considered in this section will be thought of as a triangle with
clockwise ordered vertices
A,B,C
, and will be denoted by
ABC
. Note that any vertex may be choosen to be in the first place.
In all transformations of
T
, each of the three vertices will be mapped to a
vertex denoted by the same letter, but possibly with a different index.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
T
be a dyadic triangle in the dyadic plane
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. Then

is isomorphic
to each of the following triangles with vertices having integral coordinates:

(a)

A triangle

contained in the upper halfplane of
\mathbb{D}^{2}

(b)

with

and

Proof.

(a) Let

be a triangle

with clockwise ordered vertices.
First note that
T
may be mapped to an isomorphic triangle with one vertex located at the

, and translate

to a triangle

with the image

of

located at the origin.

Then transform

to the triangle

with vertices having integral coordinates,
using Corollary

.

Now suppose that the vertex

with integral coordinates is not already on the

-axis, and write it in the form

, where
\gcd\{a,b\}=1
, as described in the proof of Lemma
4.4
. The triangle

will be mapped to the isomorphic triangle

using the matrix

of Lemma

. The coordinates of

will be still integral, with

(b) Consider the triangle

with

obtained in the first part of the proof. If the first coordinate of

is negative, then consider the dyadic plane automorphism

given by the dyadic matrix

(4.2)

\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

The automorphism

keeps the vertices

and

to

One more adjustment may be needed in the case when

), with

.
∎

5.
Characterization of pointed dyadic triangles

The next question of interest is when representative hats are isomorphic. In this section
we will consider representative hats as pointed triangles with a pointed vertex located at the
origin, and isomorphisms of representative hats as isomorphisms preserving the pointed vertex
and the orientation of vertices. This type of an isomorphism will be called a
pointed
oriented isomorphism
or briefly a
pointed isomorphism
. First note that by
Lemma
4.9
, each representative hat
T_{i,j,m}
is isomorphic to infinitely many
hats
T_{l,j,m,0}
with
l=i+jk
, where
k
is an integer.

The following proposition describes the class of representative hats pointed isomorphic to a given representative hat.

Proposition 5.1
.

Consider two representative hats
T=ABC
and
T^{\prime}=A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
with the vertices
A=A^{\prime}=(0,0)
,
B=(i,j),C=(m,0)
and
B^{\prime}=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),C^{\prime}=(m^{\prime},0)
. Let
\iota:T\rightarrow T^{\prime}
be a mapping taking
B
to
B^{\prime}
and
C
to
C^{\prime}
.

Then
\iota:T\rightarrow T^{\prime}
is a pointed oriented isomorphism precisely when
j^{\prime}=j
,
m^{\prime}=m
and
i^{\prime}=i+jk
for some even integer
k
.

Proof.

First assume that
\iota
is a pointed isomorphism.
Since any isomorphism between two triangles preserves the side types and both
T
and
T^{\prime}
are representative, it follows immediately that
m=m^{\prime}
. Then by Proposition
4.1
, the areas
P
of the convex
\mathbb{R}
-hull
\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}(T)
of
T
and
P^{\prime}
of the convex

\mathbb{R}
-hull
\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}(T^{\prime})
of
T^{\prime}
are equal. Hence
mj=mj^{\prime}
, and consequently
j=j^{\prime}
.

The mapping
\iota
is an isomorphism preserving the pointed vertex and the orientation
precisely when there exists an invertible dyadic matrix
M
such that

(5.1)

\begin{bmatrix}m&0\\
i&j\end{bmatrix}M=\begin{bmatrix}m^{\prime}&0\\
i^{\prime}&j^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}m&0\\
i^{\prime}&j\end{bmatrix}.

It follows that

(5.2)

M=\frac{1}{mj}\begin{bmatrix}j&0\\
-i&m\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}m&0\\
i^{\prime}&j\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
(-i+i^{\prime})/j&1\end{bmatrix}.

The number
k:=(i^{\prime}-i)/j
is a dyadic number precisely when
j
divides
i^{\prime}-i
. Since
i,i^{\prime},j
are integers, it follows that
k

The final part of this section is devoted to a characterization of pointed dyadic triangles.

For a dyadic triangle
T
with clockwise ordered vertices and a pointed vertex, let
\mathcal{R}(T)
be the class of all representative hats isomorphic to
T
with the same pointed vertex and the same orientation of vertices.
By Corollary
5.3
, each member of
\mathcal{R}(T)
is isomorphic to a representative hat
T_{i,j,m}
, where

j
and
m
are positive odd integers, and
i
belongs to
\{1,3,\dots,2j-1\}
.
On the other hand, if
(i,j,m)
is a triple of odd integers, where
j
and
m
are odd and

, then clearly it determines the representative hat
T_{i,j,m}
and the class of hats

, with even
k
, (pointed) isomorphic to
T_{i,j,m}
. We call such a triple an

.

The following theorem characterises pointed dyadic triangles, and provides a correction to the statement of Theorem

of
[

]

Theorem 5.7
.

Two pointed dyadic triangles with the same orientation of vertices are isomorphic if and only if they have the same encoding triples.
