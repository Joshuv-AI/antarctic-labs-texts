Non-prescription antibiotic dispensing in community pharmacies in jordan: a simulated patient study
Authors: Mona Bustami, Anas Abed, Inas Almazari, Rahaf AlQatarneh, Shahd Abu Assab, Wael Abu Dayyih, Sireen Abdul Rahim Shilbayeh, Zainab Zakaraya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInappropriate antibiotic use in community settings is a major contributor to antimicrobial resistance, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where regulatory enforcement may be inconsistent. Community pharmacies are a critical interface between health systems and the public; however, real-world dispensing practices remain insufficiently characterized in many settings. This study evaluated the prevalence, behavioral drivers, and clinical quality of non-prescription antibiotic dispensing in Jordanian community pharmacies using a simulated patient methodology.A cross-sectional simulated patient study was conducted across 200 community pharmacies in Jordan. A standardized encounter involving a three-stage progressive escalation protocol assessed dispensing behavior under increasing patient demand. The primary outcome was antibiotic supply without a valid prescription. Secondary outcomes included clinical assessment, counseling, referral behavior, and antibiotic class supplied. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify predictors of non-prescription dispensing and adequate clinical assessment.Antibiotics were dispensed without prescription in 162 encounters (81.0%; 95% CI, 75.6–86.4). During the initial symptom-based presentation, 110 pharmacies (55.0%) supplied antibiotics. Among pharmacies that initially declined, a direct antibiotic request resulted in additional dispensing in 44.4% of cases, while subsequent patient insistence led to further supply in 24.0%, producing a cumulative prevalence of 81.0%. Independent pharmacies (adjusted odds ratio [AOR], 2.48; 95% CI, 1.23–5.02) and rural locations (AOR, 2.02; 95% CI, 1.01–4.03) were associated with higher dispensing odds. History-taking occurred in 49.4% of dispensing encounters, allergy screening in 14.2%, and no counseling was provided in 29.0%. Broad-spectrum agents represented 73.5% of dispensed antibiotics.Non-prescription antibiotic dispensing remains widespread in Jordan and is influenced by patient pressure and practice characteristics. Strengthened regulatory enforcement, professional training, and public awareness interventions are urgently needed.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) remains one of the most pressing global public health threats of the twenty-first century, driven largely by the inappropriate use and overuse of antibiotics in both human and animal health [1]. Community pharmacies represent a critical point of access to antibiotics in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where pharmacists frequently serve as the most accessible healthcare professionals [2]. Although most countries legally restrict antibiotics to prescription-only status, non-prescription dispensing persists in practice, undermining antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) efforts, promoting unnecessary antibiotic exposure, and contributing to the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance resistance [3].

Jordan represents an important setting in which this regulatory–behavioural gap can be examined. National pharmacy legislation clearly mandates prescription-only antibiotic dispensing; however, prior studies have consistently documented substantial levels of non-prescription supply in community settings [4,5]. For instance, a 2015 simulated patient study in urban Amman and Irbid reported an overall dispensing rate of 74.3% across diverse scenarios, highlighting scenario-specific variations (e.g. 97.6% for sore throat) and poor counselling practices [4]. While foundational, that investigation was geographically restricted to two major cities and primarily provided descriptive prevalence estimates without examining structural determinants, ownership models, or contextual predictors of inappropriate dispensing. Moreover, it did not explicitly model the behavioural influence of progressive patient pressure within a hypothesis-driven analytical framework. Beyond Jordan, studies from other low- and middle-income settings have similarly documented widespread non-prescription antibiotic dispensing in community pharmacies, often driven by weak regulatory enforcement, consumer demand, limited access to medical consultation, and commercial pressures [6–8]. This pattern is particularly concerning in the context of upper respiratory tract infections, where antibiotics are frequently supplied despite predominantly viral aetiology and limited clinical indication. Together, these findings position inappropriate community antibiotic access as both a stewardship challenge and a contributor to AMR at population level.

Recent mixed-methods research in Jordan found strong awareness of antimicrobial resistance among community pharmacists but identified regulatory inconsistency, economic pressures, consumer demand, independent ownership, and rural practice as perceived barriers to responsible antibiotic dispensing [9]. However, that study relied on self-reported perceptions and did not directly observe dispensing behaviour. Because regulatory compliance is socially desirable, self-reported data may overestimate adherence to professional standards [10]. Reliance solely on self-reported data risks overestimating adherence to professional standards and underestimating the magnitude of inappropriate antibiotic supply.

Simulated patient methodology offers a rigorous and unobtrusive approach to evaluating real-world practice [11]. By employing trained individuals who present standardised clinical scenarios without prior notification, simulated patient studies capture authentic pharmacist decision-making under routine working conditions [12]. Within antimicrobial stewardship research, this method has become a reference standard for assessing non-prescription antibiotic dispensing, as it minimises reporting bias and reflects actual clinical interactions rather than perceived intentions [6]. Importantly, contemporary stewardship research increasingly emphasises not only estimating prevalence, but also identifying structural and contextual determinants that drive dispensing behaviour—an analytical shift necessary for designing targeted regulatory and policy interventions.

Few studies have linked perceived antimicrobial-stewardship barriers with objectively observed dispensing behaviour, and no recent multi-governorate simulated patient study has reassessed these practices in Jordan Building upon our previously developed conceptual framework of antimicrobial stewardship determinants, the present study aims to objectively evaluate antibiotic dispensing practices in Jordanian community pharmacies using a simulated patient design. By examining observable dispensing behaviour in relation to structural and contextual pharmacy characteristics, the study seeks to extend prior perception-based evidence and provide a more practice-oriented understanding of stewardship implementation. Through this approach, study aims to inform regulatory, professional, and public health strategies to reduce inappropriate antibiotic use.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study employing a simulated patient methodology was conducted to objectively evaluate non-prescription antibiotic dispensing and clinical assessment practices in community pharmacies across five governorates representing central, northern, and southern regions of Jordan. The simulated patient approach is widely regarded as a rigorous method for assessing real-world professional behaviour because it enables direct observation of routine practice while minimising social desirability bias and the Hawthorne effect [11,13].

During the single interaction, a standardised three-stage progressive escalation protocol was implemented. The simulated patient initially presented mild upper respiratory tract infection symptoms (Stage 1). If antibiotics were not supplied, the patient escalated to a direct antibiotic request (Stage 2). If refusal persisted, explicit insistence was expressed (Stage 3). Escalation ceased immediately upon antibiotic dispensing.

The standardised clinical presentation consisted of a 30-year-old adult reporting a three-day history of mild upper respiratory tract symptoms, including sore throat, nasal congestion, dry cough, and low-grade subjective fever without dyspnoea, chest pain, purulent sputum, or comorbid conditions. No prior antibiotic use was reported. The presentation was intentionally designed to be consistent with an uncomplicated, self-limiting viral upper respiratory tract infection for which antibiotic therapy is not recommended under national and international clinical guidelines. The expected stewardship-consistent response was refusal to dispense antibiotics accompanied by symptomatic treatment advice and appropriate safety-net counselling.

The study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Study population and sampling

The study population comprised licensed community pharmacies operating in Jordan. Pharmacies were eligible if they provided direct medication dispensing services to the public, while hospital-based and institutional outpatient pharmacies were excluded.

The sampling frame was derived from the official registry of the Jordan Pharmacists Association. A stratified random sampling approach was used to enhance geographic distribution across selected central, northern, and southern regions, based on the regional distribution of pharmacies in the official registry. Sample size was calculated a priori to estimate the prevalence of non-prescription antibiotic dispensing with adequate precision. Assuming a conservative prevalence of 50%, a 95% confidence level, and a margin of error of 7%, the minimum required sample was 196 pharmacies; therefore, a target of 200 completed encounters was established. This sample size also ensured an adequate number of outcome events for multivariable logistic regression analyses, exceeding recommended events-per-variable thresholds for model stability. Each selected pharmacy was visited once, and the pharmacy was considered the unit of analysis.

Development and validation of the data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured observational checklist developed following an extensive review of antimicrobial stewardship literature, community pharmacy practice standards, and international guidance on appropriate antibiotic use for respiratory conditions.

The instrument captured pharmacy characteristics, clinical assessment behaviours, counselling practices, referral decisions, and antibiotic selection. Content validity was established through review by a multidisciplinary expert panel consisting of a clinical pharmacotherapy professor, an infectious diseases physician, and a senior community pharmacist. Minor revisions were implemented to enhance clarity and contextual relevance. A pilot study involving eight simulated patient visits was conducted to assess scenario realism, checklist completeness, and operational feasibility. Findings from the pilot informed refinements to scenario delivery and data recording procedures.

Simulated patient protocol, recruitment, and training

A single standardised simulated patient protocol was constructed to reflect a mild, self-limiting upper respiratory tract infection presentation, with three sequential escalation stages based on commonly observed antibiotic demand patterns in community pharmacy practice. The encounters were conducted in Arabic using standardised phrases, which are presented here as English translations. At Stage 1, the simulated patient initiated the encounter by stating: ‘I have had a sore throat, nasal congestion, a dry cough, and a slight fever for the past three days. What do you recommend?’ If no antibiotic was supplied, the simulated patient proceeded to Stage 2 and asked directly: ‘Can you give me an antibiotic for these symptoms?’ If the staff member continued to refuse, the simulated patient proceeded to Stage 3 and stated: ‘I understand, but I still want an antibiotic. Please give me one.’ No additional pressure was applied beyond these standardised verbal statements, and escalation ceased immediately once an antibiotic was supplied or after the Stage 3 response had been recorded.

Five female pharmacy graduates served as simulated patients and underwent structured training before field implementation. Female gender was standardised across all encounters. Encounters were allocated across governorates and shifts to maintain balanced coverage and reduce detectability. Training emphasised script standardisation, natural communication, consistent verbal and non-verbal behaviour, staged escalation rules, and accurate completion of the post-encounter checklist.

Mock encounters were conducted before field implementation to ensure fidelity to the protocol, and inter-rater reliability was evaluated using recorded training interactions. All simulated patients achieved a Cohen’s kappa coefficient exceeding 0.80 before participation. Simulated patients were instructed to avoid medical terminology, maintain consistent interaction styles, and provide additional clinical details only when prompted. No medications were consumed during the study.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted between August and December 2025. Simulated patients visited selected pharmacies covertly and interacted with the dispensing staff member present at the time of the visit, reflecting routine patient experience. Because the encounters were conducted covertly, the professional designation of the dispensing staff member could not be verified; the observed staff member may therefore have been a licensed pharmacist or another pharmacy employee involved in dispensing. An outcome was recorded when the dispensing staff member selected and offered an antibiotic for sale. Simulated patients did not complete the purchase. Immediately after exiting the pharmacy, simulated patients completed an electronic data collection form to minimise recall bias and promote standardised recording of observed behaviours. Because the study aimed to evaluate real-world dispensing behaviour at the point of care, the pharmacy was treated as the unit of analysis and the observed interaction reflected the practice of the dispensing staff member present at the time of the visit. Each pharmacy received a single visit to prevent behavioural contamination. Visits were distributed across weekdays and weekends as well as morning, afternoon, and evening shifts to capture variability in staffing and workflow. Simulated patients withdrew without executing the scenario if the pharmacy was closed or if conditions prevented completion of a standardised interaction.

Outcome measures and variables

The primary outcome was non-prescription antibiotic dispensing, defined as the supply of any systemic antibiotic without a valid prescription during the simulated encounter. Secondary outcomes included the quality of clinical assessment, counselling practices, referral behaviour, and the spectrum of antibiotics supplied. A basic clinical assessment threshold was defined a priori as asking at least two clinically relevant questions prior to dispensing. No universally validated numerical threshold exists for evaluating clinical assessment intensity in this type of simulated patient study. Therefore, the cut-off was selected based on clinical reasoning and the operational objective of distinguishing a minimally structured assessment from a single, potentially perfunctory enquiry. Meeting this threshold was not intended to indicate that the assessment was comprehensive or fully safety-adequate. Clinically relevant questions included inquiries about symptom duration, fever, prior medication use, drug allergy history, and chronic medical conditions. Because these assessment domains differ in their clinical and safety importance, each component was also analysed and reported separately. As a descriptive sensitivity analysis, assessment intensity was additionally classified using a stricter threshold of three or more clinically relevant questions; this produced the same overall interpretation of limited assessment intensity.

‘No counselling’ was defined as dispensing without providing advice on dose, frequency, duration, adverse effects, or appropriate use. ‘Clear clinical explanation for refusal’ was defined as an explicit explanation that antibiotics were unnecessary, inappropriate, or not indicated for the presented symptoms. ‘Stewardship-consistent management’ was defined as refusal to dispense antibiotics together with symptomatic treatment advice and safety-net or follow-up advice.

Each supplied antibiotic was categorised at the individual active-ingredient level according to the 2025 World Health Organisation Access, Watch, and Reserve (AWaRe) classification. Antibiotics were classified as Access, Watch, or Reserve, and the frequency and proportion of each category were calculated relative to all antibiotics supplied. AWaRe classification was used as a stewardship indicator and was interpreted separately from the clinical appropriateness of dispensing, because an antibiotic classified as Access may still be inappropriate when supplied for a condition for which antibiotic treatment is not indicated.

Predictor variables were selected based on theoretical relevance, findings from previous literature, and the objectives of the study to reduce the risk of data-driven variable selection and model overfitting.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Al-Ahliyya Amman University (IRB No. AAU/1/9/2024-2025), Jordan. Because prior awareness could alter professional behaviour and compromise study validity, a waiver of informed consent was granted.

No identifiable information related to pharmacies or staff members was recorded. All data were anonymized prior to analysis and reported in aggregate form. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarised pharmacy characteristics, dispensing outcomes, clinical assessment practices, counselling behaviours, referral patterns, and the types of antibiotics supplied. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, with 95% confidence intervals reported where appropriate. Dispensed antibiotics were additionally summarised by WHO AWaRe category, with Access, Watch, and Reserve proportions calculated using the total number of antibiotics supplied as the denominator.

Associations between categorical variables were examined using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate according to cell counts. Because dispensing behaviour across the three escalation stages occurred sequentially within the same simulated encounter and therefore represented related observations rather than independent events, differences across stages were evaluated using Cochran’s Q test. When the overall test was statistically significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using McNemar’s test with Bonferroni adjustment.

Two multivariable logistic regression models were specified a priori. The first model examined factors associated with non-prescription antibiotic dispensing across all 200 pharmacy encounters, with dispensing coded as the modelled event. The second model examined factors associated with meeting the predefined basic clinical assessment threshold among the 162 pharmacies that dispensed antibiotics, with asking at least two clinically relevant questions coded as the modelled event.

The same four covariates were entered simultaneously into each model: pharmacy type (independent vs chain), practice setting (urban vs rural/semi-rural), time of visit (evening vs morning/afternoon), and day of visit (weekend vs weekday). These variables were selected a priori based on the conceptual framework underlying the study, findings from previous community-pharmacy and antimicrobial-stewardship literature, and their plausible operational relationship with dispensing and assessment practices. Covariates were not selected using univariable statistical screening.

The dispensing model included 162 outcome events and four covariate parameters, corresponding to 40.5 events per parameter. The smaller outcome category comprised 38 non-dispensing encounters, corresponding to 9.5 observations per parameter. The clinical-assessment model included 58 outcome events and four covariate parameters, corresponding to 14.5 events per parameter.

Governorate was considered as a potential covariate but was not included in the primary models. Entering the five-level governorate variable would have required four additional parameters and substantially reduced model parsimony, particularly given the limited number of non-dispensing outcomes. Governorate also overlapped conceptually and geographically with the urban versus rural/semi-rural practice-setting variable, which was the contextual exposure of primary interest.

Because encounters were conducted by five simulated patients, possible simulated-patient-level variation was examined in sensitivity analyses by additionally adjusting for simulated-patient identity. Simulated-patient identity was entered as a fixed categorical factor because conventional cluster-robust standard errors may be unreliable with only five clusters. An interaction term between pharmacy ownership type and practice setting was also tested in each model to assess whether the association between ownership and the outcome differed according to urban or rural/semi-rural location.

Variables included in the regression models had complete data; therefore, no imputation procedures were required. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and explanatory power was summarised using Nagelkerke R2. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals. Because the modelled outcomes were not rare, odds ratios were interpreted strictly as measures of relative odds and not as risk ratios or relative risks. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For exploratory subgroup comparisons describing dispensing behaviour across contextual pharmacy strata, p-values were reported without further multiplicity adjustment because these analyses were secondary and intended to provide descriptive contextual interpretation rather than serve as primary inferential endpoints. Emphasis was placed on effect sizes, confidence intervals, and consistency of observed patterns rather than on statistical significance alone.

Results

Characteristics of participating community pharmacies

A total of 214 community pharmacies were approached during the study period. Of these, 200 pharmacies completed the simulated patient encounters and were included in the final analysis, yielding a completion rate of 93.5%. Fourteen visits were excluded due to pharmacy closure at the time of approach (n = 6) or disruptions that prevented completion of the standardised scenario (n = 8) (Figure 1).

As shown in Table 1, the participating pharmacies were distributed across five governorates to provide geographic coverage of selected central, northern, and southern regions of Jordan. Amman contributed the largest proportion of visits (41.5%), followed by Irbid (19.0%), Zarqa (14.5%), Balqa (13.0%), and Karak (12.0%). Overall, 145 pharmacies (72.5%) were located in urban areas, while 55 (27.5%) operated in rural or semi-rural settings. Consistent with national workforce patterns, the majority of visited sites were independent community pharmacies (n = 138, 69.0%), whereas 62 (31.0%) were affiliated with regional or national chain networks. Most pharmacies were situated in mixed residential–commercial neighbourhoods with high walk-in traffic.

Dispensing patterns across progressive escalation stages

Dispensing behaviour demonstrated a clear escalation pattern across the progressive interaction stages as summarised in the flow diagram in Figure 2. At the initial symptom-based presentation (Stage 1), 110 pharmacies (55.0%) supplied antibiotics without prescription. Among the 90 pharmacies that initially declined dispensing, escalation to a direct antibiotic request (Stage 2) resulted in an additional 40 dispensing events (44.4% conditional on prior refusal), increasing the cumulative prevalence to 75.0% (150/200). Of the remaining 50 pharmacies that continued to refuse, explicit patient insistence (Stage 3) led to 12 additional dispensing events (24.0% conditional), producing a final cumulative prevalence of 81.0% (162/200). The remaining 38 pharmacies (19.0%) did not dispense antibiotics at any stage. Cochran’s Q test demonstrated a statistically significant difference across escalation stages, indicating that antibiotic supply became more frequent as patient demand intensified during the encounter.

Among the 162 pharmacies that dispensed antibiotics without prescription, the extent of clinical assessment varied substantially (Table 3). Overall, 80 pharmacies (49.4%) asked at least one question related to the patient’s symptoms prior to dispensing, whereas 82 (50.6%) supplied the antibiotic without conducting any meaningful clinical enquiry.

The most frequently asked question concerned symptom duration, reported in 55 encounters (34.0%), followed by the presence of fever (46 pharmacies, 28.4%) and prior medication use (29 pharmacies, 17.9%). Screening for drug allergy was uncommon, occurring in only 23 encounters (14.2%), while assessment of chronic medical conditions was observed in 15 pharmacies (9.3%).

Comprehensive assessment, defined as asking three or more clinically relevant questions, was documented in just 18 encounters (11.1%), indicating that structured patient evaluation was infrequently performed.

Counselling practices demonstrated similar variability. Basic instructions regarding dosage and frequency were provided in 88 dispensing encounters (54.3%), yet guidance on treatment duration was offered in only 63 cases (38.9%). Advice related to potential adverse effects was observed in 27 pharmacies (16.7%), and only 18 pharmacists (11.1%) mentioned antimicrobial resistance or emphasised the importance of appropriate antibiotic use. Notably, 47 pharmacies (29.0%) dispensed antibiotics with no counselling whatsoever.

From a safety perspective, potentially inappropriate antibiotic selection for symptoms consistent with self-limiting viral infections was observed in the majority of dispensing encounters. Broad-spectrum agents, particularly amoxicillin-clavulanate and azithromycin, were frequently supplied without documented clinical justification.

Collectively, these findings suggest that non-prescription antibiotic dispensing is often accompanied by limited clinical assessment and inconsistent counselling, raising concerns regarding the quality of frontline antimicrobial stewardship practices.

After adjustment for potential confounders, pharmacy ownership type and practice setting emerged as significant predictors of dispensing behaviour. Independent pharmacies demonstrated significantly higher odds of supplying antibiotics without prescription compared with chain pharmacies (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] 2.48, 95% CI 1.23–5.02, p = 0.011). Similarly, pharmacies located in rural/semi-rural areas had higher adjusted odds of dispensing antibiotics than those in urban settings (AOR 2.02, 95% CI 1.01–4.03, p = 0.046).

Time of visit (evening vs morning/afternoon) and day of encounter (weekend vs weekday) were not significantly associated with dispensing practices.

The model demonstrated acceptable calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit p = 0.67) and moderate explanatory power (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.31), indicating that structural characteristics explained a meaningful proportion of dispensing variability. The interaction between pharmacy ownership type and practice setting was not statistically significant (p = 0.651), providing no evidence that the association between ownership type and dispensing differed materially between urban and rural/semi-rural settings. Adjustment for simulated-patient identity in the sensitivity analysis did not materially alter the direction or magnitude of the principal associations. Because non-prescription dispensing was a common outcome, the adjusted odds ratios are interpreted as relative odds rather than relative risks.

Descriptive subgroup comparisons showed the same overall pattern as the adjusted model. Non-prescription antibiotic dispensing was more common in independent than chain pharmacies and in rural/semi-rural than urban pharmacies, with the highest crude prevalence observed among pharmacies characterised as both independent and rural/semi-rural. These descriptive findings were consistent with the direction of association observed in the multivariable analysis.

Types and WHO AWaRe classification of antibiotics dispensed

A total of 162 antibiotics were offered during the simulated patient encounters.

Broad-spectrum agents accounted for the majority of dispensed medications. Amoxicillin–clavulanate was the most frequently supplied antibiotic, dispensed in 60 encounters (37.0%), followed by azithromycin in 42 pharmacies (25.9%), and amoxicillin in 27 encounters (16.7%). Fluoroquinolone dispensing was observed in 16 encounters (9.9%), comprising ciprofloxacin in 12 encounters (7.4%) and levofloxacin in four encounters (2.5%), while oral second- and third-generation cephalosporins were supplied in 11 cases (6.8%). A small proportion of pharmacies (6 encounters, 3.7%) provided alternative agents, including clarithromycin or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (Table 5).

According to the WHO AWaRe classification, 89 supplied antibiotics (54.9%) belonged to the Access group and 73 (45.1%) belonged to the Watch group. No Reserve-group antibiotics were supplied. Amoxicillin–clavulanate was the most frequently dispensed Access antibiotic, whereas azithromycin was the most frequently dispensed Watch antibiotic.

Although amoxicillin–clavulanate is categorised as an Access antibiotic under the WHO AWaRe framework, its provision remained clinically inappropriate in the present scenario because the simulated symptoms were consistent with an uncomplicated, self-limiting viral upper respiratory tract infection. Similarly, the substantial proportion of Watch-group antibiotics, including azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, cefuroxime, and cefpodoxime, is concerning because these agents have greater resistance-selection potential and should be monitored as priority targets for antimicrobial stewardship.

In several encounters, the dispensing staff member offered more than one antibiotic option before the simulated patient selected a product, suggesting that antibiotic selection was not consistently guided by a structured clinical assessment. Overall, the AWaRe distribution reinforces concerns regarding both unnecessary antibiotic access and the frequent supply of agents requiring closer stewardship monitoring.

Referral and alternative management practices

Among the 38 pharmacies that declined to dispense antibiotics, 24 referred the simulated patient to a physician, whereas 14 recommended symptomatic treatment without antibiotic therapy. Explicit explanation that antibiotics were unnecessary and provision of symptom-monitoring advice were inconsistent across these encounters. Overall, stewardship-consistent management, defined as refusal to dispense antibiotics together with symptomatic treatment and follow-up or safety-net advice, was observed in a minority of non-dispensing encounters. No statistically significant differences in referral behaviour were observed according to pharmacy ownership type or practice setting.

Factors associated with clinical assessment quality

Among the 162 pharmacies that dispensed antibiotics, 58 pharmacies (35.8%) met the predefined basic clinical assessment threshold by asking at least two clinically relevant questions before supplying the medication. The remaining 104 pharmacies (64.2%) asked fewer than two clinically relevant questions (Table 6). This count-based classification was used as an operational measure of assessment intensity and did not imply that all clinically important or safety-critical domains had been evaluated.

Discussion

This multi-governorate simulated patient study found widespread non-prescription antibiotic dispensing in Jordanian community pharmacies despite a presentation consistent with an uncomplicated, self-limiting viral upper respiratory tract infection. The persistence of this practice despite prescription-only legislation and national antimicrobial-resistance initiatives indicates a substantial gap between regulation and routine community-pharmacy practice. Viewed longitudinally, the earlier Jordanian simulated patient study [9] and the current findings indicate that high levels of non-prescription dispensing have persisted despite legal restrictions. This suggests that prescription-only classification alone has not produced sustained behavioural change at the point of care.

In global context, the observed prevalence exceeds pooled estimates from a large systematic review and meta-analysis of simulated patient studies, which reported an overall non-prescription antibiotic supply rate of approximately 62% across 52 countries [6]. Similar patterns have been documented in Pakistan [14], Vietnam [15], and Indonesia prior to regulatory reform [16], underscoring that inappropriate community access is a shared challenge across LMICs. Jordan therefore appears positioned toward the higher end of the global distribution, particularly for respiratory tract presentations.

The progressive escalation design revealed a clear behavioural gradient, with dispensing increasing substantially under direct request and further under explicit insistence. This pattern suggest that patient pressure is an important factor associated with inappropriate supply during community pharmacy encounters, although the study design does not permit causal inference regarding dispensing motivations. Meta-analytic evidence similarly demonstrates significantly higher supply rates when antibiotics are explicitly requested [6]. Qualitative work in the region has described pharmacists’ perceived obligation to maintain customer relationships within competitive retail environments [17,18]. The present findings extend this literature by demonstrating that such sociocultural pressures translate into measurable behavioural compliance under escalating demand.

Systematic reviews of community-pharmacy practices in low- and middle-income countries have identified weak regulatory oversight, commercial pressures, consumer demand, and limited access to formal healthcare as recurring drivers of non-prescription antibiotic sales [8,19]. Rural settings may amplify informal antibiotic access where physician availability is limited and pharmacies serve as de facto primary care entry points [20]. Recent evidence from southern Jordan similarly reported that rural practice location and structural factors were significantly associated with inappropriate antibiotic dispensing and stewardship challenges among community pharmacists [21]. Rural environments may shift pharmacies into de facto primary care roles when formal access is constrained, increasing susceptibility to patient insistence. Importantly, recent mixed-methods evidence from Jordan identified economic dependency on antibiotic sales, inconsistent regulatory enforcement, and consumer demand as dominant structural barriers sustaining non-prescription supply. The concordance between previously reported barriers and the observed dispensing patterns in the current study suggests that structural and economic context may contribute to inappropriate supply alongside professional knowledge and other unmeasured factors.

Beyond access, the quality of clinical engagement raises substantial patient safety concerns. A considerable proportion of dispensing encounters involved no meaningful clinical questioning, allergy screening was infrequent, and counselling was inconsistently delivered. This limited history-taking and safety screening observed in the present study are consistent with a systematic review of simulated patient pharmacy studies, which documented frequent omission of symptom duration, comorbidities, contraindications, and other clinically important information [13]. The stewardship challenge therefore encompasses both inappropriate access and suboptimal clinical process during dispensing.

Antibiotic selection patterns further intensified the stewardship concern. According to the WHO AWaRe classification, 89 of the 162 supplied antibiotics (54.9%) belonged to the Access group, whereas 73 (45.1%) belonged to the Watch group; no Reserve-group antibiotics were dispensed. Watch-group antibiotics included azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, clarithromycin, and oral second- or third-generation cephalosporins. The substantial proportion of Watch antibiotics is concerning because these agents have greater resistance-selection potential and are priority targets for antimicrobial-use monitoring and stewardship interventions [22]. Importantly, AWaRe categorisation should not be interpreted as an assessment of clinical indication. Although amoxicillin–clavulanate, the most frequently supplied antibiotic, is categorised as an Access agent, its dispensing remained inappropriate because the standardised presentation was consistent with a self-limiting viral upper respiratory tract infection for which antibiotic therapy was not indicated. These findings therefore demonstrate two related stewardship concerns: unnecessary antibiotic dispensing irrespective of AWaRe category and substantial exposure to Watch-group agents in the absence of a clinical indication.

The policy implications of these findings are reinforced when interpreted longitudinally and comparatively. A decade ago, simulated patient research in Jordan demonstrated that 74.3% of pharmacies dispensed antibiotics without prescription, explicitly calling for stricter enforcement of national regulations. The present findings indicate that this structural gap persists despite legal prohibition, suggesting that prescription-only classification alone is insufficient to alter frontline behaviour. More recent mixed-methods analysis of Jordanian community pharmacists identified regulatory inconsistency, economic dependency on antibiotic sales, and patient-driven demand as the principal determinants sustaining non-prescription dispensing.

Taken together, with previous Jordanian mixed-methods evidence, these findings suggest that knowledge deficits alone are unlikely to explain the observed dispensing pattern and that structural, economic, regulatory, and demand-side pressures likely play an important role.

Accordingly, the practical stewardship implications of these findings can be organised across regulatory, professional, and public levels. At the regulatory level, priorities include strengthened and risk-based pharmacy inspection, practical prescription-verification mechanisms, proportionate sanctions for repeated non-compliance, and integration of community-pharmacy dispensing into national AWaRe-based monitoring systems. Comparative evidence from Saudi Arabia indicates that intensified enforcement can substantially reduce unauthorised antibiotic dispensing [23], however, broader evidence suggests that the effects of enforcement may be difficult to sustain if economic incentives and patient demand remain unaddressed [16].

Looking ahead, digital stewardship infrastructure may complement conventional regulatory and professional interventions. Artificial intelligence- and machine-learning-supported systems could potentially assist with prescription verification, antimicrobial-use surveillance, identification of high-risk dispensing patterns, and clinical decision support. Recent systematic reviews indicate promising predictive performance of such approaches in antimicrobial stewardship, although limited external validation, implementation evidence, explainability, and governance remain important barriers [24,25]. These technologies should therefore be viewed as potential adjuncts to, rather than replacements for, regulatory enforcement and professional judgement.

At the professional level, continuing professional development should extend beyond knowledge-based education to include communication training, standardised refusal scripts, appropriate symptomatic-management pathways, and clear referral and safety-netting guidance. Routine audit and feedback could also help pharmacies compare their dispensing practices with stewardship standards. Incorporating these competencies into professional accreditation, licensing renewal, and pharmacy quality-assurance criteria may further support consistent implementation.

At the public level, education campaigns should focus on the predominantly viral nature of uncomplicated upper respiratory tract infections, their expected symptom duration, the limited benefit of antibiotics in such cases, and the individual and societal harms associated with unnecessary antibiotic use. Reducing patient expectations and demand is particularly important given the progressive increase in dispensing observed after direct request and explicit insistence.

These proposed interventions are consistent with the observed dispensing patterns and previous literature; however, the present study did not evaluate their effectiveness. Future implementation studies should therefore assess which combinations of regulatory, professional, and demand-reduction strategies produce sustained improvements in community antibiotic stewardship.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgement. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study evaluated only one standardised clinical scenario involving an uncomplicated, likely viral upper respiratory tract infection for which antibiotic therapy was not indicated. Dispensing responses may differ for other presentations, including urinary tract infection, diarrhoea, dental pain, or other conditions; therefore, the findings should not be generalised to all possible clinical scenarios.

Second, each pharmacy was visited only once. Although this approach minimised the risk of detection and behavioural contamination, it did not capture potential within-pharmacy variation across different staff members, shifts, or repeated encounters. In addition, because the visits were conducted covertly, the professional designation and experience of the dispensing staff member could not be verified; some encounters may therefore have involved a licensed pharmacist, whereas others may have involved another pharmacy employee engaged in dispensing activities.

Third, the study relied on immediate post-encounter documentation by the simulated patients. Although the structured electronic checklist and prompt recording were intended to minimise recall bias, minor inaccuracies in recalling specific questions or counselling details cannot be excluded.

Fourth, the predefined basic clinical assessment threshold was based on the number of clinically relevant questions asked and did not weight individual questions according to their clinical or safety importance. Consequently, meeting the threshold should not be interpreted as evidence that a comprehensive or fully safety-appropriate assessment was performed. To address this limitation, individual assessment components, including allergy screening, prior medication use, and chronic medical conditions, were reported separately.

Fifth, although the sample was geographically distributed across five major Jordanian governorates and included central, northern, and southern regions, it did not include all governorates and may not capture all local variations in community-pharmacy practice. The findings therefore should not be interpreted as fully nationally representative.

Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding structural determinants. Nevertheless, the escalation protocol and multivariable modelling strengthen internal validity and policy relevance. Although the observed patterns were consistent across several analyses, the findings should be interpreted within the limits of a cross-sectional simulated patient design conducted in selected governorates, and causal explanations for dispensing behaviour cannot be established.

Conclusion

Non-prescription antibiotic dispensing in Jordanian community pharmacies was common in this multi-governorate sample and was significantly associated with patient-demand escalation and pharmacy-level contextual characteristics. Limited clinical assessment and frequent dispensing of broad-spectrum agents raise important stewardship concerns. These findings support the need for coordinated regulatory, professional, and public-level strategies, although further research is needed to clarify causal mechanisms and evaluate the effectiveness of specific interventions.
