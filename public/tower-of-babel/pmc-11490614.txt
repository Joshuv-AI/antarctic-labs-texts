The Climate Data for Adaptation and Vulnerability Assessments and the Spatial Interactions Downscaling Method
Authors: Andre Geraldo de Lima Moraes, Sajad Khoshnood Motlagh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents the spatial interactions downscaling (SPID) method and introduces the climate data for adaptation and vulnerability assessments (ClimAVA) dataset. SPID employs random forest models to capture the relationship between spatial patterns at global circulation model (GCM) resolution and fine-resolution pixel values. In summary, a random forest model is trained for each fine spatial resolution pixel of the reference data as the predictand, and nine pixels from the spatially resampled (coarser) version of the reference data at the GCM’s resolutions as predictors. Models are then utilized to downscale the bias-corrected GCM data. The ClimAVA-SW dataset offers a high-resolution (4 km), bias-corrected, downscaled future climate projection derived from seventeen CMIP6 GCMs. It includes three variables (daily precipitation, minimum and maximum temperature) for three shared socioeconomic pathways (SSP245, SSP370, SSP585) across the U.S. Southwest region. The ClimAVA dataset sets itself apart with the SPID method’s capacity to provide remarkable climate realism, high physical plausibility of change, and excellent representation of extreme events while maintaining user-friendliness and requiring relatively low computational resources.Subject terms: Climate and Earth system modelling, Projection and prediction

Background & Summary

The repercussions of global climate change on the environment, economy, and social frameworks, once considered theoretical projections just a few decades ago, have materialized into reality1. Independent of future emission choices, the world is committed to serious economic and environmental impacts2, compelling us to adapt to already rapidly changing climate conditions. Adapting to climate impacts requires careful justification and planning, which should be grounded in a thorough understanding of these impacts’ expected scale and geographic patterns3. A key component in this effort is leveraging future climate projections generated by GCMs to inform decision-making and planning processes. However, a common challenge in local assessments of climate change impacts is the inherent spatial coarseness of outputs from GCMs (50–300 km), rendering them unsuitable for direct application in local impact models4.

Local to regional climate change impact, adaptation, and vulnerability assessments necessitate the utilization of data at higher spatial resolutions5–7. Transforming coarse data to the desired high-resolution format is known as downscaling. Downscaling is crucial for converting the projected large-scale climate from GCMs into scales that properly represent the specific local areas of interest and their needs8,9. Two main categories of downscaling methods exist: statistical downscaling and dynamical downscaling, also known as regional climate models (RCMs)10. Dynamical downscaling involves the utilization of an RCM for a specific region of interest nested within a GCM11,12. However, dynamical downscaling comes with drawbacks such as significant computational requirements, substantial data storage needs—particularly when multiple experiment realizations are necessary—and the propagation of biases from GCMs to RCMs13,14. On the other hand, statistical downscaling is considered more efficient and straightforward. These methods directly incorporate the relationship between observed data and GCM outputs5.

Statistical methods, which involve establishing statistical relationships between coarse-scale (GCM-scale) and fine-scale climate from observed data and subsequently applying these relationships to future projections, have seen more application compared to dynamical model downscaling in studies addressing hydrologic impacts of climate change in the western United States8,9,13,15,16. The most common statistical downscaling methods used in the literature include the delta method17 and its variations bias correction spatial disaggregation (BCSD)13, and delta hybrid18; the localized constructed analogs (LOCA)19, multivariate adaptive constructed analogs (MACA)20 and deterministic methods21.

Delta-based methods apply the shift (difference/delta) from GCM historical and future projections to observed data. It produces spatially and temporally consistent datasets with high climate realism, as precipitation and temperature events are equal to observed events plus the shift from the GCM projections. However, its main disadvantage is that the sequence of events on the future projections is the same as observed in the past - plus the shift (delta). This issue is known as low physical plausibility of change, and it limits the usability of these datasets for several studies; for example, studies that account for the number of dry days, length of the growing season, and the number of days over or under a temperature or precipitation threshold will likely present virtually the same result from the past (observed) climatology and not from the GCM projections22. BCSD acknowledges that GCMs have biases, so it applies a bias correction step before spatially disaggregating climate data (similar to the delta method). The delta hybrid method took BCSD one step further, using a quantile delta mapping scheme to map monthly shifts from the GCMs to selected months of observed data18. Quantile delta mapping adds more “variability” to the downscaled dataset; however, it suffers from disadvantages similar to those of the delta method as it keeps the sequence of daily events from the observed/past climatology from the selected months.

Deterministic downscaling methods use models trained on predictands (fine-scale climate data of interest) and predictors (coarse-scale climate data) and apply those models to downscale coarse GCMs. Under the umbrella of deterministic methods, the most used are regression models, support vector machines (SVM), and artificial neural networks (ANN)21. Predictors can be any climatic variable present in both observed data and data generated by the GCM, such as different wind components, pressure, vorticity, etc. The advantage of these methods is that they keep the signal and sequence of events from the GCM, increasing the physical plausibility of change of the downscaled projections. Other statistical methods focus on using Constructed Analogs19,20. The main disadvantages of statistical methods are (1) the need for matching predictands and predictors from observed and GCM data, although some methods can extrapolate values, (2) most methods are limited by the data provided during training, (3) data manipulation is more complex than delta-based methods, these methods are (4) more computationally demanding when compared to delta-based methods, and (5) often present low climate realism, especially for daily and monthly precipitation.

In summary, the application of an ideal downscaling method should result in a high spatial resolution dataset that is bias-corrected, offers high climate realism, maintains physical plausibility of change from GCMs, maintains changes of signal from the GCMs, provides an accurate representation of extreme events, is easy to apply, and is not overly computationally demanding. Efforts to develop such an ideal method should be pursued to facilitate the democratization of the use of future climate data in local to regional climate change impact, adaptation, and vulnerability assessments.

This paper aims to introduce the spatial interactions downscaling (SPID) method and the climate data for adaptation and vulnerability assessments (ClimAVA) dataset, providing a high-resolution (4 km) bias-corrected, downscaled projection of future climate based on seventeen CMIP6 GCMs. The dataset includes three variables (pr, tasmin, tasmax) and three scenarios (SSP245, SSP370, SSP585) for the entire U.S. Southwest region. The ClimAVA dataset sets itself apart with the SPID method’s capacity to provide remarkable climate realism, high physical plausibility of change, and excellent representation of extreme events while maintaining user-friendliness and requiring relatively low computational resources.

Methods

Data acquisition

Gridded datasets of daily precipitation (pr), minimum surface air temperature (tasmin), and maximum surface air temperature (tasmax) at 4 km spatial resolution, covering the period from 1981 to 2020, were acquired from the parameter-elevation relationships on independent slopes model (PRISM 4 K) project (https://prism.oregonstate.edu/) to be utilized for both bias correction and the training of downscaling models. The PRISM gridding method integrates geographically and elevation-weighted regression, station weighting based on topography, distance to the coast, and atmospheric variables23. This dataset was chosen as our reference data for its temporal coverage from 1881 to the present, well-suited 4 km spatial resolution for our objectives, demonstrated reliability in representing precipitation patterns in complex terrains24, favorable performance compared to alternative datasets25, and its status as one of the most commonly used spatial climate datasets in the United States26. Hereafter, PRISM data will be referred to as reference data.

Coupled model intercomparison project phase 6 (CMIP6) historical and future climate projection data27, generated through GCMs for pr, tasmax, and tasmin, were obtained from the ESGF MetaGrid (https://aims2.llnl.gov/search). The selection of models adhered to specific criteria: (1) daily temporal resolution; (2) inclusion of all three shared socioeconomic pathways (SSP) of interest – SSP245, SSP370, and SSP585 within the same model and realization; (3) availability of data for all variables of interest – pr, tasmax, and tasmin; and (4) nominal resolution falling between 100 and 250 km. Due to data storage limitations, only the first realization of each model was acquired. These criteria led to a total of 17 models (Table 1).

Area of interest and data preparation

The ClimAVA-SW28 dataset encompasses the geopolitical boundaries of the six states in the United States Southwest: California, Nevada, Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, and Colorado. To facilitate hydrology modeling efforts, the complete area of watersheds that run into these states has also been incorporated into the spatial coverage of the dataset (Fig. 1).

Schematic diagram of the SPID approach, which involves the following steps: (1) downloading reference and (2) CMIP6 (GCMs) data, (3) resampling reference data to match the native resolutions of GCMs, (4) bias correcting GCM data using (a) Quantile Mapping (QM) for pr and (b) Equidistant Quantile Matching (EDCDFm) for tasmax and tasmin, (5) training random forest models for each pixel based on original (fine resolution) and resampled (coarse resolution) pixels from the reference data, and finally (6) downscaling the bias-corrected data from step 4 using the random forest models trained in step 5.

Before conducting any analysis, all datasets were subsetted to the designated area of interest. Among the 17 chosen GCMs, there are 15 different grid spacings (spatial resolutions) (Table 1). Grid space varies from 0.703125° × 0703125° (EC-Earth3 models) to 1.875° × 2.5° (NorESM2-LM). Our aim is to demonstrate the performance of SPID in downscaling from various resolutions without the need to regrid the GCMs. This means that all analyses are conducted on the models’ native grid, thereby avoiding any loss of information or distortion caused by regridding the data. To enable bias correction and training of the downscaling method, the observed dataset was resampled to match each of the 15 different grid spacings corresponding to the grid spacing of each GCM. A conservative resampling method was applied to adjust the reference data to match the GCM resolutions. Essentially, the resampled pixel represents the average value of all the pixels from the reference data that fell within each pixel of the GCM. This procedure was consistently applied to every GCM under consideration.

Bias correction

Bias correction was accomplished by quantile mapping (QM) for pr, and equidistant quantile matching (EDCDFm) for tasmax and tasmim (Fig. 1). Quantile mapping17,29 corrects the bias in a model value by adjusting it to the observed value (here represented by the resampled PRISM data) at the quantile within the model’s historical distribution. The EDCDFm method30 corrects the bias in model value by adding the difference (∆) between the model future and historical value to the observed values based on the quantile from the model value (CDFs are constructed in 30-year windows). When bias-correcting historical data, EDCDFm behaves as QM (Fig. 1). Different methods were applied to different variables due to the concern that QM may alter the magnitude and even direction of mean changes projected from the original GCM31–34, especially for temperature data. Table 2 depicts the distribution for all 17 models of the difference in mean change from historical (1985–2014) and future (1971–2100) at SSP585 before and after bias correction. Differences were negligible up to 99 percentiles, indicating these methods successfully maintained the mean change (in magnitude and direction) from the original GCM after bias correction.

Downscaling

The SPID method assumes that climatic spatial patterns observed in the resampled (coarser) reference data are analogous to spatial climate patterns in GCMs. If those relationships hold true, they can be used to downscale GCM data. SPID employs a random forest model35 to translate the relationship between spatial patterns at coarse resolution and fine-resolution pixel values. A random forest algorithm grows numerous decision trees on bootstrapped subsamples of the data. It combines the predictions of all these trees to forecast the out-of-bag data, which were not utilized in training the trees, thus quantifying prediction error. This algorithm is a fast, supervised classification method that enhances classification performance through the amalgamation of multiple decision trees and introduces stochasticity in the modeling process. Consequently, it can effectively handle correlated and interacting predictor variables36. A random forest model is trained for each pixel, with the finer pixel of the observed data as the predictand and nine pixels from the spatially resampled (coarser) version of the reference data (at the GCMs spatial resolution) as predictors.

In practice, each fine-resolution pixel is associated with the corresponding coarse pixel (resampled observed data), which contains the finer pixel within its spatial boundaries. The model selects the eight surrounding coarse pixels, totaling nine predictors per pixel (see Fig. 1). All available daily values are used to train the model. Subsequently, during downscaling, the random forest model translates spatial patterns from coarse-resolution values (from GCM) to finer-resolution values on a daily basis. This process is repeated for each fine pixel, i.e., there is a random forest model for each pixel. The number of predictors may be less than nine for pixels located along the coast or the U.S. border. This limitation arises due to spatial coverage constraints within the reference dataset.

Using only one coarse-resolution pixel—the central one—as a predictor significantly diminishes the effectiveness of the downscaling process. On the other hand, employing more than nine predictors (e.g., 25) did not substantially enhance downscaling performance and led to substantially increased computational demands.

For the validation process, models were trained using 30 years of data (1981–2010) and validated using 10 years (2011–2020). Models intended for the downscaling application to create the ClimAVA-SW28 dataset are trained using all 40 years of available data. The effectiveness of models is influenced by the “events” they learned during training. Consequently, they perform best with a larger training dataset as it provides more climatic spatial patterns to learn from over an extended time series. This limitation becomes particularly significant with the intensification of extreme precipitation and heat/cold waves37,38 and their implications for human society39,40 and the environment41. For instance, extreme precipitation events not observed in the training datasets might be poorly represented by the downscaling process, as random forest models struggle to extrapolate information beyond its training range. More details regarding SPID’s capabilities in downscaling extreme events are discussed in the validation section.

SPID computational demand

The SPID method has been shown to be computationally efficient. To enhance speed while utilizing available resources (AMD 7713 P nodes with 64 cores and 256 GB memory), especially memory RAM, we train and export the models for each pixel. In our setup, training each model takes less than a second for pr and less than 2 seconds for tasmax and tasmin, per core. Parallelization significantly expedites the process. With all models trained, downscaling the entire Southwest region of the United States for five years’ worth of daily data requires only 11 minutes for pr and 31 minutes for tasmax and tasmin, utilizing 20 cores and 80GB of memory per job. Another advantage of SPID is its straightforward coding, which, when coupled with its relatively low computational requirements, positions it as a valuable tool for democratizing access to high-quality downscaled future climate projections; especially in areas where high-performance computing resources are not readily available.

Data Records

ClimAVA-SW28 provides daily climatic data at ~4 km spatial resolution for 17 GCMs (Table 1), three different climatic variables (pr, tasmax, and tasmin), and three different shared socioeconomic pathways (SSP245, SSP370, and SSP585). Historical runs span from January 1, 1981, to December 31, 2014. Future scenarios span from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2100. All years have 365 days (leap years are not included). Data is provided as NetCDF4 files. The ClimAVA-SW28 dataset encompasses the geopolitical boundaries of the six states in the southwestern United States: California, Nevada, Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, and Colorado, and watersheds that run into these states (Fig. 1).

ClimAVA-SW28 can be found at the World Data Center for Climate (WDCC) (10.26050/WDCC/ClimAVA-SW).

Technical Validation

As discussed, grid spacing (spatial resolution) varies considerably among the 17 selected GCMs. To demonstrate SPID downscaling capabilities, validation results are shown from downscaling done for two different grid spacings, representing the finest (0.703125° × 0.703125° from EC-Earth3) and coarsest (1.875° × 2.5° from NorESM2-LM) resolutions, hereafter referred to as FR and CR, respectively.

In this section, the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean prediction error (MPE) are employed to assess the comparison between SPID downscaled results and reference data on a daily time scale; the analysis of 10-year annual averages offers insights into the climate realism and spatial variability of SPID downscaled outcomes; the examination of temporal variability in annual data at specific locations in northern Utah (Cache Valley and Bear River Range) and California (Sacramento and High Sierras), provides insights into how SPID maintains temporal consistency in its downscaled results and; the distribution of daily event magnitudes in percentiles is further explored to evaluate SPID’s capability to downscale extreme events. Additionally, the matching of dry-wet days for precipitation is demonstrated.

Precipitation

SPID shows satisfactory results when downscaling both from FR and CR; however, downscaling from FR yields better results than those from CR (Fig. 2). The mean R2 for FR was 0.88 against 0.72 from CR. Downscaling from both resolutions yields higher R2 values in mountain complex regions (for ex., the Intermountain West and High Sieras) than in flatter regions (Western Colorado and Western New Mexico). I.e., SPID yields more accurate results in complex climatic regions where precipitation events are influenced by orographic processes compared to flatter regions where meteorology is strongly linked to synoptic signatures. As expected, RMSE is slightly lower for FR (1.08 mm) than CR (1.65 mm). Higher RMSE values are in higher precipitation regions, such as the High Sierras and the Pacific Coast Range. Meaning the errors are small when compared as a ratio of total daily precipitation, and flatter regions of Western Colorado and Western New Mexico. Mean MPEs were −0.04 mm and −0.05 for FR and CR, respectively. However, spatial and histogram distribution of MPE reveals how SPID performs best when downscaling from finer resolutions.

Evaluation of the 10-year annual average precipitation demonstrates how SPID maintains climate realism and spatial consistency in downscaled data (Fig. 3). FR and CR show mean negative biases of −15.9 and −18.2 mm, respectively. Further analysis of the bias distribution indicates a generally dispersed pattern throughout the region of interest. However, larger disparities are notable in high-precipitation areas, where errors are relatively small when considered as a proportion of total precipitation. It is worth noting that the scattered errors observed may stem from spatial/temporal inhomogeneities in the reference dataset. Such inconsistencies could arise, for example, from temporal inconsistencies in station data, where station data are used when available but disappear from the station network used in the interpolation process on days (months, years) with missing data25. Further analysis of the bias distribution in seasonal precipitation (Supplementary Figure 1) reveals a generally dispersed pattern similar to that observed in the annual data, indicating that errors are distributed similarly across different seasons.

A comprehensive analysis of multiple locations was conducted to assess the potential impact of the biases encountered in the 10-year annual average analysis on climatic trends in downscaled data. For brevity, an analysis comparing annual data from the reference with downscaled data at four representative locations characterized by complex terrain is presented (Fig. 4). Results indicate that the overall bias observed in the 10-year annual average precipitation is distributed over time and does not influence trends. Downscaled annual precipitation values closely align with the reference data across the majority of locations. However, a few exceptions are notable, such as Naomi Peak in Northern Utah (Fig. 4).

GCMs, due in part to their coarse spatial resolution, often exhibit many days with minimal precipitation, commonly referred to as “drizzle precipitation”42–44. Given that drizzle precipitation is often unrealistic and can adversely affect subsequent analyses, it’s crucial to ensure that downscaled products do not include artificial drizzle precipitation. For validation purposes, days with precipitation amounts below < 0.1 mm/day were categorized as dry days32,45, after which the correspondence between dry and wet days in downscaled data and the reference dataset was computed (Fig. 5). Downscaling from FR and CR resulted in mean correspondences of 93.0% and 91.4%, respectively. Lower correspondence percentages were noted in the mountainous and topographically complex regions of the Intermountain West, particularly in Colorado. However, the percentages rarely dipped below 75%, even in areas with lower correspondence.

Correspondence between precipitation/non-precipitation days in reference data and SPID downscaled data from finest (FR) and coarsest (CR) spatial resolutions. Non-precipitation days were considered as days with less than 0.1 mm of precipitation.

Climate change has intensified the frequency and severity of extreme precipitation events, especially in dry areas like the Southwestern U.S.37. Given the substantial impacts of these events and their anticipated increase with global warming, accurately representing them in terms of spatial distribution and magnitude through downscaling is crucial. SPID has exhibited an exceptional ability to downscale these extreme events (Fig. 6). For daily precipitation events at the 95th percentile, downscaling from both FR and CR yielded results that approach perfection, with mean differences of −0.03 mm and −0.12 mm, respectively, and values closely approximating zero across the region of interest. At the 99th percentile, mean differences were recorded as −0.99 mm and −2.25 mm for FR and CR, respectively. Once again, higher errors were detected in high-precipitation areas like the High Sierras and the Pacific Coast Range, as well as in the flatter regions of Western Colorado and Western New Mexico. As with previous analyses, downscaling from finer spatial resolutions yields better results than coarser resolutions, as observed in mean errors and the spatial distributions of errors.

Difference between the 95th and 99th percentile of daily precipitation events from the reference data (Obs) and SPID downscaled data from the finest (FR) and coarsest (CR) spatial resolutions.

It is worth noting that for daily extreme precipitation events, most differences between the reference and downscaled data were negative, indicating a tendency to underpredict extreme events. This phenomenon can be attributed to the nature of the downscaling method employed in SPID, which relies on Random Forest algorithms. Notably, only events present in the training dataset can be accurately reproduced during the downscaling process, as random forest models have limited capacity to extrapolate from values in the training data set. Therefore, it is hypothesized that the larger and more diverse the training dataset, the better the downscaling results should be, particularly when capturing the uniqueness of extreme events.

Downscaled GCM data

As described before, SPID employs a model per pixel and downscales daily data. This implies that each downscaled value is processed independently of its neighboring pixels or the preceding and succeeding days’ values. Consequently, the downscaling procedure is both temporally and spatially independent. One might wonder if climate realism is maintained in such an approach. Figure 13 illustrates the spatial distribution of climatic patterns for a single day, a month, and a year of pr and tasmax downscaled from the EC-Earth3 model. Precipitation patterns (on this wet day) conform to expectations, exhibiting smooth transitions that depict precipitation over mountainous terrain, flat landscapes, and regions with zero precipitation. Figure 16 illustrates the differences in extreme events from ClimAVA-SW28 and LOCA247 for the EC-Earth3 GCM (r1i1p1f1) under the SSP585 scenario by the end of the century (1970–2100). At the 95th percentile, extreme precipitation events show virtually identical results between LOCA247 and ClimAVA. However, at the 99th percentile, noticeable, albeit small, differences emerge, particularly in mountainous areas. ClimAVA tends to present slightly greater precipitation events on windward slopes and lower values in the rain shadows. For tasmax, relatively small differences arise at the 1st and 5th percentiles in high-elevation areas where ClimAVA is systematically hotter. The relationship inverts at the 95th and especially the 99th percentiles, with ClimAVA being substantially cooler than LOCA247. For Tasmin, ClimAVA shows systematically higher temperatures at the 1st and 5th percentiles, particularly at high elevations, which is also observed at the 50th percentile, though less substantially. Interestingly, both datasets present similar temperatures at the 95th and 99th percentiles in the high elevations of the Intermountain West. However, ClimAVA is systematically cooler at lower elevations. In California, ClimAVA is hotter on the Pacific Coast and Sierras at the 95th and 99th percentiles. These results also demonstrate how LOCA247 presents a bigger range in extreme temperatures than ClimAVA, i.e. systematic cooler 1st percentiles and hotter 99th percentiles.

Differences in daily precipitation (pr) and maximum (tasmax) and minimum (tasmax) air temperature events from ClimAVA-SW28 and LOCA247 for the EC-Earth3 GCM (r1i1p1f1) under the SSP585 scenario by the end of the century (1970–2100) at different percentiles. The analysis is done at the LOCA247 grid with Climava values being extracted by the nearest neighbour. The difference is calculated as LOCA247 minus ClimAVA-SW28.

As ClimAVA and LOCA247 use the same bias correction method for temperatures, the observed differences could mainly stem from two causes: the downscaling method itself or the reference datasets used. To determine if the differences arise from the downscaling methods, the methods should be compared using the same dataset and bias correction scheme. However, we hypothesize that the differences primarily stem from the reference datasets. ClimAVA uses PRISM data from 1981 to 2020, while LOCA247 uses data from Pierce et al.48, an adaptation from Livneh et al.49, covering the period from 1915 to 2018. As previously stated, the larger the reference dataset, the better the representation of extreme events, as it provides more variability to sample from. However, it is crucial to understand how the reference data can affect downscaling extreme events from both LOCA and SPID methods.

Attention has been given to comparing precipitation datasets24 and developing extreme-preserving long-term gridded daily precipitation datasets48. We hypothesize that PRISM and Pierce et al.48 present similar extreme precipitation events, mostly covered in both datasets, as extreme precipitation events have increased37, and the most significant events might be present in the 1981–2020 period covered by both datasets, explaining why extreme precipitation events look similar in both downscaled products. However, a thorough examination of extreme temperatures in gridded datasets is warranted, particularly for the first half of the 20th century when station densities were significantly lower49. This issue is especially pertinent in mountainous areas, where larger differences were found. These studies will be essential to clarify if non-climatic extreme temperature events are present in the reference datasets and how these features can influence the results of extreme events in downscaled products.

As noted, downscaling methods like SPID and LOCA have difficulty downscaling values that exceed those present in their reference data. Therefore, care should be taken when analyzing the magnitude of future extreme record events. However, examining how frequently extreme events present in the reference data might occur in the future under different scenarios is highly recommended.
