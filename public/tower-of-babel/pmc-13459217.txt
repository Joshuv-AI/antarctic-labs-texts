Survival over competence: a qualitative study of senior nursing students’ clinical experiences in Karachi, Pakistan
Authors: Sheheryar Shahid, Salma Rattani, Saima Sachwani, Saira Lalani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical placement is the cornerstone of nursing education. In Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) like Pakistan, the lack of affiliated teaching hospitals in private nursing colleges requires students to undertake clinical rotations in resource-constrained public facilities, shaping challenging learning environments. This study explores the clinical experiences, challenges, and coping strategies of senior undergraduate nursing students.MethodsAn exploratory descriptive qualitative design was employed, underpinned by Benner’s from Novice to Expert theoretical framework. Data were collected through eight semi-structured focus group discussions (FGDs) with 59 Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) students from three private nursing colleges in Karachi, Pakistan, using purposive maximum variation sampling. Data was analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsData were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis, and Patricia Benner’s Novice to Expert Model was applied as a guiding theoretical lens across the analysis; the integrated interpretation is presented in Fig. 1. The analysis revealed a profound disconnect between academic preparation and clinical reality, encapsulated in five major themes: (1) Systemic and Environmental Deficiencies, characterized by institutional neglect and severe resource scarcity leading to unsafe practices; (2) A Culture of Supervisory Neglect and Hostility, where students felt abandoned by instructors and devalued by hospital staff; (3) The Pervasive Theory–Practice Chasm, marked by the cognitive dissonance of witnessing normalized unsafe protocols; (4) The Crucible of Confidence, detailing the psychological toll on students and their development of resilience and “digital mentorship” as survival mechanisms; and (5) A Call for Systemic Reform, outlining student-driven recommendations for accountability and improved supervision.ConclusionsThe current model of clinical education for private college students in public hospitals in Karachi is fraught with systemic failures that compromise patient safety and student well-being. Students’ progress toward competence is not through structured mentorship, but through resilience developed in a harsh, unsupported environment. Urgent reforms in regulatory oversight, preceptor training, and resource allocation are required to bridge the theory-practice gap and ensure safe clinical learning environments.

Introduction

Clinical education is central to nursing preparation because it integrates theoretical knowledge with hands-on patient care in real healthcare settings. The Clinical Learning Environment (CLE) is more than a venue for skills practice; it is a complex social and organizational space where students develop clinical competence, exercise critical judgment, and begin professional socialization [1, 2]. Across contexts, the quality of supervision, psychosocial interactions, and institutional support within the CLE strongly shapes students’ confidence and readiness for practice [3]. In well-resourced systems, formal preceptorship models and adequate infrastructure partially buffer students from clinical pressures. However, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), persistent shortages of staff, space, and materials fundamentally alter how students experience and learn in clinical settings [4, 5].

These structural constraints are especially visible in Pakistan, where rapid expansion of nursing education has been driven largely by private colleges. Many of these institutions lack affiliated teaching hospitals and therefore depend on formal affiliations with publicly funded tertiary care hospitals to meet mandatory clinical hour requirements [6, 7]. While this model exposes students to diverse pathologies and high patient volumes, it places them in overcrowded wards characterized by understaffing and limited resources [8]. Students often report a stark contrast between the controlled academic environment of their colleges and the chaotic realities of public hospital wards, intensifying the theory–practice gap and limiting opportunities for safe, supervised learning [1, 9]. Evidence from comparable LMIC contexts further suggests that, under such pressures, staff may perceive students as an additional burden rather than learners, weakening the supervisory relationships essential for effective clinical education [10, 11].

However, beyond resource limitations and supervisory strain, an important structural dimension remains insufficiently explored: the ‘pedagogical outsider’ status of private-sector nursing students who train in public-sector hospitals. Within this affiliation model, students pay for private education but enter clinical spaces where they lack institutional standing, formal preceptors, and access to protected learning opportunities. Quantitative evidence from Karachi indicates dissatisfaction with clinical experiences [8], yet little is known about how this ‘guest status’ reshapes students’ learning processes, professional identity formation, and ability to progress clinically. Understanding this dynamic is essential because it may shift students from a pedagogical mode of learning to a survivalist mode, where coping with the environment takes precedence over structured skill development.

This study addresses this gap by qualitatively examining the clinical learning experiences of senior undergraduate nursing students from private colleges placed in public tertiary hospitals in Karachi. The inquiry is guided by Patricia Benner’s Novice to Expert model, used here as an analytical lens rather than a skills taxonomy [12]. Benner emphasizes that progression from advanced beginner to competent practitioner requires situated coaching through reflective supervision. By examining students’ accounts through this lens, the study seeks to illuminate how systemic neglect and strained supervision interrupt the coaching necessary for professional progression within the clinical learning environment. Therefore, this study aims to explore and describe the clinical experiences of third- and fourth-year undergraduate nursing students from private colleges in Karachi, focusing on the challenges they encounter, the learning opportunities available, and how these experiences influence their development as future healthcare professionals.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed an exploratory, descriptive, qualitative design, chosen for its ability to illustrate under-researched phenomena through rich, participant-driven insights [13]. This design was particularly appropriate for researching into the unique clinical experiences of undergraduate nursing students from private colleges in Karachi, a context previously identified as critical yet not well understood, who train in affiliated public hospitals. Moreover, the exploration enabled the discovery of themes and insights without predefined theories [14]. Furthermore, this approach facilitated an in-depth examination of students’ multi-layered challenges, support systems, and coping strategies during clinical placements, thus laying a robust foundation for future research [15].

In addition, the descriptive component was vital for capturing the essence of students’ experiences, and presented through their own lens [15]. Alongside, it aimed to document the complexities of clinical learning, emotions, and interactions within public hospital settings [16].

Lastly, a qualitative approach was deemed appropriate due to its capacity to explore the meanings individuals assigned to experiences, moving beyond numerical data to access depth and context [14, 17, 18]. Likewise, by emphasizing participants’ voices within their real-world contexts, this method aligned with the study’s goal of understanding how students navigated the distinct dynamics of their clinical placements [19].

The research setting comprised three private nursing colleges in Karachi, a major metropolitan city with numerous private colleges offering undergraduate nursing programmes in Pakistan. To specify further, the selected colleges did not have their hospitals for clinical training. Consequently, undergraduate nursing students in their Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) programmes were required to undertake clinical learning in public tertiary care hospitals within the city. For these placements, colleges typically engage experienced nurses or instructors from public hospitals on a service-rendered basis to accompany and supervise students.

Firstly, this specific setting was purposefully selected, aligning directly with the study’s core objectives and offering a unique lens into the research problem. The placement of private college students in public hospital settings introduced a complex interplay of factors likely to influence clinical learning experiences. For instance, public hospitals in Karachi typically operate under high patient loads, resource constraints, and diverse workforce dynamics. Thus, these conditions potentially affected students who are not institutionally enrolled in these clinical environments.

This unique arrangement offered a critical opportunity to examine key dimensions of clinical education, including students’ integration into hospital teams, access to learning resources, the nature of supervision and mentorship, the application of theoretical knowledge in real-world contexts, and the impact of different institutional cultures. Thus, understanding these dynamics was essential for assessing how such inter-institutional arrangements influenced student preparation for professional nursing practice.

Participants and sampling

This study employed purposive maximum variation sampling to capture the full breadth and depth of clinical learning experiences among undergraduate nursing students, ensuring that the findings reflected a systemic perspective rather than localized issues. Variation was systematically sought across three core dimensions. First, institutional affiliation was considered by recruiting participants from three different private nursing colleges (Colleges A, B, and C) that utilise the same public tertiary hospital for clinical rotations. This enabled triangulation of data across different campus cultures to determine whether challenges such as ‘supervisory neglect’ were institutional or systemic.

Second, academic progression and clinical diversity were incorporated into the sampling strategy. The study focused on third- and fourth-year BSN students, who have transitioned from foundational learning to managing complex care scenarios, situating them at a critical stage of professional identity formation. These senior students typically demonstrate the cognitive and ‘reflective maturity’ required to articulate their learning trajectory from ‘Advanced Beginner’ toward ‘Competence’ [20]. Clinical diversity was ensured by including students with exposure to various specialties, ranging from general Medical/Surgical wards to high-acuity units such as the Emergency Room and Intensive Care Units. To maintain data integrity, specific exclusion criteria were applied. Students on academic probation or suspension were excluded to avoid bias related to academic grievances, and students whose experience was predominantly simulation-based were excluded to ensure that the data reflected real-world clinical realities.

The total student population across the three participating colleges was 260. This included 65 students from College A, 105 from College B, and 90 from College C. The cohort was comprised of 155 Year 3 students (125 male, 20 female) and 105 Year 4 students (94 male, 6 female). After applying the inclusion criteria, 15 students were excluded because of ineligibility criteria, resulting in a total eligible population of 245 students.

From this eligible pool, 59 participants were recruited on a voluntary basis. The demographic breakdown of participants in comparison to the total eligible population is presented in Table 1. The final sample size (N = 59) was determined by thematic saturation, assessed through a concurrent process of data collection and reflexive analysis [21]. Recruitment and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) continued until the ‘information power’ of the data was deemed sufficient to answer the research questions. By the seventh FGD, the research team determined that no novel codes or conceptual insights regarding the ‘theory-practice chasm’ or ‘institutional neglect’ were emerging. An eighth FGD was conducted as a confirmatory session to ensure the robustness of the themes and to verify that the diverse experiences of participants across all three colleges were fully represented.

Data collection and instrumentation

Data was collected between June 2025 and July 2025 using semi-structured Focus Group Discussions (FGDs). This method was chosen to facilitate interaction among participants, helping to surface shared experiences and cultural norms within the student body [22]. A total of eight FGDs were conducted (2 at College A, 3 at College B, 3 at College C), with group sizes ranging from 6 to 8 participants [22]. In all, 59 participants were enrolled in this study. Table 1 presents the institutional distribution of FGDs.

Among these 59 participants, 47 (79.7%) were males, and 12 (20.3%) were females (Table 1), reflecting the increasing trend of male enrolment in nursing. Historically, nursing education in Pakistan was predominantly female-oriented, and admission policies regulated by the Pakistan Nursing Council limited the proportion of male students in nursing programmes. Earlier admission policies for BSN programmes allocated approximately 90% of seats to female students and only about 10% to male students, reflecting the traditional perception of nursing as a female-dominated profession [23]. However, regulatory directions changed, emphasizing open-merit admissions without strict gender-based quotas, allowing institutions to admit both male and female candidates more equitably [24]. This policy shift has contributed to a gradual increase in the enrolment of male students in several BSN programmes across the country.

The participants were recruited through a systematic and ethical process emphasising voluntariness, transparency, and confidentiality. Moreover, after obtaining ethical approval from the Aga Khan University Ethical Review Committee (ERC approval number: 025-11205-34329), formal permissions were sought from the principals of the three selected private nursing colleges in Karachi. Thus, each college received a formal request package that included study details and consent protocols.

Furthermore, each private college had designated year coordinators, who were faculty members assigned to each cohort (years 1–4) who managed academic scheduling, student communications, and logistical support. Following the college’s approval, the third- and fourth-year coordinators assisted in arranging a classroom venue for FGDs and approached eligible BSN students. Study information was then distributed during brief in-class sessions. All recruitment materials clearly described the voluntary nature of participation, measures to protect confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any point without academic or personal consequences.

Next, the interested students contacted the researcher directly and underwent a brief eligibility screening. Consequently, those who qualified were provided with detailed consent forms in English or Urdu, with adequate time for review before signing. In each college, focus group discussions were scheduled at times convenient for the participants and were grouped by year level and college. Meanwhile, the entire recruitment process prioritised informed consent, participant autonomy, and ethical transparency throughout.

The primary method for data collection in this qualitative study was Focus Group Discussions (FGDs). These served as a valuable technique for gathering rich data by group interaction to explore participants’ perspectives, experiences, and the meanings they attributed to them [22, 25]. Moreover, this interactive format is particularly suited for examining shared experiences and identifying common themes among individuals with similar backgrounds or exposures, such as nursing students from the same cohort and college [25]. Finally, all procedures for recruiting participants, obtaining informed consent, conducting discussions, and handling recordings were conducted in alignment with the COREQ 32-item checklist to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting [26]. The completed checklist is provided as Additional File 1.

Semi-structured focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted using a pre-developed interview guide that contained open-ended questions and prompts. This approach ensured that key research topics were addressed consistently across groups while allowing flexibility to explore emergent themes and follow lines of discussion introduced by participants themselves [19, 27]. This flexibility was essential for the exploratory study, as it enabled a deeper understanding of the students’ lived experiences.

Each focus group discussion (FGD) session lasted 60 to 90 min. This duration is generally considered sufficient to cover the key topics in adequate depth without causing undue fatigue among participants [22]. The total duration of these discussions was 500 min, with an average of 62 min.

Moreover, the FGDs were conducted in a quiet, private, and comfortable setting within the premises of the participants’ respective nursing colleges. These venues were arranged at a mutually agreed-upon neutral location with the support of the administrative staff from each college. In addition, a “Do Not Disturb” sign was posted outside the venue to ensure an undisturbed environment for data collection. Ensuring a conducive and non-threatening environment is important for open and honest discussion [28].

The FGDs were moderated by the principal researcher, who was trained and experienced in qualitative interviewing and group facilitation. Firstly, the researcher established a rapport with the participants, explained to them the study’s purpose and ground rules, and guided the discussion using a semi-structured interview guide. Moreover, open-ended questions and probes encouraged rich responses, balanced participation, and a focus on the research topic. Neutrality was preserved to avoid bias.

With written consent, each FGD was audio-recorded using a high-quality digital recorder for accurate data capture, allowing the moderator to focus [13, 29]. Moreover, the participants were assured that the recordings were for research use only and stored securely. Next, the recordings were transcribed verbatim by the researcher. Given the bilingual nature of the clinical setting, discussions were conducted in a mix of Urdu and English. To ensure data integrity, the audio recordings were first transcribed verbatim including both languages, after which the Urdu segments were translated into English. Also, the researcher verified the transcripts for accuracy and semantic equivalence and removed pseudonymised identifying details.

The decision to employ focus group discussions lies in its capacity to generate rich, in-depth data through synergistic peer interaction. In these sessions, nursing students stimulated one another’s recall and elaboration of clinical experiences, yielding insights potentially hidden in one-on-one interviews [22]. Moreover, the collective setting facilitated the emergence of shared themes and group norms surrounding clinical learning, providing a clearer picture of common challenges and supports [19]. Similarly, the participants’ joint reflection enabled them to compare and contrast perspectives, often surfacing novel interpretations of their practise [13]. The interactive nature of FGDs also facilitated the exploration of shared learning challenges. It allows students to articulate their progression through aspects of Benner’s stages, discussing how their understanding and approach to patient care have evolved with experience.

To maximise inclusivity and depth, clear ground rules were established, including mutual respect, active listening, confidentiality, and equal speaking opportunities. Equally important, the participants were reminded that all perspectives were valuable. In addition, the facilitator encouraged quieter members and redirected dominant voices to maintain balance. Seating was circular to foster open communication and reduce power dynamics, and the moderator (principal researcher) used verbal prompts and nonverbal cues to create a welcoming, non-judgmental environment [28]. These general practises enhanced participant comfort, ensuring that the data reflected a wide range of experiences and voices. This aligned with the study’s qualitative goals, credibility, and trustworthiness, thereby contributing to findings [30].

To ensure the quality, relevance, and appropriateness of the interview guide, several validation and refinement steps were undertaken:

A draft of the interview guide was reviewed by the research team, MScN-qualified nurses who had conducted qualitative research and were working as nursing faculty in different colleges and schools of nursing in Pakistan. Hence, their feedback on question clarity, alignment with research objectives, neutrality of language, and the potential to elicit rich data-informed revisions to the guide before the main data collection.

Two pilot FGDs were conducted with a small group of students who met the inclusion criteria but were not part of the main study sample. These students were current third- or fourth-year BSN students from an alternate private nursing college in Karachi, thereby maintaining consistency with the participant profile of the main study. Thus, these pilots served to assess the clarity and relevance of the interview guide questions [31]. They also help in evaluating the discussion flow and timing, refining the researcher’s facilitation skills and testing the recording and transcription processes. Moreover, feedback and observations from these sessions informed revisions to the interview guide and data collection procedures to ensure their suitability for the main study.

Based on expert and pilot feedback, the interview guide was refined. This process involved rephrasing questions, adding or removing probes, and reordering topics to enhance clarity and effectiveness [32]. Moreover, these revisions ensured that the final guide was robust and aligned with the study’s data collection objectives. Furthermore, to accommodate Urdu-speaking participants, the interview guide was translated using a forward and backward translation process. Meanwhile, the original English version was translated into Urdu by a bilingual professional translator, ensuring cultural appropriateness. Alongside, a second independent translator, blinded to the original, performed a backward translation from Urdu to English. Then, the two English versions were compared, and discrepancies reconciled through discussion, ensuring semantic and conceptual equivalence in the Urdu version, thereby enhancing the reliability and validity of responses. Adherence to the 32 items of the COREQ checklist for the entirety of this process is documented in Additional File 1.

The data analysis process

Data were analysed manually to ensure deep immersion in the verbatim transcripts; no qualitative data analysis software was used. Data saturation was determined after the seventh focus group when no new codes or themes emerged; one final focus group was conducted to confirm this saturation. The reflexive thematic analysis process by Braun and Clarke [21] a cyclic, flexible approach that emphasises the researcher’s active role in constructing meaning. Drawing on their six recursive phases of familiarisation, coding, theme development, review, definition, and report writing, the researcher team first immersed themselves in the verbatim transcripts through repeated readings, noting initial impressions and observations; then conducted inductive, line-by-line coding to identify salient features; organised codes into provisional themes; refined those themes against the full dataset and research questions; formulated precise definitions and names for each theme; and finally combined a coherent analytic narrative supported by illustrative quotations. These phases are not linear; researchers may move back and forth between them as analysis deepens [21]. All procedures for data handling, analysis, and reporting were conducted in alignment with the COREQ 32-item checklist to ensure methodological transparency [26].

Furthermore, to enhance trustworthiness and minimise bias, the researcher engaged in continuous reflexive awareness throughout data collection and analysis, documenting methodological decisions, emerging interpretations, and personal assumptions through field notes, coding memos, and self-reflection, particularly reflecting on potential dual-role biases arising from his position as a nursing instructor. In addition, regular peer debriefing sessions with the research team provided checks on coding consistency and thematic validity, and member-checking with a subset of participants confirmed that the analytic themes authentically captured their perspectives.

The trustworthiness / study rigour

To ensure the trustworthiness of this exploratory descriptive study, the four criteria credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability were systematically addressed (Borg, 2024). Moreover, all methodological details supporting these criteria were documented following the COREQ 32-item checklist [26].

Firstly, credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement, persistent observation, reflexive awareness, peer debriefing, and member checking. Moreover, prolonged engagement during eight FGDs (500 min total) provided an in-depth understanding of participants’ clinical experiences, language, and context.

Likewise, persistent observation, documented via detailed field notes on non-verbal behaviours, group dynamics and emotional responses, enriched the researcher’s contextual understanding. For example, participants’ visible emotion recounting a difficult emergency department experience was integrated into the analysis.

Similarly, reflexive awareness was consistently applied to mitigate researcher’s potential bias, particularly as a nursing instructor, by documenting methodological decisions, interpretations, and assumptions, ensuring analytic transparency [33].

Moreover, regular peer debriefing sessions with the research team further supported credibility. These meetings served as a form of analytic triangulation, providing external review of coding decisions and interpretive themes, and helping the researcher to identify potential blind spots in data interpretation [13].

Furthermore, member checking was conducted with a subset of participants (n = 12) to validate the accuracy and relevance of the thematic findings. Instead of full transcripts, participants were provided with concise summaries of preliminary themes, along with representative excerpts from the discussions. They were then asked to confirm whether the interpretations accurately reflected their experiences and to suggest any corrections or additions. Therefore, this process served to strengthen the validity of the findings and ensured that participant voices were authentically represented [34].

Transferability was supported through both thick description and maximum variation sampling. The thick description involved richly detailed accounts of participant demographics, college layouts, and the public tertiary hospital environment (patient mix, resource constraints, nurse-student ratios, workflow, etc). For example, verbatim quotes and contextual notes, such as a fourth-year student’s trembling voice describing a crowded ward, illustrated the setting.

In addition, maximum variation sampling ensured a wide range of perspectives. The participants included third- and fourth-year students who had rotated through specialties such as medical-surgical, paediatrics, obstetrics, psychiatry, and critical care. Moreover, both male and female students from urban and rural family backgrounds were recruited from all three private colleges (each of which followed the same BSN curriculum and sent students to the same public hospital).

Although the curriculum and clinical site remained constant, sampling across three colleges captured any subtle differences in campus culture or administrative support. By including students with diverse specialty experiences, for instance, one who had focused on paediatric care and another who had spent extensive time in the emergency department, common themes were extracted (e.g., coping with high patient volumes) and detailed variations in how the setting influenced learning.

Together, these strategies, thick description, plus maximum variation sampling created a multidimensional description of students’ clinical experiences. Thus, a reader in a different region or college could compare their context (curriculum, hospital setting, student demographics) to determine whether the findings transferred to their situation [14].

Dependability was further ensured through a concise yet comprehensive audit trail documenting each research step, allowing an external auditor to trace the study’s procedural logic. Recruitment materials (invitations, scripts, screening guides, English/Urdu consent forms) were date-stamped; hard-copy consent forms were logged by participant code and secured. In addition, detailed field notes, secured after each FGD, captured setting details, seating, nonverbal cues, and deviations. Furthermore, verbatim transcripts were stored in password-protected folders, alongside Excel coding log tracking code definitions and excerpts, and dated iterative codebook versions with change memos, ensuring data traceability. Additionally, the records of peer debriefing (including dates, agendas, actions, etc.) and member checking communications (theme summaries, participant feedback, revisions) were preserved. Thus, by maintaining clearly labelled, time-stamped, secured archives, an external auditor could follow any finding from transcript to thematic definition, confirming systematic emergence [34, 35].

Confirmability was achieved by triangulating the investigator’s perspectives and grounding interpretations in participants’ own words. The researcher’s reflexive awareness helped identify and manage personal assumptions [36], and peer debriefings provided investigator triangulation of codes and themes [17, 36]. Also, direct, anonymised quotations were used extensively in the final analysis to demonstrate that conclusions were firmly rooted in the data.

By embedding these strategies throughout data collection and analysis, the study upheld rigorous qualitative standards and ensured that its findings were robust, transparent, and credible.

Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis, and Patricia Benner’s Novice to Expert Model was applied as a guiding theoretical lens across the analysis; the integrated interpretation is presented in Fig. 1. The analysis revealed a profound disconnect between academic preparation and clinical reality, encapsulated in five major themes: [1] Systemic and Environmental Deficiencies, characterized by institutional neglect and severe resource scarcity leading to unsafe practises; [2] A Culture of Supervisory Neglect and Hostility, where students felt abandoned by instructors and devalued by hospital staff; [3] The Pervasive Theory–Practice Chasm, marked by the cognitive dissonance of witnessing normalized unsafe protocols; [4] The Crucible of Confidence, detailing the psychological toll on students and their development of resilience and ‘digital mentorship’ as survival mechanisms; and [5] A Call for Systemic Reform, outlining student-driven recommendations for accountability and improved supervision.

Theme 1: Systemic and environmental deficiencies

This theme captures students’ experiences of functioning within a structurally constrained system, characterized by institutional gaps and inadequate clinical environments that hindered effective learning even before direct patient care.

Subtheme 1.1: Institutional neglect

Participants reported significant dissatisfaction with institutional support, particularly the absence of affiliated teaching hospitals, which they perceived as a critical limitation to their clinical education. This lack of infrastructure not only created logistical challenges but also reflected broader concerns about institutional priorities.

Sir, our clinical site is at [Public Hospital Name]. This is because no hospital was attached to our college… so because of that, we have to go there and face many difficulties… It is the biggest problem. (P1, FGD-IV, Institute B)

Sir, our clinical site is at [Public Hospital Name]. This is because no hospital was attached to our college… so because of that, we have to go there and face many difficulties… It is the biggest problem. (P1, FGD-IV, Institute B)

Students also highlighted a disconnect between academic content and clinical placements. Assignments towards unrelated to their coursework limited their ability to achieve intended learning outcomes and contributed to feelings of unpreparedness.

We have skill labs, but they [faculty] didn’t have us do the procedures… when we were going there [to the hospital], we were harming the patients… we weren’t guided at all by our institute. (P1, FGD-III, Institute B)

We have skill labs, but they [faculty] didn’t have us do the procedures… when we were going there [to the hospital], we were harming the patients… we weren’t guided at all by our institute. (P1, FGD-III, Institute B)

Subtheme 1.2: Poor resources and unsafe clinical environment

The clinical setting was frequently described as under-resourced and unsafe. Participants reported persistent shortages of essential supplies, including gloves, syringes, IV cannulas, and sanitizers, which compromised both patient care and personal safety.

They didn’t have a tourniquet either; we had made the tourniquet themselves from a drip set… patients with HIV… AIDS… on their bedsides, it wasn’t written… They wouldn’t give us gloves there. (P6, FGD-IV, Institute B)

They didn’t have a tourniquet either; we had made the tourniquet themselves from a drip set… patients with HIV… AIDS… on their bedsides, it wasn’t written… They wouldn’t give us gloves there. (P6, FGD-IV, Institute B)

Concerns regarding infection control were prominent, with students recounting exposure to unsafe practices and environments. These experiences not only posed health risks but also contributed to emotional distress and ethical dilemmas in care delivery.

Those gloves were not there. We used to insert [needles] directly like that, blood would come out and get on our hands… I myself caught a fungal infection from there. (P7, FGD-I, Institute A)

Those gloves were not there. We used to insert [needles] directly like that, blood would come out and get on our hands… I myself caught a fungal infection from there. (P7, FGD-I, Institute A)

Theme 2: A culture of supervisory neglect and hostility

This theme highlights the interpersonal and professional challenges within the clinical environment, where students experienced limited supervision and a culture characterized by neglect and, at times, hostility.

Subtheme 2.1: Inadequate supervision

Participants consistently reported insufficient supervision during clinical placements. Faculty members were often described as minimally present, primarily attending for administrative purposes such as taking attendance, while leaving students unsupervised for the remainder of their shifts. Similarly, staff nurses, burdened by heavy workloads, were perceived as unavailable or unwilling to provide guidance.

The staff nurses are sleeping. At night. They go to the ICU and sleep under the AC… The government staff there doesn’t work. They have the students do all the medication… Whether they are doing it right or wrong, nobody cares about that. (P4, FGD-I, Institute A; P3, FGD-VII, Institute C)

The staff nurses are sleeping. At night. They go to the ICU and sleep under the AC… The government staff there doesn’t work. They have the students do all the medication… Whether they are doing it right or wrong, nobody cares about that. (P4, FGD-I, Institute A; P3, FGD-VII, Institute C)

This absence of supervision resulted in students undertaking complex and high-risk clinical tasks, including medication administration and blood transfusions, without adequate support, raising concerns about patient safety and skill acquisition.

Subtheme 2.2: A climate of disrespect and devaluation

Beyond supervisory gaps, students described a clinical culture marked by hierarchical dynamics and lack of professional respect. They perceived themselves as occupying the lowest position within the hierarchy, often experiencing dismissive attitudes and public criticism.

The student has no value there. You don’t get that much proper respect there… if we use a stethoscope in the hospital, all the staff look at us as if, ‘A nurse is using a stethoscope,’ I mean, it just looks strange. (P6, FGD-I, Institute A; P2, FGD-VI, Institute C)

Participants also reported being assigned non-educational tasks, which they interpreted as a form of exploitation and further evidence of their devalued role within the clinical setting.

Theme 3: The pervasive theory–practice chasm

This theme reflects the disconnect between classroom-based learning and clinical practice, which created significant cognitive and ethical tension for students. Participants consistently reported that evidence-based protocols taught during training were not followed in clinical settings and were often dismissed as impractical.

The theory we studied, and the practises happened… But what was the gap? The techniques, the protocols we had studied… were not applied in Civil Hospital. This is called wrong practise. (P2, FGD-IV, Institute B)

Students observed frequent deviations from standard procedures, including compromised infection control and improper medication practices. Efforts to apply learned techniques were often discouraged, with clinical staff emphasizing efficiency over adherence to protocols.

When you tell your staff nurse… ‘we were taught this way.’ She will say, ‘No, dear, here everything is based on experience, just get the work done’. (P3, FGD-VIII, Institute C)

This disconnect placed students in a challenging position, where adhering to best practices conflicted with prevailing clinical norms, leading to ethical dilemmas and uncertainty in decision-making.

Theme 4: The crucible of confidence: resilience, adaptation, and identity

This theme captures the psychological and professional responses of students to challenging clinical environments, highlighting both the emotional burden and the adaptive strategies that shaped their emerging professional identity.

Subtheme 4.1: The emotional and psychological toll

Participants described significant emotional strain resulting from high clinical responsibility combined with limited supervision. Experiences of anxiety, fear of adverse outcomes, and moral distress were common, particularly when performing complex procedures without adequate support.

I was remembering the words that if the patient dies, I will be sent to jail. I was completely depressed there. My blood pressure also dropped… (P4, FGD-IV, Institute B)

Subtheme 4.2: Student agency and adaptive coping

In the absence of structured guidance, students demonstrated proactive learning behaviors. Peer support and self-directed learning emerged as key coping strategies, with many relying on digital resources such as online videos and AI-based tools to acquire clinical skills.

Whatever we have done, we have done on our own, by watching or asking someone… we learned those procedures… from videos on YouTube and such. (P5, FGD-VI, Institute C)

Subtheme 4.3: Emergence of a professional self

Despite systemic and interpersonal challenges, students reported developing confidence and a sense of professional identity. These competencies were often described as being shaped through direct clinical exposure and personal perseverance rather than formal mentorship.

A case came to us, a heart attack case. I performed CPR… and he improved a little… So, then I felt that I can do it… I felt proud, that yes, I am a good nurse. (P1, FGD-V, Institute B)

Theme 5: A call for systemic reform

This theme reflects students’ transition from describing challenges to proposing solutions, highlighting their awareness of systemic gaps and the need for structural improvements in nursing education and clinical training.

Subtheme 5.1: Enhancing institutional accountability

Participants emphasized the need for stronger regulatory oversight to ensure quality clinical education. They expressed concern that existing accreditation processes focus more on infrastructure than on the quality and safety of clinical training environments. Students advocated for stricter enforcement of standards, including accountability for institutions that fail to provide appropriate clinical placements.

All nursing institutes should, if possible, establish their own hospital instead of relying on affiliations… The PNMC only looks at the building, not the students’ careers. (P3, FGD-VII, Institute C)

Subtheme 5.2: Reforming the clinical learning environment

Students highlighted the importance of structured supervision and adequate resources to support safe and effective learning. They recommended increasing the number of qualified clinical instructors and ensuring their consistent presence during clinical placements. Additionally, they stressed the need for basic supplies and safer working conditions to facilitate proper skill development.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive qualitative exploration into the systemic and pedagogical failures affecting private-sector nursing education in Karachi. By mapping these experiences onto the Novice to Expert framework [12], the analysis demonstrates how a combination of institutional neglect and a hostile clinical learning environment (CLE) does not merely challenge students but fundamentally arrests their professional development. The following sections synthesize these findings within the context of global nursing literature and theoretical models.

The broken stage: structural disruption of the novice phase

As illustrated in the Base Layer of the Integrated Framework (Fig. 1), the clinical environment in Karachi serves as a fractured stage for learning. According to Benner, the Novice and Advanced Beginner stages are characterized by a reliance on context-free rules and the need for structured, situated coaching to begin recognizing clinical patterns [12]. However, our findings reveal that for students in this context, the stage itself is structurally compromised before learning even begins.

The institutional neglect identified in this study reflects a profound disconnect between academic enrolment and pedagogical responsibility. When colleges prioritize student numbers without securing affiliated clinical sites, they create a culture shock that transcends mere logistical inconvenience [1]. This aligns with findings from other Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) such as Malawi and Ghana, where resource constraints are known to hinder the acquisition of basic competences [4, 37]. However, this study identifies a more severe consequence: the normalization of unsafe practise. When students are forced to improvise medical equipment or perform invasive procedures without personal protective equipment (PPE), their cognitive bandwidth is consumed entirely by physical survival and the avoidance of immediate harm. In such resource-exhausted settings, the Novice stage is effectively replaced by a survivalist mode of operation.

The theory-practice chasm as a barrier to competence

The Middle Layer of the framework (Fig. 1) identifies a Theory-Practice Chasm that acts as a definitive barrier to professional progression. Benner posits that the transition from an Advanced Beginner to a Competent practitioner requires the ability to prioritize patient needs based on cumulative experience and evidence-based reasoning. Our data suggests that a unique double bind blocks this transition. The systematic disregard for safety protocols observed among students is not merely a knowledge gap but, as high-impact studies define, a cultural standard in resource-limited settings [9]. This study extends that discourse by illustrating how students are forced into the 'Normalization of Deviance,' where unsafe practices are accepted as the standard of care to ensure ward efficiency.

Students who attempt to apply the evidence-based protocols taught in their academic curriculum are often met with horizontal violence and hostility from hospital staff [11]. This friction is compounded by a deep-seated educational divide in Pakistan, where BSN-track students from private colleges often face resentment from staff in public hospitals who view them as an additional burden [10]. This creates a hidden curriculum of servitude, where students are exploited for menial labour rather than engaged in clinical reasoning. This toxicity echoes findings regarding toxic hierarchies in African nursing contexts [11, 38], yet the Pakistani context introduces an additional layer of moral distress. When a student is punished for adhering to safety standards, the transition to competence is arrested, potentially leading to early-career burnout and a devalued professional identity [9, 39].

Adaptive progression: the risks of digital mentorship

A novel and significant contribution of this study is the identification of digital self-reliance as a primary coping mechanism. As shown in the Upper Layer of Fig. 1, students demonstrate remarkable agency by turning to digital mentors specifically YouTube and Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools to fill the pedagogical void left by absent instructors [20]. While Benner emphasizes that clinical expertise is born from a reflective feedback loop between a mentor and a learner, our participants are engaged in a self-teaching model out of necessity [12]. This phenomenon mirrors recent findings from Nepal, where nursing shortages have forced students to rely on digital resources rather than clinical staff [40]. While this demonstrates resilience and is a key component of professional identity formation [41], it poses a significant risk to patient safety.

However, this reliance on digital resources signifies a total breakdown of the ‘situated coaching’ required by Benner’s framework [12]. While students feel empowered, there is a significant risk of ‘Unvalidated Competence.’ As a previous study found that without expert validation in the clinical setting, students may master technical tasks while remaining deficient in the professional judgment necessary for safe patient care(1). Consequently, procedures learned in isolation via digital platforms lack the situated coaching necessary to correct subtle errors in technique. Without expert verification in the clinical setting, there is a danger that incorrect techniques will become crystallized in the student’s practise. Furthermore, the emergence of a professional self through surviving high-stakes situations creates a form of hollow confidence. While the student feels empowered by their survival, this confidence is not anchored in the validated competency required for high-quality patient care.

Implications for global nursing education and policy

The findings of this study suggest that the survival of the fittest approach currently characterizing nursing education in this region is unsustainable. The final theme, A Call for Systemic Reform, represents the students' transition from passive participants to active critics of a failing system. The Overlay in the framework (Figure 1) suggests that systemic reform is the only enabler capable of supporting progression across all stages of Benner’s model. Specifically, regulatory bodies such as the Pakistan Nursing and Midwifery Council must move beyond building-centric accreditation and focus on the quality of the clinical partnership [24]. Unlike students in high-resource settings such as those in Spain who focus on pedagogical quality [42], students in this study are struggling with basic physical safety. Without legally binding, resource-assured agreements between private colleges and public hospitals, the broken stage will continue to produce a workforce that is proficient in survival but deficient in the evidence-based standards required for professional nursing practise.

Limitations

The study was limited to Karachi; findings may not generalize to rural Pakistan. The gender imbalance (mostly male) reflects the specific demographics of the participating colleges but may obscure the unique gender-based harassment female students might face in public wards. Additionally, the study relies on student self-reports; observational data would have strengthened the findings regarding clinical safety.

Conclusion

In summary, this study conceptualizes the clinical education of private-college nursing students in Karachi as a "broken stage," where the intended developmental trajectory from novice toward competence is structurally arrested by systemic neglect and material poverty. By applying Benner’s framework, it becomes evident that the current model, characterized by placing students in resource-depleted public hospitals without dedicated situated coaching, forces learners into a survivalist mode rather than a pedagogical one. While students demonstrate remarkable agency through digital self-reliance and peer networks, this "survival of the fittest" approach cannot substitute for the expert mentorship and reflective feedback loops necessary for safe clinical judgment. To ensure the preparation of a competent nursing workforce for Pakistan, stakeholders and regulatory bodies must shift from building-centric accreditation toward enforcing legally binding, resource-assured clinical partnerships that prioritize student safety, professional identity formation, and the elimination of the theory-practice chasm.
