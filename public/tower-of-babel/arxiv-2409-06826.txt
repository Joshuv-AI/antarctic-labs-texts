Pattern formation of bulk-surface reaction-diffusion systems in a ball.
Edgardo Villar-Sepúlveda, Alan R. Champneys, Davide Cusseddu, Anotida Madzvamuse

Abstract
Weakly nonlinear amplitude equations are derived for the onset of spatially extended patterns on a general class of n n -component bulk-surface reaction-diffusion systems in a ball, under the assumption of linear kinetics in the bulk and coupling Robin-type boundary conditions . Linear analysis shows conditions under which various pattern modes can become unstable to either generalised pitchfork or transcritical bifurcations depending on the parity of the spatial wavenumber. Weakly nonlinear analysis is used to derive general expressions for the multi-component amplitude equations of different patterned states. These reduced-order systems are found to agree with prior normal forms for pattern formation bifurcations with O ⁡ ( 3 ) O(3) symmetry and they provide information on the stability of bifurcating patterns of different symmetry types. The analysis is complemented with numerical results using a dedicated bulk-surface finite-element method. The theory is illustrated in two examples: a bulk-surface version of the Brusselator and a four-component bulk-surface cell-polarity model.

Introduction

Recently, there have been substantial developments in extending the ground-breaking theory for pattern formation proposed by Alan Turing in his seminal paper in 1952
[
62
]
; see, for example,
[
37
,
45
]
and references therein. One such development, driven primarily by experimental observations in biology and material science, is the formulation of bulk-surface reaction-diffusion models (BS-RDEs), see e.g
[
43
,
44
,
54
,
55
]
.
A bulk-surface reaction-diffusion system is one posed
on two different, yet compatible geometries; one denoted the
bulk
, which describes the interior of the domain; and the
surface
, which describes the enclosing smooth manifold representing its boundary. These models naturally describe many biological and physical processes, such as symmetry-breaking and polarity formation in cell biology
[
12
,
25
,
26
]
, surfactant dynamics
[
20
,
31
,
35
]
, and spatiotemporal electrodeposition in batteries
[
32
]
, to give just a few examples.

The complex coupled nature of such models tends to involve not only nonlinear reaction kinetics but also different spatial operators (Laplace and Laplace-Beltrami) for fields posed on the interior and boundary of the domain. The mathematical analysis of the resulting so-called bulk-surface partial differential equations is still in its infancy, and we do not attempt a full existence and uniqueness theory here. Instead, we conduct linear, weakly nonlinear, and numerical analysis of a general class bulk-surface reaction-diffusion systems (BS-RDEs) in 3D.
Analytical studies on the existence, uniqueness, and uniform boundedness of solutions of bulk-surface reaction-diffusion systems have been recently carried out (see
[
49
,
48
,
59
]
for specific details). In
[
59
]
, criteria for guaranteeing uniform boundedness of solutions in time for BS-RDEs was established. The analysis was based on the assumption that the reaction kinetics are locally Lipschitz and quasi-positive. Global well-posedness and global existence of solutions for BS-RDEs were subsequently shown in
[
48
,
49
]
. The reaction kinetics considered in this study fulfill these assumptions, thereby guaranteeing the existence and uniqueness of solutions for this generalised BS-RDEs.

The class of BS-RDEs we consider consists of an arbitrary number
n\geq 2
of components whose dynamics on the bulk and surface are coupled through linear mixed Robin (inhomogeneous Neumann) boundary conditions.
To date, most of the analysis has been restricted to the case where there is a natural compatibility between the eigenfunctions of the
ground state
steady solution with respect to the Laplace and Laplace-Beltrami operators.
The simplest geometries with natural compatibility are a circle in 2D (the bulk being the interior of the circle and the surface being its boundary) and a ball in 3D.
Hence, in this study
we restrict
our
analysis to the case of the ball with linear kinetics in the bulk; see Sec.
2
for more details.

This study aims
to understand the nature of patterned states that can form under parameter variation as a bifurcation from a trivial radially-symmetric ground state. One inspiration for the present work is due to Paquin-Lefebvre
et al

[
51
]
, who follow a similar procedure on a disk, where rotational symmetry is exploited to carry out the analysis. However, the case of a bulk-surface reaction-diffusion model posed on a ball is more complex due to the extra symmetries involved.
The interested reader is referred to

[
19
,
46
,
47
,
14
]
and references therein for analogous bifurcation results for reaction-diffusion systems posed on the surface of a sphere.

The main result (see Sec.
4
) is to derive general expressions for amplitude equations for small-amplitude steady patterns that can be used to determine the bifurcation and stability of different patterned states. We find that these amplitude equations take the same form as those describing Turing bifurcations of PDE systems posed on the surface of the sphere
[
14
]
, owing to the underlying
O(3)
symmetry of both problems.

We complement this analytical work with numerical solutions to the initial value problem to illustrate patterns emerging from the fully nonlinear system and to show their transient dynamics. These results are developed using a state-of-the-art finite element method for nonlinear semilinear parabolic partial differential equations (PDEs) of which BS-PDEs are one such class
[
6
,
27
,
28
,
43
,
44
]
.

To illustrate our results, we choose two examples. One is a bulk-surface version of the well-studied Brusselator model
[
52
]
in which there is an activator and an inhibitor field. The other is a four-component system that is inspired by problems in cell biology, specifically the reaction-diffusion kinetics of small GTPases; these are key molecular proteins that exhibit co-localisation of their molecular concentrations and interactions in both the bulk and surface geometries as active and inactive species.
The reader is referred to
[
8
,
16
]
for general introductions to models describing
such biological processes and
[
10
,
25
,
26
,
60
]
for
work
on specific bulk-surface versions of them.

For each example, we identify bifurcation curves in a parameter plane, corresponding to instabilities of the ground state for different spatial wavenumbers. From there, we derive the normal forms and give predictions for the bifurcation and stability of different kinds of steady patterns. These are corroborated by numerical simulations, which suggest the existence of more spatially localised patterns that are at least meta-stable. However, for higher wavenumbers, the amplitude equations have many components, and their full analysis is unknown. A complete analysis of their spatiotemporal dynamics, including the analogs of possible localised patterns after subcritical bifurcation
(see e.g.,
[
11
,
15
]
)
, will be left for future work.

The rest of the article is structured as follows. In Section
2
we present the general class of BS-RDEs
under study
, its linearised analysis together with the main result on how to construct the appropriate weakly nonlinear normal forms at pattern formation instabilities of different wavenumbers. The normal forms that arise have been analysed before in the context of systems with
O(3)
symmetry, and we outline some of their dynamics in Section
3
. The proof of the main result is contained in Section
4
. Section
5
presents two examples where we show how the results of the explicit computation of normal form coefficients explain what is observed in the finite-element simulations. Finally, Section
6
contains concluding remarks and suggests avenues for future work.

2
General theory

We consider a general class of BS-RDEs posed in a ball of radius
R>0
. For analytical purposes, we restrict the class of PDEs in the bulk to be linear. We then find a general expression for its ground state and categorise its spectrum using modified spherical Bessel functions and Legendre polynomials. This leads to a statement of our main result, which is to provide closed-form expressions for the amplitude equations valid in a neighbourhood of pattern formation bifurcations for different wavenumbers.

2.1
A class of bulk-surface reaction-diffusion systems in a ball

(1)

where

2.2
The ground state solution

We seek a radially symmetric steady state for the coupled system.
We denote this symmetric steady state as the
ground state,
which we define as a time-independent, spatially inhomogeneous solution. To proceed,
let
r=\norm{\mbf x}
, and seek
a ground state solution

\mathbf{P}=\left(\mathbf{U}^{*},\mathbf{u}^{*}\right)^{\intercal}=\left(\mathbf{U}^{*}(r),\mathbf{u}^{*}\right)^{\intercal}
of the system,
which satisfies

\partial_{t}\mathbf{U}^{*}=\mathbf{0}
and
\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}^{*}=\mathbf{0}

\mathbf{U}^{*}(r)

\displaystyle r^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{U}^{*}}{\partial r^{2}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2r}\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{U}^{*}}{\partial r}-r^{2}\,\mathbb{D}_{U}^{-1}\,B\,\mathbf{U}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},\qquad\text{for }r\in(0,R).

(2)

We seek a solution to (
2

\mathbf{U}^{*}=i_{0}\left(\bm{\omega}\,r\right)\,\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}
, where
\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\bm{\omega}=\diag\left(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\right)
, with
\omega_{p}=\sqrt{\sigma_{p}/D_{p}}

, and
i_{0}\left(\bm{\omega}\,r\right)=\diag\left(i_{0}\big(\omega_{1}\,r\big),\ldots,i_{0}\big(\omega_{n}\,r\big)\right)

is a modified spherical Bessel function of the first kind
[

To determine
\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}
and
\mathbf{u}^{*}
, we use the

1

\mathbb{D}_{U}\,i_{0}^{\prime}(\bm{\omega}R)\,\bm{\omega}\,\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}=K\left(\mathbf{u}^{*}-i_{0}(\bm{\omega}R)\,\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\right)
, which can be rearranged into the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}=S_{0}\,\mathbf{u}^{*},\quad\mbox{where }S_{0}\coloneqq\left(\mathbb{D}_{U}\,i_{0}^{\prime}(\bm{\omega}R)\,\bm{\omega}+K\,i_{0}(\bm{\omega}R)\right)^{-1}K.

(3)

Here, we use

to denote the diagonal matrix with entries
i_{0}^{\prime}\left(\omega_{p}\,r\right)
,
p=1,\ldots,n
, and a prime means differentiation with respect to

.

Finally, we use the third
set of equations on
(
1

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u}^{*})-K\left(I-i_{0}(\bm{\omega}R)\,S_{0}\right)\mathbf{u}^{*}=\mathbf{0},

(4)

where

is
the
n\times n

. Therefore, a radially-symmetric steady state of the form
\mathbf{P}=\left(\mathbf{U}^{*}(r),\mathbf{u}^{*}\right)^{\intercal}
can be found whenever there is a solution

to (

\mathbf{U}^{*}=i_{0}(\bm{\omega}r)\,S_{0}\,\mathbf{u}^{*}

Linearised analysis about the ground state

The main result

Here,

Neglecting higher-order terms, (

On the other hand, looking at the structure of (

\varepsilon+a\,A_{0}^{2}-2\,a\,A_{-1}\,A_{1}=0

when differentiating this new variable with respect to

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=2\,a\,A_{0}\,\dot{A}_{0}-2\,a\,\left(\dot{A}_{-1}\,A_{1}+A_{-1}\,\dot{A}_{1}\right)\>=2\,a\,z\left(A_{0}^{2}+2\,\absolutevalue{A_1}^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\norm{\rtext{\mbf A_1}}^{5}\right)

\displaystyle=2\,z(z-\varepsilon)+\mathcal{O}\left(\norm{\rtext{\mbf A_1}}^{5}\right).

Thus

4
Calculation of the amplitude equations

We provide a proof of Theorem
1
using
the adaptation of the method of multiple scales for computation of normal forms at bifurcations
introduced by Elphick
et al

and adapted to the context of infinite-dimensional spatial operators by a number of authors
[
29
,
51
,
3
,
64
]
. The method supposes that for each
\ell
, the normal form can be expressed using the ansatz (
7
) as an ordinary differential equation (ODE) for the complex amplitude vectors
\mathbf{A}_{\ell}
. The right-hand side of these ODEs is determined as an asymptotic expansion in a small book-keeping parameter that captures the powers of components of
|\mathbf{A}_{\ell}|
. The coefficients of these expansions are obtained order-by-order using the solvability conditions derived using the Fredholm alternative. The first three orders are dealt with in Subsections

4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
respectively. There, we suppose that all functions are evaluated precisely at the bifurcation point
\mu=\mu{{}^{*}}
. Subsection
4.4

then
deals with the unfolding of the bifurcation. That is, the computation of the leading-order coefficient of
\absolutevalue{\mu- \mu^*}
in the normal form.

Suppose that, at the bifurcation point
\mu=\mu^{*}
, the ground state
\mathbf{P}
fulfills the conditions stated in Theorem
1
. We proceed to build the normal form using ansatz (
7
).
In particular, we shall prove that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}_{\ell}}
solves an equation of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1}=-\ell}^{\ell}\mathbf{h}_{m_{1}}^{[1]}(A_{m_{1}})+\sum_{-\ell\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq\ell}\mathbf{h}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{[2]}(A_{m_{1}},A_{m_{2}})+\ldots,

(23)

where each function
\mathbf{h}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{p}}^{[p]}\left(A_{m_{1}},\ldots,A_{m_{p}}\right)
corresponds to a linear function in
\prod_{j=1}^{p}A_{m_{j}}
. To find these functions, we use the ansatz

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}\\
\mathbf{u}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{P}+\sum_{q_{1}=-\ell}^{\ell}\mathbf{W}_{q_{1}}^{[1]}(A_{q_{1}})+\sum_{-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell}\mathbf{W}_{q_{1},q_{2}}^{[2]}(A_{q_{1}},A_{q_{2}})+\ldots,

where we need to find the vector functions
\mathbf{W}_{q_{1}}^{[1]},\mathbf{W}_{q_{1},q_{2}}^{[2]},\ldots
such that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}_{\ell}}
solves an equation of the form (
23
). We proceed order by order.

4.1
Order 1

At this order, (
1
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{q_{1}=-\ell}^{\ell}\partial_{A_{q_{1}}}\mathbf{W}_{q_{1}}^{[1]}\sum_{m_{1}=-\ell}^{\ell}\,h_{m_{1},q_{1}}^{[1]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q_{1}=-\ell}^{\ell}\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\bm{\nabla}^{2}\right)\mathbf{W}_{q_{1}}^{[1]},

(24)

where
q_{1}
in the sub-script of
h_{m_{1},q_{1}}^{[1]}
indicates the component of
\mathbf{h}_{m_{1}}^{[1]}
. Therefore,
as our analysis is based on an ansatz and
\det\left(\mathbb{B}_{\ell}\right)=0
, then we note that taking
h_{m_{1},q_{1}}^{[1]}\equiv 0
for every
-\ell\leq m_{1},q_{1}\leq\ell
will let us define a nontrivial solution —as explained below— and keep carrying out our analysis. With this,
(
24
) becomes:

\displaystyle\sum_{q_{1}=-\ell}^{\ell}\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\bm{\nabla}^{2}\right)\mathbf{W}_{q_{1}}^{[1]}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}.

(25)

Here, note that the sum on the left-hand side is taken over all the amplitudes, which multiply orthogonal functions. Therefore, we must set each component of the sum to zero independently. Thus, for each

, we must find
\mathbf{W}_{q_{1}}^{[1]}=\left(\mathbf{U}(r,\theta,\phi),\mathbf{u}(\theta,\phi)\right)^{\intercal}
such that

\displaystyle\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\bm{\nabla}^{2}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}\\
\mathbf{u}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}.

Now, note that the first
n
equations can be written as
-B\,\mathbf{U}+\mathbb{D}_{U}\,\nabla_{\Omega}^{2}\,\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{0}
, from where we can choose the following non-zero solution
\mathbf{U}=i_{\ell}\left(\bm{\omega}\,r\right)\,P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos(\theta))\,e^{im\phi}\,\bm{\hat{\varphi}}
, where
\bm{\hat{\varphi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
is a constant vector, and
i_{\ell}\left(\bm{\omega}\,r\right)=\diag\left(i_{\ell}\big(\omega_{1}\,r\big),\ldots,i_{\ell}\big(\omega_{n}\,r\big)\right)
, with

being a modified spherical Bessel function of the first kind. Therefore, if we take
\mathbf{u}=P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos(\theta))\,e^{im\phi}\,\bm{\varphi}
, the boundary conditions imply that
\bm{\hat{\varphi}}=S_{\ell}\,\bm{\varphi}
. Thus, the last
n
equations of (
25
) become

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{B}_{\ell}}\,\bm{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}.

This equation has a one-parameter family of nontrivial solutions as, by assumption,
\det\left(\mathbb{B}_{\ell}\right)=0
.
We can choose
\bm{\varphi}
to be any non-zero vector in the kernel
of
\mathbb{B}_{\ell}
. With this, we define the non-zero vector

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{q_{1}}^{[1]}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{W}_{q_{1},1}^{[1]}\\[6.45831pt]
\mathbf{W}_{q_{1},2}^{[1]}\end{pmatrix}=A_{q_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}i_{\ell}\left(\bm{\omega}\,r\right)\,\bm{\hat{\varphi}}\\
\bm{\varphi}\end{pmatrix}P_{\ell}^{q_{1}}(\cos(\theta))\,e^{iq_{1}\phi},

where

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{B}_{\ell}}\,\bm{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bm{\hat{\varphi}}=S_{\ell}\,\bm{\varphi}.

4.2
Order 2

At this order, (
1
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{b=-\ell}^{\ell}\partial_{A_{b}}\mathbf{W}_{b}^{[1]}\sum_{-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell}h_{q_{1},q_{2},b}^{[2]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell}\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\bm{\nabla}^{2}\right)\mathbf{W}_{q_{1},q_{2}}^{[2]}

\displaystyle+\sum_{-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{n}\\
\mathbf{G}_{2}\left(\mathbf{W}_{q_{1},2}^{[1]},\mathbf{W}_{q_{2},2}^{[1]}\right)\end{pmatrix},

(26)

which is equivalent to

\sum_{-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell}\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{W}_{q_{1},q_{2}}^{[2]}=\sum_{b=-\ell}^{\ell}\partial_{A_{b}}\mathbf{W}_{b}^{[1]}\sum_{-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell}h_{q_{1},q_{2},b}^{[2]}\\
-\sum_{-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell}A_{q_{1}}A_{q_{2}}\,P_{\ell}^{q_{1}}(\cos(\theta))\,P_{\ell}^{q_{2}}(\cos(\theta))\,e^{i\left(q_{1}+q_{2}\right)\phi}\,\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{n}\\
\mathbf{G}_{2}\left(\bm{\varphi},\bm{\varphi}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

Here, the subscript
b
on

refers to the coefficient of the corresponding quadratic term
A_{q_{1}}A_{q_{2}}
.

Before continuing, we need to choose an inner product to establish solvability conditions using the Fredholm alternative. We make the following natural choice: suppose for
\mathbf{a}_{1}
,
\mathbf{a}_{2}
,
\mathbf{b}_{1}
,
\mathbf{b}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
f_{1},g_{1}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\Omega;\mathbb{R}\right)
, and
f_{2},g_{2}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\Gamma;\mathbb{R}\right)
, then we define

\displaystyle\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}\,\mathbf{a}_{1}\\
f_{2}\,\mathbf{a}_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}\,\mathbf{b}_{1}\\
g_{2}\,\mathbf{b}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle:=\frac{1}{2\,\pi}\left(\iiint_{\Omega}f_{1}(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{a}_{1}\,{\cdot}\,\overline{g_{1}(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{b}_{1}}\,\differential\Omega\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\left.+R^{2}{\iint_{\Gamma}f_{2}(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{a}_{2}\cdot\,\overline{g_{2}(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{b}_{2}}\,\differential\Gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\frac{1}{2\,\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{R}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f_{1}(r,\theta,\phi)\,\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdot\overline{g_{1}(r,\theta,\phi)\,\mathbf{b}_{1}}\,r^{2}\sin(\theta)\,\differential\theta\,\differential\phi\,\differential r\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\left.+R^{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f_{2}(\theta,\phi)\,\mathbf{a}_{2}\cdot\overline{g_{2}(\theta,\phi)\,\mathbf{b}_{2}}\,\sin(\theta)\,\differential\theta\,\differential\phi\right),

(27)

where
\cdot
denotes the usual scalar product of
n
-component vectors.

Now, we want to expand
P_{\ell}^{q_{1}}(\cos(\theta))\,P_{\ell}^{q_{2}}(\cos(\theta))
in terms of the family of functions
\left\{P_{p}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(\cos(\theta))\right\}_{p=\absolutevalue{q_1 + q_2}}^{\infty}
. To do this, consider the following result.

Lemma 2
.

For every
-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell
, the function
P_{\ell}^{q_{1}}(x)\,P_{\ell}^{q_{2}}(x)
can be written as a linear combination of
\left\{P_{p}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(x)\right\}_{p=\absolutevalue{q_1 + q_2}}^{2\ell}
.

Proof.

As the associated Legendre polynomials fulfill
P_{\ell}^{-m}(x)=(-1)^{m}\,P_{\ell}^{m}(x)
, then we assume, without loss of generality, that
q_{1},q_{2}>0
. By definition, for
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
with
0\leq m\leq\ell
,
P_{\ell}^{m}(x)=\mathcal{N}_{\ell,m}\,\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{m/2}\frac{\differential^{\ell+m}}{\differential x^{\ell+m}}\left(x^{2}-1\right)^{\ell}
, where
\mathcal{N}_{\ell,m}\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant that normalizes the associated Legendre polynomial. Note that

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\ell}^{q_{1}}(x)\,P_{\ell}^{q_{2}}(x)}{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{\left(q_{1}+q_{2}\right)/2}}=\mathcal{N}_{\ell,q_{1}}\,\mathcal{N}_{\ell,q_{2}}\,\frac{\differential^{\ell+q_{1}}}{\differential x^{\ell+q_{1}}}\left(x^{2}-1\right)^{\ell}\,\frac{\differential^{\ell+q_{2}}}{\differential x^{\ell+q_{2}}}\left(x^{2}-1\right)^{\ell},

is a polynomial of degree
2\ell-\left(q_{1}+q_{2}\right)
, and

\displaystyle\frac{P_{p}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(x)}{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{\left(q_{1}+q_{2}\right)/2}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{p,q_{1}+q_{2}}\,\frac{\differential^{p+q_{1}+q_{2}}}{\differential x^{p+q_{1}+q_{2}}}\left(x^{2}-1\right)^{p},

(28)

is a polynomial of degree
p-\left(q_{1}+q_{2}\right)
, for
p\geq q_{1}+q_{2}
. Moreover, note that
P_{p}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}\equiv 0
when
\absolutevalue{q_1 + q_2}>p
. Hence, this implies that the family of polynomials
\left\{\frac{P_{p}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(x)}{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{\left(q_{1}+q_{2}\right)/2}}\right\}_{p=\absolutevalue{q_1 + q_2}}^{2\ell}
forms a basis of the space of polynomials of degree
2\ell-\left(q_{1}+q_{2}\right)
. Thus,
P_{\ell}^{q_{1}}(x)\,P_{\ell}^{q_{2}}(x)
can be expanded as a linear combination of
\left\{P_{p}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(x)\right\}_{p=\absolutevalue{q_1 + q_2}}^{2\ell}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Now, thanks to Lemma
2
and the inner product (
27
), we have

\displaystyle P_{\ell}^{q_{1}}(\cos(\theta))\,P_{\ell}^{q_{2}}(\cos(\theta))

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=\absolutevalue{q_1 + q_2}}^{2\ell}d_{p,q_{1},q_{2}}^{[2]}\,P_{p}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(\cos(\theta)),

where

\displaystyle d_{p,q_{1},q_{2}}^{[2]}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}P_{\ell}^{q_{1}}(\cos(\theta))\,P_{\ell}^{q_{2}}(\cos(\theta))\,P_{p}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(\cos(\theta))\sin(\theta)\,\differential\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\ell}^{q_{1}}(x)\,P_{\ell}^{q_{2}}(x)\,P_{p}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(x)\,\differential\theta,

for every integer
\absolutevalue{q_1 + q_2}\leq p\leq 2\ell
. Owing to the parity of the associated Legendre polynomials, it follows that
d_{p,q_{1},q_{2}}^{[2]}=0
for every odd

.

With this, equation (

) becomes

\sum_{-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell}\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\nabla^{2}\right)\mathbf{W}_{q_{1},q_{2}}^{[2]}=\sum_{b=-\ell}^{\ell}\partial_{A_{b}}\mathbf{W}_{b}^{[1]}\sum_{-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell}h_{q_{1},q_{2},b}^{[2]}\\
-\sum_{-\ell\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq\ell}A_{q_{1}}A_{q_{2}}\sum_{p=\absolutevalue{q_1 + q_2}}^{2\ell}d_{p,q_{1},q_{2}}^{[2]}\,P_{p}^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(\cos(\theta))\,e^{i\left(q_{1}+q_{2}\right)\phi}\,\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{n}\\
\mathbf{G}_{2}\left(\bm{\varphi},\bm{\varphi}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

Here, there might be secular terms if

is even

. To find it, we can use the Fredholm alternative with the vector functions

Lemma 3
.

With the inner product defined by (

\displaystyle\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\bm{\nabla}^{2}\right)^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}-B+\mathbb{D}_{U}\,\nabla_{\Omega}^{2}&\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}\\

Proof.

By the definition of the adjoint matrix

\left\langle\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\bm{\nabla}^{2}\right)^{*}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\hat{U}}\\
\mathbf{\hat{u}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}\\
\mathbf{u}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\,\pi}\left(\iiint_{\Omega}\left(-B\,\mathbf{\hat{U}}\cdot\mathbf{\bar{U}}+\mathbb{D}_{U}\,\nabla_{\Omega}^{2}\,\mathbf{\hat{U}}\cdot\mathbf{\bar{U}}\right)\,\differential\Omega\right.\\
\left.+R^{2}\iint_{\Gamma}\left(K\left.\mathbf{\hat{U}}\right|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r=R}}\cdot\mathbf{\bar{u}}-K\,\mathbf{\hat{u}}\cdot\mathbf{\bar{u}}+J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{g}\left(\mathbf{u}^{*}\right)^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{\hat{u}}\cdot\mathbf{\bar{u}}+\mathbb{D}_{u}^{\intercal}\,\nabla_{\Gamma}^{2}\,\mathbf{\hat{u}}\cdot\mathbf{\bar{u}}\right)\differential\Gamma\right),

and

\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\hat{U}}\\
\mathbf{\hat{u}}\end{pmatrix},\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\bm{\nabla}^{2}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}\\
\mathbf{u}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\,\pi}\left(\iiint_{\Omega}\left(-B\,\mathbf{\bar{U}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{U}}+\mathbb{D}_{U}\,\nabla_{\Omega}^{2}\,\mathbf{\bar{U}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{U}}\right)\differential\Omega\right.\\
\left.+R^{2}\iint_{\Gamma}\left(K\left.\mathbf{\bar{U}}\right|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r=R}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{u}}-K\,\mathbf{\bar{u}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{u}}+J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{g}\left(\mathbf{u}^{*}\right)\,\mathbf{\bar{u}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{u}}+\mathbb{D}_{u}\,\nabla_{\Gamma}^{2}\,\mathbf{\bar{u}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{u}}\right)\differential\Gamma\right).

Therefore,

\left\langle\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\bm{\nabla}^{2}\right)^{*}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\hat{U}}\\
\mathbf{\hat{u}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}\\
\mathbf{u}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle-\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\hat{U}}\\
\mathbf{\hat{u}}\end{pmatrix},\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\,\bm{\nabla}^{2}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}\\
\mathbf{u}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\\
=\frac{1}{2\,\pi}\left(\iint_{\Gamma}\left(\mathbb{D}_{U}\left.\frac{\differential\mathbf{\hat{U}}}{\differential\nu}\right|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r=R}}\cdot\left.\mathbf{\bar{U}}\right|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r=R}}-\mathbb{D}_{U}\left.\frac{\differential\mathbf{\bar{U}}}{\differential\nu}\right|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r=R}}\cdot\left.\mathbf{\hat{U}}\right|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r=R}}\right)\differential\Omega\right.\\
\left.+R^{2}\iint_{\Gamma}\left(K\left.\mathbf{\hat{U}}\right|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r=R}}\cdot\mathbf{\bar{u}}-K\left.\mathbf{\bar{U}}\right|_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r=R}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{u}}\right)\differential\Gamma\right)=0,

which proves the result.
∎

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(J_{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})+\mathbb{D}\bm{\nabla}^{2}\right)\mathbf{W}_{q_{1},q_{2}}^{[2]},\bm{\psi}_{m}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{R}i_{\ell}(\bm{\omega}r)S_{\ell}\,\bm{\varphi}\cdot i_{\ell}(\bm{\omega}r)S_{\ell}\bm{\psi}\,r^{2}\differential r+R^{2}\bm{\varphi}\cdot\bm{\psi}\right)-A_{q_{1}}A_{q_{2}}\,R^{2}\,\delta_{m,q_{1}+q_{2}}\;\times

We consider the case where there are source and loss terms, in contrast to other cell-polarity BS-RDE models that show species conservation

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have provided a method for computing the linear and nonlinear parameters necessary for analysing (Turing) transcritical and pitchfork bifurcations for a broad class of bulk-surface reaction-diffusion systems posed on a sphere. In particular, we consider the physically realistic case where the nonlinear interaction kinetics occurs on the surface, with linear diffusion and decay in the bulk, coupled by a boundary condition that has a natural interpretation in terms of concentration-driven flux.

The case of the sphere with linear kinetics in the bulk was tractable because of the compatibility of the eigenfunctions of the Laplace and Laplace-Beltrami operators in the bulk and on the surface. This enables nonlinear terms arising from products to be expressed in terms of the same eigenbases, leading to closed-form expressions for the required amplitude-equation coefficients.
This, in turn, enables us to find closed-form solvability conditions in Sec. 4 to calculate the normal-form coefficients. The method then proceeds as it would for a problem without the complexity of having separate bulk and surface PDEs, although some of the expressions become rather cumbersome. To aid the reader, the first author
[
65
]
has provided both symbolic and numerical code for evaluating the necessary coefficients for any example system of the form (
1
).

We note that other assumptions on the form of coupling between bulk and surface concentrations are possible; for example, in principle, we could include nonlinearty in the coupling boundary conditions. Other possibilities might be to allow the matrix
K
in (
1
) to be singular. Such a decoupling would effectively allow a different number of species to evolve in the bulk and on the surface. Another possible simplification could be to take the limit that some of the bulk diffusion constants tend to infinity (as in
[
55
]
). In principle, nonlinearity could be included in both the bulk PDEs as well, although care would need to be taken in the form of coupling boundary conditions. All such considerations are left for future work
.

Compared
to
bifurcations in planar geometries, the amplitude equations are a little harder to analyze in the case studied because of the
O(3)
-symmetry of the sphere
[
33
]
. Without making an
a priori
assumption, we find that the coefficients follow the normal forms predicted by the theory of such symmetry-breaking
[
14
,
23
]
. Our main contribution then is to show how to calculate such normal forms for our class of bulk-surface systems. Moreover, we have provided software implementation of this general method
[
65
]
. We have also backed up our theory with computational results using a state-of-the-art finite element method.

Note, however, that we have not fully explored the stability of steady solutions that can exist, especially for higher than
\ell=1,2
, as the number of components of the amplitude equations becomes prohibitive. Nevertheless, we have been able to calculate whether the primary bifurcation is super
-
or sub
-
critical. There has been recent interest in sub-critical bifurcations and how they can lead to localised structures (see e.g.
[
3
,
64
,
18
,
13
,
63
]
) and references therein. The analogy of such states in bulk-surface systems remains unexplored.

Perhaps some of the spatio-temporal behaviour, including the temporally periodic orbit we observed for
\ell=1
in the cell polarity model, may be explainable by studying the complete dynamics of the relevant normal form. Also, we established that the normal forms arising at each kind of bifurcation have several symmetries, with consequent invariant subspaces.
A more complete investigation of the dynamics of these normal forms is left for future work.

For
\ell>1
, the full dynamics of each normal form is likely to be yet more complex, not least due to the many additional invariant subspaces. In such situations, one might expect to see parameters where there is repetitive metastable dynamics due to robust heteroclinic cycles, see e.g.
[
38
,
39
]
. In the present context of evolution equations with
O(3)
-symmetry, Chossat and co-workers
[
7
,
21
,
22
]
have established conditions under which such robust cycles exist close to parameter values where there is simultaneous instability for two different values of
\ell
. Investigation of the dynamics close to such codimension-two bifurcation points for the systems considered here is left for future work.

Finally, we remark on how the method developed in this paper could be adapted to provide amplitude equations for bulk-surface reaction-diffusion systems in other geometries.
The presented
approach is general, and extensions to other geometries may well be possible, provided a suitable alternative to Lemma
2
can be established for that geometry.
Other possibilities include abandoning the method of
[
29
]
for normal form computation and resorting to fixed-point methods. Alternatively, as in
[
55
]
, one could explore certain limits in which the bulk dynamics become trivial, leading to nonlocal surface-only equations, which can be posed on more complex geometries (see
[
9
]
for example). These considerations are left for future work.

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-K_{p}\left(\mathbb{M}^{\Gamma}{\mathbf{a}^{p,k}}-\mathbb{J}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}^{p,k}\right),

for
p=1,\ldots,n
. The above temporal discretisation scheme is known as IMEX, because the diffusion terms are considered implicitly and the reaction terms explicitly
[
56
,
42
]
. The explicit treatment of the reaction terms constitutes a way of obtaining a linear system of equations. By solving the above systems, we get the solution of the surface equations at time
t_{k+1}
. To obtain the solution of the bulk equations at time
t_{k+1}
, we solve the following systems

\displaystyle\left(\tau^{-1}\mathbb{M}^{\Omega}+D_{p}\,\mathbb{K}^{\Omega}+K_{p}\,\mathbb{H}^{\Omega}+\sigma_{p}\,\mathbb{M}^{\Omega}\right){\mathbf{A}^{p,k+1}}=\tau^{-1}\mathbb{M}^{\Omega}{\mathbf{A}^{p,k}}+K_{p}\,\mathbb{J}\,{\mathbf{a}^{p,k+1}},

(41)

for
p=1,\ldots,n
. By taking advantage of the linear reactions in the bulk, and having already calculated the surface solutions at
t_{k+1}
, we can solve the bulk systems fully implicitly.
