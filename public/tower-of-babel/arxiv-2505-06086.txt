Validating Griffith fracture propagation in the phase-field approach to fracture: The case of Mode III by means of the trousers test Journal: International Journal of Fracture
F. Kamarei, E. Breedlove, O. Lopez-Pamies

Abstract
At present, there is an abundance of results showing that the phase-field approach to fracture in elastic brittle materials — when properly accounting for material strength — describes the nucleation of fracture from large pre-existing cracks in a manner that is consistent with the Griffith competition between bulk deformation energy and surface fracture energy. By contrast, results that demonstrate the ability of this approach to describe Griffith fracture propagation are scarce and restricted to Mode I. Aimed at addressing this lacuna, the main objective of this paper is to show that the phase-field approach to fracture describes Mode III fracture propagation in a manner that is indeed consistent with the Griffith energy competition. This is accomplished via direct comparisons between phase-field predictions for fracture propagation in the so–called trousers test and the corresponding results that emerge from the Griffith energy competition. The latter are generated from full-field finite-element solutions that — as an additional critical contribution of this paper — also serve to bring to light the hitherto unexplored limitations of the classical Rivlin-Thomas-Greensmith formulas that are routinely used to analyze the trousers test.

2
The trousers test

The trousers test is one of three famous tests that
Rivlin and Thomas 1953
introduced to investigate the validity of the Griffith energy criterion

G:=-\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}}=G_{c}

(1)

to describe the
nucleation
of fracture from a large crack in elastic brittle materials within the setting of finite quasi-static deformations. In expression (
1
), the left-hand side
-\partial\mathcal{W}/\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}
denotes the change in stored elastic energy in the specimen at hand with respect to an added surface area
{\rm d}\mathrm{\Gamma}
to an existing crack
\mathrm{\Gamma}
in its reference state — under conditions of fixed deformation of those parts of the boundary that are not traction-free so that no work is done by the external forces — while the right-hand side
G_{c}
stands for the toughness or critical energy release rate, a macroscopic material property. In a companion contribution,
Greensmith and Thomas 1955
recognized that the trousers test is also particulary expedient to investigate the validity of the Griffith energy criterion (
1
) to describe fracture
propagation
. As elaborated in the sequel, this is because the crack in such a test can be made to propagate at a constant speed,
\dot{a}
say, that is directly controlled by the applied boundary conditions.

, and the length of the pre-existing crack is larger than the height of the specimen but smaller than its length,

The specimen is loaded by first bending its two legs in opposite directions to bring them into the same plane. Subsequently, their bottom ends are held firmly by stiff grips, which are then pulled apart; think of the tearing of a paper sheet (
\sim 0.1

. When a force is applied, it is typically ramped up over an initial period

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathcal{W}=

\displaystyle\left.\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial l}\right|_{\mathrm{\Gamma}}{\rm d}l+\left.\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}}\right|_{l}{\rm d}\mathrm{\Gamma}=P(t){\rm d}l+\left.\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}}\right|_{l}{\rm d}\mathrm{\Gamma},

where we have made use of the connection
P(t)=\partial\mathcal{W}/\partial l|_{\mathrm{\Gamma}}
. Upon using the condition
{\rm d}P=0

\displaystyle\left.-\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}}\right|_{l}=

\displaystyle P(t)\dfrac{{\rm d}l}{{\rm d}\mathrm{\Gamma}}-\dfrac{{\rm d}\mathcal{W}}{{\rm d}\mathrm{\Gamma}}=P(t)\left.\dfrac{\partial l}{\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}}\right|_{P}-\left.\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}}\right|_{P}.

Making use of the estimate

\left.\dfrac{\partial l}{\partial a}\right|_{P}=2\lambda(t),\quad\text{ so that }\quad\left.\dfrac{\partial l}{\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}}\right|_{P}=\dfrac{2}{B}\lambda(t),

(3)

2

-\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}}=\dfrac{2}{B}P(t)\lambda(t)-HW_{\texttt{ut}}(\lambda(t))=:G_{RT},

(4)

-\partial\mathcal{W}/

\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}

i.

As expected

Griffith 1921
)
, the formula (
4
) is nothing more than the change in the work done by the applied forces,
2P(t)\lambda(t)/B
, minus the change in stored elastic energy,
HW_{\texttt{ut}}(\lambda(t))
, per surface
{\rm d}\mathrm{\Gamma}
of added crack, or, in other words, the negative of the change in potential energy per surface of added crack;

ii.

Irrespective of whether the test is carried out by prescribing a force
P(t)
or a deformation

4
) amounts simply to having knowledge of the response of the material under uniaxial tension, via

iii.

By construction, the formula (

iv.

For linear elastic brittle materials,

4

-\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}}=\dfrac{2}{B}P(t)-\dfrac{2}{B^{2}HE}P^{2}(t),

where

stands for the Young’s modulus of the material.

v.

For sufficiently thin specimens,
\lambda(t)\approx 1
, the contribution
HW_{\texttt{ut}}(\lambda(t))
is negligible, and the formula (
4
) renders the remarkably simple approximation

-\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial\mathrm{\Gamma}}=\dfrac{2}{B}P(t).

2.2
The classical results of Greensmith and Thomas (1955) for fracture propagation under constant grip separation rate

Granted the result (
4
), it follows from the Griffith energy criterion (
1
) that crack propagation in a trousers test takes place whenever the condition

\dfrac{2}{B}P(t)\lambda(t)-HW_{\texttt{ut}}(\lambda(t))=G_{c}

(5)

is satisfied. For the case when the deformation
l(t)
is applied at a constant rate
\dot{l}_{0}
, the force at the grips increases monotonically in time from
P(0)=0
at
t=0
until some critical time
t_{c}
at which the value
P_{c}\equiv P(t_{c})
satisfies the condition (
5
) and the crack starts to grow. Assuming that the process of crack growth is continuous in time, after remaining put at
a(t)=A
for
0\leq t<t_{c}
, the crack length grows according to

\displaystyle a(t)=

\displaystyle A+\dfrac{\dot{l}_{0}}{2\lambda_{c}}(t-t_{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A-\dfrac{l_{0}+\dot{l}_{0}t_{c}}{2\lambda_{c}}+\dfrac{1}{2\lambda_{c}}l(t)

(6)

for
t\geq t_{c}
. Here,
\lambda_{c}\equiv\lambda(t_{c})
is the stretch associated with the critical value
P_{c}
of the force
P(t)
, which remains at the constant value
P(t)=P_{c}
for
t\geq t_{c}
during the entire crack propagation process, as a result of the criticality condition (
5
) being independent of the crack length. Observe that:

i.

The speed of crack propagation

\dot{a}(t)=\dfrac{\dot{l}_{0}}{2\lambda_{c}}

(7)

is constant and given directly in terms of the rate
\dot{l}_{0}
at which the grips are separated.

ii.

In stark contrast to the speed of crack propagation (
7
), the critical value
P_{c}
of the force at which the crack propagates is independent of how fast the grips are separated.

iii.

For linear elastic brittle materials, the critical value
P_{c}
of the force at which the crack propagates is given by

\displaystyle P_{c}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\left(E-\sqrt{E^{2}-\dfrac{2EG_{c}}{H}}\right)BH.

iv.

For sufficiently thin specimens, when
\lambda_{c}\approx 1
and the contribution
HW_{\texttt{ut}}(\lambda_{c})
is negligible, the remarkably simple approximations

\displaystyle P_{c}=\dfrac{BG_{c}}{2}\quad{\rm and}\quad\dot{a}(t)=\dfrac{\dot{l}_{0}}{2}

apply.

2.3.2

3
The phase-field approach to fracture

Next, we briefly recall the phase-field formulation introduced by
Kumar et al. 2018a
to describe the nucleation and propagation of fracture in elastic brittle materials subjected to quasi-static mechanical loading. For simplicity of exposition and later comparison with the preceding results, we present the model for the basic case of elastic brittle materials with Neo-Hookean elasticity. For a complete account, including FE schemes to solve the resulting governing equations, the interested reader is referred to
Kumar et al. 2018b
;
Kumar and Lopez-Pamies 2020
;
Kumar et al. 2020
;
Kumar et al. 2022
;
Kamarei et al. 2024
.

3.1
Kinematics and boundary conditions

Consider a specimen that, initially, at time
t=0
, occupies the open bounded domain
\mathrm{\Omega}_{0}
. We denote its boundary by
\partial\mathrm{\Omega}_{0}
, its outward unit normal by
{\bf N}
, and identify material points by their initial position vector
{\bf X}\in\mathrm{\Omega}_{0}
. The specimen is subjected to a deformation
\overline{{\bf y}}({\bf X},t)
on a part
\partial\mathrm{\Omega}_{0}^{\mathcal{D}}
of the boundary, and a surface force (per unit undeformed area)
\overline{{\bf s}}({\bf X},t)
on the complementary part
\partial\mathrm{\Omega}_{0}^{\mathcal{N}}=\partial\mathrm{\Omega}_{0}\setminus\partial\mathrm{\Omega}_{0}^{\mathcal{D}}
; for simplicity, body forces are considered to be absent. In response to these boundary conditions — all of which are assumed to be applied monotonically and quasi-statically in time — the position vector
{\bf X}
of a material point in the specimen will move to a new position specified by

{\bf x}={\bf y}({\bf X},t),

(10)

where
{\bf y}({\bf X},t)
is the deformation field. We write the associated deformation gradient at
{\bf X}
and
t
as
{\bf F}({\bf X},t)=\nabla{\bf y}({\bf X},t)
. In addition to the deformation (
10
), the boundary conditions may result in the nucleation and subsequent propagation of cracks in the specimen. We describe such cracks in a regularized fashion via the phase field

z=z({\bf X},t)

taking values in the range
[0,1]
. The value
z=1
identifies the intact regions of the material and
z=0
the regions that have been fractured, while the transition from
z=1
to
z=0
is set to occur smoothly over regions of small thickness of regularization length scale
\varepsilon>0
.

3.2
Constitutive behavior

The specimen is taken to be made of a homogeneous, isotropic, elastic brittle material. Its mechanical behavior is hence characterized by three material properties: its elasticity, its strength, and its toughness.

As noted above, we take the elastic behavior of the material to be characterized by the Neo-Hookean stored-energy function (
8
).

The strength of the material is taken to be characterized by the Drucker-Prager strength surface

\mathcal{F}({\bf S})=\sqrt{\mathcal{J}_{2}}+\dfrac{s_{\texttt{ts}}}{\sqrt{3}\left(3s_{\texttt{hs}}-s_{\texttt{ts}}\right)}\mathcal{I}_{1}-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}s_{\texttt{hs}}s_{\texttt{ts}}}{3s_{\texttt{hs}}-s_{\texttt{ts}}}=0,

(11)

where
\mathcal{I}_{1}=s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}
and
\mathcal{J}_{2}=1/3(s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}+s_{3}^{2}-s_{1}s_{2}-s_{1}s_{3}-s_{2}s_{3})
stand for invariants of the Biot stress tensor
{\bf S}^{(1)}=({\bf S}^{T}{\bf R}+{\bf R}^{T}{\bf S})/2
, with
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
,
s_{3}
denoting the principal Biot stresses and
{\bf R}
the rigid rotation from the polar decomposition
{\bf F}={\bf R}{\bf U}
, while the constants
s_{\texttt{ts}}>0
and
s_{\texttt{hs}}>0
stand for the uniaxial tensile and hydrostatic strengths of the material. That is, they denote the critical nominal stress values at which fracture nucleates under uniform states of monotonically increased uniaxial tension
{\bf S}={\rm diag}(s>0,0,0)
and hydrostatic stress
{\bf S}={\rm diag}(s>0,s>0,s>0)
, respectively.

Finally, we take the critical energy release rate of the material to be given by the constant

G_{c}.

3.3

According to the phase-field fracture formulation put forth by
Kumar et al. 2018a

t_{0}=0
and

\left\{\hskip-1.42271pt\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm Div}\left[z_{k}^{2}\left(2\mu(\nabla{\bf y}_{k}-\nabla{\bf y}_{k}^{-T})+\right.\right.&\\

(12)

We close by remarking that the trousers test studied here is ideal to probe the capabilities of computational models — not just phase-field models — to describe fracture propagation. Insomuch that it should be one of the challenge problems to pass in any problem set envisioned to establish the viability of fracture models.
