Bond–electromagnetic origin of superconducting pairing across materials
Authors: Min Tae Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe develop a bond–electromagnetic framework in which superconducting pairing originates from dynamic covalent bonds whose fluctuations generate a real–space pairing kernel. Bond–centered singlets emerge from these structural fluctuations, while long–range superconductivity appears when they become electromagnetically frozen into a phase–coherent network, as quantified by the London kernel and the superfluid stiffness. Within this framework, the microscopic pairing scale is determined directly from experimentally accessible structural quantities—including bond geometry, hybridization symmetry, dielectric screening, and the logarithmic bond–frequency moment—through a structural reformulation of the Allen–Dynes expression. The conversion of into the observed superconducting transition temperature is then governed by the electromagnetic rigidity of the material. Using only structural inputs, the framework reproduces pairing scales across representative materials and coupling regimes. For elemental Al, is obtained in a structurally parameter–free manner, while for Pb it is constrained solely by independently established ranges of the coupling strength. To probe structure–driven pairing in systems with reduced phase stiffness, we analyze FeSe under hydrostatic pressure. A structurally constrained form naturally captures the pressure–induced rise, maximum, and curvature of the superconducting dome, with the resulting trajectory spanning the experimentally observed transition region from the resistive onset to the zero–resistance state without invoking pressure–dependent spectroscopic input. These results establish a structure–anchored origin of superconducting pairing across distinct materials classes and demonstrate that experimentally accessible bond dynamics provide a practical materials–level criterion for linking lattice structure, electromagnetic response, and superconducting coherence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55635-1.Subject terms: Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Superconductivity—the emergence of zero resistance and perfect diamagnetism below a critical temperature —is a paradigmatic form of macroscopic quantum order. For many elemental metals, the Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer framework and its Eliashberg extension provide a quantitatively successful description of pairing and electrodynamic response in terms of electron–phonon interactions1–5. The discovery of unconventional materials—including cuprates6,7, iron-based compounds8,9, superconducting hydrides at megabar pressures10,11, and nickelates12—has broadened this landscape and highlighted that superconductivity can arise across a wide range of coupling strengths, dimensionalities, and electronic environments.

Across these varied systems, numerous empirical observations point to a central role of bonding geometry and orbital hybridization in determining pairing. In cuprates, epitaxial strain or lattice distortions shift by unusually large amounts13, while in iron pnictides, hydrostatic or chemical pressure strongly modifies Fe–As bond angles and the resulting superconducting properties14. Stoichiometric FeSe provides a particularly clean setting: hydrostatic pressure tunes its superconductivity almost entirely through changes in Fe–Se bond geometry and layer spacing, without introducing chemical disorder15,16.

Further indicators of bond–governed behavior appear across both conventional and unconventional materials. Isotope effects frequently deviate from the canonical behavior, revealing sensitivity beyond simple phonon frequency scaling. Superfluid-density systematics—including Uemura scaling and Homes’ law17–19—suggest that structural geometry, coherence, and electrodynamics are tightly linked. Classical ultrasonic anomalies below 20,21 demonstrate that bond stiffness and electromagnetic response evolve together, indicating that superconductivity may involve not only quasiparticle pairing but also a real-space reorganization of valence bonds.

These observations motivate a formulation in which the microscopic degrees of freedom include the dynamic rearrangement of covalent bonds—specifically their geometry, hybridization symmetry, dielectric environment, and stiffness.

Consistent with this viewpoint, related approaches have shown that electron–phonon interactions can be described in terms of bond-length modulations and local structural distortions within hopping- and bond-resolved frameworks22–24. These studies indicate that the coupling between lattice dynamics and electronic states can be naturally understood at the level of real-space bonds. Building on this perspective, the present work develops a structural description of pairing in which the relevant microscopic degrees of freedom are associated with bond-resolved lattice dynamics.

In this approach, a Cooper pair is interpreted as a dynamic covalent bond: a time-dependent overlap of valence orbitals whose oscillatory interaction acts as a bond–energy mode. Superconductivity corresponds to the regime in which these dynamic bonds acquire long-range phase coherence, forming an electromagnetically frozen network consistent with Meissner electrodynamics25–27. Formally, the Eliashberg kernel is expressed in terms of directly measurable structural quantities—orbital overlap, hybridization symmetry, dielectric screening, and bond spectral stiffness—providing a transparent real-space interpretation of the effective coupling parameters.

To access this quantity, we construct the bond spectral density B(Ω ), where Ω denotes the bond-resolved vibrational frequency, from symmetry-resolved lattice dynamics—obtainable through Raman or inelastic x-ray scattering—together with measured structural and elastic parameters. These experimentally anchored inputs define a bond–electromagnetic kernel that characterizes the pairing interaction without phenomenological fitting and is applicable to weak-coupling metals such as Al, strong-coupling systems such as Pb, and stiffness-limited compounds such as FeSe. The goal is to identify how measurable bond geometry and mode symmetry encode the microscopic scale at which pairing initiates.

In the remainder of this work we establish the formal structure of this kernel and analyze its microscopic components from a materials-oriented perspective. Applications to representative systems spanning distinct coupling regimes—Al (weak coupling), Pb (strong coupling), and FeSe (pressure-tunable, stiffness-limited)—demonstrate how experimentally accessible structural information provides a materials-level route to determining the pairing scale across diverse families of superconductors. A full treatment of superfluid stiffness and vortex dynamics is deferred to a companion study.

Bond–electromagnetic framework for superconductivity

Superconductivity is the emergence of an electromagnetically rigid state, in which the internal fields satisfy and in the bulk and screening currents flow without dissipation within a London layer28–30. In the present framework this rigidity does not arise from quasiparticle kinematics but from the collective behaviour of dynamic covalent bonds: time–dependent electromagnetic bonds between neighbouring valence orbitals.

Structural origin of dynamic covalent bonds

Local bond geometry (bond length, orbital overlap, dielectric screening, and hybridization symmetry) sets the stiffness of dynamic covalent bonds— the elementary units of electromagnetic energy exchange in a metal. Unlike conventional electron–phonon pictures, the fluctuating bond is itself an electromagnetic object whose dynamics depends only on structural inputs.

The collective motion of these dynamic bonds defines a structural spectral density B(Ω ), a real-space generalisation of the Eliashberg function 2–4.

Within the conventional electron–phonon framework, the spectral function combines lattice vibrational phase space with electronic matrix elements. In the present formulation, we explicitly construct the structural component of this interaction by defining a bond-resolved spectral density B(Ω ) from phonon eigenmodes.

This construction is consistent with bond-resolved descriptions of electron–phonon coupling, in which the interaction originates from bond-length modulations that affect electronic hopping amplitudes or local potentials22–24. Accordingly, B(Ω ) captures the vibrational phase space associated with such bond distortions and establishes a direct correspondence at the level of spectral moments, while the full electronic weighting is not introduced at the same level of microscopic detail (see Supplementary Information).

In the harmonic and isotropic limit, where bond geometry becomes uniform and mode-dependent screening is absent, B(Ω ) reduces to a phonon-density-of-states-like distribution, thereby recovering the same leading spectral moments that enter the Eliashberg formulation, consistent with the standard strong-coupling framework31,32.

In this work, we use the term “electromagnetic freezing” to denote the onset of macroscopic phase coherence of bond-centered singlet correlations, governed by the emergence of finite superfluid stiffness (London rigidity). This distinguishes it from the microscopic pair-formation scale , which may remain higher than the observed superconducting transition temperature in systems with reduced phase stiffness, and may manifest as a true phase transition or as a crossover depending on dimensionality and phase stiffness.

As temperature is lowered, dynamic bonds lose their temporal fluctuations and lock into a macroscopically ordered state—a process corresponding to electromagnetic freezing as defined above.

Directional bond networks, in which singlet correlations are tied to specific real–space bond orientations, naturally generate alternating–sign gap structures—including d–wave–like patterns—consistent with the experimentally observed order–parameter symmetries in cuprates7 and with theoretical frameworks emphasizing real–space singlet formation34,35.

Extension to high- families

Although the present work benchmarks the framework on elemental low- metals—where the large phase stiffness enforces —the bond–electromagnetic kernel provides a structural perspective that may be extended to more complex superconducting systems.

The present work does not explicitly treat cuprates, pnictides, nickelates, or hydrides, where strong electronic correlations can play a dominant role. Accordingly, the current results should be understood as establishing a structural pairing scale within a lattice-based framework. Any extension to strongly correlated systems, including a detailed formulation of the phase-ordering scale and its material dependence, remains an open direction for future investigation.

In these high- families, bond-centered singlet correlations— identified here with the microscopic pairing scale — may emerge at temperatures well above the scale at which long-range phase coherence is established17,33. Fully structural prediction: elemental Al

Among elemental superconductors, Al provides the most stringent benchmark for a structural pairing theory. As a monatomic, nearly harmonic fcc solid with an extensively measured phonon spectrum, Al allows the microscopic pairing scale to be computed entirely from experimental lattice dynamics, without electronic matrix elements, tunneling inversion, or any adjustable parameters.

Semi–structural analysis: Pb

Pb provides a complementary strong–coupling test of the structural pairing framework. In contrast to Al, however, the absolute electron–phonon coupling strength in Pb cannot be fixed in a fully parameter–free manner from lattice dynamics alone. This is because the relevant electronic prefactor—commonly expressed through the Hopfield parameter or an equivalent deformation potential—depends on electronic matrix elements that are not directly accessible from neutron–scattering measurements of the phonon spectrum5,31,32. Discussion

The present work demonstrates that superconducting pairing can be formulated within a structurally anchored bond–electromagnetic framework. In this picture, pairing correlations emerge from fluctuations of dynamic covalent bonds, while macroscopic superconductivity appears when these bond-centered singlets become electromagnetically frozen into a phase-coherent state. This freezing manifests as a long-wavelength electromagnetic response consistent with London–Meissner electrodynamics28–30. In the harmonic and isotropic limit, the framework connects smoothly to conventional Eliashberg theory5,32, while making explicit the structural origin of the pairing interaction. The real-space spectral density B(Ω ) provides a direct structural link between bond-level dynamics and the microscopic pairing scale, enabling a unified description of pairing and electromagnetic response5,32.

A central outcome of the present analysis is that the microscopic pairing scale can be anchored to experimentally accessible structural quantities, most notably the bond spectral density B(Ω ). Across the three representative systems studied here—Al, Pb, and FeSe—the framework reveals a clear hierarchy of structural control: (i) in Al, the pairing scale is fully determined by structure alone; (ii) in Pb, the characteristic frequency scale is fixed structurally, while the overall coupling strength remains constrained by independent electronic input; and (iii) in FeSe, hydrostatic pressure modifies superconductivity primarily through changes in bond geometry and lattice stiffness, allowing the pressure evolution of to be reproduced without fitting to the experimental curve from structurally constrained parameters. Together, these cases clarify which aspects of superconductivity are genuinely structural and establish B(Ω ) as a unifying object controlling the microscopic pairing scale.

A broader implication concerns systems with reduced superfluid stiffness, including many high–temperature and low–dimensional superconductors. In such materials, the superconducting transition temperature is no longer set by pair formation alone but is limited by the onset of global phase coherence17,18,33. The structurally defined pairing scale identified here therefore provides a well-defined microscopic input for a complementary theory of the phase-ordering scale , in which electromagnetic rigidity and bond stiffness govern macroscopic coherence. Because the bond kernel depends only on real-space geometry and vibrational spectra, the same structural–electromagnetic framework is expected, within the limits discussed above, to extend to layered, anisotropic, and strongly correlated materials.

Overall, the bond–electromagnetic framework offers a falsifiable and quantitatively predictive structural foundation for superconducting pairing. Its consistency across weak–coupling (Al), strong–coupling (Pb), and stiffness–limited (FeSe) systems suggests that pair formation is governed by a common structural basis, while the observed diversity of superconducting transition temperatures reflects differences in electromagnetic rigidity and phase-coherence constraints. By providing a materials-level route to linking lattice structure, bond dynamics, and superconducting coherence, the present approach establishes a practical framework for comparative analysis and structure-guided exploration of superconductivity across materials classes.

While broader validation across additional material classes remains an important direction for future work, the present results establish a consistent structural benchmark across representative regimes.

Methods

Bond–electromagnetic kernel

Pairing–scale predictions are obtained from the bond–electromagnetic framework introduced in the main text. The bond spectral density B(Ω ) is constructed by projecting lattice vibrations onto nearest–neighbour bond directions, providing a structural generalization of the Eliashberg function. The corresponding bond moments and the pairing scale follow directly from B(Ω ) as defined in Eqs. (1)–(2). Full details are provided in SI Sec. 1.

Structural inputs

All structural inputs are taken from experimental phonon and elastic data reported in the literature. When phonon eigenvectors are available (e.g., Al), they are projected directly onto bond directions; when only phonon density–of–states data are available (e.g., Pb), published DOS curves are digitized to construct B(Ω ). No ab initio calculations or tunneling inversions are employed, and no adjustable parameters enter the structural construction of B(Ω ). Details are given in SI Secs. 3–4.
