Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy-linked Tnnc1 variants are associated with distinct myopathic changes in slow skeletal muscle of mice
Authors: Sonu Patel, Gabriel Leite da Silva Santos, Jaelynn Florence, Aida Rahimi Kahmini, Yun Shi, Michelle Rodriguez Garcia, Bradley S. Gordon, P. Bryant Chase, Christopher Solís, Orlando Laitano, J. Renato Pinto, Michelle S. Parvatiyar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inherited hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is considered a disease of the cardiac sarcomere and caused by pathogenic variants present in genes that encode sarcomeric proteins. The human TNNC1 gene is a designated HCM-susceptibility gene encoding the troponin C (TnC) protein, which is expressed in both cardiac and type I slow skeletal muscles and abbreviated as cTnC and ssTnC respectively. HCM patients have been reported to exhibit skeletal muscle weakness and reduced exercise tolerance. Patients bearing TNNC1 cardiac pathogenic variants also express it in type I fibers of their slow skeletal muscles. We hypothesized that the presence of TNNC1 HCM variants in type I fibers may decrease force generating capabilities and alter fatigue resistance of slow skeletal muscles. To address this, we examined the impact of HCM Tnnc1 variants in the soleus muscles of two HCM knock-in mouse models A8V+/-, A8V-/-, and C84Y+/- and their respective wild type (WT) controls. At high stimulation frequencies, we found that A8V-/- soleus muscles had lower tetanic force production than WT, however no differences in fatigue when comparing fatigue indices for either of the variants. The muscles were evaluated by comparing % specific force (N/cm2)-variant and both A8V-/- and C84+/- had increased responsiveness at low stimulation frequencies. Fiber type analysis uncovered an increase in abundance of type I fibers in A8V-/- soleus, however no other differences were observed in fiber-type percentage of either mouse model. Comparison of the cross-sectional areas (CSA) of type I fibers in soleus muscles revealed lower average values for A8V+/+ and A8V-/-, however an increase in C84Y+/- relative to controls. Examination of the CSA of type IIa fibers uncovered decreased values for both A8V+/- and A8V-/- with no changes in C84Y+/- soleus. Histological evaluation of A8V+/- and A8V-/- soleus muscles revealed central nucleation and the detection of embryonic myosin heavy chain by immunofluorescence suggested the presence of mild regeneration. In contrast, no histopathological changes were detected in the C84Y+/- soleus muscle. Serum myokines were also measured to assess systemic impacts of the pathogenic variants and alterations in the physical activity of the mice but no significant changes were found. Taken together our results suggest that the HCM A8V variant alters force production in soleus muscle that may be attributed to fiber atrophy and heightened pathophysiology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10974-026-09736-z.

Introduction

Cardiomyopathies encompass a spectrum of heart muscle diseases that can culminate in heart failure, arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death. Among these, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a prevalent form. Patients diagnosed with HCM are prescribed standard care while other non-cardiac related symptoms may not be recorded. Prior to the discovery of genes associated with familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (FHC), HCM was described as a spontaneously occurring disease with a heterogeneous presentation (Solomon et al 1990). Frequently, HCM has a known hereditary basis and is caused by pathogenic variants in genes crucial for cardiac muscle function including the cardiac troponin complex (cTn), which has an important Ca2+-regulatory role in striated muscle contraction (Semsarian et al 2015). cTn is comprised of three polypeptide subunits: cardiac troponin C (cTnC), the Ca2+ binding subunit that is essential for Ca2+ regulation of striated muscle contraction; cardiac troponin I (cTnI), the inhibitory subunit that not only maintains tropomyosin in the off state when cytoplasmic Ca2+ is low, but also is responsible for holding together the three subunits of the complex; and cardiac troponin T (cTnT), the tropomyosin (Tm) interacting subunit that is required for binding Tn to the striated muscle thin filament.

High resolution structural studies have further defined the dynamic interactions that govern muscle contraction (Oda et al 2020; Yamada et al 2020). The TNNC1 gene in humans (Tnnc1 in mice) is expressed in both cardiac and slow skeletal muscle (Parmacek et al 1994). TNNC1 has been found to be a target of pathogenic gene variants that cause either dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) or HCM (Reinoso et al 2021; Tadros et al 2020). To date, a handful of HCM-causing variants have been identified in TNNC1 including Ala8Val (A8V) (Landstrom et al 2008), Leu29Gln (L29Q) (Hoffmann et al 2001), Ala31Ser (A31S) (Parvatiyar et al 2012), Cys84Tyr (C84Y) (Landstrom et al 2008), and Asp145Glu (D145E) (Landstrom et al 2008), and Gln122AlafsX30 (Q122AfsX30) (Chung et al 2011). These variants have been linked to disease in patients, alterations in cardiac structure (Risi et al 2023), perturbed in situ fiber function, and impairments in whole heart mouse physiology (Landstrom et al 2008; Martins et al 2015; Parvatiyar et al 2012; Pinto et al 2009). Variants of TNNC1 associated with HCM and other cardiomyopathies have been extensively reviewed (Reinoso et al 2021; Tadros et al 2020). In addition to TNNC1 several other genes encode cardiac sarcomeric proteins including MYH7—beta-myosin heavy chain—that is also expressed in type I (slow) skeletal muscle (Schiaffino & Reggiani 1996) can also affect skeletal muscle function (Kirschner et al 2005; Kohler et al 2002; Seebohm et al 2009; Gao et al. 2018).

The impact of HCM-associated variants on skeletal muscle function has been investigated however it remains incompletely understood. Skeletal muscle weakness and fatigue are common symptoms reported in HCM patients (Karandreas et al 2000) and electromyography studies have uncovered signs of myopathy that could be caused by gene mutations or genetic modifiers (Solomon et al 1990) (Smith et al 1976) (Middlekauff 2010) (Karandreas et al 2000) (Caforio et al 1989). HCM patients however have reported reduced exercise capacity that may also be linked to dyspnea or angina-related symptoms (Tkaczyszyn et al 2024). The shared expression of TNNC1 in cardiac and slow skeletal muscle raises the possibility that pathogenic variants identified in the heart (Kirschner et al 2005; Kohler et al 2002; Seebohm et al 2009) may also instigate pathology in skeletal muscle and be responsible for broader systemic consequences.

Slow skeletal muscles predominantly composed of type I fibers, e.g., soleus and postural back muscles utilize aerobic respiration as their primary energy source and are recruited for activities requiring sustained activation. Many muscles are comprised of hybrid fibers expressing more than one myosin heavy chain (MHC) isoform including (I/IIa, IIa/IIx, I/IIa/IIx) combinations (Plotkin et al 2021). Therefore, HCM variants in TNNC1 are also expressed in hybrid muscles containing slow twitch type I fibers – highlighting the potential for systemwide impacts (Parmacek et al 1994). Cardiomyopathic disease in humans can culminate in chronic heart failure and patients stably managed using medical therapies have been shown to have smaller muscle cross-sectional areas (CSA) compared to healthy subjects (Middlekauff 2010). In this study it was suggested that the myogenic myopathy-related subclinical electromyographic alterations found in these cardiomyopathy patients were unrelated to the degree of left ventricular dysfunction (Caforio et al 1989). These myogenic changes, therefore, could be likened to other congenital and idiopathic myopathies and may be distinct from those associated with secondary heart failure (Miller et al 2009). In addition, it remains an outstanding question whether lower exercise capacity in HCM results from disease-related alterations in skeletal muscle not addressed by standard treatment regimens.

The generation of mouse models bearing pathogenic A8V and C84Y variants allows us to study the impact of HCM-associated TNNC1 variants on slow skeletal muscle function and gather relevant and timely information that can inform clinical practice. Earlier studies have underscored the relevance of such investigations by demonstrating significant physiological alterations attributed to these two variants in the heart (Jaafar et al 2015; Martins et al 2015). Our previous cardiac transcriptome study found downregulation of branched chain amino acid degradation in A8V-/- mouse hearts (Dieseldorff Jones et al 2020). Relatedly, HCM variants have been shown to create differing levels of energetic stress and depletion (Ashrafian et al 2003). In our current study the cardiomyopathy mouse models allow us to explore the contributions of these genetic substitutions (i.e., TNNC1 variants) on soleus muscle weakness, fatigue, and development of myopathy. Given the significant inhibition of canonical valine degradation in our knock-in mouse model, we also evaluated lipid accumulation in soleus muscle as a functional readout of disrupted branched-chain amino acid (BCAA)-lipid metabolic crosstalk. In this study we utilized two A8V genotypes heterozygous +/- and homozygous -/- mice and C84Y heterozygous+/- mice as -/- mice could not be obtained due to embryonic lethality. In this study we found distinct presentations in the Tnnc1 mutant soleus muscle with A8V-/- showing reduced tetanic force generation, generalized atrophy, and histopathological changes whereas C84Y+/- had preserved tetanic force generation, type I fiber hypertrophy, and no evidence of histopathology.

Methods

Mouse models

Knock-in mice utilized in this study were between 7–7.5 months old and generated at the University of Miami (Tnnc1WT/A8V and Tnnc1A8V/A8V) (Martins et al 2015) or the University of Arizona, GEMM Core (Tnnc1WT/C84Y) (Marques et al 2021). Throughout the manuscript the genotypes of the cTnC mice or tissues will be referred to as A8V +/+, +/-, and -/- to reflect WT, heterozygous, and homozygous genotypes and C84Y+/+ and +/- to reflect WT and heterozygous genotypes. WT littermates were utilized as controls for each respective mutant, which are predominately C57BL/6J in background. The backgrounds of the A8V KI mice are C57BL/6J.129/Sv and the C84Y KI mice is C57BL/6J.FVB/N.129/Sv. Both female and male mice were used and initially analyzed separately to detect whether sex dependent differences are present. All protocols and experimental procedures follow guidelines set forth by the National Institutes of Health and the Florida State University Animal Care and Use Committee.

Force and fatigue measurements

Ex-vivo soleus contractile analysis was performed for each cTnC HCM mouse compared to their respective controls. The soleus muscles were isolated from the mice and placed in a Sylgard-coated Petri dish bathed with Krebs solution equilibrated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Proximal and distal tendons were secured with nonabsorbable braided silk sutures (Fine Science Tools) and the preparation was transferred to a tissue bath system (Model 800A, Dual Mode Muscle Lever 300C; Aurora Scientific) filled with equilibrated Krebs solution maintained at 32 °C. The distal tendon was fixed to a rigid post, while the proximal tendon was attached to a force transducer at resting length.

Isometric fatigue was assessed using a dual-mode muscle lever system (Aurora Scientific, Aurora, ON, Canada). Following the determination of optimal muscle length (Lo) and maximal tetanic force, the muscle was held at L0 and subjected to a repeated stimulation protocol designed to induce fatigue. Muscles were stimulated with supramaximal square-wave pulses delivered via platinum electrodes, evoking isometric contractions at fixed intervals (e.g., one contraction every 1 s) for a duration of 5 min. Each contraction consisted of a tetanic train delivered at 90 Hz for 450 ms. Force production was recorded continuously throughout the protocol, and fatigue was quantified as the decline in peak isometric force over time, expressed either as absolute force or normalized to the initial contraction (% initial force). Lo was measured with a digital caliper (Mitutoyo, Illinois, USA), after which tendons were trimmed, muscles blotted to remove excess Krebs solution, and wet weight determined. CSA was estimated from muscle weight and length for normalization of force (specific force, N/cm2). Muscle force analyses were performed using Dynamic Muscle Analysis (DMA) software (Aurora Scientific). The fatigue index was calculated as the force produced during the final contraction of the fatigue protocol expressed as a percentage of the force produced during the initial contraction.

Fiber typing analysis

Soleus muscles were isolated from the WT and cTnC HCM mice, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (O.C.T.) compound (Fisher Healthcare Tissue-Plus 4585), and flash frozen in liquid N2-cooled isopentane (Supelco MX0760-1). The muscles were sectioned at their midpoints at 7 μm thickness and -22 °C, placed on microscope slides, and stored at -80 °C until use. Immunofluorescence was used to visualize the percentage of different fiber types in the soleus muscles according to best practices (Brooks et al 2024). Briefly, the muscle sections were washed with phosphate buffered saline with 0.01% tween (PBST), blocked in 10% normal goat serum (NGS) (Vector Laboratories S-1000) and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with primary antibody cocktail containing BA-D5 (type I) IgG2b (1:50), SC-71 (type IIa) IgG1 (1:50), BF-F3 (type IIb) IgM (1:50) (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, Iowa City, IA). After washing with PBST, the tissues were incubated in the dark for 1 h at room temperature (RT) with a secondary antibody cocktail of Alexa Fluor 350 (IgG2b) goat anti-mouse (1:250) (Invitrogen; A21140) (blue), Alexa Fluor 647 (IgM mu chain) goat anti-mouse (1:500) (Abcam; ab150123) (red), and Alexa Fluor 488 (IgG1) goat anti-mouse (1:500) (Invitrogen; A21121) (green).

After 5 × 5 min washes in PBST, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 555 Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA) (Invitrogen; W32464) (stained yellow) at (1:500) for 1 h in the dark at RT. After 2 × 5 min washes in PBST, tissues were covered with Prolong Gold and imaged using a Leica DMi8 fluorescence microscope equipped with the Leica DFC7000T camera. Images were acquired at 10 × utilizing LAS X software and the spiral function used to stitch images together to obtain complete images of the entire section. Type I (slow- twitch oxidative fibers were stained blue), type IIa (intermediate twitch or fast oxidative glycolytic fibers were stained green), and type IIb (fast-twitch or fast glycolytic fibers were stained red). Cell borders were stained yellow with WGA and unstained cells were considered fast-twitch type IIx fibers.

Cross-Sectional area measurements and fiber type analysis

ImageJ was used to manually measure areas of stitched images of the entire soleus muscle (10X) with genotypes blinded. The scale was set using analyze > set scale using the straight-line tool which was used to measure the image scale bar to define the unit/pixel ratio. A manual selection tool was utilized to outline the cell borders and analyze > analyze particles to calculate individual CSA. The ‘Area’ is checked in Analyze > Set Measurements to obtain calibrated units (µm2). The formula used for these measurements was Area = Number of Pixels in Selection x (Known Distance/Pixel Length)2. For fiber analysis a manual blinded approach was utilized to count the number of blue, green, unstained, and red fibers divided by the total number of fibers in each muscle section and expressed as percentages.

Histological evaluation

Transverse sections (7 μm thick) of soleus muscles obtained from WT and cTnC HCM mice were used for tissue histology. Tissues were stained with hematoxylin and O Red Oil for analysis of fat deposition and histological features including central nucleated fibers. The number of centrally nucleated fibers were recorded using a blinded, manual approach and compared to the total number of fibers in each entire muscle section and reported as %. Images were captured at 20X, and Image J was utilized to label the counted fibers.

For assessment of intramyocellular lipid droplets, the following ImageJ quantification regimen was used. The channels were separated using image > color > color devolution to isolate the red channel from the blue nuclei. The region of interest was manually defined using the freehand selection tool to exclude extramyocellular adipocytes or interstitial fat artifacts. Thresholding was applied to generate a binary mask (highlighting red/fluorescent lipid droplets). Measurement set-up selections included area, area fraction, and limit to threshold. For analysis the following was used Analyze > analyze particles the minimum particle size threshold was set at size (pixel^2) 0-Infinity and circularity 0.00–1.00 and exclude on edges. The results were reported as % Area Fraction (total lipid area/total muscle area), providing a direct metric for lipid accumulation.

Evaluation of regeneration

Immunofluorescence was utilized to detect the presence of embryonic myosin heavy chain (eMHC) expression in the soleus muscle. The muscle sections were blocked in 10% NGS and incubated overnight with an eMHC primary antibody (0.3 µg/ml) Iowa Hybridoma Bank (DSHB, cat# F1.652) and anti-mouse secondary antibody-568 (red) (1:500 dilution (Invitrogen, cat AA11004). Next the tissues were incubated with WGA-488 (green) (AAT, cat 25530) at (1:500) for 1 h in the dark at RT (green). Tissues were covered with Prolong Gold plus Dapi to visualize nuclei (blue) and imaged using a Leica DMi8 fluorescence microscope equipped with the Leica DFC7000T camera and 20 × objective. Quantification of eMHC+ fibers was performed in a blinded manner, and measurements were performed manually of the entire soleus muscle. The number of eMHC positive fibers (red) were counted, and eMHC+ fibers were divided by the total number of fibers to determine the % eMHC+ fibers in each transverse section obtained from different mice.

Immunoblotting

Soleus muscle was pulverized in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle. Muscles were weighed and resuspended in RIPA buffer containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors and subjected to Dounce homogenization. Protein concentrations of the RIPA lysates were determined using the Pierce Bradford method according to manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Scientific, cat 22660). For each WT and mutant 35 µg total protein was loaded on a 12% Tris/SDS gel and proteins resolved using PAGE, transferred to a PVDF membrane (0.2 µm) and blocked in 3% BSA and 0.01% Tween-20. The blots were incubated with anti-mouse anti-ss/cTnC antibody (Santa Cruz, cat sc-52265) (1:500) and detected using the secondary antibody IRDye® 800CW goat anti-mouse (Licor, cat 926–32210) at a 1:20,000 dilution. Loading was assessed using an anti-Tubulin mouse McAb antibody (Proteintech, cat 66240–1-1 g) at 1:2,000 dilution and detected with the secondary antibody IRDye® 800CW goat anti-mouse (Licor, cat 926–32210) at a 1:20,000 dilution.

Densitometry was performed with ImageJ using the profile plot method which uses the toolbar Rectangle tool to entirely enclose the first band that is locked using Go to Analyze > Gels > Select First Lane. The subsequent lanes were selected by dragging the rectangle over the second band, Analyze > Gels > Select Next Lane and this step was repeated for all remaining lanes. A profile plot was generated with band histogram plot and the straight line tool used to draw a flat line across the base of each peak, closing off the areas under the peaks to exclude background. The wand tool was selected to measure the area inside of each enclosed peak. The peak areas of the protein of interest were divided by the loading control band of the same lane. The fold change was calculated by averaging the normalized values of the control group and dividing all sample values by this average to determine the relative fold change.

Blood serum myokine analysis

Blood was collected from WT and cTnC HCM mice and serum was isolated for myokine analysis and exposed to magnetic beads containing antibodies to common myokines or cytokines. The samples were mixed in a 96 well plate containing the Millipore Mouse Myokine Magnetic Bead Panel according to manufacturer’s recommendations. The samples and standards were analyzed using MagPix Luminex and median fluorescent intensity (MFI) output was converted to pg/ml. A principal component analysis (PCA) was run for each mutant and its respective WT control.

Results

Soleus muscle obtained from HCM mice exhibit differences in specific force measurements

Ex-vivo soleus contractile analysis was performed using muscles from each cTnC HCM mouse model. Each muscle was stimulated with increasing frequencies to determine whether the cardiomyopathy-linked Tnnc1 variants A8V and C84Y affected its force-generating capabilities. Examination of force between A8V+/+ (WT), A8V+/- and A8V-/- soleus muscles revealed no differences between genotypes as shown (Fig. 1A). However, there was a significant difference in specific force measurements between WT and A8V-/- soleus resulting in lower force production in the -/- muscle at 200 Hz (Fig. 1B). The % of max force (for mutant and WT) was compared in a force-frequency (FF) curve of the calculated FF50 (stimulation frequency required to reach 50% of max force) and it was found that both the A8V+/- and C84Y+/- soleus muscles had significantly increased sensitivity at 20 Hz stimulation frequency (Figs. 1C and 2C) respectively. There were, however, no differences in force measurements for the C84Y variant (Fig. 2A-B) or fatigue – max force by 20’s intervals for either A8V or C84Y variants (Figs. 1D and 2D) respectively. In addition, there were no significant differences in fatigue in soleus muscles containing the HCM variants (Figs. 1E and 2E). Fatiguability was similar indicated by the SF or MF fatigue indices for both A8V (Fig. 1F and G) or C84Y (Fig. 2F and G) variants. In Figs. 1D, E, 2D, and E time(s) indicate the number of 20 s intervals. In Figs. 1E and 2E the Y-axis reports specific force, while fatigue is an interpretation of the data shown. Immunoblots of soleus muscles were performed to confirm similar amounts of cTnC protein between samples (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Fiber typing analysis reveals an increased percentage of type I fibers in A8V-/- mutant soleus muscle

Shifts in MHC isoform expression can alter contractile profiles and ATPase rates of muscle fibers. Therefore, cross sections of soleus muscle were examined to determine whether the presence of HCM variants altered the content of soleus muscles compared to their respective WT controls. Figure 3A shows immunofluorescence-stained images of whole cross sections of soleus muscles obtained from C84Y+/-, A8V+/-, or A8V-/- mice and respective WT controls. The panels show merged and individual images of wheat germ agglutinin staining (yellow) indicating muscle fiber borders and antibodies specific to type I (blue), IIa (green), and IIb (red) MHC isoforms. Unstained fibers were considered type IIx-containing fibers. In Fig. 3B the % of each fiber type are reported for WT and A8V variants and Fig. 3C of the WT versus C84Y mutant relative to the total number in each section. The fiber typing results of the WT and A8V and C84Y mutant soleus muscles are reported in Table 1. There was one significant difference observed between A8V+/- and A8V-/- with an increase in type I fibers in the A8V-/- muscles.

The HCM mutants cause opposing alterations in type I fiber cross-sectional areas

We compared the distribution of CSA (μm2) of the most abundant fiber types detected (type I and IIa) in the cTnC mouse soleus muscle (Fig. 4). The histogram of CSA values shows the general distribution of type I fiber areas in A8V+/- and A8V-/- (Fig. 4A) and type IIa fiber areas in (Fig. 4B). There was a significantly higher percentage of fibers with smaller CSA in the soleus muscles containing both A8V+/- and A8V-/- variants compared to WT (Fig. 4A) and overall decrease in type I and type IIa fiber CSA (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, type IIa fiber CSA was significantly larger in the A8V-/- compared to A8V+/- suggesting some type of compensation (Fig. 4D). When comparing the CSA of all the fibers in the muscles there was an overall decrease in fiber CSA suggesting generalized atrophy (Fig. 5E). Table 2 provides a summary of the CSAs for the different fiber types shown in Fig. 4. This is supported by the Chi square analysis that shows a significantly greater percent of smaller type I and type IIa fibers in the A8V+/- and A8V-/- soleus muscle.

In Fig. 5 the results from C84Y+/- soleus muscle are shown. When examining the distribution of CSA (μm2) of the abundant fiber types (I and IIa) in C84Y+/- compared to WT soleus no significant differences were detected in type I fibers (Fig. 5A) or type IIa fiber (Fig. 5B) distributions. However, when comparing the overall type I fiber CSA measurements obtained (Fig. 5C) it was evident that the C84Y+/- fibers had larger CSA than WT while type IIa were unchanged (Fig. 5D). When considering the overall average CSA of all fibers measured the C84Y+/-had greater fiber CSA compared to WT, likely driven by type I fiber hypertrophy (Fig. 5E). See Table 2 for a summary of CSAs measured for the different fiber types shown in Fig. 5. Chi square analysis showed a significant reduction in the % of small type I fibers compared to large (Fig. 5F) with no differences in type IIa (Fig. 5G).

Soleus muscles obtained from HCM A8V-/- mice exhibit myopathic changes

The muscles were subjected to O Red Oil staining (hematoxylin + fat-soluble diazo dye) to determine whether the presence of the variants impacted muscle lipid content. Histological evaluation of soleus muscles revealed a greater percentage of centrally nucleated muscle fibers in the A8V+/- and A8V-/- soleus muscle compared to WT (Fig. 6A and B). This finding is suggestive of myopathic processes that become more pronounced as the mutant protein levels are increased. On the other hand, very little central nucleation was observed in the C84Y+/- muscle (Fig. 6A). We assessed embryonic myosin heavy chain expression (eMHC), a marker of regeneration, to assess whether active regeneration accompanied the myopathic changes in the mutant soleus muscles (Fig. 6C and D). The A8V-/- soleus muscle expressed significantly more eMHC than A8V+/- and WT, however at low levels between 1–2% (Fig. 6D) and was undetectable in the C84Y+/- (Fig. 6C and D). The soleus muscles were examined for intramyocellular lipid staining to assess whether the presence of mutant cTnC proteins contributed to altered fatty acid metabolism and it was found that A8V-/- soleus muscle exhibited a significant increase in % Area Fraction of lipid staining (Fig. 6E and 6F).

No differences were observed in serum myokines obtained from HCM mice

We examined blood serum myokine levels to assess whether the presence of the pathogenic variants coincided with overall systemic differences in myokine levels. The changes in myokines detected in the A8V+/- and C84Y+/- mice mostly occurred in the same direction, although we noted that the WT control samples from the two lines of mice exhibited some baseline differences. Supplemental Fig. 2 (S2) shows results for myokines with known roles in maintenance of skeletal muscle including: brain neurotrophic factor (BDNF; Figure S2-A), interleukin-6 (IL-6; Figure S2-B), follistatin-related protein (FSTL-1; Figure S2-C), fibroblast growth factor-21 (FGF-21; Figure S2-D), myostatin-growth differentiation factor 8 (MSTN-GDFB; Figure S2-E), secreted protein acidic and cysteine rich (SPARC; Figure S2-F), and osteocrin (OSTN; Figure S2-G). Several patterns of myokine expression were noted between mutants and respective controls: a) opposing differences between variants (BDNF, FGF-21, and MSTN-GDFB) as in Figure S2-A, -D, and -E; b) lower in both variants (IL-6, FSTL-1, and SPARC) shown in Fig. S2-B, -C, and -F; or c) higher in both variants (OSTN) in Fig. S2-G. Taken together, the cytokine release profiles suggest complex responses arising as a result of altered skeletal and cardiac muscle function.

Discussion

In our study we found that the presence the A8V-/- variant in soleus muscle was associated with distinctive alterations in force generation, fiber atrophy, and signs of myopathy. The C84Y+/- variant did not have altered force generation, however we observed type I fiber hypertrophy. This suggests different pathological mechanisms are at play and that HCM variants may exert variant-specific effects in slow skeletal muscle. Interestingly, although the A8V variant demonstrated increased central nucleation and embryonic myosin heavy chain expression consistent with ongoing degeneration-regeneration cycling—the C84Y variant primarily exhibited type I fiber hypertrophy without overt regenerative markers. These results lead us to hypothesize that sarcomeric HCM mutations may differentially affect skeletal muscle adaptation, with some variants promoting compensatory hypertrophic remodeling while others induce sufficient mechanical, metabolic, or calcium-mediated stress to trigger myofiber injury and regeneration.

The purpose of our study was to understand whether cTnC HCM variants unleash pathological mechanisms in slow skeletal muscle fibers and may contribute to muscle weakness and fatigue, two of the most common reported muscle related symptoms in HCM patients. Our current study leverages our ability to study these cardiomyopathy-linked variants in intact muscles and more fully address their impact on slow skeletal muscle physiology in a live animal.

Examination of the functional impact of cardiomyopathy variants in slow skeletal muscle

In our current study, we utilized two mouse models to investigate the impact of the Tnnc1 HCM variants A8V and C84Y on skeletal muscle structure and function. Because ss/cTnC is expressed in both cardiac and slow skeletal muscle we expected that only slow twitch fibers in the soleus muscle of the cardiomyopathy mouse models would be affected. These missense variants A8V (Landstrom et al 2008; Patsalis et al 2025; Pinto et al 2009; Ploski et al 2016) and C84Y (Landstrom et al 2008) were identified in HCM patient cohorts and functional studies demonstrated increased Ca2+ sensitivity of force development in skinned porcine fibers reconstituted with HCM variants. The cTnC-A8V mouse model developed cardiomyopathy that recapitulated disease observed in patients (Martins et al 2015). The A8V variant is located in the N-domain of cTnC, which has an important role in modulating the number and rate of cycling cross-bridges in cardiac muscle, however it remains unclear its impact on soleus muscle function (Gonzalez-Martinez et al 2018). The expression of slow skeletal isoforms has been documented in the human heart, including slow skeletal Troponin T (hssTnT) and the functional consequences have been explored (Pinto et al 2012). It was demonstrated that hssTnT homologues of cTnT (normally expressed in the heart) possess distinctive functional properties suggesting differing roles in muscle regulation.

In our previous study we utilized reconstituted in situ approaches to examine the impact of HCM-associated cTnC (A8V, C84Y, D134E, and D145E) variants on Ca2+ sensitivity of isometric force and ATPase activation at saturating Ca2+ concentrations (Veltri et al 2017). No differences in maximal force were observed in skinned rabbit soleus fibers reconstituted with WT or HCM (A8V or C84Y) cTnC variant proteins. Overall, we found that these functional phenotypes of cTnC A8V and D145E were more prominent in cardiac than in slow skeletal muscles. When reconstituted in type I (slow) skeletal muscle the cTnC C84Y variant had decreased maximal ATPase activity at pCa 5.0 compared to controls (Veltri et al 2017). In contrast, in our current study we assessed variant specific alterations in tetanic force in intact soleus muscles. We observed a frequency-dependent decrement in specific force (N/cm2) in the A8V-/- mutant at 200 Hz, the highest stimulation frequency. Interestingly, neither variant caused an increase in muscle fatiguability. Differences in our results are likely due to use of intact mouse muscle. In our previous study force measurements were performed in Tnnc1 HCM variant-reconstituted rabbit soleus muscle, which contains around 80% type I slow twitch fibers (Veltri et al 2017). Future studies using single type I fibers from these mice could provide better clarity concerning the direct impact of the variants on sarcomere function and altered force generation per fiber cross sectional area.

Assessing soleus muscle fiber type in cardiomyopathic mouse models

We performed fiber typing of the soleus muscle obtained from the Tnnc1 HCM mice to assess whether any alterations contributed to the observed differences in force production. Slow twitch fibers play a crucial role in providing postural support and are subjected to sustained, low-intensity contractions. A8V-/- soleus muscle had significantly greater percentage of type I fibers compared to WT (Fig. 3 and Table 1). Assuming similar muscle size, an increase in type I fibers would contribute to lower tetanic force generation since type I fibers generate less force per CSA than other fiber types. This is largely due to differences between myosin heavy chain isoforms, which influence cross-bridge cycling kinetics, and therefore force production (Brooks et al 1990).

Our fiber type percentage estimates align with antibody-based reports for mouse soleus muscle, which typically show 35–45% type I and 50–60% type IIa fibers in C57 background mice (Augusto 2004), though type I proportions can vary (Carroll et al 2011). In contrast, human soleus muscle and other postural muscles in the legs and back are predominately slow with up to 80% type I slow-twitch fibers, though there are variable estimates ranging between 64–100% (Gollnick et al 1974). The fiber type distributions of mouse soleus muscles differ among mouse strains and have a mixed composition compared to humans. The disparities in soleus muscle composition between humans and mice can be explained, at least in part, by its location in biped versus quadruped animals and its reduced postural role in mice. Our study illustrates the potential biological significance of cardiomyopathy-causing Tnnc1 variants expressed in type I fibers in the slow skeletal muscles “sedentary” non-exercised mice (no access to voluntary (wheel running) or forced (treadmill)) and maintained under identical housing conditions. If the significant reduction in force is due to the presence of the A8V-/- mouse soleus muscles comprised of 46.8% type I fibers, it may have greater impact in human soleus muscle since it contains considerably more type I fibers. Therefore, we expect that a larger percentage of fibers would express the A8V variant. To determine whether the A8V+/-variant is directly driving pathological changes other potential pathological mechanisms would need to be evaluated including muscle Ca2+ sensitivity, cross-bridge kinetics, passive tension, and energy metabolism.

Histological evaluation of cardiomyopathic mouse soleus muscle

Various congenital myopathies manifest with muscle weakness and type I fiber atrophy especially when variants are contained in both slow skeletal and cardiac isoforms (Imoto & Nonaka 2001). We assessed the mice in this study for specific changes in type I fibers as they express the mutant ssTnC proteins. The A8V+/- and -/- soleus muscles also exhibited central nucleation—which can indicate increased regeneration in skeletal muscle and is frequently observed in affected muscles in neuromuscular diseases (Meyer 2018). Our histological evaluation of the soleus muscle obtained from the HCM mouse models, revealed distinctive findings between the cTnnc1-A8V and -C84Y mouse models. Our findings in A8V mice differ from the human familial neuromuscular disease termed “type I hypotrophy with central nuclei” observed by (Karpati et al 1970) (Engel et al 1968) since central nucleation and atrophy wasn't confined to type I fibers.

The changes in the A8V mutant soleus muscle are consistent with atrophy. Conversely, the presence of the C84Y mutant resulted in type I fiber hypertrophy without alterations in type IIa fiber CSA (Fig. 5). In the A8V variant-containing soleus muscle both type I and type IIa fibers had overall lower CSA. This may indicate activation of generalized atrophic mechanisms (Sartori et al 2021) or signal some degree of unloading or loss of use (Gao et al 2018). The combination of type I and type IIa fiber atrophy, the presence of centrally nucleated fibers in the A8V+/- and A8V-/- soleus muscle, and muscle weakness suggest ongoing myopathic changes in these mice. In normal adult skeletal muscle eMHC expression is essentially absent with only 0–0.1% fibers staining positive for the embryonic myosin isoform (Charge & Rudnicki 2004). The A8V-/- soleus muscle showed signs of mild regeneration with 1–2% eMHC positive fibers (Fig. 6) indicating low level or resolving injury. Interestingly, there appeared to be some compensation in the A8V-/- soleus by fast (IIa) fibers which had greater CSA while type I CSA was further reduced (Table 2). This interplay between slow and fast fibers was previously observed in a dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) Tnnc1 mouse model (D73N) where lower force generation by slow fibers was compensated by fast fiber hypertrophy assumably to maintain normal force generation by the entire muscle (Tikunova et al 2018). The increase in type I fiber CSA in C84Y+/- muscle suggests that the mutant imparts functional changes such as increased Ca2+ sensitivity of force development – though this was not directly measured in this study (Veltri et al 2017).

The small but significant increase in intramyocellular oil red staining in the A8V-/- suggests that the metabolic perturbations in the heart may also exist in some capacity in slow skeletal muscle. Because valine is a BCAA, inhibition of its canonical degradation pathway could disrupt the normal balance between BCAA catabolism, mitochondrial substrate oxidation, and fatty-acid handling in skeletal muscle (Mann et al 2021). Skeletal muscle is a major site of BCAA oxidation, and impaired BCAA catabolism has been linked to altered fatty-acid oxidation, incomplete substrate utilization, insulin resistance, and lipid deposition (Newgard 2012). In turn, increased skeletal muscle lipid accumulation could cause lipotoxic stress. Our findings of increased lipid accumulation in A8V-/- soleus muscle is consistent with findings in our previous cardiac transcriptomic study showing downregulation of canonical valine degradation. Since the mouse soleus muscle is slow-twitch, highly oxidative muscle composed of primarily Type I and Type IIa fibers it has a natural tendency to store more lipid droplets that provide fuel for continuous activity (Kaneko et al 2011). While an increase in small lipid droplets allow rapid liberation of energy, excess fat accumulation in muscles can lead to build up of toxic lipid intermediates that can interfere with a muscle’s contractile speed and force capacity (Morales et al 2017).

Evaluation of serum myokines suggests limited global impacts of cardiomyopathy variants

We examined secreted myokines in the blood serum of the HCM mice to better understand the systemic impacts of the cardiomyopathy variants in skeletal muscle (Supplemental Fig. 2). Myokines can be either cytokines or other peptides that are released in response to various stimuli including muscle contractions (Pedersen et al 2007). BDNF is typically increased by exercise and thus the lower levels in the A8V+/- mice may reflect lower physical activity. This however has not been studied and needs to be followed up with increased sample numbers. Furthermore, the trend toward reduction of the contraction sensitive cytokines/myokines IL-6 and FSTL-1 also supports the notion that the A8V+/- mice are less active than their WT counterparts (Xi et al 2021). The cTnC A8V+/- mice had higher serum FGF-21 levels (Fig. 5D), which is positively correlated with aging-related sarcopenia (Tezze et al 2017). A limitation of these findings was that we were unable to include myokine results from a larger cohort of mice that included A8V-/-, which showed the most severe phenotype. Evaluation of myokines within the muscles themselves would provide more direct information concerning myopathic changes within the muscles themselves.

Additional studies are warranted to better understand whether defective motor innervation (neuropathy) also exists that might be responsible for the downstream changes in the soleus muscles. While our results are intriguing and may be informative of the potential connection between HCM and skeletal muscle abnormalities. We identify several limitations of our study including the greatest pathological alterations are detected in the homozygous A8V mutant while most patients are heterozygous for this variant. Inclusion of a time course study would have allowed us to determine the timing of pathological changes (atrophy and regeneration) in the A8V mutant mice and assess whether type I fibers were first affected while development of type IIa fiber atrophy occurred at later time points. This type of study would have allowed us to conclude whether type IIa fibers were secondarily affected. There is the possibility that the A8V-/- mice had reduced exercise capacity due to cardiac symptoms (though cardiomyopathy manifested much later in these mice) and their soleus muscles were subjected to reduced use or load. The increase in type I fiber-type percentage in A8V-/- muscle argues against disuse, which causes a transition from slow to fast isoforms (Booth & Gollnick 1983). Another limitation is the small sample size, this was particularly impactful for the C84Y+/- mice, which exhibited greater variations in force, fatigue measurements, and fiber composition.

Conclusions

Our study provides the first direct in vivo evidence that the presence of HCM-associated ss/cTnC variants can impact slow skeletal muscle pathophysiology. In soleus muscle containing the A8V variants the homozygotes had the most pronounced reduction in specific force production, type I and IIa fiber atrophy, increased central nucleation, and signs of regeneration. Whereas soleus muscle containing the C84Y variant maintained specific force production and manifested type I fiber hypertrophy suggesting a compensatory adaptive remodeling response rather than ongoing degeneration-regeneration cycling. Our study suggests that the HCM TNNC1 variants studied here may contribute to muscle pathophysiology in patients and is consistent with previous findings that distinct HCM variants do not simply contribute to different severities of the same disease – but can produce qualitatively distinct biomechanical and metabolic states.
