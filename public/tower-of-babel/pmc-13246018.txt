Facial Asymmetry as a Universal Signature: A Morphometric, Perceptual, and Artistic Approach
Authors: Marc Divaris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Facial asymmetry is universal, yet its directionality and its relationship with patients’ self-perception remain underexplored. This study evaluates the association between 3-dimensional–measured malar asymmetry and the patient’s declared “better side” in an aesthetic population.Methods:One hundred twenty consecutive patients were photographed in standardized frontal view using the Vectra H2 system. Malar width was assessed by measuring the zygion–midline distance on each side, recorded twice and averaged. Patients completed a 10-item ad hoc questionnaire addressing their preferred side, perceived asymmetries, photographic habits, and mirror–photograph differences. Observed frequencies were compared to a theoretical 50/50 distribution using chi-square testing.Results:Objective asymmetry was present in 98.3% of subjects, with a wider left side in 118 cases (P < 0.001). In contrast, side preferences were evenly distributed between left (45%) and right (52%) (P = 0.41). No correlation was found between the anatomically wider side and the declared preferred side (P = 0.62). Forty percent of patients reported noticeable differences between their mirror image and photographs.Conclusions:Although a left-sided directional asymmetry predominates, it does not align with patients’ stated preferences or their subjective self-image. Recognizing this dissociation between structure and perception during consultation improves expectation management and reduces postoperative misunderstandings in facial aesthetic procedures.

Takeaways

Question: Does objective facial asymmetry determine the patient’s perceived “better side?”

Findings: Three-dimensional morphometric analysis confirmed that facial asymmetry is nearly universal and revealed a predominant left-sided malar width in most individuals. However, patient-reported side preference showed no significant association with the morphometrically broader side and was distributed almost equally between left and right.

Meaning: The perception of a “better side” does not primarily reflect structural facial asymmetry but rather a complex perceptual construction influenced by mirror exposure, photographic habits, and expressive factors. Recognizing this dissociation is important in aesthetic consultation to align expectations and explain normal facial asymmetry.

INTRODUCTION

Perfect symmetry of the human face does not exist. Since antiquity, artists and anatomists have emphasized the constant presence of asymmetry between the 2 hemifaces, and Western painting has recurrently favored the left three-quarter view, often perceived as more expressive or harmonious.1–4

Neuropsychological studies have suggested that this preference may be related to emotional lateralization, with the right hemisphere playing a major role in facial expression analysis, rendering the left hemiface more perceptually salient to the observer.5–7

From a morphological standpoint, morphometric studies confirm that facial asymmetry is a constant feature of the human face, although its expression varies among individuals and according to the analytical framework employed. Findings regarding the directionality of asymmetry remain heterogeneous in the literature, largely due to methodological differences, particularly in landmark selection and axis definition.8–11

In parallel, the widespread use of digital photography and selfies has reinforced the subjective perception of a “better side.” This declared preference does not always correspond to measured morphological asymmetry, partly due to well-documented differences between mirror images and photographic images, which alter overall facial perception and influence self-evaluation.12–14

In this context, understanding the relationship between objectively measured morphological asymmetry and subjective perception represents an important clinical issue: to analyze patient demand, anticipate expectations in aesthetic surgery or medicine, and prevent postoperative misunderstandings when asymmetry has not been previously explained.

The objective of this study was to examine, in 120 patients, the relationship between 3-dimensional–measured facial asymmetry, declared “better side” preference, and perceptual references derived from artistic and neuropsychological literature.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Population

This prospective monocentric study included 120 consecutive patients consulting at a specialized center for facial aesthetic concerns. The mean age was 43 years (range: 18–72 y), with 97 women and 23 men. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Image Acquisition

Photographic acquisitions were performed using the Vectra H2 system (Canfield Scientific), following a strictly standardized protocol including Frankfurt horizontal plane positioning, neutral facial expression, constant lighting, fixed distance, absence of head rotation or tilt, and exclusive use of strictly frontal views.

Morphometric Measurements

The facial midline was defined using two cutaneous landmarks visible on standardized frontal views: the subnasale and the cutaneous gnathion.

The zygion point was identified as the most lateral point of the zygomatic contour visible in frontal projection. The linear distance between the zygion and the facial midline was measured bilaterally and used solely to classify the broader side and the narrower side. This specific axial measurement was not intended to characterize global facial asymmetry.

Each measurement was performed twice by the same operator and averaged to improve intraobserver reliability.

The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05. The study was not powered for multivariate analyses, as its objective was descriptive and exploratory. Analyses related to declared preference for a “better side” were restricted to participants expressing a clear preference (left or right), with uncertain respondents or those without awareness of a preferred side excluded from this specific analysis.

RESULTS

Morphological Analysis

Among the 120 patients, 118 (98.3%) presented a greater left malar width, defined by a larger zygion–midline distance on the left side. Only 2 (1.7%) patients showed the opposite configuration with a wider right side. Clinically, the left side tended to appear fuller, more stable, and more mature, whereas the right side appeared narrower, more elevated, and more juvenile. A chi-square test confirmed that this distribution significantly differed from a random 50/50 pattern (P < 0.001).

Perceptual Questionnaire Analysis

Fifty-two percent of patients (62 of 120) reported being aware of having a “better side,” whereas 26% (31 of 120) were unsure and 22% (27 of 120) reported no awareness of any side preference. Regarding the preferred side, 45% (54 of 120) favored the left profile and 52% (62 of 120) favored the right, whereas 3% (4 of 120) reported no preference; this distribution did not differ significantly from a theoretical 50/50 model (P = 0.41). No correlation was found between the objectively wider side and the preferred side (P = 0.62).

Patients described various reasons for their preference, most frequently citing photogenic appearance, impression of youthfulness, finer contour, or better harmony. Spontaneous reports of asymmetry included a lower oral commissure (12 cases), a “heavier” or “more descended” side (11 cases), nasal asymmetry (12 cases), and a lower eyebrow (6 cases). Overall, 58% of patients considered their preferred side to be the most expressive, and 31% consciously modified their pose during photographs. Forty percent reported a noticeable discrepancy between their mirror reflection and their photographs. Asymmetry was judged aesthetically major in 15% of cases, secondary in 40%, and negligible in 45%. Emotionally, 29% perceived their asymmetry as distinctive, 18% as bothersome, and 53% as neutral. When asked about potential correction, 35% would consider a partial correction, whereas 61% preferred to retain their asymmetry and 4% were undecided.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that all faces in our cohort exhibited asymmetry and highlights, according to the morphometric axis analyzed—frontal malar width—a predominantly left-oriented lateralization pattern, observed in 98.3% of patients (Fig. 1). This predominance does not correspond to an anomaly, but rather to a physiological morphological variation observed during craniofacial development and dependent on the analytical framework applied.

The literature on directional facial asymmetry is heterogeneous14–19 and does not support the existence of a universal lateralization (left or right) across all facial regions and methodological approaches. Several studies have described specific regional asymmetries, particularly at the level of the chin or mandible, with orientations varying according to populations, anatomical landmarks, and axes of analysis used.15–19 Our results should therefore be interpreted as identifying an emerging left-oriented pattern according to a specific morphometric criterion, without claiming to summarize all dimensions of facial asymmetry, which remains a frequent and multidimensional characteristic of the human face.18,19

From a perceptual standpoint, objectively measured asymmetry overlaps imperfectly with the subjective experience of patients. In our series, only 52% of participants reported awareness of a “better side,” whereas 26% were uncertain and 22% reported no such awareness. Preferences were almost evenly distributed between the left side (45%) and the right side (52%), despite the fact that nearly all faces exhibited a morphometrically broader left side. Beyond this directional discordance, 58% of patients considered their preferred side to be more expressive, 31% consciously adapted their pose in photographs, and 40% reported a noticeable difference between their mirror reflection and their photographic images. Furthermore, although 35% would consider partial correction of their asymmetry, 61% preferred to preserve it, and only 18% experienced it as genuinely bothersome. In other words, objectively measured asymmetry does not overlap with declared preferences or with the emotional experience of asymmetry. This discrepancy reflects the way self-image is constructed: first through the mirror, which reverses laterality,13,14 and subsequently through photographs, where pose, lighting, and expression often outweigh structural geometry.

It is essential to distinguish between facial self-representation—constructed from mirror exposure and perceptual habituation—and the perspective of an external observer (photographer, painter, or clinician) confronted with a noninverted image. These 2 perceptual reference frames are not superimposable. In the era of selfies, repeated preferential posing, chosen angles, and the conscious selection of the “good profile” reinforce a preference built over time, independently of actual structural asymmetry.

This dissociation between structure and perception is not unique to the contemporary era. Numerous iconographic analyses have shown that Western painting, particularly during the Renaissance, favored the left three-quarter view when representing the human face.20 Leonardo da Vinci frequently used this orientation, both in his anatomical drawings and in his portraits. The three-quarter view represents an intermediate orientation between strict frontal view and profile, allowing simultaneous visualization of zygomatic projection, mandibular contour, and part of the contralateral hemiface. This position enhances the reading of lateral volumes while attenuating certain asymmetries, which explains its recurrent use throughout art history.

In this context, it is noteworthy that a morphometric composite portrait established in a previous study, based on the geometric mean of a large sample of contemporary faces, reproduced a similar pattern, with slightly greater left convexity and more pronounced right verticality.21 This average facial pattern presents a striking resemblance to certain frontal sketches by Leonardo da Vinci, in which this subtle asymmetry between the 2 hemifaces is already perceptible (Fig. 2). Without establishing a direct link, this convergence suggests that the directional asymmetry identified by modern morphometry may have been intuitively recognized by Renaissance artists. This composite portrait therefore constitutes a relevant visual reference to illustrate the morphological constant observed.

From a clinical perspective, these observations have direct implications for the preoperative consultation. Facial asymmetry is constant, yet rarely identified or fully understood by the patient, who relies on a self-image constructed from mirror exposure, preferred photographs, and individual emotional filters. Even subtle asymmetry may become more visible after rejuvenation or volumetric restoration procedures if it has not been explained beforehand. Conversely, certain perceived asymmetries may correspond to expected physiological variations. Showing patients their standardized views, commenting on the existing asymmetry, and explaining the difference between mirror perception and photographic image may—particularly in patients highly attentive to their appearance or expressing a lateralized preference—facilitates understanding, recalibrates expectations, and prevents certain postoperative misunderstandings. The objective is not to pursue ideal symmetry, but to restore credible harmony while respecting the asymmetric signature unique to each face.

This study reflects a typical aesthetic population, predominantly female, with a smaller male subgroup, which limits sex-specific extrapolation. Furthermore, recruitment involved patients consulting for aesthetic concerns, which may introduce a selection bias toward individuals more attentive to facial appearance and potentially more sensitive to morphological variations, without, however, affecting the descriptive validity of the objective measurements performed. Patient perception was potentially influenced by the type of procedure sought. Nevertheless, the study was not designed or powered to stratify perceptual responses according to the specific procedure sought (eg, rhinoplasty, facelift, or nonsurgical treatments). Moreover, morphometric analysis deliberately focused on a single axis—frontal malar width—to ensure reproducibility and limit bias related to parameter multiplication.

Other parameters, such as bigonial width or angle, bitemporal distance, facial inclination, yaw rotation, and mandibular asymmetry, may indeed influence patient perception and partially explain the discrepancy between objective measurements and subjective preference. These parameters were not included, as the analysis intentionally concentrated on a single frontal axis to prioritize reproducibility and reduce methodological variability. The results should therefore be interpreted as specific to the frontal malar axis studied and not as a comprehensive description of facial asymmetry.

The absence of multivariate analysis and correlation with additional morphological or functional parameters must be considered an acknowledged limitation of the study, consistent with its descriptive and exploratory objective. Similarly, no in-depth socio-demographic analysis (age, education level, profession, or cultural exposure to image) was included, as the study was not dimensioned for such investigation. These factors may nevertheless influence the perceptual construction of the “better side” and merit exploration in future studies specifically designed for that purpose.

Finally, the evaluation of subjective perception relied on self-reported data, inherently exposed to individual and contextual variability. Nonetheless, the observed discordance between objective asymmetry and declared preference constitutes in itself a relevant finding, illustrating the complexity of perceptual mechanisms involved in facial self-representation.12–14,22–26

Despite these limitations, this study highlights 2 central elements. First, facial asymmetry appears to be a universal characteristic of the human face, reflecting physiological morphological organization rather than anomaly. Second, no systematic correspondence exists between objective structural asymmetry and subjective perception of the face. This discordance underscores that self-representation relies on a complex perceptual construction integrating visual, emotional, and cultural factors beyond morphometry alone. From a clinical standpoint, these results invite clinicians to consider asymmetry not as a defect to be corrected, but as a structural feature to be integrated into the therapeutic strategy.

Preoperative identification and explanation of asymmetry, along with clarification of the difference between mirror and photographic perception, allows better expectation alignment and helps prevent certain postoperative misunderstandings. In practice, these elements should guide the surgeon in adapting the aesthetic procedure to each patient, both in planning and in intraoperative execution. The objective of aesthetic management is not the pursuit of ideal symmetry, but the restoration of credible harmony, respectful of each face’s unique asymmetric signature. The present study focuses on physiologic, mild, facial asymmetry in an aesthetic population and does not propose a diagnostic threshold for abnormal asymmetry. Clinically, correction is generally considered when asymmetry is structural, clearly visible, functionally significant (eg, occlusal or skeletal involvement), progressive, syndromic, or posttraumatic in origin, or when it generates significant patient distress despite appropriate counseling.

CONCLUSIONS

This study confirms that facial asymmetry is a constant characteristic of the human face and that, in most cases, it follows a similar directional pattern with a slightly broader left side. However, this objective asymmetry does not always correspond to patient-declared preferences, indicating that perception of one’s own face results from a complex construction involving visual habits, mirror images, and photographic representations.

Acknowledging this dissociation between real structure and subjective perception is essential in facial aesthetic surgery. Identifying and explaining asymmetries during consultation helps align expectations, prevent postoperative misunderstandings, and integrate the aesthetic intervention into a more realistic and individualized approach.

Understanding this dynamic—constant asymmetry, a predominant orientation, but variable perception—constitutes a valuable tool today for guiding preoperative analysis, improving patient–surgeon communication, and enhancing the relevance of therapeutic decisions.
