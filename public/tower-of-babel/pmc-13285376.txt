Small intracellular vesicle-liposome fusogenic nanoplatform sIVs-LPs@Hes: Nebulized delivery targeting pulmonary inflammatory microenvironment to improve ALI
Authors: Bichao Lu, Jie Su, Yu Long, Xuefeng Liu, Tongfei Fu, Ningfeng Mao, Miao Wang, Wei Guo, Ke Yi, Shuhan Zhou, Wenliang Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute lung injury (ALI) and its more severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), are severe respiratory diseases with a high mortality rate, characterized by pathological lung damage induced by excessive inflammation. Current therapeutic strategies for ALI are limited; conventional treatments are associated with severe side effects and lack targeted efficacy. Hesperetin(Hes)is a natural flavanone abundant in citrus fruits that exhibits promising anti-inflammatory potential, yet its further clinical application is hindered by poor solubility and low bioavailability. Small intracellular vesicles (sIVs) derived from mesenchymal stem cells have been demonstrated to possess the advantages of high yield, rapid cellular uptake and excellent stability. In view of this, the present study constructed a Hes-loaded sIVs-liposome nanodelivery system (sIVs-LPs@Hes) for the treatment of ALI via nebulized inhalation. Our findings revealed that sIVs-LPs@Hes could be efficiently absorbed and persistently retained in the lung, and it exerted a superior protective effect by inhibiting glycolysis through the MCU-mCa2+-NF-κB pathway, promoting M2 polarization of macrophages, ameliorating pulmonary inflammatory status, reducing pulmonary edema and repairing pathological lung damage. In conclusion, the present study not only elucidated the specific anti-inflammatory mechanism of Hes, but also developed a novel composite nanodelivery system by integrating the merits of sIVs and liposomes, thereby providing a promising potential therapeutic strategy for ALI.

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) is a clinical complication secondary to sepsis, influenza infection and other pathological conditions, which can further progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [[1], [2], [3]]. ALI and ARDS are primarily characterized by extensive inflammatory cell infiltration in the lung parenchyma, and recent studies have elucidated that M1 polarization and glycolysis of macrophages are crucial contributing factors to the pathogenesis of ALI [[4], [5], [6]]. Although the therapeutic regimen centered on lung-protective ventilation strategies, adjuvanted with glucocorticoids and antibiotics, has been widely applied in the clinical management of ALI, its unsatisfactory efficacy and severe adverse effects have limited its clinical utility [7,8]. Thus, the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for ALI is an urgent clinical need.

A variety of natural products have been extensively investigated for the treatment of ALI due to their favorable biocompatibility and prominent anti-inflammatory properties [9,10]. Hes, a natural flavanone abundant in citrus fruits, has been proven to inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway and promote M2 polarization of macrophages [11,12]. However, the poor water solubility and low bioavailability of Hes, together with the unclear underlying therapeutic mechanisms, have restricted its further clinical translation. Therefore, Hes-loaded delivery systems represented by liposomes have exhibited promising application prospects [13,14]. Nevertheless, liposomes are rapidly cleared in vivo after cellular uptake, with drawbacks including a short circulatory half-life, complete lack of active targeting ability, short in vivo retention time and limited duration of therapeutic effect, which result in suboptimal efficacy of single liposomal delivery systems.

sIVs derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have been demonstrated to possess the advantages of high yield, rapid cellular internalization and excellent stability, and also exert intrinsic anti-inflammatory effects [15,16]. Notably, the vesicles constructed in our present study are generated via sonication-induced cell membrane disruption and subsequent membrane reassembly, which are heterogeneous membrane-originated nanovesicles rather than naturally secreted intracellular vesicles. These IVs are enriched in proteins associated with the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, exhibit unique microRNA signatures related to the intracellular membrane system, and contain high levels of phospholipids such as phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine [15]. They have been shown to exert excellent therapeutic effects in the treatment of retinal injury, burn injury, temporomandibular joint injury and other pathological conditions [17,18].

Accordingly, the present study first identified the specific mechanism by which Hes modulates M2 polarization of macrophages, namely the inhibition of glycolysis via the MCU-mCa2+-NF-κB axis. We then constructed a Hes-loaded sIVs-liposome composite nanodelivery system (sIVs-LPs@Hes). Our findings demonstrated that the sIVs-LPs@Hes composite nanodelivery system could be efficiently delivered to the lung via nebulized inhalation and achieve sustained retention for up to 48 h. Hes and sIVs exerted a synergistic anti-inflammatory effect, which collectively ameliorated pathological lung damage, alleviated pulmonary inflammatory responses, promoted M2 macrophage polarization through metabolic reprogramming, and reduced pulmonary edema, thus leading to the comprehensive improvement of lung pathological changes. In summary, this nanodelivery platform serves as a promising therapeutic strategy for ALI and ARDS, and exhibits great translational potential for clinical application.

Materials and methods

Materials

All chemical reagents were of analytical grade and purchased from commercial sources, and used as received without further purification. Hesperetin (HY-N0168, MedChemExpress, MCE); Lecithin and cholesterol were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), PKH26, PKH67 and DIR were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

scRNA-seq analysis

Data related to the GSE207651 dataset were downloaded from the GEO database and analyzed using the Seurat package. Strict quality control (QC) criteria were applied: cells with UMI counts >30,000, gene counts <200 or >5,000, and mitochondrial gene content >20% were removed. Dimensionality reduction and cell clustering were performed using Seurat v3.1.2. All gene expression values were normalized and scaled with the Normalize Data and Scale Data functions. The top 2000 highly variable genes were identified by the FindVariable Features function for principal component analysis (PCA). Based on the top 20 principal components, cells were divided into several clusters via the FindClusters function, and the clusters were visualized using the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) algorithm. Cell type annotation of each cluster was accomplished according to the expression levels of canonical markers in differentially expressed genes, combined with the SynEcoSys database. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using the R package clusterProfiler 3.16.123. Pathways with a P-value <0.05 were considered significantly enriched, and GO gene sets covering molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), and cellular component (CC) were used as the reference background for enrichment analysis.

Cell culture

Bone mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and RAW 264.7 murine macrophage cell line were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). RAW 264.7 cells were maintained in DMEM medium (Cat. No. G4515-500 ML, Servicebio, China) supplemented with 10% FBS (Procell) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Procell). Cells at passages 3 to 8 were used for all subsequent experiments. RAW 264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well, followed by incubation in complete medium for 24 h before corresponding treatments.

Macrophages were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 250 ng/mL) combined with interferon-γ (IFN-γ, 100 ng/mL) to induce M1 polarization, followed by treatment with Hes (25, 50 μM) for 24 h. In addition, RU360 (10 μM) was added 1 h prior to the induction of M1 macrophage polarization.

Preparation of sIVs

Upon reaching 90% confluence, cells were trypsinized, neutralized, and washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cell density was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells per milliliter. The ultrasonic probe was then positioned at the center of the liquid surface, with the sample tube placed on ice simultaneously. Cells were sonicated at 20% amplitude. After sonication, the lysate was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min, followed by an additional centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was passed through a 220 nm filter membrane, then transferred to an ultracentrifuge tube and ultracentrifuged at 150,000 × g for 70 min. All procedures were performed on ice. The obtained pellets were designated as sIVs and resuspended in PBS for subsequent use.

Synthesis and Characterization of sIVs-LPs@Hes

Soybean lecithin, cholesterol and hesperetin were dissolved at a mass ratio of 8:1:1 in a mixed solvent of dichloromethane and methanol (2:1, v/v). The mixture was magnetically stirred at 50 °C until complete dissolution, then transferred to a rotary evaporator for vacuum rotary evaporation to remove organic solvents. A certain volume of PBS was subsequently added, and the mixture was sonicated for 15 min to achieve sufficient hydration. Liposome@Hes (LPs@Hes) was prepared by extrusion through a liposome extruder. Blank liposomes (LPs) were synthesized using the identical protocol without the addition of hesperetin. The prepared LPs@Hes and extracted sIVs were co-dissolved in PBS, sonicated for 15 min, and then extruded through the extruder to obtain the chimeric liposomal nanocomposite sIVs-LPs@Hes.

Particle size distribution and zeta potential of sIVs-LPs@Hes

0.2 mL of sIVs-LPs@Hes liposomes was diluted with 5 mL of distilled water, and approximately 3.5 mL of the diluted solution was transferred to a cuvette. The particle size and zeta potential of sIVs-LPs@Hes were determined using a nanoparticle size analyzer. The particle sizes and zeta potentials were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano (series ZS-90).

TEM of sIVs-LPs@Hes liposomes

After diluting the freshly prepared liposomes to an appropriate concentration, samples were prepared on copper grids. After the samples were dried, negative staining was performed using 3% phosphotungstic acid. Following staining and subsequent drying, the samples were examined using transmission electron microscopy (Tecnai 12, Philips, Holland).

Colloidal stability of sIVs-LPs@Hes liposomes

1 mL of sIVs-LPs@Hes liposomes was diluted with 25 mL of different physiological media. Aliquots of the diluted solutions were collected at 24 h, 48 h and 72 h post-dilution, and the particle size and zeta potential were measured using a nanoparticle size analyzer at each time point.

Determination of drug loading efficiency and in vitro drug release behavior

The drug loading efficiency and release profile of sIVs-LPs@Hes were determined by UV-Vis(Agilent Cary 5000). The free and encapsulated Hes were separated via the ultrafiltration centrifugation method: the sIVs-LPs@Hes suspension was added into an ultrafiltration centrifuge tube with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 10 kDa, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The filtrate was collected to quantify the content of free Hes. A 50% (v/v) ethanol aqueous solution was added to the upper retentate to fully lyse the nanoparticles and release the encapsulated drug. After centrifugation, the supernatant was harvested to measure the total Hes content. The absorbance was detected at the wavelength of 285 nm, and the drug concentration was calculated by substituting the absorbance values into the pre-established standard curve. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading capacity (DLC) were calculated according to the following formulas:Wencap=Wtotal−WfreeEE(%)=WencapWtotal×100%DLC(%)=WencapWNPs×100%Where:Wencap is the mass of encapsulated Hes, Wtotal is the total mass of Hes added, Wfree is the mass of free drug, and WNPs​ is the total mass of the nanoformulation.

The in vitro drug release profile was determined via a dialysis bag method with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 10 kDa. Briefly, 2 mL of sIVs-LPs@Hes suspension was sealed in a dialysis bag, which was then fully immersed in 50 mL of PBS. The system was maintained at 37 °C with constant stirring at 500 rpm to ensure sink conditions throughout the experiment. At predetermined time points (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24, 36 and 48 h), 1 mL of the external release medium was withdrawn, and an equal volume of pre-warmed fresh corresponding release medium was immediately supplemented. The collected samples were filtered, followed by absorbance measurement at 285 nm to calculate the cumulative release percentage of Hes at each time point.

In Vivo Biodistribution and pulmonary retention behavior of liposomes

Mice with successfully established ALI models were randomly divided into four groups (n = 3): the Free-DIR group, the LPs group,the sIVs-LPs group and the sIVs-LPs@Hes group. LPs, sIVs-LPs and sIVs-LPs@Hes were fluorescently labeled with DIR dye. Mice in each group received nebulized inhalation of different therapeutic formulations (1 mL total volume) via a Lung Fluid Quantitative Nebulizer (Yuyan Bio, Shanghai, China) to ensure uniform pulmonary distribution. The fluorescent distribution of labeled nanoparticles was analyzed using an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) Lumina Series III (PerkinElmer, USA) at an excitation wavelength (Ex) of 780 nm and an emission wavelength (Em) of 748 nm. After in vivo imaging, the major organs of mice were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence detection.

CCK-8 assay

The cytotoxicity of hesperetin was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. RAW 264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and incubated for 24 h in complete medium. The cells were then treated with various gradient concentrations of hesperetin for an additional 24 h. A CCK-8 working solution was prepared by mixing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent with 90 μL of fresh DMEM (100 μL total volume per well). The cells were incubated with the CCK-8 working solution for 1 h, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

In vitro cellular uptake

RAW 264.7 cells were co-incubated with PKH26-labeled LPs@Hes or sIVs-LPs@Hes for 4 h, then fixed and stained with Hoechst 33,342 dye. The cellular uptake efficiency of the liposomal formulations was determined using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (CLSM, Carl Zeiss NOL-LSM 710 instrument.).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using an RNA Extraction Kit (Beyotime, China), and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with a cDNA Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa, Japan) following the manufacturers' instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the Perfecta SYBR Green PCR Kit (TaKaRa, Japan) on a real-time PCR system. The mRNA expression levels of target genes were normalized to the housekeeping gene β-actin, and the relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. The complete sequences of the qRT-PCR primers are provided in Table S1 (Supplementary Materials).

Western blot assay

Total protein was extracted from cells and lung tissues using RIPA Lysis Buffer (G2002-30 ML, Servicebio, China) supplemented with a Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (G2007-1 ML, Servicebio, China) and PMSF (G2008-1 ML, Servicebio, China). The protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (G2026-1000T, Servicebio, China). Equal amounts of total protein were separated by 10–15% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at RT, then incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by three washes with TBST (Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20). The membranes were subsequently incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT. Finally, the protein bands were visualized using an ECL Chemiluminescence Kit, and the band intensity was quantified by densitometry.

Flow cytometry

RAW 264.7 cells were treated with LPS plus IFN-γ and further incubated with hesperetin. The cells were harvested, washed with cold PBS, and incubated with anti-CD86 (PE/Cy7) antibody (A28598, ABclonal, China) for 0.5 h at 4 °C in the dark. After three washes with PBS, the stained cells were analyzed using a FACS Canto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). The acquired data were processed and analyzed using FlowJo software (Version 10.0).

LPS-induced ALI mouse model

Thirty male specific pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of China Three Gorges University (Yichang, Hubei, China) and acclimatized for one week under standard laboratory conditions. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hubei University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. HUCMS69900019) and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0.

Mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6 per group): the control group, the LPS induced model group (treated with PBS), the sIVs-LPs group, the sIVs-LPs@Hes group, and the free Hes group. According to a similar method, Hes (8 μM),sIVs-LPs@Hes (8 μM), or sIVs-LPs(equal volume to sIVs-LPs@Hes) were separately administered to mice by intratracheal nebulization for 20 min using a lung fluid quantitative nebulizer (Yuyan Bio, Shanghai, China) [[19], [20], [21]].

Acute lung injury (ALI) was induced in all experimental groups except the control group by intratracheal instillation of 50 μL LPS (5 mg/kg body weight), while the control group received an equal volume of normal saline instead. Nebulization treatment was performed at 2 h after LPS challenge. All mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation at 24 h after modeling. Lung tissues and peripheral blood samples were immediately collected for subsequent experiments.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and immunofluorescence (IF) assay

Harvested lung tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, then dehydrated, cleared, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm-thick serial sections. The sections were stained with H&E staining, and the pathological changes of lung tissues were observed and evaluated under an Olympus BX51 light microscope.

For IHC staining, Briefly, paraffin sections were first subjected to dewaxing, rehydration, and antigen retrieval. After cooling to room temperature, the sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h to reduce non-specific binding. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted, then observed and imaged under an Olympus BX53 brightfield microscope (Olympus, Japan).

For IF assay, After dewaxing, rehydration and antigen retrieval, the paraffin sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and then blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. The sections were incubated with corresponding primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (A0423, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (A1143-100 μL, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 45 min at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, G1407-25 ML, Servicebio, China), and fluorescence images were finally acquired using a CLSM.

Quantitative analysis of glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activity

The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) (an indicator of glycolysis) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) (an indicator of OXPHOS) were measured using a Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). RAW 264.7 cells were seeded into XF24 cell culture microplates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and treated with Hes, LPS plus IFN-γ for 6 h. For glycolysis analysis, glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (0.5 μM), and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, 100 mM) were sequentially injected into the microplates to achieve the final concentrations, and ECAR was recorded in real time. For OXPHOS analysis, oligomycin (0.5 μM), carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP, 1 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (5 μM) were automatically injected, and OCR was detected following the manufacturer's instructions of Seahorse Bioscience.

Lactate production, LDH activity and glucose uptake assays

The levels of lactate production, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity and glucose uptake in cell supernatants or lysates were measured using a Lactate Assay Kit (S0208S, Beyotime, China), an LDH Assay Kit (P0393S, Beyotime, China) and a Glucose Uptake Assay Kit (S0554S, Beyotime, China), respectively, in strict accordance with the respective manufacturers' protocols.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA). The sample size (n) for each group is indicated in the corresponding Figure legends, and all experiments were performed with independent biological replicates. Prior to statistical analysis, the normality test and homogeneity of variance test were performed on all data. An unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) followed by a post-hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Hes promotes macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype

To clarify the critical role of macrophages in acute lung injury (ALI), we first analyzed the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the public database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE207651). The two groups were the 24-h ALI model induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) mediated sepsis and the sham operation group, respectively. A total of 9 cell clusters with putative biological identities were identified based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 1A). To further investigate the heterogeneity and functional characteristics of mononuclear phagocytes, unsupervised clustering analysis was performed on the mononuclear phagocyte population. According to canonical marker expression, 6 subclusters emerged within the mononuclear phagocyte lineage, namely: Macrophages; M1 Macrophages; M2 Macrophages; Conventional type 1 dendritic cells; Conventional type 2 dendritic cells Classical monocytes (Fig. 1B). Further analysis of the expression levels of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in macrophages revealed that pro-inflammatory genes, including Saa3, Lcn2, Ifitm1, Cd38, Sdc4, Pla2g7, Il1b, Il1rn and Cxcl3, were significantly upregulated. These genes directly promote the secretion of inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, increased expression of chemotactic and migratory molecules (Cxcl13, CD11b) indicates inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. S1A). Collectively, these results demonstrate that macrophages exhibit a strong pro-inflammatory phenotype in the acute lung injury (ALI) microenvironment. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the macrophage clusters. In addition, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that the TNF-α and IL-17 signaling pathways were significantly enriched (Fig. S1B–C). In addition, the proportion of M1-type macrophages in the ALI group was significantly higher than that in the Sham group, while the proportion of M2 type macrophages showed the opposite trend. These results indicated that macrophages were significantly polarized towards the M1 phenotype during ALI, suggesting that regulating macrophage polarization is a critical node in ALI treatment (Fig. 1C).

To verify the ability of Hes to regulate macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, we first evaluated the effect of Hes on the proliferative capacity of RAW264.7 cells using the CCK-8 assay. The results showed that Hes at concentrations below 50 μM had no inhibitory effect on the proliferation of RAW264.7 cells, whereas concentrations above 100 μM inhibited cell proliferation. (Fig. 1E). Therefore, 25 μM and 50 μM of Hes were selected for subsequent experiments. LPS combined with IFN-γ was used to induce M1 polarization of macrophages, followed by Hes treatment. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to detect mRNA expression levels. The results showed that M1-related genes (iNOS, TNF-α, and CD86) were significantly upregulated after LPS&IFN-γ treatment, while M2-related genes (CD206, Ym1, and IL-10) were significantly downregulated. However, Hes treatment reversed this phenomenon (Fig. 1F–G). Flow cytometry analysis showed that the number of CD86+ cells was significantly increased after LPS&IFN-γ treatment, and Hes reduced the number of CD86+ macrophages in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1H–I). Immunofluorescence (IF) assay confirmed the same trend, showing that Hes attenuated the LPS&IFN-γ-induced upregulation of CD86(M1 macrophage marker protein) and downregulation of CD206(M2 macrophage marker protein) (Fig. 1J–K). Further phalloidin staining of the cytoskeleton revealed that cells in the LPS group exhibited a round and flattened morphology, whereas Hes treatment induced a spindle-shaped cell morphology (Fig. S2A). Western blot assay was used to evaluate the expression levels of inflammation-related proteins. The results demonstrated that Hes promoted the expression of anti-inflammatory proteins (IL-10, Arg-1) and reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory proteins (IL-1β, iNOS) (Fig. 1L–M). Collectively, these findings indicated that Hes could promote macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, and the high dose (50 μM) exerted a more potent effect than the low dose (25 μM).

Hes inhibits glycolysis via the MCU-mCa2+ pathway

Metabolic reprogramming is crucial for macrophage polarization. Glycolysis in macrophages is significantly enhanced in ALI: specifically, M1 macrophages exhibit increased glycolysis and a truncated tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, while M2 macrophages display metabolic features including oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), fatty acid oxidation (FAO), and reduced glycolysis [5,22]. First, we examined the expression levels of glycolysis-related genes in macrophages using scRNA-seq data. The expression of genes such as HIF1A, LDHA, ALDOA, and PKM2 was significantly upregulated, indicating a marked enhancement of glycolytic activity in macrophages during ALI (Fig. 2A). We hypothesized that Hes promotes M2 macrophage polarization by regulating glycolysis. A Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer was used to measure the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR, an indicator of glycolysis) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR, an indicator of OXPHOS) in macrophages. The results showed that 50 μM Hes significantly increased OCR, as evidenced by the marked restoration of Basal Respiration, ATP production, Maximal Respiration, and Spare Respiratory Capacity (Fig. 2B–C). Meanwhile, 50 μM Hes significantly decreased ECAR (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, we detected glucose uptake efficiency in different treatment groups. The results demonstrated that LPS&IFN-γ treatment significantly enhanced glucose uptake, while Hes treatment restored it to the baseline level (Figure S3 A-B). Mitochondria, as key organelles for cellular energy metabolism, have been reported previously to play a pivotal role in glycolysis, with MCU (mitochondrial calcium uniporter) acting as a critical regulator of mitochondrial Ca2+ (mCa2+) uptake [23,24]. We conducted preliminary studies on the effect of Hes on macrophage glycolytic metabolism via regulating the MCU-mCa2+ axis. Immunofluorescence (IF) assay and Western blot analysis revealed that MCU expression was inhibited in RAW264.7 cells challenged with LPS&IFN-γ, while Hes upregulated MCU expression—with 50 μM exerting a stronger effect than 25 μM (Fig. 2E–H). Meanwhile, we assessed the mCa2+ level: the LPS&IFN-γ group showed a significantly lower mCa2+ level than the control group, and Hes treatment partially restored it, with 50 μM exhibiting a significantly more potent effect than 25 μM (Fig. 2I–J). In addition, JC-1 was used to detect mitochondrial membrane potential. The results indicated that LPS&IFN-γ significantly reduced the mitochondrial membrane potential of macrophages, while Hes treatment maintained a high mitochondrial membrane potential, providing energy support for oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. S4A–B). Mitochondrial calcium homeostasis bidirectionally regulates glycolysis by modulating mitochondrial metabolic efficiency, energy sensor activity, and the post-translational modification and transcriptional expression of glycolytic enzymes [24,25]. Therefore, glycolysis-related protein expression was further detected. The results showed that LPS&IFN-γ significantly promoted the expression of HIF-1α, LDHA, P-4E-BP1, HK2, and c-Myc, while inhibiting PGC-1α expression—effects that were reversed by Hes (Fig. 2K–L). Collectively, these results suggest that Hes may primarily act through upregulating MCU expression to promote mitochondrial calcium influx, thereby inhibiting the glycolytic process, reprogramming the metabolic phenotype of M1 macrophages, and driving their phenotypic transition towards M2 macrophages.

Immunofluorescence assay further confirmed that LPS&IFN-γ treatment induced increased p65 expression and obvious nuclear translocation, while Hes treatment decreased the fluorescence intensity of p65, which was mainly localized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3F). Fig. S5A–D showed that RU360 attenuated the Hes-induced upregulation of MCU expression and sustained mitochondrial calcium at low levels.

We first assessed the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype; immunofluorescence assay revealed that RU360 downregulated the enhanced CD206 fluorescence intensity induced by Hes treatment and concomitantly upregulated CD86 fluorescence intensity (Fig. S6C–E). Flow cytometry was used to quantify the number of CD86+ macrophages: Hes reduced the LPS&IFN-γ-induced increase in CD86+ macrophage numbers, while RU360 treatment reversed this effect and led to a rebound in CD86+ macrophage numbers (Fig. S6F–G).

Western blot analysis was performed to detect the expression level of MCU protein. The results showed that RU360 abrogated the Hes-induced upregulation of MCU, and comprehensively reversed the multiple effects of Hes, including the promotion of anti-inflammatory protein expression (Arg-1, IL-10), the inhibition of pro-inflammatory protein expression (IL-1β, iNOS), and the suppression of p-p65 (Fig. 3G–H). In addition, compared with the Hes-treated group, the expression levels of glycolysis-related proteins (mTOR, c-Myc, P-4E-BP1, HIF-1α, HK2, and LDHA) were significantly restored in the RU360 plus Hes group, while the expression of PGC-1α was decreased (Fig. 3I–J). Collectively, these results suggest that the MCU-mCa2+-NF-κB signaling pathway may serve as one of the important pathways by which Hes inhibits glycolysis and facilitates M2 macrophage polarization. Inhibition of MCU activity could weaken the regulatory effects of Hes on macrophage phenotypic polarization and cellular glycolytic metabolism.

To enhance the cellular uptake efficiency of liposomes (LPs), the present study designed and fabricated an sIVs-LPs fusogenic system loaded with Hes for the inhalational therapy of acute lung injury (ALI). CLSM images showed extensive colocalization between DiD-labeled macrophage cell membranes and DiL-labeled lipid membranes (Fig. 4C). Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of sIVs-associated proteins, including HSP70, TSG101, CLTB and CLTA, in sIVs, sIVs-LPs and sIVs-LPs@Hes (Fig. S7A). sIVs exhibit a significantly higher cellular uptake efficiency than sEVs, yet the underlying molecular mechanism remains elusive. It has been reported that the expression levels of CLTA and CLTB in sIVs are higher than those in sEVs [15]. and CLTA and CLTB have been proven to be involved in the cellular uptake of EVs [[28], [29], [30]]. First, the protein expression levels of sEVs and sIVs with the same particle number were detected. BMSCs, sEVs and sIVs all expressed typical exosome markers, including ALG-2 interacting protein X (ALIX), heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) and tumor susceptibility gene 101 (TSG101), whereas the expression level of Rab7 in sEVs and sIVs was low or even undetectable. Interestingly, the expression levels of CLTB and CLTA in sIVs were significantly higher than those in sEVs, with CLTB showing a higher expression level (Fig. 4D). To verify that CLTB is one of the key factors responsible for the high uptake efficiency of sIVs, CLTB expression in BMSCs was first knocked down via Si-CLTB transfection (Fig. 4H), and the CLTB expression level in sIVs derived from Si-CLTB-transfected BMSCs and normal BMSCs was further detected. The results showed that sIVs isolated from CLTB-knockdown BMSCs also had lower CLTB protein expression (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, fluorescent labeling was used to detect the cellular uptake of LPs, sIVs-LPs@Hes and Si-CLTB-sIVs-LPs@Hes by macrophages. The results indicated that the fusion of sIVs with LPs significantly enhanced macrophage uptake of the nanoplatform, while CLTB knockdown markedly reduced the uptake efficiency of the nanoplatform by macrophages (Fig. 4J). These findings suggested that the fusion of sIVs and LPs significantly improves the macrophage uptake efficiency of the nanoplatform, which is associated with the high abundance of CLTA and CLTB in sIVs. UV-visible spectrophotometry analysis showed that the encapsulation efficiency of sIVs-LPs@Hes was 82.18 ± 1.68% with a drug loading efficiency of 4.79 ± 0.23%. The sustained release profile revealed that the cumulative release of Hes reached approximately 60% at 48 h. These results demonstrated that sIVs-LPs can serve as an excellent nanocarrier for Hes (Fig. S7E) In addition, we repeatedly prepared sIVs-LPs@Hes. Fig. S7F–G shows that nanoparticles from different batches exhibited similar particle size distributions, indicating that the preparation method has good reproducibility.

sIVs derived from MSCs have been demonstrated to exert certain immunomodulatory effects [18,31]. The present study further evaluated the regulatory effect of sIVs-LPs@Hes on macrophage phenotypes. Immunofluorescence results showed that both Hes and sIVs-LPs promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, as evidenced by upregulated CD206 expression and downregulated CD86 expression; moreover, the effect of Hes was stronger than that of sIVs-LPs. This regulatory effect was further enhanced in the sIVs-LPs@Hes group (Figure S8 A–C). Flow cytometric analysis yielded consistent results: both Hes and sIVs-LPs reduced the number of CD86+ macrophages, and sIVs-LPs@Hes exhibited an even more pronounced effect (Figure S8 D–E).

Western blot was performed to verify whether sIVs-LPs@Hes promotes M2 polarization of macrophages via the MCU-mCa2+-NF-κB signaling axis. As shown in Fig. S9A, LPS&IFN-γ treatment significantly downregulated the expression of IL-10 and Arg-1, while markedly upregulated iNOS expression in macrophages. This effect was reversed by Hes or sIVs-LPs treatment, with Hes exerting a stronger regulatory effect than sIVs-LPs, whereas sIVs-LPs@Hes exhibited the most potent immunomodulatory activity, which was consistent with the results of immunofluorescence and flow cytometry assays. Both Hes and sIVs-LPs@Hes reversed the downregulation of MCU expression induced by LPS&IFN-γ treatment. Furthermore, the phosphorylation level of p65 was decreased in the Hes and sIVs-LPs@Hes groups, confirming that sIVs-LPs@Hes can modulate the MCU-NF-κB signaling pathway. However, sIVs-LPs failed to upregulate MCU expression that was downregulated by LPS&IFN-γ treatment, and only slightly reduced p65 phosphorylation, suggesting that the immunomodulatory effect of sIVs-LPs may be mediated through other pathways. Further evaluation of glycolysis levels showed that Hes, sIVs-LPs and sIVs-LPs@Hes all reduced the expression of HIF-1α, HK-2 and LDHA to a certain extent, and the effects of Hes and sIVs-LPs@Hes were significantly stronger than those of sIVs-LPs. These results indicate that sIVs-LPs@Hes can deliver Hes, inhibit glycolysis via the MCU-mCa2+-NF-κB signaling pathway, and improve the water solubility and anti-inflammatory efficacy of Hes.

Next, we further compared sIVs-LPs@Hes with interleukin-4 (IL-4), a classic macrophage M2 polarization inducer, to demonstrate the advantages of sIVs-LPs@Hes. Western blot results showed that both sIVs-LPs@Hes and IL-4 reduced the expression levels of iNOS and CD86, with the effect of the IL-4 group only slightly stronger than that of the sIVs-LPs@Hes group. In addition, the expression levels of Arg-1, CD206 and IL-10 were significantly increased after sIVs-LPs@Hes or IL-4 treatment, with no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. S10B). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was further performed to detect the protein levels of TNF-α and IL-10 in the macrophage supernatant. Both sIVs-LPs@Hes and IL-4 significantly inhibited TNF-α secretion and upregulated IL-10 level, indicating that both groups had potent anti-inflammatory activity, while no significant difference was observed between the sIVs-LPs@Hes and IL-4 groups (Fig. S10C). These results demonstrate that sIVs-LPs@Hes has a strong anti-inflammatory capacity comparable to that of IL-4, with higher stability and more convenient administration, suggesting that sIVs-LPs@Hes has favorable potential for clinical translation.

Collectively, these results confirmed the successful fabrication of the sIVs-LPs@Hes nanosystem. The integration of sIVs not only improves the uptake efficiency of the nanocarrier by macrophages through CLTA and CLTB, but also combines the immunomodulatory ability of sIVs to regulate macrophage polarization and anti-inflammatory responses, thereby enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of free Hes.

Discussion and conclusion

Therapeutic strategies for acute lung injury (ALI) based on natural products have been extensively investigated. For example, wampee leaf extract (active component: Balasubramide), marigold active ingredient (Wedelolactone), and Cornus officinalis have been confirmed to exert therapeutic effects on ALI [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Hes has been shown to attenuate ventilator-induced acute lung injury by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway, and also exerts a preventive effect against acute pneumonia [12,35]. The present study further verified that Hes exerts a therapeutic effect on acute lung injury by promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype via MCU-mCa2+-NF-κB-mediated metabolic reprogramming, and proposes a novel delivery strategy.

Drug delivery efficiency in lung tissue is crucial for the treatment of ALI. The delivery efficiency of liposomes is governed by multiple factors, including surface charge, particle size, interaction with pulmonary surfactants, responsiveness to the microenvironment, and surface modifications. For instance, negatively charged particles tend to be internalized by alveolar macrophages, whereas positively charged particles are readily taken up by neutrophils [20,36]. In terms of surface activity, pulmonary surfactants can enhance the wettability, solubility, and dissolution rate of poorly soluble particles. When lipophilic small molecules deposit in alveolar lining fluid (LLF), they may first interact with phospholipids (PS), which promotes drug dissolution and absorption and shortens their residence time in the respiratory tract. Moreover, given the enhanced pulmonary permeability, passive targeted delivery can be achieved using nanocarriers responsive to the microenvironment (ROS, pH, and inflammation). In contrast, surface modifications (e.g., antibody conjugation) enable active targeting of drugs to specific lung cells [[37], [38], [39]]. Regarding particle size, particles ≤100 nm exhibit enhanced ability to penetrate the epithelial barrier, followed by redistribution in the lungs or accumulation in secondary organs. Notably, non-cationic nanoparticles ≤34 nm have been shown to rapidly translocate from the lungs to mediastinal lymph nodes [40,41]. Liposomes are the only novel carrier-based drug delivery system that has successfully advanced to clinical trials for inhalation applications. Their properties can be flexibly tuned through various techniques, such as ultrasonication, extrusion, homogenization, and microfluidic methods [42]. Therefore, optimizing the particle size of sIVs-LPs@Hes is essential for improving its therapeutic efficacy against ALI in future research.

In addition, exosome-based nebulized inhalation therapy has been studied in ALI, pulmonary fibrosis, and radiation-induced lung injury. For instance, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs (hUCMSC-EVs) ameliorated bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice, increasing the survival rate from 20% to 80% and improving lung function. Furthermore, subsequent clinical trials showed significant clinical regression of pulmonary fibrosis after hUCMSC-EVs treatment [43]. However, there are variations in the doses of EVs used in different studies, and EVs exhibit large differences in yield and stability, which are limiting factors for MSCs-EVs-based therapy.

Recent studies have shown that sIVs are smaller than small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), with simpler isolation methods, higher yield, unique molecular structure, and more efficient drug delivery [15]. Furthermore, sIVs exert intrinsic anti-inflammatory activity. In diabetic wounds, sIVs regulate macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype by inhibiting the cGAS-STING signaling pathway and suppressing the hyperactivation of NF-κB18. Given that drug absorption efficiency and anti-inflammatory capacity are critical for the treatment of acute lung injury (ALI), we incorporated sIVs as a functional composite component of the nanoplatform to construct the sIVs-LPs@Hes nanodelivery system. In addition, the present study preliminarily confirmed that sIVs exert synergistic anti-inflammatory activity with Hes, and the incorporation of sIVs facilitates the rapid uptake of nanoparticles by lung tissues, thereby potentiating the therapeutic effect.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study remain. For instance, the in vivo effective dose range of sIVs-LPs@Hes has not yet been systematically investigated; additionally, whether there are common underlying mechanisms for the potential synergistic therapeutic effect between sIVs and Hes, and whether this formulation can achieve long-term improvement of lung function, are all key issues limiting the subsequent clinical translation of sIVs-LPs@Hes.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that sIVs-LPs@Hes, as a promising nanodelivery platform for ALI treatment, exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects, reduces pulmonary edema, and improves lung pathological manifestations after nebulized inhalation, holding great potential for translational application.
