The Impact of Metal–Support Interaction on the Structure and Activity of Carbon-Supported Ni Nanoparticle Catalysts for Alkaline Hydrogen Oxidation Reaction
Authors: Yoshiki Akamine, Fumiya Nishino, Takashi Yamashita, Masayuki Tsushida, Keisuke Awaya, Masato Machida, Syeda Mushrifa Zahan, Dario R Dekel, Junya Ohyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effect of the carbon support—Ketjenblack (CKB) and Vulcan (CV)—on the Ni/C catalyst properties toward alkaline hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) was investigated. Both Ni/CKB and Ni/CV presented improved catalytic activity with an increase in Ni particle size; however, Ni/CKB showed activity significantly higher than that of Ni/CV at the same particle size. The reason for the difference in the HOR activity due to the types of carbon support was revealed to be the difference in the 3D structure of Ni nanoparticles determined by metal–support interaction, which is changed by the surface structures of carbon supports. These findings bring light to the critical importance of the metal–support interaction in the development of the HOR catalysts.Keywords: hydrogen oxidation reaction, anion exchange membrane fuel cell, platinum group metal free, nickel, metal−support interaction

Introduction

As the cell performance of anion exchange membrane fuel cells (AEMFCs) is getting higher and approaches levels of practical applications, the development of catalysts for AEMFCs has become increasingly important.1−3 In AEMFCs, various metals other than platinum group metals (PGMs) can be used due to the low-corrosion environment within the electrodes. Taking advantage of this benefit, PGM-free catalysts for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) on the cathode have been widely studied so far, and numerous PGM-free catalysts with ORR activity that surpass that of Pt catalysts have already been discovered.4−8 However, the challenge lies in the anode catalyst.9−16 The rate of the hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) in AEMFCs is 2 orders of magnitude slower than that in the acidic counterpart of proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs), requiring a large amount of PGM catalysts at the anode to achieve practical power.17 Thus, aiming to fulfill the potential of the AEMFCs, it is necessary to develop PGM-free anode catalysts with high activity comparable to that of Pt catalysts under AEMFC conditions.

Achieving the HOR activity comparable to that of PGM catalysts with PGM-free catalysts is extremely challenging because the molecular adsorption properties of PGM-free catalysts are less suitable compared to those of PGM catalysts.18−21 In addition, the alkaline HOR has additional challenges due to the OH species that are involved in the oxidation reaction.22 Previous studies have demonstrated that there are volcano-type relationships between the HOR activity and the adsorption energy of H and OH species on catalysts, where PGMs are located near the top of the volcano plots.12,18−21 However, PGM-free catalysts exhibit too strong or too weak adsorption of H and OH species that deviate from the peak of the volcano plots, resulting in low HOR activity.23 To improve the HOR activity of PGM-free catalysts, modification of their adsorption properties by the development of active site structures is required.

Among PGM-free catalysts, Ni shows H and OH adsorption properties closest to those of PGMs, and thus, Ni-based materials have been extensively explored as the HOR catalysts.9,24,25 In the development of active site structures in Ni-based catalysts, alloying with another metal and incorporating nonmetallic elements have been investigated, demonstrating that changes in the electronic state of Ni can improve adsorption properties and HOR activity.21,26−28 Additionally, interactions with supports have been shown to be effective in controlling active site structures. For instance, the effect of different transition metal carbides on PtRu catalyst properties was studied, showing a strong impact of the catalyst–support metal interaction on the activity enhancement toward the alkaline HOR.29 Likewise, it has been reported that interactions with N/B/S-doped carbon or hexagonal BN improve the catalytic activity of Ni nanoparticles.21,30−34 However, there are still few detailed systematic studies on the effects of supports on the activity of Ni catalysts. Meanwhile, in supported metal catalysts used for thermal catalytic reactions, such as automotive exhaust purification catalysts, the effects of metal–support interaction have been investigated so far as an important factor controlling catalytic activity.35−38 The metal–support interaction has been revealed to significantly influence the active site structure on supported metal particles and, consequently, the catalytic activity. Therefore, understanding and designing the metal–support interaction will be crucial for the development of PGM-free HOR catalysts as the metal–support interaction has the potential to significantly modify their structure and therefore change the adsorption energy of H and OH species compared to PGM catalysts.

In this study, we investigate the support effect of carbon-supported Ni catalysts employing Ketjenblack EC600JD (CKB) and Vulcan XC-72R (CV) as carbon supports. Note that although these carbons are commonly used as supports of fuel cell catalysts, to our knowledge, there are no studies showing how they affect the structure, properties, and activity of supported metal nanoparticle catalysts. Ni nanoparticles of various sizes were prepared by varying the Ni loading on both carbon supports, and their HOR activity was evaluated. The comparison of the size-dependent HOR activity of Ni/CKB and Ni/CV clearly demonstrates the difference in activity due to the carbon support, i.e., the support effect. The catalyst analyses using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and atomic-scale and three-dimensional (3D) observations by Cs-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) reveal that this support effect is derived from the difference in hydrogen adsorption properties due to the support-affected structures of the Ni nanoparticles.

Results and Discussion

Ni/CKB and Ni/CV with a variety of Ni loadings from 10 to 70 wt % were prepared by an impregnation method and H2 treatment at 600 °C. The HOR activity tests were performed using a rotating disk electrode (RDE) method in a H2-saturated 0.1 M aq. NaOH solution. Figure 1a shows the linear sweep voltammograms (LSVs) for the HOR on 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 70 wt % Ni/CKB and Ni/CV (30 μgNi cm–2 on an RDE). The exchange current density on each catalyst was determined from the slope of the LSV in the micro-overvoltage region (−10 to 10 mA). The mass activity (MA) was determined by normalizing the exchange current density by the Ni weight. As shown in Figure 1b, all Ni/CKB catalysts exhibited 5–7 times higher MA than Ni/CV catalysts. To confirm the higher activity of Ni/CKB as compared to Ni/CV, AEMFC tests were performed using 50 wt % Ni/CKB and 50 wt % Ni/CV as anode catalysts (loading of 5.47 ± 0.07 mgNi cm–2) coupled with standard 40 wt % Pt/C (Thermo Scientific, 40% Pt on carbon black) as a cathode catalyst (loading of 0.65 ± 0.05 mgPt cm–2). In the AEMFC tests, a trimethylamine-functionalized low-density polyethylene membrane with a thickness of 25 μm was used as the solid electrolyte anion-exchange membrane,39 and the cell was operated at 60 and 80 °C with 500 mL min–1 of H2 and O2 flows and a backpressure of 250 kPa on the anode and cathode. The resultant polarization curves and the summary of AEMFC performance are presented in Figure S1 and Tables S1–S2. The performance of the Ni/CKB AEMFC with a peak power density of 236 and 285 mW cm–2 at 60 and 80 °C, respectively, is superior to that of the Ni/CV AEMFC with a peak power density of 120 and 160 mW cm–2 at 60 and 80 °C, respectively. Both AEMFCs were identical, so the difference in performance is clearly attributed to the difference in catalytic performance of Ni/CKB and Ni/CV, confirming the higher activity of the Ni/CKB catalyst, as was measured in the RDE tests, and therefore verifying the superiority of the CKB as a support.

Each catalyst was loaded onto a disk with 30 μgNi cm–2. (b) Comparison of the MA of Ni/CKB and Ni/CV with different loadings. Each test was performed three times, and the calculated standard deviations are shown as the error bars.

The cyclic voltammograms of all Ni/C catalysts are shown in Figure S2. The electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) of each catalyst was calculated from the redox charge using the previously reported conversion factor (see Methods) and is presented in Table S3.30,40−42 In both Ni/CKB and Ni/CV, the ECSA values decreased as the Ni loading increased. The size of the Ni nanoparticles was calculated from the ECSA assuming a spherical shape and is shown in Table S3. The size of Ni nanoparticles increased with the Ni loading in both cases of Ni/CKB and Ni/CV catalysts.

Figure 2 shows the HAADF-STEM images of the Ni/C catalysts and the size distributions of the Ni nanoparticles. The volume-based mean particle sizes are listed in Table S3. The Ni nanoparticle size was also evaluated from the ECSA assuming a spherical shape and is presented in Table S3. Both values of particle size evaluated from HAADF-STEM and ECSA became larger with the Ni loading. In addition, Ni/CKB exhibited smaller sizes than Ni/CV when compared to the same Ni loading. The catalysts were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD), which showed the formation of Ni metal with the face-centered cubic (fcc) structure (Figure S3). The Ni metal crystallite sizes evaluated from the XRD patterns are listed in Table S3. The variation in crystallite size with the Ni loadings and types of carbon support was the same as the variation in particle size. The results of STEM, ECSA, and XRD analyses presented a consistent trend in variation of Ni nanoparticle size with Ni loadings and types of carbon support. Notably, the size of Ni nanoparticles on CKB was smaller than that on CV when compared with the same Ni loading. This is attributable to the higher surface area of CKB (1403 m2 g–1) compared to that of CV (237 m2 g–1).

Typical HAADF-STEM images and particle size distributions of (a) Ni/CKB and (b) Ni/CV.

The specific activity of the catalysts was evaluated using both the ECSA and the geometric surface area, which are denoted as SAe and SAg, respectively. The geometric surface area was determined from the average particle size measured by HAADF-STEM analysis (Figure 2, Table S3). Figure 3a shows SAe and SAg as a function of the particle size measured by HAADF-STEM. Both SA values increased with the particle size in both Ni/CKB and Ni/CV. Such size-dependent SA has also been observed in Pt/C in the literature and is attributed to the variation in the fraction of coordinatively unsaturated/saturated surface atoms. Specifically, this trend suggests that the saturated surface Ni atoms or Ni atoms in plane surfaces are catalytically more active than the unsaturated surface ones or those on steps, edges, and corners of particles. It is also suggested that metallic Ni species are active, while oxidized or OH-poisoned Ni species are not because coordinatively saturated surface Ni atoms are more capable of maintaining a metallic state than coordinatively unsaturated ones.

(b) H2-TPD of 50 wt % Ni/CKB (red), 50 wt % Ni/CV (light blue), 10 wt % Ni/CV (blue), and Pt/C (black). (c) Plots of the Ni metal proportion against the Ni nanoparticle size of Ni/CKB (red circle) and Ni/CV (blue triangle). The size was measured by HAADF-STEM analysis. (d) C 1s XPS of CKB and CV. The particle sizes in panels (a) and (c) are the average particle sizes measured by HAADF-STEM analysis.

As shown in Figure 3a, it is particularly noteworthy that Ni/CKB exhibited SA values significantly higher than those of Ni/CV when compared to the same particle size. This means that Ni nanoparticles on CKB may have higher HOR activity than those on CV. To investigate the difference in the HOR activity with the carbon supports, the H2 adsorption property was evaluated. Figure 3b compares the hydrogen temperature-programmed desorption (H2-TPD) of 50 wt % Ni/CKB with 10 and 50 wt % Ni/CV. Note that 50 wt % Ni/CKB has a similar average size of Ni nanoparticles as 10 wt % Ni/CV (Table S3). The H2-TPD of 50 wt % Pt/C is also presented as a reference of the HOR active catalyst. Ni/CKB exhibited H2 desorption from 60 °C, whereas Pt/C also showed H2 desorption. In contrast, 10 and 50 wt % Ni/CV needed a temperature above 450 °C to exhibit the H2 desorption signal. This means that Ni/CKB has a lower hydrogen binding energy (HBE) than Ni/CV. Previous studies have suggested that lowering the HBE of Ni can improve catalytic activity for alkaline HOR.18,19 Thus, the lower HBE of Ni/CKB is responsible for its higher HOR activity compared to that of Ni/CV.

In order to investigate the origin of the different H2 adsorption and HOR activities with carbon supports, the surface chemical states were analyzed using XPS. Ni 2p XPS of Ni/CKB and Ni/CV are shown in Figure S4. All the spectra exhibited Ni 2p3/2 peaks due to Ni metal, NiO, and Ni(OH)2 at 853.2, 854.6, and 856.4 eV and 2p1/2 peaks due to Ni metal, NiO, and Ni(OH)2 at 870.5, 871.9, and 873.7 eV, respectively.43 Based on the area of these peaks, the proportion of Ni metal was determined. Figure 3c plots the Ni metal proportions against the particle size. For both Ni/CKB and Ni/CV, the Ni metal proportion increases with the particle size. In addition, for the same particle size, the Ni metal proportion of Ni/CKB is higher than that of Ni/CV. These trends are consistent with those observed in the HOR activity (Figure 3a). Therefore, metallic Ni sites are active for the HOR. More importantly, the carbon supports can change the electronic state of the supported Ni nanoparticles.

The surface states of the two carbon supports were investigated using C 1s XPS as shown in Figure 3d. The main peak of both carbon supports at 285.0 eV is assignable to C=C (∼284.6 eV) and C–C (∼285.1 eV).44 More importantly, the C 1s XPS spectra of CKB exhibited smaller intensity due to C–O–C/C–OH groups around 286 eV.44 In addition, CKB exhibited a larger peak due to graphite π–π* shakeup at 291 eV compared to CV.44 On the other hand, CV presented a peak due to the O–C=O group at 288.5 eV. It should also be noted that the same differences were observed between Ni/CKB and Ni/CV (Figure S5). These results indicate that CKB has a more graphitic surface than CV. This result was also supported by the Raman spectra (Figure S6).45 Thus, the Ni nanoparticles in Ni/CKB interact with the graphene carbon surface, while those in Ni/CV can interact with the C–O–C/C–OH and O–C=O groups. This different metal–support interaction would be the cause of the variation in the electronic state of Ni.46 More specifically, the interaction with graphitic carbon keeps Ni in a metallic state, which is responsible for the catalysis in the HOR.

The Ni nanoparticles in Ni/CKB and Ni/CV were observed on an atomic scale using Cs-corrected HAADF-STEM and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Figures 4 and S7). The Ni nanoparticles in Ni/CKB exhibited angular shapes of polycrystalline consisting of small Ni metal crystallites, exposing numerous facets. On the other hand, the Ni nanoparticles in Ni/CV showed rounded shapes, and the lattice fringes of Ni metal were indistinctly observed, particularly near the surface. In addition, EDS analysis revealed that oxygen is located at the Ni nanoparticle surface of Ni/CV. In contrast, Ni/CKB does not show such a concentrated oxygen region on the surface or inside the Ni nanoparticle. The result agrees well with that of Ni 2p XPS analysis (Figure S4). Therefore, Ni/CKB can maintain a metallic Ni surface, whereas Ni/CV cannot. This difference can be accounted for by the Ni nanoparticle structures determined by the metal–support interaction.

HAADF-STEM images and corresponding EDS maps (Ni + O) of a representative Ni nanoparticle in (a) 50 wt % Ni/CKB and (b) 10 wt % Ni/CV.

The three-dimensional structures of Ni/CKB and Ni/CV were visualized to confirm the particle shape and to investigate how the Ni nanoparticles interact with carbon supports (Figures S8 and S9).47−50 Movies showing the 3D structures of Ni/CKB and Ni/CV are presented in the SI. Figure 5 shows the images captured from selected angles. As seen in Figure 5a, the Ni nanoparticles in Ni/CKB present angular shapes. On the other hand, the Ni nanoparticles in Ni/CV are more rounded as shown in Figure 5b. The angular shape with planar surfaces is less reactive than the rounded shape with stepped surfaces or coordinatively unsaturated surface atoms. Thus, the faceted Ni nanoparticles of Ni/CKB retain a more metallic surface compared to the rounded ones of Ni/CV. In addition, it was found that the shape of Ni nanoparticles on CKB was flatter to have more contact with the carbon support compared to Ni/CV. This finding suggests that Ni interacts with graphitic carbon more strongly than with C–O–C/C–OH and O–C=O groups. It is concluded that the interaction between Ni and graphitic carbon leads to the formation of a faceted Ni surface maintaining metallic Ni species, which adsorbs H2 weakly as Pt/C and catalyzes the HOR. This conclusion highlights the importance of metal–support interaction for Ni-based HOR catalysts.

Three-dimensional structural analysis of Ni nanoparticles in (a) 50 wt % Ni/CKB and (b) 10 wt % Ni/CV.

Conclusions

The support effects of Ni/C catalysts on the HOR activity were investigated using 10–70 wt % Ni/CKB and Ni/CV. Both Ni/C catalysts showed a consistent effect of Ni nanoparticle size on the HOR activity; however, Ni/CKB exhibited much larger MA and SA values than Ni/CV when compared with the same particle size. The higher activity of Ni/CKB was accounted for by its ability for weak H2 adsorption on the metallic surface of angular-shaped Ni nanoparticles formed through interaction between Ni and the graphitic carbon surface of CKB. On the other hand, the lower activity of Ni/CV was explained by its less ability for weak H2 adsorption derived from the oxidized surface of round-shaped Ni nanoparticles formed through interaction between Ni and the carbon surface with more C–O–C/C–OH and O–C=O groups of CV. The results clearly demonstrate that the metal–support interaction is a significant factor controlling the particle structure and electronic state, the adsorption property, and ultimately the HOR activity of Ni nanoparticles. We believe that the findings in this study will initiate the study and design of metal–support interactions for the development of PGM-free catalysts for alkaline HOR.

Methods

Catalyst Preparation

The 10–70 wt % Ni/C catalysts were prepared using a wet impregnation method. The two kinds of carbon supports, CKB and CV, were impregnated in aqueous solutions of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp.) at 95 °C. After drying overnight at 110 °C, the samples were treated under a 5% H2/N2 flow of 200 mL min–1 at 600 °C for 2 h.

Electrochemical Measurement

All electrochemical measurements in this study were performed on an HZ-7000 potentiostat (MEIDEN HOKUTO Corp.). A catalyst ink was prepared by mixing catalyst powder (58.9, 29.5, 19.6, 14.7, 11.8, and 8.4 mg for 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 70 wt % Ni/C, respectively) with 2.5 mL of ultrapure water, 2.5 mL of isopropanol, and 10 μL of 5% Nafion dispersion (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp.) by sonication for 1 h. The catalyst ink was dropped onto a glassy carbon electrode (Φ 5 mm) to be 30 μgmetal cm–2.

The HOR activity was evaluated by linear sweep voltammetry in a H2-saturated 0.1 M NaOH aqueous solution at 30 °C. The potential was scanned from −0.05 to 0.3 V vs a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) at a rate of 10 mV s–1. The rotational speed of the disk electrode was set at 2500 rpm. The exchange current density was determined from the slope of the current density between −10 and 10 mV using the approximated Butler–Volmer equation in a low-overpotential region: J = J0 (nFη/RT), where J is the measured current density (mA cmdisk–2), J0 is the exchange current density (mA cmdisk–2), n is the number of reaction electrons (2), F is the Faraday constant (C mol–1), R is the gas constant (J K–1 mol–1), T is the measurement temperature (K), and η is the overpotential (V). The MA was defined as the exchange current density divided by the amount of Ni used.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were performed at −0.15 to 0.5 V vs RHE. The ECSAs of the Ni/C catalysts were evaluated from the charge due to the reduction of Ni(OH)2 to Ni according to the method reported elsewhere.30,40−42 The reduction charge was evaluated from the cathodic current during the backward scan subtracted by that during the forward scan and then divided by the reported charge density for monolayer adsorption of OH on Ni (514 μC cmNi–2) and the Ni weight loaded on the disk electrode to determine the ECSA.30,40−42

In Operando AEMFC Tests

An anode ink was prepared by grounding and sonicating a mixture of 256 mg of Ni/C catalyst powder with a 1:12 mixture of deionized water and isopropyl alcohol. To the resulting slurry was added 1.2 mL of PiperION anion exchange ionomer with an ionomer:catalyst ratio of 1:4, and then the mixture was sonicated for 1 h. A cathode ink was prepared by grounding and sonicating a mixture of 90 mg of Pt/C with a 1:12 mixture of deionized water and isopropyl alcohol on ice water. To the slurry obtained, 271 μL of PiperION anion exchange ionomer was added with an ionomer:catalyst ratio of 1:4. The anode and cathode inks were sprayed onto Toray carbon paper 060 with 20% and 5% PTFE wet-proofing gas diffusion layers (GDLs), respectively, with an area of 2.5 cm2 using an ultrasonic spraying robot. The loading of the anode electrodes was 5.47 ± 0.07 mgNi/cm2 and that of the cathode electrodes was 0.65 ± 0.05 mgPt/cm2.

The electrodes were soaked in an aqueous 2 M KOH aq. solution for 12 h, and the solution was changed every 20 min in the last hour. A trimethylamine-functionalized low-density polyethylene membrane with 25 μm of thickness was soaked for 1 h in 2 M KOH while changing the solution every 20 min to convert ionomers to their hydroxide form. The membrane electrode assemblies were sandwiched between Teflon gaskets and a pair of two single-serpentine graphite bipolar flow fields, and the fuel cell was bolted with a torque of 4.5 N m. The AEMFC tests were performed by using a Scribner 850e test station. The cell was heated to 60 and 80 °C while flowing 500 mL min–1 of H2 to the anode and 500 mL min–1 of O2 to the cathode, both with a backpressure of 250 kPa. During the fuel cell tests, the cathode and anode relative humidity levels were adjusted for optimum performance.

Characterization

The XRD measurements were performed on a Rigaku Multiflex instrument where Cu Kα radiation (1.540 Å) was used (40 kV, 40 mA). The XPS measurements were conducted using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha X-ray photoelectron spectrometer where monochromatized Al Kα radiation (accelerating voltage: 12 kV, beam current: 6 mA, spot size: 400 μm) was used. The energy of the XPS spectra was corrected using the C 1s XPS peak energy set at 285.0 eV. The specific surface areas of CKB and CV were evaluated by N2 adsorption on a BELSORP-mini II using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) equation. The Raman spectra were taken using a green laser (532 nm) on a JASCO NRS-3100.

The high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) STEM observations for the particle size analysis of Ni/C catalysts were performed using an FEI TECNAI F20 at 200 kV of accelerating voltage. The atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM images were taken using a JEM-ARM200F instrument (JEOL Ltd.). The HAADF-STEM observation sample was prepared by dropping an ethanol suspension of catalyst powder onto a Cu grid with a carbon film (Quantifoil, EM Japan Co., Ltd.) and then drying.

The tilt series of HAADF-STEM images were obtained using a JEM-ARM200F (JEOL Ltd.). A Cu grid with a carbon film was treated by Au sputtering using an E-1010 ion sputter coater (Hitachi High-Tech Corp.) to disperse Au nanoparticles with approximately 2 nm diameter on the carbon film as the makers to determine the tilt angle. An ethanol suspension of catalyst powder was dropped onto a Au-sputtered grid (Figure S10). The tilt series were obtained from a tilting angle of −50° to 60° or −64° to 68° at 2° increments. The HAADF-STEM images before and after the tilt series observations were taken to confirm no significant change during the observations (Figures S11 and S12). 3D reconstruction from the tilt series was performed using the iterative series reduction (ISER) algorithm, which adapts a compressed sensing method, on TEMography software.47

The H2-TPD experiments were conducted using a BELCAT II instrument equipped with a BELMASS quadrupole mass spectrometer (MicrotracBEL). 50 mg of a Ni/C catalyst was placed in a quartz tube, heated at a rate of 10 °C min–1 under an Ar flow of 50 mL min–1, and then held at 600 °C for 1 h under a 10% H2/Ar flow of 50 mL min–1. The sample was then cooled to 50 °C at a rate of 10 °C min–1 under a 10% H2/Ar flow of 50 mL min–1 and then held for 2 h under a H2 flow of 50 mL min–1, followed by an additional 2 h under an Ar flow of 50 mL min–1. Subsequently, the catalysts were heated from 50 to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C min–1 while the effluent gas was analyzed using the mass spectrometer.
