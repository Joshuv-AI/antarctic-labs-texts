PFKFB3 inhibition dictates the adaptive responses and cell fates in tumor cells
Authors: Dianpeng Zheng, Yaowen Mao, Zhipeng Ai, Yue Song, Xiaoyu Liu, Ruoqing Xu, Chenyang Ma, Weikang Meng, Feng He, Hongqing Liang, Jun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary6-Phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3), a key regulator of glycolysis highly expressed in many tumor cells, has a critical role in promoting glycolysis and tumor progression. Consequently, anti-cancer strategies have sought to inhibit PFKFB3 to exploit this metabolic dependency of tumor cells. In this study, we employed a controlled protein degradation system to manipulate the kinetics of PFKFB3 depletion in investigating its impact on tumor cell growth. We show that, while slow depletion allows metabolic adaptation with a minimal effect on proliferation, rapid depletion or acute inhibition suppresses cell growth despite a similar glycolytic reduction. We show that rapid PFKFB3 loss activates AMPK, which perturbs cell cycle progression through the MAPK/ERK pathway. Importantly, AMPK inhibition was sufficient to rescue the adaptation failure caused by rapid PFKFB3 depletion. Together, our findings document that dynamic targeting of PFKFB3 can drive heterogeneous cancer cell fates, shedding light on cells’ potential path toward developing resistance to anti-PFKFB3 treatments.

Introduction

Glycolysis is a key metabolic pathway for ATP generation to meet cellular energy demands, particularly in cancer cells. Unlike normal cells, most cancer cells preferentially utilize aerobic glycolysis even under normoxic conditions, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect.1 The enzyme 6-phosphofructo-1-kinase (PFK-1) catalyzes the first committed and irreversible step of glycolysis, converting fructose-6-phosphate (F6P) to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (F1,6BP). As a master regulatory hub, PFK-1 integrates multiple metabolic signals, including allosteric regulation by ATP and AMP in sensing the energy status. PFK-1 is also subject to activation by its potent agonist fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP), whose level is controlled by enzymes in the phosphofructokinase-2/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase (PFKFB) family. These are bifunctional enzymes possessing both kinase and phosphatase domains, thus both catalyzing the synthesis of F2,6BP from F6P and reverting it back to F6P. By antagonizing ATP-mediated inhibition of PFK-1, F2,6BP bestows upon PFKFB proteins the potential of an enormous power in regulating the entire glycolytic flux. The human genome encodes four PFKFB isoenzymes. Unlike other members in the family that have well-balanced kinase and phosphatase activities, PFKFB3 uniquely has a kinase activity that far exceeds its phosphatase activity (with an activity ratio of 710).2 This particular property of PFKFB3 allows it to function almost exclusively in F2,6BP production, thereby acting as a critical regulator in promoting glycolysis flux.

A hallmark of cancer cells is the preferential reliance on the glycolytic pathway and, given its central role in regulating glycolysis, PFKFB3 is generally overexpressed in tumor cells or tissues.3 Recent studies have shown that PFKFB3 expression during mitosis provides an energetic boost that facilitates mitotic progression.4 Inhibition of PFKFB3 by PFK-015, a specific inhibitor that binds directly to PFKFB3 and potently inhibits its kinase activity, induces G1/S cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis.5 These findings suggest a functional coupling between PFKFB3 expression cell cycle progression, where a PFKFB3 depletion is expected to cause cell cycle defects. PFKFB3 also mediates cell survival under stress conditions. For instance, upon DNA damage induced by cisplatin treatment, PFKFB3 translocates from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, where it enhances glycolysis to support cell survival.6 In hypoxic conditions, HIF-α upregulates PFKFB3 transcription to further promote glycolytic flux.7 Given its critical role in driving glycolysis, PFKFB3 has been considered as an important therapeutic target in cancer.3,8 However, cancer cells can undergo metabolic reprogramming to adapt to a low-glycolysis state upon anti-metabolic therapeutic treatments, often leading to tumor recurrence in clinical trials.9,10,11 This suggests that metabolic inhibition may give rise to distinct cell fate outcomes particularly at the resolution of single cells. To date, whether and how PFKFB3 loss may elicit distinct dynamic cellular responses at the single cell level remains an open question.

To investigate the cell fate outcomes of PFKFB3 loss, we established an inducible degradation system for this protein in HeLa cells. Using time-lapse imaging, we found that differences in PFKFB3 depletion kinetics are responsible for distinct cellular responses. While slow depletion allowed cells to successfully adapt to a low-glycolysis state without compromising the cell proliferation state, rapid depletion or acute pharmacological inhibition of its kinase activity led to cell cycle defects. Furthermore, rapid PFKFB3 depletion at an early time could ultimately lead to severe proliferative defects that could not be rescued by a subsequent re-introduction of the protein into the system. Mechanistically, we found that, under the regimes of acute PFKFB3 depletion, increased AMP/ATP ratio drives the activation of AMPK, resulting in a failure in adaptation to low-glycolysis state through inhibiting the MAPK/ERK pathway. Together, our findings demonstrate that the kinetics of PFKFB3 depletion is of relevance to the ultimate cell fates during anti-PFKFB3 treatment in cancer cells, underscoring the importance of drug-target kinetics in the efficacy of anti-cancer therapies.

Results

Distinct cell cycle responses under different PFKFB3 inhibition treatments

To investigate the cellular responses of PFKFB3 depletion, we selected HeLa cells, a well-established cancer cell model for studying this protein’s functions.6,12,13 We established a PFKFB3 reporter system compatible with both live-imaging detection and inducible protein depletion14 (Figure 1A). Both alleles of the PFKFB3 gene were endogenously tagged with miniIAA7-mTurquoise2, together with constitutive expression of the E3 ligase AtAFB2 in HeLa cells (Figures 1A and S1A). At the population level, the endogenously expressed PFKFB3 fusion protein became virtually undetectable within 24 h after 500 μM IAA addition (Figure 1B). To verify the sensitivity of our experimental system with regard to the role of PFKFB3 in glycolysis, we used the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) as a direct measure of glycolytic flux. Compared to control groups, both PFKFB3 depletion and treatment with the PFKFB3 inhibitor PFK-015 led to markedly suppressed glycolysis, especially for glycolysis rates (Figures 1C and 1D). These results confirm previously documented effect of PFKFB3 inhibition in attenuating glycolytic flux.15 To investigate the role of pro-glycolysis activity of PFKFB3 in cell proliferation at the single cell level, we engineered a PFKFB3-AID system together with stable integration of a mMaroon1-tagged H1.0 (nuclear marker) and a mKO2-tagged Cdt1 (aa30-120, G1/S transition marker) (Figures 1E and S1C; Video S1; see legend and STAR Methods for details).16 Live-cell imaging was performed to track the dynamic profiles of PFKFB3, Cdt1, and histone H1.0 levels in proliferating cells under both unperturbed and treated conditions (Figure 1F). To quantify cell cycle progression, we define cell cycle length (hr) as the greater of (tM2 – tM1) and (tG1S2 – tG1S1) (Figure 1G). Here, tM1 and tM2 denote the time points reaching the first and second mitosis, respectively, measured as the time at which histone H1.0 signal reaches its peak; tG1S1 and tG1S2 correspond to the time points reaching the first and second G1/S transitions, respectively, measured as the time at which Cdt1 signal reaches its peak. Under unperturbed conditions, over 95% of cells remained proliferative, with an average cell cycle length of ∼25 h (Figure 1H). In contrast to treatment with 1 μM PFK-015 which prolonged cell cycle length in both G1 and S/G2/M phases, PFKFB3 depletion did not cause a significant effect on cell cycle progression with respect to either G1 or S/G2/M phase length (Figure 1H; Video S2). Moreover, the cell-cycle defect induced by PFK-015 treatment was abolished when PFKFB3 had been pre-depleted, suggesting that that the effect of 1 μM PFK-015 on cell cycle delay is mediated through PFKFB3 (Figure S1D). Together, these results show that, while both PFKFB3 depletion and PFK-015 treatment similarly inhibited glycolysis, they exerted divergent effects on cell cycle states, suggesting distinct cell fate outcomes between genetic depletion or pharmacological inhibition of PFKFB3.

Rapid PFKFB3 depletion delays cell cycle progression

The kinetics of PFKFB3 inhibition by PFK-015 is rapid whose effect on glycolytic suppression could be detected ∼3 h, which is considerably faster than PFKFB3 depletion via the AID system (see Figure 1B).17 Moreover, we found that PFK-015 at concentrations below 1 μM did not perturb cell cycle (Figure S1D). This dose-dependent effect of PFK-015 on cell-cycle progression is consistent with a hypothesis where the differential cell-cycle outcomes observed between PFK-015 treatment and PFKFB3 depletion may arise from the distinct kinetics of PFKFB3 loss. To directly test this hypothesis, we aimed to generate a varied protein degradation version with rapid PFKFB3 depletion. The efficiency of protein degradation in the AID system is primarily determined by two factors: IAA concentration and AtAFB2 protein level18 (Figure 2A). To achieve the rapid depletion objective, we generated PFKFB3-AID HeLa cells with doxycycline-inducible expression of AtAFB2. At the population level, increasing doses of doxycycline led to a gradual elevation of AtAFB2 protein level (Figure 2B). We next performed live-cell imaging to quantitatively assess the contributions of IAA and AtAFB2 levels to PFKFB3 depletion kinetics (Figure 2C). At a fixed AtAFB2 level, raising IAA concentrations from 60 μM to 2000 μM reduced the time needed for PFKFB3 depletion by ∼5 h. Conversely, at a fixed IAA concentration, increasing doxycycline from 0 to 10 μg/mL shortened the depletion time by more than 10 h (Figure 2D). Notably, the kinetics of PFKFB3 depletion plateaued at IAA concentrations above 500 μM, whereas it continued to improve substantially with higher AtAFB2 expression (Figure 2E). To explore whether PFKFB3 depletion kinetics have a role in determining the cell fates after depletion, we performed live-cell imaging under titrating concentrations of doxycycline (Figure 2F). Increasing AtAFB2 protein level via doxycycline (from 0 to 10 μg/mL) alone had little effect on cell cycle length in the absence of IAA; however, upon PFKFB3 depletion, cell cycle length was significantly prolonged (from ∼25 to 30 h) (Figures 2G and S2A). To account for cell-to-cell heterogeneity in protein depletion efficiency, we compared cell cycle lengths at both single-cell and population levels across conditions with varying kinetics of PFKFB3 depletion (Figures 2H and 2I). Notably, when depletion time was less than 12 h, cell cycle length exhibited a strong negative correlation with depletion time (Pearson r = −0.91, p < 0.0001). In contrast, such correlation was absent when PFKFB3 depletion time exceeded 12 h (Pearson r = −0.54, p = 0.07) (Figures 2H, 2I, S2B, and S2C). To objectively determine the threshold for this biphasic relationship, we performed a segmented regression analysis, which estimated the breakpoint at 12.34 h. Importantly, this biphasic effect of PFKFB3 depletion kinetics on cellular adaptation to a low-glycolysis state was also observed under CoCl2-induced hypoxic conditions (Figure S3). Together, these results show that PFKFB3 depletion kinetics affect, in a biphasic manner, cells’ ability to gain metabolic adaptation to a low-glycolysis state.

Rapid PFKFB3 depletion kinetic alters cell proliferation state in long term

For convenience, we refer to the 0 and 10 μg/mL doxycycline pretreatment conditions as “slow D” and “rapid D” subgroups, respectively, reflective of the kinetics of PFKFB3 depletion (Videos S3 and S4).

We found that glycolytic flux remained consistently low upon PFKFB3 depletion under all conditions tested (Figures 3A and 3B), supportive of the critical role of PFKFB3 in glycolysis. Notably, cell cycle distribution of the “slow D” subgroup showed no significant changes even after 30 days of sustained PFKFB3 depletion (Figures 3C and 3D; Table S1). In contrast, the proliferation capacity of both the “rapid D” subgroup and PFK-015-treated cells gradually declined over time compared to the “slow D” and control subgroups (Figure 3E; Table S2). We also performed live-cell imaging to examine cell cycle outcome upon re-supplementing PFKFB3 7 days after the depletion (Figures 3F and 3G). With IAA addition to deplete PFKFB3, the average cell cycle length was significantly longer in the “rapid D” subgroup than in the “slow D” subgroup (“slow D”: 26.02 ± 2.66 h; “rapid D”: 27.74 ± 3.48 h). Upon IAA removal to allow the re-introduction of PFKFB3 (Figures 3F and 3G), while cell cycle length in the “slow D Rec” subgroup was not negatively affected, it became further prolonged in the “rapid D Rec” subgroup (“slow D Rec”: 24.29 ± 2.02 h; “rapid D Rec”: 30.56 ± 4.23 h) (Figure 3H). Rapid PFKFB3 depletion attenuates pro-proliferative pathway

To investigate the molecular mechanisms by which rapid depletion resulted in cell cycle delay, we performed RNA-seq on control, “slow D”, “rapid D” and PFK-015-treated subgroups. Compared to the control group, only a small number of genes were differentially expressed in the “slow D” subgroup, even under prolonged treatment or recovery conditions (Figures 4A, S4A, and S4B). By contrast, the “rapid D” and PFK-015-treated subgroups each had over 400 genes with significant expression changes (Figure 4A). We found that the MAPK pathway was downregulated under the conditions of both rapid PFKFB3 depletion and PFK-015 treatment (Figure 4B). Among the genes that were dysregulated by both rapid PFKFB3 depletion and PFK-015 treatment, ∼200 were upregulated and ∼100 were downregulated (Figure 4C). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated that the upregulated genes were enriched in the aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway, whereas the downregulated genes were significantly enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway (Figures 4B and 4D). The downregulation of MAPK pathway genes suggests suppression of pro-proliferative signaling under both conditions, implying that the observed cell-cycle delay may arise from attenuation of the MAPK pathway. To further dissect the transcriptional response, we compared normal HeLa cells to those subjected to “rapid D” or PFK-015 treatment. MAPK pathway genes could be stratified into two clusters, downregulated (cluster 1) and up-regulated (cluster 2). Cluster 1 genes were associated with cell proliferation, whereas cluster 2 genes were linked to cell death (Figure 4E; Table S3). The temporal precedence of AMPK activation over ERK1/2 suppression raised the possibility that AMPK may serve as an upstream mediator. To test this causality directly, we used dorsomorphin (compound C) to abrogate pAMPK under the condition of rapid PFKFB3 depletion. As shown in Figure 6C, dorsomorphin treatment rescued pERK1/2 levels, establishing that AMPK activation is required for MAPK/ERK suppression upon rapid PFKFB3 depletion. To extend these findings, we generated HEK-293T cells carrying an inducible PFKFB3 degradation system. Unlike in HeLa cells, endogenous PFKFB3 was completely degraded within 2 h of induction in HEK-293T cells (Figure 6D). Consistently, PFKFB3 degradation in HEK-293T cells similarly elevated pAMPK and reduced pERK1/2, effects that were also reversed by dorsomorphin (Figure 6E). These results confirm that the AMPK-MAPK/ERK axis of regulation is not peculiar to HeLa cells. Together, these data establish that rapid PFKFB3 depletion activates AMPK, which in turn suppresses the MAPK/ERK pathway, a cascade that contributes to the anti-proliferative effects of rapid PFKFB3 degradation.

To directly evaluate the role of AMPK activation in rapid depletion cells with regard to cell cycle progression, HeLa cells were co-treated with the AMPK inhibitor dorsomorphin under rapid depletion or PFK-015 treatment conditions (Figure S5). Figure 6F show that AMPK inhibition led to a rescue of the cell cycle delay observed in both the “rapid D” and PFK-015-treated subgroups. Together, these results suggest that rapid PFKFB3 depletion evokes AMPK activation, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation at low-glycolysis state.

In summary, our findings establish that rapid PFKFB3 depletion leads to AMPK activation and subsequent inhibition of the MAPK/ERK pathway, a chain of events that do not take place under slow PFKFB3 depletion. Thus, the differential responses to rapid and slow PFKFB3 depletion route cells to distinct proliferation states (Figure 6G). This work unveils dynamic cellular outcomes resulting from differential kinetics of PFKFB3 depletion. It highlights the importance of the kinetic aspect of regulatory mechanisms in our understanding of the heterogenous responses in anti-cancer therapies.

Discussion

PFKFB3 is a key regulator of glycolysis characterized by a markedly higher kinase-to-phosphatase activity ratio, which enables it to catalyze the conversion of the glycolytic intermediate F6P to F2,6BP. F2,6BP serves as the most potent allosteric activator of PFK-1, a rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis. Given its widespread upregulation across various tumor types, PFKFB3 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in cancer.17 Treatment with PFK-015, a small molecule that binds to PFKFB3 and inhibits its kinase activity, causes cell cycle defects in many cancer cell types and suppresses tumor growth in vivo.17 Paradoxically, a previous study reported that PFKFB3 knockout did not cause cell death or proliferation defects under normal conditions.6 The mechanism underlying these apparently contradictory findings remains unclear. More broadly, clinical therapy targeting metabolism often faces challenges due to tumor recurrence, suggesting that cancer cells can adapt to metabolic stress through unknown mechanisms.21,22 Thus, insights into how cancer cells respond to metabolic stress likely will improve our understanding of such adaptation.

To address this question, we first employed quantitative live-cell imaging and tunable regulation to explore the relationship between PFKFB3 loss and cell fate outcome. We found that the rate of PFKFB3 depletion dictates distinct cellular outcomes. Slow depletion (>12 h to depletion) allowed cells to progressively adapt to a low-glycolysis state with minimal impact on proliferation or transcription. In contrast, rapid depletion (<12 h to depletion) led to reduced glycolytic flux and suppressed proliferation, indicative of a failure in adaptation. To our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind in documenting the existence of a threshold of PFKFB3 depletion rates in mediating a bifurcation in cellular adaptation.

PFK-015 treatment has been reported to inhibit cancer cell proliferation in previous studies.5,8,23 The similar proliferation defect phenotypes observed with PFK-015 treatment and rapid PFKFB3 depletion suggest that they may converge on the same signaling pathway. Interestingly, we found that both conditions activate the AMPK pathway and inhibit the MAPK/ERK pathway. AMPK inhibition rescued the proliferation defect under rapid PFKFB3 depletion; slow PFKFB3 depletion did not activate AMPK. Metabolic profiling indicated that the AMP/ATP ratio increased only under rapid PFKFB3 depletion. It is well established that AMPK serves as a sensor of cellular energy status via the AMP/ATP ratio. It has been reported that metabolic sensors, such as the AMPK and mTOR pathways, are activated when cells encounter nutrient limitation.19,20,24 Upon activation, these sensors trigger broad transcriptional and translational reprogramming. In our study, however, we found that these sensors were activated only under conditions of rapid PFKFB3 depletion or PFK-015 treatment, not when PFKFB3 was depleted slowly. This suggests that activation of metabolic sensors may require a sharp decline in glycolytic flux. Our results point to a potential mechanism by which cancer cells adapt to a low-glycolysis state, possibly by circumventing canonical metabolic sensor activation. We performed long-term transcriptional tracking of slow PFKFB3 depletion from 1 to 30 days, and even up to 4 months (data not shown), but there was a minimal degree of transcriptional dysregulation that could be detected. This observation suggests that the adaptation mechanism operates largely at the post-transcriptional or post-translational level, rather than through transcriptional reprogramming. Precisely how this circumvention is achieved remains an open question.

Recently, an increasing number of studies have focused on distinct cell fate outcomes following anti-cancer treatment at single-cell resolution. For example, the early dynamics of p21, a key cell cycle inhibitor, induced by DNA-damaging agents determines the final proliferation-senescence cell fates, providing a potential explanation for tumor recurrence after therapy.25 In our own study, we have also found previously that mother cells play a preemptive role in instructing daughter cells’ proliferation-quiescence decisions under nutrient limitation.14,26 p21 senses AMPK activation to induce quiescence in daughter cells when mother cells face nutrient limitation during G1 phase, but not during S phase.14 These studies highlight how cellular heterogeneity determines cell fate outcomes. Although many studies have demonstrated the important role of PFKFB3 in regulating cell proliferation and survival at the population level, they have often overlooked cellular heterogeneity. Here, our results reveal an unexpected but critical role of PFKFB3 depletion kinetics in dictating cells’ adaptation to a low-glycolysis state. Both rapid PFKFB3 depletion and PFK-015 treatment attenuate the MAPK/ERK pathway and cell cycle progression via AMPK activation, effects that are absent under slow PFKFB3 depletion. These results suggest that dynamic PFKFB3 targeting can drive heterogeneous cancer cell fates, unveiling potential routes of resistance to anti-PFKFB3 therapy.

In this study, we used HeLa cells, which are HPV-positive and functionally p53-deficient, a feature that uniquely influences both metabolic regulation and cell-cycle checkpoints. Notably, p53 deficiency is common in many cancer types,27 particularly in high-grade malignancies. Our findings therefore raise the possibility that p53-deficient cancer cells may be more prone to adapt to a low-glycolysis state following anti-metabolic therapy. While additional validation in p53-proficient cancer cell lines would be valuable, our observation that the AMPK-MAPK/ERK axis of regulation is also operative in HEK-293T cells suggests that the core mechanism is not limited to p53-deficient cancer cells.

In summary, our findings reveal a previously unexplored mechanism by which cancer cells adapt to a low-glycolysis state, a process that occurs only when PFKFB3 is depleted slowly without causing AMPK activation. By integrating quantitative imaging with controlled protein depletion, this work establishes a dynamic framework for understanding metabolic adaptation and offers new insights into how the rate of target inhibition can determine therapeutic outcomes. Our results suggest that partial or slow-acting PFKFB3 inhibitors may inadvertently promote adaptation rather than cell death. Whether this notion is of clinical relevance remains to be known.

Limitations of the study

Our study takes a system-level approach to understanding how the kinetics of PFKFB3 loss influence cell fate decisions. While we have successfully defined the threshold of PFKFB3 depletion rates that mediate cellular adaptation to low glycolysis, several limitations and open questions remain. First, we confirmed that AMPK activation attenuates MAPK/ERK signaling upon rapid PFKFB3 degradation in both HeLa (p53-deficient) and HEK-293T (p53-proficient) cells. The fact that this mechanism operates in two cell lines with distinct p53 statuses supports its general relevance. Nevertheless, given the central role of p53 in cancer therapy and metabolism, validation in additional p53-proficient cancer cell lines, such as HCT116 or A549, would further strengthen the broader applicability of our kinetic model. Second, our pharmacological inhibition experiments relied exclusively on PFK-015. While this compound is widely used for PFKFB3 inhibition, testing an additional PFKFB3 inhibitor with a distinct chemical scaffold would help rule out compound-specific effects and reinforce our conclusions. Third, we used CoCl2 to stabilize HIF-1α and mimic hypoxic transcriptional programs. However, CoCl2 does not fully recapitulate physiological hypoxia, as mitochondrial respiration remains intact. Future studies under genuine hypoxic conditions (e.g., 1%–3% O2) would provide more definitive validation of the hypoxia-associated adaptive responses. Fourth, compound C was used as a pharmacological AMPK inhibitor. Given its known off-target effects, genetic loss-of-function approaches (e.g., AMPK knockdown or knockout) would offer more conclusive evidence for the specificity of AMPK-mediated effects. Despite these limitations, our findings establish a kinetic framework for understanding how the rate of PFKFB3 loss determines cellular adaptation to metabolic stress, providing a foundation for future mechanistic and translational studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

HEK293T and HeLa cells were maintained in DMEM (11965092; Thermofisher) supplemented with 10% FBS (SE100-B; VisTech), penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/ml each) at 5% CO2 and 37°C. Media were changed every 2 days and passaged every 2–3 days.

Method details

Generating PFKFB3-miniIAA7-mTurquoise2 HeLa cell lines

The generation of endogenously tagged PFKFB3-miniIAA7-mTurquoise2 cells was as reported in our previous studies.14 In brief, the endogenous PFKFB3 gene was tagged at the C terminus using CRISPR-mediated gene tagging. The gRNA for PFKFB3 nearby the stop codon was used. The PFKFB3-gRNA plasmid (5′- gactcctccaggaaacactg-3′) and the linearized PCR product of PFKFB3-miniIAA7-mTurquoise2-IRES-neoR donor sequence was transfected into HeLa cells at the ratio of 1:1 using jetPRIME, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (101000046; Polyplus). Cells were treated with 500 μg/mL G418 until outgrown of the colonies. Finally, homozygous knock-in clones were verified by PCR and Western blotting.

Generating AtAFB2 HeLa (HeLa PFKFB3-AID) stable cell lines

HEK293T cells at approximately 70% confluence were transfected with psPAX2, pMD2.G, FUGW-AtAFB2-mCherry-weak NLS at ratio 0.75:0.5:1 using jetPRIME (101000046; Polyplus), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Two days after transfection, supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone filter and was used to infect PFKFB3-miniIAA7-mTurquoise2 HeLa cells. After 7 days, mCherry-positive cells were sorted out via flow cytometry (Moflo Astrios EQ; Beckman).

Generating Cdt1(30–120)-H1 HeLa PFKFB3-AID stable cell lines

HEK293T cells at approximately 70% confluence were transfected with psPAX2, pMD2.G, and FUGW-H1-mMaroon1-IRES-mKO2-Cdt1(30–120) at the ratio of 0.75:0.5:1 using jetPRIME (101000046; Polyplus), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Two days after transfection, supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone filter and was used to infect HeLa PFKFB3-AID cells. After 7 days, and mMaroon1-positive cells were sorted out via flow cytometry.

Generating inducible AtAFB2 HeLa PFKFB3-AID stable cell lines

HEK293T cells at approximately 70% confluence were transfected with psPAX2, pMD2.G, and PCW 57.1- AtAFB2-mCherry-weak NLS at ratio 0.75:0.5:1 using jetPRIME (101000046; Polyplus), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Two days after transfection, supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone filter and was used to infect Cdt1(30–120)-H1 HeLa PFKFB3-AID cells. Cells were treated with 10 μg/mL puromycin until outgrown of the colonies. Finally, clones were verified by PCR and Western blotting.

Chemical regents

Drugs used in this study include: PFK-015 (HY-12204; MedChemExpress), Dorsomorphin (Compound C) (S7840; Selleck), cobalt chloride (Y024817; Beyotime), auxin (I5148; Sigma) and doxycycline (ST039A; Beyotime). Auxin, and doxycycline were reconstituted in water, while the others were reconstituted in DMSO. All drugs were stored in aliquots at −20°C.

Extracellular acidification rate

ECAR was measured using a Seahorse XF96 analyzer (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were counted and seeded into Seahorse 96-well plates at a density of 8,000 cells per well and cultured for 12 h prior to assay. On the day of measurement, glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (2 μM) and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (50 mM) were sequentially injected into the designated ports of the Seahorse cartridge. All data were normalized to the protein concentration by the BCA assay as described above.

Time-lapse microscopy

24-well plates (142485; ThermoFisher) were pre-coated with 0.1% gelatin (#07903; StemCell). Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well 24 h prior to imaging. Live-cell imaging was performed in a humidified chamber at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Images were acquired every 15 min using a Nikon Ti2-E inverted microscope (Nikon) in the cyan (excitation CWL/BW: 440/10 nm; emission CWL/BW: 460/30 nm), red (545/10 nm; 575/25 nm), and Cy5 (610/10 nm; 670/65 nm) channels. Total light exposure time was maintained below 500 ms per snapshot, and laser intensity for each channel was kept below 5%. For quantification of PFKFB3 levels, the cyan channel exposure time and intensity were consistently set to 200 ms and 5%, respectively, throughout all experiments.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

For library construction, cell pellets containing approximately 1× 105 cells were snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was extracted from each sample using the RNAiso Plus kit (9109; Takara). cDNA was then used for library preparation with the VAHTS Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NR605-01; Vazyme). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq platform using PE150 chemistry. Two biological replicates were included for each condition. Sequence quality was assessed using FastQC v0.11.2. Adapter sequences were trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.32.31 Clean reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 v2.0.3-beta2.32 Fragments mapped to genes were quantified using featureCounts.33 Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 v1.10.1.34 Genes with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and a |log2 (fold change) | > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. KEGG pathway enrichment and heatmap visualization were carried out using the ClusterGVis package.35

Western blotting

Cells were collected and directly lysed in RIPA buffer (FD009; FUDE). Whole-cell lysates were subjected to electrophoresis on 12% SDS-PAGE gels (FD346; FUDE) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010; Millipore). After transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for at least 2 h at room temperature with gentle rocking. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in TBST. The next day, membranes were washed four times (5 min each) in TBST and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse (FDM007; FUDE) or anti-rabbit (FDR007; FUDE) secondary antibodies diluted 1:10,000 in TBST. After four additional 5-min washes in TBST, protein bands were visualized using ECL Western Blotting Substrate (E412-01; Vazyme Biotech) on a gel imaging system (Clinx ChemiScope 3000 Series). Images were analyzed using Gel-Pro Analyzer software (v4.0.0.001). The following primary antibodies were used for Western blotting: PFKFB3 (13763; Proteintech), GAPDH (A19056; Abclonal), mCherry (26765; Proteintech), AMPK (A27740; Abclonal), phospho-AMPK (AP1441; Abclonal), phospho-4E-BP1 (Thr37) (81812-4-RR; Proteintech), 4E-BP1 (60246-1-Ig; Proteintech), phospho-ACC1 (S79) (AP0298; Abclonal), ACC1(A19627; Abclonal), HIF-1α (20960-1-AP; Proteintech), ERK1/2 (A4782; Abclonal) and phospho-ERK1 (T202/Y204)/ERK2 (T185/Y187) (MAB1018; R & D).

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were fixed with ice-cold 75% ethanol at 4 °C overnight and then washed three times with ice-cold PBS. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 50 μg/mL RNase A and 40 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI) (BB-4104; Bestbio) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Cell cycle analysis was performed using a Beckman Coulter DxFLEX flow cytometer, and data were analyzed and visualized with FlowJo software (v10.8.1).

Cell proliferation analysis

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (C0040; Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were counted and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2,000 cells per well. At the indicated time points, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 1 h, after which the absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) was measured.

Metabolite profiling

To determine the relative abundance of intracellular metabolites, HeLa cells were treated with or without 10 μg/mL doxycycline for 24 h, followed by treatment with 500 μM IAA for an additional 24 h 1×107 cells were first collected and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen to quench metabolic activity, then polar metabolites were extracted using a pre-chilled solvent such as 80% methanol. The extracts were subsequently analyzed on a high-resolution Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometry platform, with the HQ non-targeted metabolomics Pro version employing both C18 and HILIC columns to achieve broad coverage from polar to non-polar compounds; metabolites of central carbon metabolism (glycolysis, TCA cycle, and pentose phosphate pathway) were identified by searching against Novogene’s proprietary NovoMetDB database.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Image analysis

Cell segmentation and tracking

All image analyses utilized the Histone H1.0-mMaroon1 signal as a nuclear mask. We employed the automated segmentation software, Cellpose 2.0,36 to segment individual cells within an image based on the Histone H1.0-mMaroon1 signal. For tracking individual cells over time, we applied a mathematical framework known as the Linear Assignment Problem (LAP)37 to link nuclear signals between consecutive frames.

Measuring Cdt1 levels

Cdt1 levels were represented by the mean Cdt1 intensity measured within the nuclear region defined by the H1.0-mMaroon1 mask.

Measuring PFKFB3 levels

PFKFB3 levels were quantified as the mean fluorescence intensity within the nuclear region defined by the H1.0-mMaroon1 mask. The time of absolute PFKFB3 depletion was defined as the time point at which PFKFB3 levels dropped below 0.1-fold above background signals.

Definition of the time to reach M phase

Given that Histone H1.0 undergoes dynamic assembly and disassembly during mitosis, resulting in peak intensity during M phase, the time to reach M phase (tM) was defined as the time point corresponding to the peak of Histone H1.0 signal.

Definition of the time to reach G1/S transition

The time to reach the G1/S transition (tG1S) was defined as the inflection point of Cdt1 levels, as described previously.16

Definition of cell cycle length

Cell cycle length (hours) was defined as the greater of (tM2 – tM1) and (tG1S2 – tG1S1). tM1/ tM2 indicate the time of the first/second mitosis (peak Histone H1.0); tG1S1/ tG1S2 indicate the time of the first/second G1/S transition (peak Cdt1). We used the greater value, rather than the mean, based on the following considerations: (1) phase-specific delays varied between G1 and S/G2/M, and the greater value better captures the rate-limiting step; (2) within the 45-h imaging window, many cells reached tG1S2 but not tM2, and using the mean would have excluded them, whereas the greater value allowed retention and avoided censoring bias.

Statistical analysis

For scatterplots, data were shown as mean ± SEM. For bar plots, data were shown as mean ± SD. Violin plots displayed the median with interquartile range (IQR) and whiskers. Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA for bar plots, depending on the experimental design. For violin plots, Student’s t test, one-way ANOVA or mixed-effects analysis was used as appropriate. Significance levels are indicated as follows: “ns”, >0.05; ∗, <0.05; ∗∗, <10–2; ∗∗∗, <10–3; ∗∗∗∗, <10–4.
