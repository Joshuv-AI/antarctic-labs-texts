Selective percutaneous myofasciotomy in persistent toe-walking: Anatomical rationale and surgical technique

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent toe-walking is a gait abnormality characterized by forefoot initial contact and reduced or absent heel strike beyond early childhood. Although often regarded as a transient developmental variant, persistence of this pattern may lead to progressive limitation of ankle dorsiflexion, fixed equinus contracture, and long-term functional impairment. Toe-walking may occur as an isolated idiopathic condition or in association with neurological, neuromuscular, or genetic disorders. Increasing evidence suggests that a subset of patients previously classified as idiopathic harbor underlying hereditary or neuromuscular abnormalities. Traditional surgical lengthening procedures can restore ankle dorsiflexion but are associated with risks such as over-lengthening, postoperative weakness, recurrence, and soft-tissue morbidity. Introduction

Toe-walking is a gait pattern characterized by forefoot initial contact during ambulation with absent or reduced heel strike [1]. While commonly observed during early gait development [2], persistence beyond early childhood may be associated with progressive limitation of ankle dorsiflexion, fixed equinus contracture, gait dysfunction, and musculoskeletal symptoms [3], [4], [5]. Persistent toe walking may occur as an isolated condition or in association with neurological, neuromuscular, or developmental disorders.

Although independent ambulation is usually not delayed, the gait pattern is often inefficient and mechanically disadvantageous. Reduced ankle dorsiflexion impairs normal tibial progression during stance, resulting in diminished push-off, increased energy expenditure, and compensatory movements at the knee, hip, and trunk. Over time, these adaptations may contribute to muscle fatigue, pain in the lower extremities or back, frequent stumbling, and limitations in physical activity [6].

Management of persistent toe walking is guided by patient age, ankle range of motion, and the presence and severity of gastrocnemius–soleus contracture. Initial treatment generally consists of conservative measures, including pyramidal insoles, physiotherapy, stretching, orthoses, and serial casting, whereas surgical intervention is reserved for selected patients with more severe deformity or contracture [7].

Surgical treatment of fixed equinus associated with persistent toe walking has traditionally relied on procedures targeting the gastrocnemius–soleus complex and Achilles tendon. Depending on the anatomical level of contracture, the underlying pathology, and the results of the Silfverskiöld test, commonly performed procedures include isolated gastrocnemius recession (e.g., Strayer and Baumann procedures), gastrocnemius–soleus recession (e.g., Vulpius procedure), and Achilles tendon lengthening. These procedures have been shown to improve ankle dorsiflexion and gait in appropriately selected patients; however, each has distinct indications, technical considerations, and potential complications [8].

Gastrocnemius recession is generally reserved for isolated gastrocnemius contracture, whereas Achilles tendon lengthening is typically indicated when both the gastrocnemius and soleus contribute to fixed equinus. Potential complications include over-lengthening, postoperative calf weakness, altered push-off mechanics, recurrence, scar formation, and the need for prolonged postoperative immobilization or rehabilitation. Although minimally invasive modifications have been introduced, most techniques remain based on tendon or aponeurotic lengthening [9], [10].

Selective percutaneous myofasciotomy differs conceptually from these procedures because it aims to release localized fibrotic myofascial restrictions through subcutaneous puncture incisions while preserving the overall continuity of the musculotendinous unit. Rather than replacing established tendon-lengthening procedures, the technique is intended as a minimally invasive alternative for carefully selected patients with fixed myofascial contractures. The purpose of this Technical Note is not to establish superiority over existing procedures but to describe the surgical rationale, operative technique, and practical considerations associated with this approach.

Pathogenesis

Toe-walking may occur as a manifestation of a variety of underlying neurological and neuromuscular disorders, or present as a persistent gait pattern in the absence of identifiable pathology. The underlying mechanisms differ between these groups but commonly involve altered neuromuscular control and muscle imbalance affecting ankle dorsiflexion. As well as genetic underlying causes are involved [11], [12], [13], [14], [15].

Genetics

For many years, persistent and idiopathic toe-walking were regarded as conditions without a clearly identifiable cause. Increasing evidence, however, suggests that genetic factors may contribute to the development of toe-walking in a subset of patients. This hypothesis is supported by the frequent observation of toe-walking in multiple members of the same family in about 90% and by the association of the gait pattern with additional phenotypic features suggestive of inherited disorders [16].

Several studies have reported a positive family history in children with persistent or idiopathic toe-walking, indicating a possible hereditary component. In addition to the gait anomaly itself, affected patients may present with other clinical features such as pes cavus, clinodactyly, brachydactyly, pectus deformities, or speech development disorders, further supporting the concept of a systemic rather than purely local musculoskeletal condition [17].

Recent studies using targeted gene panels have demonstrated that a considerable proportion of patients diagnosed with idiopathic toe-walking carry genetic variants classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, or of uncertain significance in genes associated with neuromuscular or metabolic disorders. In a retrospective cohort analysis, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in genes related to neuropathies or myopathies were identified in a subset of patients previously classified as idiopathic, suggesting that toe-walking may represent an early or isolated manifestation of underlying hereditary disease in some cases [18].

Variants of uncertain significance are frequently detected in this population, and their clinical relevance remains unclear. Nevertheless, the high prevalence of such variants, combined with associated clinical features and family history, indicates that genetic factors may play a contributory role in the pathogenesis of persistent toe-walking. Importantly, the identification of genetic abnormalities may have implications for clinical management, as the response to conservative or surgical treatment may differ depending on the underlying neuromuscular pathology [19].

Genetic testing therefore represents a valuable adjunct in the evaluation of children with persistent toe-walking, particularly in cases with atypical features, progressive deformity, family history, or associated neurological or developmental findings. Recognition of an underlying hereditary condition may allow more accurate diagnosis, individualized treatment planning, and improved counseling of patients and families regarding prognosis and long-term outcomes.

Neurological conditions

Toe-walking is frequently observed in children with disorders of the central nervous system. It is a common feature in spastic forms of cerebral palsy, where increased muscle tone and impaired selective motor control lead to persistent plantarflexor overactivity. Toe-walking has also been described in children with periventricular leukomalacia, spinal cord abnormalities, and other conditions affecting descending motor pathways. In these disorders, impaired central motor regulation results in abnormal gait patterns that often progress to fixed equinus deformity with growth [20], [21], [22], [23], [24].

Neuromuscular and muscular disorders

Neuromuscular diseases may also present with toe-walking, either due to muscle weakness, imbalance, or altered muscle activation patterns. Conditions such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy, Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, and other peripheral neuropathies have been associated with forefoot gait [25], [26].

Pathological anatomical aspects

The primary pathological changes involve the soft tissues of the posterior compartment of the lower leg and the plantar structures of the foot. Contracture and shortening of the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles are commonly present, often accompanied by tightening of the Achilles tendon. These changes result in persistent plantarflexion of the ankle and restriction of dorsiflexion. This plantarflexed posture corresponds to an equinus foot deformity, characterized by limited heel contact and impaired ankle dorsiflexion.

The gastrocnemius muscle, particularly its medial head, is frequently involved and may demonstrate increased stiffness and reduced extensibility. The soleus muscle is often affected concurrently, contributing to limitation of ankle dorsiflexion both with the knee extended and flexed. In addition to muscular contracture, surrounding connective tissues may undergo adaptive changes in response to altered mechanical loading. Experimental studies have demonstrated that deep fascia is a biologically active tissue capable of extracellular matrix remodeling, with alterations in collagen composition and fascial organization occurring under sustained mechanical tension. These findings suggest that connective tissue adaptation may contribute to the mechanical behavior of the musculotendinous unit, although its precise role in persistent toe walking remains incompletely understood [27].

Persistent toe walking is associated with neuromuscular abnormalities in some patients, while limitation of ankle dorsiflexion is accompanied by secondary biomechanical and soft-tissue adaptations. Biomechanical studies have shown that limitation of ankle dorsiflexion is associated with altered muscle activation patterns and compensatory gait strategies affecting the ankle, knee, and hip, emphasizing that both neural control and mechanical factors contribute to the clinical presentation [28], [29].

Although selective percutaneous myofasciotomy is intended to reduce pathological soft-tissue tension through targeted release of myofascial structures, the relative contributions of muscle, fascia, tendon, and neural mechanisms to its clinical effect remain uncertain. Further studies are required to clarify the biological mechanisms underlying this approach and to determine which tissue components contribute most to correction of equinus deformity.

Clinical features

Persistent toe walking is characterized by a gait pattern in which initial foot contact occurs at the forefoot rather than with a physiological heel strike and roll-over. Clinically, toe walking is typically considered relevant when it is present for a substantial proportion of daily ambulation and does not resolve spontaneously with growth. While many children exhibit transient toe walking during early gait development, persistence beyond early childhood is associated with functional limitations and may warrant further evaluation.

A central clinical feature in patients presenting for treatment is restricted mobility of the upper ankle joint, most commonly manifesting as limited ankle dorsiflexion. This limitation is frequently associated with an equinus position of the foot during stance and gait. Using the neutral zero method, normal upper ankle joint mobility is commonly referenced as 20–0–50° [30], and patients indicated for intervention typically demonstrate a clinically relevant reduction in dorsiflexion compared with this reference range. Reduced ankle mobility contributes to an abnormal gait pattern, impaired push-off mechanics, and compensatory strategies during walking.

In addition to gait abnormalities, patients may present with functional complaints such as frequent tripping or stumbling, reduced endurance, and difficulties with balance. In older children, adolescents, and adults [31], persistent toe walking may be associated with musculoskeletal discomfort, including pain in the feet, calves, knees, or lower back. Social and psychological factors, such as self-consciousness or teasing due to the conspicuous gait pattern, have also been reported as contributing reasons for seeking treatment.

Illustrative clinical cases

To illustrate the clinical application of selective percutaneous myofasciotomy, three representative patients treated at our institution are presented.

Case 1

A patient presented with persistent toe walking since early childhood associated with progressive worsening of gait, pain during walking, and lumbar hyperlordosis. Preoperative examination demonstrated marked restriction of ankle dorsiflexion (left: 0–10–50°, right: 0–20–50°). Selective percutaneous myofasciotomy was performed because of the severity of the deformity. At follow-up, the patient no longer demonstrated toe walking during provocation testing. Lumbar hyperlordosis had resolved, ankle dorsiflexion improved bilaterally to 15–0–50°, and walking-related pain was reduced.

Case 2

A patient with persistent toe walking since the onset of independent walking presented with progressive gait impairment and ankle pain. Preoperative ankle dorsiflexion measured 0–10–50° bilaterally. Following selective percutaneous myofasciotomy and postoperative treatment with orthoses and night splints, the patient achieved spontaneous plantigrade gait. Ankle dorsiflexion improved bilaterally to 25–0–50°, and the overall gait pattern was markedly improved.

Case 3

A 15-year-old patient with persistent toe walking since the onset of walking presented with severe forefoot gait and difficulty walking on the heels. Preoperative ankle dorsiflexion measured 5–0–50° on the left and 0–0–50° on the right. After selective percutaneous myofasciotomy, the patient no longer demonstrated toe walking during provocation testing. Bilateral ankle dorsiflexion improved to 15–0–50°, accompanied by reduced walking-related complaints.

Treatment

The primary therapeutic goal in the treatment of toe-walking with equinus deformity is the restoration of sufficient ankle dorsiflexion to allow physiological heel contact during stance and a more normalized gait pattern. Persistent limitation of dorsiflexion is most commonly caused by fixed soft-tissue contractures of the posterior lower leg and plantar structures, which cannot be adequately addressed once they have progressed beyond a functional stage.

Selective percutaneous myofasciotomy is based on the concept that equinus in toe-walking is predominantly a soft-tissue–driven deformity resulting from localized fibrotic and myofascial shortening rather than primary osseous malalignment. The rationale of this approach is to target these pathological soft-tissue constraints in a minimally invasive manner, with the aim of improving muscle elasticity and increasing the functional range of motion of the ankle joint.

By addressing the anatomical substrate responsible for fixed equinus, selective percutaneous myofasciotomy seeks to improve upper ankle joint mobility while preserving overall muscle function and minimizing surgical trauma. This approach is intended to facilitate subsequent gait normalization and to enhance the effectiveness of postoperative rehabilitation.

Conventional management strategies for persistent toe walking

Current treatment strategies for persistent toe walking are primarily guided by patient age, ankle range of motion, presence of fixed contracture, and foot morphology. In children without fixed contracture, nonsurgical management such as physiotherapy, stretching, and bracing is commonly recommended [32]. However, available evidence suggests that these interventions result in only minimal improvements in ankle dorsiflexion, typically in the range of 1–2°, and rarely lead to sustained normalization of gait [33].

A retrospective case series of 193 children reported that treatment with pyramidal insoles was successful in approximately 90% of cases, with significant improvement in ankle dorsiflexion. Better outcomes were observed when treatment was initiated at a younger age, whereas older age at presentation and declining ankle range of motion during therapy were associated with treatment failure [34].

Serial casting has been shown to improve ankle dorsiflexion and gait parameters in children with toe walking, with normalization of several functional measures reported at mid-term follow-up. However, outcomes vary across studies, and long-term durability remains inconsistent, particularly in patients with established contractures [22], [35], [36].

Botulinum toxin A has been investigated as a treatment option for idiopathic toe walking; however, high-level evidence does not demonstrate a clinically meaningful benefit. Its use has not been shown to result in improvements in heel-strike gait, or overall toe-walking severity compared with established conservative measures, while being associated with a notable rate of adverse events. Consequently, current evidence does not support the routine use of botulinum toxin A in the management of idiopathic toe walking [37].

In cases of fixed equinus deformity, surgical lengthening of the gastrocnemius–soleus complex and, when indicated, plantar fascia release are widely accepted treatment options. These procedures can effectively improve ankle dorsiflexion [38]; however, they are associated with inherent risks, including over-lengthening, postoperative weakness, recurrence, and incomplete normalization of gait mechanics. Furthermore, these approaches typically involve open or mini-open techniques with greater soft-tissue disruption [39], [40].

Taken together, these limitations highlight the need for alternative treatment strategies that address fixed soft-tissue contractures while minimizing surgical trauma and preserving muscle function.

Surgical technique: selective percutaneous myofasciotomy

Selective percutaneous myofasciotomy (SPM), also referred to as selective myofibrofasciotomy (SMFT) or the Ulzibat method, is a minimally invasive soft-tissue procedure designed to address fixed myofascial contractures contributing to equinus deformity in toe-walking patients. The technique is based on the selective subcutaneous release of fibrotic muscle fibers and fascia while preserving surrounding healthy tissue and normal neuromuscular structures. By targeting pathological myofascial restrictions rather than performing extensive tendon lengthening, SPM aims to restore joint range of motion with minimal surgical trauma and a short recovery period. The following section provides a detailed description of the indications, operative setup, surgical steps, and postoperative management of this procedure, with particular emphasis on its application in the treatment of ankle equinus associated with persistent toe walking.

3.Instruments

Instruments

Selective percutaneous myofasciotomy is performed using a specialized scalpel specifically designed for subcutaneous, selective dissection of fibrotic muscle fibers and fascia (Fig. 6). The instrument allows the procedure to be carried out through a skin puncture of less than 1 mm, eliminating the need for open incisions. The shape and cutting edge of the blade are engineered to enable precise, controlled dissection of fibrotic tissue while minimizing trauma to surrounding healthy muscle fibers and adjacent structures.

Specialized scalpel used for selective percutaneous myofasciotomy (Illustration created by Antonia Graaf).

The technique does not require extensive surgical instrumentation. Standard surgical equipment for skin preparation, antiseptic wound care, and basic hemostasis is sufficient. No retractors or visualization instruments are necessary, as the procedure is performed in a closed, subcutaneous manner. The limited instrumentation contributes to the short operative time and low soft-tissue trauma associated with the method.

Because the procedure is performed without direct visual control of the blade within the tissues, surgeon experience and familiarity with the instrument are essential to ensure selective release of fibrotic structures and to minimize the risk of iatrogenic injury to neurovascular elements.
