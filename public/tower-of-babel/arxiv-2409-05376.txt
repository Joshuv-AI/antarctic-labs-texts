The heat semigroup associated with the Jacobi–Cherednik operator and its applications
Anirudha Poria, Ramakrishnan Radha

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the heat equation associated with the Jacobi–Cherednik operator on the real line. We establish some basic properties of the Jacobi–Cherednik heat kernel and heat semigroup. We also provide a solution to the Cauchy problem for the Jacobi–Cherednik heat operator and prove that the heat kernel is strictly positive. Then, we characterize the image of the space L 2 ​ ( ℝ , A α , β ) L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta}) under the Jacobi–Cherednik heat semigroup as a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. As an application, we solve the modified Poisson equation and present the Jacobi–Cherednik–Markov processes.

1.
Introduction

During the last three decades, considerable attention has been devoted to studying the Dunkl, Jacobi–Dunkl, and Jacobi–Cherednik differential-difference operators due to their relevance in various fields of mathematics and physical applications. These operators play an important role in the harmonic analysis related to the theory of Heckman, Opdam and Cherednik (see
[
10
,
17
,
18
]
). Let
\alpha\geq-\frac{1}{2}
, the Dunkl operator
D_{\alpha}
acting on smooth functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}
, is defined by (see
[
5
]
)

D_{\alpha}f(x)=\frac{d}{dx}f(x)+\frac{2\alpha+1}{x}\left(\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2}\right).

For
\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, the Jacobi–Dunkl differential-difference operator
\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}
defined on
\mathbb{R}
by (see
[
10
,
17
]
)

\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)=\frac{d}{dx}f(x)+\Big[(2\alpha+1)\coth x+(2\beta+1)\tanh x\Big]\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2}.

It is the Jacobi or hyperbolic analogue of the Dunkl operator and well-defined for
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. In this work, we consider the Jacobi–Cherednik differential-difference operator
T_{\alpha,\beta}
defined on
\mathbb{R}
by

T_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)=\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)-\rho f(-x),

where
\alpha,\beta
satisfying
\alpha\geq\beta\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
, and
\rho=\alpha+\beta+1
. The square
D^{2}_{\alpha}
of the Dunkl operator investigated in
[
23
]
and the square
\Lambda^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
of the Jacobi–Dunkl operator investigated in
[
4
]
. Here, we attempt to study the operator
T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
, which is the square of the Jacobi–Cherednik operator.

In
[
23
]
, the author studied the heat equation associated with Dunkl’s Laplacian and proved that the corresponding Cauchy problem is governed by a positive one-parameter semigroup by using the maximum principle for the generalized Laplacian. Then, the authors in
[
4
]
investigated the heat equation associated with the Jacobi–Dunkl operator and proved that the heat semigroup has a strictly positive kernel and a finite Green operator. In this paper, we generalize the results investigated in
[
23
,
4
]
and study the heat equation associated with the Jacobi–Cherednik operator on the real line. We establish some basic properties of the Jacobi–Cherednik heat kernel and heat semigroup. We also provide an explicit solution to the Cauchy problem for the Jacobi–Cherednik heat operator using the heat kernel and prove that the heat kernel is strictly positive. Moreover, we characterize the image of the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
under the Jacobi–Cherednik heat semigroup as a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. As an application, we solve the modified Poisson equation and present the Jacobi–Cherednik–Markov processes.

An important motivation to study the Jacobi–Cherednik operators arises from their relevance in the algebraic description of exactly solvable quantum many-body systems of Calogero–Moser–Sutherland type (see
[
26
,
11
]
) and they provide a useful tool in the study of special functions with root systems (see
[
5
,
10
]
). These describe algebraically integrable systems in one dimension and have gained considerable interest in mathematical physics. Another motivation for the investigation of the Jacobi–Cherednik operator is to generalize the previous subjects which are bound with the physics. For a more detailed discussion, we refer to
[
13
,
20
]
. Considerable attention has been devoted to discovering applications of these operators to new contexts (see
[
12
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
19
]
). In this paper, we attempt to study the heat equation associated with the Jacobi–Cherednik operator. The Jacobi–Cherednik operators have implications in two main areas: quantum mechanics and signal analysis, and the heat equation is widely used in these areas (see
[
11
,
23
]
). We hope that the study of the Jacobi–Cherednik heat kernel and heat semigroup makes a significant impact in these areas.

The main aim of this paper is to show that the heat equation associated with the Jacobi–Cherednik operator has a solution of the form

P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(x,y)f(y)A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)dy,

where
p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(x,y)
is a strictly positive kernel. We also prove that
\left(P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t},\;t\geq 0\right)
is a strongly continuous semigroup of operators on the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
consisting of square-integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}
with respect to the measure
A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx
. Using the Opdam–Cherednik transform and structure of the semigroup, we obtain a spectral representation of the heat kernel
p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}
of the form

p_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-y)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda),\quad\text{for all\;}x,y\in\mathbb{R},\;t>0,

where
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}
is the eigenfunction of the Jacobi–Cherednik operator
T_{\alpha,\beta}
and
d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}
is the generalized Plancherel measure.

In
[
3
]
, Bargmann first studied the image of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
under Segal–Bargmann transform as a space of analytic functions, square integrable with some non-negative weight function. Over the years, this topic became popular and have drawn significant attention among many researchers, see, for example,
[
9
,
25
,
21
]
, and references therein. Here, we consider the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
and characterize the image of this space under the Jacobi–Cherednik heat semigroup
\left(P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t},\;t\geq 0\right)
as a reproducing kernel Hilbert space.

Moreover, we obtain a family of Markov processes
\left(X_{t},\;t\geq 0\right)
from the semigroups
\left(P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t},\;t\geq 0\right)
on the real line, which we call Jacobi–Cherednik processes with transition probability densities given by
p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(x,y)A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
. Then, we show that the absolute value
\left(|X_{t}|,\;t\geq 0\right)
of a Jacobi–Cherednik process is a diffusion process with infinitesimal generator

\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\Big[(2\alpha+1)\coth x+(2\beta+1)\tanh x\Big]\frac{d}{dx}+\rho^{2}I.

Finally, we solve the modified Poisson equation
\frac{1}{2}\left(T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}-\rho^{2}\right)u=-f
for all
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present some preliminaries related to the Jacobi–Cherednik operator. In Section
3
, we establish some basic properties of the Jacobi–Cherednik heat kernel and heat semigroup. We also provide a solution to the Cauchy problem for the Jacobi–Cherednik heat operator and prove that the heat kernel is strictly positive. In Section
4
, we characterize the image of the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
under the Jacobi–Cherednik heat semigroup
\left(P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t},\;t\geq 0\right)
as a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Finally, in Section
5
, we introduce the Jacobi–Cherednik–Markov processes and solve the modified Poisson equation.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we give a brief overview of the Jacobi–Cherednik operator and related harmonic analysis. The main references for this section are
[
12
,
17
,
18
,
24
]
. However, we will use the same notation as in
[
19
]
.

Let
T_{\alpha,\beta}
denote the Jacobi–Cherednik differential–difference operator (also called the Dunkl–Cherednik operator)

T_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)=\frac{d}{dx}f(x)+\Big[(2\alpha+1)\coth x+(2\beta+1)\tanh x\Big]\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2}-\rho f(-x),

where
\alpha,\beta
are two parameters satisfying
\alpha\geq\beta\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
, and
\rho=\alpha+\beta+1
.
It can also be written in the form

T_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)=\frac{d}{dx}f(x)+\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}\left(\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2}\right)-\rho f(-x),

where

A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=(\sinh|x|)^{2\alpha+1}(\cosh x)^{2\beta+1}.

Let
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
. The Opdam hypergeometric functions
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}
on
\mathbb{R}
are eigenfunctions
T_{\alpha,\beta}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)=i\lambda G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)
of
T_{\alpha,\beta}
that are normalized such that
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(0)=1
. The eigenfunction
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}
is given by

G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)=\varphi^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)-\frac{1}{\rho-i\lambda}\frac{d}{dx}\varphi^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)=\varphi^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)+\frac{\rho+i\lambda}{4(\alpha+1)}\sinh 2x\;\varphi^{\alpha+1,\beta+1}_{\lambda}(x),

where
\varphi^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)={}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{\rho+i\lambda}{2},\frac{\rho-i\lambda}{2};\alpha+1;-\sinh^{2}x\right)
is the hypergeometric function. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}
is real and strictly positive.

Let
p
and
q
be polynomials of degree
m
and
n
. Then there exists a positive constant
C
such that for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\left|p\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\right)q\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\right|\leq C(1+|x|)^{n}(1+|\lambda|)^{m}e^{-\rho|x|}e^{|\mathrm{Im}\lambda||x|}.

(1)

In particular, for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the eigenfunction

G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}
satisfy

\left|\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\right|\leq C(1+|x|)^{n}e^{-\rho|x|}e^{|\mathrm{Im}\lambda||x|}.

(2)

The function
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{0}
is strictly positive and bounded above by
C(1+x)e^{-\rho x}
, if
x\geq 0
and
Ce^{\rho x}
, if
x\leq 0
. Also, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
|G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)|\leq G^{\alpha,\beta}_{0}(x)
. The hypergeometric functions
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}
satisfy
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(tx)=G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda t}(x)
, for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
x,t\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, for
t=0
, we have
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{0}(x)=G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(0)=1
. For a detailed study of the theory of Opdam hypergeometric functions
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}
, we refer to
[
24
]
.

Let us denote by
C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
the space of continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}
with compact support. The Opdam–Cherednik transform is the Fourier transform in the trigonometric Dunkl setting, and it is defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\alpha\geq\beta\geq-\frac{1}{2}
with
\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
. The Opdam–Cherednik transform
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)
of a function
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
is defined by

{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}.

The inverse Opdam–Cherednik transform for a suitable function
g
on
\mathbb{R}
is given by

{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}(g)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(\lambda)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R},

where

d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)=\left(1-\dfrac{\rho}{i\lambda}\right)\dfrac{d\lambda}{8\pi|C_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)|^{2}}

and

C_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)=\dfrac{2^{\rho-i\lambda}\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(i\lambda)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\rho+i\lambda}{2}\right)\;\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha-\beta+1+i\lambda}{2}\right)},\quad\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus i\mathbb{N}.

The Plancherel formula is given by

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)\overline{{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(\check{f})(-\lambda)}\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda),

(3)

where
\check{f}(x):=f(-x)
. Since
G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(tx)=G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda t}(x)
, for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
x,t\in\mathbb{R}
, using the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha,\beta}
, we obtain
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha,\beta}(\check{f})(-\lambda)=\mathcal{H}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)
. Therefore, we can rewrite the Plancherel formula (
3
) as follows:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathcal{H}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)\right|^{2}d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda).

(4)

Let
L^{p}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
(resp.
L^{p}(\mathbb{R},\sigma_{\alpha,\beta})
),
p\in[1,\infty]
, denote the
L^{p}
-spaces corresponding to the measure
A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx
(resp.
d|\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}|(x)
). The Schwartz space
{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathbb{R})=(\cosh x)^{-\rho}{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R})
is defined as the space of all differentiable functions
f
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\;(1+|x|)^{m}e^{\rho|x|}\left|\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}f(x)\right|<\infty,

for all
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
, equipped with the obvious seminorms. The Opdam–Cherednik transform
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}
and its inverse
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}
are topological isomorphisms between the space
{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
and the space
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R})
(see
[
24
]
, Theorem 4.1).

Let
{\mathcal{S}}^{r}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
be the weighted Schwartz space defined by
{\mathcal{S}}^{r}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathbb{R})=(\cosh x)^{-\frac{\rho}{r}}{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R})
, equipped with the topology induced by the seminorms

\mathcal{N}_{m,n}^{r}(f)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}\\
0\leq k\leq n\end{subarray}}(\cosh x)^{\frac{\rho}{r}}\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{m}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{~d}x^{k}}f(x)\right|<\infty,\quad\text{for \;all\;}m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

For
0<r<1
, let
{\mathcal{S}}\left(\Omega_{\varepsilon(r)}\right)
be the extended Schwartz space of all functions
h
that are analytic in the interior of
\Omega_{\varepsilon(r)}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\;:\;|\operatorname{Im}(z)|\leq\varepsilon(r)\}
, where
\varepsilon(r)=\left(\frac{1}{r}-1\right)\rho
, and such that
h
together with all its derivatives extend continuously to
\Omega_{\varepsilon(r)}
and satisfy

\sup_{\lambda\in\Omega_{\varepsilon(r)}}(1+|\lambda|)^{m}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{~d}\lambda^{n}}h(\lambda)\right|<\infty,\quad\text{for \;all\;}m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

If
r=1
, the space
{\mathcal{S}}\left(\Omega_{\varepsilon(1)}\right)
is the usual Schwartz space
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R})
. For all
0<r\leq 1
, the Opdam–Cherednik transform
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}
is a topological isomorphism between

{\mathcal{S}}^{r}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{S}}\left(\Omega_{\varepsilon(r)}\right).

(5)

Also, the Opdam–Cherednik transform
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}
extends uniquely to an isometric isomorphism from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
onto
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\sigma_{\alpha,\beta})
.

Since
T_{\alpha,\beta}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)=i\lambda G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)
, we have

T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)=i\lambda\;T_{\alpha,\beta}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)=i\lambda\left(i\lambda G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\right)=-\lambda^{2}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x).

(6)

3.
The heat equation for the Jacobi–Cherednik operator

3.1.
The differential–difference operator
T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}

Here, we study the operator
T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
. Let

Df(x)=\frac{d}{dx}f(x),\quad Mf(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2},\quad\text{and}\quad Sf(x)=f(-x).

Then, for
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, using the fact that
\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}}{A_{\alpha,\beta}}
is an odd function, we obtain

\displaystyle T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)=T_{\alpha,\beta}\left(T_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\left[D+\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}}{A_{\alpha,\beta}}\;M-\rho S\right]\left(\frac{d}{dx}f(x)+\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}\;Mf(x)-\rho\;Sf(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}f(x)+\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}}{A_{\alpha,\beta}}\;Mf\right)(x)+\rho f^{\prime}(-x)+\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}Mf^{\prime}(x)-\rho\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}Mf(-x)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}\;M\left(\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}}{A_{\alpha,\beta}}\;Mf\right)(x)-\rho f^{\prime}(-x)+\rho\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}Mf(-x)+\rho^{2}f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}f(x)+\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}}{A_{\alpha,\beta}}\right)(x)Mf(x)+\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}f^{\prime}(x)+\rho^{2}f(x).

(7)

Now, we consider a subspace of
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
consisting of functions satisfying

\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}f(x_{0})+\rho^{2}f(x_{0})\leq 0\quad\text{if}\quad\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}f(x_{0})\leq 0,

(8)

for some point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. We denote this subspace as
C^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R})
, and show that
C^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R})
is non-empty. We first give an example to show that
C^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbb{R})
is non-empty.

Example 3.1
.

Let
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta=-\frac{1}{2}
. Then
\rho=1
. Let us take the Gaussian function
f(x)=e^{-x^{2}}
and
x_{0}=0
. Then
\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}f(x_{0})=-2<0
, and the function
f
satisfies the condition
\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}f(x_{0})+\rho^{2}f(x_{0})=-1<0
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
be open and symmetric. If a real valued function
f\in C^{2}_{\rho}(\Omega)
attains an absolute maximum at
x_{0}\in\Omega
, i.e.,
f(x_{0})=\sup_{x\in\Omega}f(x)
, then
T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}f(x_{0})\leq 0
.

Proof.

Since
f\left(x_{0}\right)=\sup_{x\in\Omega}f(x)
, we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}f\left(x_{0}\right)=0
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{~d}x^{2}}f\left(x_{0}\right)\leq 0
. Also, as
f\in C^{2}_{\rho}(\Omega)
, we get
\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{~d}x^{2}}f(x_{0})+\rho^{2}f(x_{0})\leq 0
. Since
\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}}{A_{\alpha,\beta}}
is a decreasing function, we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}}{A_{\alpha,\beta}}\right)(x_{0})\leq 0
, and
Mf(x_{0})=\frac{f\left(x_{0}\right)-f\left(-x_{0}\right)}{2}\geq 0
. Hence, from (
7
), we get
T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}f(x_{0})\leq 0
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{r}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}f\right)(\lambda)=-\lambda^{2}{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda).

(9)

Proof.

Using the definition of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}
, integrating by parts and change of variables, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(T_{\alpha,\beta}f\right)(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}T_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{d}{dx}f(x)+\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}\left(\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2}\right)-\rho f(-x)\right)G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\left(fA_{\alpha,\beta}\right)(x)-A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)\left(\frac{f(x)+f(-x)}{2}\right)-\rho A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)f(-x)\right)G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\left(\frac{d}{dx}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)+\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}\left(\frac{G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)-G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)}{2}\right)-\rho G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\right)A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\;T_{\alpha,\beta}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx.

Now, using the relation (
6
), we get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}f\right)(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}}T_{\alpha,\beta}f(x)\;T_{\alpha,\beta}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\;T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-x)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda^{2}{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda).

∎

3.2.
The Jacobi–Cherednik heat kernel

In this subsection, we consider the heat equation with Cauchy data for the Heckman–Opdam Laplacian. The Heckman–Opdam Laplacian (or the modified Laplacian)
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}
is defined by

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}-\rho^{2}\right).

The Jacobi–Cherednik heat operator
\mathrm{H}
is defined by

\mathrm{H}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,\beta}

on
C^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
is open and
T>0
. Let
C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
denote the space of continuous and bounded functions on
\mathbb{R}
. Here, we consider the homogeneous Cauchy problem: Given
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
, find
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,T])\cap C(\mathbb{R}\times[0,T])
, such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{H}u=0,\quad\text{on}\;\;\mathbb{R}\times(0,T),\\
u(\cdot,0)=f.\end{array}\right.

(10)

Now, we define the function
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}
by

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}(x)={\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}\left(e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\right)(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\;t>0,

(11)

which is the fundamental solution for the Jacobi–Cherednik heat equation. First, we prove the following proposition.

Theorem 3.7

Let

(c)

(a) To prove the theorem and characterize generators of positive one-parameter contraction semigroups, we use a version of the Hille–Yosida–Phillip theorem (see
[

23

(i)

(ii)

3.2

Corollary 3.8
.

For each

, the Jacobi–Cherednik heat kernel

\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}

From Theorem

(b), the heat kernel

is non-negative on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. For

, the function

is real and strictly positive. Now, we have

Theorem 3.9

(20)

17

4.

For

\displaystyle\left|P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}f(x)\right|

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\left|{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)\right|d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-t\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)\right|^{2}d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)\right)^{1/2}

{\mathbb{C}}
. Therefore,
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
is a subspace of space of all analytic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}
. Further, it is obvious that
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\to P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
is linear and bijective. Hence,
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
can be made into a Hilbert space simply by transferring the Hilbert space structure of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
to
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
so that the Jacobi–Cherednik heat semigroup
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}
is an isometric isomorphism from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
onto
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
. We give the inner product structure in
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
as follows:

\left\langle P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}f,P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}g\right\rangle_{P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)}:=\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})}\quad\text{for}\;f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta}).

Here, we prove that the space
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space.

The convolution product associated with the Opdam–Cherednik transform is defined for two suitable functions
f
and
g
by
[
1
]

(f*_{\alpha,\beta}g)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\;\tau_{x}^{\alpha,\beta}g(-y)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;dy.

Also, we have

{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f*_{\alpha,\beta}g)={\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)\;{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(g).

(22)

From the relation (
11
), we get

{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}\right)(\lambda)=e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\;t>0.

(23)

Now, using (
23
) in the relation (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}\right)(\lambda)\;{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(f*_{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}\right)(\lambda)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}\left({\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(f*_{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}\right)\right)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(f*_{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}\right)(x).

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
,
\left(f*_{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}\right)(x)
has holomorphic extension on
{\mathbb{C}}
, where

\left(f*_{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}\right)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\;\tau_{x}^{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}(-y)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;dy.

Let
\mathcal{O}({\mathbb{C}})
be the space of all analytic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}
. The heat semigroup
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\to\mathcal{O}({\mathbb{C}})
such that
f\mapsto f*_{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}
, is one to one.

Theorem 4.1
.

The space
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel

K^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(z,u)=p^{\alpha,\beta}_{2t}(-z,u),\quad z,u\in{\mathbb{C}}.

Proof.

We give the inner product structure in
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
as follows:

\left\langle F,G\right\rangle_{P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)}:=\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})},

where
f
and
g
are preimages of
F
and
G
respectively under the heat semigroup
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}
. Since
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
is a Hilbert space of analytic functions, the point evaluations are continuous. Therefore
P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. For
F\in P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)
, there exists
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
such that for
u\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we have

\displaystyle F(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(f*_{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}\right)(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\;\tau_{u}^{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}(-y)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\;p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(-u,-y)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle f,\;\overline{p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(-u,-\;\cdot)}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})}.

Since
F(u)=\left\langle F,K^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(\cdot,u)\right\rangle_{P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})\right)}
, we get

\displaystyle K^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(z,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}\left(\overline{p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(-u,-\;\cdot)}\right)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\overline{p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(-u,-\;\cdot)}*_{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}\right)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(-u,-y)}\;\tau_{z}^{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}(-y)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(-u,y)}\;\tau_{z}^{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(-z,y)\;\overline{p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(-u,y)}\;A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(-z,\cdot),\;p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(-u,\cdot)\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}\left(e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-z)\right)(-\;\cdot),\;{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}\left(e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-u)\right)(-\;\cdot)\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-z),\;e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-u)\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\sigma_{\alpha,\beta})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-z)\;e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\;\overline{G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-u)}\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-t\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-z)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-u)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{\alpha,\beta}_{2t}(-z,u).

This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Applications

5.1.
The Jacobi–Cherednik–Markov processes

In this subsection, we give a direct application of the Jacobi–Cherednik heat semigroup in terms of Markov theory and introduce a new family of one-dimensional Markov processes. We define the transition kernels
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}
associate to the heat semigroup
\left(P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t},\;t\geq 0\right)
by

\mathcal{K}^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(x,dy)=p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(x,y)A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)dy,\quad t>0,

(25)

which are probability measures on the Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}}
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t>0
. If a Markov process
X=\left(X_{t},\;t\geq 0\right)
defined on some filtered probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathbb{F},\left(\mathbb{F}_{t},\;t\geq 0\right),\mathbb{P}\right)
has transition kernels given by (
25
), then we call it a Jacobi–Cherednik process of index
(\alpha,\beta)
. Also, the properties of the Jacobi–Cherednik processes follow from the structure of the semigroup
\left(P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t},\;t\geq 0\right)
. Moreover, the absolute value
|X|=\left(|X_{t}|,\;t\geq 0\right)
of a Jacobi–Cherednik process is a diffusion process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In particular, we have the following result.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\left(X_{t},\;t\geq 0\right)
be a Jacobi–Cherednik process on
\mathbb{R}
with transition kernels given by (
25
). Then the absolute value
\left(|X_{t}|,\;t\geq 0\right)
is a diffusion process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with infinitesimal generator the operator

\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}\frac{d}{dx}+\rho^{2}I.

(26)

Proof.

The proof follows from the fact that the restriction of
T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
given by (
7
) on even functions is the operator of the form (
26
).
∎

If
\rho=0
, then the process
\left(|X_{t}|,\;t\geq 0\right)
with generator
\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{A^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}{A_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}\frac{d}{dx}
is called the Jacobi diffusion process of index
(\alpha,\beta)
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
(see
[
7
,
8
]
). Also, the Jacobi process is the radial part of the Brownian motion on a Riemannian symmetric space for some special values of
(\alpha,\beta)
(see
[
8
]
). In particular, for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta=-\frac{1}{2}
the Jacobi process is the hyperbolic-Bessel process (see
[
7
,
22
]
). Moreover, if the Jacobi–Cherednik process has a jump at time
s
, then
X_{s}=-X_{s^{-}}
and this implies that
\left(|X_{t}|,\;t\geq 0\right)
is a continuous process. The Dunkl processes, which are the Markov processes associated with the square of the Dunkl operator
D_{\alpha}
were studied in
[
6
]
. Furthermore, if
\left(P^{\alpha,\beta}_{t},\;t>0\right)
is a Feller-semigroup, then the Jacobi–Cherednik processes have a version with cádlàg trajectories and satisfy the strong Markov property (see
[
22
]
).

5.2.
The modified Poisson equation

Here, we use the results on the heat semigroup to solve the modified Poisson equation
\frac{1}{2}\left(T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}-\rho^{2}\right)u=-f
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
such that
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R},\sigma_{\alpha,\beta})
. Then the function

\mathcal{G}f(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(x,y)f(y)A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;dydt

(27)

is in
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, bounded and satisfies the modified Poisson equation
\frac{1}{2}\left(T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}-\rho^{2}\right)\mathcal{G}f=-f
.

Proof.

First, we prove that
\mathcal{G}f
is well-defined and bounded. For
f\geq 0
, using Proposition
3.5
(a), we get

\mathcal{G}f(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-y)f(y)A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)dydt.

Since
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R},A_{\alpha,\beta})
, using
|G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)|\leq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\left|G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(-y)f(y)A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\right|\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}f(y)A_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)dy<\infty.

Now, using the definition of the Opdam–Cherednik transform and Fubini’s theorem, we have

\mathcal{G}f(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)dt.

(28)

Hence,

|\mathcal{G}f(x)|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)}\left|{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)\right|d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)dt=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left|{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)\right|}{\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}}\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)<\infty.

Therefore,
\mathcal{G}f
is well-defined and bounded on
\mathbb{R}
. Here, we call the operator
\mathcal{G}
the Green operator. Now, applying Fubini’s theorem in (
28
), we obtain

\mathcal{G}f(x)=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}}\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda).

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{G}f(x)=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}}\;T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda).

Using the relation (
6
) and the definition of the inverse Opdam–Cherednik transform, we get

\displaystyle T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}\mathcal{G}f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}}\;\lambda^{2}G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)\;\frac{\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}-\rho^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}}\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)+2\rho^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\;\frac{{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2}}\;G^{\alpha,\beta}_{\lambda}(x)\;d\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2f(x)+\rho^{2}\mathcal{G}f(x).

Hence, the function
\mathcal{G}f
satisfies the modified Poisson equation
\frac{1}{2}\left(T^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}-\rho^{2}\right)\mathcal{G}f=-f
.
∎
