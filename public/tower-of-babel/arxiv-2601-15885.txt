Fermion Doubling in Dirac Quantum Walks
Chaitanya Gupta, Anthony J. Short

Abstract
We consider discrete spacetime models known as quantum walks, which can be used to simulate Dirac particles. In particular we look at fermion doubling in these models, in which high momentum states yield additional low energy solutions which behave like Dirac particles. The presence of doublers carries over to the ‘second quantised’ version of the walks represented by quantum cellular automata, which may lead to spurious solutions when introducing interactions. Moreover, we also consider pseudo-doublers, which have high energy but behave like low energy Dirac particles, and cause potential problems regarding the stability of the vacuum. To address these issues, we propose a family of quantum walks, that are free of these doublers and pseudo-doublers, but still simulate the Dirac equation in the continuum limit. However, there remain a small number of additional low energy solutions which do not directly correspond to Dirac particles. While the conventional Dirac walk always has a zero-probability for the walker staying at the same point, we obtain the family of walks by allowing this probability to be non-zero.



I
Introduction

Quantum Cellular Automata (QCA) and Quantum Walks (QW) have been shown to provide a discrete spacetime model of quantum field theories, with the latter being a single particle limit of the former
11
;
9
;
5
. While these models provide an interesting avenue for quantum simulations of particle physics, they may also provide insight into physical models of reality itself —if reality is indeed discrete. Of particular interest are discrete models that are able to simulate free Dirac particles. In particular, the Dirac QW is able to simulate free Dirac particles in the continuum limit
17
;
3
;
10
. Its ‘second quantised’ cousin, the Dirac QCA simulates free Dirac particles at a field theoretical level
7
;
4
;
9
. Recent papers have introduced interactions to the Dirac QCA to simulate other theories such as QED
1
;
8
. Moreover, renormalisation of QCA models has also been studied, with the potential application of simulating more phenomena and reducing the complexity of existing models
19
;
20
.

Now, similarly to other discrete theories
16
, if we were to attempt scattering calculations using these models, we would be likely to run into trouble if there was any fermion doubling. By fermion doubling, we mean the presence of high momentum particles that have low energy, and behave like additional species of Dirac fermions in the continuum limit.

If we look at the Dirac QW in 1+1-D, we do not get fermion doubling
2
, but nevertheless, find high momentum particles that behave like additional species of Dirac particles in the continuum limit but do not have low energy. We call these particles pseudo-doublers as they do not have the same energy as the low-momentum particles, hence, are not doublers in a traditional sense. Moreover, in the 3+1-D walk, in addition to the presence of these pseudo-doublers, we also have fermion doubling in a more traditional sense. We argued in Ref.
12
that pseudo-doublers are likely to cause problems in interacting models by making the vacuum state highly unstable.

In this work, we present a new family of quantum walks for 1+1D and 3+1D, which yield the Dirac equation in the continuum limit, with the aim of eliminating fermion doubling and pseudo-doubling.
We do so by constructing a very general unitary which moves a particle by at most one lattice site in each direction in a single time step. Now, in the conventional walk, the probability of the particle to stay where it is always zero. We relax this condition to obtain a broader category of walks. By choosing appropriate values of a parameter, we find that, we are able to get rid of all fermion doubling and pseudo-doubling. However, in 3+1-D, the walk still has some extraneous low-energy solutions which do not act as traditional fermion doublers.

There has been some previous interest in the literature in exploring doubling in Dirac quantum walks. Ref.
14
explored an interesting family of twisted quantum walks in 1+1-D, in which a particle moves multiple lattice sites in a single time-step, that yields both the Dirac equation and a Laplacian operator in the continuum walk. These walks admitted fermion doubling, in a manner that is very similar to lattice field theories, and the work suggested an approach to solve the doubling by modifying the walks. Moreover, Ref.
2
explored the doubling in a Dirac walk, and suggested interpreting the doublers as different flavours. While, it would be interesting to explore such an approach, it is still not clear how such an identification would lead to correct interactions in the continuum theory.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
II
, we introduce the conventional Dirac QWs in 1+1-D and 3+1-D and discuss the presence of doublers and pseudo-doublers. In Section
III
, we introduce the parametrised family of Dirac QWs in 1+1-D and 3+1-D which reduce the doublers and pseudo-doublers. We have provided the details of the QCA based on these walks in the Appendix. Note that, throughout the paper, we shall set
\hbar=1
, and let
c
be the speed of light.

Fermion Doubling in Conventional Dirac Walks

To consider the continuum limit of this walk, we define momentum eigenstates and the momentum operator as

\!\ket{p}=\sqrt{\frac{\delta x}{2\pi}}\sum_{n}e^{ipn\,\delta x}\ket{n}\,\text{and}\,P=\!\int_{-\pi/\delta x}^{\pi/\delta x}\!\!dp\,p\outerproduct{p}{p},

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{\uparrow_z}{\uparrow_z}e^{-iP\,\delta x}+\outerproduct{\downarrow_z}{\downarrow_z}e^{iP\,\delta x}

\displaystyle=\text{Exp}\!\left[-iP\sigma_{z}\delta x\right].

\sigma_{z}\equiv\outerproduct{\uparrow_z}{\uparrow_z}-\outerproduct{\downarrow_z}{\downarrow_z}

Now, let us look at the continuum limit. Consider a state
\ket{\psi_p}=\ket{p}\otimes\ket{s}

and
\alpha\delta t\ll 1

\displaystyle U\ket{\psi_p}

\displaystyle=WT\ket{\psi_p}

\displaystyle=\text{Exp}\!\left[-iM\delta t\right]\text{Exp}\!\left[-iP\sigma_{z}\delta t\right]\ket{p}\ket{s}

\displaystyle=\text{Exp}\!\left[-iM\delta t\right]\text{Exp}\!\left[-ip\sigma_{z}\delta t\right]\ket{p}\ket{s}

\displaystyle=(1-iM\delta t+\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{2}))(1-ip\sigma_{z}\delta x+\mathcal{O}(\delta x^{2}))\ket{p}\ket{s}

\displaystyle=(1-iM\delta t-ip\sigma_{z}\delta x+\mathcal{O}(\delta x^{2},\delta x\delta t,\delta t^{2}))\ket{p}\ket{s}

\displaystyle=(1-iM\delta t-iP\sigma_{z}\delta x+\mathcal{O}(\delta x^{2},\delta x\delta t,\delta t^{2}))\ket{\psi_p}.

(7)

Looking at the expansion, we can write an effective Hamiltonian as,

H_{\text{eff}}=M+Pv\sigma_{z}.

(8)

in the continuum limit where
v\equiv\delta{x}/\delta{t}
, such that

U\ket{\psi_p}=e^{-iH_{\text{eff}}\delta t}\ket{\psi_p}+\mathcal{O}(\delta x^{2},\delta x\delta t,\delta t^{2}).

(9)

Now, with
\sigma_{x}\equiv\outerproduct{\uparrow_z}{\downarrow_z}+\outerproduct{\downarrow_z}{\uparrow_z}

and
v=c

m

H_{\text{eff}}=mc^{2}\sigma_{x}+Pc\sigma_{z}.

(10)

which is the 1+1-D Dirac Hamiltonian. Moreover, with the above choice of

U
describes the conventional Dirac QW.

While we have defined a notion of an effective Hamiltonian for only small momentum and mass, we can define the energy of the particle in general. Consider the operator

\displaystyle U(p)

\displaystyle\equiv\text{Exp}\!\left[-ip\sigma_{z}\delta x\right]\text{Exp}\!\left[-iM\delta t\right]

(11)

acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}
. Let
\ket{s_p}
be eigenstates of
U(p)
with the respective eigenvalues being
e^{-iE_{p}\delta t}
. Restricting
E_{p}
in
(-\pi/\delta t,\pi/\delta t]
, and thereby choosing a branch cut, we can identify
E_{p}
as the possible energy eigenvalues of a particle in the energy eigenstate
\ket{p}\ket{s_p}
. Moreover, because of the unitary equivalence of
U
to
U^{\dagger}
, for every energy eigenvalue
E_{p}>0
,
-E_{p}<0
is also a possible energy eigenvalue of the walker.

Now, having defined a notion of energy for all momentum, and having seen that the walk behaves like a Dirac particle for small momentum (for a choice of
v
and
M
), we wish to know if the walk behaves like Dirac particles at any other point in momentum space.

Let us consider
p=\pm\pi/\delta x+\eta
. We have,

U(\pm\pi/\delta x+\eta)=-U(\eta).

(12)

As this differs from
U(\eta)
only by a global phase, particles with momentum in a small ball around
\pm\pi/\delta x
with
\alpha\delta x\ll 1
also behave like low-momentum particles, as was pointed out in
12
;
2
. However, they have energy near
E=\pm\pi/\delta t
. As their energy is different from low energy particles, we do not exactly have fermion doubling. We call these particles pseudo-doublers.

Going back to our model, we note that, for the appropriate choice of
\delta t
and
M
, particles near both
p=0
(
E=0
) and
p=\pm\pi/\delta x
(
E=\pm\pi/\delta t
) behave like Dirac particles. If were to second quantise this model to a QCA, the presence of these pseudo-doublers carries over (See Appendix
G
for details of the QCA). In
12
, we had argued that, the presence of such solutions causes the Dirac vacuum to be unstable.

II.2
3+1-D Conventional Dirac Walk

Let us now look at a higher dimensional case. We shall start by constructing the Weyl walk, and then introduce a mass term to yield the Dirac walk. Consider a 3-D cubic lattice with the lattice spacing being
\delta x
. A particle lives on the lattice and is described by the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{Z}^{3}}
with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{Z}^{3}}=\text{span}\{\ket{\vec{n}}|\vec{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
as before.

We wish to define a quantum walk describing the evolution of the particle. To that goal, let
\gamma^{j}_{\mu}
be linear operators on
\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}
with
\mu\in\{+,0,-\}
and
j\in\{x,y,z\}
. Then, we let

K=K_{z}K_{y}K_{x}

(13)

define a walk which describes the evolution of the system in time step
\delta t
, and where

\displaystyle K_{j}=\gamma^{j}_{+}S_{j}+\gamma^{j}_{0}+\gamma^{j}_{-}S_{j}^{\dagger}

(14)

are operators on
\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{Z}^{3}}
. Here,
S_{j}
is a shift operator in the
j
-direction; for instance,
S_{x}\ket{(n_x,n_y,n_z)}=\ket{(n_x+1,n_y,n_z)}
.

Again,
K
is local and translationally invariant. We now wish to pick our
\gamma_{\mu}^{j}
to ensure unitarity of
K
. This can be done by ensuring that each
K_{j}
is unitary. A common choice in literature is to pick
9

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}^{j}=\outerproduct{\uparrow_j}{\uparrow_j},\gamma_{-}^{j}=\outerproduct{\downarrow_j}{\downarrow_j},\text{ and }\gamma_{0}^{j}=0

(15)

where
\ket{\uparrow_j}
and
\ket{\downarrow_j}
are defined via
\sigma_{j}\ket{\uparrow_j}=\ket{\uparrow_j}
and
\sigma_{j}\ket{\downarrow_j}=-\ket{\downarrow_j}
. Here,
\sigma_{y}\equiv-i\ket{\uparrow_z}\bra{\downarrow_z}+i\ket{\downarrow_z}\bra{\uparrow_z}
.
With this choice,
K
becomes the Weyl walk in 3+1-D.

Let us now go to momentum space. We define the momentum eigenstates and the momentum operators as

\displaystyle\ket{\vec{p}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\delta x}{2\pi}\right)^{\tfrac{3}{2}}\sum_{\vec{n}}\text{Exp}\!\left[i{\vec{p}}\cdot\vec{n}\,\delta x\right]\ket{\vec{n}}\;\text{and}\;

\displaystyle\vec{P}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}d^{3}p\,\vec{p}\outerproduct{\vec{p}}{\vec{p}},

(16)

where

\Theta=(-\pi/\delta x,\pi/\delta x]^{3}

(17)

and
\vec{P}
is understood to be a vector of operators.

We can now write the shift operators as
S_{j}=\text{Exp}\!\left[-iP_{j}\delta r_{j}\right]
. This allows us to rewrite
K_{j}
as

\displaystyle K_{j}

\displaystyle=\text{Exp}\!\left[-i\vec{P}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\delta x\right].

(18)

where
\vec{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})^{\mathbf{T}}
.

Then, we have,

K=\text{Exp}\!\left[-iP_{z}\sigma_{z}\delta x\right]\text{Exp}\!\left[-iP_{y}\sigma_{y}\delta x\right]\text{Exp}\!\left[-iP_{z}\sigma_{z}\delta x\right].

(19)

Let us now look at the continuum limit. Similar to the 1+1-D case, we consider
\ket{\psi_\vp}=\ket{\vec{p}}\ket{s}
where
{\vec{p}}\in\Theta
and
\ket{s}\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}
. Then, assuming that

\displaystyle K_{z}K_{y}K_{z}\ket{\psi_\vp}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j}\text{Exp}\!\left[-iP_{j}\sigma_{j}\delta x\right]\ket{\vp}\ket{s}

Again, this allows to define an effective Hamiltonian for this regime,

where

, we get the Weyl equation.

We can again define energy

\text{Exp}\!\left[-iE_{{\vec{p}}}\delta t\right]

K({\vec{p}})

K({\vec{p}})=\text{Exp}\!\left[-ip_{z}\sigma_{z}\delta x\right]\text{Exp}\!\left[-ip_{y}\sigma_{y}\delta x\right]\text{Exp}\!\left[-ip_{x}\sigma_{x}\delta x\right].

(22)

Having looked at its low-momentum limit, we wish to know if for any other

{\vec{p}}=(\pm\pi/\delta x+\eta,\pm\pi/\delta x+\eta,0)

(23)

Thus, particles near the point above also behave like low-momentum particles and have energy near

. This is an example of fermion doubling.

Also, for
{\vec{p}}=(\pm\pi/\delta x+\eta,\pm\pi/\delta x+\eta,\pm\pi/\delta x+\eta)
and
{\vec{p}}=(\pm\pi/\delta x+\eta,0,0)
with its respective permutations, we have

K({\vec{p}}+\eta)=-K(\eta).

(24)

Thus, particles near this point also behave like low-momentum particles but have energy near
\pm\pi/\delta t

We also have another set of points

We now want to introduce a mass term. To do so, we need to double the internal space. Let the particle’s state be now described by
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{int}}

\mathcal{H}_{\text{int}}=\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}^{\otimes 2}\cong\mathbb{C}^{4}

Then, introduce the unitary,

W=\text{Exp}\!\left[-imc^{2}\beta\,\delta t\right]

where

\beta=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{I}\\

(26)

and

is the mass of the particle.
Then, the walk,

U=W\begin{pmatrix}K_{z}K_{y}K_{x}&0\\

describes the 3+1-D Dirac walk

;

In the continuum limit (which can be obtained by considering a state with definite momentum
{\vec{p}}
such that

), we have

\displaystyle H_{\text{eff}}=\begin{pmatrix}\vec{P}\cdot\vec{\sigma}v&\!\!\!mc^{2}\\

Thus, again setting

Similar to previous cases, we can write

We have the same doublers and pseudo-doublers as in the case of the Weyl walk except for the one at
{\vec{p}}=(\pm\pi/2\delta x,\pm\pi/2\delta x,\pm\pi/2\delta x)
. Now, at this point, the Dirac walk does not have a good continuum limit. While we do not consider it as a doubler or a pseudo-doubler, its energy eigenvalues do lie near both

(supposing small

Having presented these walks, we note that we can second quantise these walks to have a QCA model (Appendix

). It is highlighted in

We also expect the normal doublers to interfere with any scattering calculations. We shall now construct a new walk to get rid of both the doublers and pseudo-doublers.

IV
Discussion

The QWs presented here can be second quantised to obtain their respective QCA versions (See Appendix
G
). We can also add further interactions to such models. For instance, we provide an interacting model constructed from the family of quantum walks in Appendix
H
. In particular, we introduce electric interactions in 1+1-D, thereby developing a QCA version of the Schwinger model by following Ref.
1
.

The notion of doublers and pseudo-doublers carries over to the respective QCA models. In particular, there are pseudo-doublers, particles that behave like low-momentum particles, but with energy near
\pm\pi/\delta t
in the conventional Dirac QCA. We had pointed out in Ref.
12
that these pseudo-doublers, for the conventional Dirac QCAs, are likely to make the vacuum state highly unstable if we were to introduce interactions.

For the Dirac field, we can define vacuum state by filling up all the negative energy states
5
. All the filled negative energy states form what is called the Dirac sea. Unlike the continuum limit, where there is only one boundary between the Dirac sea and empty positive energy states, in the discrete theory, we have two boundaries between the Dirac sea and the positive energy states —
E=0
and
E=\pm\pi/\delta t
. In the conventional Dirac QCA, if we were to introduce some interactions, we would expect high energy pair creation to take place near the
E=\pm\pi/\delta t
boundary due to the presence of pseudo-doublers there. Moreover, particles with momentum close to
p_{j}=\pm\pi/(2\delta x)
in 3+1-D, also have energy near both the boundaries, and thus may also contribute to the pair creation.

We can use the family of Dirac QCAs to solve this problem. By choosing an appropriate value of
\theta
, we can make sure that there are no energy eigenvalues near
\pm\pi/\delta t
. Using the results about the eigenvalues of a product of unitaries discussed in Appendix
D
, we show, in Appendix
E
, that we can choose a
\theta
such that
\absolutevalue{E_p\delta t}\leq\pi/2
if given small enough values of mass. In Figure
2
and Figure
5
, one can see that the energy eigenvalues strictly lie between
-\pi/2\delta x
and
\pi/2\delta x
.

In our previous work, we had introduced a modified quantum walk that also resolved this issue. However, while this walk got rid of any pseudo-doublers and other particles near
E=\pm\pi/\delta t
, it had introduced more fermion doubling near
E=0
. On the contrary, the family of walks, that we have presented in this paper, are mostly free of both doublers and pseudo-doublers (for clever choices of
\theta\neq 0
).

Although, these walks are not free of all extraneous low-energy solutions. Considering the 3+1-D family of Dirac walks, for each value of
\theta
, note that we have have two extraneous low energy solutions (
\pm\vec{q}(\theta)
). Note that while we have constructed the 1+1-D walk in a very general manner, we have constructed the 3+1-D walk by simply combining the 1+1-D walks in each direction. We might be able to get rid of any extraneous low-energy solutions by considering a more general construction in higher dimensions. This could be an avenue to explore in a future work.

Finally, we have based our argument on free theories. For future work, it would be interesting to explore scattering calculations in explicit interaction models
6
;
8
, including the one in Appendix
H
, to have a closer look at the issues caused by the presence of these doublers and pseudo-doublers.

Appendix A
Fermion Doubling in 3+1-D at

Consider the 3+1-D conventional Weyl walk from Section
II.2
. We have,

K_{j}(\pm\pi/2\delta x+\eta)=\mp i\sigma_{j}K_{j}(\eta)

Power expanding for

\displaystyle K_{j}(\pm\pi/2\delta x+\eta)

\displaystyle=\mp i\sigma_{j}(I-i\eta\sigma_{j}\delta x)+\mathcal{O}(\delta x^{2})

\displaystyle=\mp(i\sigma_{j}+\eta\delta x)+\mathcal{O}(\delta x^{2}).

\pm\pi/\delta t

We also have, for
{\vec{p}}=(-\pi/2\delta x,-\pi/2\delta x,-\pi/2\delta x)^{\mathbb{T}}
or
{\vec{p}}=(-\pi/2\delta x,\pi/2\delta x,\pi/2\delta x)^{\mathbb{T}}
(including all the permutations),

\displaystyle K({\vec{p}}+\eta)

\displaystyle=(I+i\vec{\eta}\!\cdot\vec{\sigma}\,^{\prime\prime}\delta x)+\mathcal{O}(\delta x^{2}).

(52)

Thus, these points behave like Weyl particles of opposite chirality, with energy near
0
, and hence we could consider them doublers.

Let us now look at the Dirac walk. With
\ket{\psi_p}
as an energy eigenstate, for
{\vec{p}}=(\pi/2\delta x,\pi/2\delta x,\pi/2\delta x)^{\mathbb{T}}
,

\displaystyle T\ket{\psi_p}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}K_{z}K_{y}K_{x}&0\\
0&K_{z}^{\dagger}K_{y}^{\dagger}K_{x}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\!\ket{\psi_p}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{pmatrix}-I+i\vec{p}\!\cdot\vec{\sigma}\,^{\prime}\delta x&0\\
0&I+i\vec{p}\!\cdot\vec{\sigma}\,^{\prime}\delta x\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(\delta x^{2})\right)\!\ket{\psi_p}.

Then, with the mass term
W
, we have,

\displaystyle WT\ket{\psi_p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\left[\begin{pmatrix}-I+i\vec{p}\!\cdot\vec{\sigma}\,^{\prime}\delta x&-imc^{2}\delta t\\
-imc^{2}\delta t&I+i\vec{p}\!\cdot\vec{\sigma}\,^{\prime}\delta x\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(\delta x^{2},\delta x\delta t,\delta t^{2})\right]\!\ket{\psi_p}.

We get a similar expression for the other points. We do not have a good continuum limit around these point for the massive case.

Fermion Doubling in the Weyl Walks

We shall consider the family of Weyl walks,
K^{+}=K_{z}K_{y}K_{x}
and
K^{-}=K_{z}^{\dagger}K_{y}^{\dagger}K_{x}^{\dagger}
from section
III
at the point
\vec{q}=q(1,,1,1)^{\mathbf{T}}
where

\tan(q(\theta) \delta x/2)=\frac{1}{\cos(\theta)+\sin(\theta)}.

(
44
)

First, note that from Eq. (
67
), we can write
K_{z}(p)
as

\displaystyle K_{z}(p)

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}(p\delta x/2)-i\sin(p\delta x)\cos(\theta)\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-e^{-i2\theta\sigma_{x}}\sin^{2}(p\delta x/2).

(79)

Then, after some algebra, we can write
K_{z}(q(\theta))
as

\displaystyle K_{z}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+cs}\left(s(s+c)-ic(s+c)\sigma_{z}+isc\sigma_{x}\right).

where

and

.

We can similarly write
K_{y}(q(\theta))
and
K_{x}(q(\theta))

\displaystyle K_{y}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+cs}\left(s(s+c)-ic(s+c)\sigma_{y}+isc\sigma_{z}\right).

(81a)

\displaystyle K_{x}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+cs}\left(s(s+c)-ic(s+c)\sigma_{x}+isc\sigma_{y}\right).

(81b)

Then, we can verify that for any
\theta
,

K^{+}(\vec{q}(\theta))=K_{z}(q)K_{y}(q)K_{x}(q)=I.

(82)

Thus, we do have a good continuum limit at this point.

Let us now find the effective Hamiltonian.
With

K_{j}=\text{Exp}\!\left[-iP_{j}\sigma^{j}_{\theta}\delta x/2\right]\text{Exp}\!\left[-iP_{j}\sigma^{j}_{-\theta}\delta x/2\right],

(83)

we get, for
\eta_{j}\delta x\ll 1
,

K_{j}(p_{j}+\eta_{j})\approx K_{j}(p_{j})-\frac{i}{2}\eta_{j}\delta x(\sigma_{\theta}^{j}T_{j}(p_{j})+T_{j}(p_{j})\sigma_{-\theta}^{j})

(84)

upto first order.

Then, for
K(\vec{p}+\vec{\eta})
, we get

\displaystyle K^{+}(\vec{p}+\vec{\eta})

\displaystyle\approx K_{z}(p)K_{y}(p)K_{x}(p)

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\eta_{x}\delta xK_{z}(p_{z})K_{y}(p_{y})(\sigma_{\theta}^{x}K_{x}(p_{x})+K_{x}(p_{x})\sigma_{-\theta}^{x})

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\eta_{y}\delta xK_{z}(p_{z})(\sigma_{\theta}^{y}K_{y}(p_{y})+K_{y}(p_{y})\sigma_{-\theta}^{y})K_{x}(p_{x})

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\eta_{z}\delta x(\sigma_{\theta}^{z}K_{z}(p_{z})+K_{z}(p_{z})\sigma_{-\theta}^{z})K_{y}(p_{y})K_{x}(p_{x}).

(85)

Now, consider
\vec{p}=\vec{q}(\theta)
. Using
K_{z}(q)K_{y}(q)K_{x}(q)=I

\displaystyle K^{+}(\vec{q}+\vec{\eta})\approx I

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\eta_{x}\delta x(K_{x}^{\dagger}(q_{x})\sigma_{\theta}^{x}K_{x}(q_{x})+\sigma_{-\theta}^{x})

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\eta_{y}\delta x(K_{z}(q_{z})\sigma_{\theta}^{y}K_{z}^{\dagger}(q_{x})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+K^{\dagger}_{x}(q_{x})\sigma_{-\theta}^{y}K_{x}(q_{x}))

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\eta_{z}\delta x(\sigma_{\theta}^{z}+K_{z}(q_{z})\sigma_{-\theta}^{z}K_{z}^{\dagger}(q_{z})).

(86)

We can rewrite the above equation, compactly, as

K^{+}(\vec{q}+\vec{\eta})\approx I-i\cos(\theta)\vec{\eta}\cdot\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}\delta x

(87)

where

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{x}

\displaystyle=(K_{x}^{\dagger}(q_{x})\sigma_{\theta}^{x}K_{x}(q_{x})+\sigma_{-\theta}^{x})/\cos(\theta),

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{y}

\displaystyle=(K_{z}(q_{z})\sigma_{\theta}^{y}K_{z}^{\dagger}(q_{x})+K^{\dagger}_{x}(q_{x})\sigma_{-\theta}^{y}K_{x}(q_{x}))/\cos(\theta),

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{z}

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{\theta}^{z}+K_{z}(q_{z})\sigma_{-\theta}^{z}K_{z}^{\dagger}(q_{z}))/\cos(\theta).

(88)

Using a computer algebra system, we verified that

. Thus,
\{\sigma^{\prime}_{j}\}
are an alternative representation of Pauli operators.

Now, consider
K^{-}

K^{-}(-\vec{q}+\vec{\eta})\approx I+i\cos(\theta)\vec{\eta}\cdot\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}\delta x.

(89)

Thus, we can see from Eqs. (
87

89

behave like a Weyl particle at

Are there any other doublers for the Weyl walks

\theta\neq 0

Appendix H
Interacting Quantum Cellular Automata: Schwinger Model

We shall now the use the framework of Ref.
1
to introduce electromagnetic interactions to the Dirac QCAs in 1+1-D. We expect the model to describe quantum electrodynamics (QED) in 1+1-D, also known as the Schwinger model.

Let us start with the model in the previous section. Let us define the unitary
G_{\alpha}
where
\alpha:\mathbb{Z}\to[0,2\pi)
to implement gauge transformations as follows,

G_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\psi_{n}^{a}G_{\alpha}=\psi_{n}^{a}e^{i\alpha_{n}}.

(101)

Clearly,
T
as defined in Eq. (
91
) is not gauge invariant, i.e.,
[G_{\alpha},T]\neq 0
in general.

We wish to construct a unitary that is gauge invariant. To this, goal, we at each consecutive lattice sites labelled by
n\in\mathbb{Z}+1/2
. At each link, we introduce a Hilbert space spanned by orthonormal basis
\{\ket{l}_{n},l\in\mathbb{Z}
}. We define the operators
E_{n}
and
V_{n}
implicitly via
E_{n}\ket{l}_{n}=l\ket{l}_{n}
and
[V_{n},E_{n}]=V_{n}
. Note that the operators on different lattice sites commute with each other.

We define the gauge transformation on
\ket{l}_{n}
as

G_{\alpha}\ket{l}_{n}=\text{Exp}\!\left[il(\alpha_{n+1}-\alpha_{n})\right]\ket{l}_{n}.

(102)

Now, we construct the following gauge invariant operators,

\bar{\psi}^{a}_{n}=\psi^{a}_{n}\prod_{m>n}V_{m}.

(103)

We can verify that
[\psi^{a}_{n},G_{\alpha}]=0
for all
\alpha
.

Now, wish to construct a gauge-invariant QCA. We do so by constructing it using gauge invariant operators —
\bar{\psi}^{a}_{n}
and
E_{n}
.

Let us now define a gauge-invariant unitary
\widetilde{T}
.

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{n}\widetilde{T}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{a}\Pi_{b}\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{n+1}+(\Pi_{a}\Pi_{\bar{b}}

\displaystyle+\Pi_{\bar{a}}\Pi_{{b}})\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{n}+\Pi_{\bar{a}}\Pi_{\bar{b}}\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{n-1}.

(104)

Rewriting,

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{n}\widetilde{T}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{a}\Pi_{b}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{n+1}V_{n+1}^{\dagger}+(\Pi_{a}\Pi_{\bar{b}}

\displaystyle+\Pi_{\bar{a}}\Pi_{{b}})\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{n}+\Pi_{\bar{a}}\Pi_{\bar{b}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{n-1}V_{n},

(105)

we can see that it is indeed local.

Now, note that
W
, as defined before, is indeed gauge-invariant. We can rewrite
W
by replacing the unbarred operators in its definition with barred operators to make its gauge invariance explicit.

Consider
D_{\text{Dirac}}=W\widetilde{T}
. This is a gauge-invariant version of the Dirac QCAs. With a choice of the projection operators, we can obtain a gauge-invariant version of either the conventional Dirac QCA or the more general family of Dirac QCAs.

Now, so far, we have not introduced interactions. Introducing a kinetic term for the electric field induces interactions;

D_{E}=\bigotimes_{n\in\mathbb{Z}+1/2}\text{Exp}\!\left[-i\frac{g^{2}c^{2}\delta t^{3}E_{n}^{2}}{2\epsilon_{0}\delta x}\right]

(106)

where
g
is the charge of the fermionic particle and
\epsilon_{0}
the permittivity of free space.

Then,

D=D_{\text{Dirac}}D_{E}

(107)

defines a gauge-invariant QCA with electric interactions in 1+1-D. Thus, we have obtained a family of QCAs that are a discrete spacetime version of the Schwinger model. This is a generalisation of the QCA presented in Ref.
1
.

Finally, we must discuss Gauss’s law. Consider the following operator

\displaystyle J_{n}

\displaystyle=E_{n+1/2}-E_{n-1/2}-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{n}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{n}

\displaystyle=E_{n+1/2}-E_{n-1/2}-\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{n}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\bar{\Psi}}_{n}.

(108)

In the Schrödinger picture, we demand

J_{n}\ket{\text{phys}}=0

(109)

for any physical state
\ket{\text{phys}}
. Ensuring that the initial state is physical is sufficient. This is because
J_{n}
is the generator of gauge transformations. By demanding that our QCA is gauge invariant, we have already insured that the unitary defining the QCA commutes with
J_{n}
.

Finally, note that, to implement this QCA on a physical system, we can restrict
l
to be between
-L
and
L
where
L
is some integer.
