Decarbonizing surgical care: a qualitative systematic review guided by the Congruence Model
Authors: Leonard Kloevekorn, Oskar Roemeling, Amal Fakha, Eveline Hage, Edin Smailhodzic
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe healthcare sector must navigate the challenge of caring for individuals affected by climate change while being a significant emitter of carbon emissions. This study focuses on direct sources of carbon emissions from hospital surgical care, a major contributor to the sector’s overall carbon footprint. The goal is to identify the main sources of direct carbon emissions in surgical care and to analyze these sources according to the Congruence Model. We employ the Congruence Model for a systemic analysis of emission sources within the organizational context. The change-oriented model examines (in)congruences across the domains of People, Work, Culture, and Structure, aiding in the understanding of organizational change.MethodsA qualitative systematic literature review was conducted following PRISMA guidelines, covering three extensive databases: PubMed, Business Source Premier, and EBSCOhost. The review provides a broad perspective on the topic under study. The qualitative analysis is guided by the Congruence Model, which serves as a conceptual lens to analyze and interpret the findings.ResultsThe study offers a comprehensive overview of research focused on emissions related to surgery. Hotspots of carbon emissions in surgical care, such as anesthetic gases, sterilization procedures, and habitual behaviors, are linked with the Work and People elements of the Congruence Model. Additionally, sustainability measures are predominantly associated with the Structural element of the Congruence Model, including policies for recycling and waste segregation. The research reflects on the (in)congruencies between different factors of the Congruence Model and actions related to reducing carbon emissions.ConclusionsThe Congruence Model provides a useful conceptual lens to categorize the sources of carbon emissions in terms of People, Work, Structure and Culture. In addition, the Congruence Model allows us to explore the (in)congruencies between these functional elements. Ultimately, our research identifies opportunities to improve carbon emissions related to the surgical care process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11929-6.Keywords: Literature review, Surgery, Hospital, Congruence model, Carbon emission, Greenhouse gasses, Sustainability, Healthcare

Background

Climate change, rising temperatures, destroyed biosphere, and a more frequent occurrence of natural disasters have far-reaching negative impacts on various ecological, environmental, socio-economic, and socio-political disciplines [1]. Despite the increased societal, political, and economic awareness in recent years, addressing climate change remains an issue of uttermost importance [1, 2]. Particularly the increase in the emission of greenhouse gasses (GHG) from human activity fosters climatic transformation and is thus a paramount issue [3]. Carbon emissions serve as a measurement tool of the total GHG directly or indirectly caused by an individual or organization and thus provide a good indication for understanding the respective climate impact [4].

Climate change has direct negative impacts not only on the environment but also on human health. The heat-related mortality has risen due to human-induced climate change [5]. Relatedly, extreme weather events, the rise of infectious diseases, and water scarcity will result in increased need for and utilization of the healthcare system in the coming years [6, 7]. In this light, healthcare institutions are traditionally seen as places of treatment and recovery. However, the healthcare sector itself significantly contributes to climate change through its energy and resource intensive activities [8–11].

Especially in developed countries, the impact of healthcare on carbon emissions is significant. In the United States, approximately 8% of the country’s total carbon emissions originate from healthcare. Similarly numbers can be found in the Netherlands with 4% to 8% and Australia with 7%. In addition, studies indicate that globally healthcare accounts for approximately 4% to 5% of the total carbon emissions [12–14].

Within the healthcare sector, particularly hospitals are substantial emitters of GHG due to highly energy-intensive operations, extensive waste generation, and resource-heavy practices [15]. Here, research suggests that 30–40% of all carbon emissions in the healthcare sector originate from hospitals [16]. Thus, in addressing the carbon footprint of the healthcare sector, it is imperative to assess their role. By identifying the sources of carbon emissions in hospitals and through implementing practices that aim to lower this impact, hospitals can make a significant contribution towards achieving climate-related milestones.

With this study, we aim to better understand where in the hospital care process emissions are created, and how various organizational components facilitate or contribute to emission production in order to provide input for reducing hospital emissions [9, 14]. In essence, it seems that the existing body of research has had limited attention for the core care process in hospitals, i.e. the action of delivering or providing care to the patient in the hospital setting. In our current study, we choose to focus on the clinical care process itself. As clinical activities account for a significant share of hospitals' carbon emissions [14]. More specifically, we zoom in on specific hospital domains and related care processes, as this is likely to provide a deeper understanding of GHG emissions within the hospital setting.

When thinking of care processes, the surgical department particularly stands out among all hospital units as a typical clinical environment, and even more so when considering its carbon footprint [8, 9, 17]. Whilst surgical operations are widely accepted as a necessity, their significant environmental impact often lacks a critical evaluation [18]. Research suggests that the operating room (OR) is among the most energy-intensive and waste-generating departments of the hospital [8, 10, 19]. Moreover, it should be noted that the OR has an important role in creating revenue for hospitals, but at the same time is a high-cost department. As such, it is possible that efforts in reducing GHG will reduce OR expenses. As with other departments, research on the carbon footprint of the OR has predominately focused on the energy intensity and the life cycle analysis of medical products [20, 21]. It seems that research on patient care-specific sources of carbon emissions, which for example include emissions related to the use of anesthesia agents or sterilization processes, amongst other elements, lack a comprehensive overview. Given the significant contribution of OR to hospitals' carbon footprint, which is three to six times higher compared to other hospital departments [18], this review focuses specifically on the environmental impact of hospital surgery care.

We focus mainly on direct emission types as these are under more direct organizational control, compared to indirect emissions that are created elsewhere in the care chain. Direct hospital emissions are reported to account for 15–50% of the total hospital emissions [18], and sustainability efforts mainly target the energy efficiency of buildings, energy sourcing, low-carbon transportation, water management, and food waste or sourcing [10]. However, next to direct sources, we focus on strategic decision making related to indirect emission sources, such as the use of disposable materials and equipment. This indirect emission type is deeply intertwined in the patient-care-process and contributes disproportionately to emissions in surgical care as our analysis will show. Hospital-level strategic decisions with respect to the use or abandonment of disposables, are within direct organizational control and likely of critical importance in any attempt to reduce surgical emissions.

Optimizing the delivery of clinical care towards more sustainable practices without sacrificing the quality of care constitutes a significant challenge. The identification of sources of carbon emissions and the formulation of mitigation strategies requires a shift in individual and organizational behavior [13]. In essence, sustainability efforts require organizational change as well as behavioral change of individuals in healthcare. With change in mind, this study applies the Congruence Model (ConMod, depicted in Fig. 1) developed by Nadler and Tushman (1980) [22] as a conceptual lens.

The congruence Model (adapted from 26, p. 47)

The ConMod provides a framework for identifying root causes of organizational performance issues and their co-dependencies, thereby offering insights to resolve the incongruences and [23, 24] misalignments between organizational elements hindering performance. The ConMod is based on the principle that a team or organization can only succeed when 1) the work tasks and processes, 2) the people who perform it, 3) the organizational structure and policies, and 4) the culture, all fit together – or, in terms of the model, are congruent. When there is a poor fit, or incongruence, between the four functional elements we might expect problems to arise and organizational performance to suffer. Through a joint examination of the functional elements Work, People, Structure, and Culture, the model allows for systemic organizational analysis and contributes to the development of a holistic perspective imperative for designing and generating sustainable change processes [23, 24].

The Work element consists of individual and group tasks which the organization must perform to realize its strategic objectives [23]. The People element in the model encompasses three aspects: individual competencies, personal motivational structure, and team structure [23]. The Structure element relates to the formal organization, encompassing formal structures and systems [23]. In the context of this study, Structure mainly refers to policies and best practices aimed at reducing the carbon footprint. The Cultural element contains informal structures and socially oriented aspects of organizations [23]. For example, an organization that focused on environmental sustainability would be considered congruent if: the tasks performed by employees do not harm the environment (Work), the employees have the necessary competencies, including skills, knowledge and abilities, to perform the tasks (People), reporting structures and reward systems, for example, do not impede the tasks (Structure), and power structures, communication patterns, group behavioral norms, values, and attitudes do not hinder efforts on sustainability issues (Culture). While the need for this alignment might seem obvious, empirical studies of organizations trying to make their operations more sustainable show that creating such alignment is no easy task [25].

In our study we adopt the ConMod as a conceptual lens, it allows us to position sources of carbon emissions in surgery care across the four functional elements of the model. Whilst the ConMod is originally intended to be (single) organization-focused, we broaden its scope by applying it to assess problem areas and incongruencies sector-wide. This is a novel application of the model, and it allows for unique insights concerning a broader unit of analysis but with a clearly defined scope. With this approach, we obtain a better understanding of problem areas that might be attributed to different organizational elements [23]. To identify the underlying explanations and generate possible solutions, the relationships between elements are analyzed to discover possible incongruencies [23]. For example, one could think of a mismatch between the structural requirement for cost-saving measures encouraging the use of disposables and the informal organizational culture emphasizing sustainability. Assuming that it is in the best interest of healthcare institutions to lower their carbon footprint whilst maintaining the quality of care, the assessment of incongruencies between the organizational elements of hospital surgery departments contributes to the identification of hotspots and the formulation of mitigation strategies.

The environmental impact of hospitals, especially surgical care constitutes a serious concern given the alarming amount of carbon emissions [9, 13]. Existing research identifies a wide array of factors contributing to the carbon footprint of hospitals and ORs. We aim to identify main sources of direct carbon emissions and explore how (mis)alignment of day-to-day activities, organizational policies, people’s skills and attitudes, and organizational culture embeds emission production into the organization.

In order to comprehensively investigate the sources of carbon emissions, and to obtain a better understanding of sustainability practices in hospitals’ surgical care, the following research question is formulated: What are sources of direct carbon emissions in surgical care and how can they be explored using the Congruence Model? To answer our research question, we conduct a qualitative systematic literature review following PRISMA guidelines [26]. Systematic reviews contribute to the understanding of the environmental impact of hospitals and the healthcare sector and help to formulate policy guidelines and mitigation strategies [27].

Methods

A qualitative systematic review was conducted following the updated PRISMA 2020 guidelines for reporting systematic reviews. Page et al. (2021) [26] suggest that the use of the PRISMA 2020 guidelines is associated with increased transparency, accuracy, and completeness of the SLR making it more valuable to the reader and the academic community.

Search strategy

Three databases were used in this review: PubMed, Business Source Premier, and EBSCOhost. PubMed includes all reports of the MEDLINE database and is considered the main database for health science research [28]. Business Source Premier is a widely used database for business and management research. To further increase the rigor of this study, EBSCOhost was added as it is a major database in terms of publication volume and quality [29] and enriches this review through its multidisciplinary approach.

In order to determine the keywords for our search string, we performed an exploratory search that showed “carbon emissions” [13, 30–32] and “carbon footprint” [33–36] to be commonly used in studies investigating the environmental impact of healthcare organizations. In addition, our exploration revealed that applying the keyword “hospital”, instead of terms related to surgery, consistently yielded better results regarding relevance and quantity of studies. As literature on surgery care employs multifaceted language including the terms surgery, surgical procedure, operation, operating room, and operating theater as well as specific medical terms. Thus, recognizing the diversity of terms used to describe the domain, we decided to use the more comprehensive keyword “hospital”; a subsequent manual filtering process allowed us to identify records relevant to the surgical domain. The manual filtering process consisted of screening of titles and abstracts and looking for earlier described terms and terms that describe the surgical domain. Ultimately, the databases were searched using the following search string: (“carbon emissions” OR “carbon footprint”) AND (“hospital”). Here, we would like to emphasize that the search string was deliberately kept broad in order to ensure a comprehensive coverage of the available literature related to the research question and increase the chances of identifying relevant studies.

Selection criteria

For our literature review we opted for the common five-year window as this ensures the inclusion of the most relevant, high-quality, and current research, providing a comprehensive overview of recent advancements in the field. The search process started in December 2023 and the final search was conducted on January 7th of 2024 where we consulted all three databases, we included the previous five complete calendar years (i.e. starting from 2018), and extended the search to include studies up until January 2024. For each database, the following manual filters were applied: Language (English), Year (from 2018), Type (Article). Articles were excluded if they were concerned with surgery- and patient-care that failed to address sources of carbon emissions or measures to reduce the carbon footprint, had a primary focus on financial or economic aspects such as cost-effectiveness, had a primary focus on hospital building infrastructure, administrative and managerial processes, energy sourcing and efficiency, or transportation, or primarily addressed telemedicine. Telemedicine is an emergent topic in healthcare research and has the potential to reduce the overall carbon footprint of healthcare hospitals [37–39]. However, for this review, we do not consider telemedicine as directly related to the internal care process and thus excluded the respective articles.

After the removal of duplicates, the first author (LK) selected articles using the inclusion/exclusion criteria by applying filters for language, publication type, and date of publication. Afterwards (LK) screened the titles and abstracts using the content criterion listed earlier, and then the full texts of those articles deemed relevant. Despite using the search term “hospital”, many records appeared in the search that described general healthcare topics or entirely unrelated domains which were subsequently excluded from the review.

Of these excluded studies, a number applied life-cycle analysis (LCA) of supplies and equipment used in the operating room. Within the LCA, most emissions are related to upstream activities, such as manufacturing, packaging, and transportation. Here, records primarily concerned with LCA were not included, as we are primarily concerned with the care process within hospitals and not in other parts of the care chain. The articles remaining after the full-text screening were categorized by the respective hospital department they addressed. Studies not addressing the surgery domain of hospitals were excluded from this review except for three articles addressing anesthetic practices, a domain closely linked to surgery. Furthermore, three articles [18, 40, 41] were added to the review after cross checking the citations in the earlier identified articles. The final set of articles underwent a quality check following the procedure of Kmet et al. (2004) [42] which was performed jointly by four authors (OR, AF, EH, ES). Here, the authors divided the articles evenly and filled in the quality criteria. Of the included articles, Grinberg et al. (2021) [43] and Pascual-Prieto et al. (2023) [44] did not meet the threshold of 0.55. These were again screened and discussed, and we reached consensus on including these documents.

Data collection and extraction

A data extraction form was developed based on the formulated research question and the planned assessment of the included articles [45]. Thus it included the identified sources of carbon emissions and sustainability practices. Furthermore, the data extraction form was used to gather the following data of the included articles: authors, year, country, methodology, data collection, the aim of the study, hospital department addressed, and main findings.

Data analysis and synthesis

Data retrieved from the included articles was analyzed using qualitative content analysis facilitated by Atlas.ti software. Initially, open coding was performed to identify and label concepts emerging from the articles [46, 47]. This resulted in the identification of numerous quotations referring to sources of carbon emissions and measures aimed at mitigating the carbon footprint of the OR which were grouped then into themes. Hereby, sources of carbon emissions are understood not only as direct sources of GHG emissions but also as indirect contributions through mechanisms that enable GHG emissions such as resistance to change or lack of environmental awareness among staff. The ConMod was also applied to inform and enhance further the data analysis. During axial coding, the categorization of initial codes into structured groups [46] was performed, and the themes that emerged from open coding were assigned to the four organizational elements of the ConMod: work, people, culture, and structure. Finally, selective coding was applied to identify the underlying patterns and relationships [46, 48]. In line with the ConMod, this step was done by identifying and addressing the congruences and incongruencies between the four organizational elements [23, 24]. All steps of the coding procedure were initially performed in an inductive way by continuously re-reading the literature and iteratively formulating the different codes, themes, and categories as they emerge [47]. By simultaneously placing the emerging concepts amongst the elements of the ConMod, it was possible to outline problem and conflict areas within the surgery care domain. The code book can be found in Appendix A.

Results

The detailed inclusion and exclusion process is visualized in the PRISMA flow diagram in Fig. 2. Ultimately, a total of 18 studies were included in this review. Most studies (n = 10) were published in 2023, which highlights the timeliness of the topic. The countries of origin were as follows: United States (six studies), United Kingdom (three studies), Australia (2 studies), Canada (two studies), and Turkey, France, Spain, Denmark, and Ireland (all one study). As shown in Table 1, out of the 18 studies included in this review, 13 applied quantitative methods such as cohort analysis [55, 59] or carbon footprint analysis [18, 57]. Four studies applied qualitative methods, such as in-depth interviews with surgical care staff [51, 52] and one study applied both qualitative and quantitative methods [56].

The most frequently addressed sources of carbon emissions and sustainability measures comprised use of reusable supplies and equipment versus the use of disposables which was addressed in ten articles [40, 41, 43, 44, 49, 50, 52, 53, 56, 57]. Followed by emissions connected to anesthesia and subsequent measures for mitigation, which were addressed in nine articles [18, 40, 41, 43, 49, 51, 52, 54, 61]. Thirdly, the role of awareness and knowledge of climate change among clinicians was addressed in eight papers [49–53, 58, 61, 62].

The functional elements

In this section, the identified sources of carbon emissions in surgery care as well as the identified sustainability measures will be presented and classified following the elements of the ConMod. A summarizing overview of the classification of themes including explanatory remarks can be found in Appendix B.

Work

Several sources of carbon emissions emerged from the reviewed literature that originated directly from the operational tasks in surgery care. Standing out were the emissions from the use of anesthetic gasses due to their heat-trapping characteristics. Candan Dönmez et al. (2019) add that “the global warming potential of a halogenated anesthetic is up to 2,000 times greater than CO2” (p.6) [52]. Breth-Petersen et al. (2023) [51] find that anesthesia agents can account for up to one-third of the total GHG emissions generated in the OR, especially desflurane accounts for a high amount of GHG in surgical care [18, 54, 61]. The carbon emissions originating from the use of anesthetic gasses represent the most frequently addressed source of emissions within the work domain [18, 41, 43, 51, 52, 54, 61]. Next to their negative environmental impact anesthetic gasses were also found to be a health hazard for the surgical staff [54, 61].

The disposal and subsequent waste generation of opened but unused drugs [44] and supplies [40, 49, 50, 53] was also frequently mentioned as a source of carbon emissions. While for drugs this is mainly due to the expiration date and short shelf life [44], unused but discarded supplies mainly come from sterile parcels containing a range of instruments [40, 49, 50]. Bravo et al. (2023) [50] found that as much as 22.6% of the items in the custom pack are wasted, proving that unused supplies generate large amounts of waste and thus carbon emissions [49, 53].

Moreover, sterilization creates large amounts of carbon emissions in the OR. Kodumuri et al. (2023) [53] found that the “re-sterilization of a generic hand set alone generated 14.1 kg of CO2 emissions” (p.5), to put this into perspective, one re-sterilization equates to over 50 cups of espresso. Other authors also suggest that sterilization procedures contribute significantly to the total carbon footprint of the OR [56, 57]. Finally, two studies highlighted the high carbon footprint of robotic surgery [44, 55], which can be up to eight times higher compared to conventional, non-robotic-assisted, methods [44].

Next to the sources of carbon emissions in surgery care that were placed under the work element of the ConMod, several measures to reduce the negative environmental impact could also be identified. The measures mainly address the high carbon footprint of anesthetic gasses. Baxter et al. (2021) [49] argue that replacing general anesthesia with local anesthesia can significantly lower carbon emissions while simultaneously increasing patient satisfaction.

Thiel et al. (2019) [40] found similar evidence for this so-called wide-awake approach. Furthermore, Wyssusek et al. (2022) [61] suggest that intravenous anesthesia has a lower carbon footprint than the use of anesthetic gases. Whereby the use of general anesthesia and the use of gases is required nevertheless, GHG reduction can be achieved by substituting the anesthetic agent [18, 41, 43, 51, 54, 61]. A study by Thiel et al. (2018) found that “the removal of desflurane and replacement with a lower GHG option such as sevoflurane reduces GHGs by more than 25%” (p.4) [41]. Wyssusek et al. (2022) substantiate this claim by arguing that the use of one bottle of desflurane during surgery is equivalent to driving 3539 km in a petrol car, compared to 196 km for one bottle of sevoflurane [61]. Spoyalo et al. (2023) suggest that reducing the number of surgical procedures and testing generally by avoiding unnecessary procedures would also result in reduced carbon emissions [59]. While this approach would certainly reduce the carbon footprint, it was not touched upon by other authors.

People

The most frequently classified contributor to the carbon footprint under the people element was lack of awareness and knowledge. Hereby, lack of awareness and knowledge refers to the ignorance or unknowingness of clinicians regarding the environmental impact of their daily work. Candan Dönmez et al. (2019) [52] suggest that most clinical staff members are generally concerned about the threat of climate change, however, there appears to be a disparity between this general concern and the awareness of their individual impact. Baxter et al. (2021) find that between 50 and 60% of surgeons underestimate the GHG emissions originating from the healthcare [49] sector as well as the amount of waste generated in the OR. This claim is supported by Candan Dönmez et al. (2019) whose study in Turkish hospitals finds that surgical care staff is not sufficiently educated in sustainability and the benefits or opportunities of waste reduction [52]. Whilst several studies observe this lack of knowledge and awareness [49, 51, 52, 60], others suggest raising awareness amongst staff through simple interventions can result in a shift in mindsets and ultimately in reduced carbon emissions [50, 53, 61]. Breth-Petersen et al. (2023) for example suggest that many anesthetists refrain from switching to more sustainable alternatives because they perceive a lack of conclusive evidence regarding the clinical and environmental impacts of these procedures [51]. The lack of awareness and knowledge can partly be attributed to a lack of formal training or educational programs which also emerged as a source of carbon emissions [51, 52, 58]. Training and education programs are measures to address and overcome the identified problem areas [51, 52, 58, 60, 61]. Hereby, training and education programs can emphasize the benefits of sustainable hospitals generally [52], but also address specific problem areas such as recycling [58, 60], or anesthetic practices [51, 61].

Another factor facilitating GHG emissions in surgery care that can be classified under the people element is habitual behavior [49–51]. Even when environmental awareness has been raised, clinicians tend to stick with the less environmentally friendly procedures due to change resistance. Baxter et al. (2021) find that “many surgeons are resistant to change because of the fear of losing comfort with familiar supplies and risking a poor outcome” (p. 6) [49]. In a series of in-depth interviews, Breth-Petersen et al. (2023) found that anesthetists were reluctant to use improved techniques aimed at lowering the carbon footprint of anesthesia and considered them “exceptional anesthetics”, “non-mainstream”, or not in line with “just going to work and doing what they were taught to what they’re comfortable with day-in-day-out” (p. 10) [51].

Structure

The most frequently addressed source of carbon emissions attributed to the structural element of the ConMod is related to strategic decision making with respect to the use of disposable supplies and equipment [40, 43, 44, 52, 53, 56, 57]. According to Grinberg et al. (2021) the GHG emissions attributable to disposable medical supplies account for 86% of the total carbon footprint of a conventional heart surgery procedure [43]. Disposable supplies such as drapes, gowns, or instrument tables are responsible for most of the clinical waste [53, 57]. The frequent use of disposables is often justified by the high sterility requirements [56] and costs [52, 56]. However, Rizan et al. (2023) found that switching from single-use equipment to reusable equipment in laparoscopic procedures resulted not only in reduced carbon emission but also in financial savings [57]. This study also suggested that, according to the Pareto Principle, only relatively few of the used supplies (mean average = 23%) are associated with over 80% of the total carbon emissions of all used products [57]. Similarly, Ramos et al. (2023) argue that instead of replacing all single-use products, the identification and replacement of a few products associated with significant carbon emissions would already have a considerable impact [56].

Consequently, incentivizing the use of reusable supplies and equipment is among the most frequently addressed structural policies for carbon mitigation [41, 43, 44, 49, 50, 52, 53, 57]. Pascual-Prieto et al. (2023) found that the carbon footprint of single cataract surgery in a hospital in India is fifteen times lower compared to the same procedure performed in a hospital in Spain [44]. The authors suggest that this disparity can mainly be attributed to the greater use of reusable materials in the hospital in India [44]. However, it was also observed that reusing medical supplies creates substantial carbon emissions from re-sterilization [56]. Overall, there is consensus amongst the reviewed studies that the use of reusable medical supplies and equipment over disposables has a positive impact on the carbon footprint [49, 50, 52, 57].

Further measures towards reducing the carbon footprint in the OR frequently addressed in the reviewed literature are policies for waste segregation and recycling [18, 41, 50, 52, 56–58, 60].While the use of reusable equipment aims at reducing waste, surgical procedures inevitably produce waste. The reviewed studies found that by recycling and segregating waste carbon emission can be lowered [57, 58, 60]. The adherence to existing recycling policies can hereby be achieved and improved through waste supervision [52], stringent labeling [57], the allocation of appropriate bins [57], and the promotion of recycling programs among surgical staff [60]. Waste segregation in the OR can further improve the carbon footprint when segregating paper, plastics, sharp wastes, metals, biohazardous waste, and radioactive waste [41, 52, 56]. Sullivan, et al. (2023) found that “recycling programs are often sought after and well received among OR staff members” (p.3) and that recycling is generally prioritized among clinicians [60]. Nevertheless, Ramos et al. (2023) show that less than 10% of all plastics in the OR have been recycled [56]. Other studies also suggest that there is a large potential to increase recycling efforts [41, 50]. The unused recycling potential can partly be attributed to the complexity of sorting and cleaning post-use plastics [56].

Particularly the recycling and segregation of biohazardous waste emerged as a problem area in the reviewed literature [18, 52, 53, 58]. According to Rooney et al. (2023), “recyclable materials often get mixed with clinical risk waste at the point of generation, resulting in high environmental and cost implications” (p.1) [58]. These claims are substantiated by Candan Dönmez et al. (2019) and Kodumuri et al. (2023) who find that approximately 90% of items placed in infectious waste containers could be recycled [52, 53]. According to Kodumuri et al. (2023), biohazardous waste “makes up only 24% of the total waste volume but requires 86% of the overall costs required to dispose it safely” (p.6) [53]. Reassessing the policies and practices for segregating biohazardous waste could thus result in significant cost and carbon emission savings.

Furthermore, monitoring and measuring the GHG emissions of surgical care emerged as a structural (policy) measure [18, 43, 49, 53]. Incorporating assessments such as eco-audits or carbon footprint calculations into daily operations is a prerequisite for developing environmentally friendly best practices in the OR [18, 43].

In line with the lean philosophy, which is addressed in the culture element of the ConMod, several authors suggest the implementation of policies aimed at minimizing the used and required equipment in the OR [40, 41, 44, 49, 53, 57]. By implementing smaller trays with essential equipment and optimized configuration the problem of unused but discarded medical supplies can be addressed [49, 53]. Reassessing the required contents of these trays and custom packs would considerably reduce the waste generated from disposables [49, 53] and the emission resulting from re-sterilization [44]. A comparative study by Thiel et al. (2019) between a conventional and minimal custom pack resulted in a 13% waste reduction per case [40]. Rizan et al. (2023) furthermore suggest reducing the amount of used products by replacing non-sterile gloves with handwashing [57]. In practice, exchanging gloves with handwashing makes sense, as non-sterile gloves are similarly susceptible to bacteria contamination as hands.

Culture

In the study by Breth-Petersen et al. (2023), anesthetists value a perceived culture of patient well-being over environmentally friendly proceedings [51]. Furthermore, cost-effectiveness and time pressure are named as reasons for deciding against more environmentally friendly anesthetics which might also be attributed to underlying organizational beliefs or values [51]. Organizational culture can both be a context for, and an object of change. Shared beliefs and values that support sustainable world views can hereby lead to the implementation of carbon reduction strategies. One example is the 5R strategy, according to which the organizational focus should be on reducing, reusing, rethinking, recycling, and researching [50]. Furthermore, the commitment to a lean philosophy has also shown to be effective in reducing the carbon footprint in hospitals. Hereby lean philosophy refers to the minimization of waste and maximization of healthcare value which is also associated with reduced resource consumption and thus carbon emissions [49]. The studies by Kodumuri et al. (2023) and Bravo et al. (2023) also showed that the implementation of lean principles in the OR significantly reduces the carbon footprint [50, 53].

Figure 3 summarizes our review findings and visualizes key (in)congruences between key elements of the ConMod. Importantly, factors related to the people domain are well-studied and typically understood as critically important to the successful incorporation of sustainable policies and cultures into work practices. In the next section we elaborate further on the (in)congruences between the four core domains of the ConMod.

Summary of key findings, congruences and incongruences

Addressing the (in)congruence between the functional elements

Nadler and Tushman (1980) introduce the concept of congruence as a “measure of how well pairs of components fit together (p.45) [22]. Assessing the fit between the functional elements of the ConMod allows us to identify problem areas regarding carbon emissions and sustainability in surgical care [22, 24]. In the following section of this paper the most important congruences and incongruences between the function elements of the ConMod are discussed.

Work – people

Incongruence can be observed between the work and the people elements of the ConMod. Habitual behavior and resistance to changing daily proceedings prevent anesthetists from adopting available low-carbon alternatives to anesthetic agents like sevoflurane. This claim is supported by Breth-Petersen et al. (2023), who observed that members of surgical staff developed a standardized way of working, including the choice of anesthetics, that is perceived as most comfortable and predictable [51]. Work elements such as the substitution of anesthetic gasses or the avoidance of general anesthesia are therefore misaligned with the practice habits of OR staff and the resulting resistance to change. Habitual behavior and change resistance are not the only reasons that influence work elements, practitioners are also confronted with a lack of training and education in the domain of suitability methods. In turn, this prevents OR staff members from engaging in alternative, more sustainable behavior [51, 61]. Specifically for anesthesia, Wyssusek et al. (2022) suggest that encouraging education and training regarding the substitution of desflurane, avoidance of general anesthesia, and use of low-flow anesthesia promotes the adoption of these methods and consequently a reduction of carbon emissions from routine anesthetic practice [61].

Work – structure

Incongruencies between the structural and the work element of the ConMod can be observed in the form of deviations between policies and practice. While this might be the case in some hospitals or ORs, the reviewed literature did not reveal any noteworthy deviations between the rules and policies of the formal organization and the way tasks and activities are performed.

Congruence can be observed between the structural policy of minimizing the required supplies and equipment and the discard of unused supplies and equipment. In several studies, alignment was observed between policy changes regarding the reduction of essential supplies brought into the OR and the reduced disposal of unused supplies and equipment [40, 44, 49, 53, 61]. The structural reassessment of sterile packs and standardized surgical trays positively influences the carbon emission resulting from tasks and activities in the OR. Similarly, policies limiting the use of disposable supplies and equipment align with the reduced disposal of unused supplies and equipment, reducing the total carbon emissions of the surgical care process [43, 50, 52, 53].

Work – culture

The theme of patient care over environmental care emerged in another study [51]. This observation was attributed to the cultural element of the ConMod as it represents a shared value of clinical staff members, embedded in the organizational culture. This cultural element misaligns with most sustainability-oriented themes within the work element, for example, the emission from anesthetic gasses. OR staff members prioritize patient safety and well-being over environmental impacts when making the decision between local and general anesthesia or choosing the anesthetic agent [51]. It became apparent that the aim of improving the carbon footprint of surgery care should not be at the expense of the quality of care, a notion that is also linked with the role of improvement and quality within the Lean philosophy.

Structure – people

To mitigate the carbon footprint of surgical care, several incongruencies between the structural element and the people element of the ConMod must be addressed. Again, the important role of knowledge, awareness, and education is highlighted. A lack of awareness and knowledge among surgical staff regarding the environmental impact for example prevents the successful implementation of recycling practices [58, 60]. Generally, a lack of awareness and knowledge of staff misaligns with the successful implementation of policies and structures aimed at mitigating the carbon footprint. This also applies to the structural policies of monitoring and measuring GHG emissions and reassessing the disposal of biohazardous waste.

Furthermore, even if staff members are aware of the environmental impact of their work, incongruencies exist between successful policy implementation and the level of training and education. The implementation of new recycling and waste segregation standards for example misaligns with a lack of in-house training opportunities [52, 58, 60]. Training and education moreover enable successful reporting and measurement of carbon emissions, a crucial prerequisite for mitigation strategies [18].

Changes to the formal organization such as the implementation of new policies are misaligned with the habitual behavior and resistance to change of individuals and groups in the OR. Baxter et al. (2021) for example found that despite the introduction of policies minimizing the number of supplies on surgical trays, many surgeons resisted the adoption of this policy due to “the fear of losing comfort with familiar supplies and risking a poor outcome” (p. 6) [49]. Moreover, many sterile packs are customized according to surgeons’ preferences. Bravo et al. (2023) suggest that surgeons should be encouraged to update their preferences regularly and exclude not routinely used supplies, thereby overcoming their habits [50].

Structure – culture

When addressing the relationship between the structural and cultural elements, congruence can be observed between the cultural elements 5Rs and lean philosophy and the successful implementation of structural policies. Introducing the 5Rs (reduce, reuse, rethink, recycle, research) as part of the organizational culture in hospitals aligns well with structural policies such as the use of reusable supplies and equipment, waste segregation and recycling, or the minimization of required supplies and equipment [50].

Eliminating waste is a fundamental component of lean philosophy in organizations [49, 63] and therefore aligns well with the structural policies of emphasizing reusable supplies and equipment, minimizing the required supplies, and waste segregation and recycling in the OR. Several studies in this review highlight the positive impact of lean models and philosophy which resulted in the mitigation of carbon emissions [49, 50, 53].

People – culture

Assessing the interplay between the people element and the culture element of the ConMod also reveals congruences and incongruences. Incongruency can be observed between the implementation of 5R culture or lean philosophy and the resistance to change or habitual behavior. Surgical care staff acting according to habits as described by Baxter et al. (2021), Bravo et al. (2023), and Breth-Petersen et al. (2023) misaligned with successfully integrating new aspects of sustainable organizational culture [49–51]. Raising awareness and knowledge about the benefits of these approaches as well as training and education programs might prevent this misalignment.

Discussion

The global threat of climate change has called for an assessment of carbon emissions and mitigation strategies in healthcare. Surgical care, whilst remaining fundamental for public health, faces the paradox of caring for those affected by the negative health effects of climate change, while simultaneously being a significant producer of carbon emissions [64]. The objective of this study was to provide an overview of sources of carbon emissions and sustainability measures in surgical care through the perspective of the ConMod as a conceptual lens. By conducting a systematic literature review we addressed the research question: What are the sources of carbon emissions in surgical care and how can they be explored using the Congruence Model?

As depicted in Fig. 3 in the Results, we were able to identify various sources of carbon emission and measures or strategies aimed at reducing emissions. Ultimately, we identified 11 different sources of carbon emissions or mechanisms enabling the emission of GHG. These findings are largely in line with previous reviews, which also recognized emissions from anesthetic gasses, the discard of unused medical supplies, robotically assisted surgery, and sterilization procedures as hotspots of GHG emissions [8–10, 65, 66]. In addition, this study underlines the importance of raising environmental awareness and education, as called for by previous reviews [8–10]. Moreover, we identified 12 measures or strategies aimed at reducing the carbon footprint of surgical care. The identified measures are largely in line with existing reviews which emphasized minimizing the required instruments on surgical trays, the use of reusable medical supplies, as well as the substitution of anesthetic agents [8, 9, 65]. However, decreasing the carbon footprint by integrating the Lean Management philosophy into the organizational culture appears not to have been discussed by earlier review studies.

This research adds to the already extensive body of knowledge on sustainability in the healthcare domain, while at the same time offering unique insights. Whilst previous studies recognized similar sources of carbon emissions in the OR, our study shifts attention toward the surgical care process itself. By narrowing down the scope of this review to care process-specific activities, this paper contributes to the general understanding of the environmental impact of surgery.

Another main contribution of this research is the systematic categorization of care process-specific findings across the functional elements of the ConMod. Here, most sources of carbon emissions were attributed to either the People or the Work element while the majority of sustainability measures were attributed to the Structural element. This implies that carbon emissions in surgical care are substantially linked to human behavior, either through the execution of operational tasks or the skills, beliefs, and behaviors of staff members. Conversely, the large amount of sustainability measures attributed to the Structural element of the ConMod highlights the necessity for systemic change and policy adjustments. Through applying the ConMod as a conceptual lens, the added value of a holistic approach addressing the interplay between the Structure, Culture, People, and Work elements becomes apparent.

Theoretical implications

The findings of this study contributes to the broader body of knowledge focused on understanding sustainability issues in healthcare [11]. In our research, we opted for a narrow focus on surgery, in line with other more focused studies such as the work of Molero et al. (2021) dedicated to laboratory medicine [67], or the study of Buchan et al. (2022) dedicated to eye-care [68]. Our findings are in line with the results of Gordon (2020), Engler et al. (2022), and Phoon et al. (2022) that also highlighted the impact of anesthetic agents, single use devices, or waste segregation [69–71]. In addition, our findings align with the insights of Dreyfus, Nair and Rosales (2020) [72], the authors show the impact of improving the planning mechanism for items needed for surgery. The study underlines that improved planning can reduce waste, and therefor emissions as less material would be used in the OR. However, our research contributes to the current views by adding the organizational perspective of the ConMod.

This study further contributes to the understanding of the complex interplay between organizational-level factors such as structural policies and organizational culture, and individual- or group-level factors such as awareness, education, and resistance in sustainability. It emphasizes the need for alignment between the four functional elements of the ConMod to successfully work towards sustainability in surgery care.

Managerial implications

This study emphasizes the importance of aligning the awareness and knowledge of OR staff with the structural and cultural goals. This can be achieved through education and training programs ranging from the creation of green teams [52], simple interventions like posters, or presentations on the environmental impact of anesthetic agents [61]. Misalignment might occur when large amounts of carbon emission originate from work processes and there is a lack of awareness and knowledge among staff members. Moreover, structural policies such as the implementation of recycling policies will likely fail if they misalign with peoples’ individual skills and knowledge, for example not knowing how to correctly segregate biohazardous waste.

To improve the carbon footprint of their OR, hospital management should strategically integrate sustainability measures into their structural policies. The current review showed that whilst the sources of carbon emissions are mainly attributed to the People and Work elements of the ConMod, most carbon reduction strategies are attributed to the Structural element of the model. Congruence analysis can therefore be used to inform the identification of problem areas and to facilitate a targeted approach to the reduction of GHG emissions.

Hospital managers might use our findings to assess their current surgical practices. Specifically, anesthetic practices, including the choice of anesthetic gasses, the use of disposable medical supplies, and sterilization procedures are hotspots of carbon emissions in surgery care. Mechanisms that enable the emission of GHG are pre-eminently habitual behaviors, change resistance, lacking awareness and knowledge, and insufficient training and education. These areas should be assessed and monitored when working towards more sustainable ORs. Moreover, given that behavioral change, such as the behavioral changes implied by the suggestions listed above, calls for a well-thought through change strategy [73], decision makers should ensure that resources are available to facilitate behavioral changes of OR staff. Following the ConMod our findings imply that top managers in hospitals will have to make strategic choices, weighing sustainability as one amongst multiple organizational interests. If, as an outcome of this strategic decision making process, top managers choose to prioritize decarbonizing the OR, our study highlights that strategic success will depend on the extent to which resources -in terms of time for example- are made available to facilitate intervention implementation. To mitigate the carbon footprint, this study also suggests several practical measures. Policymakers can use the comprehensive overview of care process-specific sustainability measures as a starting point to address the hotspots of GHG emissions in the OR.

Limitations and future research

As all research, our study is not free of limitations which we aim to highlight here. The SLR is a practical methodology to consolidate prior research [74, 75], and we followed the PRISMA guidelines closely [26]. However, our study was not registered on a SLR platform before execution, and this might have created overlap with other research or resulted in less applicable search terms. Relatedly, we did not prepare a dedicated protocol before the execution of the study. Furthermore, screening and extraction was performed by one of the researchers, this deviates from the double-independent procedure typically recommended to ensure objectivity. Hence, our study might show bias in the included studies. To mitigate this, we had broad selection criteria, regular meetings focused on methodological decisions, and we conducted a final quality check on the included literature. Moreover, as our analysis was inductive in nature, the data collection and coding process involved a certain extent of subjectivity. Albeit that our analysis was guided by an established conceptual model. For example, when identifying sources of carbon emission from the literature and mapping those across the functional elements of the ConMod.

In our study, we choose to specifically focus on the care process related to surgery and identify sources of carbon emissions. However, factors such as electricity, packaging, and transportation, which were not at the heart of our study, will obviously impact aspects of sustainability as well. Relatedly, we omitted Life Cycle Analysis studies from our research as these are concerned with elements outside of the hospital’s control. However, this exclusion might have influenced included studies and have resulted in biases in include studies. Moreover, clinical outcomes, such as complication rates, length of hospital stay, and complementary diagnostic tests, also could influence carbon emissions. Future research might consider including more upstream or downstream sources together with topics related to clinical outcomes to provide a more comprehensive overview of care processes.

When thinking of generalizability, it needs mentioning that most included studies were from Western-Europe of the United States. A larger geographical variety might show different results, for example when thinking of the influence of climate. Which also implies that our study is not exhaustive, more sources of emissions are to be expected.

Finally, the novel application of the ConMod provides us with new insights, but it did require us to expand the model beyond the traditional single organizational focus. Moreover, the ConMod was a conceptual lens, but we did not incorporate the entire framework, and we forwent discussion related to Inputs or Outputs. Future studies could explore ways to integrate the entire model, including its Inputs and Output components.

Conclusions

In this research, we set out to answer the following research question: What are sources of carbon emissions in surgical care and how can they be explored using the Congruence Model? Through a literature review, we were able to map various sources of carbon emissions in hospitals across the elements of the Congruence Model. Ultimately, our study provides an extensive overview of prior research that focused on emissions related to surgery. In addition, by applying the Congruence Model as a conceptual lens, we were able to link hotspots of carbon emissions in surgical care with Work and People elements, and we linked sustainability measures to the Structure element. The concluding reflection on the identified (in)congruencies between the different elements of the Congruence Model and the identified actions related to (reducing) carbon emissions, could aid practitioners in their endeavors to improve the sustainability of the surgery domain.
