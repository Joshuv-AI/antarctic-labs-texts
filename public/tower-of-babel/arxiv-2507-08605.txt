Remote Sensing Reveals Adoption of Sustainable Rice Farming Practices Across Punjab, India
Ando Shah \sthanks Corresponding author: ando@berkeley.edu 1 , Rajveer Singh 2 , Akram Zaytar 3 , Girmaw Abebe Tadesse 3 , Caleb Robinson 3 , Negar Tafti 2 , Stephen A. Wood 2,4 , Rahul Dodhia 3 , Juan M. Lavista Ferres 3

Abstract
Rice cultivation consumes 24-30% of global freshwater, creating critical water management challenges in major rice-producing regions. Sustainable irrigation practices like direct seeded rice (DSR) and alternate wetting and drying (AWD) can reduce water use by 20-40% while maintaining yields, helping secure long-term agricultural productivity as water scarcity intensifies—a key component of the Zero Hunger Sustainable Development Goal. However, limited data on adoption rates of these practices prevents evidence-based policymaking and targeted resource allocation. We developed a novel remote sensing framework to monitor sustainable water management practices at scale in Punjab, India—a region facing severe groundwater depletion of 41.6 cm/year. To collect essential ground truth data, we partnered with the Nature Conservancy’s Promoting Regenerative and No-burn Agriculture (PRANA), which trained approximately 1,400 farmers on water-saving techniques while documenting their field-level practices. Using this data, we created a classification system with Sentinel-1 satellite imagery that separates water management along sowing and irrigation dimensions. Our approach achieved a 78% F1-score in distinguishing DSR from traditional puddled transplanted rice without requiring prior knowledge of planting dates. We demonstrated scalability by mapping DSR adoption across approximately 3 million agricultural plots in Punjab, with district-level predictions showing strong correlation (Pearson=0.77, RBO= 0.77) with government records. This study provides policymakers with a powerful tool to track sustainable water management adoption, target interventions, and measure program impacts at scale.

Introduction

Rice is a staple food for half of the world’s population, but traditional paddy cultivation requires enormous quantities of water—2,000–5,000 liters per kilogram of rice produced
[
1
]
. As climate change intensifies water scarcity in many regions, sustainable water management in rice cultivation becomes essential for ensuring long-term food security, critical to addressing the U.N. Zero Hunger Sustainable Development Goal (SDG).
Two water-saving practices have emerged as particularly promising: Direct Seeded Rice (DSR) and Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD). DSR eliminates the need to raise and transplant seedlings by directly sowing seeds into the field, reducing water consumption by 20-40%
[
2
,
1

, relative to traditionally practiced puddled transplanted rice (PTR). AWD involves periodically allowing fields to dry before re-flooding, reducing water use by up to 30% compared to traditional continuous flooding (CF)
[
3

. When properly managed, these techniques maintain or even enhance yields, presenting a balanced approach for sustainable rice production
[
4
]
.

Despite the promise of these practices, their scale of adoption remains poorly understood. This knowledge gap severely hampers evidence-based policymaking, resource allocation, and intervention planning for several reasons:

•

Ground surveys are labor-intensive, expensive, and often constrained by limited access to remote fields

•

Manual methods struggle to differentiate between traditional and newly adopted practices

•

Adoption patterns are not routinely tracked in national or sub-national surveys

•

Understanding current adoption helps target resources where they’re most needed

Background

2.1
Study Area

Our study focuses on Punjab, India (Fig.
5
), a semi-arid region where rice dominates agriculture, covering 87% (3.13 million hectares) of farmland during the 2022-23 monsoon season
[

]
. In 2023, Punjab produced 14.36 million tons of rice—10.4% of India’s production and approximately 3.3% of global output

. The region typically employs a wheat-rice double-cropping system using PTR and CF methods

This agricultural intensity has created a severe water crisis, with 73% of irrigation water drawn from rapidly depleting underground aquifers. The 2022 Central Ground Water Board report classified 114 of Punjab’s 150 blocks as over-exploited, with an additional 19 blocks categorized as critical or semi-critical
[
18
]
. Although the state government has implemented conservation measures, including legislation restricting early rice transplanting
[
15
]
, groundwater levels continue to decline at an alarming rate of 41.6 cm/year, expected to drop below 300 metres by 2039
[

,

]
.

2.2
Challenges

While satellite-based monitoring offers a potential solution, several technical challenges have limited its application:

2.2.1
Water Management Practices and Separability

Water-saving practices in rice cultivation occur at different crop stages and create distinct water level patterns. DSR affects water management during early sowing and tillering phases, while AWD impacts water dynamics from tillering through harvest.

As shown in Figure
4
, these practices create unique temporal signatures in water levels and soil moisture that can be detected via Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) remote sensing
[
19
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
. PTR maintains consistently high water levels, while DSR shows a sharp initial decline after pre-sowing irrigation. Similarly, CF maintains flooded fields throughout the season, whereas AWD creates cyclical wet-dry patterns.

2.2.2
Dimensional Classification Approach

Attempting to classify all water management combinations simultaneously poses substantial challenges due to overlapping characteristics. We address this by separating practices into two independent dimensions:

sowing dimension
, distinguishing between DSR and PTR practices
and
irrigation dimension
, separating AWD from CF practices.

This dimensional approach, illustrated in Figure
4
, allows us to cleanly separate practices and collect training data that isolates specific dimensions of interest. Control plots employ PTR and CF practices, DSR plots practice DSR and CF, and AWD plots practice PTR and AWD. Plots practicing mixed combinations were excluded to avoid confounding effects.

2.2.3
Planting Date Heterogeneity

An additional challenge for satellite-based monitoring is the significant variation in planting schedules. Previous studies have relied on synchronizing satellite data with known planting dates
[
8
,
9
]
– a luxury unavailable when monitoring millions of plots. In our study area, planting dates span up to 110 days, with the majority of DSR plots typically sown first, followed by control plots and finally AWD plots (Fig. S3). This temporal variability introduces natural differences between classes that must be accounted for, and in fact is exploited, in our modeling approach.

2.2.4
Plot-level Predictions

To be able to provide fine-grained insights, we must be able to both identify individual plot boundaries as well as make predictions about these monolithic entities. We employ Fields of the World (FTW) algorithm
[
20
]
to automate the extraction of plot boundaries, enabling us to make predictions at an individual plot level.

Our research question thus becomes:
How effectively can we classify water management practices by separating them along sowing and irrigation dimensions using satellite imagery, despite significant heterogeneity in planting dates, while predicting at the plot-level?

3
Materials and Methods

3.1
Ground Truth Data Collection

To obtain reliable training data, we partnered with The Nature Conservancy’s PRANA project, which trains farmers on sustainable water management practices across Punjab. Between May and December 2024, local field teams collected detailed plot-level data from PRANA intervention sites across 18 districts. The resulting dataset includes 452 AWD plots, 420 DSR plots and 411 PTR plots employing CF. Their average sizes are 4.2, 2.7 and 2.1 acres, respectively. Refer to Fig. S1 for details of AWD measuring devices and visuals of DSR plotsin the PRANA program.

For each plot, we documented sowing dates, transplantation dates (for PTR/AWD), irrigation schedules, crop varieties, harvesting dates, and precise field boundaries. Field delineations were conducted through on-site visits using custom software and Google Earth Pro. We selected only plots with clearly visible boundaries and a minimum area of 2000 square meters to ensure sufficient quality in satellite data analysis. A 10-meter negative buffer was applied to all boundaries to eliminate edge effects while preserving adequate pixels for analysis.

All SAR imagery was processed using the SNAP toolbox
[
26
]
to extract time series information at 10-meter spatial resolution. We extract Sentinel-1 VV and VH polarization bands from multiple orbit passes over Punjab. For each image, we apply standard preprocessing steps: orbit file application, noise removal, radiometric calibration, speckle filtering using multi-temporal refined-Lee filter
[
27
]
, and terrain correction to derive terrain-geocoded backscatter coefficient (
\gamma_{0}
) at 10-meter resolution
[
26
]
.

For each agricultural plot polygon in the training corpus, we calculate mean backscatter values for every timestep, generating a timeseries per plot. This process serves both as speckle noise reduction for the SAR imagery, and as a summary of plot-level conditions. To account for temporal gaps between satellite passes and enable consistent analysis, we apply temporal smoothing with a 1-D Gaussian filter (standard deviation = 0.5) followed by fitting a third-degree polynomial spline. This allows us to sample the time series at regular intervals despite variable satellite acquisition dates–steps that are crucial in dealing with missing data in the timeseries. We additionally create a VV/VH band ratio time series, which has proven effective for rice mapping in previous studies
[
9
,
28
,
29
]

3.3
Feature Engineering

We develop two complementary sets of features:

Hand-crafted Features:

For each band (VV, VH, VV/VH), we extract temporal characteristics that include the position and amplitude of the first
k
troughs, crests, and inflection points; the total number of troughs and crests; summary statistics (mean, minimum, maximum amplitude); parameters from Gaussian kernel fitting to the VV/VH ratio (amplitude, peak day, standard deviation and
R^{2}
)
[
28
]
; and radar vegetation index (RVI) values
[
30
]
.

Pretrained Features:

We leverage the Pretrained Remote Sensing Transformer (Presto)
[
31
]
, a deep learning model pre-trained on diverse remote sensing time series data. We picked this remote sensing foundation model as it works at a pixel level as opposed to patch level, which would be difficult due to the irregular size of plots. We extract embeddings by passing ERA5 climate variables and Sentinel-1 timeseries data through the frozen Presto encoder, generating features that capture complex temporal patterns without requiring extensive labeled data. We utilize the embeddings from all timesteps (“temporal tokens”). A limitation of this model is that it can process at most 24 timesteps. We experiment with resampling the smoothed spline in the previous step with frequencies of 4, 7 and 10 days. This is done in order to increase the temporal resolution of the data where possible (when the total number of timesteps
\leq
24) or to accommodate longer temporal ranges (such as rows 11-12 in
Table 2
).

3.4
Classification Approach

We test two ensemble learning methods—Random Forest (RF) and Gradient Boosted Trees (GB)—for their proven effectiveness in agricultural classification tasks
[
32
,
33
]
. These models effectively handle the heterogeneous feature sets while remaining computationally efficient.
Following our dimensional framework, we structure three classification tasks: a) AWD vs. DSR vs. Control (null hypothesis: all practices on same dimension), b) DSR vs. PTR (sowing dimension), and c) AWD vs. CF (irrigation dimension). For each task, we split data into non-overlapping train/test sets (90:10 ratio) using stratified sampling. We optimize hyperparameters using Optuna
[
34
]
and evaluate performance using overall accuracy and F1 scores (macro and globally weighted averages). We compare against a baseline that randomly assigns classes based on class distribution proportions.
This approach tests our hypothesis that separating water management practices along their natural dimensions (sowing and irrigation) produces more accurate results than attempting simultaneous multi-class classification (Fig. S1 for the complete workflow).

4.2
Temporal Features Drive Classification Performance

Our analysis reveals two distinct types of temporal features affecting classification performance:

Lag features
capture differences in planting schedules between farms. PRANA collected an independent dataset documenting planting dates across practice types (Supp. Fig. S3), which reveals that while substantial variance exists in planting dates across the state, each practice type exhibits a considerably tighter distribution. These temporal offsets provide significant discriminatory power; however, this discriminatory capability depends on the persistence of such patterns over different seasons.

Practice features
reflect water management patterns unique to each technique. Sowing features (active for DSR/PTR) are most distinct during the first weeks after planting, while AWD/CF irrigation patterns emerge later in the growing season, as a function of their irrigation frequency, which typically ranges from 4 to over 10 on average (Fig.
4
).

To assess the relative importance of these features, we selectively constrained features to different temporal windows during model training (
Table 2
), thereby highlighting the differential prominence of each feature type. For sowing classification, models exposed to sufficient lag features (>~50%) and containing the majority of DSR and PTR sowing features (rows 1, 3, 10-11) demonstrate strong performance. When DSR features are absent (rows 4-9) or insufficient lag features are available (row 2), DSR classification performance deteriorates markedly. This pattern indicates that sowing models leverage both practice and lag features, conferring partial resilience to changes in cropping calendars. Furthermore, the optimal model appears robust to sampling frequency selection, as the top two performing models employ different sampling frequencies (f=4,7 for rows 3,4).

For irrigation classification, however, we observe strong dependence on lag features, with performance peaking when all features are available (row 11) and declining progressively as lag features are reduced (rows 11 >> row 8). Moreover, performance collapses in the absence of sowing features (rows 5-9), while models maintain reasonable performance even without irrigation features (rows 1-2). This indicates that AWD performance is predominantly driven by lag features and largely disregards irrigation features. This counterintuitive result suggests that AWD irrigation signals are not effectively captured with Sentinel-1A’s 12-day revisit frequency, which proves insufficient for detecting AWD irrigation cycles that typically occur every 4-10 days. This interpretation is further supported by evidence from rows 11-12, where increased sampling frequency to 10 days (i.e. lower temporal resolution incapable of capturing high frequency AWD events) yields optimal model performance.

4.4
Optimizing for Large-Scale Monitoring

For operational monitoring at scale, we tested various satellite data configurations. We found that ascending orbit data consistently outperforms descending orbit data for DSR classification, with no significant benefit from combining both orbits. Additionally, including climatology variables (temperature and precipitation) provides no performance boost (Fig. S5). These findings suggest that a streamlined approach using only ascending orbit data without climatology variables is optimal for large-scale monitoring, reducing computational requirements while maintaining classification performance.

4.5
Inference at scale

A key challenge in promoting sustainable agriculture is understanding adoption patterns of water-saving practices across large regions. The PRANA project trained 150,000 farmers on water-saving techniques, but tracking their implementation was impossible through traditional surveys. Our satellite-based approach enables monitoring at unprecedented scale, providing critical insights for food security policies.
To apply our classification framework across Punjab, we first needed to delineate individual agricultural plots. With approximately 3.2 million hectares of rice cultivation and average plot sizes of 0.5-2 hectares, millions of individual fields required identification. We automated this process using the Fields of the World (FTW) algorithm
[
20
]
, which processes bi-temporal Sentinel-2 imagery to identify field boundaries.
Our workflow (Fig. S2) begins with acquiring cloud-free Sentinel-2 imagery from May and October 2024. After preprocessing and dividing the region into manageable tiles, we applied the FTW model to generate field polygons, filtering for size (2,000
\text{m}^{2}
to 10 hectares) to match typical smallholder farms.
For each identified field, we processed Sentinel-1 time-series data and extracted embeddings from Presto to capture temporal signatures. To address the challenge of sparse training data (1,300 labeled plots within approximately 3 million agricultural plots extracted), we implemented an ensemble approach, selecting the top model from both RF and GB frameworks. These models’ predictions were aggregated by taking the modal value for each rice-growing plot, enhancing robustness and mitigating individual model biases, with the final results in Fig.
1
.

5
Limitations

Our approach, while effective, faces several constraints that merit consideration. First, the current Sentinel-1 revisit frequency of 12 days limits our ability to capture high-frequency irrigation variability characteristic of AWD practices. This temporal resolution challenge could be addressed by integrating data from ascending and descending orbits or incorporating forthcoming satellite missions such as Sentinel-1C, which would provide more frequent observations of irrigation cycles.

Second, field boundary extraction using the FTW algorithm is constrained by Sentinel-2’s spatial resolution, which imposes a minimum detectable plot size. This limitation is particularly significant in smallholder farming contexts where field sizes may fall below this detection threshold, potentially excluding smaller agricultural parcels from analysis.

Finally, our models’ performance relies heavily on lag features that capture temporal distinctions between different water management practices. The effectiveness of this approach depends on DSR practices maintaining sufficient temporal separation from other water management methods. Before generalizing these methods to other rice-growing regions globally or even temporally in the same region, additional validation studies must be conducted to determine whether these temporal patterns persist across diverse agroecological contexts and farming calendars.

6
Conclusion & Future Work

Our study demonstrates that satellite monitoring of rice water management practices is feasible, offering a powerful tool for tracking sustainable agriculture adoption. By classifying practices along separate sowing and irrigation dimensions, we achieved 78% F1-score in distinguishing direct seeded rice from traditional transplanted methods across highly variable planting dates.

This dimensional approach proved critical—DSR classification performed significantly better than attempting to classify all practices simultaneously. The framework successfully scaled to approximately 3 million plots across Punjab, with district-level predictions showing strong correlation (0.79) with government records. Our method requires no prior knowledge of planting dates, making it uniquely suited for real-world agricultural landscapes where cropping schedules vary widely.

While DSR detection performed robustly, monitoring irrigation practices like AWD remains challenging with current satellite revisit frequencies. The upcoming Sentinel-1C mission, expected in 2025, will provide the 6-day temporal resolution needed to better capture irrigation patterns.

This framework directly supports food security initiatives by enabling: 1) evidence-based resource allocation to promote sustainable water management, 2) measurement of intervention impacts at regional and farm scales, 3) targeted support for regions with low adoption rates, and 4) comprehensive monitoring without costly ground surveys

Future work should extend this approach to other major rice-producing regions facing water scarcity. By adapting the framework to diverse agricultural contexts, we can support sustainable intensification globally. Additional opportunities include developing region-specific models, integrating deep learning foundation models like Presto, and creating open data platforms for policymakers and researchers.

As climate change intensifies water scarcity challenges, scalable monitoring of sustainable water management will become increasingly vital for maintaining agricultural productivity and food security. This framework provides a foundation for tracking and promoting practices that allow farmers to produce more food with fewer resources while maintaining regional water security—directly supporting the Zero Hunger Sustainable Development Goal.

Previous studies that discriminated DSR from PTR relied on synchronizing SAR backscatter data with cropping calendars, enabling direct modeling of temporal signatures

,

. While effective, this approach is inherently limited in scalability, particularly in regions with unknown or heterogeneous planting dates.

Figure A1:
Planting date distributions across water management practices of interest.

As shown in Fig.
A1
, the planting dates in our dataset exhibit a roughly trimodal distribution: DSR plots are sown first, followed by control plots, and finally AWD plots. This temporal disparity spans up to 110 days between the first and last planting dates, introducing substantial temporal variability both between and within classes. This heterogeneous distribution naturally introduces temporal differences between classes, and where the distributions are wide enough - within classes. By not synchronizing cropping calenders across sample plots, we introduce
lagged features
—temporal offsets capturing the relative differences in planting dates—into our analysis. Lagged features enable the model to account for inter-class and intra-class variability in planting schedules, providing discriminatory power without requiring explicit cropping calendar data. Therefore lagged features can act as a proxy for cropping calenders as long as they exhibit low dispersion within a class. In practice, however, these features also reflect the limitations imposed by wide planting date distributions, which can span up to 2.5 months within individual classes like DSR and PTR as seen in Fig.
A1
.

Although planting and harvesting dates were available in our dataset, we intentionally excluded them from the modeling process to enhance the generalizability of our framework. This decision ensures that our approach remains applicable to regions where such information is unavailable. Additionally, farmer interviews revealed the presence of "third crops" (such as lentils, maize, or green manuring crops

) planted just before the kharif rice season. These crops introduce further variability in SAR backscatter signatures, complicating the detection of water management practices. Due to incomplete data on these practices, third-crop effects were not explicitly modeled but represent an additional source of temporal heterogeneity.

Modeling Details

We experiment with ensemble-based learning methods including random forest (RF) and gradient-boosted trees (GB) to classify rice plots into one of 3 possible classes: AWD, DSR or PTR. Previous works have shown the suitability of these methods to LULC and agricultural practice mappings

Hand-crafted Features:
We generate a set of hand-crafted features from each S-1 band (
VV,VH,VV/VH
) per orbit, as the summary statistics for a rice-growing plot. For each band, we locate the positions of the first
K
troughs and crests as well as the first
K
inflection points. We extract the timestamp and amplitude at these points, measuring timestamp relative to the first date in the timeseries, in number of days. Experimentally, it was found that
K=3
would cover all timeseries in the training dataset, with larger
K

[
28
]

R^{2}
of kernel fit) as additional features. Additionally, we calculate the Radar Vegetation Index (RVI) as modified for S-1
[
30

as it has been shown to be useful in detecting crop height. The timeseries generation process for S-1 bands after processing and additional hand-crafted features are visualized in

Appendix D
Ablations

D.1
Effect of Sentinel-1 Orbit

For DSR classification, ascending orbits consistently outperformed descending orbits, as shown in
Figure A3
(left). This suggests that radiometric properties of the descending orbit make it suboptimal for capturing DSR features in this region. The use of both orbits showed no significant advantage over ascending orbit alone, and the additional effort required to process both orbits may not be justified. For AWD classification, orbit direction had less impact, likely due to lag features driving performance regardless of orbit, while AWD-specific features were not captured effectively. This highlights that ascending orbits better capture DSR features compared to descending orbits in this study.

D.2
Effect of Climatology Data

High overlap in CIs for models with and without climatology data indicates no significant benefit from including these variables (
Figure A3
, right). As the average performance without ERA5-Land data was slightly higher, and considering the effort required to acquire and integrate this dataset, there appears to be no inherent advantage to its inclusion for this region and task.

These findings suggest that consistent use of ascending orbits is optimal for DSR classification, while climatology data may be omitted without loss of performance, simplifying large-scale inference.

Appendix F
Large-scale Inference

An important goal of this study is to understand the spatial extent of use of water-saving rice management practices in Punjab. Out of the 150,000 farmers who received training and support for the 2024 kharif season, it was infeasible to track the practices of all participants and their networks during the season to measure the efficacy of these interventions. The data collection efforts of PRANA were limited to only a small fraction of the potential market. Here we present our findings on scaling the model’s predictions across the state at a plot-level.

The entire workflow for large-scale inference is outlined in
Figure A7
. In order to make predictions at a plot-level, we first need to delineate agricultural plots across the state. There are no current estimates of the number of plots, although the total rice-growing area in 2021 was roughly 3.2 million hectares (ha) as surmised from Han et al.
[
17

We merge the tiles and stack the bi-temporal images as a single 8-band image (Fig
