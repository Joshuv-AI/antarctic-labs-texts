Investigating blood cell images for enhanced hematologic disorder detection using multi-scale feature learning with a hybrid deep learning model
Authors: Mutasim Al Sadig, Jamal Alsamri, Nouf Helal Alharbi, Mashael M Asiri, Mukhtar Ghaleb, Asma A Alhashmi, Hamad Almansour, Ohud Alasmari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHaematological diseases, also known as blood disorders, encompass a range of conditions that affect the blood and its components, including platelets, red blood cells, plasma, white blood cells, and the bone marrow. These illnesses can disrupt the normal function, production, or quantity of blood cells, leading to severe health problems. Standard types of haematological diseases include lymphoma, anaemia, multiple myeloma, leukaemia, thrombocytopenia, and haemophilia. Diagnosis typically involves blood tests and microscopic examinations of bone marrow or blood samples. At the same time, treatment varies according to the form and severity of the disease, often including chemotherapy, medication, or bone marrow transplantation. Screening for haematological disorders is crucial for their diagnosis and effective treatment. The medical diagnosis of a haematological disorder is mainly dependent on laboratory blood tests. Still, the most experienced haematology professional can supervise designs, relations, and deviations among the growing number of blood parameters that modern laboratories compute. Deep learning has experienced significant growth over the last decade and is being applied effectively in various intelligent applications, including the detection of haematological disorders. This paper presents a Multi-Scale Feature Learning-Based Enhanced Hematologic Disorder Detection Using Hybrid Deep Classification Model (MSFLHDD-HDCM) approach. The goal is to develop an accurate model for detecting and classifying hematologic disorders using microscopic images of blood cells. Initially, the image pre-processing stage employs the geometric mean filter to enhance image quality by effectively removing noise and standardizing the input data, thereby improving the accuracy of subsequent analysis. Furthermore, the MSFLHDD-HDCM method utilizes the inception modules technique for feature extraction. Finally, a hybrid model combining a convolutional neural network and a bidirectional gated recurrent unit is utilized for classification. A comprehensive experiment of the MSFLHDD-HDCM method is performed under the blood cell image dataset. The comparison analysis of the MSFLHDD-HDCM method demonstrated a superior accuracy value of 99.67% over existing techniques.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21412-9.Keywords: Multi-scale feature learning, Hematologic disorder, Deep learning, Blood cell images analysis, Artificial intelligenceSubject terms: Computer science, Information technology

Introduction

An unusual blood count or blood cell appearance doesn’t always suggest a primary haematological disorder, as it might indicate an underlying non-haematological condition or be caused by medical treatments1. Anaemia is seen in various situations; however, an initial haematological disorder must be suspected if a patient presents with an enlarged spleen, swollen lymph nodes, a tendency to bleed or clot, and/or vague symptoms typical of lymphomas and leukaemias, namely, weight loss, sweats, or malaise2. As with any medical issue, the initial steps in diagnosing a condition involve taking a detailed medical and medication history, as well as conducting a thorough physical examination. The outcome of these factors, along with gender, age, ethnic background, family history, social factors, and awareness of locally common illnesses, will guide further laboratory tests3. Although the variety of haematological examinations available to support medical and public health services is vast, the simplest examinations are often the most beneficial in identifying the diagnosis. Even under-equipped labs offer a primary test set, including WBC count, haemoglobin concentration, examination of a peripheral blood film, and platelet count, to assess different cellular morphology and leucocyte count4. These screening assessments help in quickly suspecting underlying diseases. Leukaemia is a fast proliferation of irregular WBC (leukocytes). Anaemia is identified by a decrease in the RBC (erythrocytes) morphology5. The reason for anaemia is an abnormal size or quantity of RBC in the blood plasma of the human body. That, consecutively, is because of the lower haemoglobin count or partial haemoglobin occurrence in RBCs. This disorder is medically termed microcytic anaemia6. Thalassemia, a type of anaemia, occurs due to RBC abnormalities. Azotemia is another type of haematological disorder caused by excessive nitrogen compounds, which is characterized by variations in the micromorphological features of blood cells. The domains of machine learning (ML), DL, and artificial intelligence (AI) are expected to transform the way medicine is practised7.

The current medicinal literature on ML and DL usage is growing rapidly. However, many of these technologies are still inadequate, with a lack of potential and practical applications for the majority of suggested models. The incorporation of ML and DL in healthcare faces several moral and practical challenges8. Still, the domain is promising, considering the larger quantity of data produced by the existing healthcare system and the capability of ML and DL to examine, handle, and recognize patterns9. The domain of haematology (benign and malignant) is also quickly developing with advances in genomic/molecular knowledge and the rise in novel treatments entering development. For example, utilizing DL and ML in genomics may aid in overcoming the issue of an enormous volume of data, resulting in better diagnosis, detection of gene patterns, and improved efficacy10. Early detection of hematologic disorders is crucial for effective treatment and patient care. Discrepancies in blood cells may be a sign of blood cancers or an immune system disorder. However, these variances may also be due to other health issues or treatments, making a precise diagnosis challenging. Utilizing advanced image analysis techniques can aid in distinguishing subtle changes in blood cells, leading to improved diagnostic accuracy. Integrating multi-scale feature learning with hybrid DL methods presents a promising approach to improve detection capabilities and support clinical decision-making.

Survey on hematologic disease detection methods

Prakash et al.11 presented a robust and precise technique for segmenting and classifying blood cells in a Peripheral Blood Smear image (PBSI). This technique includes a pre-processing stage comprising decorrelation stretching (DCS), Fuzzy C-means (FCM) clustering, and histogram matching for a stain normalization approach to segment RBC. The segmented blood cells are classified as normal or abnormal based on irregularities using the SqueezeNet DL paradigm. Kandasamy et al.12 introduced a new deep CNN (DCNN) that employs hybrid category-enabled and multi-objective optimizer approaches to enhance its parameters. Early identification and treatment are hindered by the need for pathologists to classify samples that are sensitive and require a long process. Mondal et al.13 presented a DL-aided automatic model for blood cell classification and counts from microscopic images. The model also utilized the watershed algorithm (WSA). Classify nine categories of blood cells, such as Neutrophil, Erythrocyte, Monocyte, Erythroblast, Basophil, Eosinophil, Lymphocyte, Platelet, and Immature Granulocytes. Numerous pre-processing phases, including rescaling, image resizing, augmentation, and contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE), were employed. Kanavos et al.14 presented a new CNN framework developed specially for WBC image classification. This framework, trained on a broad dataset, automates the process of extracting distinctive features for precise subtype recognition. The advanced techniques significantly enhance this framework, surpassing current methods in the exact classification of WBCs into their corresponding subtypes.

Shahin et al.15 recommended a system that incorporates the YOLO-v5 object detection architecture with an Enhanced Graywolf Optimiser (EGWO) and an AM to improve the precision and efficacy of RBC recognition. This model is enhanced through the EGWO technique, whereas the AM concentrates on removing crucial features. Mayrose et al.16 explored the use of pre-trained CNNs for automated dengue fever recognition. Transfer learning (TL) is applied to three sophisticated CNNs: MobileNetV3Large, ResNet50, and MobileNetV3Small, to modify these models for distinguishing between dengue-infected and non-dengue-infected blood smears. An explainable AI (XAI) methodology is employed to facilitate accurate region imaging on the smears, which aids in making predictions. Kaur et al.17 proposed a new Deep Skip Connections-driven Dense Network (DSCNet) personalized for ALL analysis, leveraging PBSI. The DSCNet model combines custom image filtering, skip connections, dropout regularisation, and KL divergence loss to enhance its efficiency and generality. DSCNet utilizes skip links to address the gradient vanishing issue and capture long-term dependencies, while custom image filtering enhances pertinent features in the input data. Kumar and Babulal18 suggested an image analysis perspective for characterizing erythrocytes based on morphological variations. The primary objective of this study is to advance the first erythrocyte screening by examining colour, textural, and morphological characteristics using the FC-TriSDR approach. Methodological framework

In this study, the MSFLHDD-HDCM model is presented. This paper aims to develop an accurate model for classifying and detecting hematologic disorders using microscopic images of blood cells. To achieve this, the MSFLHDD-HDCM approach incorporates noise reduction through GMF, feature representation via the Inception module, and hybrid classification models. Pre-processing stage with GMF technique

At the primary stage, image pre-processing applies the GMF model to enhance image quality by removing noise and standardizing input data for improved analysis19. This model is chosen for its superior capability in mitigating noise while preserving crucial image details and edges, which is significant in medical image analysis. This technique preserves the integrity of blood cell structures, ensuring more precise and more accurate feature extraction downstream, unlike arithmetic mean filters, which can blur edges and lose crucial data. Furthermore, GMF performs well against impulsive and Gaussian noise, presenting a balanced smoothing effect without excessive distortion. Its computational efficiency and simplicity also make it an ideal choice over more complex denoising techniques, enabling faster processing without compromising image quality. Overall, GMF enhances the quality of input data, thereby improving the robustness and accuracy of the subsequent classification model.

Additive white Gaussian noise is a standard method used in images. White noise is generated by arbitrary variations in signal-coloured cameras, where more expansion is employed in the blue channel, in addition to the red and green channels. Although the image will be altered by adding challenging Gaussian noise, all pixels of the image will be affected. It splits the green, blue, and red channels. Every filtered channel is integrated to form subsequent colored images.

GMF substitutes the colouring of all pixel values with a geometric mean of colored pixel values from a wider area near it, depending on the dimensions of the filter (3 × 3 or 5 × 5), and produces a more substantial effect of the filter. GMF performed better than conventional models.

Inception module-based feature learning

Following this, the MSFLHDD-HDCM model utilizes the Inception Modules technique for feature extraction20. This model is chosen for its superior capability in capturing multi-scale information by simultaneously applying convolutional filters of diverse sizes within the same layer. This enables the model to extract both fine-grained and coarse features from blood cell images, which is crucial for detecting subtle discrepancies associated with hematologic disorders. This module enhances representational power while keeping computational costs manageable through dimensionality reduction techniques, such as 1 × 1 convolutions, unlike conventional convolutional layers. Its parallel architecture allows efficient learning of diverse patterns, enhancing feature richness and robustness. Thus, the inception module contributes significantly to accurate and comprehensive feature extraction.

Inception architectures are characterized by their Inception modules, which comprise numerous parallel convolutional filters of varying sizes. These modules are designed to remove features at various scales within a single layer. Therefore, the network can process information at changing resolutions successfully. This model eliminates the need for larger, computationally expensive filters, thereby guaranteeing higher efficiency. Using small filters reduces either the number of parameters or the computational cost, thereby improving the efficiency of the network. Inception frameworks have proven to be effective in various computer vision (CV) tasks, including applications such as object detection and image classification. They signify a model for FL that strikes a balance between precision and computational complexity, making it suitable for real-time applications where either factor is significant.

GoogleNet, also known as Inception-v1, is a 22-layer CNN network that utilizes the inception model. In creating this CNN network, it acknowledges that objects within images may differ in dimensions, requiring kernels of varying sizes to facilitate data distribution. Hence, in the inception model, dissimilar‐sized kernels are applied at related CNN networking levels. GoogleNet contains 22 deep convolutional layers. If layers with pooling are also tested regarding depth, it may have 27 deep layers. Following the first dual convolutional and max pooling layers, GoogleNet utilizes nine inception modules. During the naïve inception module, three dissimilar-sized convolutional layers are used to process the output of the preceding layer or component, such as the feature matrix. The kernel sizes utilized in these convolutional layers are , and . Furthermore, a maximum pooling layer is applied. During the module, the outputs from every convolutional and max pooling layer are combined and then transferred to the following inception module.

To reduce the computational load in the pure inception module, a dimension-reduced inception module is proposed. Here, a convolutional layer is deployed before the 3 × 3 and 5 × 5 convolutional layers. Moreover, a 3 × 3 maximal pooling layer is followed by a convolutional layer afterwards. The aim of combining dimension-reduced inception modules is to reduce the parameter count and computational needs, while still successfully capturing the main features. To achieve this, 1 × 1 convolutional layers are applied to reduce the feature mapping dimensionality before using the more computationally demanding and convolutional layers. These outcomes are presented in a more computationally efficient structure, while also benefiting from the feature diversity provided by the Inception framework.

Inception-v2 is an enhanced form of the GoogleNet (Inception-v1) CNN structure. It is an improved DL network that acts as Inception-v2, streamlining the complexity established in the GoogleNet structure, making the convolutional neural network (CNN) more adjustable to different conditions. Some main principles and optimization concepts were presented alongside Inception-v2 to enable the scalability of the convolutional network, including preventing representational bottlenecks, balancing the size of the network and its layers, and facilitating the easy processing of high-dimensional representations within the local network. During GoogleNet, 5 × 5 convolutional layers were applied in a few cases to considerably lower input sizes, which can result in a reduction in the precision of the CNN. In GoogleNet, -sized filters are applied; however, in the Inception-v2 model, they selected consecutive -sized filters. This modification helped reduce the computational load, resulting in an enhanced method.

Inception-v2 processes input differently compared to the direct operation. Rather than directly using the convolution on the × input, it consecutively uses 1 × n and n × 1 convolutions, for example: in the convolution layer of the Inception-v2 network, the input tensor is initially convolved with a 1 × 3 kernel, and then a 3 × 1 kernel is used on the output, leading to the last output. This model decreases the computational overhead by nearly 33%.

Generally, convolutional networks have depended on pooling operations to downsize feature maps. The network filters’ sizes are optimized in advance to avoid a bottleneck in data representation. Inception-v3 is considered one of the most advanced and popular structures for image classification tasks. It is an optimization and improvement over its predecessors, v1 and v2, providing a deep convolutional framework while maintaining higher efficacy, faster handling, and considerably lower error rates. This model contains 421 layers and utilizes a model which incorporates convolutional filters in different dimensions into a single filter. It is subjected to updates and modifications in Inception modules, the grid system, and the auxiliary classifier. Therefore, Inception-V3 achieves higher efficacy with reduced computational overloads, resulting in smaller parameters for training and lower computational complexity.

CNN-BiGRU-based classification architecture

At last, the hybrid CNN-BiGRU technique is employed for the classification model21. This technique is chosen for its efficiency in combining spatial and temporal feature learning capabilities. The CNN layer effectively extracts local and hierarchical spatial features from blood cell images, capturing crucial visual patterns. Meanwhile, the BiGRU processes sequential data in both forward and backwards directions, enabling the model to comprehend temporal dependencies and context within the extracted features. This hybrid model enhances the technique’s capability in recognizing intrinsic patterns that may evolve over spatial sequences, thereby improving classification accuracy. Moreover, the BiGRU’s gating mechanism helps mitigate vanishing gradient issues, ensuring robust learning and improved generalization compared to conventional RNNs. CNN is utilized in DL-based feature extraction, particularly in image analysis and time series data. In this model, the CNN module is used for extracting high-dimensional features from transaction patterns and data, providing enriched inputs to the Bi-GRU layer. The CNN technique comprises convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers. Initially, input data is sent to the convolutional layer by utilizing multiple 1 × 7 kernels, followed by LeakyReLU activation for nonlinear feature extraction. The resulting feature maps are then downsampled by a max-pooling layer, which mitigates the data size from 10 × 128 to 5 × 128 while preserving crucial features. This pooling step helps mitigate overfitting and improves the model’s generalization.

Moreover, the data enters the second pooling and convolution layers to eliminate high‐level features. The 2nd convolutional layer employs 1281 × 128 convolution kernels for convolution and is handled by the activation and LeakyReLU functions. Subsequently, in the second layer of max pooling, the data size can also be reduced to 1 × 64. Afterwards, the dual pooling and convolution operation is performed, allowing the CNN to remove high-level characteristics, thereby offering rich data input for the further layer of Bi-GRU.

Finally, the pooling and convolution data are converted into a 1D vector through flattening and then input into the FC layer. It maps eliminated features to the previous output area over the activation function and linear transformation.

The image size is 360 × 363 pixels, and professional pathologists might explain them. Conclusion

In this study, the MSFLHDD-HDCM approach is presented. This paper aims to develop an accurate model for classifying and detecting hematologic disorders using microscopic images of blood cells. For this, the MSFLHDD-HDCM approach comprises noise reduction through GMF, feature representation via the Inception module, and hybrid classification models. Initially, the image pre-processing step utilizes the GMF model to enhance image quality by removing noise and standardizing input data for improved analysis. Following this, the MSFLHDD-HDCM model utilizes the Inception Modules technique for feature extraction in the design of its Inception Modules. Finally, the hybrid CNN-BiGRU model is employed for classification. A comprehensive experiment of the MSFLHDD-HDCM method is performed under the blood cell image dataset. The comparison analysis of the MSFLHDD-HDCM method demonstrated a superior accuracy value of 99.67% over existing techniques. The limitations of the MSFLHDD-HDCM method include its dependence on a relatively restricted dataset, which may affect the generalizability of the outcomes across diverse populations and varied imaging conditions. Furthermore, the model lacks real-time processing capabilities, which could limit its applicability in urgent clinical settings. The study also lacks extensive validation on multi-center datasets to confirm robustness across diverse medical environments. Future work should focus on expanding the dataset size and diversity, integrating real-time analysis for faster diagnosis, and exploring explainable AI methods to improve interpretability. Furthermore, incorporating this model with other diagnostic modalities could improve overall accuracy and clinical relevance.
