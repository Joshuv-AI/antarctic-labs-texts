Satisfaction With Sexual and Reproductive Health Services and Associated Factors Among Women Visiting Public Health Facilities of Kembata Zone, Ethiopia
Authors: Desta Erkalo Abame, Mihiret Mengistu, Dawit Sullamo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClient satisfaction is a key measure of healthcare quality with direct implications for service utilization and health outcomes. Despite its importance, evidence on women’s satisfaction with sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services and their determinants remains limited in Ethiopia. This study aimed to assess the level of satisfaction with SRH services and associated factors among women attending public health facilities in Kembata Zone, Central Ethiopia.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 569 women attending public health facilities in Kembata Zone, recruited using simple random sampling. Data were collected through exit interviews, entered into EpiData 3.1, and analyzed using SPSS 23. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with women’s satisfaction. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05, and the strength of association was measured using AOR with 95% CI.ResultsThe overall women’s satisfaction with SRH services was 79.2% (95% CI: 72.1–84.5). Women with college-level education or above (AOR = 2.51, 95% CI: 1.10, 5.74), clinical staff showed respect (AOR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.02, 2.53), and waiting time 30–60 minutes AOR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.17, 0.86) were significantly associated factors.ConclusionWomen’s satisfaction with SRH services was below the national target. Educational status, respectful care by clinical staff, and waiting time were the key determinants of satisfaction. Interventions should focus on training healthcare providers in respectful, person-centered care, reducing waiting times through efficient service organization, and targeting women with lower educational attainment to improve overall SRH service satisfaction.Keywords: satisfaction, sexual and reproductive health, women, public health facilities, Ethiopia

Introduction

Client satisfaction in healthcare reflects the gap between what patients expect and what they actually experience. 1 It is a crucial metric for evaluating healthcare quality in facilities, yielding sustained advantages for clients and communities. 2 This is particularly important in sexual and reproductive health (SRH), which encompasses maternal and child care, family planning (including infertility services), abortion, and STI/HIV services, along with associated information and counseling. 3 A woman’s satisfaction with these services significantly influences her immediate and future health outcomes and service utilization. 4

In Ethiopia, recent data from the 2019 EDHS mini report indicates increased utilization of skilled provider attendance at antenatal care (ANC) 74% and institutional deliveries have risen from 26% to 48% between 2016 and 2019, and home deliveries have correspondingly fallen from 73% to 51%. 5 Despite these gains, Ethiopian women continue to face compounding challenges to SRH access, among them, low educational attainment, unemployment, and extreme poverty, leaving significant gaps in community-level service delivery. 6

Evidence from developing countries indicates that women’s satisfaction with SRH services remains suboptimal. Studies conducted in South Africa and Kenya found that only 51.9% and 56% of mothers, respectively, were satisfied with delivery services. 7 Similarly, research conducted at a maternity referral hospital in Addis Ababa showed that only 2.4% to 21% of mothers were fully satisfied with maternal health services. 8 Another study in northern Ethiopia on client satisfaction and contributing factors towards sexual and reproductive health service delivery found that 65.5% of women reported satisfaction. 9 The figures highlight that SRH service satisfaction is low in most developing countries.

The policy context reinforces the urgency of addressing this gap. Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target 3.7 aims for universal access to SRH services by 2030. 10 In line with this goal, Ethiopia’s National Reproductive Health Strategy (2016–2020) emphasizes the integration of STI/HIV prevention and treatment into broader reproductive health services. 11 Yet common barriers, including drug shortages, insufficiently skilled providers, and long distances to facilities, poor service quality, provider unavailability, high costs, and limited awareness, continue to undermine utilization.7,9 Education on the cognitive, emotional, social, and physical dimensions of sexuality is also recognized as an important enabler of SRH engagement, particularly among young people.7,9,12 Recognizing both the global imperative and comprehensive evidence remains limited, particularly in the Kembata zone. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the level of satisfaction and identify factors associated with satisfaction among women utilizing SRH services in public health facilities in the Kembata zone, Ethiopia.

Methods and Materials

Study Area and Period

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted in public health facilities of the Kembata Zone, Ethiopia, from January 1 to 30, 2025. Kembata Zone is located 293 km south of Addis Ababa and consists of seven Woreda health offices and six town administrative health units. The zone has four hospitals (one general hospital and three primary hospitals), 31 health centers (28 governmental and three non-governmental), 136 health posts, and a total of 1,170 health professionals. According to the 2007 national census, the zone has a total population of 1,080,837, of whom 544,161 are women, and approximately 85.64% reside in rural areas. According to the 2024 report from the zonal health department, there were 201,836 women of reproductive age (15–49 years) in the zone, of whom 34,061 were pregnant. 13

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

All reproductive-age women who visited the selected public health facilities and utilized at least one SRH service during the data collection period were eligible for inclusion. For this study, SRH services were defined as: Antenatal care (ANC), delivery, and postnatal care (PNC), Family planning services, Infertility-related care, Safe abortion care, sexually transmitted infection (STI) and HIV-related services, and SRH counseling and information services.

Exclusion Criteria

Women who met any of the following conditions were excluded from the study: Non-residents of the Kembata Zone (defined as residing in the zone for less than six months). Individuals presenting with severe illness at the time of data collection. Individuals with speech or hearing impairments that prevented effective communication during the interview process.

Sample Size

The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula based on the following assumptions: a 95% confidence level (Zα/2 = 1.96), a margin of error of 5% (d = 0.05), and a prevalence of client satisfaction with SRH services of 65%, obtained from a previous study conducted in northern Ethiopia. 14 Because a multistage sampling technique was employed, a design effect of 1.5 was applied to account for sampling variability. After adding a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was calculated to be 578.

Sampling Procedure

A multistage sampling technique was used to select study districts, health facilities, and participants. Initially, the Kembata Zone was stratified into rural districts and urban administrative towns. Following the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation to select 30% of administrative units, three rural districts (Angacha, Doyogena, and Hadero Tunto) were selected from the seven rural districts, and two administrative towns (Durame and Shinshicho) were selected from the six town administrations in the zone. Subsequently, nine public health facilities, comprising one hospital and eight health centers, were selected by lottery. The total sample size (n = 578) was then proportionally allocated to each selected health facility according to the average monthly client flow for SRH services, based on the District Health Information System (DHIS) report for the corresponding month of the previous year. Finally, study participants were recruited using simple random sampling during exit interviews. Participants were women of reproductive age who attended selected public health facilities in the Kembata Zone to receive SRH services during the study period. Recruitment occurred at the point of exit after receiving services to ensure that responses reflected their immediate service experience.

Study Variables

Women’s satisfaction with SRH services served as the outcome variable of this study. Satisfaction was measured using 12 items assessed on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from “very dissatisfied” and “dissatisfied” through “neutral” to “satisfied” and “very satisfied.” An overall satisfaction score was computed by summing responses across the 12 items. Participants who scored at or above the mean were categorized as “satisfied,” while those who scored below the mean were categorized as “not satisfied.” Independent variables included socio-demographic, facility-related, and healthcare provider-related factors. Socio-demographic variables included age, place of residence, marital status, educational status, occupation, religion, monthly income, and family size. Facility-related factors included waiting time, women’s attitudes toward SRH services, cleanliness of the clinic, availability of educational posters with key messages in service rooms, frequency of visits, and type of SRH services received. Healthcare provider-related factors included appointment arrangements, maintenance of privacy during counselling and procedures, respectful treatment by healthcare providers, and the adequacy of explanations provided regarding procedures and the possible effects of treatment or services.

Data Collection Tools and Techniques

Data were collected using a pretested, interviewer-administered structured questionnaire adapted from relevant literature and modified to fit the local context. The questionnaire comprised three main sections: socio-demographic characteristics, client- and facility-related factors, and healthcare provider-related factors associated with satisfaction with SRH services. The questionnaire was initially developed in English and subsequently translated into the two local languages, Kembatissa and Amharic. To ensure linguistic consistency and conceptual equivalence, the translated versions were back-translated into English by professional language translators. Data collection was carried out by six diploma-holding midwives under the supervision of two BSc-qualified public health professionals. Before data collection, both data collectors and supervisors underwent two days of training covering the study objectives, data collection procedures, interview techniques, and ethical considerations. The principal investigator closely supervised the data collection process by reviewing the completeness and consistency of completed questionnaires on a daily basis and providing timely feedback to data collectors. To ensure data quality, the questionnaire was pretested on 10% of the total sample size at Halaba General Hospital before the commencement of the actual data collection period.

Women’s satisfaction with SRH services was evaluated using a 12-item composite satisfaction scale, with responses rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “very dissatisfied” to “very satisfied.” The internal consistency reliability of the scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.79, indicating acceptable reliability. Participants were classified by satisfaction level using the mean composite score as the cutoff point, with those scoring at or above the mean categorized as “satisfied” and those scoring below the mean categorized as “not satisfied.”

Data Analysis

All completed questionnaires were checked for completeness, edited, cleaned, and coded before being entered into EpiData version 3.1. Following double-entry verification in EpiData, the dataset was exported to SPSS version 23 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed, and results were presented in tables and figures. Bivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between each independent variable and client satisfaction with SRH services. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were considered candidate variables and entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Multivariable logistic regression was then conducted using the backward likelihood ratio (LR) method to identify factors independently associated with the outcome variable. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05, and the strength of association was interpreted using Adjusted Odds Ratios (AOR) with corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), which yielded a value of 1, indicating the absence of multicollinearity. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, which produced a p-value of 0.307, confirming that the model adequately fit the data.

Results

Socio-Demographic and Socio-Economic Characteristics of Respondents

Of the 578 sampled women, 569 participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 98.3%. Nine women were excluded due to incomplete responses. Nearly half, 279 (49.0%) of the participants, were in the age range of 25–34 years. The median age was 26 years (IQR: 22–36). Among the respondents, 441 (77.5%) were married, and 72 (12.7%) had a diploma or higher educational qualification. Regarding occupation, 293 (51.5%) were housewives, and 91 (16.0%) were government employees (Table 1).

Facility and Client-Related Characteristics of Respondents

Among the respondents, 103 (18.1%) waited less than 30 minutes, 244 (42.9%) waited between 30 minutes and one hour, and nearly two-fifths, 222 (39.0%), waited one hour or more before receiving SRH services. Almost all respondents, 541 (95.0%), received the specific service they sought. Regarding the physical environment, nearly one quarter of respondents, 145 (25.5%), were not comfortable with the cleanliness of the clinic, and three-quarters, 427 (75.0%), reported that service rooms had no posters with key health messages. In terms of visit frequency, 355 (62.4%) were first-time visitors, while 214 (37.6%) had visited the facility for SRH services more than once. Regarding the type of SRH services utilized, nearly half of the respondents, 278 (48.9%), used both long- and short-acting contraceptive methods (Figure 1).

Among all respondents, 267 (46.9%) reported that providers explained the possible side effects of the services or methods used, and 405 (71.2%) reported that healthcare providers treated them with respect. Regarding confidentiality, 355 (62.4%) of respondents indicated that their privacy was maintained during service delivery (Table 2).

Level of Women’s Satisfaction With SRH Services

Women’s satisfaction with various aspects of SRH services was assessed using a 12-item scale rated on a five-point Likert scale. Respondents were categorized as “not satisfied” if they scored below the mean or “satisfied” if they scored at or above the mean of the composite satisfaction scale.

The overall level of client satisfaction with SRH services was 79.2% (95%CI: 72.1–84.5). Regarding individual satisfaction items, the majority of respondents, 497 (87.3%), were satisfied with the provision of health information in clear, understandable language, making it the highest-rated service component. In contrast, nearly half of the respondents, 291 (51.1%), expressed dissatisfaction with the availability of adequate drugs and medical supplies at the facility, representing the lowest-rated component of service satisfaction (Table 3).

Multivariable Logistic Regression Analysis of Variables With Client Satisfaction of Women With SRH Services in Kembata Zone, Central Ethiopia

To identify candidate variables for the multivariable logistic regression model, bivariable logistic regression analysis was first conducted. Variables with a p-value less than 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were selected and entered into the multivariable model. Accordingly, age, educational status, occupational status, place of residence, appointments given, privacy maintained, clinical staff showing respect, providers explaining procedures, side effects explained, waiting time, cleanliness of clinic, and rooms with message posters were all candidates that met this threshold and were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Variables with a p-value less than 0.05 in the multivariable logistic regression analysis were considered statistically significant factors of women’s satisfaction with SRH services. Based on the final adjusted model, educational status, clinical staff showing respect, and waiting time were the variables that had a statistically significant independent association with client satisfaction with SRH services.

Therefore, the final multivariable model showed that there was a statistically significant association between the educational statuses of women’s satisfaction with SRH services. The odds of satisfaction with SRH services were 2.51 times higher for those women with an education of college and above than clients with no education (AOR= 2.51, 95%CI: 1.10, 5.74, p < 0.05).

Women who reported that clinical staff showed them respect during their visit were 1.62 times more likely to be satisfied with SRH services compared to those who did not experience respectful care (AOR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.02, 2.53, p < 0.05).

Waiting time was negatively associated with client satisfaction. Women who waited between 30 and 60 minutes were 62% less likely to be satisfied compared to those who waited less than 30 minutes (AOR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.17, 0.86, p < 0.05). More strikingly, women who waited 1 hour or more were 82% less likely to be satisfied compared to those who waited less than 30 minutes (AOR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.08, 0.39, p < 0.01) (Table 4).

Discussion

Ethiopia is among the few countries that account for a disproportionate share of global maternal deaths, largely due to a lack of quality maternal health care before, during, and after childbirth. Client satisfaction is widely recognized as an essential indicator of health care quality for women. 15 This study found that overall client satisfaction with SRH services was 79. 2%. This finding is in line with the studies in Southwestern Ethiopia, which reported a satisfaction rate of (79.1%), 16 and the Gaza Strip of Palestine, which reported (77.3%). 17 However, the satisfaction rate in this study was higher than that reported at the Family Guidance Association of North Ethiopia, where satisfaction stood at (65.52%). 14 This difference may reflect variations in study scope, target population, and study area. In contrast, satisfaction in this study was lower than from a study conducted in Nigeria, where satisfaction reached (87.2%). 18 This variation may stem from cultural and expectation differences, as cultural factors such as communication styles, attitudes toward health care, and expectations of service quality are known to influence client satisfaction across settings. 19

The educational status of women was a significant independent predictor of client satisfaction with SRH services. Women who had attained college-level education or higher were 2.51 times more likely to be satisfied with SRH services than those with no formal education (AOR = 2.51, 95%CI: 1.10, 5.74). This finding is consistent with a study conducted in North Ethiopia, where college-educated women were also significantly more likely to be satisfied. 14 Well-educated women are more likely to be satisfied with and utilize SRH services because education empowers them to understand their health needs, make informed decisions, seek appropriate information and services, and advocate for themselves. 20

In this study, women who reported that clinical staff showed them respect were 1.62 times more likely to be satisfied with SRH services compared to those who did not (AOR = 1.62, 95%CI: 1.02, 2.53). This finding is consistent with a study conducted at FGAE clinics in North Ethiopia, where clients who experienced respectful provider interactions were 5.59 times more likely to be satisfied, 14 and with a study on maternal satisfaction in Central Ethiopia, 21 which similarly reported that respectful care was a significant predictor of satisfaction. The possible explanation is that when clinical staff show respect, women feel valued, dignified, and emotionally safe to discuss sensitive reproductive health matters openly. 20 Also, respectful interactions foster trust, improve provider-client communication, and empower women to actively participate in their own healthcare decisions, all of which collectively contribute to higher satisfaction with SRH services.

Waiting time demonstrated a significant negative association with client satisfaction. Women who waited between 30 and 60 minutes were 62% less likely to be satisfied compared to those who waited less than 30 minutes (AOR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.17, 0.86), while women who waited 10 hours or more were 82% less likely to be satisfied (AOR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.08, 0.39). This finding is consistent with a study conducted at public hospitals of Guji Zone, Southern Ethiopia, which reported that women who waited 30 minutes or less were nearly five times more likely to be satisfied with delivery services compared to those who waited longer. 20 Similarly, a study on family planning satisfaction in Northwest Ethiopia found a clear gradient, where clients waiting less than 30 minutes, 30 to 60 minutes, and one to two hours were progressively less satisfied compared to those waiting over two hours. 22 Waiting for extended periods can be stressful and frustrating for clients, while shorter waiting times create a more positive perception of services and signal organizational efficiency and responsiveness.

Limitations

This study acknowledges some limitations that must be considered in the interpretation of its findings. Firstly, as the study employed a cross-sectional design, causal inferences between SRH and the associated factors might not be established. Moreover, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the potential for recall bias, as participants may struggle to accurately remember past SRH experiences, especially in relation to healthcare visits, contraceptive use, and obstetric history. Additionally, socially desirable response bias may have influenced participants’ responses, as women might have underreported or overreported certain maternal and reproductive behaviours due to perceived social norms and expectations. Furthermore, the use of interviewer-administered questionnaires poses a risk of interviewer bias, which, despite efforts to ensure neutrality and confidentiality, cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusion

This study found that the overall level of women’s satisfaction with SRH services is moderate relative to other comparable studies. In the final adjusted model, educational status, clinical staff showing respect, and waiting time were the independent predictors of women’s satisfaction with SRH services. Women with college-level education or above were more likely to be satisfied, while longer waiting times were negatively associated with satisfaction. Respectful conduct by clinical staff was also a significant positive predictor of satisfaction.

To improve women’s satisfaction with SRH services, healthcare providers should receive regular training on respectful, person-centered care, as interpersonal quality of care was a key determinant of women’s satisfaction. Health facility managers should implement strategies to reduce waiting times through better appointment scheduling, adequate staffing, and efficient service organization. Efforts to improve women’s educational attainment, in collaboration with relevant sectors, may also contribute to better engagement with and satisfaction from SRH services over time. A mixed-methods study incorporating qualitative exploration of women’s experiences with SRH services is recommended to provide deeper insight into the factors shaping satisfaction beyond what quantitative measures can capture.
