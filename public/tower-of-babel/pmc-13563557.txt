Prognostic relevance of secondary actionable fusions in MLH1-deficient dMMR/MSI-H gastrointestinal tumors treated with pembrolizumab

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the treatment of mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) tumors, leading to durable responses and long-term survival in a subset of patients. Pembrolizumab has become a standard treatment option for advanced dMMR/MSI-H gastrointestinal tumors, supported by pivotal studies including KEYNOTE-164, KEYNOTE-158, and KEYNOTE-177.1, 2, 3

Nonetheless, resistance to ICIs, either primary or acquired, remains a major clinical challenge. Although factors such as misclassification of MSI status and atypical response patterns may contribute to a minority of cases,4, 5, 6 clinical outcomes remain heterogeneous even among accurately classified dMMR/MSI-H tumors. Approximately 15%-30% of patients exhibit primary resistance, although others experience disease progression after an initial response, whereas a substantial proportion achieve durable long-term benefit.1, 2, 3 These observations suggest that biological determinants beyond MSI status alone may influence both sensitivity to treatment and the durability of response under programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade.

In colorectal cancer (CRC), secondary actionable fusions involving kinase genes represent clinically relevant but uncommon molecular events. Among these, NTRK and RET fusions are of particular interest because they are therapeutically actionable and have been reported to occur preferentially in sporadic MutL homolog 1 (MLH1)-deficient, RAS/BRAF wild-type dMMR/MSI-H CRC.7, 8, 9 Reported frequencies range between 3% and 8% in unselected MSI-H/dMMR CRC series, although substantially higher frequencies have been described in molecularly selected subgroups enriched for MLH1 deficiency and RAS/BRAF wild-type status.9 This molecular context is biologically relevant because RAS or BRAF mutations, particularly BRAF V600E, may represent dominant oncogenic drivers, whereas kinase fusions may constitute alternative actionable events in mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway wild-type tumors. NTRK and RET inhibitors have demonstrated substantial and durable activity across tumor types, including gastrointestinal cancers.10, 11, 12 Although NTRK and RET fusions are pan-tumor alterations, their prevalence, molecular context, and clinical implications may differ across gastrointestinal tumor types.10, 11, 12

Beyond their established therapeutic relevance, the prognostic significance of secondary actionable fusions in patients receiving PD-1 blockade remains uncertain. In particular, it is unknown whether these alterations simply expand treatment options at disease progression or identify tumors with potentially distinct long-term outcomes under immunotherapy. This predefined molecular subanalysis of the previously published GAstrointestinal INestable (GAIN) real-world cohort13 aimed to characterize secondary actionable fusions detected by RNA sequencing in MLH1-deficient dMMR/MSI-H gastrointestinal tumors and to explore their potential prognostic relevance in patients treated with pembrolizumab.

Materials and methods

This retrospective multicenter study represents a predefined molecular subanalysis of the GAIN real-world cohort, previously reported elsewhere.13 The parent cohort included patients with advanced or metastatic dMMR/MSI-H gastrointestinal tumors treated with ICIs in routine clinical practice across five university hospitals belonging to the Galician Research Group on Gastrointestinal Tumors. dMMR/MSI-H status was established by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and/or PCR according to local standards.

For the present molecular subanalysis, patients were selected according to predefined biological criteria, including loss of MLH1 expression by IHC and a minimum follow-up of 24 months. RAS/BRAF wild-type status was required for CRC and small bowel adenocarcinoma, according to clinical relevance, whereas RAS/BRAF testing was not routinely carried out in gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma. After applying these molecular selection criteria, all patients included in this subanalysis had been treated with pembrolizumab monotherapy; no additional selection based on the ICI regimen was applied. This selection was biologically driven by prior evidence that kinase fusions are enriched in MLH1-deficient, RAS/BRAF wild-type dMMR/MSI-H CRC, whereas RAS or BRAF mutations may represent alternative dominant MAPK pathway drivers.

Molecular profiling was centralized at the Pathology Department of the Clinical University Hospital of Santiago de Compostela. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples were analyzed using a custom RNA sequencing-based panel designed with SureDesign software (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and intended for the detection of clinically relevant gene fusions. The complete list of genes included for fusion detection is provided in Supplementary Table S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100411). Archival tumor tissue from either primary tumors or metastatic lesions was used according to material availability; however, the exact sample source was not systematically recorded in the study database.

RNA was extracted from macrodissected FFPE tumor areas containing ≥20% tumor cells using the RNeasy FFPE Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies). For library preparation, 200 ng of RNA were used as input. RNA libraries were generated using the SureSelect XT HS2 RNA Reagent Kit on the automated Magnis NGS Prep System (Agilent Technologies), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Prepared libraries were sequenced using the NextSeq 550 Mid Output Kit v2.5 (150 cycles) on the Illumina NextSeq 550 system as 2 × 76-bp paired-end reads (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Sequencing data were processed and analyzed using the SeqOne software platform (SeqOne Genomics, Montpellier, France). Bioinformatic analysis included quality control assessment, read alignment to the reference genome, and detection of gene fusion events. Fusion calls were considered positive when supported by at least three spanning junction reads. Gene fusions were classified according to AMP/American Society of Clinical Oncology/College of American Pathologists recommendations.

RET fusions identified by RNA sequencing were confirmed by FISH using the 10q11 RET Break-Apart FISH Probe (Abbott Molecular, Des Plaines, IL). NTRK rearrangements were screened by pan-tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) IHC using clone EPR17341 (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ). For the purpose of this analysis, secondary actionable fusions were defined as oncogenic fusion events involving therapeutically targetable genes detected by the RNA sequencing-based panel.

Clinical, pathological, and molecular data were retrieved from electronic medical records. MLH1 promoter methylation status and germline MLH1 testing were recorded when available. Somatic MLH1 mutation status was not systematically assessed. Tumor response was assessed by treating physicians according to RECIST version 1.1. Objective response rate (ORR) was defined as complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) and disease control rate (DCR) was defined as CR, PR, or stable disease (SD).

Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from pembrolizumab initiation to disease progression or death and overall survival (OS) as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause. Patients without events were censored at the date of last follow-up. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Median PFS and OS were reported with 95% confidence intervals when estimable. Median follow-up was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Given the limited sample size, all analyses were considered exploratory.

The study was approved by the Galician Research Ethics Committee (CEIm-G, code 2020/458) and all patients provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Twenty patients with advanced dMMR/MSI-H gastrointestinal tumors meeting the predefined molecular selection criteria were included, comprising 9 CRC and 11 non-CRC tumors (Figure 1). Median age at initiation of pembrolizumab was 77 (range 55-86) years, 60% of patients were male, and 80% had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) of 0-1. Primary tumor resection had been carried out in 70% of cases. At baseline, liver and peritoneal metastases were present in 35% and 40% of patients, respectively and 25% had three or more metastatic sites.

Among CRC cases, most primary tumors were right-sided, whereas the non-CRC group consisted predominantly of gastroesophageal adenocarcinomas (91%), with one small bowel adenocarcinoma. High-grade histology was observed in 75% of tumors. Nineteen of 20 patients were assessable for response. ORR was 84.2%, including 57.9% CR and 26.3% PR, with a DCR of 89.5%. By subgroup, patients with CRC with fusions achieved two CR and two PR (ORR 100%), whereas patients with CRC without fusions achieved three CR and two PR (ORR 100%). In the non-CRC group (n = 10 assessable), responses included six CR, one PR, one SD, and two progressive disease (ORR 70.0%). Detailed response distributions are provided in Table 2.

Discussion

In this predefined real-world molecular subanalysis of the previously published GAIN cohort,13 we evaluated the potential prognostic relevance of secondary actionable fusions in patients with MLH1-deficient dMMR/MSI-H gastrointestinal tumors treated with pembrolizumab. Secondary actionable fusions were detected in CRC cases and were not detected in the non-CRC tumors analyzed in this cohort.

This observation is consistent with previous genomic studies reporting an increased prevalence of NTRK and RET fusions in molecularly selected dMMR/MSI-H CRC, particularly in tumors with MLH1 deficiency and RAS/BRAF wild-type status.7, 8, 9 These findings should be interpreted within the context of the parent GAIN cohort, in which clinical outcomes were primarily driven by baseline prognostic factors, particularly ECOG PS.13 The present analysis expands those observations by exploring whether molecular heterogeneity within this biologically selected subgroup may further contribute to outcome differences.

The molecular selection used in this subanalysis was biologically driven. In CRC, NTRK and RET fusions have been reported to occur preferentially in MLH1-deficient, RAS/BRAF wild-type tumors, whereas RAS or BRAF mutations, particularly BRAF V600E, may represent dominant MAPK pathway drivers. Therefore, focusing on MLH1-deficient tumors and requiring RAS/BRAF wild-type status for CRC and small bowel adenocarcinoma was intended to enrich for cases in which kinase fusions could represent relevant secondary actionable events. In gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma, RAS/BRAF testing was not routinely carried out according to clinical practice and this should be considered when interpreting the molecular selection of the non-CRC subgroup. Importantly, NTRK and RET fusions are pan-tumor alterations and the absence of detected fusions in the non-CRC tumors analyzed should be interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence of tumor-type exclusivity.

Importantly, the presence of secondary actionable fusions did not appear to compromise initial sensitivity to pembrolizumab. All four fusion-positive patients with CRC achieved an objective response and response rates were comparable between fusion-positive and fusion-negative CRC. However, despite preserved initial responses, survival analyses suggested a potential divergence in long-term outcomes. Median OS was shorter in fusion-positive CRC than in fusion-negative CRC, and, in the exploratory CRC-only analysis, NTRK1 fusion-positive cases showed shorter long-term survival than NTRK-negative CRC. Given the very small number of patients, particularly in the NTRK1 fusion-positive subgroup, this observation should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of an independent prognostic effect. The biological basis of this observation remains incompletely understood. Resistance to ICIs in MSI-H/dMMR CRC is heterogeneous and involves multiple mechanisms of primary and acquired immune escape. Although misclassification and atypical response patterns may account for a minority of cases,4, 5, 6 all patients included in this study fulfilled local criteria for dMMR/MSI-H disease, making these explanations less likely to fully account for the observed differences in long-term outcomes. At the molecular level, alterations affecting antigen presentation pathways, particularly beta-2-microglobulin loss, are among the best-characterized mechanisms of resistance in MSI-H/dMMR CRC.14 More recently, structural genomic instability has emerged as a potential contributor to tumor evolution under immune pressure. Chromothripsis can generate oncogenic fusions while simultaneously promoting clonal diversification and immune evasion.15, 16, 17

Within this framework, actionable fusions may represent a surrogate marker of broader genomic complexity rather than acting solely as isolated oncogenic drivers. Although structural variants, copy-number alterations, beta-2-microglobulin status, and other potential mechanisms of immune escape were not systematically evaluated in the present study, this hypothesis provides a biologically plausible explanation for the shorter long-term survival observed in fusion-positive tumors and warrants further investigation.

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the potential value of comprehensive molecular profiling in dMMR/MSI-H CRC beyond MSI status alone. Larotrectinib and entrectinib have demonstrated high and durable response rates in NTRK fusion-positive gastrointestinal cancers,10,11 whereas selpercatinib has shown meaningful activity in RET-driven tumors.12 The main clinical implication of identifying NTRK or RET fusions in this setting may emerge at the time of progression after PD-1 blockade, when fusion-positive patients may have access to matched targeted therapies that are not available for fusion-negative disease. In the present series, none of the four fusion-positive patients received subsequent fusion-directed targeted therapy, precluding any assessment of the therapeutic impact of these agents after pembrolizumab. The identification of actionable fusions may therefore be clinically relevant in patients who experience disease progression after immunotherapy and supports further investigation of strategies integrating ICIs and fusion-directed therapies.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it is a retrospective molecular subanalysis of a previously published real-world cohort and includes a small number of patients, particularly in the fusion-positive subgroup. The study was not powered to carry out adjusted survival analyses or to formally assess the independent prognostic effect of fusion status. In addition, confidence intervals for median PFS and OS were not estimable in several subgroups because of the limited sample size and censoring pattern and survival comparisons should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Secondly, the absence of an independent validation cohort limits the generalizability of the findings. Thirdly, molecular profiling was carried out on archival tumor tissue selected according to material availability and the exact source of the analyzed sample, primary tumor versus metastatic lesion, was not systematically recorded. Fourthly, somatic MLH1 mutation status was not systematically assessed. Finally, all patients included in this subanalysis had received pembrolizumab monotherapy after application of the predefined molecular selection criteria; therefore, these findings may not be directly generalizable to patients treated with dual immune checkpoint blockade.

Despite these limitations, this analysis provides hypothesis-generating real-world evidence suggesting that secondary actionable fusions may identify a subset of MLH1-deficient dMMR/MSI-H CRC with preserved initial sensitivity to pembrolizumab but potentially shorter long-term survival. These findings support comprehensive molecular profiling in this setting and warrant validation in larger cohorts, including studies designed to clarify the optimal sequencing of immunotherapy and fusion-directed targeted therapies.

Conclusion

In this selected cohort of MLH1-deficient dMMR/MSI-H gastrointestinal tumors treated with pembrolizumab, secondary actionable fusions were detected among CRC cases and were not detected in the non-CRC tumors analyzed. Fusion-positive CRC showed preserved initial sensitivity to pembrolizumab, although exploratory survival analyses suggested potentially shorter long-term survival, particularly among neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase 1 (NTRK1) fusion-positive cases. These findings suggest that actionable fusions may have potential prognostic relevance in addition to their established predictive role for targeted therapies. Comprehensive molecular profiling may therefore provide clinically relevant information in this setting. Further studies are warranted to validate these observations and to clarify the optimal sequencing of immunotherapy and fusion-directed targeted therapies in this molecular subset.
