Chlorogenic acid modulates gut microbiota and metabolites to alleviate intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy: Insights from 16S rRNA sequencing and metabolomics
Authors: Danli Ma, Yanwen Xu, Ruixia Liang, Qingju Meng, Yanli Liu, Danling Hu, Bin Zhu, Yongxia Zheng, Qiwei Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) is a liver disorder marked by impaired bile flow, elevated serum bile acids, and pruritus, posing significant risks to maternal and fetal health. Current treatments, including ursodeoxycholic acid, have shown limited efficacy, underscoring the need for more effective therapies. Chlorogenic acid (CGA), a polyphenolic compound with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and hepatoprotective properties, has shown promise in managing liver diseases, but its role in ICP remains poorly understood. This study investigated the therapeutic effects of CGA in a rat model of ICP induced by 17α-ethinylestradiol. CGA treatment significantly reduced liver enzyme levels, total bile acids, and bilirubin, while improving histopathological liver damage. CGA also modulated key proteins involved in bile acid synthesis and transport, including FXR, CYP7A1, NTCP, and BSEP. Additionally, CGA treatment improved intestinal barrier function by upregulating tight junction proteins, including ZO-1. Metabolomics and 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed that CGA treatment restored gut microbiota balance in ICP rats. CGA demonstrated a dose-dependent response, with higher doses providing more pronounced therapeutic effects. These findings suggest that CGA alleviates ICP by regulating bile acid metabolism, improving liver function, and modulating the gut microbiome, highlighting its potential as an effective therapeutic option for managing ICP.Keywords: Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, Chlorogenic acid, Bile acid metabolism, Gut microbiota, Hepatoprotective effects

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) is a liver disorder that typically manifests in the second or third trimester. It is characterized by impaired bile flow in the liver, leading to the accumulation of bile acids in the bloodstream [1]. The incidence of ICP varies significantly across geographical regions and ethnic groups. In China, the prevalence of ICP is approximately 6.06% [2]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis covering nearly 43 million pregnancies across 302 studies found the global prevalence of ICP to be approximately 2.9% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.5–3.3%), highlighting a notably higher incidence in Asian countries, while the prevalence tends to be lower in high-income nations, reflecting the substantial impact of socioeconomic and geographical factors on the occurrence of ICP [3]. The primary clinical features of ICP include elevated serum bile acid levels and unexplained pruritus, which are hallmark symptoms [4]. These symptoms generally resolve within 1–3 weeks of delivery. Of particular concern is the significantly increased risk of fetal death associated with ICP, which is notably higher than that in the general pregnant population. This risk is especially pronounced when the total serum bile acid (TBA) level exceeds 100 μmol/L [5]. Although ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) is the standard treatment for ICP, studies have shown that it does not significantly reduce the risk of fetal death [6]. This finding underscores the limitations of current treatment options and highlights the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies. Recent studies also suggest a potential association between early onset ICP and gestational diabetes mellitus, indicating that the pathogenesis of ICP may not only be related to liver and bile factors, but also to metabolic disturbances [7]. Therefore, the development of new therapeutic agents for ICP remains a critical and unresolved challenge in the field of obstetric care.

Traditional Chinese Medicine has a long history of use owing to its therapeutic properties, especially in the treatment of liver-related conditions [8]. Chlorogenic acid (CGA), a traditional Chinese medicinal compound found in plants such as Coffea arabica and Echinacea purpurea, has attracted significant interest because of its potential pharmacological benefits [9]. Chlorogenic acid is a polyphenolic compound belonging to the cinnamate ester class and is known for its potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and hepatoprotective properties [10]. It has traditionally been used as a bioactive ingredient in various liver conditions, including liver inflammation, fatty liver disease, and cholestasis-related disorders [11,12]. Research has demonstrated that chlorogenic acid possesses several pharmacological properties that contribute to its therapeutic potential, including its ability to reduce liver inflammation, enhance antioxidant defense, and improve liver enzyme function [13,14]. Particularly, CGA has been shown to regulate key enzymes involved in bile acid synthesis and metabolism, suggesting a beneficial role in improving bile flow and alleviating cholestasis symptoms [15]. In the context of ICP, a pregnancy-specific liver disorder characterized by elevated serum bile acids and pruritus, the anti-inflammatory effects of chlorogenic acid and its ability to modulate bile acid levels are promising candidates for ICP treatment [16]. However, although preclinical data support its potential therapeutic use, there is limited clinical evidence regarding its efficacy in treating ICP, and the exact mechanisms underlying its effects on liver function and cholestasis remain unclear. Further clinical investigations are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of chlorogenic acid as a treatment for ICP and clarify its pharmacological mechanisms.

The gut microbiome has recently emerged as a critical regulator of bile acid metabolism, and its disruption has been linked to various liver diseases, including ICP [17]. Growing evidence suggests that an imbalance in the gut microbiota can significantly affect the synthesis, absorption, and enterohepatic circulation of bile acids, which are essential for maintaining liver function [18]. In the context of ICP, clinical studies have revealed notable differences in gut microbiota composition between women with ICP and healthy pregnant women, emphasizing the importance of microbial balance in liver health [19,20]. This dysbiosis may contribute to the pathophysiology of ICP by disturbing bile acid metabolism, leading to elevated serum bile acid levels, which is one of the defining characteristics of the disease. Additionally, the gut microbiota plays a key role in modulating immune responses and liver inflammation, which are central to the development of cholestasis [21]. Alterations in the microbiome can potentially exacerbate the inflammatory processes in the liver, thus accelerating the onset and progression of ICP [22]. Therefore, restoring a balanced microbiome may be critical for managing ICP, as it may help normalize bile acid metabolism, alleviate liver inflammation, and improve liver function. Recent studies have proposed that interventions aimed at modulating the gut microbiota, such as prebiotics, probiotics, or specific therapeutic agents, could offer novel treatment strategies for ICP and improve clinical outcomes [23]. Previous studies have shown that CGA can improve alcohol-induced liver injury in mice [14] and alleviate fructose-induced salt-sensitive hypertension in mice by regulating both gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism [24]. CGA may influence the intestinal microbiome of patients with ICP. Understanding how CGA modulates the gut microbiota could provide valuable insights into its mechanism of action in managing this condition.

In our study, ICP was induced in Sprague-Dawley pregnant rats using 17α-ethinylestradiol. The therapeutic effects of CGA were assessed by measuring serum biochemical parameters and evaluating histopathological changes in the liver. To identify the differential metabolites across the experimental groups, an untargeted metabolomics approach was employed, and 16S rRNA sequencing was used to analyze the composition of the fecal microbiota. This study also investigated the impact of CGA on the farnesoid X receptor (FXR)/cholesterol 7 alpha-hydroxylase (CYP7A1)/sodium taurocholate co-transporting polypeptide (NTCP) signaling pathway and intestinal barrier function. These results contribute to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying ICP and offer promising insights for the development of new therapeutic strategies for managing cholestatic liver injury during pregnancy.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatments

Eight-week-old male and female Sprague-Dawley rats were obtained from Guangdong Weitong Lihua Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. After a one-week acclimatization period in a specific pathogen-free environment, male and female rats were housed together in a 2:1 ratio during the estrous cycle. Successful mating was confirmed when a whitish or pale-yellow vaginal plug or sperm was observed in a vaginal smear under a microscope, marking the start of pregnancy on day 1. Specifically, a total of 36 pregnant rats were included in the study and were randomly divided into six groups: the Control group (n = 6), ICP model group (n = 6), YZH group (n = 6), L-CGA group (n = 6), M-CGA group (n = 6), and H-CGA group (n = 6). To induce the ICP rat model, pregnant rats were injected with 17α-ethynylestradiol (2.5 mg/kg, Shanghai Quanyu Biotechnology Animal Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China), dissolved in 1,2-propylene glycol (2.5 mL/kg, Solarbio, China), via intramuscular injection at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg/day from days 12 to 18 of gestation. The Control group received 2.5 mL/kg of 1,2-propylene glycol by the same route. Based on previous studies on CGA in rats with cholestatic liver injury, rats in the treatment group were administered CGA via gavage at low, medium, or high doses (25, 50, and 100 mg/kg, respectively), with a volume of 10 mL/kg once daily for five consecutive days during the modeling phase [[25], [26], [27]]. The positive Control group was administered Yinzhihuang Oral Liquid at a dose of 10 mL/kg/day. Blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta of the rats on day 18.5 of pregnancy, and fecal samples were collected for further analysis. All the procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Huateng Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Approval No.: B202504-42).

Analysis of embryo survival rate in an ICP rat model

Embryo survival rate in the rat model of ICP was assessed by calculating the proportion of live fetuses at term. The total number of fetuses in each litter was counted, and stillborn fetuses were recorded. The embryo survival rate was calculated by subtracting the number of stillborn fetuses from the total number of fetuses, dividing this value by the total number of fetuses, and multiplying by 100 to express the result as a percentage.

Histological analysis

At the end of the experiment, liver tissues from rats in each group were harvested. Tissues were thoroughly rinsed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline to remove any residual blood or debris. To maintain the structural integrity of the tissues, the samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (v/v) for 2 days. After fixation, the tissues were dehydrated to prepare them for embedding. Once embedded and sectioned, tissue samples were stained with hematoxylin and eosin to assess histological changes. Pathological alterations in liver tissue were observed under a light microscope at 200 × magnification (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Biochemical index detection

The animals were first anesthetized, and blood samples were drawn from the abdominal aorta to isolate the serum. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), and TBA were measured using commercial assay kits (Jianglai Biotechnology, Nanjing, China). Assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions using the following kits: ALT (JL-T0873), AST (JL-T0875), TBIL (JL-T1398), DBIL (JL-T1397), and TBA (JL-T1373). All the measurements were performed using the microplate method.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

To extract RNA from the rat liver tissue, 1 mL of TRIzol reagent was added to 100 mg of liver tissue. After grinding, 200 μL of chloroform was added, and the mixture was shaken and mixed for 15 s. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was collected, and an equal volume of isopropanol was added. The mixture was centrifuged again at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. After discarding the supernatant, the RNA pellet was washed thrice with 1 mL of 75% ethanol. Finally, the supernatant was discarded, and 20 μL of diethylpyrocarbonate-treated water was added, followed by natural drying of the RNA pellet. The RNA concentration was measured. For reverse transcription, all samples were converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) using a reverse transcription protocol (AQ631; Quanshijin Biotechnology, Beijing, China). Primers used in this study were purchased from Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). For quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), a 10 μL reaction mixture was prepared containing 5 μL of Genious 2X SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix, 0.2 μL of forward primer, 0.2 μL of reverse primer, 1 μL of cDNA, and 3.6 μL of double-distilled water. The qRT-PCR protocol was set according to the experimental plan, and quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a BioSystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (ABI, California, USA). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization, and the primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Western blotting

After extracting liver tissue proteins from rats, the protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were loaded onto each lane, and proteins were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, then transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Servicebio, Beijing, China). The PVDF membrane was incubated in Tris-buffered saline with Tween containing non-fat milk for 1.5 h on a shaking platform, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: GAPDH (1:10,000; GB15004; Servicebio), FXR (1:1000; DF12511; Affinity), CYP7A1 (1:1000; GB150112; Servicebio), NTCP (1:1000; BS-1958R; BIOSS), bile salt export pump (BSEP; 1:1000; BS-42133R; BIOSS), and Zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1; 1:2000; AB229630; ABCAM). The membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000; GB111402; Servicebio) for 1 h at room temperature. Images were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system (SCG-W3000, Service Bio). Protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ2 software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA).

Paired-end reads from raw DNA fragments were merged using FLASH, a tool designed to efficiently join overlapping paired-end reads. The Linear Discriminant Analysis effect size (lefSe) method was used to analyze quantitative biomarkers within each group.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis

A Dionex Ultimate 3000 RS High-Performance Liquid Chromatography system coupled with a Q-Exactive quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and equipped with a heated electrospray ionization source, was used to analyze the metabolomic profile in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. The separation was performed using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm). The binary gradient elution system consisted of (A) water containing 0.1% formic acid (v/v) and (B) acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (v/v). The separation gradient was as follows: 0–2 min, 5–20% B; 2–4 min, 20–60% B; 4–11 min, 60–100% B; hold at 100% B for 2 min; 13–13.5 min, hold at 100% B; and 13.5–14.5 min, return to 5% B. The flow rate was set to 0.35 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 45 °C. All samples of liver tissue in rats were kept at 4 °C during analysis. The injection volume was 2 μL. The mass range was 100–1000 m/z. The resolution of the full scans was set to 70,000, and the resolution of the MS/MS scans was set to 17,500. The collision energies were set to 10, 20, and 40 eV. The mass spectrometer was operated as follows: the spray voltage for the positive ion mode was 3800 V, and that for the negative ion mode was 3000 V. The sheath gas flow rate was set at 35 arbitrary units; the auxiliary gas flow rate was set at 8 arbitrary units; and the capillary temperature was 320 °C.

Statistical analysis

Raw MS data were converted to MzXML files using ProteoWizard MS Convert and then imported into XCMS for peak detection. Peaks were picked using the following parameters: centWave m/z = 10 ppm, peak width = c (10, 60), prefilter = c (10, 100). Peak grouping was performed with bw = 5, mzwid = 0.025, and minfrac = 0.5. Isotopes and adducts were annotated using CAMERA (Collection of Algorithms of MEtabolite pRofile Annotation). Only variables with more than 50% of non-zero measurements across at least one sample set were retained. Compounds were identified by comparing m/z values (<10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra to an internal database of authentic standards. Metabolites with a match score >0.7 were further validated by comparing retention times with the standards to minimize false positives. After summation and normalization, multivariate analysis was performed using the R package ropls. Pareto-scale Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) were conducted. To assess model robustness, seven-fold cross-validation and response permutation tests were performed. The Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) value > 1 and p-values <0.05 were used to screen metabolites with significant changes. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. To assess the differences between the two independent groups, an unpaired two-tailed t-test was used. For comparisons involving more than two groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by post-hoc tests when necessary. All experiments were independently repeated three times to ensure the consistency and reliability of the results. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

CGA treatment alleviates ICP-induced liver injury and improves pregnancy outcomes

To investigate the effects of CGA on ICP, we assessed key biochemical markers and liver pathology in an ICP rat model treated with different doses of CGA (low-CGA, L-CGA; medium CGA, M-CGA; high-CGA, H-CGA). CGA treatment significantly ameliorated the detrimental effects of ICP on liver function and pregnancy outcomes (Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 1A, the ICP model group exhibited a significantly higher abortion rate than the Control group, indicating severe reproductive damage due to cholestasis. Treatment with all doses of CGA (L-CGA, M-CGA, and H-CGA) significantly reduced the abortion rate, with the YZH group also showing a reduction, similar to the CGA treatment groups, suggesting that CGA plays a protective role in pregnancy outcomes. The YZH treatment was effective but not as pronounced as the higher doses of CGA. Similarly, Fig. 1B–F demonstrates that serum levels of liver enzymes (ALT and AST), bilirubin (DBIL and TBIL), and TBA were markedly elevated in the ICP model group, reflecting liver dysfunction. CGA treatment significantly reduced these markers, especially in the M-CGA and H-CGA groups, and restored liver function and bile acid metabolism toward normal levels. The YZH group also showed improvements in these biochemical markers, albeit to a lesser extent than the CGA-treated groups. Histological examination of the liver tissues (Fig. 1G) revealed severe hepatocellular swelling, necrosis, and bile duct proliferation in the ICP model group. In contrast, liver tissues from rats treated with CGA (L-CGA, M-CGA, and H-CGA) exhibited significantly reduced hepatic damage, with the H-CGA group showing near-normal liver architecture. The YZH group also exhibited some liver protection, but the results were less pronounced compared to the CGA treatment groups, indicating that CGA effectively alleviated ICP-induced liver injury. Collectively, these findings indicated that CGA treatment significantly improved pregnancy outcomes, liver function, and histopathological alterations in an ICP rat model, highlighting its therapeutic potential for managing ICP.

CGA treatment modulates bile acid synthesis and transport in ICP rats

To investigate the effects of CGA on bile acid synthesis and transport in the ICP model, Western blot analysis (Fig. 2A and B) and qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 2C–J) were performed to evaluate the expression of key bile acid synthesis- and transport-related markers in the livers of rats from different treatment groups. Western blot analysis showed that, compared with the Control group, the ICP model group exhibited significantly increased protein expression levels of FXR and CYP7A1, whereas the protein expression levels of NTCP and BSEP were significantly decreased. In parallel, qRT-PCR results revealed that the mRNA expression levels of FXR, SHP, FGFR4, CYP7A1, and OSTβ were significantly elevated in the ICP model group, while the mRNA expression levels of MRP2, NTCP, and BSEP were markedly reduced, indicating substantial disturbances in bile acid synthesis and transport in ICP [28]. After treatment, these abnormal changes were reversed to different extents. In particular, CGA treatment, especially at the medium and high doses, markedly decreased the expression of FXR and CYP7A1 at both the protein and mRNA levels, while significantly increasing the expression of NTCP and BSEP. In addition, CGA also downregulated the elevated mRNA expression of SHP, FGFR4, and OSTβ, and restored the reduced mRNA expression of MRP2. Although YZH treatment also partially improved these alterations, its regulatory effects were generally less pronounced than those observed in the CGA-treated groups [29,30]. Taken together, these findings suggest that CGA, particularly at medium and high doses, alleviates ICP-associated bile acid metabolic disorders by modulating the expression of key genes and proteins involved in bile acid synthesis and transport, thereby contributing to the restoration of bile acid homeostasis.

CGA improves intestinal barrier function in ICP rats

To evaluate the effects of CGA on intestinal barrier function in a rat model of ICP, we assessed the expression of key tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-4) in the ileal tissue using western blotting and qRT-PCR. Western blot analysis of ZO-1 protein expression (Fig. 3A) revealed a significant reduction in the ICP model group compared to the Control group, indicating compromised intestinal barrier integrity. Treatment with CGA significantly increased ZO-1 expression, with the L-CGA group showing the greatest restoration. This suggests that CGA protects against intestinal permeability in ICP. The YZH group also showed increased ZO-1 expression, although the restoration was less pronounced compared to the CGA-treated groups. The RT-PCR results (Fig. 3C–E) further supported these findings. The ICP model group exhibited significantly higher mRNA expression levels of Claudin-4 than the Control group (Fig. 3D), while ZO-1 and Occludin mRNA levels were significantly lower in the ICP model group, indicating impaired tight junction integrity. Treatment with various doses of CGA significantly restored the mRNA expression of these genes, particularly in the M-CGA and H-CGA groups. L-CGA treatment resulted in the most pronounced upregulation of ZO-1, Claudin-4, and Occludin, suggesting that CGA can significantly improve the intestinal barrier function in ICP rats. Similarly, the YZH group exhibited some restoration of tight junction protein expression, though the effect was not as robust as in the CGA-treated groups. These findings indicate that CGA treatment restores intestinal barrier integrity by upregulating tight junction protein expression, particularly with the M-CGA and L-CGA doses.

Multivariate statistical analysis of liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry

To assess the metabolic profile differences among the experimental groups, multivariate statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were performed. The PCA score plot showed an overall separation among the Control, ICP model, and CGA groups (Fig. 4A), with PC1 and PC2 explaining 41.50% and 17.60% of the total variance, respectively, indicating clear metabolic differences among the three groups. Pairwise PCA further demonstrated distinct metabolic profiles between the ICP model and CGA groups (Fig. 4B), with PC1 explaining 39.10% of the total variance, and between the Control and ICP model groups (Fig. 4C), where PC1 and PC2 explained 47.40% and 20.20% of the total variance, respectively. OPLS-DA was subsequently performed to further validate the separation between groups. As shown in Fig. 4D, the ICP model and CGA groups were clearly distinguished, with Component 1 and Orthogonal Component 1 explaining 56.30% and 20.70% of the variance, respectively. Similarly, a clear separation was also observed between the Control and ICP model groups in Fig. 4E. To evaluate the robustness of the models and exclude overfitting, 200-permutation tests were conducted. The corresponding permutation tests yielded R2 = 0.6299 and Q2 = −0.5899 for one model (Fig. 4F), and R2 = 0.3943 and Q2 = −0.2519 for the other model (Fig. 4G), supporting the reliability and validity of the models.

Metabolite identification and pathway analysis in CGA-treated rats with ICP

Detailed statistical analyses were performed to further investigate the metabolic differences among the Control, ICP model, and CGA groups. A comparison of the significantly differential metabolites across the groups is shown in Fig. 5A, where the numbers of upregulated (red) and downregulated (blue) metabolites in each pairwise comparison are presented. In the Control vs. ICP model comparison, five metabolites were significantly upregulated, and two were downregulated. Modulation of gut microbiota in ICP rats by CGA treatments

To assess the impact of CGA treatment on fecal microbiota in a rat model of bile acid accumulation, we conducted comprehensive microbiota analyses across various taxonomic levels, including phylum, class, order, family, and genus. Fig. 6A shows a Venn diagram of the amplicon sequence variant (ASV) analysis at a 97% similarity level, revealing that 79 ASVs were shared across the Control, model, and CGA groups. This overlap highlights the common microbiota components across all groups, while also indicating distinct differences in microbial composition. At the phylum level (Fig. 6B), Bacillota was significantly elevated in the model group compared with the Control group, whereas Pseudomonadota decreased. Both phyla were restored following treatment with CGA, indicating that these treatments helped restore the disrupted microbial composition in bile duct-obstructed rats. Microbial diversity was further evaluated using Shannon and Simpson indices, as shown in Fig. 7A and B. The Shannon index, which measures microbial richness, was significantly reduced in the model group, indicating a loss of microbial diversity. Conversely, the Simpson index, which reflects the dominance of specific species, was significantly increased in the model group, suggesting a simplified microbial community. However, following treatment with CGA, both indices showed improvements, with the Shannon index returning closer to the Control levels and the Simpson index decreasing, indicating the recovery of microbial diversity and stability. Fig. 7C confirms that the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the majority of the microbial diversity present in the samples, ensuring a robust representation of the microbial communities. Beta diversity analysis, which evaluates differences in microbial community structure across groups, was performed using NMDS, principal coordinate analysis, and PCA (Fig. 8A–C). The results from all three methods revealed significant differences between the Control and model groups, with a clear separation observed in the NMDS, Principal Coordinate Analysis, and PCA plots. Additionally, significant differences were observed between the CGA groups and the model group, further demonstrating that CGA treatment has a substantial impact on modulating the gut microbiota in bile duct-obstructed rats. These findings confirm that both treatments significantly altered the microbial community structure, helping to restore the microbial diversity disrupted by cholestasis. In summary, our data indicated that CGA treatment significantly influenced the gut microbiota in bile duct-obstructed rats. These treatments appeared to restore microbial diversity and composition, highlighting their therapeutic potential in addressing gut microbiota dysbiosis associated with bile acid accumulation and cholestasis.

Detailed taxonomic shifts in gut microbiota following CGA treatment in ICP rats

To explore the effect of CGA treatment on the fecal microbiota in ICP, we performed the Kruskal–Wallis H test and LEfSe analysis across various taxonomic levels (phylum, class, order, family, and genus). The Kruskal–Wallis H test was used to evaluate significant differences in microbial abundance between the groups at each taxonomic level. At the phylum level (Fig. 9A), Bacillota was significantly increased in the model group compared with the Control group, whereas Pseudomonadota showed a significant decrease. Both Bacillota and Pseudomonadota were restored toward normal levels after CGA treatment. Additionally, at the class level (Fig. 9B), significant changes were observed in the distribution of Clostridia and Bacilli, with Bacilli showing increased proportions in the CGA treatment groups, whereas Clostridia were significantly reduced in the model group. At the order level (Fig. 9C), Gammaproteobacteria and Saccharimonadales exhibited marked shifts, whereas Gammaproteobacteria increased and Saccharimonadales decreased in the model group. CGA treatment successfully restored these changes. At the family level (Fig. 9D), Lachnospiraceae and Enterobacteriaceae were significantly elevated in the model group, whereas Lactobacillaceae was decreased. The treatment groups (CGA) showed a recovery in the abundance of these families, reflecting the restoration of microbial community balance. At the genus level (Fig. 9E), Escherichia-Shigella and Phascolarctobacterium were significantly higher in the model group than in the Control group, and both genera showed a significant reduction following CGA treatments. Conversely, Lactobacillus and Rothia, which were significantly reduced in the model group, exhibited a significant increase in abundance after treatment. LEfSe analysis (Fig. 9F) further supported these findings, indicating that Bacillota significantly increased and Pseudomonadota significantly decreased in the model group, which were restored following treatment with CGA. Furthermore, the genus-level analysis confirmed that Escherichia-Shigella, Phascolarctobacterium, Holdemania, and Parabacteroides were significantly elevated in the model group and returned to normal levels after treatment (Fig. 9G–N). Overall, these results demonstrate that CGA treatment effectively modulates the gut microbiota in a bile duct-obstructed rat model, restoring the microbial diversity and composition disrupted by cholestasis. These findings underscore the potential of CGA as a therapeutic agent for modulating gut microbiota dysbiosis in liver diseases, particularly under conditions such as intrahepatic cholestasis during pregnancy.

Discussion

Our study provides compelling evidence of the therapeutic potential of CGA in alleviating the pathological effects of ICP, a disorder characterized by impaired bile flow and elevated serum bile acid levels. The results demonstrated that CGA significantly reduced liver enzyme, total bile acid, and bilirubin levels, which are key indicators of liver dysfunction in ICP. Histopathological analysis revealed that CGA treatment effectively reduced liver damage, as evidenced by the improved liver architecture, particularly at medium to higher doses. The more significant improvement in liver function observed in the M-CGA group may be attributed to an optimal dose, where moderate doses strike a balance between efficacy and safety, producing the most pronounced therapeutic effect. In contrast, higher doses, like those in the H-CGA group, did not yield further improvement and may have limitations in efficacy or potential adverse effects. These findings are consistent with recent studies showing that CGA exerts hepatoprotective effects in various liver conditions, including fatty liver disease and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis [14,31,32]. The ability of CGA to modulate bile acid metabolism by regulating key enzymes, such as FXR and CYP7A1, further supports its role in managing cholestasis. Previous studies have shown that CGA can regulate bile acid synthesis and transport, suggesting that the therapeutic effects of CGA may stem from its ability to restore bile flow and homeostasis. The findings of this study provide a broader understanding of the multifaceted role of CGA in protecting the liver, which may explain its efficacy in addressing hepatic dysfunction and ICP complications. Moreover, the known anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of CGA add additional layers of therapeutic benefits [33], improving overall liver function and potentially mitigating the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes associated with ICP. Overall, the protective effects of CGA against liver injury and its potential to restore normal bile acid metabolism make it a promising candidate for ICP treatment, especially in cases where current therapies show limited efficacy.

A key finding of this study is the significant restoration of bile acid metabolism following CGA treatment, as evidenced by the upregulation of FXR, CYP7A1, NTCP, and BSEP expression in the liver. FXR, a nuclear receptor involved in bile acid regulation, plays a pivotal role in maintaining bile acid homeostasis [34,35]. In the ICP model, these proteins were downregulated, suggesting impaired bile acid synthesis and transport [28,29,36]. However, treatment with medium or high doses of CGA significantly restored the CGA expression. These results are consistent with those of animal model studies showing that CGA can modulate bile acid metabolism by regulating key liver proteins and enzymes involved in bile acid synthesis and efflux [16]. Additionally, the observed improvement in bile acid transporters is particularly important in the context of cholestasis because dysregulated bile acid efflux contributes to liver injury [37]. The restoration of bile acid homeostasis supports the idea that CGA mitigates the pathophysiological effects of ICP by regulating both bile acid synthesis and secretion. These findings align with a growing body of literature suggesting that bile acid modulation is a key therapeutic strategy for managing ICP and other liver diseases [17,28]. By targeting both synthesis and transport pathways, CGA may offer a more comprehensive solution for cholestasis-related liver damage, highlighting its potential as a dual-target therapy for ICP. Restoration of these pathways further underscores the therapeutic potential of CGA as a safer and more effective alternative to current treatment options, such as UDCA.

This study also explored the impact of CGA on intestinal barrier integrity, which is increasingly being recognized as a critical factor in liver diseases, including ICP. CGA treatment significantly upregulates tight junction proteins such as ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-4, which are essential for maintaining intestinal barrier function [38,39]. The impaired intestinal permeability observed in the ICP model was restored following CGA treatment, suggesting that CGA has a protective effect on the gut-liver axis. This finding is consistent with recent research showing that alterations in gut microbiota and increased intestinal permeability contribute to the progression of liver diseases by facilitating the translocation of bacterial products and inflammatory mediators [[40], [41], [42]]. The ability of CGA to restore intestinal barrier function may be critical for preventing systemic inflammation and further liver injury in ICP, highlighting its potential as a multitarget therapy for this condition. Moreover, the effects of CGA on the intestinal barrier are consistent with its known anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [43], which may contribute to its hepatoprotective effects. This observation is supported by studies indicating that CGA reduces inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which are key factors in the pathogenesis of liver diseases [44]. By improving intestinal permeability, CGA may also help reduce gut-derived toxins that exacerbate liver injury, offering an integrated approach for managing ICP and similar liver-related disorders. This dual action, which protects both liver and intestinal functions, further positions CGA as a promising candidate for addressing the complex nature of ICP.

Our findings also demonstrated that CGA treatment altered gut microbiota composition and restored microbial diversity and balance in ICP rats. The significant shift in microbial communities, particularly in the restoration of the Bacillota and Pseudomonadota phyla, is noteworthy [45]. Previous studies have highlighted the critical role of the gut microbiome in bile acid metabolism and liver health, particularly in cholestasis-related disorders [46,47]. In our study, CGA treatment helped to normalize the gut microbiota, which may, in turn, influence bile acid metabolism and liver inflammation. The gut-liver axis plays a central role in the pathogenesis of ICP, and restoring the microbial balance could be an important therapeutic approach [21]. Recent studies have shown that dysbiosis, characterized by a reduction in microbial diversity and an overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria, exacerbates liver inflammation and bile acid retention in ICP [48]. The beneficial effects of CGA on the microbiota are consistent with previous findings showing that CGA can modulate the gut microbiome in models of alcohol-induced liver injury [14,49]. These results suggest that the microbiota-modulating properties of CGA are an integral part of its therapeutic effects, offering a novel approach for managing ICP by restoring gut health. CGA may help optimize liver function and reduce systemic inflammation by improving the composition of the gut microbiota. The findings of this study contribute to a growing body of evidence suggesting that targeting the gut microbiome could provide new avenues for treating liver diseases, particularly ICP, by restoring the microbial balance and improving bile acid metabolism.

Several important factors must be considered for the clinical translation of CGA as a therapeutic agent for ICP. While CGA has demonstrated promising therapeutic effects in preclinical models, its safety profile, bioavailability, and feasibility for use during pregnancy need thorough evaluation. Despite CGA's beneficial pharmacological properties, its long-term safety, particularly in pregnant individuals, remains uncertain. It is crucial to conduct comprehensive toxicity studies, including assessments of maternal and fetal health, before advancing to clinical use. Additionally, the low bioavailability of CGA when administered orally could pose a limitation to its therapeutic efficacy. Future studies should focus on improving its absorption and bioavailability through innovative formulation strategies, which would optimize its clinical application. The mechanistic pathways through which CGA exerts its effects, especially concerning bile acid metabolism, are not fully understood. While our study highlights its therapeutic potential, the lack of validation using FXR agonists or antagonists to assess the role of FXR represents a limitation. Further research incorporating these methods would provide deeper insights into CGA's molecular mechanisms. Regarding the positive control drug selection, while Yinzhihuang Oral Liquid was used as a positive control, incorporating ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) as an additional control in future studies would allow for more direct comparisons with existing therapies. This would help assess CGA's potential relative to other established treatments. Given the promising preclinical results, further studies are needed to determine the optimal dosing regimen, evaluate its safety during pregnancy, and explore potential synergistic effects with other therapies.

In summary, CGA demonstrated a therapeutic effect in the ICP model, resulting in pronounced improvements in liver function, bile acid metabolism, and intestinal barrier integrity. The nature of the effects of CGA underscores the importance of optimizing the therapeutic dose for maximal efficacy, particularly in clinical settings. Additionally, the dose-dependent improvement in histopathological liver damage and metabolic profiles supports the idea that CGA exerts its therapeutic effects through multiple mechanisms, including the modulation of bile acid metabolism, regulation of gut microbiota, and restoration of intestinal barrier function. The significant restoration of liver function and bile acid homeostasis observed at higher CGA doses further supports the hypothesis that CGA regulates multiple key pathways involved in liver injury and cholestasis. Given the promising results observed in the preclinical model, further studies are needed to determine the optimal dosing regimen for CGA in clinical settings and to explore its potential synergistic effects with other therapies.
