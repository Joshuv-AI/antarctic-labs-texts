The Effects of Breathing Exercises and Meditation on Quality of Life Among Eye Disease Patients: A Systematic Review
Authors: Michael Huang, Jason Sheng, Brian Yu, Cindy Hutnik, Amardeep Thind, Monali S Malvankar-Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThose diagnosed with eye disease are at a higher risk for experiencing a decreased quality of life (QOL). Meditation and breathing exercises have been linked with improvements in this area, but research in populations of eye disease patients is relatively scarce. We aim to bridge this knowledge gap by synthesizing the available evidence on the effects of meditation and breathing exercises on QOL in those with eye diseases.MethodsA scoping search was performed in PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, Web of Science, and CINAHL databases up to September 16th, 2025, to find relevant studies for review. Inclusion criteria were experimental studies investigating meditation or breathing exercises on any QOL outcome in populations with any eye disease. Study quality was assessed with the Cochrane Risk-of-bias tool and the Modified Downs and Black Checklist.ResultsOf the seven studies, six RCTs and one single-arm trial were included. Five studies examined QOL as an outcome, while four measured anxiety symptoms, two measured depression symptoms, and two measured sleep quality. Overall, beneficial effects of meditation and breathing exercises on these outcomes were found, although there were concerns with study quality and follow-up length.ConclusionWhile preliminary findings suggest that meditation and breathing exercises may improve QOL for individuals with eye diseases, additional studies are required to establish the definitive impact of these interventions on the well-being of patients with eye conditions. Studies with longer follow-up times and higher methodological quality would be useful in continuing to explore the effects of these interventions on QOL outcomes.Keywords: meditation, quality of life, anxiety, depression, eye disease, systematic review

Introduction

Eye diseases refer to a broad range of conditions that affect the health and functionality of one’s eyes, often leading to impaired vision and decreased quality of life (QOL). Common examples of such conditions include glaucoma, cataracts, age-related macular degeneration, dry eye disease, and diabetic retinopathy. When visual function is impaired, eye diseases might interfere with everyday tasks such as reading and driving, increase susceptibility to injury, limit one’s independence, and hinder social activities, all of which may in turn affect an individual’s quality of life.1,2 Furthermore, those diagnosed with eye diseases may be at a higher risk of developing depression and anxiety.2 Potential explanations for this increased risk include the aforementioned decrease in quality of life, in conjunction with fears about disease progression, decreased visual function and potential blindness, and requiring lifelong or invasive treatments.

Currently, there is evidence that suggests a relationship between eye disease and worsened quality of life. One review of systematic reviews found that globally, adults with visual impairment or eye disease tended to have poorer quality of life outcome scores, including subscale measurements such as mental health, psychosocial functioning, and fatigue.3 Another review found that those diagnosed with dry eye disease have higher odds of experiencing depression and anxiety, and that these outcomes are correlated with dry eye symptoms.4 Similar associations have been found in populations with glaucoma,5 cataracts,6 and macular degeneration.7

Meditation and breathing exercises are frequently performed with the purpose of focusing the mind, facilitating relaxation, and enhancing mental wellness. Numerous studies have demonstrated the potential benefits of meditation and breathing exercises on symptoms of anxiety,8–10 depression,10–12 and sleep quality.12,13 Since eye disease patients tend to have a lower quality of life,3 meditation and breathing exercises may be beneficial in helping to address these problems. The advantages of these practices are that they require little to no equipment, can be done at virtually no cost, and individuals may freely choose when to practice.

Although an extensive number of studies exist regarding the effects of meditation and breathing exercises on quality of life, research examining these effects in a population of eye disease patients is relatively limited. The available evidence, to our knowledge, has not been comprehensively synthesized in a systematic review focused on quality-of-life outcomes in this population. Therefore, the aim of this review was to systematically evaluate the effects of meditation and breathing exercises on quality of life among individuals with eye disease.

Methods

The systematic review was performed and written in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines.14

Search Strategy

A scoping search was performed on the PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, Web of Science, and CINAHL databases to locate records investigating the effects of meditation or breathing exercises on quality of life in eye disease patients. Searches were conducted until September 16th, 2025, using search terms for breathing exercises, meditation, depression, anxiety, sleep quality, and eye disease, both broadly and including terms of specific eye diseases (Table 1). The database searches were performed with no restrictions in terms of the date or location of study publication, but with a restriction to studies available in English. Backward citation tracing was also performed on studies that had reached the full-text screening stage in order to locate potentially relevant articles that were not discovered by the scoping search.

Study Selection

After performing the aforementioned search strategy, the results were imported into Covidence, an online software for systematic review management. The software automatically removed any duplicate studies before completing two levels of screening. The first level of screening—title and abstract screening—was conducted by two independent reviewers (MH and JS). Studies passed this stage if they had mentioned both of the following: any form of meditation or breathing exercise, and any outcome directly related to quality of life, including anxiety, depression, or sleep quality. Studies failing to mention either criteria in the title or abstract were excluded. Mentioning the study population was not a requirement to pass title and abstract screening.

The second (full-text) screening stage was conducted by the same two independent reviewers (MH and JS). Studies at this stage were screened in full and remained if they were experimental studies, included a patient population with any type of eye disease, used any combination of meditation or breathing exercise as an intervention, and took any measurement of quality of life, including anxiety, depression, or sleep quality as one of the study outcomes. Studies failing to meet any of these criteria were excluded. Non-experimental studies were excluded from this stage, including observational studies, conference abstracts, and expert opinions. Backward citation tracing was performed on the studies that had passed title and abstract screening to find additional studies eligible for data extraction based on the criteria used for full-text screening. All conflicts during either stage of screening were resolved through a discussion between the two reviewers until a consensus was reached. Agreement between the two reviewers along with Cohen’s kappa coefficient were calculated at both stages of screening.

Data Extraction

A total of seven studies remained after screening, and data were extracted from these studies by one reviewer (MH). Outcome data extracted included quality of life, anxiety, depression, and sleep quality scores. All outcome data were collected for the baseline and final time points in each study, as well as the overall change over the entire study period where available. The seven studies used various questionnaires to measure each of these outcomes. Five studies measured quality of life using either the abbreviated World Health Organization Quality of Life scale (WHOQOL-BREF), the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), or the Visual Function Questionnaire 25 (VFQ-25). Four studies measured anxiety using either the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), the anxiety subscale of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-A), or the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). Two studies measured depression using the depression subscale of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-D) or the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), and two studies measured sleep quality using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). In addition to outcome data, the name of the first author, year of publication, study location, participant ages, study design characteristics, type of intervention, length of follow-up, and statistical analysis methods were extracted (Tables 2–4). A meta-analysis was not performed due to high heterogeneity among the interventions used and study outcomes measured.

Risk of Bias Assessments

A risk of bias assessment was conducted for each of the seven studies by one reviewer (MH). The revised Cochrane Risk-of-bias Tool for Randomized Trials22 was used for the six randomized studies extracted, while the Modified Downs and Black Checklist23 was used for the single-arm study.

Results

Search Strategy and Screening

The initial search strategy yielded a total of 2542 records (Figure 1). These were imported into Covidence to automatically filter out duplicate results, after which 2297 unique studies remained. During title and abstract screening, 2252 studies were excluded and forty-five continued on to full-text screening (Cohen’s kappa = 0.32). After full-text screening, forty-one studies were excluded and four studies were deemed relevant according to the aforementioned criteria (Cohen’s kappa = 0.80). Backward citation tracing found an additional three studies that were deemed relevant, resulting in a total of seven studies used for data extraction and analysis.

PRISMA flow diagram of records obtained and excluded from search strategy.

Study Characteristics

Of the seven studies included in the qualitative analysis, four were conducted in India,15,16,18,20 one was conducted in the USA,17 one was conducted in Egypt,19 and one was conducted in Canada.21 Six of these studies were randomized controlled trials (RCTs)15,16,18–21 while one study used a single-arm study design.17 For the intervention, four studies examined the effects of meditation alone,15,16,20,21 one examined breathing exercises alone,19 one combined meditation and breathing exercises,18 while one examined a combination of breathing exercises, meditation, and traditional yoga.17 For the population of interest, four studies recruited patients with primary open-angle glaucoma,16,18,19 one included participants with glaucoma or glaucoma suspects,21 one included people with ocular hypertension,15 one included those with central serous chorioretinopathy,20 and one included participants with any visual impairment.17 Finally, for outcome measurements, four studies examined quality of life,15,16,18,19 four examined anxiety,17,19–21 two examined depression,19,21 while two examined sleep quality.17,21

Risk of Bias

Using the revised Cochrane Risk-of-bias Tool for Randomized Trials22 for the six RCTs15,16,18–21 revealed some concerns regarding bias in the outcome measurement (Table 5). Due to the nature of the intervention, participants are certainly aware of the treatment group assigned to them. Furthermore, the majority of the questionnaires assessing outcomes such as quality of life, anxiety, and depression are self-reported. As a result, there is a possibility that outcome assessment is influenced by participants knowing which group they had been assigned to. Additionally, descriptions of steps taken to conceal allocation were not sufficient to rule out potential bias in many studies. Assessment of the single-arm study using the Modified Downs and Black Checklist for Assessment of Methodological Quality23 yielded a score of 15 (Figure 2), which corresponded to a “fair” quality in accordance with the scoring ranges reported in a prior systematic review.24 The majority of points lost were due to the study not being a controlled trial, in addition to a lack of information regarding the characteristics of participants who had dropped out of the study.

Meditation and Quality of Life

Four studies had assessed the effects of meditation alone on quality of life.15,16,20,21 Dada et al (2022) assessed quality of life broadly using the abbreviated World Health Organization Quality of Life (WHOQOL-BREF) scale among those with ocular hypertension.15 Baseline measurements of the two groups indicated no statistically significant difference in WHOQOL-BREF scores (p = 0.29).15 After six weeks of follow-up, paired t-tests showed a statistically significant improvement in quality of life scores among those in the meditation group (from 85.23 ± 3.89 at baseline to 93.40 ± 3.25 after six weeks; p = 0.001) while there was no statistically significant difference in the control group (from 86.27 ± 3.66 at baseline to 86.23 ± 3.28 after six weeks; p = 0.891).15

Dada et al (2018) examined the effects of meditation alone on quality of life, again using the WHOQOL-BREF to measure the outcome.16 The population of interest included those with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG). Quality of life scores were comparable between groups at baseline (scores not reported; p > 0.05).16 After three weeks of follow-up, significant improvements in all four domains of the WHOQOL-BREF were seen in the meditation group (scores not reported; p < 0.001) but not in the control group (scores not reported; p > 0.05).16 Additionally, scores were significantly better in all four domains in the meditation group when compared to the control group after three weeks (scores not reported; p < 0.001).16

Nongrem et al (2021) examined the effects of meditation alone on anxiety using Forms Y1 (state) and Y2 (trait) of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) in a population of patients with central serous chorioretinopathy (CSC).20 Baseline scores were similar between the intervention and control groups for both STAI Y1 (p = 0.9) and Y2 (p = 0.13).20 After four weeks of follow-up, a statistically significant improvement was found in STAI Y1 scores (from 46.3 ± 1 at baseline to 41.65 ± 3 after four weeks; p = 0.001) and Y2 scores (from 45.8 ± 1.9 at baseline to 41.6 ± 3 after four weeks; p = 0.001) in the meditation group. No significant improvement was seen in the control group for either STAI Y1 (from 45.5 ± 1.5 at baseline to 44.1 ± 3.4 after four weeks; p = 0.09) or Y2 (from 46.5 ± 2.3 at baseline to 44.2 ± 3.5 after four weeks; p = 0.09).20 Despite the decreasing trend in the intervention group, all of the scores in both groups pre- and post-intervention indicated presence of anxiety.20 Notably, eight of 20 participants in the meditation group were excluded for failing to complete training, which may bias the results if they systematically differ from those who did complete training in some way. For example, if those who were excluded had significantly worse quality of life outcomes that would not have been improved by the intervention, the results would be biased in favour of showing that meditation improves quality of life. Malvankar-Mehta et al (2025) examined the effects of Sahaj Samadhi Meditation on vision-related quality of life (VRQoL) using the VFQ-25, anxiety using the STAI, depression using the CES-D, and sleep quality using the PSQI.21 Participants in the intervention arm underwent a weekly meditation intervention for 12 weeks and completed follow-up questionnaires at 24 weeks. VRQoL scores did not change significantly in either the control group (from 85.9 ± 12.1 at baseline to 87.0 ± 6.6 after 24 weeks, p = 0.866) or the intervention group (from 94.5 ± 4.8 at baseline to 93.7 ± 6.7 after 24 weeks, p = 0.745). The difference was also not statistically significant between groups (p = 0.489). Average CES-D scores increased by 5.3 in the control group (p = 0.453) and decreased by 6.1 in the intervention group (p = 0.490), neither of which were statistically significant. However, the difference in changes over time between the two groups was statistically significant (p = 0.043). STAI scores also did not change significantly in either the control group (from 30.5 ± 9.7 at baseline to 35.1 ± 15.0 after 24 weeks, p = 0.558) or the intervention group (from 33.3 ± 12.4 at baseline to 26.7 ± 8.9 after 24 weeks), and the difference between groups was also not statistically significant (p = 0.139). Average PSQI scores increased by 1.3 in the control group (p = 0.054) and decreased by 2.4 in the intervention group (p = 0.062). The difference in changes in PSQI after 24 weeks between the two groups was statistically significant (p = 0.004).21

Breathing Exercises and Quality of Life

Only one study used breathing exercises alone as its intervention—Ismail et al (2023) investigated the effects of an alternate-nostril breathing exercise on anxiety using the HADS-A, depression using the HADS-D, and health-related quality of life using the SF-36 scales in a population of elderly individuals with systemic hypertension and POAG.19 Baseline scores between the intervention and control groups were similar for anxiety (p = 0.151), depression (p = 0.79), and quality of life (p = 0.674).19 After four weeks of follow-up, statistically significant improvements were found in the intervention group for HADS-A scores (from 8.43 ± 1.47 at baseline to 6.73 ± 1.41 after four weeks; p < 0.001), HADS-D scores (from 9.06 ± 1.46 at baseline to 7.96 ± 1.35 after four weeks; p < 0.001), and SF-36 scores (from 58.30 ± 11.25 at baseline to 69.16 ± 10.15 after four weeks; p < 0.001), while no statistically significant changes were observed in the control group for HADS-A scores (from 8.96 ± 1.35 at baseline to 9.06 ± 2.01 after four weeks; p = 0.827), HADS-D scores (from 9.16 ± 1.44 at baseline to 9.20 ± 2.05 after four weeks; p = 0.931), or SF-36 scores (from 57.26 ± 7.42 at baseline to 56.63 ± 7.95 after four weeks; p = 0.616).19

Combined Meditation and Breathing Exercises

Two studies investigated a combination of meditation and breathing exercises as part of their interventions.17,18 Gagrani et al (2018) examined the effects of a meditation intervention with a focus on breathing on quality of life (WHOQOL-BREF) in POAG patients.18 WHOQOL-BREF scores were comparable between groups at baseline (p = 0.06).18 After a six-week follow-up, scores improved significantly among those in the intervention group (from 86.6 ± 6.16 at baseline to 93.3 ± 5.66 after six weeks; p = 0.0001) while there was no significant change in the control group (from 89 ± 7.25 at baseline to 89.07 ± 3.24 after six weeks; p = 0.74).18

Dowling and Vogtle (2022) examined the effects of a combination of meditation, breathing exercises, and yoga on anxiety (BAI) and sleep quality (PSQI) in a group of people with visual impairment.17 These impairments arose as a result of various conditions, including optic neuritis, age-related macular degeneration, glaucoma, cataracts, retinitis pigmentosa, Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy, von Hippel-Lindau disease, and coloboma. This was the only study in this review to use a single-arm design. After six weeks, a statistically significant improvement was found in both BAI score (Z = −3.062, p = 0.002) and PSQI score (Z = −3.062, p = 0.002) using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.17

Discussion

Although there have been numerous studies examining the effects of meditation and breathing exercises on quality of life outcomes, few studies have been conducted specifically in populations of eye disease patients despite its frequency and continuously increasing prevalence.25 Since no other systematic review assessing meditation and breathing exercises in eye disease patients currently exists, the aim of this review was to synthesize data from current studies, evaluate the evidence, and summarize these findings in the context of the existing evidence.

The studies in this review all found beneficial effects from meditation and breathing exercises on quality-of-life outcomes in eye disease patients. In the intervention groups of nearly all seven studies, statistically significant improvements were found in nearly every quality-of-life outcome at the follow-up measurement when compared to baseline. Only one study did not report statistically significant improvements in all outcomes.21 In all six randomized trials, no statistically significant difference was found in any of the outcome measures for the control groups. These findings seem to indicate that meditation and breathing exercises may have a beneficial effect on various domains of eye disease patients’ quality of life. However, these studies did not report minimally clinically important differences, nor did they evaluate whether these observed chances were perceived as meaningful by patients. Future studies may wish to report clinically meaningful thresholds to help with interpretation of the reported effects. The small number of studies in the synthesis as well as their heterogeneity should lead one to interpret the findings with caution. Firstly, meditation can take various forms and is not a standardized practice, adding numerous uncontrolled variables into the equation. Additionally, the studies in this review recruited participants with various eye diseases that may affect individuals differently, whether physically or psychologically. The outcome measures also varied greatly across studies. Outcomes studied included health-related quality of life, vision-related quality of life, anxiety, depression, and sleep quality. Even studies assessing the same outcome (eg anxiety) may have used different tools to measure its severity (eg HADS-A vs BAI). Some studies focused on within-groups differences rather than between-groups differences, the latter of which is more useful in evaluating the efficacy of the intervention. Future studies should also aim to provide more information regarding participant adherence to the intervention, as this could influence the observed effects. Finally, publication bias may play a role in these findings, where studies reporting statistically significant results are more likely to be published. The search would also have missed clinical trials that had been registered but not yet published.

Previous systematic reviews have tended to find positive effects of meditation and breathing exercises on quality-of-life outcomes in various populations,26–28 but some have also shown mixed evidence. One review of 47 meditation trials had found moderate improvements in anxiety and depression, but low or no evidence for improvements in mental health-related quality of life, mood, or sleep.29 Another review of meditation in people with spinal cord injury showed mixed evidence of improvement in various QOL-related domains, but that anxiety and depression symptoms in particular may be improved with the use of meditation.30 A common concern with existing meditation studies is the relatively short follow-up period. A review of studies administering meditation to participants with lower back pain found minimal short-term improvements for depression symptoms, but effects decreased over time.31 At 20 weeks of follow-up, all effects were no longer statistically significant,31 a period of time longer than any study follow-up period in this current review. Furthermore, study quality is another concern in meditation studies. In particular, due to the nature of the intervention, it is difficult to blind participants to their assigned group which could influence outcome reporting. This greatly increases the risk of bias, in particular due to the subjectivity and self-reporting of these measurements. For example, those assigned to the intervention group may report better quality-of-life outcomes after the intervention due to expectancy effects if they hold the belief that breathing exercises and meditation might help improve their quality of life. Finally, it is important to consider the clinical significance of these findings. Although studies may report a significant improvement in questionnaire scores, future studies should seek to evaluate whether this improvement provides any concrete benefit to the patients.

Although meditation and breathing exercises do not aim to treat the underlying eye disease, they may provide some benefit as a supplement to improve quality of life when performed alongside usual care practices for eye disease. Those suffering from eye disease may experience a decreased quality of life due to loss of independence and fear of disease progression,1,2 so quality of life may be an important concern for some patients. Future studies seeking to investigate these effects may wish to incorporate an active control group to enable blinding and use a longer follow-up period to investigate how the effects of meditation and breathing exercises hold up over a longer period of time.

One limitation of the methodology in the review is that data extraction and risk of bias assessments were performed by one reviewer rather than independently by two reviewers. Although standard procedures were used, this approach may increase the risk of extraction errors and subjective bias in study appraisal. Additionally, many of the studies shared overlapping authorship and focused on glaucoma, which may limit the applicability of results to other eye diseases or eye disease in general.

Conclusion

The current systematic review suggests short-term benefits of meditation and breathing exercises in various quality of life measures among people diagnosed with eye disease when compared with controls. However, these results should be interpreted with caution due to limited study quality and relatively short follow-up times. Additionally, the generalizability of the results may be limited by the small number of studies, which primarily focus on glaucoma, as well as the geographical concentration of the studies. Although meditation and breathing exercise do not seek to treat the underlying eye disease, there could be potential applications for alleviating symptoms of anxiety and depression as well as improving quality of life and sleep quality. Additional studies of high quality are needed to further investigate these effects in a population of patients with eye disease.
