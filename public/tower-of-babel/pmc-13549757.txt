Beyond anatomy – A commentary on "Pituitary magnetic resonance imaging abnormalities and 1-year growth response in children with idiopathic growth hormone deficiency"
Authors: Se Young Kim
Published: 2026

The diagnostic work-up for growth hormone deficiency (GHD) in children has relied on a multidisciplinary paradigm integrating auxological data (including growth velocity), biochemical stimulation tests, and neuroimaging studies [1]. Current consensus guidelines firmly recommend brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with specific attention to the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, to exclude organic lesions, including brain tumors (such as craniopharyngiomas) or severe congenital malformations (such as pituitary stalk interruption syndrome) [2]. However, for clinicians managing idiopathic GHD (IGHD)—where overt organic causes are absent—the clinical interpretation of mild, isolated structural variants remains a source of persistent conceptual ambiguity [3]. Does isolated anterior pituitary hypoplasia or a Rathke cleft cyst merely represent an incidental anatomical variant, or does it portend a distinct pathophysiological entity with poorer therapeutic outcomes? While Western registries have historically provided foundational insights [4], large-scale, region-specific data validating the prognostic value of these neuroimaging findings in Asian cohorts have been remarkably scarce. In this issue, a multicenter retrospective study by Choe et al. [5] addresses this clinical conundrum using the LG Growth Study registry. Investigating a substantial cohort of 457 Korean children with IGHD, the authors demonstrated that structural brain MRI abnormalities were prevalent, occurring in 26.7% of the study population, with pituitary hypoplasia being the most common finding (61.6% of abnormal cases) [5]. While an unadjusted baseline comparison hinted at a slightly lower 1-year growth response (change in height standard deviation score [SDS]) in the MRI-abnormal group, this difference disappeared after clinical and biochemical covariates were included in the regression models [5]. Instead, classical determinants—such as a younger age at treatment initiation, a higher baseline body mass index SDS, and a higher recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) dose—emerged as independent predictors of early catch-up growth [5]. This finding is consistent with recent work by Jung et al. [6], which noted that although pituitary abnormalities are frequent among IGHD patients, these structural findings do not translate into reliable prognostic information for GH treatment response. Furthermore, Oh et al. [7] recently demonstrated that while quantitative pituitary parameters such as volume influence growth response in true GHD, they show limited generalizability to other cohorts with short stature, reinforcing the complexity of predicting clinical responses from anatomy alone. The wide variation in outcomes suggests that the response to rhGH is more strongly influenced by peripheral receptor sensitivity, genetics, and adherence than by macroanatomical dimensions [1]. Nevertheless, certain methodological constraints merit discussion. The study’s multicenter, retrospective design introduced inherent interobserver variability due to the lack of a standardized quantitative imaging protocol across centers [5]. Additionally, as emphasized in a recent comprehensive review by Geffner et al. [8] on large-scale growth databases, long-term monitoring is crucial because the 1-year follow-up limited the assessment to short-term growth outcomes. Ultimately, this study by Choe et al. [5] serves as a pivotal reminder that we must look beyond anatomy when managing children with IGHD. While brain MRI remains an indispensable diagnostic tool to exclude severe intracranial pathology [9], its utility as an independent prognostic marker for short-term rhGH efficacy is limited [5]. Clinicians should avoid overinterpreting isolated or mild pituitary anomalies as predictors of poor treatment response and instead maintain a balanced focus on optimizing modifiable clinical factors, ensuring treatment adherence, and initiating therapy early. Moving forward, integrating high-resolution quantitative MRI with genetic profiling and dynamic biomarker assessments will be essential to uncover the relationship between structure and function in patients with GHD.
