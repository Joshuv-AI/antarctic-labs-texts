Policy and advocacy considerations for the prevention and care of preterm birth
Authors: Wondimu Gudu, Catalina M Valencia, Leticia I Ojeda, Priya Soma‐Pillay, the FIGO Committee on Preterm Birth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreterm birth (PTB) is the leading cause of mortality in children aged under 5 years and a major contributor to lifelong morbidity, disproportionately affecting low‐ and middle‐income countries. Despite its significant burden, PTB remains underprioritized on global and national health agendas, with limited integration into monitoring frameworks, funding streams, and policy strategies. This paper, developed by the FIGO Committee on Preterm Birth, reviews current evidence and international experiences to highlight the urgency of elevating PTB as a maternal and newborn health priority. Effective, low‐cost interventions—including quality antenatal care (ANC) and neonatal care, support for breastfeeding, infection management, and access to corticosteroids—could prevent up to three‐quarters of PTB‐related deaths, yet their implementation remains uneven. Moreover, addressing social determinants of health, such as poverty, gender‐based violence, inadequate maternity leave, and poor access to health care, is essential to reducing inequities in outcomes. Policy innovations from Latin American countries demonstrate how national legislation and protocols can improve prevention and care. To achieve meaningful progress, PTB must be integrated into Sustainable Development Goal strategies and the Every Newborn Action Plan, supported by strong political will, multisectoral collaboration, and investment in research and innovation. Advocacy efforts should focus on developing national prevention programs, engaging community health workers (CHWs), standardizing care guidelines, and strengthening public–private and international partnerships. A comprehensive approach that integrates evidence‐based interventions with policy reforms is crucial to reducing PTB incidence, improving survival, and ensuring equitable outcomes for mothers and newborns worldwide.Keywords: health policy and advocacy, maternal and newborn health, preterm birth prevention, social determinants of health

INTRODUCTION

Preterm birth (PTB) is the delivery of infants before 37 0/7 weeks of pregnancy. It is sub‐categorized into three groups based on gestational age: extremely preterm (<28 weeks), very preterm (28 to <32 weeks), and moderate to late preterm (32–37 weeks).

Infants may be born preterm because of spontaneous preterm labor or because there is a medical indication to plan an induction of labor or cesarean birth early. 1

The rate of preterm delivery varies worldwide, at 4%–16%, with a reported average of 10%. 2 An estimated 13.4 million infants were born too early in 2020, which is more than 1 in 10 infants. 2 Although PTB occurs across communities and geographies, 80% of PTBs occur in South Asia and sub‐Saharan Africa. 3 , 4 Approximately 900 000 children died in 2019 of complications of PTB. 5 Many survivors face a lifetime of disability, including learning disabilities and visual and hearing problems. Three‐quarters of these deaths could be prevented with current, cost‐effective interventions.

Globally, prematurity is the leading cause of death in children aged under 5 years. Inequalities in survival rates around the world are stark. In low‐income settings, half of the infants born at or below 32 weeks die due to a lack of feasible, cost‐effective care such as warmth, breastfeeding support, and basic care for infections and breathing difficulties. 1 In high‐income countries, almost all these infants survive. For example, more than 90% of extremely preterm infants (<28 weeks) born in low‐income countries (LICs) die within the first few days of life, yet less than 10% of extremely preterm infants die in high‐income settings. Suboptimal use of technology in middle‐income settings causes an increased burden of disability among preterm infants who survive the neonatal period. 1 Regular in‐service training of healthcare professionals and ensuring the availability of basic equipment for resuscitation of preterm neonates would have an impact on overall outcome of PTB in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). 6

In 2012, a seminal report, Born Too Soon: The Global Action Report on Preterm Birth, led by the March of Dimes, the Partnership for Maternal, Newborn & Child Health (PMNCH), Save the Children, and WHO, provided the first‐ever estimates of the global burden of PTB. 7 It also introduced a global action agenda to reduce the PTB rate by 50% by 2025.

However, despite the huge global burden of prematurity and its health impacts, progress has been slow, and PTB remains largely hidden on the global policy agenda. Policymakers have failed to adequately prioritize PTB. Current levels of political attention and investment, among other factors, have contributed to the lack of mobilization of the global community to achieve the 2012 Born Too Soon report goal of reducing the PTB rate by 50% by 2025. 8

Reducing PTB is essential to achieve the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) for health (SDG 3), including the key targets on newborn and child mortality, yet prematurity is not measured in the indicators or monitoring frameworks. 3 Similarly, the Every Woman Every Child Global Strategy for Women's, Children's and Adolescents' Health (2016–2030) has a strong focus on newborns and preventing stillbirths, but not prematurity. In addition, most countries do not report national data on PTBs and the World Bank's Global Financing Facility for Women, Children, and Adolescents (GFF) does not monitor PTB rates. 8 In order to reduce PTBs, prematurity must gain increased political priority, that is, “the degree to which international and national political leaders actively give attention to an issue, and back up that attention with the provision of financial, technical, and human resources that are commensurate with the severity of the issue”. 8

Given the complexity of preterm labor and its wide‐ranging implications, effective policy interventions and advocacy are crucial in tackling this issue. Policymakers, public health officials, and healthcare providers must collaborate to implement strategies that focus on both prevention and clinical care for affected infants and mothers. A notable example comes from Latin America, where increasing awareness of prematurity among health authorities has led to the development of public health policies aimed at preventing premature births and improving care for preterm infants. Countries such as Argentina 9 and Peru 10 have enacted national legislation to guarantee the right of pregnant individuals and preterm infants to access high‐quality, specialized medical care. In other nations, reference documents 11 , 12 and care protocols 13 have been established to guide both the prevention of prematurity and the care of preterm infants during early childhood.

RATIONALE

In contemporary maternal health care, much emphasis is placed on the physical and medical aspects of pregnancy; however, it is the interaction and overlap of numerous social determinants that ultimately shape the health of pregnant women and their newborns. 13 PTB results from a complex interaction of socioeconomic, behavioral, and biological factors. Among all obstetric complications, the interplay of social determinants of health is particularly pronounced in PTB. The health of pregnant women is impacted by factors such as income security, workplace issues, employment policies, lifestyle, and access to health care. 14 Despite the significant scale and wide‐ranging consequences of PTB, global political commitment, multisectoral collaboration, and overall attention to the issue remain insufficient. In most countries, PTB is still not prioritized in national health policies. The economic costs of PTB's contribution to childhood disability should also grab the attention of policymakers as the annual societal costs for one disabled child could be up to the country's GDP per capita. The burden is heavier on households in LICs as most of the costs are paid out‐of‐pocket, leading to impoverishment of the whole family. 15

Hence, policy and advocacy strategies to address the entire range of factors that influence PTB and are designed to reach all women of childbearing age are imperative. These strategies will require multisectoral collaboration and involve actions targeted at the societal, community, and system levels. In addition, strong policy and advocacy strategies are essential for building a robust health system that benefits both women and their neonates.

Most previous publications by the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) did not address policy and advocacy considerations for the prevention and care of PTB. As an organization committed to advancing women's health, it is imperative to develop practice recommendations (statements) on policy and advocacy related to PTB grounded in existing scientific evidence.

SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH IN THE CONTEXT OF PTB

WHO defines social determinants of health (SDOH) as “nonmedical factors that influence health outcomes”; such factors include “where people are born, grow, work, live, and age and the wider set of forces and systems shaping the conditions of daily life” (Figure 1). Despite initiatives that have sought to better understand and address the intersecting factors that result in inequities in obstetrical outcomes, the optimal methods by which SDOH are defined and quantified, the pathways by which SDOH affect intra‐ and intergenerational health, and the most effective means to combat the deleterious effects of adverse SDOH remain uncertain. 16 , 17

Addressing the social determinants of PTB requires a realistic, multifaceted approach that combines effective policy and advocacy. Targeting the key domains of economic stability, education, social and community context, healthcare access, and the built environment, policymakers and advocates can create systemic changes that reduce the risk of PTB (Figure 2). Through collaborative efforts and policy reforms, it is possible to improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes and significantly reduce PTB rates.

POLICY AND ADVOCACY CONSIDERATIONS (RECOMMENDATIONS) FOR PTB

Advocate PTB as a priority maternal health agenda

Framing PTB as one newborn health condition among many has diluted policymakers focus and stalled action on the issue. There is a real risk that PTB will continue to be overlooked, jeopardizing progress across the Reproductive Maternal Newborn and Child Health (RMNCH) continuum. Framing PTB as a health condition across the RMNCH continuum of care is crucial for effective prevention and treatment. Addressing PTB is also essential to achieving the SDG and Every Newborn Action Plan (ENAP) targets, ensuring improved maternal and neonatal health and aligning efforts with global health and development. 8

Hence, at both global and national levels, the magnitude of PTB should be clearly recognized and prioritized as a key health agenda. This will ensure it receives the necessary attention from policymakers and governments, leading to the implementation of targeted interventions and policies aimed at reducing PTB rates and improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Strategies to address disparities

Strategies and policies should be developed to address racial and ethnic disparities in maternal and neonatal health, focusing on benefiting socially disadvantaged or vulnerable groups. These efforts must be grounded in the principles of equity, ensuring that all individuals, regardless of their background, have access to the necessary resources, health care, and support to reduce the risk of PTB and improve health outcomes. By prioritizing equity, policies can help close the gaps in healthcare access and outcomes for marginalized communities. 17

National PTB prevention programs and networks

Establishing national PTB prevention programs and networks is crucial for reducing preterm birth rates. 18 These programs should bring together a network of researchers, policymakers, healthcare professionals, and other key stakeholders to collaborate on effective strategies, share best practices, and drive evidence‐based interventions. 8 By fostering collaboration across sectors, such programs can strengthen the national response to PTB, ensuring that prevention efforts are comprehensive, well‐funded, and widely implemented. This network can also advocate for policies that prioritize maternal and neonatal health, ensuring that resources are directed toward those most at risk of preterm delivery.

In the absence of comprehensive public policy addressing these issues, non‐governmental organization have played a crucial role in bridging the gap. Through establishing care networks and training programs for healthcare professionals, these organizations have promoted a more holistic approach to prematurity prevention. A notable example is the Argentine Network of Maternal‐Fetal Medicine, 19 which, since 2017, has advocated preventive strategies from the preconception period through the fourth trimester of pregnancy. Its initiatives have since expanded into creating a broader Latin American network, highlighting the need for regional collaboration and sustained investment in maternal and neonatal health.

Promotion of school attendance and completion

Girls' education will empower them and boost their decision‐making for their own health. Moreover, educated girls and women are more likely to be exposed to information on the consequences of early marriage and PTB. Although the importance of education and women's empowerment in preventing prematurity is well recognized, it is equally essential to incorporate policy strategies focused on pregnancy planning and preconception care as part of a comprehensive approach to preventing PTBs.

Fertility policy on artificial reproductive technology (ART)

The development of national regulations regarding the maximum number of embryos that may be transferred in ART programs, as well as regulations governing the prescription of ovulation induction agents, is recommended. 9

Global prematurity day for advocacy on PTB

The global “World Prematurity Day,” observed every November 17, brings attention to PTB and its prevention. The day acts as a platform for advocacy groups to educate the public, share personal stories, and promote policy reforms. 20 It has increased awareness in various countries, resulting in greater government commitment to reducing PTB. This will be especially vital in countries with a high rate of prematurity.

Including PTB as an indicator of perinatal health

The preterm birth community and partners should recognize PTB as essential to achieving RMNCH goals in both global and national strategic documents and include specific indicators to reduce PTB. 8 Establishing integrated data‐capturing mechanisms for PTB at national and international levels will be important.

Developing national guidelines, protocols, and care plans for PTB

Developing national guidelines, protocols, and care plans for PTB is essential to standardize care, improve outcomes, and reduce the incidence of PTBs. These guidelines should be evidence‐based and tailored to address the unique needs of diverse populations. These national guidelines and care plans should be regularly reviewed and updated based on new research and data, ensuring continuous improvement in preventing and managing PTB nationwide.

Public–private partnership for the prevention of PTB

Public–private partnerships (PPPs) are crucial for preventing PTB by combining the strengths of both sectors. Governments provide policy support and infrastructure, while private organizations offer innovation, expertise, and resources. These collaborations can improve access to quality prenatal care, fund research, train healthcare professionals, and expand prevention programs, particularly in underserved areas. By working together, PPPs can create a coordinated, cost‐effective approach to reducing PTB rates and improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Promoting international collaboration

Since PTB is a global health issue, international collaboration on policy and research is vital. Policymakers should support global partnerships, such as those led by WHO, to share data, research, and best practices for preventing PTB worldwide. 8 By adopting successful strategies from other countries, nations can work together to reduce PTB rates on a global scale.

Collaborative efforts between high‐ and low‐resource settings have facilitated the sharing of best practices in PTB prevention. Programs that focus on improving healthcare delivery in LICs have shown success in reducing the incidence of preterm labor through better prenatal care, improved sanitation, and community‐based interventions. 8

Policies to protect pregnant women

The health of pregnant women is affected by factors such as income security, workplace issues, employment policies, lifestyle, and access to health care. 8 , 14 Social policies on enhanced income security, child benefits, and improved maternity and parental leave have been suggested to prevent financial insecurity. 14

Minimum paid pregnancy leave, liberal pregnancy leave, and time off for prenatal visits

Strengthening maternal leave and workplace policies: Paid maternity leave and flexible workplace policies are essential for allowing pregnant women to receive adequate prenatal care and rest. Furthermore, stress reduction during pregnancy has been shown to reduce the risk of PTB. Advocating for stronger family leave policies can give women the time and resources they need to have a healthy pregnancy.

Exemption from night shifts and strenuous work conditions

A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis showed that pregnant women in physically demanding jobs, those working long hours or on shifts, and those exposed to whole‐body vibration have an increased risk of PTB. It was recommended that employers should establish supportive workplaces, policymakers implement protective measures (e.g. providing alternative work assignments when a job involves strenuous activity or prolonged standing), and healthcare providers conduct screenings. 22 , 23

Protection from workplace hazards and environmental toxics

These include a smoke‐free environment and reducing exposure to toxic substances. It may be beneficial to integrate environmental health into obstetrics and gynecology training and practice. Advocating for policies that will reduce harmful exposures and protect the health of pregnant individuals and their children is encouraged. 23

Strong policy on substances

Public policy and legislation, such as health warnings on cigarette packages, limitations on the sale of cigarettes to minors, and restrictions on smoking in public places, and other policies and interventions aimed at changing attitudes, behavior, and social norms are recommended.

Nutrition programs for girls and adolescents

A nutrition program for at‐risk pregnant women should include food supplementation, regular nutrition assessments, counseling, and support. It should also involve interagency referrals to ensure comprehensive care, aiming to reduce the risk of PTB by addressing nutritional needs and promoting healthier pregnancies. 18

Increasing roles of healthcare professionals as change leaders (advocates)

This leadership role may involve advocating for public policies, community awareness, and interagency collaboration, ensuring a comprehensive strategy for reducing PTB rates and improving overall health outcomes.

Improving access to preconception care

A policy focused on improving access to preconception care as a priority agenda for the prevention of PTB should be considered. These include integrating PCC into primary health care, collaboration with community‐based organizations and health insurance coverage. 18 It should also include key components of the PCC, such as the provision of important nutritional supplements, including micronutrients, especially folic acid and iron, for preconception and pregnant women.

Engaging low‐level health workers in the provision of ANC and preconception care

Policies should integrate innovative models of care for preterm labor into existing maternal health systems, including providing basic preconception and ANC by CHWs, especially in remote areas. CHWs are an underutilized and uniquely equipped workforce to reduce longstanding disparities in PTB. This approach improves access to care and reduces PTB risks, particularly for underserved populations. 24 Simplified antenatal and postnatal checklists can be developed that could be used easily by CHWs. Doulas are also well‐positioned in the community to provide culturally appropriate care.

Grassroots advocacy

A notable weakness within the PTB community is the lack of political and civil society champions from LMICs. 7 Therefore, grassroots advocacy will contribute to efforts to prevent PTB.

POLICY CONSIDERATIONS FOR PTB CARE PROVISION

Improve access and quality of ANC

Strategies to improve the content and quality of ANC should be considered. Changing the focus of prenatal care from a strictly medical model to a more comprehensive one that includes social, educational, and economic support has been recommended. Policies to incentivize health systems and providers to adopt evidence‐based, equity‐centered models of care, as well as policies aimed at improving access to prenatal care, care incorporating the provision of important multivitamin supplements (such as iron and folic acid), particularly for vulnerable populations, are critical in improving outcomes of PTBs. 24

Increase access and coverage to simple, cost‐effective interventions to improve neonatal outcomes

There are specific evidence‐based, simple, and cost‐effective interventions that may reduce perinatal mortality and morbidity associated with PTB or even prevent its occurrence.

The FIGO PremPrep‐5 initiative comprises five essential perinatal interventions, which include the use of magnesium sulphate for neuroprotection and administration of corticosteroids for promoting fetal lung maturity, which, when implemented together, have the potential to significantly improve perinatal outcomes in PTB. 25 These interventions are designed to be easily adopted across diverse healthcare settings.

A total of 30 years have passed since the first publication in which the cervical length measured by ultrasound was associated with PTB. 26 At present, a substantial body of evidence supports universal intravaginal ultrasound cervical length screening before 24 weeks as a strategy to detect patients at risk 27 and to apply interventions to prevent PTB, such as vaginal progesterone or cerclage. 28 , 29

Though the efficacy of all these interventions has been accepted worldwide, strong institutional policies are crucial to train caregivers at all settings and levels and to support their implementation, as one of the primary challenges lies in overcoming resistance to change and promoting the benefits of these interventions over existing practices. 25

Adopting an evidence‐based infant feeding policy with adequate provision of maternity leave, implemented by a skilled multidisciplinary team, including lactation consultants and nursing staff who provide breastfeeding support while the mother is in the hospital, is recommended. 30

Education and training

Policies that support continuing medical education for obstetricians, gynecologists, midwives, and neonatal intensive care unit staff are essential, particularly those that promote the use of evidence‐based practices.

Implementing such guidelines can help standardize care and improve health outcomes for preterm infants and their mothers.

Addressing social determinants of health at the health facility level

Clinicians can volunteer for and support community organizations and raise personal awareness of SDOH in their practices. They should listen to the unique needs of patients and the community and develop a network for referrals and SDOH support. Furthermore, they should have an in‐depth conversation with the patient to understand how the community systems are, or are not, working for them. 14 Healthcare policies for the provision of culturally appropriate services are needed for minorities. 16 , 24

Establish policies on collaborative clinical care

Effective care for women at risk of preterm labor requires a multidisciplinary approach. Obstetricians, maternal‐fetal medicine specialists, neonatologists, and pediatricians should collaborate to manage these high‐risk pregnancies, ensuring optimal outcomes for both mothers and infants.

Considerations for the establishment of specialized prematurity clinics

Establishing specialized prematurity clinics can be considered for improving the prevention and care of PTBs though specific evidence to support the utility of preterm birth clinics as a whole is still evolving. 31 These clinics should focus on providing comprehensive prenatal care, including early screenings and risk assessments for women at higher risk of preterm delivery. A multidisciplinary approach involving obstetricians, neonatologists, nurses, and mental health professionals is essential to address the complex needs of PTB families. 32

Support for PTB research

Funding for research on the biological, social, and environmental factors that contribute to PTB as well as generating data on cost‐saving potential of prevention strategies for PTB must be considered. 16 , 33 Research will also be important to assess new models of care in PTB. In addition, the efforts to improve care and prevention of PTBs require the inclusion of individuals from groups historically underrepresented in biomedical research (e.g. those from minorities' communities, with lower economic status, and from rural locations). 34

Innovative interventions

Promoting the development and implementation of innovative treatments, such as new medications or technologies/innovations to reduce preterm birth rates, is necessary.

Evaluation of policy effectiveness

Collecting data on PTB rates, the effectiveness of interventions, and the impact of policies to continuously improve approaches should be considered.

CONCLUSIONS

PTB remains a significant public health challenge, especially in regions with limited access to maternal healthcare services. Policy and advocacy efforts are crucial for reducing PTB rates worldwide. Comprehensive policy and advocacy considerations should focus on key areas such as public health education, social determinants of health, workplace safety for pregnant women, community‐based initiatives, access to prenatal care, evidence‐based clinical care, and investment in research and innovation. These efforts are crucial in preventing preterm labor and enhancing health outcomes for both mothers and newborns. Ongoing research, funding, and collaboration among governments, healthcare professionals, and advocacy organizations are essential to achieving meaningful reductions in PTB rates.
