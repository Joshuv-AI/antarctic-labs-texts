Methodological Variations in Environmental Justice Screening Tools and Their Impact on Disadvantaged Community Designations
Authors: Thomas Robbins, Qingran Li, Stephen Bird, Susan E Powers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGovernment agencies in the United States have developed environmental justice screening tools (EJSTs) and designations to promote equitable distribution of benefits from environmental policies to disadvantaged communities (DACs). However, the methodological choices embedded in these tools, including the selection, weighting, and aggregation of environmental justice indicators, vary across EJSTs, and their implications for DAC designation outcomes are poorly understood. Here we systematically compare four EJSTs at the federal and state levels and show that these tools produce substantial variation in the DAC census tracts, with some tools emphasizing socioeconomic burdens while others prioritize environmental risks. Using quantitative regression models, we further show that indicator weighting and other design choices shape final DAC designations in ways that are not always made explicit. These findings highlight the need for greater transparency in EJST methodological choices to support informed policymaking and public understanding of DAC eligibility.Subject terms: Policy, Climate change, Energy justice

Introduction

Environmental policies and public investments often involve balancing economic efficiency with equity considerations in pursuit of environmental justice (EJ). At its core, EJ seeks to ensure fair distribution of environmental burdens and environmental benefits, as well as equitable treatment for all individuals and communities in environmental decision-making processes1. With climate change projected to cause an additional 14.5 trillion dollars in damages to the U.S. economy by 20702, there is an imperative for policies that tackle climate challenges but also prevent the inequitable distribution of these damages, rectify historical injustices, and promote equity3,4.

In response, the Justice40 Initiative5 was launched under the 2021 Biden administration with the goal of directing 40% of the benefits from certain federal investments to disadvantaged communities. This initiative mandated the development of environmental justice screening tools (EJSTs) by federal agencies and motivated many state initiatives. These tools identify, map, and designate disadvantaged communities (DACs) based on cumulative impacts of environmental and socioeconomic burdens, so that public investments can be guided toward these areas. The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) authorized approximately 40 billion dollars to support investments in DACs6. While some funding streams were paused or subject to congressional rollbacks following administrative changes in 2025, others remain unchanged or under review according to the IRA Tracker7. Nevertheless, many states have continued to mandate DAC designations and directed funding accordingly despite changes in federal policies since 2025. Prominent examples (see Supplementary Table 2) include California’s Senate Bill 535, which directs Cap-and-Trade proceeds to CalEPA-defined DACs8, and New York’s use of its DAC map9 to guide state climate and energy funding under the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act. Washington and Illinois have adopted similar place-based designations for EJ policies10,11.

While these EJ initiatives reflect growing momentum toward equitable investments directed by EJSTs, these EJSTs have been developed independently across agencies and governments, each reflecting different methodological choices in how EJ indicators are selected, weighted, and aggregated. Two critical questions, therefore, arise: How does this diverse approach, spanning multiple agencies and levels of government, influence the identification of DACs? And what are the policy implications of variation across EJSTs when they are used to allocate public investments? Addressing these questions requires clarifying how tool design choices generate the differences in DAC identification. Providing this transparency is the central focus of this study. Such transparency matters to multiple stakeholders, as it not only informs policymakers and agencies designing or selecting EJSTs to align with their policy goals, but also helps the public understand why their community is or is not designated as a DAC and therefore eligible for certain program benefits.

Growing evidence from EJ studies suggests that some communities are disproportionately exposed to environmental and socioeconomic burdens4,12,13. Addressing these disparities requires identifying and measuring multiple, often overlapping burdens, which led to the creation of EJSTs14. In recent years, communities designated as DACs—the critical outputs from EJSTs—are prioritized to receive a wide range of public investments, including billions of dollars in energy efficiency, affordable housing, and clean transportation funding15. As the DAC mapping outcomes directly influence policymaking and public investments, a stakeholder’s EJST choice could significantly impact the distributional costs and benefits of its policies, particularly if variations in DAC methodologies and outcomes exist across different tools. For the same reason, the public has a direct stake in understanding how their community’s DAC status is determined and whether that designation reflects the burdens that relevant policies are meant to address.

Given the wide range of EJ concerns, policy priorities, and regional contexts, it is expected that different EJSTs would reflect varied methodological approaches to identify and aggregate various burdens, thus producing tool-dependent differences in the DAC designations. Without transparency about the sources of these variations, policymakers, especially those not directly involved in developing these tools, may unknowingly apply a tool with implicit priorities that are misaligned with their policy’s equity goals. For example, allocating energy efficiency grants according to an EJST that primarily designates DACs based on transportation access indicators could result in inefficiency in the use of these grants. This concern is supported by Bakkensen et al.16, who showed that certain communities may satisfy the DAC criteria defined by socioeconomic EJ indicators but not natural disaster risk, due to their weak correlations. Further, an approach heavily dependent on an income-based indicator could miss communities experiencing disproportionate impacts17. However, few studies have quantified the tool-driven variations in DAC outcomes or empirically examined how DAC outcomes based on cumulative impacts map to individual EJ burdens. This study addresses this gap by undertaking a systematic comparative analysis of several EJSTs, focusing on how their choice of EJ indicators and the associated weights of these indicators result in varying DAC outcomes.

This study analyzes four EJSTs in the United States: three federal tools and one state-level tool. Published versions and access links for the four EJSTs are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The three federal tools examined are the White House’s Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool (CEJST)18, the Department of Energy’s (DOE) Disadvantaged Communities Reporter19, and the Department of Transportation’s (DOT) Equitable Transportation Community Explorer20, all developed in response to the Justice40 Initiative. Under this initiative, CEJST serves as the primary tool for identifying DACs, while agency-specific tools may be applied in special circumstances with justifiable need21. Despite different methods used to aggregate cumulative impacts, all these EJSTs generate binary DAC designations at the census tract level, allowing us to focus our comparison on the connection between their methodological disparities and the resulting variations in DAC mapping. The binary designation outcome is a critical feature for our quantitative analysis. Some popular EJ policy tools, such as EPA’s EJScreen and CDC’s EJ Index map, are excluded from our analysis because they do not directly generate a binary DAC designation.

This study also compares the methodologies of federal EJSTs with a state-level tool, using New York State’s DAC mapping tool as an example case. With the growing trend of states developing their own DAC criteria22, policymakers are increasingly faced with the decision between adopting national versus regional EJSTs. Nineteen states have current or past EJSTs, and a small number of states, such as California8, Maryland23, and Washington24, were pioneers, developing their EJSTs proactively years before the Justice40 Initiative. The majority of state tools were released after the January 2021 announcement of Justice40, suggesting they were likely developed in direct response to the federal policy. This difference in timeline implies that later tools may be more explicitly framed to align with federal funding eligibility criteria. For example, state DAC maps were used alongside federal tools to determine eligibility for federal grants passed through state agencies25, or to facilitate compliance with Justice40 flow-down requirements in state implementation plans26. To identify a state tool suitable for comparison with the federal EJSTs, we reviewed a list of active state EJSTs, in Supplementary Table 2, and selected the tool developed by New York State’s Climate Justice Working Group, as it shares several important features with the federal EJSTs.

While states vary considerably in their priorities and approaches, New York’s EJST provides a useful case for comparison because its methodological features align closely with those of the federal EJSTs. New York State’s tool produces a binary DAC designation, making it directly comparable to the three federal EJSTs within the consistent framework of our quantitative analysis. Moreover, while many states advise using EJSTs to guide public investment without a clear mandate for policy use, New York’s DAC map carries direct policy implications under the state’s Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (CLCPA or Climate Act)27. This law requires that at least 35% of the benefits from the clean transition investments be directed to DACs identified in New York state’s EJST. For example, subsidies under the Charge Ready NY 2.0 program are higher for electric vehicle charging stations located within DACs28. By comparing this state-level tool with the three federal EJSTs, we find notable variation in DAC mapping outcomes across tools, highlighting the risk that a state may select an EJST without fully understanding the link between its methodological choices and the ultimate DAC designations. Understanding these potential biases and the factors that contribute to them provides crucial insights into how EJ is operationalized across varying levels of governance in the United States.

The DAC designation methods of various EJSTs often differ significantly in terms of their choice of EJ indicators, treatment of population demographics (especially race and ethnicity), screening algorithms, and the format of DAC outputs (e.g., tiered disadvantage scores, binary index). Besides qualitatively comparing these methodological components across EJSTs, this study uses linear and logistic regression models to quantify the EJ indicator weights employed by different EJSTs when aggregating EJ burdens to the cumulative impact. Identifying these indicator weights is critical for discussing the role of EJSTs in effectively supporting various policy objectives, which is an important concern given the significant disparities in DAC designations across different tools. By comparing the weighting of EJ indicators, we recognize tool-dependent DAC mapping disparities and identify disparate influential indicator categories driving these disparities. This, alongside the EJSTs’ descriptive differences, allows for a measured comparison between EJST choices, adding a new layer of information to guide EJST policy applications.

Disparities across EJSTs are not inherently problematic. Although a common standard and method for operationalizing EJ is useful to reduce complexity and increase synergistic benefits from complementary policy initiatives, different agencies and government entities may justifiably emphasize different EJ characteristics in line with their specialized missions. Nonetheless, these differences do raise important concerns when EJSTs are used to guide location-based policies with large distribution effects. Depending on which EJST is applied, the communities eligible for EJ programs could vary substantially (Supplementary Fig. 2). For example, the marginalized representation of rural areas in some EJSTs could lead policymakers to overlook rural-specific environmental burdens29–31, thus resulting in a population density bias, reducing long-term economic development investments in these regions and potentially contributing to further outmigration from rural areas.

In this work, we systematically compare four EJSTs and quantitatively show that indicator weighting and other design choices shape final DAC designations in ways that are not always made explicit. Our analysis, therefore, provides a step toward the informed selection and application of EJSTs and greater public understanding of how DAC designations are determined, while regulatory entities continue to explore best practices for supporting equitable policymaking.

Results

Geographical disparities in DAC designations across EJSTs

Before analyzing how methodological variations and indicator weighting contribute to differences among EJSTs, we first document the geographical disparities in DAC designations. Establishing these outcome differences provides context for understanding how methodological choices may contribute to the observed inconsistencies. Figure 1 compares the mapping of DACs across the three federal EJSTs (CEJST, DOE, and DOT), revealing notable differences in the percentage of census tracts designated as DACs within each U.S. state. CEJST identifies a significantly higher proportion of DACs in many states, particularly those with larger Tribal Nation populations, such as Oklahoma and Montana, and states with higher poverty rates, including Mississippi, Louisiana, and New Mexico. DOT shows a similar pattern to CEJST in the southern and southeastern states, but also highlights a higher proportion of DACs in states around the Great Lakes region, such as Michigan, Indiana, and Ohio.

DOE designates a lower percentage of DACs across all states. Nationally, only 18.3% of census tracts are identified as DACs in DOE’s tool, compared to around 35% in CEJST and DOT. This pattern reflects DOE’s methodological choice to restrict DAC designations to the top 20% of census tracts within each state (i.e., those above the 80th percentile). Because this threshold is applied within each state’s own distribution, every state is guaranteed a fixed share of DACs regardless of its overall burden levels relative to other states. Such an approach helps ensure that all states have representation, which is particularly useful when federal resources are allocated through state governments. However, it may systematically exclude highly burdened communities in states with high overall levels of vulnerability. This illustrates the inherent trade-offs in different EJST methodologies between ensuring geographical equity across states and identifying communities with the most disadvantaged status nationwide.

To compare the DAC designations between federal and state EJ initiatives, Fig. 2a zooms in on New York State (see “Data Development” in “Methods”), showing the number of DAC designations a census tract receives based on the mapping results from four EJSTs—the three federal tools and the state tool developed under New York State’s Climate Act27. Except for DOE, which caps DAC designations at 20%, the other EJSTs each designate about 35% of the census tracts in New York State as DACs (Supplementary Table 3). However, the spatial distribution of these DACs varies considerably across EJSTs. For example, several suburban communities in the Mid-Hudson region are identified as DACs by New York’s tool but not by any of the federal EJSTs. Within the state, 51.3% of the census tracts are identified as DACs by at least one EJST, with only about 14% of the tracts being jointly identified by all four EJSTs. Urban communities are more likely to be designated as disadvantaged and show a higher level of agreement across the four EJSTs, as indicated by the large share of red-colored areas in Fig. 2b, c. This urban preference is particularly pronounced for DOE, which does not designate any DACs in rural areas.

New York’s tool, compared to the federal EJSTs, identifies the largest number of rural and suburban DACs. This reflects a key distinction in the state’s screening methodology. The state’s tool applies a DAC designation threshold that selects a census tract if its estimated environmental burden falls within the top 27.4% of either the statewide distribution or the distribution within two regions: New York City or Rest-of-State. This practice allows for more DACs to be designated in less urbanized regions outside of New York City, thus facilitating EJ policies that target critical rural burdens (e.g., demographic changes, declining industries, and transportation isolation).

The variation in DAC designations means that a community might qualify as disadvantaged in one policy jurisdiction but not another. For instance, we find that CEJST and New York’s tool show a relatively balanced geographic profile between urban and other areas, while the DOE and DOT tools exhibit significantly less representation of rural areas (Supplementary Table 3). Officially, the CEJST was established as the primary tool for the Justice40 Initiative, and other federal agencies are directed to use it to identify candidate tracts21. However, agencies like DOE and DOT may use their own tools to further prioritize within the CEJST-designated tracts using mission-specific indicators or justify needs for special circumstances. Our findings suggest that rural CEJST tracts, which are already recognized as disadvantaged, could be systematically deprioritized or ineligible for sector-specific investments in energy and transportation. Although the reduced rural representation in DOE and DOT tools may partly reflect intentional mission-specific priorities, some of these geographical disparities may also stem from methodological choices in developing these tools. Without transparency about the sources of such disparities, it is difficult to conclude whether it is a deliberate policy choice or an unintended consequence of tool design. To the extent that such unintended consequences influence DAC eligibility and funding allocation, our analysis indicates a potential misalignment in the prioritization process that could undermine the equity goals of the Justice40 Initiative and similar state and local EJ programs.

Methodological variations in DAC screening

The geographical disparity in DAC designations suggests critical variations in how each tool interprets and applies EJ criteria. To understand the sources of these differences, we reviewed the technical documentation of each EJST and found notable differences in their DAC screening methodologies, particularly in how they select EJ indicators, reduce the dimensions of multiple burdens, and set eligibility criteria for binary DAC status. As summarized in Table 1, the EJSTs generally employ a multi-step process that involves generating a cumulative score for each census tract based on various EJ indicators. These scores are then compared against certain thresholds, with higher scores indicating greater cumulative burdens. Census tracts that exceed certain thresholds are assigned a DAC index value of one.

Although the EJSTs differ in their indicator selections, they all recognize the necessity of measuring cumulative impacts of the socioeconomic burdens, environmental pollution, climate risk, and health vulnerabilities. Our review highlights a strong emphasis on income equity in CEJST’s indicator choices compared to other EJSTs. The White House’s CEJST is distinct in its approach by focusing on binary criteria without generating a cumulative score. Namely, CEJST does not rank census tracts based on a numerical score; instead, DAC designation is determined by whether a census tract meets pre-defined percentile thresholds across multiple EJ burden categories. Under the Socioeconomic Burden category, CEJST defines low-income populations as those earning less than twice the federal poverty level, excluding higher education students. Based on this definition, a census tract is assigned low income = 1 if its share of low-income population exceeds the 65th percentile. This low-income indicator plays a critical role in DAC designations, intersecting with all subcategories (except Workforce Development) under the Environmental, Climate, or Other Burdens category to define cumulative burdens. Although low income is not directly listed under Workforce Development, other indicators in this subcategory, such as high poverty, low median income, and unemployment, are highly correlated with it. This suggests a significant emphasis on economic vulnerability as a defining feature of DACs under the CEJST framework.

In contrast to CEJST’s binary approach, the remaining EJSTs in our study employ a scoring-based method to identify DACs. That is, these EJSTs aggregate EJ indicators under various burden categories into a cumulative score and compare census tracts against a percentile threshold. For the scoring-based EJSTs, we observe significant variations in both the weighting schemes and the screening threshold choices, suggesting the absence of a standardized practice. These methods of applying DAC thresholds are different in two primary ways (Table 1, column Step 3). First, the cutoff percentile selected to determine DAC census tracts varies across tools. The DOE tool applies the most stringent threshold at the 80th percentile, resulting in a smaller set of DACs compared to other EJSTs, where the threshold is set at 72.6 for New York State’s tool and 65 for the DOT tool.

Second, the cumulative score distributions used for applying these thresholds are different: DOT uses a national distribution, DOE uses a state-wide distribution, and New York State applies both state-wide and regional distributions. Despite these differences, it is unclear what criteria justify the selection of these thresholds and the scale of distributions, leaving much to the discretion of the tool developers. The impact of such variations, however, is significant. By setting the highest cutoff percentile, the DOE tool could miss identifying certain census tracts that have high cumulative scores in a national comparison (Supplementary Fig. 3), whereas the DOT and New York State’s tools avoid this issue by assigning DAC status to all census tracts with scores around the 75th percentile or higher. Moreover, consideration of regional relativity in the New York State tool is particularly notable in the case of nonurban census tracts, which can be designated as DACs despite having lower-ranking scores in the state-wide distribution. This finding is supported by the observation that some nonurban tracts (purple box markers in the DAC tracts plot in Supplementary Fig. 3) are designed as DACs by the New York State tool, despite their cumulative scores being in the 25th to 50th percentile rank.

The scoring-based EJSTs also differ in how they aggregate multiple EJ indicators into a composite index, particularly in terms of the weighting schemes applied (Table 1, column Step 2). While DOE applies equal weight to all EJ indicators, both DOT and New York State adopt a two-step approach; that is, indicators are first weighted within their respective EJ categories, generating a category score, which is then further weighted based on the category’s importance. In the DOT tool, the Transportation Insecurity category is double-weighted, emphasizing the tool’s focus on transportation inequity. Similarly, in New York State’s tool, the Climate Risk subcategory is double-weighted, implying the state’s prioritization of climate vulnerability. While the technical documents of these tools provide detailed discussion on indicator selection and data sources, they often lack transparency regarding the rationale for their weighting schemes, despite these schemes having a significant impact on the composite index32. This, together with the other methodological variations, further complicates direct comparisons between the EJSTs. In the next section, we further investigate the weighting schemes using linear and logistic regression models and quantitatively compare the relative importance of indicators in DAC screening.

Relative importance of indicators in DAC screening

To better understand the relative importance of EJ indicators in determining DAC designations, we employed post-LASSO regression models to estimate the relative weight of indicators in each EJST while addressing multicollinearity among the indicators (see “Regression analysis” in “Methods”). We used a linear model to examine the relative weights of EJ indicators in determining the cumulative scores (in percentile rank, Fig. 3). We then used a logistic model to estimate the relative indicator weights in determining the binary DAC designation (Fig. 4). Examining the coefficient estimates from the linear regression model, as specified by Eq. (1) in “Methods”, for the three scoring-based EJSTs (i.e., DOE, DOT, and New York State), we find that indicators related to socioeconomic vulnerabilities—such as education level, low income, disadvantaged employment and housing status—are among the most influential determinants of the percentile rank of census tracts’ cumulative scores (Fig. 3). Additionally, DOE’s method exhibits equal weighting across all EJ indicators, resulting in a balanced treatment of various burden categories. In contrast, the Transportation Access indicator in DOT’s tool shows more than twice the magnitude of the coefficient compared to other indicators, reflecting DOT’s double weighting of the Transportation Insecurity category. Our results also highlight the prominence of climate hazards and risks in both DOT and New York State’s scoring methods. Notably, New York State’s tool assigns double weight to the Climate Change Risks category, resulting in five indicators in this subcategory ranking in the top ten influential indicators, as shown in Fig. 3. Our quantitative approach provides statistical evidence of the critical role of indicator weighting schemes for measuring cumulative burdens in terms of percentile scores.

Beyond indicator weights, the influence of these indicators on final DAC designations also depends on how each EJST applies its DAC threshold and defines the scope of the score distribution. To investigate this distinction, we next examined the likelihood of census tracts being designated as DACs using a logistic regression model, as specified by Eq. (2) in “Methods”, identifying the most influential EJ indicators affecting this likelihood. The results are presented in Fig. 4. First, our results confirm that Low Income (below 200% of the Federal Poverty Line) is the most influential factor in determining DAC designations in CEJST, followed by Workforce Development indicators—specifically Linguistic Isolation and Unemployment—which represent a strong emphasis on socioeconomic vulnerabilities.

Second, the impact of non-weighting design parameters on DAC outcomes is notable. A comparison between Fig. 4 and Fig. 3 reveals shifts in the most influential indicators in both the DOE and New York State tools. For DOE, although the represented EJ categories remain the same, the list of top indicators changes substantially. In particular, energy-related burdens carry significantly less weight in determining DAC designations, compared to their relative weights in the cumulative score calculation. This suggests that DOE’s use of state-wide percentile thresholds in its DAC screening process diminishes the relative importance of energy inequity, despite DOE’s methodology claiming equal weighting across all indicator categories. When energy burdens are not tightly correlated with socioeconomic disadvantages, communities with pressing energy challenges may be deprioritized. For example, households in remote areas with limited fuel access but incomes above the poverty line, or communities in high climate-risk regions experiencing recurring power interruptions from extreme weather, are less likely to be designated as DACs. By contrast, communities with high socioeconomic vulnerabilities and moderate energy burdens are more likely to qualify. Our findings imply a potentially unintended bias introduced by the percentile distribution choice in shaping the final DAC outcomes, a nuance that would be overlooked without this quantitative analysis. To illustrate these dynamics, we provided some geographic examples in Supplementary Discussion (and Supplementary Fig. 1), mapping DACs across census tracts that exemplify these contrasting burden profiles.

Turning to New York State’s tool, the rank of top indicators shifts slightly between Fig. 4 and Fig. 3. Though climate-related vulnerabilities remain a dominant EJ category for identifying DACs, some indicators, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and Diesel Truck and Bus Traffic, appear to have a greater effect on the likelihood of DAC designation than on the cumulative score’s percentile rank. This likely stems from the state’s emphasis on regional relative disadvantage through its use of separate percentile distributions (New York City versus the rest of the state) in addition to the state-wide score rank. Communities in rural areas face disproportionately high risks for COPD due to more smoking and solid fuel use33. And urban areas, due to their proximity to major sources of diesel emissions such as ports and heavily trafficked roads, experience elevated concentrations of diesel particulate matter34. Our results suggest that the use of regional percentile distributions gives greater weight to environmental issues that are acute in one part of the state but less common in another, thereby amplifying the role of geo-specific environmental burdens in DAC designation.

Discussion

EJSTs play a critical role in ensuring equitable community investment, as designated DAC status often determines eligibility for federal and state funding. This study reveals significant disparities in how different EJSTs identify DACs. These methodological variations are not necessarily problematic, as agencies and governments may reasonably prioritize different EJ indicators based on their programmatic focus and the specific populations. However, when the sources of DAC variation are not transparent, policymakers risk selecting tools whose implicit priorities are misaligned with their policy goals, potentially directing benefits away from the communities most in need. Agencies that develop EJSTs, despite being familiar with the methods, may not explicitly quantify how specific methodological choices affect DAC designations, a gap that can persist even through periodic tool updates. EJSTs are also frequently used or referenced by other government entities25,35, policymakers36, researchers37,38, and members of the public39,40. These users may rely on DAC designations without being fully aware of the assumptions, tradeoffs, and implicit priorities embedded in the tool’s design.

Our work contributes to the existing literature in two ways. First, whereas previous studies have typically focused on one or a few DAC and EJ indices16,41, our study adopts a broader approach by analyzing four EJSTs at both the federal and state levels in the United States. For states developing their own EJSTs, our analysis underscores the importance of tailoring tools to state-specific priorities. The case of New York demonstrates how state-level tools may better prioritize regional concerns, such as urban air pollution and rural health burdens, compared to federal tools. Another major difference between the federal EJSTs and New York’s tool is the use (or absence) of race and ethnicity indicators. Racial demographics are excluded from the methodologies in CEJST, DOE, and DOT’s tools, although CEJST’s user interface displays such information along with the DAC map. Whereas the percentages of Latino/a or Hispanic population, Black or African American population, and Asian population are listed under the Race and Ethnicity subcategory (under Characteristics and Health Vulnerabilities) in New York State’s tool. On the one hand, racial demographic indicators can directly address concerns of environmental racism, which may be less visible and more persistent than income inequality17,42. On the other hand, the omission of race from federal tools may reflect a strategic legal choice in light of the Supreme Court’s 2023 precedent on affirmative action, which has raised concerns about the use of race as a determining factor in government decision-making43. Developing EJSTs is a resource-intensive process, and not all states may have the capacity to create their own tools. In such cases, federal EJSTs can serve as a methodological reference or supplementary data source, even where they are not formally adopted as the official DAC designation tools. For example, Iowa’s 2024 NEVI development plan44 (before funding was paused) drew on CEJST and DOT’s tool to identify DACs for electric vehicle infrastructure investments. Delaware’s State Energy Plan maps energy DACs directly based on CEJST’s Energy Burden definition45. However, states should carefully evaluate whether national-scale EJSTs, like CEJST, align with their unique environmental and socioeconomic landscapes, and consider adding supplementary EJ indicators to accurately address environmental injustices and meet localized needs.

The second contribution of our study lies in its emphasis on quantitative analysis, addressing a gap in the existing literature. The DAC designation methods employed by various EJSTs differ significantly. Prior studies have largely focused on comparing these methodological components qualitatively, highlighting potential data gaps in EJ metrics and the need for developing suitable EJ metrics and inclusive processes of designating DACs17,46–48. However, these analyses often overlook the fact that different EJSTs, when evaluating cumulative impacts of multiple burdens, may apply varying weights to representative indicators, which can substantially influence DAC mapping outcomes. To date, there has been a lack of transparency in how each tool selects its weights and no quantitative analysis to examine and compare these weighting variations across EJSTs. Our study addresses this gap by estimating the relative importance of EJ indicators in determining a community’s DAC designation using linear and logistic regression models. Using the published versions of federal EJSTs from 2022 to 2024, we provide quantitative evidence that DAC outcomes from some EJSTs, especially the White House’s CEJST and DOE, place greater emphasis on socioeconomic burdens, such as low-income and poverty. This stands in contrast to New York’s DAC map (published in 2023), which explicitly assigns a higher weight to climate risk indicators. Understanding which EJ indicators receive greater or lesser weight in DAC screening is important because these methodological choices directly shape which communities become eligible for the policy benefits, such as targeted investments under Justice40, Weatherization Assistance Program49, Urban and Community Forestry Program50, and Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities program51. When energy burdens or climate-exposure indicators are down-weighted, communities facing high utility costs, energy insecurity, or repeated climate-driven outages may be less likely to qualify, while areas with stronger socioeconomic signals may be prioritized.

A key advantage of adopting a quantitative approach in our analysis is the ability to systematically identify and explain potential inconsistencies across different EJSTs, which may be obscured by the diverse methods these tools use to screen DACs. Given the wide range of EJ concerns and policy priorities across agencies and jurisdictions, it is expected that different tools would adopt varied approaches to evaluate cumulative impacts41,52. However, the distinct EJST methodological standards adopted make it difficult to identify the sources of DAC variation and their policy implications based on qualitative analysis alone. Clark, Millet, and Marshall53 revealed that inequality in NO2 pollution exposure is more pronounced than income inequality. This suggests that, under certain policy scenarios, an over-reliance on income-based indicators in EJSTs could inadvertently divert pollution control resources from achieving their intended outcomes, i.e., reducing environmental burden for the most vulnerable population.

Given the growing emphasis on climate resilience and equity, ongoing research and refinement of EJST methodologies and applications are crucial. Variation in indicator selection and weighting across tools can produce substantial differences in DAC designations, complicating efforts to evaluate their policy implications. For instance, consider a hypothetical but plausible scenario: a community qualifies for charging infrastructure subsidies under an energy department program but not for electric bus grants under a transportation department program, because the two agencies used different DAC designations. Since charging networks and electric bus deployment are complementary investments that generate greater value when implemented together, such a case could reduce the synergistic impact of coordinated EJ programs. While documenting the real-world extent of this risk would require developing a counterfactual beyond the scope of this study, our findings highlight the importance of transparency in EJST methodologies for enabling more coordinated and effective EJ policy. Future research should examine different use cases of EJSTs across programs, including whether policy synergy across complementary investments should be considered in EJST applications, and what benefits a more coordinated approach would yield.

Although the Justice40 Initiative and several environmental grant programs have been scaled back or discontinued in 202554, the need for equitable resource distribution remains. State and local governments now carry greater responsibility for protecting vulnerable communities, and within the context of U.S. federalism, are well-placed to implement state-level structures. Transparent DAC screening tools are essential for guiding investments, land-use decisions, and resilience planning in the absence of consistent federal support. While this study focuses on New York’s DAC map as a state-level case, many state EJSTs remain actively used. As a pioneer state that established its own EJST before Justice40, California’s SB 535 DAC Designation governs the distribution of state Cap-and-Trade proceeds8. Many states that developed EJSTs after Justice40 have also integrated these tools into public decision-making, such as permitting requirements and grant eligibility determinations. For example, Connecticut’s DEEP Climate Resilience Fund55 lists EJ community status as an explicit review criterion and qualifies projects for enhanced matching fund percentages. PennEnviroScreen56 is used to support the implementation of the Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection (DEP)’s EJ Policy (effective January 3, 2026). New Jersey’s EJ Law57 allows mandatory permit denials if the proposed facility will adversely affect overburdened communities identified by the state’s EJMAP58 and applies stricter conditions for renewals and expansions of current facilities. These examples illustrate that state EJSTs have become institutionalized instruments of environmental policy, thus making it important to systematically analyze how their methodological choices shape the distribution of policy benefits.

The state EJSTs exhibit methodologically distinct screening criteria. These criteria, despite carrying direct consequences for the final DAC outcomes, are rarely compared or evaluated across tools. As our study shows, the regional approach adopted by New York adjusts indicator weights to favor geo-specific environmental burdens. Other state EJSTs that select DACs based on percentile scores have adopted different approaches. California’s SB 535 Designation8 not only filters DACs for the top 25% total score but also considers alternative criteria based on data unavailability and historical DAC status. Pennsylvania selects the top 20% based on scores computed by PennEnviroScreen56, and the developers suggest that future updates may consider factors introducing differentiated regional treatments, such as relative weighting of indicators, hyperlocal data, and gentrification. These varied approaches reflect the underlying policy values about how equity should be defined for the affected communities. Therefore, policymakers at state and local agencies should be aware of the methodological disparities of the EJSTs and the resulting jurisdictional variations in DAC mapping outcomes. Selecting the appropriate tool should be based on alignment with specific policy goals and the EJ burdens relevant to the targeted region or population. This approach would promote a more intentional and effective rollout of EJ programs.

Transparent and data-driven methodologies are needed to improve the consistency and accountability of these screening tools. For example, research developing a more explicit definition of the weighting scheme, where weights are tied to distributional effects of policies, would provide clearer decision rationales for agencies selecting or designing EJSTs. We encourage policymakers across jurisdictions to increase transparency in EJST development, both to support institutions and states considering whether to adopt or adapt existing tools, and to advance public understanding and scholarly analysis of policy development.

In conclusion, this research provides an important step toward understanding the complexities of EJ screening in the United States. The comparative analysis of EJST methodologies and outcomes reveals both the potential and the limitations of current tools. As the responsibility for environmental justice increasingly shifts to state and local governments, it is essential that DAC screening tools are designed to reflect diverse community needs and support effective and equitable policy implementation. Future research should build on this foundation by examining a broader range of state EJSTs, exploring how design differences across jurisdictions translate into DAC designation outcomes and their downstream policy implications.

Methods

Data development

To facilitate the analysis of DAC designations across EJSTs, we collected data from each EJST’s online publication (see published version described in Supplementary Table 1). These data include the census-tract level DAC designations for each tool as well as values for all the independent variables used in the EJST algorithms. We reconciled census tract identifiers across different census years—CEJST (2010 census map), DOT (2020 census map), DOE, and New York State (2019 census geography for American Community Survey). A key challenge was aligning census tract boundaries between the 2010 and 2020 census maps. To address this, we utilized the National Historical Geographic Information System (NHGIS) geographic crosswalk files provided by IPUMS (https://www.nhgis.org/geographic-crosswalks). These crosswalks match census tracts from different map years based on the proportion of overlapping land area, selecting the census geographic identifier (GEOID) from the area that accounts for the highest proportion. This reconciliation process allowed for the integration of data from multiple EJSTs and ensured compatibility in our quantitative analysis for comparing DAC mapping outcomes. Census tracts with no land areas or population were excluded from the study, which resulted in 84,094 tracts nationally and 5393 tracts within New York State. Values in figures and tables are calculated based on the 2020 census map.

We compiled a list of indicators and their categories for all EJSTs analyzed in this study (Supplementary Data 1). Each indicator was assigned to an EJ category and a subcategory based on descriptions in the EJSTs’ technical documentation. For indicators associated with the same core concept across different tools, such as low income and poverty, we reconciled the names and added a short description to facilitate comparison. It is worth noting that core EJ concepts related to economic vulnerability are not always operationalized consistently across EJSTs, even when indicators share similar names. For example, while Low Income (below 200% FPL) is consistently defined across CEJST, DOE, and DOT, indicators related to area median income (AMI) differ. CEJST classifies a tract as burdened if its income relative to AMI falls within the 90th percentile in reverse rank of burden nationally, whereas New York’s DAC map applies a fixed threshold of 80% of AMI for computing the low-income population share. These naming and operational differences do not affect our regression analysis, which treats each EJST’s indicators independently within their own tool rather than assuming equivalence across tools.

Regression analysis

We utilized linear regression for the cumulative scores (in percentile rank) and logistic regression for binary DAC designations to identify the relative importance of EJ indicators in DAC screening across different EJSTs. Due to the special treatment of tribal lands and indigenous communities in EJSTs, we exclude these census tracts from the regression analysis. To address the issue of multicollinearity among the indicators, we applied post-LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression, in which LASSO optimizes variable selection while minimizing overfitting before estimating the linear and logistic regression models.

LASSO regression. We performed cross-validation (CV) to select the optimal penalty tuning parameter (λ) that minimizes the CV error. The final value of λ is computed by taking the average of ten CV iterations. Our main results in Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 are based on error-minimizing λ. As a sensitivity analysis, we also estimated the models using λ within one standard error of the minimum. This provides a more conservative model, and the results are included in Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5.
