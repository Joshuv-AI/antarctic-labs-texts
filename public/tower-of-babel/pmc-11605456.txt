Balancing safety and efficacy of low-molecular-weight heparins in neonates: a systematic review
Authors: Marieke Verweij, Mirjam M. van Weissenbruch, Irene L.M. Klaassen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The rising incidence of venous thromboembolism (VTE) in neonates has led to increased use of low-molecular-weight heparins (LMWHs), but optimal dosages remain uncertain. A serious adverse effect of LMWHs is major bleeding. Given the vulnerability of neonates to major bleeding, we aimed to review therapeutic and prophylactic LMWH dosages to achieve target anti-factor Xa ranges of 0.5 and 1.0 U/mL and 0.1 and 0.4 U/mL, respectively. Our secondary aim was to assess the safety and efficacy of LMWHs in neonates. A systematic review of all published studies between 1996 and 2023 that pertained to the dosing, safety, or efficacy of LMWH in preterm and term neonates. Studies were identified through the Medline database. Data on LMWH dosages, bleeding events, resolution and recurrence, and anti-factor Xa levels were analyzed. A total of 38 studies involving 1145 neonates were included. To achieve a therapeutic or prophylactic target range, weight-adjusted initial dosages of LMWH had to be increased by 21% particularly in premature neonates. During therapeutic therapy, major bleeding occurred in 4.1% and minor bleeding in 7.1%. During prophylactic therapy, 11.4% experienced major bleeding and 17.1% minor bleeding. With therapeutic dosages, 55.8% achieved complete VTE resolution. Additionally, 68.5% of neonates initially failed to achieve therapeutic anti-factor Xa levels, persisting in 29.4% despite dose adjustments. A higher initial therapeutic dosage of LMWHs may be needed in neonates. In addition, the patient’s gestational age must be considered in the dosing strategy to optimize outcomes. Although this must be weighed against bleeding risk at an individual level.

Introduction

In the past decade, there has been a worrisome increase in the prevalence of venous thromboembolism (VTE) in neonates. The prevalence among hospitalized children has increased approximately 45% [1,2]. The growing number of VTE cases in neonates is attributed to higher survival rates of premature and critically ill infants, increased use of central venous catheters, and advancements in diagnostic practices [3,4]. In addition, the extended use of total parenteral nutrition in neonates and the continued need for central venous catheters even after a VTE have highlighted the necessity for preventive strategies for VTE [5].

Treatment and prevention of VTE can be crucial for both short-term and long-term outcomes. In neonates, treatment of choice are still low-molecular-weight heparins (LMWHs) in the majority of cases, because of their predictable pharmacokinetics, more predictable dose response, and quick onset of action [6,7].

In adults, LMWH use has proven to be safe and effective [8,9]. However, the challenge arises when dealing with neonates. The dosage of LMWH for neonates is often based on data obtained from studies conducted on adults. However, the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles in neonates significantly differ from those in adults. Neonates have a unique body composition, characterized by a higher proportion of total body water and lower levels of fat tissue than adults, which affects drug distribution [10,11]. Additionally, neonatal liver function is significantly underdeveloped in comparison with that of adults. These factors influence drug metabolism, making neonates a distinct population for whom adult data cannot be directly extrapolated.

Side effects of LMWHs are another challenge in neonates. Bleeding is the most common side effect of LMWH, but neonates are already very vulnerable to bleeding, especially intraventricular hemorrhage [12,13]. Use of anticoagulant therapy increases the bleeding risk. Therefore, dosing LMWHs in neonates is a subtle balance between safety and efficacy.

Understanding the optimal and well-tolerated dosages, safety profiles, and best clinical outcomes of therapeutic or prophylactic LMWH use in neonates is essential for ensuring the best care. However, studies on this topic are limited, and the neonatal population is small. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to provide insights into the optimum therapeutic or prophylactic dosage, safety and efficacy of LMWHs in neonates.

Methods

The systematic review was carried out according to the PRISMA guidelines [14]. To investigators (M.V. and I.L.M.K.) screened all published studies until September 2023 that evaluated dosing, safety, or efficacy of LMWH in neonates for relevancy.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) 1 type of LMWH was used for therapeutic treatment of VTE or thromboprophylaxis; (2) data on dosing, safety, or effectiveness were reported; (3) in case of therapeutic LMWH treatment, the diagnosis of VTE was confirmed through radiographic imaging; and (4) preterm and term neonates <2 months postnatal were included.

Studies were excluded if other anticoagulants were administered simultaneously, if they did not report any key relevant to this review, if the full-text was not accessible, or if the articles were not written in English. The search strategy was developed and executed in collaboration with an experienced librarian. Additionally, reference lists were examined for any additional relevant studies.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were the average therapeutic and prophylactic LMWH dosages of all types of LMWH to achieve the therapeutic or prophylactic target range, for premature neonates <37 weeks, and full-term neonates <2 months.

Safety outcomes were the prevalence of major bleeding, clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding or minor bleeding for both therapeutic and prophylactic LMWH. Efficacy outcomes were evaluated based on thrombus resolution, attainment of therapeutic anti-factor Xa levels, and recurrence of VTE for therapeutic LMWH and attainment of prophylactic anti-factor Xa levels development of new VTE for prophylactic LMWH.

Definitions

Recurrent VTE was defined as confirmed VTE detected through imaging triggered by symptoms suggestive of VTE. These symptoms encompassed leg pain, swelling, or discoloration, unexplained thrombocytopenia, and catheter dysfunction.

Premature neonates were defined as <37 weeks’ gestational age and full-term neonates were defined as ≥37 weeks’ gestational age until <2 months. This definition was chosen based on the cutoff criteria established in the relevant literature on this topic.

Therapeutic anti-factor Xa (TTR) levels were defined as anti-factor Xa activity ranging from 0.5 to 1.0 units/mL in a sample obtained 4 to 6 hours after the subcutaneous administration of LMWH, either once or twice daily. Adequate prophylaxis for achieving anti-factor Xa levels was defined as an anti-factor Xa activity falling between 0.1 and 0.4 units/mL, measured within 4 to 6 hours following subcutaneous administration [5].

To create as uniform an outcome as possible, we aimed to categorize all bleeding events based on the criteria established by the Perinatal and Paediatric Haemostasis Subcommittee of the Scientific and Standardization Committee of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) [15]. Some studies categorized bleeding events according to these criteria. In studies where bleeding complications were not classified according to the ISTH criteria, but information on the site and severity of bleeding was available, 2 reviewers categorized them according to those criteria (M.V. and I.L.M.K). Bleeding events were classified as major, nonmajor, or minor as follows: major bleeding was a fatal or clinically evident bleeding, characterized by a decrease in hemoglobin of at least 20 g/L in a 24-hour period, occurring in regions such as retroperitoneal, pulmonary, intracranial, or otherwise involved the central nervous system, or requiring surgical intervention in an operating room. Nonmajor bleeding was defined as overt bleeding necessitating the administration of a blood product and not directly attributable to the patient’s underlying medical condition, as well as bleeding requiring medical or surgical intervention to restore hemostasis outside of an operation room. All other bleedings were categorized as minor. Thrombus resolution was defined as partial or complete resolution, confirmed by radiographic imaging.

Study assessment

The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess the quality of the cohort studies. All studies were independently reviewed by 2 authors (M.V. and I.L.M.K). If the assessment was not consistent, an independent third reviewer was consulted.

Data collection and synthesis

Data collection included the following variables: publication date, research design, number of patients, type of LMWH, final LMWH dosage needed to achieve therapeutic or prophylactic anti-factor Xa levels, rates in reaching therapeutic or prophylactic anti-factor Xa targets level, number of dose adjustments, the prevalence and severity of bleeding complications, thrombus resolution, follow-up duration, and occurrences of recurrent VTE. Two reviewers (M.V. and I.L.M.K.) verified the accuracy of data extractions.

Statistical analysis

Therapeutic and prophylactic dosage responses for specific types of LMWH were determined by calculating the average dosage administered to premature neonates (<37 weeks’ gestation) and full-term neonates (<2 months) based on the mean doses reported in the selected studies. The prevalence rates of achieving the targeted anti-factor Xa levels for therapeutic or prophylactic purposes were estimated by dividing the number of successful cases by the total number of cases. Weighted averages (±SD) were computed by multiplying each data point by its corresponding weight, determined by sample size, and then dividing the sum of these weighted products by the total of all weights.

Safety outcomes were assessed by calculating the percentages of major bleeding, clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding, and minor bleeding complications associated with therapeutic or prophylactic LMWH. This was done by dividing the number of cases experiencing bleeding complications by the total number of reported patients. The occurrences of thrombus resolution and recurrent VTE were determined by dividing the number of cases exhibiting thrombus resolution or recurrent VTE by the total number of reported patients. All outcomes were expressed as means along with 95% CIs. IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0.1 for Windows was used for analyses.

Another 20 articles were included from references. Subsequently, 19 studies were eliminated, leaving 38 studies, representing 1145 patients to be included in this review (Figure). Safety

A total of 31 studies, including includes 1,063 neonates, reported bleeding events in neonates (Supplementary Table 3). Range of the NOS score for these studies was 4 to 8. Overall, the quality was rated as good in 12 studies and fair in 19 studies (Supplementary Table 2).

Major bleeding events were observed in 44 cases (4.1%). The most common reported bleeding sites were intracranial hemorrhage (n = 6, 13.0%), thigh hematomas (n = 2, 4.3%), adrenal hemorrhage (n = 1, 2.2%), and gastrointestinal bleeding (n = 1, 2.2%). In 32 cases, the bleeding site was not specified [19,20,23,30,31,34,35,38,39,41,46,47,49]. Nonmajor bleeding events occurred in 3 neonates (0.3%) [46,47,49]. Minor bleeding episodes were documented in 76 neonates (7.1%). It was not specified if the bleeding events occurred in premature or full-term neonates [18,21,27,28,[31], [32], [33], [34], [35],39,45,[47], [48], [49]].

Eleven studies, comprising 42 patients, measured anti-Xa levels at the time of bleeding. No supratherapeutic were observed within this subcohort. One major bleeding event was reported when the anti-Xa level was subtherapeutic. Anti-Xa Levels were measured in 22 out of 44 cases of major bleeding, 2 out of 3 cases of nonmajor bleeding, and 18 out of 76 cases of minor bleeding events [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23],25,[27], [28], [29], [30],[32], [33], [34], [35], [36],38,39,41,[45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52]].

Efficacy

Efficacy of LMWH treatment was reported in 24 studies, encompassing 858 neonates. Range of the NOS score for these studies was 4 to 6. In summary, 8 studies were rated as good quality, while the remaining 16 studies were rated as fair quality (Supplementary Table 2).

Radiographic evaluation was conducted in all 24 studies. Median time of follow-up was 3 months (range, 1-3 months). In 16 studies, duration of follow-up was not specified. Overall, 55.8% (range, 2.6%-100%) of neonates experienced complete VTE resolution. Partial resolution occurred in 19.6% (range, 0%-72.2%) and 10.1% (range, 0%-40%) failed thrombus resolution [16,18,19,20,22,23,28,[30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35],39,40,[45], [46], [47],49,53].

Anti-factor Xa levels were examined in 23 studies, representing 805 neonates. Range of the NOS score for these studies was 4 to 7. Overall, the quality was rated as good in 9 studies and fair in 14 studies (Supplementary Table 2).

Initially, 29.4% of the neonates had anti-factor Xa levels within the therapeutic range. A majority of 68.5%, fell below the therapeutic range. Supratherapeutic levels were found in 5.04%. After dose adjustments of LMWH in patients with subtherapeutic or supratherapeutic anti-factor Xa levels, 56.23% achieved anti-factor Xa levels within the therapeutic range, while 29.4% still did not reach therapeutic anti-factor Xa levels. Supratherapeutic levels were found in 7.65% after dose adjustments [[16], [17], [18],20,24,25,27,28,[31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39],41,44,47,48,50]. Sixteen studies reported the time required to reach the therapeutic target range, with an average time of 5.6 days to attain the target therapeutic LMWH level. Range of the NOS score for these studies was 4 to 7. In summary, 8 studies were rated as good quality, whereas the other 5 studies were rated as fair quality (range, 2-32 days) (Supplementary Table 2) [16,17,20,27,31,32,34,35,39,41,43,46,48].

Recurrent VTE

Recurrent VTE was reported in 16 studies, encompassing 637 neonates. Recurrent VTE occurred in 2.3% (range, 0%-10%). Range of the NOS score for these studies was 5 to 6. To summarize, the quality was rated as good in 5 studies and fair in 11 studies (Supplementary Table 2). All patients used therapeutic anticoagulant therapy at the time of recurrence [16,[18], [19], [20],22,25,31,32,34,35,38,40,45,46,48].

One retrospective study by Hofmann et al. [28] reported a single neonate who received subcutaneous administration. This study was rated as fair quality. The final prophylactic dose administered was 75 IU/kg/24 hours [28].

Safety

Bleeding events were reported in 6 studies, including 35 neonates (Supplementary Table S4). The range of the NOS score for these studies was 4 to 6. In summary, 2 studies were rated as good quality, whereas the other 4 studies were rated as fair quality (Supplementary Table S2). Minor bleeding was reported in 2 neonates (5.7%) [33]. In all cases, the anti-factor Xa level was not supratherapeutic. No major bleeding and nonmajor bleeding events were reported [23,28,33,36,39,45].

Efficacy

A total of 4 studies, encompassing 27 neonates, reported the efficacy of thromboprophylaxis. The range of the NOS score for 1 study was 7, which was rated as good. For the 3 remaining studies, the NOS score was 5, which was rated as fair (Supplementary Table S2). Thrombus resolution or recurrent VTE was not documented in 55.6% of the cases. Overall, 8 of the 27 (29.6%) neonates had complete VTE resolution, while 1 of the 27 (3.7%) experienced partial resolution. Another 3 of the 27 (11.1%) encountered recurrent VTE [28,33,39,45].

Discussion

This review suggests that neonates may need a higher weight-adjusted dosages of all types of LMWH than initially administered, for both therapeutic and prophylactic purposes. Overall, half of the patients had complete thrombus resolution.

The dosage of LMWH for neonates is initially derived from adult data, failing to account for the unique physiologic characteristics of neonates. With the current therapeutic weight-adjusted dosages of LMWH, approximately 70% of the neonates did not meet the therapeutic range. Even more worryingly, 30% never reached the therapeutic target range. Premature neonates require dosage 153% higher and full-term neonates need dosage 120.7% higher than the initial dosage to achieve therapeutic anti-factor Xa levels. This aligns with an enoxaparin study by Malowany et al. [54], which suggested that the weight-adjusted starting dose of enoxaparin in neonates should be increased. Studies on prophylactic LMWH are sparse. However, in our review, studies with enoxaparin suggest that these neonates may need a higher dosage than initially recommended.

A critical aspect of LMWH therapy in neonates is the associated bleeding risk. The prevalence of major bleeding was low during therapeutic LMWH administration. Two other studies, comprising 447 neonates, had similar findings. The anti-factor Xa levels at time of the bleeding event was unknown in these studies [55,56]. Remarkably, this review showed no bleeding events during supratherapeutic anti-factor Xa levels. This might suggest other factors are involved during the bleeding events. Although this must be interpreted with great caution, as in the majority as patients with bleeding events anti-Xa levels at time of bleeding were unknown. Administration of prophylactic LMWH showed an increased major bleeding risk of 11.4%. However, this is probably an overestimate due to the small patient population of the studies. Despite these limitations, these findings emphasize the importance of weighing the benefits of anticoagulant therapy against the potential bleeding risks in this vulnerable population. Careful monitoring and individualized assessment are crucial to balance the potential advantages of LMWH with the associated risks.

The recurrent VTE rate during therapeutic LMWH use was low in this review. During prophylactic LMWH use, 11% of the neonates developed VTE despite therapy. This is higher than expected. Most likely, high bleeding percentage reflects a selection bias in this very small population.

This study has several strengths. First, this study provides a complete oversight of dosage, safety, and efficacy in relation to anti-factor Xa levels for both therapeutic and prophylactic LMWH use in neonates. In addition, this systematic review included a large number of patients, which is significant given that studies in neonates are typically small, making data representative of the neonatal population. This review also recognizes the importance of distinguishing between preterm and full-term neonates, as the physiologic differences between these groups can significantly impact drug responses and safety. This distinction allows for more tailored LMWH therapy for neonates.

It is also essential to acknowledge the limitations of this review. A notable limitation is the small number of patients in subanalyses, which may introduce biases and overestimation of outcomes, thereby reducing the overall quality of evidence. Furthermore, studies over the past 27 years were included. It is imaginable that starting doses may have changed over time, potentially influencing the outcomes. Finally, not all studies standardized bleeding categories, making it challenging to compare and synthesize the data. To address this, we categorized the bleeding events in a standardized manner to facilitate accurate analysis.

Conclusion

A higher initial therapeutic dosage of LMWHs may be needed in neonates. In addition, the patient’s gestational age must be considered in the dosing strategy to optimize outcomes. However, this must be weighed against bleeding risk at an individual level.
