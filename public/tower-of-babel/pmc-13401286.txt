Two contrasting cases of adrenal insufficiency in adults: a case report
Authors: Yingqi Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdrenal insufficiency (AI) is a clinical syndrome characterized by insufficient secretion of glucocorticoids and/or mineralocorticoids. The incidence of AI is low, and its clinical manifestations are non-specific, including fatigue, generalized weakness, nausea, and weight loss. Consequently, the condition can easily be misdiagnosed or overlooked. If not treated promptly, it may progress to a life-threatening adrenal crisis. This paper reports one case of primary and one case of secondary adrenal insufficiency, aiming to enhance clinical vigilance regarding this disease through a comparative case analysis.Case presentationBoth patients were of East Asian descent, comprising a 67-year-old female and an 80-year-old male. They were hospitalized with long-standing histories of recurrent, unexplained electrolyte disturbances, anorexia, nausea, and vomiting. Subsequent investigations revealed the following: in Patient 1, the serum cortisol level was markedly decreased, while the adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) level was compensatorily elevated to an extreme degree. Combined with imaging studies that identified bilateral adrenal calcifications, a diagnosis of primary adrenal insufficiency (potentially triggered by tuberculosis) was ultimately established. In contrast, Patient 2 was found to have significantly low levels of both serum cortisol and ACTH. A pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a sellar mass, leading to a diagnosis of secondary adrenal insufficiency. Following the initiation of glucocorticoid replacement therapy, the clinical symptoms and electrolyte abnormalities in both patients improved rapidly. Long-term follow-up indicated a favorable prognosis.ConclusionThese cases aim to highlight that adrenal insufficiency should be considered in the differential diagnosis for patients presenting with non-specific symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, fatigue, hypotension, or refractory electrolyte imbalances. Relevant diagnostic tests should be promptly completed to establish the etiology and initiate treatment as early as possible.

Introduction

Adrenal insufficiency represents a spectrum of disorders characterized by the inadequate production of glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, or adrenal androgens. It is fundamentally classified into primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI), resulting from the destruction of the adrenal cortex, and secondary adrenal insufficiency (SAI), caused by dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary signaling cascade [1, 2]. While adrenal insufficiency (AI) is relatively rare, with an estimated prevalence of 100–140 cases per million for PAI and 150–280 per million for SAI [3], it poses a significant diagnostic challenge. The clinical presentation is notoriously insidious and non-specific; patients frequently present with vague constitutional symptoms such as chronic fatigue, nausea, vomiting, and weight loss, which mimic common gastrointestinal or psychiatric disorders [4].

Consequently, the diagnosis is often delayed. Bleicken et al. reported that nearly 50% of patients with PAI consult at least three physicians before a correct diagnosis is established [5, 6]. This diagnostic gap is perilous, as untreated AI can rapidly deteriorate into an adrenal crisis, a medical emergency associated with high mortality rates [7]. Furthermore, the etiology of AI varies geographically and demographically. While autoimmune adrenalitis is the predominant cause of PAI in Western populations, infectious etiologies—particularly tuberculosis—remain a critical consideration in developing regions and specific patient cohorts [8, 9]. Conversely, SAI is frequently associated with pituitary adenomas or the aftermath of their treatment.

In this report, we present two cases of adrenal insufficiency in elderly patients of East Asian descent: one case of PAI attributed to adrenal tuberculosis and one case of SAI secondary to a pituitary macroadenoma. Both patients presented primarily with refractory gastrointestinal symptoms and electrolyte disturbances, specifically hyponatremia, masking the underlying endocrine pathology. By comparing the clinical trajectories, biochemical profiles, and imaging findings of these cases, we aim to highlight the necessity of including adrenal function assessment in the differential diagnosis of unexplained hyponatremia and persistent nausea to prevent life-threatening complications.

Clinical presentation

Case 1: primary adrenal insufficiency

A 67-year-old East Asian female patient was admitted to the nephrology department due to persistent anorexia, nausea, and vomiting recurring over nine months, with significant worsening within seven days. Her medical history indicated multiple previous hospitalizations for hypotension, hyponatremia, hypochloremia, and hyperkalemia, including one instance of hemodialysis for severe hyperkalemia. An unintentional weight loss of approximately 10 kg over the past six months was reported. A history of pulmonary tuberculosis, diagnosed and treated 40 years prior, was noted, but no regular follow-up had been maintained. A known allergy to sulfonamide drugs was documented. Physical examination revealed a blood pressure of 90/52 mmHg, a body temperature of 37.0 °C, lethargy, and generalized weakness. Notably, hyperpigmentation of the lips and oral mucosa was observed. Pulmonary auscultation detected coarse breath sounds with fine crackles; no peripheral edema was present.

Laboratory investigations revealed severe hyponatremia (Na+ 111 mmol/L), hypochloremia (Cl− 90 mmol/L), hyperkalemia (K+ 7.6 mmol/L), and elevated serum creatinine (186 μmol/L). The patient's thyroid function test indicated an elevated TSH level (TSH: 21.287 uIU/mL), with normal FT3 and FT4 levels, suggesting subclinical hypothyroidism, but the patient’s Thyroglobulin Antibody (TG-Ab) levels were abnormally elevated, which indicated the presence of autoimmune thyroid disease. The patient’s Coombs test was positive. Initially, the patient was treated for suspected bacterial pneumonia and hyponatremia. Despite a three-day course of antibiotics and intravenous sodium supplementation, her symptoms persisted, and serum sodium only rose to 125 mmol/L. Due to suspicion of primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI), an endocrinology consultation was requested. The subsequent endocrine evaluation was pivotal. An ACTH stimulation test was performed. Urinary cortisol and 17-hydroxycorticosteroid levels were low, while the plasma renin level was elevated; urinary aldosterone was within the normal range. A markedly low morning (8:00 AM) serum cortisol level (1.7 μg/dL; reference range: 6.02–18.4 μg/dL) with a loss of diurnal rhythm was documented. In contrast, the plasma ACTH level was extremely elevated at 922 pg/mL (reference range: 7.2–63.3 pg/mL). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of Primary Adrenal Insufficiency. Intravenous hydrocortisone (50 mg every 8 hours) was immediately initiated. Her symptoms improved significantly, and electrolyte levels normalized. Hydrocortisone was gradually tapered and transitioned to oral prednisone (7.5 mg daily). Thyroid hormones accelerate the clearance of glucocorticoids by the liver; priority administration of thyroid hormones may induce an adrenal crisis. Therefore, the patient should first receive glucocorticoid supplementation, and then take 50 μg of Euthyrox orally daily after their condition stabilizes. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid suggested Pneumocystis jirovecii (PJP) infection. Given the patient's sulfonamide allergy, antifungal therapy with caspofungin was concurrently administered following a multidisciplinary consultation. Case 2: secondary adrenal insufficiency

An 80-year-old East Asian male patient was admitted with a one-month history of persistent anorexia, nausea, and vomiting. He had been previously hospitalized at a local medical institution for hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and renal insufficiency. The patient reported a 10 kg weight loss over the past month. His past medical history was otherwise unremarkable. Upon admission, his blood pressure was 99/64 mmHg; he was conscious but exhibited lethargy and reduced verbal output. Physical examination revealed no significant abnormalities. No abnormal pigmentation was observed, breath sounds were clear bilaterally without audible crackles or wheezing, and there was no peripheral edema.

Laboratory findings included hyponatremia (Na+ 126 mmol/L), hyperkalemia (K+ 6.0 mmol/L), and elevated serum creatinine (248 μmol/L) (see Fig. 4), The patient had mild hyperkalemia upon admission, which was considered to be associated with acute kidney injury caused by prolonged anorexia, nausea and vomiting prior to admission. The patient's serum potassium quickly returned to normal after treatment. The hormonal assessment was diagnostic. An ACTH stimulation test was also performed, which revealed an extremely low morning (8:00 AM) cortisol level (0.5 μg/dL) and an inappropriately low ACTH level (7.0 pg/mL). The thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level was elevated (4.875 uIU/mL). Both free T4 (9.26 pmol/L) and free T3 (2.91 pmol/L) were decreased. Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) was elevated (32.74 mIU/mL), as was the prolactin (PRL) level (35.35 ng/mL). This “low-low” profile of cortisol and ACTH was consistent with secondary adrenal insufficiency (SAI). A subsequent pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a sellar mass, likely a pituitary adenoma (see Fig. 3). The patient presented with no symptoms such as acromegaly, cutaneous striae, or central obesity, relevant pituitary hormone tests were completed, showing elevated FSH and PRL levels while the other indicators were normal; therefore, the patient’s pituitary space-occupying lesion is highly suspected to be a pituitary macroadenoma. The patient presented with a mild elevation of TSH, along with decreased FT3 and FT4 levels, both Thyroid peroxidase autoantibodies (TPO-Ab) and TG-Ab tests turned out negative, leading to the diagnosis of secondary central hypothyroidism. Combined with the patient’s imaging findings, this condition was considered to be associated with the patient’s intrinsic pituitary tumor. The pituitary tumor damages pituitary cells and affects TSH secretion via the hypothalamic–pituitary-thyroid axis. Meanwhile, the elevated TSH may abnormally activate gonadal receptors on pituitary cells through a molecular mimicry mechanism, which indirectly enhances the biological effects of FSH and PRL, thus resulting in the increased levels of FSH and PRL. Following consultations with endocrinology and neurosurgery, the patient was started on oral prednisone (10 mg daily). His appetite returned, nausea and vomiting resolved, and electrolyte levels normalized rapidly (see Fig. 4). Discussion

Adrenal insufficiency (AI) is defined as a clinical syndrome resulting from impaired synthesis and release of adrenal corticosteroids [1–3], which is closely associated with the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. Based on its etiology, AI is classified into primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI), secondary adrenal insufficiency (SAI), and iatrogenic AI. As a rare endocrine disorder, AI has a low overall incidence, an insidious onset, and its prevalence and causative factors exhibit geographical variation.

Primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI), also known as Addison's disease, is caused by direct damage to the adrenal cortex. It manifests as a deficiency in one or more hormones produced by the adrenal cortex, including glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, and androgens. The etiology of PAI is diverse, encompassing autoimmune diseases, infectious diseases, connective tissue disorders, malignancies, and surgical resection of adrenal tissue [4]. In China, infections, particularly tuberculosis, represent the leading cause of PAI [5]. Mycobacterium tuberculosis disseminates to the adrenal glands via the bloodstream or lymphatic system, typically resulting in bilateral involvement; unilateral involvement is relatively uncommon. Imaging often reveals adrenal calcifications, which are associated with various adrenal conditions including adrenal hemorrhage, hemangioma, pheochromocytoma, adrenocortical carcinoma, neuroblastoma, and adrenal tuberculosis. These calcifications are secondary to the underlying disease process, whereas primary adrenal calcifications without associated adrenal pathology are extremely rare in clinical practice. Currently, no specific symptoms are pathognomonic for adrenal calcifications. Diagnosis relies on imaging studies (for example, ultrasound, CT, MRI), laboratory tests (hormonal level assays), and biopsy when necessary, with a definitive diagnosis requiring comprehensive evaluation based on clinical presentation and etiology. In contrast, in Europe, the predominant cause of PAI is autoimmune dysregulation, followed by tuberculosis; infections with Mycobacterium TuBerculosis (MTB), Human Immunodefi ciency Virus (HIV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), and fungi also constitute common etiological factors for PAI. Secondary adrenal insufficiency (SAI) results from impaired production of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) due to pathologies affecting the pituitary gland or hypothalamus. Etiologies include pituitary AI, insufficient secretion of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) from the hypothalamus, and suppression of hypothalamic and pituitary function caused by chronically elevated levels of exogenous glucocorticoids [1, 6]. The diagnosis and etiological differentiation of AI should be based on a comprehensive evaluation incorporating medical history, clinical manifestations, laboratory investigations, and functional tests [7]. The most critical diagnostic measures involve the assessment of morning (8:00 AM) serum cortisol, plasma ACTH, the ACTH stimulation test, and imaging studies. In PAI patients, ACTH levels are significantly elevated, and levels of pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) and plasma renin activity (PRA) are increased, while serum cortisol and aldosterone are subnormal. Consequently, patients with PAI typically present with generalized hyperpigmentation. In contrast, SAI patients exhibit decreased ACTH concentrations, low or low-normal cortisol levels, and normal aldosterone levels.

The cornerstone of treatment for both PAI and SAI is lifelong adrenal hormone replacement therapy. Patients with primary AI require replacement of both glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids. Patients with ACTH deficiency due to pituitary or hypothalamic dysfunction, often resulting from exogenous steroid use, typically require only glucocorticoid replacement. Similarly, patients with secondary or tertiary AI, possessing an intact mineralocorticoid production pathway, require solely glucocorticoid replacement. In clinical practice, if a patient presents with life-threatening clinical manifestations such as coma, refractory hypotension, or severe electrolyte disturbance, adrenal insufficiency or adrenal crisis should be highly suspected. After ruling out contraindications including severe infection, hormone replacement therapy should be initiated as early as possible. Once the patient's condition stabilizes, the therapy should be switched to the oral maintenance dose. Long-term management of AI patients is crucial. Comprehensive patient education is imperative, instructing them to double their glucocorticoid dosage during febrile illness, infection, surgery, or other stressors to prevent life-threatening adrenal crisis, with subsequent tapering as the condition resolves.

This article aims to enhance awareness of this condition through a discussion of the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment of AI. The clinical manifestations of AI are often non-specific, and diagnosis can be challenging. When patients present with persistent, unexplained symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, fatigue, hypotension, or hyponatremia [8, 9], AI should be highly suspected. Measurements of serum cortisol and ACTH, along with dynamic tests like the ACTH stimulation test, should be promptly performed to establish an early diagnosis, initiate timely treatment, and reduce disease-associated mortality.

Conclusions

These two cases demonstrate that adrenal insufficiency must be highly suspected in patients presenting with non-specific constitutional and gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly when accompanied by persistent hypotension and electrolyte disturbances. The measurement of morning serum cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) levels, combined with the performance of an ACTH stimulation test, constitutes the most critical step for confirming the diagnosis and differentiating between primary and secondary etiologies, thereby providing a definitive direction for subsequent management.
