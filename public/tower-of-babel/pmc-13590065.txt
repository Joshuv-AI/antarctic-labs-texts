Seasonal feed deficits and low nutritional quality of major feed resources affect ruminant production in Tigray, Northern Ethiopia
Authors: Abraha Gebru, Negasi Solomon, Mitiku Haile, Yirga Gufi, Kidane Gidey, Yemane Adane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Crop residues dominate livestock feed, with seasonal shifts in availability.•Midlands show higher dry matter and nutrient supply than lowlands.•Estimated DM supply met approximately 70.5% of livestock maintenance requirements.•Available feed resources showed a 14.7% digestible crude protein deficit.•C. dactylon and C. gayana are top indigenous species for palatability and yield.

Introduction

Livestock production is a central component in rural livelihoods and agricultural systems in Ethiopia, contributing substantially to household income, food security, draught power, manure, and risk buffering. Despite Ethiopia’s large livestock population, productivity remains low mainly due to chronic feed shortages and poor feed quality, particularly during the dry season (FAO, 2023; Herrero et al., 2010; Shapiro et al., 2017). Natural pastures and crop residues dominate feed resource in mixed crop–livestock systems, yet are generally characterized by low crude protein, high fiber fractions, and limited digestibility, which constrain growth, milk production, and reproductive performance (Deresse et al., 2014; Keno et al., 2021; Tahir et al., 2018). Nationally, natural pasture contributes about 55–60% of dry matter (DM), crop residues 30–35%, while improved forages remain minimal, leading to only about 63% of DM requirements being met (FAO, 2023; Feyisa et al., 2024; Gebremariam & Belay, 2016, 2023).

Feed scarcity is further intensified by land fragmentation, expansion of cultivated land, overgrazing, recurrent drought, and rangeland degradation, which reduce both quantity and quality of forage resources (Gebremariam & Belay, 2016; Keno et al., 2021). These pressures increase seasonal variability in feed availability, with natural pastures dominating in the wet season and crop residues in the dry season. However, crop residues are generally low in protein and highly lignified, limiting voluntary intake, digestibility and nutrient utilization and contributing to chronic nutritional stress in livestock systems (Keno et al., 2021; Tolera & Sundstøl, 2000).

Under these constraints, indigenous fodder trees, grasses, and shrubs (IFTGSs) provide an important adaptive strategy, particularly during periods of seasonal feed shortage. Farmers possess rich indigenous knowledge on palatability, seasonal availability, drought tolerance, biomass yield, and multipurpose uses such as fuel and soil fertility improvement (Feyisa et al., 2024; Gebremariam & Belay, 2023). In practice, farmers often evaluate feed resources using broader criteria beyond nutritive value alone, including reliability during drought periods, accessibility, storage potential, and multifunctional benefits, meaning that local preferences may not always align with laboratory-based feed quality assessments.

Despite increasing research on livestock feed resource in Ethiopia, important gaps remain, including limited integration of seasonal feed dynamics, household-level feed balance, laboratory-based nutritional evaluation, and indigenous knowledge within a single analytical framework (Deresse et al., 2014; Gebremariam & Belay, 2023; Tahir et al., 2018; Tsegaye et al., 2008). Hawzen district in northern Ethiopia provides a relevant case study due to its dependence on communal grazing, crop residues, and degraded rangelands under recurrent drought and land pressure. However, integrated evidence linking feed availability, feed quality, livestock nutrient balance, and farmer-based fodder evaluation remains limited in the area.

Addressing these gaps requires an integrated assessment that combines feed availability, nutritional quality, livestock nutrient balance, and indigenous knowledge within a unified analytical framework. The study was guided by the hypothesis that seasonal dependence on crop residues and declining grazing resources would lead to significant dry matter and protein deficits, and that farmers' feed preferences would only partially correspond with laboratory-determined nutritional quality because feed evaluation also reflects ecological, socioeconomic, and practical considerations. Accordingly, the study aimed to assess seasonal feed availability, feed quality, livestock nutrient balance, and indigenous fodder evaluation in the mixed crop–livestock systems of Hawzen District by integrating household surveys, participatory methods, and laboratory analyses.

Materials and methods

Study area and site selection

The study was conducted in Hawzen district, Tigray, Northern Ethiopia (13°50′–13°53′ N, 39°26′–39°29′ E) (Fig. 1), at elevation ranging from 1554–2919 m above sea level (Gebremariam & Hagos, 2018). The district experiences a unimodal rainfall pattern, with a mean annual precipitation of approximately 544 mm (range: 340–886 mm), most of which occurs between late June and mid-September, resulting in a growing season of about 100 days. Mean monthly temperatures range from 16.6 to 18.2 °C.Fig. 1Map of Hawzen District, Tigray region, northern Ethiopia.

Map of Hawzen District, Tigray region, northern Ethiopia.

According to the traditional Ethiopian agro-ecological classification system, which broadly categorizes areas into bereha (desert), kolla (lowland), woina dega (midland), dega (highland), and wurch (cold highland) zones (Hurni, 1998), approximately 85.8% of Hawzen district falls within the dry ‘woina dega’ (midland) zone. This classification was used to characterize the agro-ecological conditions of the study district based on the spatial distribution of the respective classification units. Hawzen covers about 80,949 ha, comprising 17,687 ha of arable land, 23,011 ha of forest, 24,724 ha of grazing land and 15,527 ha under other land uses. Mixed crop-livestock farming predominates, with barley, maize, sorghum, wheat, faba bean, field pea, and lentil as the major crops.

Hawzen district was purposively selected because its dryland mixed crop–livestock production system, short growing season, variable feed resources, and pronounced seasonal feed shortages provide an appropriate setting for evaluating feed availability, nutritional quality, livestock nutrient balance, and indigenous fodder resources.

Household survey, focus group discussions and key informant interviews

A multistage sampling approach was employed to collect quantitative and qualitative data on livestock feed resources and management practices. Hawzen district comprises 25 peasant associations (PAs), which were stratified into lowland (kolla) and midland (woina dega) ecological zones following Hurni (1998).

Two PAs were purposively selected from each zone based on livestock production potential, variation in feed resource, and accessibility, resulting in four study PAs. As the PAs were purposively selected, the findings should be interpreted within the context of the selected study sites rather than generalized to the entire district.

The household sample size was determined using Yamane's (1973) formula (Eq. (1)) with a 5% margin of error.(1)n=N1+N(e)2where n is the sample size, N is the number of households in the four selected PAs, and e is the level of precision.

From the 422 households in the selected PAs, 206 households were selected using stratified random sampling, with equal representation from the lowland (103 households) and midland (103 households) agro-ecological zones.

Qualitative data were collected through four focus group discussions (FGDs), one in each PA, involving ten participants per group, and 16 key informant interviews (KIIs; four per PA) with development agents, local experts, community leaders, and experienced livestock keepers. Participants were purposively selected for their knowledge of livestock production, seasonal feed resources, and local feeding practices.

A semi-structured questionnaire, translated into Tigrigna, was used to collect data on household characteristics, livestock holdings, feed resources, feeding practices, grazing management, and seasonal feed availability. Qualitative information was organized into major themes, including feed resource availability, seasonal feed shortages, indigenous feed classification, feed conservation, and livestock feeding strategies. Thematic analysis was applied, and the findings were triangulated with household survey and laboratory data to support interpretation of feed availability, nutritional quality, livestock nutrient balance, and indigenous fodder evaluation.

Feed sample collection, laboratory analysis, and feed supply estimation

Feed resource assessment followed the methodologies described by FAO (2012) and Stur and Duncan (2013). Sampling was conducted across the major livestock feed sources in the study area, including natural pastures, croplands, and forest/shrubland areas. Within each agro-ecological zone, two representative peasant associations (PAs) were selected, and ten randomly located 1 m × 1 m quadrats were established in each land-use type, resulting in a total of 120 quadrats (2 agro-ecological zones × 2 PAs × 3 land-use types × 10 quadrats).

Herbaceous biomass was harvested immediately after the main rainy season (October–November 2023), corresponding to the period of peak biomass production. Owing to logistical and financial constraints, seasonal resampling was not undertaken. Consequently, biomass measurements were used to estimate feed supply based on peak-biomass conditions rather than directly measure feed availability throughout the year.

For laboratory analysis, twenty composite samples were collected for each major crop residue type: wheat straw, barley straw, teff straw, maize stover, sorghum stover, and faba bean haulms. Representative composite samples were also prepared from natural pasture and forest/shrubland. All samples were air-dried to constant weight under ambient laboratory conditions, then ground to pass through a 1-mm sieve and stored in airtight containers until analysis.

Dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), and ash were determined following the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (1990). Neutral detergent fiber (NDF), acid detergent fiber (ADF), and acid detergent lignin (ADL) were analyzed using the procedures described by Van Soest et al. (1991). All chemical analyses were performed in duplicate for each sample. Hemicellulose and cellulose were calculated by difference, while soluble matter (SM) and soluble carbohydrates (SC) were calculated from the corresponding chemical fractions. Specifically, SM was calculated as 100 − NDF, and SC as 100 − (NDF + CP + ash).

In vitro dry matter digestibility (IVDMD) was determined using the two-stage Tilley and Terry (1963) techniques. Rumen fluid was collected from two rumen-fistulated cattle maintained on a standard hay-based diet and pooled immediately before incubation. Each sample was analyzed in duplicate, and the digestibility assay was conducted in duplicate runs. Standard reference samples and blanks were included in each analytical batch as quality-control measures. Metabolizable energy (ME) and digestible crude protein (DCP) were calculated from the laboratory-derived nutritional parameters using established equations. ME was estimated from IVDMD as ME (MJ kg⁻¹ DM) = 0.17 × IVDMD (%) − 2.0 (FAO, 2012), while DCP was calculated from CP as DCP (g kg⁻¹ DM) = 0.929 × CP (g kg⁻¹ DM) − 3.48 (Church & Pond, 1988).

Feed-supply estimates were derived from field biomass measurements using standard conversion factors and an adjustment for potentially accessible biomass. Estimated DM supply from natural pasture, aftermath grazing, shrublands, and forestlands was calculated using conversion factors of 2.0, 0.5, 0.7, and 1.8 t DM ha-1 year-1, respectively (De Leeuw & Tothill, 1990; Stur & Duncan, 2013). Crop residue yields were estimated from grain production using residue-to-grain ratios of 1.5 for wheat, barley, and teff; 1.2 for faba bean and field pea; 4.0 for noug and linseed; 2.0 for maize; and 2.5 for sorghum (Chenost & Kayouli, 1997; De Leeuw & Tothill, 1990; FAO, 2012; Kossila, 1988). A uniform 10% adjustment was applied to account for biomass losses during harvesting, transport, and storage (Tolera et al., 2012). Because feed-specific loss estimates were unavailable, this standardized adjustment was applied uniformly across feed-resource categories.

Because biomass sampling was conducted during the post-rainy season under peak biomass conditions, and seasonal resampling was not undertaken, the resulting feed-supply values represent calculated estimates derived from biomass measurements, conversion factors, and the loss adjustment rather than direct measurements of year-round feed availability. Accordingly, estimated DM, DCP, and ME supplies were interpreted as calculated estimates rather than continuous seasonal measurements. In addition, chemical characterization was limited by sample availability; therefore, comparisons were restricted to feed resources with available measurements.

Estimation of nutrient supply and requirement

Livestock populations were converted into Tropical Livestock Units (TLU) following Jahnke (1982): oxen and bulls (1.0), cows (0.7), heifers (0.5), calves (0.2), sheep/goats (0.1), horses (0.8), donkeys (0.5), mules (0.7), and poultry (0.01).

Household nutrient requirements were estimated from the total TLU using the standard maintenance requirement for one TLU, represented by a 250-kg tropical bovine, equivalent to 6.25 kg DM day-1 (≈2281 kg year-1) (Jahnke, 1982). Daily CP and ME requirements were obtained from Kearl (1982) and FAO (2012), respectively. Household, DM, CP, DCP, and ME balances were calculated by comparing the estimated nutrient supply from available feed resources with the corresponding livestock maintenance requirements.

The TLU-based maintenance requirement provides a practical approach for estimating household feed balance. However, because it does not account for differences in species, age, body weight, physiological status, or production level, the estimated nutrient balances should be interpreted as household-level approximations relative to maintenance requirements rather than precise estimates for individual animal categories.

Fodder value index (FVI)

IFTGSs were evaluated using participatory rural appraisal (PRA) approaches adapted from ethnobotanical and livestock feed evaluation frameworks (Chambers, 1994; Hassen et al., 2010; Martin, 1995; Stur & Duncan, 2013). The fodder value index (FVI) comprised three complementary components: animal-based assessment (ABA), reflecting palatability, voluntary intake, and observed animal performance; plant-based assessment (PBA), representing biomass production, regrowth capacity, drought tolerance, and seasonal availability; and multipurpose-based assessment (MPA), capturing additional uses such as fuelwood, fencing, and medicinal value.

Each component was scored on a five-point scale (1 = very low to 5 = very high) through consensus during FGDs and KIIs. Scores were assigned based on participants’ collective experience and agreement for each evaluation criterion. Equal weighting was applied to the three components to integrate farmers’ perceptions of feeding value, agronomic performance, and multipurpose utility without prioritizing any single dimension, thereby providing a balanced assessment of fodder value in mixed crop-livestock systems. The fodder value score (FVS) for each species was calculated as the mean of ABA, PBA, and MPA scores, and species were ranked using the fodder value equivalent (FVE). The highest-ranked and most frequently cited species were subsequently selected for laboratory analysis, and their nutritional composition was compared with participatory rankings to assess agreement between farmers' indigenous knowledge and laboratory-derived indicators of feed quality.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages were used to summarize household characteristics, livestock holdings, crop yields, and feed resource availability. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare household-level continuous variables between the lowland and midland agro-ecological zones. Farmer preferences for IFTGSs were quantified using weighted ranking indices as per Naah (2018):(2)Index=RnC1+Rn−1C2+…+R1Cn∑(R1C1+Rn−1C2+…+R1Cn)x100where, Rn ​ is the weight assigned to the highest numerical rank, Rn-1 is the weight assigned to the next highest rank, and R1 is the weight assigned to the lowest rank. C1, C2, …, Cn ​are the frequencies of responses assigned to the first, second, …, and last ranking categories, respectively. Four ranking categories were used, with weights of 4, 3, 2, and 1 assigned to the first, second, third, and fourth ranks, respectively.

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's HSD test, was used to compare nutritional parameters among feed types. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to examine relationships between farmer-based assessment indices (ABA, PBA, MPA, TA, and FVE) and laboratory-derived nutritional indicators (CP, NDF, ADL, and IVDMD). Feed balance was assessed by comparing available DM supply with livestock requirements and expressed as a supply-to-requirement ratio. Where supply was below requirement, the percentage deficit was calculated as [1 − (supply/requirement)] × 100. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed before analysis, and statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Socioeconomic characteristics of households

The demographic and landholding characteristics of sampled respondents are presented in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 and Supplementary Figures S1 and S2. The majority of respondents were male (86.7%), with a mean household size of 5.03 persons and an average age of 44 years. Among the respondents, 54.2% of respondents were literate, whereas 45.8% were illiterate.

Landholding size was generally small and fragmented in the study area. The average cultivated landholding was 0.346 ha in the lowlands and 0.427 ha in the midlands, while private grazing land was either very limited or completely absent in most households. The overall mean landholding (0.55 ha) was lower than landholding estimates reported in previous studies for Ethiopia (Central Statistical Agency, 2014; Shapiro et al., 2017).

Crop production remained the primary livelihood source, with an average grain yield of 24.40 quintals per household (Supplementary Figure S2). Wheat and maize contributed the largest shares of production, and their residues constituted important livestock feed resources. This finding is consistent with previous studies of Ethiopian mixed farming systems, where crop residues represent a critical feed resource during the dry season (Fekadu et al., 2010; Tolera et al., 2012; Herrero et al., 2010).

Livestock ownership averaged 5.39 TLU per household, with mean holdings of 5.33 TLU in the lowlands and 5.43 TLU in the midlands (Supplementary Table S2). Cattle dominated herd composition (71.4% of total TLU), followed by sheep (13.7%) and donkeys (11.7%). The predominance of cattle is consistent with previous reports from mixed crop–livestock systems in Ethiopia (Gebremariam & Belay, 2016; Shapiro et al., 2017; Rufino et al., 2013).

The seasonal dependence on crop residues has nutritional implications because these feeds generally contain less CP, more structured fiber and have lower digestibility than fresh pasture. Thus, although crop residues provide an important feed source during the dry season, their nutritional characteristics may constrain livestock performance, particularly when higher-quality feeds are scarce.

Information from the FGDs indicated that crop residues were mainly available from December to June, while stubble grazing supplemented livestock feed from November to February. Grass hay was available only during January and February, indicating its relatively limited contribution as a conserved feed resource. The period from March to May was identified by farmers as the most critical feed-shortage period, during which livestock feeding relied largely on crop residues and available browse resources. Farmers reported prioritizing limited high-quality feed for lactating cows and young animals, while also purchasing supplementary feeds or reducing herd size to cope with seasonal feed scarcity. Similar adaptive strategies have been documented in other Ethiopian mixed farming systems experiencing recurrent seasonal feed shortages (Amsalu & Addisu, 2014; Fekadu et al., 2010; Tahir et al., 2018; Tolera et al., 2012). Overall, the findings indicate that seasonal feed scarcity is characterized by both limited availability and a mismatch between the timing of feed supply and livestock demand. The greater availability of pasture during the wet season and increased reliance on lower-quality crop residues during the dry season may therefore create a seasonal nutritional constraint, particularly for livestock with higher nutrient requirements.

The estimated DM supply from crop residues averaged 4.19 t household-1, with significantly greater production in the midlands (4.83 t household-1) than in the lowlands (3.55 t household-1) (P < 0.05; Table 2). Wheat straw contributed the largest proportion of the residue supply, followed by maize and sorghum stover. Crop residues were also used for household fuel, construction, and other domestic purposes, which may reduce their effective availability for livestock. The lower dry matter yield recorded in the present study than that reported for Adami Tullu Jiddo Kombolcha District (Belay & Negesse, 2018) may reflect differences in cropping patterns, agro-ecological conditions, crop productivity, and residue utilization practices between the two study areas. Nutritional quality of the local feed resources

Chemical analysis showed significant variation (p < 0.001) in feed composition and nutritive value among the local feed resources (Table 3). DM content was highest in cereal residues, particularly barley and teff straws (>93%), while natural pasture and wheat straw also exhibited high DM levels. The high DM content observed in these mature feed resources indicates their relatively low moisture content; however, DM concentration alone does not indicate overall nutritional quality. Ash concentration was numerically highest in sorghum (11.59%) and lowest in barley straw (4.49%). CP varied considerably, with sorghum (7.64%) and faba bean haulms (6.56%) showing relatively higher protein content, whereas wheat and teff straws had the lowest. This pattern is consistent with previous reports highlighting differences in the chemical composition and protein contribution of crop residues in mixed crop–livestock systems (Bayissa et al., 2022; Tolera et al., 2012; Wanapat et al., 1985).

Fiber fractions also varied substantially among feed resources. NDF was highest in teff straw (79.52%), followed by maize (76.31%) and wheat straw (75.62%), whereas sorghum had the lowest NDF concentration (49.97%). ADF was highest in maize straw (53.85%), while ADL was also highest in maize straw (10.22%). Maize straw had relatively low IVDMD (51.48%), whereas sorghum had the lowest NDF, ADF, and ADL concentrations and relatively high IVDMD (63.12%), ME (8.29 MJ/kg DM), and DCP (67.22 g/kg), indicating comparatively higher nutritive value. The calculated cellulose and hemicellulose fractions also varied among feed resources, reflecting differences in cell-wall composition. These patterns are consistent with established relationships between cell-wall concentration, lignification and feed digestibility (Tolera & Sundstøl, 2000; Van Soest, 1994; Van Soest et al., 1991).

Wheat straw had the lowest ME (6.40 MJ/kg DM), whereas teff straw had the lowest DCP (25.44 g/kg). The CP concentration of faba bean haulms (6.56%) further indicates their potential nutritional contribution compared with the low-CP wheat, teff, and maize residues. Similar variation in the nutritional characteristics of crop residues across agro-ecological conditions has been reported in Ethiopia (Bayissa et al., 2022; Desta, 2023). However, because several chemical parameters were not determined for faba bean haulms due to insufficient sample availability, their overall nutritive value could not be fully compared with the other feed resources. These differences among feed resources highlight the potential value of combining or supplementing lower-quality residues with higher-quality feeds to improve the overall nutritional supply to livestock, consistent with evidence from mixed crop–livestock systems in Ethiopia and East Africa (Herrero et al., 2010; Tolera et al., 2012; Rufino et al., 2013).

Because feed sampling was conducted during the post-rainy season of a single production year, the observed nutritive values represent conditions during the sampling period and may not capture seasonal or interannual variation. In dryland mixed farming systems, feed quantity and quality can vary between seasons and years depending on rainfall variability, drought occurrence, and grazing pressure. Consequently, CP, digestibility, and biomass availability may differ during prolonged dry periods. Future studies should therefore incorporate repeated seasonal sampling across multiple years to better capture temporal variability in feed dynamics and livestock nutritional stress.

Livestock feed supply and demand balance

Based on the post-rainy-season biomass measurements and calculation procedure used in this study, the annual DM supply was estimated at 8.02 t household⁻¹ in the lowlands and 9.27 t household⁻¹ in the midlands, with an overall mean of 8.65 t household⁻¹ (Table 4). Feed resources were dominated by crop-derived resources (crop residues and stubble grazing), with wheat straw being the largest contributor (22.2–24.7%), followed by maize stover (12.8–15.1%) and sorghum stover (2.2–9.7%). Maize residues were more important in the lowlands, whereas wheat and sorghum residues contributed relatively more in the midlands. Natural pasture contributed an estimated 14.4–17.2% of total DM supply, while forest and shrub lands represented the largest single feed resource, accounting for 30.6–35.4% of total DM and providing substantial proportions of annual DCP (35.1–41.1%) and ME (31.5–37.0%). Comparison of feed supply with livestock requirements showed that DM supply was insufficient to meet livestock requirements. Overall, DM availability represented approximately 70.5% of the estimated requirement, indicating a 29.5% supply–requirement gap. DM coverage was approximately 66.0% in the lowlands and 74.8% in the midlands, indicating a relatively greater shortfall in the lowlands. Thus, despite the higher DM supply in the midlands, neither agro-ecological zone had sufficient DM to meet the estimated requirements of the livestock holdings. In contrast, calculated ME supply exceeded the corresponding annual requirements, reaching 116.8% in the lowlands and 144.0% in the midlands. However, these values represent calculated ME availability rather than effective energy intake, because the estimates assume that the ME contained in the available feed is accessible to livestock. Several dominant feed resources, particularly crop residues, had relatively high concentrations of structural fiber, and the relatively high NDF and ADL concentrations observed in some feed resources (Table 3) may reduce voluntary intake and digestibility and consequently limit effective nutrient utilization. The predominance of crop residues, together with the substantial contributions of natural pasture and forest/shrublands, demonstrates the importance of both cultivated and natural feed resources in Ethiopian mixed crop–livestock systems (Amsalu & Addisu, 2014; Aregawi & Abera, 2020; Belay & Negesse, 2018; Fekadu et al., 2010; Tolera et al., 2012). Thus, the feed balance indicates a constraint in DM quantity, while the nutritional quality of the available feed may further limit effective nutrient utilization.

In comparison to the findings of Bogale et al. (2008), who reported adequate DM supply and ME availability exceeding maintenance requirements (131%) in Sinana and Dinsho districts, the present study showed a lower DM supply relative to livestock requirements. This difference may be attributed to variations in agro-ecological conditions, livestock holdings, feed-resource availability, crop production, and estimation procedures.

The feed balance should be interpreted in light of the timing of biomass assessment. The measurements were obtained during the post-rainy period and therefore reflect the biomass recorded during the sampling period rather than direct measurements of feed consumed or continuously available to livestock throughout the year. Actual availability may vary with rainfall, grazing pressure, crop-residue utilization, and seasonal depletion of natural grazing and browsing resources. Accordingly, the 29.5% DM supply–requirement represents a calculated imbalance based on the study procedure and should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of a year-round feed deficit.

Although laboratory analysis showed relatively higher CP content and digestibility for sorghum, while faba bean residues also showed relatively high CP content (Table 3), field observations and farmer discussions indicated continued reliance on cereal crop residues, particularly wheat straw, because of their greater availability, higher biomass yield, and ease of storage. This suggests that feed selection was influenced not only by nutritive quality but also by biomass availability, seasonal reliability, and storage potential. Similar findings have been reported in Ethiopian mixed crop–livestock systems, where farmers’ feeding decisions are shaped by practical constraints such as seasonal availability, ease of access, and multifunctional uses (Ayenew et al., 2021; Mekoya et al., 2008; Talore, 2015). These observations highlight that farmers integrate palatability, biomass, seasonal accessibility, and multiple-use benefits alongside nutrient content when selecting feeds, demonstrating the multidimensional nature of farmers' feed selection decisions.

Farmer-lab correlation insights

Pearson's correlation between farmer evaluation indices and laboratory nutritional parameters revealed varying degrees of agreement between indigenous knowledge and measured feed quality indicators (Table 7). MPA showed strong positive correlations with CP (r = 0.960), CP Index (r = 0.958), ADL (r = 0.975), ADF (r = 0.862) and Nutritive Value Index (NVI) (r = 0.905) (all p < 0.01), while showing strong negative correlations with ME and ME index (r = −0.863 to −0.865) and ash content (r = −0.820). The positive association between MPA and ADL may reflect the inclusion of woody browse species, that provide multiple household uses while also containing higher lignin concentrations than herbaceous species. Similarly, ABA showed moderate to strong positive correlations with CP (r = 0.656), CP Index (r = 0.672), and NVI (r = 0.632) (p < 0.05), whereas PBA and FVE showed weak or non-significant relationships with most laboratory parameters. The observed relationships are consistent with previous studies in Ethiopian (Abraham et al., 2023; Mekoya et al., 2008), which reported positive relationships between farmers’ preferences and laboratory indicators such as CP and digestibility. Similarly, Ayenew et al. (2021) and Tamou et al. (2018) demonstrated that farmers evaluate fodder resources using multiple criteria beyond nutritive value, including seasonal availability, drought tolerance, ease of harvesting, storage potential, and other management considerations. The relatively weak or inconsistent correlations observed for some laboratory parameters therefore indicate that indigenous feed evaluation integrates both nutritional and practical attributes.

Although the strong correlations observed between the MPA and key nutritional indicators suggest that farmers evaluations are broadly consistent with laboratory-derived measures of feed quality, these findings should be interpreted with caution because only a limited number of fodder species were evaluated and the analysis was based on correlations rather than causal relationships. The results indicate that indigenous knowledge can complement, rather than replace, laboratory-based feed evaluation. Together, these findings demonstrate the value of combining participatory assessments with laboratory analyses to provide a more comprehensive and context-specific evaluation of fodder resources in dryland mixed crop–livestock systems.

Conclusions

Livestock feeding systems in the study area were characterized by pronounced seasonal variation in feed availability and quality. Natural pasture constituted the principal grazing resource during the wet season, whereas crop residues became the dominant feed source during the dry season. Although forest and shrub lands contributed substantially to the calculated feed supply, based on post-rainy-season biomass measurements and the study’s calculation procedure, estimated DM supply met about 70.5% of livestock maintenance requirements, indicating a 29.5% supply–requirement gap. The low CP and high fiber contents of dominant crop residues further limited feed utilization despite apparently adequate ME supply.

Farmers evaluated indigenous fodder species using multiple criteria, including animal preference, plant performance, seasonal availability, adaptability, and multipurpose use. The partial agreement between participatory evaluations and laboratory-derived nutritional indicators suggests that indigenous knowledge captures both nutritional and practical attributes of fodder resources. Integrating participatory assessments with laboratory analysis therefore provides a more comprehensive basis for feed resource evaluation and the development of locally relevant feeding strategies in dryland mixed crop–livestock systems.

Improving livestock productivity will require integrated interventions that enhance the nutritional value of crop residues, promote adaptable forage species, strengthen feed conservation practices, and improve sustainable management of grazing resources.

The findings should be interpreted in light of the study's limitations. Feed sampling was conducted during a single post-rainy season, and feed and nutrient balance estimates were based on maintenance requirements and purposively selected study sites. Consequently, the results represent a system-level assessment of the conditions observed during the study period and should not be generalized to all production systems or animal categories without further validation.
