Social determinants of health and menopausal symptoms: path analysis using the WHO framework
Authors: Fatemeh Vakili, Malihe Nasiri, Shayesteh Jahanfar, Sara Shishehgar, Zohreh Mahmoodi, Zeinab Hamzehgardeshi, Seideh Hanieh Alamolhoda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMenopausal symptoms frequently occur in middle-aged women and severely reduce their quality of life worldwide. Considering the potential influences of social determinants of health on menopausal symptoms, this study investigated the relationship between social determinants of health, as outlined by the World Health Organization framework, and menopausal symptoms among Iranian women.MethodThis cross-sectional study was conducted on 465 women aged 45 to 55 attending health centers in Kashan, Iran, between March and September 2024. Participants were selected via convenience sampling from a pool of women attending routine check-ups. Data were collected using six validated questionnaires: (1) a demographic and obstetric information survey (age, parity, etc.) (2), a socioeconomic status assessment (income, education) (3), the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) (4), the WHO Intimate Partner Violence Questionnaire (WHO-IPV) (5), Sharkey Physical Activity Questionnaire, and (6) the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) for symptom severity. Data were analyzed using SPSS 27 for descriptive statistics and LISREL 8.8 for path analysis, which modeled direct and indirect effects.ResultsPath analysis identified several social determinants directly influencing menopausal symptom severity. Domestic violence (B = 0.073, p < 0.05) and parity (B = 0.84, p < 0.01) were positively associated with symptom severity, indicating that higher exposure to violence or more childbirths worsened symptoms. In contrast, perceived social support (B=-0.15, p < 0.05) and physical activity (B=-0.25, p < 0.01) showed negative associations, suggesting protective effects. Indirect effects emerged from socioeconomic status (B=-0.29, p < 0.05), age (B = 0.11, p < 0.05), and spouse’s education (B=-0.25, p < 0.05), mediated through variables like support and activity levels. BMI uniquely influenced symptoms via direct and indirect paths (B = 0.38, p < 0.01), with higher BMI linked to greater severity. The model demonstrated excellent fit: RMSEA = 0.027 (< 0.06), GFI = 0.99 (> 0.90), CFI = 0.97 (> 0.90), and χ²/df = 2.24 (< 3), confirming its robustness.ConclusionSocial determinants of health significantly influence menopausal symptom severity. Domestic violence and higher parity worsened symptoms, while social support and physical activity had protective effects. Socioeconomic status, spouse’s education, and BMI also contributed through direct and indirect pathways. These findings could help healthcare providers identify menopausal and perimenopausal women at risk for severe symptoms and provide women with strategies to reduce symptoms, such as weight loss and physical activity.

Introduction

Menopause, the permanent end of menstruation due to declining ovarian function, marks a significant transition in a woman’s life. Officially defined as occurring one year after the final menstrual period [1] It is generally classified into premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal phases [2]. The average age of natural menopause is between 45 and 55 years (average 51.5) [3]; however, research in Iran suggests that the average age may be slightly younger, ranging from 46.9 to 49.6 years [3, 4].

A wide range of menopausal symptoms exists, with the four most commonly experienced by middle-aged women being vasomotor symptoms (such as night sweats and hot flashes), sleep disturbances or insomnia, vaginal dryness and dyspareunia, as well as negative mood states or depression [5]. These symptoms can persist for several years during the postmenopausal phase [6]. Menopause-related symptoms, which often commence before the cessation of menstrual cycles and are commonly observed in middle-aged women, can be profoundly distressing and significantly impact various aspects of women’s personal, social, and professional lives [7, 8]. In addition, the adverse influence of menopausal symptoms on the quality of life (QOL) of individuals can be considerable [9].

In a study conducted in Iran, involving 1,520 women, the prevalence of vasomotor symptoms, such as hot flashes, was 66.4%. Additionally, 41.1% of participants reported experiencing vaginal dryness [10].

Different studies have shown that various factors, such as age, obstetric characteristics, ethnicity, education, and lifestyle, can affect the severity of menopausal symptoms [5, 11–13].

The World Health Organization (WHO) characterizes the social determinants of health (SDH) as the conditions under which individuals are born, develop, reside, engage in work, and age, along with the frameworks established to provide healthcare and services to the community [14]. Various social and behavioral factors, including limited income, inadequate educational attainment, insufficient physical activity, and poor dietary habits, significantly contribute to the onset and progression of health conditions [15]. In 2010, the WHO Commission for SDH introduced a model outlining the social factors influencing health. These factors are classified into two categories: structural social determinants, which encompass education, income, gender, and ethnicity, and intermediate social determinants, which include material conditions, behavioral and biological factors, psychosocial factors, and the health system. The model underscores the interplay between structural and intermediate determinants, emphasizing that structural determinants shape health outcomes by impacting intermediate factors [16]. This framework highlights the socioeconomic and political contexts that contribute to health inequities, with health consequences such as menopausal symptoms being influenced by both structural and intermediate social factors.

Due to the high prevalence of menopausal symptoms among Iranian women [17], identifying risk factors for this condition is necessary. The SDH model is recognized as one of the most comprehensive frameworks available. In light of this, the models proposed by the WHO, developed by a panel of experts in this research, are structured to be applicable in different countries. Consequently, this study aims to explore the correlation between SDH and menopausal symptoms following the WHO’s 2010 Social Determinants of Health model. The study posits two primary hypotheses: (1) that structural social determinants of health have a significant association with menopausal symptoms, and (2) that intermediate social determinants of health are also related to menopausal symptoms.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted among 465 women between aged 45 to 55 who visited primary health centers in Kashan, Iran, in 2025. This study aimed to investigate the direct and indirect relationships between social determinants of health and menopausal symptoms using path analysis within the WHO conceptual framework to inform targeted interventions.

Sample size and sampling method

Women were chosen through a multi-stage sampling method between January and March 2025. The total number of health centers of Kashan City is more than 20 and we divided the city into five distinct regions: North, South, East, West, and Center and from each of these regions, one center was randomly selected. Subsequently, continuous sampling was conducted from 5 health centers based on the population associated with the chosen centers.

The sample size (n = 465) was calculated using standard sample size calculation methods based on the results of Masjoudi et al. [19], which investigated the severity and frequency of menopausal symptoms among middle-aged women in Rasht, Iran. Their findings provided the necessary parameters for calculating an appropriate sample size for the current.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were being between 45 and 55 years old, Iranian nationality, reading and writing literacy, being married, not using Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT), having a health record in the health center, having no history of mental illness, having a desire to participate in the study, and being willing to provide written consent.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were inappropriate questionnaire completion (failure to answer more than 20% of the questionnaire questions) and a history of gynecological surgeries such as hysterectomy and oophorectomy.

Data collection

Data were collected using a demographic and obstetric questionnaire, the Socioeconomic Status Questionnaire by Ghodratnama et al. (2013), the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) by Zimet et al.(1990), Sharkey Physical Activity Questionnaire, and Intimate Partner Violence by WHO to examine the variables related to the social determinants of health. The Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) was used to measure menopausal symptoms among women. The first author and a research assistant collected the data and and a private room was provided for participants to feel comfortable while answering questions.

Demographic and obstetric questionnaire

The demographic and obstetric questionnaire collected essential background information including participants’ age and their husband’s age (reported in completed years), ethnicity (categorized as “Fars” or “Others”), gravidity (total number of pregnancies, including current if applicable), parity (number of pregnancies carried to viable gestational age, typically ≥ 20 weeks), number of living children, and spouse (or partner) education level, which was classified into five categories: below diploma, diploma, associate degree, bachelor’s degree, and master’s degree or higher. Additionally, participants self-reported their height (in meters) and weight (in kilograms), which were used to calculate Body Mass Index (BMI) as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). All data were self-reported by participants through structured interviews and used to analyze potential associations with menopausal symptoms.

Socioeconomic status questionnaire

The socioeconomic status questionnaire consisted of 12 items assessing participants’ social and economic conditions. Key items relevant to this study included: Income sufficiency: Participants responded to the question, “How sufficient do you think your or your family’s income is for your living expenses?” with options ranging from “Very insufficient” to “Very sufficient”. Economic class self-assessment: Participants classified their family into one of five economic classes: “Very low,” “Low,” “Average,” “High,” and “Very high”. Parents’ educational level: Participants reported their father’s and mother’s education level using the following categories: “Below diploma,” “Diploma,” “Associate degree,” “Bachelor’s degree,” and “Master’s degree or higher”. Housing value: Participants who owned their home evaluated its market value on a scale from “Very low” to “Very high”. Financial ability to purchase housing: For participants or families who did not own a home, they rated their financial ability to purchase a home on a scale from “Very low” to “Very high”. The minimum and maximum scores obtained from this tool are 6 and 34, and higher scores suggested a higher socioeconomic status [20]. According to one study, Cronbach’s alpha for this questionnaire was 0.82 [21].

Multidimensional scale of perceived social support (MSPSS)

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) consists of 12 items designed to assess perceived social support across three distinct dimensions: family (items 3, 4, 8, 11), friends (items 6, 7, 9, 12), and a specific individual (items 1, 2, 5, 10). Responses to the items are measured using a five-point Likert scale, where a score of 5 indicates complete agreement, 4 signifies agreement, 3 denotes neutrality, 2 indicates disagreement, and one corresponds to complete disagreement. The total scores for the MSPSS range from a minimum of 12 to a maximum of 60, with higher scores indicating a greater level of perceived social support [22]. The MSPSS has been validated and deemed reliable within the context of Iran [23].

WHO intimate partner violence questionnaire (WHO-IPV)

The modified standard WHO Intimate Partner Violence Questionnaire (WHO-IPV), which relies on self-reporting, was employed to detect instances of domestic violence among women. This instrument comprises 31 questions that are categorized into three distinct domains of abuse: physical, psychological, and sexual. Specifically, the section addressing physical intimate partner violence (IPV) includes 10 questions, while psychological IPV is assessed through 15 questions, and 9 questions represent sexual IPV. The minimum and maximum scores obtained from this tool are 34 and 170, and higher scores suggest a higher level of domestic violence. The internal consistency of this instrument, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha across three domains, was found to be 92% for physical violence, 89% for psychological violence, and 88% for sexual violence [24]. Its validity has been assessed in various Iranian studies as appropriate [25].

Sharkey physical activity questionnaire

Physical activity was assessed using a five-item questionnaire evaluating participants’ exercise habits, including their general activity level (ranging from no exercise to national-level athlete), frequency of exercise sessions per week or month (from less than once a month to almost every day), exercise intensity per session (from light to very intense with heavy breathing throughout), duration of each exercise session (from less than 10 min to more than 45 min), and duration of consistent engagement in physical activity (from less than 3 months to more than one year). These self-reported measures were used to characterize physical activity patterns and explore their relationship with menopausal symptoms. Each question is assigned a minimum score of 1 and a maximum score of 5, resulting in a total score range of 5 to 25. A higher score reflects a greater level of physical activity [26]. The face and content validity of the questionnaire have been validated by experts in physical education in Iran. Additionally, the reliability of the questionnaire has been reported as 0.85, as determined by Cronbach’s alpha [27].

Menopause rating scale (MRS)

The Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) is divided into three primary categories: physical, psychological, and urogenital symptoms. The subcategories are as follows: (1) physical symptoms, which include hot flushes or sweating, heart discomfort, sleep disturbances, and musculoskeletal issues; (2) psychological symptoms, encompassing depressive moods, irritability, anxiety, and fatigue; and (3) urogenital symptoms, which consist of sexual dysfunction, bladder issues, and vaginal dryness. Participants were presented with five response options: no symptoms, mild to moderate symptoms, marked symptoms, and severe symptoms. The MRS yields a total score ranging from 0, indicating no symptoms, to 44, reflecting the highest level of complaints. According to existing literature, total scores of ≤ 11, 12–35, and ≥ 36 are classified as asymptomatic, mild to moderate, and severe to very severe, respectively. The 11 symptoms were organized into the three aforementioned subgroups [28]. The internal consistency of the Persian version of MRS items was found to be 0.85, as determined by Cronbach’s alpha, demonstrating a high level of reliability for the scale [29].

Data analysis

The data obtained were subjected to analysis using LISREL-8.8 and SPSS-27. The Pearson correlation test was employed to investigate the relationships among the variables under study (the dependent variable was menopausal symptoms, and the independent variables were age, socioeconomic status, spouse education, BMI, parity, intimate partner violence, social support, and physical activity). Furthermore, path analysis was conducted to assess the “direct effect” and “indirect effect” of the variables on each other. The model demonstrated an acceptable fit, with a comparative fit index (CFI), Goodness of fit index (GFI), and Bentler-Bonett Normed fit index (NFI) cutoff value set at 0.9, alongside a root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA) threshold of less than 0.05 [30].

Ethical considerations

The Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (IR.SBMU.PHARMACY.REC.1403.281) granted ethics approval for all experimental protocols. Following a comprehensive explanation of the study’s objectives to the eligible candidates, they provided their written informed consent, and it was explained to them that the questionnaires were anonymous. Participants were guaranteed the confidentiality of their personal information. They were informed of their right to withdraw from the study without any penalty, including their access to healthcare services. Free counselling and a social emergency hotline were provided for women subjected to IPV, and the women who expressed any mental stress or psychological illness.

Discussion

This study revealed significant associations between various social determinants and menopausal symptoms. Age, BMI, parity, and intimate partner violence were positively correlated with symptom severity, whereas higher socioeconomic status, spouse’s education, physical activity, and social support showed protective.

The observed mild to moderate severity of menopausal symptoms aligns with previous Iranian studies [19, 31]. This aligns with cultural and environmental factors that potentially moderate symptom expression in this population.

There was a positive relationship between age and menopausal symptoms in this study, which is consistent with findings of other studies [32, 33]. This may be due to the higher secretion of estrogen in younger women, which has a positive effect on reducing vasomotor and sexual symptoms [34]. This hormonal shift also contributes to changes in neurotransmitter activity, influencing psychological well-being [35].

Another finding of the current study was the negative association between spouse education level and socioeconomic status (SES) with menopausal symptoms, which is in line with results of previous studies in Iran and other countries [36, 37]. Greater SES likely enhances access to healthcare and awareness of symptom management strategies [38], though our study did not directly assess these mechanisms. Furthermore, social determinants like income and education influence health behaviors and stress exposure, affecting symptom expression [39].

The current study found a negative relationship between social support and menopausal symptoms, which is in agreement with the results of former studies in Turkey [40] and China [41]. In addition, a Japanese study conducted on teachers found that higher levels of perceived support were associated with less severe psychological and somatic symptoms [42]. Emotional and informational support from social networks enhances women’s ability to cope with stress, thereby reducing its psychological and physiological impacts [43]. Therefore, interventions aimed at strengthening social support networks—whether through family, peer groups, or community programs—may be effective in reducing menopausal symptom burden and improving women’s health outcomes [44].

In contrast, intimate partner violence (IPV) positively influenced symptom severity, a finding echoed by Gibson et al. [45], in the USA. IPV’s impact on midlife women underscores the need for healthcare providers to screen for and address violence as a modulator of menopausal health [46]. One reason for this association could be that, according to previous studies, women exposed to domestic violence are more likely to develop ovarian failure, which could affect the severity of menopausal symptoms in these women [47]. Also, IPV, as a chronic psychological trauma and stressor, may activate the HPA axis (hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal), leading to increased cortisol and other stress hormones. These hormonal changes can exacerbate menopausal symptoms such as hot flashes and sleep disturbances [48]. Previous studies have also shown that domestic violence is associated with lower health literacy, which could somehow explain the results of the present study [49].

Physical activity exhibited a direct negative effect on menopausal symptoms, consistent with prior research [12, 31, 50]. Engaging in physical activity that stimulates the release of endorphins into the bloodstream contributes to the alleviation of vasomotor symptoms, such as hot flashes and sweating. Additionally, it has been shown to have beneficial effects on women’s mood, overall well-being, sleep quality, and cognitive abilities [51, 52].

According to the results of this study, there is a positive and causal relationship between BMI and menopausal symptoms, which is in line with the results of previous studies [53, 54]. In women experiencing obesity, diminished levels of circulating adiponectin, along with central leptin resistance, may play a significant role in exacerbating the severity of symptoms associated with menopause [55].

Parity also demonstrated a direct positive association with menopausal symptoms, consistent with previous findings [13, 56, 57] but inconsistent with results of one study in China [58]. Repeated pregnancies may induce cumulative hormonal fluctuations and structural changes in the reproductive organs and pelvic floor [59], thereby sensitizing tissues and aggravating symptoms such as urogenital discomfort. In addition, women with higher parity frequently come from low-income and low-educational backgrounds. Conversely, women who are affluent and well-educated tend to employ family planning strategies to limit the number of their offspring [57].

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted.

First, its cross-sectional design restricts the ability to infer causality between social determinants and menopausal symptoms. While path analysis suggests directional relationships, longitudinal studies are necessary to confirm the temporal influence of social determinants on symptom progression. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data for variables such as intimate partner violence, socioeconomic status, and menopausal symptoms may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias, particularly for sensitive topics like domestic violence.

Another limitation is the use of convenience sampling, which may affect the generalizability of findings beyond the study population in Kashan, Iran. A more representative sample, including women from diverse socioeconomic and geographic backgrounds, would enhance the applicability of the results. Furthermore, the study did not account for unmeasured confounders such as dietary habits, genetic predispositions, mental health history, and hormonal levels, all of which could influence menopausal symptoms and mediate the effects of social determinants.

Strengths

This study uniquely integrates multiple SDH within the WHO framework, offering a holistic view of menopausal symptom determinants in an Iranian context.

Conclusion

The results of this study showed that structural and intermediary determinants of health influence the severity of menopausal symptoms. Therefore, these results can assist physicians and midwives in managing menopausal women by identifying risk factors for severe menopausal symptoms. Additionally, they can aid women and their families in providing perspectives and strategies to alleviate these symptoms, such as weight loss, offering psychological support to menopausal women, promoting physical activity, and more.
