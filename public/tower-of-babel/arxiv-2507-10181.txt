A simple formalization of alpha-equivalence
Kalmer Apinis ORCID:0009-0006-2395-6584, Danel Ahman ORCID:0000-0001-6595-2756

Abstract
. While teaching untyped λ \lambda -calculus to undergraduate students, we were wondering why α \alpha -equivalence is not directly inductively defined. In this paper, we demonstrate that this is indeed feasible. Specifically, we provide a grounded, inductive definition for α \alpha -equivalence and show that it conforms to the specification provided in the literature. The work presented in this paper is fully formalized in the Rocq Prover.

1.
Introduction

As proposed by
[
Chu41
]
,
\lambda
-calculus (originally called the
calculus of
\lambda
-K-conversions
) defines three conversion rules.
The first rule, in effect, says that we are free to rename any local variable
x
to any variable
y
which does not occur in the term — establishing the intuitive notion that "the names of local variables do not matter".
This rule has been later (already in
[
CFC58
]
) called the
\alpha
-rule.
However, for Church, the first rule is still a conversion — no explicit notion is introduced that such a transformation produces an equivalent term in some general sense.
Moreover, the conversion operation only defines a choice — we are not explicitly compelled to perform any specific transformation.

However, as defined by Church, the
\alpha
-rule can be used to convert the term to meet the preconditions for the second rule.
The second rule, in effect, says that we can replace a function call with the body of the function where the actual argument replaces the formal parameter — with the precondition that local variables are distinct from parameters and global variables.
This rule has later been named the
\beta
-rule.

Subsequently,
\lambda
-terms are viewed as representatives of equivalence classes induced by the
\alpha
-rule. Then, conversion using the
\alpha
-rule is either performed implicitly (e.g., following
[
Bar84
]
) or as part of the substitution while performing the conversion using the
\beta
-rule (e.g.,
[
HS86
]
).
Alternatively, to identify
\lambda
-terms with the equivalence classes they denote, one can forgo variable names altogether and work with de Brujin indices
[
De 72
]
.

Although many interesting properties can be stated concisely, such that they are suitable for teaching
\lambda
-calculus to undergraduate students, their formal proofs have caveats.
For example, using de Brujin indexes instead of variable names effectively reduces
\alpha
-equivalence problems to equality problems.
However, suppose we are still interested in properties of terms with variable names. In that case, the detour via de Brujin indexes might not make proofs easier overall, as we need to relate terms written with de Brujin indexes back to terms with named variables. This step is not trivial
[
Gor94
]
.

Implicit application of the
\alpha
-rule was popularised by
[
Bar84
]
— meaning that the precondition for the
\beta
-rule and issues related to
\alpha
-equivalence are effectively ignored.
This, however, is unwarranted for undergraduate students who must first learn the basics of
\lambda
-calculus.
Also, ignoring potentially complicated parts of proofs might be seen as a weakness in formalization.

A concise way of proving properties of
\lambda
-terms was founded by
[
GM96
]
, by defining
alpha
-structural principles which provides an induction principle for
\alpha
-equivalence classes.
This has lead to interesting results
[
Pit06
,
ABW07
,
Urb08
,
Cop+16
]
.

Explicit use of the
\alpha
-rule while performing a substitution is already mentioned by
[
CFC58
]
.
More informal definitions of
\alpha
-equivalence define it as just the equivalence relation (reflexive, symmetric, transitive relation) that also contains the
\alpha
-rule
[
HS86
,
Han94
]
.
However, formal definitions sometimes employ a swap operation, which swaps two given variable names with each other in a given term
[
Pit03
,
UN05
,
ABW07
,
Cop+16
]
.
However, note that such a swap operation generally does not follow variable scopes.
Thus, in some cases, one might prefer not to introduce additional technical operations that do not follow the usual semantics of
\lambda
-terms.
While people have also been thinking about and proposed more direct definitions of
\alpha
-equivalence
[
Bau17
,
Bau12
]
, we have not found any proper formalizations of such definitions with accompanying proofs.

Thus, the question arises whether it is reasonable to formalize
\alpha
-equivalence of untyped
\lambda
-terms in the style of
[
HS86
]
and
[
CFC58
]
. The aim of this paper is to positively answer this question using the Rocq Prover
[
Roc
]
.

2.
Untyped
\lambda
-calculus

Let

\Lambda::=V\;|\;(\Lambda\ \Lambda)\;|\;(\lambda V.\;\Lambda)

[

\lambda
-abstraction

The convention is to avoid parentheses by assuming that application is left-associative and abstraction binds as much on the right as possible. E.g.,
the term
\lambda x.\ \lambda y.\ x\ y\ z

(\lambda x.\ (\lambda y.\ ((x\ y)\ z)))

At the top level, a term

An abstraction

, where

In the function body

.
For example,
(\lambda x.\ x)
is the identity function, whereas
(\lambda x.\ y)
is the constant function that discards its argument and returns the global variable
y
.
Application

For example,
(\lambda x.\ x)\ z

3.
Definition of
\alpha
-equivalence

The intuitive idea that "the names of local variables do not matter" is captured by
\alpha
-equivalence
[
Chu41
,
CFC58
]
. For example,
(\lambda x.\ \lambda y.\ y)
encodes the function that takes two arguments and returns the second one, as does
(\lambda a.\ \lambda b.\ b)
. We denote this by writing
(\lambda x.\ \lambda y.\ y)=_{\alpha}(\lambda a.\ \lambda b.\ b)
.
Now, if we want to consider the terms
\alpha
-equivalent,
y
in the context
(\lambda x.\ \lambda y.\ \_)
must be
\alpha
-equivalent to
b
in the context
(\lambda a.\ \lambda b.\ \_)
.
To state this formally, we do not need to consider the full context of the respective subexpression, but only the respective abstraction variables outside the subexpression.
Thus, we might write the
\alpha
-equivalence of these subexpressions as
{\left<[x,y],y\right>}=_{\alpha}{\left<[a,b],b\right>}
, where we explicitly keep track of the respective abstraction variables.

to replace capture-avoiding substitutions with non-capture-avoiding substitutions. Thus, it is sufficient to prove

{\texttt{ssubst}}\ ({\texttt{ssubst}}\ t_{1}^{\prime}\ x\ u_{1}^{\prime})\ y\ u_{2}^{\prime}{\;=_{\alpha}\;}\!{\texttt{ssubst}}\ ({\texttt{ssubst}}\ t_{1}^{\prime}\ y\ u_{2}^{\prime})\ x\ ({\texttt{ssubst}}\ u_{1}^{\prime}\ y\ u_{2}^{\prime}).

However, this can be proven directly via induction on the term
t_{1}^{\prime}
provided that
x\not=y
,
x\notin\textsf{FV}(u_{2})
, and
y
is not a bound variable in
t_{1}^{\prime}
. Variables
x
and
y
are different and
x\notin\textsf{FV}(u_{2})
because we assumed it in the substitution lemma. Variable
y
is not bound in
t_{1}^{\prime}={\texttt{bvcRefresh}}\ t_{1}\ (\textsf{FV}\ u_{2}\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{ $\bm{++}$ }}\textsf{FV}\ u_{1}\raisebox{1.29167pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{ $\bm{++}$ }}[y;x])
because of the
BVC
property. Thus, we have proven the substitution lemma for our definition of substitution and
\alpha
-equivalence.

11.
Conclusion

Our goal was to investigate whether it is reasonable to formalize
\alpha
-equivalence of untyped
\lambda
-terms in the style of
[
HS86
]
and
[
CFC58
]
.
We have shown that our proposed definition of
\alpha
-equivalence satisfies standard properties commonly expected from it in the literature.
All our definitions and proofs have been formalized in the Rocq Prover.
In addition, our in Rocq formalization includes a decision procedure for checking
\alpha
-equivalence.
To demonstrate the feasibility of our approach, we provide a proof of the substitution lemma by explicitly using Barendregt’s Variable Convention.
