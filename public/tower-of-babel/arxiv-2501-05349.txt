Fermionic cellular automata in one dimension
Lorenzo S. Trezzini, Matteo Lugli, Paolo Meda, Alessandro Bisio, Paolo Perinotti, Alessandro Tosini

Abstract
We consider quantum cellular automata for one-dimensional chains of Fermionic modes and study their implementability as finite depth quantum circuits. Fermionic automata have been classified in terms of an index modulo circuits and the addition of ancillary systems. We strengthen this result removing the ancilla degrees of freedom in defining the equivalence classes. A complete characterization of nearest-neighbours automata is given. A class of Fermionic automata is found which cannot be expressed in terms of single mode and controlled-phase gates composed with shifts, as is the case for qubit cellular automata.



I
Introduction

Besides the perspectives in enhancing classical computation and
communication protocols, one of the major applications of quantum computers
is to provide a tool for the
simulation of quantum physical systems. A natural architecture toward
this goal is the quantum generalization of cellular automata, whose
power in simulating classical dynamical systems has been largely
proved
[
1
,
2
]
. Cellular automata were
envisioned
[
3
]
as a mesh of locally interconnected
finite state machines, called cells. The cells change their states synchronously
depending on their state and the nearby cells’ one at each time step,
thus embodying the
locality of interactions
typical of physical systems.
Other relevant properties with a physical counterpart,
such as
reversibility
can be programmed by
suitably choosing the local update rule.

If cellular automata bridge complex dynamical systems and computation,
quantum cellular automata (QCAs)
[
4
]
are
the natural setting for the efficient
simulation of quantum systems, and at the same time they provide a model of
universal quantum
computation
[
5
,
6
]
. Tensor
networks
[
7
,
8
]
,
the state of the art of nowadays quantum simulations, have also been
linked
[
9
,
10
]
to QCAs in the
notion of matrix-product unitaries, tensor networks describing time
evolution and unitary symmetries of quantum systems. Following the
growing interest on the computational features of non-abelian matter
fields
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
,
cellular automata, initially introduced for qudits, have been
generalized to Fermionic
systems
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
,
taking care to keep the parallelism with the literature on tensor
networks
[
18
]
.

As quantum simulators, QCAs have been successful in describing several
phenomena of many-body quantum systems with local interactions, such
as Floquet systems, and in classification of the topological phases of
matter
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
,
which has become a concept of fundamental importance in condensed
matter. Efforts have also been given on identifying classes of QCAs
that admit of a simple particle interpretation, that is automata whose
dynamics can be interpreted in terms of the propagation and scattering
of bosonic and Fermionic particles in quantum field theories or many
body systems
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
. A primary intent underlying discrete quantum simulations is on one hand the development of methods to access their continuum limit and effective dynamics

[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
, and on the other hand to model the dynamics of quantum systems in an emergent
space-time
[
23
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
.

Despite several formal definitions of QCAs over the
years
[
36
,
17
]
, a systematic
exploration of the general properties of such systems and of their
potential for computational applications was started quite
recently. In Ref.
[
4
]
an
algebraic axiomatic approach is proposed to describe a grid of finite dimensional
quantum systems with translation symmetry undergoing a reversible
discrete time evolution driven by a local rule. At each site local
operators (in place of local Hilbert spaces) are considered, whose

C^{*}
-algebra structure can be extended to the whole infinite grid
of quantum systems. Among various alternative approaches, it is worth mentioning a
constructive method to obtain QCAs, consisting in partitioning the
system into blocks of cells, applying blockwise unitary
transformations, and possibly iterating such operations. A major
result is the proof
[
37
]
that any QCA can be implemented in the above
constructive way with the aid of auxiliary systems. The main advantage of the partitioned scheme is that
the global unitary evolution can be obtained as the combination of
many local unitary gates involving few nearby systems, with a clear
operational (and experimental) interpretation. Most of the results
on universality of cellular automata
[
5
,
6
,
38
]
have been obtained within the partitioned scenario.

The partitioned scheme and the result of Ref.
[
37
]

seed one of the core questions on the structure of QCAs that is their
implementability by quantum circuits of finite depth. The answer to
this question has been given in one space dimension, where QCAs are
classified in terms of a topological invariant named
index
, a
quantity that summarises the local invariants of any QCA, i.e. any quantity
that can be computed knowing the local evolution of a finite number of cells,
and in intuitive terms represents the balance of information flow in the
two possible directions on the one-dimensional lattice. The classification
is in the following sense
[
39
]
: if two automata have the same
index, then necessarily one can be obtained from the other by postponing
a finite depth circuit. Having the same index establishes an
equivalence

relation. Moreover, a QCA is implementable by a circuit if
and only if its index is equal to one. Recently, preliminary results
have been obtained in the generalization of index theory for lattices
having spatial dimension larger that
one
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
,
and for Fermionic systems
[
15
]
—which is the focus of the
present manuscript. The present knowledge about the Fermionic index highlights two
main differences with respect to the qudits one. i) In the case of a qudit QCA
the index is a rational number, while it may take
irrational values in the Fermionic case. ii)
The classification of Fermionic Cellular Automata (FCAs) is based on a weaker
notion of equivalence, denoted
stable equivalence
: two
Fermionic automata are stably equivalent if upon appropriately
enlarging the Hilbert space by appending ancillary Fermionic
degrees of freedom that do not evolve, one can find a finite depth circuit
connecting them. In other words to connect cellular automata in the same equivalence
class one needs to apply circuits that involve not only the physical systems at hand,
but also auxiliary systems which will be left invariant by the evolution.

In the first part of this work it is shown how, also in the Fermionic case,
the classification of FCAs
[
15
]
in one dimension can
actually be established in terms of equivalence classes modulo finite depth
Fermionic circuits, without the need of ancillary degrees of
freedom. The proof is based on the procedure of ancilla removal introduced
in Ref.
[
41
]
. A complete index
theory for Fermionic automata in one space dimension follows,
were automata with the same index are equivalent modulo circuits
involving only the physical systems, like in the quantum case.

The second result of this work is an explicit characterization of all
FCAs in one space dimension and with nearest-neighbouring
interaction. This is done by providing the explicit form of the most
general FCA having unit index and noticing that, as in the
quantum case, a generic automaton is obtained by composition of a unit
index instance with a Fermionic shift. The set of shifts for local
Fermionic modes is strictly larger than the qubits counterpart, due to
the
parity superselection rule
. The Fermionic local algebra
is indeed graded, and its even and odd modules can
be independently translated on the
lattice
[
15
]
. This leads to the phenomenon of
irrational index, corresponding to a net “flow” amounting to less than a cell per step,
and yet non balanced, which is not achievable for “quantum information”. Besides
Fermionic shifts without quantum counterpart, we find solely Fermionic instances also
in the equivalence classes corresponding to a rational index. The full
characterization of nearest-neighbour qubits QCAs was derived in
Ref.
[
4
]
, with all QCAs corresponding to local
unitaries or controlled-phase gates, while the Fermionic local rules were
classified in in Ref.
[
43
]
with a technique that gives no insight
in their structure. Here we show that Fermionic systems support a non-trivial FCA,
that we deem
forking
automaton, which lies outside the above classes generated
by local gates together with d-phases.

II
Algebra of a Fermionic chain

This section is devoted to the algebraic description of a chain of
finitely many
local Fermionic modes
arranged on the sites
x
of a
one-dimensional lattice
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{Z}
, as for Kitaev
chains of spinless Fermionic modes
[
12
]
. The
computational properties of spinless Fermions were first studied in
Ref.
[
44
]
and later further explored in several
works
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a (one-particle) Hilbert space and consider the Fermi-Fock space

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{-}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\overline{\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\operatorname{ASym}\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}

(1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathcal{H}\oplus\left(\operatorname{ASym}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})\right)\oplus\ldots,

where
\operatorname{ASym}T
denotes the antisymmetrization operator
acting on tensor products, while the overline denotes the completion
of the tensor product of copies of
\mathcal{H}
. Let

\phi_{x}^{\dagger}
,
\phi_{x}
be respectively the creation and
annihilation operators over the vector space
\mathcal{H}
of each
local Fermionic mode labeled by
x\in\mathcal{L}
. These operators satisfy
Canonical Anticommutation Relations (CAR)

\{\phi_{{x}},\phi_{{y}}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{{x}{y}}I,\qquad\{\phi_{x},\phi_{x}\}=\{\phi^{\dagger}_{{x}},\phi^{\dagger}_{{y}}\}=0,

(2)

for all
{x},{y}\in\mathcal{L}
, where
\delta_{{x}{y}}
is the
standard Kronecker delta over integers,
\{A,B\}\coloneqq AB+BA
is
the anticommutator and
I
is the identity operator. We consider now
the occupation number operator

N_{{x}}\coloneqq\phi_{{x}}^{\dagger}\phi_{{x}}
on the site
x
and its
basis
\{|0_{{x}}\rangle,|1_{{x}}\rangle\}
of eigenvectors

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &N_{{x}}|0_{{x}}\rangle=0,\quad N_{{x}}|1_{{x}}\rangle=|1_{{x}}\rangle,\\
&\phi_{{x}}|1_{{x}}\rangle=|0_{{x}}\rangle,\quad\phi^{\dagger}_{{x}}|0_{{x}}\rangle=|1_{{x}}\rangle,\quad\phi_{{x}}|0_{{x}}\rangle=\phi^{\dagger}_{{x}}|1_{{x}}\rangle=0.\end{aligned}

(3)

In the
local Fermionic mode picture
the above vectors are
interpreted as zero and one particle states at position

{x}
, with
\phi_{x},\phi_{x}^{\dagger}
the creation/annihilation operators of a
Fermionic excitation in the
x
-th site.

Let us consider the
vacuum
with trivial multiplicity

{\Omega}\in\mathbb{C}
, i.e., the unique vector state which is
simultaneous eigenvector with eigenvalue 0 common to all
N_{x}
, namely

N_{x}|\Omega\rangle=0\hskip 10.0pt\forall x\in\mathcal{L}
. The
vacuum state, corresponding to all sites of the lattice being
unoccupied, is annihilated by all the annihilation operators, i.e.,

\phi_{x}|\Omega\rangle=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{L}
. By acting on

|\Omega\rangle
with arbitrary combinations of products of creation
operators
\phi^{\dagger}_{x}
, the Fock space for a system of local
Fermionic modes given in Eq. (
1
) may be identified
with the space

\mathscr{F}=\operatorname{Span_{\mathbb{R}}}\left\{\prod_{{x}\in\mathcal{L}}(\phi_{{x}}^{\dagger})^{p_{{x}}}|\Omega\rangle,\quad p_{{x}}=0,1\ \forall{x}\in\mathcal{L}\right\},

(4)

where
p_{x}
denotes the occupation number at the
x
-th site, and the product

\Pi_{x\in\mathcal{L}}
is well defined provided that a total (immaterial) ordering is
introduced on the denumerable set
\mathcal{L}
.

Using the creation/annihilation operators
\phi_{x},\phi^{\dagger}_{x}
, one
can define the Fermionic Pauli matrices

\displaystyle X_{x}=\frac{\phi_{x}+\phi_{x}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}},

\displaystyle Y_{x}=\frac{-i(\phi_{x}-\phi^{\dagger}_{x})}{\sqrt{2}},

(5)

for each site
x
, whence

\displaystyle\phi_{x}=\frac{(X_{x}-iY_{x})}{\sqrt{2}},

\displaystyle\phi_{x}^{\dagger}=\frac{(X_{x}+iY_{x})}{\sqrt{2}}.

(6)

The Fermionic Pauli matrix
Z_{x}
is defined as

\displaystyle Z_{x}\coloneqq iY_{x}X_{x}=\frac{\phi_{x}^{\dagger}\phi_{x}-\phi_{x}\phi^{\dagger}_{x}}{2}.

In the orthonormal basis of
Eq. (
3
), the matrix representation of
X_{x},Y_{x},Z_{x}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} X_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}

This notation should not be confused with the usual qubits Pauli
matrices
\sigma^{(x)}_{x},\sigma^{(y)}_{x},\sigma^{(z)}_{x}
that commute
on different sites of the lattice, unlike
X_{x},Y_{x},Z_{x}
, which anticommute.

II.1
CAR and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded
quasi-local
algebras

In the framework of operator algebra and local quantum theory, a set
of Fermionic modes arranged on the lattice

\mathcal{L}
is described by a
C^{*}
-algebra
over an infinite dimensional complex Hilbert space, called

CAR algebra
. For a general review of CAR algebras, see, e.g.,

[
48
,
49
,
50
]
. In
this algebraic approach, a pair of self-adjoint anticommuting
operators
(\xi,\eta)
is assigned to each site
x\in\mathcal{L}
,
such as the Fermionic Pauli Matrices
(X_{x},Y_{x})
defined in
Eq. (
5
). These operators are then promoted to be the
generators of the self-adjoint algebra of operators localized at

x
. Each local algebra is isomorphic to the algebra of
2\times 2
complex
matrices containing the identity
I_{x}
, in such a way that each
operator
O_{x}
on
x
is a linear combinations of the
identity and the Fermionic Pauli matrices (
5
)

O_{x}=aI+bX_{x}+cY_{x}+dZ_{x},\qquad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}.

Unlike the case of qubits, Fermionic
systems obey the
parity superselection

rule. The Fock space (
4
) can be decomposed as follows

\mathscr{F}=\mathscr{F}_{e}\oplus\mathscr{F}_{o},

(7)

where
\mathscr{F}_{e/o}
are the eigenspaces of the parity operator

P=\frac{1}{2}\{I+\prod_{{x}\in\mathcal{L}}(\phi_{{x}}\phi^{\dagger}_{{x}}-\phi_{{x}}^{\dagger}\phi_{{x}})\}

(8)

with eigenvalues
0/1
, respectively. The subspaces

\mathscr{F}_{e/o}
contain vectors corresponding to an even/odd total occupation
number, and parity superselection amounts to requiring that superpositions of
vectors with different occupation number parity are forbidden.
More precisely, the states of a Fermionic chain are density matrices with a
well-defined parity:

Theorem 1

(Parity Superselection)
.

Density matrices representing Fermionic states commute with the total parity operator
P
given in Eq. (
8
).

Consequently, linear maps representing transformations are bound to
respect the parity superselction of states. As shown in Ref.
[
46
]
, this implies that the operators corresponding to Kraus operators of allowed maps have a well-defined parity, thus form two subspaces (even or odd) of the CAR algebra. For instance, both
X_{x}
and
Y_{x}
are odd operators
since they anticommute with
P
, whereas their product
Z_{x}
commutes with
P
and
is an even operator. This turns the CAR algebra into a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded
algebra
[
48
]
.

Definition 1

(
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded algebra
)
.

A
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded algebra
\mathsf{A}
is a sequence

\mathsf{A}=(\mathsf{A}^{0},\mathsf{A}^{1}),

where
\mathsf{A}^{0,1}
are respectively the modules of even and odd operators.
\mathsf{A}
is equipped with the following operations:

1.

Graded sum: The sum is defined only between elements of
\mathsf{A}
with the same parity, i.e.,

\displaystyle O_{1},O_{2}\in\mathsf{A}^{p},\qquad O_{1}+O_{2}\in\mathsf{A}^{p}.

2.

Graded product: For every two operators we have

\displaystyle O_{1}\in\mathsf{A}^{p},O_{2}\in\mathsf{A}^{q},\qquad O_{1}O_{2}\in\mathsf{A}^{p\oplus q},

where
\oplus
denotes sum modulo 2.

3.

Graded tensor product: For every two
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded algebras
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
, we can define their graded tensor product
\mathsf{A}\boxtimes\mathsf{B}
as

\begin{split}&\mathsf{A}\boxtimes\mathsf{B}=((\mathsf{A}\boxtimes\mathsf{B})^{0},(\mathsf{A}\boxtimes\mathsf{B})^{1}),\\
&(\mathsf{A}\boxtimes\mathsf{B})^{i}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,q=0,1\\
p\oplus q=i\end{subarray}}\mathsf{A}^{p}\boxtimes\mathsf{B}^{q},\end{split}

and denoting by
g(T)
the grade of
T
, i.e.
g(T)=0
if
T\in\mathsf{A}^{0}
and

g(T)=1
if
T\in\mathsf{A}^{1}
, we have

\displaystyle(T\boxtimes I)(I\boxtimes S)=(-1)^{g(S)g(T)}(I\boxtimes S)(T\boxtimes I),

where
I
denotes the identity operator with
g(I)=0
.

Notice that, by the above definition,
g(A\boxtimes B)=g(A)\oplus g(B)
.
It is also convenient to introduce the following notion.

Definition 2

(Graded commutator)
.

For every
O_{1},O_{2}\in\mathsf{A}
, we define
the graded commutator as:

\{[O_{1},O_{2}]\}=O_{1}O_{2}-(-1)^{g(O_{1})g(O_{2})}O_{2}O_{1}.

The graded commutator reduces to the anticommutator if
O_{1},O_{2}
are both odd,
and to the commutator otherwise.

We can now define the local Fermionic algebra on
\mathcal{L}
as the algebra of local operators, i.e. operators acting non-trivially on
finitely many sites of the lattice
\mathcal{L}
, whose completion
in the operator norm provides a graded algebra called
quasi-local

algebra
[
48
]
. More precisely, upon denoting the single

x
-th cell algebra generated by the pair
(X_{x},Y_{x})
as
\mathsf{A}_{x}
,
if we consider a finite cardinality subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}
of the
chain, we can define the
local
algebra at
\Lambda
as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{\Lambda_{1}}\coloneqq\boxtimes_{x\in\Lambda_{1}}\mathsf{A}_{x},\qquad O_{\Lambda_{i}}\in\mathsf{A}_{\Lambda_{i}},

and then the
local algebra
on
\mathcal{L}
will be characterised by the product

\displaystyle O_{\Lambda_{1}}O_{\Lambda_{2}}\coloneqq(O_{\Lambda_{1}}\boxtimes I_{\Lambda_{2}\setminus\Lambda_{1}})(I_{\Lambda_{1}\setminus\Lambda_{2}}\boxtimes O_{\Lambda_{2}}).

Since
\mathsf{A}_{x}
, as well as
\mathsf{A}_{\Lambda}
, is a normed
space with the standard operator norm, we can consider the closure of
the local algebra in this norm, providing the graded

quasi-local
algebra
\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})
over

\mathbb{Z}
. This is the algebra of operators that can be
approximated with arbitrary precision with local operators.

III
One Dimensional Fermionic Cellular Automata

The notion of a Fermionic Cellular Automaton is the Fermionic counterpart of
that of Quantum Cellular Automaton defined in
[
4
]
,
which is an automorphism of
\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})
. Preliminarly, we
introduce a special class of FCA, called shifts, which implement lattice
translations.

Definition 3

(Shift
\tau_{x}
)
.

A shift
\tau_{x}
is the automorphism of
\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})
such that

\displaystyle\tau_{x}(X_{y})

\displaystyle\coloneqq X_{x+y},\qquad\tau_{x}(Y_{y})\coloneqq Y_{x+y}.

Fermionic Cellular Automata are then defined as follows:

Definition 4
.

A
\emph{FermionicCellularAutomaton}
(FCA)
with finite neighbourhood scheme
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}
is an
automorphism

\mathcal{T}:\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})
of the quasi-local
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded
algebra such that

1.

\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{A}(\Lambda))\subset\mathsf{A}(\Lambda+\mathcal{N})\quad\forall\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}
(locality),

2.

\mathcal{T}\circ\tau_{x}=\tau_{x}\circ\mathcal{T}\quad

\forall x\in\mathbb{Z}
(homogeneity),

where
\Lambda+\mathcal{N}\coloneqq\{y+x|y\in\Lambda,x\in\mathcal{N}\}
.
The homomorphism
\mathcal{T}_{0}:\mathsf{A}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathsf{A}(\mathcal{N})
given by the
restriction of the FCA to the
0
cell is called the

\emph{localtransitionrule}
.

Defining a FCA
\mathcal{T}
as a
*
-automorphism over

\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})=(\mathsf{A}_{0},\mathsf{A}_{1})
implies that each
\mathcal{T}
preserves the parity
of every element in
\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})
. In particular,

\{\mathcal{T}(\xi),\mathcal{T}(\eta)\}=0
for all pair of odd generators

(\xi,\eta)
satisfying
\{\xi,\eta\}=0
. Moreover, since the
full-graded matrix algebra is generated by two anticommuting odd
operators, it is sufficient to specify the action of
\mathcal{T}
over those
operators to fully characterize
\mathcal{T}
over
\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Finally, the following theorem specifies the relation between the local and the global rules of the FCA, i.e., respectively the *-homomorphisms describing the global time evolution of
\mathcal{T}
and its restriction to the single cell algebra
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2
.

The following statements hold:

1.

The global *-homomorphism
\mathcal{T}
is uniquely determined
by the local transition rule
\mathcal{T}_{0}
.

2.

A *-homomorphism
\mathcal{T}_{0}:\mathsf{A}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathsf{A}_{\mathcal{N}}
is the transition
rule of a FCA if and only if, for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mathcal{N}\cap(\mathcal{N}+x)\neq\emptyset
, the algebras
\mathcal{T}_{0}(\mathsf{A}_{0})
and
\tau_{x}\mathcal{T}_{0}\tau_{x}^{-1}(\mathsf{A}_{x})
graded-commute element-wise.

The relation between the local rule and the global evolution is given by

\mathcal{T}(\boxtimes_{x\in\Lambda}\mathsf{A}_{x})=\prod_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{T}_{x}(\mathsf{A}_{x}),

(9)

where
\mathcal{T}_{x}\coloneqq\tau_{x}\mathcal{T}_{0}\tau_{x}^{-1}
, and the same (immaterial) ordering of

\Lambda
is implicit in both products.

As illustrative examples of FCA, we consider the family of conjugation
automorphisms and the so-called
Majorana
-shift. On the one
hand, every conjugation maps a single-cell operator into another one
by conjugation with a parity preserving unitary matrix. We remind that the group of
reversible maps on a single Fermionic mode is

G\cong\mathbb{U}(1)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2},

(10)

whose elements are given by

\displaystyle U(\theta,n)\coloneqq e^{i\theta Z}X^{n},\quad n=0,1.

(11)

Definition 5

(Conjugation
\mathscr{U}_{x}
)
.

A conjugation automaton is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{x}^{(\theta,n)}:\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z}),

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{x}^{(\theta,n)}(\mathsf{A}_{x})\coloneqq U(\theta,n)^{\dagger}\mathsf{A}_{x}U(\theta,n).

In the remainder, we will omit the parameters
\theta,n
when they are clearly identified
by the context. On the other hand, the Majorana shift is defined as follows.

Definition 6

(
Majorana
-shift
\sigma_{\pm}
)
.

The Majorana-shifts

\sigma_{\pm}:\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})

(12)

are the (unique) homogeneous FCAs such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\pm}

\displaystyle:(X_{x},Y_{x})\mapsto(Y_{x},X_{x\pm 1}),

(13)

along with their inverses

\displaystyle\sigma^{-1}_{\pm}

\displaystyle:(X_{x},Y_{x})\mapsto(Y_{x\mp 1},X_{x}).

(14)

Remark 1

(Majorana modes)
.

Up to now we have considered chains where at each site
x
lies a
Fermion, whose algebra
\mathsf{A}_{x}
is generated by
the pair
(X_{x},Y_{x})
. It is possible to decouple each Fermionic
mode, say the
i
-th one, into
two
Majorana modes
as follows:

\displaystyle\xi_{2x}\coloneqq X_{x},\quad\xi_{2x+1}\coloneqq Y_{x}.

(15)

Accordingly, we can think of a chain of “complex”
n

Fermionic modes as a chain of
2m
Majorana modes, with two
Majorana modes for each site
x
. Based on
Eqs. (
15
) and (
6
) one can
identify Majorana modes with the generators of the local
algebra, thus justifying the “Majorna-shifts” nomenclature
for the automata (
12
). Indeed,
\sigma_{+}
(and its inverse)
move the Majorana modes to the right (left)
[
15
]
.
On the other hand,
\sigma_{-}
(and its inverse) have a similar behaviour,
provided that we first exchange
\xi_{2x}
and
\xi_{2x+1}
.

III.1
Index for FCA

We now review the concept of index for a one-dimensional Fermionic
QCA. The theory was presented in
[
39
]
for qudits
quantum walks and QCA and it was then extended to the Fermionic case
in
[
15
]
. For a review of the index theory, see
e.g.
[
17
]
. Naively speaking, the index
summarises all local invariants of a QCA—i.e. quantities that can be evaluated
on finitely many sites and are invariant along the lattice—
We begin reviewing the concept of
support algebras

[
4
]
[
15
]
.

Definition 7

(
Support Algebra
)
.

Lemma 1

Let

III.2
Local implementability and equivalence relations

As discussed in Refs.
[
39
,
15
]
, the
index of a (Fermionic) Quantum Cellular Automaton determines its

circuital implementability
, namely whether it can be
represented by finite sequences of blocks of unitaries
(
gates
). To elaborate on this property, it is convenient to
introduce the notion of
finite depth Fermionic circuit
, which is the
prototype of implementable quantum circuit.

Definition 9

(Finite Depth Fermionic Circuit (FDFC))
.

A Finite Depth Fermionic Circuit is an automorphism
\mathcal{F}
of the quasi-local graded algebra
\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})
, such that there exist a positive integer
D<\infty
(the
depth
of
\mathcal{F}
) and, for every
t=1,2,\ldots D
,
a bounded size partition
\{\mu_{i,t}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of the lattice

\Lambda
and for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and
1\leq t\leq D
a unitary

U_{i,t}\in\mathsf{A}_{\mu_{i,t}}
, so that
for every local operator
T\in\mathsf{A}_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(T)=F_{\Lambda}TF_{\Lambda}^{\dagger},

(21)

\displaystyle F_{\Lambda}\coloneqq\prod_{s=0}^{D-1}\bigboxtimes_{i\in J_{D-s}}U_{i,D-s},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}\coloneqq\Lambda,\quad J_{t}\coloneqq\{i\in\mathbb{Z}\mid\mu_{i,t}\cap\Lambda_{t-1}\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in J_{t}}\mu_{i,t}.

In words, a FDFC is a sequence of layers of unitary transformations, where each
unitary in a given layer acts over a finite set of cells in a partition of the lattice
that generally changes in each layer.

Remark 2
.

Clearly, any FDFC (
21
) is an example of FCA. Indeed,
taking into account the local rule of the FDFC as in Definition

4
, the condition
|\mu_{i,t}|<k
for every pair
i,t

ensures that there
exists a finite neighbourhood scheme

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}\subset\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{A}(\Lambda))\subset\mathsf{A}(\Lambda+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}),\qquad\lvert\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}\rvert<\infty.

Thus,
\mathcal{F}
has a bounded neighbourhood scheme. On
the other hand, it is not required that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}
to be a
nearest neighbourhood scheme, i.e., generally

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}\neq\{-1,0,1\}
.

A relevant class of FDFC is that of Margolus Partition schemes, i.e. FDFC with depth
D=2
and nearest neighbourhood scheme.

Definition 10

(Margolus Partitioned Scheme (MS))
.

A Margolus Partitioned Scheme
\mathcal{M}
is an FDFC of depth
D=2
, with
the two layers of unitaries over nearest neighbours
\{2x,2x+1\}
and

\{2x+1,2x\}
respectively, namely, for
T\in\mathsf{A}_{\Lambda}
, defining

J_{\Lambda}\coloneqq\{x\mid 2x\in\Lambda\vee 2x+1\in\Lambda\}
and

K_{\Lambda}\coloneqq\{x\mid 2x+2\in\Lambda\vee 2x+1\in\Lambda\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}\circ\mathcal{M}_{1}

(22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1}(T)\coloneqq\left(\bigboxtimes_{x\in J_{\Lambda}}M^{(1)}_{2x}\right)T\left(\bigboxtimes_{x^{\prime}\in J_{\Lambda}}M^{(1)}_{2x^{\prime}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}(T)\coloneqq\left(\bigboxtimes_{x\in K_{\Lambda}}M^{(2)}_{2x+1}\right)T\left(\bigboxtimes_{x^{\prime}\in K_{\Lambda}}M^{(2)}_{2x^{\prime}+1}\right),

with the unitaries
M^{(i)}_{y}
acting over
\{y,y+1\}
.

Based on the definitions of FDFC and MS and inspired by
Ref.
[

]

implementability for FCA in terms of FDFC and MS, respectively.

Definition 11

\mathcal{F}
-Implementability)

A FCA

(

In the special case of FDFC replaced by MS, this definition reduces to

already defined in

Definition 12

A FCA
\mathcal{T}:\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\mathsf{A}(\mathbb{Z})
is said to be

\mathcal{M}

When we consider Eqs. (
24
),(

), the whole

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
acts trivially over the ancillary systems, but

do not.
It would be desirable to replace the property of stable

-equivalence Eq. (

), which does not
involve ancillae, and thus refer to a more intrinsic notion of local
implementability of the evolution.

In Ref.
[

, the authors
introduced a method called

, which consists of
exploiting physical cells to play the role of the ancillary systems,
thus avoiding the addition of auxiliary systems. Our goal is to take
advantage of ancilla removal to show that two index one FCA are
always connected through an FDFC without referring to further
ancillary systems, i.e., they are equivalent according to Definition

13
. We observe that the local implementation of
a FCA based on a FDFC on the physical cells only can be more involved
than the simple MS needed with ancillae.

The possibility of performing ancilla removal with a bounded number of

ancillae, in the following proposition, we prove that only a single
ancilla per cell is needed for an index one FCA to achieve the stable

Proposition 1
.

Two index one FCA
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{S}
are related through the following
stable equivalence relation:

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\circ(\mathcal{T}\boxtimes\mathcal{I})=\mathcal{S}\boxtimes\mathcal{I},

(28)

The equivalence can be obtained through a MS.

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem 9 given in
[
39
]
it was proven
that two QCA with isomorphic support algebras are related through an
MS. Thus the proof reduces to show the isomorphism of the support
algebras
\mathsf{L}_{2x}
of
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{S}
(and the same for
\mathsf{R}_{2x+1}
).
In the non-graded case, this is
trivially true for every index 1 QCA. Indeed :

\displaystyle\operatorname{ind}[\mathcal{T}]=\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{dim}[\mathsf{L}_{2x}]}{\operatorname{dim}[\mathsf{A}_{2x}]}}=\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{dim}[\mathsf{A}_{2x+1}]}{\mathsf{R}_{2x+1}}}=1,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}[\mathsf{L}_{2x}]=\operatorname{dim}[\mathsf{R}_{2x+1}]=\operatorname{dim}[\mathsf{A}_{2x}]=\operatorname{dim}[\mathsf{A}_{2x+1}].

A key consideration in the proof is that full matrix algebras
with the same dimension are isomorphic. This allows one to conclude that all
support algebras are isomorphic to
\mathsf{A}_{x}
, and thus all index one QCA are
implementable through an MS: in a sketchy recap, the first layer arranges the
counter-image of
\mathsf{L}_{2x}
in the cell
2x
and that of
\mathsf{R}_{2x+1}
in the cell
2x+1
, and the second layer distributes
\mathsf{L}_{2x}
and
\mathsf{R}_{2x-1}
in the two
cells
2x-1
and
2x
. Since two index 1 QCA are implementable through a MS,

\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}^{-1}
is
\mathcal{F}
-implementable, too, and thus
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{F}\mathcal{T}
,
without the necessity of ancillary systems.
On the other hand, in the graded case the single cell algebra generated by
n

Fermionic modes is a
full-matrix graded algebra
\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{n\lvert n})
. The support algebras
\mathsf{L}_{2x}
and

\mathsf{R}_{2x+1}
can be either Clifford algebras
\mathit{C}\ell_{1}(p\lvert q)
or full matrix graded
algebra
\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{p\lvert q})
. However, the Clifford case is ruled out by imposing that
the dimension of the support algebra is equal to the dimension of the
full matrix. Indeed, we have:

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}[\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{p\lvert q})]=(p+q)^{2}=\operatorname{dim}[\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{n\lvert n})]=(2n)^{2}.

Moreover—contrarily to the non-graded case—two graded algebras with equal
dimension are not necessarily isomorphic
to each other. In particular there may exist two index 1 QCA
\mathcal{T}

and
\mathcal{S}
having supports isomorphic to
\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{p\lvert q})
and
\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{p^{\prime}\lvert q^{\prime}})

such that:

\begin{split}&(p+q)^{2}=(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime})^{2}=(2n)^{2},\\
&p\neq p^{\prime},\hskip 20.0ptq\neq q^{\prime}.\end{split}

(29)

The introduction of ancillary systems overcomes this peculiarity of
graded algebras. Indeed, by paring with an ancillary cell
\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{n\lvert n})

we have:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{p\lvert q})\boxtimes\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{n\lvert n})\simeq\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{n(p+q)\lvert n(p+q)}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{p^{\prime}\lvert q^{\prime}})\boxtimes\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{n\lvert n})\simeq\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{n(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime})\lvert n(p^{\prime}+q^{\prime})}).

Hence they are both isomorphic to
\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{2n^{2}\lvert 2n^{2}})
due to
Eq. (
29
). Therefore only one ancillary system for each
cell, isomorphic to the cell itself, is sufficient to enable the same
argument as for QCAs.

\,\blacksquare

Classification of One dimensional Nearest Neighbours FCA

In this section, we classify all the possible evolutions of FCA on a
one-dimensional lattice
\Lambda
with a single Fermionic mode per
site, namely the support algebra per site is isomorphic to

\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{1\lvert 1})
.

We recall that each local Fermionic mode located at

is
generated by
(X_{x},Y_{x})
. Based on Theorem

, the
form of the automorphism implementing the FCA is pinned down only by
the graded commutation relations on the overlapping regions of the
neighbourhoods. In particular, for a nearest neighbour FCA

\mathcal{T}
, i.e. with
\mathcal{N}=\{-1,0,1\}

that

\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{A}_{x})\subseteq\mathsf{A}_{x-1}\boxtimes\mathsf{A}_{x}\boxtimes\mathsf{A}_{x+1}.

(31)

Considering for example the region

\mathsf{A}^{0}\boxtimes\mathsf{A}^{1}\boxtimes\mathsf{A}^{2}\boxtimes\mathsf{A}^{3}\boxtimes\mathsf{A}^{4}

\displaystyle\{[\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{A}_{1}),\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{A}_{3})]\}=0,

(32)

\displaystyle\{[\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{A}_{1}),\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{A}_{2})]\}=0.

(33)

In particular, we can define the support algebras:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{S}(\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{A}_{x}),\mathsf{A}_{x-1}),

(34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{R}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{S}(\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{A}_{x}),\mathsf{A}_{x+1}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{C}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{S}(\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{A}_{x}),\mathsf{A}_{x}).

In the translationally invariant case, those support algebras do not depend
on

\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{A}_{x})\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{L}\boxtimes\mathcal{E}_{C}\boxtimes\mathcal{E}_{R}.

(35)

Lemma
2
allows us then to derive the following necessary condition from Eq. (

)

\{[\mathcal{E}_{L},\mathcal{E}_{R}]\}=0.

(36)

Another preliminary result for the scope of the classification of
FCA is the following Lemma and along with its corollary.

Lemma 8
.

Operators in the same cell with different parity that are not multiples of
I
cannot graded-commute

Proof.

The thesis immediately follows considering that odd operators are of the form

aX+bY
and even operators are of the form

, and that

\{[X,Z]\}=[X,Z]=-iY\neq 0.

\,\blacksquare

The previous Lemma has two relevant corollaries.

Corollary 3
.

The graded center of the full matrix graded

is trivial.

Corollary 4
.

One of the following cases holds

1.

\mathcal{E}_{L}
(or

2.

with a suitable choice of basis,

This straightforwardly follows from the above Lemma and the first condition in
Eq. (

). One needs to consider that the only subalgebra of
\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{1\lvert 1})

. On the other hand if
\mathcal{E}_{R}
and

contain odd selfadjoint operators, say

with

\,\blacksquare

over

.

The Majorana shift
\sigma_{\pm}
in Eq. (

(37)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ind}(\sigma_{\pm}^{-1})=2^{\mp\frac{1}{2}}.

Applying

Unit Index FCA

We begin proving that for index one FCA over
\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{1\lvert 1})

Lemma 10
.

For FCA with cells isomorphic to
\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{1\lvert 1})
and
\operatorname{ind}[\mathcal{T}]=1

\mathcal{E}_{L}\simeq\mathcal{E}_{R}
.

We proceed exhausting all cases. Without loss of generality, we will
enumerate all possibile forms of
\mathcal{E}_{L}
: the same arguments clearly hold
exchanging
\mathcal{E}_{L}
and
\mathcal{E}_{R}

1.

\mathcal{E}_{L}\simeq\operatorname{Mat}(\mathbb{C}^{1\lvert 1})
. The graded commutation relation in

immediately

\mathcal{E}_{R}\simeq I

. Therefore, the automaton is isomorphic to a left

2.

\mathcal{E}_{L}
contains an odd operator, that we will call

) and Theorem

\mathcal{E}_{R}

and
\mathcal{E}_{L}
generated by

3.

\mathcal{E}_{L}

\mathcal{E}_{L}\simeq\mathbb{C}I

The classification proceeds as follows. Based on the previous Lemma,

1.

VI
Outlook

One dimensional quantum cellular automata, representing the dynamics of chains
of locally interacting quantum systems (e.g. qubits in the simplest case),
have been extensively studied under various aspects. In particular, an interesting
facet of the theory of QCAs is based on their local algebraic
tensor product structure. Operators localised on disjoint regions of
the lattice commute with each other and the evolution spreads them over finite
regions, preserving commutation relations. The analogous scheme for local Fermionic
modes, governed by CAR algebras, is more tricky, since the
simple tensor product structure has to be replaced by graded tensor product.
This issue is typically bypassed referring to concrete representations of
CAR algebras on qubits (i.e. the Jordan-Wigner representation), which
makes it possible to use the standard concepts of quantum information theory,
subsequently imposing parity superselection rule. At first sight this approach seems
to confine Fermionic cellular automata to a subset of the admissible
local quantum dynamics.

On the contrary, the classification of local dynamics for Fermionic
modes
[
15
]
brought out core-level
discrepancies with respect to its standard quantum counterpart. In
both cases, cellular automata are divided in equivalence classes labelled by
an index, representing the unique topological invariant for one
dimensional lattices. The values of the index, which encode the ratio of
information flow of a given cellular automaton in the two directions on the
lattice (right/left), span all the rational numbers in the quantum case,
while for Fermionic systems it can take irrational values.
This is reminiscent of the local graded-commuting structure of Fermionic algebras,
which allows for moving (Majorana shifts) odd Fermionic degrees of freedom that
lack a qubits analogue.

The above classification is given modulo finite depth Fermionic circuits
for QCAs
[
39
]
, and modulo finite depth Fermionic circuits
with the addition of ancillae for FCAs
[
15
]
. Via
ancilla removal
[
41
]
, we
proved that also in the Fermionic case the equivalence relation boils
down to the quantum one, namely two FCAs have the same index if and
only if they are connected by a Fermionic circuit of finite depth.

As an outcome of Fermionic index properties, every FCA is
equivalent to a unit index automaton, which has balanced right/left
flow of information, composed with a Fermionic shift, introducing an
arbitrary degree of imbalance. Accordingly, an exhaustive
parametrization of Fermionic automata follows from characterizing all
unit index representatives. This is achieved in this paper, showing a
discrepancy between quantum and Fermionic automata already within
locally implementable instances. In other words, there are finite depth
Fermionic circuits that cannot be implemented as finite depth qubit circuits.
In Ref.
[
4
]
is was shown that every QCA with
circuital realization is made of single system and controlled-phase
gates. On the other hand, Fermionic modes allow for a localizable
automaton that does not admit such a realization.

\{\xi,\eta\}=0
, then the
form of the evolution of
\mathcal{T}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\xi)=\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}|i\rangle\langle i|\boxtimes U_{i,j}(\xi)U_{i,j}^{\dagger}\boxtimes|j\rangle\langle j|,

(50)

where
U_{i,j}\in SU^{n}(2)
,
n=0,1
, satisfy the following constraint
\forall k,l=0,1

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}\{[U_{k,i}(\xi)U_{k,i}^{\dagger}\boxtimes|j\rangle\langle j|,|i\rangle\langle i|\boxtimes U_{j,l}(\eta)U_{j,l}^{\dagger}]\}=0.

(51)

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{E}_{L},\mathcal{E}_{R}
are generated by
Z
, we can decompose the action on
an odd operator
\xi
as:

\mathcal{T}(\xi)=\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}|i\rangle\langle i|\boxtimes\mathsf{A}_{i,j}(\xi)\boxtimes|j\rangle\langle j|,

(52)

where
|i\rangle,|j\rangle
are eigenstates of
Z
.
To impose the preservation of the CAR, it should hold that
\{\mathcal{T}(\xi),\mathcal{T}(\eta)\}=0
, and thus:

\sum_{i,j=0,1}|i\rangle\langle i|\boxtimes\{\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(\xi),\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(\eta)\}\boxtimes|j\rangle\langle j|=0,

(53)

which yields to:

\{\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(\xi),\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(\eta)\}=0\hskip 10.0pt\forall i,j\in\{0,1\}.

(54)

This means that also
\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(\xi)
and

\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(\eta)
are representation of the CAR. Since every
two representations of the CAR are related through a unitary
transformation we can write
\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(\xi),\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(\eta)
as a unitary
rotation of
\xi,\eta
with defined parity, i.e.

\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(\xi)=U_{i,j}\xi U_{i,j}^{\dagger}\hskip 5.0ptU_{i,j}\in SU^{n}(2)\hskip 5.0pt\forall i,j\in\{0,1\},

(55)

for a fixed
n=0,1
. Clearly, the same holds for
\eta

\mathcal{T}(\xi)=\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}|i\rangle\langle i|\boxtimes U_{i,j}(\xi)U_{i,j}^{\dagger}\boxtimes|j\rangle\langle j|.

(56)

On this evolution, we still have to impose the graded commutation
relation
\{[\mathcal{T}(\xi)\boxtimes I,I\boxtimes\mathcal{T}(\eta)]\}
. This leads to:

\displaystyle\{[\mathcal{T}(\xi)\boxtimes I,I\boxtimes\mathcal{T}(\eta)]\}=

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}\{[U_{k,i}(\xi)U_{k,i}^{\dagger}\boxtimes|j\rangle\langle j|,|i\rangle\langle i|\boxtimes U_{j,l}(\eta)U_{j,l}^{\dagger}]\}=0,

\forall k,l=0,1
and this concludes the proof.

\,\blacksquare

Lemma 12
.

Let
i,j=0,1
and
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2},\{V_{j}\}_{j=1}^{2}
be two sets of
unitary
2\times 2
matrices satisfying:

\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}\{U_{i}(\xi)U_{i}^{\dagger}\boxtimes|j\rangle\langle j|,|i\rangle\langle i|\boxtimes V_{j}(\eta)V_{j}^{\dagger}\}=0.

Then
U_{i}U_{j}^{\dagger},V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger}
are simultaneously diagonalizable in
the
|i\rangle\langle i|
eigenstate basis of
Z
.

Proof.

Let
E_{i}=U_{i}\xi U_{i}^{\dagger}
and
F_{j}=V_{j}\eta V_{j}
. We can cast the CAR as:

\sum_{i,j}E_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|\boxtimes|j\rangle\langle j|F_{j}=\sum_{i,j}|i\rangle\langle i|E_{i}\boxtimes F_{j}|j\rangle\langle j|.

Notice that we exploited

\sum_{i,j}(|i\rangle\langle i|\boxtimes F_{j})(E_{i}\boxtimes|j\rangle\langle j|)=-\sum_{i,j}|i\rangle\langle i|E_{i}\boxtimes F_{j}|j\rangle\langle j|
, i.e. the anticommutation of
E_{i}
and
F_{j}
on
different sites. To simplify the sum, we can multiply on the left
with
|l\rangle\langle l|\boxtimes|m\rangle\langle m|
and on the right with

|a\rangle\langle a|\boxtimes|b\rangle\langle b|
. In this way, we get :

\displaystyle|l\rangle\langle l|E_{a}|a\rangle\langle a|\boxtimes|m\rangle\langle m|F_{m}|b\rangle\langle b|=

\displaystyle=|l\rangle\langle l|E_{l}|a\rangle\langle a|\boxtimes|m\rangle\langle m|F_{b}|b\rangle\langle b|.

Again, we can exploit the invariance of the classification under local
unitary rotations, and then choose
F_{b}
in such a way that

\langle m|F_{b}|b\rangle=0
. In particular, we can choose

\eta=V^{\dagger}_{b}|m^{\prime}\rangle\langle b^{\prime}|V_{b}
, with
m^{\prime}\neq m
. Thereby, the
left hand side of the previous equation vanishes and we get:

|l\rangle\langle l|E_{a}|a\rangle\langle a|\boxtimes|m\rangle\langle m|V_{m}V_{b}^{\dagger}|m^{\prime}\rangle\langle b^{\prime}|V_{b}V_{m}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|=0.

(57)

Finally, we notice that we can always choose
\xi
in such a way that

|l\rangle\langle l|E_{a}|a\rangle\langle a|\neq 0
and
b^{\prime}
in such a way that

\langle b^{\prime}|V_{b}V_{m}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\neq 0
. Thus, this leads to:

\langle m|V_{m}V_{b}^{\dagger}|m^{\prime}\rangle=0,

which proves the lemma for
V_{m}V_{b}^{\dagger}

\forall m,b
. Repeating the
same procedure with the roles of
V
and
U
exchanged, we get to the
same conclusion for
U_{i}U_{j}^{\dagger}
.

\,\blacksquare

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
3

We want now to prove the Main Theorem
3
. The proof
proceeds as follows: first, we find in Proposition
4

the most generic FCA with
\mathcal{E}_{L}
and
\mathcal{E}_{R}
generated by odd
anticommuting operators, whose evolution is constrained by two
mutually exclusive conditions. Then in Lemma
13

we prove that only one of the two conditions is compatible with the
assumptions on the support algebras
\mathcal{E}_{L},\mathcal{E}_{R}
. Finally, we show that
such a condition leads to the
Forking automaton
defined in
Eq. (
45
).

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be an arbitrary FCA having
\mathcal{E}_{L}
and
\mathcal{E}_{R}
generated by
two odd anticommuting operators. The most general form of the
evolution of the FCA acting on an odd operator
\xi
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\xi)=b_{\xi}X\boxtimes I\boxtimes I+c_{\xi}I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y+I\boxtimes\mathsf{D}_{\xi}\boxtimes I,

(58)

whith
D_{\xi}
an odd operator, along with one of the following two conditions holding:

\displaystyle D_{\xi}=0,\qquad b_{\xi}c_{\xi}=0.

(59)

Proof.

If we assume both the left and right support algebra to be generated
by odd operators
\mathsf{G}_{L},\mathsf{G}_{R}
, the graded commutation
relation imposes:

\{[\mathsf{G}_{L},\mathsf{G}_{R}]\}=\{\mathsf{G}_{L},\mathsf{G}_{R}\}=0.

Notice that this means that
\mathsf{G}_{L}
and
\mathsf{G}_{R}
are
another representation of the CAR. Since we are interested to
classify automata modulo unitary transformations, we can take without
loss of generality
\mathsf{G}_{L}
and
\mathsf{G}_{R}
to be
X,Y
. Let
us consider the general decomposition for the evolution of an odd
operator
\xi
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}(\xi)=&X\boxtimes\mathsf{A}_{\xi}\boxtimes Y+X\boxtimes\mathsf{B}_{\xi}\boxtimes I\\
&+I\boxtimes\mathsf{C}_{\xi}\boxtimes Y+I\boxtimes\mathsf{D}_{\xi}\boxtimes I.\end{split}

Since the evolution should be parity preserving we have:

\mathsf{A}_{\xi},\mathsf{D}_{\xi}\in\mathsf{A}_{1},\qquad\mathsf{B}_{\xi},\mathsf{C}_{\xi}\in\mathsf{A}_{0}.

In other words,
\mathsf{A}_{\xi},\mathsf{D}_{\xi}
should be odd operators while

\mathsf{B}_{\xi},\mathsf{C}_{\xi}
should be even. For the scope of the
proposition it is sufficient to impose the following graded
commutation relation

\{[\mathcal{T}(\xi)\boxtimes I,I\boxtimes\mathcal{T}(\xi)]\}=0.

(60)

We can write the graded commutator as:

\begin{split}&\{[\mathcal{T}(\xi)\boxtimes I,I\boxtimes\mathcal{T}(\xi)]\}=\\
&=X\boxtimes\mathsf{E}\boxtimes Y+X\boxtimes\mathsf{F}\boxtimes I+\\
&+I\boxtimes\mathsf{G}\boxtimes Y+I\boxtimes\mathsf{H}\boxtimes I=0.\end{split}

(61)

Since each term is tensorized by linearly independent operators, they should identically vanish.
Computing
\mathsf{E}=0
we get:

\mathsf{E}=[\mathsf{A}_{\xi}\boxtimes Y,X\boxtimes\mathsf{A}_{\xi}]+\mathsf{A}_{\xi}\boxtimes[Y,\mathsf{C}_{\xi}]+[\mathsf{B}_{\xi},X]\boxtimes A_{\xi}=0.

We can now exploit the fact that the even sector of two Fermionic
modes is given by the direct sum of the two even sectors of the single
Fermionic modes and the two odd sectors, namely:

\mathsf{A}_{0,1}=(\mathsf{A}^{0}_{0}\boxtimes\mathsf{A}^{0}_{1})\oplus(\mathsf{A}^{1}_{0}\boxtimes\mathsf{A}^{1}_{1}).

This means that
\mathsf{E}
decomposes as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}=(\mathsf{E}_{00},\mathsf{E}_{11}),

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{00}=[\mathsf{A}_{\xi}\boxtimes Y,X\boxtimes\mathsf{A}_{\xi}],

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{11}=\mathsf{A}_{\xi}\boxtimes[Y,\mathsf{C}_{\xi}]+[\mathsf{B}_{\xi},X]\boxtimes A_{\xi},

in which each component should vanish. We then have:

[\mathsf{A}_{\xi}\boxtimes Y,X\boxtimes\mathsf{A}_{\xi}]=0.

(62)

Writing
\mathsf{A}_{\xi}=\vec{p}\cdot\vec{\sigma}
with
\vec{p}\cdot\hat{z}=0
, we get:

p_{x}(\vec{p}\times\hat{y})I\boxtimes Z+p_{y}(\vec{p}\times\hat{x})Z\boxtimes I=0,

but this is true
iff

\vec{p}=0
and thus
\mathsf{A}_{\xi}=0
for
every even operator
\xi
, which means:

\mathcal{T}(\xi)=X\boxtimes\mathsf{B}_{\xi}\boxtimes I+I\boxtimes\mathsf{C}_{\xi}\boxtimes Y+I\boxtimes\mathsf{D}_{\xi}\boxtimes I.

(63)

Notice that with this choice
\mathsf{E}_{11}=0
. Computing the operators

\mathsf{F}
and
\mathsf{G}
in Eq. (
61
) for this new form of the
evolution, we have:

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}=[\mathsf{B}_{\xi},X]\boxtimes\mathsf{B}_{\xi},\qquad\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{C}_{\xi}\boxtimes[Y,\mathsf{C}_{\xi}].

Imposing
\mathsf{F}=\mathsf{G}=0
and remembering that
\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{C}
are even
operators, we get
\mathsf{B}_{\xi},\mathsf{C}_{\xi}\propto I
. Thus we can write the general form of the evolution as:

\mathcal{T}(\xi)=b_{\xi}X\boxtimes I\boxtimes I+c_{\xi}I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y+I\boxtimes\mathsf{D}_{\xi}\boxtimes I,

(64)

with
b_{\xi},c_{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}
. Finally, we can compute:

\mathsf{H}=b_{\xi}\{D_{\xi},X\}\boxtimes I+c_{\xi}I\boxtimes\{D_{\xi},Y\}=0.

Since
D_{\xi}
cannot anticommute with both
X
and
Y
, the solutions of
the previous equations yields the constraints in
Eq. (
59
).

\,\blacksquare

Lemma 13
.

The FCA
\mathcal{T}
given in Eq. (
58
) with
b_{\xi}c_{\xi}=0
is
not compatible with the assumption of having
\mathcal{E}_{L}\simeq\mathcal{E}_{R}
both
generated by odd operators.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we study the case where
b_{\xi}=0
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{b_{\xi}=0}(\xi)=I\boxtimes D_{\xi}\boxtimes I+c_{\xi}I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y.

Considering the action over another odd operator
\eta
such that

\{\xi,\eta\}=0
and assuming
D_{\eta}\neq 0
we get to one of the
following equations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\xi)=I\boxtimes D_{\xi}\boxtimes I+c_{\xi}I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\eta)=I\boxtimes D_{\eta}\boxtimes I+c_{\eta}I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y,

or

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\xi)=I\boxtimes D_{\xi}\boxtimes I+c_{\xi}I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\eta)=I\boxtimes D_{\eta}\boxtimes I+b_{\eta}X\boxtimes I\boxtimes I.

The first case is ruled out by the choice
\mathcal{E}_{L}\simeq\mathcal{E}_{R}
because

\mathcal{E}_{L}\simeq I
, while
\mathcal{E}_{R}
is generated by Y. Let us focus on the
second case. Lemma
2
tells us that if

\{[\mathcal{T}(\xi),\mathcal{T}(\eta)]\}=0
then the whole support algebras should
graded commute. This means that we can combine the elements of

\mathcal{T}(\xi)
and
\mathcal{T}(\eta)
and impose the graded-commutation of the
operators

\displaystyle(I\boxtimes D_{\xi}\boxtimes I)c_{\xi}(I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y)=c_{\xi}(I\boxtimes D_{\xi}\boxtimes Y),

\displaystyle(I\boxtimes D_{\eta}\boxtimes I)b_{\eta}(X\boxtimes I\boxtimes I)=b_{\eta}(X\boxtimes D_{\eta}\boxtimes I)

i.e.:

\{[I\boxtimes D_{\xi}\boxtimes Y,X\boxtimes D_{\eta}\boxtimes I]\}=X\boxtimes[D_{\xi},D_{\eta}]\boxtimes Y=0.

This means
D_{\xi}=D_{\eta}\eqqcolon D
. Imposing

\{[\mathcal{T}(\xi)\boxtimes I,I\boxtimes\mathcal{T}(\xi)]\}=\{[\mathcal{T}(\eta)\boxtimes I,I\boxtimes\mathcal{T}(\eta)]\}=0
and exploiting again Lemma
2

for the operator of the support
\{[I\boxtimes D,I\boxtimes Y]\}
and

\{[X\boxtimes I,D\boxtimes I]\}
we have:

\displaystyle D\boxtimes\{D,Y\}=\{D,X\}\boxtimes D=0.

This is clearly compatible only with
D=0
. Finally, we want to analyze the case:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\xi)=I\boxtimes D_{\xi}\boxtimes I+c_{\xi}I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\eta)=c_{\eta}I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y+b_{\eta}X\boxtimes I\boxtimes I.

Imposing

\{[\mathcal{T}(\xi)\boxtimes I,I\boxtimes\mathcal{T}(\eta)]\}=\{[\mathcal{T}(\eta)\boxtimes I,I\boxtimes\mathcal{T}(\xi)]\}=0
and exploiting again Lemma
2

for the operators

c_{\xi}b_{\xi}\{[I\boxtimes I\boxtimes D_{\eta},X\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y]\},\qquad\{[D_{\eta}\boxtimes I\boxtimes I,X\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y]\}

we have:

\displaystyle X\boxtimes\{D_{\eta},Y\}=\{D_{\eta},X\}\boxtimes Y=0.

Again, this is only possible if
D_{\eta}=0
, which concludes the proof.

\,\blacksquare

We are ready to prove the Main Theorem
3
.

Proof.

Thanks to the last lemma, we can impose
D_{\xi}=D_{\eta}=0
in Eq. (
58
), i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\xi)=b_{\xi}X\boxtimes I\boxtimes I+c_{\xi}I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\eta)=b_{\eta}X\boxtimes I\boxtimes I+c_{\eta}I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y.

Imposing
\{[\mathcal{T}(\xi),\mathcal{T}(\eta)]\}=0
, we get:

\displaystyle(b_{\xi}b_{\eta}+c_{\xi}c_{\eta})I\boxtimes I\boxtimes I=0.

This means that if we arrange

in vectors

\displaystyle v_{\xi/\eta}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{\xi/\eta}\\

then it holds that
v_{\xi}\cdot v_{\eta}=0
. However, we can always choose

\xi,\eta

and
v_{\eta}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}
so that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\xi)=I\boxtimes I\boxtimes Y,\qquad\mathcal{T}(\eta)=X\boxtimes I\boxtimes I,

\,\blacksquare

B.1

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{0}=\mathcal{T}_{0}\circ\mathcal{U},
