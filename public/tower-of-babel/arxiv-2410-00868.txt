Fine-Grained Gradient Restriction: A Simple Approach for Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting
Bo Liu, Mao Ye, Peter Stone, Qiang Liu

Abstract
A fundamental challenge in continual learning is to balance the trade-off between learning new tasks and remembering the previously acquired knowledge. Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) ( Lopez-Paz & Ranzato 2017 ) achieves this balance by utilizing a subset of past training samples to restrict the update direction of the model parameters. In this work, we start by analyzing a often overlooked hyper-parameter in GEM, the memory strength, which boosts the empirical performance by further constraining the update direction. We show that memory strength is effective mainly because it improves GEM’s generalization ability and therefore leads to a more favorable trade-off. By this finding, we propose two approaches that more flexibly constrain the update direction. Our methods are able to achieve uniformly better Pareto Frontiers of remembering old and learning new knowledge than using the memory strength. We further propose a computationally efficient method to approximately solve the optimization problem with more constraints.



1
Introduction

Intelligent creatures can learn continually over their lifetime,
which helps them survive in the constantly changing world.
Continual learning (CL) studies the very problem of learning
continually over a sequence of different tasks with a limited memory.
The main challenge of CL is to strike a balance between remembering the previously acquired knowledge and learning new knowledge for new tasks.
Given a parameterized model, e.g. a deep neural network, at one extreme, directly learning on a new task with gradient descent will often drastically degrade the model’s performance on previously learned tasks.
This phenomenon is called
catastrophic forgetting
.
At the other extreme, the model will obviously not forget the learned task if we fix its parameters, but as a consequence, it will not learn any future task.
This phenomenon is called
intransigence

(
Chaudhry et al. 2018
)
, i.e., the inability to update knowledge for a new task. With a limited memory, CL algorithms applied on a fixed-size model must seek an optimal trade-off between conflicting forgetting and intransigence.

Figure 1:
When progressing from task 1 to task 2, GEM computes
\tilde{g}_{1}
, the gradient on the episodic memory stored for task
1
. Then GEM ensures that the update direction is within the half-space defined by
\tilde{g}_{1}
. We propose to decompose
\tilde{g}_{1}
into multiple vectors, i.e.
\tilde{g}_{1}^{1},\tilde{g}_{1}^{2}
. The search space then becomes more constrained based on how we divide
\tilde{g}_{1}
.

One popular method to trade-off forgetting and intransigence in CL is gradient episodic memory (GEM)
(
Lopez-Paz & Ranzato 2017
)
. Intuitively, at each iteration of gradient descent, GEM searches an update direction in a space where its inner products with the gradients on past tasks are non-negative (See Figure
1
). Thus every direction in that search space does not increase the loss of past tasks. Within that search space, the update direction that minimizes its distance to the gradient of the currently learning task is used to update the model. As we are not able to store every observed data, the gradients on past tasks are approximated by the gradients calculated on sub-sampled data points from each of the prior tasks, which are called the episodic memories.

Ideally, with infinite size episodic memories, we can expect that GEM can learn without forgetting. However, in practice, as episodic memories are limited in size, GEM only ensures that loss computed on the episodic memory will not increase, which not necessarily implies the loss on past tasks will not increase. In other words, there exists a generalization gap due to the fact that episodic memories cannot cover the entire observed data.

In this work, we find that this generalization error can be effectively reduced by enforcing stronger and non-trivial constraints on the search space of the update direction. Specifically, we propose to divide the stored data points in the episodic memory into multiple splits and form new gradient constraints on each split, or separately apply GEM constraints to different parts of the underlying model parameters.
Our method is able to reduce catastrophic forgetting without exacerbating intransigence much and therefore achieves a better trade-off of them. To mitigate the overhead from introducing new constraints, we propose a computationally efficient method to approximately solve the optimization problem with more constraints. We call our algorithm modular GEM (mGEM). We demonstrate that mGEM can consistently achieve better Pareto Frontiers of remembering old and learning new knowledge than GEM, on both standard CL benchmarks and multi-domain datasets.

Background

In the continual learning problem, the learning machine will observe a sequence of data

(x_{1},t_{1},y_{1}),...,(x_{i},t_{i},y_{i}),...,(x_{n},t_{n},y_{n}),

where

x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are the input features and the corresponding
labels, and
t_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
is an integer task descriptor
identifying the associated task.
For each task
t
, we assume the
input features and labels come from a joint distribution
(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{t}
.
For different tasks
t
and
t^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{D}_{t}
and
\mathcal{D}_{t^{\prime}}
are different in general.
We adopt the standard assumption
(
Lopez-Paz & Ranzato 2017
)
that every triplet
(x_{i},t_{i},y_{i})
is
locally
identically and independently distributed, i.e.
(x_{i},y_{i})\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}_{t_{i}}
.
Our goal is to construct a model
f_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
parameterized
by
\theta
that is able to accurately predict
y
given
x
, while
f
can only go over the stream of data in an online fashion with a limited memory.

The key challenge of continual learning comes from the fact that examples are not drawn i.i.d. from a fixed probability distribution across different tasks.
In particular, the model loses its access to prior data once it starts to learn on a new task. The consequence of this non i.i.d.
pattern is often catastrophic forgetting, i.e. learning new tasks may significantly hurt the learner’s performance on old tasks. To achieve better overall performance, the model needs to simultaneously prevent catastrophic forgetting and enable learning of new knowledge. Therefore, continual learning algorithms can be seen as attempting to improve the
Pareto frontier
of these two conflicting objectives. In this paper, following
(
Riemer et al. 2018
)
, we measure the ability of learning new task by forward transfer and the ability of improving the performance of learned past task after learning a new task by backward transfer (see Eq
12
for details).

Notation

We denote the integer set
\{1,2,....n\}
as
[n]
. The
\ell_{2}
vector norm is denoted as
\left\|\cdot\right\|
. Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we define the loss over
\mathcal{D}
by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\ell(f;x,y)
, where
\ell(f;x,y)
is the loss of the learning machine
f
on data pair
(x,y)
. For any two vectors
\boldsymbol{h}
,
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}
with the same dimension, we denote
\boldsymbol{h}\geq\boldsymbol{h}
if
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\geq\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}_{i}
for every coordinate
i
. We denote the Rademacher complexity with sample size
n
of set
S
as
\mathfrak{R}_{n}[S]
. Given any set
S
, we denote its cardinality as
|S|
.

2.1
Gradient of Episodic Memory

In practice, the learning machine is usually trained with gradient descent. Given the loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]
on some dataset
\mathcal{D}
, suppose the learning machine is parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, using the gradient descent, the parameter is updated iteratively by

\boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}-\epsilon\boldsymbol{g},

where
\boldsymbol{g}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}
is the gradient of loss at current iteration and
\epsilon
is the learning rate. Naively applying gradient descent for the continual learning problem is sub-optimal due to the catastrophic forgetting. To alleviate forgetting, Gradient Episodic Memory
(GEM)
(
Lopez-Paz & Ranzato 2017
)
searches a different updating direction
\boldsymbol{z}
that does not increase the loss on prior tasks and decreases the loss on the current task as much as possible.

To achieve this, before learning a task
t
, GEM allocates an “episodic memory”
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}
, a subset of observed examples from
\mathcal{D}_{s}
, for every past task
s\in[t-1]
. At each iteration, GEM replaces
\boldsymbol{g}
with a modified update direction
\boldsymbol{z}
by solving the following constrained optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{z}}\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\|^{2}\ \ \text{s.t.\ }\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq 0,\ \forall s<t.

(1)

Here we define
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]
, the gradient computed for the current task
t
. Moreover,
\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]
is the gradient computed on the episodic memory
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}
from the past task
s
. Finally, the model is updated with the solution
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
of (
1
) in place of the vanilla gradient

\boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}-\epsilon\boldsymbol{z}.

To see the intuition behind Equation (
1
), with the small learning rate
\epsilon
, using the Taylor expansion, we obtain that for any prior task
s
,

\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right]-\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}-\epsilon\boldsymbol{z}}\right]\approx\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right),

(2)

and for the current task
t
,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right]-\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}-\epsilon\boldsymbol{z}}\right]\approx\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right).

(3)

If we ignore the smaller order term
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, constraints in Equation (
1
) ensures that
\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle\geq 0
. Meanwhile, the objective in Equation (
1
) ensures that
\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle
is large.

The decision variable
\boldsymbol{z}
in the primal problem (
1
) has the same size of the model’s parameters, which can be millions for a deep neural network. Therefore, in practice, GEM solves the corresponding dual problem

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\textbf{G}}{}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\right\|^{2},\ \ \text{s.t.\ }\boldsymbol{v}\geq\boldsymbol{0}.

(4)

Here
\hat{\textbf{G}}=-[\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{1},...,\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{t-1}]^{\top}
. Now the dual decision variable
\boldsymbol{v}
only has
t-1
dimensions, and (
4
) can be efficiently solved by standard quadratic programming solvers. Based on the strong duality, the solution to the primal problem is given by
\hat{\textbf{G}}{}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}+\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
where
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
is the solution of (
4
).

Memory Strength

In actual implementation, GEM introduces an extra “memory strength” hyper-parameter,
which yields an optimization with a stronger constraint:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\textbf{G}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\right\|^{2},\ \ \text{s.t.\ }\boldsymbol{v}\geq\boldsymbol{q},

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{q}\geq\bm{0}
is called the memory strength. Although it was only treated as an implementation detail in the paper, we find that the memory strength can boost up the performance of GEM. For example, for common benchmarks in CL (Section
6.1
), memory strength improves the average accuracy after learning on all tasks by
\bm{1.85}
(MNIST Permutations) and
\bm{1.55}
(MNIST Rotations).

2.2
The Pareto Frontier of Forward and Backward Transfer

Our analysis in Section
4
shows that the primal problem of (
5
) is actually equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\|^{2}\ \text{s.t.}\ \left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{s},\ \forall s\in[t-1],

(6)

where
\gamma_{s}
are some positive constants depending on
q
. GEM with larger
q
puts a stronger constraint on
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle
, which enforces its value to be larger and thus increases the backward transfer, e.g. lower loss on past tasks. In Section
4
, we show that this stronger constraint is important because it mitigates the generalization gap caused by using a subset of the whole data points as the episodic memory. On the other hand, with stronger constraints, the feasible set becomes smaller and thus returns a larger solution to the objective, e.g. loss on the new task is reduced less. Figure
2
(blue line) provides an example of such trade-off. Each line is obtained by varying the memory strength
\boldsymbol{q}
.

3
Modular GEM

GEM is able to achieve a good Pareto frontier between backward and forward transfer with the introduced memory strength. Motivated by this, we propose two new simple but effective ways to more flexibly constrain the search space of the update direction, which are shown to achieve an (uniformly) better Pareto frontier compared with GEM with memory strength (see Figure
2
).
GEM computes the gradient of the full model on the entire episodic memory, which results in one constraint per task. Our method splits that gradient into several gradients computed either from different parts of the model or from different divides of the episodic memory. Since essentially we subdivides the gradient into multiple modules, we name our method modular-GEM (mGEM).

Parameter-wise mGEM

Our first scheme is to partition the parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
of neural network
into
D
sub-coordinates:
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\boldsymbol{\theta}^{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{2},...,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{D}]
.
Then we separately apply GEM to each sub-coordinate. We call this method p-mGEM.

\begin{split}&\min_{\boldsymbol{z}}\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\|^{2}\\
\text{s.t.\ }&\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{s}^{d},\ \forall s\in[t-1],\ d\in[D],\end{split}

(7)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{d}}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]
. Notice that all
\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d}
can be computed by back-propagating
once
on the full model.

Data-wise mGEM

Similarly, we can also partition the episodic memory into several
groups:
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}=\cup_{d=1}^{D}\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{s}^{d}
and then separately apply GEM.
We call this method d-mGEM and consider the following problem

\begin{split}&\min_{\boldsymbol{z}}\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\|^{2}\\
\text{s.t.\ }&\left\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{s}^{d},\ \forall s\in[t-1],\ d\in[D],\end{split}

(8)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{s}^{d}}[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]
. For data-wise mGEM, the computation time becomes
D
times longer since we need to separately computes the gradient for each split of the episodic memory.

To summarize, both parameter-wise and data-wise mGEM result in more restricted search space than vanilla GEM when
D>1
, and reduce to GEM when
D=1
. By adjusting the value of
\gamma_{s}^{d}
(or the
\boldsymbol{q}
in the dual problem), mGEM can also incorporate the idea of memory strength.

3.1
mGEM in Practice

Although introducing more constraints naturally leads to more restricted search space and thus helps prevent forgetting, it also introduces computation overhead. Therefore, we propose to approximately solve the optimization problems in (
7
) and (
8
). For each
d
, the optimization corresponding to
d
mimics that of the original GEM method. Therefore we similarly solve the dual problem, which is of the form

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\textbf{G}}{}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{g}\right\|^{2},\ \ \text{s.t.\ }\boldsymbol{v}\geq\boldsymbol{q},~~\text{where}~\boldsymbol{q}>0.

(9)

Instead of solving the optimization exactly with a quadratic programming solver, we propose to solve the optimization approximately in two stages. We first ignore the constraints and solve the optimization in closed-form, then project the solution back to the feasible space. To be specific,

•

In stage one, we solve

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\nu}}\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\textbf{G}}{}^{\top}\bm{\nu}+\boldsymbol{g}\right\|^{2}.

The optimal solution is
\bm{\nu}^{*}=-(\hat{\textbf{G}}\hat{\textbf{G}}{}^{\top})^{-1}\hat{\textbf{G}}\boldsymbol{g}
. Recall that for task
t
,
\hat{\textbf{G}}=-[\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{1},\dots\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{t-1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t-1)\times n}
, where
n
denotes the parameter size of the model
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Here computing the term
(\hat{\textbf{G}}\hat{\textbf{G}}{}^{\top})^{-1}
takes roughly
\mathcal{O}(t^{2}n)
time. As random high dimensional vectors have a near zero inner product, we simplify the calculation by approximating
(\hat{\textbf{G}}\hat{\textbf{G}}{}^{\top})^{-1}
with its diagonal matrix, which results in the solution

\begin{split}\tilde{\bm{\nu}}^{*}&=-\textbf{H}\hat{\textbf{G}}\boldsymbol{g},~\text{where}\\
\textbf{H}&=\begin{bmatrix}(\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{1}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{1})^{-1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&(\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{(t-1)}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{(t-1)})^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

Note that
H
can be efficiently computed in a batch using standard deep learning libraries.

•

In stage two, we project the solution back to the feasible space and ensure that
\boldsymbol{v}\geq\boldsymbol{q}
:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}^{*}=[{\boldsymbol{v}}^{*}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{v}}^{*}_{t-1}]^{\top},\ \ \text{where}\ {\boldsymbol{v}}^{*}_{i}=\max(\tilde{\bm{\nu}}^{*}_{i},{\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}).

To summarize, the final update direction is given by

\boldsymbol{z}=\hat{\textbf{G}}^{\top}\max(-\textbf{H}\hat{\textbf{G}}\boldsymbol{g},~\boldsymbol{q})+\boldsymbol{g}_{t}.

(10)

We then apply Equation (
10
) to all modules considered in mGEM.

4
Analysis

In this section, we provide analysis on why the proposed mGEM is able to improve the performance. We divide this question into three steps. For simplicity, we consider the case that there is only two tasks: a past task
s
that the machine has learned, and the current task
t
that the machine is learning. Generalizing our analysis to an arbitrary finite number of tasks is straightforward.

4.1
The generalization of GEM

Despite the fact that GEM is guaranteed to prevent
\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}
from increasing, GEM is not able to ensure that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{s}}

is not increasing. The reason is that the the episodic memory is
only a subset of observed examples and thus there is a generalization
gap between the loss on
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}
and that on
\mathcal{D}_{s}
. Suppose
that we have
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq\gamma
,
the uniform concentration inequality shows that given any
\delta>0
,
we have, with probability
1-\delta
,
\left\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq\gamma-\Delta
,
where
\Delta
is some bias induced by the complexity of the search space
of
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\delta
.
Specifically, we have the following result.

Assumption 1

For all previous tasks, the true gradient norm on those tasks are bounded:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{s}\right\|_{2,\infty}:=\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{s},\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}};x,y)\right\|<\infty.

Proposition 1

Given some search space
\mathcal{Z}
of
\boldsymbol{z}
, suppose
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
is the solution of the following problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\|^{2}\ \text{s.t.\ }\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq 0,\ \forall s\in[t-1].

Given any
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\right\rangle-\Delta,

\displaystyle\Delta=2\mathfrak{R}_{\left|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}\right|}\left[\mathcal{Z}\right]+\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\|\boldsymbol{z}\right\|\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{s}\right\|_{2,\infty}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/\left|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}\right|},

where
\Delta
is the generalization gap
and
\mathfrak{R}_{\left|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}\right|}\left[\mathcal{Z}\right]
denotes the Rademacher complexity of set
\mathcal{Z}
.

The above Proposition characterizes the worst-case generalization property on the backward transferring. We can see that, to improve the backward transferring, we can increase
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\right\rangle
, reduce the generalization gap, or do both.

4.2
Understanding Memory Strength

Our analysis on the generalization property explains why memory strength is an effective trick in GEM. We start with one of the most natural ways to improve the backward transferring: increasing
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\right\rangle
. By applying a stronger constraint, we consider the problem (
6
) mentioned earlier.
Denote
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=[\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{t-1}]^{\top}
is the given non-negative hyper-parameter. By encouraging
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle
to be strictly positive, we are able to improve the probability of non-negative backward transfer according to Proposition
1
. The dual problem of (
6
) is

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}\left\|\hat{\textbf{G}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\right\|^{2}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v},\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \boldsymbol{v}\geq\boldsymbol{0}.

(11)

Compared with the dual problem (
5
) of GEM with memory strength, (
11
) enforces a positive
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
(memory strength) by adding a regularization term (
-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}
) in the objective. From this perspective, GEM with memory strength is equivalent to problem (
6
). For simplicity, in the rest of the paper, we also refer to (
11
) as GEM with memory strength.

4.3
Worst Case Analysis on Backward Transfer

Following the analysis in Proposition
1
, we know that GEM reduces the generalization gap by encouraging a larger
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle
. Similarly, mGEM also encourages a larger inner product and therefore better generalization. In particular, we have the following proposition

Proposition 2

Suppose
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
is the solution of (
1
) and
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}

is the solution of (
6
) with memory strength
\gamma_{s}
. For parameter-wise GEM, suppose that
\sum_{d=1}^{D}\gamma_{s}^{d}\geq\gamma_{s}

and returns solution
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{pmGEM}}^{*}
. For data-wise GEM,
suppose that
\min_{d\in[D]}\gamma_{s}^{d}\geq\gamma_{s}
, and returns
solution
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{dmGEM}}^{*}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{p-mGEM}}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{}}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{d-mGEM}}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{}}^{*}\right\rangle.

Therefore, in terms of reducing the generalization gap, both p-mGEM and d-mGEM perform better than GEM with memory strength, which further is better than the vanilla GEM.

The above analysis provides a theoretical insight on how mGEM and GEM with memory strength improve the backward transferring through a worse-case analysis.

4.4
mGEM Achieves a Better Pareto Frontier

Compared with GEM with memory strength, mGEM gives two different ways to modify the constraints and thus can achieve a better Pareto frontier on forward and backward transfer. To show this, we apply p-mGEM and d-mGEM with different number of modules and different memory strengths to the Split Cifar100 benchmark. We plot the Pareto-frontier of the local backward and forward transfer. As described in Equation (
2
) and (
3
), we measure the forward transfer by calculating the average value of
\left\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle
. To measure the backward transfer, we consider
\left\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle
, where the gradient on a past task is computed on the full training set rather than on episodic memories. We refer readers to the Appendix for more details. Figure
2
presents the result. We see that the backward transfer is larger when p-mGEM/d-mGEM have more modules and forward transfer is larger with fewer modules. Since the region corresponding to larger backward transfer (and thus less forgetting) is more important for typical CL problems, mGEM gives a clear improvement.
Note that by allowing an adaptive number of modules, mGEM achieves a uniformly better Pareto frontier (yellow line in Figure
2
) than GEM.

5
Related Work

To prevent catastrophic forgetting in deep learning, three main categories of methods have been proposed recently.

Regularization-based methods

Regularization-based methods introduce additional training objectives that constrain the model parameters to stay close to the previously learned ones. Popular methods often adopt a probabilistic perspective and view the old parameter as the prior on the model parameter before learning the new task. Specifically, EWC
(
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
)
uses the Fisher information matrix computed from previous tasks to regularize the learning. PI
(
Zenke et al. 2017
)
computes a local ”importance” measure for each neuron and the update is regularized according to that importance. RWALK
(
Chaudhry et al. 2018
)
and VCL
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
)
use the KL-divergence as the regularization.

Expandable architecture

Instead of regularizing the update direction for a fixed-size model, expandable architecture attempts to maximally re-use part of the parameters learned from previous tasks and introduces new parameters when intransigence happens. PROG-NN
(
Rusu et al. 2016
)
introduces a fixed number of neurons for each layer of the network and learns the lateral connections. DEN
(
Yoon et al. 2017
)
, Learn-to-grow
(
Li et al. 2019
)
and CPG
(
Hung et al. 2019
)
focus on more efficient growth of the network by allowing dynamic expansions.

Memory-based approaches

A simple yet efficient way to prevent forgetting is to store a small subset of samples (i.e. the episodic memory) for memory consolidation. While methods like DGR
(
Shin et al. 2017
)
learn a generative model that can generate artificial memories, they introduce the memory overhead of storing a generative model. On the other hand, GEM
(
Lopez-Paz & Ranzato 2017
)
uses the episodic memory to setup a feasible space to search for new gradient update direction. OGD
(
Farajtabar et al. 2020
)
projects the gradient on the new task to a subspace where the projected gradient does not affect the model’s output on old tasks. MER
(
Riemer et al. 2018
)
is recently proposed to adopt a meta-learning objective to learn to not forget. As the optimal solution from GEM is a linear combination of gradients the current and previous tasks, MEGA
(
Guo et al. 2019
)
studies how to form a better linear combination by considering the task losses. While MEGA also extends GEM, our method is orthogonal to MEGA. Besides studying the usage of episodic memories,
Aljundi et al. 2019
also studies how to optimally select the them.

Our proposed method is closely related to GEM. However, we focus on explaining the trade-off of shrinking the feasible space in which the new update direction is searched. Particularly, besides how to pick and use the episodic memory, we focus on how these stored memories can form a better feasible space such that the found update direction achieves a better Pareto frontier.

6
Experiments

In this section, we first apply mGEM on the standard continual learning benchmarks and show that mGEM can outperform GEM. Then we further apply mGEM to datasets with more non-stationarity where each subtask is from a different domain. We compare mGEM against the following relevant baselines:

•

Single:
a single model that directly learns over the sequence of the task with stochastic gradient descent (SGD). It corresponds to one extreme where the model completely ignores forgetting and focuses only on learning the current task.

•

Independent:
an independent predictor for each task with fewer hidden neurons proportional to the number of tasks.

•

EWC
: Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)
(
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
)
is a popular regularization-based method that adopts the Fisher Information matrix to prevent catastrophic forgetting.

•

GEM
: Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM)
(
Lopez-Paz & Ranzato 2017
)
is what we mainly compare against. GEM uses the episodic memory to compute a feasible space to search for the new update direction.

•

MER
: Meta Episodic Memory
(
Riemer et al. 2018
)
a state-of-the-art episodic memory baseline that adopts meta learning objective to prevent forgetting.

Following
Lopez-Paz & Ranzato 2017
, we report the final retained accuracy (ACC) and the backward transfer of knowledge (BWD) as the main metrics for comparison. Moreover, we also report the average learning accuracy, which is the average accuracy for each task right after it is learned, as a measure of the how much the model can learn forwardly on the new task (FWD). Specifically, assume there are
T
tasks in total and denote
R_{i,j}
as the retained accuracy on task
j
after learning to task
i
, then

\begin{split}&\text{ACC}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}R_{T,i}\\
&\text{BWD}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}R_{T,i}-R_{i,i}\\
&\text{FWD}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}R_{i,i}\\
\end{split}.

(12)

It follows that
\text{ACC}=\text{BWD}+\text{FWD}
, which means BWD and FWD naturally trade-off between each other and therefore better algorithm will result in better ACC.

6.1
Standard Continual Learning Benchmarks

We first apply mGEM to the three standard continual learning benchmarks used in
Lopez-Paz & Ranzato 2017
.
MNIST Permutations
consists of a sequence of 20 tasks, each of which is generated by a fixed permutation of the MNIST pixels.
MNIST Rotations
is another variant of 20 consecutive tasks based on MNIST
(
LeCun et al. 1998
)
. Each task is generated by rotating the MNIST digits by a fixed angle between 0 and 180 degrees. Therefore, both MNIST Permutations and MNIST Rotations have 20 10-class classification tasks.
Split CIFAR100
splits the 100 classes from CIFAR dataset
(
Krizhevsky et al. 2009
)
into 20 subtasks, each is a 5-class classification task. For MNIST Permutations and MNIST Rotations, we test on a two-layer neural network with 100 hidden units. Moreover, we adopt the original setting from
(
Lopez-Paz & Ranzato 2017
)
where 256 random samples from each task is stored as the episodic memory. For each task, we train the model for 500 iterations with a batch size of 10 before progressing to the next task. For Split CIFAR100, we test on a larger network consists of a stack of 5 convolutional layers. We sample 64 samples from each task as the episodic memory, and we train each task for 100 iterations with a batch size of 25. For both GEM and mGEM, we pick the best
\boldsymbol{q}
from
\{0.0,0.05,0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5,0.8,1.0\}
using a validation set. We provide more detailed information of the architecture and hyperparameters in the Appendix.

Table
1
shows the result on the three benchmarks. Note that on MNIST Permutations and MNIST Rotations, MER achieves the best final accuracy. But after a comprehensive search of hyper-parameters, we find MER performs much worse on the Split CIFAR100 dataset. We think there are mainly two reasons. First, as the tasks in MNIST Permutations and MNIST Rotations are highly correlated, MER’s meta training objective helps improve the zero-shot generalization so that MER can achieve higher FWD. But the tasks within the Split CIFAR100 dataset have less correlations so the meta learning objective becomes less effective. Secondly, meta learning is known to be data hungry. Therefore, on dataset like Split CIFAR100 which is more challenging but has fewer samples, it becomes harder for a meta learning algorithm like MER to learn. On the other hand, notice that on all datasets, mGEM outperforms GEM and improves BWD, which consolidates that adding more restrictions helps the model forget less. This effect is less prominent on the two MNIST datasets because of the size of the architecture. But on split CIFAR100, mGEM improves the average accuracy by
\bm{1.93}
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we analyze how the Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) balances remembering old and learning new knowledge. Specifically, we show that restricting the search space of the update direction essentially reduces the generalization gap caused by limited size of episodic memory. Based on the analysis, we propose two novel ways of introducing additional gradient restrictions to achieve better Pareto Frontiers. Experiments on standard continual learning benchmarks and multi-domain datasets demonstrate that the proposed methods outperform GEM consistently.

A.1
Details about Figure 2

In the experiment section (Section 6), we adopt the conventional metrics used in previous continual learning literature to evaluate the performance of different methods. These metrics, e.g. ACC, BWD, and FWD, are all computed based on the learning accuracy, which might not directly correspond to the training objective, which is based on the inner product between gradients. As a result, in Figure 2, we directly plot the trade-off between forward and backward transfer using the computed inner products between the update direction and the gradient on the current and previous tasks. Specifically, in Figure 2, we calculate these inner products after the model learns over the second task, i.e.
s=1
and
t=2
. Therefore, the x-axis corresponds to the average inner products between the update direction
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{s}
, and similarly the y-axis corresponds to the average inner products between the update direction
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
.

B.1
Proof of Proposition 1

Notice that
\left\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{s}}\left\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},x,y),\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle

and
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}\left\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},x,y),\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle
.
Define
R=\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\|\boldsymbol{z}\right\|\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{s}\right\|_{2,\infty}

and consider the function class

H=\left\{h_{\boldsymbol{z}}(x,y)=\frac{\left\langle\boldsymbol{z},\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},x,y)\right\rangle}{R}:\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}\right\}.

Notice that
\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z},x,y}\left\langle\boldsymbol{z},\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},x,y)\right\rangle\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\|\boldsymbol{z}\right\|\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{s}\right\|_{2,\infty}.

Using uniform concentration inequality, given any probability, for
any
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have, for
any

\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{s}}h_{\boldsymbol{z}}(x,y)\geq\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}}h_{\boldsymbol{z}}(x,y)+2\mathfrak{R}_{\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}[H]+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}},

where
\mathfrak{R}_{m}[H]
is the expected Rademacher complexity of
H

defined by

\mathfrak{R}_{m}[H]=\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma_{i}}\left[\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\left\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ell(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},x_{i},y_{i}),\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle/R\right].

Multiple
R
on both sides gives that

\left\langle\boldsymbol{g}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle+2\mathfrak{R}_{\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}[\mathcal{Z}]+R\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}},

where

which gives the desired result.

\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\right\rangle<\gamma_{s}

and thus
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{s}>\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{}}^{*}\right\rangle.

In both cases, we have
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{}}^{*}\right\rangle
.

Proof of
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{p-mGEM}}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle

We then prove
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{p-mGEM}}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle
.
We consider two cases. Case 1:
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle=\gamma_{s}
.
In this case, we have
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{p-mGEM}}^{*}\right\rangle=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{p-mGEM}}^{*d}\right\rangle\geq\sum_{d=1}^{D}\gamma_{s}^{d}\geq\gamma_{s}.

Case 2:
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle>\gamma_{s}
.
Notice that this problem has strong duality and thus using KKT conditions,
we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}-\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\|^{2}+\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\left(\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle-\gamma_{s}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\left(\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle-\gamma_{s}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

Notice that as
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle>\gamma_{s}
,
we have
v^{*}=0
and this implies that
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}=g_{t}
.
For each
d\in[D]
, if
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*d}\right\rangle\leq\gamma_{s}^{d}

(here we partite
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}
by
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}=[\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*1},...,\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*D}]
),
then we have
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{p-mGEM}}^{*d}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{s}^{d}\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*d}\right\rangle
.
If
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*d}\right\rangle>\gamma_{s}^{d}
,
then we know
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*d}=g_{t}^{d}
is also the solution
of

\min_{\boldsymbol{z}^{d}}\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}^{d}-\boldsymbol{z}^{d}\right\|^{2}\text{s.t.}\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d},\boldsymbol{z}^{d}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{s}^{d}.

Using Lemma 1 we know that
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*d}=\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{p-mGEM}}^{*d}
.
For this case, we thus conclude that
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{p-mGEM}}^{*d}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*d}\right\rangle
.

Proof of
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{d-mGEM}}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle
.

We now prove
\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{d-mGEM}}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle
.
We consider two cases. Case 1:
\left\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{s}^{d}

for all
d\in[D]
. In this case, using similar argument, we can show
that
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}=\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{d-mGEM}}^{*}
. Case 2:
\left\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle<\gamma_{s}^{d}

for some
d\in[D]
. In this case

\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\right\rangle\leq\gamma_{s}\leq\min_{d\in[D]}\gamma_{s}^{d}\leq\min_{d\in[D]}\left\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s}^{d},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{d-mGEM}}^{*}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}_{s},\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{d-mGEM}}^{*}\right\rangle.

Combine the two cases, we have the desired result.

Proof of the generalization gap statement

For GEM with memory strength
\gamma_{s}
, we define the following
set

\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},n}(\gamma_{s})=\cup_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}\left\{z:\left\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}],\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{s}\right\}.

Notice that GEM without memory strength is the case
\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},n}(0)
.
Here
\cup_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}
denotes the union of all possible

\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}
with
n
i.i.d. training points. Similarly,
for p-mGEM and d-mGEM we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\text{p-mGEM},n}(\gamma_{s})

\displaystyle=\cup_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}\left\{\boldsymbol{z}:\left\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{d}}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}],\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{s}^{d},\ \forall d\in[d]\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\text{d-mGEM},n}(\gamma_{s})

\displaystyle=\cup_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}\left\{\boldsymbol{z}:\left\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}}[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}],\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\geq\gamma_{s}^{d},\ \forall d\in[d]\right\}.

Notice that we have
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},\left|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}\right|}(0)
,

\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{MS}}^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},\left|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}\right|}(\gamma_{s})
,

\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{p-mGEM}}^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\text{p-mGEM},\left|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}\right|}(\gamma_{s})

and
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{d-mGEM}}^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\text{d-mGEM},\left|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{s}\right|}(\gamma_{s})
.
Also notice that
\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},n}(\gamma_{s})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},n}(0)
,

\mathcal{Z}_{\text{p-mGEM},n}(\gamma_{s})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},n}(\gamma_{s})

and
\mathcal{Z}_{\text{d-mGEM},n}(\gamma_{s})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},n}(\gamma_{s})
.
This implies that

\mathfrak{R}_{\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}[\mathcal{Z}_{\text{p-mGEM},\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}(\gamma_{s})],\ \mathfrak{R}_{\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}[\mathcal{Z}_{\text{d-mGEM},\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}(\gamma_{s})]\leq\mathfrak{R}_{\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}[\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}(\gamma_{s})]\leq\mathfrak{R}_{\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}[\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}(0)].

Also we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\text{p-mGEM},\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}(\gamma_{s})}\left\|\boldsymbol{z}\right\|,\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\text{d-mGEM},\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}(\gamma_{s})}\left\|\boldsymbol{z}\right\|\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}(\gamma_{s})}\left\|\boldsymbol{z}\right\|\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\text{MS},\left|\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{s}\right|}(0)}\left\|\boldsymbol{z}\right\|.

This gives the desired result.
