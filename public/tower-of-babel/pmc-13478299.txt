The role of teacher-student relationships in mediating the impact of peer relationships and grit on student engagement
Authors: Ge Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Student engagement is a key predictor of academic success in EFL contexts, yet a network of interpersonal and intrapersonal factors shapes it. Among these, peer relationships, grit, and the teacher–student relationship are increasingly recognized as central to explaining why some students participate more actively and persist in language learning than others, particularly in Chinese EFL classrooms. This study investigates how peer relationships and grit are associated with student engagement in Chinese EFL learning, with a specific focus on the mediating role of the teacher–student relationship. It examines (a) the direct effects of peer relationships and grit on the teacher–student relationship, (b) the direct effect of the teacher–student relationship on student engagement, and (c) whether the teacher–student relationship mediates the links between peer relationships and student engagement. Participants were 486 Chinese EFL students who completed a questionnaire battery measuring peer relationships, grit, teacher–student relationships, and student engagement. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to validate the measurement model, and structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to test the hypothesized direct and indirect paths among the constructs. SEM analysis revealed that both peer relationships (beta = 0.418, p < 0.001$) and grit \beta = 0.396, p < 0.001) exerted significant positive effects on the teacher–student relationship. In turn, the teacher–student relationship significantly predicted student engagement (beta = 0.567, p < 0.001). Mediation analyses demonstrated that the teacher–student relationship fully mediates the link between peer relationships and student engagement, while it partially mediates the relationship between grit and student engagement. Theoretically, these findings position the teacher–student relationship as the primary conduit through which peer support and personal grit are translated into classroom engagement. This extends social capital theory by demonstrating that intrapersonal and interpersonal resources are not disparate drivers of engagement, but are channeled through the quality of instructor-student rapport. These results suggest that for educators and policymakers in the Chinese EFL context, fostering high-quality teacher–student relationships is the most effective strategy for amplifying the benefits of peer support and student grit.

Introduction

Student engagement is a critical factor in academic success, impacting students’ motivation, learning outcomes, and overall educational progress1,2. Given its significance, research has extensively examined the predictors of engagement, with growing recognition of the pivotal roles played by peer relationships, grit, and the teacher–student relationship3,4.

Peer relationships are essential for learners’ social and emotional growth5,6. Positive interactions with peers contribute to a sense of belonging and promote pro-social behaviors, fostering a more inclusive classroom environment7. Furthermore, grit—defined as perseverance and passion for long-term goals—has been identified as a favorable predictor of student engagement8,9. Students with higher levels of grit demonstrate greater persistence in their learning endeavors, which often leads them to actively seek assistance and establish positive rapport with their teachers10,11. Finally, the teacher–student relationship is widely regarded as a primary engine for engagement; the quality of support, trust, and communication between instructors and learners directly influences students’ willingness to participate in the learning process12–14.

We recognize that other factors (e.g., instructional practices, motivation, perceived competence, communication anxiety) also predict language learning engagement15–18. We do not claim peer relationships, grit, and teacher-student relationship are the only or most important predictors. We focus on them for two reasons. First, they cover three distinct sources – interpersonal (peers, teacher) and intrapersonal (grit). Prior EFL research has mostly examined them in isolation or as direct predictors, but not whether the teacher-student relationship mediates the effects of peer support and grit on engagement. This mediation pathway has not been tested in Chinese EFL. Second, while these three are well established in general education, very few studies have combined them in a single model for language learners. Thus, our gap is not discovering new predictors, but testing a novel mechanism – how these three work together through the teacher-student bond.

Despite established links between these variables and academic outcomes, the literature is hindered by two significant limitations. First, research often treats intrapersonal traits (grit) and interpersonal factors (peer and teacher-student relationships) in isolation, failing to examine their interaction within a unified framework. Second, empirical evidence regarding the mechanisms linking these factors—particularly within the Chinese EFL context—remains fragmented. While the influence of teachers and peers is recognized, the teacher–student relationship is rarely tested as a mechanism that channels the effects of grit and peer support into student engagement. Crucially, the possibility that grit influences engagement indirectly through teacher rapport remains under-examined, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of high-stakes EFL environments, where relational harmony is paramount.

The teacher–student relationship is conceptualized here as a mediator rather than a moderator to reflect the transactional nature of the classroom. While moderation would treat this relationship merely as a condition that alters the influence of grit or peer support, mediation frames it as an active process. Within this framework, grit and peer support function as foundational inputs that facilitate positive rapport. Students with higher intrapersonal and social resources navigate classroom interactions more effectively, cultivating stronger teacher–student bonds. Consequently, this bond serves as the essential communicative bridge, operationalizing personal and social assets into sustained classroom engagement.

Three established theories support this directional ordering. First, social capital theory19 distinguishes between “bonding capital” (trust and support from peers) and “linking capital” (connections with authority figures, such as teachers). In Chinese EFL classrooms, peer support alone rarely translates into academic effort unless the teacher validates it. Second, self-determination theory20 holds that students’ need for relatedness is primarily satisfied by warm, responsive teachers, not by peers. Thus, peer relationships and grit are expected to influence engagement mainly through the teacher-student bond. Third, social cognitive theory21 suggests that students model their behavior on credible adults. A gritty student who seeks help from teachers is more likely to be noticed and rewarded, further strengthening the teacher-student relationship. We acknowledge that reciprocal effects are possible – for example, engaged students may also build better relationships with teachers.

This study addresses these gaps by proposing and testing a structural model that integrates peer relationships, grit, and the teacher–student relationship. By doing so, it determines whether the teacher–student relationship functions as a mediator between these antecedents and student engagement in the Chinese EFL landscape. The objectives of this study are twofold:

Literature review

Student engagement

A large body of research on language learning has identified multiple factors that shape student engagement, including instructional practices, motivation, perceived competence, and communication anxiety15–18. Our study does not claim that peer relationships, grit, and teacher-student relationships are the only or the most important predictors. Instead, we focus on them because they represent three distinct sources of influence – interpersonal (peers, teacher) and intrapersonal (grit) – that have rarely been examined together in a single mediation model within EFL contexts. Prior studies have mostly tested these factors in isolation or as direct predictors. What is missing is an empirical test of whether the teacher-student relationship acts as a mechanism that channels the effects of peer support and grit into engagement. This study fills that gap by testing a novel mediation pathway in the Chinese EFL setting.

In recent years, L2 researchers have pivoted toward understanding student engagement as a dynamic, multifaceted construct18,22–24 that is deeply intertwined with affective variables15–17. While general education research originally proposed three dimensions—behavioral, cognitive, and emotional1—contemporary EFL research emphasizes the situational nature of these dimensions. Emotional engagement involves positive emotional disposition and interest25,26, behavioral engagement refers to active classroom participation23, and cognitive engagement relates to intellectual commitment and concentration1,27. Recent studies among Chinese EFL undergraduates suggest that these engagement levels are often moderate and heavily influenced by control-value appraisals and academic emotions28. Specifically, positive academic emotions serve as crucial mediators that convert high value-appraisals into sustained engagement28. Expanding on this emotional architecture, recent empirical work has demonstrated that the broader classroom social climate (CSC) promotes engagement through the sequential mediating roles of academic buoyancy and foreign language enjoyment (FLE)29. This suggests that a supportive social environment provides the emotional buoyancy necessary for students to remain engaged even when facing academic challenges.

The intrapersonal dimension of engagement in language learning is increasingly linked to L2 grit. Although general education research has long associated grit with academic persistence8, recent EFL-specific inquiries highlight that L2 grit—comprising consistency of interest and perseverance of effort—serves as a primary engine for engagement30,31. In the Iranian and Chinese EFL contexts, L2 grit has been found to partially mediate the impact of growth mindsets and the “ideal L2 self” on student engagement30. Furthermore, while perseverance of effort directly predicts engagement, the relationship is often bolstered by foreign language enjoyment (FLE)31.

Equally vital is the interpersonal context, specifically the classroom social climate (CSC) and the teacher-student relationship (TSR). While early studies in middle schools found that peer support promotes engagement32, current L2 research suggests that the social environment operates through emotional pathways. For example, a positive CSC and high levels of FLE significantly predict engagement among L2 learners33. Crucially, the perceived TSR and a student’s growth mindset act as powerful predictors of engagement, a process that is frequently facilitated by the presence of positive emotions like enjoyment34. By integrating these perspectives, the current study investigates how the TSR provides the relational infrastructure necessary to translate both peer support and individual grit into active, multidimensional engagement.

Peer relationships

Adolescents interact extensively with same-aged peers, and these relationships significantly shape their self-perceptions and understanding of others35,36. Scholars have emphasized the multidimensional nature of peer relationships, noting both positive and negative aspects within dyads and groups36,37. According to Hupp and Jewell38, positive peer interactions include friendship quality, emotional bonding, and peer acceptance, whereas negative interactions involve bullying, rejection, neglect, and victimization39.

Peer relationships also play a pivotal role in developing social skills, emotional regulation, and self-identity11,40. Rubin et al.37 assert that peer interactions equip children with abilities and experiences that affect their social, emotional, and cognitive functioning. For example, Ladd and Pettit41 found that children with positive peer relationships showed higher social competence, academic achievement, and emotional well-being than those with negative or no peer relationships.

Recent research has highlighted the importance of peer relationships in academic settings. Fischer and Rode42 pointed to their instrumental role in promoting academic achievement, while Lee et al.40 emphasized their facilitative effect on information exchange among students. Gristy43 added that peer relationships are key contributors to student engagement and overall well-being. To nurture these connections, Warner et al.44 recommended peer mentoring, coaching, and group work as strategies to foster peer support and academic growth. Vygotsky’s sociocultural theory45 provides the foundational lens for this study, as it posits that cognitive and affective development are inherently social processes. In this framework, peers are not merely external influences but active participants in the “scaffolding” of a learner’s engagement46,47. This sociocultural perspective aligns with Social Capital Theory19, where peer networks provide the “bonding capital”—the trust and emotional security—necessary for students to take risks in a language-learning environment. As active partners, peers can offer guidance, feedback, and support, thereby enriching each other’s learning experiences47.

Positive peer interactions foster students’ sense of belonging, motivation, and cooperation, which in turn enhance their engagement7. In a study comparing first-generation and continuing-generation college students, Gillen-O’Neel48 found that sense of belonging promoted engagement for both groups. On days when students felt a stronger sense of belonging, they reported greater involvement in academic and extracurricular activities and higher social satisfaction. Peer encouragement and support have also been linked to higher engagement and motivation47. Johnson and Johnson49 showed that cooperative learning positively affects student engagement; student-to-student interaction and collaborative teamwork provided support, feedback, and inspiration, thereby strengthening motivation and engagement.

In second language learning, peer interaction is considered essential50. Research indicates that peer interactions involving negotiation for meaning, scaffolding, and feedback effectively advance language development51. Students helped each other negotiate meaning, scaffold learning, and give constructive feedback, which led to noticeable gains in grammatical complexity and vocabulary use.

Grit

Grit, a concept introduced by Angela Duckworth, refers to an individual’s tenacity and perseverance in the face of challenges and setbacks while maintaining strong motivation to achieve long-term goals8. It embodies the ability to persist despite adversity and maintain unwavering determination towards future-oriented objectives52. In essence, grit signifies the capacity to surmount obstacles and maintain resolute aspiration8,52–54. According to Duckworth8, grit comprises two fundamental trait-level components: passion and perseverance, which enable individuals to pursue enduring goals with steadfast determination, even in the face of challenges and adversities. The passion element represents the ability to sustain interest in a specific task or goal over an extended period. The perseverance element, on the other hand, denotes the extent to which individuals can withstand obstacles and challenges55. In other words, gritty individuals not only maintain their interest but also exert sustained effort in pursuing their goals, despite encountering obstacles along the way52,55,56.

Although grit is often viewed as a static intrapersonal trait, this study recontextualizes it through the lens of Social Cognitive Theory (SCT)21. According to SCT’s principle of reciprocal determinism, personal factors (like grit) and environmental factors (like classroom relationships) are in a constant state of mutual influence. In this study, grit is treated as the agentic component of engagement: gritty students do not just persist in isolation; they actively navigate their social environment to maintain their momentum10,57. Indeed, recent mixed-methods research among Chinese EFL learners suggests that L2 grit is not merely a direct predictor of engagement but also functions as a vital mediator. Specifically, both consistency of interest and perseverance of effort have been found to mediate the paths between student-student or student-content interactions and overall engagement58. This indicates that the quality of classroom interactions provides the necessary input that triggers a student’s gritty persistence, which then drives active engagement.

Andersson and Bergman53 emphasize that grit plays a crucial role in achieving success by highlighting the importance of perseverance and unwavering dedication towards objectives, both of which are essential for remarkable accomplishments59. Similarly, Bashant60 and Teimouri et al.57 assert that grit is a multifaceted construct encompassing vital components for academic success, such as resilience, conscientiousness, self-discipline, and persistence. Essentially, grit aims to encompass these critical elements in a single measure, providing a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of an individual’s ability to overcome obstacles and achieve goals despite setbacks or difficulties along the way10,57,61.

A plethora of research studies in academic contexts consistently highlight the significance of grit as a critical factor contributing to academic success52,53,62. Wolters and Hussain63 conducted a study involving 213 college students from diverse ethnic backgrounds and found that perseverance of effort was positively associated with self-regulation and academic achievement. Collectively, these studies suggest that grit may facilitate greater engagement with academic tasks and improve academic outcomes.

The literature available also suggests that grit plays a crucial role in promoting student engagement within various educational settings56,64–66. A preliminary study by Yau and Shu66 explored the relationships among grit, grit-PE (a component of grit), negotiable fate, and student engagement during the pandemic. The findings indicated positive correlations between grit, grit-PE, negotiable fate, and student engagement. Moreover, Robinson65 investigated the association between grit and course engagement among nursing students, revealing a substantial interconnection between the two, with higher grit levels associated with greater engagement in coursework. Similarly, Liu64 examined the relationship between grit and L2 (second-language) engagement among Chinese university students and explored whether instructors could foster grit among their students. The results indicated a positive relationship between grit and L2 engagement, suggesting that higher grit was associated with greater involvement in L2 learning.

Teacher-student relationships

Teacher-student relationships are fundamental connections that encompass emotional bonds and interactions between educators and their students in educational settings14,67,68. This construct reflects the quality of communication, support, and trust established between teachers and learners, influencing various aspects of students’ academic experiences, such as their attitudes, motivation, and engagement in the learning process4. The Teacher-Student Relationship (TSR) serves as the primary “linking agent” in our integrated model. Drawing on Self-Determination Theory (SDT)20, we argue that the TSR satisfies the basic psychological need for relatedness. However, we extend this by integrating the sociocultural view: the teacher provides the “expert scaffolding”45 that allows both peers’ bonding capital and the intrapersonal energy of grit to be directed toward meaningful classroom engagement. Consequently, positive TSRs are characterized by warmth, understanding, and respect, creating a conducive, nurturing learning environment4. Conversely, negative or strained relationships may result in disengagement, hindering students’ academic progress69. Extensive research has consistently demonstrated the pivotal role of teacher-student relationships in students’ academic outcomes and achievement4,69,70. When students experience positive and supportive interactions with their teachers, they are more likely to feel motivated and engaged in the learning process13.

Engaged students who develop strong bonds with their teachers tend to exhibit higher levels of academic achievement2,71. Positive emotional connections foster a sense of belonging and commitment, encouraging students to actively participate and excel in their academic endeavors11,72. Moreover, teacher-student relationships have been related to increased motivation, with students feeling a sense of purpose and determination in their pursuit of knowledge73. Positive teacher-student relationships also play a significant role in improving attendance rates and classroom participation72,73. Students who feel attached and valued by their teachers are more likely to be present in class and actively engage in discussions and activities74,75. This active involvement fosters a positive learning context in which students feel encouraged to share their thoughts and ideas, enhancing their overall learning experience5,12.

In addition to their influence on academic success, positive teacher-student relationships have a profound impact on learners’ social-emotional growth4. Students who experience warm and supportive interactions with their teachers tend to develop higher levels of social skills and emotional intelligence76. These positive relationships contribute to the development of empathy, cooperation, and pro-social behaviors, fostering a more harmonious and inclusive classroom environment4.

Moreover, positive teacher-student relationships have been linked to reduced behavioral problems13. When learners feel attached to their teachers, they are less likely to engage in disruptive behavior or exhibit challenging conduct in the classroom4. Instead, they are more inclined to demonstrate responsible and respectful behavior, thereby contributing to a positive and conducive learning atmosphere6. Taken together, teacher-student relationships significantly impact not only learners’ academic success but also their holistic development. Positive interactions with teachers foster engagement, motivation, and scholarly outcomes. Additionally, these relationships play a vital role in promoting students’ social-emotional growth, leading to improved social skills and reduced behavioral problems. As such, fostering strong, supportive teacher-student relationships remains a critical endeavor for creating a thriving educational environment that nurtures students’ well-rounded development.

The research hypotheses

Our hypothesized model is grounded in an integrated framework that combines Social Cognitive Theory21 and Social Capital Theory19 to explain student engagement. We propose that engagement results from a “relational-agentic” synergy: Social Capital Theory explains the interpersonal resources (bonding capital from peers and linking capital from teachers), while Social Cognitive Theory explains the intrapersonal agency (grit). Central to this integration is Self-Determination Theory20, which identifies the teacher-student relationship as the critical environment for satisfying the “relatedness” needs that allow these resources to manifest as engagement.

By synthesizing these perspectives, we frame the teacher-student relationship as a functional mediator. It serves as the bridge where the “social support” of peers and the “internal drive” of grit meet to produce sustained participation.

We also recognize that some published findings (e.g9,11., report alternative patterns – for instance, grit serving as a mediator. Those studies are cited here as evidence that the constructs are associated, not as proof that the relationship runs only one way. Our cross-sectional design tests a theoretically ordered model; we discuss the possibility of reciprocal effects in the limitations section. The present study tests a hypothesized model (see Fig. 1) that specifies how peer relationships, grit, teacher-student relationships, and student engagement are related. The hypotheses are as follows:

H1

Positive peer relationships significantly and positively predict the quality of teacher-student relationships.

Rationale: From a social capital perspective19, peer interactions provide “bonding capital” – emotional support and trust. However, in teacher-centered classrooms like the Chinese EFL context, this bonding capital usually needs validation from a vertical authority figure (the teacher) to affect academic outcomes. Students who have supportive friends tend to develop better social skills and confidence, which help them approach teachers more openly35,47. Therefore, we model peer relationship as an antecedent of teacher-student rapport.

H2

Grit significantly and positively predicts the quality of teacher-student relationships.

Rationale: According to social cognitive theory21, gritty students persist through difficulties and actively seek help. Teachers observe these effortful behaviors and often respond with more warmth, encouragement, and individual attention10. Positive correlations between grit and teacher-student relationship quality have been reported9,11, but our directional choice is theoretical: grit is a personal disposition that expresses itself in observable actions, which then shape how teachers treat the student. We do not claim that this is the only possible direction; a supportive teacher may also nurture grit over time.

H3

Positive teacher-student relationships directly promote student engagement.

Rationale: Self-determination theory20 states that relatedness – feeling connected to a caring adult – is a basic psychological need. When teachers provide support, trust, and responsiveness, students feel safer and more motivated to participate. Meta-analytic evidence4 and longitudinal research12 consistently support this path. Hence, teacher-student relationship is modeled as a direct predictor of engagement.

H4

The teacher-student relationship mediates the relationship between peer relationships and student engagement.

Rationale: Social capital theory19 suggests that bonding capital (peer support) alone does not automatically increase academic engagement. For peer resources to be converted into classroom effort, they must be “activated” by a linking agent – here, the teacher. Students with good peer relationships are more likely to build positive rapport with teachers (as stated in H1), and that rapport in turn drives engagement (H3). Thus, we hypothesize an indirect effect of peer support through the teacher-student relationship.

H5

The teacher-student relationship mediates the relationship between grit and student engagement.

Rationale: Gritty students may persist on their own, but their effort is more likely to translate into sustained classroom engagement when teachers recognize and support them9. The teacher-student relationship acts as a channel that amplifies the motivational energy of grit. This hypothesis allows for a remaining direct effect of grit on engagement (partial mediation), which is tested in the model.

Methods

Participants

This research was conducted in mainland China and targeted university students enrolled in mandatory English courses as part of their academic program. The participants were 503 s-year undergraduates from three major Chinese universities, all pursuing degrees in non-English fields (e.g., Engineering, Business, Computer Science, and Social Sciences). The sample achieved a balanced gender distribution with 222 females and 281 males. The average participant age was 20.06 years (SD = 1.82), indicating a relatively consistent age range.

Students were recruited from various College English classes across the universities, specifically those enrolled in non-English majors. These College English courses served a dual purpose: developing core language skills (listening, speaking, reading, and writing) and fulfilling a mandatory requirement for freshmen and sophomores in the participating institutions. Consequently, the chosen sample comprised a diverse group of undergraduates from various academic backgrounds, brought together by their participation in the compulsory College English courses.

Instruments

Teacher-student relationship

Assessing the quality of the teacher-student relationship involved employing a survey based on the work of Pianta and Nimetz77. The survey consisted of 15 items, focusing on three components: closeness, positive reactivity, and conflict. Closeness refers to warmth and open communication (example item: “I share a close relationship with my teacher”). Positive reactivity captures the teacher’s responsiveness and encouragement (example item: “My teacher praises me when I try hard”). Conflict reflects tension or disagreement (example item: “My teacher and I often argue”). These dimensions aimed to capture students’ perceptions of their interactions with their teacher. This scale is relevant to EFL classrooms because supportive teacher-student relationships can lower foreign language anxiety, encourage students to take risks in speaking, and provide constructive feedback – all of which are critical for language development. Participants were required to rate the extent to which each statement reflected their current head teacher’s behavior on a 5-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Participants’ scores were computed by averaging their responses across items, with higher scores indicating a more favorable teacher-student relationship. The reliability and validity of this survey have been established in prior research78.

Peer relationship

To assess peer relationships, the Peer Relationship Quality Scale for Adolescents, developed by Bae et al.79, was employed. This scale comprises two sub-factors: positive and negative peer relationships, both of which were comprehensively considered and analyzed in this study. Although this scale was not designed specifically for language learning, it is relevant because peer interactions in EFL classrooms often involve collaborative tasks, shared goal setting, and mutual help with vocabulary, grammar, or pronunciation. For example, positive peer relationships can include classmates providing corrections, explaining difficult points, or practicing dialogues together. The scale consists of 13 items. Among them, eight items, such as “Friends appreciate and support me,” evaluate positive peer relationships, while five items, including “I often have disagreements with my friends,” assess negative peer relationships. It is essential to note that items related to negative peer relationships were reversed before conducting the analysis. The scale’s overall consistency, assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, was reported as 0.85 in the original study by Bae et al.79. Furthermore, other studies, including11, have confirmed the scale’s strength and reliability through psychometric assessments.

Student engagement

In this research, participant engagement was gauged using the Student Engagement Scale, which was validated by Reeve80. This scale, tailored for university students, evaluates four aspects of the engagement construct: Agentic Engagement, Behavioural Engagement, Cognitive Engagement, and Emotional Engagement. Each aspect comprises multiple items, and participants indicate their level of agreement on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Reeve80 reported robust internal consistency coefficients for all four questionnaire components. An example item of the scale is “I try hard to do well in this class.” The psychometric qualities of this scale have been approved in the literature81.

L2 grit

To assess L2 grit, the L2 grit scale developed by Teimouri et al.57 was used in this study. The scale consists of 9 items, measured on a five-point Likert scale. It includes two sub-scales: consistency of interest (CI) and perseverance of effort (CE). Examples of items from the scale include “I am not as interested in learning English as I used to be” (CI) and “Now that I have decided to learn English, nothing can prevent me from reaching this goal” (CE). Prior studies by Teimouri et al.57 have established sound construct validity and reliability, ranging from acceptable to high, for the diverse elements of this questionnaire.

Procedure

The data collection process for this study was carefully conducted to ensure ethical compliance and data accuracy. We sought ethical clearance from the appropriate institutional review board, placing paramount emphasis on safeguarding participants’ rights and welfare. We ensured that all participants provided informed consent, with a transparent and thorough explanation of the study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of their responses.

Participants were recruited from various universities in mainland China, where English-related courses were compulsory for all students. Second-year undergraduate students majoring in non-English disciplines were selected as the target group. These students were actively enrolled in courses such as college English, integrated English, or English listening and speaking, aimed at improving their language skills in listening, speaking, reading, and writing.

A structured questionnaire was designed to collect the necessary data, with items based on validated measures related to peer relationships, grit, teacher-student relationships, and student engagement. Participants used a Likert-scale format to indicate their level of agreement or disagreement with each item. The questionnaire was administered during their regular College English classes or at designated locations within the university. Trained research assistants or investigators explained the study’s purpose and provided clear instructions on completing the questionnaire. Participants were given sufficient time to read and respond to the items, ensuring comprehension and accuracy.

Data collection occurred over a specific period, taking into account participants’ availability and convenience. Participants completed the questionnaire independently, ensuring anonymity and confidentiality of their responses. Measures were taken to minimize missing or incomplete responses, and research assistants were available to address any questions or uncertainties, ensuring high-quality data collection.

Data analysis

The data analysis process for this study involved several steps to explore the relationships among the variables and test the research hypothesis. Initially, descriptive and correlation analyses were conducted using SPSS 28.0 to gain insights into the variables’ characteristics and associations. To evaluate the research hypothesis, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was utilized with the Amos program (version 26.0). The analysis began by assessing the measurement model to ensure the scales’ construct validity. Subsequently, the structural model was examined to investigate the hypothesized relationships between the variables. This analysis allowed for the evaluation of both direct and indirect effects within the theoretical framework.

Various fit indices were employed to assess the overall fit of the hypothesized model. The χ2-goodness of fit to degree of freedom (df) ratio was scrutinized, with a value less than 3 indicating a satisfactory fit. Additionally, the Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI) and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) were used, with values of 0.90 or higher indicating a good fit. The Root-Mean-Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and the Standardized Root-Mean-Square Residual (SRMR) were also considered as fit indices. Typically, an RMSEA value below 0.08 and an SRMR value under 0.10 are regarded as signs of a strong fit82,83.

Because SEM is a confirmatory technique that tests a theory-driven structure specified a priori, we also explored a post-hoc reversed model where teacher-student relationship was specified as a predictor of peer relationships and grit, and peer relationships and grit as joint predictors of engagement. This reversed model showed poorer fit (CFI = 0.89, RMSEA = 0.09) compared to our hypothesized partial mediation model (Model C). However, given the cross-sectional data, we interpret all mediation findings as statistically consistent with mediation, not as evidence of causal processes. Alternative directional or reciprocal models cannot be fully ruled out.

Then convergent and discriminant validity were examined84. Table 3 provides information on the convergent and discriminant validity, as well as the composite reliability, of the variables in the study. The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values indicate the amount of variance in the observed variables that can be explained by their respective latent constructs. While the AVE values for student engagement (0.512) and peer relationships (0.423) approach or exceed conventional thresholds, the AVE values for grit (0.361) and teacher-student relationships (0.293) fall below the standard 0.50 benchmark. However, convergent validity is still considered acceptable in this study based on the criteria established by Fornell and Larcker84. They posit that if the Composite Reliability (CR) exceeds 0.70, the convergent validity of the construct is deemed sufficient even when the AVE is less than 0.50, as the CR provides a more accurate estimate of the construct’s reliability. Given that the CR values for all constructs in this study (Grit = 0.793; Peer relationship = 0.772; T-S relationship = 0.804; Student engagement = 0.821) are well above 0.70, we maintain that our measurement model demonstrates adequate internal consistency and convergent validity.

The Maximum Shared Squared Variance (MSW) values represent the extent to which the observed variables share variance with other variables in the study. Lower MSW values, as observed for student engagement (0.426), peer relationship (0.691), and grit (0.389), indicate a lower degree of shared variance with other variables, suggesting the constructs are distinct. The Average Shared Squared Variance (ASW) values reflect the specific shared variance between a construct and the other constructs included in the analysis. Lower ASW values, as seen for student engagement (0.303), peer relationship (0.272), and grit (0.289), indicate less shared variance specific to the constructs and not shared with other variables.

In addition to comparing the three nested models (direct, full mediation, partial mediation), we examined a post-hoc reversed model where teacher-student relationship predicted peer relationships and grit, which then predicted engagement. This reversed model yielded CFI = 0.89, RMSEA = 0.09, and SRMR = 0.15 – all worse than the fit of our hypothesized Model C (CFI = 0.977, RMSEA = 0.036, SRMR = 0.063). Thus, while the reversed model does not fit the data as well, cross-sectional SEM cannot definitively rule out alternative orderings.

Subsequently, the examination of potential mediation by the teacher-student relationship between the constructs followed Baron and Kenny’s85 approach. The direct model (Table 5) revealed noteworthy coefficients linking grit, peer relationship, and student engagement (grit → student engagement: 0.373, p < 0.01; peer-relation → student engagement: 0.193, p < 0.05), thereby confirming the first step of Baron and Kenny’s method. Furthermore, the full mediation model demonstrated significant coefficients between grit and peer relationship on grit (grit → teacher-student relationship: 0.356, p < 0.001; peer-relation → T-S relation: 0.446, p < 0.001), affirming the second step of the method.

Finally, in the partial mediation model, grit positively influenced student engagement (β = 0.283, p < 0.01). When considering the indirect effect mediated by the teacher-student relationship, the influence of grit on student engagement was 0.224 (0.396 × 0.567), which was smaller than the direct effect (0.224 < 0.283) of grit on student engagement. Therefore, it can be concluded that the teacher-student relationship partially mediates the relationship between grit and student engagement. On the other hand, the path coefficient of peer relationship on student engagement was not statistically significant. However, the teacher-student relationship was identified as a full mediator between peer relationship and student engagement (0.418 × 0.567 = 0.237 > 0.089). This suggests that the impact of peer relationships on student engagement is channeled through the teacher-student relationship, enhancing student engagement.

To address potential common-method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted by combining all latent variables measured through self-report, namely grit, peer relationship, student engagement, and teacher-student relationship. The first factor extracted explained 41.36% of the variance, which is below the 50% threshold. This suggests that common method bias was not a significant concern in this study.

Discussion

The current study investigated the relationships among peer relationships, grit, the teacher-student relationship, and student engagement in the Chinese EFL context. We recognize that other factors also shape engagement in language learning, such as instructional practices, motivation, perceived competence, and communication anxiety15–18. Our study does not claim that peer relationships, grit, and teacher-student relationship are the most important predictors. Instead, we focused on them because they represent three distinct sources of influence – interpersonal (peers, teacher) and intrapersonal (grit) – that have rarely been tested together in a single mediation model within EFL research. Prior studies have mostly examined these factors in isolation or as direct predictors. The specific gap we fill is testing whether the teacher-student relationship acts as a mechanism that channels the effects of peer support and grit into engagement. This mediation pathway has not been empirically examined in the Chinese EFL context, which is our main contribution.

Before discussing the findings, a methodological note is needed. As discussed previously, our model is grounded in social capital theory, self-determination theory, and social cognitive theory. These frameworks were specified a priori, not added after seeing the results. SEM is fundamentally confirmatory: it tests a model derived from theory, but it cannot prove causation, especially with cross-sectional data. The observed indirect effects (teacher-student relationship as a pathway from peer relationships and grit to engagement) are statistically consistent with our theoretical model, but alternative directional models – for example, where engagement improves teacher-student rapport, or where teacher-student relationship is an antecedent of peer relationships – cannot be ruled out. Therefore, we use the terms “mediation” and “indirect effect” here in a statistical, not causal, sense. We have expanded the limitations section to address this more fully.

The results revealed several significant insights that extend our understanding of how interpersonal and intrapersonal resources function within language learning environments.

The direct association between grit and student engagement indicates that within high-stakes language learning environments, perseverance and sustained interest function as vital internal drivers that keep students cognitively and behaviorally invested over time. Grit enables learners to sustain effort and interest even in the face of linguistic challenges52,54. Rather than merely predicting performance, grit operationalizes a long-term commitment that is particularly crucial for language acquisition—a process often marked by high anxiety and a persistent need for tolerance of ambiguity. Higher levels of grit are associated with a greater likelihood of goal achievement, driven by enhanced tenacity and commitment8,52,53. In the context of EFL learning, this suggests that gritty students view structural or communicative hurdles not as definitive failures but as temporary setbacks, allowing them to remain deeply involved in classroom tasks10,56,57. This critical link lies in how grit converts internal persistence into active, observable classroom participation. Engaged students tend to experience intrinsic motivation, a sense of belonging, and a profound interest in the subject matter86. Fredricks et al.1 propose that engaged students are more likely to invest additional effort and time in their learning activities. Our findings suggest that grit acts as the underlying psychological engine for this sustained investment. Students who exhibit high passion and perseverance navigate the arduous process of foreign language acquisition more actively, actively seeking out learning opportunities and maintaining their engagement because they are intrinsically aligned with distal mastery goals62,63. Therefore, developing grit is not just about building resilience; it directly stimulates the behavioral and cognitive investments necessary for successful language development.

Furthermore, the strong predictive power of the teacher-student relationship on student engagement highlights the instructor’s role as the primary socio-emotional anchor in the classroom. From a social cognitive perspective, the direct influence of the teacher-student relationship on student engagement can be elucidated through Bandura’s21 social learning theory. When teachers establish positive, supportive relationships with their students, they become influential role models, demonstrating the importance of active participation and commitment to learning5,87. In collectivist educational contexts, this relationship carries significant moral and instructional authority. Students do not merely observe their instructors; they seek relational validation from them. A supportive instructor reduces the perceived threat of communicative failure in a foreign language, making students much more willing to participate without the fear of negative evaluation76.

Insights derived from self-determination theory shed further light on the association between the teacher-student relationship and student engagement20. This theoretical framework proposes that individuals possess three essential psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. In our study, the teacher-student relationship appears to act as the primary structural vehicle for fulfilling these needs in the classroom. As students perceive that their teachers value their input, offer meaningful feedback, and establish a supportive learning environment, they experience greater feelings of autonomy, competence, and connection9,35. This need fulfillment subsequently lowers affective barriers, which directly triggers higher levels of student engagement12,71.

Furthermore, our findings validate the investment model of teacher-student relationships87 by demonstrating that reciprocal emotional dynamics drive student action. When teachers invest time and effort in establishing positive and supportive relationships with their students, they cultivate a sense of belonging and emotional connection4,68,75. In return, students reciprocate this relational investment by aligning with the teacher’s pedagogical goals and increasing their active classroom participation14. Conversely, when this interpersonal bond is weak or strained, the psychological safety of the learning environment diminishes, causing students to disengage as a defensive strategy against perceived incompetence11,71.

Finally, the study found that peer relationships did not directly affect student engagement; however, the teacher-student relationship fully mediated this path. According to social capital theory, peer relationships contribute to the development of social resources, such as social support, trust, and shared norms19. In the context of student engagement, positive peer relationships foster a sense of belonging, promote cooperation, and offer emotional support11,40,72. However, the complete mediation observed in our study suggests that horizontal peer resources lack the necessary pedagogical legitimacy to directly translate into academic engagement in highly structured or teacher-centered learning contexts. Instead, peer-based social capital requires validation from a vertical authority figure—the teacher.

The mechanisms behind this full mediation can be further understood through the lens of Social Capital and Social Cognitive theories. From a Social Capital perspective, peer interactions provide “bonding capital”—emotional closeness and mutual support—which is essentially a social lubricant. However, for this social energy to be converted into “linking capital” (the academic engagement required in an EFL setting), it must pass through the teacher. In the Chinese context, peer support remains a social asset until the instructor integrates it into the learning framework. Without the teacher’s guidance, peer interactions may improve the classroom “vibe” but do not necessarily drive students to invest more effort in their language tasks. Social Cognitive Theory21 complements this by suggesting that students look to the teacher for vicarious reinforcement. The teacher’s approval or structuring of peer activities provides the signal that these social interactions are valid paths to success. Thus, the teacher-student relationship acts as the psychological bridge that gives peer support its academic meaning.

A significant finding of this study is the distinction between the partial mediation of grit and the full mediation of peer relationships. This difference highlights the contrast between intrapersonal and interpersonal resources. Grit, being an internal trait, possesses a degree of functional autonomy; a gritty student is psychologically predisposed to persist and engage directly with the material, even in the absence of external support. This explains why grit retains a direct effect on engagement alongside its indirect effect through the teacher-student relationship. Conversely, peer relationships are purely environmental and social. Unlike an internal trait, social support is highly dependent on the classroom ecosystem. Because the teacher serves as the primary architect of this ecosystem in Chinese higher education, the academic benefits of peer support are entirely filtered through the teacher’s role. While grit allows a student to push forward independently, peer support requires the teacher’s mediation to become a productive force for engagement. This suggests that while internal resilience can bypass relational hurdles, social resources are only as effective as the teacher allows them to be.

Finally, the cross-sectional design prevents us from confirming the direction of influence. While we grounded our paths in theory, the opposite ordering is also plausible – for example, engaged students may build stronger relationships with teachers, and positive teacher-student rapport may increase grit over time. Moreover, because SEM is a confirmatory technique that requires a theory-driven structure specified in advance, our comparison of nested models (direct, full, partial mediation) does not constitute a test of all possible alternative models. The post-hoc reversed model we examined showed poorer fit, but other reciprocal arrangements could also exist. Our data cannot rule out such alternative or bidirectional effects. Therefore, we interpret the indirect effects as statistically consistent with mediation, not as proof of causal mediation. Longitudinal research with three or more time points is needed to test genuine mediation and reciprocal dynamics.

Conclusion

The findings of this study offer a cohesive architecture of student engagement in the Chinese EFL context, illustrating how intrapersonal traits and interpersonal resources function in a hierarchical manner. Our contribution is not claiming these three factors are the most important, but rather showing a statistically indirect pathway: the teacher-student relationship is statistically consistent with a mediating role for both peer support and grit on engagement, a pathway not previously tested in EFL research. We emphasize that these findings are correlational and should not be interpreted as causal mediation. Our theoretically derived model demonstrates that while grit provides an internal foundation for persistence, it is the teacher-student relationship that serves as the central operational mechanism for translating both personal grit and peer social capital into active classroom engagement. We do not claim that these paths are the only possible directions; rather, they represent a theory-driven ordering that fits the data well.

The implications for practice must therefore center on the role of the teacher as the primary architect of the learning environment. Rather than promoting collaborative learning or peer activities as standalone strategies, our findings suggest that these interventions are only effective when filtered through a supportive teacher-student relationship. Educators should prioritize ‘relational pedagogy,’ wherein the teacher actively structures peer interaction to ensure it aligns with the psychological safety established in the classroom. This moves beyond generic ‘group work’ toward a model where the teacher facilitates peer support, thereby validating peer interaction as a legitimate vehicle for academic engagement. Similarly, professional development programs should shift away from general interpersonal training toward specific strategies for creating high-trust environments that fulfill students’ needs for autonomy and relatedness.

For policymakers, these results suggest that the emphasis on curriculum and technology in EFL settings may overlook the critical role of teacher-student rapport. Policy frameworks should reallocate support toward initiatives that foster a positive ‘classroom climate.’ By equipping teachers with the resources to cultivate these bonds, institutions can leverage the teacher as a conduit to activate student grit and integrate peer support, both of which are currently underutilized resources in the absence of a strong teacher-student connection.

Despite these insights, several limitations warrant caution when interpreting the findings. First, the generalizability of our results is constrained by the sample of 503 Chinese EFL learners. Given that cultural and systemic variations significantly impact educational dynamics, replication studies across diverse populations are necessary to validate these patterns.

Second, our reliance on self-report instruments introduces potential social desirability bias and subjective interpretation. Future research should triangulate data using objective measures, such as teacher evaluations or direct classroom observations, and employ mixed-methods designs to capture the qualitative depth often missed by structured questionnaires.

Finally, the cross-sectional design prevents us from confirming the direction of influence. While we grounded our paths in theory, the opposite ordering is also plausible – for example, engaged students may build stronger relationships with teachers, and positive teacher-student rapport may increase grit over time. Our data cannot rule out such reciprocal effects. Longitudinal research is needed to track how these constructs influence each other across time.
