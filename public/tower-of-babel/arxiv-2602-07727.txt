On Heights and Diameters of Ternary Cyclotomic and Inclusion-Exclusion Polynomials
Gennady Bachman

Abstract
. For the n n th cyclotomic polynomial Φ n \Phi_{n} , let A ⁡ ( n ) A(n) denote the greatest absolute value of its coefficients, its height, and let D ⁡ ( n ) D(n) denote the difference between its largest and smallest coefficients, its diameter. We show that for any odd prime p p and an integer h h in the range 1 ≤ h ≤ ( p + 1 ) / 2 1\leq h\leq(p+1)/2 , there are arbitrarily large primes q q and r r such that Φ p ​ q ​ r \Phi_{pqr} has the height h h . This certainly answers the question of whether every natural number occurs as the height of some cyclotomic polynomial. Our construction specifies explicit choices of q q and r r with A ⁡ ( p ​ q ​ r ) = h A(pqr)=h , and for these choices D ⁡ ( p ​ q ​ r ) D(pqr) has one of two values: it is either 2 ​ h 2h or 2 ​ h − 1 2h-1 , depending on the congruence class of h h modulo p p .

1.
Introduction

Let
\Phi_{n}
be the
n
th cyclotomic polynomial, that is,
\Phi_{n}(x)=\prod(x-\zeta)
, where the product is taken over the primitive
n
th roots of unity
\zeta
, and write

\Phi_{n}(x)=\sum_{0\leq m\leq\varphi(n)}a_{m}x^{m}\qquad[a_{m}=a(m;n)],

where
\varphi
is the Euler’s totient function. It is well known that the coefficients
a_{m}
are integral and there is a growing body of literature studying their properties and especially properties of the function

A(n)\coloneqq\max_{m}|a_{m}|=\max_{m}|a(m;n)|.

(See
[
7
,
8
]
and the references therein.) As has become increasingly common in the literature, we shall refer to
A(n)
as the height of
\Phi_{n}
. It is an interesting and nontrivial question if there is a polynomial
\Phi_{n}
of any given height
h
? To gain a better understanding of this question we review the following easy facts (see, for example,
[
17
]
). To investigate possible heights, it suffices to study possible values of
A(\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}p_{i})
, for primes
2<p_{1}<\dots<p_{k}
. This is because if
\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}p_{i}
is the “odd squarefree kernel” of
n
, then
A(n)=A(\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}p_{i})
. If
k
is the number of distinct odd prime factors of
n
, polynomial
\Phi_{n}
is said to be of order
k
. The height question naturally splits into the family of equations

(1)

A\Bigl(\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}p_{i}\Bigr)=h,

one equation for each order
k
. It is an easy fact that
A(\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}p_{i})\equiv 1
, for
k<3
. For
k\geq 3
the problem is challenging and wide open.

Recent progress in the study of ternary cyclotomic polynomials (
k=3
) furnished tools that allowed one to tackle the solubility of

(2)

A(pqr)=h

in odd primes
p<q<r
. Early successes were limited to some special cases where
h
is small and are as follows.

•

Bachman
[
3
]
: For every prime
p
,
A(pqr)=1
is soluble in arbitrary large primes
q
and
r
.

•

Elder
[
9
, unpublished]
/Zhang
[
19
]
: For every prime
p
,
A(pqr)=2
is soluble in arbitrary large primes
q
and
r
.

•

Zhang
[
20
]
: For every prime
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
,
A(pqr)=3
is soluble in arbitrary large primes
q
and
r
.

The latter result of Zhang was preceded by a conditional result of Gallot, Moree and Wilms
[
11
]
: For every prime
p
such that
2p-1
is also prime,
A(p(2p-1)r)=3
is soluble in arbitrary large primes
r
. In view of the history of this problem, the case
h=1
is particularly intriguing (see discussion in
[
3
]
) and also the easiest (unless one treats
h
in (
2
) as a function of
p
and takes
h=(p+1)/2
) and certainly the best understood case to date—see
[
13
,
14
,
9
,
5
]
. A particularly appealing result in this case is due to Kaplan
[
13
]
and is stated as Theorem
4
in the next section.

For an arbitrary
h
, a conditional affirmative answer to (
2
) was recently given by Kosyak, Moree, Sofos and Zhang
[
15
]
. They showed that if a certain short interval
I_{h}
depending on
h
contains a prime
p
, roughly

(3)

2h-1-\sqrt{2h-1}<p\leq 2h-1,

then there are arbitrarily large primes
q
and
r
such that
A(pqr)=h
. Of course, the difficulty here is that the existence of a prime in a short interval (
3
) remains an open problem—it should be compared with Legendre’s conjecture that there is always a prime between consecutive squares. However, using a recent advance on the distribution of primes due to Heath-Brown
[
12
]
, they are able to say that
A(pqr)=h
is soluble for almost all
h
. More precisely, they conclude that the number of “bad”
h\leq x
is
O_{\varepsilon}(x^{3/5+\varepsilon})
, as
x
goes to infinity.

Using a different approach, Bachman, Bao and Wu
[
6
]
showed that for every positive integer
h
the equation

A(pqr)=h\quad\text{or}\quad h+1

is always soluble in arbitrarily large primes
q
and
r
. A notable feature of their approach is that it has only minimal requirements from the theory of distribution of primes, namely, the Dirichlet’s theorem for primes in arithmetic progressions. They also make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

For an odd prime
p
, let
M(p)\coloneqq\max_{q,r}A(pqr)
. Then for every
p
and
1\leq h\leq M(p)
, the equation
A(pqr)=h
is soluble in arbitrarily large primes
q
and
r
.

Establishing the solubility of (
2
) for all
h\geq 1
was the impetus for the current work. Our analysis yields a number of new results for heights and diameters of ternary cyclotomic and inclusion-exclusion polynomials. Those will be given in the next section after the necessary background material has been presented. But we include in this section the simplest and cleanest of our results, a result which may be thought of as a step towards Conjecture
1
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
h
be any integer in the range
1\leq h\leq(p+1)/2
. Then there exist arbitrarily large primes
q
and
r
such that
A(pqr)=h
.

We conclude this section with a quick ward about the equation (
1
) for
k\geq 4
. Aside from explicitly calculating
A(n)
for specific values of
n
, almost nothing is known. A particularly appealing old problem is to determine whether there are polynomials
\Phi_{n}
of arbitrarily high order and height 1. Polynomials of height 1 are said to be flat. Despite extensive computations, no flat polynomials of order 5 or higher have ever been found. Kaplan
[
14
]
proved the following general result. Let
n
be arbitrary and let primes
r
and
R
satisfy
R>r>n
and
R\equiv r\pmod{n}
. Then
A(nR)=A(nr)
. Combining this result with computationally obtained evaluation
A(3\cdot 5\cdot 31\cdot 929)=1
, he showed that there are infinitely many flat polynomials of the form
\Phi_{3\cdot 5\cdot 31\cdot R}
.

2.
Background and Results

Modifying our earlier convention, we now let
p
,
q
and
r
denote three relatively prime in pairs positive integers
\geq 3
(so they need not be primes). Put
T=\{p,q,r\}
and

(4)

Q_{T}(x)=Q_{\{p,q,r\}}(x)\coloneqq\frac{(x^{pqr}-1)(x^{p}-1)(x^{q}-1)(x^{r}-1)}{(x^{pq}-1)(x^{qr}-1)(x^{rp}-1)(x-1)}.

Routine application of the inclusion-exclusion principle to the roots of the polynomials on the right side shows that
Q_{T}
reduces to a polynomial, a
ternary inclusion-exclusion polynomial
. We refer the reader to
[
4
]
for an introduction to inclusion-exclusion polynomials. This class of polynomials may be thought of as a tool for studying coefficients of cyclotomic polynomials and other divisors of
x^{n}-1
, or as looking at cyclotomic polynomials from a combinatorial rather than algebraic point of view. In particular, when the parameters
p
,
q
and
r
are distinct odd primes, the polynomial
Q_{\{p,q,r\}}
is better known as the cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{pqr}
.

Polynomial
Q_{T}
is of degree

(5)

\varphi(T)=\varphi(p,q,r)\coloneqq(p-1)(q-1)(r-1)

and we write, as before,

Q_{T}(x)=\sum_{0\leq m\leq\varphi(T)}a_{m}x^{m}\qquad[a_{m}=a(m;T)].

We also put

\mathcal{A}_{T}\coloneqq\{a_{m}\}\quad\text{and}\quad A(T)\coloneqq\max_{m}|a_{m}|,

as well as

A^{+}(T)\coloneqq\max_{m}a_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad A^{-}(T)\coloneqq\min_{m}a_{m}.

Quantities
A^{\pm}(T)
completely determine
\mathcal{A}_{T}
for it is known (see
[
10
]
and
[
4
]
) that

(6)

\mathcal{A}_{T}=[A^{-}(T),A^{+}(T)]\cap\mathbb{Z}.

We shall see that the following assumptions on triples
T
allow us to evaluate
A^{\pm}(T)
with a reasonable amount of effort. Let
p\geq 3
be an arbitrary fixed integer and let
t\in[1,p-1]
be relatively prime to
p
. For each such pair of
p
and
t
, let
q
,
r
and
r^{\prime}
be any integers satisfying the conditions

(7)

r,r^{\prime}>q>p^{2},\quad q\equiv t\pmod{p},\quad rt\equiv 1\pmod{pq},

and

(8)

r^{\prime}t\equiv-1\pmod{pq}.

Note that if
t\neq 1
, then any positive
n
satisfying
nt\equiv\pm 1\pmod{pq}
is automatically
>q
, so our assumption
r,r^{\prime}>q
was only needed to preclude the possibility
r=t=1
in (
7
). Note also that the triples
T=\{p,q,r\}
and
T^{\prime}=\{p,q,r^{\prime}\}
are each relatively prime in pairs. Evaluation of
A^{\pm}
for such triples depends on the parameters
\underline{t}
and
\underline{s}
defined as follows. First, let
s\in[1,p-1]
be the inverse of
t
modulo
p
, so that
r\equiv s\pmod{p}
. Now put

\underline{t}\coloneqq\min(t,p-t)\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{s}\coloneq\min(s,p-s).

We are now ready to state the result that underpins all the findings in our paper.

Theorem 3
.

Let
T=\{p,q,r\}
and
T^{\prime}=\{p,q,r^{\prime}\}
be any triples satisfying (
7
) and (
8
). Then the sets
\mathcal{A}_{T}
and
\mathcal{A}_{T^{\prime}}
satisfy the identity

(9)

\mathcal{A}_{T^{\prime}}=-\mathcal{A}_{T}.

Therefore, by (
6
), we only need to evaluate
A^{\pm}(T)
. These are as follows.

(i)

If
t=s=1
, then

A^{-}(T)=-1,\quad A^{+}(T)=1,\quad A(T)=1.

(ii)

If
t>1
and
\underline{s}<\underline{t}
, then

A^{-}(T)=-\underline{s}-1,\quad A^{+}(T)=\underline{s}+1,\quad A(T)=\underline{s}+1.

(iii)

If
t>1
,
\underline{s}\geq\underline{t}
and
(\underline{t},\underline{s})=(t,s)
or
(p-t,p-s)
, then

A^{-}(T)=-\underline{s},\quad A^{+}(T)=\underline{s}+1,\quad A(T)=\underline{s}+1.

(iv)

If
t>1

\underline{s}\geq\underline{t}
and
(\underline{t},\underline{s})=(t,p-s)
or
(p-t,s)
, then

A^{-}(T)=-\underline{s}-1,\quad A^{+}(T)=\underline{s},\quad A(T)=\underline{s}+1.

Example 1
.

Recall that cyclotomic polynomials of order
<3
are all flat. The first non-flat cyclotomic polynomial is
\Phi_{105}
, and it is a classic example that its coefficients form a set
\mathcal{A}_{\{3,5,7\}}=\{-2,-1,0,1\}
. It is interesting to note that Theorem
3
gives a theoretic evaluation of
\mathcal{A}_{\{3,5,7\}}
. To see this we take

p=3,\quad t=2,\quad s=2,\quad q=5,\quad r^{\prime}=7,

and ingnore the requirement
q>p^{2}
. Then

(\underline{t},\underline{s})=(1,1)=(p-t,p-s)

and Theorem
3
(iii) gives

A^{-}(3,5,r)=-1\quad\text{and}\quad A^{+}(3,5,r)=2,

from which the conclusion follows by (
9
) and (
6
).

This is a good place to make a brief comment on our requirement
q>p^{2}
in Theorem
3
. This condition will be seen to yield technical simplifications in our proof of the theorem. We did not explore the extent to which this requirement can be relaxed, and it is entirely possible that simply having
q>p
is not sufficient for our conclusions. But it happens to be sufficient in this example.

Part (i) of Theorem
3
is not new and in certain important ways is different from the other parts, as we now explain. In
[
3
]
, the author gives the result

q\equiv-1\pmod{p}\quad\text{and}\quad r\equiv 1\pmod{pq}\implies A(p,q,r)=1.

But in fact, the method of that paper reaches the same conclusion under any of the four assumptions
q\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}
and
r\equiv\pm 1\pmod{pq}
(containing Theorem
3
(i)). Then Kaplan
[
13
]
showed that the condition
r\equiv\pm 1\pmod{pq}
alone is sufficient for this.

Theorem 4
.

A(p,q,r)=1
, if
r\equiv\pm 1\pmod{pq}
.

Later, Bachman and Moree
[
5
]
studied heights of polynomials
Q_{\{p,q,r\}}
, where
p<q<r
and
r
is of the form
r=kpq\pm r_{0}
,
1\leq r_{0}<p
(prime to
pq
). The method developed in that work reduces the height
A(p,q,r)
to that of
A(p,q,r_{0})
and, in particular, gives a new proof of Theorem
4
. And the point that we are making now is that:

a)

The subclass of ternary inclusion-exclusion polynomials studied in that paper and the subclass analysed in Theorem
3
intersect precisely when
r_{0}=1
and
t=s=1
; and

b)

The method of Bachman and Moree is really the preferred way of looking at these polynomials.

At any rate, one readily verifies that Theorem
3
(i) is contained in Theorem
4
and we choose not to include here its new proof which follows the method developed here.

Strictly speaking, the forgoing discussion of earlier work related to Theorem
4
is somewhat inaccurate and requires clarification. Kaplan’s proof of Theorem
4
as well as related work of Bachman preceding it, were carried out only in the context of cyclotomic polynomials. Their arguments, however, readily carry over to and remain valid for the entire class of inclusion-exclusion polynomials. In the subsequent discussion, we will encounter a number of other situations which are analogous to the situation we just described. To avoid repetition, we will no longer mention this point and simply state a result as valid for the inclusion-exclusion polynomials if we know it to be so.

Next, we record all the cases of the equation
A(p,q,r)=h
that can be solved using Theorem
3
.

Corollary 5
.

Fix
p\geq 3
. The equation
A(p,q,r)=h
certainly has solutions in arbitrarily large
q
and
r
, which may be taken to be prime, for
h=1
and
h
satisfying
\gcd(h-1,p)=1
in the range:

•

2\leq h\leq(p+1)/2
, if
p
is odd, and

•

h=2k
,
1\leq k\leq l
, if
p=4l
or
4l+2
.

For an odd prime
p
, Corollary
5
implies the solubility of
A(p,q,r)=h
in arbitrarily large primes
q
and
r
, for all
1\leq h\leq(p+1)/2
. This principal special case of Corollary
5
was stated as Theorem
2
in the introduction.

The diameter of a polynomial
Q_{T}
is defined to be

D(T)=D(p,q,r)\coloneqq A^{+}(p,q,r)-A^{-}(p,q,r).

It is known that

(10)

2\leq D(T)\leq p\qquad[p=\min(p,q,r)].

The lower bound is trivial for it is very easy to see that every
Q_{T}
has positive and negative coefficients.
D(T)=2
if
Q_{T}
is flat. The upper bound was given in
[
1
, Corollary 3]
. This bound is also sharp and examples of cyclotomic polynomials satisfying
D(p,q,r)=p
were given in
[
2
]
and then by Moree and Roşu in
[
18
]
. It is interesting to point out that in both of these papers the evaluation of heights and diameters were indirect and ran as follows. For a fixed prime
p
, it was shown how to choose (large) primes
q
and
r
such that it was possible to identify a specific “small”coefficient with value
-\frac{p-1}{2}+k
and a specific “large” coefficient with value
\frac{p+1}{2}+k
. In
[
2
]
this was done only for
k=0
, and in
[
18
]
this was done for every
k
in the range

0\leq k\leq\frac{1}{4}(\sqrt{4p-11}-1).

Then the authors observed that the difference of the large and small coefficients was
=p
. It automatically followed, by
(
10
), that the height must be
\frac{p+1}{2}+k
and the perimeter
p
. Notice also that this result of Moree and Roşu shows that
M(p)>\frac{p+1}{2}
for all primes
p\geq 11
.

Findings of Theorem
3
yield new results for the diameter problem. For the sake of simplicity, let us eliminate the requirement
\gcd(h-1,p)=1
in Corollary
5
by assuming that
p
is an odd prime. In fact, we will state our diameter results in the language of cyclotomic polynomials
\Phi_{pqr}
and write
D(pqr)
in place of
D(p,q,r)
. Thus, we are now concerned with solving the equation

(11)

D(pqr)=d\qquad[2\leq d\leq p,\ r>q>p].

Example 2
.

Let us begin with four examples, starting with two evaluations of
D(pqr)
that were previously known.

a)

Theorem
3
(i) solves (
11
) with
d=2
, for each (prime)
p
.

b)

Theorem
3
(iv) with
t=2
and
s=\frac{p+1}{2}
, so that
(\underline{t},\underline{s})=(2,\frac{p-1}{2})
, solves (
11
) with
d=p
, for each
p
.

c)

Theorem
3
(iii) with

, so that
\underline{t}=\underline{s}=1
, solves (
11
) with
d=3
, for each
p
.

d)

It is not possible to use Theorem
3
to solve (
11
) with
d=4
.

We should also remark that the existence of arbitrarily large primes
q
and
r
satisfying the required conditions (
7
) is guaranteed by the Dirichlet’s theorem for primes in arithmetic progressions.

With
p
fixed,
D(pqr)
takes on at most
p-1
possible values, by (
10
). Theorem
3
is sufficient to show that
D(pqr)
can assume at least half of these values.

Corollary 6
.

With
p
fixed, the equation
D(pqr)=d
is soluble in arbitrary large primes
q
and
r
for at least
(p+1)/2
different values of
2\leq d\leq p
.

We saw that Theorem
3
yields no information about polynomials with diameter 4, but shows that
D(pqr)=2,3,p
are always possible, for a fixed
p
. In fact, we can do a little better than this.

Corollary 7
.

Fix
p
. There are arbitrarily large
q
and
r
such that
D(pqr)=2
, and
D(pqr)=2h
, for each
3\leq h<\sqrt{p}
. Moreover, there are arbitrarily large
q
and
r
such that
D(pqr)=p
, and
D(pqr)=p-2k
, for each
1\leq k<\sqrt{p}/2-1
.

So Corollary
7
shows that every even number
\geq 6
is a diameter of some
\Phi_{pqr}
with
p
sufficiently large. We do not have an analogue of this for odd numbers
\geq 5
. But at least we can assert the following weaker conclusion.

Corollary 8
.

For each odd number
d\geq 3
, the equation
D(pqr)=d
is soluble for at least one prime
p
.

We give proofs of all the corollaries first and collect them in the next section. The rest of the paper gives a proof of Theorem
3
, which is elementary but rather lengthy.

3.
Proofs of Corollaries

3.1.
Proof of Corollary
5

Theorem
4
takes care of
h=1
. If
h-1<p/2
and
(h-1,p)=1
, let
1\leq t<p
be such that
t(h-1)\equiv 1\pmod{p}
. Now take
q
and
r
satisfying the condition (
7
). Then
\underline{s}=s=h-1
and, by Theorem
3
,
A(p,q,r)=h
. Note that, by the Dirichlet’s theorem for primes in arithmetic progressions,
q
and
r
may be taken to be prime and arbitrarily large.

It remains to verify the upper bounds in the range of
h
. Observe that if
p
is odd, then
(\frac{p-1}{2},p)=(p-1,p)=1
and we may take
h-1
above as large as
\frac{p-1}{2}
. If
p
is even, say
p=4l
or
p=4l+2
, then
(2l-1,p)=1
and we may take
h-1
as large as
2l-1
in this case.

3.2.
Proof of Corollary
6

We begin by considering special cases of Theorem
3
. The first special case corresponds to the pair
\underline{t}=\underline{s}=1
. This pair corresponds to the two values of
t
, 1 and
p-1
, yielding the diameters 2 and 3, as discussed in Example
2
(a,c). The other special case occurs only for primes
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and corresponds to the fact that
-1
is a quadratic residue for such primes. So let
u
be the unique integer in
[2,\frac{p-1}{2}]
such that
u^{2}\equiv-1\pmod{p}
. Taking
t=u
or
t=p-u
results in the pair
\underline{t}=\underline{s}=u
and, by Theorem
3
(iii,iv), either of these choices of

yields the diameter
2u+1
.

In the remaining cases
\underline{t}\neq\underline{s}
and Theorem
3
yields the evaluation

(12)

D(pqr)=\begin{cases}2\underline{s}+2,&\text{if }\underline{s}<\underline{t}\\
2\underline{s}+1,&\text{if }\underline{s}>\underline{t},\end{cases}

for primes

and

satisfying (
7
). Now, for each
a
in the range

there corresponds a unique

2\leq b\leq\frac{p-1}{2}
, such that

. It follows that the set of integers

in the range
2\leq n\leq\frac{p-1}{2}
partitions uniquely into pairs

(13)

\Bigl\{\,a,b\mid 2\leq a<b\leq\frac{p-1}{2}\text{ and }ab\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}\,\Bigr\}.

For each such pair
\{a,b\}
, taking
t=b
, so that
\underline{t}=b
and
\underline{s}=a
, and taking
t=a
, so that
\underline{t}=a
and
\underline{s}=b
, yields two different values of

in (

), namely

and

. Plainly, these values do not repeat for different choices of
\{a,b\}
.

The proof is completed by counting the distinct values of

generated above. One readily verifies that in both cases

the count yields the value
\frac{p+1}{2}
.

3.3.
Proof of Corollary

and consider pairs

given in (

. The first claim now follows on observing that, for

We now address the odd diameter claim. In Example

for each

. It remains to consider

for

and

. We argue in essentially the same way as in the even case. This time, for each pair
\{a,b\}

f(l^{\prime})\not\equiv f(L)\pmod{pq}\implies f(l^{\prime})<L/r
.

Knowing this allows us to determine
x_{l^{\prime}}
and
f(l^{\prime})
as follows (of course,
y_{l^{\prime}}=0
). By (

x_{l^{\prime}}=\langle x_{L^{\prime}}+x_{Q}\rangle_{p}=\langle a+b+x_{Q}\rangle_{p},

and we have the evaluation

But, by (

(a+b+x_{Q})q=\frac{L}{r}+(p+b-x_{R})t+x_{Q}q.

Therefore, it must be that

(96)

\displaystyle f(l^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{r}+(p+b-x_{R})t+x_{Q}q-pq

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{r}+(p+b-x_{R})t-x^{\prime}_{Q}q,

and that

(97)

x_{l^{\prime}}=\langle a+b+x_{Q}\rangle_{p}=a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q}.

In particular, we see that the parameters

and

must also satisfy the requirement

Finally, combining (

), (

) and (

).

The existence of integers

and

), as required by the hypothesis on

of this case, implies that, in addition to

and we see that

. In the opposite direction, assume that

. Now let

be any integer satisfying

\max(1,x_{R}-x_{Q}+1)\leq b<x_{R},

so that
x^{\prime}_{Q}-b<x^{\prime}_{R}
, and take any integer

in the range

\max(0,x^{\prime}_{Q}-b)\leq a<x^{\prime}_{R}.

Note that every such pair of

and

satisfy (
84
). Now let
l
and

be given by the formulas in (
83
), and let

be any integer in the range (

), so that

and

. One readily verifies that the relevant parts of the forgoing argument are reversible showing that

and

satisfy (

Lemma 22
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma

and for

of the form (

In the present case we work with the supper ranges of the form

for

and
l+Q+R
, where the parameter

) and (

in the previous cases. Indeed, recall that

contains both

\mathcal{R}_{3}(l)\supset I_{M}

For the range
\mathcal{R}_{3}(l)
we show that

(101)

\chi(l+i)=1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\max(b-x_{R}+1,-a)\leq i\leq 0,

(102)

f(l+i)\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{l+i}{r}\Big\rfloor\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\max(b-x_{R}+1,-a)\leq i\leq 0.

By Lemma
18
, it only remains to consider
b-x_{R}<i\leq 0
. Here is the list of ingredients we need.

\displaystyle\bullet\quad

\displaystyle\Big\lfloor\frac{l+i}{r}\Big\rfloor=\frac{l}{r}+\Big\lfloor\frac{i}{r}\Big\rfloor.

\displaystyle\bullet\quad

\displaystyle f(l+i)=\langle a+i\rangle_{p}q+\langle\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t-\vartheta i\rangle_{q}p.

\displaystyle\bullet\quad

\displaystyle\langle\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t-\vartheta i\rangle_{q}=\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t-\vartheta i,\quad\text{since }b\leq b-i<x_{R}.

\displaystyle\bullet\quad

\displaystyle\langle a+i\rangle_{p}=\begin{cases}a+i,&\text{for }\max(b-x_{R}+1,-a)\leq i\leq 0\\
a+i+p,&\text{for }b-x_{R}<i<\max(b-x_{R}+1,-a).\end{cases}

\displaystyle\bullet\quad

\displaystyle(a+i)q+(\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t-\vartheta i)p=\frac{l}{r}+it.

From this list the assertion (
102
), and hence (
101
), are immediate.

Next we show that

(103)

\chi\equiv 0\quad\text{on}\quad\mathcal{R}_{3}(l+R).

Lemma
18
takes care of
l+R+i
for
0<i<p
, and we know that

\chi(l+R)=\chi(L)=0\quad\text{and}\quad f(l+R)=\frac{l}{r}+\varepsilon_{R}+pq.

To evaluate

f(l+R+i)=\langle a+x_{R}+i\rangle_{p}q+\langle\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t+y_{R}-\vartheta i\rangle_{q}p

in the remaining range
b-x_{R}<i<0
, we observe that, by (
89
),

\langle a+x_{R}+i\rangle_{p}=a+x_{R}+i=x_{L}+i,

and that, by (
89
) and (
90
),

\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t+y_{R}=y_{L}=\vartheta(p+b-x_{R}),

whence

\langle\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t+y_{R}-\vartheta i\rangle_{q}=\vartheta(p+b-x_{R}-i)=y_{L}-\vartheta i.

This shows that, for
b-x_{R}<i\leq 0
, we have

\displaystyle f(l+R+i)

\displaystyle=(x_{L}+i)q+(y_{L}-\vartheta i)p=\frac{l+R}{r}+pq+it

\displaystyle>\Big\lfloor\frac{l+R+i}{r}\Big\rfloor,

and (
103
) follows.

Next we consider the range
\mathcal{R}_{3}(l+Q+R)
. In this range it is more convenient to work with respect to
l^{\prime}
, which was determined to be of the form

l^{\prime}=l+Q+R+p+b-x_{R}=l+R+x^{\prime}_{R}+b+Q

in Lemma
21
. To that end, we note that

\mathcal{R}_{3}(l+Q+R)=(l^{\prime}-p,l^{\prime}+x_{R}-b)\cap\mathbb{N}.

So we wish to evaluate
f(l^{\prime}+j)
, for
-p<j<x_{R}-b
, and compare it to

(104)

\Big\lfloor\frac{l^{\prime}+j}{r}\Big\rfloor=\frac{l}{r}+\varepsilon_{R}+\big\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{Q}}{r}\Big\rfloor.

We will show that, in this range,

(105)

f(l^{\prime}+j)\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{l^{\prime}+j}{r}\Big\rfloor\quad\text{if and only if}\quad x^{\prime}_{Q}-a-b\leq j\leq 0.

For the convenience of the reader, let us now recall the key attributes of
l^{\prime}
determined in Lemma
21
. We have, by (
97
) and (
96
):

•

x_{l^{\prime}}=a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q},\ y_{l^{\prime}}=0
,

•

f(l^{\prime})=(a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q})q=l/r+\varepsilon_{R}+(x^{\prime}_{R}+b)t-x^{\prime}_{Q}q
.

We are now ready to proceed. In preparation for comparing
f(l^{\prime}+j)
to (
104
) we observe that

(106)

f(l^{\prime}+j)=\langle a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q}+j\rangle_{p}q+\langle-\vartheta j\rangle_{q}p

and

(107)

(a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q}+j)q-\vartheta jp=\frac{l}{r}+\varepsilon_{R}+(x^{\prime}_{R}+b+j)t-x^{\prime}_{Q}q.

We have,

(108)

\langle-\vartheta j\rangle_{q}=\begin{cases}q-\vartheta j,&\text{for }0<j<x_{R}-b\\
-\vartheta j,&\text{for }-p<j\leq 0.\end{cases}

Note that, by (
84
),

(a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q})+(x_{R}-b)=a-x^{\prime}_{R}+x_{Q}<x_{Q}<p,

whence

(109)

\langle a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q}+j\rangle_{p}=\begin{cases}a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q}+j,&\text{for }x^{\prime}_{Q}-a-b\leq j<x_{R}-b\\
a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q}+j+p,&\text{for }-p<j<x^{\prime}_{Q}-a-b.\end{cases}

Combining (
106
)-(
109
) and (
104
) yields (
105
).

Condition (
105
) determines, in the usual way, the evaluation of
\chi
on
\mathcal{R}_{3}(l+Q+R)
. However, we will find it helpful to have this evaluation with respect to
l+Q+R
rather than
l^{\prime}
, and, moreover, in the following form. We have,

(110)

\chi(l+Q+R+i)=1

if and only if
(p+b-x_{R})-(a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q})\leq i\leq p+b-x_{R}
. This is immediate from (
105
) and (
83
). It helps to note that, by (
84
),
a+b<p
, so that

(p+b-x_{R})-(a+b-x^{\prime}_{Q})>b-x_{R}+x^{\prime}_{Q}>b-x_{R}.

We now come to the range
\mathcal{R}_{3}(l+Q)
. Our analysis of this range will proceed along familiar lines, but it will require somewhat greater effort than the other ranges. We shall see that there are two cases resulting in different outcomes. Namely, if
a<x^{\prime}_{Q}
, then, for
b-x_{R}<i<p
, we have

(111)

\chi(l+Q+i)=1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad x^{\prime}_{Q}-a\leq i<b,

but if
a\geq x^{\prime}_{Q}
, then

(112)

\chi(l+Q+i)=1

if and only if
\max(b-x_{R}+1,x^{\prime}_{Q}-a)\leq i<b
or
p+x^{\prime}_{Q}-a\leq i<p
. As usual, we will obtain these evaluations by solving the inequality

f(l+Q+i)\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{l+Q+i}{r}\Big\rfloor=\frac{l}{r}+\Big\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon_{Q}}{r}\Big\rfloor,

for the range of
i
in question. Note that since
f(l+Q+i)\not\equiv l/r\pmod{pq}
, this inequality is equivalent to the inequality

(113)

f(l+Q+i)<l/r,

and we wish to show that it holds precisely for the subranges of

given in (
111
) and (
112
), according to the corresponding case.

We begin by listing basic facts of the case. We have:

\displaystyle x_{l+Q}=\langle a+x_{Q}\rangle_{p},\quad y_{l+Q}=y_{l}=\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t,

(114)

\displaystyle f(l+Q+i)=\langle a+x_{Q}+i\rangle_{p}q+\langle\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t-\vartheta i\rangle_{q}p,

(115)

\displaystyle(a+x_{Q}+i)q+(\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t-\vartheta i)p=l/r+x_{Q}q+it,

and

(116)

\langle\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t-\vartheta i\rangle_{q}=\begin{cases}\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t-\vartheta i+q,&\text{for }b\leq i<p\\
\vartheta b+\eta_{R}-t-\vartheta i,&\text{for }b-x_{R}<i<b.\end{cases}

The analogue of (
116
) for
\langle a+x_{Q}+i\rangle_{p}
requires us to consider the two cases mentioned above. We treat the simpler of the two cases

(117)

\langle a+x_{Q}+i\rangle_{p}=\begin{cases}a+x_{Q}+i-p,&\text{for }x^{\prime}_{Q}-a\leq i<p\\

since, by (

),
a+b+x_{Q}\geq p
. Combining (

)-(

), we deduce that (

) holds if and only if
x^{\prime}_{Q}-a\leq i<b

Now suppose that

, so that

x_{l+Q}=\langle a+x_{Q}\rangle_{p}=a-x^{\prime}_{Q}.

In this case it is better to rewrite (

) and (

) in the form

\langle a-x^{\prime}_{Q}+i\rangle_{p}=\begin{cases}a-x^{\prime}_{Q}+i-p,&\text{for }p+x^{\prime}_{Q}-a\leq i<p\\

a-x^{\prime}_{Q}+i+p,&\text{for }b-x_{R}<i<x^{\prime}_{Q}-a,\end{cases}

where, of course, the third equality above is omitted if the range

it follows that (

f(l+Q+i)=l/r-x^{\prime}_{Q}q+it.

To deduce the range of

where this equality holds we appeal to (

)-(

) and (

, which follows by (

) and (

. As we pointed out at the outset of this proof,

Our next step is to show that the choice of

\chi(l+i)=\chi(l+Q+R+i)=0,

So the crux of the matter is the evaluation of

Finally, recall that we showed in Lemma

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(Q,R;M)\in\mathcal{M}\\
