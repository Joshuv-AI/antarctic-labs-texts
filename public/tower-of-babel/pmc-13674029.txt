Septum-guided segmentectomy for deep early-stage non–small cell lung cancer: A novel, margin-independent, parenchyma-sparing concept
Authors: Qing Wang, Xinshu Wu, Zhexu Wen, Yang Liu, Jun Zhang, Feng Yao, Zhigang Li, Junfeng Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe application of segmentectomy for deep-located tumors remains controversial as the result of margin concerns. This study evaluated septum-guided segmentectomy, which uses intersegmental septa and veins as anatomical boundaries to ensure precise dissection independent of fixed linear margin requirements.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 292 patients with deep-located cT1a-bN0M0 non–small cell lung cancer undergoing septum-guided segmentectomy (n = 130) or lobectomy (n = 162) via a minimally invasive approach (January 2019 to March 2025). To rigorously control for selection bias, propensity-score matching incorporating key physiological and anatomical confounders was performed, generating 88 well-balanced matched pairs (n = 176). Perioperative outcomes, locoregional recurrence, recurrence-free survival, and overall survival were evaluated.ResultsAfter propensity-score matching, baseline balance was achieved across key covariates. Survival analysis using stratified methods revealed no significant differences in recurrence-free survival or overall survival between the groups (P> .05). Notably, despite a substantial proportion of patients in the segmentectomy group not fulfilling standard National Comprehensive Cancer Network margin criteria (>20 mm or greater tumor size)—with a mean margin distance of 14.1 mm—no locoregional recurrence was observed during follow-up. R0 resection was achieved in all patients. Although segmentectomy involved longer operative times (P< .001), perioperative complications and hospital stays were highly comparable between the 2 cohorts.ConclusionsSeptum-guided segmentectomy demonstrates preliminary oncologic safety and perioperative outcomes comparable with lobectomy for deep-located early-stage non–small cell lung cancer. By using the intersegmental septum as a reliable anatomical barrier, this concept establishes a precise surgical paradigm that safely reduces the reliance on fixed margin distances.Key Words: septum-guided segmentectomy, anatomical resection, deep-located lung cancer, intersegmental septum, pulmonary segmentectomy, intersegmental vein

Anatomical concept of septum-guided segmentectomy for LS3 resection.

Central Message

Septum-guided segmentectomy uses anatomical boundaries to achieve comparable short-term oncologic outcomes to lobectomy, independent of linear margin distance requirements.

Perspective

Deep-located NSCLC poses challenges for sublobar resection. This study demonstrates that septum-guided segmentectomy, by prioritizing anatomical integrity over rigid margin distances, provides a safe, parenchymal-sparing alternative to lobectomy. These findings support shifting the surgical paradigm from geometric margins to anatomical boundaries.

Historically, achieving an adequate margin distance or margin-to-tumor ratio ≥1 has been a fundamental oncologic principle for patient selection in sublobar resection.1,2 Recent trials, including JCOG0802, have established segmentectomy as a standard for small peripheral non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).3,4 However, applying this procedure to deep-located tumors remains controversial.5 The inherent conical geometry of pulmonary segments—tapering toward the hilum—often makes achieving rigid linear margins (eg, >2 cm or greater tumor diameter) anatomically infeasible without sacrificing excessive healthy tissue or converting to lobectomy.6,7 Consequently, strict adherence to margin-distance mandates limits the applicability of segmentectomy for these deep-seated lesions.8

To address this dilemma, we revisited the fundamental anatomy of the lung to propose a solution determined by anatomical integrity rather than linear distance. Embryologically, intersegmental septa originate from early connective tissue partitions, establishing segmental independence.9 Recent anatomical and imaging studies further substantiate that these septa form dense, well-defined layers containing veins and lymphatics, exhibiting a structural logic that parallels the secondary pulmonary lobule.10,11

On the basis of this strong embryologic and anatomical evidence, we propose “septum-guided segmentectomy.” This concept uses the intersegmental septa and veins as definitive natural boundaries, prioritizing the integrity of these anatomical barriers over subjectively set linear margin distances. It is critical to distinguish septum-guided segmentectomy from traditional subsegmentectomy and conventional precise segmentectomy. Although subsegmentectomy merely describes the resection of a smaller anatomical volume, our septum-guided approach represents a distinct surgical philosophy. Conventional precise segmentectomies often rely on “functional boundaries”—using inflation-deflation or indocyanine green fluorescence. In contrast, the septum-guided technique relies exclusively on “anatomical boundaries” by meticulously dissecting intersegmental veins, offering a novel paradigm for sublobar resection. This study evaluates whether this concept can achieve short-term oncologic outcomes comparable with lobectomy.

Methods

Ethics Statement

The study was approved by the institutional review board of Shanghai Chest Hospital (no. IS24131; September 2, 2024), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective design.

Patients

We retrospectively reviewed patients with deep-located cT1abN0M0 NSCLC (inner two-thirds; tumor centrality ratio <0.67) undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) surgery between January 2019 and March 2025. The septum-guided segmentectomy cohort was managed by a single surgical group to ensure technical consistency, whereas the lobectomy cohort included patients treated by other surgical teams at the same institution as controls. Inclusion criteria were deep-located tumors (tumor centrality ratio <0.67), confirmed NSCLC histology, tumor size ≤2 cm, and negative nodal involvement (cN0). Exclusion criteria were small cell lung cancer, right middle lobe lesions, neoadjuvant therapy, or incomplete data. Staging followed the ninth edition tumor, node, metastasis classification.12 The selection process is detailed in Figure E1.

Study flowchart. SCLC, Small cell lung cancer; PSM, propensity-score matching.

Preoperative Evaluation

Preoperative evaluation included high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT), 3-dimensional reconstruction, 18-fluoro-2-deoxyglucose-positron emission tomography/computed tomography (CT), and pulmonary function testing. The surgical procedure was determined by the multidisciplinary team on the basis of tumor characteristics and patient preference. Deep-located tumors were defined by a tumor centrality ratio <0.67 (Figure 1, A-C). Crucially, preoperative planning stratified lesions into 3 types on the basis of their spatial relationship to subsegmental and intersegmental planes to determine the precise extent of resection (eg, isolated subsegmentectomy vs combined resection), as detailed in Figure 1, D.

Preoperative planning. A, Schematic of tumor centrality ratio. B, Axial computed tomography showing a deep-located subsolid nodule (arrow) in the right upper lobe. C, Three-dimensional reconstruction confirming deep localization (ratio 0.59). D, Preoperative planning for varying lesion types.

Surgical Procedure

All procedures used a uniportal VATS approach via a single 3-cm incision in the fourth, fifth, or sixth intercostal space depending on the target segment. CT-guided localization was applied when necessary.

For septum-guided segmentectomy, intersegmental veins were identified on preoperative HRCT and visualized on the lung surface intraoperatively (Video 1). The core technical principle involved tracking these veins to dissect the intersegmental septa, thereby defining the resection boundaries. Within these septa, segmental arteries and bronchi were identified and divided. A modified inflation/deflation method was selectively used to validate boundaries.13 Crucially, for the inner two-thirds of the lung parenchyma, energy-based instruments were used for sharp dissection strictly along the intersegmental septum, and dissection was carried distal to the subsubsegmental bronchial orifice; linear staplers were used only for the outer third. This strategy allowed en bloc resection while preserving functional tissue (Figure E2). The specific anatomical application for LS3 resection is demonstrated in Figure 2. Standard VATS lobectomy and systematic lymph node dissection were performed according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines.14

Intraoperative frozen section analysis was routinely performed for the tumor, bronchial stumps, and suspicious lymph nodes. Assessment of the pulmonary resection margin relied on visual evaluation by the surgeon and assistant without frozen pathologic examination; the margin distance was measured on the specimen from the tumor to the closest resection edge. If either operator considered the margin insufficient, further resection (such as additional subsegmentectomy) was performed to ensure achievement of R0 resection. Notably, in this series, no cases required conversion to lobectomy because of positive margins. The surgical strategy in this study emphasized strict adherence to anatomical boundaries and obtaining histologically negative margins, rather than enforcing a fixed linear margin distance (eg, >2 cm). Remarkably, postoperative paraffin pathology confirmed negative margins in all cases.

Follow-Up Examination

Postoperative surveillance included CT of the chest, physical examination, and tumor markers every 6 to 12 months for the first 5 years depending on histologic invasiveness, followed by annual check-ups. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain and positron emission tomography-CT were selectively performed at 1.5 and 5 years for invasive carcinomas.

Outcomes

Primary end points were recurrence-free survival (RFS; time to recurrence or death) and overall survival (OS; time to death from any cause). Crucially, locoregional recurrence rate was evaluated as a key secondary end point, specifically focusing on the oncological efficacy of the anatomical boundaries. Other secondary end points included operative time (skin-to-skin), intraoperative blood loss, and perioperative complications (eg, prolonged air leakage, chylothorax).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test and categorical variables using the χ2 or Fisher exact test. To control for confounders, 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity-score matching (PSM) without replacement was performed (caliper = 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score). Matching covariates included age, sex, tumor size, lesion type, location, and multiplicity. Postmatch balance was assessed using the standardized mean difference (SMD < 0.20). Survival curves for OS and RFS were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with stratified log-rank tests to account for the matched cohort's paired structure. Analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.3).

Results

Patient Selection and Grouping

From 3264 screened patients, 492 presented with deep-located lesions. After exclusions detailed in Figure E1, 292 eligible patients were categorized into lobectomy (n = 162) and segmentectomy (n = 130) groups. PSM generated 88 matched pairs for final comparative analysis (Figure E3).

Surgical Site, Segmentation Type, and Margin Analysis

Surgical site and segmentation type distributions remained consistent postmatching (Figures E4 and E5). Margin analysis revealed no significant differences in mean margin distance (11.2 vs 14.1 mm, P= .789) or in the rate of compliance with NCCN distance criteria (61.5% vs 65.2%, P= .767; Figure 3). Notably, approximately 35% of patients in each group failed to meet the segmentectomy-specific distance thresholds (>2 cm or greater than tumor diameter). Among the 32 segmentectomy cases that did not meet these distance criteria, zero locoregional recurrences were observed during the follow-up period.

Perioperative and Pathologic Outcomes

Compared with lobectomy, segmentectomy was associated with significantly longer operative times both before and after propensity matching (P< .001; Table 2). No significant differences were observed between groups in postoperative recovery outcomes, including length of hospital stay, duration of chest drainage, and incidence of complications such as prolonged air leak or chylothorax. Pathologic examination revealed more aggressive tumor features in the lobectomy cohort; even after matching, micropapillary and solid components were significantly more frequent in lobectomy specimens (P= .03, .002), whereas other histologic parameters were comparable between groups (Online Data Supplement).

Survival Outcomes

Survival analysis revealed comparable outcomes between the 2 groups. Before matching, no significant differences were observed in OS (P= .15) or RFS (P= .075; Figure 4, A and C). During a median 24-month follow-up, 7 events (4 deaths and 5 recurrences) occurred in the lobectomy group (Online Data Supplement). After PSM, this trend remained consistent, with no statistically significant differences in OS (P= 1) or RFS (P= 1; Figure 4, B and D). Within the matched cohort, no patients recorded events.

Discussion

This PSM analysis demonstrates that septum-guided segmentectomy provides short-term oncologic outcomes comparable with lobectomy for deep cT1abN0M0 NSCLC. Although meticulous dissection prolonged operative times, perioperative safety remained comparable without increased complications. Crucially, despite ∼35% of segmentectomies failing NCCN linear margin thresholds (>2 cm or greater tumor diameter), locoregional control and OS matched those of lobectomy. These results challenge the reliance on rigid geometric margins, suggesting oncologic success for deep tumors depends more on anatomical boundary integrity than linear distance. In lobectomy, margin distances often deemed “insufficient” because of the anatomical constraints of the interlobar fissures are nevertheless considered oncologically acceptable. We propose that septum-guided segmentectomy achieves comparable oncologic safety by using the intersegmental septa as its intrinsic anatomic boundary, just as lobectomy relies on the interlobar fissure irrespective of absolute margin distance. The rationale for this concept stems from the unique anatomical constraints posed by deep-seated lesions. The inherent conical shape of pulmonary segments, which taper toward the hilum, often makes a 2-cm margin spatially unattainable without resecting excessive parenchyma or converting to lobectomy.15,16 Our septum-guided strategy addresses this geometric dilemma by shifting the paradigm from distance-based to boundary-based resection. Anatomically, intersegmental septa are dense connective tissue layers containing veins and lymphatics and serve as natural physical and oncologic barriers.16 These septa function as lymphatic “watersheds,” isolating the drainage of adjacent segments. By meticulously tracing intersegmental veins and preserving septal integrity, we ensure complete removal of the tumor-bearing unit together with its lymphatic drainage system. This may explain the absence of local recurrence even in cases with sub-centimeter margins, provided the septal boundary remained intact.

Although landmark studies established the “2 cm or tumor diameter” rule,1,17 and some recent protocols still mandate these strict margins for deep lesions,18,19 our strategy offers a more flexible, boundary-based alternative. Growing evidence, however, increasingly supports anatomical resection over linear metrics. Multi-institutional analyses indicate that once R0 resection is attained, margin distance does not independently predict survival.20 Similarly, other recent studies focusing on deep or inner-located tumors report equivalent outcomes for segmentectomy and lobectomy despite significantly shorter margins.8,21 Our findings align with this evolving view. We speculate that biological containment within the intersegmental septum acts as a more reliable anatomical barrier against local spread (eg, spread through air spaces) than a linearly measured distance that crosses anatomical planes. In conventional practice, to achieve linear margins for deep lesions, surgeons frequently perform an “extended segmentectomy” by intentionally firing staplers across anatomical planes into neighboring segments. Although securing a macroscopic distance, this aggressive stapling sacrifices healthy, functional lung tissue—sometimes resulting in a volume loss approximating a lobectomy. In contrast, our septum-guided approach strictly respects the natural intersegmental plane, maximizing parenchyma preservation.

Technically, the septum-guided approach provides notable adaptability. By precisely defining intersegmental planes, surgeons can perform tailored resections—including combined subsegmentectomies—that extend beyond rigid anatomical templates (Figure E4). Although this “bloodless dissection” along the septal plane prolongs operative time compared with stapler-based lobectomy, our data confirm this time investment yields no detrimental clinical effects. Instead, it offers a clear advantage: preservation of natural tissue barriers likely contributed to the low rate of prolonged air leakage in our cohort, comparable with that of lobectomy. Unlike conventional techniques, in which staplers or energy devices transect parenchyma—often causing occult alveolar injury and distortion—our method gently separates tissue along the physiological “expansion plane” of intersegmental veins. This maintains visceral pleural-like integrity of the remaining segments, thereby minimizing alveolar air leaks.

This study has several limitations. First, in this retrospective, single-center analysis, unmeasured confounders may remain despite rigorous PSM. Notably, treatment was clustered: a specialized group performed the segmentectomies, whereas other teams performed control lobectomies, potentially introducing minor perioperative variations. Second, the short median follow-up (24 months) yielded extremely few oncologic events, precluding multivariable regression. Consequently, the study is underpowered to formally prove noninferiority. The lack of significant survival differences indicates preliminary short-term safety rather than definitive equivalence, carrying a potential risk of type II error. Third, although our segmentectomy cohort safely included some pure solid (n = 7) and solid-predominant (consolidation-to-tumor ratio >0.5) nodules, it predominantly comprised ground-glass opacity–type lesions. Because aggressive solid tumors have a greater propensity for microscopic spread (eg, spread through air spaces), the safety of narrow septal margins for these subtypes requires extended validation. Fourth, widespread adoption may be limited by infrastructure. Although proficient surgeons rely on intraoperative landmarks, initial safe implementation requires HRCT and 3-dimensional reconstruction, which are not universally available.

Conclusions

Septum-guided segmentectomy provides perioperative safety and short-term oncologic outcomes comparable with lobectomy for deep-located early-stage NSCLC. By using intersegmental septa as natural anatomical barriers, this concept enables a precise and parenchyma-sparing paradigm that safely reduces the absolute reliance on rigid linear margin criteria.
