IL-6 trans-signaling in cancer metabolism, multidrug resistance, and chronic inflammation
Authors: Federica Melisi, Lucia Stefania Pasquale, Teresa Peluso, Cinzia Graziano, Clara Iannarone, Margherita Vicario, Maria Roberta De Iesu, Martina Izzo, Rosalba Ricciardi, Marco Bocchetti, Alessia Maria Cossu, Giuseppe Matarese, Marianna Scrima, Michele Caraglia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interleukin-6 (IL-6) trans-signaling plays a pivotal role in cancer progression and the regulation of metabolic pathways, exhibiting significant tissue specificity. IL-6, a pleiotropic cytokine, mediates cellular communication and inflammatory responses through distinct pathways with both beneficial and detrimental effects. Trans-signaling occurs when IL-6 binds to its soluble receptor (sIL-6Rɑ), forming a complex with the signal transducer gp130. This pathway is associated with a pro-inflammatory phenotype, and not by chance, IL-6 trans-signaling also contributes to tumorigenesis, promoting angiogenesis, resistance to apoptosis, and metastasis. This review highlights the significance and tissue-specificity of IL-6 trans-signaling activation in cancer biology, metabolism, and drug resistance, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target. Indeed, IL-6 trans-signaling influences metabolic processes by altering glucose and lipid metabolism, thereby supporting energy demands from rapidly proliferating cancer cells. Through metabolic reprogramming IL-6 trans-signaling not only fuels cancer cell growth but also contributes to the development of a tumor-friendly microenvironment that is IL-6-dependent and self-sustaining. These events contribute also to development of multidrug resistance during cancer progression. An in-depth understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying IL-6 trans-signaling will offer novel insights into precision medicine for cancer treatment and for the sensitization of cancer cells to antitumor weapons.

Background

IL-6 signaling cascade: Mechanisms and effects

IL-6 governs various cellular and molecular pathways that participate in both physiological and pathological conditions, thereby endowing this cytokine with a role in controlling organismal homeostasis at a different level. Intracellular signaling associated with IL-6 has pleiotropic effects in managing inflammation and immunity, as well as tissue regeneration and proliferation. It is possible to include IL-6 downstream pathways in both conventional and non-conventional signaling cascade (Figure 1). However, the IL-6 signaling is also linked to the transition from acute to chronic inflammation. Indeed, it promotes several inflammatory conditions (i.e., autoimmune diseases) through the sIL-6Rɑ in a biological pathway known as trans-signaling (1). Classical IL-6 signaling occurs when IL-6 binds to mIL-6Rα, which is generally expressed on the cell surface of specific cell types, such as hepatocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, and resting lymphocytes (2). Conversely, trans-signaling depends on sIL-6Rɑ, which is produced through alternative splicing and receptor shedding, i.e., proteolytic cleavage originating from the transmembrane IL-6R of specific cellular subgroups (3), such as leukocytes, neutrophils, and hepatocytes (4, 5). IL-6 Rɑ shedding is mediated by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase (ADAM) family member, specifically ADAM17. It orchestrates the release of the soluble portion of IL-6Rɑ in the extracellular microenvironment and regulates various biological processes dependent on IL-6 (6).

In both classical and trans-signaling, the functionality of the membrane-bound or soluble IL-6Rɑ isoforms centers on gp130, a non-binding receptor subunit that is universally expressed, enabling even cells lacking IL6-Rɑ (7) to respond to IL-6-induced stimuli. In both IL-6 classic and trans-signaling, the downstream activation of gp130 triggers intracellular signaling cascades by activating downstream pathways such as JAK/STAT3, MAPK, and PI3K/AKT (8). However, evidence suggests an imbalance favoring enhanced activation of the JAK/STAT3 pathway over the other two in conditions involving inflammation and trans-signaling stimulation (9). IL-6 trans-signaling produces a more substantial and sustained signal amplification in response to IL-6 stimuli (10), which has been linked to immunological stress, chronic inflammation, and cancer. The IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway is overactivated in cancer, thus influencing tumor microenvironment and promoting transformed cell proliferation, survival, invasion, and metastasis. Targeting the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway is expected to offer potential therapeutic benefits and blocking IL-6 trans-signaling is a promising therapeutic strategy for several pathological conditions (11).

Finally, there is a third mechanism by which IL-6 facilitates cell-cell communication through direct interactions that are often disrupted in autoimmune conditions. This process, known as IL-6 trans-presentation, is key in initiating differentiation of proinflammatory T-Helper 17 cells (Th17). Indeed, Th17 skewing occurs when dendritic cells (DCs) capture IL-6 and present it linked to their IL-6 receptor (IL-6Ra) to T cells expressing gp130 (12). Dysregulation of this process can result in the transition to a pathogenic Th17 cell phenotype, in which hyperactivation of Th17 cells against autoantigens triggers the breach of immunological self-tolerance typical of autoimmune disorders (13).

Approaches targeting IL-6 trans-signaling pathways

IL-6 and its associated trans-signaling pathway have emerged as the most significant biological process through which IL-6 exerts its pathological activities. Unlike IL-6 classic signaling, trans-signaling has been suggested as a potential therapeutic target to counteract tumor progression and chronic inflammatory diseases (10). Given the pleiotropic nature of IL-6, trans-signaling enables activation of cells lacking membrane-bound IL-6R (mIL-6R), expanding its influence beyond immune cells to epithelial, endothelial, and connective tissue. Consequently, there is a consistent interest in IL-6 trans-signaling in tumor growth, metastasis, immune escape, and drug resistance.

Modulating IL-6 trans-signaling is challenging due to its complex regulatory axis. Endogenous soluble gp130 (sgp130) acts as a natural inhibitor by sequestering IL-6/sIL-6R complexes, preventing activation (14). To counteract these pro-inflammatory and cancer-promoting effects, molecules such as Hyper-IL-6 and sgp130fc have been developed. Hyper-IL-6 selectively activates trans-signaling without interfering with classical signaling, which depends on IL-6 binding to its membrane receptor.

Structurally, Hyper-IL-6 is a fusion protein where sIL-6R is linked to IL-6 via a flexible 13-amino acid connector, forming a stable ligand/receptor complex (15). In contrast, sgp130fc (Olamkicept), consisting of the extracellular domain of gp130 fused to the Fc portion of human IgG1, neutralizes the IL-6/sIL-6R complex by blocking trans-signaling while preserving classical signaling. Specificity is ensured because IL-6 and sIL-6R alone cannot bind gp130, and natural sgp130 interacts with membrane IL-6/IL-6R complexes only in the absence of cell surface gp130 (10). Nonetheless, the inhibitory effect of sgp130fc does not reach a complete inhibition. A number of studies indicate that gp130 colocalizes with mIL-6R, preventing sgp130fc from inhibiting classical signaling (15). Other studies suggest that its efficacy depends on the ratio of IL-6/sIL-6R complexes (16).

These findings highlight how subtle molecular mechanisms may affect this pathway regulation, often deregulated in cancer, and provide the molecular basis for precision medicine approaches aimed at selectively blocking pro-inflammatory trans-signaling. The cellular response to IL-6 trans-signaling depends also on receptor component ratios, such as IL-6Rα and gp130, which vary across tissues. This tissue-specific distribution influences both the strength and nature of IL-6 signaling, allowing distinct roles in diverse microenvironmental conditions. For instance, in some tissues IL-6 trans-signaling predominantly drives pro-inflammatory responses, whereas in others it modulates metabolic or regenerative functions. Indeed, the dual and context-dependent functions of IL-6 impose intrinsic limitations on the use of non-selective IL-6 pathway inhibitors, such as Tocilizumab, in oncology. Because these agents block both classical and trans-signaling, they suppress not only the pro-inflammatory, tumor-promoting arm of IL-6 activity but also its physiological regenerative and immunoprotective functions. This lack of selectivity contributes to broad immunosuppression, impaired tissue repair, and ultimately suboptimal therapeutic benefit in cancer settings. In contrast, Olamkicept selectively inhibits IL-6 trans-signaling while sparing classical signaling, thereby preserving homeostatic IL-6 functions. This confers a more favorable safety profile and positions Olamkicept as a promising strategy to attenuate chronic inflammation and tumor-supportive cytokine circuits without compromising essential IL-6–mediated physiological processes. Further understanding these phenomena will be essential for designing targeted therapies that selectively block IL-6 trans-signaling in defined disease settings.

Effects of IL-6 on the modulation of inflammatory pathways

The IL-6 trans-signaling process plays a crucial role in chronic inflammatory responses and various inflammatory diseases (Table 1). While IL-6 signaling is involved in acute inflammation, prolonged activity contributes to progression of chronic autoimmune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (17). In IBD, elevated sIL-6R levels correlate with disease activity. Mitsuyama et al. observed that patients with active IBD had higher sIL-6R levels than those with inactive IBD, also associated with elevated C-reactive protein, a marker of inflammation. The IL-6/sIL-6R complex activates T cells in the lamina propria, promoting apoptosis resistance and persistence of inflammation. In vitro studies confirmed that inhibiting trans-signaling using sgp130 or neutralizing antibodies against IL-6R promoted T cell apoptosis, suggesting that T cell retention via IL-6-mediated signaling is a key factor in chronic inflammation (18). Role of IL-6 Trans-signalling in inflammatory and autoimmune diseases

In RA, elevated sIL-6R and IL-6 levels are linked to joint destruction. It has been hypothesized that sIL-6R originates from infiltrating leukocytes in arthritic joints, suggesting that its regulation may play a crucial role in the development of joint diseases. Studies in mice have shown that inhibiting IL-6 trans-signaling, through neutralising antibodies or sgp130, reduced joint inflammation, indicating its therapeutic potential also for arthritis (20). In this context, IL-6 trans-signaling mediates expansion of pathogenic Th17 cells in RA (21), driving tissue and organ infiltration by leukocytes through proinflammatory cytokines. Expansion of Th17 cells can be inhibited by the IL-6 trans-signaling inhibitor sgp130Fc (22, 23, 24).

Ongoing inflammatory responses lead to chronic inflammation, linked to autoimmune diseases. Breakdown of immune tolerance activates autoreactive immune cells, which release cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1 and recruit more T and B cells to the inflammation site (25). IL-6 plays a critical role in innate and adaptive immune systems, with increased IL-6 trans-signaling observed in various autoimmune diseases. For example, in Diabetic Retinopathy (DR) (26), it contributes to ocular inflammation and oxidative stress, with elevated sIL-6R levels in serum and aqueous humor (27, 28). In osteoarthritis (OA) (29), chondrocytes produce and release sIL-6R. IL-6/sIL-6R complex signaling increases production of NO, PGE2, MMP1, and glycolytic ATP, while reducing cartilage protein and mitochondrial ATP production. In scleroderma (30), IL-6 trans-signaling could promote fibrosis, as demonstrated by humanized mice with scleroderma skin grafts, exhibiting T cell activation markers, including increased MHC class II gene expression. Activated CD4+ T cells produce sIL-6R, which interacts with IL-6 to induce excessive ECM gene expression in fibroblasts. In experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), a model of multiple sclerosis, IL-6 trans-signaling contributes to autoimmune inflammation of the central nervous system by regulating early expression of adhesion molecules like VCAM at the blood-brain barrier, and its blockade reduces T cell and macrophage/microglia numbers (31). Th17 differentiation in EAE is modulated by C1q/TNF-related protein 4 (CTRP4) in response to IL-6 trans-signaling (32).

In patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and lupus nephritis (LN), altered glycosphingolipids impair IL-6 secretion and signaling. In patients with LN, sIL-6R levels are higher than sgp130, which may reflect overactive IL-6 trans-signaling (33, 34). In this context, selective inhibition of IL-6 trans-signaling may represent a promising therapeutic breakthrough. Reducing chronic inflammation without compromising IL-6 regenerative functions offers a balance between efficacy and safety. In a clinical landscape where side effects often limit long-term treatments, sgp130Fc emerges as a refined and sustainable strategy, with potential oncological and metabolic applications.

Effects of IL-6 on the modulation of cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and metastatization

IL-6 plays a multifaceted role also in cancer biology through its trans-signaling pathway (Table 2). The IL-6/sIL-6R complex binds gp130, triggering signaling cascades such as JAK/STAT3, Ras/Raf/MAPK, and PI3K/Akt, driving cell survival, proliferation, and mitotic activity (14, 15). IL-6 trans-signaling hyperactivation shapes cancer cell behavior and the tumor microenvironment, promoting proliferation, metastatic, and metabolic reprogramming (10). Persistent STAT3 activation is linked to decreased survival in prostate cancer (PCa) patients (16, 35), and, through SOCS3 suppression, enhances tumor invasiveness, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and metastasis in breast cancer (BC) (36), and colorectal cancer (CRC) via carcinoembryonic antigens (CEACAM5 and CEACAM6) expression (37). Elevated IL-6 and autocrine STAT3 signaling also drive therapy resistance: indeed, Santer et al. (38) showed that IL-6/sIL-6R increases PCa cell motility and migration while reducing adhesion, facilitating metastases (39). IL-6 trans-signaling downregulates the tumor suppressor Maspin. In an Azoxymethane-Dextran Sodium Sulfate (AOM-DSS) colitis-associated carcinogenesis model, dominant-negative TGF-beta receptor II variant in T cells of mice increases tumor frequency and size, consistent with mutations observed in CRC patients. Scheller et al. demonstrated that reduced TGF-β stimulation in T cells increases IL-6 production, which binds sIL6-R secreted by tumor epithelial cells following ADAM17-mediated cleavage. Role of IL-6 trans-signaling in cancer biology

IL-6 supports cancer stem cells (CSCs) and dormant tumor cells (DTCs), contributing to therapy resistance and metastatic recurrence (42). In a model of Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC), Ogawa et al. demonstrated that IL-6 secretion by CSCs drives tumor organoid formation, suggesting that IL-6 inhibition could disrupt CSC-driven growth. This supports the feasibility of repurposing IL-6 inhibitors, such as Tocilizumab, for NSCLC (43). The same effect on stem-like phenotype was observed by Khatib et al., who identified glutathione peroxidase 8 (GPX8) as a regulator of cancer stemness associated with the expression of mesenchymal markers in BC (44). GPX8 ensures IL-6 receptor maturation and enables sIL-6R to activate the JAK/STAT3 pathway. Loss of GPX8 impairs sIL-6R functionality, reducing tumor cell aggressiveness and metastatic potential. IL-6 trans-signaling also plays a fundamental role in recruiting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), establishing a feedback loop that inhibits immune responses while promoting disease progression (45).

Several studies highlight the involvement of IL-6 trans-signaling in a signaling axis regulated by microRNAs (miRNAs), acting as oncogenes or oncosuppressors. We identified sIL-6R as a target of miR-449a, which exhibits anti-tumorigenic effects by inhibiting IL-6 trans-signaling, preventing STAT3 and MAPK activation, and blocking epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in laryngeal cancer (LCa) (46). miR-449a synergises with sgp130fc (Olamkicept), effectively blocking IL-6 trans-signaling even in the presence of Hyper-IL6. Cheng-Chia Yu et al. explored the role of miR-145 in head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC), showing that miR-145 suppresses IL-6 and sIL-6R, key mediators of tumor-initiating cells (TICs) formation, by targeting SOX9 and ADAM17. Clinically, low miR-145 combined with high SOX9 and ADAM17 correlates with poor prognosis, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of the miR-145/SOX9/ADAM17 pathway and its impact on IL-6 paracrine signaling. Preclinical studies demonstrate that curcumin administration or lentiviral delivery of miR-145 restores its expression, blocks TICs, and significantly prevents tumor progression (47).

IL-6 signaling is more impactful on tissues lacking the membrane receptor, being associated with pro-inflammatory and pathological actions. Nevertheless, recent studies have underlined the importance of IL-6 trans-signaling even in tissues expressing membrane-bound IL-6R, demonstrating its role in promoting cancer growth, shaping the tumour microenvironment, and influencing metabolic pathways. In the liver context, IL-6 exhibits physiological and regulatory properties, leading to the rapid regeneration of hepatic tissues after partial hepatectomy (48, 49). Conversely, IL-6 trans-signaling has also been associated with tumour growth, cell survival, self-renewal, angiogenesis, invasiveness, and metastasis of Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) cells. Many studies (50) indicate that targeting this pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy in HCC. In particular, IL-6 trans-signaling is critical for HCC pathogenesis, through two distinct mechanisms: inhibition of apoptosis and promotion of angiogenesis (51). IL-6 trans-signaling can prevent DNA damage-induced apoptosis by suppressing p53 and activating β-catenin. Bergmann et al. demonstrated that the proliferation of endothelial cells in the liver is stimulated in a cancerous environment by IL-6 trans-signaling, enhancing neoangiogenesis, another hallmark of cancer. IL-6 trans-signaling contributes to the formation of malignant ascites by increasing capillary permeability, stimulating angiogenesis, and facilitating tumor cell intravasation, also in ovarian cancer (OC). This effect is amplified by the activation of the tyrosine kinase Src, which phosphorylates VE-cadherin at tyrosine 685 (Y685), destabilizing endothelial junctions and increasing vascular permeability. The IL-6/MAPK/SRC pathway is further involved in maintaining vascular integrity, contributing to the hyperpermeability observed in malignant ascites. Lastly, IL-6 trans-signaling reduces chemotherapy efficacy by promoting endothelial cell survival through ERK activation, thereby preventing apoptosis (52).

Effects of IL-6 on multidrug resistance

IL-6 also plays a role in multidrug resistance, influencing therapy response in cancer. It contributes to drug resistance by activating survival pathways in tumor cells, such as anti-apoptotic signaling, which helps them evade drug-induced cell death (53). Furthermore, IL-6 can control the levels of drug efflux pumps, such as P-glycoprotein, which expels chemotherapy drugs from cells, thereby improving their resistance properties. Indeed, Allman et al. demonstrated that levels of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) drug efflux transporters in human T cells exposed to an HIV pseudotype virus are related to mRNA and protein levels of IL-6, suggesting an interesting regulatory loop that influences antiretroviral drug penetration in activated T cells (54). In B cell and T cell lymphoma cell lines, mutations and amplification of PI3KCA contribute to resistance to PI3K inhibitors, such as Copanlisib and Duvelisib. Interestingly, IL-6 levels and the activation of its downstream pathways, mediated by pSTAT3 and pSTAT5, are increased in resistant cells.

On the other hand, the depletion of IL-6 sensitizes cells to treatment, and the use of JAK inhibitors enhances the sensitivity to immunotherapies (55). IL-6 represents a potential therapeutic target also in Cisplatin-resistant gastric cancer. In this context, IL-6 secretion increases following Cisplatin treatment, and activation of STAT3 signaling is also supported by GP130 overexpression. This mechanism led to resistance to chemotherapy, creating a pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironment that reduces the effects of drugs, promoting tumor cell survival and proliferation (56). Similar effects are reported also in thyroid carcinoma cells. Indeed, the sensitivity to BRAF inhibitors treatments, like PLX4032, is modulated by the activation of the IL-6/STAT3 axis, which promotes resistance mechanisms and increased IL-6 production (57).

However, the mechanism of action of IL-6 is part of a more complex network in which regulatory feedback loops are established between different cell types within a tumor context characterized by an inflammatory microenvironment. Several studies have shown that cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) actively contribute to tumor evolution by establishing paracrine stimulation mechanisms where secreted cytokines and chemokines, including IL-6, act on nearby cells, influencing the microenvironment. Studies performed by Cheteh et al. investigated the protective role exerted by IL-6 secreted by CAFs in PCa cells. In detail, both TP53 and pro-apoptotic BAX levels in tumor cells are negatively modulated by CAF-derived IL-6, via the JAK/STAT pathway. This effect is also exerted upon Doxorubicin treatment, suggesting that CAF-derived IL-6 is a key factor in protecting cancer cells from death induced by chemotherapy (58). This crosstalk also defines the sensitivity of BC cells, sustained by the complex environment that includes stromal cells along with other immune cells, to radiotherapy. Not by chance, the co-injection of tumor cells with CAFs in an in vivo model shows a better response to radiation in terms of tumor growth and radio resistance. Interestingly, nude mice treated with Tocilizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting IL-6R, exhibit a reversal of CAF-induced radio resistance (59).

These results suggest that blocking IL-6 or its downstream signaling pathways may be explored as a strategy to reverse drug resistance and enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy in resistant cells. Furthermore, understanding the IL-6 effect in tumor resistance mechanisms is necessary to develop more efficient therapeutic approaches. Indeed, in the evolving landscape of cancer therapy, IL-6 has emerged as a driver of intrinsic resistance in tumor cells and also as a mediator in adaptive resistance circuits, since its production could be a consequence of specific therapies. On one hand, IL-6 influences responses to chemotherapy, targeted agents, and even radiation; on the other hand, it manages a complex network of interactions, defined as paracrine interactions, which involve different cell types and inflammatory feedback loops that protect tumor cells from therapeutic approaches. In this complex framework, therapies should be optimized to target not only the cancer, often sustained by specific mutations and molecular alterations regardless of IL-6 action, but also modulate the interactions between stromal and immune cells within the microenvironment. Additionally, blocking IL-6 signaling may not reverse established resistance programs in late-stage diseases, as the tumor becomes sustained by multi-cytokine feedback networks that cannot be dismantled by IL-6 inhibition alone.

Interplay between IL-6 and cell metabolism

Cancer cells undergo complex metabolic adaptations to meet the high energy demands required for proliferation and motility—functions essential for tumor progression and intravasation during metastasis. Within this framework, a critical interconnection emerges between IL-6 trans-signaling and mitochondrial function. Xu et al. identified the gp130–JAK1/STAT1/3–HIF1α/ERRα axis as a central IL-6–responsive pathway controlling mitochondrial activity. Silencing HIF1A in hTERT RPE-1 cells increased basal mitochondrial ATP production and respiration, and it prevented the IL-6/sIL-6R–induced suppression of mitochondrial ATP output and respiratory parameters. HIF1A loss also enhanced maximal and spare respiration in IL-6/sIL-6R–treated cells. In contrast, HIF1A silencing reduced glycolysis under basal conditions but allowed IL-6/sIL-6R to increase glycolytic flux despite preserved mitochondrial respiration. This paradox may reflect compensatory HIF2A upregulation upon HIF1A silencing, consistent with prior reports, enabling glycolysis to rise without impairing mitochondrial function (60). Thus, inhibiting IL-6 signaling may preserve mitochondrial function and prevent conditions such as sarcopenia and frailty (61). This metabolic shift is closely linked to EMT, as IL-6 promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition and establishes a feed-forward loop in which metabolic reprogramming and EMT reinforce one another. Consistently, glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and lipid-metabolism genes correlate with EMT markers including SNAIL, TWIST, ZEB1, E-cadherin, and N-cadherin (62).

Moreover, ROS metabolism further intertwines with IL-6 signaling through GPX8, an endoplasmic reticulum–resident redox enzyme that regulates IL-6 production and drives STAT3 activation. In cancer cells, GPX8 expression is associated with EMT and aggressiveness by protecting against oxidative stress. The oxidative tumor environment promotes autocrine IL-6 secretion, which in turn sustains inflammation, ROS production, and GPX8 expression. Thus, GPX8 acts as a facilitator of IL-6-driven signaling, and its targeting may disrupt the IL-6/STAT3 loop, weakening tumor progression (44).

The influence of IL-6 trans-signaling extends beyond cancer, being implicated in metabolic conditions such as diabetes and obesity (63, 64), as well as alcohol-induced liver disease and infections (65). Obesity, for instance, induces chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, and ultimately type 2 diabetes (T2D) (66). The action of IL-6 on glucose flux exerts both anti- and pro-inflammatory effects. Indeed, classical IL-6 signaling drives glucose entry into the TCA cycle through the STAT3/PDK1/PDHA axis (67) whereas the activation of the pro-inflammatory trans-signaling pathway stimulates STAT3/LDHA/SIRT2 axis (68), with LDHA converting pyruvate to lactate (69), and thereby shifting metabolism toward anaerobic glycolysis (68). This metabolic reprogramming also affects hepatic metabolism, favouring liver carcinogenesis and leding to fibrosis progression in Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (70), particularly in diabetic patients (71).

Beyond glycolysis, IL-6 also significantly impacts lipid metabolism. It induces lipid droplet formation and arachidonic acid storage, fuelling the synthesis of prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and thromboxanes—lipid mediators that sustain inflammation and tumor–immune cell communication (72). In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), adipocyte-derived IL-6 activates JAK/STAT3 in tumor cells, increasing CXCL1 secretion. CXCL1 then binds CXCR2 on adipocytes, stimulating NF-κB and further IL-6 production, establishing a feedback loop that amplifies tumor invasion, migration, and TNBC progression (73). Systemically, IL-6 promotes adipose lipolysis and muscle catabolism, driving cachexia through inter-tissue signaling and inflammation (74). IL-6/sIL-6R complex selectively induces VLDLR, without affecting LDLR or LRP-1, highlighting a distinct regulatory axis. Whereas LDLR primarily mediates hepatic clearance of circulating LDL particles, VLDLR is expressed in peripheral tissues such as adipose tissue, heart, and vascular smooth muscle, where it promotes tissue lipid uptake. Thus, IL-6–driven VLDLR upregulation shifts lipid handling toward peripheral deposition rather than hepatic clearance, contributing to altered systemic lipid metabolism (75). Circulating monocytes accumulate lipid droplets and acquire increased adhesive capacity toward the endothelium, thereby promoting vascular inflammation. This phenotype is accompanied by elevated production of cytokines such as IL-6, contributing to early atherosclerosis (76). Understanding all these mechanisms opens promising avenues for therapies aimed at disrupting the IL-6-dependent inflammatory-metabolic axis.

IL-6 trans-signaling as a novel target for anti-cancer interventions in multidrug resistance

Cancer cells undergo a metabolic shift to sustain rapid growth, often favouring high-rate aerobic glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation, even when oxygen is sufficient to support mitochondrial respiration, in a process known as “the Warburg effect”. As previously mentioned, IL-6 plays a role in this transition, intervening in various phases of the metabolic switch that fuels tumour development. IL-6 enhances glucose uptake and glycolysis, while also regulating mitochondrial function, thereby affecting energy production. It interacts with the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathways, which control metabolism and cell survival (77). These metabolic adaptations influence response to anti-cancer treatments, and cancer cells developed the ability to shift reversibly between different types of metabolism to bypass drug effects and therapeutic stress (78). Recent studies suggest that targeting IL-6 could disrupt cancer metabolism, making tumours more vulnerable to treatment. Indeed, IL-6-induced glycolysis is directly related to radioresistance, as the activation of glycolytic pathways increases the production of ATP, a substrate for cellular repair processes following exposure to radiation. As a consequence, IL-6 stimulates increased expression of glycolytic enzymes and glucose transporters, supporting cell energy needs by reducing oxidative stress and by resisting therapy-induced damage. Not by chance, does the inhibition of glycolysis reverse the protective effects of IL-6 on the maintenance of mitochondrial integrity after radiotherapy. The impact of reduced ATP production then translates into an alteration of the mechanisms of repair and cellular survival, confirming IL-6 signaling as a potential target to bypass radio-resistance (77).

Other therapeutic approaches involve modulating the expression and activity of proteins activated downstream of the IL-6 signaling pathway. An example is the knockdown of Mitofusin 1 gene, whose expression is upregulated by IL-6; this strategy inhibits the protective effect exerted by this protein on mitochondrial function and contributes to sensitize Acute Myeloid Leukaemia cells to Cytarabine treatment (79). Targeting IL-6 trans-signaling by using IL-6R inhibitors, such as Tocilizumab, also represents a promising approach to limit the stem-phenotype in BC models. In this context, BC stem-like cells sustain tumorigenesis by different mechanisms, including immune evasion and drug resistance. In detail, therapy resistance is mediated by the ability of these cells to dynamically transform or transition between epithelial-like and mesenchymal-like states, modulating biological processes such as quiescence, DNA repair, drug efflux, and the modulation of the tumor microenvironment. Interestingly, a combinatorial approach targeting both HER2 and IL-6R (Trastuzumab + Tocilizumab) has shown promising therapeutic properties in HER2+ BC, disrupting both epithelial and mesenchymal states and reducing metastasis (80).

What makes IL-6 particularly insidious as a target in cancer is its duality since IL-6 regulates immune response and trans-signaling can exert also an anti-tumor effect by promoting the recruitment and activation of cytotoxic T cells, enhancing their ability to target and destroy tumor cells (81). In this context, commonly in the early stage of the disease, it facilitates T cell trafficking into the tumor microenvironment, potentially overcoming barriers that limit immune cell infiltration. Additionally, IL-6 can shift the immune response towards a more protective state, tilting the balance away from tumor growth and survival. This highlights how the action of IL-6 itself is strictly associated with multiple aspects of the disease such as the involvement of the immune system, the staging, and the presence of molecular alterations that hyperactivate tumorigenic pathways.

Pan-cancer analysis validated serum IL-6 as a prognostic inflammatory biomarker across different malignancies by reflecting systemic inflammation and by modulating tumor immune microenvironment, regulating immune checkpoint gene expression (82). Its superior prognostic accuracy compared to other inflammatory markers confirms it as a reliable indicator for predicting overall survival and disease progression in cancer. These observations raise important questions regarding the therapeutic value of IL-6 inhibition in tumorigenic processes. Although selectively blocking IL-6 trans-signaling can modulate cancer-associated pathways, including proliferation, invasion, and chronic inflammation, the clinical applicability of IL-6 targeted therapies is far from straightforward. Several factors must be considered: the presence of dominant oncogenic drivers that are more effectively targeted with established therapies; the complexity of the tumor microenvironment, which relies on redundant and compensatory multi-cytokine networks; the stage-specific role of IL-6 during tumor evolution; and the need to preserve immune competence, which may be inadvertently impaired by broad cytokine blockade.

Collectively, these elements complicate the interpretation of IL-6 as a standalone diagnostic or predictive biomarker in oncology and highlight why IL-6 inhibition, while mechanistically appealing, has not yet translated into consistent clinical benefit across cancer types.

IL-6 trans-signaling as a new target in inflammatory diseases

Targeted inhibition of IL-6 trans-signaling, obtained through genetic overexpression or pharmacological treatments with soluble proteins like sgp130Fc, has been shown to reduce inflammation in various experimental models without interfering with IL-6 signaling through the membrane-bound receptor, which is responsible for IL-6 regenerative and anti-inflammatory functions. Therefore, IL-6 trans-signaling is a mechanism in chronic inflammatory responses, and targeted therapeutic interventions could improve the conditions of diseases such as IBD and RA by reducing pathological inflammation while preserving the protective functions of IL-6 (83). The results suggest that IL-6 trans-signaling could be a therapeutic target to prevent macrophage accumulation in adipose tissue and reduce insulin resistance in obesity. Through genetic or pharmacological approaches, removing macrophages has been shown to decrease local and systemic inflammatory markers and improve insulin sensitivity in mouse models fed a high-fat diet. This approach could represent a therapeutic breakthrough in treating type 2 diabetes (T2D), as sgp130Fc is already in clinical trials for autoimmune diseases. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that IL-6 trans-signaling is a chemotactic signal for ATM recruitment in adipose tissue, and blocking trans-signaling with sgp130Fc prevents ATM recruitment induced by a high-fat diet. While this treatment does not directly improve insulin resistance, it also does not exacerbate weight gain, liver steatosis, or insulin resistance.

Furthermore, selective inhibition of IL-6 trans-signaling has shown more promise than complete IL-6R blockade (84). In animal models, blocking trans-signaling prevented the progression of inflammation without causing side effects, such as weight gain or insulin resistance, which were observed with complete IL-6R blockade (85). These findings suggest that targeting trans-signaling may offer a therapeutic strategy with fewer undesirable effects for chronic inflammatory diseases (27). In addition, IL-6 trans-signaling also contributes to the inflammatory response, tumor response, T cell proliferation, leukocyte recruitment, and stromal cell activation in oral carcinoma (86). It is well known how head and neck cancer development and outcome are associated with Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection, one of its most significant risk factors. HPV stimulates the release of cytokines, such as IL-6 and IL-1, via the acute phase inflammatory cascade, which may also impact IL-6 trans-signaling activation. This process plays a crucial role in the malignant transformation of HPV + cells, promoting tumor proliferation. In contrast, trans-signaling in HPV cells is linked to increased therapy resistance and poorer prognosis. Consequently, HPV + patients have a better prognosis, with higher 5-year survival rates (above 80%) compared to HPV- patients (around 50%) (87, 88). Furthermore, aberrant regulation of the JAK-STAT pathway, particularly upregulation of STAT3, contributes to tumor development and therapy resistance in both HPV+ and HPV- patients (89, 90).

Conclusion

Studies highlight how, among the various cytokines, IL-6 plays a central role in tumor progression and is validated as a prognostic biomarker in cancer and inflammatory diseases. However, there are critical issues that hinder its validation as a sole therapeutic target. Pharmacological approaches should be optimized to avoid affecting the physiological action of IL-6, which plays a role in regulating the immune response in the acute phase and early stages of the disease. Considering that cancer is a multifactorial disease characterized by the coexistence of molecular alterations, networks involving other cytokines, and feedback loops that characterize the tumor microenvironment, such modulation is complex. A deeper understanding of IL-6 trans-signaling across different tissues will be crucial for tailored interventions that balance its regenerative potential with oncogenic risks. Furthermore, modulating this complex pathway and its biological axis, which involves numerous molecular and cellular components, could be a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome drug resistance in cancer and enhance treatment efficacy.
