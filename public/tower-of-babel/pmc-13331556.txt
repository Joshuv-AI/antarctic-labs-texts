A microscopically assisted limited colectomy mouse model of early colon cancer
Authors: Yu Han, Yixin Zhang, Dazhi Gao, Xuefeng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrently, there is a lack of an adequate rodent model for investigating surgical techniques and comprehensive treatment options for patients with colorectal cancer (CRC). This study presents a mouse model that involves the orthotopic implantation of colon cancer, followed by a limited colectomy, with the aim of addressing this issue.MethodsTo establish an early colon tumor model, luciferase‐tagged CT‐26 cells were implanted into the ascending colon wall of BALB/c mice. After a 1‐week observation period, a limited colectomy procedure was performed. The progression of cancer and the impact of resection were monitored using bioluminescence imaging. Additionally, both short‐term and long‐term effects of surgical intervention were assessed.ResultsThe study demonstrated a 100% success rate (40/40) in establishing the BALB/c mouse model of early colon cancer. The technical success rate of limited colectomy was also 100%, with no instances of early mortality or morbidity. After the limited colectomy procedure, none of the animals exhibited any primary tumors, bloody ascites, recurrence or local invasion, intestinal obstruction, lymph node metastasis, peritoneal seeding, or anastomotic leakage. Furthermore, there were no reported deaths during the postresection observation period in animals treated with limited colectomy.ConclusionIn summary, our research group has successfully described a novel and reproducible surgical resection model for colon cancer in mice, which reflects the clinical scenario.Keywords: BALB/c mouse, early colon cancer, microscopically assisted limited colectomy, orthotopic model, surgery model

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a highly prevalent malignancy worldwide, with an estimated incidence of 1.4 million new cases annually. 1 Surgical resection continues to be the preferred and primary treatment modality for CRC stages I–III. However, it is disheartening to note that even with optimal treatment, approximately 50% of CRC patients are prone to developing recurrent disease, emphasizing the existence of several practical challenges that need to be addressed within the current standard treatment approach for CRC.

In recent years, a significant number of rodent models for CRC have been developed. These models have been instrumental in studying the progression, mechanisms of metastasis, and responses of novel drugs in CRC. 2 , 3 A large number of animal models are available, including chemically induced models, spontaneous mouse models, and genetically modified models. Such preclinical research has been extensively useful for investigating CRC characteristics, providing evidence relevant to clinical applications, and improving the survival quality of patients with CRC liver metastasis. However, most rodent models focused only on the evaluation of the metastatic mechanism and the establishment of novel drugs. 4 A few surgical models of CRC were used to find the optimal surgery‐based combination therapy for nonadvanced CRC.

To address the aforementioned challenges, this study aimed to develop a preclinical model that more closely mimics the clinical scenario of early‐stage colon cancer with local growth and surgical intervention, thereby filling a gap in the existing research framework. 5 Traditional mouse models for CRC, such as subcutaneous xenograft models, are widely used for drug screening and metastasis mechanism studies. However, their growth environment and anatomical location significantly differ from those of primary tumors, making it difficult to fully replicate the complex biological interactions at the tumor–host interface and the local environment of surgical resection. 6 , 7 Therefore, developing a model that precisely simulates the early site of human colon cancer and allows for standardized surgical manipulation is crucial for optimizing surgery‐based combination treatment strategies.

In this study, we selected the murine colon cancer cell line CT‐26 to establish the model. The CT‐26 cell line exhibits stable tumorigenicity and transplantability in syngeneic BALB/c mice and has been widely used in research on CRC immunotherapy, tumor microenvironment, and drug efficacy evaluation. 8 , 9 , 10 Unlike the common subcutaneous tumor‐bearing model, we performed surgical exposure and direct visual injection of a CT‐26 cell suspension into the wall of the mouse ascending colon to simulate tumor implantation and early growth within the microenvironment. 11 This orthotopic injection method allows the tumor to develop within the authentic intestinal anatomy and extracellular matrix, potentially providing a more accurate representation of the interactions between the tumor and the intestinal mucosal immune system, gut microbiota, and local vasculature, all of which play key roles in tumor progression and therapeutic response. 6 , 12

Building upon this, we performed a microscopy‐assisted limited colectomy on the tumor‐bearing mice. This step aimed to preliminarily verify the technical feasibility and perioperative safety of simulating radical surgery for early human colon cancer in a small animal model. Performing intestinal segment resection and anastomosis using meticulous microsurgical techniques can minimize surgical trauma, allow the observation of postoperative recovery, and lay the technical foundation for subsequent studies on the potential impact of surgical stress on tumor recurrence or occult metastasis. 13 The establishment of this model system not only provides a new tool for studying the local biological behavior of early colon cancer but also, more importantly, creates a reproducible platform for future evaluation of the efficacy of neoadjuvant/adjuvant therapies (e.g., chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy) in combination with surgery. 5 , 11 For instance, this model could be used to investigate whether strategies combining immune checkpoint inhibitors 9 or modulating the tumor microbiota 12 before or after surgical resection can effectively eliminate minimal residual disease and establish long‐term immunological memory, thereby reducing the risk of recurrence. This has significant translational implications for improving the clinical prognosis of patients with nonadvanced CRC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical compliance

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Xinhua Hospital of Dalian University (Dalian, China). All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guideline.

Cell lines and culture

A mouse‐derived colon cancer cell, firefly luciferase‐expressing CT‐26 (CT‐26‐Fluc), was provided by the Central Laboratory of the Affiliated Xinhua Hospital of Dalian University and was grown as monolayers in RPMI‐1640 medium (Invitrogen, CA, USA) that was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (UT, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Microscopically assisted primary early colon cancer model procedure

The study design is shown in Figure 1A. A cohort comprising 40 female BALB/c mice was obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Dalian Medical University. After a 1‐week period of stabilization, a primary early colon cancer model was established based on the seminal study conducted by Céspedes et al. 14 These mice underwent a 12‐h fasting period with access to drinking water, after which they were anesthetized using a xylazine (10 mg/kg) and ketamine (70 mg/kg) mixture. The complete inhibition of the toe pinch withdrawal reflex served as evidence that the mice were adequately anesthetized. Then, their colon, cecum, and ileum were exteriorized by laparotomy under microscopy. A suspension of tumor cells (5 × 105) was prepared in 30 μL of Hank's balanced salt solution and subsequently injected into a sterile 31‐gauge needle. The cell suspension was slowly injected using a binocular lens (Heine, Herrsching, Germany) at approximately 10°, with the tip penetrating to a depth of 2.5 mm into the ascending colonic wall. Then, a gentle pressure was applied with a sterile swab at a distance of 5 mm from the injection site. After the withdrawal of the syringe, the periphery of the injection site was meticulously cleaned with 70% ethanol solution to prevent the leakage of cells into the abdominal cavity. The small diameter of the needle hole and consummated manipulation of administration were achieved using a microscopically assisted technique (Figure 1B). After injection, we put the gut back into the abdominal cavity and closed the abdominal incision step by step. The animals were treated for limited colectomy under microscopy 1 week later.

Microscopically assisted limited colectomy for primary early colon cancer model procedure

One week after modeling, all the models were randomly divided into two groups based on the different operation modes (n = 20 mice per group): the limited colectomy group (study group) and sham surgery group (control group). Anesthesia and laparotomy were performed using the aforementioned procedures. The control group underwent a sham surgery; then the gut was placed in the abdominal cavity and closed using surgical instruments immediately after laparotomy; the study group underwent a limited colectomy; the procedure was as follows. (i) After their colon, cecum, and ileum were exteriorized by a laparotomy, the ascending colon was identified and placed on saline moistened gauzes. (ii) The tumor was identified, and the colonic segment was marked 1.0 cm beyond the upper and lower margins of the tumor by placing two titanium clips in the corresponding location; then the malignant bowel was ligated using nonabsorbable braided silk 4/0. (iii) Malignant colon was transected using tissue scissors along the gaps between the titanium clip and stitch. (iv) After the resection of the colonic segment, colonic end‐to‐end anastomosis was performed using 10–0 vascular sutures. (v) The anastomosis was covered with Surgicel, and the abdominal incision was closed (shown in Figure 1C). After the surgery, the general conditions of the model mice, namely active state, weight, fur color, and mortality, were recorded once a day. To minimize animal distress, mice were killed in accordance with established criteria humanely; 20 mice from the control and study group (10 in each group) were killed to obtain the tumors 3 weeks after surgery. Then the short‐term outcomes, including tumor volume, tumor weight, bloody ascites, recurrence or local invasion, intestinal obstruction, lymph node metastasis, hepatic metastasis, peritoneal seeding, and anastomotic leakage, were observed. In addition, another 20 mice were followed up for an average of ~8 weeks after surgery.

Bioluminescence images

The IVIS200 (in vivo imaging system, Xenogen, Alameda, CA, USA) was used for data acquisition and analysis. Tumor area was measured using the region‐of‐interest contour tool, as shown in the Figure 2A.

(A) In vivo imaging system (IVIS) results of CT‐26 cell intracolonic injection; (B) necropsy images are shown in the limited colectomy groups. No ascites, seeding, mesenteric node, distant lymph node (LN) metastasis, and obstruction; (C) necropsy images are shown in the sham surgery groups. Bloody ascites, local invasion, and serious obstruction were observed; (D) microscopic appearance of tumor growth in limited colectomy group; (E) microscopic appearance of tumor growth in sham surgery group; (F) Kaplan–Meier plot of overall survival of models in both groups (n = 10 for each group).

Histopathological examination

Malignant tissues that were removed during the operation were examined for signs of malignancy and pathological stage using hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining. Three weeks after surgery, mice from each group in the primary therapy model were killed. Tissue samples from the tumor bed, peritumoral lymphovascular structures, and liver were fixed using a 4% formaldehyde solution in phosphate buffered saline. Subsequently, the specimens were subjected to ethanol dehydration, embedded in paraffin blocks, sectioned (4 μm thick), and stained with H&E and KITENIN.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS, version 20.0, statistical software. Differences in bioluminescence signals were analyzed using Mann–Whitney test. Differences in the incidence of mucosal invasion, peritumoral lymphovascular invasion, and liver metastasis were analyzed using two‐way Fisher's exact and χ 2 tests. The difference in tumor volume was analyzed using Student's two‐tailed t‐test. The survival analysis of both groups was performed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log‐rank statistics. In all cases, p < 0.05 was considered to be significant.

RESULTS

The establishment of early colon cancer model

To objectively simulate the growth process of colon tumor, we deposited CT‐26‐Fluc close to the colonic mucosa, where CRC is initiated, using a sterile 31‐gauge needle and a binocular lens to increase the fidelity of the injection. Six days after injection, IVIS imaging indicated obvious blue spots on the area besides the primary site in both groups of mice. Limited colectomy group and sham surgery group, respectively, yielded primary tumors with an average bioluminescence of 7.22 × 106 ± 0.836 × 106 P/s/cm2/sr and 7.33 × 106 ± 0.794 × 106 P/s/cm2/sr (shown in Figure 2A). All the mice in the limited colectomy group (20/20) and sham surgery group (20/20) had tumor uptake detected using bioluminescence imaging before surgery (shown in Figure 2A). H&E staining demonstrated that the cancer cells penetrated the mucosal layer and invaded the submucosal or muscle layer, but no primary tumors were observed invading serosa (shown in Figure 1D).

The short‐term effect of limited colectomy for early colon cancer

One week after modeling, the control group underwent a sham surgery, and the study group had a limited colectomy. The surgical success rates were 100% (40/40). Measurements taken 21 days after surgery showed that the limited colectomy group yielded primary tumors with the lowest average bioluminescence (0.24 × 106 ± 0.107 × 106 P/s/cm2/sr) compared with the sham surgery group (107.1 × 106 ± 8.837 × 106 photons; p < 0.001) or the outcome 1 day before operation from the limited colectomy group (7.22 × 106 ± 0.836 × 106 P/s/cm2/sr; p < 0.001) and the sham surgery group (7.33 × 106 ± 0.794 × 106 P/s/cm2/sr; p < 0.001) (shown in Figure 2A). Then we collected the malignant tissues to investigate micromorphological and histopathological differences between the tumors of both groups. It was found that no primary tumors, bloody ascites, recurrence or local invasion, intestinal obstruction, lymph node metastasis, hepatic metastasis, and peritoneal seeding were observed in the limited colectomy group, which contrasts with the results of the sham surgery group (shown in Figure 2B–E; Table S1).

The long‐term outcome of limited colectomy for early colon cancer

To evaluate the long‐term effect of limited colectomy for early colon cancer, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed using overall 8‐week survival data. During the follow‐up period, 7 of 10 mice in the sham surgery group (70%) died, but none of the animals in the limited colectomy group exhibited any signs of morbidity or died (0%). All dead mice in the long‐term sham group died of obstruction (shown in Figure S1A–C). In addition, there were no local recurrences in the long‐term colectomy group (shown in Figure S1D–F). The overall survival rate of mice treated by limited colectomy, as determined by the log‐rank test, was significantly higher than that in the sham surgery group (p = 0.001) (shown in Figure 2F). Overall, these data further supported a curative effect of limited colectomy for early colon cancer.

DISCUSSION

Since developing the chemically induced mouse model of CRC over 80 years ago, a large number of rodent models have been established to study CRC because of their valuable features involving similar anatomy and biology to humans, compact size, strong breeding capacity, easy operation, and short gestation time. 15 It was reported that spontaneous orthotopic mouse models have similar vascularity, histology, gene expression, and metastatic pattern as human cancer. 16 Thus, such models have been frequently applied, especially for fundamental research to depict the metastatic process of CRC. In addition, this orthotopic approach was used to determine the role of certain signal pathways by directly analyzing the metastatic properties of a given tumor cell with its genetically modified counterpart. 17 These methods have significantly improved our understanding of metastasis. Unfortunately, the application of the current rodent models was confined to the detection of novel anticancer drugs 4 or the revelation of the novel targets 18 for advanced CRC. No reliable mouse model has been developed to test the surgical resection and surgery‐based combinational therapies for CRC, although surgical resection is the major curative treatment.

Previously, some simple surgical models have been developed for certain scientific purposes. For example, in 1986, to examine whether excision of the primary tumor accelerates tumor metastasis in the nude mouse model, Giavazzi et al. 19 injected tumor cell lines into the hind thigh of mice; 6 months after the initial implantation of tumor cells, they found resection of the CRC growing in the hind thigh did not increase the production of metastases. In 1989, a study to investigate the biology and therapy of local–regional recurrent CRC was performed by Schackert and Fidler. 20 By resecting mice cecum at various time points after injecting tumor cell into lymphoid follicle of the cecum, they showed that most of the models presented with recurrent tumor at the site of resection and in regional mesenteric lymph nodes. In 1995, to test the hypothesis that colorectal tumors would be less easily established and grow less aggressively after laparoscopy than after laparotomy, Allendorf et al. 21 carried out a double‐blind, randomized, controlled trial with 140 C3H/He female mice divided into three groups: laparotomy (underwent only laparotomy), insufflation (underwent only peritoneal insufflation), and anesthesia control groups (underwent no procedure). They indicated that tumors were less easily established and grew less aggressively after insufflation than after laparotomy, which can be explained by the fact of an immune advantage to laparoscopy over laparotomy. Although these aforementioned ectopic or orthotopic surgical models have led to an in‐depth understanding of tumor growth and recurrence, none of them can absolutely reflect real‐clinic situations.

This study is unique because we first established an orthotopic xenograft mouse model for curative surgical resection of colon cancer. One week after tumor cells were injected into the wall of the ascending colon, a partial colectomy with an anastomosis was performed in mouse model of early colon cancer under microscopy. Surgery difficulties in mice were overcome using several measures as follows. (i) The location of the transplanted tumors chosen: cecum of rodent animal is the most used transplantation site because of the thick intestinal wall. In addition, it has been found that the cecum of mice is not fixed by mesenteries and is thus freely movable in the abdominal cavity. 22 Therefore, the tumors growing in the cecum can be surgically removed with ease, whereas surgical resection of colon tumors at any other site along the murine bowel wall is technically difficult. However, the uneven size of the cecum makes anastomosis very tough. Thus, the ascending colon near the cecum was chosen as the injection site due to its similar thickness and movability as the cecum, besides its uniform size. (ii) Determination of the site of incisal margin: ischemia is considered one of the most important factors for the healing of gastrointestinal anastomosis. Therefore, protecting the blood supply of anastomosis is key to achieving good surgical outcome. Thus, avascular regions about 1 cm away from tumor margins were identified as the surgical site to avoid vessel injury. (iii) Micro‐anastomosis training: colon diameter in mice is significantly smaller than that in humans, which in turn greatly increases the difficulty in anastomosis. Therefore, we took two steps to overcome this obstacle and successfully perform an end‐to‐end anastomosis. To begin with, all surgical procedures were performed under microscopy, which can magnify the intestinal canal 40 times in diameter and make the operation field clear. Moreover, to become proficient with the anastomotic skills under microscopy, we established a scientific and systematic training method of anastomosis. First, micro‐anastomosis training using medical sterile gloves was conducted (detailed description is shown in Figure S2). Second, suturing stomach incision of mice was practiced because the gastric wall is thicker and easier to anastomose than colon. Third, microscopically assisted gastrojejunostomy was performed (Figure S3). When the aforementioned procedures were completed to perfection, end‐to‐end anastomosis of colon was performed. Therefore, we can summarize the following practice guidelines of anastomosis: from glove to mice, from big to small, from thick to thin. Due to substantial systematic practice, the surgical success rates in this study were 100% (40/40), and no anastomotic leakage was observed in any surgical model.

On the contrary, colectomy combined with regional lymphadenectomy is the standard treatment for local advanced CRC. However, the extent of lymphadenectomy has not reached consensus. Therefore, another challenge involved in the colectomy model is to reduce the difficulty in operation and better simulate CRC surgery. To overcome this problem, we first developed an orthotopic mouse model of early colon cancer by injecting CT‐26 cells into the ascending colon wall, which is near the cecum. Previously, Céspedes et al., 14 Lee et al., 17 and Hite et al. 23 once reported on intracecal injection of tumor cells using different human colon cancer cell lines (HCT‐116, SW‐620, DLD‐1, and HT29). All of them confirmed a successful tumor intake rate of 75%–100% similar to that (100%) of our experiment. Although Céspedes et al. reported a success rate of 42% (2/7) for model stage I CRC using DLD‐1 cells, its time‐consuming nature and low success rate impede the spread and application of the early CRC model. In this study, the success rate of stage I colon cancer was found to be 100% after 1 week of initial cell injection by pathological examination, which was significantly better than that reported by Céspedes et al. Thus, our early colon cancer model may provide a better platform for researchers to conduct further study for the treatment of CRC.

Then, the postoperative short‐ and long‐term effects were evaluated. Our results indicated that the limited colectomy for early CRC significantly improves the survival quality of the experimental mouse, which closely stimulates human disease when a CRC patient undergoes surgical treatment at an early phase. In addition, the mice in the limited colectomy group exhibited better health status without bloody ascites, intestinal obstruction, and lymph node metastasis compared with the sham surgery group 3 weeks after surgery. Currently, in vivo optical imaging techniques, including fluorescence imaging and bioluminescence imaging, have been developed to detect the growth of tumors. In the current study, mice with colon cancer were carefully monitored for any progression, metastasis, or reoccurrence. Preoperative and postoperative bioluminescence signals demonstrated a radical resection and recurrence and metastasis rate of 0% in the limited colectomy group. All these findings indicated a safe and reproducible surgical model for colon cancer to be successfully established. Interestingly, Ni et al. 24 and Pang et al. 25 recently reported a robust and reproducible technique for a surgical model of pancreatic cancer in mice and indicated this model to be useful for the testing of both preoperative and postoperative adjuvant treatments. Similarly, the limited colectomy animal model we presented in this study could also be a useful tool to detect the therapies and the surgery‐based combined therapy. 5 , 11 For instance, this model could be used to explore whether strategies combining immune checkpoint inhibitors 9 or modulating the tumor microbiota 11 , 12 before or after surgical resection can effectively eliminate minimal residual disease and establish long‐term immunological memory, thereby reducing the risk of recurrence.

Admittedly, this study also had limitations. For example, the follow‐up time of 8 weeks may be relatively short, although there are obvious reasons for this. Two points would contribute to the choice of follow‐up time. First, during the follow‐up period of 8 weeks, 7 of the 10 mice in the sham surgery group (70%) died, and the rest also died of obstruction in the next few days. Therefore, if these animals in the colectomy group were to be followed up longer, there would be no matched group. Second, in our preliminary experiments, we have followed these mice treated with limited colectomy for over 12 weeks, yet none of them have local or distant recurrence demonstrated by necroscopy and interval intravital imaging. Therefore, the 8‐week time limit was chosen. But to obtain more scientific and rigorous results, we will set a sham implantation group to extend the follow‐up time in our future research. In addition, the lack of comparison among different types of colon cancer cell lines, especially human colon cancer lines, may exert some negative impact on the results. Therefore, flushing these types of questions out with multiple types of colon cancer cell lines is possibly the important parts of characterizing a model and will make the work more generally scientific. 7 , 26 Future research could integrate patient‐derived organoids 26 or more sophisticated in vitro systems 27 to further enhance the predictive value and clinical translational potential of the models.

CONCLUSION

Briefly, this study demonstrated our practice mode of anastomosis to be very effective and feasible, which makes the limited colectomy mouse model technically reproducible. Based on this, surgical mouse models of CRC stages II–IV will be established by altering resection time points to detect the efficacy of surgery‐based combined therapy in CRC treatment in the future.
