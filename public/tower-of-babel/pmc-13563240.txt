Endodontic potential of eugenol against Enterococcus faecalis: Antibacterial, antibiofilm, and network pharmacology insights
Authors: Kannika Parameshwari Kannan, A.S Smiline Girija
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe present study aimed to evaluate the antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy of eugenol against clinical isolates of Enterococcus faecalis obtained from persistent root canal infections and to investigate its potential interactions with virulence-associated target proteins using network pharmacology and molecular docking approaches.MethodsClinical isolates of E. faecalis were recovered from infected root canal samples and identified using standard microbiological methods. Antibacterial activity of 2% and 4% eugenol was assessed using the agar disc diffusion assay, with further determination of MIC and MBC values using microbroth dilution method. Antibiofilm activity was evaluated using an ex vivo dentinal root canal biofilm model followed by CFU analysis. Potential molecular targets of eugenol were identified further using standard computational tools.Results4% eugenol exhibited the largest zone of inhibition (23.4 ± 1.4 mm) with the MIC and MBC values at 0.04 mg/mL and 0.4 mg/mL, respectively. In the ex vivo biofilm model, 4% eugenol significantly reduced bacterial colony counts and demonstrated antibiofilm activity comparable to 2% CHX. Network pharmacology analysis identified five potential target proteins, namely pyrG, thyA, accD, tgt, and mtnN and the molecular docking demonstrated favorable binding interactions between eugenol and the selected targets.ConclusionEugenol demonstrated significant antibacterial and antibiofilm activity against E. faecalis and the computational analyses suggesting the potential interactions with virulence-associated proteins involved in important metabolic pathways. These findings indicate the potential of eugenol as a plant-derived antimicrobial candidate for endodontic applications; however, further in vivo and toxicity studies are required before clinical application.

Introduction

Persistent endodontic infections (PEIs) are common oral ailments associated with gum fistula, alveolar abscess, chronic periapical periodontitis, root resorption, and potential tooth loss.1,2 These infections are often polymicrobial and predominantly involve Gram-positive facultative anaerobic species, with nearly 70% consisting of obligate anaerobes or microaerophiles.3 Key pathogens contributing to periapical periodontitis include Enterococcus faecalis, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Propionibacterium spp., and Parvimonas micra.4 Among these, E. faecalis is frequently isolated from failed root canal treatments and is recognized for its ability to survive under harsh environmental conditions, penetrate dentinal tubules, and form persistent biofilms.5, 6, 7 These characteristics significantly contribute to its resistance against antimicrobial agents and make eradication from infected root canals particularly challenging.8,9

Endodontic treatment primarily focuses on reducing or eliminating microbial infections through chemo-mechanical preparation and the use of disinfecting irrigation solutions. NaOCl and CHX are among the most commonly used irrigants in endodontic therapy.10 However, reinfection and persistent inflammation may still occur following treatment due to the survival of residual microorganisms within the root canal system.11

Plant-derived bioactive compounds have attracted considerable attention because of their broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties and lower tendency to induce microbial resistance.12 Essential oils (EOs) obtained from medicinal plants contain several biologically active constituents, including thymol, cinnamaldehyde, citral, carvacrol, and eugenol.13 Among these compounds, eugenol, the principal active component of clove oil, has demonstrated diverse pharmacological activities, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, anesthetic, and anticancer properties.14,15 Previous studies have also reported the antibacterial potential of eugenol against several oral and non-oral pathogens.16,17 However, its effectiveness against persistent biofilm-forming E. faecalis isolates from root canal infections remains inadequately explored.

Recent advances in computational biology have significantly contributed to antimicrobial drug discovery and target identification. Molecular docking enables the prediction of ligand–protein interactions at the atomic level and facilitates the identification of potential therapeutic targets involved in bacterial virulence and survival pathways.18 E. faecalis exhibits unique metabolic adaptations, stress response mechanisms, and virulence-associated pathways that contribute to its persistence within the root canal environment.19 These features make several of its metabolic proteins attractive but underexplored therapeutic targets.

Despite the widespread use of sodium hypochlorite and CHX as endodontic irrigants, their prolonged application is associated with several limitations, including cytotoxicity, tissue irritation, allergic reactions, unpleasant taste, and incomplete elimination of biofilm-associated pathogens such as E. faecalis.20 Therefore, there is an increasing need to identify safer and more effective plant-derived antimicrobial agents capable of targeting both planktonic cells and biofilm-associated infections. 21Furthermore, limited studies have integrated ex vivo antibiofilm models with computational network pharmacology approaches to identify novel therapeutic targets in E. faecalis. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy of eugenol against clinical isolates of E. faecalis obtained from infected root canals and to investigate its interactions with virulence-associated target proteins using network pharmacology and molecular docking approaches.

Materials and methods

Isolation and identification of E. faecalis from infected root canals

This study was conducted between January 2023 and May 2023 at a tertiary care dental hospital. A total of 50 patients presenting with previously root canal-treated single-rooted teeth exhibiting radiographic evidence of persistent apical periodontitis were included in the study. The study was designed as an exploratory in vitro investigation; the number of patients was based on the availability of eligible participants during the study period and predefined inclusion criteria. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee prior to commencement of the study, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients who had received systemic antibiotic therapy within the preceding three months or presented with periodontal disease were excluded to minimize potential confounding factors affecting the root canal microbiota.

Root canal sampling was performed under strict aseptic conditions. Following isolation of the operative field and disinfection of the tooth surface, sterile paper points were inserted into the root canal to the working length and retained for 60 s to absorb intracanal contents. For previously root canal-treated teeth, gutta-percha was removed during retreatment without the use of any gutta-percha solvents. The paper points were immediately transferred into sterile brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (Himedia, Mumbai, India) and incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24 h. Following incubation, bacterial cultures were streaked onto bile esculin agar for selective isolation. Presumptive E. faecalis isolates were identified based on colony morphology, Gram staining characteristics, catalase negativity, and bile esculin hydrolysis. Further, confirmed E. faecalis isolates were obtained and preserved in 20% glycerol stocks at −20°C for subsequent antimicrobial and antibiofilm analyses. Of the 50 patients enrolled, 10 yielded confirmed E. faecalis isolates, with one isolate recovered from each positive patient. All isolates were preserved in 20% glycerol stocks at −20°C. All 10 isolates were included for antimicrobial susceptibility (antibiogram) profiling. Based on their consistent phenotypic characteristics, three representative isolates were selected for subsequent agar disc diffusion, MIC, MBC, and ex vivo biofilm analyses.

Antibiogram profile

Antimicrobial susceptibility profiling of the confirmed E. faecalis isolates was performed using the Kirby–Bauer agar disc diffusion method in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines.22 Briefly, overnight bacterial cultures were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity standards and uniformly inoculated onto Mueller Hinton agar (MHA) plates (Himedia, Mumbai, India) using sterile cotton swabs. Commercially available antibiotic discs, including ampicillin (10 μg), gentamicin (10 μg), erythromycin (15 μg), levofloxacin (5 μg), linezolid (30 μg), vancomycin (30 μg), and rifampicin (5 μg) (Himedia, Mumbai, India), were aseptically placed onto the inoculated agar surface. The plates were incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24 h, following which the diameter of inhibition zones was measured in millimeters. The susceptibility profiles of the isolates were interpreted as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant according to CLSI breakpoint criteria.

Antibacterial activity of eugenol assessed by agar disc diffusion assay

The antibacterial activity of purified eugenol (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) against E. faecalis isolates was evaluated using the Kirby–Bauer agar disc diffusion method. Three representative E. faecalis isolates, selected based on their consistent phenotypic characteristics, were used for this assay. Eugenol was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare 2% and 4% solutions, and no additional emulsifier was used. Overnight bacterial cultures were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity standards and uniformly inoculated onto MHA plates using sterile cotton swabs. Sterile paper discs (6 mm diameter) were loaded with a defined volume of the prepared eugenol solutions (2% and 4%) and aseptically placed onto the inoculated agar surface. 2% CHX was used as the positive control, while DMSO served as the vehicle (negative) control. The plates were incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24 h. Following incubation, the diameter of the inhibition zones surrounding each disc was measured in millimeters using a digital Vernier caliper. All experiments were carried out in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The broth microdilution method was employed to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of eugenol against E. faecalis. The MIC and MBC assays were performed using the same three representative E. faecalis isolates selected for antimicrobial evaluation. Briefly, bacterial suspensions were prepared in Mueller Hinton broth (MHB) (Himedia, Mumbai, India) and adjusted to approximately 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. Serial two-fold dilutions of eugenol were prepared in sterile 96-well microtiter plates. A total volume of 100 μL containing bacterial suspension and respective concentrations of eugenol was added into each well and incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24 h. Following incubation, bacterial growth was assessed spectrophotometrically by measuring optical density at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Readwell TOUCH™, ROBONIK, India). Percentage growth inhibition was calculated using the following equation: [Growth (%) = [(Sample OD)/(Control OD)] × 100. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of eugenol exhibiting ≥50% inhibition of bacterial growth compared with the untreated control. The MBC was determined as the lowest concentration showing ≥99.9% bacterial killing, confirmed by the absence of visible bacterial growth on MHA plates following subculture. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Preparation of dentinal root specimens

A total of 40 freshly extracted human single-rooted teeth free from caries, fractures, resorption, and previous endodontic treatment were selected. The number of specimens was selected based on previous comparable ex vivo endodontic biofilm studies and to ensure equal allocation among the experimental groups.23,24 Soft tissue debris was removed, and the teeth were stored in sterile saline until use. Crowns were decoronated using a diamond disc under water cooling to obtain standardized 12-mm root segments. Root canals were instrumented up to ISO size 20 K-file at an 11-mm working length. Irrigation was performed using 6 mL of 5.25% sodium hypochlorite followed by 2 mL of 17% EDTA to remove the smear layer. Residual NaOCl was neutralized with 10% sodium thiosulfate, and specimens were rinsed with sterile distilled water. The prepared root specimens were sterilized by autoclaving at 121°C for 15 min prior to biofilm inoculation.

Ex vivo biofilm preparation

The three-representative E. faecalis isolates selected for antimicrobial evaluation were used to establish the ex vivo biofilm model. Overnight cultures of E. faecalis were prepared in BHI broth at 37°C and adjusted to approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL using McFarland standards. Sterile root specimens were individually transferred into sterile eppendorf tubes and inoculated with 100 μL of bacterial suspension into the canal lumen. The specimens were incubated aerobically at 37°C for 28 days to facilitate mature biofilm formation, with fresh, sterile BHI broth replenished every 48 h under aseptic conditions. Following incubation, external root surfaces were cleaned with sterile gauze, and apical foramina were sealed using epoxy resin. Pre-irrigation microbial sampling was performed using sterile paper points placed at the working length for 30 s and subsequently transferred into sterile tubes containing 4 mL of BHI broth for microbiological analysis.

Irrigation protocol

Following the 28-day biofilm incubation period, the root specimens were randomly allocated into four experimental groups (n = 10/group). An equal group size (n = 10) was used to ensure standardized experimental conditions and adequate biological replication for statistical comparison. Group I – saline control; Group II – 2% chlorhexidine (CHX); Group III – 2% eugenol; and Group IV – 4% eugenol. Sterile saline was used in Group I as the negative control because it does not possess significant antimicrobial activity, thereby providing a baseline for evaluating the bacterial reduction attributable to the test irrigants. Each root canal was irrigated with 5 mL of the respective irrigant solution using a sterile 27-gauge side-vented irrigation needle under aseptic conditions.

Root canal sampling and colony count analysis

Following irrigation, sterile F2 paper points were inserted into the root canals for 30 s to collect intracanal contents. The paper points were then transferred into sterile eppendorf tubes containing 1 mL sterile saline and vortexed for 1 min to disperse the bacterial cells. Serial tenfold dilutions were prepared up to the 10−3 dilution, and 100 μL aliquots were plated onto MHA plates. The plates were incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24–48 h, and colony-forming units (CFU/mL) were enumerated to assess the antibiofilm efficacy of the tested irrigants.

PPI network analysis and target identification

The three-dimensional structure of eugenol in SDF format was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and uploaded to the PharmMapper server (https://bio.tools/pharmmapper) for reverse pharmacophore-based target prediction. Predicted targets with normalized fit scores above the default threshold were selected for further analysis. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis was subsequently performed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) (version 11.0), with the organism restricted to E. faecalis and a high-confidence interaction score cut-off of 0.70. The generated interaction network was imported into Cytoscape (https://cytoscape.org/) version 3.9.1 for visualization and topological analysis. Network parameters, including node degree and interaction characteristics, were analyzed using the Network Analyzer plugin. Gene Ontology (GO) biological process enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed to identify biologically relevant pathways associated with the predicted targets. Based on pathway enrichment significance, node connectivity, and biological relevance, five potential virulence-associated targets, namely pyrG, thyA, accD, tgt, and mtnN, were selected for subsequent molecular docking analysis.

Protein preparation and validation

The crystal structure of thymidylate synthase (thyA) from E. faecalis was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) (PDB ID: 6QYA). Protein structures for pyrG, accD, tgt, and mtnN were predicted using the SWISS-MODEL server (https://bio.tools/swiss_model). Structural validation was performed using the SAVES-PROCHECK server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/) through Ramachandran plot analysis, and protein models demonstrating acceptable stereochemical quality with the majority of residues located in favored regions were considered suitable for docking studies. Protein preparation was carried out using AutoDock Tools 4.2 (https://autodock.scripps.edu/) by removing water molecules, adding Kollman charges and polar hydrogen atoms, and assigning solvation parameters prior to molecular docking analysis.

Ligand preparation

The two-dimensional structure of eugenol was prepared using ACD/ChemSketch (https://www.acdlabs.com/resources/free-chemistry-software-apps/chemsketch-freeware/) and converted into a three-dimensional optimized structure. Energy minimization was performed prior to docking analysis to obtain a stable ligand conformation. The optimized ligand structure was converted from MOL to PDB format using Open Babel (https://openbabel.org/index.html) for subsequent molecular docking studies.

Molecular docking visualization

Molecular docking was performed as an independent in silico analysis using publicly available protein structures and was not based on the clinical E. faecalis isolates. Molecular docking studies between eugenol and the selected target proteins were performed using AutoDock 4.2 (https://autodock.scripps.edu/). Grid boxes were generated around the active binding regions of each protein using AutoGrid. Docking simulations were carried out using the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm with default parameters. Binding affinity was assessed based on docking energy values (kcal/mol), hydrogen bond interactions, and ligand orientation within the active site. Lower binding energy values indicated stronger protein–ligand interactions. The best docking conformations were selected based on minimum binding energy and interaction stability. Protein–ligand interaction profiles were visualized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer (https://www.3ds.com/products/biovia/discovery-studio/visualization).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons among experimental groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were performed using independent biological replicates, and assays were conducted in triplicate wherever applicable.

Determination of MIC and MBC

The MIC and MBC of eugenol against E. faecalis were determined using the broth microdilution method. Eugenol exhibited an MIC value of 0.04 mg/mL and an MBC value of 0.4 mg/mL against the tested isolates (Fig. 2B and C). All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Antibiofilm activity by post-treatment colony count

The antibiofilm efficacy of eugenol against E. faecalis biofilm was evaluated using CFU analysis following irrigation treatment. Compared with the saline control group, both 2% and 4% eugenol demonstrated significant reductions in bacterial colony counts (p < 0.05). Among the tested concentrations, 4% eugenol exhibited greater antibiofilm activity and demonstrated efficacy comparable to 2% CHX (Fig. 2D). A total of 300 potential targets for eugenol were predicted using the PharmMapper database. PPI analysis was subsequently performed using the STRING database with a high-confidence interaction score threshold of 0.70. KEGG pathway and biological process enrichment analysis

GO biological process analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed to evaluate the biological relevance of the predicted eugenol targets in E. faecalis. KEGG pathway analysis of 45 target genes identified two major enriched pathways, namely metabolic pathways and pyrimidine metabolism. In addition, 18 significant biological processes were identified and summarized in the corresponding table. Integration of pathway enrichment data with protein–protein interaction network analysis led to the identification of five potential target genes, namely pyrG, thyA, accD, tgt, and mtnN, which were subsequently selected for molecular docking analysis (Fig. 3B).

Molecular docking analysis

The selected target proteins pyrG, thyA, accD, tgt, and mtnN were subjected to molecular docking analysis against eugenol using AutoDock 4.2. Discussion

Microorganisms present within the root canal system are the primary cause of post-treatment infections.25 To improve the success rate of root canal treatments, mechanical instrumentation and chemical debridement are employed to reduce the number of microorganisms remaining in the canal space post-treatment. However, the complexity of the root canal system and the resilience of intracanal bacteria to irrigants and medicaments make it challenging to eliminate all microorganisms.26 E. faecalis was selected for this study due to its widespread occurrence in secondary endodontic infections. Herbal medicaments are becoming increasingly significant due to their therapeutic attributes. This study assessed the antibacterial effectiveness of eugenol irrigants as compared to 2% CHX.

In the present study, eugenol irrigants demonstrated inhibitory activity against both planktonic and biofilm-associated E. faecalis. These findings suggest that eugenol may possess inhibitory activity against planktonic and biofilm-associated E. faecalis under the tested experimental conditions. To evaluate the antibiofilm activity, an ex vivo dentinal root canal biofilm model was developed using E. faecalis. This approach allowed us to more closely replicate conditions resembling an infected root canal environment and evaluate the impact of eugenol on E. faecalis in biofilm form attached to the root canal. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis suggested potential interactions between eugenol and selected target proteins of E. faecalis.

Previous studies have explored the activity of eugenol in various areas, including its antitumor effects,27 antioxidant properties,28 antidiabetic potential,29 antimicrobial activity,30 and anti-inflammatory effects.31 Devi et al.32 reported that a 5% v/v concentration of eugenol inhibited Salmonella typhi. In the present study, eugenol demonstrated considerable inhibitory activity against clinical isolates of E. faecalis. These findings suggest that eugenol exhibited inhibitory activity against E. faecalis at lower concentrations than those previously reported against S. typhi. Eugenol demonstrated significant antibacterial activity against E. faecalis, with MIC and MBC values of 0.04 mg/mL and 0.4 mg/mL, respectively. In comparison, a recent study by Bai et al.33 reported even lower antibacterial activity of eugenol against the gram-positive organism Staphylococcus aureus, with MIC and MBC values of 0.26 mg/mL and 0.52 mg/mL. These findings suggest that eugenol's efficacy can vary substantially between different bacterial species, this report indicating that eugenol is effective against E. faecalis at lower concentrations. This variation emphasizes the necessity of evaluating antimicrobial agents within the specific context of the target pathogen. The structural composition of Gram-positive bacterial cell walls may influence susceptibility to certain antimicrobial compounds, as previously highlighted by Wang et al.34

In discussing the enhancement of root canal treatment efficacy, employing a biocompatible intracanal irrigant capable of reducing or eradicating bacteria from the root canal becomes imperative, as emphasized by Kuruvilla and Kamath.35 Our study focused on utilizing eugenol at varying concentrations (2% and 4%) as an irrigant solution, while 2% CHX served as a positive control for the irrigation procedure. We specifically chose a 28-day period for biofilm growth to achieve an optimal bacterial biofilm density for subsequent bacterial counts. Notably, a previous study reported the efficacy of root canal irrigation using clove essential oil, which demonstrated 100% inhibition against E. faecalis in 24-h biofilm formations.36 Although 4% eugenol demonstrated substantial antibiofilm activity, its efficacy remained slightly lower than that of 2% CHX, indicating that further formulation optimization may be necessary before clinical translation. In the present study, 2% CHX was selected as the positive control because of its well-established antimicrobial efficacy against E. faecalis and its widespread use as an alternative endodontic irrigant. Although NaOCl remains the gold standard irrigant owing to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and tissue-dissolving properties, it was not included in the present investigation. Therefore, future studies comparing eugenol directly with NaOCl are warranted to better establish its clinical applicability.

The active target genes predominantly present in E. faecalis are involved in various metabolic pathways, including fatty acid metabolism, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and cellulose-associated pathways. Ramsey et al.37 reported the involvement of regulatory components associated with sugar metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, glycerol metabolism, α-keto acid metabolism, and pyruvate synthesis, highlighting the diverse biological functions of these pathways. These findings suggest that eugenol may influence the expression of genes associated with virulence and microbial adaptation. Such effects may be attributed to the ability of eugenol to interfere with bacterial signaling pathways and virulence-associated mechanisms.

This study aimed to investigate the antimicrobial and antibiofilm effects of eugenol derived from clove oil on the predominant endodontic pathogen E. faecalis. To gain better insight into the interactions between different proteins and eugenol, molecular docking was performed using the AutoDock 4.2 tool. The docking experiments focused on the interactions between eugenol and several key protein structures: pyrG, thyA, accD, tgt, and mtnN. These proteins were chosen due to their relevance in the biochemical pathways of E. faecalis. Previous research demonstrated eugenol's antibacterial activity against ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae, with significant inhibition observed on the CTXM-9 protein.38 Our docking results demonstrated favorable binding interactions between eugenol and the selected target proteins.

Furthermore, our docking findings were consistent with the observed in vitro antibacterial and antibiofilm activities of eugenol against E. faecalis. In this study, we employed molecular docking to investigate the binding patterns of eugenol with active binding sites involved in major biological pathways. A total of 45 target genes of eugenol were identified against E. faecalis, based on a network comprising 44 nodes and 338 edges. Through this analysis, 20 metabolic pathways involving these interactive genes were identified. Notably, molecular docking suggested favorable interactions between eugenol and pyrG, indicating its possible involvement in pyrimidine metabolism-related pathways. pyrG functions as CTP synthase in pyrimidine metabolism, whereas thyA plays an important role in thymidylate biosynthesis required for DNA synthesis.39 Furthermore, eugenol demonstrated favorable binding interactions with accD, a gene playing a significant role in antibiotic resistance,40 and tgt, a tRNA-guanine transglycosylase crucial for the pathogenicity of microorganisms.41 Additionally, eugenol demonstrated favorable docking interactions with mtnN, which regulates methionine biosynthesis and transport genes essential for bacterial replication through diverse RNA- and DNA-level regulatory systems.42 These findings suggest that eugenol may interfere with metabolic pathways associated with bacterial survival, virulence, and biofilm formation.

A major strength of the present study is the integration of in vitro antibacterial and antibiofilm analyses with computational network pharmacology and molecular docking approaches. Additionally, the use of clinical E. faecalis isolates obtained from persistent root canal infections and the utilization of an ex vivo dentinal biofilm model enhanced the translational relevance of the experimental findings.

The present study has several limitations. First, it was conducted under in vitro and ex vivo conditions, which may not fully replicate the complex clinical environment of root canal infections. Second, only a limited number of E. faecalis clinical isolates were included, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, E. faecalis identification was based on conventional phenotypic methods without molecular confirmation by PCR or 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Furthermore, sodium hypochlorite, the gold standard endodontic irrigant, was not included as a comparator, limiting direct comparison of eugenol with current clinical practice. Although the ex vivo dentinal biofilm model simulated root canal conditions, the clinical efficacy and biocompatibility of eugenol as an endodontic irrigant require validation through in vivo and clinical studies. Furthermore, the molecular docking analysis provides only predictive evidence of protein–ligand interactions, and the cytotoxicity and long-term biocompatibility of eugenol were not evaluated. Therefore, further molecular, toxicological, pharmacological, and clinical studies are warranted to validate the therapeutic potential and safety of eugenol for endodontic applications.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that eugenol exhibited significant antibacterial and antibiofilm activity against E. faecalis under in vitro and ex vivo conditions. Network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses further suggested favorable interactions between eugenol and selected virulence-associated target proteins involved in important metabolic pathways. Collectively, these findings indicate that eugenol is a promising plant-derived antimicrobial compound against E. faecalis under the experimental conditions of this study. However, further investigations evaluating dentin interaction, tissue dissolution, smear layer removal, cytotoxicity, and in vivo efficacy are required before its clinical application as a root canal irrigant can be considered.
