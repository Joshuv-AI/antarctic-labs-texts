Effect of laser on the decontamination ability and plaque reattachment on the surface of implant restoration materials
Authors: Haisheng Ai, Xiangtao Ma, Jianing Song, Xiaoman Guo, Wei Li, Mingxuan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe plaque biofilm is a structurally diverse and compositionally complex aggregate, serving as the initiating factor of peri-implantitis and is closely related to the onset and progression of this disease (BMC Oral Health 24:105, 2024), (NPJ Biofilms Microbiomes 10:12, 2024). Previous studies have shown that compared to the microbial communities in healthy peri-implant tissues, the microbial communities beneath the mucosa in peri-implantitis are more complex and diverse, with a significant increase in periodontal pathogens. Due to the lack of cementum and periodontal ligament as a protective system, dental implants are more susceptible to bacterial infections than natural teeth. Therefore, the elimination of plaque biofilm is crucial in the treatment of peri-implantitis; the biofilm formed after implant debridement may affect the treatment outcome and the long-term stability of the implant. To explore these two issues, we conducted the following experiment. This experiment comparatively observed the microstructural changes on the surfaces of commonly used implant materials after traditional and laser treatments, evaluated their decontamination capabilities and antibacterial effects, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the use of lasers in the prevention and treatment of peri-implantitis.MethodThe experiment is divided into 5 groups: normal group (A), carbon fiber scraping group (B), sandblasting group (C), Er: YAG laser group (E), and Nd: YAG laser group (N).Use a K-type thermocouple temperature measuring instrument to monitor the temperature changes on the material surface during processing; use a roughness measuring instrument to measure the surface roughness (Ra) of the material after processing. Plaque biofilm is formed on the surface of the material. Normal form. The plate colony count showed that the remaining bacterial content on the surface of the two materials in groups B, C, E, and N was much smaller than that in group A (P<0.05). Introduction

Peri-implantitis is one of the main complications that affects the long-term stability of implants [1]. The disease can involve soft and hard tissues, leading to soft tissue inflammation around the implant and absorption of surrounding bone tissue. The main factor contributing to its occurrence and progression is the accumulation of plaque around the implant [2]. Patients who have poor plaque biofilm control and cannot maintain long-term maintenance treatment are at a higher risk of developing peri-implantitis. Patients who already have peri-implant mucositis may also progress to peri-implantitis due to plaque accumulation. Ultimately, this results in three-dimensional absorption of the surrounding alveolar ridge, reduction in bone mass, and loosening and loss of the implant. Therefore, the treatment and prevention of peri-implantitis are the focus of maintaining the health of peri-implant tissues, and it is also a difficult point in research.

At present, various treatment methods are used to treat peri-implantitis, and the increasingly mature clinical application of lasers provides a new direction for the treatment of this disease [3]. It can output light continuously and coherently as a fine, almost non-divergent beam with high energy, which has a good inactivation effect on pathogenic microorganisms [4]; in addition, laser treatment fibers can reach deep bone defect areas and implant thread areas that are difficult for mechanical treatment instruments to access, thereby further improving the sterilization ability. Er: YAG and Nd: YAG lasers not only have a good ability to clear pathogens associated with implants, but also, under appropriate output power and energy parameters, can effectively inhibit the expression of inflammatory factors, and have the ability to promote the proliferation of cells on the surface of implants and osteogenic differentiation [5]. However, existing studies still dispute whether Nd: YAG lasers may cause damage to the surface of implants, and whether the surface of implants after irradiation with Er: YAG and Nd: YAG lasers will affect the re-deposition of plaque biofilms remains to be further studied.

This experiment compares the changes in surface characteristics and temperature of two commonly used implant restoration materials, pure titanium and zirconia, after treatment with carbon fiber curettes, sandblasting, Er: YAG laser, and water mist Nd: YAG laser. It evaluates their ability to remove plaque and their impact on plaque reattachment, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the use of lasers in the prevention and treatment of peri-implantitis.

Materials and methods

Preparation of titanium and zirconia oxide sheet specimens

A square specimen with a side length of 5 mm and a thickness of 1 mm is polished with silicon carbide sandpaper step by step until there are no obvious scratches on the surface, ultrasonically washed with acetone, anhydrous ethanol, and triple distilled water for 15 min each, dried, and then sterilized with ethylene oxide gas.

Manufacture personalized dental appliances

Use methyl methacrylate and cobalt-chromium metal wire to make an upper maxillary removable denture consisting of four single-arm clasps and a U-shaped palatal base. On each side of the base, grind out a rectangular void measuring 18 mm x 6 mm x 3 mm into the tissue surface. Secure the specimen and denture using BEAUTIFIL Flow Plus (F00).

Constructing a biofilm model of plaque

Volunteers will wear dentures for 48 h, remove titanium and zirconia plates, randomly select samples, and use scanning electron microscopy to detect biofilm formation;

Volunteers can maintain a normal diet during the wearing process, but avoid consuming pigmented beverages for 48 h before the start of the experiment. Every morning and evening, they should use the provided non-antibacterial toothpaste to clean their teeth and other oral soft and hard tissues.When brushing teeth or using other oral cleaning tools, the dentures must be removed and temporarily stored in a moist, sealed environment. The areas other than the tissue surface of the dentures can be cleaned, and the dentures should be put back in the mouth after cleaning. The use of any type of mouthwash and other oral care products is prohibited during the wearing of the dentures.

Detect temperature

Use a K-type thermocouple instrument to record the temperature values in real-time during the processing of materials (measurement rate 2 times per second, resolution 0.01℃, accuracy ± 0.3%|t|+0.40℃), record the minimum temperature (Tmin) and peak temperature (Tmax) separately, calculate the temperature difference (Tmax-Tmin), and take the average of 3 repetitions.

Roughness measurement

Calibrate the roughness tester (TR-410 S) using a standard block with a specified roughness (Ra = 2.20 μm); use the roughness tester (sampling length (lr) = 0.80 mm, evaluation length (Ln) = 5, RAN = ± 80 μm, feed rate = 0.1 mm/s, indication accuracy value = 0.001 μm) to measure the initial and final surface roughness of each sample. Record the roughness Ra at three different points on each sample and take the average of the results.

Observe the remaining plaque on the material surface with scanning electron microscopy

Remove the samples from the dentures in the volunteers’ mouths, with 15 titanium and 15 zirconia discs randomly assigned to each group and treated separately, washed with PBS solution; fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at room temperature for 2 h; dehydrated with ethanol in stages; dried; mounted, coated with MCI000 ion sputtering device; placed under a scanning electron microscope to observe the remaining plaque biofilm conditions.

The total number of remaining colonies was determined by the colony count method on a flat surface

Samples were taken from the dentures in the mouths of volunteers, randomly assigned to groups, and treated in different ways. After washing away the floating plaque with PBS, the residual plaque from the surface of the 10 groups of samples was transferred to centrifuge tubes containing 1 ml of sterile PBS using a cell scraper. The samples were then thoroughly mixed using a vortex mixer and serially diluted to an appropriate concentration; 100µL of the final dilution was evenly spread onto pre-labeled BHI-A agar plates. The plates were placed into an anaerobic jar (37 °C, 80% N2 and 20% CO2) and incubated for 48 h, after which the number of colony-forming units (CFU) per milliliter was calculated.

Live-dead bacterial staining - observation with laser scanning confocal microscopy

Prepare the staining working solution by mixing the BB cell Probe® N01 and PI fluorescent dyes from the live/dead bacterial staining kit.

Randomly assign samples to groups (n = 3), and treat them with carbon fiber curettes, sandblasting, Er: YAG laser, and water mist Nd: YAG laser, respectively. Wash away the floating plaque on the surface of the samples; apply 300µL of the staining solution to the surface of the samples, incubate in the dark at room temperature for 15 min, discard the staining agent, and rinse. Place the samples on slides and observe under a laser scanning confocal microscope, capturing images at three different sites. Use Image J software to convert RGB format images to grayscale and calculate the mean fluorescence intensity (Mean fluorescence intensity) of the total bacterial adhesion, determining the ratio of live/dead bacteria in each group.

Determine the reattachment of plaque on the surface of materials

Treat titanium and zirconia discs separately with carbon fiber curettes, sandblasting, Er: YAG laser, and water mist Nd: YAG laser. Wash with PBS 10 times, dry, sterilize by ethylene oxide gas, and then bond to dentures. Volunteers wear them in the mouth for 24 h before separating some samples from the dentures.

After treating the samples with live/dead bacterial staining reagents, observe and capture images using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis of the data groups was performed using SPSS 27.0 statistical software, with single-sample t-test, paired-sample t-test, and one-way ANOVA for inter-group comparisons. Graphs were made using GraphPad Prism software, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

There is a wide variety of materials available for oral implant restorations. Pure titanium and its alloys have always been the preferred choice for oral implant materials due to their high hardness, excellent osseointegration capability, and biocompatibility [6]. Zirconia can avoid aesthetic issues such as gingival metal pigmentation and metal color exposure [7, 8]. There are very few reports on polyetheretherketone being used to manufacture finished implants or abutments [9]. Therefore, this study selected two common implant restoration materials (pure titanium and zirconia) for research. Plaque biofilm is a highly diverse ecosystem containing hundreds of bacterial species [10, 11]. From 2005 to the present, many scholars have used the combination of acrylic splints and titanium sheets for intraoral modeling, focusing on the establishment of early supragingival plaque biofilm models [12, 13]. Their findings indicate that the optimal time for bacterial biofilm maturation is 48 h. This experiment also chose to have subjects wear denture devices for 48 h to establish supragingival plaque biofilms. Electron microscopy results showed that the surfaces of both materials were completely covered by plaque biofilms, indicating successful modeling. However, to date, the establishment of subgingival plaque biofilms similar to peri-implantitis has not been successful [14], and further research is still needed in this area. In the clinical treatment of peri-implantitis, if the temperature of bone tissue increases by 10 °C for 60 s, it will lead to permanent changes in the bone tissue structure. Therefore, a temperature rise of less than 10 °C in the peri-implant tissue is considered a safe range [15, 16]. The temperature measurements from the four treatment methods involved in this experiment all fell within the safe range. The water-mist Nd: YAG laser caused a slightly higher temperature rise on the titanium sheet compared to zirconia, which may be due to the difference in thermal conductivity between metal and ceramic [17]. The Er: YAG laser can effectively control heat generation within an appropriate power range. Jacek Matys et al. explored the safe treatment parameters of the Er: YAG laser under the condition that the temperature increase does not exceed 10 °C. They found that both 50 mJ (1.5 W) and 100 mJ (3 W) could achieve temperature control. However, under SEM observation, it was noted that the Er: YAG laser at 100 mJ (3 W) caused cracks on the titanium surface. Therefore, it is recommended to use the energy parameter of 50 mJ/30Hz for implant surface debridement in non-surgical treatments [18]. The results of this experiment are similar to those obtained by Jacek Matys.

Nd: YAG laser can be selectively absorbed by blood components and pigments to generate photothermal effects, so thermal damage should be avoided during its application [19, 20]. The Nd: YAG laser used in the experiment is equipped with a water/air spray cooling system, which can form a mist-like protective layer on the surface of the irradiated material during the laser operation, thereby eliminating the thermal damage caused by the high heat of the laser. The thermocouple thermometer results show that the surface temperature of the material increases by approximately 4.2–4.4 °C after 60 s of Nd: YAG laser spot irradiation with water mist, indicating an acceptable cooling effect. Li Qian et al. [21] conducted a comparative analysis of the temperature changes after 30 s of mobile irradiation with Nd: YAG laser (20 mJ/10Hz, 50 mJ/10Hz, 50 mJ/20Hz) with and without water vapor cooling. The results showed that the surface temperature of the laser with water mist cooling increased by approximately 3.7–3.9 °C, while the temperature of the laser without water mist cooling exceeded 10 °C within 6.2–13.8 s. In this experiment, the parameters of the Nd: YAG laser were 25 mJ, 60 Hz, 1.5 W with a fixed-point irradiation for 60 s, slightly higher than their experimental results but still within the safe range. R A Eriksson et al. [15] compared the water cooling system and the gas cooling system of the laser. When a 250 mJ Er: YAG laser was cooled by gas, the temperature could rise up to 31.4 °C, while using a combined water and gas cooling system, the temperature only increased by 4.4 °C, demonstrating that water flow has a strong cooling capability on the laser’s temperature rise. In summary, this provides an important basis for clinical operation standards—during prolonged treatments, the water supply should be simultaneously increased and the laser handpiece should be kept moving.

This experiment evaluated the formation of dental plaque biofilms after treatment with carbon fiber curettes, sandblasting, Er: YAG laser, and water mist Nd: YAG laser, and also studied and compared the decontamination ability of the implant surface with the aforementioned treatments. Under the scanning electron microscope at low magnification (×40), Group C showed better plaque removal effects. At high magnification (×5000), Groups C, E, and N exhibited bacteria with ruptured bodies and loss of normal morphology, which to some extent reflects bacterial vitality; the plate colony counting method and live/dead bacterial staining both verified that Groups C, E, and N had excellent decontamination abilities, and there was no significant difference between groups (P>0.05). During the operation, we found that sandblasting with water mist had a strong and wide-ranging flushing effect, carbon fiber curettes had a large contact area and a wide range of one-time curettage, and the water vapor pressure of the laser was slightly smaller with a fine optical fiber and a small contact area at the working end; the flexible and fine optical fiber structure of the laser reduced the pain sensation of patients during treatment, achieving the goal of minimally invasive and comfortable treatment [22], which to some extent increased the working time, however, the high efficiency of the laser’s sterilization ability made up for this shortcoming. The Er: YAG laser can effectively remove bacterial biofilms from mechanically polished and acid-etched sandblasted titanium implants without adversely affecting the surface morphology [23], and this experiment also verified this conclusion. In studies using the same modeling approach as this experiment, the Er: YAG laser also showed good bactericidal effects [13, 24]. Compared to the Er: YAG laser, the water mist Nd: YAG laser exhibited similar decontamination abilities.

Osseointegration is a direct structural and functional connection between living bone and the surface of a load-bearing implant [25], which is key to maintaining stability and long-term survival of the implant within the bone tissue. The surface roughness and morphology of implant materials affect osteogenic/bone-inductive signaling pathways and also influence surface-mediated factors that promote osteoclast generation (such as interleukin-1β, interleukin-18) [26]. Different materials may have similar effects on osseointegration. Simone F M Janner et al. evaluated the bone response to two-piece zirconia implants and titanium implants in the mandibles of dogs at 4 and 16 weeks post-implantation and found that both types of implants exhibited similar osseointegration phenomena at both time points [27]; Dieter D Bosshardt et al. [28] also concluded that implants made of commercially pure grade 4 titanium and zirconia with micro-rough surfaces can achieve rapid osseointegration and exhibit good biological tolerance. However, the presence of micro-rough surfaces also increases the adhesion of bacteria to the implant, with a lower plaque biofilm deposition on surfaces with minimal roughness (Sa = 0.5–1.0.5.0 micrometers) compared to those with moderate roughness (Sa = 1.1–2.0.1.0 micrometers) [29], as the surface roughness increases, so does the amount of biofilm accumulation. The results of this experiment show that, apart from carbon fiber curettage, sandblasting, Er: YAG laser, and water mist Nd: YAG laser did not cause changes in the surface roughness of the material, and the possible slight scratches on the material surface after carbon fiber curettage may be one of the reasons for the increase in roughness. Materials treated in different ways were allowed to remain in the mouth for 24 h to form plaque deposits, and the bacterial count on the surface was calculated using the plate colony counting method. The results showed that, apart from the curettage group, the total amount of bacteria reattached to the surfaces of the materials after the other three treatments was less than that of the control group, but there was no significant difference (P>0.05); through the analysis of the average fluorescence intensity of images captured by laser scanning confocal microscopy, the TC, TE, TN, ZN groups were significantly lower than the TA and ZA groups (P<0.05), and the average fluorescence intensity (green fluorescence) of the C, E, N groups was lower than that of the A group (P<0.05), while the average fluorescence intensity (red fluorescence) of the ZE, ZN groups was higher than that of the ZA group (P<0.05), and the TN group was higher than the TA group. Both testing methods indicated that sandblasting, Er: YAG laser, and water mist Nd: YAG laser could reduce the re-adhesion of bacteria to the material surface to some extent, but some of the statistical analysis results showed no significant difference, therefore further research with an expanded sample size is still needed. Combining the roughness measurement results, it is inferred that carbon fiber curettage increases the surface roughness of pure titanium and zirconia, and also increases the amount of plaque re-adhesion on the material surface in the short term (24 h); sandblasting, Er: YAG laser, and water mist Nd: YAG laser did not affect the surface roughness, nor did they significantly affect the total amount of short-term plaque re-adhesion on the material surface, but the total amount of dead bacteria in the re-adhered plaque in both laser groups significantly increased. The purpose of this experiment was to discuss the effects of different treatment methods on the short-term re-adhesion of plaque on different material surfaces, considering that clinical patients perform at least one oral self-care behavior within 24 h after treatment, the experiment chose 24 h as the observation point.

The surface free energy and wettability of zirconia are lower than those of pure titanium, and it has a more excellent ability to inhibit bacterial adhesion, indicating that to some extent, in an environment with only bacterial suspension, zirconia implants or abutments have stronger antibacterial capabilities than pure titanium [30]; however, this experiment shows that after both materials are treated in the same way, there is no significant difference in the total amount of bacterial plaque re-adhered to the zirconia surface compared to the pure titanium group (P >0.05). Some studies have shown that under conditions without saliva film adsorption, when the charge carried by bacteria is close to that of conductive materials such as metals, mirror charges of opposite electrical polarity will be generated on the material surface, which under the action of electrostatic attraction causes stronger adhesion between bacteria and conductive materials; however, when there is a saliva film, the behavior of charge transfer between bacteria and materials will be isolated [31], which may be the reason why there is no significant difference in the total amount of bacterial plaque re-adhered to the surface of the two materials in this experiment.

A literature search on the reattachment of plaque on implant surfaces revealed that the research conditions varied greatly, such as: implant material/surface treatment method, treatment/handling method, selection of laser parameters, types of bacteria, establishment of in vivo/in vitro models, and time points for growth, etc [29, 32–34]. In experiments, the laser parameters were set according to the recommended therapeutic parameters of the laser manufacturer, which are close to the clinical post-treatment situation, but cannot be precisely compared with various basic researches. It is suggested that in future studies, the range of parameter settings (energy density, frequency, power, etc.) should be expanded.

Conclusions

Er: YAG laser and water mist Nd: YAG laser use does not adversely affect the surface morphology, roughness, and temperature changes of pure titanium and zirconia discs. The safety of laser use is higher, and it can efficiently remove the biofilm of plaque from the material surface, with effects superior to carbon fiber curettes. Irradiation with Er: YAG and water mist Nd: YAG lasers can increase the proportion of dead bacteria in the reattached plaque.
