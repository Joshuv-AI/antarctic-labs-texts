Network analysis of school refusal behavior and internalizing and externalizing problems among Chinese adolescents
Authors: Chaoqun Dong, Xinnan Mao, Haiyan Hu, Jiankang He, Gaoyang Liu, Wendan Chen, Chengjia Zhao, Liping Yang, Jingjing Zhang, Guohua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchool refusal behavior (SRB) in adolescence is associated with short- and long-term adverse outcomes and frequently co-occurs with internalizing and externalizing problems. However, the intricate interrelationships among these domains remain insufficiently understood. This study aimed to explore the network structure linking SRB, internalizing problems, and externalizing problems in adolescents.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in two psychiatric hospitals in Zhejiang Province, China. A total of 303 adolescents who met the criteria for SRB completed a demographic questionnaire and validated instruments assessing SRB, anxiety, depressive symptoms, non-suicidal self-injury, insomnia, and internet addiction. Network analysis was performed in R to examine the associations among SRB, internalizing problems, and externalizing problems.ResultsIn the network, the strongest associations were observed between depressive symptoms and non-suicidal self-injury, as well as between anxiety and avoidance of negative emotions at school. Depressive symptoms and avoidance of negative emotions at school emerged as the most central nodes. SRB, internalizing problems, and externalizing problems were interconnected primarily through depressive symptoms, anxiety, insomnia, and avoidance of negative emotions at school.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the central and bridging roles of key nodes linking SRB with internalizing and externalizing problems in adolescents. By clarifying the network structure underlying their co-occurrence, this study advances understanding of the interconnected psychopathological processes characterizing adolescents with SRB and highlights the clinical relevance of targeting central symptoms in intervention efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04494-6.

Introduction

School refusal behavior (SRB) is characterized by an adolescent’s persistent refusal to attend school or difficulty remaining at school for a full day due to psychological or social factors [35, 38]. Approximately 17.8% of school-aged children and adolescents are estimated to experience SRB [64], with referral rates to child and adolescent psychiatric services ranging from 5 to 7% [36]. The prevalence of SRB has increased in recent years, exerting profound negative effects on psychological well-being, academic achievement, parent–child relationships, and social functioning [53]. It is associated with various internalizing and externalizing behavior problems [22, 33], both of which have attracted increasing attention due to their deleterious health consequences. Given the profound developmental changes that occur during adolescence, this vulnerable period constitutes a critical window for examining the interrelationships among SRB, internalizing problems, and externalizing problems [14].

Internalizing problems refer to a propensity to experience distress inwardly [40], and they are highly prevalent among adolescents with SRB [5]. They typically encompass anxiety, depressive symptoms, and insomnia [47]. Existing research consistently indicates that anxiety and depressive symptoms are particularly prominent in this population, with comorbidity rates ranging from 31 to 69% [45, 63]. A substantial proportion of adolescents with SRB meet diagnostic criteria for anxiety disorders [26], and they exhibit excessive worry and heightened concerns regarding academic performance [45]. Depressive symptoms in this group often manifest as social withdrawal, reduced motivation, and negative self-appraisal, which may exacerbate academic difficulties and increase vulnerability to persistent emotional distress [11]. Empirical evidence suggests that SRB is closely intertwined with anxiety and depressive symptoms, with adolescents frequently avoiding school to escape environments that evoke these aversive emotional states [31, 67]. In addition, adolescents with SRB also experience sleep disturbances, including difficulties initiating and maintaining sleep [49]. Insomnia has been shown to be positively associated with SRB, with affected adolescents demonstrating higher levels of SRB than their peers without sleep problems [29]. Given these detrimental impacts and interconnections among anxiety, depressive symptoms, insomnia, and SRB, it is crucial to focus on these internalizing problems of adolescents with SRB.

Research has found a positive association between SRB and externalizing problems [17]. Externalizing problems manifest as overt behaviors such as non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) and internet addiction [16, 19]. In particular, adolescents with SRB are at elevated risk for engaging in NSSI [15]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 32 observational studies further indicated that SRB is associated with NSSI-related outcomes [19]. Additionally, a cross-sectional study conducted in China involving 11,880 adolescents aged 10–20 years found that SRB was significantly associated with deliberate NSSI behavior [75]. Internet addiction has also been frequently observed among adolescents exhibiting SRB [54]. Approximately 41.1% of adolescents with SRB exhibit problematic internet use, accompanied by higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms as well as greater academic impairment [25]. In addition, internalizing and externalizing problems may exert reciprocal influences. Previous research has demonstrated that adolescents experiencing depressive symptoms and anxiety may engage in NSSI as a maladaptive strategy to regulate or alleviate psychological distress [20]. Although previous studies have consistently linked SRB to internalizing problems [1, 13] or externalizing problems [2, 12], the interplay among SRB, internalizing problems, and externalizing problems remains poorly understood. In particular, the key symptoms and core pathways through which these domains interact in adolescents with SRB have yet to be clearly delineated.

The interconnections between SRB, internalizing problems, and externalizing problems further suggest that behavioral and psychological problems rarely occur in isolation. Instead, they may interact dynamically and mutually reinforce one another, forming interconnected systems [6]. Such interrelations highlight the importance of examining associations among variables rather than treating each construct independently. From this perspective, SRB, internalizing problems, and externalizing problems can be conceptualized as mutually influencing components within a complex system [7]. Network analysis provides a data-driven approach for modeling such complexity by representing variables as interconnected nodes within a system [18]. This approach enables the identification of central and bridge nodes that may play critical roles in linking SRB with internalizing and externalizing problems. By capturing the intricate interactions among symptoms, network analysis offers novel insights into adolescent mental health that extend beyond the limitations of traditional analytic approaches [71]. A central aim of this method is to identify core and bridge nodes and to explore the mechanisms underlying their connectivity [57]. Accordingly, the present study applies network analysis to examine the structural relationships among SRB, internalizing problems, and externalizing problems in adolescents.

The present study employed network analysis to examine the complex interrelationships among SRB, internalizing problems, and externalizing problems in adolescents. By identifying core and bridge nodes within this network, the study aims to provide effective guidance to inform prevention and intervention strategies and to advance understanding of the psychopathological processes underlying SRB in adolescence.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study adopted a cross-sectional design. A total of 324 adolescents were recruited from two psychiatric hospitals located in Wenzhou and Lishui, Zhejiang Province, China. Participants were enrolled using a convenience sampling approach and were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged between 12 and 18 years; (2) able to comprehend the study procedures and participate voluntarily, with written informed consent obtained from both the adolescent and a legal guardian; (3) able to fully understand and complete the questionnaire; (4) fulfillment of Berg’s [4] criteria for SRB, including: (a) persistent unwillingness or refusal to attend school, often resulting in excessive and prolonged absenteeism; (b) remaining at home during school hours with parental awareness rather than concealing the behavior; (c) experiencing physical complaints or significant emotional distress associated with school attendance; (d) absence of severe antisocial behavior; and (e) evidence that parents made reasonable efforts to ensure the adolescent’s safety at school. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cognitive impairment or acute psychiatric illness; (2) withdrawal from the study before completion. Of the 324 adolescents initially enrolled, 21 were excluded due to incomplete questionnaire responses, yielding a final valid sample of 303 participants. The response rate was 93.5%.

In accordance with the sample size criteria for network analysis [18], the sample size should be larger than the total number of parameters, including both threshold parameters and pairwise association parameters, with the threshold parameter corresponding to the number of nodes. The pairwise association parameters are calculated as (total number of nodes) × (total number of nodes − 1)/2. A total of 11 nodes were included in our study. The threshold parameters totaled 11, and the number of pairwise association parameters was 55. Consequently, the minimal sample size was 66. To ensure model stability, 3–5 participants per parameter were required, leading to a minimum sample size of 198 to 330 participants. The final sample consisted of 303 participants, meeting the necessary criteria.

Procedure and ethical considerations

Eligible participants were informed of the study aims, significance, and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained. Participants subsequently completed an anonymous paper-based questionnaire under standardized conditions, with assurances regarding the confidentiality of all study data. The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kangning Hospital Affiliated to Wenzhou Medical University (KNLL-20211011002). The study protocol adhered to the standards of the Declaration of Helsinki [74].

Measurement

General demographic questionnaire

The characteristics of adolescents included age, gender, residence, family structure, sibling status, duration of SRB, learning state, and parents’ education level.

School Refusal Assessment Scale-Revised (SRAS-R)

The SRAS-R was used to evaluate SRB in adolescents by Yu et al. [77]. The scale comprises 21 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = never to 7 = always). It consists of 5 dimensions of SRB: avoid negative emotions at school, pursuit of reinforcement outside school, frequent absenteeism to reduce psychosocial problems, conditions that would increase motivation to go to school, and preference to stay with family over going to school. High scores indicate a higher level of the specific motivational function or condition of SRB. The SRAS-R has acceptable reliability among Chinese adolescents [77]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients of the subscales in the present study were 0.86, 0.81, 0.85, 0.76, and 0.86, respectively.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale-7 (GAD-7)

Anxiety symptoms over the preceding two weeks were assessed using the GAD-7 [61]. The scale consists of seven items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all to 3 = nearly every day), with total scores ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate greater anxiety severity, with a cut-off score of 10 or higher suggesting clinically significant symptoms. The GAD-7 has demonstrated acceptable reliability among Chinese adolescents [58]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the GAD-7 was 0.93.

Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

Depressive symptoms during the past two weeks were assessed using the PHQ-9, which is based on the diagnostic criteria for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) [39]. The PHQ-9 consists of nine items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all to 3 = nearly every day), yielding total scores between 0 and 27. Higher scores reflect greater depressive symptom severity, with scores above 10 indicating clinically relevant symptoms. The Chinese version of the PHQ-9 has been widely validated in Chinese adolescents, demonstrating good psychometric properties [41]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the PHQ-9 was 0.90.

Adolescent Non-Suicidal Self-Injury Assessment Questionnaire (ANSSIAQ)

The Chinese version of the Adolescent Non-Suicidal Self-Injury Assessment Questionnaire was employed to evaluate adolescent NSSI behavior throughout the past year [68]. The instrument comprises a 12-item behavioral subscale assessing NSSI frequency and a 19-item functional subscale assessing motivational factors, both rated on 5-point scales. The measurement has demonstrated acceptable reliability in Chinese adolescents [68]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for behavior and function subscales was 0.89 and 0.76, respectively.

Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS)

The AIS was used to assess the severity of insomnia symptoms [59]. The 8-item scale includes 5 items on nocturnal sleep disturbances and 3 items on daytime dysfunction. Each item is rated from 0 (no problem) to 3 (severe problem), yielding total scores ranging from 0 to 24. The scale has acceptable reliability among adolescents in China [69]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the AIS in this study was 0.84.

Young Diagnostic Questionnaire (YDQ)

The YDQ, originally developed by Young [76] and revised by Li et al. [42], was used to assess internet addiction. The scale comprises eight dichotomous items scored as 1 (yes) or 0 (no), yielding total scores ranging from 0 to 8. Respondents endorsing five or more affirmative responses met criteria for internet addiction classification. The scale has been widely used among Chinese adolescents [79]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this questionnaire in this study was 0.73.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), and network analysis was performed using R (version 4.4.2) within the RStudio environment (version 1.4.1106) [56].

The R package qgraph (version 1.9.8) and bootnet (version 1.5.6) were used to estimate and visualize the symptom network, in which the five SRB dimensions were modeled alongside internalizing and externalizing problems. Network estimation was conducted using a Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM) regularized with the graphical least absolute shrinkage and selection operator combined with the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBICglasso) [10]. To achieve an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity, the EBIC hyperparameter γ was set to 0.5 [23]. In the network, nodes represented individual variables, whereas edges represented regularized partial correlations between nodes [18]. Nodes with higher expected influence (EI) were considered more influential within the network [55]. Edge thickness and color intensity reflected the magnitude of associations, with blue edges indicating positive relationships and red edges indicating negative relationships [18].

The most commonly used centrality measure, EI, is calculated as the total of all edge weights linked to a node, preserving both their strength and sign [55]. It is appropriate for networks that contain both positive and negative connections and exhibits great stability in weighted networks [8]. Strength was defined as the sum of the absolute weights of all edges connected to a given node, reflecting the overall importance of that node [50]. Higher values on these indices indicate greater centrality. Other centrality metrics, including closeness and betweenness, were not prioritized because of their potential instability [8, 48]. In addition, bridge centrality was examined to identify nodes that served as critical connectors between distinct symptom communities, with higher values indicating stronger cross-community connectivity [34]. Bridge expected influence (BEI) measures the extent to which a node is connected to nodes in other predefined communities, thereby helping to identify important cross-cluster connectivity [18].

The accuracy and stability of the estimated network were evaluated using the R package bootnet [18]. Edge weight accuracy and centrality stability were assessed via nonparametric bootstrapping and case-dropping bootstrap procedures with 1,000 resamples. Network stability was quantified using the correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient), defined as the maximum proportion of cases that could be removed while maintaining a correlation of at least 0.70 between the original and subset centrality estimates. In line with established guidelines, a CS-coefficient above 0.25 was considered acceptable, with values exceeding 0.50 indicating good stability [18].

The sample was predominantly female (73.9%, n = 224), while males accounted for 26.1% (n = 79). The majority of the participants (68.6%, n = 208) resided in urban areas, while 31.4% (n = 95) were from rural areas. The resulting intervals were relatively narrow, indicating good precision in the estimation of edge strengths within the network (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, case-dropping bootstrap analyses demonstrated that the CS-coefficients for centrality indices exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.50, thereby indicating satisfactory stability of the network model (Figure 4; Supplementary Figures 2 and 3). Discussion

The present study employed a network analysis approach to examine the interrelationships among SRB, internalizing problems, and externalizing problems in a clinical sample of 303 Chinese adolescents. The results revealed that depressive symptoms and avoidance of negative emotions at school emerged as central nodes within the network, while depressive symptoms, anxiety, insomnia, and avoidance of negative emotions at school functioned as key bridge nodes linking SRB with internalizing and externalizing problems. These findings enhance our understanding of the mechanisms connecting SRB, internalizing, and externalizing problems, and may inform targeted interventions to reduce SRB in adolescents.

In the network, we found an association between depressive symptoms and NSSI behavior, which aligns with previous studies [24, 28]. Adolescence is characterized by heightened emotional reactivity [9]. When individuals experience depressive symptoms in response to stressors, they may engage in NSSI as a maladaptive emotion regulation strategy to temporarily alleviate psychological distress [65]. According to the emotion regulation theory of NSSI, such behaviors serve to modulate intense negative affect and provide short-term relief from depressive states, albeit at the cost of long-term maladjustment [37]. In addition, the present findings revealed a strong link between anxiety and avoidance of negative emotions at school, in line with functional models of SRB. Prior research has shown that adolescents with elevated anxiety are more likely to perceive school-related environments as threatening and to adopt avoidance strategies to manage emotional discomfort [26]. Similarly, the results showed a connection between depressive symptoms and avoidance of negative emotions at school. Depressive symptoms may be associated with increased reluctance to attend school by fostering negative thoughts about themselves and their environment. SRB may be linked to reduced social participation, potentially exacerbating distress [70].

The present study found that depressive symptoms had the highest EI and strength centrality in the network, followed by avoidance of negative emotions at school. Previous studies suggest that targeting highly central nodes may exert broader influence within the network [62, 78]. Depressive symptoms constitute a multidimensional syndrome encompassing emotional, cognitive, motivational, and physiological domains [27]. Adolescents experiencing depressive symptoms often display negative cognitive biases and heightened negative affect, which may increase vulnerability to both internalizing and externalizing problems as maladaptive coping responses [66]. Furthermore, the centrality of avoidance of negative emotions at school underscores the pivotal role of emotional avoidance in maintaining SRB. Although avoidance may provide short-term emotional relief, it may be maintained through negative reinforcement processes and may ultimately exacerbate stress exposure, functional impairment, and comorbid psychopathology [21]. These findings suggest that interventions may prioritize reducing depressive symptoms and maladaptive avoidance by strengthening social support, enhancing coping skills, and fostering adaptive emotion regulation strategies among adolescents with SRB.

The present study identified four nodes with the highest BEI across SRB, internalizing, and externalizing problems networks. Depressive symptoms, anxiety, insomnia, and avoidance of negative emotions at school had the strongest BEI, suggesting that these nodes may serve as key bridge nodes linking SRB with internalizing and externalizing problems. This finding suggests that depressive symptoms may facilitate cross-community connectivity, thereby contributing to the co-occurrence and maintenance of SRB with both internalizing and externalizing problems. This result is consistent with previous research showing that adolescents with higher levels of depressive symptoms tend to exhibit reduced attention, impaired decision-making, and diminished behavioral initiative, which may contribute to decreased interest and motivation in learning [32]. In addition, when adolescents experience depressive symptoms, their emotion regulation capacity and frustration tolerance may decline, increasing the likelihood of engaging in immediate, low-effort but high-risk externalizing behaviors to relieve distress or obtain short-term mood improvement [3].

Furthermore, anxiety exhibited strong BEI and connected SRB with internalizing and externalizing problems. This finding indicates that anxiety is closely associated with SRB, and changes in anxiety may be linked to variations in adolescents’ SRB and externalizing problems. Our findings align with prior research suggesting that anxiety may act as a proximal factor associated with SRB by increasing anticipatory worry in school environments [60].

In addition, insomnia emerged as a salient bridge node within the network, consistent with previous evidence linking sleep disturbances to emotional and behavioral problems in adolescence [49]. Liu [43] demonstrated that severe sleep problems were associated with higher levels of externalizing symptoms later in adolescence. This finding suggests that insufficient or poor-quality sleep may aggravate fatigue, impair sustained attention, lower the threshold for expressing negative affect, and hinder impulse and emotion regulation, which may contribute to higher levels of SRB [44]. These findings highlight the importance of paying attention to adolescents’ sleep problems and strengthening sleep education for adolescents [73].

The present findings indicate that avoidance of negative emotions at school has strong BEI in the network. SRB can be seen as a form of functional avoidance, in which emotion-driven behaviors manifest within the school context. Adolescents may engage in both externalizing behaviors and internal emotional distress as a way to avoid uncomfortable academic or social situations, which aligns with findings from prior research [30]. Hence, clinicians and mental health professionals may consider developing targeted interventions to enhance adolescents’ ability to recognize emotions. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy may be adopted, as they have been shown to be effective in relieving anxiety and depressive symptoms [51, 72].

Limitations

Several limitations of the current study should be noted. First, this study used cross-sectional data, which limited our ability to draw conclusions regarding causality. Future research employing longitudinal network analyses, potentially incorporating the duration of SRB, is needed to clarify temporal dynamics and causal pathways. Second, this study was retrospective, rendering recall bias inevitable. Third, the survey sample was restricted to China, and thus the generalizability of the findings to adolescents in other countries or cultural contexts remains unclear. Finally, although the overall sample size was adequate for network analysis, it remained relatively moderate, with a substantially higher proportion of females than males, which might have limited statistical power. Future studies should recruit larger and more balanced samples and examine potential gender differences in network structure and symptom interrelations.

Implications

The present findings underscore the need for early identification and targeted intervention among adolescents with SRB, given the co-occurrence of SRB with internalizing and externalizing problems. Clinicians and mental health professionals may enhance emotional well-being by implementing evidence-based emotion regulation strategies and by creating supportive environments in which adolescents feel safe to express and manage emotions [52]. Moreover, the early detection of anxiety and depressive symptoms may facilitate timely and effective interventions. Insights derived from the current study can inform clinical assessment and treatment planning by emphasizing the interconnected nature of symptoms. Recognizing these interdependencies enables clinicians to develop comprehensive intervention strategies that address not only isolated symptoms but also the broader network of emotional and behavioral difficulties experienced by adolescents with SRB. Such approaches may include the application of empirically supported treatments, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy, that target core symptoms and promote adaptive coping mechanisms [46]. In addition, it is important to screen for NSSI among adolescents with SRB.

Parents also play a crucial role in adolescent development. Increased awareness of the central symptoms identified in this study may assist parents in more accurately recognizing SRB and associated internalizing and externalizing problems in their children. By monitoring adolescents’ anxiety and depressive symptoms and supporting healthy sleep practices, parents may help mitigate SRB and contribute to improvements in their child’s psychological well-being.

Conclusion

This study employed network analysis to examine the associations among school refusal behavior, internalizing, and externalizing problems in adolescents. The findings indicated that depressive symptoms and avoidance of negative emotions at school occupied central positions within the symptom network. Additionally, depressive symptoms, anxiety, insomnia, and avoidance of negative emotions at school emerged as key bridge symptoms, highlighting their potential as strategic targets for interventions aimed at alleviating both internalizing and externalizing difficulties in adolescents with school refusal behavior.
