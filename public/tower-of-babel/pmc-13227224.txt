Balancing the benefits and risks of GLP-1 receptor agonists: a clinical guide for shared decision-making
Authors: Areesha Moiz, Tricia M Peters, Michael A Tsoukas, Oriana HY Yu, Kristian B Filion, Mark J Eisenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are now used widely to manage diabetes, obesity, and an expanding set of metabolic and cardiorenal conditions. As prescribing grows in younger, healthier, and more diverse populations, clinicians increasingly require practical guidance that balances therapeutic benefit with individualized safety considerations. This review synthesizes the current evidence on the physiological mechanisms, clinical indications, and practical safety considerations of this drug class. We aim to provide a structured, clinically oriented approach to monitoring and managing adverse effects, with emphasis on gastrointestinal intolerance, supported by an algorithm for early symptom management. Finally, we outline communication strategies that support shared decision-making, reduce stigma, and align therapy with patient goals. This framework aims to help clinicians deliver safe, informed, and patient-centered care as GLP-1-based therapies continue to evolve.FundingThis study received no external funding.Keywords: Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, Obesity pharmacotherapy, Safety monitoring, Adverse effect management, Shared decision-making, Stigma-free communication

Introduction

The use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) has rapidly expanded beyond management of type 2 diabetes into pharmacological treatment for obesity as well as a growing range of metabolic and cardiorenal indications.1 Over the past five years, prescriptions for GLP-1 RAs have increased roughly five-to ten-fold across the United States and Canada in both primary and specialty care settings,2,3 reflecting their dual appeal: potent weight loss and broad cardiometabolic benefits. Real-world practice now includes use of these medications among younger adults, patients with fewer comorbidities, and those seeking preventive or quality-of-life benefits, signaling a shift from therapeutic necessity to elective optimization of health. As these agents reach broader, lower-risk populations, clinicians are increasingly encountering practical challenges in patient selection, monitoring, and counseling. While the efficacy of GLP-1 RAs is well established, their integration into routine care requires a nuanced understanding of risk mitigation and individualized treatment strategies. Moreover, as many patients are now initiating therapy through telemedicine or commercial weight management programs outside of traditional healthcare settings, the need for standardized clinical guidance is critical.

This review provides a concise, patient-oriented framework for balancing the benefits and risks of GLP-1 RAs through a shared decision-making lens. We summarize key physiological mechanisms and clinical benefits, and outline strategies to manage adverse events, counsel patients, and reduce stigma, aiming to equip clinicians with a practical guide. Importantly, while many recommendations are supported by randomized trial data, others reflect pragmatic strategies derived from observational data and clinical experience in the absence of high-quality comparative evidence.

Search strategy and selection criteria

Evidence for this clinical guide was identified through searches of PubMed, Google Scholar, and clinicaltrials.gov covering the period from database inception to December 2025. Search terms included but were not limited to: “glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists,” “GLP-1-based therapies,” “semaglutide,” “tirzepatide,” “retatrutide,” “obesity pharmacotherapy,” “weight loss,” “cardiometabolic outcomes,” “adverse events,” “safety,” “treatment discontinuation,” “weight regain,” “shared decision-making,” “risk communication,” and “stigma-free care.” Articles were screened for originality, methodological rigor, and clinical relevance, with priority given to large randomized controlled trials, observational studies, systematic review/meta-analyses, and consensus guidelines.

Mechanistic and physiological overview

GLP-1 RAs mimic the endogenous incretin hormone GLP-1, enhancing insulin secretion, suppressing glucagon release, and reducing appetite through central hypothalamic pathways.4,5 These mechanisms collectively improve postprandial glucose control and promote satiety, leading to clinically meaningful weight loss. GLP-1 receptor activation has also been shown to delay gastric emptying; however, the magnitude and persistence of this effect during long-term therapy remain uncertain, with limited data available from studies using standardized objective measures such as scintigraphy.6,7 Unlike many older pharmacotherapies, GLP-1 RAs exert their glucose-lowering effects in a glucose-dependent manner, thereby minimizing the risk of hypoglycemia when used as monotherapy. Beyond metabolic regulation, GLP-1 RAs exhibit pleiotropic effects across multiple organ systems (Table 1).1 These broad physiological effects highlight why GLP-1 RAs have generated interest far beyond their use for glycemic management and may also explain many of their potential side effects: the same mechanisms that slow gastric emptying and suppress appetite may contribute to nausea and vomiting, and alterations in biliary motility may underlie gallbladder complications, among others. Appreciating this mechanistic overlap between benefit and risk is essential for clinicians to optimize outcomes while minimizing adverse events.

Established and emerging clinical indications

GLP-1 RAs demonstrate benefits across multiple conditions, with the magnitude of these benefits varying by agent, dose, and patient population. Across overweight and obesity trials, semaglutide and tirzepatide have shown substantial weight loss, typically in the range of 10–18% of baseline body weight, with newer pre-market agents approaching reductions of 20–25%.8, 9, 10 These effects are accompanied by improvements in glycemia, blood pressure, lipid profiles, and inflammatory markers, even among individuals without diabetes. In type 2 diabetes, GLP-1 RAs remain effective glucose-lowering therapies with low hypoglycemic risk and additive benefits when used alongside other agents in both patients with or without concomitant obesity.11

Large cardiovascular outcome trials in patients with type 2 diabetes and established or high cardiovascular risk have consistently shown reductions of 10–15% in major adverse cardiovascular events.12 These trials, which assessed kidney outcomes as secondary endpoints, also demonstrated reductions in albuminuria and slower decline in renal function. More recently, the FLOW trial, a dedicated kidney outcomes trial conducted in patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease, demonstrated a 20–25% reduction in major kidney disease events.13 Data in individuals with overweight or obesity but without diabetes, including the landmark SELECT trial, further support cardioprotective effects independent of glucose-lowering.14 There is also growing interest in the use of GLP-1 RAs for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction15,16 given their effects on weight, inflammation, and hemodynamics.

Beyond cardiorenal outcomes, GLP-1 RAs show potential benefits across metabolic comorbidities, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease,17, 18, 19 obstructive sleep apnea,20 knee osteoarthritis,21 and polycystic ovary syndrome,22, 23, 24 though durability of response requires longer-term data. Preliminary studies have also explored reproductive or fertility-related outcomes,25 largely in the context of metabolic and hormonal improvements. Interest has now expanded into neurological, behavioural, and inflammatory conditions. Early-phase trials in Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease suggest possible neuroprotective effects,26, 27, 28, 29 and emerging studies indicate potential applications for eating-related disorders,30 substance-use disorders,31, 32, 33 and immune-mediated diseases such as psoriasis or hidradenitis suppurativa.34,35 Early exploratory work has also evaluated their role in headache and pain disorders,36 likely related to their anti-inflammatory and central neuronal effects. Most of these areas remain exploratory, and none should currently guide prescribing outside of clinical trials.

Weight regain post-treatment discontinuation

Although the use of GLP-1 RAs produces substantial weight loss during active treatment, their discontinuation is consistently followed by meaningful weight regain, with clinical trials demonstrating that approximately two-thirds of lost weight is regained within one year.37, 38, 39 This observation is further supported by meta-analytic data estimating an average regain of 0.5–0.8 kg per month, with a projected return to baseline weight within 1.5–1.6 years after treatment discontinuation.40 Importantly, these changes are accompanied by a progressive reversal of cardiometabolic improvements, including increases in HbA1c, fasting glucose, blood pressure, and lipid levels, with pooled analyses indicating that most markers return to baseline within approximately 1.4 years after discontinuation. These findings underscore that GLP-1 RAs should be considered within a chronic disease management framework, where discontinuation is often associated with reversal of therapeutic benefits and may necessitate ongoing treatment or alternative long-term strategies.

Safety considerations

As GLP-1 RAs are increasingly prescribed across a broader spectrum of patients, balancing benefit with patient-specific safety considerations is essential. While the overall safety record of this class is reassuring,8 certain groups warrant more tailored counseling and monitoring. Population-based considerations

Women of reproductive age

Animal studies have shown fetal abnormalities associated with GLP-1 receptor activation, though human data remain limited.41,42 Current guidance recommends discontinuing GLP-1 RAs prior to conception and avoiding their use during pregnancy or lactation.43 However, recent large pharmacoepidemiologic studies have not demonstrated an increased risk of major congenital malformations associated with periconceptional or early pregnancy exposure to GLP-1 RAs compared with insulin, providing some reassurance if inadvertent exposure occurs prior to pregnancy recognition.44 Because delayed gastric emptying may reduce the absorption of oral contraceptives,45 clinicians should review contraceptive reliability and discuss non-oral options. Importantly, as GLP-1 RA-associated metabolic and ovulatory improvements may restore fertility in women with obesity or polycystic ovary syndrome,25,46 proactive contraceptive counseling is essential, even among those previously considered subfertile.

Perimenopausal and postmenopausal women

GLP-1 RA-related nausea, appetite changes, or fatigue may overlap with menopausal symptoms, complicating attribution. Weight loss and improved metabolic health may be especially relevant in this group, but rapid or excessive weight loss may exacerbate bone and lean mass decline. Ensuring adequate protein intake and monitoring for osteoporosis is advisable, particularly in women with baseline osteopenia.47

Older adults

Older adults are more susceptible to dehydration, orthostatic hypotension, and sarcopenia due to age-related physiological changes. These risks are amplified with rapid or excessive weight loss. Slow titration, adequate hydration, and regular assessment of blood pressure and functional status can help mitigate these effects. Resistance exercise and sufficient dietary protein support maintenance of strength and mobility.

Adolescents

Semaglutide is now approved in the United States for chronic weight management in adolescents aged 12–17 years with obesity (typically body mass index [BMI] ≥ 95th percentile for age and sex, often with weight-related comorbidities), although long-term effects on growth, puberty, and psychological well-being are still being studied.48 Most pediatric obesity guidelines recommend pharmacotherapy only for severe or refractory cases following lifestyle intervention.49 Clinicians should frame treatment around metabolic health rather than appearance and include discussions about body image, expectations, and family engagement. Ongoing monitoring for psychological distress or disordered eating patterns is essential.

Patients with mental health concerns

In individuals with pre-existing psychiatric conditions, rapid weight change may coincide with transient mood fluctuations or emotional distress.50,51 Although reports of depression and suicidal ideation have emerged in post-marketing surveillance, clinical trial and pharmacoepidemiologic data have not shown a causal link between GLP-1 RAs and either new-onset or worsening psychiatric disorders.52 Nonetheless, baseline screening and periodic follow-up for mood and anxiety symptoms are prudent, particularly among adolescents and young adults.

Comorbidity-based considerations

Pancreatitis and gallbladder disease

GLP-1 RAs have been associated with an increased risk of pancreatitis,53,54 although large randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have not demonstrated a clear causal relationship.55 This signal may reflect biliary alterations due to rapid weight loss or underlying patient risk factors rather than a direct drug effect.56 Clinicians should remain vigilant for symptoms of pancreatitis, particularly in individuals with pre-existing risk factors such as cholelithiasis or significant hypertriglyceridemia.

Thyroid disorders

GLP-1 RAs are contraindicated in individuals with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2. This recommendation is precautionary, based on rodent data which showed C-cell hyperplasia and thyroid tumors at supratherapeutic exposures.57 Importantly, subsequent human trials and post-marketing data have not confirmed an increased incidence of thyroid malignancy or elevated calcitonin levels.58,59 These species differences are thought to reflect lower GLP-1 receptor expression in human thyroid tissue. Clinicians can reassure patients without a personal or family history of these conditions that there is no available evidence that suggests that the experimental findings translate into human risk.

Type I diabetes

GLP-1 RAs are not approved for the treatment of type 1 diabetes and current guidelines do not recommend their use in this population, in part because individuals with type 1 diabetes were excluded from major cardiovascular and renal outcome trials.60 Evidence supporting their use remains limited to small studies demonstrating modest weight loss and reductions in insulin requirements but inconsistent effects on glycemic management.61, 62, 63, 64 These benefits must be balanced against the risk of hypoglycemia and, in some cases, diabetic ketoacidosis if insulin is inappropriately reduced. In clinical practice, use may be considered on a case-by-case basis in individuals with type 1 diabetes and obesity but should be undertaken with careful monitoring and without excessive reduction of basal insulin.

Diabetic retinopathy

Rapid improvements in glycemic control have been associated with transient worsening of diabetic retinopathy, particularly among patients with pre-existing disease.65 This phenomenon, observed in some clinical trials, is thought to reflect the rate of HbA1c reduction rather than a direct drug effect.66,67 Clinicians should consider baseline ophthalmologic status and ensure appropriate retinal screening and follow-up of patients with diabetes, especially during early treatment.

Concomitant medications

Drug-to-drug interactions warrant attention, particularly when co-administered medications depend on consistent gastrointestinal absorption or have narrow therapeutic windows.45 Absorption of certain oral agents (e.g., contraceptives, levothyroxine, certain antibiotics and extended-release formulations) may be altered due to GLP-1 RA-associated delayed gastric emptying.45 Clinicians should counsel patients to monitor for changes in symptom control or medication effect, and adjust dosing times if needed.

As patients experience substantial weight loss, medications with weight-based dosing (e.g., insulin) or those targeting cardiometabolic risk factors (e.g., antihypertensive, glucose-lowering, or lipid-lowering agents) may require dose adjustment or de-intensification to prevent adverse events associated with overtreatment (e.g., hypotension or hypoglycemia). Close monitoring of blood pressure and glycemic control is recommended during initiation and dose escalation with ongoing adjustment of background therapies recommended as needed.68

Perioperative management

Perioperative management has become increasingly relevant as more patients undergo elective procedures while exposed to GLP-1 RAs. Consensus guidelines recommend withholding weekly formulations for at least one dosing interval before elective surgery to reduce aspiration risk from delayed gastric emptying.69,70 Close coordination between the prescriber, anesthesiologist, and surgical team ensures safe perioperative management.

Emerging concerns

Non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy

Recent observational studies have reported an increased risk of non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy with semaglutide,71,72 leading the European Medicines Agency to list it as a very rare adverse effect (1 in 10,000 people) in the product information for semaglutide-containing medicines.73 Absolute risk remains low, with randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses reporting very few events.74 Data for other GLP-1 RAs remain limited so a class-wide causal relationship has not been definitively established. Patients should be advised to report any acute vision changes, particularly if they have pre-existing optic nerve disease or vascular risk factors.

Metabolic acidosis

Rare cases of metabolic acidosis have been reported in association with tirzepatide, primarily in the context of reduced caloric intake, dehydration, or concomitant illness.75, 76, 77, 78 The mechanism remains unclear and current evidence is limited to case reports and post-marketing data. Clinicians should remain vigilant for symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, or malaise, particularly in higher-risk individuals, and consider appropriate evaluation if clinically indicated.

Monitoring and managing adverse effects

Once therapy is initiated, ongoing monitoring and anticipatory counseling are critical to optimizing tolerance, adherence, and safety. Most adverse effects of GLP-1 RAs are predictable, dose-dependent, and readily managed through structured titration and clear patient education. Gastrointestinal symptoms and titration strategy

Evidence guiding the management of gastrointestinal symptoms remains limited as most clinical trials rely on patient-reported symptoms rather than standardized objective measures.81 Nausea, early satiety, bloating, and mild constipation are common during initiation and dose escalation, likely reflecting delayed gastric emptying. These effects are typically managed with gradual titration, smaller and slower meals, and adequate hydration. Persistent vomiting or severe intolerance may reflect overly rapid dose escalation or insufficient caloric intake. Clinicians should consider pausing or stepping back one dose level and reassessing hydration and nutrition before resuming. Reassessment of electrolytes and renal function may be considered in patients with sustained vomiting, diarrhea, or dehydration, particularly in those with chronic kidney disease or in those taking diuretics, as volume depletion can precipitate acute kidney injury. Symptoms generally improve over time but reinforcing dietary strategies (smaller portions, reduced fat intake, avoiding late-night eating) may enhance long-term tolerability.

Gallbladder, hepatobiliary, and pancreatic monitoring

Rapid weight loss, irrespective of cause, can predispose to gallstone formation and cholecystitis.82 Patients should be advised to promptly report persistent upper abdominal pain, nausea, or vomiting. Ultrasound evaluation is appropriate when symptoms persist beyond several days or are accompanied by fever or elevated liver enzymes. Severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, particularly when associated with vomiting, should raise concern for pancreatitis. In these cases, immediate cessation and medical evaluation are warranted. GLP-1 therapy should not be restarted after a confirmed drug-associated event.

Cardiorenal monitoring

Modest reductions in blood pressure are commonly observed with GLP-1 RAs and are generally favorable, reflecting mild natriuresis, improved endothelial function, and reduced sympathetic tone.83 These hemodynamic effects may, however, predispose frail adults or those receiving concomitant antihypertensive drugs or diuretics to dizziness or orthostatic hypotension. Periodic monitoring of blood pressure, hydration status, and renal function is advisable in higher-risk individuals. Encouraging adequate fluid intake and gradual titration may further minimize volume-related side effects. Episodes of orthostasis or fatigue may likely improve with conservative management, such as adjusting background antihypertensive therapy or spacing diuretics appropriately.

Laboratory and clinical monitoring

Laboratory monitoring should be pragmatic and individualized. Baseline renal and hepatic function testing is reasonable prior to initiation, with testing during follow-up guided by clinical context rather than fixed intervals. In individuals with risk factors for pancreatitis, baseline triglyceride levels may be considered to help identify those at higher risk prior to initiation. Routine thyroid screening or calcitonin measurement is not recommended.57 However, before initiation, clinicians should verify that the patient and their first-degree relatives have no history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2, which remain absolute contraindications. Benign thyroid conditions such as isolated nodules, goitre, or autoimmune thyroiditis are not contraindications and do not require additional testing beyond standard clinical care.84

During prolonged therapy, periodic assessment of body composition and functional status can help identify lean mass loss, particularly in individuals experiencing substantial weight reduction. Clinical trial data suggests that approximately 25–40% of total weight loss with GLP-1 RAs and co-agonists may reflect reductions in lean mass, with systematic reviews showing a median of 34.9%.85, 86, 87 However, the clinical significance of this lean mass reduction remains uncertain. While absolute lean mass decreases, relative lean mass is generally preserved and short-to-mid-term trials have shown preserved or even improved muscle strength despite lean mass reductions.85,88 The concern of muscle loss may be most legitimate for older adults (≥65 years) with preexisting sarcopenia or frailty, where functional decline may occur with prolonged use.89 For these higher-risk populations, regular assessment using office-based screening (e.g., 5 sit-to-stand tests, grip or knee extensor strength tests with a dynamometer) is recommended.90 Encouraging adequate protein intake (≥1.3 g/kg/day for older adults), regular resistance exercise (≥2 days per week), and attention to calcium, vitamin D, and bone health can help preserve muscle and skeletal integrity. Weight loss exceeding 5% per month or substantial weight reduction (≥14%) over 3–4 months warrants particular attention to bone density monitoring.91

It is also worth noting that a subset of patients may not achieve clinically meaningful weight loss with the use of GLP-1 RAs.92 The American Diabetes Association 2026 Standands of Care guidelines recommend evaluating weight loss response at 3 months on the maximum tolerated dose: patients achieiving ≥5% weight loss should continue long-term, while those with <5% should undergo reassessement of adherence and dose optimization.93 Where appropriate, switching to an alternative agent (e.g., from semaglutide to tirzepatide) is reasonable,94 though a small retrospective study suggests that response to tirzepatide may be attenuated in true non-responders compared to those who initially responded to semaglutide but plateaued.95

Mental health and behavioural monitoring

Mental health monitoring is another important component of routine follow-up. Clinicians should consider routinely inquiring about energy, sleep, motivation, and mood, particularly during the first months of therapy or when rapid weight loss occurs. This includes vigilance for both new-onset and pre-existing psychiatric symptoms. Collaboration with primary care or mental health providers supports early intervention if distress, fatigue, or behavioural changes emerge.

When patients understand which symptoms are common, which require simple dose adjustments, and which warrant urgent evaluation, adherence and comfort improve markedly.96 Pairing structured titration approaches with clear, anticipatory communication may support a safe and tolerable early treatment phase.97

Communicating risks, benefits, and stigma-free care

Effective communication is central to the safe and sustained use of GLP-1 RAs. These medications may carry both high expectations and misconceptions, and conversations about them may become emotionally charged.98 Patients may approach treatment with hope, hesitation, or guilt, reflecting past experiences with weight bias or ineffective therapies. The clinician's task is to translate evidence into balanced, accessible information while creating an environment of trust and psychological safety. Shared decision-making should begin before initiation, continue during dose escalation, and be revisited as goals evolve over time (Fig. 2).

How these risks and benefits are communicated may be equally important. Weight management has historically been steeped in moral judgment, and many individuals have experienced shame or bias in healthcare settings.100 To counter this, clinicians should adopt stigma-free, person-first language and an empathic tone. Terms such as “individuals living with obesity” are preferable to labels like “obese patients.” Acknowledging the biological complexity of obesity and normalizing the role for medical treatment may reframe the discussion away from personal blame. Simple practices, such as asking permission to discuss weight, validating the patient's effort, and avoiding assumptions about behaviour, reinforce respect and partnership. Clinicians should also remain aware of their own implicit biases, which may subtly influence tone, facial expressions, and word choice.

When discussing long-term expectations, it is essential to normalize weight plateaus and the physiological tendency toward weight regain after discontinuation. Patients should understand that stopping therapy frequently leads to weight regain,37, 38, 39 not due to a lack of discipline but because of a predictable response that occurs when pharmacologically induced appetite suppression is withdrawn.101, 102, 103 Framing GLP-1 RAs as a therapy for chronic disease management, similar to antihypertensive drugs for blood pressure, situates treatment within a broader, long-term health strategy. At the same time, clinicians should consider emphasizing health improvements beyond the scale: energy, mobility, sleep, metabolic markers, reduced cravings, and improved relationship with food. Success should be defined collaboratively, focusing on health and quality of life rather than appearance. In line with The Obesity Society and Obesity Canada guidelines, clinicians can frame treatment goals around achieving a patient's “best weight”, the weight at which health is optimized and sustainable lifestyle habits can be maintained, rather than an arbitrary or cosmetic ideal.104, 105, 106 Framing progress in this way may help patients reimagine success as multidimensional rather than purely numeric, encouraging sustained engagement.

When communication is transparent, empathetic, and stigma-free, patients feel respected and supported. This, in turn, improves adherence, facilitates early reporting of adverse effects, and reinforces the shared nature of treatment.96 In many cases, how clinicians communicate may be as important as the pharmacotherapy itself.

Future directions

As the use of GLP-1 RAs continues to expand, several areas remain uncertain in clinical practice, including optimal strategies for managing gastrointestinal symptoms, the long-term effects on gastric emptying, and approaches to treatment in underrepresented populations such as individuals with type 1 diabetes. Additional questions relate to the composition of weight loss, particularly the extent and clinical implications of lean mass reduction, and how best to preserve muscle and functional status during prolonged therapy. There is also a need to better understand long-term durability of treatment in real-world settings, including patterns of discontinuation, weight regain, and cardiometabolic trajectories after therapy is stopped. Finally, as prescribing increasingly occurs outside traditional specialist care, further work is needed to define standardized approaches to patient selection, monitoring, and follow-up that are both evidence-informed and feasible in routine practice. Addressing these gaps will be critical to refining clinical guidance and ensuring safe, effective, and sustainable use of these therapies.

Conclusions

GLP-1 RAs have become an integral tool in obesity and cardiometabolic medicine, with benefits supported across weight, metabolic parameters, and several related comorbid conditions. As their use expands to younger, healthier, and more diverse patient populations, thoughtful clinical oversight is increasingly important. Effective prescribing requires careful patient selection, attention to safety considerations, and early, anticipatory counseling to prevent avoidable adverse effects. Equally essential is clear, stigma-free communication that aligns treatment decisions with patient goals, expectations, and lived experiences. Although many emerging indications remain investigational, the rapid evolution of this therapeutic class highlights the need for clinicians to remain engaged with the evidence and to guide patients through a landscape often shaped by commercial programs and variable access to longitudinal care. Integrating GLP-1 RAs into a structured, patient-centered management plan that emphasizes partnership and realistic long-term planning may help translate their growing potential into meaningful and durable improvements in population-level health.
