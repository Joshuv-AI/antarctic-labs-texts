Scalar emission from neutron star-black hole binaries in scalar-tensor theories with kinetic screening
Ramiro Cayuso, Adrien Kuntz, Miguel Bezares, Enrico Barausse

Abstract
We explore scalar radiation from neutron star-black hole binaries in scalar-tensor theories with kinetic screening ( K K -essence). Using 3+1 numerical relativity simulations in the decoupling limit, we investigate scalar dipole and quadrupole radiation for different values of the strong coupling constant Λ \Lambda . Our results show that kinetic screening effectively suppresses the scalar dipole radiation as Λ \Lambda decreases. This is validated by comparing to analytic predictions for the screening of dipole scalar emission, with which our numerical results show good agreement. However, our numerical simulations show that the suppression of scalar quadrupole radiation is less efficient, even when the screening radius exceeds the wavelength of the emitted radiation. In fact, the dependence of the scalar quadrupole amplitude on Λ \Lambda flattens out for the smallest Λ \Lambda that we can simulate, and the quadrupole amplitude is suppressed only by a factor ≲ 3 \lesssim 3 relative to the Fierz-Jordan-Brans-Dicke case. Overall, our study shows that scalar quadrupole radiation from mixed binaries may be used to place constraints on K K -essence theories with next-generation gravitational-wave detectors.

We find that the scalar dipole emission is suppressed (i.e. screened), but kinetic screening is less efficient for the scalar quadrupole emission, even when a clear separation of scales between the scalar emission’s wavelength and the screening radius is present.
Despite the simplifications inherent to our decoupling-limit treatment, our results seem to suggest that GW generation by binary systems may be used to place constraints on
K
-essence theories, and therefore on effective field theories of dark energy.

From the analytic side, it is difficult to solve the equation of motion (
6

K
-essence can be summarized as follows:

•

Isolated bodies
: The spherical and static case was considered in Refs.
[
18
,
20
,
21
,
23
]

[
21

22

also considered oscillated stars. These systems show evidence for kinetic screening.

•

Binaries
: Already for a two-body

system in the decoupling limit, there is no analytic expression for the two-body potential in
K
-essence theories. Refs.
[
19

24
]
studied this static problem semi-analytically and numerically, and found the screening to be present, although its efficiency is slightly reduced compared to isolated bodies, especially in the region near the binary’s saddle point
[
24
]
. As mentioned in the introduction, Ref.
[
36
]
performed
full-fledged numerical relativity simulations, whose results we are aiming to extend with this paper.

Besides this work in
K
-essence, there exists a significant body of work on binaries in Galileon theories with Vainshtein screening
[
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]

While the treatment of binaries is approximate – assuming not only the decoupling limit, but also extreme mass ratios, making it similar in spirit to the oscillating stars of Refs.
[
21
,
22
]
– these results show that scalar waves should be screened, if the wavelength of the corresponding radiation is smaller than the screening radius.

For comparable-mass binaries, the problem of analytically solving for scalar emission is still open. Refs.
[
45
,
44
]
proposed to deal with these systems
in Galileon theories by linearizing equations of motion over a static background field generated by a fictitious particle located at the center-of-mass of the binary. While the idea is interesting, this description cannot account for
the details of the field’s dynamics in the near zone
(i.e. the region, of size comparable to the radiation’s wavelength, where retardation effects are negligible).
In fact, as shown explicitly in Ref.
[
62
]
, screening becomes ineffective at the saddle point of a binary system. For comparable-mass binaries, this corresponds to the
the center of mass. This effect is not accounted for in the framework of Refs.
[

,
44
]
.

In this work, we will adopt an analytic approach similar to Refs.
[
45
,
45
]
, while at the same time consistently solving for the field dynamics in the near zone of a black hole-neutron star system. We will use the formalism of matched asymptotic expansions, a tool used with success in the traditional post-Newtonian approach to the two-body problem in GR

\Omega
is the Kepler frequency defined in Eq. (

III.1
Static one-body problem and near-zone solution

Let us start by reviewing the solution to the field equations when the point-particle representing the neutron star is at rest
[

,
20
,
21

23

\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{r}\big(r^{2}K^{\prime}(\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}^{\prime 2})\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}^{\prime}\big)=-\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}m_{\mathrm{NS}}}{8\pi r^{2}M_{\mathrm{P}}}\delta\big(r\big)\;,

(14)

where
r=|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{NS}}|
is the distance to the neutron star, and
\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}
denotes the spherically symmetric field obtained in this static configuration. Integrating once, we obtain

K^{\prime}(\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}^{\prime 2})\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}^{\prime}=-\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}m_{\mathrm{NS}}}{8\pi r^{2}M_{\mathrm{P}}}\;.

(15)

For our choice of

K(X)\approx-X/2
(where non-linear terms are negligible and screening is not active), or the non-linear regime in which

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}(r)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}m_{\mathrm{NS}}}{4\pi M_{\mathrm{P}}r}+\dots,\quad r\gg r_{*}\;,

(16)

\displaystyle=C+\frac{5}{3}\left(\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}m_{\mathrm{NS}}\Lambda^{8}r^{3}}{3\pi M_{\mathrm{P}}}\right)^{1/5}+\dots,\quad r\ll r_{*}\;,

(17)

where we have imposed that the scalar field vanishes at infinity,

is a constant of integration, and

is the nonlinear screening radius separating the two regimes. In order to find the precise value of

15

where

(19)

Using the cosmologically motivated value of the strong-coupling constant,

\partial_{i}(K^{\prime}(X)\partial^{i}\varphi)=-\frac{\alpha_{\rm NS}m_{\rm NS}}{2M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\delta^{(3)}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\rm NS}(t))\;,

(20)

where
X=\delta^{ij}\partial_{i}\varphi\partial_{j}\varphi
and we have used the fact that
u^{t}=1+{\cal O}(1/c^{2})
and
g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+{\cal O}(1/c^{2})
. (Note that we use this flat-spacetime assumption exactly throughout this paper motivated by the decoupling limit, but even away from that limit, one can always set the metric to flat space in the near zone at leading Newtonian order
[
64
,
24
]
).

It is then immediate to observe that a boost of the isolated star solution provides a solution to Eq. (
20
), i.e. in the near zone one has

\varphi(\bm{x})=\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}(|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{NS}}(t)|)\,.

(21)

Similarly, if one has a closed-form solution for a two-particle system, one could boost it to obtain a solution for a neutron star binary
[
24
]
. Unfortunately, static two particle solutions cannot be obtained analytically
[
19
,
24
]
.

III.2
Multipole expansion of the large-distance field

Let us now consider the (vacuum) region at large distances from the source
|\bm{x}|\gg a
. We will attempt to solve the scalar equation (
6
) by modeling the scalar profile with a
single-particle static solution located at the origin of the coordinates, plus a small time-dependent perturbation:

\varphi(\mathbf{x})=\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}(|\mathbf{x}|)+\varphi_{1}(t,\mathbf{x})\,.

(22)

We will solve for the field
\varphi_{1}
by linearizing the equation, and we will check
a posteriori
the self-consistency of the method, i.e. we will determine the minimum distance at which
\varphi_{1}\ll\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}
. For more details, see Appendix
C
, where we will find this distance to be the binary’s separation
a
.

The linearized equation for
\varphi_{1}
reads

\partial_{\mu}\bigg[K^{\prime}(X_{\mathrm{SS}})\partial^{\mu}\varphi_{1}+2\partial^{\mu}\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}K^{\prime\prime}(X_{\mathrm{SS}})\partial_{\nu}\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}\partial^{\nu}\varphi_{1}\bigg]=0\,,

(23)

where
X_{\mathrm{SS}}=(\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}^{\prime})^{2}
. We recall that the right-hand side is zero because we are solving the scalar equation in vacuum far from the source. Because this equation is linear, we can expand
\varphi_{1}
in Fourier modes and spherical harmonics:

\varphi_{1}=\int\mathrm{d}\omega e^{-i\omega t}\sum_{\ell,m}\mathcal{A}_{\ell m\omega}R_{\ell m\omega}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)+\mathrm{c.c.}

(24)

where “c.c.” denotes complex conjugation, and
\mathcal{A}_{\ell m\omega}
is an amplitude which is arbitrary at the moment.
The differential equation for the radial function
R_{\ell m\omega}
(which in the following we will refer to simply as
R
to avoid clutter) is

\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{r}\big[r^{2}(K^{\prime}+2X_{\mathrm{SS}}K^{\prime\prime})R^{\prime}\big]+K^{\prime}\bigg(\omega^{2}-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}\bigg)R=0\,.

(25)

We solve this equation with outgoing boundary conditions, i.e.
R\simeq e^{i\omega r}
for
r\rightarrow\infty
, which of course satisfy the scalar equation (
25
) at large
r
(outside the screening radius).
Given the complexity of the background scalar profile
\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}
, we could not obtain explicit analytic solutions to Eq. (
25
) valid at all
r
. However, it is possible to obtain an approximate solution by matching together two limiting regimes. We present the resulting solution for the radial function
R
explicitly in App.
A
.

To determine the amplitude
\mathcal{A}_{\ell m\omega}
, we can match the solution for the field at large distances obtained in this Section to the near-zone solution of Sec.
III.1
. The matching is performed
explicitly in App.
B
for the dipole mode
\ell=m=1
, and yields

\mathcal{A}_{11\omega}=\delta(\omega-\Omega)\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{11\Omega}
, with

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{11\Omega}

\displaystyle=ie^{2i\pi/5}\sqrt{\frac{10}{3}}\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}m_{\mathrm{NS}}}{4\Gamma(7/10)M_{\mathrm{P}}}

\displaystyle\times r_{*}^{-4/5}\bigg(\frac{\Omega}{2\sqrt{5}}\bigg)^{7/10}\frac{m_{\mathrm{BH}}}{m_{\mathrm{BH}}+m_{\mathrm{NS}}}a\,.

(26)

It should be stressed that this matching procedure assumes implicitly
that there is a
buffer zone
where both solutions are valid. Indeed, by self-consistently solving the equations, we prove in App.
C
that the buffer zone is finite and corresponds to the range
a\ll|\bm{x}|\ll 2\pi/\Omega
.

III.3
Screening of dipole emission

Our final solution for the dipole scalar field is

\varphi_{1}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{11\Omega}R_{11\Omega}(r)Y_{11}(\theta,\phi)e^{-i\Omega t}+\mathrm{c.c.}\,,

(27)

with the radial function
R_{11\Omega}(r)
presented in App.
A
, c.f. Eqs. (
46
)-(
47
).

In Fig.
2
, we show the amplitude of the dipole predicted by Eq. (
27
), for different values of the screening radius.

More precisely, the scaling of the dipole field outside the screening radius relative to the single-particle solution

\varphi_{1}/\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}\sim v

Before moving on, let us comment on higher-order terms in the multipole expansion. At quadrupole order, one needs to perturb the large-distance field to second order
\varphi=\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}+\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}
with
\varphi_{2}\ll\varphi_{1}
. Inserting this ansatz in Eq. (
6
), one obtains a differential equation similar to Eq. (
25
), but with a source term (quadratic in the dipole perturbation
\varphi_{1}
) on the right-hand side. The solution to this equation is more involved than in the dipole case, and will be left to future work.

IV
Numerical Simulations

In this section, we evolve neutron star-black hole binaries in the decoupling limit, by evolving Eq. (
6
) with the source (
12
) in flat space. As we will see, this approach will allow us to simulate more realistic values of the strong coupling scale
\Lambda
than possible with full-fledged numerical relativity simulations
[
36
]
.

We consider systems where the neutron star and the black hole have the same mass,
m_{\mathrm{BH}}=m_{\mathrm{NS}}=M
. While unrealistic from an astrophysical perspective, a light black hole mass yields a lower binary relative velocity for a fixed orbital frequency. This makes it easier to evolve numerically the equations for systems that satisfy the expected hierarchy of scales between the scalar perturbation’s wavelength
\lambda
and the screening radius
r_{*}
(
\lambda\ll r_{*}
). Indeed, the mass of the black hole enters the simulations only through the Keplerian orbital frequency (
13
).

IV.1
Matter source

When it comes to performing numerical simulations, the point particle approach is no longer viable. In order to model the matter source corresponding to the neutron star in the binary, we instead adopt a smooth spherical profile to regularize the Dirac delta in Eq. (
12
), i.e.
\delta^{3}(\bm{x})\to\rho(r)
with

\rho(r)=-\frac{A\,\tilde{r}^{2}}{(2\pi\sigma)^{3/2}}\exp(-\left(\frac{\tilde{r} - r_{p}}{\sigma}\right)^{2}),

(29)

where
\tilde{r}=\sqrt{x(t)^{2}+y(t)^{2}+z(t)^{2}}
, being
x(t)=x+a/2\cos{(\Omega t)}
,
y(t)=y+a/2\sin{(\Omega t)}
and
z(t)=z
, where we recall that
a
is the radius entering Kepler’s law in (
13
) and
A
is a normalization constant ensuring that
\int 4\pi r^{2}\rho\mathrm{d}r=1
. The value of
\sigma
is fixed such that
99\%
of the mass is contained within a radius
r_{s}
, which, from now on, we will refer to as the radius of the star.
This describes a smooth shell-like profile, and
r_{p}
is a parameter that adjusts the shell’s size. The factor of
\tilde{r}^{2}
in front of the exponential is included to enforce
\rho
to vanish at the center of the star.

This seemingly involved choice of the matter profile is useful to limit the resolution needed to model isolated-star screening solutions. To illustrate this point, Fig.
3
shows the static solutions for the shell-like profile for different values of
\Lambda
. As can be seen, when
\Lambda
decreases, the scalar field gradient gets more and more suppressed as a result of screening. Also, the screening radius within which the suppression occurs increases as
\Lambda
decreases, as predicted by Eq. (
19
).

Moreover, for fixed
\Lambda
, we compare the solutions
obtained with the shell-like profile
of Eq. (
29
) to those obtained
with a Gaussian profile, i.e. by
removing the
\tilde{r}^{2}
term in Eq. (
29
) and setting
r_{p}=0
. In the latter case, the derivative of the scalar field remains non-zero well inside the star, in contrast to the shell-like solutions. Note that regularity demands the gradient of the scalar field to vanish at the origin.
Indeed, as shown in Ref.
[
20
]
, the scalar field gradient does indeed go to zero linearly in
r
near the origin, but only for very small
r
, c.f. their Fig. 1.
In order to numerically resolve the scales at which the scalar field gradient becomes linear in
r
, a very fine spatial resolution is therefore required, which is restrictive in
3+1
simulations. We thus avoid this problem altogether by using the shell-like profile in our simulations.

We stress that this choice does not affect our results and conclusions. Indeed, Fig.
3
shows that the exterior scalar profile is the same for the shell-like and Gaussian profiles. We show this in more detail in App.
D
, where we look at the impact of the profile choice on dynamical quantities (such as outgoing radiation).

IV.2
Initial conditions

To maintain numerical stability during the evolution, especially as
\Lambda
decreases, it is crucial to prescribe appropriate initial conditions for the scalar field
\varphi
. These can, in principle, be produced by boosting the shell-like solutions for
\varphi
described in the previous section with the orbital velocity given
by Eq. (
13
). As for
the scalar field derivative, one may try to set
\partial_{t}\varphi=0
initially, letting the system relax and reach the physically realistic configuration
after an initial transient.

While this transient phase is generally not problematic, for high orbital frequencies
\Omega
and small values of
\Lambda
, the evolution equations’ characteristic structure can lead to catastrophic outcomes. Indeed, during the transient phase, the field can develop large characteristic speeds, causing a Keldysh-type breakdown of the Cauchy problem and requiring extremely restrictive Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) conditions to keep the simulation feasible. These CFL conditions seem necessary throughout the simulation due to “shock-like” features originating in the transient phase and persisting afterwards. To avoid these issues, we use the static solutions from the previous section as initial conditions for
\varphi
, but we boost them by prescribing a time-dependent orbital velocity
\Omega(t)
for the source, which slowly

IV.3
Set-up and Numerical scheme

For the simulations presented in this work, we
assume that neutron star sensitivities vanish, so that
\alpha_{\rm NS}=\alpha
, and we
set the conformal coupling to
\alpha=1/2
. We fix the mass of the star to one solar mass
m_{NS}=M_{\odot}
, its radius to
r_{s}=13.3\text{km}
, and the orbital separation to
a=52.8\text{km}
. The values of
\Lambda
are as high as
16.4\text{MeV}
and as low as
0.39\text{MeV}
, with
r_{*}\sim 2100\text{km}
. We also simulate the FJBD case, corresponding to the limit
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
.

To evolve Eq. (
6
), we reduce it to a first-order system by introducing the first-order variables
\Psi\equiv\partial_{t}\varphi
,
\psi_{x}\equiv\partial_{x}\varphi
,
\psi_{y}\equiv\partial_{y}\varphi
and
\psi_{z}\equiv\partial_{z}\varphi
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(K^{\prime}(X)\Psi\right)=\partial_{i}\left(K^{\prime}(X)\psi_{i}\right)-\Sigma\penalty\ ,

(30)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi_{i}=\partial_{i}\Psi\penalty\ ,

(31)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi=\Psi\penalty\ .

(32)

Our numerical code is a Simflowny-based high-performance 3D code
[
65
,
66
,
67
,
68
]
and runs under the SAMRAI infrastructure
[
69
,
70
,
71
]
, which provides parallelization and the adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) required to solve the different scales in the problem. We use fourth-order finite difference operators to discretize our equations and a fourth-order Runge-Kutta time integrator. Our computational domain corresponds to the range
[-12500,12500]^{3}\text{km}
and contains 10 refinement levels. Each level has twice the resolution of the previous one, achieving a resolution of
\Delta x_{10}=0.123\text{km}
on the finest grid. We use a Courant factor
\lambda_{c}\equiv\Delta t_{l}/\Delta x_{l}=0.3
on each refinement level
l
.

The large spatial domain is necessary to adequately extract the outgoing radiation at radii larger than the screening radius
r_{*}
. The response of a detector to scalar radiation is encoded in the Jordan-frame Newman-Penrose invariant
\phi_{22}

[
72
]
, which far from the source can be approximated
[
73
,
21
]
by

\phi_{22}=-\alpha\sqrt{16\pi G}\partial_{t}^{2}\varphi+\mathcal{O}\left(1/r^{2}\right)\,.

(33)

This expression assumes that the scalar field decays as
\propto 1/r
, which is a good approximation at distances larger than the screening radius
r_{*}
. The strain
h_{s}
produced by the scalar radiation is computed by integrating
\phi_{22}
twice in time, i.e.
\phi_{22}=\partial_{t}^{2}h_{s}
, which implies
h_{s}\propto\alpha\varphi
. Therefore, to study scalar emission, we extract
\varphi
at extraction radii
\mathcal{R}_{E}=\{3692,4429,5168,5900,7383,8860,10336,11813\}\text{km}
, decompose it in spherical harmonics, and extrapolate its value to infinity. For this work, we will focus on the dominant contribution from the dipole (
l=m=1
), and the quadrupole
l=m=2
component.

V
Conclusions

In this work, we investigated the scalar emission from neutron star-black hole binaries in scalar-tensor theories with kinetic screening (
K
-essence theories). Our approach includes both 3+1 dimensional numerical relativity simulations in the decoupling limit and analytical predictions derived from matched asymptotic expansions.

Our simulations show that the scalar dipole emission is strongly suppressed by kinetic screening, especially as the strong coupling constant
\Lambda
decreases and the screening radius grows larger than the wavelength of the emitted radiation. This suppression of dipole radiation agrees with analytical predictions, with phase shifts and amplitude reductions that match well within the expected range.

The quadrupole scalar emission, however, shows a different behavior. While screening does initially suppress the quadrupole amplitude as
\Lambda
decreases, like for the scalar dipole, the suppression eventually slows down when
\Lambda\to 0
, even when the screening radius exceeds the radiation’s wavelength. Indeed, the scalar quadrupole amplitude appears to asymptote to a constant as
\Lambda\to 0
, with an overall suppression at most by a factor
\sim 3
relative to quadrupole emission in FJBD theories at the lowest
\Lambda
we can simulate. This suggests that scalar quadrupole radiation could play a crucial role in constraining
K
-essence theories, particularly in the context of next-generation gravitational-wave detectors
[
74
,
75
]
, which will observe mixed neutron star-black hole binaries in the early inspiral phase. Indeed, a quadrupole scalar emission that is only partially screened
could speed up the phase evolution of mixed binaries due to the enhanced radiation reaction.

In this Appendix, we will construct the radial function
R

\omega r\gg 1

We change variables to define a rescaled radial function

\Psi^{\prime\prime}+\bigg[\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{g}-2\bigg(\frac{g^{\prime}}{g}\bigg)^{2}+2\frac{g^{\prime}}{rg}-K^{\prime}g^{2}\bigg(\omega^{2}-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]\Psi=0\;.

(35)

In the limit
\omega r\gg 1
, most terms inside the brackets are suppressed. We are thus left with a simple second-order equation on
\Psi
,

\Psi^{\prime\prime}+\omega^{2}\frac{K^{\prime}}{K^{\prime}+2X_{\mathrm{SS}}K^{\prime\prime}}\Psi=0\;.

(36)

The potential
V(r)=\omega^{2}K^{\prime}/(K^{\prime}+2X_{\mathrm{SS}}K^{\prime\prime})
is plotted in Fig.
9
for the choice of
K(X)
used in this work. It asymptotes to
\omega^{2}/5
at distances
r\ll r_{*}
smaller than the screening radius, and to
\omega^{2}
outside the screening radius (
r\gg r_{*}
). More importantly,
V(r)
never vanishes: this means that we can use a Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin (WKB) approximation to find a solution
[
76
]
,

Figure 9:
Plot of the potential
V(r)/\omega^{2}=K^{\prime}/(K^{\prime}+2X_{\mathrm{SS}}K^{\prime\prime})
entering the equations of motion in the WKB regime, c.f. Eq. (
36
).

\Psi_{\mathrm{WKB}}(r)=V(r)^{-1/4}\exp\bigg(+ i \int_0^r V(r')^{1/2} \mathrm{d}r'\bigg)\;,

(37)

where the plus sign has been chosen in the exponential in order for the solution to satisfy outgoing boundary conditions at infinity.
This solution will be accurate as long as
V^{1/2}r\gg 1
. Thus, the solution for
R
is

\displaystyle R(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r\omega^{1/2}\big(K^{\prime}(K^{\prime}+2X_{\mathrm{SS}}K^{\prime\prime})\big)^{1/4}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\bigg( i \omega\int_0^r \sqrt{\frac{K'}{K'+2X_\mathrm{SS}K''}} \mathrm{d}r' \bigg)\;.

(38)

In the following, it will be useful to have asymptotic expressions for
R
at distances large and small relative to the screening radius. Using the solution for the spherically symmetric field in (
17
)-(
16
), we obtain

\displaystyle R(r)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\sqrt{2}e^{i\omega r/\sqrt{5}}}{5^{1/4}\omega^{1/2}r^{1/5}r_{*}^{4/5}}\;,\quad r\ll r_{*}\;,

(39)

where we have used the definition of the screening radius in (
19
), and

\displaystyle R(r)

\displaystyle\simeq e^{i\omega r}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r\omega^{1/2}}\exp\bigg( i \omega\int_0^\infty\bigg[ \sqrt{\frac{K'}{K'+2X_\mathrm{SS}K''}} - 1 \bigg] \mathrm{d}r' \bigg)\;,\quad r\gg r_{*}\;.

(40)

Note that the integral involved in the
r\gg r_{*}
case is convergent, but cannot be computed analytically in closed-form. However, it is straightforward to numerically solve for the spherically symmetric field in Eq. (
15
) and then compute the integral, obtaining

\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg[\sqrt{\frac{K^{\prime}}{K^{\prime}+2X_{\mathrm{SS}}K^{\prime\prime}}}-1\bigg]\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}\simeq-0.56r_{*}\;.

(41)

The
\omega r\gg 1
limit does not allow one to obtain the radial function in the wave zone. However, there is another limit in which we can analytically compute the radial function, i.e. when the radius is small compared to the screening scale,
r\ll r_{*}
. In this case, Eq. (
25
) becomes

r^{-2/5}\partial_{r}\big(r^{2/5}R^{\prime}\big)+\frac{1}{5}\bigg(\omega^{2}-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}\bigg)R=0\;.

(42)

The outgoing solution is given by the Hankel function,

R(r)=\mathcal{N}r^{3/10}H_{\nu}^{(1)}\bigg(\frac{\omega r}{\sqrt{5}}\bigg)\;,

(43)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization constant, and
\nu=\sqrt{9+20\ell(\ell+1)}/10
. We fix
\mathcal{N}
by requiring that the solution should be equal to the WKB solution in the matching zone
1/\omega\ll r\ll r_{*}
. We will thus need the asymptotic expansion of
H_{\nu}^{(1)}
for
\omega r\gg 1
, given by

\displaystyle R(r)

\displaystyle\simeq-\mathcal{N}\frac{e^{i\omega r/\sqrt{5}}}{\omega^{1/2}r^{1/5}}\sqrt{\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{\pi}}e^{i\pi(3/4-\nu/2)}\;,\quad r\omega\gg 1\;.

(44)

Comparing with Eq. (
39
), we obtain the value of the normalization factor,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{5}}e^{i\pi(\nu/2-3/4)}r_{*}^{-4/5}\;.

(45)

To summarize, we have obtained the radial function for all values of the radius:

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r\omega^{1/2}\big(K^{\prime}(K^{\prime}+2X_{\mathrm{SS}}K^{\prime\prime})\big)^{1/4}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\bigg( i \omega\int_0^r \sqrt{\frac{K'}{K'+2X_\mathrm{SS}K''}} \mathrm{d}r' \bigg)\;,\;r\omega\gg 1\;,

(46)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}r^{3/10}H_{\nu}^{(1)}\bigg(\frac{\omega r}{\sqrt{5}}\bigg)\;,\quad r\ll r_{*}\;.

(47)

Appendix B
Matching multipole and near-zone solutions

In this Appendix, we explicitly perform the matching of the near-zone solution obtained in Sec.
III.1
, Eq. (
21
) (valid for
|\bm{x}|\ll 2\pi/\Omega
), and the multipole expansion obtained in Sec.
III.2
,
\varphi=\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}+\varphi_{1}
, where
\varphi_{1}
is given by Eq. (
24
).
To this aim, let us perform a multipole expansion of the near-zone solution (
21
), and match the coefficients of these decompositions.

To perform the multipole expansion, we note that at large distances from the source
|\bm{x}|\gg a
(but still inside the near zone
|\bm{x}|\ll 2\pi/\Omega
) we can write, at lowest order,

\displaystyle\varphi(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}(|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{NS}}(t)|)

\displaystyle\simeq\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}(|\bm{x}|)-x_{\mathrm{NS}}^{i}n_{i}\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}^{\prime}(|\bm{x}|)+\dots

(48)

where
n_{i}=x_{i}/|\bm{x}|
. The only angular dependence is in the scalar product
x_{\mathrm{NS}}^{i}n_{i}
, whose decomposition in spherical harmonics gives a dipole term:

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{NS}}^{i}n_{i}=-\frac{m_{\mathrm{BH}}}{m_{\mathrm{BH}}+m_{\mathrm{NS}}}a\sin\theta\cos(\Omega t - \phi)

(49)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{3}}\frac{m_{\mathrm{BH}}}{m_{\mathrm{BH}}+m_{\mathrm{NS}}}aY_{11}(\theta,\phi)e^{-i\Omega t}+\mathrm{c.c.}

(50)

where
\theta
and
\phi
are the angles of the observation direction
n_{i}
in spherical coordinates.
Let us now perform the matching. The monopole term
\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}(|\bm{x}|)
is the same in the near-zone solution [Eq. (
48
)] and in the multipole expansion [Eq. (
22
)] provided that we set
\mathcal{A}_{00\omega}=0
.
On the other hand, the dipole term will match if

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\omega e^{-i\omega t}\mathcal{A}_{11\omega}R_{11\omega}(r)

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{3}}\frac{m_{\mathrm{BH}}}{m_{\mathrm{BH}}+m_{\mathrm{NS}}}

\displaystyle\times ae^{-i\Omega t}\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}^{\prime}(r)\;.

(51)

At this order in the expansion in
a/r
, we can set all other multipoles to zero.

Let us now see how we can satisfy this matching condition. As before, we assume that the near zone is completely contained inside the screening radius
r_{*}
, i.e.
2\pi/\Omega\ll r_{*}
, which is very well verified for typical astrophysical sources. The radial function
R
for
r\ll r_{*}
has been determined in App.
A
, c.f. Eq. (
47
). We can then further simplify the latter, because in the wave zone
\omega r\ll 1
:

R_{11\omega}(r)\simeq\frac{-i\mathcal{N}}{\pi r^{2/5}}\Gamma(7/10)\bigg(\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{\omega}\bigg)^{7/10}\;,

(52)

where we have kept the most diverging term in the Hankel function, and
\mathcal{N}
is given by Eq. (
45
). On the other hand, in the screening region we have [from Eq. (
17
)]

\varphi^{\prime}_{\mathrm{SS}}=\left(\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}m_{\mathrm{NS}}\Lambda^{8}}{3\pi M_{\mathrm{P}}r^{2}}\right)^{1/5}\;.

(53)

Thus, the
r
-dependence of the multipole solution and of the near-zone solution in Eq. (
51
) is the same.

Therefore, one just needs to match the constant
\mathcal{A}_{11\omega}
. Let us factor out a delta function ensuring the matching of the time-dependent terms in Eq. (
51
),
\mathcal{A}_{11\omega}=\delta(\omega-\Omega)\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{11\Omega}
. We find

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{11\Omega}

\displaystyle=ie^{2i\pi/5}\sqrt{\frac{10}{3}}\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}m_{\mathrm{NS}}}{4\Gamma(7/10)M_{\mathrm{P}}}

\displaystyle\times r_{*}^{-4/5}\bigg(\frac{\Omega}{2\sqrt{5}}\bigg)^{7/10}\frac{m_{\mathrm{BH}}}{m_{\mathrm{BH}}+m_{\mathrm{NS}}}a\;,

(54)

which is the expression presented in Sec.
III.2
.

Appendix C
Solution for the scalar in different regimes

In this Appendix, we will study the behavior of the dipole scalar (
27
) in three different asymptotic zones, in order to explain the scaling observed in Fig.
2
: in the wave zone (
a\ll r\ll 2\pi/\Omega
); between the wave zone and the screening radius (
2\pi/\Omega\ll r\ll r_{*}
); and outside the screening radius (
r\gg r_{*}
).
Let us discuss separately these three cases. We will pay particular attention to the scaling of the field in each of these regimes, and check that our basic assumption
\varphi_{1}\ll\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}
is always valid as long as
r\gg a
, thus proving the existence of a buffer zone
a posteriori
.

C.1
a\ll r\ll 2\pi/\Omega

In this case, we have already determined the field in Eq. (
51
), so we have

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{11\Omega}R_{11\Omega}(r)\simeq-\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{3}}a\frac{m_{\mathrm{BH}}}{m_{\mathrm{NS}}+m_{\mathrm{BH}}}\bigg(\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}m_{\mathrm{NS}}\Lambda^{8}}{3\pi M_{\mathrm{P}}r^{2}}\bigg)^{1/5}\;.

(55)

Let us do a simple order-of-magnitude estimate, neglecting all order-one factors and setting
m_{\mathrm{BH}}/(m_{\mathrm{NS}}+m_{\mathrm{BH}})\simeq 1
. Normalizing the field to the background value of the scalar
\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}
, we obtain

\frac{\varphi_{1}}{\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}}\sim\frac{a}{r}\ll 1\;.

(56)

This is the standard result for a dipole field in the wave zone, and shows that our assumption
\varphi_{1}\ll\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}
is justified in this regime.

C.2
2\pi/\Omega\ll r\ll r_{*}

In this case, we can use Eq. (
39
) to obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{11\Omega}R_{11\Omega}(r)\simeq\frac{ie^{2i\pi/5}}{\Gamma(7/10)5^{7/10}2^{11/5}3^{1/2}}\frac{e^{i\Omega r/\sqrt{5}}}{r^{1/5}r_{*}^{8/5}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}m_{\mathrm{NS}}}{M_{\mathrm{P}}}a\frac{m_{\mathrm{BH}}}{m_{\mathrm{NS}}+m_{\mathrm{BH}}}\Omega^{1/5}\;.

(57)

The order-of-magnitude estimate gives

\frac{\varphi_{1}}{\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}}\sim a\bigg(\frac{\Omega}{r^{4}}\bigg)^{1/5}\sim v^{1/5}\bigg(\frac{a}{r}\bigg)^{4/5}\ll 1\;,

(58)

where we have introduced the typical velocity of the point particle
v=\Omega a
. Note that the two order-of-magnitude estimates in Eqs. (
56
) and (
58
) give the same value at the boundary of their respective domains,
r\sim 2\pi/\Omega
:
\varphi_{1}/\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}\sim\Omega a
. Note also that the estimate (
58
) implies that the dipole term continues to fall off with
r
more quickly than the background field
\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}
inside the screening radius, while the ratio
\varphi_{1}/\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}
would be a (small) constant for a FJBD field outside the near zone. This indicates that the field is probably screened outside the screening radius, as we will now confirm.

C.3
r\gg r_{*}

In this case, we use Eq. (
40
) to obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{11\Omega}R_{11\Omega}(r)\simeq ie^{2i\pi/5}\frac{5^{3/20}}{\Gamma(7/10)2^{17/10}3^{1/2}}\frac{e^{i\Omega(r-0.56r_{*})}}{r}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}m_{\mathrm{NS}}}{M_{\mathrm{P}}}\frac{\Omega^{1/5}}{r_{*}^{4/5}}a\frac{m_{\mathrm{BH}}}{m_{\mathrm{BH}}+m_{\mathrm{NS}}}\;,

(59)

where the factor of
0.56
comes from the numerical evaluation of the integral in Eq. (
41
).
An order-of-magnitude estimate gives

\frac{\varphi_{1}}{\varphi_{\mathrm{SS}}}\sim v^{1/5}\bigg(\frac{a}{r_{*}}\bigg)^{4/5}\ll 1\;,

(60)

where we have used the definition of the screening radius in Eq. (
19
). Note once again that the two order-of-magnitude estimates (
58
) and (
60
) agree at their common boundary
r\sim r_{*}
.
