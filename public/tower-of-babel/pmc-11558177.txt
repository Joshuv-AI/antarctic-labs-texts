The relationships between wanting to nap, actually napping, and depressed and anxious mood
Authors: Katherine Domar Ostrow, Lillian Shattuck, Martin Seehuus
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesThese studies disentangle the relationships between wanting to nap (nap desire), actually napping (nap behavior), and depressed and anxious mood. Study 1 partially replicated and extended findings connecting napping and depressed and anxious mood. Study 2 explored the distinction between nap desire and behavior using a new, larger sample and a different technique.MethodsStudy 1 used a longitudinal, multimethod approach to understand napping and mood among undergraduate students in the United States (N = 104). In Study 2, a cross-sectional survey was conducted on adults over 25 in the continental United States (N = 1406), including items from the DASS-21 and questions about nap desire and behavior.ResultsStudy 1 found a significant relationship between same-day napping behavior and depressed mood (Mnap = 1.61, SEnap = 0.08 vs. Mno nap = 1.44, SEno nap = 0.06, p = .018) but not anxious mood (p = .766). Study 2 partially replicated those findings; Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) showed that napping desire had a significant effect on anxious (F(1, 1291) = 6.86, p = .009, partial η2 = .005) and depressed mood (F(1, 1291) = 13.46, p < .001, partial η2 = .010), accounting for age, gender, and restedness, but napping behavior did not add to that effect.ConclusionsWanting to nap is related to greater depressed and anxious mood, but actual napping did not add to that relationship. These results have implications for clinicians using sleep assessment as a screening tool for mental health and highlight the need for further research on napping motivation.Keywords: napping, depression, anxiety, mental health

Statement of Significance

Wanting to take a nap (nap desire) is related to increased depressed and anxious mood, but actually taking a nap (nap behavior) does not add to that relationship. That is, nap behavior does not meaningfully change the relationship between nap desire and depressive or anxious symptoms. This suggests that nap desire is better than nap behavior as a screening tool for symptoms of depression or anxiety and suggests that future researchers should explore nap desire and motivation more thoroughly in order to understand the bidirectional relationships between napping and depressed or anxious mood.

Napping has a range of positive and negative effects on well-being, nighttime sleep, and mental health [1–4], but the distinction between wanting to take a nap and actually napping has not been explored. Such a distinction may speak to the mechanism by which napping (or wanting to nap) is connected to depressed or anxious mood and may be useful in screening for depressive and anxious disorders [5]. The studies described here first sought to replicate and extend the findings of the relationships between taking a nap (nap behavior) and depressed and anxious mood in college students, and then, as a follow-up study, to distinguish between the relationships among depressed and anxious mood, and (1) nap desire and (2) nap behavior. In Study 1, we examine the relationships between napping and symptoms of depression and anxiety in college students. In Study 2, we aimed to both replicate the findings of Study 1 in a larger, more diverse sample and to tease apart the incremental effect of nap behavior over and above the effect of nap desire.

The relationships between nighttime sleep and mood and anxiety disorders are well-established [6–11]. About half of those with anxiety experience insomnia [2], and at least that proportion of individuals experiencing a depressive episode report poor sleep quality [12]. Insomnia is the most prevalent sleep disorder [11, 13] though many other types of sleep problems (e.g. hypersomnia, nightmares, sleep apnea, and abnormalities found in sleep architecture and/or circadian rhythms) have also been associated with a wide range of disorders. To varying degrees, such disturbances have been linked to major depressive disorder (MDD) [1, 5], bipolar disorder[1], seasonal affective disorder [14], generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) [2–4], posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [2–4], panic disorder [2–4], obsessive-compulsive disorder [2–4], and simple and specific phobias [4].

Sleep difficulties, particularly insomnia, can predict the development of mood and anxiety disorders, as well as exacerbate concurrent clinical symptoms [1, 2, 15–17]. Among anxiety disorders, insomnia, nightmares, and other sleep issues are actually components of the diagnoses for GAD and PTSD [6]. There is significant overlap between treatments for anxiety, depression, and insomnia—the three may share common factors such as vulnerabilities to negative emotional states and may act as risk factors for each other, particularly in older adults [18].

Despite the wealth of evidence linking sleep to mental health, there is a noticeable gap in the literature in terms of napping. The existing evidence surrounding the effects of napping behavior in the general population is mixed [1–3], although most of the research on college students suggests that napping is problematic in that population, possibly due to its prevalence among college students and the inconsistent sleep patterns common in that age group [12, 15, 16, 19]. Patterns that are commonly observed in college students, such as frequent napping or napping close to normal nighttime sleep onset, have been associated with increased sleep fragmentation, poorer nighttime sleep quality, and an increased risk for more severe sleep deprivation [10, 14]. Furthermore, nap motivation may account for at least some of that effect [4].

In the present set of studies, we first (Study 1) sought to replicate and clarify the established relationship between nap behavior and mental health in college students. Given unexpected post hoc findings from Study 1, Study 2 accomplished two goals. First, it conducted a replication of the exploratory results of Study 1 in a larger and more diverse sample, and second, it tested the incremental effect of nap behavior over and above nap desire with a different population and methodology.

Sleep, depression, and anxiety in college students

In general, poor sleep in college students is related to depression/depressive symptoms and anxiety/anxious symptoms [17, 18, 20, 21]. Dinis and Bragança reported in their review [22] that there are strong associations between sleep quality and hygiene and depression and depressive symptoms among college students, but also noted that there are mixed findings regarding the directionality of these relationships, with only some studies reporting bidirectionality. Indeed, college students with sleep disturbance have reported significantly greater levels of depression (though not always) and anxiety [17], and those with more severe depression [23] and anxiety [20, 23] have exhibited worse sleep quality. It should be noted that some of these more recent studies were conducted in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, which saw an increase in the prevalence of depression and anxiety in college students [24] as well as poorer sleep [25] in this population. It is also worth noting that most of these studies relied on subjective measures of sleep (i.e. self-report, as opposed to the gold standard of polysomnography or other objective sleep measures such as actigraphy).

There has been substantial research into several factors that may underlie or otherwise influence the links between sleep and depression and anxiety in this population. One such factor is chronotype, which describes one’s degree of “morningness” versus “eveningness”; in other words, it captures circadian preferences. Not only is a shift toward eveningness (i.e. staying up and waking up later) observed during adolescence [26], but a host of evidence has associated this particular chronotype with poorer sleep quality and depressive and/or anxious symptomology in college students [26–28]. Evening chronotype seems to be related to longer nap duration [29] and positive emotional benefits [30] from napping, but otherwise, little work has been done on chronotype and napping. Furthermore, a network analysis modeling connections between symptoms of sleep disturbance, depression, and anxiety found sleep dissatisfaction, poor sleep quality, and uncontrollable worry to have the highest strength values for node centrality, while sleep, guilt, restlessness, irritability, and feeling afraid were found to be the strongest bridge symptoms [21]. These results highlight the meaningful overlap in these three conditions as well as the transdiagnostic potential of targeting sleep in the treatment of various disorders, specifically among those in college. The use of mobile devices [31] as well as substances such as tobacco [23] also connect sleep, depression, and anxiety in college students.

Napping

In general, males (46.1%) tend to engage in napping behavior more than females (36.9%), and people with chronic conditions such as obesity, hypertension, diabetes, depression, and anxiety are more likely to take a nap than healthy individuals [32]. Although napping may have beneficial effects on the cognitive, stress, and immune systems, there is also evidence that napping behavior can have deleterious effects, specifically in longer, evening, and afternoon naps [2]. Furthermore, sleep inertia, a potential harmful consequence of napping behavior, is associated with disorientation and a decline in cognitive performance following naps exceeding 30 minutes [2]. Owusu and colleagues [33] asked participants aged 65 and below to self-report past-month napping intention (whether the nap was intentional or unintentional), napping frequency, and average nap duration. They found nap duration and frequency, as well as the intentionality or motivation of the nap—that is, whether the nap was a conscious and explicit choice—to be associated with cognitive performance [33]. Mednick’s recent editorial [34] further emphasizes the importance of understanding nap motivation, past the simple report of nap behavior. Regarding nap duration, there is evidence of a bidirectional relationship between napping and nighttime sleep. For example, a later bedtime is associated with longer naps and later naps that same day, as well as the day after [11]. A longer duration of nighttime sleep is associated with having taken a nap that day, and a shorter duration of nighttime sleep is associated with increased odds of napping the next day and taking an earlier nap the following day [11]. The implications of these results should be further examined.

The state of college student napping

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends that adults get at least 7 hours of sleep each night, with teenagers needing another hour [35, 36]; college students seem to be just barely achieving even the lower goal [37, 38]. There have been many concerning findings regarding the sleep habits and quality of college students [38–40]. A review by Wang and Bíró [15] identified several determinants of poor sleep quality in college students, including lifestyle (sedentary behavior, caffeine/stimulants, media use habits, etc.), mental health (depression, psychiatric disorders, perceived stress, anxiety), social (racial discrimination, academic performance), and physical (pain, fatigue) factors. Poor sleep quality in college students has been linked to a number of negative outcomes, both academically [41, 42] and in terms of mental and physical health [38, 40].

Napping is less studied among college students even though napping is common in this age group, with studies suggesting around half of college students nap at least weekly [4, 12, 16, 37]. While daytime sleepiness seems to be the most common reason students choose to nap [4, 37], Duggan and colleagues [4] identified five major categories of reasons that students engage in this behavior in their DREAM model: dysregulative, restorative, emotional, appetitive, and mindful. They only found emotional reasons for napping behavior to be related to lower well-being, and women in their sample were more likely to endorse these reasons than men. On the other hand, appetitive reasons were significantly related to better nighttime sleep quality. Similarly, Du et al. [43] posited the I-DREAM model which consists of six factors for napping motivation in college students—induced, dysregulative, restorative, exercise naps, appetitive, and mindful—with differing effects on napping patterns and nighttime sleep. These important findings suggest that motivation for napping behavior may be at least as important as nap behavior in determining the impact of a nap on nighttime sleep and mental health.

Sleep measurement

Study 1 was part of a larger study exploring multimodal assessment of sleep, including subjective and objective measures, such as daily self-report of sleep, retrospective sleep diaries, standardized survey instruments, and actigraphy [44–46]. Few studies have used a multimethod approach to sleep measurement in adolescents or college students. An exception is Doane and colleagues’ longitudinal study [47], which evaluated high school seniors’ sleep via actigraphy, sleep diary, and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [48] in relation to depressive and anxious symptoms; these measures were repeated during their first semester of college, and once more during their second semester, which found significant relationships between subjective sleep and anxiety symptoms.

The present studies

We conducted two studies on two distinct populations—college students and adults older than college-aged. The first study, with college students at a small liberal arts college in the northeastern United States, was largely exploratory. Data were collected via in-person surveys, ActiGraph wGT3X-BT [49] activity and sleep monitoring wearable devices that participants wore for a week, and daily momentary ecological assessment-style text-message multiple-choice questions about how much they slept the previous night, if they took any naps, and two questions assessing anxiety and depressive symptoms. We sought to replicate previous findings around the relationships between napping, sleep, and mental health [7, 13, 50] and to compare methods of sleep measurement: actigraphy [49, 51], ecological momentary assessment [52], retrospective sleep diaries [53], and validated instruments [54].

Two factors drove the design of the second study. First, an exploratory analysis conducted in Study 1 looking at the difference in the relationships between nap behavior and anxious mood and nap behavior and depressed mood, produced unexpected results that merited further exploration; second, around 10 participants commented casually to the researchers after the end of the study that they had wanted to take a nap but could not for logistical reasons, which raised an interesting and related research question. That is, expanding on the findings of Duggan et al. [4] and Du et al. [43], we sought to measure the incremental effect of nap behavior over and above nap desire. Study 2 was developed to test these hypotheses in a fundamentally different sample—adults over the age of 25, cross-sectionally collected from across the United States over the course of 7 days.

Study 1

Study 1 was a longitudinal study comparing the relationship between multiple modes of sleep assessment (actigraphy, daily self-report, validated measure, and retrospective sleep diary) and anxious and depressed mood. As part of the sleep measurement, participants were asked in a T0 survey how many days per week they typically napped. A post hoc, exploratory analysis was conducted to confirm the previous finding that napping was associated with increased levels of depressive and anxious symptoms [5, 32, 55, 56]. This was not one of the original hypotheses of the study.

Study 1 Materials and Methods

The study was approved by the senior author’s Institutional Review Board before to start. Participants (N = 104) were recruited from both a psychology student participant pool and the general population of a small liberal arts college in the United States from Fall 2022 through Fall 2023. Participants provided informed consent in person before the start of data collection. For the 7-night longitudinal study, participants completed an initial survey and were fitted with an activity monitoring watch [49, 57] at T0 which they were instructed to wear for the entire duration of the study, including while they slept in their place of residence. For T1 − T6, at 11:00 am, each morning participants reported the duration and timing of their previous night’s sleep. At 7:00 pm, they reported on their within-day nap behavior, and intensity of depressed and anxious mood via response to text messages. At T7, participants returned the watches and completed another survey and a retrospective sleep diary. Participants received course credit or were entered into a raffle to win one of a set of prizes (e.g. video projector, gift cards) worth a combined total of $350 USD. The analyses reported here focus on the relationship between daily napping and daily reports of depressive and anxious symptoms.

Participants.

Of the 104 participants who completed the initial T0 survey, 96 completed the final T7 survey, and one participant did not provide basic demographic information, leaving a usable sample of N = 95. Women made up 54.74% (n = 52) of the sample, men 41.05% (n = 39), and trans or gender diverse (TGD) 4.21% (n = 4). Participants reported their race as Asian (n = 16, 16.84%), Black (n = 6, 6.32%), White (n = 62, 65.26%), and other or more than one (n = 11, 11.58%). Age ranged from 18 to 24, with a mean of 19.22 and SD = 1.23. Days per week napping ranged from 0 to 7, mean = 1.56, SD = 1.48.

Measures and procedure.

Participants were asked about their age, race, and gender identity at T0. Nap frequency (“On average, how many days per week do you take at least one nap during the day?” and “On days that you do nap, how many naps do you tend to take on that day?”), nap duration (“On average, how many minutes total do you spend napping per day”), and subjective experience of napping (with options such as, “I tend to feel rested when I wake up from a nap” and “On days when I nap, I tend to have better sleep quality that night”) were also assessed in this initial survey via multiple-choice questions. Descriptive statistics are reported above. At T1 through T7, participants were asked, via Likert-style questions, about nap duration (“How long (total) have you napped for today?”), from 0 minutes (no nap) to more than 2 hours. This question was dichotomized to become an assessment of whether each participant napped on a given day (represented as nap = 1; 25% of responses) or not (represented as nap = 0; 75% of responses). Across the 7 days of the study, n = 57 (60.00%) of participants napped at least once, leaving n = 38 (40.00%) who did not nap at all.

At T1 through T7, participants were also asked, via Likert-style questions, (1) “How anxious do you feel right now?,” with answers from not at all anxious (0) to very anxious (4), and (2) “How depressed do you feel right now?,” with answers from not at all depressed (0) to very depressed (4).

Study 1 Results

Two different approaches were taken to analyze the relationship between depressed and anxious mood and napping behavior; see Figure 1 for an illustration of both analyses. In the first, the average of the daily scores for depressed mood and those for anxious mood were compared between participants who napped at least once and those who did not nap at all. This analysis looks at the relationship between napping behavior and depressed and anxious symptoms across the entire week but does not assess any within-day effects. The detection of a significant difference in this analysis would suggest that the relationship between napping behavior and depressed or anxious mood spans multiple days within a single week. The within-participant average of the daily scores for depressed mood was not significantly different between those who napped at least once, M = 1.55, SD = 0.62, and those who did not nap at all, M = 1.41, SD = 0.46, t(90) = −1.15, p = .26. The same was true for anxious mood, with those who napped, M = 1.87, SD = 0.60, not differing from those who did not, M = 1.88, SD = 0.65, t(90) = 0.03, p = .98.

Study 1: the effect of napping on depressed and anxious mood.

The second approach to analyzing the relationship between napping behavior and depressed or anxious mood was to analyze the connection between a self-reported nap and self-reported depressed and anxious symptoms on the same day. The detection of a significant difference between nappers and nonnappers in this analysis would suggest that napping behavior was related to depressed or anxious mood within the same day that the nap occurred. To assess the within-day relationship between napping behavior and depressed or anxious mood while accounting for the nonindependence of repeated measures over the course of the week, linear mixed-effects (LME) models were estimated. We chose to use LME for this analysis because the LME framework is highly flexible, accounts for the nonindependence of repeated measures, and allows for a relatively straightforward interpretation. The model indicated a significant main effect between same-day napping behavior and depressed mood, F(1, 463.02) = 5.68, p = .018, but not anxious mood, F(1, 476.78) = 0.09, p = .766. The estimated marginal means show that the relationship between napping behavior and depressed mood was significant (Mnap = 1.61, SEnap = 0.08 vs. Mno nap = 1.44, SEno nap = 0.06, p = .018) but not large, and the relationship between napping behavior and anxious mood was not significant (Mnap = 1.85, SEnap = 0.10 vs. Mno nap = 1.88, SEno nap = 0.07, p = .77).

Study 1 Discussion

These post hoc analyses suggest that napping behavior is associated with greater depressive symptoms but not greater anxious symptoms. These findings are informative in light of the well-established relationship linking depression and anxiety [58, 59] and may reflect that, while depression and anxiety can be conceptualized as different manifestations of a fundamental issue with emotion regulation [60, 61], they differ in their relationship to sleep [62]. While the DSM-5-TR diagnostic criteria include sleep disruption and fatigue for both MDD and GAD, anxiety is generally associated with a higher state of systemic arousal than depression [11, 63, 64], which may potentially interfere with the ability to nap (for instance, if one is worried being able to fall asleep and sleeping too long). Along this vein, mood disorders appear to be associated with hypersomnolence more so than other types of psychiatric disorders [65, 66], which may help explain why napping behavior was related to only depressive symptoms in the present sample. The distinction between depressed and anxious mood is of potential interest in terms of understanding the mechanisms involved in the relationships and as a potential differential screening question.

There are a number of important limitations, beyond the exploratory nature of this analysis, that suggest caution in this interpretation. Spontaneous poststudy comments from some (~10) participants suggested that their napping desire and napping behavior did not always coincide; that is, they sometimes wanted to nap but did not. Nap desire was not measured in this study, and thus, we are unable to distinguish between nap behavior and nap desire. While this study was intended to measure the relationship between napping and anxious and depressed mood, a more thorough examination that includes chronotype; sleep patterns, behavior, and quality; and sleep consistency would have allowed for a more nuanced analysis. In addition, this study has a relatively small sample size, used single-item measures, and was limited to relatively young college students. Thus, these results, while interesting, are of limited generalizability.

Study 2

The results of Study 1 suggest that napping is differentially related to anxious and depressed mood, but those results are limited by the exploratory nature of the analyses, the restricted age range of the sample, and the lack of a distinction between wanting to nap (nap desire) and actually napping (nap behavior). Study 2 was designed to address some of those limitations by extending the analysis in two ways: first, with the use of a larger, older sample, recruited using a different method, and second, by refining the napping questions to allow for assessment of the relationship between depressed and anxious mood and (1) nap desire, (2) nap behavior, and (3) the incremental influence of nap behavior given nap desire. Consistent with Study 1 and the literature reviewed in the primary Introduction, which suggests that napping is associated with more anxious and depressed mood, we hypothesized that: (H1) participants who expressed nap desire, whether they napped or not, would report more depressed and anxious mood; (H2) participants who took a nap (nap behavior) would report more depressed and anxious mood; and (H3) the incremental effect of nap behavior over nap desire would be significant, positive, and greater for depressed than anxious mood. That is, in H3, we sought to measure the effect of wanting to take a nap over and above the effect of actually taking a nap.

Study 2 Materials and Methods

An online cross-sectional survey was conducted through Prolific [67] in mid-January 2024, recruiting 200 participants per day for 7 days, for a targeted total of N = 1400 and an actual total of N = 1406. Participants were offered $1.50 USD to complete the survey, which had a median completion time of 4 minutes 28 seconds. The survey was made available at the same time every day (4:00 PM EST) and locked at the same time every day (7:00 PM EST). This was to make it more likely that the participants who had wanted to nap that day had had the chance to do so.

Participants.

The goals of Study 2 included replicating and clarifying the findings of Study 1 and extending those findings to a broader population. To that end, participants for Study 2 were limited to those 25 years and older. Furthermore, participants were restricted to those in the continental United States, to restrict the time of day during which participants completed the survey.

A total of N = 1406 participants completed the survey. The quality of participant responses was assessed in three independent ways. Participant data quality was assessed using Google’s Recaptcha [68] and Imperium’s RelevantID [69] as implemented by Qualtrics [70]. Participants who scored less than 0.5 on the Recaptcha bot detection test had a RelevantID Fraud score of 30 or more, or who failed a RelevantID Duplicate test (n = 32) were excluded from the study. There were also two attention check questions [71, 72], and participants who failed both questions (n = 2) were excluded. Finally, participants were asked for their age at the start of the survey and their year of birth at the end, and the two were compared. Participants who did not provide an age or year of birth (n = 5) or for whom their age was not feasible given their year of birth (n = 47) were excluded. Participants who failed one quality check were likely to fail others; a total of n = 91 (6.48%) participants were excluded for failing at least one of the five data quality checks, leaving N = 1315. The resulting participants were evenly distributed across days of the week, varying from a minimum of n = 179 valid responses on Thursday to a maximum n = 193 on Friday. Some significant differences by gender identification were observed; see Table 1 for demographics by gender.

Measures.

Demographics

Participants were asked their age, race, gender identity, and socioeconomic status [73]. Descriptive statistics are reported above.

Depressed and anxious mood

Participants completed the 14 items from the 21-item form of the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21) [74], answering only the items for the depression and anxiety subscales. The subscales used consist of 14 items including “I found it difficult to work up the initiative to do things,” (depression) and “I felt scared without any good reason,” (anxiety). Subscale scores range from 0 to 42, with higher scores meaning more distress. Both subscales show adequate internal reliability at α > .80 and Ω > .80 for both scales.

Napping

Respondents were asked “Did you take a nap today?” and “How often do you take naps, generally speaking?” (nap behavior). Those who indicated that they did not take a nap were asked “Did you want to take a nap today?” (nap desire). For those with nap desire but not nap behavior, participants were asked “Why didn’t you take a nap?” with a select all that apply option. See Table 2 for variables of interest by napping status. This structure of questions allows several comparisons to be made, including (H1) comparing those with and without nap desire, (H2) comparing those with and without nap behavior, and (H3) measuring the incremental effect of nap behavior over and above the effect of nap desire. The structure explicitly assumes that those who took a nap wanted to take a nap. Please see Study 2 limitations for further discussion.

Sleep and restedness

Participants indicated, via single-item measures, how long they had slept the night before (from less than 2 hours to more than 12 hours, in 30 minutes increments) and how rested they felt via Likert scale from one (not at all rested) to four (very rested).

Procedure

The study was approved by the senior author’s Institutional Review Board before to start. Participants were recruited through Prolific, with survey visibility limited to the inclusion criteria specified above (at least 25 years old, continental United States). Participants could only complete one survey day (e.g. if a participant had completed the Tuesday survey, the Wednesday—Sunday surveys would not be visible to that participant). Participants completed informed consent and the survey online through Qualtrics, and were compensated ($1.50 USD) through Prolific.

The effect of wanting to nap and the incremental effect of actually napping on depressed and anxious mood.

The relationship between depressed and anxious mood and nap desire and nap behavior.

H1 and H2 were addressed using two distinct techniques. First, simple t-tests were conducted to compare the relationships between nap desire and nap behavior, and depressed and anxious mood. Second, to allow for the inclusion of relevant covariates, four bootstrapped ANCOVAs were estimated with (1) nap desire or (2) nap behavior as predictor variables, and (1) anxious mood or (2) depressed mood as the dependent variable. For all four ANCOVAs, restedness, age, and socioeconomic status (SES) were included as covariates. While the previous night’s sleep duration was measured as part of the larger study, restedness and sleep duration were so strongly correlated that they could not both be included in the same model. We determined that restedness was more appropriate for inclusion in the model, since subjective restedness is more likely to be related to napping. Note that bootstrapping was used for all four ANCOVA models because the Breusch–Pagan–Koenker tests for each model suggested that each model had significant heteroscedasticity. Models A1, describing the relationship between anxious mood and nap desire and covariates, BP(4) = 62.09, p < .001, and A2, describing the relationship between depressed mood and nap desire and covariates, BP(4) = 90.39, p < .001 both showed significant heteroscedasticity, as did models B1, describing the relationship between anxious mood and nap desire and covariates, BP(4) = 91.71, p < .001, and B2, describing the relationship between depressed mood and nap desire and covariates, BP(4) = 91.73, p < .001.

T-tests without covariates.

The t-tests showed a significant relationship between nap desire and anxious mood, t(1294) = −5.30, p < .001, d = −0.30, with those with nap desire reporting higher levels of anxious mood, M = 7.75, SD = 7.04, than those without nap desire, M = 5.71, SD = 6.75. The incremental effect of nap behavior over and above nap desire.

Two bootstrapped ANCOVAs—one examining depressed mood and one examining anxious mood—were conducted to measure the incremental effect of nap behavior over and above nap desire. Note that bootstrapping was used for both ANCOVAs, given that the results of Breusch–Pagan–Koenker [75, 76] tests for homoscedasticity for both depressed mood, BP(4) = 37.36, p < .001, and anxious mood, BP(4) = 28.62, p < .001 suggest that the underlying data do not meet the assumptions of the more traditional ANCOVA.

The ANCOVA examining depression symptom scores in relation to napping behavior (0,1), age, SES, and morning restedness did not detect a significant difference in depressive symptom scores between participants with both napping desire and napping behavior and participants with only napping desire, F(1, 607) = 2.15, p = .134, η2 = .004. The covariates of age (p < .001), SES (p < .001), and morning restedness (p < .001) were all significantly related to depressed mood, with a total R2 = .210.

The ANCOVA examining anxiety symptom scores in relation to napping behavior (0,1), age, SES, and morning restedness did not detect a significant difference in anxiety symptom scores between participants with both napping desire and napping behavior and participants with only napping behavior, F(1, 607) = 0.260, p = .610. The covariates of age (p < .001), SES (p < .001), and morning restedness (p < .001) were all significantly related to anxious mood, with a total R2 = .11.

The results of both ANCOVAs suggest that nap behavior is not related to a change in neither depressed nor anxious mood, but that nap desire is.

Study 2 Discussion

The first two hypotheses were supported, but the third, more complex hypothesis, was not. The first two hypotheses involved simple comparisons of nap desire versus no nap desire (H1) and napping versus not napping (H2), without estimating the effect of nap behavior over and above nap desire.

Both while accounting for the relevant covariates (age, SES, and restedness; see above and Table 4) and using simple t-tests, participants with nap desire reported significantly higher levels of depressive and anxious symptomology than those without nap desire (H1), and participants with nap behavior reported significantly higher levels of depressive and anxious symptomology than those who did not nap (H2). The third hypothesis was not supported. No significant effect of nap behavior was observed after accounting for nap desire. That is, while wanting to take a nap was associated with worse anxious and depressed mood, whether or not one actually takes a nap is not, and this effect (or lack thereof) persists even when accounting for how rested participants felt that morning (among other covariates).

This pattern of results (1) suggests that the desire to nap is related to depressed and anxious mood, even after considering the effect of restedness, and (2) highlights the value of nap desire as distinct from actual nap behavior. Although other interpretations cannot be ruled out, it would appear that for those with nap desire, actually napping during the day may well be a function of logistics more than either restedness or mental health burden. Nap desire, however, appears to be indicative of both low restedness and, separately, increased mental health burden. It is feasible that those who want to nap but are not able to experience more incremental distress because they experience greater discordance between their desire and their behavior. Furthermore, as naps under certain conditions (i.e. depending on duration, time of day, etc.) can have beneficial effects on mood and stress [77], those with napping desire only may suffer the consequences of not receiving this boost in effect and subjective well-being: that is, greater depressive and/or anxious symptomology. While these results do not clarify the mechanism of action involved, they do suggest that clinicians who are interested in assessing or screening for depressed or anxious mood via sleep behavior should ask about nap desire and not nap behavior.

Discussion

The hypotheses of both studies were partially supported. Consistent with previous work [5, 78], nap behavior was found to be positively related to depressed mood (Study 1) and anxious and depressed mood (Study 2), and similar relationships were observed for nap desire (Study 2). However, Study 2 allowed for a more subtle and novel analysis: the incremental effect of nap behavior over and above nap desire while accounting for important covariates such as restedness. That is, is actually taking a nap related to depression and anxiety, or is the effect found in wanting to take a nap? Study 2 suggests that the primary way in which depression and anxiety and napping are related is through nap desire, not actual nap behavior, even after accounting for the influence of restedness. This is consistent with the logical observation that taking a nap requires the means and opportunity to take a nap, while nap desire speaks solely to the motivation for taking a nap. Thus, the relationship between nap desire and nap behavior is moderated by a large number of idiosyncratic factors, including (but not limited to) employment flexibility, child care, ambient noise and light, and personal experience with napping.

These findings also suggest that the actual act of napping—having a short period of sleep temporally distinct from the usual, longer stretch of sleep—is not, in itself, strongly causally connected to a depressed or anxious mood. If nap behavior produced a same-day increase in depressed or anxious mood, the incremental influence of nap behavior would have been significant and positive. These results do not rule out a longer-term causal chain. Since both studies were about within-day experiences, it remains possible that napping behavior has an effect on depression or anxiety by altering sleep behavior over a longer period of time.

The results of Study 2 are also consistent with the difference in the relationship between nap behavior and depressed and anxious mood observed in Study 1. While the difference is small, the effect sizes observed in the models predicting anxiety (0.139, 0.136) are smaller than in the models predicting depression (0.215, 0.236). This is consistent with the preliminary Study 1 finding that there was a stronger relationship between nap behavior and depressed mood than between nap behavior and anxious mood. Given that the absolute difference in effect sizes is relatively small, it is premature to conclude that this difference is of diagnostic or screening utility, but future researchers may wish to test this relationship directly.

These studies have important limitations that should be considered when interpreting these findings. Study 1 was limited by sample make-up and size, with a relatively small group of college students. Study 2 used a larger and older sample but was collected online. While participants recruited through Prolific and similar organizations are broadly similar to the general population [67], differences remain possible, particularly around the availability of time to nap among those who work from home or have multiple gig positions. We did not assess napping behavior objectively in Study 2 as we did in Study 1 (with actigraphy), so it is not possible to assess the accuracy of the sample’s self-reports of napping behavior from Study 2. Study 2 also makes the assumption that everyone who napped wanted to—that is, we did not assess for the possibility of an involuntary nap. Across both studies, we did not control for nighttime sleep characteristics such as sleep environment (e.g. college dormitory vs. off-campus housing in Study 1, urban vs. rural in Study 2), chronotype, or other health-related factors such as substance use and diet, all of which may reasonably affect nighttime and/or daytime sleep quality, patterns, etc. Finally, the cross-sectional design of Study 2 in particular restrains us from being able to establish bidirectional links between napping behavior, napping desire, and depressive and anxious symptoms.

Conclusion

Future research on the mental health effects of napping should distinguish carefully between nap desire and nap behavior, particularly while accounting for the influence of restedness, with the goal of replicating and refining these results. We further advise future researchers to follow the path set by Duggan et al. [4], Du et al. [43], and Mednick [4] by exploring the motivation for napping. While very early thought suggested that sleep could be an escape mechanism [79], modern researchers have largely not directly tested the role that sleep plays as an avoidance behavior, although the connections between coping behaviors and sleep are better explored [80–82]. Furthermore, these results support a recommendation for clinicians interested in using sleep assessment as a screening tool for mental health: ask about whether your patient wanted to nap, not whether, or not they did nap.
