Temperature-dependent infectious stability of Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus
Authors: Samuel NYAMPONG, Miyuka NISHIZATO, Weiyin HU, Hiroshi SHIMODA, Daisuke HAYASAKA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV) is an emerging tick-borne zoonotic pathogen of increasing concern in East Asia, including Japan. Not only humans but also domestic cats are highly susceptible and frequently develop severe disease. Despite its public and veterinary health significance, data on the environmental stability of SFTSV remain limited. In this study, we evaluated the stability profiles of SFTSV under various temperature and inactivation conditions. As comparative controls, Kabuto Mountain virus (KAMV) which belongs to the same class and order as SFTSV, Japanese Encephalitis virus (JEV) representing a different type of enveloped virus, and Feline Calicivirus (FCV) representing an example of non-enveloped virus, were also tested. SFTSV showed higher infectious stability at 4°C, 23°C and 37°C compared to KAMV, JEV and FCV. In addition, SFTSV demonstrated comparatively higher resistance to heat inactivation at 56°C and 94°C. On the other hand, SFTSV was easily inactivated by ethanol, hypochlorite and ultraviolet irradiation. These findings provide important insights into the environmental persistence and inactivation requirements of SFTSV, suggesting that appropriate inactivation procedures are important to reduce the risk of SFTSV infection from the contaminated samples at clinical sites and laboratories.Keywords: inactivation, infectious stability, Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus, temperature-dependence

INTRODUCTION

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) virus (SFTSV) is an emerging tick-borne pathogen that first emerged in China [50]. Currently, it is known that SFTSV is distributed in East to Southeast Asian countries, including Japan, South Korea, Vietnam, and Taiwan [21, 26, 43, 44]. Clinical manifestations of SFTS in human cases include fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, and multi-organ failure, with a fatality rate of as high as 30% [22, 27]. SFTSV is classified within the family Phenuiviridae, genus Bandavirus, and species Bandavirus dabieense. It is an enveloped virus with a genome composed of three RNA segments: S, M, and L [25, 53]. SFTSV is transmitted by ticks such as Haemaphysalis longicornis and Amblyomma testudinarium [51,52,53], and virus infections typically occur through tick bites, and human-to-human transmission from SFTS cases has also been reported [10, 13].

SFTSV-antibody-positive animals have been reported, including wild boar, deer, goats, cattle, pigs, and chickens [8, 31, 42]. Of note, SFTS cases have been identified in cats, dogs, and cheetahs [29, 30, 33, 38]. In particular, numerous cat cases have been documented, and they appear to be highly susceptible to SFTSV infection, with mortality rates of up to 60% [30]. Notably, infectious SFTSV and viral genomes have been detected in body fluids such as saliva, tears, and urine from SFTS cases [23, 45, 49], raising the risk of infection from animals infected with SFTSV, especially for veterinarians, veterinary nurses and pet owners. Therefore, to reduce the risk of this zoonotic infection, implementing prevention measures is crucial in veterinary practice, including safe handling of animals, the use of personal protective equipment, and proper disinfection and sterilization. In addition, understanding the environmental stability of SFTSV and its susceptibility to inactivation is key for infection control in animal hospitals. Previous studies showed that SFTSV was stable at relatively high temperatures [15], suggesting that the infectious status of the virus may be stable at temperature-dependent conditions and resistant to heat inactivation.

In this study, we examined the infectious stability of SFTSV across a range of temperatures from as low as 4°C to as high as 94°C. Additionally, we evaluated the inactivation effects of ethanol, sodium hypochlorite and ultraviolet (UV) irradiation. Our results provide valuable information on preventive measures for SFTSV based on its temperature-dependent stability.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cells and viruses

SFTSV Tk-F123 strain was isolated from cat serum in Tokushima [48]. Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV) YD-1 strain, isolated from a mosquito collected in Yamaguchi, Japan, was kindly provided by Ken Maeda of Japan Institute for Health Security [24]. Kabuto mountain virus (KAMV) Nag strain was isolated from Haemaphysalis flava collected in Hirado, Nagasaki, Japan (unpublished results). Feline calicivirus (FCV) was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. SFTSV and KAMV were propagated in Vero E6 cell lines, JEV in baby hamster kidney (BHK)-21, FCV in Crandell-Reese feline kidney (CRFK) cell lines. Supernatants from virus-infected cells were harvested after 2 days for JEV, 4 days for KAMV and FCV, and 5 days for SFTSV, prior to the appearance of significant cytopathic effects, and viral stocks were prepared. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Serena Europe GmbH, Pessin, Germany) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan), and maintained at 37°C in 5% CO2. Experiments with infectious SFTSV were conducted in a biosafety level (BSL)-3 laboratory at Yamaguchi University, following standard BSL-3 guidelines. KAMV, JEV, and FCV experiments were carried out in a BSL-2 laboratory.

Focus Forming Assay (FFA)

SFTSV infectious titers were determined by FFA as previously described [16]. Briefly, confluent VeroE6 cells were inoculated with serially diluted culture supernatants of the SFTSV stock and incubated with overlaid 2% FBS DMEM containing 1% methylcellulose (MC) (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for 3 days. The viral foci were detected using SFTSV antiserum, peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (American Qualex, San Clemente, CA, USA), and DAB substrate (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd.). Viral titers were expressed as ffu/mL.

Plaque Forming Assay (PFA)

Infectious titers of KAMV, JEV, and FCV were measured by PFA [17]. Briefly, serially diluted viruses were inoculated into each confluent cell culture and incubated for 90 min; thereafter, 1% MC DMEM was applied. Plaques were visualized with 1% crystal violet (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp.). Viral titers were expressed as pfu/mL.

Mice experiments

Type-I interferon receptor KO (A129) mice were purchased from B&K Universal Limited and mated at Yamaguchi University, Japan. Twenty-five mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 105 ffu of SFTSV. The mice were euthanized at 3 days post-infection (dpi) and blood samples were collected and pooled. The animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Fundamental Guidelines for the Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments and Related Activities in Academic Research Institutions under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology. The Animal Care and Use Committee of Yamaguchi University approved all the experimental protocols (approval number: 05-31-565).

Virus incubation at different temperatures

Each virus was diluted to 5 × 105 ffu/mL or pfu/mL in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS, and 0.25 mL of each virus aliquot was incubated at 4°C, 23°C, 37°C, 56°C and 94°C in polypropylene tubes. Pooled blood samples were aliquoted into 0.15 mL portions in tubes. Samples stored at 4°C, 23°C and 37°C were collected daily by 7 dpi, and those at 56°C and 94°C were collected after 1, 10, 30 and 60 min. Retrieved samples were stored at −80°C until titration. All experiments were conducted in duplicate with three independent repetitions.

Inactivation of SFTSV by ethanol and sodium hypochlorite

Each virus was mixed with 70% ethanol (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd.) or 100 ppm stable sodium hypochlorite (Wish-s-SH, Chappy Co., Ltd., Yamaguchi, Japan) at the ratio of 1:9 and incubated for 1, 5 and 10 min. Retrieved samples were stored at −80°C until use, and viral titers were determined by FFA. All experiments were conducted in duplicate with three independent experiments.

UV irradiation of SFTSV

UV irradiation was performed as previously described [39]. Briefly, virus solutions were placed on a 50-mm dish (Sumitomo Bakelite Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and irradiated for 4, 8, 12 and 20 sec (254 nm UV-CCL), 7, 14, 21 and 28 sec (265 nm DUV-LED), and 15, 30, 45 and 60 sec (275 nm DUV-LED) (Stanley Electric Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). UV intensity (mW/cm2) was measured using a chemical actinometer; KI/KIO3 [36], and calculated with the Lambert-Beer law [3]. All experiments were performed in duplicate with three independent experiments.

RESULTS

We first examined the infectious stability of SFTSV at 4°C, 23°C and 37°C, simulating conditions representative of temperatures throughout the year. These temperatures also represent typical refrigerator, room and incubator for cell culture, respectively. As comparative control belonging to the same class Bunyaviricetes, order Harenavirals as SFTSV, KAMV (family Phenuiviridae, genus Uukuvirus) was tested. In addition, JEV (family Flaviviridae, genus Orthoflavivirus) and FCV (family Caliciviridae, genus Vesivirus) were used as representatives of a different enveloped virus and a non-enveloped virus, respectively.

At 4°C, infectious titers of SFTSV and JEV showed almost no decrease over 7 days, while titers of KAMV and FCV decreased by 1.0 and 1.26 log ffu/mL (differences relative to the titers of day 0 controls) after 7 days, respectively (Fig. 1). At 23°C, SFTSV experienced a slight decrease with a 1.73 log ffu/mL reduction after 7 days (Fig. 1). JEV showed a 2.36 log ffu/mL reduction after 7 days (Fig. 1). In contrast, KAMV and FCV markedly reduced their titers to below detection limits by 5 days and 2 days, respectively (Fig. 1). At 37°C, SFTSV declined gradually, but its titer remained at 2.71 log ffu/mL after 7 days (Fig. 1). On the other hand, infectious titers of KAMV, JEV and FCV decreased significantly to below detection limits by 3 days, 2 days and 1 day, respectively (Fig. 1).

Approximately 1.25 × 105 ffu/mL of each virus in 0.25 mL of 2% FCS MEM were incubated at these temperatures, with samples collected daily. Viral titers were measured as ffu/mL (SFTSV) or pfu/mL (KAMV, JEV, FCV). Control viral titers (day 0) were 105.34 ffu/mL (SFTSV), 104.74 pfu/mL (KAMV), 105.03 pfu/mL (JEV) and 105.03 pfu/mL (FCV). Each data was obtained from three independent experiments at the indicated time points. Error bars represent standard errors. The dotted line indicates the detection limit of SFTSV (101.3 ffu/mL).

These observations indicate that SFTSV was remarkably stable at 4 to 37°C compared with other viruses, suggesting that SFTSV-contaminated samples may retain infectious ability in a clinical environment for a long time throughout the year. The data also provide useful information indicating that SFTSV samples can be stored at 4°C without significant reduction of infectious titers for diagnoses by viral detection.

We next evaluated the heat stability of SFTSV at 56°C and 94°C, simulating typical serum complement inactivation and near-boiling temperature, respectively. Interestingly, infectious SFTSV remained at 56°C with a 2.32 log ffu/mL reduction after 60 min, whereas KAMV, JEV and FCV titers decreased significantly to below detection limits by 30, 10 and 1 min, respectively (Fig. 2). Furthermore, even at 94°C, infectious SFTSV somehow persisted for 1 min with just 1.94 log ffu/mL reduction, whereas KAMV, JEV and FCV were rapidly inactivated to below detection limits within 1 min (Fig. 2). These results suggest that SFTSV potentially possess a resistance against heat inactivation.

To confirm the infectious stability of SFTSV in clinical samples and assess the potential risk of infection in clinical practice, blood samples were prepared and evaluated for stability at different temperatures. A pooled blood sample from SFTSV-infected A129 mice was prepared to contain a high viral titer of 106.33 ffu/mL. Those aliquots were incubated at 4°C, 23°C, 37°C, 56°C and 94°C, and infectious titers were measured daily (Fig. 3). Infectious titers did not decrease over 7 days at 4°C and 23°C and showed just a 1.72 log ffu/mL reduction at 37°C after 7 days (Fig. 3A). At 56°C, infectious titers did not significantly decrease after 10 min, and the reductions were limited even after 60 min with a 1.98 log ffu/mL reduction (Fig. 3B). At 94°C, 1 min incubation showed only a 1.14 log ffu/mL reduction (Fig. 3B). These observations suggest that SFTSV-contaminated clinical samples possess infectious stability at lower to higher temperature and heat inactivation requires sufficient incubation time for SFTSV-contaminated clinical samples.

Control viral titers (day 0) was 106.33 ffu/mL. Each data was obtained from three independent experiments at the specified time points. Error bars indicate standard errors. The dotted line indicates the detection limit of SFTSV (101.3 ffu/mL).

High-temperature stability and resistance to heat inactivation of SFTSV raised an infectious risk at clinical sites where SFTSV-infected animals are treated. Thus, to confirm the appropriate methods for reducing the risk, we examined the efficacy of common disinfectants, ethanol and sodium hypochlorite against SFTSV. Exposure to 70% ethanol and 100 ppm sodium hypochlorite resulted in complete loss of viral infectivity within 1 min (Fig. 4). Thus, it is suggested that these detergents show sufficient virucidal effects against SFTSV and are useful for reduce the risk of SFTSV infection from clinical samples.

Viral infectious titers of Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV) after treatment with 70% ethanol and sodium hypochlorite solutions. Control viral titers (day 0) was 105.56 ffu/mL. Each data was obtained from three independent experiments at the specified time points. Error bars indicate standard errors. The dotted line indicates the detection limit of SFTSV (101.3 ffu/mL).

We also evaluated the inactivation efficacy of UV against SFTSV using 254 nm UV-CCL, 265 nm DUV-LED, and 275 nm DUV-LED irradiations. Compared to the non-irradiated control (5.22 log ffu/mL), the infectious titers were significantly reduced by 254 nm (20 sec of radiation: 2.84 log ffu/mL), 265 nm (28 sec of radiation: 1.79 log ffu/mL), and 275 nm (60 sec of radiation: 1.56 log ffu/mL). Based on these data, UV doses (mJ/cm2) were calculated for the inactivation of SFTSV (Fig. 5). The UV doses required to achieve a 2-log reduction in virus titers were 2.99, 3.57, and 5.44 mJ/cm2 following 254 nm UV-CCL, 265 nm DUV-LED, and 275 nm DUV-LED irradiation, respectively (Fig. 5). These data indicate that UV irradiation is effective at inactivating SFTSV with shorter wavelengths being more effective.

Each data was obtained from three independent experiments, and each line indicates the approximation line calculated from the results of each wavelength.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we showed a temperature-dependent resistance of SFTSV. The infectivity of SFTSV was sustained at 4°C to 37°C for several days compared to the enveloped JEV, KAMV and the non-enveloped FCV, suggesting that SFTSV-contaminated samples may retain the infectivity at room temperature conditions throughout the seasons. In addition, SFTSV has a comparative resistance against heat inactivation conditions at 56°C and 94°C. In particular, the inactivation effect of SFTSV was limited under general complement inactivation condition for serum at 56°C for 30 min. On the other hand, SFTSV was easily inactivated by ethanol, hypochlorite and UV irradiation, indicating that an inactivation procedure by appropriate treatment is important to reduce the risk of SFTSV infection from the contaminated samples at clinical sites and laboratories.

The increased stability of SFTSV at higher temperatures, especially 56°C and 94°C, highlights its remarkable heat tolerance. This thermotolerance may associated with multiple structural features of SFTSV. Accumulating evidence suggests that certain members of the bunyaviruses exhibit structural features that enhance virion stability [14, 46, 47]. Cryo-electron microscopy studies of SFTSV have demonstrated that the Gn and Gc glycoproteins form an organized, lattice-like surface architecture that contributes to envelope rigidity and particle integrity [9, 28]. Such ordered glycoprotein assemblies may be responsible for the reduction in conformational disruption under transient thermal stress [9, 27]. Previous studies have indicated that some filoviruses and bunyaviruses have significant heat resistance [3, 34, 47]. This resistance can be mechanistically linked to the virus’s cholesterol-rich membranes and the highly ordered structures of its nucleocapsid glycoproteins, which provide thermal protection [3, 34, 47]. During budding, Bunyaviruses acquire host-derived lipids, including cholesterol, and cholesterol-embedded membranes have been shown to increase membrane packing density and provide resistance to thermal perturbation [11, 46]. In addition, the ribonucleoprotein, which forms stable complexes of SFTSV, is also tightly encapsulated by nucleoprotein, forming stable helical structures that may protect genomic RNA from heat-induced degradation [18, 54]. Importantly, the observation that SFTSV retains infectivity at near-boiling temperature (94°C) is a novel finding that greatly advances our understanding of its thermotolerance and suggests potential adaptations that could facilitate viral survival across different environments. Further structural and mechanistic analyses will be necessary to identify the specific molecular factors responsible for the thermal resistance of SFTSV.

Another important finding in our study is the sustained infectivity of SFTSV at 23°C and 37°C. Although previous studies showed that SFTSV infection risk increases at temperatures below 17°C [41], our results demonstrate that SFTSV remains highly infectious at both 23°C and 37°C. This suggests that SFTSV-contaminated bodily fluids could pose a significant transmission risk within these temperature ranges, which is a potential threat to vulnerable populations. Our findings highlight the importance of monitoring environmental conditions and their effects on SFTSV infectivity, which could help shape public health strategies to reduce transmission risk.

Exposure to low temperatures, such as 4°C, significantly enhances viral stability by slowing physicochemical processes that lead to structural degradation and loss of infectivity [19]. This stabilization reduces molecular motion and preserves ribonucleoprotein complexes. [19, 35]. The enhanced stability of SFTSV at 4°C aligns with these principles and may also be attributed to the orderly arrangement of its lipid bilayer envelope, which reduces fluidity and makes it less susceptible to disruption [7, 19]. The relatively high stability of SFTSV at 4°C may be advantageous for long-term storage and transportation to the institutes where a diagnosis of SFTS is conducted at the request of clinical hospitals.

Chemical disinfection assays demonstrated that SFTSV infectivity is effectively eliminated by 70% ethanol and sodium hypochlorite. Detergents and chlorine-based disinfectants disrupt viral lipid envelopes, resulting in loss of infectivity, and enveloped viruses are consistently susceptible to these agents [6, 37]. These results indicate that commonly used disinfectants are effective against SFTSV and support their continued use for surface and equipment decontamination in veterinary clinical and laboratory environments. However, the effectiveness of disinfectant may decrease in the presence of organic materials such as blood, serum proteins, or tissue debris, which can hinder chemical activity [1, 2, 4]. Thus, further detailed evaluation under conditions of organic material contamination will provide more useful information for reducing a risk of infection from SFTSV-contaminated samples in clinical practice.

UV-C irradiation resulted in wavelength-dependent inactivation of SFTSV. UV-C wavelengths (200–280 nm) inactivate viruses primarily by damaging viral nucleic acids or proteins, thereby impairing replication [39, 40]. These findings are consistent with previous work demonstrating a significant reduction in SFTSV infectivity with UV-LED irradiation at 280 ± 5 nm [20]. However, the energy required for inactivation in the present study was lower. This difference underscores the importance of wavelength selection and system optimization in the application of UV-LED technology for viral inactivation. Furthermore, UV irradiation is limited by line-of-sight exposure and surface irregularities, which cause shadowing, scattering, or absorption of UV light. These factors decrease the UV dose received by the organism and reduce the methodological efficiency [5, 12, 32]. Therefore, proper pre-cleaning and appropriate UV system designs are important for achieving effective reductions in real-world settings.

From a veterinary microbiology perspective, the demonstrated stability of SFTSV across a range of temperatures has important implications for infection control. The virus’s ability to retain infectivity during sample storage, transportation, and routine handling underscores the need for validated inactivation procedures when processing clinical specimens from animals in endemic regions. Although our current data do not show the specific mechanism of SFTSV stability, future research employing biophysical or structural analyses will be required to reveal the specific mechanisms.
