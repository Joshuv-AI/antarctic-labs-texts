High intensity 40 Hz flicker improves amyloid pathology and cognition with autophagy in 3xTg mice
Authors: Zhongya Gu, Yufeng Fu, Yaqi Huang, Chengchao Zuo, Yu Song, Yu Guo, Huan Cao, Yongsheng Jiang, Furong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGamma-frequency (40 Hz) flicker stimulation has emerged as a noninvasive approach for modulating Alzheimer’s disease (AD)-related pathology, yet the influence of stimulation intensity remains unclear. We examined whether visual 40 Hz stimulation at different light intensities elicits distinct neurobiological effects in 19–24-week-old 3xTg-AD mice. High-intensity stimulation (1100–1500 lux) improved cognitive performance, modulated AMPK/mTOR signaling and autophagy-related markers, reduced neuronal Aβ burden, and preserved synaptic and neuronal integrity in both the primary visual cortex and hippocampus. In contrast, low-intensity stimulation (100–500 lux) primarily induced molecular changes in the primary visual cortex, with limited effects on hippocampal pathology or cognition. Pharmacological inhibition of autophagy with 3-methyladenine attenuated these effects. Together, these findings suggest graded responses to visual 40 Hz stimulation within the tested intensity range and implicate stimulation intensity as a contributing factor to region-specific and cognitive outcomes in early-stage AD models.Subject areas: photomedicine, biological sciences, neuroscience, cognitive neuroscience, techniques in neuroscience

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is an age-related neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive memory loss and cognitive decline.1 Its pathological hallmarks include extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques, intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau, and pronounced neuronal and synaptic loss.2 Despite affecting over 50 million individuals worldwide,3 effective disease-modifying therapies for AD remain unavailable.

Increasing evidence suggests that intracellular Aβ accumulation disrupts proteostasis and promotes oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and neuronal death.4 Autophagy, a central pathway for protein clearance, is impaired early in AD and contributes to Aβ accumulation, synaptic dysfunction, and cognitive decline.5,6 Accordingly, restoration of autophagic processes has emerged as a promising strategy to facilitate Aβ degradation and improve cognitive outcomes in experimental models.7

Light stimulation, a noninvasive neuromodulatory approach, has garnered increasing attention for its potential relevance to AD.8,9,10 Beyond its role in visual perception, light exposure can modulate neural activity, cognition, and behavior.11,12,13 However, the contribution of illumination intensity, an essential yet insufficiently characterized parameter, remains poorly defined in the context of AD intervention. Clinical and experimental evidence suggests that illumination intensity critically influences the efficacy of light-based interventions; for example, bright light exposure at approximately 1000 lux has been reported to delay cognitive decline in early-stage dementia.14 In contrast, preclinical work reported that light exposure to continuous light exceeding 3,000 lux did not result in significant cognitive improvement in an AD mouse model15; however, evidence at this intensity range remains limited.

Notably, prior studies primarily used steady or ambient illumination, which engages neural mechanisms fundamentally distinct from gamma-frequency (40 Hz) flicker. Unlike continuous light exposure, 40 Hz stimulation selectively enhances cortical gamma oscillations—critical for cognition and disrupted in AD. Consistently, 40 Hz (not 20 or 80 Hz) flicker stimulation reduces Aβ burden and improves memory in AD mouse models,16,17,18 effects tightly linked to gamma activity.19,20 While most previous work has focused on frequency, light intensity has received less attention. Brighter 40 Hz stimulation induces stronger gamma entrainment, with higher luminance expanding the spatial extent and robustness of cortical responses.21,22 Accordingly, 40 Hz stimulation (∼200–1000 lux) improves cognition and mitigates neuropathology in AD mice without apparent adverse effects.18,23 Building on this, we selected a slightly higher yet well-tolerated range (1100–1500 lux) to ensure robust entrainment during chronic exposure. We thus hypothesized that increasing intensity within a physiologically tolerable window would enhance the therapeutic efficacy of 40 Hz stimulation in AD.

Recent studies indicate that visible-light exposure can regulate autophagy in vitro24,25; however, whether it modulates autophagy in vivo—particularly in the context of AD—remains unclear. Our previous work showed that 2 Hz visual stimulation improved spatial learning in 3xTg-AD mice by upregulating Homer1a,26 a synaptic immediate-early gene that has been shown in neuronal cell models to support neuronal survival through AMPK-dependent autophagy.27 Activation of AMPK promotes autophagy by suppressing mTOR signaling, thereby facilitating Aβ clearance and cognitive improvement.28 Consistent with this, 40 Hz light stimulation has been reported to increase p-AMPK levels,29 though whether this effect is intensity-dependent remains unexamined. Given that AMPK activity is reduced in 3xTg-AD mice,30 the AMPK/mTOR pathway represents a compelling mechanism by which gamma-frequency flicker may drive autophagy-related neuroprotection.

In this study, we compared the effects of low- (100–500 lux) and high-intensity (1100–1500 lux) 40 Hz light stimulation on cognition, Aβ burden, and neuronal integrity in 19–24-week-old 3xTg-AD mice to determine whether therapeutic efficacy depends on light intensity. Although shorter stimulation paradigms have reported acute molecular effects, it remains unclear whether brief exposure induces durable behavioral and neuropathological changes in established AD pathology.

Therefore, we employed a 42-day stimulation protocol to evaluate sustained cognitive and molecular outcomes under chronic 40 Hz visual stimulation. We further examined whether these effects were associated with autophagy-related processes involving the AMPK/mTOR pathway and probed this relationship using the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA). Together, these findings suggest that higher-intensity 40 Hz stimulation may be associated with improved cognitive performance, enhanced autophagy-related signaling, and reduced neuronal loss in the hippocampus and primary visual cortex, supporting a potential role for AMPK/mTOR-linked autophagy in mediating the neuroprotective effects of gamma-frequency light stimulation.

Results

High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation alleviates cognitive deficits and reduces Aβ burden in 3xTg-AD mice

To examine whether flicker intensity modulates the efficacy of gamma entrainment, 3xTg-AD mice were exposed to 40 Hz stimulation at two illuminance levels: low-intensity (100–500 lux) and high-intensity (1100–1500 lux). In the open-field test (OFT), one-way ANOVA revealed a significant group effect on total distance traveled (Figure S3A; Table S9). Post hoc Tukey’s tests indicated that 3xTg-AD mice exhibited a significantly reduced total distance compared with control mice. However, no significant differences were observed among AD groups receiving low- or high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation. In contrast, time spent in the center zone did not differ significantly among groups (Figure S3B; Table S9), and no pairwise comparisons reached statistical significance. These results suggest that 40 Hz flicker stimulation, at either intensity, did not significantly affect anxiety-like behavior in 3xTg-AD mice. In the novel object recognition (NOR) test (Figure 1B; Table S1), one-way ANOVA revealed a significant group effect on the novelty index. Post hoc Tukey’s tests showed that 3xTg-AD mice exhibited a significantly reduced novelty index compared with control mice.

High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly increased the novelty index relative to the 3xTg-AD group, whereas low-intensity 40 Hz stimulation did not. Total exploration time did not differ among groups (Figure S3C; Table S9), indicating comparable exploratory activity.

Spatial learning and memory were further assessed using the Morris water maze (MWM). During acquisition, escape latency decreased progressively across training days in all groups (Figure 1C; Table S1). On day 5, 3xTg-AD mice displayed significantly longer escape latencies than controls, whereas high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly shortened escape latency compared with 3xTg-AD mice; low-intensity 40 Hz stimulation had no significant effect. Mean swimming speed did not differ among groups (Figure S3D; Table S9), excluding motor confounds.

In the probe trial, one-way ANOVA revealed a significant group effect on time spent in the target quadrant (Figure 1D; Table S1). 3xTg-AD mice spent significantly less time in the target quadrant than controls, whereas high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly increased target quadrant occupancy relative to 3xTg-AD mice; no improvement was observed with low-intensity stimulation. Consistently, one-sample analyses demonstrated that control mice and high-intensity-stimulated 3xTg-AD mice spent significantly more time in the target quadrant than the chance level (25%), whereas 3xTg-AD mice and low-intensity-stimulated mice did not (Figure S3E; Table S9). A similar pattern was observed for platform crossings (Figure 1E; Table S1).

To determine whether 40 Hz flicker intensity affected cognition under non-pathological conditions, wild-type mice were examined. During acquisition, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of training day but no significant effect of stimulation intensity or interaction (Figure S2E; Table S8). Post hoc comparisons at Day 5 likewise showed no differences in escape latency among stimulation intensities. However, exposure to excessively bright flicker (2600–3000 lux) was accompanied by a modest reduction in NOR performance compared with controls (Figure S2C; Table S8).

In parallel with the behavioral improvements, Aβ accumulation in the hippocampal CA1 region differed significantly among groups (Figures 1G and 1H; Table S1). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant group effect on 6E10-immunoreactive area. Post hoc analyses showed that Aβ burden was markedly increased in 3xTg-AD mice compared with WT controls and significantly reduced by high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation, whereas low-intensity 40 Hz stimulation had no significant effect. Notably, high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation resulted in lower Aβ accumulation than low-intensity 40 Hz stimulation, supporting an intensity-dependent effect. Consistently, the proportion of Aβ-immunoreactive NeuN+ neurons in CA1 differed significantly among groups (Figures S4A and S4B; Table S10). 3xTg-AD mice exhibited a marked increase in neuronal Aβ accumulation compared with controls. This increase was significantly attenuated by high-intensity stimulation but not by low-intensity stimulation, with the high-intensity group showing lower neuronal Aβ levels than the low-intensity group. Moreover, a similar pattern was observed in the hippocampal CA3 region (Figures S5A and S5C; Table S11), where only high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly reduced Aβ accumulation relative to 3xTg-AD mice.

Similarly, hippocampal soluble Aβ1–42 levels differed significantly among groups (Figure 1I; Table S1). Soluble Aβ1–42 was elevated in 3xTg-AD mice and significantly reduced by high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation, whereas low-intensity 40 Hz stimulation had no significant effect. Soluble Aβ1–42 levels were also significantly lower in the high-intensity group than in the low-intensity group.

High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation modulates autophagy-related markers and AMPK/mTOR signaling

Autophagy is a multistep process involving phagophore formation, autophagosome maturation, and lysosomal degradation. Accordingly, three commonly used autophagy-related markers were examined: Beclin-1, a core component of the class III PI3K complex involved in autophagosome initiation; the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, reflecting lipidated LC3 incorporation into autophagosomal membranes; and p62/SQSTM1, a selective autophagy receptor degraded during autophagy.31,32 Western blot analysis revealed significant alterations in both the primary visual cortex and hippocampus of 3xTg-AD mice (Figure 2). Compared with WT controls, 3xTg-AD mice exhibited reduced LC3-II/LC3-I ratios and Beclin-1 levels, together with increased p62 expression in both regions (primary visual cortex: Figures 2A, 2B, and 2I; hippocampus; Figures 2C, 2D, and 2J; Table S2). One-way ANOVA demonstrated significant group effects for all three markers in both brain areas. In the primary visual cortex, post hoc Tukey’s tests showed that both low- and high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratios and Beclin-1 levels while reducing p62 expression relative to 3xTg-AD mice. In contrast, in the hippocampus, only high-intensity stimulation significantly modulated these markers, whereas low-intensity stimulation had no significant effect. Consistently, the magnitude of change was greater following high-intensity stimulation than low-intensity stimulation in both regions (Figure 2; Table S2).

Immunofluorescence analysis showed that p62 predominantly colocalized with NeuN+ neurons in the hippocampal CA1 region (Figure S6A). Quantification revealed increased p62 and reduced LC3 signals in 3xTg-AD mice compared with WT controls in both CA1 and the primary visual cortex (p62: Figures S6B, S6C, S7A, and S7B; LC3; Figures S6D, S6E, S7C, and S7D; Table S12 and S13), with significant group effects detected in each region. In the hippocampus, only high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly decreased p62 and increased LC3 relative to 3xTg-AD mice, whereas low-intensity stimulation had no significant effect (Figures S6B–S6E; Table S12). In contrast, in the primary visual cortex, both low- and high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly modulated these markers (Figures S7A–S7D; Table S13). Transmission electron microscopy revealed fewer double-membrane vesicular structures consistent with autophagosomes in CA1 pyramidal neurons of 3xTg-AD mice, with an apparent increase following high-intensity stimulation (Figures S4C and S4D). These ultrastructural observations were descriptive and not subjected to quantitative analysis.

Western blot analysis demonstrated significant alterations in AMPK/mTOR signaling in 3xTg-AD mice compared with WT controls in both the primary visual cortex and hippocampus (Figures 2E–2H; Table S2), characterized by reduced p-AMPK/AMPK and elevated p-mTOR/mTOR ratios. High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly increased p-AMPK/AMPK and decreased p-mTOR/mTOR ratios in both regions. Low-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly modulated these ratios in the primary visual cortex but not in the hippocampus (Figures 2E–2H; Table S2).

High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation promotes neuronal survival and preserves synaptic integrity

Consistent with previous reports showing early-onset neuronal reduction in this model,33,34 our immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant reduction in NeuN+ neuronal density in 3xTg-AD mice compared with WT controls across the primary visual cortex and hippocampal subfields (CA1, DG, and CA3) (Figures 3A–3D; Table S3; Figures S5A and S5D; Table S11). One-way ANOVA demonstrated significant group effects in all regions. Post hoc analyses showed that low-intensity 40 Hz stimulation modestly increased NeuN+ neuronal density in the primary visual cortex but did not significantly affect CA1, DG, or CA3. In contrast, high-intensity stimulation significantly increased neuronal density across all examined regions and exerted significantly greater effects than low-intensity stimulation in the primary visual cortex, CA1, and CA3.

Hippocampal levels of the synaptic proteins PSD95 and synaptophysin (SYP) were assessed by western blotting (Figures 3E–3G; Table S3). 3xTg-AD mice exhibited significantly reduced PSD95 and SYP expression relative to WT controls. High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly restored both proteins, whereas low-intensity stimulation had no significant effect, resulting in higher PSD95 and SYP levels in the high-intensity group compared with the low-intensity group. Consistently, in the CA3 subregion, only high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly increased PSD95 immunofluorescence intensity (Figures S5B and S5E; Table S11).

Apoptosis-related markers were further examined, including the cleaved caspase-3/caspase-3 ratio and the Bcl-2/Bax ratio. One-way ANOVA revealed significant group effects for both measures (Figures 3G–3I; Table S3). Compared with WT controls, 3xTg-AD mice showed a reduced Bcl-2/Bax ratio and an increased cleaved caspase-3/caspase-3 ratio. High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly reversed these alterations, whereas low-intensity stimulation had no significant effect. Moreover, the cleaved caspase-3/caspase-3 ratio was significantly lower in the high-intensity 40 Hz group than in the low-intensity group.

Autophagy inhibition by 3-MA attenuates the behavioral and molecular effects of high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation

Abundant evidence indicates that pharmacological inhibition of autophagy by 3-MA impairs cognitive improvement in AD models.35,36 Based on this rationale, we examined whether autophagy inhibition modulates the cognitive benefits of high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation in 3xTg-AD mice. In the NOR test, two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of 3-MA treatment and 40 Hz stimulation (1100–1500 lux) on the novelty index, with no significant interaction between the two factors (Figure 4B; Table S4). Post hoc Sidak’s tests showed that high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly increased the novelty index in vehicle-treated 3xTg-AD mice. This improvement was no longer observed following 3-MA administration, as no significant difference was detected between stimulated AD mice with or without 3-MA treatment (Figure 4B; Table S4). Notably, 3-MA treatment alone did not significantly affect the novelty index in unstimulated AD mice. Total exploration time was unchanged across groups (Figure S3F; Table S9), indicating that differences in recognition performance were not attributable to altered exploratory activity.

Spatial learning was further evaluated using the MWM. During acquisition, escape latency decreased across training days in all groups (Figure 4C), with two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealing a significant main effect of training day but no significant interaction between stimulation and training day (Table S4). On the final training day, high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly reduced escape latency in vehicle-treated 3xTg-AD mice compared with 3xTg-AD mice, whereas this effect was not observed under 3-MA co-treatment. Mean swimming speed did not differ among groups (Figure S3G; Table S9), indicating no motor confound. In the probe trial, two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of both 40 Hz stimulation (1100–1500 lux) and 3-MA treatment on time spent in the target quadrant and platform crossings, without significant interaction (Figures 4D and 4F; Table S4). High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly improved both measures in vehicle-treated 3xTg-AD mice, whereas these improvements were not detected under 3-MA co-treatment. Notably, 3-MA alone did not alter baseline spatial performance in unstimulated AD mice. Consistently, one-sample analyses showed that only vehicle-treated AD mice receiving high-intensity stimulation performed above chance level (25%) (Figure S3H; Table S9).

At the molecular level, two-way ANOVA revealed no significant interaction between 40 Hz stimulation (1100–1500 lux) and 3-MA treatment on the p-AMPK/AMPK ratio in the primary visual cortex or hippocampus (Figures 5A–5C and 5F–5H; Table S5), although significant main effects of both factors were observed. Post hoc Sidak’s tests showed that high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation increased p-AMPK/AMPK in vehicle-treated 3xTg-AD mice compared with 3xTg-AD mice, and this increase was attenuated by 3-MA co-treatment in both regions. Similarly, no significant interaction was detected for p-mTOR/mTOR, despite significant main effects of stimulation and 3-MA. High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly reduced p-mTOR/mTOR in vehicle-treated 3xTg-AD mice, and this reduction was partially attenuated by 3-MA, as stimulated mice receiving 3-MA exhibited higher p-mTOR/mTOR levels than vehicle-treated stimulated mice. In contrast, 3-MA alone did not affect basal p-mTOR/mTOR levels.

Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between 40 Hz stimulation (1100–1500 lux) and 3-MA treatment on LC3-II/LC3-I levels in both the primary visual cortex (Figures 5A and 5E) and hippocampus (Figures 5F and 5J; Table S5). Post hoc analyses showed that high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly increased LC3-II/LC3-I in vehicle-treated 3xTg-AD mice relative to unstimulated 3xTg-AD mice, and this increase was significantly attenuated by 3-MA co-treatment. Autophagy inhibition by 3-MA limits the Aβ-reducing and neuroprotective effects of high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation

Consistent with previous observations, hippocampal Aβ pathology differed significantly across groups, as assessed by both immunofluorescence staining and ELISA (Figures 4G–4I; Table S4). Two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of 40 Hz stimulation (1100–1500 lux) and 3-MA treatment on hippocampal Aβ burden, with a significant interaction detected for soluble Aβ1–42 levels but not for Aβ-positive area (Figures 4G–4I; Table S4). Post hoc analyses showed that high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly reduced Aβ accumulation and soluble Aβ1–42 levels in vehicle-treated 3xTg-AD mice. These reductions were markedly attenuated by 3-MA co-treatment, as both soluble Aβ1–42 levels and Aβ burden were higher in the 3xTg-AD+40 Hz (1100–1500 lux)+3-MA group than in the corresponding vehicle-treated stimulated group. Notably, 3-MA alone did not significantly alter hippocampal Aβ1–42 levels under unstimulated conditions. Two-way ANOVA further revealed significant effects on NeuN+ neuronal density across the primary visual cortex, CA1, and DG (Figures 6A–6D; Table S6). High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation significantly increased neuronal density in vehicle-treated 3xTg-AD mice in all examined regions. This increase was significantly reduced by 3-MA co-treatment, as NeuN+ density in 3xTg-AD+40 Hz (1100–1500 lux)+3-MA mice was significantly lower than in vehicle-treated stimulated mice. No significant differences were observed between unstimulated 3xTg-AD+vehicle and 3xTg-AD+3-MA groups.

Discussion

Effective disease-modifying therapies for 3xTg-AD remain limited, prompting exploration of alternative noninvasive neuromodulation strategies. Among these, 40 Hz sensory stimulation has attracted considerable interest due to its reported ability to entrain gamma oscillations and modulate AD-related pathology.37,38 In the present study, we evaluated the effects of 40 Hz light stimulation at different intensities in 3xTg-AD mice and assessed associated cognitive, molecular, and cellular alterations using a chronic 42-day stimulation paradigm, allowing evaluation of sustained behavioral and neuropathological outcomes beyond previously reported short-term molecular effects. High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation (1100–1500 lux) was associated with improved cognitive performance, modulation of autophagy-related markers, reduced Aβ burden, and preservation of neuronal and synaptic integrity. These effects were attenuated by pharmacological inhibition of autophagy, supporting a close association between autophagy-related processes and the observed neuroprotective phenotype. In contrast, low-intensity stimulation (100–500 lux) produced detectable molecular changes primarily in the primary visual cortex but did not significantly influence hippocampal pathology or hippocampus-dependent behaviors, including the NOR and MWM tests.

While most previous studies have focused primarily on stimulation frequency, accumulating evidence suggests that stimulus intensity critically shapes neural and biological responses to gamma-frequency stimulation.39,40 Visually driven gamma entrainment has been shown to emerge most robustly within the visual cortex,41 whereas hippocampal engagement is not consistently evoked by visual stimulation alone and may depend on stimulation strength, duration, and broader network integration.42,43 The absence of statistically significant differences between low- and high-intensity stimulation in the visual cortex may reflect the high sensitivity of this primary sensory region, such that both intensities are sufficient to elicit molecular responses. In contrast, downstream structures such as the hippocampus may require a higher stimulation threshold for functionally meaningful engagement. Subtle behavioral alterations observed in wild-type mice at the highest tested intensity further underscore the importance of defining safety boundaries. Collectively, these findings identify light intensity as a key determinant of region-specific and behavioral outcomes in gamma-frequency photic stimulation and highlight the need for systematic dose-response and safety evaluation.

Recent studies have highlighted variability in the effects of 40 Hz sensory stimulation across AD models and disease stages. In aggressive amyloid models such as 5xFAD and APP/PS1, Soula et al. and Yang & Lai reported that 40 Hz stimulation (∼480 lux) did not significantly reduce plaque burden or broadly alter microglial states, and predominantly elicited localized narrow-band responses within the visual cortex without consistent hippocampal recruitment.44,45 However, responses to 40 Hz stimulation have not been uniformly negative. For example, Shen et al. demonstrated that in 6-month-old APP/PS1 mice, daily 40 Hz flicker for 7 days significantly reduced both soluble and insoluble Aβ species in cortical and hippocampal regions,46 suggesting that responsiveness may depend on disease stage, stimulation duration, and stimulation parameters. Within this heterogeneous literature, our findings identify luminance as an additional factor influencing regional and behavioral responsiveness to 40 Hz stimulation. Lower-intensity stimulation preferentially modulated molecular markers in the primary visual cortex, whereas hippocampal Aβ reduction, autophagy-related alterations, and cognitive improvement were primarily observed under higher luminance conditions. The chronic stimulation paradigm used here may also represent one factor associated with the sustained behavioral and molecular changes observed. This pattern aligns with reports indicating that hippocampal engagement is more difficult to achieve than cortical entrainment and may require stronger or more sustained network activation.

Accumulating evidence implicates impaired autophagy in AD pathogenesis, as defective protein clearance contributes to Aβ accumulation.47 Beclin-1 is reduced in AD brains, and its upregulation promotes neuronal autophagy and attenuates Aβ pathology.48,49 Consistent with reports of early autophagy impairment in 3xTg-AD mice by six months of age,50 we observed coordinated alterations in autophagy-related markers across hippocampal and cortical regions, including reduced LC3-II/LC3-I ratios and Beclin-1 levels, increased p62 accumulation, and fewer autophagosome-like structures in CA1 neurons. High-intensity 40 Hz stimulation was associated with normalization of these changes, whereas low-intensity stimulation produced more limited or regionally restricted effects. Together, these findings suggest that higher stimulation intensity may be required to more broadly engage autophagy-related processes, in parallel with reductions in Aβ burden and behavioral improvement.

AMPK, a central cellular energy sensor regulating autophagy, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial homeostasis, has been increasingly implicated in AD pathogenesis.51 The AMPK/mTOR pathway serves as a key regulator of autophagy,52 and aberrant mTOR activation is recognized as a pathological hallmark in AD.53 Prior studies have suggested that AMPK-mediated suppression of mTORC1 is associated with enhanced autophagy in AD-related contexts.54 In the present study, high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation was associated with increased AMPK phosphorylation and reduced mTOR activation, accompanied by parallel changes in autophagy-related markers and Aβ levels. Notably, these signaling changes were not observed under low-intensity stimulation, supporting an intensity-dependent association between gamma-frequency stimulation and engagement of AMPK/mTOR-related pathways.

Synaptic dysfunction is an early hallmark of AD that precedes overt plaque deposition and contributes to cognitive decline.55 Aβ aggregates disrupt long-term potentiation, impair synaptic trafficking, and induce excitotoxic signaling, promoting synaptic loss.56,57 Preservation of synaptic proteins such as PSD95 and SYP, which are essential for synaptic structure and plasticity, is closely linked to cognitive function.58,59 Consistent with prior reports of synaptic modulation by gamma-frequency stimulation in AD models,17,23 high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation increased hippocampal PSD95 and SYP expression and enhanced PSD95 immunoreactivity in CA3, suggesting attenuation of synaptic degeneration in this AD-vulnerable region. Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, these synaptic changes paralleled alterations in autophagy-related markers and reductions in Aβ burden, raising the possibility that improved proteostasis and reduced amyloid-associated synaptic toxicity contributed to synaptic resilience. Previous studies further support a role for 40 Hz stimulation in modulating hippocampal plasticity beyond AD models: it restored slow gamma oscillations and LTP in cerebral ischemia60 and enhanced dentate gyrus neurogenesis and spatial learning following prolonged exposure.61 Although electrophysiological plasticity was not directly assessed, these findings provide contextual evidence that gamma-frequency stimulation can engage synaptic and circuit-level mechanisms. Together, our results suggest that high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation preserves synaptic protein expression in AD-vulnerable hippocampal regions, potentially contributing to cognitive improvement.

In addition to synaptic markers, high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation was associated with shifts in apoptotic indices, including an increased Bcl-2/Bax ratio, a reduced cleaved caspase-3/caspase-3 ratio, and improved NeuN+ neuronal density in 3xTg-AD mice—effects not observed under low-intensity stimulation. Although overt neurodegeneration in 3xTg-AD mice is more consistently reported at later ages, early hippocampal vulnerability has been documented beginning around 4–6 months, including structural atrophy, modest reductions in neuronal density, and increased pro-apoptotic signaling.33,34,62 Given the age at study completion (∼6–7 months), the observed NeuN+ reduction likely reflects an early-to-intermediate pathological stage characterized by regional vulnerability rather than widespread neuronal loss. These apoptotic alterations occurred in parallel with synaptic and autophagy-related changes, without implying direct causal relationships among these processes. Collectively, the data indicate that high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation is associated with improved synaptic integrity and neuronal viability, providing cellular correlates for the observed behavioral benefits. Future studies incorporating electrophysiological and ultrastructural analyses will be required to determine whether these molecular and structural changes translate into sustained functional plasticity.

To further examine the involvement of autophagy-related processes in responses to 40 Hz stimulation, we employed 3-MA, an inhibitor of class III PI3K and autophagosome formation.63 Consistent with previous reports, 3-MA reduced autophagy-related readouts in our model, including altered p62 expression and LC3-II/LC3-I ratios, accompanied by decreased AMPK phosphorylation and increased mTOR activity. Under high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation, 3-MA markedly attenuated stimulation-associated changes in autophagy markers and signaling pathways, as well as neuronal and behavioral improvements. Notably, 3-MA alone did not significantly exacerbate baseline AD-related phenotypes in 3xTg-AD mice, consistent with prior reports that autophagy inhibition may limit protective responses without worsening established AD pathology.36,64 In addition, 3-MA attenuated the anti-apoptotic shifts associated with high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation. Together, these findings support the involvement of autophagy-associated pathways in mediating the cellular and behavioral effects of high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation, while not excluding contributions from additional mechanisms.

Beyond AD, 40 Hz sensory stimulation has been explored in multiple neurological and neuropsychiatric disease models. In Parkinson’s disease models, multisensory 40 Hz stimulation reduces α-synuclein pathology and improve motor performance.65 In epilepsy, autism spectrum disorder, and sleep-related studies, visual 40 Hz stimulation produces regionally restricted effects, including suppression of epileptiform activity through modulation of local inhibitory circuits,66 restoration of excitation–inhibition balance in prefrontal cortical regions,67 and regulation of sleep–wake behavior via adenosine signaling within the visual cortex.40 Collectively, these studies suggest that 40 Hz stimulation does not uniformly engage the entire brain; rather, its effects are region-specific and shaped by stimulation intensity and disease context.

Consistent with this framework, our findings in 3xTg-AD mice indicate a graded regional response. Molecular alterations were evident in the primary visual cortex at both tested intensities, whereas hippocampal engagement—reflected by coordinated changes in autophagy-related markers, reduced Aβ burden, and improved cognition—was observed predominantly under higher-intensity stimulation (1100–1500 lux). These results suggest that visual 40 Hz flicker preferentially modulates sensory regions and may engage downstream cognition-relevant structures when stimulation intensity exceeds a functional threshold. Accordingly, higher-intensity stimulation was associated with more consistent behavioral and cellular benefits than lower-intensity stimulation, although generalizability beyond the tested range remains to be determined.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, although glial responses have been implicated in gamma-frequency entrainment, the present study focused primarily on neuronal autophagy and neuronal Aβ processing. Microglial and astrocytic contributions were not directly examined and warrant further investigation.23,68,69 Second, mechanistic interpretation is limited by the use of 3-MA, which lacks specificity, and by reliance on molecular and morphological markers rather than direct assessment of autophagic flux. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as modulation of autophagy-related processes rather than definitive restoration of autophagic activity. Third, only two intensity ranges were tested, and the relatively small sample size without formal a priori power analysis may have limited detection of modest or dose-dependent effects. Thus, the dose–response profile, safety window, and long-term consequences remain to be established. Moreover, the absence of a random-frequency control group limits conclusions regarding gamma specificity, or effects at other frequencies or under constant illumination. Fourth, experiments were conducted exclusively in male 3xTg-AD mice, limiting generalizability across sexes. Finally, although this model develops both amyloid and tau pathology, the present study focused primarily on Aβ-related outcomes, and potential effects on tau pathology remain to be determined.

In summary, high-intensity 40 Hz stimulation (1100–1500 lux) was associated with more robust behavioral and cellular effects in 3xTg-AD mice than lower-intensity stimulation (100–500 lux). These effects coincided with modulation of autophagy-related signaling, reduced Aβ burden, and improved synaptic and neuronal integrity. While consistent with an intensity-dependent effect within the tested range, further studies incorporating systematic dose–response paradigms and direct measurements of autophagic flux will be required to clarify mechanistic underpinnings and define the therapeutic window.

Experimental model and study participant details

Homozygous 3xTg-AD mice (B6;129-Tg(APPSwe,tauP301L)1Lfa Psen1tm1Mpm/Mmjax; The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA; Stock No. 034830) were originally obtained from The Jackson Laboratory and maintained by in-house breeding.70 Age-matched wild-type littermates derived from the same breeding pairs were used as control (CON) animals to ensure comparable genetic background (C57BL/6 J × 129 S1/SvImJ hybrid). Only male mice aged 19–24 weeks were used in this study; therefore, potential sex-specific effects were not evaluated. Mice were group-housed (3–4 per cage) under controlled conditions (22–25°C, 60–70% humidity) on a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and investigators were blinded to group allocation during data collection and analysis. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (TJH-202201019) and were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Method details

Light flicker stimulation and drug administration

The light stimulation protocol was adapted from established GENUS paradigms with minor modifications.16,18,23 The selected illuminance ranges were defined based on previously reported 40 Hz visual stimulation paradigms in AD models (typically ∼200–1000 lux) and adjusted to create distinct intensity tiers while maintaining tolerability and spatial uniformity in freely moving mice. Mice (3–4 per cage) were placed in a dark chamber and exposed to white LED flicker (10 W, 40 Hz, 50% duty cycle) from above for 1 h/day over 42 consecutive days during the light phase (9:00–10:00 a.m.). Chow and water were removed only during the 1-hour stimulation period to prevent light obstruction and spillage and were returned immediately afterward, ensuring ad libitum access (>23 h/day). No distress or body weight loss was observed. Illuminance was measured using a calibrated lux meter at the cage floor and animal eye level (∼5 cm above bedding), sampled at multiple predefined positions within the cage, as shown in Figure S1. Two intensities were used: low (100–500 lux; eye-level ∼300 lux) and high (1100–1500 lux; eye-level ∼1200 lux). Light intensity was calibrated before each batch and verified weekly. Control mice received steady, non-flickering illumination (∼300 lux at eye level) under identical conditions. Calibrated sound measurements confirmed no detectable acoustic differences between flicker-on and flicker-off states. In the baseline experiment, mice were randomly assigned to four groups: wild-type control (CON), 3xTg-AD, 3xTg-AD+40 Hz (100–500 lux), and 3xTg-AD+40 Hz (1100–1500 lux). In the validation experiment, mice were randomly assigned to four groups: 3xTg-AD+vehicle, 3xTg-AD+3-MA, 3xTg-AD+40 Hz (1100–1500 lux)+vehicle, and 3xTg-AD+40 Hz (1100–1500 lux)+3-MA. For pharmacological inhibition, 3-methyladenine (3-MA; 30 mg/kg, i.p.) or saline was administered 30 min before daily stimulation for 42 days.71,72 In supplementary experiments (Figure S2), wild-type mice were assigned to CON, or 40 Hz or 40 Hz light stimulation at low (100–500 lux), medium (1100–1500 lux), or high (2600–3000 lux) intensities under identical conditions.

Behavioral testing

Behavioral testing was performed according to the timelines indicated in the corresponding figures. All procedures were conducted accordingly unless otherwise specified. Behavioral testing was performed at least 6 hours after daily 40 Hz light stimulation to minimize potential acute effects. Mice were habituated to the behavioral testing room for 3 consecutive days prior to the first assessment and were transferred to the room at least 30 minutes before each testing session. All behavioral tests were conducted during the light phase at consistent times across experimental groups.

Open-field test (OFT)

The OFT was conducted in an independent cohort of age-matched baseline mice. The test was conducted in white PVC arenas (40 × 40 × 50 cm) under dim, diffuse illumination (∼50 lux at the arena floor). Each mouse was placed in the center of the arena and allowed to freely explore for 5 minutes. Locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior were automatically recorded and analyzed using ANY-maze software (Stoelting, USA). Total distance traveled and time spent in the center area were quantified during the 5-minute testing period. After each trial, the arena was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol to eliminate olfactory cues before introducing the next animal.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

NOR testing was conducted according to established protocols with minor modifications,73 following the experimental timelines illustrated in the corresponding figures. The NOR test was performed in white PVC arenas (40 × 40 × 50 cm) under dim, diffuse illumination (∼50 lux at the arena floor). Mice were habituated to the behavioral testing room for at least 30 minutes prior to each session. The task consisted of a habituation session (5 minutes), followed 24 hours later by a familiarization phase with two identical objects (10 minutes), and a test phase in which one familiar object was replaced with a novel object (10 minutes). Object exploration was automatically recorded and analyzed using ANY-maze software (Stoelting, USA). Total exploration time was defined as the sum of time spent exploring both objects. The novelty index was calculated as the percentage of time spent exploring the novel object relative to total exploration time.

Morris water maze (MWM) test

In the MWM test, mice were habituated to the behavioral testing room for at least 30 min prior to testing. Mice were trained in a circular tank (120 cm diameter) filled with opaque water maintained at 21 ± 1°C, with a hidden platform submerged 1 cm below the water surface in one quadrant. Animals underwent five consecutive days of acquisition training (four trials per day), starting from randomly selected positions,74 with a maximum trial duration of 60 s; mice that failed to locate the platform were guided to it and allowed to remain there briefly. On the final day, a probe trial was performed in which the platform was removed, and mice were allowed to swim freely for 60 s. Escape latency, time spent in the target quadrant, mean swimming speed, and the number of platform crossings were recorded and analyzed using ANY-maze software. Spatial memory was assessed by the percentage of target quadrant occupancy relative to chance (25%).

Tissue preparation

Tissue collection was performed 24 h after completion of the final behavioral test. Mice were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection). For molecular analyses, mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold 0.9% physiological saline to remove blood and then decapitated for rapid dissection. The primary visual cortex and hippocampus were dissected on ice within 90 s, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until use. For histological analyses, mice underwent transcardial perfusion with saline followed by perfusion with precooled 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Brains were post-fixed at 4°C for 24 h, cryoprotected in graded sucrose solutions, embedded in OCT compound, and stored at −80°C prior to cryosectioning. Sample sizes (n) for each analysis are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

Mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS (0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline, pH 7.4) to remove blood. Brains were rapidly removed, and the hippocampal cornu ammonis 1 (CA1) region was dissected on ice and trimmed into small blocks (∼1 × 2 × 2 mm). Tissue samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4°C overnight, followed by postfixation in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h at room temperature. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series and acetone, samples were infiltrated with epoxy resin (EMBed-812, SPI), embedded, and polymerized at 60°C for 48 h. Semithin sections (1.5 μm) were cut and stained with toluidine blue to confirm the CA1 region. Ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were collected onto 150-mesh formvar-coated copper grids and post-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Grids were examined using a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope operated at 80 kV, and images were acquired digitally at appropriate magnifications. For each mouse, multiple non-overlapping fields within CA1 were selected using a systematic sampling approach. Image acquisition and field selection were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Hippocampal tissues were homogenized in ice-cold PBS (containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors) at 100 mg tissue per mL buffer, incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Supernatants were collected, and total protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0010). Aβ1-42 levels were quantified via ELISA (Human Amyloid β (aa1-42) Immunoassay, R&D Systems, DAB142) per manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of total protein were loaded per well, and all samples were measured in technical triplicates. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA). Aβ1-42 concentrations were interpolated from the kit’s standard curve and expressed as pg Aβ1-42 per mg total protein. Measurements were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Immunofluorescence

Coronal brain cryosections (12 μm) were prepared using a cryostat (CryoStar NX70, Thermo Scientific) from approximately bregma −2.9 to −3.3 mm, as defined by the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas.75 Sections were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min and blocked for 15 min at room temperature using QuickBlock™ Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, P0260). Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in QuickBlock™ Primary Antibody Dilution Buffer (Beyotime, P0262), including anti-NeuN (1:500, Millipore), anti-Aβ (6E10; 1:500, BioLegend), anti-Iba1 (1:500, Abcam), anti-GFAP (1:500, Abcam), anti-p62/SQSTM1 (1:200, Abcam), anti-LC3B (1:200, Sigma), and anti-PSD95 (1:500, Abcam). After three 10-min washes in PBST (PBS, 0.01 M, pH 7.4, containing 0.1% Tween-20), sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with Cy3-conjugated donkey anti-chicken IgG (1:500, Jackson ImmunoResearch), Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (1:500, Abcam), and Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, Abcam), together with Hoechst 33342 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology) diluted in QuickBlock™ Secondary Antibody Dilution Buffer (Beyotime, P0265). Additional antibody details are provided in the key resources table. Sections were mounted and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (FV1000, Olympus) under identical acquisition settings across groups. Images were acquired from the primary visual cortex and hippocampal subfields (CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus [DG]) based on atlas-defined anatomical landmarks using ×10, ×20, ×40, or ×60 objectives. Additional digital zoom was applied when necessary. Quantification was performed in ImageJ (NIH, USA; version 1.53) by investigators blinded to group allocation. NeuN+ neurons were manually counted using the Cell Counter plugin, with neuronal identity confirmed by co-localization with Hoechst-stained nuclei. In CA1, Aβ immunoreactive burden (6E10) was quantified as the percentage of positive area relative to total ROI area using threshold-based segmentation. Neuronal Aβ accumulation was further assessed by calculating the proportion of NeuN-positive area colocalized with 6E10 immunoreactivity. For LC3 and p62, mean fluorescence intensity within neuronal somata (identified by morphology and NeuN co-staining) was measured after background subtraction. PSD95 fluorescence intensity was quantified within predefined ROIs. Quantitative fluorescence measurements (Aβ, LC3, p62, and PSD95) were normalized to the corresponding disease control group within each experimental set and batch (3xTg-AD for baseline experiments; 3xTg-AD + vehicle for 3-MA experiments). For each marker, nine evenly spaced coronal sections per mouse were analyzed in each region of interest.

Western blotting

Primary visual cortex and hippocampal tissues were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with 1× protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0010). Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were separated by SDS–PAGE on 8–15% gradient gels and transferred to 0.22-μm nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. TBST consisted of Tris-buffered saline (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies including anti-β-actin (1:2000, Boster), anti-LC3 (1:2000, Abcam), anti-p62 (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-mTOR (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p-mTOR (1:2000, Abcam), anti-AMPK (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p-AMPK (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-GAPDH (1:2000, Proteintech), anti-NR2A (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-NR2B (1:2000, Abcam), anti-NR1 (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-tubulin (1:2000, Boster), anti-SYP (1:2000, Abcam), anti-PSD95 (1:2000, Abcam), anti-Bax (1:2000, Proteintech), and anti-Bcl-2 (1:2000, Abcam) diluted in antibody dilution buffer as follows: total protein antibodies in 5% nonfat milk/TBST and phospho-specific antibodies in 5% BSA/TBST. After three 10-min washes in TBST, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (1:5000 in TBST). Additional antibody details are provided in the key resources table. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and imaged with a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ (NIH, USA) by investigators blinded to group allocation. Target protein signals were normalized to the appropriate loading control on the same membrane (β-actin, GAPDH, or β-tubulin, as indicated in the figure legends). Phosphorylated proteins were quantified as the ratio of phosphorylated to total protein.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software). Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Biological replicates (n) represent independent animals, as indicated in the figure legends. Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. For comparisons among multiple independent groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was applied as appropriate. Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used following one-way ANOVA, whereas Sidak’s test was used following two-way ANOVA when significant main effects or interactions were detected. Dunnett’s test was applied when multiple treatments were compared against a single control group. Morris water maze acquisition data were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with training day as the within-subject factor and group as the between-subject factor. The Geisser–Greenhouse correction was applied when sphericity was violated. Probe trial performance was evaluated using a one-sample t-test against chance level (25%). When parametric assumptions were not met, non-parametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis followed by Dunn’s test) were used. Detailed statistical information, including exact n values and tests used, is provided in the figure legends and results section. Complete statistical analyses are summarized in Tables S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S8, S9, S10, S11, S12, and S13.
