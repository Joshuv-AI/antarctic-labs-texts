Frustration-free free fermions and beyond
Rintaro Masaoka, Seishiro Ono, Hoi Chun Po, Haruki Watanabe

Abstract
Frustration-free Hamiltonians provide pivotal models for understanding quantum many-body systems. In this paper, we establish a general framework for frustration-free fermionic systems. First, we derive a necessary and sufficient condition for a free fermion model to be frustration-free. In the case of translation-invariant, noninteracting systems, we show that any band touching between the valence and conduction bands is at least quadratic. Furthermore, by extending the Gosset-Huang inequality to fermionic systems, we demonstrate that even in interacting and non-translation-invariant cases, the finite-size gap of gapless excitations scales as O ⁡ ( ( log ⁡ L ) 2 / L 2 ) O((\log L)^{2}/L^{2}) , provided the ground-state correlation function exhibits a power-law decay. Our results provide a foundation for studying frustration-free fermionic systems, including flat-band ferromagnetism and η \eta -pairing states.

Introduction

In condensed matter physics,
frustration
—the impossibility of minimizing all terms in the Hamiltonian simultaneously—is a key concept.
One of the most fundamental examples is geometrical frustration in magnets.
For example, in the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg model on a triangular lattice, no spin configuration can simultaneously minimize all antiferromagnetic exchange interactions.

In contrast, we say that a Hamiltonian is
frustration-free
if the ground state of the total Hamiltonian is also a ground state of each local term in the Hamiltonian.
Although frustration-free systems may appear to form a narrow class, they contain representative models of a diverse range of quantum phases of matter, such as quantum critical points, spontaneously symmetry breaking (SSB) phases, symmetry protected topological (SPT) phases, and topologically ordered phases.

U(1)-symmetric free fermionic systems

In this section, we consider noninteracting fermions described by a tight-binding model with U(1) symmetry.

II.1
Setting

In general, the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
is given by a sum of local terms
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
, which take the quadratic form

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{R}^{\prime},\bm{R}^{\prime\prime}\in\Lambda}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\bm{R}}}\hat{c}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau}^{\dagger}(t_{\bm{R}})_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau,\bm{R}^{\prime\prime}\tau^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\bm{R}^{\prime\prime}\tau^{\prime}}+C_{\bm{R}}.

(1)

Here,
C_{\bm{R}}
is a constant that will be chosen later,
\bm{R}\in\Lambda
is the unit cell label, and the indices
\tau,\tau^{\prime}=1,2,\cdots,N_{\bm{R}}
include all internal degrees of freedom.
The creation/annihilation operators satisfy the anti-commutation relations
\{\hat{c}_{\bm{R}\tau},\hat{c}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{\bm{R},\bm{R}^{\prime}}\delta_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}
and
\{\hat{c}_{\bm{R}\tau},\hat{c}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}}\}=0
. The Hamiltonian is local in the sense that
(t_{\bm{R}})_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau,\bm{R}^{\prime\prime}\tau^{\prime}}=0
whenever
|\bm{R}^{\prime}-\bm{R}|>r
or
|\bm{R}^{\prime\prime}-\bm{R}|>r
. This
r
is called the range of the hopping, which is assumed to be finite and independent of the system size.
Hence, we can regard the hopping amplitude
(t_{\bm{R}})_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau,\bm{R}^{\prime\prime}\tau^{\prime}}
in Eq. (
1
) as a matrix with indices
\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau
and
\bm{R}^{\prime\prime}\tau^{\prime}
and
\hat{c}_{\bm{R}^{\prime\prime}\tau^{\prime}}
as a vector with an index
\bm{R}^{\prime\prime}\tau^{\prime}
. Then the local term can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{R}}^{\dagger}h_{\bm{R}}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{R}}+C_{\bm{R}}.

(2)

We note that the frustration-free condition derived in this work has been extended to quasi-local Hamiltonians with interaction tails decaying faster than any power law
[
36

37
]
.

The general expressions in Eqs. (
1
) and (

II.2
Condition for frustration-freeness

First, let us derive the condition for the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
to be frustration-free. If the ground state energy of the local Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
is
E_{\bm{R}}
, then
\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
is frustration free if and only if there exists a state
|\Phi\rangle
such that

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle=E_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle.

(3)

for all
\bm{R}
simultaneously.

For example, when
C_{\bm{R}}=0
and
h_{\bm{R}}
is positive-semidefinite (i.e., its eigenvalues are all nonnegative), then the Fock vacuum
|0\rangle
satisfies this condition. In this sense, any tight-binding model is frustration-free as long as the chemical potential is set to sufficiently low.

To investigate the possibility of more nontrivial ground states,
let
\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}>0
(
\alpha=1,2,\cdots,A_{\bm{R}}
) be positive eigenvalues of
h_{\bm{R}}
and
\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}
be the corresponding orthonormalized eigenvectors.
Also, let
-\nu_{\bm{R}\beta}<0
(
\beta=1,2,\cdots,B_{\bm{R}}
) be negative eigenvalues and
\bm{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta}
be the corresponding orthonormalized eigenvectors.
There might be eigenvectors with precisely zero eigenvalues but they will not be used in the following discussions. It follows by definition that

\displaystyle h_{\bm{R}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{A_{\bm{R}}}\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}-\sum_{\beta=1}^{B_{\bm{R}}}\nu_{\bm{R}\beta}\bm{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta}\bm{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta}^{\dagger}.

(4)

We define corresponding annihilation operators by

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\coloneqq\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{R}},

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta}\coloneqq\bm{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta}^{\dagger}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{R}}.

(6)

By construction, these operators within the same unit cell
\bm{R}
satisfy

\displaystyle\{\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}},\quad\{\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta},\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{\beta,\beta^{\prime}},

(7)

\displaystyle\{\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta}^{\dagger}\}=0

(8)

for any
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}
. For different unit cells
\bm{R}^{\prime}\neq\bm{R}

\{\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}
,
\{\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta},\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}

\displaystyle\{\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\}=\{\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta},\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}\}=\{\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\beta}\}=0

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}^{(+)}

(10)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}^{(-)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\beta=1}^{B_{\bm{R}}}\nu_{\bm{R}\beta}\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta}\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta}^{\dagger}.

Note that both

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}|\Phi\rangle=\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\beta}^{\dagger}|\Phi\rangle=0.

(12)

for all
\bm{R}

\bm{R}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\{\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\beta}^{\dagger}\}=0\quad\text{for all $\bm{R}$, $\bm{R}^{\prime}$, $\alpha$, $\beta$.}

The necessity of Eq. (

II.3
Translation invariant models

Consider a translation-invariant frustration-free free fermionic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}}\hat{H}^{\prime}_{\bm{R}}
.
Let
\hat{T}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}
denote the translation operator by a vector
\bm{R}^{\prime}
.
Note that the translation invariance of the total Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
does not necessarily imply that the local terms
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{\bm{R}}
respect the translation symmetry, i.e.,
\hat{T}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}\hat{H}^{\prime}_{\bm{R}}\hat{T}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}=\hat{H}^{\prime}_{\bm{R}+\bm{R}^{\prime}}
. However, we can always construct a frustration-free decomposition
\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
that satisfies this condition by defining

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}\hat{T}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}\hat{H}^{\prime}_{\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime}}\hat{T}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}^{\dagger},

(16)

where
V
is the number of unit cells
2
2

2

Note, however, that
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
constructed this way may depend on
V
.
. Consequently, we may drop the subscript
\bm{R}
from
N_{\bm{R}}
,
A_{\bm{R}}
,
B_{\bm{R}}
,
\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}
, and
\nu_{\bm{R}\beta}
.
The tight-binding model
\hat{H}
has
N_{\bm{R}}=N
bands in total.
Introducing the Fourier transformation by
\hat{c}_{\bm{R}\tau}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\tau}^{\dagger}
(
\tau=1,2,\cdots,N

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}^{(+)}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{k}},

(17)

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}^{(-)}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\Big(\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{k}}+\sum_{\beta=1}^{B}\nu_{\beta}\Big).

(18)

Here,
\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}=(\hat{c}_{\bm{k}1}^{\dagger},\cdots,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}N}^{\dagger})
is the
N
-component row vector and
H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}
and
H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}
are
N
-dimensional Hermitian matrices.
The commutation relation in Eq. (
15
) implies
[H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)},H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}]=0
.
Therefore, the band structure of the
N
-dimensional matrix
H_{\bm{k}}\coloneqq H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}+H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}
can be computed by diagonalizing
H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}
and
H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}
separately.
This implies that the band dispersions of
H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}
and
H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}

\bm{k}(t)
in the Brillouin zone (see Appendix
A

Among the eigenvalues of
H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}
, suppose
\omega_{\bm{k}n}^{(+)}\geq 0
(
n=1,2,\cdots,N^{(+)}
) are not identically zero on the entire Brillouin zone.
Namely,
\omega_{\bm{k}n}^{(+)}>0
at some
\bm{k}
.
Similarly, the eigenvalues
\omega_{\bm{k}m}^{(-)}\leq 0
(
m=1,2,\cdots,N^{(-)}
) of
H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}
are not identically zero.
As a result, other
N-N^{(+)}-N^{(-)}\geq 0

0

In general, the numbers of bands are bounded as

\displaystyle N^{(+)}\leq A,\quad N^{(-)}\leq B,\quad N^{(+)}+N^{(-)}\leq N.

(19)

A ground state of
\hat{H}
can be obtained by fully occupying all negative energy states
\omega_{\bm{k}m}^{(-)}<0
and leaving all positive energy states
\omega_{\bm{k}n}^{(+)}>0
completely unoccupied. Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta=1}^{B}\nu_{\beta}=-\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{\bm{k}}\sum_{m=1}^{N^{(-)}}\omega_{\bm{k}m}^{(-)}.

(20)

Zero-energy states, if exist, can be either occupied or unoccupied. Hence, if there are
N_{0}
zero-energy states, the ground state degeneracy is
2^{N_{0}}
-fold. If a flat band exists, the ground state degeneracy is exponentially large.

The system is gapless if and only if at least one of the bands touches the zero-energy level. For example, suppose
\omega_{\bm{k}m}^{(+)}=0
at
\bm{k}=\bm{k}_{0}^{(+)}
. Then the analyticity of
\omega_{\bm{k}m}^{(+)}
combined with
\omega_{\bm{k}m}^{(+)}\geq 0
implies that the form of band dispersion around
\bm{k}=\bm{k}_{0}^{(+)}
is quadratic or softer, i.e.,
\omega_{\bm{k}m}^{(+)}=O(|\bm{k}-\bm{k}_{0}^{(+)})|^{2})
. The same argument applies to
\omega_{\bm{k}m}^{(-)}\leq 0
. Namely, when
\omega_{\bm{k}m}^{(-)}=0

\bm{k}=\bm{k}_{0}^{(-)}
, then

.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\quad+U\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\bm{R}}}\hat{c}_{\bm{R}\tau\uparrow}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\bm{R}\tau\uparrow}\hat{c}_{\bm{R}\tau\downarrow}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\bm{R}\tau\downarrow}.

(26)

Suppose that
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
is positive semidefinite and
\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
at
U=0
has a flat band.
Let

be the fully polarized state in which all zero-energy single-particle states are occupied by spin-up electrons. Then, for any

and its SU(2)-rotated version

.

II.5
Frustration-freeness in momentum space

The Fourier transforms of Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
) yield

\displaystyle H^{(+)}_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}[\sqrt{\mu_{\alpha}}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}(e^{{\mathrm{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{1}},\ldots,e^{{\mathrm{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{d}})]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times[\sqrt{\mu_{\alpha}}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}(e^{{\mathrm{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{1}},\ldots,e^{{\mathrm{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{d}})]^{\dagger},

(27)

\displaystyle-H^{(-)}_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta}[\sqrt{\nu_{\beta}}\bm{\phi}_{\beta}(e^{{\mathrm{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{1}},\ldots,e^{{\mathrm{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{d}})]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times[\sqrt{\nu_{\beta}}\bm{\phi}_{\beta}(e^{{\mathrm{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{1}},\ldots,e^{{\mathrm{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{d}})]^{\dagger},

(28)

where the matrices
H^{(+)}_{\bm{k}}
and
H^{(-)}_{\bm{k}}
are defined in Eqs. (
17
) and (
18
) and
\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}
and
\bm{\phi}_{\beta}
are finite-degree vector polynomials in
e^{{\mathrm{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{j}}
(
j=1,2,\ldots,d
) defined by the Fourier transforms of Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
).
Note that the above equations are identities of Laurent polynomials, not just an equality on the discrete momentum points in the Brillouin zone, if the system size is sufficiently large
3
3

3

If the side-lengths of the system are more than twice of the maximum width of local orbials
\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}
and
\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta}
, then the momentum points are dense enough to determine polynomials in Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
) uniquely.
.
Thus, frustration-freeness requires that positive semidefinite Laurent polynomials
H^{(+)}_{\bm{k}}
and
-H^{(-)}_{\bm{k}}
can be expressed as sums of squares of finite-degree polynomials.
Conversely, if there exist finite-degree vector polynomials
\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}
and
\bm{\phi}_{\beta}
such that Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
) hold, we can define local orbitals
\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}
and
\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\beta}
by the inverse Fourier transforms of Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
).
Therefore, frustration-freeness in translation-invariant free fermionic systems is equivalent to the existence of sum-of-squares decompositions of Laurent polynomials
\pm H^{(\pm)}_{\bm{k}}
.

While finding such decompositions is not a simple task in general, it is necessary that
H^{(\pm)}_{\bm{k}}
are at least Laurent polynomials for the system to be frustration-free.
This necessary condition can be used to rule out frustration-freeness in most cases.

A natural question is whether positive semidefinite Laurent polynomials
\pm H^{(\pm)}_{\bm{k}}
always imply the existence of sum-of-squares decompositions as in Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
) and hence frustration-freeness.
Actually, this question has been extensively studied in mathematics. In one or two dimensions, any positive semidefinite Laurent polynomial can be expressed as a sum of squares of polynomials
[
44
,
45
]
, while in three or higher dimensions
d\geq 3
, there are counterexamples
[
46
]
.
Therefore, in
d=1,2
, the necessary condition above is also sufficient for frustration-freeness, while in
d\geq 3
, there are cases where the necessary condition holds but the system is not frustration-free.

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{b}_{\bm{R}}-\hat{a}_{\bm{R}}+\hat{d}_{\bm{R}-\bm{a}_{1}}-\hat{b}_{\bm{R}-\bm{a}_{1}}+\hat{a}_{\bm{R}-\bm{a}_{2}}-\hat{d}_{\bm{R}-\bm{a}_{2}}}{\sqrt{6}},

(71)

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{\bm{R}4}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{a}_{\bm{R}-\bm{a}_{2}}-\hat{b}_{\bm{R}-\bm{a}_{1}}+\hat{c}_{\bm{R}-\bm{a}_{1}}-\hat{a}_{\bm{R}-\bm{a}_{3}}+\hat{b}_{\bm{R}-\bm{a}_{3}}-\hat{c}_{\bm{R}-\bm{a}_{2}}}{\sqrt{6}}.

(72)

We find that if we use all of these four hexagonal states by adding

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{H}}^{(-)}=\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\sum_{\ell=1}^{4}\tilde{\nu}\hat{w}_{\bm{R}\ell}\hat{w}_{\bm{R}\ell}^{\dagger}

(73)

to the Hamiltonian, we can induce a dispersion

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{\bm{k}1}^{(-)}=\tilde{\omega}_{\bm{k}2}^{(-)}=-\frac{\tilde{\nu}}{3}e_{\bm{k}}

(74)

to the flat bands without breaking the symmetry of the pyrochlore lattice.

IV
U(1) breaking models

So far, we have considered free fermions with U(1) symmetry.
In this section, we discuss the frustration-free condition without U(1) symmetry.

IV.1
Derivation of BdG form

Let us first show that the most general local Hamiltonian can be expressed in the Bogoliubov-de Gennes (BdG) form:
\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
with

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}=\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{R}}^{\dagger}h_{\bm{R}}\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{R}}+C_{\bm{R}},

(75)

where
\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{R}}^{\dagger}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{R}}^{\dagger}&\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{R}}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}

is the Nambu spinor and
h_{\bm{R}}
is a
2N_{r}
-dimensional matrix with the particle-hole symmetry

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{P}}h_{\bm{R}}^{\top}U_{\mathcal{P}}^{\dagger}=-h_{\bm{R}},\quad U_{\mathcal{P}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}O&\mathbbm{1}\\
\mathbbm{1}&O\end{pmatrix}.

(76)

The Nambu spinor contains
N_{r}
pairs of creation and annihilation operators and satisfies
\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{R}}^{\dagger}=\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{R}}^{\top}U_{\mathcal{P}}
.

To derive the BdG form, let us start with a general Hamiltonian on the Majorana basis:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}\coloneqq i\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\bm{R}}^{\top}A_{\bm{R}}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\bm{R}}+C_{\bm{R}},

(77)

where
A_{\bm{R}}
is a
2N_{r}
-dimensional real antisymmetric matrix and
\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\bm{R}}
is a vector composed of
2N_{r}
Majorana fermions
\hat{\gamma}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau i}
within the range
r
from
\bm{R}
.
The Majorana operators satisfy
\hat{\gamma}_{\bm{R}\tau i}^{\dagger}=\hat{\gamma}_{\bm{R}\tau i}

(i=1,2)
and
\{\hat{\gamma}_{\bm{R}\tau i},\hat{\gamma}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}j}\}=2\delta_{\bm{R}\bm{R}^{\prime}}\delta_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}\delta_{ij}
.
We define complex fermions by

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{\bm{R}\tau}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{\gamma}_{\bm{R}\tau 1}+i\hat{\gamma}_{\bm{R}\tau 2}\right).

(78)

Then the vector
\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{R}}
and the matrix
h_{\bm{R}}
in the BdG form are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{R}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}&i\mathbbm{1}\\
\mathbbm{1}&-i\mathbbm{1}\end{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\bm{R}},

(79)

\displaystyle h_{\bm{R}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}&i\mathbbm{1}\\
\mathbbm{1}&-i\mathbbm{1}\end{pmatrix}iA_{\bm{R}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}&\mathbbm{1}\\
-i\mathbbm{1}&i\mathbbm{1}\end{pmatrix}.

(80)

For example, the symmetry condition (
76
) can be shown as

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{P}}h_{\bm{R}}^{\top}U_{\mathcal{P}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}O&\mathbbm{1}\\
\mathbbm{1}&O\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}&-i\mathbbm{1}\\
\mathbbm{1}&i\mathbbm{1}\end{pmatrix}iA_{\bm{R}}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}&\mathbbm{1}\\
i\mathbbm{1}&-i\mathbbm{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}O&\mathbbm{1}\\
\mathbbm{1}&O\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}&i\mathbbm{1}\\
\mathbbm{1}&-i\mathbbm{1}\end{pmatrix}(-iA_{\bm{R}})\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}&\mathbbm{1}\\
-i\mathbbm{1}&i\mathbbm{1}\end{pmatrix}=-h_{\bm{R}}.

(81)

IV.2
Frustration-free condition

Now, we are ready to derive the frustration-free condition. Let

\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}

(\alpha=1,2,\cdots,A_{\bm{R}})
be eigenvectors of
h_{\bm{R}}
with positive eigenvalues
\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}/2>0
.
Due to the symmetry relation (
76
),
\bm{\phi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\coloneqq U_{\mathcal{P}}\bm{\psi}^{*}_{\bm{R}\alpha}
is an eigenvector with a negative eigenvalue
-\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}/2<0
; that is,

\displaystyle h_{\bm{R}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{A_{\bm{R}}}\frac{\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}}{2}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{A_{\bm{R}}}\frac{\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}}{2}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{*}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\top}.

(82)

We define corresponding fermionic operators by

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}=\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{R}}.

(83)

In this case,
\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}=\bm{\phi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{R}}
coincides with
\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}
and we will not use it in the following. The anticommutation relations

\displaystyle\{\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha^{\prime}}=\delta_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}

(84)

and
\{\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\}=\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\bm{\phi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}=0
follow from the anticommutation relations among
\hat{c}_{\bm{R}\tau}
and the orthogonality of eigenvectors of
h_{\bm{R}}
.

Choosing
C_{\bm{R}}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{A_{\bm{R}}}\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}/2
, we rewrite
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{A_{\bm{R}}}\frac{\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}}{2}\big(\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}+\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\hat{\phi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{A_{\bm{R}}}\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}.

(85)

Consider a state given by
\ket{\Phi_{\bm{R}}}\coloneqq\prod_{\alpha=1}^{A_{\bm{R}}}\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\ket{0}
, which satisfies
\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\ket{\Phi_{\bm{R}}}=0
for all
\alpha
.
Hence,
\ket{\Phi_{\bm{R}}}
is a ground state of
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
and satisfies
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}\ket{\Phi_{\bm{R}}}=0
.
Following the same discussion as the U(1)-symmetric case, we arrive at the frustration-free condition

\displaystyle\{\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\}=0\quad\text{for all $\bm{R}$, $\bm{R}^{\prime}$, $\alpha$, $\alpha^{\prime}$.}

(86)

The Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{A_{\bm{R}}}\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}

(87)

with anticommutation relation in (
84
) and (
86
) can be accurately represented by an auxiliary U(1) symmetric Hamiltonian in which the number of fermion species is doubled. To see this correspondence concretely, let us introduce
\hat{f}_{\bm{R}\tau i}
(
i=1,2
) for every
\hat{c}_{\bm{R}\tau}
.
These operators are complex fermions satisfying

\{\hat{f}_{\bm{R}\tau i},\hat{f}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}j}\}=0
and
\{\hat{f}_{\bm{R}\tau i},\hat{f}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}j}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{\bm{R},\bm{R}^{\prime}}\delta_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\delta_{i,j}
.
We replace the Nambu spinor
\hat{\Psi}_{\bm{R}}^{\dagger}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{R}}^{\dagger}&\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{R}}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}
by

\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\bm{R}}^{\dagger}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{f}}_{\bm{R},1}^{\dagger}&\hat{\bm{f}}_{\bm{R},2}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}
and
h_{\bm{R}}
in Eq. (
82
) by

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\bm{R}}\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha=1}^{A_{\bm{R}}}\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}.

(88)

The operator
\hat{\tilde{\psi}}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\coloneqq\bm{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\bm{R}}

satisfies not only

\displaystyle\{\hat{\tilde{\psi}}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\tilde{\psi}}_{\bm{R}\alpha^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}

(89)

within a unit cell
\bm{R}
but also

\displaystyle\{\hat{\tilde{\psi}}_{\bm{R}\alpha},\hat{\tilde{\psi}}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\}=0\quad\text{for all $\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\bm{R},\bm{R}^{\prime}$}

(90)

as a trivial relation for the U(1) symmetric case. Observe that the anticommutation relations of
\hat{\tilde{\psi}}_{\bm{R}\alpha}
’s in (
89
) and (
90
) and
\hat{\psi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}
’s in (
84
) and (
86
) are the same.
As a consequence, the spectrum of

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{H}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\bm{R}}^{\dagger}\tilde{h}_{\bm{R}}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\bm{R}}=\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{A_{\bm{R}}}\mu_{\bm{R}\alpha}\hat{\tilde{\psi}}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{\psi}}_{\bm{R}\alpha}

(91)

and
\hat{H}
in (
87
) is identical, except for the degeneracy originating from the flat bands associated with
\hat{\tilde{\phi}}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\coloneqq\bm{\phi}_{\bm{R}\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\tilde{\Psi}}_{\bm{R}}
.
In particular, if the system is gapless then the low-energy dispersion is quadratic or softer as shown in Sec.
II
. In Appendix
B
, we present an alternative derivation which does not invoke the auxiliary fermionic system.

IV.3
Example

As an example, let us discuss a fermionic model that corresponds to the combination of the XY spin chain under a magnetic field and the cluster model:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\Big(J_{x}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{x}+J_{y}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{y}\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{y}+B\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}-A\hat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{x}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{x}\Big).

(92)

Introducing the Jordan-Wigner transformation

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}

\displaystyle=e^{i\pi\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}}(\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}+\hat{c}_{i}),

(93)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{y}

\displaystyle=-ie^{i\pi\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}}(\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}-\hat{c}_{i}),

(94)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}

\displaystyle=2\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i}-1,

(95)

we obtain the fermionic representation

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\Big(-t(\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i+1}+\hat{c}_{i+1}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i})-\Delta(\hat{c}_{i+1}\hat{c}_{i}+\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i+1}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle+\mu(\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i}-1/2)+\frac{1}{2}t^{\prime}(\hat{c}_{i-1}^{\dagger}-\hat{c}_{i-1})(\hat{c}_{i+1}^{\dagger}+\hat{c}_{i+1})\Big),

(96)

where
t=-(J_{x}+J_{y})
,
\Delta=J_{y}-J_{x}
,
\mu=2B
, and
t^{\prime}=2A
. We assume the periodic boundary condition for the fermionic Hamiltonian.

IV.3.1
The Kitaev chain

Let us start with the case
t^{\prime}=0
. The model is equivalent to the Kitaev chain
[
48
]
, whose local Hamiltonian can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}\coloneqq\frac{t_{0}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{\Psi}_{i}^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\mu}{2}&-t&0&-\Delta\\
-t&\frac{\mu}{2}&\Delta&0\\
0&\Delta&-\frac{\mu}{2}&t\\
-\Delta&0&t&-\frac{\mu}{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\hat{\Psi}_{i}

(97)

with
\hat{\Psi}_{i}^{\top}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{i}&\hat{c}_{i+1}&\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}&\hat{c}_{i+1}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}
. When
t=t_{0}/2>0
,
\mu=t_{0}\cos 2\theta
, and
\Delta=-t_{0}\sin\theta\cos\theta
, the Hamiltonian is frustration-free regardless of the choice of
\theta

[
20
]
4
4

4

In Ref.
[
20
]
, a nearest-neighbor interaction
U
is added while maintaining the frustration-free property of the model.
. Indeed, we find
\hat{H}_{i}=t_{0}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{i}
with

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{i}=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{c}_{i}-\hat{c}_{i+1})+\frac{\sin\theta}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}+\hat{c}_{i+1}^{\dagger})

(98)

and
\{\hat{\psi}_{i},\hat{\psi}_{j}\}=0
for any
i,j
.

If we define the operator

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}e^{-ikj}\hat{\psi}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{2}}(1-e^{ik})\hat{c}_{k}+\frac{\sin\theta}{\sqrt{2}}(1+e^{ik})\hat{c}_{-k}^{\dagger},

(99)

which satisfies
\{\hat{\alpha}_{k},\hat{\alpha}_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}(1-\cos 2\theta\cos k)
, the Hamiltonian is written as
\hat{H}=\sum_{k}t_{0}\hat{\alpha}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\alpha}_{k}
. Therefore, the low-energy dispersion is given by
\omega_{k}=t_{0}(1-\cos 2\theta\cos k)
. Unless
\theta\neq 0
or
\pi/2
, the excitation gap originates from
\Delta\neq 0
. When
\theta=0
or
\pi/2
,
\Delta
vanishes and
\omega_{k}
is gapless and quadratic
[
21
]
.

This dispersion can also be derived from the standard method. After the Fourier transformation, the BdG Hamiltonian can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\frac{Lt_{0}}{2}+\sum_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}&\hat{c}_{-k}\end{pmatrix}(h_{k}\sigma_{3}+\delta_{k}\sigma_{2})\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{k}\\
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{-k}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{k},

(100)

where
h_{k}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mu-t\cos k
and
\delta_{k}=\Delta\sin k
, and
\omega_{k}=2\sqrt{h_{k}^{2}+\delta_{k}^{2}}=t_{0}(1-\cos 2\theta\cos k)
.

IV.3.2
Kinetic Ising model

Next, let us consider the case
t^{\prime}\neq 0
.

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}

\displaystyle=t_{0}+\frac{1}{4}\hat{\Psi}_{i}^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}0&-t&t^{\prime}&0&-\Delta&t^{\prime}\\
-t&2\mu&-t&\Delta&0&-\Delta\\
t^{\prime}&-t&0&-t^{\prime}&\Delta&0\\
0&\Delta&-t^{\prime}&0&t&-t^{\prime}\\
-\Delta&0&\Delta&t&-2\mu&t\\
t^{\prime}&-\Delta&0&-t^{\prime}&t&0\end{pmatrix}\hat{\Psi}_{i},

(101)

where

\hat{\Psi}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{i-1}&\hat{c}_{i}&\hat{c}_{i+1}&\hat{c}_{i-1}^{\dagger}&\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}&\hat{c}_{i+1}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
.
When
t=\Delta=t_{0}\sin\theta\cos\theta
(
t_{0}>0
),
\mu=t_{0}\cos^{2}\theta
, and
t^{\prime}=t_{0}\sin^{2}\theta
, the Hamiltonian is frustration-free. We find
\hat{H}_{i}=t_{0}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{i}
with

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\cos\theta(\hat{c}_{i-1}+\hat{c}_{i+1}-\hat{c}_{i-1}^{\dagger}+\hat{c}_{i+1}^{\dagger})-\sin\theta\,\hat{c}_{i}.

(102)

Since
\{\hat{\psi}_{i},\hat{\psi}_{j}\}=0
for any
i,j
, the Hamiltonian is frustration free.

The operator

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}e^{-ikj}\hat{\psi}_{j}

\displaystyle=\cos\theta(\cos k\hat{c}_{k}+i\sin k\hat{c}_{-k}^{\dagger})-\sin\theta\hat{c}_{k}

(103)

satisfies
\{\hat{\alpha}_{k},\hat{\alpha}_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}(1-\sin 2\theta\cos k)
and the Hamiltonian becomes
\hat{H}=\sum_{k}t_{0}\hat{\alpha}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\alpha}_{k}
. Hence, the dispersion is given by
\omega_{k}=t_{0}(1-\sin 2\theta\cos k)
.

This model can be mapped to the kinetic Ising model by replacing
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}\to\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\to-\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}
and setting
2\beta J=\mathrm{arctanh}(\sin 2\theta)
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}=\frac{t_{0}}{2\cosh(\beta J(\hat{\sigma}_{i-1}^z+\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^z))}\Big(e^{-\beta J\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}(\hat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{z}+\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{z})}-\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}\Big).

(104)

At
\theta=\pi/4
, which corresponds to
\beta J=\infty
(i.e., the zero temperature), the model reduces to the uncle Hamiltonian for the GHZ state:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}=\frac{t_{0}}{4}\big(2-\hat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}-\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{z}-\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}+\hat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{z}\big).

(105)

The gapless excitation of the model is associated with the critical nature of the classical Ising model at zero temperature.

V
Topological bands in frustration-free free-fermionic models

In this section, we investigate what kinds of topological phases can be realized by frustration-free free fermionic Hamiltonians.

V.1
Translationally invariance and Wannier localizability

We consider the Hamiltonian in momentum space:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}H_{\bm{k}}\hat{\bm{c}}_{\bm{k}}.

(106)

The matrix
H_{\bm{k}}
can always be decomposed into two Hermitian matrices
H^{(+)}_{\bm{k}}
and
H^{(-)}_{\bm{k}}
:

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=H^{(+)}_{\bm{k}}+H^{(-)}_{\bm{k}},

(107)

whose eigenvalues are denoted by
\omega_{n\bm{k}}^{(+)}\geq 0\ (n=1,2,\cdots,N^{(+)})
and
\omega_{m\bm{k}}^{(-)}\leq 0\ (m=1,2,\cdots,N^{(-)})
, respectively.

We prove the following theorem: Consider a translationally invariant free fermionic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
with the corresponding Hermitian matrix decomposition
H_{\bm{k}}=H^{(+)}_{\bm{k}}+H^{(-)}_{\bm{k}}
.
If
\hat{H}
is frustration-free and
\omega_{m\bm{k}}^{(-)}<0
for all
m
and
\bm{k}
, there exists an (over)complete set of compactly supported Wannier-type functions.

What we have to show is as follows.
Let us define the space of occupied states by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{occ}}\coloneqq\mathrm{Span}_{n,\bm{k}}\left\{\ket{\gamma_{n\bm{k}}}:\hat{H}\ket{\gamma_{n\bm{k}}}=\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}\ket{\gamma_{n\bm{k}}},\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}<0\right\},

(108)

where
\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{k},n}\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}\hat{\gamma}_{n\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{n\bm{k}}
and
|\gamma_{n\bm{k}}\rangle=\hat{\gamma}_{n\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\ket{0}
.
It suffices to show that there exists an (over)complete set of orbitals
\{\ket{f_{n}}\}_{n}
such that they span
\mathcal{E}_{\text{occ}}
and their supports are strictly finite.
We claim that
\{\ket{\phi_{\bm{R}\beta}}\coloneqq\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}\beta}\ket{0}\}_{R,\beta}
is indeed such a set.

To prove this claim, we use the following basic knowledge of free-fermionic systems:
Let
\{|f_{n}\rangle\coloneqq\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{n}\ket{0}\}_{n=1}^{M}
be a set of mutually orthonormal one-particle states.
Define the space
\mathcal{W}_{f}\coloneqq\mathrm{Span}\left\{|f_{n}\rangle\right\}_{n=1}^{M}
and the orthogonal projector
P_{\mathcal{W}_{f}}\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{M}|f_{n}\rangle\langle f_{n}|
.
Suppose that a ground state
|\Phi\rangle
is the Slater determinant of the
f
-orbitals, i.e.,

|\Phi\rangle=\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{2}\cdots\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{M}\ket{0}.

(109)

Then, the projector is equivalent to the two-point correlation function:

\displaystyle{}_{ij}=\langle i|P_{\mathcal{W}_{f}}|j\rangle=\langle\Phi|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{c}_{i}|\Phi\rangle,

(110)

where
\{|i\rangle\coloneqq\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}|0\rangle\}_{i}
is a basis set of the one-particle Hilbert space.
For completeness, we provide the derivation of the relation (
110
) in Appendix
C
.

Now, we are ready to prove the existence of the overcomplete set of compactly supported Wannier-type functions.
Since we are interested in a gapped free fermionic Hamiltonian, the ground state is unique and is constructed by filling the orbitals defined by
\{\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}\beta}\}_{\bm{R},\beta}
.
We can always construct an orthonormal set of orbitals
\{|f_{n}\rangle\}_{n=1}^{VN^{(-)}}
from linear combinations of
\{|\phi_{\bm{R}\beta}\rangle\}_{\bm{R},\beta}
, and the ground state is written as the Slater determinant of these
f
-orbitals.
Alternatively, the same ground state can be expressed as the Slater determinant of
\{\gamma^{\dagger}_{n\bm{k}}\}_{n,\bm{k}}
.
Consequently, the projector onto
\mathrm{Span}\{|\phi_{\bm{R}\beta}\rangle\}_{\bm{R},\beta}
and that onto
\mathcal{E}_{\text{occ}}
coincide.
Thus, the spaces are identical.
We conclude that
\{|\phi_{\bm{R}\beta}\rangle\}_{\bm{R},\beta}
is an (over)complete set of orbitals spanning
\mathcal{E}_{\text{occ}}
.
By definition,
\hat{\phi}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}\beta}
has strictly local support around
\bm{R}
.
This completes the proof.

An analogous argument holds for the case where
\omega_{n\bm{k}}^{(+)}>0
for all
n
and
\bm{k}
.
In this case,
\{\ket{\psi_{\bm{R}\alpha}}\coloneqq\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}\alpha}\ket{0}\}_{\bm{R},\alpha}
constitutes an overcomplete set of compactly supported Wannier-type orbitals that span
\mathcal{E}_{\text{unocc}}
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{unocc}}\coloneqq\mathrm{Span}_{n,\bm{k}}\left\{\ket{\gamma_{n\bm{k}}}:\hat{H}\ket{\gamma_{n\bm{k}}}=\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}\ket{\gamma_{n\bm{k}}},\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}>0\right\}.

(111)

The proof proceeds exactly as in the case where
\omega_{m\bm{k}}^{(-)}<0
for all
m
and
\bm{k}
.

It is natural to ask what kinds of topological phases allow for overcomplete sets of compactly supported Wannier-type functions.
Ref.
[
28
]
addresses this question, classifying topological phases that admit such Wannier-type functions. It shows that they exist only in topological phases originating from the stacking of zero- and one-dimensional topological phases in the Altland-Zirnbauer symmetry classes.

We present our argument on frustration-free realizable topological phases by combining free fermionic frustration-freeness and the results of Ref.
[
28
]
.
If either
\omega_{m\bm{k}}^{(-)}<0
or
\omega_{n\bm{k}}^{(+)}>0
holds, then
H^{(-)}_{\bm{k}}
or
H^{(+)}_{\bm{k}}
admits an overcomplete set of compactly supported Wannier-type functions.
As a result, the allowed stable topological phases are limited to the stacking of zero- and one-dimensional topological phases, as shown in Ref.
[
28
]
.
Since a system must be topologically trivial when all bands are occupied,
H_{\bm{k}}^{(\pm)}
must possess opposite topological invariants.
Thus, the topological restriction on one of
H^{(\pm)}_{\bm{k}}
applies to the other as well.
We arrive at the following theorem:
For translationally invariant frustration-free free fermionic Hamiltonians, if
\omega_{m\bm{k}}^{(-)}<0
or
\omega_{n\bm{k}}^{(+)}>0
, stable topological phases arise from stacking of zero- and one-dimensional topological phases.
This theorem implies that other gapped topological phases, such as Chern insulators and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topological insulators, cannot be realized by frustration-free free fermionic Hamiltonians.

We make three remarks.
First, we emphasize that strict locality is essential for the preceding argument.
Various topological phases, such as those with nontrivial three-dimensional winding numbers in class AIII, are known to be Wannierizable in the sense that they admit
exponentially
localized Wannier functions
[
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
.
However, such phases cannot be realized by frustration-free free fermionic Hamiltonians due to the lack of compactly supported Wannier-type functions.
Second, we cannot rule out nontrivial topology beyond Ref.
[
28
]
if there exist two momenta
\bm{k}_{1}
and
\bm{k}_{2}\ (\bm{k}_{1}\neq\bm{k}_{2})
such that
\omega_{m\bm{k}_{1}}^{(-)}=0
and
\omega_{n\bm{k}_{2}}^{(+)}=0
.
While the system is gapless, we can still discuss band topology of
H_{\bm{k}}^{(\pm)}
even in this case.
However, our argument does not apply immediately to such a system.
Finally, we do not consider crystalline symmetries beyond lattice translation symmetry.
Recent studies demonstrate that topological crystalline phases can be constructed from lower-dimensional topological phases protected by onsite symmetries
[
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
,
62
]
.
Consequently, topological crystalline phases arising from decoupled wire constructions of one-dimensional topological phases can be realized by frustration-free free fermionic Hamiltonians, provided that each building block can be frustration-free. Furthermore, we conjecture that even in the presence of crystalline symmetries, frustration-free realizable free fermionic topological phases are limited to obstructed atomic limits
[
63
,
64
]
and decoupled wire constructions of one-dimensional topological phases.

V.2
Examples

We examine several examples to illustrate the validity of the general statement presented above.
In Sec.
II.5
, we show that if a given free fermionic Hamiltonian is frustration-free, then
H_{\bm{k}}^{(\pm)}
are Laurent polynomials of
e^{{\mathrm{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_{i}}\ (i=1,2,\cdots,d)
.
By contraposition, if
H_{\bm{k}}^{(\pm)}
are not Laurent polynomials, then the Hamiltonian cannot be frustration-free.
We utilize this statement to show that a given free fermionic Hamiltonian is not frustration-free.

V.2.1
Kitaev chain

We begin with the Kitaev chain introduced in Sec.
IV.3.1
, whose Hamiltonian in momentum space is given in Eq. (
100
).
The Hermitian matrix
H_{k}
and the energy dispersion
\epsilon_{k}
are given by

\displaystyle H_{k}=(-t\cos k+\mu/2)\sigma_{z}+\Delta\sin k\sigma_{y},

(112)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}=\sqrt{(t\cos k-\mu/2)^{2}+(\Delta\sin k)^{2}}.

We can diagonalize
H_{k}
and obtain eigenvectors
\Psi_{k,\pm}
corresponding to eigenvalues
\pm\epsilon_{k}
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{k,+}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{k}+(-t\cos k+\mu/2)}{2\epsilon_{k}}}\\
\frac{{\mathrm{i}}\Delta\sin k}{\sqrt{2\epsilon_{k}(\epsilon_{k}+(-t\cos k+\mu/2))}}\end{pmatrix},

(113)

\displaystyle\Psi_{k,-}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{{\mathrm{i}}\Delta\sin k}{\sqrt{2\epsilon_{k}(\epsilon_{k}+(-t\cos k+\mu/2))}}\\
\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{k}+(-t\cos k+\mu/2)}{2\epsilon_{k}}}\end{pmatrix}.

The Hamiltonian can always be decomposed into two terms:

\displaystyle H_{k}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{k}\Psi_{k,+}\Psi_{k,+}^{\dagger}-\epsilon_{k}\Psi_{k,-}\Psi_{k,-}^{\dagger}

(114)

\displaystyle=H_{k}^{(+)}+H_{k}^{(-)},

where we note that

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}{2}\mathds{1}_{2}+\frac{H_{\bm{k}}}{2},

(115)

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}{2}\mathds{1}_{2}+\frac{H_{\bm{k}}}{2}.

For a general parameter choice,
H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}
and
H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}
are not Laurent polynomials of
e^{{\mathrm{i}}k}
.
Therefore, the Hamiltonian is not frustration-free.

However, as discussed in Sec.
IV.3.1
, the Hamiltonian becomes frustration-free for a specific parameter choice:
(t,\Delta,\mu)=(t_{0}/2,-t_{0}\sin 2\theta/2,t_{0}\cos 2\theta)
with
t_{0}>0,\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
For this choice, we obtain

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}=t_{0}(1-\cos 2\theta\cos k)/2.

(116)

Consequently,
H_{\bm{k}}^{(\pm)}
become

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}^{(\pm)}=\pm[t_{0}(1-\cos 2\theta\cos k)/2]\mathds{1}_{2}+\frac{H_{\bm{k}}}{2},

(117)

which are indeed Laurent polynomials of
e^{{\mathrm{i}}k}
.
Furthermore, with an appropriate gauge choice, we find

\displaystyle\Psi_{k,-}\propto\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta(e^{{\mathrm{i}}k}+1)\\
\sin\theta(e^{{\mathrm{i}}k}-1)\end{pmatrix}.

(118)

Note that the unnormalized version of
\Psi_{k,-}
is a Laurent polynomial of
e^{{\mathrm{i}}k}
, implying that the corresponding Wannier-type function is compactly supported.

V.2.2
Chern insulator

Next, we consider the Chern insulator, whose Hamiltonian in momentum space is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{\bm{k},1}^{\dagger}&\hat{c}_{\bm{k},2}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}H_{\bm{k}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{\bm{k},1}\\
\hat{c}_{\bm{k},2}\end{pmatrix},

(119)

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}=t(\sin k_{x}\sigma_{x}+\sin k_{y}\sigma_{y})+M_{\bm{k}}\sigma_{z}\ \ (t\in\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle M_{\bm{k}}=m+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\cos k_{i}\ \ (m\in\mathbb{R}).

The Chern number
\mathrm{Ch}_{1}
is computed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ch}_{1}=\begin{cases}0&|m|>2,\\
-\mathrm{sgn}\ m&0<|m|<2.\\
\end{cases}

(120)

It is well known that any set of bands with a nonzero Chern number does not admit compactly supported Wannier functions
[
65
]
.
In the following, we explicitly show that the Hamiltonian is not frustration-free.

The energy dispersion
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
of
H_{\bm{k}}
is

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{M_{\bm{k}}^{2}+t^{2}(\sin^{2}k_{x}+\sin^{2}k_{y})}.

(121)

The projections onto the occupied and unoccupied bands are given by

\displaystyle P_{\bm{k},\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{2}\pm\frac{H_{\bm{k}}}{2\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}.

(122)

Thus,
H_{\bm{k}}
can be decomposed into two terms:

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}P_{\bm{k},+}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}P_{\bm{k},-}=H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}+H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)},

(123)

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}=\frac{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}{2}\mathds{1}_{2}+\frac{H_{\bm{k}}}{2},

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}=-\frac{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}{2}\mathds{1}_{2}+\frac{H_{\bm{k}}}{2}.

Although
H_{\bm{k}}
is a Laurent polynomial,
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
is not for any parameter set
(m,t)
.
Consequently,
H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}
and
H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}
are not Laurent polynomials.
Therefore, the Hamiltonian is not frustration-free. However, we note that if one relaxes the strict locality condition to allow for exponentially decaying hoppings, it is possible to construct a frustration-free parent Hamiltonian for the Chern insulator, as demonstrated in Ref.
[
37
]
.

V.2.3
Three-dimensional class AIII

As a final example, we consider a three-dimensional Hamiltonian in class AIII:

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=t\left(\sin k_{x}\tau_{x}\sigma_{x}+\sin k_{y}\tau_{x}\sigma_{y}+\sin k_{z}\tau_{x}\sigma_{z}\right)+M_{\bm{k}}\tau_{y}\sigma_{0},

(124)

\displaystyle M_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=m+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\cos k_{i},

\displaystyle U_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\tau_{z}\sigma_{0},

where
U_{\Gamma}H_{\bm{k}}=-H_{\bm{k}}U_{\Gamma}
.
The three-dimensional winding number

of

is given by

\displaystyle w_{3\text{d}}=\begin{cases}0&|m|>3,\\
-2\ \mathrm{sgn}\ t&|m|<1,\\
\mathrm{sgn}\ t&1<|m|<3.\\
\end{cases}

(125)

Due to accidental degeneracy, the magnitudes of all eigenvalues of
H_{\bm{k}}
are identical and are given by

\epsilon_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{M_{\bm{k}}^{2}+t^{2}(\sin^{2}k_{x}+\sin^{2}k_{y}+\sin^{2}k_{z})}.

(126)

The corresponding eigenvectors are

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bm{k},\pm}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}\begin{pmatrix}t\sin k_{z}-{\mathrm{i}}M_{\bm{k}}\\
t(\sin k_{x}+{\mathrm{i}}\sin k_{y})\\
\pm\epsilon_{\bm{k}}\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(127)

\pm\epsilon_{\bm{k}}\end{pmatrix},

where
\pm
corresponds to positive and negative eigenvalues.
The eigenvectors are non-singular at any momentum for
|m|\neq 1,3
; thus, they are analytic functions of
\bm{k}
in the gapped regime
|m|\neq 1,3
.
Therefore, the Wannier functions are exponentially localized.

The projections onto the occupied and unoccupied bands are computed as

\displaystyle P_{\bm{k},\pm}=\left(\Psi_{\bm{k},\pm}^{(1)}\Psi_{\bm{k},\pm}^{(2)}\right)\left(\Psi_{\bm{k},\pm}^{(1)}\Psi_{\bm{k},\pm}^{(2)}\right)^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{4}\pm\frac{H_{\bm{k}}}{2\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}.

(128)

Again, we decompose the Hamiltonian into

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}P_{\bm{k},+}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}P_{\bm{k},-}=H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}+H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)},

(129)

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}=\frac{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}{2}\mathds{1}_{4}+\frac{H_{\bm{k}}}{2},

\displaystyle H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}=-\frac{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}{2}\mathds{1}_{4}+\frac{H_{\bm{k}}}{2}.

While
H_{\bm{k}}
is a Laurent polynomial,
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
is not for any parameter
(m,t)
.
Therefore, we conclude that
H_{\bm{k}}^{(+)}
and
H_{\bm{k}}^{(-)}
are not Laurent polynomials; i.e., the Hamiltonian is not frustration-free.

VI
Gosset-Huang inequality and its implication

In this section, we discuss the scaling of finite-size gaps in frustration-free systems assuming power-law behaviors of correlation functions.

VI.1
Statement

Let
E_{n}
(
n=1,2,\cdots
) be the many-body energy eigenvalues of
\hat{H}
arranged in the increasing order:

\displaystyle E_{1}\leq E_{2}\leq\cdots.

(147)

We assume
E_{1}=E_{2}=\cdots=E_{N_{\mathrm{deg}}}=0
and
E_{N_{\mathrm{deg}}+1}>0
. Then the finite-size gap is defined as

\displaystyle\epsilon=E_{N_{\mathrm{deg}}+1}-E_{N_{\mathrm{deg}}}.

(148)

In gapless systems,
\epsilon
vanishes in the thermodynamic limit. Namely, if
L
is the linear size of the system,
\lim_{L\to\infty}\epsilon=0
in gapless systems and
\lim_{L\to\infty}\epsilon>0
in gapped systems
[
14
]
.

We consider local operators
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
acting nontrivially around sites
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, respectively. For any ground state
|\Phi\rangle
of a frustration-free Hamiltonian, the correlation function between
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
satisfies the following Gosset–Huang inequality
[
69
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{|\langle\Phi|\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}_{\bm{y}}|\Phi\rangle|}{\|\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\dagger}_{\bm{x}}|\Phi\rangle\|\|\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}_{\bm{y}}|\Phi\rangle\|}

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{2}\exp(-\frac{D(\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{y}}')-1}{c-1}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{g^2+\epsilon}}),

(149)

where
\hat{G}
is the projector onto the ground space,
D(\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{y}}^{\prime})
is the distance between the two operators, and
c
and
g
are integer constants determined by details of the Hamiltonian.
We include the accurate definitions of
D(\cdot,\cdot)
,
c
and
g
in Appendix
D
.
The proof of this inequality is in Ref.
[
69
]
and Ref.
[
18
]
.
Although this inequality was originally derived for bosonic systems, it can be extended to fermionic systems with bosonic local Hamiltonians. Importantly, the local operators
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
do not need to be bosonic.

Suppose that operators
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
with the distance
D(\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\sim L
shows power-law decay

\displaystyle\frac{|\langle\Phi|\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}_{\bm{y}}|\Phi\rangle|}{\|\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}}|\Phi\rangle\|\|\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}_{\bm{y}}|\Phi\rangle\|}\geq CL^{-\Delta}

(150)

for positive constants
C>0
and
\Delta>0
.
Then the Gosset–Huang inequality implies that the finite-size gap satisfies
[
18
]

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq C^{\prime}\frac{(\log L)^{2}}{L^{2}},

(151)

where
C^{\prime}
is a positive constant.

A finite energy gap in the thermodynamic limit guarantees exponential decay of correlation functions in the ground state, for both bosonic and fermionic systems
[
70
]
. Therefore the correlation function in Eq. (
150
) in the ground state implies that the Hamiltonian is gapless.
In Appendix
E
, we provide two free-fermion examples of such power-law decaying correlation functions, and show that the existence of a singular band touching point between the valence and conduction bands imply power-law decaying ground-state correlation functions.

VI.2
Examples

VI.2.1
Tight-binding model on checkerboard lattice

Let us first check the validity of the bound (
151
) using the tight-binding model. As an example, we take the tight-binding model on the checkerboard lattice discussed in Sec.
III.2
. Let
|\Phi\rangle
be the ground state in which all states in the lower band, except for the one at
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
, are occupied. The state shows the power-law correlation

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle\approx\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{R_{y}^{\,2}-R_{x}^{\,2}}{|\bm{R}|^{4}}(-1)^{R_{x}+R_{y}}

(152)

asymptotically for a large
|\bm{R}|
.
See Appendix
E
for the derivation. Combined with
\|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle\|\approx 1/\sqrt{2}
, we find the desired power-law decay. Then the bound (
151
) implies a low-energy excitation, which is consistent with the quadratic band touching discussed in Sec.
II.3
.

VI.2.2
\eta
-pairing state

The bound (
151
) applies not only to free fermions but also to interacting fermions with or without the translation invariance. For example, the
\eta
-pairing state defined on a
d
-dimensional hypercubic lattice exhibits an off-diagonal long-rage order
[
71
]
, which may be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Phi_{N}|\hat{\eta}_{x}^{\dagger}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{\eta}_{y}|\Phi_{N}\rangle}{\|\hat{\eta}_{x}|\Phi_{N}\rangle\|\|\hat{\eta}_{y}|\Phi_{N}\rangle\|}=-\frac{N-1}{V(V-1)}.

(153)

Here
V=L^{d}
is the system size and
N
is the number of
\eta
-pairs. For some parameter range, these
\eta
-pairing states become ground states of a generalized Hubbard model and the model is frustration-free
[
72
,
73
,
74
]
. The power-law correlation function then indicates the presence of gapless excitations bounded by (
151
).

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have studied frustration-free fermionic systems and established fundamental properties that characterize their low-energy excitations. For free fermionic systems, we have derived a necessary and sufficient condition for frustration-freeness.
In particular, we have demonstrated that, in the presence of translation symmetry, frustration-free free fermionic systems exhibit quadratic dispersions for low-energy excitations.
Furthermore, we have generalized our findings to interacting and non-translation-invariant fermionic systems by extending the Gosset-Huang inequality.
Specifically, we have shown that, if the ground-state correlation function decays as a power law, the finite-size scaling of gapless excitations is
O((\log L)^{2}/L^{2})
even in the presence of interactions and/or the absence of translation symmetry. This result implies, among other things, that the effective field theory of gapless frustration-free fermionic systems cannot be of Dirac type; in particular, it must break Lorentz invariance.

Appendix A
Analytic property of band dispersion

In this appendix, we discuss the analytic property of band dispersion
\omega_{\bm{k}n}
.

In one dimension, it is known that eigenvalues of
H(k)
can be labeled in such a way that
\omega_{kn}
(
i=1,2,\cdots,N_{\mathrm{band}}
) are all power series of
k
convergent for small
|k|
, as long as each component of
H(k)
is a power series of
k

[
75
]
.
To see this, recall that eigenvalues of
H(k)
are roots of the
N_{\mathrm{band}}
-th order polynomial

\displaystyle P(\omega)=\det(H(k)-\omega I_{N_{\mathrm{band}}})

(154)

Then the Newton-Puiseux theorem suggests that every root of this equation can be expressed in the form of the Puiseux series (a generalization of the Laurant series)
[
76
]
,

\displaystyle\omega=\sum_{m\geq m_{0}}c_{m}k^{m/q},

(155)

where
m_{0}
is an integer (can be negative),
q
is a positive integer,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
c_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
. For a given polynomial, a step-by-step procedure of finding roots in the Puiseux series form using the Newton polygon is known.

Since
H(k)
is Hermitian and its dependence on
k
is analytic,
\omega
must be real and finite for any
k
. This is possible only when all powers appearing in the Puiseux series are nonnegative integers.

In higher dimension, this argument applies to any one-dimensional curve
\bm{k}(t)
in the Brillouin zone.

Appendix B

Appendix D
Gosset–Huang inequality for fermions

D.1
Settings

Let us consider a frustration-free Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
defined on a
d
-dimensional lattice
\Lambda
.
Without loss of generality, we assume all
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
are positive-semidefinite and have at least one zero-eigenvalue.
We consider both bosonic and fermionic systems.
In the case of fermionic systems, each
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
is assumed to be bosonic, which means
[\hat{H}_{\bm{R}},(-1)^{\hat{F}}]=0
where
(-1)^{\hat{F}}
is the fermion parity.
Fermionic local terms are excluded because physical interaction terms should be local, i.e., sufficiently distant local Hamiltonians commute (not anticommute) with each other.

D.2
Interaction graph

For the formulation and the proof of the Gosset–Huang inequality, it is useful to define the interaction graph depicted in Fig.
6
.

Figure 6:
An example of the interaction graph with three vertices. The vertices correspond to local Hamiltonians
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
and the edges represent the noncommutativity between the local Hamiltonians.

The interaction graph is defined for a decomposition
\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
of a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, where
\Lambda
is the index set consisting of the lattice sites and other degrees of freedom.
The interaction graph for this Hamiltonian has
|\Lambda|
vertices labeled by
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
(
\bm{R}\in\Lambda
) and there is an edge between vertex
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
and vertex
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}
when
[\hat{H}_{\bm{R}},\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}]\neq 0
.
If two vertices
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
and
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}
share an edge, we say that
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
is adjacent to
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}
.
The degree
g_{\bm{R}}
of a vertex
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
is given by the number of vertices adjacent to
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
.
The maximum degree
g
of the graph is defined by
g\coloneqq\max_{\bm{R}}g_{\bm{R}}
. Thus,
g
is defined by

\displaystyle g\coloneqq\max_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\Big|\{\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}\mid[\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}},\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}]\neq 0\}\Big|.

(195)

We only consider the case where the maximum degree
g
is finite and independent of the system size in the large system limit.

We define another integer
c
that characterizes the interaction graph, known as the chromatic number.
The chromatic number
c
of the interaction graph is the smallest number of colors needed for coloring the vertices so that no two adjacent vertices have the same color.
The chromatic number
c
is also finite if the maximum degree
g
is finite since the greedy coloring algorithm guarantees
c\leq g+1
.

The interaction graph offers a natural definition of the distance between two local operators.
The distance
d(\hat{H}_{\bm{R}},\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}})
between two vertices
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
and
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}
of the interaction graph is given by the length of the shortest path connecting the two vertices.
In other words, the distance between
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
and
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}
is measured by the shortest length
l
of a chain of local Hamiltonians

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}_{0}},\hat{H}_{\bm{R}_{1}},\cdots,\hat{H}_{\bm{R}_{l}},

(196)

where
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}_{0}}=\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
,
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}_{l}}=\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}}
, and
[\hat{H}_{\bm{R}_{i}},\hat{H}_{\bm{R}_{i+1}}]\neq 0
for
i=0,1,\cdots,l-1
.
The distance between general operators
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
is defined by

\displaystyle D(\hat{\mathcal{O}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\big\{d(\hat{H}_{\bm{R}},\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}})\mid[\hat{H}_{\bm{R}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}]\neq 0,[\hat{H}_{\bm{R}^{\prime}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}]\neq 0\big\}.

(197)

Strictly speaking,
D(\hat{\mathcal{O}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime})
is not a distance in the mathematical sense because it does not satisfy the triangle inequality.
We also note that the commutator
[\hat{\mathcal{O}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}]
does not appear in the definition of the distance, and thus the nonzero commutator
[\hat{\mathcal{O}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}]\neq 0
does not necessarily imply a short distance between these operators.
Therefore, the distance
D(\hat{\mathcal{O}},\hat{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}})
also works for the case that
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
are fermionic local operators.

D.3
The Gosset–Huang inequality

Theorem 1

(Gosset and Huang
[
69
]
)
.

Let
\hat{H}
be a frustration-free Hamiltonian with the decomposition
\hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{R}\in\Lambda}\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
where each
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
is a positive semidefinite operator
Let
\hat{G}
be the orthogonal projector onto the ground space and
\epsilon
be the spectral gap.
For operators
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
, the following inequality holds.

\displaystyle\frac{|\langle\Phi|\hat{\mathcal{O}}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}|\Phi\rangle|}{\|\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\dagger}|\Phi\rangle\|\|\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}|\Phi\rangle\|}

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{2}\exp\left\lparen-\frac{D(\hat{\mathcal{O}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime})-1}{c-1}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{g^{2}\max_{\bm{R}}\|\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}\|}+\epsilon}\right\rparen.

(198)

The definitions of the maximum degree
g
, the chromatic number
c
, and the distance
D(\hat{\mathcal{O}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime})
between
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
are given in the previous subsection.

The proof of the Gosset–Huang inequality is in the original paper
[
69
]
and Ref.
[
18
]
.
Although the Gosset–Huang inequality is originally derived for bosonic systems, the extension to fermionic systems with bosonic local Hamiltonians is straightforward.

To obtain a nontrivial inequality,
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
must be local in the sense that
[\hat{\mathcal{O}},\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}]=0
for sufficiently distant
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
and
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
(and similarly for
\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
).
However, the assumption that
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
commute is not necessary.
Therefore we can use the Gosset–Huang inequality to correlation functions of fermionic operators that anticommute with each other.

Now, suppose that there exist operators
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
,
\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
, separated by a distance of order the system size
L
, such that their correlation function shows the power-law decay

\displaystyle\frac{|\langle\Phi|\hat{\mathcal{O}}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}|\Phi\rangle|}{\|\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\dagger}|\Phi\rangle\|\|\hat{\mathcal{O}}|\Phi\rangle\|}\geq CL^{-\Delta},

(199)

where
C
and
\Delta
are positive constants.
Then it follows by the Gosset–Huang inequality (
198
) that

\displaystyle CL^{-\Delta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\langle\Phi|\hat{\mathcal{O}}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}|\Phi\rangle|}{\|\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\dagger}|\Phi\rangle\|\|\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}|\Phi\rangle\|}\lesssim\exp\left\lparen-O(L\sqrt{\epsilon})\right\rparen.

(200)

This implies an upper bound on the finite-size gap as

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq C^{\prime}\frac{(\log L)^{2}}{L^{2}},

(201)

where
C^{\prime}
is a positive constant, which prohibits the dispersion relation
\omega_{\bm{k}}\sim|\bm{k}|^{z}
of low-energy modes with
z<2
.
We emphasize that our assumption concerns power-law decay as a function of
L
rather than
D(\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{x}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}_{\bm{y}})
.
Therefore a correlation function like
1/L^{\Delta}
that does not depend on
D(\hat{\mathcal{O}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime})
is sufficient to apply the above argument and we will see an example in Eq. (
205
).

Appendix E
Power-law decaying correlation functions

In this Appendix, we provide two examples of power-law decaying correlation functions satisfying the assumption in Eq. (
199
). We also provide a general argument proving that singular band touching points lead to power-law decaying correlation functions.

E.1
One-band model

Let us see a power-law decaying correlation function for the one-band model given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i+1}\hat{c}_{i+1}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}-\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i+1}\hat{c}_{i}-\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{c}_{i+1}).

(202)

We can check the frustration-freeness of this model by the following decomposition.

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\hat{H}_{i},\quad\hat{H}_{i}=\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{\psi}_{i},\quad\hat{\psi}_{i}=\hat{c}_{i+1}-\hat{c}_{i}.

(203)

This model is gapless with the quadratic dispersion relation
\omega_{k}=2(1-\cos k)
.
We see this property from the power-law decaying correlation.
The ground states are given by

\displaystyle|0\rangle,\quad|\Phi_{1}\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{i}\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}|0\rangle,

(204)

where
|0\rangle
is the Fock vacuum state.
We consider the correlation function between
\hat{c}_{i}
and
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}
for
i\neq j
on the ground state
|0\rangle
.
The calculation is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\langle 0|\hat{c}_{i}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}|0\rangle}{\|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}|0\rangle\|\|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}|0\rangle\|}

\displaystyle=\langle 0|\hat{c}_{i}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle 0|\hat{c}_{i}|\Phi_{1}\rangle\langle\Phi_{1}|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k,l}\langle 0|\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}|0\rangle\langle 0|\hat{c}_{l}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{L}.

(205)

Here we used the fact that

\displaystyle\langle 0|\hat{c}_{i}\hat{\mathbbm{1}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}|0\rangle=\langle 0|\hat{c}_{i}|0\rangle\langle 0|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}|0\rangle=0.

(206)

Let
j>i
without loss of generality. The distance between
\hat{c}_{i}
and
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}
is given by

\displaystyle D(\hat{c}_{i},\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j})=d(\hat{H}_{i},\hat{H}_{j-1})=j-i-1.

(207)

By taking
|i-j|\sim L
, the assumption in Eq. (
199
) is satisfied and we obtain the upper bound on the finite-size gap in Eq. (
201
).

E.2
Checkerboard lattice

Next, we consider the model on the checkerboard lattice discussed in the main text.
For simplicity, we move the origin of momentum from
(0,0)
to
(\pi,\pi)
so that the zero-energy mode is located at
\bm{k}=(0,0)
.
In other words, we redefine the fermion operators as

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{\bm{R}}\to(-1)^{R_{x}+R_{y}}\hat{a}_{\bm{R}},\quad\hat{b}_{\bm{R}}\to(-1)^{R_{x}+R_{y}}\hat{b}_{\bm{R}}.

(208)

Then, the unnormalized Bloch state for the occupied band in Eq. (
45
) is replaced by

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{\bm{k}}=\left\lparen\begin{matrix}1-e^{-ik_{x}}\\
-1+e^{-ik_{y}}\end{matrix}\right\rparen.

(209)

The zero-energy modes are generated by
\hat{a}_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{b}_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}^{\dagger}
.
The ground states are given by the following four states.

\displaystyle|\Phi\rangle\coloneqq\prod_{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}(w_{\bm{k}1}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}+w_{\bm{k}2}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}})|0\rangle,

(210)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{a}\rangle\coloneqq\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle,

(211)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{b}\rangle\coloneqq\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle,

(212)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{ab}\rangle\coloneqq\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle.

(213)

Let us compute the following correlation function.

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle}{\|a^{\dagger}_{\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle\|\|a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle\|}.

(214)

Correlation functions involving ground states other than
|\Phi\rangle
, as well as those involving
\hat{b}_{\bm{R}}
instead of
\hat{a}_{\bm{R}}
, can be computed similarly as follows.
The distance between
\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}
is given by
|R_{x}|+|R_{y}|-1
.
The contribution from the projector
\hat{G}
onto the ground space is written as

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}\hat{G}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle\langle\Phi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle+\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}|\Phi_{a}\rangle\langle\Phi_{a}|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}|\Phi_{b}\rangle\langle\Phi_{b}|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle+\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}|\Phi_{ab}\rangle\langle\Phi_{ab}|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle.

(215)

The first term and the last term vanish because of the conservation of the fermion number. The third term also vanishes because

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{b}|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle=\langle\Phi|\hat{b}_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle=-\langle\Phi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}\hat{b}_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle=0

(216)

The second term is computed as
1/V
using the following calculation.

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{a}|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{R}}\langle\Phi_{a}|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\langle\Phi_{a}|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}=\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}.

(217)

The contribution from the identity
\hat{\mathbbm{1}}
is described as

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}\hat{\mathbbm{1}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}\|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}|\Phi\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}(P_{\mathrm{unoc}}(\bm{k}))_{11},

(218)

where
P_{\mathrm{unoc}}(\bm{k})
is a projector onto the vector space spanned by the Bloch states of the unoccupied bands at the momentum
\bm{k}
.
This is computed for
\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}
as

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{unoc}}(\bm{k})=\mathbbm{1}-\frac{\bm{w}_{\bm{k}}\bm{w}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}}{\|\bm{w}_{\bm{k}}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4-2\cos k_{x}-2\cos k_{y}}

\displaystyle\times\left\lparen\begin{matrix}2-2\cos k_{y}&(1-e^{-ik_{x}})(1-e^{ik_{y}})\\
(1-e^{ik_{x}})(1-e^{-ik_{y}})&2-2\cos k_{x}\end{matrix}\right\rparen.

(219)

For
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
, we have
P_{\mathrm{unoc}}(\bm{k}=\bm{0})=\mathbbm{1}
from the definition of the ground state
|\Phi\rangle
.
Therefore, the integrand of the Fourier transformation in Eq. (
218
) is given by

\displaystyle\|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}|\Phi\rangle\|^{2}=\frac{1-\cos k_{y}}{2-\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y}}+\frac{\delta(\bm{k})}{V}.

(220)

Here, the delta function is defined as

\displaystyle\delta(\bm{k})=\begin{cases}V&(\bm{k}=\bm{0}),\\
0&(\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}).\end{cases}

(221)

The contribution from the delta function is canceled by the contribution from the projector
\hat{G}
.
Using continuum approximation, the unnormalized correlation function is computed as

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}{{e}}^{{{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}\frac{1-\cos k_{y}}{2-\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y}}

\displaystyle\approx\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}{{e}}^{{{i}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}\frac{k_{y}^{\,2}}{k_{x}^{\,2}+k_{y}^{\,2}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}^{\,2}\left\lparen\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|\bm{R}|\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{R_{y}^{\,2}-R_{x}^{\,2}}{|\bm{R}|^{4}}.

(222)

Here, we used the fact that the Green function of the Laplacian operator in two dimensions is given by
(1/2\pi)\ln|\bm{R}|
. Since the normalization factor satisfies
\|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle\|\|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle\|=\langle\Phi|a_{\bm{R}}a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle\approx 1/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{|\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle|}{\|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle\|\|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle\|}\sim\frac{1}{L^{2}},

(223)

by taking
D(\hat{a}_{\bm{0}},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}})\sim L
.
Therefore, the upper bound on the finite-size gap in Eq. (
201
) also holds for this case.
The same algebraic dependence of the correlation function can be obtained for the model on the Kagome lattice discussed in Sec.
III
.

E.3
General argument for singular band touching points

In the preceding calculations, we observed that the long-range behavior of the correlation function differs significantly depending on the model, with some exhibiting a power-law decay with distance.
This long-range behavior is distinct from finite-size effects, which scale with the overall system size.
This difference can be systematically understood by characterizing the nature of the band touching points. We define a band touching point as singular if the quantum distance between nearby Bloch states remains finite as the touching point is approached
[
77
]
. In this section, we provide the general discussion that it is precisely this singularity that gives rise to the power-law correlations observed previously.

The quantum distance between the Bloch states
w_{\bm{k}}
is defined by

\displaystyle d(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})\coloneqq 1-\frac{w_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}w_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}}{\|w_{\bm{k}}\|\|w_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\|}.

(224)

Typically,
d(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})\to 0
as
|\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}|\to 0
, but it can remain finite in the vicinity of a singular band touching point.

The correlation function under consideration is

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}(\hat{\mathbbm{1}}-\hat{G})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle.

(225)

Here, the normalization constants
\|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}|\Phi\rangle\|
and
\|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle\|
are omitted as they do not alter the scaling of the correlation function.
The contribution from the identity operator is given by

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{i,\bm{0}}\hat{\mathbbm{1}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j,\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{i,\bm{k}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i,\bm{k}}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}(P_{\mathrm{unoc}}(\bm{k}))_{ii},

(226)

where
P_{\mathrm{unoc}}(\bm{k})
is the projection operator onto the unoccupied bands at momentum
\bm{k}
.
It should be noted that for a frustration-free system, the positive and negative energy bands are completely separated, except at band touching points.

For simplicity, we consider systems with one singular band touching point at
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
. In such systems, the projector
P_{\mathrm{unoc}}(\bm{k})
exhibits singular behavior near
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
. However, if the singular band touching consists entirely of positive-energy bands or entirely of negative-energy bands, the singularity in
P_{\mathrm{unoc}}(\bm{k})
is canceled. Furthermore, there is a degree of freedom in
|\Phi\rangle
regarding the excitation of the
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
mode. This introduces a correction to
P_{\mathrm{unoc}}(\bm{k})
that is proportional to
\delta(\bm{k})/V
.

The contribution from the projection onto the ground state,
\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{\bm{0}}\hat{G}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle
, provides an
\bm{R}
-independent term of the order of
\delta(\bm{k})/V
. If this term does not perfectly cancel the delta function arising from the identity operator’s contribution, it results in a finite-size effect that is independent of the distance
\bm{R}
. It is noteworthy that while such a finite-size effect in the correlation function is a general property not restricted to singular band touching, it is sufficient to guarantee the gaplessness of the system via the Gosset-Huang inequality.

Since our interest lies in the spatial dependence of the correlation function, we return to the evaluation of Eq. (
226
).
Let
w_{\alpha,i}(\bm{k})
be a Bloch state where
\alpha
labels the band index and
i
denotes the internal degrees of freedom of the fermions.
Near the touching point
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
, the projection onto the unoccupied bands is expressed using the Bloch states as

\displaystyle(P_{\mathrm{unoc}}(\bm{k}))_{ii}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathrm{unoc}}\frac{w_{\alpha,i}^{*}(\bm{k})w_{\alpha,i}(\bm{k})}{\|w_{\alpha}(\bm{k})\|^{2}}\approx g(\Omega_{\bm{k}})+O(|\bm{k}|),

(227)

where the function
g(\Omega_{\bm{k}})
depends only on the solid angle
\Omega_{\bm{k}}
of the momentum vector
\bm{k}
, and not on its magnitude
|\bm{k}|
. If the band touching point is not singular, meaning it does not provide a finite quantum distance around the touching point,
g(\Omega_{\bm{k}})
becomes a direction-independent constant.

The long-range behavior of the correlation function is determined by the Fourier transform of
g(\Omega_{\bm{k}})
. For sufficiently large
|\bm{R}|
, the dominant contribution to the integral comes from a small sphere of radius
|\bm{k}|\sim O(|\bm{R}|^{-1})
. Therefore, the integration domain can be extended from the Brillouin zone to all of momentum space.
Using polar coordinates
\bm{k}=(k,\Omega_{\bm{k}})
and
\bm{R}=(R,\Omega_{\bm{r}})
, we have
d^{d}k=k^{d-1}dkd\Omega_{\bm{k}}
and
\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}=kR\cos\theta
. The integral can thus be written as:

\displaystyle\langle\Phi|\hat{a}_{i,\bm{0}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j,\bm{R}}|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\int d\Omega g(\Omega)\int_{0}^{\infty}k^{d-1}dk\;e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}

\displaystyle=R^{-d}\int d\Omega g(\Omega)\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{d-1}du\;e^{iu\cos\theta}.

(228)

Thus, the correlation function is proportional to
R^{-d}
. However, if
g(\Omega)
is direction-independent, the integral vanishes for
\bm{R}\neq\bm{0}
. This implies that the power-law decay of the ground-state correlation function is a characteristic feature of singular band touching points.
