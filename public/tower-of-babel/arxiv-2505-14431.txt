1.
Introduction

We start with an elliptic equation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
, where

U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a simply connected bounded domain:

\Delta u(x)+A(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)-V(x)u(x)=0\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}$}.

(1.1)

Henceforth we set
H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}):=\{u;\,u|_{M}\in H^{2}(M)\,\,\mbox{for any bounded domain $M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}$}\}
.

We are concerned with decay rates of the solution
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U})
as
|x|\to\infty

which imply that
u\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
.

In a special case where
A=0
and
V=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that is,

u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
the classical Liouville theorem reads

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|u(x)|=0

(1.2)

implies that
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We understand that (
1.2
) is the weakest
decay condition, but (
1.2
) does not imply
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in general
with
A\not\equiv 0
or
V\not\equiv 0
.

For an equation

\Delta u(x)-V(x)u(x)=0\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

with
V\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, Landis posed a conjecture that if
there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

|u(x)|\leq C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}|x|^{1+\varepsilon})\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$},

then
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(Kondratiev and Landis
[
16
]
).
Meshkov
[
19
]
demonstrated that Landis’ conjecture does not hold
by constructing a complex-valued solution
u\not\equiv 0
satisfying

|u(x)|\leq C_{1}\exp\left(-C_{2}|x|^{\frac{4}{3}}\right)\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}$}

and that if there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
a solution
u
to (
1.2
) satisfies

|u(x)|\leq C_{1}\exp\left(-C_{2}|x|^{\frac{4}{3}+\varepsilon}\right)\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}$},

(1.3)

then
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In other words,
\frac{4}{3}
is the
”critical decay rate for uniqueness” of solution
u
.

After paper of Meshkov
[
19
]
, there have been many important works
concerning Landis’
conjecture. As for the two dimensional case

n=2
, we can refer to Davey
[
5
]
,
Davey, Kenig and Wang
[
7
]
,
[

]
, Davey and Wang
[

]
,

[

]
, Kenig and Wang
[
15
]
,
Le Balc’H and de Souza
[
17
]
, Logunov, Malinnikova, Nadirashvili and
Nazarov
[
18
]
.
As for
n\geq 3
, see Davey
[
4
]
,
[
6
]
,
Davey and Zhu
[
10
]
.
In particular, for the equation
\Delta u+A(x)\cdot\nabla u-V(x)u=0

with
n\geq 3
, Theorems 2-4 in Davey and Zhu
[

]
give
the critical decay rate for uniqueness according to Lebesgue spaces to
which
A
and
V
belong, while Davey
[
4
]
can provide the
critical decay rates for uniqueness in terms of the polynomial decay
rates of
A(x)
and
V(x)
as
|x|\to\infty
.
See also Rossi

.

Now we start to formulate our problem and state the main results.
Throughout the article, let functions under consideration be real-valued.
We can modify the arguments for the case of complex-valued functions.

For arbitrarily fixed
m\in\{1,2,...,n\}
, we set

x=(x_{1},..,x_{m},x_{m+1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad y:=(x_{1},...,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad z:=(x_{m+1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}.

We write
|y|:=(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{m}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}

and
|z|:=(x_{m+1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and

\overline{U}
denotes the closure of a set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We denote
\partial_{k}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}
for
1\leq k\leq n
.

In this article, we consider elliptic differential inequalities,
not elliptic equations, because our method does not need to
assume elliptic equations. More precisely,
we arbitrarily choose a bounded domain
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We assume

|\Delta u(x)|\leq C_{0}(|y|^{-\gamma}|u(x)|+|y|^{-\theta}|\nabla u(x)|),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}

(1.4)

with some constants
C_{0}>0
and
\gamma,\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Our main purpose is to provide values of critical decay rates
of
u
which imply that
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
, in terms of
the constants
\gamma,\theta
, and moreover we are interested in
reducing the exponential decay
y
-coordinates.

We define a constant
\alpha>0
by

\alpha:=\max\left\{\frac{2}{3}-\frac{\gamma}{3},\,\,1-\theta,\,\,\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right\}

(1.5)

with arbitrarily chosen
\varepsilon>0
and the constants
\gamma,\theta

in (
1.4
).

Let
g(r)\geq 0
for
r>0
, satisfy

\int^{\infty}_{0}g(r)^{2}r^{n-m-1}dr<\infty\quad\mbox{if $m\in\{1,...,n-1\}$.}

(1.6)

For
m=n
, we do not need to introduce
g(r)
.

Example of
g
. If
g\in C[0,\infty)
satisfies

g(r)\sim r^{\frac{m-n}{2}-\delta}
as
r\to\infty
with some
constant
\delta>0
, then (1.6) holds.

We have

Theorem 1.1
.

We assume that
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U})
satisfies (
1.4
) and
there exist constants
C>0
,
C_{1}>0
and
\beta>\alpha
such that

|u(x)|\leq Cg(|z|)\exp(-C_{1}|y|^{2\beta}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}.

(1.7)

Then
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
.

Corollary 1.2
.

We assume that
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U})
satisfies (
1.4
).
Let
m=n
and
\beta>\alpha
, and there exist constants
C,C_{1}>0
such
that

|u(x)|\leq C\exp(-C_{1}|x|^{2\beta}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}.

(1.8)

Then
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
.

Corollary 1.3
.

We assume that
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U})
satisfies

|\Delta u(x)|\leq C_{0}|u(x)|)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
. If

|u(x)|\leq Cg(|z\rvert)\exp(-C_{1}|y|^{\frac{4}{3}+\varepsilon}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}

with some constants
\varepsilon>0
and
C,C_{1}>0
, then
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
.

In Corollary 1.3, as is seen by the proof of Theorem 1.1, since
we can choose the constant
\theta>0
in (1.4) arbitrarily,
we can satisfy (1.5) with
\alpha=\frac{2}{3}
, so that

2\beta>\frac{4}{3}
in the required decay condition.

To the best knowledge of the authors, there are no publications
for the uniqueness
u=0
by means of condition (
1.7
) requiring the
exponential decay only in the limited coordinate components

(x_{1},...,x_{m})
and the polynomial decay
in the rest components
(x_{m+1},...,x_{n})

with any choice of
m\in\{1,2,...,n\}
.
The key is a Carleman estimate (Lemma 2.1) in Section 2
where the weight function depends only on
x_{1},...,x_{m}
.

In Theorem
1.1
, the assumption means a fast exponential decay of
the solution
u(x)
in
m
components of the variable

x
and a polynomial decay
in the rest
(n-m)
components of
x
.
Moreover, such critical exponential decay
rate
2\beta
is smaller if the exponent
\gamma
of the behavior of
V(x)

is larger, but
2\beta
cannot be smaller or equal to
1
.
For larger

, the polynomial decay condition in
x_{m+1},...,x_{n}
becomes
more generous.
In particular, in the case of
m=1
,
we can restrict the decay condition (
1.7
) to
one side
x_{1}>1
. More precisely, we can prove

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
g
satisfy (
1.6
) with

, and
\alpha
be given by
(1.5). We choose

r_{1}>0
such that
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\,x_{1}>r_{1}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
.
We assume that
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U})
satisfies (
1.4
)
and there exist constants

\beta>\alpha
and
C>0
,
C_{1}>0
such that

|u(x)|\leq Cg((x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp(-C_{1}|x_{1}|^{2\beta}),\quad\mbox{$(x_{2},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$
and $x_{1}>r_{1}$ or $x_{1}<-r_{1}$}.

(1.9)

Then
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
.

Note that we assume (
1.9
) for
x_{1}>r_{1}
or
x_{1}<-r_{1}
, but not for

|x_{1}|>r_{1}
.

Roughly speaking,
the existing results (e.g.,
[
14
]
,
[
18
]
) in the

are more refined than the case

.
However, our main technique by a Carleman estimate can treat any dimensions

, but, restricted to the case of

, our result is not the best

We emphasize that replacing an inequality (
1.4
) by an elliptic

1.1
), we can

1.1
and
1.4
,
Corollaries

More precisely, in (

). Then,

.

) with

in

.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We recall that
x=(x_{1},...,x_{n})=(y,z)
, where

y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
.

Since
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain, we can choose sufficiently large

R_{0}>0
such that
\overline{U}\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\,|x|<R_{0}\}
,
and so
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\,|y|>R_{0}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
.
Hence, since
\{(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m};\,|y|>R_{0}\}\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\,|x|>R_{0}\}
, we see that

\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m};\,|y|>R_{0}\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
. Moreover, without loss of generality,
we can assume that
R_{0}=1
.
If we finish the proof that
u\equiv 0
in
\{|y|>1\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
, then the unique continuation (e.g., Choulli
[
2
]
)
yields that
u=0
in each component of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
which is connected with
the point at infinity in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
U
is simply connected, such a
connected component is nothing but
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
.
Therefore,
u\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
. Thus it suffices to argue
in the domain
(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
, where
y:=(x_{1},...,x_{m})
,

z:=(x_{m+1},...,x_{n})
and
x:=(y,z)
.
We recall that
\alpha
is defined by (
1.5
) and
\alpha<\beta

is satisfied.

First Step.

For the constant
\alpha>0
defined by (1.5), we set

\varphi(x)=|y|^{2\alpha},\quad x:=(y,z)\in(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}.

(2.1)

Then,

Lemma 2.1
.

(Carleman estimate).
Let
D\subset(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
be an arbitrarily chosen bounded domain with
piecewise smooth boundary
\partial D
. Then, there exist constants

s_{0}>0
and
C>0
such that

\int_{D}(s^{3}|y|^{6\alpha-4}|u|^{2}+s|y|^{2\alpha-2}|\nabla u|^{2})e^{2s\varphi}dx\leq C\int_{D}|\Delta u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dx

for all
s\geq s_{0}
and
u\in H^{2}_{0}(D)
.

Here and henceforth we remark that
C>0
and
s_{0}>0
denote
generic constants which are
independent of the choices of piecewise smooth bounded domains

D\subset(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
, and
are dependent on
C_{0}>0
and
\theta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.

The proof of Lemma 2.1 is standard as elliptic Carleman estimate, but
for demonstrating the uniformity of the estimate with respect to
D
,
in Section 5, we provide a
direct proof of Lemma 2.1. As for the recipe of the proof,
we can refer for example to Chapter 8 in Yamamoto
[
21
]
, and see also
Choulli
[
2
]
.

Henceforth we set

\sigma_{1}:=\max\{2|\theta|,\,\,2|\gamma|,\,\,4\alpha\}.

(2.2)

Next, we will prove

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
D\subset(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
be a bounded domain with piecewise smooth

\partial D
, and let
q\in C^{2}_{0}(D)
and
q\geq 0
in
D
.
We assume that
u\in H^{2}(D)
satisfies (
1.4
) in
(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
. Then

\int_{D}q(x)|\nabla u(x)|^{2}dx\leq C\int_{D}(|\Delta q(x)|+|y|^{\sigma_{1}}q(x))|u(x)|^{2}dx.

Here
C>0
is independent of choices of
q
and piecewise smooth bounded
domains
D\subset(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
.

Suitable choices of
q
produce necessary inequalities for the proof of
Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

By
q\in H^{2}_{0}(D)
, the integration by parts yields

-\int_{D}uq\Delta udx=\int_{D}q\nabla u\cdot\nabla u+\int_{D}u\nabla q\cdot\nabla udx,

and so

\displaystyle\int_{D}q|\nabla u|^{2}dx=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}\nabla q\cdot\nabla(u^{2})dx-\int_{D}qu\Delta udx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}(\Delta q)u^{2}dx-\int_{D}uq\Delta udx.

By (
1.4
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{D}q|\nabla u|^{2}dx\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}|\Delta q|u^{2}dx+\int_{D}q|u||\Delta u|dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}|\Delta q|u^{2}dx+C_{0}\int_{D}q|u|(|y|^{-\gamma}|u|+|y|^{-\theta}|\nabla u|)dx.

Moreover, for small
\varepsilon>0
, we can find a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0

such that

\displaystyle\int_{D}q|u||y|^{-\theta}|\nabla u|dx=\int_{D}\sqrt{q}|\nabla u|\sqrt{q}|y|^{-\theta}|u|dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int_{D}q|\nabla u|^{2}dx+C_{\varepsilon}\int_{D}q|y|^{-2\theta}|u|^{2}dx.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{D}q|\nabla u|^{2}dx\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}|\Delta q|u^{2}dx+C_{0}\int_{D}q|y|^{-\gamma}|u|^{2}dx

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{0}\varepsilon\int_{D}q|\nabla u|^{2}dx+C_{0}C_{\varepsilon}\int_{D}q|y|^{-2\theta}|u|^{2}dx.

Choosing
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we can absorb the third term
on the right-hand side into the left-hand side, so that by
|y|\geq 1

in
D
, we complete the proof of Lemma
2.2
.

\blacksquare

Second Step.

For the proof, we need cut-off functions defined as follows.
We arbitrarily choose
\chi_{0}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
0\leq\chi_{0}\leq 1
and

\chi_{0}(\xi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&1,\quad\xi\geq 1,\\
&0,\quad\xi<0.\end{array}\right.

We note that
\chi_{0}^{\prime}(\xi)\neq 0
only if
0\leq\xi\leq 1
.
Then, for constants
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
satisfying

1\leq\rho_{1}<\rho_{1}+1<\rho_{2}
, we define

\chi_{\rho_{1}+1,\rho_{2}}(\xi):=\chi_{0}(\xi-\rho_{1})-\chi_{0}(\xi-\rho_{2}).

Then

\chi_{\rho_{1}+1,\rho_{2}}(\xi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&0,\quad\xi\leq\rho_{1},\\
&1,\quad\rho_{1}+1\leq\xi\leq\rho_{2},\\
&0,\quad\xi\geq\rho_{2}+1\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

and

\chi_{\rho_{1}+1,\rho_{2}}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

\mbox{supp}\,\chi_{\rho_{1}+1,\rho_{2}}\subset[\rho_{1},\,\rho_{2}+1].

Next, for a constant
\rho_{3}>0
, we set

\mu_{\rho_{3}}(\xi):=1-\chi_{0}(\xi-\rho_{3})
.
Then,
\mu_{\rho_{3}}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and

\mu_{\rho_{3}}(\xi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&1,\quad\xi\leq\rho_{3},\\
&0,\quad\xi\geq\rho_{3}+1.\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

For
n_{1}\in\{1,2,...,n\}
, we set
\xi:=(\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n_{1}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and

\eta:=(\eta_{1},...,\eta_{n-n_{1}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-n_{1}}
.
For
n=n_{1}
, we understand that
\xi=(\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

and the components
\eta
do not appear.
Then,

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

4.
Supplementary uniqueness results

In this section, we consider elliptic equations.
Let

satisfy

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}(x)\partial_{j}u(x))+A(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)-V(x)u(x)=0\quad\mbox{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

(4.1)

Here we assume that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&a_{ij}=a_{ji}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\quad\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\},\\
&\mbox{and there exists a constant $\kappa>0$ such that}\\
&\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geq\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}^{2}\quad\mbox{for all
$x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}$}.\end{array}\right.

(4.2)

The main interest in the existing works
is the critical exponentially decay rate of
u

which implies that
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
As is referred in Section 1, this is related to the celebrated
Landis’ conjecture and there are many remarkable results.
Needless to say, the critical rate means that whenever
each

u
decays
with such a rate, we can conclude that
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
On the other hand, with special
A(x)
and
V(x)
, we can prove
that much weaker rates imply that
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as follows.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfy (
4.1
) with (4.2).
Assume that
\mbox{div}\thinspace A\geq 0
and
V\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
A:=(a_{1},...,a_{n})\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),V\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
If there exists a sequence
R_{\ell}>0
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}R_{\ell}=\infty
and

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{1}{R_{\ell}}\int_{R_{\ell}\leq|x|\leq 2R_{\ell}}u^{2}dx=0,

(4.3)

then
u\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Assume that
\mbox{div}\thinspace A\geq 0
and
V\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
A:=(a_{1},...,a_{n})\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),V\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
If a solution
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to (
4.1
) with (4.2) satisfies

|u(x)|\leq C|x|^{-\frac{n}{2}+\varepsilon}\quad\mbox{for $|x|>1$}

with some
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
, then
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For div
A\geq 0
and
V\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if we assume some decay
conditions on both
u(x)
and
\nabla u(x)
as
|x|\to\infty
, then multiplying (4.1) by
u
and integrating by parts,
we can immediately prove
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We emphasize that
we never assume any decay conditions of
|\nabla u(x)|
.

The proof is based on an energy estimate which is used for the
Liouville problem for the Navier-Stokes equations
(e.g., the proof of Theorem X.9.5 on pp. 729-730
in Galdi
[
11
]
).

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

First Step.

We choose
\psi_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
0\leq\psi_{0}\leq 1
and

\psi_{0}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&1,\quad r\leq 1,\\
&0,\quad r\geq 2.\end{array}\right.

For
R>0
, we set
\psi_{R}(r):=\psi_{0}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)
.
Then,

\psi_{R}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&1,\quad r\leq R,\\
&0,\quad r\geq 2R.\end{array}\right.

Moreover, we can prove: there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
R>1
,
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi_{R}(|x|)\leq\frac{C}{R}\left(\frac{1}{R}+\frac{1}{|x|}\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\},\\
&\|\nabla\psi_{R}(|x|)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\frac{C}{R}\quad\mbox{for $1\leq i,j\leq n$ and $R\geq 1$}.\end{array}\right.

(4.4)

Verification of (4.4).

Direct calculations yield

\partial_{i}\psi_{R}(|x|)=\frac{1}{R}\partial_{i}(|x|)\frac{d\psi_{0}}{dr}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)=\frac{1}{R}\frac{x_{i}}{|x|}\frac{d\psi_{0}}{dr}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)

and

\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi_{R}(|x|)=\frac{1}{R^{2}}\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{|x|^{2}}\frac{d^{2}\psi_{0}}{dr^{2}}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)+\frac{1}{R}\frac{|x|^{2}\delta_{ij}-x_{i}x_{j}}{|x|^{3}}\frac{d\psi_{0}}{dr}\left(\frac{|x|}{R}\right)

for
1\leq i,j\leq n
, where we set
\delta_{ij}=0
if
i\neq j

and
\delta_{ii}=1
.
Therefore,

|\partial_{i}\psi_{R}(|x|)|\leq\frac{C}{R},\quad|\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi_{R}(|x|)|\leq\frac{C}{R}\left(\frac{1}{R}+\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\quad\mbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}$}.

The proof of (
4.4
) is complete.

\blacksquare

Second Step.

We set
B_{R}:=\{|x|<R\}
and

\Sigma(R):=B_{2R}\setminus B_{R}
.
Multiply (
4.1
) in
B_{2R}
with
u\psi_{R}
, and we have

-\int_{B_{2R}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}\partial_{j}u)\right)u\psi_{R}dx=\int_{B_{2R}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(\partial_{k}u)u\psi_{R}dx-\int_{B_{2R}}Vu^{2}\psi_{R}dx.

By
\psi_{R}=0
on
\partial B_{2R}
, the integration by parts implies

-\int_{B_{2R}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}\partial_{j}u)\right)u\psi_{R}dx=\int_{B_{2R}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(\partial_{j}u)(\partial_{i}u)\psi_{R}dx+\int_{B_{2R}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(\partial_{j}u)u\partial_{i}\psi_{R}dx.

Moreover, since the definition yields

\partial_{k}\psi_{R}(|x|)=0
for
k=1,...,n
and

|x|<R
or
|x|>2R
,
\psi_{R}(|x|)=0
for

|x|=2R
, we see

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2R}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(\partial_{j}u)u\partial_{i}\psi_{R}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\partial_{j}(u^{2})\partial_{i}\psi_{R}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}((\partial_{j}a_{ij})(\partial_{i}\psi_{R})+a_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi_{R})u^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma(R)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}((\partial_{j}a_{ij})(\partial_{i}\psi_{R})+a_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi_{R})u^{2}dx,

so that (4.2) yields

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{2R}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}\partial_{j}u)\right)u\psi_{R}dx\geq\int_{B_{2R}}\kappa|\nabla u|^{2}\psi_{R}dx

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma(R)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}((\partial_{j}a_{ij})(\partial_{i}\psi_{R})+a_{ij}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi_{R})u^{2}dx.

Furthermore, again applying
\partial_{k}\psi_{R}=0
on
\partial B_{2R}\cup B_{R}
and
\psi_{R}=0
on
\partial B_{2R}
and

\mbox{div}\thinspace A\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2R}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(\partial_{k}u)u\psi_{R}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\partial_{k}(u^{2})\psi_{R}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{k}(a_{k}\psi_{R})u^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial B_{2R}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\psi_{R}u^{2}\nu_{k}dS

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{k}a_{k}\right)\psi_{R}u^{2}dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(\partial_{k}\psi_{R})u^{2}dx\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma(R)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(\partial_{k}\psi_{R})u^{2}dx

Therefore, by
V\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we reach

\displaystyle\kappa\int_{B_{2R}}|\nabla u|^{2}\psi_{R}dx\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma(R)}u^{2}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{j}a_{ij})\partial_{i}\psi_{R}+a_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi_{R})\right)dx

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma(R)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(\partial_{k}\psi_{R})u^{2}dx-\int_{B_{2R}}Vu^{2}\psi_{R}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma(R)}u^{2}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{j}a_{ij})\partial_{i}\psi_{R}+a_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi_{R})\right)dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma(R)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(\partial_{k}\psi_{R})u^{2}dx.

Hence, since
\frac{1}{R}+\frac{1}{|x|}\leq 2
for

R>1
and
|x|\geq R
, estimate (
4.4
) implies

\displaystyle\kappa\int_{B_{2R}}|\nabla u|^{2}\psi_{R}dx\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma(R)}u^{2}\left|\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{j}a_{ij})\partial_{i}\psi_{R}+a_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi_{R})\right)\right|dx

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma(R)}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(\partial_{k}\psi_{R})u^{2}\right|dx\leq C\int_{\Sigma(R)}u^{2}\left(\frac{1}{R}+\frac{1}{R}\left(\frac{1}{R}+\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\right)dx+C\int_{\Sigma(R)}\frac{1}{R}u^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{R}\int_{\Sigma(R)}u^{2}dx.

Hence, setting
R:=R_{\ell}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and using

\psi_{R_{\ell}}=1
in
B_{R_{\ell}}
, we have

\int_{B_{R_{\ell}}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx\leq\frac{C}{R_{\ell}}\int_{\Sigma(R_{\ell})}u^{2}dx=\frac{1}{R_{\ell}}o(R_{\ell})=o(1)

as
\ell\to\infty
. Consequently,
\nabla u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and so

u
is a constant function in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By (
4.3
), we obtain

u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

\blacksquare

Remark.

In a similar estimation for the Navier-Stokes equations (e.g.,
[
11
]
),
the extra condition

\mbox{div}\thinspace A=0
is satisfied naturally
by that the velocity field is a divergence free
vector-valued function.

In the case of
\Delta
, we can obtain sharper results than the general case

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}(a_{ij}\partial_{i})
.
More precisely,

Theorem 4.3
.

We assume that
u\in H_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies

\Delta u-V(x)u(x)=0\quad\mbox{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$},

(4.5)

and

V\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad V\geq 0\quad\mbox{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

(4.6)

(i) If there exists a sequence
R_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}R_{\ell}=\infty
and

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{1}{R_{\ell}^{2}}\int_{R_{\ell}\leq|x|\leq 2R_{\ell}}u^{2}dx=0,

then
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(ii) If there exists a sequence
R_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}R_{\ell}=\infty
and

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\int_{R_{\ell}-1\leq|x|\leq R_{\ell}+1}u^{2}dx=0,

(4.7)

then
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We note that the assumptions in (i) and (ii) are different
with the integral domains and the convergence orders of the integrals to

0
.

Corollary 4.4
.

We assume that
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies (4.5) with (4.6). If

|u(x)|\leq C|x|^{-\frac{n}{2}+\varepsilon}\quad\mbox{for $|x|\geq 1$}

with some
\varepsilon<1
, then
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In Theorem
4.3
(ii), not assuming (
4.7
),
under the conditions
V\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

V\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we see that
if
u\in H^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies (4.5), then

u\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Indeed, (4.7) follows from
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

We remark that the assumption in Corollary 4.4 does not necessarily imply

u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which means that
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is not a necessary
condition for

u\equiv 0
. Moreover, for
0\leq V(x)\leq 1
, Corollary 4.4 improves
Theorem 1.1 in Das and Pinchover
[
3
]
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
(i)
.

We recall that
B_{R}:=\{|x|<R\}
, and
\psi_{R}
is defined in the
proof of Theorem 4.1, and satisfies (
4.4
).
We multiply (
4.5
) with
u\psi_{R}
, we have

-\int_{B_{2R}}(\Delta u)u\psi_{R}dx=-\int_{B_{2R}}Vu^{2}\psi_{R}dx.

Using
\psi_{R}(|x|)=|\nabla\psi_{R}(|x|)|=0
for
|x|=2R
, by the integration by parts we obtain

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{2R}}(\Delta u)u\psi_{R}dx=\int_{B_{2R}}|\nabla u|^{2}\psi_{R}dx+\int_{B_{2R}}u\nabla u\cdot\nabla\psi_{R}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2R}}|\nabla u|^{2}\psi_{R}dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}}u^{2}\Delta\psi_{R}dx,

which implies

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2R}}|\nabla u|^{2}\psi_{R}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2R}}u^{2}\Delta\psi_{R}dx-\int_{B_{2R}}Vu^{2}\psi_{R}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{R\leq|x|\leq 2R}u^{2}\Delta\psi_{R}dx

by means of
\Delta\psi_{R}(x)=0
for
|x|\leq R
or

|x|\geq 2R
, and
V\geq 0
and
\psi_{R}\geq 0
in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
\psi_{R}(x)=1
in
B_{R}\subset B_{2R}
, in terms of
(
4.4
), we obtain

\int_{B_{R}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx\leq\frac{C}{R^{2}}\int_{R\leq|x|\leq 2R}u^{2}dx.

Choosing
R:=R_{\ell}
and letting
\ell\to\infty
, by the assumption, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx=0
, that is,

u=c_{0}
is a constant function. Again the assumption implies
c_{0}=0
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
(ii).

Setting

\widetilde{\psi}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&1,\quad r\leq 1,\\
&0,\quad r\geq 3,\end{array}\right.

we define a different cut-off function:

\widetilde{\psi}_{R}(r):=\widetilde{\psi}(r-R+2)
. Then,

\widetilde{\psi}_{R}(|x|)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&1,\quad|x|<R-1,\\
&0,\quad|x|>R+1,\end{array}\right.

and
\widetilde{\psi}_{R}(|x|)=|\nabla\widetilde{\psi}_{R}(|x|)|=0
if
|x|=R+1
, and
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},R>1}|\Delta(\widetilde{\psi}_{R}(|x|))|<\infty.

Indeed,

\partial_{i}^{2}\widetilde{\psi}_{R}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime\prime}(|x|-R+2)\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{|x|^{2}}+\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(|x|-R+2)\frac{|x|^{2}-x_{i}^{2}}{|x|^{3}},\\
&\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad R-1\leq|x|\leq R+1,\\
&0,\quad|x|\leq R-1,\,|x|\geq R+1.\end{array}\right.

Multiplying (
4.5
) with
u\widetilde{\psi}_{R}
and integrating by parts over

B_{R+1}
, similarly to the proof of Theorem
4.3
(i),
in view of
V\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the conditions of
\widetilde{\psi}_{R}
on

\partial B_{R+1}
we obtain

\int_{B_{R+1}}\widetilde{\psi}_{R}|\nabla u|^{2}dx\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{R-1\leq|x|\leq R+1}u^{2}\Delta\widetilde{\psi}_{R}dx.

Since
\widetilde{\psi}_{R}=1
for
|x|\leq R-1
, we see

\int_{B_{R-1}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx\leq C\int_{R-1\leq|x|\leq R+1}u^{2}dx.

Setting
R=R_{\ell}
and letting
\ell\to\infty
, we can reach the
conclusion of (ii).

\blacksquare

(ii) as

Theorem 4.5
.

Let

) with (

\inf_{R>1}\int_{R-1\leq|x|\leq R+1}u^{2}dx>0.

4.5
.

If the conclusion (4.8) does not hold, then we have two cases:

(I):

\int_{R_{0}-1\leq|x|\leq R_{0}+1}u^{2}dx=0\quad\mbox{with some $R_{0}>0$}.

(II):
We can find a sequence
R_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\int_{R_{\ell}-1\leq|x|\leq R_{\ell}+1}u^{2}dx=0.

Proof of Lemma
2.1

We recall that
\varphi(x)=|y|^{2\alpha}
for

x:=(y,z)\in(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
.

First Step.

We prove

Lemma 5.1
.

We assume
\varphi\in C^{2}(\overline{D})
and
D\subset(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
is
a piecewise smooth bounded domain. Then, there exist constants

C>0
and
s_{0}>1
such that

\displaystyle\int_{D}\{4s^{3}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)(\partial_{i}\varphi)(\partial_{j}\varphi)-s(\Delta^{2}\varphi)\}|u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dx+4s\int_{D}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)\partial_{i}(ue^{s\varphi})\partial_{j}(ue^{s\varphi})dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{D}|\Delta u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dx

for all
s\geq s_{0}
and
u\in H^{2}_{0}(D)
.
Here the constants
C
and
s_{0}
are independent of choices of piecewise
smooth bounded domain
D\subset(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

For treating the weighted
L^{2}
-norms, we introduce

w(x)=e^{s\varphi(x)}u(x),\quad Pw(x):=e^{s\varphi}(-\Delta)(e^{-s\varphi}w).

Henceforth we write

(u\cdot v):=\int_{D}u(x)v(x)dx,\quad\|v\|:=(v\cdot v)^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\mbox{for $u,v\in L^{2}(D)$}.

Our goal is to obtain a lower estimate of
\|Pw\|^{2}
.
Direct calculations yield

Pw=-\Delta w+2s\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla w+(-s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+s\Delta\varphi)w.

A traditional way for obtaining a lower estimate for

\|Pw\|^{2}
, is the decomposition of the operator

P
into the symmetric part
P_{+}
and the antisymmetric part

P_{-}
, that is,
Pw=P_{+}w+P_{-}w
.
We consider the formal adjoint operator
P^{*}
to
P
:

(Pu\cdot v)=(u\cdot P^{*}v),\quad u,v\in C^{\infty}_{0}(D).

Then, we can calculate as

P^{*}w=-\Delta w-2s\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla w-(s\Delta\varphi+s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2})w.

We define the symmetric part
P_{+}
and the antisymmetric part
P_{-}

of
P
by

P_{+}=\frac{1}{2}(P+P^{*}),\quad P_{-}=\frac{1}{2}(P-P^{*}).

Then,

P_{+}w=-\Delta w-s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}w,\quad P_{-}w=2s\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla w+s(\Delta\varphi)w.

(5.1)

Since
Pw=P_{+}w+P_{-}w
, we have

\begin{split}&\int_{D}|\Delta u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dx=\|P_{+}w+P_{-}w\|^{2}=\|P_{+}w\|^{2}+\|P_{-}w\|^{2}\\
+&2(P_{+}w\cdot\,P_{-}w)\geq 2(P_{+}w\cdot\,P_{-}w)+\|P_{+}w\|^{2}.\end{split}

(5.2)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\int_{D}|\Delta u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dx\geq 2(P_{+}w\cdot\,P_{-}w)=2((-\Delta w-s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}w)\,\,\cdot\,\,(2s(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla w)+s(\Delta\varphi)w))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(-\Delta w\cdot\,2s(\nabla\varphi\cdot\,\nabla w))+2(-\Delta w\cdot\,s(\Delta\varphi)w)

-2(s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}w\cdot\,2s(\nabla\varphi\cdot\,\nabla w))-2(s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}w\cdot\,s(\Delta\varphi)w)=:\sum_{k=1}^{4}J_{k}.

(5.3)

Henceforth
C>0
denotes generic constants which are independent of
s

and the choice of bounded domains
D\subset(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
, but depend on
\varphi
.
We note that
C
may change line by line.

Applying
2(\partial_{k}w)(\partial_{k}\partial_{j}w)=\partial_{j}(|\partial_{k}w|^{2})

and integration by parts, we have

\displaystyle J_{1}=-2\int_{D}(\Delta w)(2s\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla w)dx=-4s\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\int_{D}(\partial_{k}^{2}w)(\partial_{j}w)\partial_{j}\varphi dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4s\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\int_{D}(\partial_{k}w)(\partial_{k}\partial_{j}w)(\partial_{j}\varphi)dx+4s\int_{D}(\partial_{k}w)(\partial_{j}w)(\partial_{k}\partial_{j}\varphi)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2s\int_{D}(\Delta\varphi)|\nabla w|^{2}dx+4s\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\int_{D}(\partial_{j}w)(\partial_{k}w)(\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\varphi)dx.

Moreover,

2s\int_{D}(\nabla(\Delta\varphi)\,\cdot w\nabla w)dx=s\int_{D}(\nabla(\Delta\varphi)\,\cdot\nabla(|w|^{2}))dx=-s\int_{D}(\Delta^{2}\varphi)|w|^{2}dx

Hence, the Green formula yields

\displaystyle J_{2}=-2\int_{D}(\Delta w)s(\Delta\varphi)wdx=2s\int_{D}(\nabla w\cdot\nabla((\Delta\varphi)w))dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2s\int_{D}(\Delta\varphi)|\nabla w|^{2}dx+2s\int_{D}(\nabla(\Delta\varphi)\,\cdot w\nabla w)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2s\int_{D}(\Delta\varphi)|\nabla w|^{2}dx-s\int_{D}(\Delta^{2}\varphi)|w|^{2}dx.

Next, since

\nabla(|\nabla\varphi|^{2})\cdot\nabla\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\partial_{j}\varphi|^{2}\right)\partial_{i}\varphi=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}2(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)(\partial_{i}\varphi)\partial_{j}\varphi,

we obtain

\displaystyle J_{3}=-2\int_{D}s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}w\cdot 2s(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla w)dx=-4s\sum^{n}_{i=1}\int_{D}(s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}w)(\partial_{i}\varphi)(\partial_{i}w)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2s\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{D}s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}(\partial_{i}\varphi)\partial_{i}(w^{2})dx=2s\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{D}\partial_{i}(s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\partial_{i}\varphi)w^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2s\int_{D}\{(\nabla(s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2})\cdot\,\nabla\varphi)+s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\Delta\varphi\}w^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4s^{3}\int_{D}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)(\partial_{i}\varphi)(\partial_{j}\varphi)w^{2}dx+2s^{3}\int_{D}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}(\Delta\varphi)w^{2}dx.

Finally

J_{4}=-2s\int_{D}s^{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}w(\Delta\varphi)wdx=-2s^{3}\int_{D}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}(\Delta\varphi)w^{2}dx.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{4}J_{k}=4s\int_{D}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)(\partial_{i}w)(\partial_{j}w)dx-s\int_{D}(\Delta^{2}\varphi)|w|^{2}dx

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 4s^{3}\int_{D}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)(\partial_{i}\varphi)(\partial_{j}\varphi)|w|^{2}dx.

Thus the proof of Lemma
5.1
is complete.

\blacksquare

Second Step.

Let the function
\varphi
be defined by (
2.1
) and the constant

\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
is defined by (
1.5
).
Then,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{i}\varphi=2\alpha|y|^{2\alpha-2}y_{i},\\
&\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&4\alpha(\alpha-1)|y|^{2\alpha-4}y_{i}y_{j},\quad i\neq j,\\
&4\alpha(\alpha-1)|y|^{2\alpha-4}y_{i}^{2}+2\alpha|y|^{2\alpha-2},\quad i=j,\end{array}\right.\end{array}\right.

(5.4)

for
1\leq i,j\leq m
.
Therefore,

\begin{split}&\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)(\partial_{i}\varphi)(\partial_{j}\varphi)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)(\partial_{i}\varphi)(\partial_{j}\varphi)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}4\alpha(\alpha-1)|y|^{2\alpha-4}y_{i}y_{j}4\alpha^{2}|y|^{4\alpha-4}y_{i}y_{j}\\
+&2\alpha|y|^{2\alpha-2}4\alpha^{2}|y|^{4\alpha-4}\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}^{2}=8\alpha^{3}(2\alpha-1)|y|^{6\alpha-4}.\end{split}

(5.5)

Moreover,

|\Delta^{2}\varphi(x)|\leq C|y|^{2\alpha-4},\quad x\in(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}.

(5.6)

By (1.5), we have
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
.

For all
s>0
, by (5.5) and (5.6), we see

4s^{3}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)(\partial_{i}\varphi)(\partial_{j}\varphi)-s\Delta^{2}\varphi\geq

32\alpha^{3}(2\alpha-1)s^{3}|y|^{6\alpha-4}-Cs|y|^{2\alpha-4}\quad\mbox{in $(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{|y|\leq 1\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-m}$}.

(5.7)

Henceforth, for
a:=(a_{1},...,a_{m})
,
b=(b_{1},...,b_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we
write

(a\cdot b):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}b_{i}
and

|a|=(a\cdot a)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Using
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
and the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality

(a\cdot y)^{2}\leq|a|^{2}|y|^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)a_{i}a_{j}=2\alpha|y|^{2\alpha-2}|a|^{2}+4\alpha(\alpha-1)|y|^{2\alpha-4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}y_{i}y_{j}a_{i}a_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\alpha|y|^{2\alpha-2}|a|^{2}+(2\alpha(2\alpha-1)-2\alpha)|y|^{2\alpha-4}(a\cdot y)^{2}\geq

\displaystyle 2\alpha|y|^{2\alpha-2}|a|^{2}-2\alpha|y|^{2\alpha-4}(a\cdot y)^{2}=2\alpha|y|^{2\alpha-4}(|y|^{2}|a|^{2}-(a\cdot y)^{2}).

Consequently, we have

4s\int_{D}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)(\partial_{i}(ue^{s\varphi}))(\partial_{j}(ue^{s\varphi}))dx\geq 0.

(5.8)

Therefore, we choose
s_{0}>0
sufficiently large such that

32\alpha^{3}(2\alpha-1)s^{3}|y|^{6\alpha-4}-Cs|y|^{2\alpha-4}\geq\alpha^{3}(2\alpha-1)s^{3}|y|^{6\alpha-4},\quad|y|\geq 1

for
s\geq s_{0}
. In terms of (5.7) and (5.8), Lemma 5.1 yields that
there exist constants
s_{0}\geq 1
and
C>0
such that

\int_{D}\alpha^{3}(2\alpha-1)s^{3}|y|^{6\alpha-4}e^{2s\varphi}dx\leq C\int_{D}|\Delta u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dx

(5.9)

for all
s\geq s_{0}
and all
u\in H^{2}_{0}(D)
.

Third Step.

In addition to (5.9), we have to estimate also

|\nabla u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}
.
In view of (5.9), (
5.2
) and (
5.3
), we have

\|P_{+}w\|^{2}+s^{3}\int_{D}|y|^{6\alpha-4}u^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dx\leq C\int_{D}|\Delta u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dx.

(5.10)

Using (5.1) and (5.4), we establish

\displaystyle s\int_{D}(P_{+}w)|y|^{2\alpha-2}wdx=s\int_{D}(-\Delta w)w|y|^{2\alpha-2}dx-s^{3}\int_{D}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}|y|^{2\alpha-2}w^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s\int_{D}|\nabla w|^{2}|y|^{2\alpha-2}dx+s\int_{D}(w\nabla w\cdot y)2(\alpha-1)|y|^{2\alpha-4}dx-s^{3}\int_{D}4\alpha^{2}|y|^{6\alpha-4}w^{2}dx.

Moreover, using
\partial_{k}(|y|^{2\alpha-4})=(\alpha-2)|y|^{2\alpha-6}2y_{k}

for
1\leq k\leq m
, we obtain

\displaystyle s\int_{D}(w\nabla w\cdot y)2(\alpha-1)|y|^{2\alpha-4}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s(\alpha-1)\int_{D}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\partial_{k}(w^{2})y_{k}|y|^{2\alpha-4}dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-s(\alpha-1)\int_{D}w^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\partial_{k}(y_{k}|y|^{2\alpha-4})dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-s(\alpha-1)\int_{D}w^{2}(m|y|^{2\alpha-4}+2(\alpha-2)|y|^{2\alpha-4})dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-s(\alpha-1)(m+2(\alpha-2))\int_{D}w^{2}|y|^{2\alpha-4}dy.

Therefore,

\begin{split}&s\int_{D}|\nabla w|^{2}|y|^{2\alpha-2}dx=s\int_{D}(P_{+}w)w|y|^{2\alpha-2}dx\\
+&s(\alpha-1)(m+2(\alpha-2))\int_{D}w^{2}|y|^{2\alpha-4}dx+s^{3}\int_{D}4\alpha^{2}|y|^{6\alpha-4}w^{2}dx.\end{split}

Applying (
5.10
) and

\left|s\int_{D}(P_{+}w)w|y|^{2\alpha-2}dx\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}|P_{+}w|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}s^{2}\int_{D}|y|^{4\alpha-4}w^{2}dx,

we obtain

\displaystyle s\int_{D}|\nabla w|^{2}|y|^{2\alpha-2}dx\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}|\Delta u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}s^{2}|y|^{4\alpha-4}|w|^{2}dx

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle Cs\int_{D}w^{2}|y|^{2\alpha-4}dx+4s^{3}\int_{D}4\alpha^{2}|y|^{6\alpha-4}w^{2}dx.

(5.11)

Finally, applying (5.9) and
(\nabla u)e^{s\varphi}=-s(\nabla\varphi)w+\nabla w
and

\nabla\varphi=2\alpha|y|^{2\alpha-2}y
,
we estimate the last term in (
5.11
):

\int_{D}(s^{3}|y|^{6\alpha-4}u^{2}+s|y|^{2\alpha-2}|\nabla u|^{2})e^{2s\varphi}dx\leq C\int_{D}|\Delta u|^{2}e^{2s\varphi}dx

for all
s\geq s_{0}
and
u\in H^{2}_{0}(D)
.
Thus the proof of Lemma
2.1
is complete.

\blacksquare
