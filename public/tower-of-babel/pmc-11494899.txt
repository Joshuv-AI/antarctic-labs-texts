Estimating weight for multidimensional health poverty using Delphi method and analytic hierarchy process: a case of China
Authors: Jin Hao, Shiju Dong, Jiajia Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditionally, poverty assessment has relied on a single income criterion, which is inadequate in contexts where absolute poverty has been mitigated. This study developed a weighting scheme for the Multidimensional Health Poverty Index (MHPI) based on sustainable livelihood framework (SLF), aimed at enhancing the accuracy of poverty identification in China.MethodsA two-round Delphi process was used to determine the indicators of the index system. The weight of each indicator was calculated using the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) method. The multidimensional poverty index incorporates SLF’s five dimensions: financial, natural, physical, social, and human capital.ResultsThe initial 46 indicators formed an indicator pool for the Delphi questionnaire. Based on the final consensus of the expert panel, the Delphi consultation resulted in an index system comprising six first-level and 23 second-level indicators. The weight values of the first-level indicators (economic security, health status, education, social capital, healthcare utilisation, and living conditions) were 0.2715, 0.2593, 0.0855, 0.0657, 0.1812, and 0.1363, respectively.ConclusionThis study established a scientific and effective index to evaluate generate weight for estimating multidimensional health poverty in China. Economic security, health status, and healthcare utilisation are the most crucial aspects of multidimensional health poverty. Moreover, the results indicated that vocational training and social capital should be emphasised.Keywords: Multidimensional health poverty, Index, Delphi method, Analytic hierarchy process

Background

Poverty eradication is a common pursuit of all countries in the world, and “End poverty in all its forms everywhere” is the first on the list of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals [1, 2]. The 2023 Global Multidimensional Health Poverty Index Report compiled data from 110 developing countries and found that 1.1 billion out of 6.1 billion people (over 18% of the population) lived in acute multidimensional poverty [3]. The World Bank calculated the poverty headcount ratio of the population at $3.65 per day in 2017, adjusted for purchasing power parity, which fell from 40% in 2014 to 24% in 2019 [4]. Having achieved a miraculous reduction in absolute poverty, the world is turning its attention to relative poverty [5]. Accordingly, poverty measurement has shifted from single income to a multidimensional perspective.

Disease risk is a prominent contributor to poverty. Approximately 100 million people worldwide fall into poverty annually due to out-of-pocket health spending, and 150 million people suffer catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) [6]. Similarly, in certain regions, health expenditure increased the poverty headcount by 21% and poverty gap by 34% in Albania [7]. Additionally, over 36% of 760 adults across 20 provinces in China reported forgoing medical care due to high costs [8]. Illness-induced impoverishment is often assessed through CHE or by measuring income that falls below the poverty line due to healthcare costs [7, 9, 10]. Beyond the perspective of medical costs, Clarke et al. defined health poverty as the lack of individual health capital and the deprivation of health capabilities, drawing on human capital theory and Rasch measurement [11, 12]. Under this framework, health poverty has been measured using self-rated health (SRH) [13], EuroQol five dimensions (EQ-5D) [14], cardiovascular risk, life expectancy, and short-form six-dimensional health survey (SF-6D) [12]. In addition, the study considered both healthcare costs and health capital, defining health poverty as a state in which health deprivation and low income coexist, measured using medical expenditure, income, physical health, mental health, and social health [15].

Absolute poverty has been eliminated in China by 2020. However, due to the uncertainty of diseases and vulnerability to poverty, illness remains the leading cause of multidimensional poverty, accounting for 44% [16]. Health poverty remains a key focus in the post-poverty era [15]. Several measures have been implemented to tackle health poverty, including improving healthcare access, expanding medical insurance benefits, and enhancing public health services in impoverished regions. Impoverished households vary in their needs depending on age and causes of poverty. Identify poor people according to the poverty line may lead to inadequate targeting of poverty eradication measures due to the multiple vulnerabilities of the poor [17]. For instance, individuals and households above the poverty line still endure multidimensional deprivation in education, health, employment, food and nutrition, housing, sanitation, neighborhood [18, 19], face physical environmental challenges[20], and be exposed to chronic stress[21–23]. Therefore, a more diverse and accurate health poverty identification system is essential for effectively identifying and preventing poverty. This study aims to provide a more comprehensive and multidimensional assessment of health poverty by focusing on the deprivation of essential elements for sustainable household livelihoods. It seeks to identify populations affected by health poverty, especially in countries like China that have eliminated absolute poverty.

Determining appropriate weights is a critical step in developing an effective evaluation scheme [24]. Using an appropriate weighting technique is imperative [25]. Weighting methods are generally categorized into equal and unequal weighting methods [26]. The Alkire & Foster methodology, an equal-weight method, is widely used in poverty research [27–29]. Importantly, the MPI is sufficiently flexible to accommodate additional dimensions, indicators, and changes to the weighting scheme [30]. Commonly used unequal-weighting methods include the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) [31], the Decision-Making Trial and Evaluation Laboratory (DEMATEL) [32], and Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Notably, PCA is a fully data-driven method that does not account for experts’ specific weight preference across dimension [33]. By contrast, the Delphi method relies on a combination of empirical evidence and expert consensus for indicator selection. It elicits expert recommendations through iterative rounds of surveys, followed by extensive consultations, summarization, and modifications, ultimately culminating in the development of a consensus-based indicator system [34, 35]. The Delphi method is often combined with the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) to assess the relative weights of the indicators [36]. The AHP is highly applicable to multi-level indicator systems, such as the one in this study, where weights are derived by constructing judgment matrices for each level of indicators and conducting pairwise comparisons [37].

Therefore, this study aims to establish a multidimensional health poverty index (MHPI) using the Delphi method and to develop unequal weights using AHP [38, 39]. The resulting set of indicators is intended to identify families experiencing health-poverty and provide an appropriate tool for assessing MHP and reference for the implementation of precise and categorised assistance measures in the post-poverty era.

Methods

Delphi process

The Delphi method, originally developed by Dalkey and Helmer in the 1950s and 1960s, is a structured approach for gathering expert opinions and reaching consensus [40]. This method involves selecting experts, distributing questionnaires, and collecting feedback over multiple rounds. In each round, experts rate the importance, operability, and sensitivity of the indicators, while providing revisions and comments. After the first round, the data are analyzed descriptively and feedback is given to participants in subsequent rounds, enabling them to revise their responses. The method’s popularity stems from its ability to include experts from diverse locations and fields anonymously, preventing any one expert from dominating the consensus process [35, 41].

We conducted a two-round Delphi survey, with questionnaires delivered and received by email, ensuring no personal information was included. Experts submitted their feedback independently through an online survey platform that does not track identifying details. The feedback report provided a consolidated summary of all expert opinions and suggestions, without directly citing any identifying information [42]. Following the consultation steps of anonymous feedback, data processing, re-consultation, re-anonymised feedback, and data reprocessing [43, 44], 24 experts from various work units and cities reached a consensus.

Inclusion criteria of experts and indexes

Given that health poverty is both an academic issue and closely linked to poverty alleviation practices, we involved university researchers from various provinces, with a particular emphasis on experts from economically disadvantaged regions. We also included specialists from key departments involved in health poverty alleviation, such as the Health Commission and Healthcare Security Administration, to ensure a broad range of perspectives. We assessed their expertise using the coefficient of expert familiarity and authority. Previous studies suggest that the optimal number of consultation experts ranges from 15 to 50 [45]. In this study, we invited 24 experts from the social security sector, public health system, and academic research institutions to contribute to the construction of the index system.

The inclusion criteria of experts were as follows: University researchers: (1) associate professor or above, (2) more than five years of working experience in related research fields, (3) doctoral degree. Practical workers: (1) bachelor’s degree or above, (2) more than five years of working experience in a health-related institution, (3) intermediate-level certificate or above, and (4) voluntary participation in this survey and sufficient time and energy to complete the entire process.

The inclusion criteria for the indices were as follows: average importance score above 7 and a coefficient of variation (CV) below 25%. Indicators that did not meet the inclusion criteria for mean value or CV were adjusted or removed based on expert feedback, with the mean operability and sensitivity scores used as reference [46, 47].

Round 1: rating of indicators

In the first survey round, the Delphi questionnaire consisted of three parts: the survey explanation letter, experts’ basic information, and questionnaire. Firstly, experts rated their familiarity with the indicator system on a scale of 0.2 to 1 point. Secondly, the questionnaire focused on the superiority of the MHPI, where experts rated the importance, operability, and sensitivity of each indicator on a scale of 1 to 10, and provided suggestions for modifications [48]. Thirdly, experts evaluated the basis of their judgments: practical experience, theoretical analysis, knowledge from domestic and international sources, or intuition.

Round 2: revision and grading based on the first round of feedback

After the first survey round, SPSS 25 was used to calculate indicator scores. Indicators are retained if they meet the criteria of an average importance score above 7 and a CV below 25%. If these criteria are not met, qualitative expert opinions are considered, and group discussions are held to modify or remove the indicator. This process leads to the development of a revised indicator system. For the second survey round, the new Delphi questionnaire with revised indicators consisted of three parts in Round 1 again. The letter organized the theoretical framework model for the construction of the index system and attached three summary tables: adjustment of indicators, experts’ qualitative recommendations, and MHPI. Simultaneously, the qualitative and quantitative recommendations from the first round of experts will be shared for the reference of experts. Experts were required to re-evaluate the indicators and conduct a two-by-two comparison of the indicators.

Construct the initial assessment indexes

A systematic literature search was conducted using electronic databases with the search terms ‘multidimensional health poverty’, ‘relative poverty’, and ‘health poverty’ to reveal the relevant indicators in other studies. Commonly recurring indicators in the literature include education, income, chronic disease, basic health insurance, self-assessed health status, pension insurance, work situation, social exclusion, health knowledge, housing, dietary, disability, drinking water, travel time to medical appointments, natural resources, and family intergenerational transfer payments [49]. These indicators form the indicator pool for this study.

Based on SLF developed by the UK Department for International Development in 2000, We use health shocks as vulnerability context that quantifies a household’s livelihood capital from a five-dimensional perspective: financial, natural, physical, social, and human capital [50–53].

Financial capital was reflected by income and assets. Referring the studies of Cheng et al., financial capital is quantified based on income, lack of job, lack of pension insurance, lack of liquid assets and liabilities, lack of fixed assets such as houses, and lack of financial support from children [15, 54]. Natural capital is quantified based on lack of fixed assets, such as land. Physical capital uses the Multidimensional Health Poverty as a reference to incorporate the living conditions dimension, which is quantified based on lack of clean fuel for cooking, lack of sanitary latrines, lack of clean drinking water, lack of electricity at home, lack of bathing facilities, poor housing conditions, and lack of high-value durable goods.

Social capital was reflected by social exclusion and subjective well-being. Following the study by Watson et al. [55], we quantified social exclusion based on not visiting or socialising with friends, not participating in entertainment activities, not having anyone to trust, not participating in formal organised activities, not having anyone to provide care when needed, lack of timely and convenient access to information, and lack of family support. Referring the study of Gray [56], we quantified subjective well-being based on low life satisfaction, poor cognitive ability, and low happiness.

Human capital was reflected by education and health. According to the Grossman model, healthcare utilisation impacts incremental health improvements [57]. Therefore, healthcare utilization is considered as a separate dimension. Based on previous studies [14, 57, 58], health is quantified based on poor nutritional status, poor self-reported health, poor activity capacity, poor mental health, and suffering from serious illness. Healthcare utilization is quantified based on lack of medical facilities within a 15-minute distance, lack of medical insurance, dissatisfied with the medical care level, having a chronic disease but not receiving any treatment, not being hospitalised but should be hospitalised, not using national basic public health services (NBPHS), and not receiving health checkups. Education is quantified based on low average education of the labour force, low education level of minors, and illiteracy of older people.

This study initially constructed an MHPI that corresponds to the actual situation in China. The index includes eight first-level indicators and 38 second-level indicators, as shown in Table 1.

Weight calculation of the indexes

The AHP method, developed by Saaty in the late 1970s, is a widely used multi-criteria decision-making approach [59]. It transforms subjective evaluations of various factors into quantitative weights through pairwise comparisons [45, 60]. AHP has been applied in various contexts, such as the development of clinical guidelines [61] and biomedical innovations, as well as technology development [62]. AHP’s broader acceptance, compared to other methods, stems from its ability to break down complex problems into a hierarchical structure [63].

In this study, experts used AHP to determine the final indicator weights. A pairwise comparison judgment matrix was constructed. Experts compared the indicators in the matrix and assigned a judgement score based on a nine-point Satty scale (Table 2) to evaluate the relative importance of each index [37]. For the judgement matrix constructed using each expert, the weights were calculated separately by MATLAB. The final importance of each indicator was then determined by geometric averaging of the results from 24 experts. Finally, the consistency test of the judgment matrix was conducted [64]. Statistical analysis

All data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2019 and analysed twice. SPSS 25 software was used for the statistical analysis. The composition ratios were used to analyse the basic information provided by the experts. Questionnaire recovery rates were used to reflect the degree of experts’ activity. Authority coefficients (Cr) were used to reflect the degree of expert familiarity with the indicator and indicate the authority of the experts. Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (Kendall’s W) was used to reflect the consistency of opinions.

The judgment coefficient (Ca) was calculated using theoretical analysis, practical experience, knowledge from domestic and foreign counterparts, and intuition. When Ca was 1, the degree of influence on experts was large. When Ca was 0.8, the degree was moderate. When Ca was 0.6, the degree of change was small. The quantitative table of expert judgment criteria and degree of influence is presented in Table 4 [65]. The familiarity coefficient (Cs) was divided into five levels: very familiar (1.0), familiar (0.8), relatively familiar (0.6), average (0.4), and not familiar (0.2). The formula used for Cr calculation is: Cr=(Ca+Cs)/2. Cr levels should be greater than 0.7 [66].

Establishment of the index system

The initial index framework included eight first-level and 38 second-level indicators (Table 1). After analysing and organising the consultation results, the average importance score of the indicators was 5.381–9.381 points, and the CV was 0.092–0.436. Of these, 22 indicators met the inclusion criteria. Following discussions with experts, three first-level and 14 second-level indicators were removed. Five second-level indicators were merged into two new indicators. One first-level and two second-level indicators were added. The descriptions of the indicators were refined. The revised index system includes six first-level and 23 second-level indicators, which were used as the basis for the second round of expert consultation. The results of the second round showed that the average importance score was 6.773–9.500 points and the CV was 0.071–0.322. Compared with the first round, the mean value of the importance score increased, whereas the CV decreased. These results show that the index system of the second round is of higher importance, is more scientific and reasonable, and experts’ opinions tend to be consistent, as shown in Table 7.

The results of the second round of expert consultations were analysed. The results revealed that the CV of the secondary indicator vocational training was high. This indicator is based on human capital theory proposed by economist Theodore Schultz in the 1960s. Human capital refers to the knowledge and skills acquired through education, training, and experience, which can enable poor people to improve their abilities, helping them avoid falling back into poverty and achieving sustainable poverty alleviation [69]. This indicator was retained. The CVs of the first-level indicator social capital and its second-level indicators, social integration and information access, were large. Social capital plays a role in risk-sharing, and families obtain transfer payments from relatives and friends, thereby improving their ability to cope with health shocks. Members of the social circle can seek help and expand employment opportunities for families suffering from health shocks through information sharing. If individuals have a right of speech in social activities, they can strive for more resources and support, thereby alleviating their risk of poverty [70]. Therefore, considering the experts’ opinions, social capital was retained, social integration was changed to social participation, and the indicator interpretation added the contents of social activities. Information access was retained and included internal institutions, online courses, books, and social networks. The secondary indicator healthcare accessibility has a high CV; however, this indicator measures whether medical utilisation is difficult, resulting in poor health [71]. Therefore, this indicator was retained. The CV of the secondary indicator health checkups is high, and experts argued that it is identical with the NBPHS. However, the health checkups in the NBPHS package covers older people over 65 years of age and some sick people, whereas the health checkups targets the entire population [72], which is reflected in the explanation of the indicator. Therefore, this indicator was retained. Finally, the MHPI included six first-level and 23 second-level indicators [73], as shown in Fig. 2.

Weight determination of each indicator

AHP was utilised to compare the indicators of each level in pairs and assign values using the Satty scale. A total of 24 experts evaluated seven judgment matrices, and 154 judgment matrices were constructed. The CR of each judgment matrix was less than 0.1, indicating a good consistency [45]. A total of 154 judgment matrices were tested for consistency, and any matrices that did not meet the consistency test were reevaluated by experts. All the matrices passed the test.

The normalised weight and combination weight of each final indicator were calculated using AHP (Table 8). A higher weight indicated that the indicator was important for the MHP assessment. Among first-level indicators, economic security, health status, healthcare utilisation, living conditions, education, and social capital, the weight values were 0.2715, 0.2593, 0.1812, 0.1363, 0.0855 and 0.0657, respectively.

Discussion

Studies on MHP have selected indicators of cardiovascular risk, health status, life expectancy, and malnutrition [12, 13]. Studies on multidimensional poverty have included indicators of income, health, education, living conditions, social security, and social participation [74–77]. Nevertheless, a literature analysis and expert consultation revealed that MHP is not the only unhealthy state. To prospectively predict and identify health poverty, the capital elements of health production, including economic security, health status, education, social capital, healthcare utilisation, and living conditions, should be considered. The total weight value of factors other than health status was as high as 0.7402, which indicates that experts believe that the factors that affect MHP are not limited to health status, and that other factors also have a significant impact.

This study developed an index system using SLF as the theoretical framework, including six first-level and 23 second-level indicators. A total of 24 experts showed interest in this study, and they were from various fields, such as social security and public health services. They had a high level of authority and familiarity with the indicators, and their opinions reached a consensus. The weight results satisfied the consistency requirements.

The weight of the first-level indicators was as follows: economic security (0.2715), health status (0.2593), healthcare utilisation (0.1812), living conditions (0.1363), education (0.0855) and social capital (0.0657). The weight value of economic security was first. This is a fundamental indicator of health poverty [12, 78–80]. In contrast to using single income indicators, such as relative poverty line or per capita income [15, 58], this study used employment, basic pension/medical insurance, fixed assets, and current assets to comprehensively measure economic security. As absolute poverty has been eliminated in China, the dual structure of urban and rural economics has persisted, with significant income disparities. If we adopt a uniform income standard, the poor would be concentrated in rural areas [74, 81]. The weight value of health status ranked second. It affects health abilities, and illnesses may cause loss of the ability to work. Consequently, individuals will have no fixed income, which reduces the economic secure ability, and fall into the poverty trap [82]. The weight value of healthcare utilisation ranked third. Residents enjoy free and equal basic public health services, and regular health checkups can provide timely treatment for diseases and improve health status [83]. In 2019, the utilisation rate of basic public health services is 93.6%, and the satisfaction rate is 74.7% [84], implying that some people are still unable to access or benefit from this service. The weight value of education was ranked fifth, and vocational training as part of education was ignored by the relevant studies [58, 85]. However, the combined weight of vocational training was ranked seventh, showing experts’ recognition of its importance. Vocational training has the potential to enhance individuals’ productivity, subsequently increasing their income and enabling sustainable poverty eradication; conversely, abandoning it due to health-related reasons means falling into health poverty [16]. In addition, previous studies on the MPI did not consider the social capital dimension [82, 86]. However, several studies have confirmed the negative impact of it on health poverty [87]. In this study, the social capital weight value was ranked sixth.

In terms of the combined weight values of the second-level indicators, the top five were employment (0.1089), illness (0.0814), physical health (0.0745), basic pension/medical insurance (0.0598), and mental health (0.0579). These results are consistent with the first-level indicators. Employment and basic pension/medical insurance belong to the economic security, and the employment situation is directly related to income and capital accumulation. Therefore, this is fundamental for improving the ability of economic security. Basic pension/medical insurance greatly improves the ability to cope with the risk of illness and reduces household vulnerability [88]. A study on the relationship between income levels and health outcomes in Australia showed a significant positive correlation between poor health and poverty [12]. Therefore, relevant authorities should focus on families’ economic security abilities. Illness, physical health, and mental health belong to the health status. Falling ill signifies that health condition has deteriorated, and the subsequent medical expenses directly burden the family. Health status directly affects the overall functioning of the body, and maintaining good health status reduces the risk of illness [13]. Anxiety, depression, and other negative emotions can weaken the function of the immune system, reduce the quality of sleep, and force people to neglect healthy diets and exercise, indirectly leading to overall health into a state of health poverty [14]. This is consistent with the findings of Li et al., who identified health status and disease as important factors affecting the MHP [15, 51]. Authorities should advocate healthy lifestyles, invest in health education, and promote public health measures to build a healthy, vibrant, and sustainable society.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations. Firstly, some of the indicators should be updated to reflect the ever-changing developmental progress in the MHPI. Secondly, the weight value of each index depends on the subjective judgment of experts. However, harmonious and positive communication between researchers and experts may help avoid this problem to some extent. Thirdly, although the project details were fully described before the questionnaire, experts might have varied in their understanding of the questionnaire. Fourthly, we do not provide estimates of MHP, but instead focus on identifying the relative importance of dimensions and indicators. In the future, we plan to develop a questionnaire to collect data based on the final set of indicators and compute MHP scores using appropriate statistical methods. This is a prominent manifestation of the practical value of this study; that is, it helps policymakers predict and identify health-related poverty prospectively.

Conclusion

A hierarchical index for the assessment of MHP in China was established using the Delphi process and the AHP. The involvement of experts with strong theoretical backgrounds and practical experience, filling out the questionnaire anonymously, ensures a scientifically robust process [47]. After two rounds of consultation, expert opinions converged. The results show that the CR values of the judgment matrix are all below 0.1, meeting the consistency requirement. Economic security is the primary factor contributing to MHP, followed by health status, healthcare utilisation. Special attention was paid to China’s national conditions in the selection of the indicators. A single-income poverty line is insufficient to measure economic security; rather, comprehensive indicators should be adopted. In the education dimension, the connotations were broadened to include a vocational training indicator. Healthcare utilisation is closely related to health status. NBPHS and health checkups were added to measure this. In addition, the importance of social capital cannot be ignored. This study constructed an MHPI and developed weights that provides theoretical methods and measurement tools to alleviate the problem of MHP in China’s post-poverty alleviation era. However, other countries facing similar problems should flexibly adjust the dimensions and weights of the index system according to specific situations and data availability to achieve more accurate evaluation outcomes.
