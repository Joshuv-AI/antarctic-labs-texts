Late-onset tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy in lung transplant recipient: Case report
Authors: Qiaoyan Lian, Lulin Wang, Peihang Xu, Li Wei, Shouning Zhou, Xiuhua Li, Xin Xu, Jianxing He, Chunrong Ju
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTacrolimus is regarded as a cornerstone of immunosuppressive therapy after lung transplantation. Tacrolimus-induced neurotoxicity is common, while late-onset severe encephalopathy is rare.Case presentationWe report a case of a 61-year-old woman who presented with confusion and limb stiffness 29 months after undergoing bilateral lung transplantation for bronchiectasis. She received immunosuppressive regimen consisting of low-dose tacrolimus, sirolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisone due to renal insufficiency. Neurological examination revealed gaze deviation towards the right side in both eyes, as well as nuchal rigidity. The muscle strength of her extremities was at grade 1–2, and muscle tone was increased, with a bilateral positive Babinski sign. Blood tests revealed that tacrolimus and sirolimus levels were within the therapeutic range. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed that periventricular white matter lesions were slightly more extensive than those observed prior to transplantation. Lumbar puncture showed that cerebrospinal fluid pressure and composition were normal. A diagnosis of tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy was made after a thorough clinical examination. Subsequently, cyclosporine was administered instead of tacrolimus for anti-rejection therapy, and she has remained free of neurological symptoms since then. The improvement of clinical symptoms following the withdrawal of tacrolimus supports the drug-induced etiology of this neurological disorder.ConclusionsWe highlight that tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy can occur in a late stage after lung transplantation and may occur with tacrolimus whole blood concentrations within the therapeutic range.Keywords: Lung transplant, Tacrolimus, Encephalopathy, Case report

Introduction

Tacrolimus, a cornerstone of immunosuppression after lung transplantation, is associated with a narrow therapeutic window and can cause various side effects. Some of the most commonly reported side effects include hypertension, hyperglycemia, nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and gastrointestinal toxicity [1]. Neurologic complications of tacrolimus can involve both the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral nervous system. Neurologic manifestations related to the CNS include headaches, mood disturbances, paresthesia, tremor, seizure, coma, and so on [2,3]. Among these, the most severe form of neurologic toxicity is posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) [4]. Severe neurologic toxicity generally manifests within the first 30 days following transplantation, the occurrence of late-onset severe encephalopathy is rare [2,[5], [6], [7]].

Herein, we present a case of a patient who was diagnosed with tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy in a late stage, characterized by symptoms of confusion and limb stiffness. The diagnosis of tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy was confirmed through a comprehensive clinical assessment and subsequent improvement following the withdrawal of tacrolimus.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 61-year-old woman who underwent bilateral lung transplantation for bronchiectasis in November 2020. Initially, she received standard triple immunosuppressive regimen consisting of tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and prednisone. Due to renal insufficiency, her treatment was adjusted six months post-transplantation to include low-dose tacrolimus (targeted whole blood trough level of 3–7 ng/ml), sirolimus (targeted whole blood trough level of 3–7 ng/ml), MMF, and prednisone. She enjoyed a good quality of life for more than two years. However, 29 months after the lung transplantation, in April 2023, she developed symptoms of nausea, vomiting and general weakness after catching a cold. Blood tests revealed severe hyponatremia (SNa = 104mmol/L), with tacrolimus whole blood trough level at 5.5 ng/ml and sirolimus whole blood trough level at 4.78 ng/ml. The patient exhibited a personality change with increased irritability after correcting the hyponatremia. Physical examination was unremarkable. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed periventricular white matter lesions that were slightly more extensive than those observed prior to the transplantation (Fig. 1A &B).

(B) At the time the patient appeared to have personality changes, MRI revealed periventricular white matter lesions mildly more extensive than before lung transplantation. (C) When the patient's condition rapidly worsened with confusion, MRI showed periventricular white matter lesions almost identical to those observed in (B).

Her condition rapidly worsened with confusion and inability to speak within a few days. Neurological examination revealed gaze deviation towards the right side in both eyes, as well as nuchal rigidity. Muscle strength in her extremities was at grade 1–2 out of 5 on the Muscle Research Council (MRC) scale, and muscle tone was increased with bilateral positive Babinski sign. Brain MRI showed periventricular white matter lesions similar to those observed previously (Fig. 1C). Lumbar puncture revealed normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure, with a normal CSF composition, including nucleated cells, glucose, chlorides, and protein. Tests for Epstein-Barr virus, Cytomegalovirus, and JC polyoma virus were negative in both CSF and blood. Antibodies associated with demyelination (AQP4, MOG, GFAP, MBP) in the central nervous system, as well as those against autoimmune encephalitis, were found to be negative. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) did not reveal any tumors or lymph node enlargement.

Tacrolimus, sirolimus, and MMF were discontinued, and the patient was initially treated with meropenem (2g every 8 hours), intravenous glucocorticoids (methylprednisolone, 60mg/day), and immunoglobulin (15g/day) upon admission. Systemic examination revealed no evidence of cerebrovascular disease, intracranial infection, demyelinating disease, autoimmune encephalitis, or central nervous system neoplasms. Ultimately, a diagnosis of tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy was established, and cyclosporine was administered instead of tacrolimus for anti-rejection therapy. The patient received quadruple immunosuppression consisting of cyclosporine, sirolimus, MMF, and prednisone after discharge, and she has remained free of neurological symptoms since then. Discussion

In this study, we report a patient diagnosed with late-onset tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy, occurring 29 months following lung transplantation. Her main clinical manifestations were confusion and limb stiffness, which improved upon the withdrawal of tacrolimus. A previous study has reported that the neurotoxicity related to tacrolimus varies from 7 to 32 % in solid organ transplants (SOTs) [8]. In contrast, the incidences of tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy in SOTs are much lower, ranging from 0.34 % to 6.0 % [4]. Several factors appear to predispose patients treated with tacrolimus to encephalopathy, such as underlying metabolic disturbances, worse functional status, hypertension, and allograft failure [[2], [9]].

Patients with tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy may exhibit a variety of neurological and psychiatric disorders due to potential impairment of sensory-motor functions. Mild symptoms such as headache, altered mental status, paresthesia, and tremor can be observed, while severe symptoms may manifest as seizure, speech disorders, visual disturbances, paresis, coma, or PRES [[2], [3], [4]]. The patient in our study initially presented with mood disturbance, which subsequently progressed to confusion, coinciding with the symptoms of tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy.

The pathogenesis of tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy is currently unclear. Pharmacokinetic factors may play a crucial role in the development of tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy. Tacrolimus can be transported into the brain via ATP-binding cassette sub-family B member 1 (ABCB1) and binds to high levels of FK506 binding protein (FKBP) within brain tissue [10]. Interactions with the ABCB1 transporter may also impact susceptibility to encephalopathy. There are several hypotheses have been proposed: (1) Tacrolimus may increase the permeability of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) by inducing apoptosis and nitric oxide production, as well as inhibiting P-glycoprotein function. This can lead to drug accumulation in the brain and impaired BBB function [11]; (2) Prolonged exposure to tacrolimus might induce endothelial injury, resulting in ischemia and hypoxia of the corresponding brain tissue, leading to vasogenic edema [2]; (3) Alterations in mitochondrial function induced by tacrolimus are thought to contribute to neurotoxicity [3]; (4) Since calcineurin is expressed in several areas of the brain, the complex formed by tacrolimus and immunophilins may be associated with neurotoxicity [3]. (5) Other proposed mechanisms include vasoconstriction or vascular injury, genetic polymorphisms, modulation of excitability properties, suppression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor and protein expression, and reduction of Ca2+ accumulation in the endoplasmic reticulum [3,12,13].

MRI has been proven to be the most sensitive imaging modality. Findings on MRI in cases of tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy can be heterogeneous but typically include bilateral and symmetrical signal abnormalities, primarily affecting the white matter of the cerebral hemispheres [2,14]. PRES is the most severe and well-documented complication associated with tacrolimus. It typically manifests as vasogenic edema of the subcortical white matter, primarily involving the posterior and parietal areas of the cerebral circulation, often exhibiting bilateral and symmetrical distribution [12]. Normal imaging can also be seen, even in patients who present with considerable neurological deficits [2]. The brain MRI of this case showed slightly more extensive periventricular white matter lesions compared to pre-transplant observations. These imaging findings may be attributed to previous chronic hypoxia, cerebral vascular perfusion abnormalities, and tacrolimus toxicity.

Neurological damage typically arises with high tacrolimus levels, but it can also occur within therapeutic ranges [15,16]. Therefore, whole blood concentration alone are not reliable diagnostic markers of severe neurotoxicity induced by tacrolimus [13]. A comprehensive assessment that includes clinical features, medication history, various risk factors, and imaging manifestations is crucial for diagnosing tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy. Diagnosis in this patient primarily involved excluding other potential causes, including infectious diseases (such as meningitis and encephalitis), progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, cerebrovascular disorders demyelinating disorders, autoimmune encephalitis, and malignancy. Finally, the patient was diagnosed with tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy based on the Naranjo criteria.

To date, there are no guidelines on the treatment of tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy. However, dose adjustment or discontinuation of the offending drugs can prevent severe and permanent neurological injury. In order to maintain adequate immunosuppression against rejection, patients experiencing tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy can be switched to cyclosporine, sirolimus, everolimus, or receive monthly infusion of belatacept [2,11,17]. Discontinuation of the drug typically leads to the reversal of symptoms in most patients. In this case, the patient's neurological symptoms resolved upon the withdrawal of tacrolimus, confirming the diagnosis conclusively. Following the cessation of tacrolimus, the patient's neurological symptoms progressively improved, ultimately leading to the complete resolution of these symptoms. Concurrently, the patient exhibited stable allograft function and enjoyed an ideal quality of life. Consequently, we did not proceed with a rechallenge using tacrolimus.

Conclusion

Tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy is a rare but serious neurological complication, that deserves particular attention in lung transplant recipients, especially when other clinical entities fail to explain the onset of symptoms. It's important to emphasize that tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy can occur in a late stage after lung transplantation and may occur even with tacrolimus whole blood concentrations within the therapeutic range. Immediate discontinuation of the offending drug should be considered if tacrolimus-induced encephalopathy is suspected.
