Pentagonal bipyramids lead to the smallest flexible embedded polyhedron
Matteo Gallet ⋄, Georg Grasegger ∗, Jan Legerský ∘, Josef Schicho

Abstract
Steffen’s polyhedron was believed to have the least number of vertices among polyhedra that can flex without self-intersections. Maksimov clarified that the pentagonal bipyramid with one face subdivided into three is the only polyhedron with fewer vertices for which the existence of a self-intersection-free flex was open. Since subdividing a face into three does not change the mobility, we focus on flexible pentagonal bipyramids. When a bipyramid flexes, the distance between the two opposite vertices of the two pyramids changes; associating the position of the bipyramid to this distance yields an algebraic map that determines a nontrivial extension of rational function fields. We classify flexible pentagonal bipyramids with respect to the Galois group of this field extension and provide examples for each class, building on a construction proposed by Nelson. Surprisingly, one of our constructions yields a flexible pentagonal bipyramid that can be extended to an embedded flexible polyhedron with 8 vertices. The latter hence solves the open question.

In a non-degenerate motion of a pentagonal bipyramid

Basic definitions

In this section, we provide some introductory notions about Galois theory and
we define the subgraphs of the skeleton of a bipyramid that are relevant for our arguments.
We also introduce the notions describing flexibility.
We conclude the section with recalling the types of Bricard octahedra and Nelson construction.

2.1
Basics of Galois theory

Galois theory is a subfield of algebra and number theory that was originally developed to study the solvability of univariate polynomial equations by investigating a group, called the
Galois group
of the given polynomial. The idea is, given a polynomial
f\in K[x]
, where
K
is a field, to construct a smallest superfield
F
of
K
where
f
factors into linear polynomials (such a field is called a
splitting field
of
f
over
K
); then, the Galois group of
f
is the group of automorphisms of
F
that fix
K
. This group can be thought as a subgroup of permutations that swap the roots of
f
: the structure of
f
determines which permutations are allowed and which are not.

We start by providing some basic definitions for Galois theory. Possible references are, among others, the books
[
Art98
,
Jac85
,
Art91
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
extension of fields
(or
field extension
) is an inclusion of fields
K\subset F
; since every non-trivial homomorphism of fields is injective, we can consider non-trivial homomorphisms of fields as a field extension.
A field extension
K\subset F
is called
Galois
if
F
is a splitting field of a polynomial
f\in K[x]
that factors into distinct linear polynomials in
F[x]
.
The
Galois group
of a field extension
K\subset F
is the group

\mathrm{Gal}(F/K):=\bigl\{\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(F)\,\mid\,\varphi|_{K}=\mathrm{id}_{K}\bigr\}\,,

namely, the group of automorphisms of
F
that leave
K
fixed.

In our setting, the Galois group of a flexible pentagonal bipyramid arises as an instance of the following construction.
A dominant rational map
f\colon C\dashrightarrow D
between real algebraic curves
C
and
D

determines a field extension
\mathbb{R}(D)\subset\mathbb{R}(C)
between the function fields of
D
and
C
,
respectively.
Recall that the function field of an algebraic variety
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the fraction field of the coordinate ring
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}]/I(X)
, where
I(X)
is the ideal of polynomials vanishing on
X
.
Hence, we can associate to
f
a Galois group, namely the group
\mathrm{Gal}\bigl(\mathbb{R}(C)/\mathbb{R}(D)\bigr)
.

2.3
Motions

In the following we describe the notions of realizations and motions.

Definition 2.2
.

A
realization
of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
is a map

\rho\colon V_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}

such that the images of vertices connected by an edge are distinct.
Two realizations are
congruent
if one can be obtained from the other by applying a direct isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Definition 2.3
.

A realization
\rho
of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
induces
edge lengths

\lambda_{uv}:=\left\|\rho(u)-\rho(v)\right\|
for each
\{u,v\}\in E_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}
.
Once we fix a tuple of edge lengths
\lambda=(\lambda_{e}\,\colon\,e\in E_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}})
,
we say that the
n
-gonal bipyramid is
flexible

if there exist infinitely many non-congruent realizations of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
inducing
\lambda
.
This is equivalent to the existence of a
flex

for some realization
\rho
inducing
\lambda
, namely, a continuous function

f\colon[0,1)\longrightarrow\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{3}\bigr)^{V_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}}

such that

\triangleright

f(0)=\rho
;

\triangleright

f(t)
is a realization of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}

inducing the same edge lengths as
\rho
for any
t\in[0,1)
;

\triangleright

f(t_{1})
and
f(t_{2})
are not congruent for any
t_{1}\neq t_{2}
.

From
Definition

2.3
we see that it can be useful to consider the set of realizations of a bipyramid
inducing the same edge lengths, up to the action of the group of direct isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We propose to use a notion of
configuration space
derived from the classical construction of
Cayley-Menger varieties
, similarly as Borcea did in
[
Bor02
]
.

We introduce a variety that keeps track of the distances between the vertices of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
and (in addition to the classical Cayley-Menger variety) also of the oriented volume of the tetrahedra
determined by the vertices
(i,i+1,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B})
.
Recall that the
oriented volume
of the tetrahedron in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

defined by the points
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})
is

\frac{1}{6}\det(a_{1}-a_{4}\quad a_{2}-a_{4}\quad a_{3}-a_{4})\,.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}_{n}}
be the set of equatorial edges of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
,
where we choose the following orientation

\bigl\{(1,2),\dotsc,(n-1,n),(n,1)\bigr\}\,.

Consider the map:

(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{V_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\binom{V_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}}{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}

sending a realization
\rho
of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
to the pair

\Bigl(\left(d_{vw}\right)_{v,w\in V_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}},(s_{e})_{e\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}\Bigr)\,,

where
d_{vw}=\left\|\rho(v)-\rho(w)\right\|^{2}
and
for
e=(i,i+1)
, the number
s_{e}
is the oriented volume
of the tetrahedron
(i,i+1,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B})
.
Here, whenever
i=n
, we consider
i+1
to be
1
.
For convenience, we may write
s_{i,i+1}
instead of
s_{(i,i+1)}
.
We define the
oriented Cayley-Menger variety

\mathrm{CM}^{\ast}_{n}
to be the Zariski closure of the image of this map.
By construction, the function field
\mathbb{R}(\mathrm{CM}^{\ast}_{n})
is generated by the classes of the functions
\left(d_{vw}\right)_{v,w\in V_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}},(s_{e})_{e\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}
.

Given edge lengths
\lambda
for
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
, we define the
configuration space

K_{\lambda}:=\mathrm{CM}^{\ast}_{n}\cap\bigl\{d_{vw}=\lambda^{2}_{\{v,w\}}\text{ for all }\{v,w\}\in E_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}\bigr\}\,.

We introduce some quantities that play a crucial role in
Section

3
.

Definition 2.5
.

For a realization
\rho
of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
, we denote by
\varsigma_{ij}
the oriented volume of the realization of a tetrahedron
(i,j,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B})
.
In particular, we have
\varsigma_{i,i+1}=s_{i,i+1}
and
\varsigma_{i,j}=-\varsigma_{j,i}
.
Appendix

A
reports equations relating the quantities
\varsigma_{i,j}
and the distances
d_{uv}
.

Remark 2.6
.

Any configuration space of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
is a curve since, if it was at least two-dimensional,
then we could fix the distance between
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{B}
and still have a one-dimensional motion,
but this is impossible because the structure we obtain is composed of tetrahedra with fixed edge lengths.

Definition 2.7
.

A
motion
of an
n
-gonal bipyramid is any one-dimensional component
of the configuration space
K_{\lambda}
of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
with fixed edge lengths
\lambda

whose general point is given by the distances induced by a realization of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
inducing
\lambda
.
We call a motion
non-degenerate
if the vertices of each
3
-cycle in
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
are non-collinear
and all non-edges change their lengths along the motion.

Notice that it is not possible that the dihedral angle at an edge of the equator stays constant along any motion
(equivalently,
s_{i,i+1}
is constant)
since this would fix the distance between
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{B}
, which implies all dihedral angles being constant.
Instead, if the dihedral angle at an edge of the form
\{\mathsf{T},i\}
or
\{\mathsf{B},i\}
,
where
i
is a vertex of the equator, stays constant along a motion,
then the distance between the vertices
i-1
and
i+1
(mod
n
) is constant and
so we fall back to the case of a flexible
\mathbb{B}_{n-1}
by omitting the vertex
i
and adding the edge
\{i-1,i+1\}
.
This is why we ask this not to happen for non-degenerate motions.

For any non-degenerate motion
\mathcal{M}
and almost tetrahedron
\mathbb{T}_{{i},{i+1}}
,
there is a regular algebraic map

\pi_{i,i+1}\colon\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1}\,,

where the curve
\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1}
is a subset of
the configuration curves
\mathbb{T}_{{i},{i+1}}
,
namely the general points in
\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1}
are the restrictions to
\mathbb{T}_{{i},{i+1}}

of distances determined by realizations of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
.
The fact that
\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1}
is a curve (and not a point)
is a consequence of the non-degeneracy assumption on
\mathcal{M}
.

Notice that, in order to freeze the action of the group of direct isometries, one could simply pin down the realization of a triangle in
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
:
in this way, any two different realizations with the same pinned triangle would automatically be not congruent under any direct isometry.
However, this approach would lead to some technical subtleties to be handled when, for example,
we have to consider at the same time the configuration spaces of two almost tetrahedra that do not share a triangle.
Because of this, we prefer to use the approach exposed above,
which allows us to easily restrict from the configuration space of the whole
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}

to the one of an almost tetrahedron.

2.4
Flexible octahedra and the Nelson construction

It is well-known from the works of Bricard (see
[
Bri97
]
),
that there are three kinds of motions for octahedra:

\triangleright

Type I (line-symmetric): here, in each realization, there are three pairs of vertices that are symmetric with respect to a line;

\triangleright

Type II (plane-symmetric): here, in each realization, the set of vertices is symmetric with respect to a plane; moreover, two vertices lie on the symmetry plane;

\triangleright

Type III: here, the edge lengths in each of the three induced
4
-cycles satisfy a linear relation
and there are two flat poses.

We recall a construction idea from Nelson
[
Nel10
]
,
that allows us to form a flexible pentagonal bipyramid starting from two flexible octahedra.

Let
\mathbb{O}_{\mathsf{k}}
and
\mathbb{O}_{\mathsf{p}}
be two octahedra, i.e.,
4
-gonal bipyramids.
Define
V_{\mathbb{O}_{\mathsf{k}}}:=\{1,2,3,0,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B}\}
and
V_{\mathbb{O}_{\mathsf{p}}}:=\{3,4,5,0,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B}\}
so that
the non-edges in
\mathbb{O}_{\mathsf{k}}
are
\{1,3\}
,
\{2,0\}
,
\{\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B}\}
and
the non-edges in
\mathbb{O}_{\mathsf{p}}
are
\{3,5\}
,
\{4,0\}
,
\{\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B}\}
.
A pair
(\rho_{\mathsf{k}},\rho_{\mathsf{p}})
of realizations of the two octahedra is called a
fitting pair

if the following hold:

\triangleright

\rho_{\mathsf{k}}(i)=\rho_{\mathsf{p}}(i)
for all
i\in\{0,3,T,B\}
;

\triangleright

the three points
\rho_{\mathsf{k}}(0),\rho_{\mathsf{k}}(1),\rho_{\mathsf{p}}(5)
are collinear.

A fitting pair induces a realization
\rho_{\mathbb{B}_{5}}
of the pentagonal bipyramid with vertices

1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
\mathsf{T}
,
\mathsf{B}
:
for each vertex
u
, we define
\rho_{\mathbb{B}_{5}}(u)
to be

\rho_{\mathsf{k}}(u)
or
\rho_{\mathsf{p}}(u)
,
depending which of these two values is defined.
If both values are defined, then they coincide by the definition of fitting pair,
so
\rho_{\mathbb{B}_{5}}
is well-defined.
Note that the distance between
\rho_{\mathbb{B}_{5}}(1)

and
\rho_{\mathbb{B}_{5}}(5)
is determined by the induced edge lengths of the octahedra,
up to only two possible values, namely
\lambda^{\mathsf{k}}_{\{1,0\}}+\lambda^{\mathsf{p}}_{\{5,0\}}

and
|\lambda^{\mathsf{k}}_{\{1,0\}}-\lambda^{\mathsf{p}}_{\{5,0\}}|
.

Assume now that a fitting pair
(\rho_{\mathsf{k}},\rho_{\mathsf{p}})
admits a
flex
, namely a pair
(f_{\mathsf{k}},f_{\mathsf{p}})
of flexes

f_{\mathsf{k}}\colon[0,1)\longrightarrow\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{3}\bigr)^{V_{\mathbb{O}_{\mathsf{k}}}}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\mathsf{p}}\colon[0,1)\longrightarrow\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{3}\bigr)^{V_{\mathbb{O}_{\mathsf{p}}}}

such that
\bigl(f_{\mathsf{k}}(t),f_{\mathsf{p}}(t)\bigr)
is a fitting pair for any
t\in[0,1)
.

When a fitting pair admits a flex,
by simply forgetting vertex

we obtain a flex of a pentagonal bipyramid.
This is what we call a

of a flexible pentagonal bipyramid,

3
Galois theory for motions

In this section, we define the notion of
Galois group
of a motion of a pentagonal bipyramid and we describe which groups arise as such.
This notion, however, can be defined for arbitrary
n
-gonal bipyramids.
Since the first results that we obtain are true for arbitrary bipyramids, we start with the more general setting and then we specialize to the case of pentagonal bipyramids.
We point out that some of the results in this section (
Lemma

3.11
and
Corollary

3.12
) resamble the ones by Connelly in
[
Con74
, Lemma 3 and 4]
(see also
[
AC11
, Section 2]
): however, the two techniques are different and a translation between them is not apparent, although we believe it would be worth of investigation.

We fix a non-degenerate motion
\mathcal{M}
of an
n
-gonal bipyramid and we consider the rational map

d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\colon\mathcal{M}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{R}

associating to a point of
\mathcal{M}
its
\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}
-coordinate, i.e., the squared distance between the realizations of
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{B}
.
This rational map determines a field extension
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\subset\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
,
where
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})
and
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
are the function fields of the affine line and
\mathcal{M}
, respectively.

Definition 3.1
.

We define
\mathscr{G}
to be the Galois group of the extension
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\subset\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
.

We first show that
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\subset\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
is Galois (
Lemma

3.5
) and
that
\mathscr{G}
can be considered as a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}
(
Lemma

3.6
).
The latter embedding uses in a key way the restriction of the motion
\mathcal{M}

to each of the
n
almost tetrahedra constituting the
n
-gonal bipyramid.
We then establish several relations between the existence of certain elements in the Galois group
and the oriented volumes of tetrahedra with vertices in
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
,
together with their consequences on flexible bipyramids.
After that, we specialize to pentagonal bipyramids and we explore how kinematics and geometry constrain the possible elements in the Galois group,
until we prove that there are only two possibilities (
Proposition

3.19
).
We conclude by showing that each of these groups can be realized by a Nelson construction (
Theorem

3.20
,
Theorem

3.21
).

Since
\mathcal{M}
is a subvariety of
\mathrm{CM}^{\ast}_{n}
, all the coordinate functions
d_{vw}
and
s_{i,i+1}
are elements of
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
.
Keep in mind that, for every edge
e=\{v,w\}\in E_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}
, we have the equation
d_{e}=\lambda_{e}^{2}
in
\mathcal{M}
, hence the coordinate function
d_{e}
is invariant under any element of
\mathscr{G}
.
We start by clarifying how the Galois group
\mathscr{G}
acts on the
s
-variables.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathscr{G}
. Then for every
i\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
, we have

\varphi(s_{i,i+1})=\pm s_{i,i+1}\,.

Proof.

By
Lemma

A.1
,
s_{i,i+1}
satisfies an equation of the form
s_{i,i+1}^{2}-F=0
where
F
is a polynomial in the variables
(d_{vw})_{\{v,w\}\in E_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}}
and
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}
.
Therefore, the element
F
is left invariant by
\varphi
, hence

\varphi(s_{i,i+1})^{2}=F\,,

which gives the statement.
∎

As we mentioned, the study of the restriction of the motion
\mathcal{M}
to the various almost tetrahedra
is crucial in our understanding of the Galois group.
Recall that the curve
\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1}
is the image of
\mathcal{M}
under the restriction map
\pi_{i,i+1}

between the configuration space of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
and the configuration space of the almost tetrahedron
\mathbb{T}_{{i},{i+1}}
.
Then, the map
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}
fits into a commutative diagram
\mathcal{M}
\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1}
\mathbb{R}
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}
(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})_{i,i+1}
\pi_{i,i+1}

where
(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})_{i,i+1}
is the distance (from
\mathsf{T}
to
\mathsf{B}
) map applied to realizations in
\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1}
.
Notice that
\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1}
is irreducible: its elements are configurations of the almost tetrahedron
\mathbb{T}_{{i},{i+1}}
,
which are parametrized by the angle between the triangles
\{i,i+1,\mathsf{T}\}
and
\{i,i+1,\mathsf{B}\}
.
The maps
\pi_{i,i+1}
are dominant: in fact,
\pi_{i,i+1}
is non-constant since if the distance between
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{B}

changes, then the angle in the almost tetrahedron changes as well;
in the same way, we show that the maps
(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})_{i,i+1}
are dominant as well.
Hence, we can interpret the function fields
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})

as intermediate field extensions between
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})
and
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
.
Moreover, we call the Galois group of
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\subseteq\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})
the Galois group of the almost tetrahedron
\mathbb{T}_{{i},{i+1}}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Each extension
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\subseteq\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})
is of degree
2
.
The Galois group of
\mathbb{T}_{{i},{i+1}}
is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Proof.

All the
d
-coordinate functions in
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})
are left invariant by the Galois group.
Due to
Proposition

3.2
, the only
s
-coordinate function, namely
s_{i,i+1}
, satisfies a degree
2
polynomial equation over
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})
.
If
s_{i,i+1}
satisfied a degree
1
equation over
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})
, then the complexification of the map
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\colon\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
would be of degree
1
, i.e., a general element of the codomain would have a preimage of cardinality
1
.
This, however, would contradict the fact that,
\mathbb{T}_{{i},{i+1}}
being flexible,
there is a whole interval of values for the distance between
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{B}

such that there exist two realizations with the same edge lengths and the given distance
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}
that are not related by a direct isometry.
Hence,
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\subseteq\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})
has degree
2
, so the Galois group of
\mathbb{T}_{{i},{i+1}}
is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

The field
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
is generated by the subfields
\{\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Proof.

To show that
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
is generated by
\{\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
it is enough to prove that all the coordinate functions of
\mathcal{M}
can be written as rational functions
in the coordinate functions of each
\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1}
.
Recalling
Definition

2.4
,
we then have to express all distances
d_{vw}
, where
v,w\in V_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}
,
as rational functions in the distances
d_{v^{\prime}w^{\prime}}
,
where
v^{\prime},w^{\prime}\in V_{\mathbb{T}_{{i},{i+1}}}
, and the coordinate functions
s_{i,i+1}

for some
i\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and the distance
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}
. This is a special case of
Corollary

A.5
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

The extension
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\subset\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
is Galois.

Proof.

Each extension
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\subset\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})
is quadratic by
Lemma

3.3
,
so
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})
is a splitting field of a polynomial
x^{2}-\alpha_{i,i+1}
with
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})
: its roots are the elements
s_{i,i+1}
and
-s_{i,i+1}
.
Hence, by
Lemma

3.4
the field
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
is a splitting field of the polynomial
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x^{2}-\alpha_{i})
.
Therefore, the extension
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\subset\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
is Galois.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

The Galois group
\mathscr{G}
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}
,
where
{\mathcal{E}_{n}}
is the set of equatorial edges of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
.

Proof.

Let
H_{i,i+1}
be the subgroup of
\mathscr{G}
determined by
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})
via the Galois correspondence.
Since
\mathbb{R}(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\subset\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})
is quadratic,
the subgroup
H_{i,i+1}
is normal in
\mathscr{G}
and
\mathscr{G}/H_{i,i+1}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
Since
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
is generated by
\{\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M}_{i,i+1})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
the intersection of all
\{H_{i,i+1}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is the trivial subgroup.
This implies that the map

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathscr{G}&\longrightarrow&\mathscr{G}/H_{1,2}\times\dotsb\times\mathscr{G}/H_{n,1}\,,\\
\varphi&\longmapsto&([\varphi]_{1,2},\dotsc,[\varphi]_{n,1})\end{array}

is an injective homomorphism.
∎

Definition 3.7
.

From now on, we identify elements of
\mathscr{G}
with their images in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}
.
As clarified by the proof of
Lemma

3.6
,
each factor of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}
corresponds to an almost tetrahedron.
Hence, we represent an element
\varphi
in
\mathscr{G}
by a binary tuple
such that the entry
(i,i+1)
is
1

if and only if
\varphi(s_{i,i+1})=-s_{i,i+1}
.
We denote by
{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}
the set of equatorial edges
e

such that the entry
e
in the tuple of
\varphi
is 1.

Lemma 3.8
.

For every two equatorial edges
e_{1},e_{2}\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}
, there exists
\varphi\in\mathscr{G}
such that
e_{1},e_{2}\in{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}
.

Proof.

For each equatorial edge
e
, the projection
\mathscr{G}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{2}
onto the factor corresponding to
e
is surjective.
Indeed, by the fundamental theorem of Galois theory, the image of that projection is the Galois group of
\mathbb{T}_{e}
,
which is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
by
Lemma

3.3
.
Let
\varphi,\psi\in\mathscr{G}
be such that
e_{1},e_{2}\in{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}
. Then, at least one of
\varphi
,
\psi
, or
\varphi+\psi
is a witness for the statement.
∎

The next series of results focuses on the relation between
the Galois group and the oriented volumes of tetrahedra in
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

If
\varphi\in\mathscr{G}
is such that
{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}
are consecutive equatorial edges,
namely,
\varphi
is of the form

(0,\dotsc,0,\underbrace{\overset{(i,i+1)}{1},\dotsc,\overset{(j-1,j)}{1}}_{(j-i)\text{ ones}},0,\dotsc,0)\,,

then
\varsigma_{i,j}=0
. Recall from
Definition

2.5
and
Corollary

A.5
that
\varsigma_{i,j}
is the rational function in
\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{M})
representing the volume of the tetrahedron
(i,j,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B})
. In particular, the vertices
i,j,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B}
are coplanar during the motion.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\mathscr{G}
.
We first prove that if
(i,i+1)
and
(i+1,i+2)
are in
{\psi}^{(\mathbf{1})}
, then
\psi(\varsigma_{i,i+2})=-\varsigma_{i,i+2}
.
In fact, from
Lemma

A.2
it follows that
\psi(d_{13})=d_{13}
.
Now, using
Lemma

A.4
with
k=i+1
and
j=i+2
the statement follows,
since
\psi(a_{ijk})=-a_{ijk}
while
\psi(b_{ijk})=b_{ijk}
,
because
\psi
leaves the squared volumes
W
invariant thanks to the fact that
\psi(d_{13})=d_{13}
.
By the very same argument (and using induction as in
Corollary

A.5
), when all the entries from
(i,i+1)
until
(j-1,j)
are ones, we have
\psi(\varsigma_{i,j})=-\varsigma_{i,j}
.
Similarly, if all entries from
(i,i+1)
until
(j-1,j)
of
\psi
are zero,
one has
\psi(\varsigma_{i,j})=\varsigma_{i,j}
,
since the only difference from the previous case is that
\psi(a_{ijk})=a_{ijk}
.
Hence, for the element
\varphi
from the statement, we have
\varphi(\varsigma_{i,j})=-\varsigma_{i,j}
considering the ones from entry
(i,i+1)
to entry
(j-1,j)
, and at the same time we have
\varphi(\varsigma_{i,j})=\varsigma_{i,j}
considering the zeros from entry
(j,j+1)
to entry
(i-1,i)
.
Altogether, we obtain
\varsigma_{i,j}=0
.
Since
\varsigma_{i,j}
is the a volume of the tetrahedron
(i,j,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B})
, this means that such volume is zero for a general element of the motion, hence the four vertices
i,j,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B}
stay coplanar during the motion.
∎

Since we suppose that the motion
\mathcal{M}
is non-degenerate, each tetrahedron
(i,i+1,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B})
changes its volume during the motion. In view of
Lemma

3.9
, we obtain:

Corollary 3.10
.

No element in
\mathscr{G}
has a single one or a single zero.

Lemma 3.11
.

If
\varphi\in\mathscr{G}
, then

\sum_{(i,i+1)\in{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}}s_{i,i+1}=0\,.

Proof.

We use the fact that the generalized volume of a flexible
n
-gonal bipyramid
equals zero
[
Con78
]
, but the Bellows Theorem
[
CSW97
]

stating that it is a constant would suffice as well.

For a fixed orientation of the faces of the polyhedron
(i.e., a cyclic ordering of the vertices of each face
such that each edge is ordered in the opposite direction in the two faces it belongs to),
the generalized volume of the polyhedron with realization
\rho
is defined by

\frac{1}{6}\sum\det\bigl(\rho(i),\rho(j),\rho(k)\bigr)\,,

where the sum is over all oriented faces
(i,j,k)
.

We fix an orientation of the faces of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}
as follows:

(i,i+1,\mathsf{T})
and
(i+1,i,\mathsf{B})
for
i\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
.
Since the generalized volume is invariant under direct isometries,
we can suppose that
\mathsf{B}
is at the origin.
Hence, the faces containing
\mathsf{B}
do not contribute to the generalized volume
and the faces containing
\mathsf{T}
supply the oriented volumes of the tetrahedra
(i,i+1,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B})
.
So the generalized volume of an
n
-gonal bipyramid is

W=\sum_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}s_{i,i+1}=0\,.

Taking the difference
W-\varphi(W)
yields the statement.
∎

Corollary 3.12
.

If
\varphi\in\mathscr{G}
has exactly two ones, then the two almost tetrahedra corresponding to edges in
{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}

flatten simultaneously at two poses.

Corollary 3.13
.

If
\varphi\in\mathscr{G}
has exactly two ones,
then the two edges in
{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}
have the same length.

Proof.

Let
{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}=\{(i,i+1),(j,j+1)\}
.
By
Lemma

3.11
, we know that
s_{i,i+1}=-s_{j,j+1}
, hence
s_{i,i+1}^{2}=s_{j,j+1}^{2}
.
By
Lemma

A.1
, the determinants of the matrices
M_{i,i+1,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B}}
and
M_{j,j+1,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B}}
(introduced in the lemma) are equal. These determinants are polynomials in
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}
, and comparing their leading coefficients yields the result.
∎

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
\varphi,\psi\in\mathscr{G}
.
Then

\sum_{(i,i+1)\in{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}\cap{\psi}^{(\mathbf{1})}}s_{i,i+1}=0\,.

In particular,
|{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}\cap{\psi}^{(\mathbf{1})}|\neq 1
.

Proof.

Lemma

3.11

applied to
\varphi
,
\psi
, and
\varphi+\psi
,
yields

\sum_{(i,i+1)\in{\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}}s_{i,i+1}=0\,,\qquad\sum_{(i,i+1)\in{\psi}^{(\mathbf{1})}}s_{i,i+1}=0\,,\qquad\sum_{(i,i+1)\in S}s_{i,i+1}=0\,,

where
S=({\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}\cup{\psi}^{(\mathbf{1})})\setminus({\varphi}^{(\mathbf{1})}\cap{\psi}^{(\mathbf{1})})
.
Subtracting the third equation from the sum of the first two gives the first statement.
The second one follows since no
s_{i,i+1}
can be constantly zero.
∎

The simplest possible Galois group arises only for special motions.

Proposition 3.15
.

If
\mathscr{G}=\langle(1,1,\ldots,1)\rangle
,
then there are two flat poses of the whole bipyramid.

Proof.

The proof is in the same spirit as
Lemma

3.3
:
we show that if there is at most one flat pose,
then the Galois group
\mathscr{G}
has more than
2
elements,
by inferring that the complexification of the map
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\colon\mathcal{M}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{R}
has degree greater than
2
.

For each equatorial edge
e\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}
,
let
m_{e}
be the minimal distance between
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{B}
obtainable in a realization of
\mathbb{T}_{e}
,
that induces the edge lengths of the motion under consideration;
likewise, let
M_{e}
be the maximal such distance.
These two distances correspond to the two flat poses of
\mathbb{T}_{e}
.
Notice that it is possible that neither
m_{e}
nor
M_{e}
is in the image of
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}
.
Define now
m=\max\{m_{e}\}_{e\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}
and
M=\min\{M_{e}\}_{e\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}
.
Notice that the interval
(m,M)
is not empty since
\mathcal{M}
is a motion according to
Definition

2.7
.

If
m_{e}=m
for all
e\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}
,
then the whole bipyramid has a flat pose with
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}=m^{2}
.
Hence, if the whole bipyramid does not have a flat pose with
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}=m^{2}
,
then some
m_{\bar{e}}
is smaller than
m
.
This implies that
\mathbb{T}_{\bar{e}}
is not flat when
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}=m^{2}
, since
m_{\bar{e}}<m<M\leq M_{\bar{e}}
.
Let
e^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}
be such that
m_{e^{\prime}}=m
.

We claim that
m^{2}\in d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}(\mathcal{M}_{e^{\prime}})
.
To show this, we exhibit a configuration
x\in\mathcal{M}
such that
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}(x)=m^{2}
.
Consider the complexification
(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})_{\mathbb{C}}
of the map
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\colon\mathcal{M}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
The image
Z
of the latter map equals
\mathbb{C}
with at most finitely many points removed.
Hence, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
(m^{2},m^{2}+\varepsilon)\subset Z
.
For each
t\in(m^{2},m^{2}+\varepsilon)
, let
x_{t}\in(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(t)
.
We argue that actually
x_{t}
is a real point.
Indeed, all the values of
\{d_{e}\}_{e\in E_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}}
in
x_{t}
are real by construction.
Moreover, since
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}(x_{t})=t
and
m^{2}\leq t\leq M^{2}
,

Lemma

A.1
shows that all the values of
\{s_{e}\}_{e\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}
in
x_{t}
are real.
Then
Corollary

A.5
implies that all the values
\{d_{uv}\}_{u,v\in V_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}}
in
x_{t}
are real, making
x_{t}
a real point.
Since
\mathcal{M}
is compact in the Euclidean topology, then, up to taking a subsequence, the limit
x=\lim_{t\to m^{2}}x_{t}
exists in
\mathcal{M}
.
By continuity,
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}(x)=m^{2}
.

Let
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{M}
be such that
\pi_{e^{\prime}}(\mathcal{U})
is a neighborhood of
\pi_{e^{\prime}}(x)
.
Notice that the value of
s_{e^{\prime}}=0
in
x
since
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}(x)=m^{2}
.
Let
t\in d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}(\mathcal{U})
.
Then there exist
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
such that
s_{e^{\prime}}
attains opposite sign in
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
and
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}(y_{1})=d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}(y_{2})=t
.
Possibly shrinking
\mathcal{U}
, we can assume that
s_{\bar{e}}
attains the same sign at
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
.
Since the map
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\colon\mathcal{M}_{\bar{e}}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
2:1
, there exists a configuration
y_{3}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}(y_{3})=t
,
but
s_{\bar{e}}
attains opposite signs at
y_{2}
and
y_{3}
.
This implies that
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\colon\mathcal{M}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{R}
has a fiber of cardinality
3
.

Therefore, if
\mathscr{G}
has cardinality
2
, then there must be a flat pose of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}

where the distance between
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{B}
is
m
.
Arguing similarly, if
\mathscr{G}
has cardinality
2
, then there must be a flat pose of
{\mathbb{B}_{n}}

where the distance between
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{B}
is
M
.
∎

From now on we focus on pentagonal bipyramids, so we suppose that

,

\mathsf{T}

The

is homeomorphic to a

Lemma A.1
.

The following equations hold for
\mathrm{CM}^{\ast}_{n}
. Form the matrix

M_{i,i+1,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrrrr}0&1&1&1&1\\
1&0&d_{i\,i+1}&d_{i\,\mathsf{T}}&d_{i\,\mathsf{B}}\\
1&d_{i\,i+1}&0&d_{i+1\,\mathsf{T}}&d_{i+1\,\mathsf{B}}\\
1&d_{i\,\mathsf{T}}&d_{i+1\,\mathsf{T}}&0&d_{\mathsf{T}\,\mathsf{B}}\\
1&d_{i\,\mathsf{B}}&d_{i+1\,\mathsf{B}}&d_{\mathsf{T}\,\mathsf{B}}&0\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

Then

288\,s_{i,i+1}^{2}-\det(M_{i,i+1,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{B}})=0\,.

Proof.

This is a well known fact in distance geometry (see for example
[
LL17
, Section 4.1.2]
).

Lemma A.2
.

The following equations hold for
\mathrm{CM}^{\ast}_{n}
:

a_{k}d_{ij}+b_{ijk}-288\varsigma_{ik}\varsigma_{kj}=0\,,

where

a_{k}=2(d_{k\mathsf{T}}d_{k\mathsf{B}}+d_{k\mathsf{T}}d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}+d_{k\mathsf{B}}d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})-(d_{k\mathsf{T}}^{2}+d_{k\mathsf{B}}^{2}+d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}^{2})

\displaystyle b_{ijk}=

\displaystyle\bigl(d_{ik}(d_{j\mathsf{B}}-d_{j\mathsf{T}})+d_{kj}(d_{i\mathsf{B}}-d_{i\mathsf{T}})+d_{k\mathsf{B}}(d_{i\mathsf{T}}+d_{j\mathsf{T}})-d_{k\mathsf{T}}(d_{i\mathsf{B}}+d_{j\mathsf{B}})\bigr)(d_{k\mathsf{B}}-d_{k\mathsf{T}})

\displaystyle-d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\bigl(d_{i\mathsf{T}}(d_{k\mathsf{B}}+d_{kj})+d_{i\mathsf{B}}(d_{k\mathsf{T}}+d_{kj})+d_{j\mathsf{T}}(d_{ik}+d_{k\mathsf{B}})+d_{j\mathsf{B}}(d_{ik}+d_{k\mathsf{T}})\bigr)

\displaystyle-(d_{k\mathsf{B}}+d_{k\mathsf{T}}-d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\bigl(d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}(d_{ik}+d_{kj})+(d_{i\mathsf{T}}d_{j\mathsf{B}}+d_{j\mathsf{T}}d_{i\mathsf{B}})\bigr)

\displaystyle+2(d_{i\mathsf{T}}d_{k\mathsf{B}}d_{j\mathsf{T}}+d_{i\mathsf{B}}d_{k\mathsf{T}}d_{j\mathsf{B}}+d_{k\mathsf{B}}d_{k\mathsf{T}}d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}+d_{ik}d_{kj}d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}})\,.

Notice that both
a_{k}
and
b_{ijk}
only depend on the
d
-coordinates; in particular,
a_{k}
depends on the variables
d_{k\mathsf{T}}
,
d_{k\mathsf{B}}
, and
d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}
, while
b_{ijk}
depends on the variables

,

for

,
d_{kj}

Proof.

It is well-known (see for example
[

\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}0&1&1&1&1&1\\
1&0&d_{ik}&d_{ij}&d_{i\mathsf{T}}&d_{i\mathsf{B}}\\
1&d_{ik}&0&d_{kj}&d_{k\mathsf{T}}&d_{k\mathsf{B}}\\
1&d_{ij}&d_{kj}&0&d_{j\mathsf{T}}&d_{j\mathsf{B}}\\
1&d_{i\mathsf{T}}&d_{k\mathsf{T}}&d_{j\mathsf{T}}&0&d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\\

This determinant is a polynomial in

Corollary A.5
.

For every
i,j\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
, we have that both
d_{ij}
and
\varsigma_{i,j}

are rational functions in
\{s_{e}\}_{e\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}\cup\{d_{e}\}_{e\in E_{{\mathbb{B}_{n}}}}\cup\{d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\}
in the function field of
\mathrm{CM}^{\ast}_{n}
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
|j-i|
.
Since
d_{ji}=d_{ij}
and
\varsigma_{i,j}=-\varsigma_{j,i}
, we can suppose that
j>i
.
For the base case
j=i+1
, then
\varsigma_{i,i+1}=s_{i,i+1}
, so the statement is proven.
By choosing
k=j-1
,
Lemma

A.2
shows that
d_{ij}
is a rational function in

\{\varsigma_{i,j-1},\varsigma_{j-1,j}\}\cup\{d_{i,j-1},d_{j-1,j}\}\cup\{d_{u\mathsf{T}},d_{u\mathsf{B}}\}_{u\in\{i,j-1,j\}}\cup\{d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\}
.
Moreover, again by choosing
k=j-1
,
Lemma

A.4
shows that
\varsigma_{i,j}
is a rational function in
\{\varsigma_{i,j-1},\varsigma_{j-1,j}\}\cup\{d_{i,j-1},d_{j-1,j},d_{i,j}\}\cup\{d_{u\mathsf{T}},d_{u\mathsf{B}}\}_{u\in\{i,j-1,j\}}\cup\{d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\}
: indeed, the squared volumes appearing in
a_{i,j,k}
and
b_{i,j,k}
can be expressed as polynomials in
\{d_{i,j-1},d_{j-1,j},d_{i,j}\}\cup\{d_{u\mathsf{T}},d_{u\mathsf{B}}\}_{u\in\{i,j-1,j\}}\cup\{d_{\mathsf{T}\mathsf{B}}\}
.
Hence, by the inductive step, both
d_{ij}
and
\varsigma_{i,j}
are rational functions in the desired variables.
