Edaravone attenuates doxorubicin-induced oral mucosal injury via modulation of oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and the SIRT1/TLR4/NF-kB/ACE2 axis in rats
Authors: Mert Zeytinoglu, Aras Erdil, Sefa Çolak, Mustafa Sami Demirsoy, Ali Tuğrul Akin, Necla Değer, Derya Karabulut, Serdar Savaş Gül, Hatice Aygun, Oytun Erbaş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral mucositis (OM) is a common complication of chemotherapy, particularly with anthracyclines like doxorubicin (DOX), which induce oxidative stress and inflammation. This study investigated the protective effects of edaravone (EDO), a free radical scavenger, against DOX-induced oral mucosal injury.MethodsTwenty-eight male Wistar rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 7): Control, DOX (18 mg/kg, i.p., days 19–21), and two EDO + DOX groups (1 or 30 mg/kg EDO daily for 21 days). On day 22, tongue tissues and plasma were analyzed for oxidative stress markers (MDA, GSH, SOD, TAS, TOS) and cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-10). NF-κB, SIRT1, and TLR4 expression were assessed via immunohistochemistry and ELISA, while ACE2 expression was evaluated by immunohistochemistry.ResultsDOX significantly upregulated ACE2 (p < 0.001), TLR4 (p < 0.001), NF-κB (p < 0.001), and proinflammatory cytokines in plasma and tongue tissue (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β; all p < 0.01), while reducing IL-10 (p < 0.001), GSH (p = 0.002), SOD (p = 0.001), and SIRT1 (p < 0.001). EDA treatment reduced ACE2 and TLR4 expression, with 30 mg/kg normalizing ACE2 (p = 0.978 vs. control). EDO restored antioxidant markers and significantly lowered MDA, TOS, cytokines, and NF-κB (all p < 0.05). Histological injury and inflammation scores also improved significantly (p < 0.001).ConclusionEdaravone effectively attenuates DOX-induced mucosal injury by modulating oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and TLR4/ACE2 pathways, suggesting therapeutic potential in mucosal protection.Graphical AbstractDoxorubicin induces mitochondrial ROS production, leading to oxidative stress (↑MDA, ↑TOS, ↓GSH, ↓SOD, ↓TAS). This redox imbalance downregulates SIRT1 and activates TLR4, both of which contribute to NF-κB signaling. As a result, proinflammatory cytokines (↑TNF-α, ↑IL-6, ↑IL-1β) are upregulated, while anti-inflammatory IL-10 is suppressed. This inflammatory cascade, in turn, promotes ACE2 overexpression in the tongue epithelium. Edaravone treatment mitigates these effects through ROS scavenging, restoration of SIRT1, and suppression of TLR4/NF-κB signaling, ultimately protecting the oral mucosa. Keywords: Edaravone, Oral mucositis, Doxorubicin, Oxidative stress, Inflammatory signaling, SIRT1, TLR4, ACE2, NF-κB, Antioxidant therapy

Introduction

Oral mucositis is a common and debilitating complication in patients receiving chemotherapy, especially those with head and neck cancers. Its incidence ranges from 30% to 40% with chemotherapy alone and rises to nearly 90% with concomitant radiotherapy [1–3]. Clinically, oral mucositis is characterized by erythema, ulceration, and severe oral pain, leading to weight loss, infection risk, and reduced quality of life that compromises treatment adherence [4, 5]. Given this high clinical burden, elucidating the underlying molecular pathways has become essential for developing effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

The oral mucosa, particularly the tongue epithelium, expresses Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme 2 (ACE2) abundantly, as shown by single-cell transcriptomic and immunohistochemical analyses [6–10]. Although ACE2 can act as a cellular receptor for certain coronaviruses, the present study does not assess viral entry or infection; accordingly, we interpret ACE2 modulation within the context of chemotherapy-related mucosal injury rather than as an indicator of viral susceptibility.

Doxorubicin (DOX), an anthracycline-class chemotherapeutic agent widely used to treat hematologic malignancies and diverse solid tumors, has been associated with oral mucositis in both adult and pediatric patients [11, 12]. DOX-induced tissue injury is driven in part by mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) and activation of TLR4/NF-κB signaling, which together promote upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β [13–16].

Edaravone is a clinically approved free-radical scavenger with potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties that mitigate oxidative stress–induced cellular damage and confer neuroprotection in conditions such as acute ischemic stroke and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [17]. Mechanistically, edaravone activates the Nrf2/HO-1 axis while inhibiting NF-κB signaling, thereby limiting ROS accumulation and cytokine overproduction [18]. Preclinical studies demonstrate protective effects across models of oxidative and inflammatory injury, including mucosal injury related to anticancer therapies [18, 19]. However, to our knowledge, no study has examined the impact of edaravone on DOX-induced oral mucositis—a mechanistic gap that may refine our understanding of mucosal integrity under chemotherapeutic conditions.

This study aimed to evaluate alterations in oxidative-stress and inflammatory indices, together with ACE2 and SIRT1 expression, in doxorubicin (DOX)–induced oral mucositis, and to determine the ameliorative effects of edaravone. We hypothesized that edaravone would (i) correct oxidative stress by lowering MDA and TOS while increasing GSH, SOD, and TAS; (ii) limit inflammation by upregulating SIRT1 and suppressing TLR4/NF-κB signaling; and (iii) consequently restore ACE2 expression toward physiological levels.

Material and method

Animals

Twenty-eight male Wistar albino rats (230–250 g) were used. Animals were maintained under standard conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle; lights on at 7:00 a.m.) in polypropylene cages with wire tops. Cages were cleaned every three days, and rats had free access to chow and tap water for 10 days prior to and during the experiment. The protocol was approved by the local ethics committee of Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University (2020 HADYEK-15; Approval number: 51879863-105).The animals used in this study were obtained from the Experimental Animal Research Center of Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and adhered to the principles outlined in the Basel Declaration. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to reduce the number of animals used.

Dose selection and justification

We selected 1 mg/kg as a low, literature-supported dose that demonstrated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects without exceeding clinical equivalence [20, 21]. Conversely, 30 mg/kg was chosen as a high experimental dose, previously effective in doxorubicin toxicity and mucositis models [19–23].

Experimental protocol

Following a one-week acclimatization period, all treatments were administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) once daily between 09:00 and 10:00 to maintain circadian consistency. The control group received saline (2 ml/kg, i.p.) for 21 consecutive days. Doxorubicin (DOX) was administered intraperitoneally at 18 mg/kg on days 19, 20, and 21. Edaravone (EDO) was administered for 21 consecutive days at specified doses (1 or 30 mg/kg, i.p.), (Fig. 1).

Experimental design and workflow

The animals were randomly allocated into four experimental groups (n = 7 per group) as follows:

Group I (Control): Saline (2 ml/kg, i.p.) for 21 consecutive days.

Group I (Control): Saline (2 ml/kg, i.p.) for 21 consecutive days.

Group II (DOX): DOX (18 mg/kg, i.p.) on days 19–21.

Group II (DOX): DOX (18 mg/kg, i.p.) on days 19–21.

Group III (EDO 1 mg/kg + DOX): EDO (1 mg/kg, i.p.) for 21 days + DOX as above.

Group III (EDO 1 mg/kg + DOX): EDO (1 mg/kg, i.p.) for 21 days + DOX as above.

Group IV (EDO 30 mg/kg + DOX): EDO (30 mg/kg, i.p.) for 21 days + DOX as above.

Group IV (EDO 30 mg/kg + DOX): EDO (30 mg/kg, i.p.) for 21 days + DOX as above.

Anesthesia, euthanasia, and tissue collection

Rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a mixture containing 90 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine to ensure deep sedation and analgesia. Following confirmation of adequate anesthesia, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture for subsequent biochemical analysis. After blood collection, the animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation in accordance with ethical guidelines. The tongue tissue was then carefully excised and divided for histopathological and biochemical evaluations (Fig. 1).

Histological procedure

Tissues were fixed by taking 10% formaldehyde. First, it was kept in the increasing graded alcohol series, then was treated with xylene. 5 μm thick sections were taken from tissues fixed with paraffin. Then, immunohistochemistry painting was applied to the sections. All animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and allocation concealment was maintained throughout the study. All histological and biochemical assessments were performed in a blinded fashion by independent investigators.

Immunohistochemistry protocol

Immunohistochemical staining for ACE2 was performed using a primary antibody (ACE2, MBS2027596, MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA). Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval in a microwave oven using citrate buffer. All washing steps were carried out with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody, followed by secondary antibody application and detection using diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen (TA-060-HDX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Counterstaining was performed with Gill’s hematoxylin, and sections were dehydrated through graded alcohols and cleared in xylene before mounting.

Semi-quantitative analysis of ACE2 immunoreactivity

ACE2 immunoreactivity was evaluated in all tissue sections from the experimental groups using a semi-quantitative immunohistochemical approach. For each slide representing each animal, five non-overlapping microscopic fields (400×) were randomly selected across all anatomical regions of the tongue (dorsal, ventral, lateral, and posterior surfaces), and approximately 100 cells in total were counted. Among these, the number of positively stained (brown-stained) cells was determined. In parallel, immunoreactivity intensity within the same fields was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.45s, National Institute of Health, USA; RRID: SCR_003070) using the “Color Deconvolution” function to isolate DAB signals and the “Subtract Background” tool for background correction. Thus, both the proportion of positively stained cells and the staining intensity across the analyzed fields were considered in generating a semi-quantitative immunoreactivity score.

In total, ~ 100 optical density (OD) measurements were obtained per group (all animals × fields). For each animal, the mean OD value across the five fields was calculated, and the animal served as the experimental unit for statistical analysis. Group-level values are reported as mean ± SEM to reflect the precision of the mean across animals. This approach is conceptually comparable to the classical H-score method (Remmele & Stegner, 1987), which integrates staining intensity and proportion of positive cells, but here adapted for ImageJ-based densitometry.

All evaluations were independently performed by two blinded observers, and inter-observer reliability was confirmed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC > 0.80, p < 0.001). Intergroup comparisons were conducted using appropriate statistical tests depending on data distribution.

Histopathological evaluation and scoring

All histopathological evaluations (inflammatory cell infiltration, epithelial erosion) were performed on ACE2-immunostained tongue sections to ensure topographic correspondence between immunoreactivity and structural changes. To minimize selection bias, five non-overlapping high-power fields (HPFs; 400×) per animal were sampled using systematic random sampling across all anatomical regions of the tongue (dorsal, ventral, lateral, and posterior surfaces) to ensure comprehensive evaluation of epithelial and subepithelial compartments. One representative section per animal at a standardized level was analyzed.

Two observers, blinded to group allocation, independently scored each HPF using a 4-point ordinal scale (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Inter-rater agreement was quantified using weighted Cohen’s κ (quadratic weights) across n = 140 HPFs (two raters): inflammation, κ = 0.81 (95% CI 0.72–0.89, p < 0.001); erosion, κ = 0.79 (95% CI 0.69–0.88, p < 0.001). Per animal, the five HPF scores were aggregated to a single animal-level value (median of 5 HPFs), and the animal served as the experimental unit for statistics (see Fig. 3).

ACE2 immunohistochemistry in lingual epithelium (representative images). A Control: intact epithelium, minimal ACE2. B DOX: epithelial disorganization with increased suprabasal ACE2. C DOX + EDO 1 mg/kg: partial preservation, moderate ACE2. D DOX + EDO 30 mg/kg: near-normal morphology, weak ACE2. Scale bar (100 μm); objective (×40); identical exposure/processing across groups

Rationale: Conventional H&E was not run in parallel to avoid potential loss of anatomical correspondence across adjacent sections; scoring on ACE2-stained sections permits direct topographic comparison. Sampling was standardized and observer blinding maintained to mitigate bias.

Inflammation scoring criteria

Inflammatory activity was assessed semi-quantitatively based on the density, distribution, and localization of mononuclear cell infiltrates.

Score Description.

0 No evident inflammatory cell infiltration in the subepithelial area.

1 Mild – Focal mononuclear cell infiltration with minimal edema.

2 Moderate – Diffuse mononuclear infiltration accompanied by mild tissue disorganization.

3 Severe – Widespread dense infiltration with prominent edema and disruption of tissue architecture.

Epithelial damage scoring criteria

Epithelial integrity was evaluated semi-quantitatively based on epithelial thickness, structural organization, and papillary morphology:

Score Description.

0 Well-preserved, stratified epithelial layers with intact papillae.

1 Slight surface irregularities or hyperkeratosis without loss of architectural integrity.

2 Noticeable epithelial thinning and structural disorganization.

3 Partial or complete epithelial erosion with papillary flattening or ulceration.

These complementary scoring systems were applied consistently across all samples, providing a standardized assessment of inflammatory and degenerative changes in relation to ACE2 expression.

Biochemical analyses in plasma

Plasma concentrations of proinflammatory cytokines and regulatory molecules were determined using commercially available, rat-specific ELISA or colorimetric assay kits, following manufacturers’ protocols. Whole blood was collected via cardiac puncture into EDTA-coated tubes and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Plasma aliquots were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Prior to measurement, all reagents and samples were equilibrated to room temperature.

Cytokine and inflammatory marker quantification

Plasma levels of TNF-α (Cat. No: E0764Ra), IL-6 (Cat. No: E0135Ra), IL-1β (Cat. No: E0119Ra), IL-10 (Cat. No: ERA23RB) were determined using rat-specific sandwich ELISA kits (Bioassay Technology Laboratory, Shanghai, China). Samples (50–100 µL) and standards were added in duplicate to antibody-coated 96-well plates, incubated with biotinylated detection antibodies and streptavidin–HRP, followed by TMB substrate for signal development. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Standard curves were constructed using 5-parameter logistic regression. Results were expressed as pg/mL (cytokines).

Transcription factor and enzyme measurements

Plasma levels of NF-κB were quantified using a rat-specific ELISA kit (MyBioSource, Cat. No: MBS453975, San Diego, CA, USA). Assay steps were identical to the cytokine ELISA protocol. Final concentrations were expressed in pg/mL plasma.

Sirtuin proteins were measured with the following kits: SIRT1 (Elabscience rat-specific kit (Cat. No: E-EL-R1102, Wuhan, China)) and SIRT6 (MyBioSource, Cat. No: MBS063651). ELISA assays were performed using 100 µL undiluted plasma samples. All kits followed sandwich ELISA format and results were expressed in ng/mL plasma.

Oxidative stress and antioxidant parameters

Biochemical measurements – assay for GSH

Plasma glutathione (GSH) levels were quantified using a kinetic colorimetric assay kit (Sigma, Cat. No: CS0260) optimized for rat plasma. Samples were deproteinized with 5% sulfosalicylic acid and centrifuged to remove precipitated proteins. The assay is based on the continuous reduction of DTNB (5,5′-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid) to TNB by GSH, while oxidized glutathione (GSSG) is recycled by glutathione reductase and NADPH. Absorbance of the yellow TNB product was measured at 412 nm. Results were calculated from a standard curve and expressed as mg/dL plasma.

Biochemical measurements – assay for TAS and TOS

Total antioxidant status (TAS) and total oxidant status (TOS) were evaluated using Rel Assay Diagnostics kits (Gaziantep, Turkey). TAS was based on the ABTS•⁺ inhibition method and expressed as mmol Trolox equivalent/L, while TOS relied on ferric ion–xylenol orange complex formation and was expressed as µmol H₂O₂ equivalent/L. Absorbance was measured at 530 nm (TAS) and 560 nm (TOS), respectively.

ELISA-based quantification in tongue tissue

Biochemical analyses of TLR4, MDA, SOD, TNF-α, and IL-6 in rat tongue tissue were performed using rat-specific commercial ELISA kits in sandwich format. Frozen tissue samples (~ 100 mg) were homogenized in 1 mL ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Homogenates were centrifuged at 3000–12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Each assay was performed in 96-well plates precoated with target-specific antibodies. Standards and tissue supernatants were added in duplicate or triplicate, followed by incubation with biotinylated detection antibodies and streptavidin–HRP. Color development was achieved with TMB substrate, and absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Concentrations were calculated using standard curves and normalized to total protein content (determined via the Bradford method). Results were expressed in analyte-specific units per mg of total protein.

TLR4 measurement

TLR4 protein levels in tongue tissue were quantified using a rat TLR4 ELISA kit (Cusabio, Cat. No: CSB-E15822r). The assay was conducted strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol, and results were expressed as ng/mg total protein.

MDA measurement

Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured using a rat-specific MDA ELISA kit (MyBioSource, Cat. No: MBS268427), according to the supplier’s instructions. Values were calculated from the standard curve and reported as nmol/mg total protein.

SOD activity

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzyme activity was determined using a rat-specific ELISA kit (MyBioSource, Cat. No: MBS036924), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Results were expressed as U/mg total protein.

TNF-α measurement

TNF-α concentrations were measured in tissue homogenates using a rat TNF-α ELISA kit (TNF-α (Cat. No: E0764Ra), following the detailed steps outlined in the kit manual. Data were reported as ng/L/g.

IL-6 measurement

IL-6 levels were quantified using a rat ELISA kit (Bioassay Technology Laboratory, Cat. No: E0135Ra), and the procedure was carried out exactly as described in the manufacturer’s instructions. Results were expressed as ng/L/g.

Biochemical analysis of systemic toxicity markers

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and serum creatinine levels were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter LX-2000, Brea, CA, USA) with commercial enzymatic colorimetric kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The reference ranges for control rats were as follows: AST 54–298 U/L, ALT 17–77 U/L, BUN 18–29 mg/dL, and creatinine 0.2–0.8 mg/dL.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 19.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed by Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Homogeneity of variances was tested using Levene’s test. For normally distributed variables with homogeneous variances, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. When variance heterogeneity was detected, Welch ANOVA with Tamhane’s T2 post hoc test was used. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed with the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Mann–Whitney U tests for pairwise comparisons. Results are presented as mean ± SEM for parametric data and median [25th–75th percentile] for non-parametric data. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Figures and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

G*power analysis

An a priori sample size calculation was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (version 3.1.9.7, Düsseldorf, Germany) for a one-way ANOVA design. Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s f ≈ 0.5) and drawing on previous rat studies employing 6–7 animals per group (20 Hassan et al., 2015; 22 Demir et al., 2020; 24 Bayramoğlu et al., 2022), the analysis indicated a statistical power of ≥ 0.85 at α = 0.05. Accordingly, a group size of n = 7 was selected as both ethically justified and literature-consistent.

Results

Sample size and power analysis

To further confirm adequacy, post hoc power analyses were performed for all measured parameters using G*Power 3.1. These analyses yielded very large to extremely large effect sizes (Cohen’s f = 0.51–3.44) with achieved power (1–β) ranging from 95% to 99% at α = 0.05. Thus, the chosen group size (n = 7 per group; total N = 28) was more than sufficient to detect robust and biologically meaningful differences among the experimental groups.

Histopathological evaluation of tongue epithelium and ACE2 expression

Angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2)

ACE2 values exhibited normal distribution (Kolmogorov–Smirnov, p = 0.399), while Levene’s test indicated significant variance heterogeneity (p < 0.001); therefore, Welch ANOVA and Tamhane’s T2 post hoc analysis were applied. A significant overall group difference was observed (F = 47.537, p < 0.001). DOX administration significantly increased ACE2 levels compared to the control group (181,036 ± 494.3 vs. 173,207 ± 855.8, p < 0.001), suggesting ACE2 upregulation under chemotherapeutic stress. Treatment with edaravone at 1 mg/kg slightly reduced ACE2 expression (178,897 ± 598.2) compared with the DOX group (181,036 ± 494.0), and the difference was significant (p = 0.037). Interestingly, edaravone at 30 mg/kg reduced ACE2 levels (172,472 ± 551.5), which were significantly lower than the DOX and 1 mg/kg groups (both p < 0.001), and statistically indistinguishable from the control (p = 0.978). These findings suggest that while low-dose edaravone may preserve or exacerbate ACE2 upregulation, the higher dose effectively restores ACE2 expression toward physiological levels in DOX-treated tissues (Fig. 2).

ACE2 immunoreactivity, inflammation, and epithelial erosion in tongue tissue. A ACE2 optical density; (B) inflammation score and (C) epithelial erosion score. ACE2—Welch ANOVA with Tamhane’s T2; scores—Kruskal–Wallis with Mann–Whitney U post hoc. Significance: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

Inflammation scoring

Histological inflammation scores were non-normally distributed; therefore, Kruskal–Wallis testing was applied, followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with Holm adjustment. Kruskal–Wallis revealed a significant group effect (p < 0.001). DOX-treated rats showed higher inflammation (3 [2–3]) than controls (0 [0–0]); p < 0.001). Edaravone reduced inflammation at 1 mg/kg (2 [2–3]; p = 0.007 vs. DOX) and more prominently at 30 mg/kg (1 [0–1]; p < 0.001 vs. DOX). Moreover, 30 mg/kg yielded lower scores than 1 mg/kg (p < 0.001), consistent with a dose-responsive anti-inflammatory effect (Fig. 2).

Epithelial erosion scoring

Epithelial erosion scores were non-normally distributed; therefore, Kruskal–Wallis testing was applied, followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with Holm-adjusted p values. Kruskal–Wallis showed significant differences among groups (p < 0.001). DOX-treated rats exhibited prominent epithelial injury (3 [2–3]), significantly higher than controls (0 [0 − 0.]), p < 0.001). Edaravone attenuated erosion in both treatment arms—2 [1–3] at 1 mg/kg and 1 [0–1] at 30 mg/kg—with pairwise Mann–Whitney U comparisons confirming significant reductions versus DOX (both p < 0.001, Holm-adjusted). Moreover, the 30 mg/kg dose yielded lower scores than 1 mg/kg (p < 0.001, Holm-adjusted), indicating a dose-responsive mucosal protective effect (Fig. 2).

ACE2 expression and histopathological changes in tongue mucosa in experimental groups in Fig. 3

Figure 3 illustrates representative ACE2-immunostained sections of the dorsal tongue mucosa across experimental groups, highlighting variations in epithelial integrity, inflammatory infiltration, and ACE2 expression. The control group (A) demonstrated intact epithelial stratification with negligible ACE2 staining and no evidence of inflammation. In contrast, the DOX group (B) exhibited severe epithelial thinning, architectural disorganization, and prominent ACE2 overexpression localized to suprabasal epithelial layers and papillary regions (arrows), along with dense subepithelial mononuclear infiltration. The DOX + EDO (1 mg/kg) group (C) showed partial epithelial preservation with moderate ACE2 positivity and focal inflammatory cell presence. The DOX + EDO (30 mg/kg) group (D) exhibited near-normal epithelial morphology with minimal ACE2 staining and reduced inflammatory infiltration. These findings indicate that edaravone, particularly at higher doses, ameliorates DOX-induced mucosal injury and downregulates ACE2 expression (Fig. 3).

Oxidative stress and antioxidant defense parameters in tongue tissue and plasma

Glutathione (GSH)

Shapiro–Wilk normality test indicated that GSH values did not meet the assumption of normality in the EDO 1 mg/kg group (p = 0.033). Therefore, non-parametric analyses were performed. Kruskal–Wallis test revealed a statistically significant difference among the groups (χ² = 18.842, df = 3, p < 0.001).

Mann–Whitney U test showed that GSH levels were significantly decreased in the DOX group compared to the control group (p = 0.002). Treatment with EDO at 1 mg/kg led to a significant increase in GSH compared to DOX (p = 0.004), while the 30 mg/kg dose produced a more substantial elevation (p = 0.002 vs. DOX). Furthermore, GSH levels in the 30 mg/kg group were significantly higher than in the 1 mg/kg group (p = 0.039), indicating a dose-related difference in antioxidant capacity (Fig. 4).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD)

SOD levels in tongue tissue were normally distributed, but Levene’s test revealed variance heterogeneity across groups (p = 0.003). Consequently, Tamhane’s T2 post hoc test was applied. One-way ANOVA demonstrated a highly significant group effect on SOD activity (F = 32.899, p < 0.001).

SOD activity was significantly reduced in the DOX group (16.41 ± 1.13 U/mg) compared to the control group (60.23 ± 5.16 U/mg, p = 0.001), indicating pronounced oxidative damage. Treatment with edaravone at 1 mg/kg significantly improved SOD levels (30.62 ± 2.89 U/mg) compared to the DOX group (p = 0.012). Furthermore, the 30 mg/kg edaravone group showed a markedly higher SOD activity (48.45 ± 3.02 U/mg) relative to the DOX group (p < 0.001). The difference between the 1 mg/kg and 30 mg/kg edaravone groups was also statistically significant (p = 0.007), supporting a dose-dependent enhancement of endogenous antioxidant defense by edaravone (Fig. 4).

Malondialdehyde (MDA)

MDA values were normally distributed, and Levene’s test confirmed homogeneity of variances (p = 0.603). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant group effect on MDA levels (F = 29.004, p < 0.001).

Post hoc analysis showed that MDA levels were significantly elevated in the DOX group (4.60 ± 0.34 nmol/mg) compared to the control group (1.50 ± 0.23 nmol/mg, p < 0.001). Edaravone administration at 1 mg/kg significantly reduced MDA levels (3.40 ± 0.22 nmol/mg) compared to the DOX group (p = 0.011). A further decrease was observed in the 30 mg/kg group (2.20 ± 0.16 nmol/mg), which was significantly lower than both the DOX (p < 0.001) and 1 mg/kg groups (p = 0.016) (Fig. 4).

Total antioxidant status (TAS)

Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that TAS values deviated from normality in the control group (p = 0.047); therefore, non-parametric analyses were used. Kruskal–Wallis test revealed a statistically significant difference in TAS levels among the groups (χ² = 19.245, df = 3, p < 0.001). Mann–Whitney U test showed that TAS levels were significantly lower in the DOX group (0.71 ± 0.09 mmol/L) compared to the control group (1.59 ± 0.14 mmol/L, p = 0.002). TAS levels increased to 0.99 ± 0.07 mmol/L in the DOX + EDO 1 mg/kg group, and this difference was statistically significant compared to DOX (p = 0.035). A further elevation was observed in the DOX + EDO 30 mg/kg group (1.45 ± 0.10 mmol/L), which was significantly higher than both the DOX group (p = 0.002) and the 1 mg/kg group (p = 0.006), indicating a dose-dependent improvement in antioxidant status with higher edaravone administration (Fig. 4).

Total oxidant status (TOS)

Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that TOS values were not normally distributed in the EDO 30 mg/kg group (p = 0.000); therefore, non-parametric tests were employed. Kruskal–Wallis analysis showed a significant difference in TOS levels among the groups (χ² = 15.444, df = 3, p = 0.001).

Mann–Whitney U test demonstrated that TOS levels were significantly increased in the DOX group (16.29 ± 0.80 µmol/L) compared to the control group (9.7 ± 0.4 µmol/L, p = 0.002). Treatment with edaravone at 1 mg/kg significantly reduced TOS levels (12.46 ± 1.01 µmol/L) relative to DOX (p = 0.030). A greater reduction was observed with 30 mg/kg edaravone (10.92 ± 0.67 µmol/L), which was significantly lower than the DOX group (p = 0.004). However, the difference between the 1 mg/kg and 30 mg/kg EDO groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.338) (Fig. 4).

Plasma cytokine and NF-κB levels

Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)

Shapiro–Wilk test showed that TNF-α levels were normally distributed in the control, DOX, and EDO 1 mg/kg groups (p > 0.05), whereas the EDO 30 mg/kg group exhibited a significant deviation from normality (p = 0.028). Given the violation of normality in one group, a non-parametric approach was adopted. The Kruskal–Wallis test revealed a significant difference among the groups (χ² = 18.295, df = 3, p < 0.001).

Pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests demonstrated that TNF-α levels were significantly elevated in the DOX group (246 ± 20 pg/mL) compared to the control group (108 ± 12 pg/mL; p = 0.001), indicating a robust proinflammatory response to doxorubicin. Treatment with Edaravone at 1 mg/kg significantly decreased TNF-α levels (184 ± 14 pg/mL; p = 0.038 vs. DOX), reflecting partial anti-inflammatory activity. A more pronounced reduction was observed in the EDO 30 mg/kg group (147 ± 11 pg/mL; p = 0.002 vs. DOX), although the difference between the two edaravone doses did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.097) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Oral mucositis is a frequent complication of chemotherapy that primarily affects rapidly proliferating epithelia. In the present study, doxorubicin (DOX) administration resulted in a marked increase in ACE2 and TLR4 expression in tongue tissue, accompanied by enhanced oxidative stress, as indicated by elevated MDA and TOS together with reduced GSH, SOD, and TAS. DOX exposure also shifted the cytokine profile toward inflammation, with increased NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β and decreased IL-10, while concomitantly suppressing SIRT1. edaravone treatment attenuated these alterations by lowering ACE2 and TLR4 expression, increasing SIRT1 levels, reducing NF-κB and proinflammatory cytokines, elevating IL-10, and improving redox balance. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to concurrently assess TLR4 and ACE2 in chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis and provides early evidence that, in a DOX-induced model, edaravone may modulate epithelial ACE2 expression.

One of the notable findings of this study is the upregulation of ACE2 expression in the tongue epithelium following DOX treatment. ACE2, physiologically expressed at high levels in oral tissues, especially in the tongue, serves not only as a homeostatic regulator within the renin-angiotensin system but also as the primary entry receptor for SARS-CoV-2 [7, 8]. Clinical studies have linked elevated oral ACE2 expression with higher viral load and more severe COVID-19 outcomes [25, 26]. While ACE2 can serve as a cellular receptor for certain coronaviruses, we did not assess viral entry or infection; accordingly, we interpret ACE2 changes as mucosal injury/repair biology rather than as indicators of viral susceptibility.

Although the pathogenesis of oral mucositis is not fully defined, oxidative stress is recognized as a central driver. DOX-induced ROS promotes lipid peroxidation and cytotoxicity [27–30], leading to apoptosis of basal epithelial and mucosal stem cells, which are considered critical early events in oral mucositis [31, 32]. Previous oral mucositis models induced by chemotherapeutic agents such as 5-FU and cisplatin, as well as radiotherapy-associated mucositis studies, have consistently shown decreased antioxidant defenses (SOD, CAT, GPx) together with elevated MDA, underscoring the central role of oxidative stress in mucosal injury [33–35]. In line with these, our results showed that DOX increased MDA and TOS while reducing GSH, SOD, and TAS in plasma and tongue, confirming robust oxidative stress. Importantly, edaravone attenuated these alterations, indicating strong antioxidant potential.

Numerous studies have reported that DOX-induced oxidative stress activates TLR4/NF-κB signaling, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response [36–40]. This cascade results in increased TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β and suppression of IL-10, ultimately leading to epithelial apoptosis and ulceration [37–42]. Consistent with oral mucositis models induced by 5-FU, cisplatin, and radiation, which show a similar imbalance [43–45], our results confirmed that DOX markedly increased TLR4 and NF-κB expression with a strong proinflammatory shift. Recent evidence suggests that TLR4 activation may indirectly enhance ACE2 expression via NF-κB–dependent mechanisms [15, 16], and our data support a potential role of TLR4 in DOX-induced ACE2 upregulation. Importantly, edaravone attenuated these alterations, supporting its anti-inflammatory role.

SIRT1, a NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase, has been identified as a key regulator of NF-κB and oxidative stress via deacetylation of p65 and activation of Nrf2/FOXO1 [46, 47]. Experimental studies demonstrated that DOX reduces SIRT1 expression [48, 49], whereas pharmacological activation of SIRT1 protects against DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [50]. Consistent with these findings, we observed that DOX suppressed SIRT1 in oral mucosa, while edaravone increased its levels, suggesting that its protective effects may be partly mediated through reinforcement of the SIRT1–NF-κB regulatory axis.

Additional preclinical evidence further supports edaravone’s potential in oral mucositis. Nakajima et al. [19] showed that prophylactic edaravone alleviated radiation-induced oral mucositis by reducing oxidative stress and apoptosis, though its efficacy in chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis remains unknown. Mechanistically, previous studies have demonstrated that edaravone enhances Nrf2 activity, leading to the upregulation of cytoprotective genes such as HO-1, SOD, CAT, GPx, and glutathione-synthesizing enzymes, thereby restoring redox homeostasis [51]. In parallel, it has been consistently shown to inhibit NF-κB signaling, limiting ROS accumulation and the release of proinflammatory cytokines [52, 53].

Beyond these classical antioxidant pathways, edaravone has also been shown to upregulate SIRT1 expression in various disease models, thereby reducing oxidative stress and inhibiting NF-κB–mediated cytokine production [54–57]. Importantly, several studies have demonstrated that edaravone attenuates chemotherapy-induced inflammation across diverse tissues through SIRT1 or TLR4/NF-κB modulation [17–58]. Taken together, our findings are compatible with a model in which edaravone may dampen TLR4/NF-κB–linked inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress, with SIRT1 likely acting upstream; in parallel, ACE2 modulation most plausibly reflects the mucosal injury/repair response.

Edaravone has been safely applied in oncology for radiation-related complications and suggested as a potential agent to reduce oral mucositis in head and neck cancer patients [59]. Collectively, current evidence positions edaravone as a promising candidate for oral mucositis prevention, warranting further preclinical validation in chemotherapy-based models and clinical trials to optimize dosing and efficacy.

DOX markedly increased plasma BUN, creatinine, ALT, and AST, consistent with renal and hepatic dysfunction, and edaravone significantly ameliorated these changes. These data provide supportive signals for both mucosal protection and systemic safety, warranting longer-term and organ-focused assessments.

Conclusion

DOX-induced oral mucosal injury was associated with oxidative stress, TLR4/NF-κB-driven inflammation, SIRT1 suppression, and increased ACE2. edaravone improved these disturbances across biochemical and histological endpoints and mitigated systemic toxicity markers, supporting a protective profile in this preclinical model. These findings justify further studies with pathway-specific interventions and chemotherapy-based clinical trials to refine mechanism, dosing, and translational potential.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Conducted in a Wistar rat model, its findings may not fully generalize to humans. Viral susceptibility was not directly assessed; inferences relied on ACE2 modulation. Although edaravone’s effects were shown biochemically and histologically, we lacked molecular confirmation of pathway engagement (e.g., HIF-1α stabilization, Nrf2 nuclear translocation by Western blot/immunofluorescence). Future work using transcriptomic/proteomic profiling and in vitro co-culture systems is warranted to define mechanisms in oral epithelium.

A limitation of the present study is the absence of “EDA-only” groups, which would have provided additional mechanistic insights into the baseline effects of edaravone. However, our design prioritized minimizing unnecessary animal use in line with ARRIVE guidelines. Future studies will address this gap by including edaravone-only groups.

This study was conducted only in male rats, a common practice in preclinical oral mucositis models to reduce variability, but still an important limitation. Excluding females may restrict generalizability, as sex-related hormonal and immunological differences influence inflammatory and chemotherapeutic responses [60–62]. Future studies should therefore include both sexes to better reflect translational relevance and capture potential differences in oxidative stress, inflammation, and tissue repair.
