Navigation-Assisted Minimally Invasive Direct Pars Repair for Lumbar Spondylolysis
Authors: Jack Mangan, Richard Schwend, John Anderson, Michael Benvenuti, Aaron Shaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpondylolysis is a common cause of mechanical low back pain in adolescent athletes. While nonoperative management is frequently successful, a subset of patients fail conservative treatment and require surgical intervention. Direct repair of the pars defect offers a motion-preserving alternative to fusion in appropriately selected patients, but traditional open and fluoroscopy-guided minimally invasive techniques are limited by soft-tissue morbidity and variability in screw placement accuracy, and detailed technical descriptions of navigation-assisted approaches remain limited in the literature. We present a detailed, illustrated technique for navigation-assisted minimally invasive direct pars repair, in which a single midline incision accommodates bilateral fixation with minimal soft-tissue disruption. Intraoperative navigation improves trajectory control and reduces reliance on fluoroscopy. This technique offers a reproducible, motion-preserving option for direct pars repair that improves accuracy and reduces surgical morbidity relative to existing approaches, though outcomes remain dependent on careful patient selection and navigation reliability, radiation exposure, and technical familiarity with guidewire and screw placement.Key Concepts(1)Navigation-assisted minimally invasive spine surgery technique allows pars screw placement through indirect visualization.(2)Single midline incision allows bilateral fixation with minimal soft-tissue disruption.(3)Motion-preserving alternative to fusion in appropriately selected patients.(4)Pitfalls include navigation error, inadequate compression across the defect, and poor patient selection (disc degeneration, large defects, or spondylolisthesis).Keywords: Lumbar spondylolysis, Pars interarticularis, Direct pars repair, Minimally invasive spine surgery, Surgical navigation

Introduction

Spondylolysis, a bony defect in the pars interarticularis, is a well-recognized cause of mechanical low back pain. Although a lower prevalence of spondylolysis is seen in children than in adults, it is a leading cause of low back pain in the adolescent athlete population [1]. While nonoperative management is often successful, surgical fixation may be indicated in symptomatic patients who fail conservative treatment [2]. Direct repair of the pars defect has been proposed as a motion-preserving solution for young, active patients. Fixation techniques include the use of open direct repair with screw fixation across the pars defect, cerclage wires passed around the transverse and spinous processes, and various pedicle screw constructs utilizing laminar hooks or U-shaped rods [3]. There has been a growing interest in minimally invasive spine surgery (MIS) due to advantages such as minimized blood loss, lower complication rates, decreased pain, shorter hospital stays, and decreased risk of surgical site infection [4].

MIS techniques for direct pars repair have demonstrated favorable outcomes but rely on 2-dimensional imaging to infer a 3-dimensional (3D) trajectory across a narrow isthmus. As the pars interarticularis is a small target bordered by the spinal canal medially and the pedicle laterally, achieving an accurate trajectory under fluoroscopy alone often requires multiple sequential images during instrumentation, increasing radiation exposure for the patient and the surgical team. Furthermore, fluoroscopic visualization of the pars becomes progressively more difficult at caudal levels with greater lordotic angulation (eg, the L5), where oblique orientation of the isthmus relative to standard fluoroscopic planes can obscure the trajectory and compromise accurate and reproducible screw placement. These limitations may explain why direct repair, despite its motion-preserving advantages compared with fusion, has not been more widely adopted at the L5 than at more cephalad levels.

Navigation-assisted techniques address this problem by providing real-time 3D trajectory guidance referenced to the patient’s own anatomy rather than sequential 2-dimensional projections. In addition, the navigated trajectory can be planned and confirmed prior to incision, facilitating a single midline approach for bilateral fixation, rather than utilizing two separate paramedian incisions guided by fluoroscopic landmarks, thus further reducing soft-tissue dissection relative to fluoroscopic-guided MIS techniques.

Our technique uses a minimally invasive, navigation-assisted approach for fixation of pars interarticularis defects designed to address the specific limitations of conventional fluoroscopic MIS techniques by improving trajectory accuracy, reducing cumulative radiation exposure, and ​minimizing the soft-tissue dissection required for bilateral fixation.

Description of the method

Indications and contraindications

Indications include symptomatic unilateral or bilateral spondylolysis that are refractory to conservative management. Intervertebral disc height must be preserved both cephalad and caudad to the pars defect, and the adjacent facet joints must not demonstrate signs of arthrosis. Ideally, minimal or no spondylolisthesis is present. Additionally, we reserve this technique for young or active patients in whom motion preservation is desired.

Contraindications include significant intervertebral disc degeneration or facet arthrosis adjacent to the affected level, high-grade spondylolisthesis, active infection, or poor bone quality precluding fixation. A relative contraindication would include an enlarged gap (>2 ​mm) or corticated defect margins, in which case an isolated repair may not be sufficient [2,[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]].

The increased sagittal shear forces and lordotic alignment of the L5 relative to more cephalad levels warrant ​careful consideration. While direct repair of a pars defect at the L5 is our preferred approach for symptomatic spondylolysis, we acknowledge that others have advocated for fusion at this level, particularly when significant disc degeneration or spondylolisthesis is present. We therefore reserve direct pars repair at the L5 for patients with minimal to no anterolisthesis and no greater than mild disc degeneration at the adjacent L5-S1 disc. Patients with higher-grade slip or disc degeneration at this level are counseled toward segmental instrumented fusion rather than isolated pars repair.

Preoperative imaging

Upright posterior-anterior (PA) and lateral radiographs of the lumbar spine are obtained for every patient. Plain radiographs are useful for the initial diagnosis of a pars defect, as well as evaluating sagittal alignment, spondylosis, and spondylolisthesis.

Thin-slice computed tomography (CT) scan of the lumbar spine is then obtained to characterize the pars defect and assess for defect morphology, gap width, and stage of healing. CT allows for classification of the defect as early (hairline fissure), progressive (visible gap with small fragments), or terminal (wide gap with sclerotic, atrophic margins) [5,6]. Critical assessment of defect characteristics is paramount ​as terminal-stage and wider-gap defects are associated with substantially lower union rates regardless of fixation technique [[5], [6], [7]]. CT is also used to assess for spina bifida occulta and hypoplastic posterior elements, which may compromise fixation and are considered a relative contraindication.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is obtained in all patients to assess intervertebral disc height and degeneration at the level of the defect and adjacent segments, evaluate for concomitant pathology (eg, disc herniation, facet arthrosis), and identify bone marrow edema on STIR/T2 sequences, which can support an acute or subacute stress reaction as the pain generator. MRI findings of adjacent disc degeneration or facet arthrosis are incorporated into the surgical decision-making process described earlier.

In select cases, a diagnostic pars injection under fluoroscopic or CT guidance may be used to confirm the pars defect as the primary pain generator prior to proceeding with surgery, particularly when imaging findings are equivocal or when low back pain is present in the setting of bilateral defects with asymmetric symptoms.

Patient positioning and equipment

After induction of general anesthesia, the patient is positioned prone on a radiolucent spine-specific operating room table. Care is taken to pad all bony prominences, particularly the anterior superior iliac spine where the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve is at risk for compression. Caution is taken when flexing the knees and hips ​as excessive hip flexion may lead to distraction of the defect (Fig. 1). We utilize continuous neuromonitoring including electromyography and somatosensory evoked potential for the duration of the procedure. The surgical site is then prepped and draped.

Patient positioning.

Navigation array placement

The posterior superior iliac spine (PSIS) is localized and palpated for placement of the patient array. A small stab incision is made just lateral to the PSIS, and the array pin is malleted into the pelvis until good fixation is achieved. This is best accomplished with the use of a large cylindrical mallet. The array is attached to the PSIS pin facing the navigation camera (Fig. 2). It is important to orient the array as to not block screw trajectory, particularly when instrumenting lower lumbar levels.

(A) The PSIS pin (yellow arrow) is placed just lateral to the PSIS through a small stab incision. (B) The PSIS pin is malleted into the pelvis. (C) The patient array (red arrow) is attached to the PSIS pin, facing toward the navigation camera. PSIS, posterior superior iliac spine.

Navigation calibration

With the array attached, we now obtain our 3D ​intraoperative imaging using a mobile CT-based navigation platform. The imaging device is brought into the surgical field and centered over the affected spinal level, as confirmed by PA and lateral fluoroscopic images. The imaging device is then spun and removed from the surgical field. To minimize radiation exposure, we utilize a low-dose 3D acquisition protocol, adjusting exposure parameters (kV, mA, mAs) based on patient body habitus per the specific navigation platform’s low-dose settings. Navigated instruments are then registered in reference to the navigation array.

Surgical technique

Utilizing the navigated probe, the incision is localized. An approximately 2-cm incision is made in the midline (Fig. 3). We have found that one midline incision can accommodate bilateral screw placement. Electrocautery is then utilized for hemostasis and further dissection down to the level of the fascia. Bilateral fascial incisions are created on either side of the midline, taking care to make these in-line with the desired trajectory.

The incision is localized utilizing the navigated probe (yellow arrow). An approximately 2-cm skin incision is made in the midline.

To establish our screw trajectories, a navigated drill guide is placed in the desired position on the inferior lamina and a guidewire is then advanced. The wire is then advanced in a cephalad and medial-to-lateral trajectory through the pars and across the defect and confirmed on biplanar fluoroscopic imaging (Fig. 4). Screw length is then calculated using a depth gauge.

The navigated drill is advanced cephalad from the inferior lamina with a medial-to-lateral trajectory through the pars and across the defect.

With the guidewire remaining in place, a cannulated drill is then advanced over the guidewire, followed by a cannulated tap. The tap is then advanced over the guide wire and across the defect into the proximal fragment. This ensures that placement of the screw will not further distract the gap.

Frequent fluoroscopic confirmation is essential throughout this portion of the procedure. The position of the guidewire should be reassessed each time it is advanced, as well as during passage of the drill, tap, and screw. Inadvertent advancement of the guidewire can occur easily during over-drilling and must be avoided. Additionally, the surgeon should confirm that the drill is progressing smoothly over the wire. Lack of forward advancement may indicate bending of the guidewire, which can occur when a small-diameter wire encounters sclerotic bone. In this situation, continued drilling risks shearing or damaging the guidewire and should prompt reassessment prior to proceeding.

At this point, an appropriately sized 4.0 mm cannulated screw is advanced into the position of the guidewire (Fig. 5). It is important to remember to withdraw the guidewire prior to completely advancing the screw to avoid breaking off the tip of the guidewire.

Screw placement with fluoroscopic guidance.

Although the navigation platform utilized provides real-time virtual trajectory, we elect to use a physical guidewire rather than relying on virtual trajectory alone for two reasons. First, subsequent cannulated instrumentation (drill, tap, and screw) must follow an identical mechanical path across multiple passes. A physical guidewire mechanically constrains these cannulated instruments to the same track established at the time of initial guidewire placement. Second, while navigation accuracy is generally reliable, it can be affected by registration drift, soft-tissue creep, or movement of the patient and/or array after the initial 3D imaging acquisition. Utilizing a physical guidewire allows for mitigation of the aforementioned risks, particularly give the consequences of a malpositioned screw adjacent to the spinal canal or traversing nerve root.

An alternative fixation strategy is to utilize a hydroxyapatite-coated 4.5-mm cannulated pedicle screw without an attached tulip (Fig. 6) to achieve additional fixation with the opportunity for boney ingrowth about the screw.

Hydroxyapatite-coated 4.5-mm cannulated pedicle screw without an attached tulip.

Intraoperative fluoroscopy

Appropriate screw position and fixation is assessed utilizing a combination of stand and oblique fluoroscopic projections. These views are obtained after guidewire placement, following drilling and tapping, and again after the final screw placement.

PA view

Confirms the medial-to-lateral trajectory relative to the spinous process and the pedicle and verifies that the screw remains lateral to the spinal canal and medial border of the pedicle to avoid violation of the canal or traversing nerve root (Fig. 7A).

Appropriate screw placement is confirmed with PA (A), lateral (B), and oblique (C, D) fluoroscopy. PA, posterior-anterior.

Lateral view

Confirms the cephalad-caudad trajectory across the pars defect and is utilized to calculate screw length via the depth gauge. This view also allows for assessment of screw advancement relative to the defect and ensures that the screw does not breach the anterior vertebral body or violate the adjacent disc space (Fig. 7B).

45° oblique view

In cases where intraosseous screw position remains uncertain on standard views (eg, in patients with atypical pars morphology), a 45° oblique projection provides a more tangential view down the long axis of the pars and can better delineate the screw’s containment within bone (Fig. 7C and 7D).

Closure

The navigation array and PSIS pin are then removed. The incisions are thoroughly irrigated and closed in layers followed by the application of sterile dressings. We prefer to close the incision with a subcuticular, absorbable suture that is sealed with a skin adhesive. Subcutaneous injection of a local anesthetic is then infiltrated.

Postoperative care

Patients are allowed to mobilize as tolerated without the use of a brace postoperatively. They are instructed to limit twisting and bending for 6 weeks postoperatively followed by progressive return to activities, as monitored by physical therapy starting 6 weeks following surgery with the goal of full noncontact sports participation 3 months following surgery. Routine CT imaging is not obtained postoperatively in asymptomatic patients in order to minimize radiation exposure.

Pitfalls and complications

Complications associated with this technique can be categorized as general, navigation related, or instrumentation related.

Prone positioning and pressure over the anterior superior iliac spine can result in injury to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. Thus, it is imperative to pad all bony prominences while positioning the patient. Superficial and deep surgical site infection, wound dehiscence, and postoperative hematoma are possible, although soft-tissue morbidity is minimized relative to open techniques. Complications associated with the PSIS array pin may include local irritation, infection, or rarely pelvic fracture.

Navigation registration inaccuracy can result in a discordance between the virtual and actual trajectory of instruments. Common causes of registration inaccuracy include movement of the array, soft-tissue shift, or inadequate initial 3D imaging acquisition. We aim to mitigate this potential complication via the use of frequent independent fluoroscopy.

While utilizing cannulated instruments, guidewire migration or bending can result in a deviated trajectory leading to cortical breach. Overpenetration of the screw can violate the anterior vertebral cortex or the adjacent disc space, while undercompression across the defect can result in inadequate fixation and subsequent hardware pullout, loosening, or nonunion. Nonunion remains a risk, particularly in patients with terminal-stage defects, wider defect gaps, or high-grade disc degeneration [2,[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Lastly, nerve root irritation from screw malposition has been reported. However, pooled meta-analysis data suggest ​that pedicle screw–based repair constructs carry the lowest overall complication rates compared with wire- or hook-based constructs [3].

Comparison to other methods

Minimally invasive direct repair of the pars interarticularis allows for motion preservation and reduced soft-tissue morbidity while maintaining reliable fixation in young patients. Open direct pars fixation with screws, wiring, and pedicle-based constructs have demonstrated good healing rates ​but at the cost of paraspinal muscle disruption, increased blood loss, and longer recovery times relative to MIS approaches [4].

MIS approaches have classically used two paramedian incisions to achieve bilateral fixation. More recent series have reported favorable early improvements in pain scores and return to sport utilizing fluoroscopy-guided fixation through a single midline incision [11,12]. Our technique expands on the minimally invasive rationale by using a single midline skin incision with bilateral fascial incisions to achieve bilateral fixation, avoiding additional paramedian dissection. Although decreased operative time, reduced blood loss, and faster recovery are theoretical benefits of a minimally invasive approach relative to open techniques [3,4], quantitative comparison data have not been published and remain ​an important direction for future studies.

While MIS series have demonstrated that accurate screw placement is achievable with the use of fluoroscopy alone, intraoperative navigation provides the benefit of improved trajectory confidence and reduced fluoroscopy dependence. As the pars interarticularis is a narrow target in a small surgical field when using MIS techniques, fluoroscopic instrumentation often requires multiple sequential images, increasing radiation exposure for the patient and the surgical team. Utilizing navigation, screw length and trajectory can be precisely planned relative to the patient’s own anatomy intraoperatively. This is particularly useful at more caudal levels such as the L5, where increased lordotic angulation creates an oblique relationship between the pars and standard fluoroscopic planes, potentially compromising accurate trajectory and reproducibility of screw placement. Two case series, those by Zhu et al. (11 patients, 9 at the L5) and Yurac et al. (3 patients, 2 at the L5), have used navigation for MIS pars repair with good outcomes, although both series incorporated debridement and bone grafting [13,14].

In contrast to prior series that advocate formal debridement and grafting, we do not routinely bone graft the pars defect. This approach is based on the rationale that the act of guidewire placement, cannulated drilling, and tapping across the defect may function analogously to reaming in long bone fracture surgery, thereby stimulating a local biologic healing response. In addition, many pars defects are at least partially intracapsular, potentially limiting bony graft containment. Furthermore, radiographic union is not a strict prerequisite for favorable clinical outcomes ​as symptomatic improvement can occur despite incomplete osseous healing.

Regardless of the fixation construct, lower union rates have been associated with terminal-stage defect morphology, larger defect gap, and higher-grade disc degeneration [2,[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10],15,16]. As defect gap and screw trajectory relative to the defect are modifiable factors intraoperatively, we believe that accurate navigation-assisted instrumentation provides value in cases where achieving optimal compression across the defect is technically demanding.

Summary

Navigation-assisted minimally invasive direct pars repair represents an evolution of established MIS techniques, integrating the anatomic advantages of Buck-type fixation with modern guidance technology to enhance accuracy while limiting surgical morbidity.

Key advantages of this technique include minimized muscle dissection, reduced reliance on repeated fluoroscopy, and the ability to achieve bilateral fixation through a limited exposure. Potential disadvantages include dependence on navigation accuracy and the requirement for intraoperative 3D imaging. As with all direct pars repairs, outcomes are highly dependent on appropriate patient selection, and this technique is best suited for patients without significant disc degeneration, facet arthrosis, or high-grade spondylolisthesis.
