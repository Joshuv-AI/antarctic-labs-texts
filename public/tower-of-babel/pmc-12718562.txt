ABSTRACT
Abstract Multiplex immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry (mIF/IHC) are increasingly employed antibody-based technologies that use tissue sparingly and facilitate the detection of co-localized or neighboring biomarkers. Specifically, these platforms enable spatial analyses of the tumor microenvironment as well as extended applications, for example, describing normal tissue anatomy, autoimmunity, infectious diseases, etc. mIF/IHC has greatly enhanced biomarker discovery efforts, and a growing number of studies suggest superiority to traditional IHC. Standardization of staining approaches, reporting of image analysis strategies and resultant data is critical for facilitating cross-study comparisons, validation, deployment, and generalization of findings. To address this challenge, The Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC) previously published two articles providing best practice guidelines for mIF/IHC staining, image analysis, and data sharing. Here, SITC convened stakeholders to develop the third article in the series, a consensus checklist for scientific reporting of mIF/IHC data to support and complement the best practice guidelines. The checklist includes critical components of mIF/IHC applications to be defined within publications such as detailed descriptions of analytical validation; image acquisition, selection, and registration methods; and cell clustering and spatial analysis strategies, amongst others. Such information will help with data reproducibility and comparison across studies towards future drug and assay development.Keywords: Pathology, Biomarker, Immunotherapy, Tumor microenvironment - TME

Introduction

Multiplex immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry (mIF/IHC) are antibody-based technologies that allow for multiple markers to be detected and visualized simultaneously on a single tissue section. In addition to the fact that it is tissue-sparing, mIF/IHC facilitates the detection of marker co-expression on and within individual cells and the spatial arrangements of these cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME).1 As such, mIF/IHC is well poised for the characterization of complex immune cell phenotypes and accelerating tissue-based biomarker discovery, in the context of immunotherapy and beyond, for example, infectious disease. These recommendations apply to any fluorescence or chromogenic method to detect primary antibody binding to a target antigen in situ, such as direct primary antibody labeling, an enzyme-conjugated secondary antibody, DNA barcoding, and/or fluorophore deposition. They also apply to tissue-based mass spectrometry approaches, which use elemental mass tags attached to primary antibodies, rather than fluorescent or chromogenic reporters to detect markers of interest.

Standardization and validation of an end-to-end mIF/IHC workflow are critical. While mIF/IHC is widely used for research purposes in large academic centers and industry laboratories, there are several challenges associated with technology adoption. These challenges include, but are not limited to, instrument cost, staffing, tissue controls, assay development and qualification, data management, digital image analysis tools, computational support, and data reporting standards. Without these standards, it is difficult to assess the quality of research data and findings presented, and reproducibility could suffer.

The mIF/IHC Scientific Interest Group (SIG) originated from the Tumor Immune Microenvironment laboratory at the National Cancer Institute in Bethesda, Maryland, to create a multidisciplinary network of scientists focused on developing mIF/IHC, with the dual goals of enabling better access to technology and defining its clinical utility. The SIG collaborated with the Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC) Pathology Committee to create the mIF/IHC-SIG SITC Initiative. As a part of this initiative, pathologists, medical oncologists, statisticians, and data scientists from academia, government, and industry created a consensus checklist to guide scientific reporting of mIF/IHC assay development and associated results with the intention of improving reproducibility and fueling future assay and drug development. The checklist offers a foundation for laboratories or scientists using the technology and creates a framework for technical and methodological standardization of mIF/IHC results. While the implementation of mIF/IHC in a College of American Pathologists/Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments-certified clinical laboratory is outside the scope of this article, many of the principles and quality control metrics are consistent with those requirements.

Development of Standards for Reporting of Multiplex Immunohistochemistry/Immunofluorescence Assays

The SITC Pathology Committee nominated five individuals to organize and lead this effort. In total, 53 participants, including pathologists from academia, government, and industry, provided insight for the aforementioned checklist. The full participant list and working groups are listed in online supplemental figure 1. Each participant had previous mIF/IHC experience. SITC collected participant conflicts of interest according to their policy, and all details are listed within this manuscript.

A preliminary checklist was developed by the steering committee (Drs Sater, Rodriguez-Canales, Bifulco, Taube, Yeong, and SITC leaders). Working groups met throughout 2022 to refine the checklist, ensuring clarity, specificity, and strength of each item. All participants completed a survey to assess consensus by indicating if they agreed or disagreed with each checklist item. The minimum threshold for consensus was set at 75% with 100% participation. Consensus ranged between 88% and 100% with 53/53 participants providing insight (100% participation rate). The final checklist is shown in table 1, and the rationale and relevance for each item, as well as examples from the current literature, are described.

Standards for Reporting of Multiplex Immunohistochemistry/Immunofluorescence Assays checklist

SCOPE OF STUDY

To ensure that the checklist is being correctly applied to mIF/IHC data reporting, it is important for investigators to confirm that they are using immunostaining on tissue as a primary method. This information, while broad, helps define the scope of the work and allows for further application of the remaining checklist items. Similarly, by identifying the detection method as an antibody-based multiplex approach, investigators can ensure the relevance of the checklist.

Checklist item #1: Type of study.

Descriptor: Use immunostaining as a primary method.

Participants agreed that explicitly identifying whether the study used mIF/IHC as a primary method is warranted. Participants agreed that the checklist would also be applicable in scenarios where a combination of research methods was used alongside mIF/IHC tissue-based antibody-based staining methods, such as flow cytometry, single-cell RNAseq profiling, cell culture, spatial transcriptomics, etc, as long as mIF/IHC was used to make a “primary” observation or key finding.

Examples in current literature

“Using multiplex immunofluorescence and image analysis, we assessed CD73 protein expression on tumor cells, tumor infiltrating leukocytes and stromal cells.”2

“Using multiplex immunofluorescence and image analysis, we assessed CD73 protein expression on tumor cells, tumor infiltrating leukocytes and stromal cells.”2

“We evaluated PD-L1 and VISTA expression in different cell subtypes from matched pre- and post-treatment prostate tumors …”3

“We evaluated PD-L1 and VISTA expression in different cell subtypes from matched pre- and post-treatment prostate tumors …”3

These are separate examples in literature that would be acceptable by consensus checklist standards. The first study directly indicates that mIF/IHC was used within the study. The second study, which assessed the effect of ipilimumab therapy in patients with prostate cancer, is broader and used multiple methods, including mouse studies, flow cytometry, and immunostaining, as detailed in the methods. However, major findings from this study stemmed from the mIF/IHC data, and as such, it is considered an example of a study where immunostaining is a primary method.

GENERAL INFORMATION

A detailed workflow of a study’s mIF/IHC processes and data reporting supports reviewers and readers in analyzing the outcomes. Following published validation methods can improve reproducibility and facilitate cross-study comparison of results.7,11 Additionally, validation metrics support reviewers and readers in ensuring data completeness and accuracy, while quality control metrics reinforce researchers’ commitment to data reproducibility.

Checklist item #3: System.

Descriptor: Provide details on the workflow including platforms leading to final results.

A description of the staining/imaging workflow was deemed necessary to accurately assess final results of the study. Participants noted specific details that should be included within the provided workflow including the imaging methods, imaging devices, and the software used for acquisition and analysis. Any antigen retrieval or staining equipment not described in the staining method earlier should be included here. It was noted that providing a figure or schematic is often a helpful supplement to a written workflow description.

Examples in current literature

“Protein expression of the various markers was determined by the automated quantitative analysis (AQUA) method of QIF on fluorescence images acquired using a PM-2000 system (Navigate BioPharma, Carlsbad, CA, USA).”12

“Protein expression of the various markers was determined by the automated quantitative analysis (AQUA) method of QIF on fluorescence images acquired using a PM-2000 system (Navigate BioPharma, Carlsbad, CA, USA).”12

“Automated hyperplex IF staining and imaging was performed on the COMET™ platform (Lunaphore Technologies) … The hyperplex immunocytochemistry protocol template was generated using the COMET™ Control Software (v 0.70.0.1, Lunaphore Technologies), and reagents were loaded onto the device to perform the sequential IF (seqIF™) protocol.”13

“Automated hyperplex IF staining and imaging was performed on the COMET™ platform (Lunaphore Technologies) … The hyperplex immunocytochemistry protocol template was generated using the COMET™ Control Software (v 0.70.0.1, Lunaphore Technologies), and reagents were loaded onto the device to perform the sequential IF (seqIF™) protocol.”13

The two examples here fit the consensus criteria by detailing the specific instrument and software package used for data generation.

Checklist item #4: Analytical validation.

Descriptor: Provide the metrics used for analytic validation.

Various metrics are required for validating staining (primary and secondary antibody optimization, flour-marker pairings, concordance with IHC), image acquisition (reference signal separation library details, software version used, image format, calibration), image analysis (area of slide sampled, cellular and tissue compartments analyzed, markers used to define a phenotype), and data analysis (thresholding, scoring, measurement, reporting, scripts, use of artificial intelligence, software version, and data sharing).

The intent of this checklist item is for authors to ensure that validation metrics (eg, demonstration of staining reproducibility and tissue used for that analysis) are provided within the study description, with maximum detail considered desirable. Working group participants also clarified that this specific checklist item addresses analytical validation and is not intended to require clinical validation to meet this checklist criterion.

Examples in current literature

“A six-plex mIF/IHC panel (PD-L1, PD-1, CD8, CD68, FOXP3, and CK) was rigorously optimized as determined by quantitative equivalence to immunohistochemistry (IHC) chromogenic assays. Serial sections from tonsil and breast carcinoma and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) tissue microarrays (TMAs), TSA-Opal fluorescent detection reagents, and antibodies were distributed to the six sites equipped with a Leica Bond Rx autostainer and a Vectra Polaris multispectral imaging platform. Tissue sections were stained and imaged at each site and delivered to a single site for analysis. Intersite and intrasite reproducibility were assessed by linear fits to plots of cell densities, including %PDL1 expression by TCs and ICs in the breast and NSCLC TMAs.”14

Checklist item #5: Quality control.

Descriptor: Provide the metrics used for quality control.

This checklist item is very similar to validation. Examples of commonly applied metrics include various control checks (positive, negative, batch, and library controls for staining), system settings (exposure time), expression (representative ROI selection, expression in correct cellular compartment and tissue location, pathologist oversight), aberrancy (edge or stitching effect, segmentation, phenotyping, or batch errors), data analysis (visual verification of algorithm results), etc.

One specific aspect discussed by the working group was the inclusion of details regarding the assessment of specific study items by qualified pathologists and/or imaging scientists. This includes the reviewing of an accompanying, adjacent serially sectioned H&E-stained slide to ensure analysis of relevant or representative diagnostic material prior to performing mIF/IHC. Qualified imaging scientists should also oversee the use of appropriate tissue controls as well as the resultant tissue and cell segmentation quality. Details regarding how artifacts, variability (batch, slide-to-slide, intra-slide), signal overlap, and/or if the umbrella effect was addressed are also included here.

Examples in current literature

“Prior to processing, all images were assessed for quality control. Criteria for rejection included poor tissue quality, such as tissue folds or missing tissue sections, and staining artifacts, including signal dropout and air bubbles.”14

“Prior to processing, all images were assessed for quality control. Criteria for rejection included poor tissue quality, such as tissue folds or missing tissue sections, and staining artifacts, including signal dropout and air bubbles.”14

“… results from the (multi-institutional) precision study revealed relatively low intra-run coefficient of variation (CV) in the proportion of positive cells (ie, variability is typically < 25%); whereas inter-run CV was typically well above 25%. The latter was due to batch-to-batch variability where there was considerable variation in the intensity of labeling of biomarkers across different runs. Reanalysis of the precision data using local thresholding for biomarker positivity within a run resulted in improved inter-run CV without affecting the intra-run CV.”15

“… results from the (multi-institutional) precision study revealed relatively low intra-run coefficient of variation (CV) in the proportion of positive cells (ie, variability is typically < 25%); whereas inter-run CV was typically well above 25%. The latter was due to batch-to-batch variability where there was considerable variation in the intensity of labeling of biomarkers across different runs. The first is a good example of reporting on criteria for evaluation of artifacts for sample or tissue region exclusion. The second is a good example of describing metrics used for quality control, including evaluation of singleplex to multiplex and how batch-to-batch variation was addressed.

PANEL DESIGN

Investigators should provide a descriptor of immunofluorescence markers/methods and why they chose them to allow reviewers to analyze the relevance of markers and/or establish standards for future similar studies if deemed successful. Additionally, providing evidence or references for antibody selection supports comparisons across studies.

Checklist item #6: Rationale for marker choice.

Descriptor: Provide rationale for marker choice in biological or immunological question.

The rationale for the selection of panel markers in the context of biological or immunological questions is warranted. This may include consultation with literature and/or a qualified researcher, a pathologist, immunologist, oncologist, etc.

Examples in current literature

“Together, these enable assessment of multiple lineage-selective and phenotypic biomarkers in three FFPE tissue sections quantitatively evaluated with three 12-antibody biomarker panels, that audit lymphoid and myeloid lineages, and the functional status of T cells.”16

“Together, these enable assessment of multiple lineage-selective and phenotypic biomarkers in three FFPE tissue sections quantitatively evaluated with three 12-antibody biomarker panels, that audit lymphoid and myeloid lineages, and the functional status of T cells.”16

“We selected three phospho-specific antibodies directed toward known markers of BCR activation.”17

“We selected three phospho-specific antibodies directed toward known markers of BCR activation.”17

These examples highlight the key questions (eg, lineage selective, phenotype, functional status, BCR activation) addressed to reflect on biology or immunology being investigated within the study.

Checklist item #7: Antibody selection.

Descriptor: Provide evidence and/or references to support antibody selection.

It is expected that validated antibodies will be used in the mIF/IHC assays.7 8 Appropriate data or references including analytical validation for the used antibody clones should be provided within the study. In the event that an antibody clone/IHC reagent is used that has not been validated for IHC/IF assays before, appropriate evidence of its functionality and relevance should also be included within the study. This should also include single antibody staining by IHC or IF and confirmation that further conjugation (eg, fluorophore, metal ion, DNA barcoding, etc) does not interfere with staining.

The goal of this criterion is to aid in substantiating clone selection and facilitate antibody clone comparison(s) across mIF/IHC studies. While a previous criterion focused on biological or immunological questions that an antibody needs to answer, this section simply addresses which clone was used to answer such questions.

Examples in current literature

“We tested numerous anti-phospho-LYN, SYK and BTK antibodies under a wide range of conditions against untreated or sIg-crosslinked FFPE cell lines to identify the best reagent for IHC using FFPE tissue samples, comparing results by IHC to western blots of cell lysates under the same stimulation conditions and using the same antibodies. Based on this systematic approach we found the antibodies and procedures below gave optimal performance and reproducibility in qIF compared to others.”17

“We tested numerous anti-phospho-LYN, SYK and BTK antibodies under a wide range of conditions against untreated or sIg-crosslinked FFPE cell lines to identify the best reagent for IHC using FFPE tissue samples, comparing results by IHC to western blots of cell lysates under the same stimulation conditions and using the same antibodies. Based on this systematic approach we found the antibodies and procedures below gave optimal performance and reproducibility in qIF compared to others.”17

“Antibody clones were carefully selected for panel consideration after reviewing previous literature, and signal positivity was confirmed in FFPE tissues using single- or double-IF imaging … To design the PhenoCycler panel, antibody clones were conjugated with DNA oligonucleotide barcodes … Antibody-DNA barcode conjugation was confirmed by protein gel electrophoresis …”18

“Antibody clones were carefully selected for panel consideration after reviewing previous literature, and signal positivity was confirmed in FFPE tissues using single- or double-IF imaging … To design the PhenoCycler panel, antibody clones were conjugated with DNA oligonucleotide barcodes … Antibody-DNA barcode conjugation was confirmed by protein gel electrophoresis …”18

These examples detail the antibodies tested, describe the approach to clone selection and validation (comparison to western blots or to single color IF/IHC), as well as conjugation confirmation when converting IHC/IF antibodies to be used in a murine FFPE CODEX panel.

TISSUE STAINING

Multiplex staining is one of the more mature components of the mIF/IHC assay workflow, with ample data available to support a high standard for the checklist and field as a whole. Numerous reports exist describing the best practices for mIF/IHC assay development and optimization,9 19 20 including the SITC statement on best practices for multiplex IHC and immunofluorescence (IF) staining and validation.10 General concepts that are widely endorsed include using monoclonal antibodies whenever available and robust controls at multiple steps in the mIF/IHC staining process. Information regarding how the order of markers was established and validated for a new panel is also of value.

Checklist item #8: Staining protocol.

Descriptor: Provide a staining protocol summarizing the reagents used, their order of staining, and other details (clone name, fluorophore/other labeling reagent, manufacturer, catalog, lot).

Authors should provide a staining protocol summarizing the reagents used, staining order, and other logistical details that would be required to reproduce the experiment(s). This information is often amenable to a tabular format, table 2. If a protocol is being used from a previous study, a reference to the initial publication is sufficient.

Checklist item #9: Staining controls.

Descriptor: Provide details on panel controls (positive and negative control, no antibody control, batch-to-batch control, reference control).

Participants stressed that proper controls are essential for any mIF/IHC study. Descriptions should include the positive and negative control tissues used (eg, tonsil tissue or standardized tissue microarrays, etc) and whether slides included internal positive and negative controls for each marker in the panel. More formal reference controls for individual antibodies are available for some specific markers (eg, cell pellet controls or polymer beads decorated by a known concentration of the protein of interest). In these cases, control material may already be sectioned onto glass slides to be analyzed alongside specimens of interest. Additional controls that should also be specified include those for autofluorescence. Isotype controls were also recognized to be of value, especially when polyclonal primary antibodies were used.

Participants felt that more robust controls are necessary as the stringency of data is increased. Specifically, there was significant discussion on control requirements for dichotomous (single threshold used to establish “positivity” for an individual cell) versus discretized/categorical quantitative versus continuous quantitative data. Participants agreed that controls were essential for each category and that the controls would necessarily differ based on the type of quantitation performed. The use of controls for batch-to-batch variation is also recommended.

Checklist item #10: Staining optimization.

Descriptor: Provide details on steps used in optimization (eg, singleplex to multiplex comparison).

Participants felt that optimization details would be necessary to better interpret data sets as protocols/processes become more complex. A primary example of optimization that was discussed was comparisons of singleplex data that are incorporated into a multiplexed process. Details that could be analyzed include interactions between the chemistries across the protocol, which could potentially eventuate in signal reduction, bleed-through between channels, and other interference phenomena that potentially affect signal integrity once markers are combined in the multiplex format.

Of the 44 antibodies evaluated, 86% had a high Pearson correlation (>0.8) between the first and second applications, but 81% had a lower dynamic range and SBR on the second application.”24

IMAGE ACQUISITION

There is often a practical and technical trade-off made between the number of markers visualized in a tissue section and the amount of surface area that can be imaged by a mIF/IHC platform.10 It is thus important for investigators to communicate the size of the imaged tissue and whether the whole slide or regions of interest (ROI) were imaged. If the latter approach is used, it is important to detail the number of ROIs used and how they were chosen to ensure reproducibility. ROI exclusion criteria should also be detailed (eg, necrotic tissue or tissue folds). The committee widely recognized that it is of scientific benefit to have a pathologist involved in ROI selection but also noted that criteria of field selection beyond a general statement of “pathologist-selected fields were used” is essential.

Other important reporting parameters that facilitate result reproducibility include image resolution as well as how the labels for different multiplexing markers and native tissue features (eg, chromogenic deposits, fluor spectra, tissue autofluorescence) are separated from each other to ensure accurate signal attribution for the individual markers. A closely related issue is image registration, where repeat images of the same slide or images of sequential slides are taken and then Z-stacked.26 27 In the case where the same slide is being used, alignment quality can impact whether the signal is appropriately attributed to the correct cell versus its neighbor. For analyses conducted on sequential slides that have been registered, alignment quality affects the accuracy of spatial statistics and associated analyses.

Checklist item #11: Approach to image acquisition.

Descriptor: Describe the methodology of image acquisition (eg, whole slide vs region of interest (ROI)). If ROIs, describe how selected and by whom (eg, trained pathologist assessment).

Details for acquired images—including the number of HPF/ROIs per case, size of ROI, how they were chosen, eg, tumor content—and image resolution were deemed critical. With regard to image resolution, reporting of the microscope objective and platform used is the minimum, and a standardized reporting of pixels per micron is highly preferred. Inclusion of the image output file format was also noted to be of value (eg, .tiff vs .png, etc) as it pertains to downstream ingestion for image analysis. The group also emphasized the benefit of having a trained pathologist perform field selection.

Checklist item #12: Input tissue.

Descriptions of input tissue processes were deemed important, including whether a whole slide or tissue microarray (TMA) was used. In both instances, providing details (eg, excisional specimen vs biopsy/type of biopsy) is warranted. If more than one block per patient specimen, or even multiple specimens from the same patient, were available, the authors should clarify how a specific block was chosen for analysis. Of note, TMAs were heavily discussed. While they are not used for clinical care, participants noted that they can provide efficiencies for analyses and associated biomarker discovery research.

IMAGE ANALYSIS

It was determined that current standards for the minimum amount of information being reported should be raised to facilitate interpretation of the image analysis steps used in a study for better interpretability and reproducibility by other scientists.

Checklist item #16: Image selection.

Descriptor: Describe how many images per sample were analyzed and rationale for inclusion/exclusion of certain images, including minimum quantitative and/or qualitative criteria for downstream analysis.

Selecting appropriate images or portions of images for analysis is an essential prerequisite for an accurate and meaningful image analysis, as it provides the opportunity to reduce the impact of artifacts (tissue folds, out-of-focus regions, etc) and enhances reliability and reproducibility. Authors should provide a detailed description of the image selection process, including whether the analysis was performed on individual ROIs/FOVs and how they were selected, or on a seamless, whole slide image. There was a debate over the need to include specific language on how many images per sample were analyzed, and on the requirement for a description of the rationale for including or excluding certain images. Though some participants questioned this, the majority agreed it was vital. For instance, authors conducting whole slide analyses might intentionally exclude certain areas based on their hypothesis. Providing this information would underline that such exclusion was a deliberate decision.

Checklist item #17: Image analysis workflow.

Descriptor: Describe the image analysis workflow, including tissue segmentation and cell segmentation, as appropriate. Establishing a well-defined workflow is crucial for streamlining the image analysis process and ensuring that the data are consistently processed and analyzed. The primary intent of this section is to ensure that authors have properly described the image analysis workflow used within the study. For example, details including methodology, software version, and any pertinent module or routine, and whether specific scripts were used, should be included for the community. Where research software has a primary citation, this should also be included. Other items that participants identified as important to include are information on how the authors defined positive vs negative staining, as well as information on phenotypic determination methodology such as clustering.

DATA ANALYSIS AND PRESENTATION

Regulating and standardizing data analysis and presentation are essential for ensuring data accuracy, reproducibility, and transparency. These factors help prevent data bias and redundancy, while facilitating easy replication and verification of results, thereby building on existing knowledge and advancing understanding. Importantly, these aspects increase trust and confidence in the data and conclusions. Consistent data presentation also allows for efficient data sharing and comparison across multiple datasets. When presented in a standard structure, it further enhances data interoperability and integration, which can lead to new insights and discoveries that may not otherwise have been noticed. With this ease in data interpretation, it eventually becomes easier to share knowledge and foster collaboration.

Despite the aforementioned benefits, participants noted that the field has not reached consensus about the data threshold required for publication concerning this topic. Therefore, the group detailed critical information to be included in mIF studies concerning platforms and processes used for data analyses and finalized four items within this checklist section.

Checklist item #19: Approach to data analysis.

Descriptor: Summarize the platforms and software packages (eg, MATLAB, R, Python, Prism) used for analysis including name, version, modules, etc.

Participants agreed that authors should disclose all platforms and software used for data analyses and calculations. Platforms and software should be appropriately referenced within the Materials and Methods section and should include version information, modules, etc. If applicable, novel software validation should also be included within the text. Participants were enthusiastic about authors depositing open-source code in public repositories (complete with a license statement clarifying restricted or unrestricted use) but recognized this may not be possible under all scenarios.

A comprehensive discussion of best practices for reporting novel artificial intelligence (AI)-based approaches to the evaluation of mIHC/IF assays is beyond the scope of this manuscript. However, at a minimum, studies using AI-based analyses should describe the type of algorithm or model employed (eg, supervised vs unsupervised, deep learning vs classical machine learning), the source and characteristics of training and validation data, and the performance metrics used to evaluate model accuracy and robustness. Additionally, any pre-processing steps, human-in-the-loop elements, and strategies to mitigate bias should be explicitly stated.

Checklist item #22: Data transparency.

Descriptor: Provide sufficient detail to access data and reproduce the study results, for example, provide a Zenodo DOI or GitHub link with release tag for any relevant code or run parameters. Whenever possible, provide a link to the relevant image repository, which should include any associated metadata.

There was significant discussion as to whether a section promoting data transparency should be included within the checklist. Ultimately, participants agreed to its inclusion, primarily for detailing how to access data if necessary, including study images. Many participants noted similarities with current policies on data sharing from federally funded projects and felt that this section would, in most cases, be non-problematic as authors ensure consistency with those requirements.

Discussion

Technologies for biomarker discovery and novel therapeutic regimens are being developed in parallel, and mIF/IHC has emerged as a promising tool for research, offering a wealth of information on the spatial organization of cells and tissues. mIF/IHC and other emerging spatial biology technologies are expected to help pre-select patients for therapies and aid in the development of rational combinatorial regimens by highlighting candidate target expression. They can also be used to facilitate assessment of pharmacodynamic or immunomodulatory changes induced by treatments to support mechanisms of action studies. To achieve this, it is necessary to standardize mIF/IHC assay outputs across technologies and institutions.

SITC supports widespread adoption of this checklist tool when generating and reporting mIF/IHC data, with the aim of maximizing impact and minimizing errors and variability across studies. The use of these guidelines will also contribute to the development of robust, AI-ready datasets. This article has attempted to outline a comprehensive mIF/IHC checklist, including considerations for sample preparation, image acquisition, as well as image and downstream data analysis. Adhering to these guidelines is expected to ensure that results are presented correctly, are accurate, robust, and broadly interpretable. By improving the quality of the reporting structure/formatting of the data generated by spatial biology technologies, researchers can confidently unlock new insights into cancer biology, ultimately paving the way for the development of more effective anticancer treatments. It is our hope that this checklist will serve as a valuable resource for researchers in the field by supporting harmonization of IF/IHC reporting and contributing to the advancement of cancer research.
