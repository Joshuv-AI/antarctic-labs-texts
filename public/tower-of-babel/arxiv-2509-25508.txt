Analysis of a Cahn–Hilliard model for viscoelastoplastic two-phase flows
Fan Cheng, Robert Lasarzik, Marita Thomas

Abstract
We study a Cahn–Hilliard two-phase model describing the flow of two viscoelastoplastic fluids, which arises in geodynamics. A phase-field variable indicates the proportional distribution of the two fluids in the mixture. The motion of the incompressible mixture is described in terms of the volume-averaged velocity. Besides a volume-averaged Stokes-like viscous contribution, the Cauchy stress tensor in the momentum balance contains an additional volume-averaged internal stress tensor to model the elastoplastic behavior. This internal stress has its own evolution law featuring the nonlinear Zaremba-Jaumann time-derivative and the subdifferential of a non-smooth plastic potential. The well-posedness of this system is studied in two cases: Based on a regularization by stress-diffusion we obtain the existence of Leray-Hopf-type weak solutions. In order to deduce existence results also in the absence of the regularization we introduce the concept of dissipative solutions, which is based on an estimate for the relative energy. We discuss general properties of dissipative solutions and show their existence for the viscoelastoplastic two-phase model in the setting of stress-diffusion. By a limit passage in the relative energy inequality for vanishing stress-diffusion, we conclude an existence result for the non-regularized model.



Keywords:
viscoelastoplastic fluid, Cahn-Hilliard equation with singular potential, relative energy inequality, non-smooth potential, vanishing stress diffusion, dissipative solutions, Leray-Hopf solutions

Introduction

The movement of tectonic plates driven by convective processes within the Earth’s mantle is a generally recognized theory which explains many geological phenomena, e.g., earthquakes, volcanoes, formation of mountains, ocean trenches, mid-ocean ridges, and island arcs, see
STO 01
for details. These tectonic plates form the Earth’s lithosphere, consisting of the Earth’s crust and the uppermost part of its mantle. On geological time scales of millions of years, the moving and mechanically deforming plates in the mantle are treated as non-Newtonian viscoelastoplastic fluids.

In this paper, we consider a system of equations which describes a two-phase flow of a incompressible mixture of two viscoelastoplastic fluids arising from geodynamics. In a time interval
(0,T)
, where

, and a bounded
C^{2}-

\displaystyle\mbox{div}(v)

\displaystyle=0

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\overset{\triangledown}{S}+\partial{P}(\varphi;S)-\gamma\Delta S

\displaystyle\ni\eta(\varphi)(\nabla v)_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}}

Assume for now that we have a smooth solution

). Then we multiply (

) by

(1.11)

for all

and where
\xi\in\partial{P}(\varphi;S)
. From this energy-dissipation balance, we can see that the total energy of the system is dissipated by four parts: a Newtonian viscosity, a quadratic stress diffusion, dissipation due the plastic deformation stemming from the non-smooth potential
P
and a quadratic term due to phase separation involving the chemical potential
\mu
.

The mathematical challenges associated with analysis of this system stem from the following: First of all, the momentum balance (
1.1a
) comes with all the difficulties arising from the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations. Based on the large body of analytical results on Navier–Stokes equations, cf. e.g.
CF 88
;
Lio 96
;
Gal 00
;
FGS 06
;
Soh 01
;
Rou 13
;
RRS 16
, we cannot expect a better class of solutions for the velocity field than Leray–Hopf solutions, which were introduced in
Ler 34
;
Hop 51
.

Moreover, the stress evolution equation (
1.1d
) includes a nonlinearity in the Zaremba-Jaumann derivative (
1.2
) of
S
and a multi-valued derivative due to the non-smoothness of the dissipation potential
{P}
.
LM 00
studies the global existence of solutions based on the Zaremba-Jaumann derivative together with a smooth dissipation potential such that
\partial{P}(S)=aS
. In addition, the stress diffusion
-\gamma\Delta S
with
\gamma>0
is introduced as a regularization for analytical reasons as in
EHM 22
. However, in order to get closer to models used in geoscientific applications, e.g.
MDM 02
, we aim to avoid it in our analysis.

Furthermore, this model contains more than one phase, which also gives us the difficulties arising from the coupled Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes system. We refer to
EZ 86
;
AW 07
for the study of Cahn–Hilliard equations and to
Abe 09
;
CFG 12
;
AGG 12
;
ADG 13
for the study of the Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes system. In contrast to the above-mentioned works, we additionally face the difficulties of the multi-valued stress evolution (
1.1d
). A single-phase system of the momentum and stress equations has already been studied in
EHM 22
and
EHL 23
.

Our goal is to study the existence results for system (
1.1
) not only in the case
\gamma>0
but, more importantly, also in the case
\gamma=0
. This will be achieved with the help of an alternative concept of solution: the dissipative solution. The idea of dissipative solutions is that the solutions do not satisfy the equation in the distributional sense anymore, but, instead, one controls the difference between the solutions and smooth test functions satisfying the equations in terms of the relative energy and the relative dissipation. The concept of dissipative solutions was introduced by P.L. Lions
(
Lio 96
, Sec. 4.4)
in the context of the incompressible Euler equations and his motivation stemmed from the consideration of the singular limit in the Boltzmann equation and identification of its limits
Lio 94
. Since then, dissipative solutions have been applied in different contexts, for instance to singular limits in the Boltzman equations
Sai 03
, incompressible viscous electro-magneto-hydrodynamics
ASR 19
, equations of viscoelastic diffusion in polymers
Vor 08
, the Ericksen–Leslie equations
Las 18
;
Las 19
, or finite-element approximation of nematic electrolytes
BLP 21
. Moreover, it is also applied to isothermal damped Hamiltonian systems in
Las 22
and to a viscoelastoplastic single-phase models in
EHL 23
, where this notion of solutions is called energy-variational solution. The term dissipative solutions is also used for other solution concepts. On the one hand, it is used in
Fei 14
in the context of the Navier-Stokes-Fourier system, where it basically denotes a weak solution. On the other hand the term dissipative solution is also used for different measure-valued solution frameworks, see for instance
BFH 20
;
BT 23
. These concepts are different from the dissipative solutions in
Lio 96
and we rather refer with this term to the original definition by Lions.

For the singular potential

W\in C([-1,1])\cap C^{3}(-1,1)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\varphi\to-1}W^{\prime}(\varphi)=-\infty,\,\lim\limits_{\varphi\to 1}W^{\prime}(\varphi)=\infty,\,W^{\prime}(0)=0,\,W^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)\geq-\kappa\mbox{ for some }\kappa>0.

Remark 2.6
.

(1) As an example for such a singular potential, one can consider

\displaystyle W(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\left((1+\varphi)\ln(1+\varphi)+(1-\varphi)\ln(1-\varphi)\right)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\varphi^{2},\,\varphi\in[-1,1],

for fixed

(2) Since we will show that a solution

Energy functionals.
We define the kinetic energy, the elastic energy and the phase-field energy as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{kin}}:L^{\infty}(\Omega)\times L_{\mathrm{div}}^{2}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty],\,(\varphi,v)\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\rho(\varphi)\frac{\absolutevalue{v}^{2}}{2}\differential{x},

(2.10a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{el}}:L_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{2}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty],\,S\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\frac{\absolutevalue{S}^{2}}{2}\differential{x},

(2.10b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf}}:H^{1}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty],\,\varphi\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon\frac{\absolutevalue{\nabla\varphi}^{2}}{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}W(\varphi)\differential{x}.

(2.10c)

Then, the total energy of this system is given by

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tot}}(v,S,\varphi):=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{kin}}(\varphi,v)+\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{el}}(S)+\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf}}(\varphi).

(2.10d)

Dissipation functionals:
We define the viscous Stokes-type dissipation, the quadratic stress diffusion, and the quadratic diffusion of the chemical potential according to the Cahn–Hilliard equation as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{s}}:L^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty],\,(\varphi,v)\mapsto\int_{\Omega}2\nu(\varphi)\absolutevalue{\sym{\nabla v}}^{2}\differential{x},

(2.11a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ch}}:H^{1}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty],\,\mu\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\absolutevalue{\nabla\mu}^{2}\differential{x},

(2.11b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sd},\gamma}:H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty],\,S\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\gamma\absolutevalue{\nabla S}^{2}\differential{x}.

(2.11c)

Then, the sum of Stokes-type dissipation and Cahn–Hilliard dissipation and the total dissipation potential are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{chs}}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}_{s}(\varphi,v)+\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ch}}(\mu),

(2.11d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tot}}(v,S,\varphi,\mu)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{chs}}(v,\varphi,\mu)+\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sd},\gamma}(S)+\mathcal{P}(\varphi;S).

(2.11e)

Note that (
2.11e

3
Dissipative solutions

In this section, we introduce the notion of dissipative solutions for
\gamma\geq 0
and investigate their relation to the weak solutions from Definition
2.7
in the case
\gamma>0
.

3.1
General concept of dissipative solutions

The notion of dissipative solutions is based on a relative energy inequality. In order to better explain the concept, we follow the general approach proposed in
ALR 24
. For this, we consider two reflexive Banach spaces
\mathbb{V}
and
\mathbb{Y}
with dual spaces
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{Y}^{\prime}
such that
\mathbb{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{V}\subseteq\mathbb{V}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Y}^{\prime}
and a general evolutionary PDE on time interval
(0,T)
of the form

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{U}(t)+A(\boldsymbol{U}(t))=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{Y}^{\prime}\text{ with }\quad\boldsymbol{U}(0)=\boldsymbol{U}_{0}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{V}.\,

(3.1a)

Here,
A\,:\,\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{Y}^{\prime}
denotes a differential operator and
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}
the initial datum. Let
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{V}\to[0,\infty]
and
\Psi:\mathbb{V}\to[0,\infty]
be energy functional which is assumed to be twice Gâteaux differentiable and dissipation functionals associated with (
3.1a
). Furthermore, we introduce a space of test functions
\mathfrak{T}
such that
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)),\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t))\in\mathbb{Y}
for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
and all
\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathfrak{T}
. For any initial value
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}
, a sufficiently regular solution
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{V}
with the property
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{U}(t))\in\mathbb{Y}
for all
t\in[0,T]
of (
3.1a
) formally fulfills the energy-dissipation mechanism

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{U})\Big|_{0}^{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\Psi(\boldsymbol{U})\differential{\tau}\leq 0

(3.1b)

for all
t\in[0,T]
. This is obtained by testing (
3.1a
) by
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{U}(t))\in\mathbb{Y}
, and it also implies that

\Psi(\boldsymbol{U}):=\langle A(\boldsymbol{U}),\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{U})\rangle_{\mathbb{Y}}.

(3.1c)

Moreover, we introduce a so-called regularity weight

\mathcal{K}:\mathfrak{T}\to[0,\infty]\text{ with }\mathcal{K}(0)=0,

(3.1d)

which is to be chosen such that both sides of the relative energy inequality remain finite.

In addition, we define the system operator
\mathcal{A}
directly following (
3.1a
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:\mathfrak{T}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{V}^{\prime},\,\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}):=\partial_{t}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}+A(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}).

(3.1e)

We refer to
ALR 24
for the full list of assumptions. A major assumption in
ALR 24
is that the energy
\mathcal{E}
is convex, which gives the positivity of the first-order Taylor expansion of the energy functional. Following the assertions in
(
ALR 24
, Prop. 3.6)
, we define the relative energy
\mathcal{R}
and the relative dissipation
\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{K})}
as the first-order Taylor expansion of the energy
\mathcal{E}
and the term

\Psi(\boldsymbol{U})-\langle A(\boldsymbol{U}),\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\rangle_{\mathbb{Y}}+\mathcal{K}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{U})
given as

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{U}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}):=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{U})-\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})-\langle\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}),\boldsymbol{U}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\rangle_{{\mathbb{V}}}

(3.1f)

as well as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{K})}(\boldsymbol{U}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})

\displaystyle:=\Psi(\boldsymbol{U})-\langle A(\boldsymbol{U}),\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\rangle_{{\mathbb{Y}}}-\langle A(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}),\mathrm{D}^{2}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})(\boldsymbol{U}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\rangle_{{\mathbb{Y}}}+\mathcal{K}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{U}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\,,

(3.1g)

where we use (
3.1c
). Therefore, we can formally compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{U}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\Big|_{0}^{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\Psi(\boldsymbol{U})\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\leq-\int_{0}^{t}\langle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{U},\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\rangle_{\mathbb{Y}}+\langle\boldsymbol{U}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}},\mathrm{D}^{2}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\partial_{t}\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\rangle_{\mathbb{Y}}\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle A(\boldsymbol{U}),\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\rangle_{\mathbb{Y}}+\langle\boldsymbol{U}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}},\mathrm{D}^{2}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})A(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\rangle_{\mathbb{Y}}\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mathrm{D}^{2}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})(\boldsymbol{U}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}),\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\rangle_{\mathbb{V}}\differential{\tau}

By rearranging terms on both sides and using (
3.1e
) and (
3.1g
), we infer

\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{U}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\Big|_{0}^{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{K})}(\boldsymbol{U}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})+\langle\mathrm{D}^{2}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})(\boldsymbol{U}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}),\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\rangle_{\mathbb{V}}\differential{\tau}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{U}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\differential{\tau}\,.

(3.1h)

Applying Gronwall’s inequality provides the relative energy inequality in this general setting as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{U}(t)|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{\exp}{\left(\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\differential{\tau}\right)}\left[\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{K})}(\boldsymbol{U}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})+\langle\mathrm{D}^{2}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})(\boldsymbol{U}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}),\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\rangle_{\mathbb{V}}\right]\differential{s}

(3.1i)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{U}_{0}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(0))\mathrm{\exp}{\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}})\differential{s}\right)}

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
and smooth test functions
\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathfrak{T}
.

3.2
Dissipative solutions for system (
2.23
)

A key ingredient for the notion of dissipative solutions in
ALR 24
is the convexity of the system’s energy
\mathcal{E}
. This means for our system (
2.23
) that the total energy should be convex. But note that the kinetic energy
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{kin}}
, from (
2.10
) as a function of
(\varphi,v)
is not convex. In order to overcome the non-convexity in the kinetic energy
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{kin}}
, we use transformation of variables. Expressing the energy in terms of the mass density
\rho
and the momentum
p
with
p=\rho v
instead of mass density
\rho
and velocity
v
makes the kinetic energy convex, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho,p):=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho,p)\mathrm{d}x\text{ with }\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho,p):=\begin{cases}\frac{p^{2}}{2\rho}&\text{ if }\rho>0,\\
0&\mbox{ if }\rho=0\text{ and }p=0,\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

(3.2)

Accordingly, we also denote the the total energy that depends on the momentum as a variable by
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{tot},\kappa}
.
Using the general definitions of the relative energy (
3.1f
), we find the following expression for the relative kinetic energy

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{kin}}(\rho,p|\tilde{\rho},\tilde{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{p^{2}}{\rho}-\frac{\tilde{p}^{2}}{\tilde{\rho}}-\frac{2\tilde{p}}{\tilde{\rho}}\left(p-\tilde{p}\right)+\frac{\tilde{p}^{2}}{\tilde{\rho}^{2}}\left(\rho-\tilde{\rho}\right)\differential{x}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\rho\absolutevalue{\frac{p}{\rho}-\frac{\tilde{p}}{\tilde{\rho}}}^{2}\differential{x}

(3.3)

\displaystyle+\langle 2\nu(\varphi_{k})(\nabla v_{k+1})_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}},(\nabla\Phi)_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle\mbox{div}(\eta(\varphi_{k})S_{k+1}),\Phi\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\langle(\mbox{div}(J_{k+1})-\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{h}-v_{k+1}\cdot\nabla\rho_{k})\frac{v_{k+1}}{2},\Phi\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\langle J_{k+1}\cdot\nabla v_{k+1}-\mu_{k+1}\nabla\varphi_{k},\Phi\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle f_{k+1},\Phi\rangle_{H^{1}}

for all
\Phi\in C_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

2. The weak formulation of the discrete evolution law for the stress:

\displaystyle\langle\frac{S_{k+1}-S_{k}}{h}+(v_{k+1}\cdot\nabla S_{k+1}),\Psi\rangle_{L^{2}}

(4.2d)

\displaystyle+\langle(S_{k+1}(\nabla v_{k+1})_{\mbox{\tiny{skw}}}-(\nabla v_{k+1})_{\mbox{\tiny{skw}}}S_{k+1}),\Psi\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\langle\xi_{k+1}^{k},\Psi\rangle_{H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}}+\langle\gamma\nabla S_{k+1},\nabla\Psi\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle\eta(\varphi_{k})(\nabla v_{k+1})_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}},\Psi\rangle_{L^{2}}

for all
\Psi\in C_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
, and here
\xi_{k+1}^{k}\in\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\varphi_{k};S_{k+1})\subseteq(H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}
.

3. The discrete evolution law for the phase-field variable:

\frac{\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k}}{h}+v_{k+1}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{k}=\Delta\mu_{k+1},

(4.2e)

as well as

\mu_{k+1}+\kappa\frac{\varphi_{k+1}+\varphi_{k}}{2}=-\Delta\varphi_{k+1}+W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1}),

(4.2f)

almost everywhere in
\Omega
.

Remark 4.1
.

(1) Integrating (
4.2e
) in space and performing an integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k+1}\differential{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}\differential{x}+h\int_{\Omega}-v_{k+1}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{k}+\Delta\mu_{k+1}\differential{x}=\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}\differential{x},

(4.3)

where we made use of the Neumann boundary condition (
2.23i
) and the fact that
v_{k+1}
is divergence-free. Moreover, this equality implies that
\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}\differential{x}=\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{0}\differential{x}
is a constant, i.e., the total volume conserved.

(2) By multiplying (
4.2e
) with
-\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1})
, we obtain that

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}}{h}-v_{k+1}\nabla\rho_{k}=\mbox{div}(J_{k+1}).

(4.4)

Notice that
\mbox{div}(v_{k+1}\otimes J_{k+1})=(\mbox{div}(J_{k+1}))v_{k+1}+J_{k+1}\cdot\nabla v_{k+1}
. Inserting (
4.4
) into (
4.2c
) results in

\displaystyle\langle\frac{\rho_{k+1}v_{k+1}-\rho_{k}v_{k}}{h}+\mbox{div}(\rho_{k}v_{k+1}\otimes v_{k+1}),\Phi\rangle_{L^{2}}

(4.5)

\displaystyle+\langle 2\nu(\varphi_{k})(\nabla v_{k+1})_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}},(\nabla\Phi)_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle\mbox{div}(\eta(\varphi_{k})S_{k+1}),\Phi\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\langle\mbox{div}(v_{k+1}\otimes J_{k+1}),\Phi\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle f_{k+1},\Phi\rangle_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mu_{k+1}\nabla\varphi_{k},\Phi\rangle_{L^{2}},

which is the direct weak formulation of the momentum balance (
2.23a
) with time discretization.

Before we prove the existence of solutions for the time discrete problem (
4.2
), let us first deduce an estimate for the terms in the Cahn–Hilliard part. This inequality uses a similar method as in the proof of
(
ADG 13
, Lemma 4.2)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that
\varphi_{k+1}\in\mbox{dom}(\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf},\kappa})
and
\mu_{k+1}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
are solutions to (
4.2f
) for given
\varphi_{k}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
satisfying
\absolutevalue{\varphi_{k}}\leq 1
in
\Omega
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\Omega}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k+1}\differential{x}=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\Omega}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}\differential{x}\in(-1,1).

Then there exists a positive constant
C
depending on
\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}\differential{x}
, such that

\displaystyle\lVert W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})\rVert_{L^{2}}+\absolutevalue{\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k+1} \dd{x}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\lVert\nabla\mu_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert\nabla\varphi_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert\nabla\varphi_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+1\right)

(4.6)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf},\kappa}(\varphi_{k+1})\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\lVert\mu_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+1\right)

(4.7)

Proof.

Recall from (
2.3
) that
u_{\Omega}
denotes the mean value of a function
u
in
\Omega
. Hence, for
\varphi_{k+1}
, we write
\varphi_{k+1,\Omega}
for the mean value. First, we test (
4.2f
) with
\zeta=(\varphi_{k+1}-{\varphi}_{k+1,\Omega})
to obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k+1}(\varphi_{k+1}-{\varphi}_{k+1,\Omega})\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\kappa\frac{\varphi_{k+1}+\varphi_{k}}{2}(\varphi_{k+1}-{\varphi}_{k+1,\Omega})\differential{x}

\displaystyle=-

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Delta\varphi_{k+1}\cdot(\varphi_{k+1}-{\varphi}_{k+1,\Omega})\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})(\varphi_{k+1}-{\varphi}_{k+1,\Omega})\differential{x}.

(4.8)

Writing
\mu_{0}=\mu_{k+1}-{\mu}_{k+1,\Omega}
, we can see that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k+1}(\varphi_{k+1}-{\varphi}_{k+1,\Omega})\differential{x}=\int_{\Omega}\mu_{0}\varphi_{k+1}\differential{x}.

(4.9)

In view of the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition, notice that

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{ -\int_{\Omega}\Delta\varphi_{k+1}(\varphi_{k+1}-{\varphi}_{k+1,\Omega})\dd{x}}=\absolutevalue{ \int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi_{k+1}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{k+1} \dd{x}}=\lVert\nabla\varphi_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(4.10)

Besides, by the assumption
W_{\kappa}^{\prime}\in C^{1}(-1,1)
and
\lim\limits_{s\to\pm 1}W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(s)=\pm\infty
in (
2.9
), we obtain that

\displaystyle W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})(\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k+1,\Omega})\geq C\absolutevalue{W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})}-\tilde{C}

(4.11)

for all
\varphi_{k+1}\in[-1,1]
, see
(
ADG 13
, Lemma 4.2)
for details. Inserting (
4.9
)-(
4.11
) into (
4.8
) yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\absolutevalue{W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})}\differential{x}\leq

\displaystyle C(\lVert\mu_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert\varphi_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\int_{\Omega}\kappa\frac{\varphi_{k+1}+\varphi_{k}}{2}(\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k+1,\Omega})\differential{x}+\lVert\nabla\varphi_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\lVert\nabla\mu\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert\nabla\varphi_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert\nabla\varphi_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+1\right),

(4.12)

where we used the fact that
\absolutevalue{\varphi_{k+1}},\absolutevalue{\varphi_{k}}\leq 1
and Poinaré’s inequality. Next, by directly integrating (
4.2f
), we can see that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k+1}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\kappa\frac{\varphi_{k+1}+\varphi_{k}}{2}\differential{x}=\int_{\Omega}-\Delta\varphi_{k+1}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})\differential{x}.

This implies

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{ \int_{\Omega}\mu_{k+1} \dd{x}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\absolutevalue{W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\kappa\absolutevalue{\frac{\varphi_{k+1}+\varphi_{k}}{2}}\differential{x}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\lVert\nabla\mu_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert\nabla\varphi_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert\nabla\varphi_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+1\right),

(4.13)

where we used integration by parts, Poincaré’s inequality, and the fact that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\Omega}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k+1}\differential{x}=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\Omega}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}\differential{x}.

Finally, since
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf},\kappa}(\varphi_{k+1})=-\Delta\varphi_{k+1}+W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})=\mu_{k+1}+\frac{\kappa}{2}(\varphi_{k+1}+\varphi_{k})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf},\kappa}(\varphi_{k+1})\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\lVert\mu_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\frac{\kappa}{2}(\lVert\varphi_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert\varphi_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}})\leq C\left(\lVert\mu_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+1\right).

(4.14)

Besides, using (
2.20
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\lVert W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\left(\lVert\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf},\kappa}(\varphi_{k+1})\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert\varphi_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+1\right)\leq C\left(\lVert\mu_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+1\right)^{2},

which implies

\displaystyle\lVert W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\lVert\mu_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+1\right)\leq C\left(\lVert\nabla\mu_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\absolutevalue{\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k+1} \dd{x}}+1\right).

(4.15)

Therefore, combining (
4.15
) with (
4.13
) yields the desired inequality, that is

\displaystyle\lVert W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})\rVert_{L^{2}}+\absolutevalue{\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k+1} \dd{x}}\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\lVert\nabla\mu_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert\nabla\varphi_{k+1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert\nabla\varphi_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+1\right).

∎

Now, we show the existence of solutions to the time discrete problem (

Lemma 4.3

(Existence of solutions to the time discrete problem)
.

For
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
, let
v_{k}\in L_{\mathrm{div}}^{2}(\Omega)
,
S_{k}\in L_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\varphi_{k}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
with
\absolutevalue{\varphi_{k}}\leq 1
and
\rho_{k}=\frac{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}{2}\varphi_{k}+\frac{\rho_{2}+\rho_{1}}{2}
be given, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
be as in (
4.1
), and set

X:=H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}(\Omega)\cap\mbox{dom}(\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\varphi_{k};\cdot))\times\mbox{dom}(\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf},\kappa})\times H_{\vec{n}}^{2}(\Omega).

(4.16)

Then there exists a quadruplet
(v_{k+1},S_{k+1},\varphi_{k+1},\mu_{k+1})\in X
solving (
4.2c
)-(
4.2f
). Moreover, this solution satisfies the energy dissipation estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tot}}(v_{k+1},S_{k+1},\varphi_{k+1})+h\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{chs}}(v_{k+1},\varphi_{k},\mu_{k+1})+h\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sd},\gamma}(S_{k+1})+h\langle\xi_{k+1}^{k},S_{k+1}\rangle_{H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}}

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tot}}(v_{k},S_{k},\varphi_{k})+h\langle f_{k+1},v_{k+1}\rangle_{H^{1}}.

Proof.

Step 1: A priori estimate (
4.17
).

Let
(v_{k+1},S_{k+1},\varphi_{k+1},\mu_{k+1})\in H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}(\Omega)\cap\mbox{dom}(\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\varphi_{k};\cdot))\times\mbox{dom}(\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf},\kappa})\times H_{\vec{n}}^{2}(\Omega)
be a solution to the time discrete problem (
4.2
). Observe that
v_{k+1}\in H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega)
is a suitable test function for (
4.2c
). With this in mind, we calculate that

\int_{\Omega}\left((\mbox{div}(J_{k+1}))\frac{v_{k+1}}{2}+J_{k+1}\cdot\nabla v_{k+1}\right)v_{k+1}\differential{x}=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{div}(J_{k+1}\frac{|v_{k+1}|^{2}}{2})\differential{x}=0,

(4.18)

as well as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\mbox{div}(\rho_{k}v_{k+1}\otimes v_{k+1})-(\nabla\rho_{k}\cdot v_{k+1})\frac{v_{k+1}}{2}\right)v_{k+1}\differential{x}

(4.19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\mbox{div}(\rho_{k}v_{k+1}\otimes v_{k+1})-\mbox{div}(\rho_{k}v_{k+1})\frac{v_{k+1}}{2}\right)v_{k+1}\differential{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\mbox{div}(\rho_{k}v_{k+1})|v_{k+1}|^{2}+\rho_{k}v_{k+1}\cdot\nabla(\frac{|v_{k+1}|^{2}}{2})-\mbox{div}(\rho_{k}v_{k+1})\frac{|v_{k+1}|^{2}}{2}\right)\differential{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mbox{div}(\rho_{k}v_{k+1}\frac{|v_{k+1}|^{2}}{2})\differential{x}=0,

where we used integration-by-parts and the homogeneous boundary condition (
2.23h
). Moreover, notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}(\rho_{k+1}v_{k+1}-\rho_{k}v_{k})v_{k+1}

(4.20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}(\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k})\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{h}\rho_{k}(v_{k+1}-v_{k})v_{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}(\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k})\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{h}\rho_{k}\frac{\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}}^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{h}\rho_{k}\frac{\absolutevalue{v_{k}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{h}\rho_{k}\frac{\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}-v_{k}}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k+1}\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}}^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k}\absolutevalue{v_{k}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{h}\frac{(\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k})\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k}\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}-v_{k}}^{2}}{2}.

Thanks to (
4.18
), (
4.19
) and (
4.20
), by testing (
4.2c
) with
\Phi=v_{k+1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k+1}|v_{k+1}|^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k}|v_{k+1}-v_{k}|^{2}}{2}\differential{x}

(4.21)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}2\nu(\varphi_{k})|(\nabla v_{k+1})_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}}|^{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\eta(\varphi_{k})S_{k+1}:(\nabla v_{k+1})_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}}\differential{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k+1}(\nabla\varphi_{k}\cdot v_{k+1})\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k}|v_{k}|^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\langle f_{k+1},v_{k+1}\rangle_{H^{1}}.

Next, observe that
S_{k+1}\in H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}(\Omega)
is a suitable test function for (
4.2d
). For the first term in (
4.2d
), we calculate that

\frac{1}{h}(S_{k+1}-S_{k}):S_{k+1}=\frac{1}{h}\frac{\absolutevalue{S_{k+1}}^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{h}\frac{\absolutevalue{S_{k}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{h}\frac{\absolutevalue{S_{k+1}-S_{k}}^{2}}{2}.

(4.22)

With the help of (
4.22
) and due to the fact that many terms cancel out, since

is divergence-free and
S
is symmetric, we also obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{|S_{k+1}|^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{|S_{k+1}-S_{k}|}{2}\differential{x}+\langle\xi_{k+1}^{k}:S_{k+1}\rangle_{H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}}+\int_{\Omega}\gamma|\nabla S_{k+1}|^{2}\differential{x}

(4.23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\eta(\varphi_{k})(\nabla v_{k+1})_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}}:S_{k+1}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{|S_{k}|^{2}}{2}\differential{x}.

Further, observe that
\mu_{k+1}\in H_{\vec{n}}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\frac{1}{h}(\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k})\in H^{2}(\Omega)
are suitable test functions for (
4.2e
) and (
4.2f
), respectively. With this test, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}(\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k})\mu_{k+1}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}(v_{k+1}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{k})\mu_{k+1}\differential{x}=-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mu_{k+1}|^{2}\differential{x},

(4.24)

as well as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\nabla\varphi_{k+1}\cdot(\nabla\varphi_{k+1}-\nabla\varphi_{k})\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}W_{0}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})\frac{\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k}}{h}\differential{x}

(4.25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k+1}\frac{\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k}}{h}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\kappa\frac{\varphi_{k+1}^{2}-\varphi_{k}^{2}}{2h}\differential{x}.

Now summing up (
4.21
)-(
4.25

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k}\absolutevalue{v_{k}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\absolutevalue{S_{k}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\langle f_{k+1},v_{k+1}\rangle_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k+1}\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k}\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}-v_{k}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\absolutevalue{S_{k+1}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\absolutevalue{S_{k+1}-S_{k}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}2\nu(\varphi_{k})\absolutevalue{\sym{\nabla v_{k+1}}}^{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\gamma\absolutevalue{\nabla S_{k+1}}^{2}\differential{x}+\langle\xi_{k+1}^{k},S_{k+1}\rangle_{H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}}+\int_{\Omega}\absolutevalue{\nabla\mu_{k+1}}^{2}\differential{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})\frac{\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k}}{h}\differential{x}-\int_{\Omega}\kappa\frac{\varphi_{k+1}^{2}-\varphi_{k}^{2}}{2h}\differential{x}+\frac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi_{k+1}\cdot(\nabla\varphi_{k+1}-\nabla\varphi_{k})\differential{x}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k+1}\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\rho_{k}\absolutevalue{v_{k+1}-v_{k}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\absolutevalue{S_{k+1}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\frac{\absolutevalue{S_{k+1}-S_{k}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}2\nu(\varphi_{k})\absolutevalue{\sym{\nabla v}}^{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega}\gamma\absolutevalue{\nabla S_{k+1}}^{2}\differential{x}+\langle\xi_{k+1}^{k}:S_{k+1}\rangle_{H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}}+\int_{\Omega}\absolutevalue{\nabla\mu_{k+1}}^{2}\differential{x}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}W_{\kappa}(\varphi_{k+1})-\kappa\frac{\varphi_{k+1}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}-\frac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}W_{\kappa}(\varphi_{k})-\kappa\frac{\varphi_{k}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\absolutevalue{\nabla\varphi_{k+1}-\nabla\varphi_{k}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}+\frac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\absolutevalue{\nabla\varphi_{k+1}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x}-\frac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\absolutevalue{\nabla\varphi_{k}}^{2}}{2}\differential{x},

where we have used the convexity of

, i.e.,

W_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k+1})(\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k})\geq W_{\kappa}(\varphi_{k+1})-W_{\kappa}(\varphi_{k}),

and

\nabla\varphi_{k+1}\cdot(\nabla\varphi_{k+1}-\nabla\varphi_{k})=\frac{\absolutevalue{\nabla\varphi_{k+1}}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\absolutevalue{\nabla\varphi_{k}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\absolutevalue{\nabla\varphi_{k+1}-\nabla\varphi_{k}}^{2}}{2}.

Multiplying both sides by

and rearranging terms to the left-hand side results in (
4.17
).

Step 2: Existence result via Schaefer’s fixed-point theorem
(
Eva 10
, Chapter 9.2.2, Theorem 2)
.

Suppose that

\mathscr{K}_{k}:\tilde{Y}\to\tilde{Y}

is a continuous and compact mapping. Assume further that the set

\left\{u\in\tilde{Y}:u=\lambda\mathscr{K}_{k}(u)\text{ for some }0\leq\lambda\leq 1\right\}

is bounded. Then
\mathscr{K}_{k}
has a fixed point. In order to apply Schaefer’s fixed point theorem, we will determine
\tilde{Y}
and the operator
\mathscr{K}_{k}
based on the discrete weak formulation (

). For this, using

from (

(4.26)

we define operators

as follows:

\mathscr{L}_{k}:X\to Y,\,w:=(v,S,\varphi,\mu)\mapsto\mathscr{L}_{k}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}L_{k}^{v}(v)\\
L_{k}^{s}(S)\\
-\Delta\mu+\int_{\Omega}\mu\differential{x}\\
\varphi+\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf},\kappa}(\varphi)\end{pmatrix}

(4.27)

where
L_{k}^{v}(v)
and
L_{k}^{s}(S)
are defined in the weak sense, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle L_{k}^{v}(v),\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle 2\nu(\varphi_{k})(\nabla v)_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}},(\nabla\Phi)_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle f_{k+1},\Phi\rangle_{H^{1}}

(4.28)

\displaystyle\langle L_{k}^{s}(S),\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\gamma S,\Psi\rangle_{H^{1}}+\langle\xi^{k},\Psi\rangle_{H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}}\text{ with }\xi^{k}\in\partial\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\varphi_{k};S)

(4.29)

for all test functions
\Phi\in H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega)
and
\Psi\in H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}({\Omega})
, while the third and forth entries in(
4.27
) are identified pointwise. We further introduce
\mathscr{F}_{k}
as follows

\mathscr{F}_{k}:X\to Y,\,w:=(v,S,\varphi,\mu)\mapsto\mathscr{F}_{k}(w),

(4.30)

where

From these two definitions, we can see that

is a weak solution to (

)-(

(4.32)

Properties of
\mathscr{L}_{k}
.

Now we want to prove the invertibility of the operator
\mathscr{L}_{k}
. For the first entry, we can derive the invertibility and continuity of the inverse with help of the Lax-–Milgram theorem. To show the invertibility, for all
\tilde{f}\in(H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}
, we want to prove the existence of a unique
v\in H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega)
such that
-\mbox{div}(2\nu(\varphi_{k})(\nabla v)_{\mbox{\tiny{sym}}})-f_{k+1}=\tilde{f}
. Since
f_{k+1}\in H^{-1}(\Omega)\subseteq(H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}
. There holds
\bar{f}:=\tilde{f}+f_{k+1}\in(H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}
. Observing that the operator
-\mbox{div}(2\nu(\varphi_{k})\cdot):H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega)\to(H_{0,\mathrm{div}}^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}
induces a continuous, coercive bilinear form, the Lax–Milgram theorem yields the invertibility and continuity of the inverse. For the second entry, notice that it can be viewed as the sum of a maximal monotone operator and the duality map. Hence, we can conclude the invertibility by Minty–-Browder Theorem see
(
Bar 10
, Theorem 2.2)
. To see the continuity of the inverse, let
F_{n}=-\gamma\Delta S_{n}+\xi_{n}^{k}+\gamma S_{n}
,
F=-\gamma\Delta S+\xi^{k}+\gamma S
and
F_{n}\to F
in
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}
. For all

, observe that

\displaystyle\gamma\norm{S_{n}-S}_{H^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\langle S_{n}-S,S_{n}-S\rangle_{H^{1}}+\langle\xi_{n}^{k}-\xi^{k},S_{n}-S\rangle_{H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}}

\displaystyle=\langle F_{n}-F,S_{n}-S\rangle_{H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1}}\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\norm{F_{n}-F}_{(H_{\mathrm{sym,Tr}}^{1})^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\norm{S_{n}-S}_{H^{1}}^{2}.

By rearranging terms, one can see that the inverse operator is continuous. For the third entry, let us consider the following elliptic equation

\begin{cases}-\Delta u+\int_{\Omega}u\differential{x}=g&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
\vec{n}\cdot\nabla u|_{\partial\Omega}=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where

for any given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and this can be guaranteed by
(

, Chapter 2)
. Moreover, one can also derive that

\displaystyle\lVert\mu\rVert_{H^{2}}\leq C\left(\lVert\mu\rVert_{H^{1}}+\lVert g\rVert_{L^{2}}\right).

(4.34)

This gives the continuity of the inverse operator.
For the last component of
\mathscr{L}_{k}
, notice that
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pf},\kappa}
is a maximal monotone operator. Again, by Minty–-Browder Theorem, we have the invertibility. Moreover, we want to derive the continuity of the inverse operator. To do so, we interpret the inverse operator as a mapping

for arbitrary

. Let

and

be given. Assume

in

, then

This shows that
u_{k}\to u
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Besides, due to (

is bounded in

which implies that
u_{k}\to u
in

Now, let us consider the operator

We first discuss the terms in the first line of

\displaystyle\lVert\mbox{div}(\rho_{k}v\otimes v)\rVert_{L^{\frac{3}{2}}}\leq C_{k}\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Since

\mathscr{F}_{k}
