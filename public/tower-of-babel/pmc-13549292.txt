Corneal Thickness, Curvature and Topographic Indices: Implications for Keratoconus Screening in a South African University Clinic Population
Authors: Pheagane Motsime William Nkoana, Neo Makgatho, Rethabile Ramatsobane Lekgau, Mufhatutshedzwa M Randima, Munei Masiagwala, Moloantoa Morena, Lebogang Shaun Ntsobe, Phuti Germina Matlhadisha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKeratoconus (KC) is a progressive corneal ectatic disorder characterised by thinning and irregular steepening, which may lead to visual impairment if not detected early. Most diagnostic thresholds are derived from non-African populations, limiting their relevance in local settings.AimTo profile corneal thickness, curvature, eccentricity and topographic indices, and assess their implications for keratoconus screening in a South African university clinic population.MethodsA quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted using clinical data from patients attending a university optometry clinic. Mean central corneal thickness (CCT), radius of curvature (r), eccentricity (e) and KC related indices of 300 clinic patients were measured using Oculus Pentacam. Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to analyse the data.ResultsCorneal parameters were generally within normal ranges. The mean CCT was 504.79 ± 33.12 µm. The mean r was 7.83 ± 0.28 mm, indicating relatively flatter corneas compared to some international populations. Mean e-value was 0.47 ± 0.13, suggesting prolate corneal profiles. Keratoconus related indices were largely within normal limits, although they appeared more conservative than corneal thickness in identifying suspected ectasia. Weak to moderate correlations were observed between variables.ConclusionCorneal characteristics were within normal limits but showed subtle population specific variation. A multi-parameter approach integrating thickness, curvature and indices is recommended to improve KC screening and to strengthen region-specific diagnostic thresholds.

Introduction

Corneal curvature k, central corneal thickness (CCT), corneal eccentricity (e-value) and corneal indices are used to determine corneal health.1,2 Corneal curvature (r) determines the cornea’s refractive power, which usually has an average radius of 7.80 mm. In normal corneas, corneal powers, which are inversely proportional to the radius of curvature, range between 40.5 to 46.7 D.3 It may be steeper in males than females.

Corneal thickness is crucial in evaluating the onset and prognosis of sight threatening conditions such as glaucoma and corneal ectasia. It is estimated to be 540 µm.4 Males have thicker corneas than females and CCT decreases with age.5,6 E-value is the rate at which the cornea flattens from the centre to the periphery. The average eccentricity in normal eyes is 0.53 with ranges from 0.4 to 0.6.7 Corneal indices are numerical representations that describe the topography of the cornea, used in detecting corneal ectasia at pre-clinical stages.2 These variables are significant because they describe the exact corneal shape and can help detect the presence of corneal diseases.

Keratoconus (KC) is increasingly recognised as a condition of clinical and public health relevance due to its progressive nature and the impact on visual function and quality of life.8,9 Global prevalence estimates vary widely depending on population and diagnostic criteria, with reported rates ranging from approximately 1.38 per 1000 population to higher estimates in specific regions and high-risk groups.8 A review of KC in Africa suggests a potentially higher burden at 7.9% of the population10 compared to many Western populations. Though the prevalence is higher, there is variability in study design and diagnostic criteria that may bring about significant variation when interpreting the estimates.8,9

Epidemiology of KC in South Africa remains limited and incompletely characterised. These studies have, however, highlighted an increasing number of reported cases, with a tendency for patients to present at more advanced stages of disease.11 This pattern may suggest challenges in early detection and timely diagnosis within routine clinical services.

Current research in South Africa is exploring the factors influencing KC detection and management within the local context. Variability in access to diagnostic resources, difference in practitioner experience and confidence may be influenced particularly in resource constrained settings. These studies indicate that variability in access to diagnostic resources, as well as differences in practitioner experience and confidence, may influence the identification and management of KC, particularly in resource constrained settings.12,13 In addition, broader health system factors, such as availability of specialised equipment and referral pathways, have been noted as potential considerations in the delivery of KC care.14

Further consideration relates to the interpretation of corneal diagnostic parameters. Current clinical thresholds used in screening and diagnosis are largely derived from non-African populations and may not fully reflect normal variation within South African populations. Given the influence of genetic, environmental, and contextual factors on corneal characteristics, the use of these thresholds without local validation may affect diagnostic accuracy, particularly in early or subclinical disease.1,10,14,15 This is compounded by the fact that individual corneal parameters may not consistently distinguish normal corneal health from poor health especially ectasia when considered in isolation, which may contribute to uncertainty in clinical decision making. Therefore, this study aimed to establish normative corneal characteristics and assess the usefulness of corneal thickness, curvature, eccentricity and topographic indices in identifying individuals at risk of KC in a South African university clinic population.

Methods

Study Design

A cross-sectional quantitative study was conducted to characterise corneal parameters and assess their relationships and relevance for KC screening.

Study Setting

The study was conducted at the University of Limpopo Optometry Clinic, which serves both the university community and surrounding populations and functions as a referral centre for specialised optometric services.

Study Population and Sampling

The clinic serves an estimated 3375 patients annually. A total of 300 participants were recruited consecutively as they presented to the clinic between 1 July and 31 October 2024. They were selected consecutively to allow inclusion of all eligible patients attending the clinic during the period of the study.16,17 This reduced the bias in selection of participants and ensured that a representative sample is included. Cochran’s formula for unknown population was used to calculate the minimum sample size at 95% confidence and 5% margin of error. A minimum sample size of 345 was estimated; however, 300 participants which represents 87% of the target sample were achieved. This recruitment was limited by the study period but was considered sufficient descriptive for analysis and estimation of corneal parameters within the study population. Similar studies evaluating corneal characteristics have reported comparable sample sizes.18

Patients aged 18 years and older were included while those with prior diagnosis of corneal ectasia or active corneal pathology that could interfere with measurements were excluded.

Data Collection

Participants underwent a standard clinical screening to exclude ocular conditions that could affect corneal measurements. Corneal parameters were measured using the Oculus Pentacam (Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH), which provides Scheimpflug-based tomography. Parameters including central corneal thickness, corneal curvature (mean, flattest, and steepest radius), corneal eccentricity and topographic indices including Index of Surface Variance (ISV), Index of Vertical Asymmetry, Keratoconus Index (KI), Central Keratoconus Index (CKI), Index of Height Asymmetry (IHA), Index of Height Decentration (IHD), and Minimum Radius of Curvature (Rmin) were recorded. These measurements were used to characterise corneal structure and to identify potential indicators of KC.

Data were captured in Microsoft Excel and analysed using SPSS version 28. Descriptive statistics including mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values were calculated to characterise corneal thickness, curvature, eccentricity, and indices.

Normality was assessed using Kolmogorov Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests with significance at P=0.05. As some variables were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were applied. Relationships between variables were assessed using Spearman rank correlation. The Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to assess inter ocular symmetry, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to evaluate differences by gender.

All eyes were screened for KC but to reduce bias in analysis from inter-eye correlations, findings of the right eye or OD were used as the primary analyses and comparison for age and gender variations. This is a concept commonly applied in ophthalmic research, and it assumes that measurements of the two eyes of an individual are not statistically independent.19–21

Clinical thresholds derived from established KC screening criteria were applied to identify values suggestive of corneal ectasia. The proportion of participants classified as abnormal based on each parameter was calculated to evaluate their usefulness in KC screening.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Limpopo Turfloop Research Ethics Committee (reference number: TREC/1471/2024:UG). Gatekeeper permission to conduct the study was sought from the University of Limpopo Optometry Clinic. All participants granted researchers an informed consent prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any stage without any consequences. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study. No personal identifiers were recorded, and all data were securely stored and accessible only to the research team.

Discussion

The participants were mostly young adults, with a mean age of 21.06 ± 2.57 years which is expected because the study was conducted in a university clinic environment. Most participants were female (62.73%), and all participants were Black Africans. None had previously been diagnosed with corneal ectasia. This profile is important because KC is often reported to begin during adolescence or early adulthood, suggesting that a young population is clinically relevant for identifying early corneal variations.10,15,22

Ocular itchiness, tearing, blurred vision, redness and dry eye sensation were common and hence clinically meaningful due to the evident linkages between KC development and progression with the ocular itchiness and eye rubbing.8 Eye rubbing was also highly associated with KC.15 Although participants in the present study were not diagnosed with KC but rather some identified as suspects, the presence of these symptoms suggests that some may require regular monitoring, especially where itchiness and habitual eye rubbing are present.23

The mean CCT was 504.79 ± 33.12 µm and thinner than numerous findings where values approximately 540 µm were recorded. Various studies found much thicker corneas of 532.8 ± 32.6 μm in an Egyptian population24, 531.8 ± 31.4 µm in Malaysia25, 554.2 ± 39.0 µm in the United States26 and 545.69 ± 36.88 μm in Mexico.27 In contrast, South African studies have reported values closer to those in the present study, including 493.05 ± 33.2 µm28, 519.5 ± 38.6 μm29 and 518.49 ± 33.01 µm.30

Central corneal thickness is commonly used in screening for KC and other corneal ectatic conditions.31 In this study, 30.3% of corneas were classified as KC suspects based on mean CCT alone. This is much higher than the proportion classified as suspicious by other parameters, such as e-value, mean r and KI. This suggests that CCT alone may be insufficient as a standalone indicator of KC in this population. If international cut-off points are applied without local adjustment, many normal patients may be incorrectly suspected of having corneal ectasia. This supports the need for population specific reference values.

Corneal curvature findings were generally within normal expected ranges. The mean r was 7.83 ± 0.28 mm, which is close to the commonly accepted standard value of about 7.80 mm3,7 though flatter than the mean of 7.69 ± 0.28 mm from a global survey study conducted in 10 countries across Asia, Europe, Australia and the USA.32 These findings also compared to be flatter against other studies reported from an Indian-based study which found 7.76 mm with range between: 7.67 to 7.85 mm,33 and a Turkish-based study which found 7.78 ± 0.36 mm.34 The r values observed are within normal limits, their relative flatness compared to other populations highlights the need for context specific diagnostic thresholds. For effective KC screening in this South African university clinic population, a combined approach that incorporates curvature, thickness, and other variables is essential to improve early detection and reduce the risk of missed or late diagnoses.

The mean e was 0.47 ± 0.13 which is slightly lower than the commonly reported normal average of approximately 0.53,7 with normal values often ranging from 0.4 to 0.6. The present value showed more corneal flattening than 0.46 ± 0.18 in a Japanese study35 but had lesser flattening as compared to 0.51 ± 0.21 in a German study.36 This suggests that the corneal shape of the study population may be less prolate when compared with some other populations. Although the mean e-value did not vary significantly with age or gender, its distribution remains clinically useful because abnormal e may indicate early corneal shape changes. In the context of KC, increasing mean e-value is often associated with progressive corneal protrusion and asymmetrical steepening. Therefore, the slightly lower mean e reported in this study may indicate that the baseline corneal shape in this population is relatively less prolate compared to other populations. This has important screening implications, as early KC may not present with markedly elevated e values when assessed against external reference ranges. As such, reliance on standard cut-off values alone may reduce sensitivity for early detection.

The average KC related indices were mostly within normal limits. The mean ISV was 23.33 ± 7.90, mean IVA was 0.20 ± 0.12, mean KI was 1.03 ± 0.03, mean CKI was 1.01 ± 0.01, mean IHD was 0.02 ± 0.04, and mean IHA was 6.34 ± 4.63. These indices are designed to capture subtle irregularities in corneal shape, particularly asymmetry and localised changes that may not be evident through measures such as mean r. According to Doctor et al,1,2 corneal indices are useful as they provide numerical descriptions of corneal shape and assist in detecting early or subclinical KC. In the present study, these indices appeared more conservative than CCT in classifying suspected ectasia.

The proportion of suspected KC based on different parameters showed an important pattern with mean CCT estimating 30.2% of the sample, in contrast 6.7% from mean KI, 1.5% from mean e-value, and 1.2% from mean r. This suggests that mean CCT only may over identify suspected ectasia in this population. In contrast, mean e-value,r and KI may provide better specificity. This does not mean that mean CCT is not clinically useful, but it should not be used alone but rather interpreted together with e-value,r, posterior elevation, pachymetric progression and topographic indices.

The study also found significant differences between right and left eyes for mean CCT, sagittal height, KI and CKI. This finding is clinically relevant because KC is often asymmetric, especially in early stages. However, since known ectasia cases were excluded from the study, the observed differences may represent normal inter-ocular variation rather than disease. The result also highlights the importance of assessing both eyes separately, rather than assuming symmetry.

Age and gender did not show significant variation across mean CCT, r, e-value, or KI in this population. This suggests that these parameters are relatively stable and that common screening thresholds may be applied across groups in this setting. However, age remains clinically important, as KC often develops in younger individuals, requiring careful interpretation and follow up at an individual level. Similarly, although no gender differences were observed, clinical vigilance should be maintained for both males and females. These findings support the use of unified screening criteria while emphasising the importance of individualised assessment.

The correlations between corneal parameters were statistically significant but generally weak. For example, CCT was weakly associated with mean r, e-value and KI. Eccentricity showed a stronger relationship with KI than some other variables. These weak relationships are expected in a population that is largely normal, because corneal parameters may vary within normal biological limits. Stronger relationships would likely be expected in a sample containing confirmed KC cases, where thinning, steepening, asymmetry and irregularity occur together.

The findings suggest that CCT in this population differs from many international reference values, while r and corneal indices remain largely comparable to global standards. The results support the argument that African populations require locally generated reference values to improve diagnostic accuracy. This is especially important for KC, where early diagnosis is essential to prevent progression, visual impairment and later need for advanced interventions such as corneal transplantation.

Strength and Limitations of the Study

This study provides valuable evidence on corneal thickness, curvature, eccentricity and topographic indices in a South African university clinic population, addressing a recognised gap in African‑based data. The use of a multi‑parameter approach provides strength for the study, which enhances the ability to detect early corneal changes relevant to KC screening. The relatively homogenous age profile supports clearer interpretation of findings, while the removal of outliers improves the robustness of the analysis. In addition, comparison with international studies allows for meaningful contextualisation and highlights the need for population specific diagnostic considerations.

The study is limited by its clinic-based design, which may restrict generalisability to the broader South African population, particularly community and rural settings. The relatively narrow age range limits assessment of age-related variation in corneal parameters, while the cross-sectional design does not allow evaluation of disease progression over time. In addition, some early or subclinical KC cases may not have been detected due to the conservative nature of certain topographic indices and the absence of advanced biomechanical assessment. Although the study contributes to local evidence, the continued use of diagnostic thresholds derived from non-African populations remains a limitation, highlighting the need for larger population based and longitudinal studies.

Conclusion

The study shows that mean CCT is relatively thinner in this Black South African clinical population and may be insufficient as a standalone diagnostic indicator for KC. Mean r, e-value and KI appeared to provide more clinically useful information when interpreted together. The findings therefore support a multi‑parameter approach to KC screening and strengthen the need for region specific diagnostic thresholds in South African optometric practice.
