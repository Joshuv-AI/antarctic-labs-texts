Deciphering the role of oleic acid in diabetic retinopathy: an empirical analysis of monounsaturated fatty acids
Authors: Ziyi Wang, Hui Wang, Yuxin Chen, Yang Chen, Xinlv Zhang, Anthony Diwon, Guomiao Zhang, Qichao Sheng, Huiqin Mei, Yixi Xu, Xiaoyu Zhang, Qingyang Mao, Chao Zheng, Guangyun Mao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimsThe existing literature indicates that oleic acid (OA) is the most prevalent monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) in both diet and plasma, known for its beneficial impact on insulin resistance and inflammation. However, its role in diabetic retinopathy (DR) remains unclear. This study aims to elucidate the association between OA and DR and explore its potential in DR detection.MethodsWe conducted a two-center, propensity score-matched case–control study, including 69 type 2 diabetes (T2D) patients with diagnosed DR (cases) and 69 matched T2D individuals without DR (control), in China from August 2017 to June 2018. Multiple logistic regression models analyzed the association between MUFAs and DR. The impact of 7 distinct MUFAs on DR was examined using elastic net regression (ENET), weighted quantile regression (WQS), and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR), focusing on key lipid biomarkers. The diagnostic utility of these biomarkers was assessed by calculating the AUC.ResultsA significant negative correlation was found between MUFAs and DR, with OA identified as pivotal by ENET, WQS, and BKMR. The adjusted OR and 95% CI for DR were 0.25 (0.09, 0.69) for subjects in the 2nd tertile of OA and 0.11 (0.04, 0.30) for the 3rd tertile, compared to the lowest tertile. These results were consistent across subgroup and sensitivity analyses. The AUC (95% CI) for OA alone was 0.72 (0.63, 0.81), increasing to 0.77 (0.69, 0.85) when combined with other covariates.ConclusionsOur findings reveal a robust inverse relationship between plasma OA levels and DR risk, suggesting that OA could serve as a valuable biomarker for identifying type 2 diabetic patients with DR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-024-00874-0.

Introduction

The global aging population is accelerating, leading to an alarming increase in the prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) and its complications. Despite advancements in DM management, the incidence of complications from DM remains very high. Diabetic retinopathy (DR), a prevalent and severe microvascular complication of DM, has emerged as the predominant cause of vision loss among the working-age population [1]. Current estimates indicate that over one-third of diabetic individuals exhibit signs of DR [2], a figure that is projected to rise significantly in the coming decades, escalating from 103 million cases in 2020 to 161 million cases by 2045 [3]. This projected surge poses a considerable challenge to healthcare systems already grappling with resource constraints [4]. Besides, due to the hidden onset of DR, almost all DR Patients are diagnosed in the middle or late stage [5], which not only leads to the decline of clinical efficacy, but also causes a huge waste of limited medical budget. Therefore, it is critical to search for potential biomarkers to prevent and screen for DR.

It has been reported that strict glycemic management cannot completely prevent the progression of DM to DR [6], and the disorder of lipid metabolism may play an important role in the pathogenesis and development of DR [7]. Available evidence suggest that lipids, specifically monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), may exert a significant influence on the pathogenesis of DR [8]. Notably, plasma MUFAs, exemplified by oleic acid (OA), are recognized as indicators of short-term dietary intake and metabolic processes. Existing evidence suggests that elevated plasma MUFAs levels, exemplified by oleic acid (OA), have been correlated to enhanced insulin sensitivity, improved metabolic health, and a reduced risk of diabetes [9].

A post-hoc analysis of the Prevention with Mediterranean diet (PREDIMED) study revealed a protective effect of the Mediterranean diet against DR [10]. Mediterranean diet is a diet rich in olive oil, which is particularly high in OA content. This detection has led to the hypothesis that OA may offer a beneficial impact on DR. However, existing studies on the relationship between OA and DR are limited and have yielded inconsistent results. Although some studies have reported a protective effect of OA against DR [11, 12], some have come to the opposite conclusion that patients with proliferative DR(PDR) show higher levels of OA compared to healthy controls [13]. Heterogeneity between studies may be due to an error prone assessment of MUFA intake using self-reported questionnaires or dietary records. In contrast, cyclic MUFA measures are not affected by recall bias and can objectively represent the combined effects of dietary MUFA intake and biological processes in vivo [14], Moreover, a narrow focus on the isolated effects of OA rather than a comprehensive assessment of MUFA may also contribute to the inconsistent results.

Given the complex metabolic interplay among plasma MUFAs, which may exhibit synergistic or antagonistic effects, it is imperative to investigate the independent influence of OA on DR while controlling for other MUFAs. When multiple exposures are highly correlated, traditional regression methods may fall short in dissecting the complex interactions and nonlinear relationships among multiple MUFAs and their individual impacts on DR. However, advanced analytical techniques, such as weighted quantile regression (WQS) [15] and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) [16], offer a more nuanced approach to efficiently disclose these relationships.

In this study, we have undertaken a comprehensive analysis of the combined effects of 7 MUFAs on DR, and the independent effects of OA, employing a matched case–control study design with propensity score matching (PSM). By quantifying the association between OA and DR, we aim to evaluate OA’s potential as a sensitive and specific biomarker for distinguishing type 2 diabetic patients with DR from those without DR. Our findings may contribute to a deeper understanding of DR’s pathogenesis and inform the development of groundbreaking therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This investigation adopted a two-center case–control study in China. Detailed information about the study design and participant recruitment can be found in our previous publications [17–19]. In brief, the present study comprised 195 patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), of which 83 exhibited DR and 112 did not. Participants, all aged over 35 years, were excluded if they had type 1 diabetes, cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases, hyperkalemia, cancer, infectious diseases, or other chronic systemic conditions. They were recruited from two affiliated hospitals of Wenzhou Medical University and Anhui Medical University. To mitigate potential confounding factors and ensure comparability of our results, we employed PSM. Patients with DR (cases) were matched with those without DR (controls) in a 1:1 ratio, considering age, gender, body mass index (BMI), blood pressure and HbA1c. The final study participants included 69 pairs of cases and controls. A flowchart depicting the study design was presented in the supplementary materials (Fig. S1).

Determination of monounsaturated fatty acid

The determination of plasma MUFAs was carried out by experienced professionals at the central laboratory of Prof. Leader Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Meticulous methods for metabolic analysis could be found in the supplementary materials (Appendix 1). Briefly, plasma samples were promptly dispatched to the laboratory in dry ice packaging and analyzed within 24 h. The plasma samples were then treated with an internal standard, methanol, and methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE), followed by vortex shaking, ice-water ultrasonication, resting, centrifugation, and drying prior to assessment via ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-electrospray mass spectrometry (UPLC-ESI–MS/MS) system. To minimize information bias, all measurement procedures were executed by a technician strictly adhering to Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) and professional quality control measures. The plasma samples from cases and controls were processed randomly, with the technician blinded to samples categories. The plasma components are separated and identified by UPLC-ESI–MS/MS using retention time and internal standards, with results expressed by peak area. The present study identified 7 MUFA, including Myristoleic acid (MA), Palmitoleic acid (POA), Ginkgolic acid (GA), OA, Gadoleic acid (GOA), Erucic acid (EA), and Nervonic acid (NA), facilitating subsequent data analysis.

Definition of type 2 diabetes mellitus and diabetic retinopathy

T2DM was defined as self-reported doctor diagnosis of diabetes, use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic medication, or according to the standard criteria recommended by the WHO. These criteria encompass a venous plasma glucose concentration ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, a fasting plasma glucose concentration > 7.0 mmol/L, or a 2-h blood glucose concentration ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) [20]. DR was diagnosed independently by two experienced ophthalmologists, strictly following clinical diagnostic and imaging guidelines [21]. Further details on DR diagnosis were provided in the Supplementary materials (Appendix 1).

Covariates

We systematically collected extensive personal information through a standardized questionnaire, encompassing age, sex, diabetes duration, smoking and alcohol habits (never, former, or now), insulin treatment history (yes or no), occupation (mainly manual work, mainly mental work or both), the highest educational level (junior high school or below, high school or above), dietary habits (the cooking oil for usual diet is main vegetable oil, main animal oil or basic half), and other demographic variables. This process was carried out by two researchers trained systematically in accordance with SOPs and quality control protocols. Participants also underwent physical measurements for weight, height, and sitting blood pressure, with the latter measured by three systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) readings using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer and averaged for analysis. Concurrently, BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m2). Additionally, routine laboratory indicators such as fasting blood glucose (FPG), HbAlc and others were determined using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Roche, Cobasc311).

Sample size estimation

To ensure that our sample size was statistically robust, we utilized G*Power version 3.1.9.7, a statistical power analysis software proposed by scientists from the University of California, Los Angeles. With type 1 error set at 0.05, an effect size of 0.8, and a ratio of cases vs. controls as 1:1, our sample size was deemed sufficient to achieve a power of 0.8. Additional details were available in the supplementary materials (Figure S2).

Statistical analyses

To minimize biases from missing data, which could affect the integrity of our findings, the covariates with over 25% missing values were excluded in the following analyses and we employed multiple imputation techniques for covariates with less than 25% missing data. Participant demographic and clinical characteristics were described and compared using appropriate statistical measures. Categorical variables were presented as number (N) and percentage (%), while continuous variables with normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and non-normally distributed as median and interquartile range (IQR). Differences between cases and controls were assessed using Student’s t test or Mann Whitney U tests for continuous variables depending on their distributions, and Chi-square tests for categorical variables. Due to the skewed distribution of plasma MUFAs, we normalized them to Z-scores. The Pearson correlation coefficient was utilized to assess correlations between MUFAs.

To investigate the association between MUFAs and DR risk, we employed multivariable logistic regression models, estimating odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each MUFA. We applied the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model to screen for confounders, including diabetes duration and insulin treatment. Two models were developed: model 1 (unadjusted) and model 2 (adjusted for diabetes duration and insulin therapy). Given the PSM, age, sex, BMI, blood pressure, and HbA1c were already matched, hence no further adjustments were made.

In addition, we also performed analyses to verify the individual and combined effects of MUFAs on DR, as well as to identify potential plasma MUFA biomarkers for DR. First, we addressed multicollinearity among MUFAs using elastic net regression (ENET), a penalized regression technique combined the advantages of ridge regression and LASSO regression [22], was employed to identify MUFAs associated with DR. Furthermore, the corresponding β coefficient was also calculated to quantify the relationship between plasma MUFAs and DR. Secondly, weighted quantile regression (WQS) was employed to evaluate the collective impact of the 7 MUFAs using a unified weighted indicator. The WQS can well handle the relationship between different MUFAs and DR and give the weight of different exposures when multiple exposures are highly correlated [23]. Subsequently, The BKMR model was employed to examine the comprehensive and individual impacts of 7 MUFAs on DR. This model fixed other MUFAs at different percentile levels relative to the median and assessed the relative importance of each MUFA on DR. The posterior inclusion probability (PIP) was calculated as a measure of variable importance for each predictor variable. BKMR is a non-parametric Bayesian variable selection framework that combines Bayesian and statistical learning methods, including the ability to visualize the exposure–response (to different cross-sections of the surface) to observe the nonlinear associations between individual MUFAs and DR. In this study, the model was run with 10,000 iterations using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method. It was used to estimate the overall and individua impact of the 7 MUFAs and to assess potential interactions and nonlinear associations between mixed exposures.

Our findings indicated a significant negative correlation between MUFAs and DR, with OA emerging as the most influential factor according to ENET, WQS and BKMR. Concentrations of the screened OA were further determined based on UPLC-ESIMS/MS platform. We quantified the association between OA and DR using multivariable logistic regression model, considering the metabolite as continuous [IQR scaled] and categorical (tertiles) variables. The adjusted OR and 95% CI were calculated to measure this association. To further explore potential nonlinear associations and dose–response relationship between OA and DR, we utilized the Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) regression model for the evaluation, with three nodes set for the model. The Wald test was used to determine the nonlinear trend.

To address the potential impact of other confounding factors on the association between OA and DR, we performed subgroup analyses based on age, sex, BMI, FBG, and hypertension status. Additionally, we also performed sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness and reliability of our findings. First, we excluded covariates with missing data and repeated the analyses to assess the impact of missing data on our results. Second, recognizing the correlation between OA levels and dietary habits, we further adjusted for the types of cooking oils used by the participants. The consumption levels of oil and salt were also included in the model to evaluate the resilience of the OA-DR association when accounting for dietary variations. Finally, to ease any confounding effects, we adjusted for additional variables such as systolic blood pressure, education level, and others. See the supplementary material flow chart for the statistical analysis methods used (Fig. S1).

Statistical significance was determined by a two-tailed p-value ≤ 0.05. All data management and analyses were conducted using Stata 15.0 (Stata Corp LLC) and R (version 4.1.2, R Foundation) language. and the ENET, WQS and BKMR were implemented with the respective "glmnet","gWQS"and "BKMR"R packages.

Ethics

The protocol of this study had been approved by the Eye hospital of Wenzhou Medical University ethics committee (Number: KYK [2017] 46). All participants were informed about the study, participation was voluntary, and all signed written informed consent.

Distribution of MUFAs

Utilizing an ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) system, this study successfully detected 7 distinct types of MUFAs. The distribution profiles of these MUFAs across the study population were illustrated in the supplemental materials (Table S1). Analysis revealed that the levels of all 7 MUFAs were significantly reduced in the case group, comprising T2DM individuals with DR, when compared to those without DR (the control group) (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the Spearman correlation matrix, which was presented in Figure S3 of the supplementary materials, illustrated a strong positive correlation among MUFAs.

Association of OA with DR

Through three distinct statistical methodologies, ENET, WQS and BKMR, we validated that OA had an independent and significant negative association with DR. Building on this validation, we delved deeper into the relationship between OA and DR. To accurately quantify this relationship, we measured the exact concentrations of OA using targeted lipidomics (Fig. S5). Subsequently, we applied quantile logistic regression models to evaluate the correlation. As shown in the Table 2, with per IQR increment in OA levels, there was an average 50% increase in the risk of developing DR (OR: 0.50, 95% CI: 0.30, 0.83). Further exploration of the OA-DR relationship requested repeated analyses on 69 pairs of participants, stratified based on the reference point established by the RCS model at an OA concentration of 60.2 μmol/L. This analysis revealed that participants with OA levels above this threshold exhibited an approximately 79% reduction in the risk of developing DR (OR: 0.21, 95% CI: 0.10, 0.46, p < 0.001) (Fig. S6). These findings were consistent across various subgroup analyses. Additionally, we investigated the performance of OA at various stages of DR and found that the level of OA does not further decline with the progression of the disease (Fig. S7).

In assessing potential confounding factors, none of the variables examined, including age, sex, blood pressure, and BMI, had a significant impact on the association between plasma OA levels and DR risk (P for interaction > 0.05 for all stratified variables) (Fig. S6). Finally, the sensitivity analysis revealed that the correlation between OA and DR within the model, adjusted for diet, blood pressure, and education level, was largely consistent with the results of the primary analysis (Table S5). This robustness across multiple analytical approaches strengthens the evidence supporting the inverse relationship between OA levels and the risk of DR.

Identification of DR using OA

To expand our findings, we further delve into the potential of OA to serve as a diagnostic biomarker for DR. As depicted in Table S6, even when OA was considered as the sole variable in the model, the area under the curve (AUC) from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis reached a respectable 0.72 (95% CI: 0.63, 0.81). This metric indicated that OA had a moderate to high ability to discriminate between type 2 diabetic individuals with and without DR. When the model was adjusted to include other significant confounders, the AUC rose to 0.77 (95% CI: 0.69, 0.85) (Fig. S8; Table S6), suggesting a robust predictive accuracy. The diagnostic performance of this adjusted model was further characterized by its sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), which located at 88.41%, 57.97%, 67.78%, and 83.33%, respectively (Table S6).

Discussion

This study is the first to utilize multiple statistical models and lipidomics data to analyze the combined and individual effects of multiple MUFA on DR. In addition, this case–control study based on PSM also strictly quantified the relationship between OA and DR. Our research revealed the inverse associations of 7 MUFAs on DR, with OA identified as the principal contributor to this collective effect. The consistency of our findings, validated through diverse statistical approaches including ENET, WQS, and BKMR. This association was detected to be stable across various populations. Moreover, OA might also can serve as an ideal biomarker for DR identification though its alteration was not significantly observed among different stages of DR, We proposed an optimal cutoff value of OA concentration at 60.2 μmol/L, If the OA level in diabetic patients is less than this threshold, the risk of developing DR Increases dramatically.

MUFA have long been reported to have protective effects on metabolism and cardiovascular health [24, 25]. Our research has, for the first time, indicated a potential link between higher MUFA levels and a reduced risk of DR. Accumulating evidence suggests that MUFA may play a beneficial role in DR, including improving lipid metabolism, reducing oxidative stress levels, and enhancing the anti-inflammatory ability of retinal cells [26–28]. In the association between a single MUFA and DR, MA, POA, OA, GOA, and NA were found to be significantly negatively correlated with the risk of DR, while after considering the role of other MUFAs, only OA and DR Were found to be significantly correlated. As shown by the exposure–response function between individual MUFAs and DR estimated by the BKMR model (Fig. 4B), although POA has been proposed to be associated with increased insulin sensitivity and lower diabetes incidence, no significant association between POA and DR Was found in this study after accounting for the effects of other MUFAs. Of particular note, through validation of various statistical methods, we have identified OA as playing a pivotal role in the negative correlation between different MUFAs and DR.

To date, few studies have explored the effects of multiple metabolites on DR. The role of OA as a single MUFA has been previously noted. For example, Minerva Granado-Casas et al. reported lower OA levels in diabetic patients with DR compared to those without DR [29]. A dietary survey-based case–control study also revealed an inverse relationship between OA intake and DR presence, with adjusted OR and 95% CI of 0.48 (0.23, 0.97) and 0.37 (0.16, 0.85) for moderate and high OA intake, respectively [12]. These findings are consistent with the present study’s conclusions. However, a recent study from Japan finds no link between dietary fatty acids DR prevalence [30]. This inconsistency stems not only from differences in study design, population characteristics, types of biological samples, and methods of exposure assessment but also from the failure to account for multiple homologous metabolites and the sole focus on the effects of a single metabolite. The complex interplay and metabolic effects of various fatty acids could also account for these discrepancies, highlighting the need to investigate the combined and independent effects of MUFAs, particularly OA, on DR. Traditional statistical analysis methods struggle to capture such complex relationships. Therefore, this study opted to employ multiple statistical approaches, including WQS and BKMR, which are more effective in accurately reflecting the combined and individual impacts of MUFAs on DR.

The Mediterranean diet, rich in olive oil, has been shown to exert beneficial effects in DR prevention [31]. While the health benefits of olive oil are often attributed to its minor components such as polyphenols and tocopherols [32–34], less emphasis has been placed on OA, the oil’s principal constituent (70%-80%) [35]. OA, a prominent monounsaturated omega-9 fatty acid, is abundant in the human diet and possesses numerous health benefits [36]. It is synthesized endogenously from stearic acid by stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) [37, 38] and has been implicated in beneficial roles in oxidative stress, inflammation, insulin resistance, blood pressure, endothelial function, and cholesterol metabolism [39–42]. Suzuki et al. have reported that OA enhances insulin secretion by modulating the metabolic response of pancreatic β-cells to glucose [40]. Simultaneously, OA’s anti-inflammatory properties of OA and protective impact on the retina have also been documented [43, 44].

OA has been shown to alleviate retinal inflammation, a condition characterized by increased inflammatory mediators triggered by high blood sugar levels in DR. This chronic, low-grade inflammation poses a significant threat to vision [33, 45]. By modulating the balance of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, OA plays a crucial role in managing the inflammatory response in the retina [28]. At the same time, OA also activates Silent Information Regulator 1 (SIRT1), a NAD+-dependent deacetylase that prevents inflammation and oxidative stress. Furthermore, Oleoylethanolamide (OEA), derived from OA, acts as an endogenous ligand for peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) nuclear receptor [46], possessing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects and regulating dietary fat intake and energy homeostasis [39]. OA also can reduce the levels of neutrophils and macrophages in inflammatory conditions [47].Moreover, OA preserves microvascular integrity and inhibits choroidal neovascularization (CNV), a major cause of vision loss in age-related macular degeneration [48]. Studies have shown increased mRNA expression levels of TLR4 and MCP-1 in aged, inflamed retinas [49]. However, OA-rich triglycerides (TRL) can downregulate TLR4 gene transcription and decrease MCP-1 mRNA levels in ARPE-19 cells, reducing vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression and secretion [28].

Our study introduces several novel aspects and strengths. Firstly, it is a two-center case–control study involving participants from endocrinology departments of two major hospitals in Anhui and Zhejiang provinces, China, serving a population of 150 million and ensuring representative sampling. Secondly, the use of PSM during study design balances the cases and controls in terms of age, gender, BMI, and other factors, minimizing confounding influences and bolstering result credibility. Thirdly, our approach to assessing joint and independent effects enabled us to explore the impact of MUFAs on DR using novel statistical models (i.e., ENET, WQS and BKMR) while identifying key contributors. Lastly, plasma OA levels were determined using UPLC-MS/MS system, providing accurate measurements rather than relying on self-reported dietary data. Despite these strengths, our study also has limitations. The case–control design limits causal inference between OA and DR. Future large-scale, longitudinal studies are needed to confirm our findings. The relatively small sample size might affect the results to a certain extent. However, it does not necessarily means that our sample size is insufficient. In fact, the sample size of this study in exploring the association between OA and DR is adequate to meet the statistical requirements (Power > 0.8), ensuring stable and reliable research outcomes. Lastly, dietary data collected via questionnaires could introduce self-reported bias. Future efforts will seek more objective and precise data collection methods to enhance study accuracy and reliability.

Conclusions

In summary, this study rigorously examined the association between plasma OA levels and DR Through a variety of statistical methods, detecting that higher OA levels were associated with a lower risk of DR And demonstrating its potential as a diagnostic biomarker for DR In patients with T2DM. Its inclusion in diagnostic models can significantly contribute to the early detection and subsequent management of this vision-threatening diabetic complication, and we establish an optimal critical OA concentration threshold (60.2 μmol/L) to provide a realistic target for clinical screening and dietary recommendations. Our findings may be helpful for the secondary prevention of DR in clinical practice and provide new insights into the mechanisms of DR as well as the possibility of new treatment target development.
