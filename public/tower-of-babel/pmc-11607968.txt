Evaluation of residual carious dentin detection methods after cavity preparation: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Hadiza Mohammed Abba, Paul Ikhodaro Idon, Christopher Ikeokwu Udoye, Oluwafeyisayo Francis Ikusika
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe treatment of caries in dentine poses the challenge of balancing adequate caries removal to prevent residual caries and excessive sound dentine removal. No diagnostic tool is available to define the caries removal limit clinically. This study aimed to determine the efficiency of visual-tactile, caries detector dye (CDD), and laser fluorescence (LF) device methods for diagnosing residual caries after cavity preparation.MethodThe study was conducted as a three-arm, double-blinded, parallel-group randomized trial at the Restorative Dentistryclinics of a teaching hospital in northeastern Nigeria. Individualized carious lesions in dentine were randomly allocated to three groups of residual caries diagnosis methods, A (visual-tactile), B (CDD), and C (LF). All carious lesions had caries excavation and cavity preparation using the visual-tactile method. Main outcome measures were based on the assessment of residual caries of prepared cavities in groups B and C with CDD and LF (DIAGNOdent Pen) respectively, and bacterial culture growth of dentine samples collected from all (A, B, and C) the preparedcavities.DesignThree-arm, double-blinded, parallel group randomized trial. A centralized randomization, into three groups A, B, and C, used opaque envelopes containing names of diagnostic methods allocated to teeth in the trail.SettingRestorative Dentistry clinics of a teaching hospital in northeastern Nigeria.ParticipantsNinety patients with dental caries in dentine. All 90 patients met the requirements for the study.InterventionCarious lesions were excavated, and cavity preparation was performed via the visual-tactile method. Prepared cavities in groups B and C were assessed for residual caries with CDD and LF (DIAGNOdent Pen), respectively, by a blinded independent investigator. Dentine samples were obtained from all the prepared cavities (A, B, and C) and subjected to bacterial culture. Teeth were restored and follow-up was not required.Main outcome measuresThe presence or absence of residual caries staining by CDD and fluorescence by DIAGNOdent in prepared cavities of groups B and C, respectively; the presence or absence of bacterial culture growth for all three groups.ResultsNinety participants (mean age 31.6 ± 7.480), each with one tooth with a deep carious lesion were randomized. The visual-tactile method had a specificity of 100%, CDD had 100% sensitivity and 92.9% specificity, and DIAGNOdent had 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity when measured against bacterial cultures. The positive predictive value for CDD (50%) was half that for DIAGNOdent (100%). The negative predictive value was the lowest (90%) for the visual-tactile method. The accuracy was highest (100%) for DIAGNOdent. There was perfect agreement between the DIAGNOdent and bacteriological tests (kappa = 1.00), whereas the CDD had an above-random level of agreement (kappa = 0.63).ConclusionsAll three residual caries evaluation methods were efficient, independently, in detecting residual caries in prepared cavities. Introduction

Dental caries (DC) remains one of the most common diseases, despite concerted efforts directed at its prevention [1, 2]. While DC is preventable, a combination of factors leaves many individuals with cases of active carious lesions. This is especially true in developing countries such as Nigeria, where the prevalence of untreated caries in the Northeast is as high as 81.6% [2–4].

DC is strongly associated with the activity of Streptococcus mutans, an oral symbiont. These bacteria metabolize dietary sugars and produce acidic byproducts that contribute to the loss of hard tooth tissue, which is often associated with caries [5]. The acid produced demineralizes enamel, increasing enamel porosity and creating a pathway through which the bacteria may gain access to deeper dental tissues. Histologically, carious lesions that have extended into dentine with cavitation comprise of advancing zones of sclerotic dentine and demineralization. These zones are overlaid by zones of bacterial penetration and destruction. The two advancing zones contain only acidic byproducts and are devoid of bacteria, whereas the two superficial zones have dentinal tubules laden with viable and culturable bacteria [6, 7]. The excavation of carious lesions to facilitate the restoration of the tooth has become less extensive on account of this observation. The aim of recent excavation has been to remove the caries-infected dentine (CID) in the first two layers to eliminate potential reservoirs of infection and preserve the caries-affected dentin (CAD) of the deeper layers. This approach is rationalized by the need to preserve the integrity of the pulp and preserve the structural integrity of the tooth.

While preservation of CAD may prevent pulpal exposure, some CID may inadvertently be left unremoved after excavation. CID inadvertently left within a cavity after caries excavation before a restoration is placed is referred to as residual caries [8]. It contains both demineralized but structurally intact dentine as well as infected, denatured dentine [9]. Residual caries is often a cause of restoration failure because they may become a focus for the emergence of recurrent caries. This often necessitates re-entry of such restorations and may lead to such teeth becoming structurally weaker [10]. This will significantly impact the patient’s finances and the dentist’s time. The recognition and complete removal of residual caries thus has significant benefits for ethical patient care. Additionally, residual caries, especially CID, is documented in several studies to have lower bond strength than CAD and normal dentine. This is due to increased smear layer, reduced mineral content, and loss of crystalline structure associated with CID with resultant low dentine hybridization. The outcome is a compromise in mechanical properties of bonded restorations used in cavities with CID [9, 11–14].

The detection of residual caries has traditionally relied on the dentist’s vision and tactile perception of suspicious areas within a prepared cavity. This method requires the dentist to probe discrete areas of relatively dark dentine after cavity preparation with a blunt probe. The relative hardness of demineralized versus CID is used for determining the presence and endpoint of CID removal via the visual tactile method [15], which remains the gold standard [9]. CID is often more porous, with a greater amount of denatured dentine, and feels softer than adjoining areas of normal or CAD [10]. Although still popular, the method is reported to have low sensitivity and reproducibility, with a high possibility of iatrogenic pulp exposure [16].

There are newer methods of residual caries detection that seek to improve outcomes by improving the visual perception of areas of residual caries after cavity preparation. Some of these methods also take advantage of the altered porosity of carious dentine, whereas others are sensitive to bacterial byproducts present in bacteria-infected dentine. Two of these methods involve the use of caries detector dyes (CDDs) and laser fluorescent systems such as the popular DIAGNOdent® brand. The DIAGNOdent device introduced by KaVo (Biberach, Germany) utilizes laser fluorescence (LF) and uses a diode laser that emits a beam at 655 nanometers. This beam is absorbed by bacterial metabolites in CID, which emits infrared fluorescence. This LF device captures the emitted fluorescence and translates it on a numerical scale from 0 to 99 on its screen [17, 18], reflecting the degree of fluorescence, with greater numbers indicating greater areas of decay [19]. DIAGNOdent was shown to have high intraexaminer reproducibility, with high sensitivity and specificity [20, 21].

Various CDDs have become commercially available with different chemistries claimed to aid in the differentiation of the different layers of carious dentine by staining exclusively the top, irreversibly destroyed carious layer and enabling differentiation from the bottom, intermediately demineralized but remineralizable dentin layer left in the cavity [9, 22]. CDDs indicate areas of demineralization to aid in the correct removal of CID during cavity preparation [23]. However, they are nonspecific protein dyes and cannot sufficiently discriminate CID from less mineralized normal dentine, especially in circumpulpal areas and the amelodentinal junction [22, 24, 25]. Thus, the use of CDD for the diagnosis of residual caries may be associated with excessive removal of healthy tooth tissues and the resulting risk of pulpal exposure [22, 25, 26]. However, these methods are popularly available and have been studied in vitro and in vivo with varying outcomes [26].

Despite the availability of these methods, there are still no widely accepted guidelines or adjuncts for caries removal to determine the endpoint of caries removal. Accurate confirmation of the presence or absence of residual caries via precise methods such as microbiological examination and scanning electron microscopy is not feasible intraoperatively. The diagnostic accuracy of clinical methods can, however, be assessed by precise nonclinical methods to help inform clinical decisions. With the introduction of these methods, the limitations associated with traditional methods, the sequelae of inadequate removal of CID, and the continued debate on a universally accepted gold standard method to determine the endpoint of CID removal to achieve biologically successful caries excavation justifies the need for continued investigation of the available methods. Additionally, in an environment such as ours, where oral health awareness is low, access to oral health is poor, with an increasing trend in caries prevalence [4], and where presentation for treatment is usually late with carious cavitations [27], the need for adequate caries removal when the patient eventually presents. Moreover, studies comparing methods of residual caries detection within Nigerian populations have rarely been reported in the literature. This study therefore aimed to assess the diagnostic accuracy of visual-tactile, methylene blue-based CDD and DIAGNOdent® device methods for residual caries diagnosis after cavity preparation with microbiological confirmation. We specifically sought to determine the sensitivity and specificity, establish positive and negative predictive values, and establish the level of agreement between the tested methods of residual caries detection and confirmatory bacterial culture.

Methods

Trial design

The study double-blinded, three arm (equal ratio allocation) parallel group randomized clinical trial conducted over a six-month period. There were no changes to the trial design after commencement. The reporting of the outcomes of the study followed the recommendations provided in the CONSORT guidelines for reporting parallel group randomized trials [28].

Participants

Adult patients attending restorative dental clinics of University of Maiduguri Teaching Hospital (UMTH), Borno State, Nigeria, were recruited. Inclusion in the trial was extended to individuals who presented with cavitated carious lesions in a molar or premolar. Radiographs of such carious teeth were those with lesions not extending beyond the occlusal two-thirds of the dentine. Individuals presenting with cavitated carious lesions with clinical or radiographic evidence of irreversible pulpitis, pulpal communication of the carious lesion or periapical periodontitis were excluded from the trial. Teeth with advanced periodontal disease, previously restored teeth or unrestorable teeth with cavitated carious lesions were also excluded.

These teeth were individualized, identified with Arabic numerals and randomly assigned to three treatment groups. The three treatment groups were as follows: A, visual-tactile caries excavation; B, visual-tactile + CDD; and C, visual-tactile + LF (DIAGNOdent).

Randomization

All the teeth that met the inclusion criteria in consecutive participants were individualized and numbered in ascending order as they were included in the study. The three methods of excavation were written as A, B and C on separate pieces of paper and placed in 90 opaque envelopes (30 entries for each of the 3 groups) by the principal investigator. These envelopes were placed in a sealed opaque bag and transferred to a separate room where a research assistant shook the bag vigorously and consecutively picked one envelope, opened it and assigned it to a number from 1 to 90 representing the teeth participating in the study. The assistant entered this information into a passworded personal computer. The assistant informed the principal investigator of a participant’s group just before treatment commenced without any prior notice.

Interventions

All participants underwent a routine prophylactic scale and polish by a dental therapist before the commencement of restorative treatment. Only one participant was treated daily.

Group A

The test teeth underwent caries excavation and tooth preparation for restoration with a plastic restorative material (composite) performed under rubber dam isolation. A sterile #4 round diamond bur in a high-speed handpiece under water spray was used to create access into the carious cavities, to remove the bulk of the carious lesion and undermined enamel. A sterile spoon excavator, #17, was used to remove the remaining caries on the walls of the cavities and the floor. The spoon excavator was used to excavate the dentine caries to an endpoint of leathery/firm dentine. The extent of caries removal and tooth preparation was guided by visual observation of cavity walls for areas of dentine discolouration and the absence of softened dentine, as determined with gentle tactile force via a no-23 dental explorer (#23 dental explorer, USA CE). When cavity preparation was completed, the teeth were irrigated with normal saline in a disposable syringe and dried with sterile cotton pellets. A sterile rose-head bur on a slow handpiece was then used to gently generate dentine chips from two random points on the floor of the prepared cavity. These chips were transferred to two sterile swab sticks for subsequent transmission for microbiological tests.

Group B

The teeth in this group underwent caries excavation and cavity preparation in an identical manner to the teeth in group A. After cavity preparation was completed, a CDD (Cario-Finder® (Cario Finder, United Medical Suppliers, Cairo, Egypt)) was applied to the cavity walls with an applicator brush according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cavity walls were then irrigated and dried one minute after dye application. Dentine samples (two samples per tooth) were taken, using a rose head bur mounted on a slow hand piece. The dentine chips were transferred to two sterile swab sticks for microbiological tests.

Group C

The teeth underwent visual-tactile-guided caries excavation and cavity preparation in an identical fashion to all other teeth in the trial. The LF device (DIAGNOdent-pen, LF device, Kavo Dental, Germany) was calibrated to code 82 with the aid of calibration discs. The cavities were assessed with the device following the manufacturer’s instructions and the suggestions of Lennon et al. [24]. The area of maximum fluorescence, the surface with the highest DIAGNOdent reading (numerical value greater than 15) [24], was noted and recorded. Dentine samples were then taken with two sterile rose head burs from the areas of highest florescence for microbiological examination.

Microbiological investigations

Discs of blood agar and chocolate agar were inoculated with dentine-chip samples collected on swab sticks and incubated for 24 h.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

Residual caries detection with CDD in group B and DIAGNOdent in group C following caries removal via the visual tactile method. Presence of staining of the dentine following application of CDD was assessed for teeth in group B and evaluations were converted to numeric scores of 0 when the color of the dentine remained the same (no residual caries) and 1 when the color of the dentine stained blue (residual caries). DIAGNOdent readings from areas of maximum fluorescence for cavities in group C were converted to numeric scores of 0 (negative) when the DIAGNOdent reading was 15 or less and 1 (positive) when the value was greater than 15 [24].

areas of maximum fluorescence with readings greater than 15 on the DIAGNOdent were considered positive for residual caries.

Secondary outcome

Bacterial growth and identification following culture of dentine samples from all cavities in groups A, B and C excavated with the different methods. Formation of translucent mucoid colonies (1 to 2 mm in diameter) with surrounding α-hemolysis on blood agar was taken as evidence of the presence of S. mutans and/or S. pneumonia [26, 27]. An optochin test (using 5 g of optochin) was performed on the cultures to rule out the presence of S. pneumonia. Following the optochin test, the discs were characterized as either positive (1) or negative (0) for S. mutans [30, 31].

Blinding

The principal investigator was blinded to the participants’ allocation, as this was done by the research assistant. The participants received information on the procedure to be carried out but were also blinded to the specific method of caries excavation used for their treatment. All caries excavations and cavity preparations using the visual tactile method were performed by the principal investigator. The principal investigator was blinded to the assessments of the prepared cavities with CDD and DIAGNOdent and collection of dentine samples, which were performed by a single coinvestigator. Dentine samples sent for culture were labelled and identified only by the ascending numbers allocated to the teeth during randomization. Thus, the secondary outcome assessor, the microbiologist, was blinded to the method of excavation or protocol used for the samples collected.

Statistical methods

The sociodemographics of the participants and characteristics of the treated teeth were summarized as frequencies, percentages, and means. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were determined at the 95% confidence interval via contingency tables. Fisher’s exact test was used to test for associations between the culture outcomes of the three groups. Fisher’s exact test was also used to determine the associations between the primary outcome (clinical) and secondary outcomes (laboratory) within each group. The diagnostic accuracy of each caries detection modality and the level of agreement with microbiological testing were recorded as percentages and kappa scores, respectively.

Results

Four hundred and seven patients with DC were assessed for inclusion in the study. Among these patients, 307 patients with 943 carious teeth were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria, whereas 10 patients with 13 carious lesions declined to participate. Ninety individuals, each with a single cavitated carious molar or premolar, were recruited for the trial over a six-month period. These teeth were individualized and randomized (30 per group) into the three intervention groups A, B and C. Follow-up was not required for the trial protocol. There were therefore no losses from any of the groups after randomization. Trial ended after the predetermined sample size was achieved. All carious teeth included were assessed and analysed (Consort diagram, Fig. 1).

Harms

The design of this trial did not include the collection of any data on harms from use of the assessed methods of residual caries diagnosis. Thus, there was no report on anticipated and unexpected adverse events. There were no discontinuations and withdrawals from the study due to any adverse events during caries excavation using the methods of residual caries diagnosis.

Discussion

There are differing opinions about how much dentine should be removed during caries excavation. There are concerns that aggressive removal of all carious tooth structures may jeopardize the pulp and affect the structural integrity of the tooth. Conversely, intentionally leaving carious tissue in a treated tooth is akin to inadequate treatment and planning for treatment failure. Despite this controversy, there is some measure of agreement with respect to the concepts of caries-infected and caries-affected dentinal tissue. In the present study, the CID was entirely removed, while the affected demineralized dentine was left in place to preserve the integrity of the tooth and reduce the probability of iatrogenic pulp exposure.

This study investigated the diagnostic accuracy of two methods (CDD and LF) of residual caries detection against a conventional reference standard (visual tactile) after cavity preparation. Microbiological cultures of dentine samples from the prepared cavities tested with the methods under study were carried out, as true positive and false positive outcomes cannot be determined at the chairside. The need for an objective standard against which the results obtained with each of the caries detection methods necessitated the performance of a microbial culture of retrieved dentine samples. The presence of S. mutans in carious biofilms has been demonstrated by many investigators [32, 33]. Woltjes and coworkers have historically demonstrated the presence of hemolysis on blood agar by these bacteria [34]. Jasmine and coworkers also described the identification of these bacteria using chocolate agar plates [35]. The isolation of S. mutans from dentine is an objective means of assessing cariogenic activity within dentine [36]. While, investigators like Chen and coworkers have suggested that other bacteria, notably from fermented fruits, may have cariogenic activity, they have not argued against the long-held traditional view [37]. The isolation of only S. mutans is still considered adequate by many investigators [38]. This informed our comparison of the tested modalities of residual caries detection to this microbiological control.

In this study, cavities prepared and deemed to be free of infected dentine by the visual tactile method were tested with a CDD and DIAGNOdent Pen. The sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV were used as important metrics for assessing the adequacy and usefulness of the methods under study. All tested modalities were sensitive for the detection of residual carious dentine but to different degrees. Compared with the visual-tactile method, both the CDD and DIAGNOdent methods yielded increased diagnostic accuracy. Among these methods, DIAGNOdent produced more consistent results with microbiological culture, with sensitivity and specificity values of 100%. Lennon et al. [39]. reported high sensitivity (88%) and specificity (70%) values for DIAGNOdent in the detection of residual caries, which, though lower, are similar to the results of the present study. In an in vitro study, Unlu et al. reported a low (50%) specificity but high sensitivity for DIAGNOdent (85%) [40]. The closer similarity of the results of this study to those of Lennon et al. may be due to the use of a similar cut-off point of 15 for DIAGNOdent to diagnose the presence or absence of caries in dentine after cavity preparation [39]. In interpreting the diagnostic value of DIAGNOdent, the high PPV (100%) is desirable, as it implies, as shown in the results, that false positives are minimized. This is because false positive outcomes will be associated with excessive cavity preparation with the antecedent costs of treatment of pulpal exposures and reviews.

CDD yielded similar high specificity results when compared to the findings of Yacizi et al. [41]. The PPV for CDD was half of that obtained for DIAGNOdent, thus with higher false positives and a tendency for excessive dentine removal during cavity preparation. Although the PPV for the visual tactile method could not be obtained, it had a high NPV, which is desirable, as false negatives are minimized, reducing the risk of leaving infected dentine in the prepared cavity. The very wide confidence intervals for the PPVs suggest a lack of reproducibility of these results, but as noted by Hazra [42], this could be due to a relatively small sample size.

A comparison of these methods revealed that the sensitivity was high and similar for both CDD and DIAGNOdent, but the specificity was greater for DIAGNOdent, with a perfect accuracy of 1, greater than that of CDD. Previous studies [39, 40] have reported varying outcomes. Although these previous studies were all in vitro, they were all consistent in reporting greater sensitivities of DIAGNOdent over CDD and/or visual tactile methods. Their reports also showed that CDD had the lowest accuracy, thus suggesting caution in its use, as it may result in excessive or inadequate removal of infected carious dentine [40, 41]. In contrast, although lower than that of DIAGNOdent, the accuracy of CDD was high in this study, with visual tactile data being the lowest. Although it is the lowest, the results show that the visual tactile method has relatively good accuracy in the detection of residual caries. Gimenez and coworkers, in their systematic review and meta-analysis, reported that the visual tactile method had good accuracy [43]. Thus, differences in accurate diagnoses between this method and the methods that increase its accuracy may be expected to be marginal. These small differences may be responsible for our observed wide confidence intervals. However, it may also be inferred that the observed differences are likely to be reproducible from the large confidence intervals. A larger study to test some of the findings of this study may further illuminate the subtle differences in the efficacy of residual caries detection modalities. Although the findings of this study suggest that a combination of an LF device and a visual tactile method may optimize the removal of potential areas of residual carious dentine in the treatment of dentinal carious lesions, the use of the LF in a resource limited setting such as ours may not be generally recommendable. The impact of this finding, if it is taken into clinical practice, especially in this region, would be an increased confidence in the use of the traditional visual tactile method despite the availability of the LF, a newer but more expensive option for diagnosing residual caries in prepared cavities. Aside from the cost that would be incurred in the use of the LF device, the visual tactile method has an advantage over it and the CDD if the extra time needed for their use in daily practice is considered.

The relatively few relevant literatures on the in vivo methods of diagnosing residual caries however limited extensive comparison of these findings. Also, being an in vivo study on teeth that were subsequently restored and retained, it was not possible to confirm the presence or absence of residual dentine caries by histologic examination. Despite this limitation, the agreement between the LF method and culture suggests that LF could be relied on and could be a substitute for microbiological culture of dentine samples. Microbiological testing was used because of the resources available. We are also not unaware of newer methods, such as the use of genetic studies, as demonstrated by Zhang and coworkers [44].

Conclusions

The visual tactile method, CDD and LF (DIAGNOdent pen) are very sensitive for the detection of residual carious dentine within prepared cavities. The LF pen was the most specific of the tested modalities and had the highest level of agreement with bacteriological confirmatory tests. Clinically, the CDD and the LF pen do not have significant advantage over the clinical visual-tactile method. Thus, the visual tactile method can still be recommended for use in our resource-limited environment for use as a residual caries excavation method.
