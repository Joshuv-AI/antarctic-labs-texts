Background

Hemangioma is a benign tumor that originates from blood vessels. Hemangioma most commonly occurs in the head and neck, rarely affects the digestive tract, and often presents without any clinical signs. However, in a small number of cases, hemangioma may cause gastrointestinal bleeding, abdominal pain, and other life-threatening symptoms. Esophageal hemangioma is often misdiagnosed as isolated esophageal varices [1]. The diagnosis of esophageal hemangioma is based on endoscopy, endoscopic ultrasonography, and imaging findings. It is also important to rule out differential diagnoses by checking for superior vena cava blockage, cirrhosis, and other possible causes.

The treatment of esophageal hemangioma has been widely reported internationally and comprises esophagectomy, tumor excision, endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), and endoscopic sclerotherapy [2–7]. Traditional surgical approaches are costly, invasive, and complex. However, with advancements in endoscopic technology, the use of such traditional approaches has gradually decreased. For esophageal hemangiomas with a diameter of less than 2 cm, minimally invasive surgeries, such as ESD or EMR, offer substantial advantages over invasive surgery and result in a low recurrence rate. For hemangiomas larger than 2 cm with a rich blood supply, the limitations of these minimally invasive methods include a restricted surgical field of view, inadequate exposure, and a high risk of massive bleeding. Therefore, many experts treat large hemangioma by endoscopically injecting the lesion with a sclerosing agent; however, this method is typically used to treat tumors with a diameter of less than 3 cm [8]. Although some scholars have documented the use of a ligation device in conjunction with EMR to remove esophageal venous aneurysms [9–11], it remains challenging to diagnose and treat large esophageal hemangioma.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old woman presented with the investigation of isolated esophageal varices detected by gastroscopy during the previous month. The patient was asymptomatic, with no dysphagia, hematemesis, or melena. She had previously been healthy and had no history of chronic liver disease or venous thrombosis. Physical examination demonstrated no clinically significant abnormalities.

The complete blood count, blood biochemical tests, stool examination, occult blood tests, and tumor marker concentrations were all normal. Electronic gastroscopy revealed an isolated varicose vein with a diameter of 1.2 cm on the left wall of the esophagus, located 23 cm to 28 cm from the incisors (Fig. 1A and B). Endoscopic ultrasonography indicated that the wall of the varicose vein was thickened and exhibited honeycomb-like irregularities and slightly hypoechoic changes, with abundant blood flow signals detected by ultrasonic Doppler (Fig. 1C and D). An enhanced CT scan of the chest revealed a 1.3 cm × 1.2 cm × 5 cm venous mass with a honeycomb-like appearance in the upper-middle section of the esophagus. The mass was considered highly likely to be an esophageal hemangioma. No arterial origin was detected. There were no abnormalities in the superior and inferior venae cavae, and no signs of liver cirrhosis (Fig. 2). A multidisciplinary team concluded that the lesion was a large esophageal hemangioma with high-risk, life-threatening upper gastrointestinal bleeding due to prominent vasculature.

The initial plan was to perform endoscopic treatment to prevent gastrointestinal bleeding, with surgical intervention to be considered if the endoscopic results were unsatisfactory. After discussing the options with the patient and her family, the patient underwent endoscopic ligation of the esophageal hemangioma with a COOK ligator used to place five rings (Fig. 3). There was no active bleeding during the procedure, and the patient experienced no discomfort postoperatively. One month after the operation, repeat electronic gastroscopy revealed that the original esophageal hemangioma had substantially reduced in size (Fig. 4A–D), and a second endoscopic band ligation was performed to further prevent bleeding (Fig. 4E–H). Three months later, another electronic gastroscopy showed that the mucosa at the site of the original lesion was flat and smooth, the hemangioma had disappeared, and a white scar had formed (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Esophageal hemangioma originates from the mesoderm of the esophagus and often occurs in the submucosa. The diagnosis of esophageal hemangioma relies on endoscopy, endoscopic ultrasonography, and imaging findings. On gastroscopy, esophageal hemangioma appears as a purplish-blue submucosal mass that is soft and appears very similar to esophageal varices [12]. Ultrasound gastroscopy is helpful in judging the origin, extent, and depth of invasion of the lesion, and provides the basis for endoscopic minimally invasive treatment [8, 13]. On CT, esophageal hemangioma usually appears as a well-defined soft tissue mass in the esophageal wall with the characteristic appearance of venous stones [2]. In the absence of venous stones, it is difficult to diagnose esophageal hemangioma based on imaging. The differential diagnoses are leiomyoma, polyps, fibroma, and gastrointestinal stromal cell tumor. In addition, it is very important to obtain the patient’s medical history, including the presence of superior vena cava blockage and isolated esophageal varices caused by cirrhosis. However, the gold standard for the diagnosis of esophageal hemangioma is histopathological examination. The typical histopathological features of esophageal hemangioma are lobular proliferation of capillaries of different sizes, stromal edema, and abundant dilated capillaries in loose and edematous fibrous tissue.

We report a very rare case of a 5-cm-long large esophageal hemangioma. Our initial endoscopic observation misidentified the lesion as a solitary esophageal variceal mass; however, CT and endoscopic ultrasonography examinations revealed that the lesion was an esophageal hemangioma. Traditional surgeries cause significant trauma. ESD or EMR surgeries are suitable for venous hemangioma with a diameter of less than 2 cm because of their restricted surgical view and inadequate exposure. Considering these factors and the risk of massive gastrointestinal bleeding due to the substantial protrusion of the lesion into the esophageal lumen, our multidisciplinary team opted for endoscopic band ligation, inspired by the endoscopic treatment of esophageal varices. The endoscopic band ligation technique is relatively safe and minimizes the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding caused by esophagectomy, ESD or EMR. Compared with alternative treatment modalities, endoscopic band ligation has several advantages: it is less invasive than esophagectomy, which is associated with high postoperative complications; it has a lower bleeding risk than ESD and EMR, which is crucial for vascular lesions with rich blood supply; and it is simpler to perform than endoscopic sclerotherapy, which has a higher recurrence rate for larger lesions. However, endoscopic band ligation also has limitations, such as the inability to obtain histopathological specimens and the lack of long-term data on recurrence.

A critical limitation of this case report is the absence of histopathological confirmation of the lesion. Although the diagnosis was highly suggestive based on endoscopic, endoscopic ultrasonographic, and CT findings, histopathological examination remains the gold standard for confirming esophageal hemangioma and excluding other differential diagnoses. Biopsies were not performed in this case due to the risk of massive bleeding from the vascular lesion. Additionally, the endoscopic band ligation did not allow for the collection of completely resected specimens for histopathological analysis. This limitation should be acknowledged. The diagnostic certainty of the present case is based on comprehensive imaging and endoscopic findings rather than definitive histopathological proof.

The esophageal hemangioma in the present case disappeared, leaving only a white scar on the esophageal surface. Additionally, there were no complications such as esophageal erosion or stenosis. However, it should be emphasized that this is a single case report, and the safety and efficacy of endoscopic band ligation for large esophageal hemangioma cannot be definitively confirmed based solely on this single case. Further studies with larger case series and long-term follow- up are needed to validate the clinical value of EBL in the treatment of large esophageal hemangioma.

Conclusion

This is a case report describing the successful use of endoscopic band ligation to treat a large esophageal hemangioma. Although it is difficult to obtain complete pathological specimens through ligation, this minimally invasive technique ensures safety and effectiveness in this case and also demonstrates high practicality. A crucial limitation of this study is the lack of histopathological confirmation, which should be noted when interpreting the findings. This method overcomes the drawbacks of existing treatments for esophageal venous aneurysms and may serve as a feasible and promising option for clinical application. Given the relatively short follow-up period in the present report, longer-term surveillance is necessary to monitor for recurrence or complications. Furthermore, due to the limitations of a single case report, further studies with larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up are required to verify its safety and efficacy.
