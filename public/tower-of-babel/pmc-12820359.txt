Investigating the mechanisms of lactulose on gut health and uric acid metabolism in geese via transcriptomics
Authors: Pengfei Ye, Xiaorong He, Xueqi Zhu, Jie Zhu, Xiaoxue Wang, Shiyan Cao, Yu Meng, Jing Li, Lei Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, the escalating incidence of goose gout has inflicted severe economic losses on the goose industry. This research aimed to investigate the impact of lactulose on both intestinal health and uric acid metabolism in geese, as well as to elucidate its possible mechanisms through RNA-seq analysis. A total of 180 one-day-old Yangzhou geese were assigned to three different groups: the control group (CG), which received a standard diet; the high-protein model group (MG), which was provided a diet with 21% crude protein; and the lactulose group (LS), which had a diet containing 21% crude protein along with 0.3% lactulose. The findings indicated that the LS group exhibited significantly reduced serum uric acid levels and an intact ileal mucosal structure when compared to both the CG and MG groups. Through bioinformatics analyses of the transcriptome, including GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, and trend analysis, key pathways and genes closely related to intestinal health and uric acid metabolism were identified. These key findings encompass the biological process of zinc ion import across the plasma membrane, the ABC Transporters pathway, the Wnt signaling pathway, the key uric acid transport gene ABCG2, and genes closely associated with intestinal structural integrity (KLF2, LRP6, TJP1, TJP3, PROM1). Introduction

In recent years, the goose industry has developed rapidly, while the incidence of goose gout has also increased year by year, causing huge economic losses to the industry. Avian gout (urate deposition) is mainly caused by abnormal uric acid metabolism and is often associated with genetic defects, nutritional imbalance, diseases, and environmental factors1. Uric acid is mainly derived from exogenous and endogenous purine mononucleotide catabolic processes and is mainly synthesized in the liver, intestine, and vascular endothelium2. Two-thirds of uric acid is excreted through the kidneys, and one-third is transported from the blood to the intestines for excretion3. The excretion of intestinal uric acid is also affected by the catabolism of intestinal flora4. An imbalance in intestinal uric acid excretion will increase the burden on the kidneys, making intestinal uric acid excretion a new research direction for gout and hyperuricemia5. Fan et al. successfully established a hyperuricemia poultry model by feeding a high-protein diet (HPD)6. Xi et al.7 also showed that compared with 16% and 18% crude protein diets, 22% crude protein diets could lead to intestinal epithelial cell damage and cecal flora imbalance in geese, which in turn led to kidney damage and gout. Therefore, constructing a hyperuricemia model in geese by feeding a high-protein diet has a solid theoretical foundation and prior research support. In actual goose rearing practices, the protein level in feed is typically gradually reduced to prevent early-onset gout in goslings. However, excessively low protein content may impede normal growth and development. This study aims to investigate how to maintain the metabolic health of goslings under appropriate dietary protein levels.

The intestine is not only an important digestive organ responsible for the core functions of food digestion and nutrient absorption but also has important functions in immunity, endocrine, and regulation of intestinal flora8. Additionally, unlike mammals, birds lack arginase, which is necessary for urea synthesis, and therefore need to convert purines derived from protein and nucleic acid metabolism into uric acid for excretion. When the metabolic and excretory load on the kidneys increases, the intestines serve as a significant supplementary pathway for uric acid excretion9. Studies have shown that the normal operation of intestinal flora and intestinal function plays an important role in uric acid metabolism10. The uric acid metabolism process involves a variety of uric acid transport-related proteins. Studies have shown that in the intestine, ABCG2 transporters are expressed on the apical membrane of intestinal epithelial cells and directly secrete uric acid from the blood into the intestinal lumen. Some uric acid can be transformed into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), urea, and other substances through enzymes secreted by intestinal microorganisms, thereby regulating uric acid metabolism11.

Prebiotics are indigestible carbohydrates that selectively stimulate the growth of beneficial gut bacteria, thereby increasing their population. By inhibiting the growth of harmful bacteria, prebiotics help regulate the balance of intestinal flora and promote overall health. For example, natural polysaccharides such as cellulose, hemicellulose, inulin, lactulose, galactooligosaccharides (GOS), and fructooligosaccharides (FOS) play an important role in improving the balance of intestinal flora12. These prebiotics stimulate the immune system and intestinal barrier function by acting as fermentable substrates of the intestinal microbiota to prevent pathogen adhesion13. For example, Wu’s team integrated transcriptomics, metabolomics, and metagenomics and revealed that synbiotics composed of galactooligosaccharides (GOS) and Lactobacillus reuteri promote the biosynthesis of pentadecanoic acid (C15:0) by enriching Acidaminococcus. Transcriptome analysis further showed that C15:0 could inhibit the NF-kB signaling pathway (reduce p65 phosphorylation), down-regulate pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6), and up-regulate the expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin), synergistically alleviating intestinal inflammation and repairing barrier function14. Li et al.15 found that quercetin effectively alleviated HUA in hyperuricemic chickens and changed the structure and function of intestinal microbial communities in chickens. In addition, Li et al. revealed that intestinal transporters such as ABCG2 and GLUT9 are key targets for regulating intestinal substance exchange. They found that mangiferin effectively regulated the intestinal processing of uric acid by significantly up-regulating the expression of intestinal ABCG2 and inhibiting the expression of GLUT916.

Lactulose (LS) is a synthetic disaccharide composed of galactose and fructose17. This prebiotic selectively promotes bifidobacterial and lactic acid bacterial growth in the gut ecosystem, while restricting colonization of detrimental microorganisms18. The study by Bai et al.19 showed that in the study of a colitis mouse model, lactulose significantly enriched probiotics, reduced the number of harmful bacteria, affected the purine metabolic pathway, and reduced serum uric acid levels in mice. Despite lactulose’s established prebiotic status, its regulatory mechanisms governing intestinal health and uric acid metabolism remain underexplored, particularly through multi-omics approaches like transcriptomics. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effect of LS on geese through intestinal morphology and serum uric acid level, and to identify the key candidate genes of lactulose on intestinal health and uric acid metabolism in geese using transcriptome technology, so as to provide a theoretical basis and practical reference for lactulose to improve intestinal health and reduce gout in geese.

Methods

Animals and experimental design

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments). All experimental procedures, including sample collection, were performed in compliance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals (Ministry of Science and Technology, China, revised in July 2013). The protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the College of Animal Science and Technology, Anhui Science and Technology University, Chuzhou, China (Approval No. AHSTU2023018). We obtained 180 healthy one-day-old Yangzhou geese exhibiting similar body weights from Bengbu Huaxin Poultry Co., Ltd. These geese were then randomly distributed across three treatment groups, with each group consisting of four replicate pens stocked at 15 geese per pen. Goslings acclimated for 7 days prior to experimentation. The total duration of the experiment was 42 days. The groups were designed as follows: the control group (CG) was fed a basal diet, the high-protein model group (MG) received feed containing 21% crude protein, and the lactulose group (LS) was administered feed with 21% crude protein supplemented with 0.3% lactulose. The experimental diet’s formulation and nutrient specifications are provided in Table S1. All geese were housed in climate-controlled rooms with regulated temperature and lighting, maintained on a 23-h daily lighting schedule, and received regular immunizations. Throughout the experiment, the geese had ad libitum access to both feed and water. Water was provided in semi-open plastic cylindrical drinkers, while feed was dispensed through automatic feeding troughs. Manure beneath the slatted floors was cleaned daily using an automatic manure belt system.

Sample collection

Blood samples (2 mL) were collected from the wing vein at 42 d of age. Following centrifugation (2,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), serum was separated and stored at − 20 °C pending biochemical assays. After blood collection was completed, the geese were euthanized humanely under electric anesthesia for the subsequent collection of intestinal tissue samples. A segment approximately 2 cm in length was excised from the mid-ileum, rinsed with sterile saline, and rapidly immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) fixative for preservation. These fixed ileal segments were intended for tissue sectioning and morphological observation. Additional ileal segments were transferred to RNase-free tubes, snap-frozen in liquid N₂, and maintained at − 80 °C pending RNA sequencing. One goose per replicate (randomly selected) was slaughtered for sampling, resulting in a total of 4 geese per group (12 samples).

Measurement of Serum uric acid levels and histological analysis of ileal tissues

The concentration of serum uric acid (UA) was determined using a commercial Uric Acid (UA) Assay Kit. The reaction mixture was prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and the microplate was gently shaken to ensure thorough mixing. Following 10 min incubation at 37 °C, absorbance at 510 nm was recorded per well using a microplate reader. Uric acid concentrations were derived from kit-supplied equations. For ileal processing, samples underwent primary fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). After 24 h, fixative was refreshed with new PFA to maintain preservation quality. After complete fixation, the tissues were progressively dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions, cleared in xylene, and subsequently embedded in paraffin wax. The paraffin-embedded ileal tissues were sectioned to a thickness of 5 μm, and the sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) (Biosharp, China). Finally, the stained sections were examined under a microscope, and digital images were captured using a slide scanner. Ileal histomorphometry was performed with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, USA), measuring villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) across selected fields.

RNA extraction, library construction and transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from goose ileum (n = 12) with Triozl reagent (Invitrogen, 15,596–026). RNA purity/concentration were determined via NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometry (Wilmington, DE), while integrity was verified using Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies). Following manufacturer guidelines, Illumina TruSeq mRNA was employed for cDNA library preparation. Final sequencing was performed on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (LC Bio, Hangzhou) to generate raw reads.

Sequencing data quality assessment

High-quality clean reads were obtained by using Cutadapt (v 1.10) and internal Perl scripts to process the original sequencing reads in fastq format to filter out unqualified sequences20. Hisat2 v2.0.5 was employed to build the reference genome index, followed by alignment of clean reads to the reference21. StringTie v1.3.2 was then used to quantify the gene expression level of each sample22. RSEM software was used to calculate the number of fragments per million mapped reads (FPKM) per thousand base transcripts23. The reference genome of Anser cygnoides was obtained and annotated from the GenBank database (GCA_002166845.1).

Bioinformatics analysis

DESeq2 was used to analyze the differentially expressed genes in CG, LS, and MG groups. The three groups were compared in pairs. Differentially expressed genes were screened using the following threshold: at least one group with FPKM > 3, comparison between groups with Fold Change > 1.5, and P value < 0.0524 was considered statistically significant. The ClusterProfiler R software package was used to test the statistical enrichment of DEGs in KEGG pathway25,26 and GO terms. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In this study, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GO and KEGG databases, which included all genes from three groups of samples. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using software GSEA (v4.1.0) and MSigDB. All genes were sorted according to Signal2Noise, and the enrichment score (ES) of each gene set in different groups was calculated. To reduce the effect of gene set size on ES, ES was normalized to obtain NES, and FDR value was obtained by multiple test corrections. In addition, the gene set needed to be tested to obtain a significant P value for each gene set. Conditional screening and mining of the obtained gene set were conducted. These pathways were considered to be significantly enriched when |NES|> 1, NOM.pval < 0.05, FDR.qval < 0.2527. Finally, the above gene sets were visualized by ggplot2 and ggridges software. The STEM software was used to analyze the trend of the differential genes of the three combinations of MG, CG, and LS, and a significantly enriched profile was obtained (P < 0.05). Then, GO and KEGG functional enrichment analysis was performed on the profile with significant differential expression.

RT-qPCR validation of DEGs

Six genes were randomly chosen for validation through quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR), using GAPDH as the internal reference. The primers were created using PrimerPremier 6.0 (refer to Table S2) and produced by Sangon Biotech in Shanghai. The reaction mixture, totaling 10 μL, included: 5 μL of Taq SYBR® Green qPCR Premix, 0.2 μL of each forward and reverse primer, 1 μL of cDNA template, and 3.6 μL of ddH₂O. The relative expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis of data

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (one-way ANOVA). Results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Inter-group differences were evaluated by t-test, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Figures were generated using Origin 2018 and GraphPad Prism 9.5.

Results

Serum uric acid concentration

This study investigated the effects of various feeding regimens on serum uric acid levels in geese. On the 42 nd day of the experiment, serum samples were collected to determine serum uric acid concentration in each group. As shown in Fig. 1, compared with CG group (111 μmol/L) and LS group (85 μmol/L), the serum concentration of goslings in MG group (170 μmol/L) was significantly increased (P < 0.05). Compared with CG group, the serum uric acid concentration of geese in LS group was significantly decreased (P < 0.05).

Intestinal Morphology Investigations

As shown in Fig. 2, the morphology of ileum villi in the CG group was relatively complete, and there was no obvious disorder or fracture of intestinal villi (Fig. 2A). In the MG group, there was significant damage to the ileum tissue, and the intestinal villi were not arranged neatly (Fig. 2B). The ileum tissue structure of the LS group was complete (Fig. 2C), the intestinal epithelial cells were arranged neatly, the villi were tight, and the structure was clear. VH in LS group was significantly higher than that of CG group and MG group (P < 0.05). VH in MG group was significantly lower than that of LS group and CG group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D). Additionally, VH/CD in the LS group was significantly higher than that in the CG and MG groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2F).

Quality control of RNA-Seq data

In this study, we constructed twelve cDNA libraries from the ileum of CG, LS, and MG geese, with four biological replicates for each group. The average raw read counts for the CG, LS, and MG groups were 38,832,506, 37,166,332, and 38,425,937 paired-end reads, respectively. After quality control, the average clean read counts obtained for the CG, LS, and MG groups were 37,301,336, 35,929,789, and 37,127,065, respectively. The Q30 scores ranged from 95.18 to 98.52%. Alignment against the goose reference genome yielded total mapping rates of 79.05% to 81.22% and unique mapping rates of 76.80–79.72%.

We calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient between each sample using the FPKM values of every two samples. As shown in Fig. 3, a correlation heatmap was plotted, where the horizontal and vertical axes represent the squared Pearson correlation coefficients of each sample. In this experiment, the Pearson correlation coefficients demonstrated strong intra-group correlations, indicating good reproducibility of samples across all experimental groups, which supports their suitability for subsequent data analysis. As illustrated in Fig. S1, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the ileum samples from each group were clustered together. In summary, the sequencing data from this experiment are reliable, the sample relationships align with expectations, and the accuracy of subsequent data analysis is assured.

In this study, pairwise comparisons were made among the MG, CG, and LS groups, with criteria of transcriptional FPKM levels exceeding 10 in at least one treatment group, a |Fold change| of 1.5 or greater between the groups being compared, and statistical significance indicated by P ≤ 0.05. Figure 4A–C illustrates that we identified a total of 130 DEGs (Table S3) between the MG and CG groups, consisting of 50 genes that were up-regulated and 80 that were down-regulated. For the comparison of LS with MG, we determined 323 DEGs (Table S4), out of which 149 were found to be up-regulated and 174 down-regulated.

Discussion

In this study, the high-protein feed of the MG group reduced the villus height of the ileum of the goose, increased the crypt depth, and caused pathological changes in the intestine, such as epithelial cell damage and villus shedding, resulting in a decrease in intestinal absorption capacity, which in turn affected growth performance. At the same time, serum uric acid content increased significantly, reflecting that high-protein treatment had a significant negative impact on intestinal health and uric acid metabolism of geese. In contrast, the LS group effectively alleviated the damage of high-protein feed to the intestine, significantly increased villus height and reduced crypt depth, indicating that lactulose has a positive effect on intestinal growth and development. Masanetz et al. reported significantly increased ileal villus height in calves receiving a 2% lactulose-supplemented milk replacer28. Similarly, Guerra-Ordaz et al. reported that dietary supplementation with lactulose led to an increase in ileal villus height in weaned piglets subjected to an oral challenge with enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) K8829. Elkomy et al. showed that adding 0.10 ml/kg lactulose to the diet of laying hens could significantly improve the intestinal morphology, which was manifested by the increase of villus height and crypt depth, indicating that the intestinal structure reached the best state at this dose30. These findings align with the results of our study.

In this study, transcriptome analysis was conducted on the ileum tissues of three groups. Subsequently, GO enrichment and KEGG signaling pathway analyses were performed on the DEGs to identify key genes and signaling pathways that influence goose intestinal health and uric acid transport. The results of GO enrichment analysis in the MG vs CG group showed that GO terms related to intestinal structure were significantly enriched, including microvillus, plasma membrane composition, and extracellular matrix. In addition, GO terms related to inflammation and immunity were identified, such as complement activation, classical pathways, interleukin-27 receptor binding, and negative regulation of leukocyte apoptosis processes. In this study, the genes CLDN1 and CCN131,32 related to intestinal structure were down-regulated in MG group, while the genes C1QA, MERTK and EBI333–35 regulating inflammation and immune response were significantly up-regulated. These findings indicate that there is an inflammatory response in the intestine, suggesting that intestinal epithelial cells are damaged. Matthew et al. confirmed that a high-protein diet can destroy the intestinal flora of animals, damage the intestinal mucosal barrier, increase the permeability of the intestinal wall, and promote the entry of intestinal bacteria and bacterial lipopolysaccharide into the blood, thereby stimulating the inflammatory response and promoting the development of hyperuricemia36.

KEGG analysis indicated that DEGs in the ileum tissue of the MG versus CG group were primarily enriched in pathways related to immune and inflammatory responses, including the intestinal immune network for IgA production, phagosome formation, and the PPAR signaling pathway. These findings suggest that high-protein diets may induce intestinal inflammation and compromise the integrity of the intestinal structure.

GO enrichment analysis showed that the differential genes in the LS vs MG group were significantly enriched in zinc ion transmembrane transport and extracellular exosome-related pathways. We found that the expression of solute carrier (SLC) transporter family members (SLC39A14, SLC1A1, SLC26A3) was significantly up-regulated in the LS group. SLC39A14 (ZIP14) is a key zinc transporter in the basolateral membrane of intestinal epithelial cells37. The high expression of SLC39A14 in the LS group may improve the intestinal barrier function by stabilizing the zinc ion level and enhancing the tight junction between intestinal epithelial cells38. The results showed that the up-regulated expression of SLC1A1 significantly enhanced the intestinal uptake of free amino acids, provided more adequate nutritional support for intestinal epithelial cells, and promoted the development and structural integrity of intestinal villi39. Lactulose can produce SCFAs by microbial fermentation in the intestine40. Research by Calik et al. demonstrates that lactulose significantly improves body weight gain and feed conversion ratio by increasing the villus height to crypt depth ratio and elevating the concentrations of acetic, propionic, butyric, and total short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in the cecum41. Studies have shown that SLC26A3 can enhance the absorption of intestinal lumen by mediating the transmembrane transport of short-chain fatty acids. These short-chain fatty acids mainly include acetic acid, propionic acid and butyric acid produced by the fermentation of lactulose by intestinal microflora42,43. SCFAs are not only an important energy source for intestinal villus cells but also directly stimulate the growth and function maintenance of villi44. In this study, the LS group synergistically promoted the absorption of zinc ions, amino acids, and SCFAs by up-regulating the expression of SLC39A14, SLC1A1, and SLC26A3, thereby improving intestinal morphology and enhancing barrier function, which may be one of the important mechanisms for lactulose to improve intestinal health.

KEGG analysis revealed that the DEGs in the ileum tissue of the LS versus MG group were primarily enriched in the Apelin signaling pathway, which includes EGR1, NOS2, RPS6, and KLF2. This pathway is crucial for various physiological functions and possesses anti-inflammatory properties45. It effectively inhibits the production and release of inflammatory factors in the intestine, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammatory responses46. Research indicates that KLF2 is crucial for intestinal homeostasis, encompassing barrier integrity and immune regulation47–49. Shao et al. demonstrated that mice with conditional KLF2 deficiency in T cells develop spontaneous rectal prolapse and intestinal inflammation, phenotypes overturned by eliminating microbiota or reconstituting with donor KLF2 + cells50. Consequently, lactulose may mitigate intestinal injury by activating the Apelin signaling pathway, upregulating KLF2 expression, promoting the repair of intestinal endothelial cells, and enhancing the intestinal capacity to resist injury. In this study, the intestinal tract of the MG group exhibited damage, with villi loss observed. We hypothesize that the abnormal expression of KLF2 in the MG group may be a key factor contributing to the damage of intestinal villi.

Here, through further in-depth bioinformatics analysis based on GSEA, we successfully elucidated the key signaling pathways and transport function-specific genes that exert crucial roles in uric acid transport processes. In this study, the LS group exhibited significant enrichment of the ABC transporter pathway compared to the CG and MG groups as well as showed upregulation of the ABCG2 gene. Notably, the ABCG2 gene emerged as a key player in uric acid transport within this pathway. The ABCG2 gene encodes an ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter that is crucial for the intestinal excretion of uric acid51. ABCG2 mediates uric acid excretion from intestinal epithelial cells, functioning predominantly at their brush border membrane52–54. Research has demonstrated that knockout or impaired function of the ABCG2 gene in mice results in a decrease of approximately 40% to 50% in intestinal uric acid excretion55. This finding underscores the irreplaceable role of ABCG2 in intestinal uric acid excretion, which is vital for maintaining uric acid homeostasis.

By combining the differential genes of the three control groups for trend analysis, we identified a gene expression profile with a specific expression pattern. Functional enrichment analysis revealed the potential biological function association of these dynamic expression profiles. Among them, the up-regulated profile (Profile 12, 13) was significantly enriched in the process of intestinal epithelial development, especially the activation of Wnt signaling pathway and MAPK signaling pathway. These expression changes suggest that LS treatment may activate key pathways that promote the maintenance of intestinal epithelial homeostasis and injury repair. In addition, the down-regulated profiles (Profile 2, 7) were mainly enriched in the process of immune regulation, including NF-kB signaling complex and PPAR signaling pathway. The down-regulation trend showed that with the decrease of uric acid level, the immune activation and inflammatory response related pathways were inhibited.

Trend analysis showed that LS group up-regulated the expression of LRP6, TJP3, TJP1 and PROM1. LRP6 is a Wnt co-receptor in canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling56. Studies have shown that in the DSS-induced colitis mouse model, the expression of LRP6 is reduced, and overexpression of LPR6 or its N-terminus can restore the inhibited Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and reduce intestinal inflammatory damage57. It is worth noting that the Wnt/β-catenin complex can directly bind to the gene promoter region of the ABC transporter (ABCB1, ABCG2), thereby positively regulating its expression58. As a key peripheral membrane protein, TJP3 is responsible for anchoring the actin cytoskeleton to tight junction transmembrane proteins (occludin and claudin)59,60. Therefore, LS may enhance the interaction between actin cytoskeleton and transmembrane proteins by enhancing the expression of TJP3, thereby stabilizing the structure and function of intestinal tight junctions. TJP1 (ZO-1) is not necessary to maintain the daily barrier but is a key protein to initiate effective mucosal repair. The results showed that in DSS or TNBS-induced mouse colitis models, mice with intestinal epithelial-specific deletion of ZO-1 showed significant mucosal repair disorders although only mildly increased permeability61. lactulose may up-regulate the expression of transcription factor KLF2 by activating the Apelin signaling pathway, thereby driving intestinal epithelial repair and laying a stable cellular foundation for the formation of tight junctions62,63. Studies have confirmed that KLF2 deficiency leads to a concomitant decrease in the mRNA and protein levels of ZO-1 (TJP1)64. We speculate that lactulose intervention can significantly up-regulate the expression of KLF2 and up-regulate the expression of tight junction gene (ZO-1), and quickly restore the structure and function of intestinal barrier. Karim et al.'s study showed that PROM1 deficient (Prom1−/−) mice showed more severe mucosal inflammation, dysplasia, and tumor susceptibility in the AOM/DSS-induced colitis model, suggesting that PROM1 is essential for mucosal repair and homeostasis maintenance after intestinal injury65. In general, in the context of hyperuricemia-induced intestinal injury, lactulose effectively improved the damaged intestinal mucosal barrier structure and function by up-regulating the expression of LRP6, TJP3, TJP1, and PROM1. This mechanism plays an important role in restoring intestinal microenvironment homeostasis and improving intestinal uric acid metabolism.

Conclusions

In summary, this study preliminarily screened a series of key genes that may help improve intestinal health through transcriptome analysis. These genes include KLF2, LPR6, TJP3, TJP1 and PROM1, which are closely related to intestinal structure, and SLC39A14, SLC1A1 and SLC26A3, which are involved in material transport. In addition, GSEA enrichment analysis suggested that the ABC transporter pathway may be a potential key pathway for regulating uric acid excretion. Based on the above findings, we conclude that lactulose can significantly reduce the serum uric acid level of goslings, and its mechanism may be related to promoting the healthy development of intestinal villi and reducing intestinal tissue damage. The results of this study provided theoretical basis and experimental clues for the scientific application of lactulose in goose production.
