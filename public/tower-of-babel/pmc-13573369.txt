Automated micro-CT quantification of clear aligner fit: a pilot comparison of manufacturing processes
Authors: Shuyan Liu, Ming Lv, Junyu Li, Miguizhi Zhou, Yukang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClear aligner fit accuracy significantly influences treatment efficacy. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) enables precise three-dimensional quantification of aligner-tooth adaptation, but manual measurement is time-intensive and operator-dependent.ObjectiveTo develop and validate an automated flood-fill algorithm for micro-CT gap quantification and compare fit accuracy across three aligner manufacturing processes: in-house 3D-printing, Invisalign (thermoformed), and Angelalign (thermoformed).MethodsThis in vitro pilot study used a standardized maxillary dental model to fabricate nine aligners across three manufacturing groups (three aligners per group): direct DLP 3D-printing (LuxCreo Inc., Chicago, US), Invisalign (SmartTrack™), and Angelalign (Angel Pro™). Each aligner was scanned using a Xradia 610 Versa micro-CT (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) at 58.82 μm voxel resolution. An automated flood-fill algorithm was developed in Python to segment and quantify aligner-tooth gap widths from reconstructed volumes, and validated against manual segmentation using Dice similarity coefficient, sensitivity, and specificity. Group differences in gap width were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Mann-Whitney post-hoc comparisons and Bonferroni correction.ResultsThe automated algorithm achieved a mean Dice similarity coefficient of 0.87 ± 0.04 (range: 0.81–0.94), sensitivity of 0.89 ± 0.05, and specificity of 0.96 ± 0.03. A total of 120 gap width measurements were obtained (3D-printing: n = 42, Invisalign: n = 42, Angelalign: n = 36). Mean gap widths were 193.27 ± 93.03 μm, 186.96 ± 81.86 μm, and 254.89 ± 133.21 μm, respectively. The Kruskal-Wallis test showed no significant overall difference among groups (H = 4.396, P = 0.111). Post-hoc analysis revealed no significant difference between 3D-printing and Invisalign (U = 886.0, P = 0.974, Cliff’s Delta = 0.005, negligible effect). Neither comparison with Angelalign reached statistical significance after Bonferroni correction (P = 0.070 and P = 0.065), though both showed small effect sizes (Cliff’s Delta ≈ − 0.23). Linear mixed-effects models confirmed these findings (random-effects ICC = 0.179 for aligner-level clustering).ConclusionsThe automated flood-fill algorithm may enable efficient and reproducible micro-CT gap quantification, reducing processing time from ~ 45–60 min to approximately 3.2 min per scan. No statistically significant difference in fit accuracy was observed between in-house 3D-printed and Invisalign thermoformed aligners (mean difference: 6.31 μm), providing preliminary evidence for their potential as a cost-effective manufacturing alternative; however, this should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence given the small aligner sample size. The Angelalign group showed a trend toward larger gaps with small effect sizes, warranting further investigation. Substantial within-group variability across all manufacturing methods underscores the need for systematic quality control in aligner production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08780-y.

Introduction

Clear aligner therapy has emerged as a preferred orthodontic treatment modality, offering aesthetic advantages and improved patient comfort compared to traditional fixed appliances [1, 2]. The clinical efficacy of clear aligners depends critically on precise tooth-aligner adaptation, as inadequate fit compromises force transmission and the predictability of tooth movement [3]. Clinically, studies have reported that 70–80% of clear aligner cases require refinement or adjunctive fixed appliances to complete treatment, with inadequate fit accuracy identified as a significant contributing factor [4]. Air spaces between the aligner’s internal surface and the tooth crown cause applied forces to dissipate through the combined action of entrapped air and periodontal ligament elasticity; effective orthodontic forces can only be transmitted when intimate contact is maintained [5]. Accordingly, precise quantification of aligner-tooth gap dimensions holds direct clinical relevance for optimizing treatment outcomes.

Recent advances in aligner manufacturing technology have introduced diverse production methods, each with distinct biomechanical and economic implications. Thermoforming, the established industrial standard exemplified by systems such as Invisalign and Angelalign, involves vacuum or pressure forming of thermoplastic sheets over physical or digitally printed models. However, this process is associated with dimensional instability and material property alterations [6, 7]: thermoplastic sheets of nominal 0.75 mm thickness have been measured at 0.38–0.69 mm across different regions after thermoforming [8], and a 10% reduction in aligner thickness may result in up to a 30% decrease in applied forces [9]. In contrast, direct 3D-printing using digital light processing (DLP) or stereolithography (SLA) technologies eliminates intermediate manufacturing steps, enabling in-house fabrication with potentially greater dimensional fidelity. Recent comparative studies have reported mean absolute discrepancies of 0.076–0.260 mm for thermoformed aligners versus 0.079–0.224 mm for direct-printed aligners [10], and systematic reviews indicate that 3D-printed aligners may possess advantages in accuracy and treatment efficacy, though high-quality confirmatory evidence remains limited [11, 12].

Traditional assessment methods for aligner fit, including visual inspection, tactile evaluation, and two-dimensional radiographic analysis, lack the precision necessary for comprehensive three-dimensional quantification. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) has emerged as the gold standard for aligner fit assessment, enabling non-destructive, high-resolution three-dimensional visualization of the aligner-tooth interface [13]. Lombardo et al. and Palone et al. confirmed the utility of micro-CT in characterizing aligner gap dimensions and material thickness across multiple commercial systems [13, 14]. However, existing micro-CT studies have relied exclusively on manual measurement or two-dimensional analysis protocols, which are time-consuming, susceptible to inter-operator variability, and poorly suited to high-throughput or quality control applications [14, 15].

Despite growing interest in 3D-printed aligners, the comparative fit accuracy of direct-printed versus thermoformed systems remains inadequately characterized. Most existing studies employ small sample sizes, manual measurement protocols, or focus exclusively on commercial thermoformed products [13, 14]. Critically, to our knowledge, no previous study has reported an automated algorithm for three-dimensional micro-CT gap quantification applicable to large-scale comparative analyses or manufacturing quality control. Automated image segmentation has demonstrated substantial efficiency gains in related dental imaging contexts [16–19], and the Dice similarity coefficient provides an established framework for validating such approaches [20, 21]. Flood-fill algorithms, successfully applied in diverse medical imaging segmentation tasks [22, 23], offer a methodologically tractable approach to automated gap identification in micro-CT volumes. Addressing these gaps, the present pilot study pursued two objectives: (1) to develop and validate an automated flood-fill algorithm for micro-CT gap quantification, and (2) to compare fit accuracy across three aligner manufacturing processes — in-house 3D-printing, Invisalign, and Angelalign — using this automated approach.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample preparation

This in vitro pilot study employed a standardized maxillary dental model (Nissin Dental Products Inc., Kyoto, Japan) representing normal occlusion with complete dentition. The model was digitized using an intraoral scanner (TRIOS 3, 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) with a reported accuracy of ± 20 μm. The resulting STL file served as the reference geometry for aligner fabrication across all manufacturing groups.

Three aligner manufacturing processes were evaluated. Group 1 consisted of direct 3D-printing using DLP technology (LuxCreo Inc., Chicago, US; manufactured in Ningbo, China) with 0.75 mm nominal thickness, manufactured according to the standard production protocol of the manufacturer. Group 2 comprised commercially fabricated thermoformed aligners from Invisalign (Align Technology, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) using SmartTrack™ material (0.75 mm nominal thickness), manufactured according to standard clinical protocols. Group 3 consisted of commercially fabricated thermoformed aligners from Angelalign (Angelalign Technology Inc., Shanghai, China) using Angel Pro™ material (0.75 mm nominal thickness), also manufactured according to standard protocols.

For each manufacturing method, three aligners were fabricated, resulting in a total sample of nine aligners (three per group, total N = 9). No active tooth movements were programmed; all aligners were designed as passive retainers to isolate manufacturing precision from planned tooth movement discrepancies.

It should be noted that Angelalign aligners did not extend to the second molar region in any of the three fabricated specimens, resulting in six measurable tooth positions per aligner compared to seven for the 3D-printing and Invisalign groups. This difference in distal coverage was confirmed across repeated scanning sessions and accounts for the unequal measurement numbers across groups (3D-printing: n = 42, Invisalign: n = 42, Angelalign: n = 36).

Micro-CT imaging protocol

Each aligner was carefully seated on the standardized maxillary model by a single operator using standardized finger pressure for 30 s to simulate clinical insertion. The aligner-model assembly was then stabilized using dental wax at the posterior regions to prevent movement during scanning. Dental wax was applied exclusively to the outer aligner margins distal to the last tooth position to avoid interfering with the aligner-tooth interface at measurement sites, ensuring the aligner-tooth interface remained undisturbed.

Micro-CT imaging was performed using a Xradia 610 Versa high-resolution scanner (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) with standardized parameters. The X-ray source was operated at 100 kV voltage and 200 µA current. The voxel size was set to 58.82 μm, which represents the spatial resolution of the scanner and determines the minimum detectable gap and measurement precision. Scanning was performed with a rotation step of 0.3°, frame averaging of 3, complete 360° rotation, Al + Cu filter (0.25 mm), and exposure time of 170 ms per projection.

Scans were reconstructed using Scout-and-Scan Control System software (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) with standardized beam hardening correction (30%), ring artifact reduction (8), and smoothing (0). Reconstructed volumes were exported as 16-bit TIFF image stacks for subsequent analysis.

Automated gap quantification algorithm

Algorithm development

An automated flood-fill algorithm was developed in Python 3.9 using the scikit-image library to identify and quantify aligner-tooth gaps from micro-CT volumes. The flood-fill approach, adapted from computer graphics flood-fill algorithms, exploits the air-filled nature of gaps, which appear as low-density (dark) regions in micro-CT images, contrasting with high-density tooth structure and intermediate-density aligner material.

The algorithm workflow comprised the following steps:

First, micro-CT TIFF image stacks were loaded using the tifffile library and converted to 3D numpy arrays. Grayscale normalization was applied to standardize intensity ranges across scans. Second, piecewise linear intensity stretching with fixed parameters was applied uniformly across all scans to enhance contrast between gap regions and surrounding structures by amplifying pixel value differences; voxels with stretched intensity values above 0.5 were classified as tooth-aligner material (binary value = 1), and those at or below 0.5 were classified as air/gap regions (binary value = 0). The threshold of 0.5 is not an empirical choice but a direct mathematical consequence of the stretch function design: with our parameters (T_max = 0.7), gap/air pixels are mapped to [0, 0.7] and bracket material pixels to [0.7, 1.0], making 0.5 the natural midpoint of the gap region with a safety margin of 0.2 from the material boundary. Sensitivity analysis across 106 annotated micro-CT slices confirmed that Dice coefficients remain above 0.90 when the threshold is varied by ± 0.05, demonstrating robustness to parameter perturbation (Supplementary Materials). This thresholding procedure was fully automated with no operator-dependent parameter adjustment. Third, seed points for flood-fill propagation were automatically determined by computing the centroid of each connected component identified in the binary segmentation, providing a fully automated initialization without manual placement. The flood-fill algorithm then propagated from these seed points through all connected voxels meeting the gap intensity criteria (grayscale values below threshold). Fourth, morphological operations (binary closing with 3 × 3 × 3 kernel) were applied to the flood-filled volume to remove small isolated artifacts and smooth gap boundaries. Fifth, connected component labeling (scipy.ndimage.label) was performed to identify discrete gap regions. Only components exceeding 100 voxels were retained to filter noise. Sixth, for each gap region, the three-dimensional Euclidean distance from each gap voxel to the nearest tooth surface was calculated using distance transform (scipy.ndimage.distance_transform_edt). The mean distance across all voxels within each gap region was recorded as the gap width for that region. Finally, gap width measurements were converted from voxels to micrometers by multiplying by the voxel size (58.82 μm).

All image processing and analysis were performed using Python 3.9 with NumPy (1.21.2), SciPy (1.7.1), scikit-image (0.18.3), and tifffile (2021.8.30) libraries. Three-dimensional surface deviation maps (Fig. 2C) were generated using a custom Python visualization script employing the PyVista library (latest stable version) for surface mesh rendering and scalar-based color mapping of boundary deviation distances. The complete algorithm code and processing pipeline are available in the supplementary materials. An overview of the complete algorithm pipeline is presented in Fig. 1.

Algorithm validation

To validate the automated algorithm’s accuracy, a subset of gap measurements was compared against manual segmentation performed by two independent operators (interobserver reliability assessment). It should be noted that the algorithm processes the complete three-dimensional CT volume across all slices, and gap width measurements are derived from a 3D Euclidean distance transform (scipy.ndimage.distance_transform_edt) applied to the entire reconstructed volume — not computed slice-by-slice independently. This constitutes inherently three-dimensional gap quantification. For validation purposes, every fifth slice from each aligner scan was systematically selected (interval sampling, 1/5 of total slices per aligner), ensuring uniform spatial coverage across the full scanning extent. Manual segmentation was performed using Mimics (version 21.0, Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) with operators blinded to automated algorithm results and each other’s measurements. Gap regions were manually traced, and areas were recorded for comparison with automated segmentation. Dice similarity coefficient, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated on a per-slice basis by comparing automated segmentation outputs projected onto the corresponding cross-sectional planes with manual tracings. Since gap width is mathematically derived from the segmentation boundary via distance transform, accurate segmentation (confirmed by DSC = 0.87 ± 0.04) directly implies accurate gap width computation; the 2D slice-level validation therefore constitutes an indirect but logically coherent validation of the three-dimensional gap width measurements.

Algorithm performance was quantified using three metrics. First, the Dice similarity coefficient was calculated to assess spatial overlap between automated and manual segmentations using the formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Dice}= 2|\mathrm{A}\cap\:\mathrm{M}|/(|{A}|+|{M}|)$$\end{document}, where A represents automated segmentation and M represents manual segmentation. Values range from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect overlap), with Dice ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable for medical image segmentation. Second, sensitivity (true positive rate) was calculated as the proportion of manually identified gap pixels correctly identified by the algorithm: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Sensitivity}=\mathrm{TP}/\left(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}\right)$$\end{document}, where TP represents true positives and FN represents false negatives. Third, specificity (true negative rate) was calculated as the proportion of non-gap pixels correctly classified: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Specificity}=\mathrm{TN}/\left(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}\right)$$\end{document}, where TN represents true negatives and FP represents false positives.

Interobserver reliability between the two manual raters was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with two-way random effects model for absolute agreement (ICC [1, 2]). ICC values were interpreted as: <0.50 = poor, 0.50–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.90 = good, > 0.90 = excellent reliability.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.9 with SciPy (version 1.7.1) and NumPy (version 1.21.2) libraries. Descriptive statistics were calculated for gap width measurements in each manufacturing group, including mean, standard deviation (SD), median, interquartile range (IQR), and range. Data distribution normality was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests for each group. Given the non-normal distributions observed in all groups (Shapiro-Wilk W < 0.92, P < 0.05 for all groups) and the right-skewed distribution characteristics observed in preliminary data exploration, non-parametric statistical methods were employed throughout.

Overall differences among the three manufacturing groups were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, a non-parametric alternative to one-way ANOVA that compares median ranks across groups. Regardless of the overall Kruskal-Wallis result, pre-specified post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Mann-Whitney U tests (Wilcoxon rank-sum tests), as this pilot study was designed for exploratory parameter estimation rather than confirmatory hypothesis testing. This approach is consistent with the study’s primary goal of characterizing manufacturing differences for future sample size calculations.

To control Type I error inflation from multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied by dividing the nominal significance level by the number of comparisons: α = 0.05 / 3 = 0.017. Effect sizes for pairwise comparisons were quantified using Cliff’s Delta (δ), a non-parametric effect size measure robust to outliers and appropriate for ordinal data. Cliff’s Delta ranges from − 1 to + 1, with interpretation as follows: |δ| < 0.147 = negligible, 0.147 ≤ |δ| < 0.330 = small, 0.330 ≤ |δ| < 0.474 = medium, |δ| ≥ 0.474 = large effect.

Multiple gap width measurements from the same aligner represent clustered data that may violate independence assumptions of standard non-parametric tests. To address potential clustering effects and validate the robustness of our findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted using linear mixed-effects models (statsmodels library) with aligner identity as a random effect and manufacturing group as a fixed effect. The primary statistical unit of analysis was individual gap measurements, consistent with established practices in micro-CT aligner research where measurement precision across multiple anatomical sites is the focus of investigation. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.017 for post-hoc comparisons (Bonferroni-corrected) and P < 0.05 for the overall Kruskal-Wallis test. Data visualization was performed using Matplotlib (version 3.4.3) and Seaborn (version 0.11.2).

Results

Algorithm validation

The automated flood-fill algorithm demonstrated reliable performance across all validation metrics when compared with manual segmentation. The average Dice similarity coefficient was 0.87 ± 0.04 (range: 0.81–0.94), indicating good spatial agreement between automated and manual gap identification. Mean sensitivity was 0.89 ± 0.05, demonstrating that the algorithm correctly identified 89% of gap pixels marked by human operators. Mean specificity was 0.96 ± 0.03, indicating that 96% of non-gap regions were correctly classified.

Interobserver reliability between the two manual raters was excellent (ICC = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.97), validating the objectivity of the manual segmentation reference standard. Processing time for the fully automated algorithm averaged 3.2 ± 0.8 min per complete aligner scan on standard desktop hardware (Intel Core i7, 16GB RAM). This compares favorably to approximately 45–60 min for complete manual segmentation, representing an approximately 14–19-fold improvement in workflow efficiency. Comparative statistical analysis

The Kruskal-Wallis H test assessing overall differences among the three manufacturing groups did not reach statistical significance (H = 4.396, P = 0.111), suggesting no significant overall difference in gap width distributions across manufacturing methods at the α = 0.05 level.

Post-hoc pairwise comparisons using Mann-Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.017) were performed to evaluate specific group differences. Results are summarized in Table 2.

The comparison between 3D-printing and Invisalign revealed no statistically significant difference (U = 886.0, P = 0.974), with a negligible effect size (Cliff’s Delta = 0.005). The mean difference between these groups was only 6.31 μm (193.27–186.96 μm), which is far below the voxel resolution of the scanning system (58.82 μm) and well within the range of measurement uncertainty, indicating that this difference is not clinically meaningful. This finding is consistent with the absence of a statistically significant difference between in-house 3D-printed and Invisalign thermoformed aligners in terms of fit accuracy, which was visually confirmed by substantial overlap in gap width distributions with nearly identical median values and distribution shapes (Figs. 3, 4 and 6).

Comparisons of both 3D-printing and Invisalign against Angelalign showed trends toward larger gaps in the Angelalign group (3D vs. Angelalign: P = 0.070; Invisalign vs. Angelalign: P = 0.065), but neither comparison achieved statistical significance after Bonferroni correction (α = 0.017). Effect sizes for these comparisons were small (Cliff’s Delta = -0.232 and − 0.237, respectively), suggesting modest practical differences. The Angelalign group displayed a tendency toward higher median values and greater variability, with consistently elevated gap measurements across all tooth positions and arch regions, though with considerable distributional overlap with the other two groups (Figs. 4, 5, 6).

Sensitivity analyses using linear mixed-effects models confirmed these findings. The intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.179, indicating that approximately 17.9% of total variance was attributable to aligner-level clustering, while 82.1% represented measurement-level variation across tooth positions and anatomical regions. Mixed-effects pairwise comparisons yielded results consistent with non-parametric tests: 3D-printing vs. Invisalign (β = −6.31 μm, 95% CI [− 52.28, 39.66], P = 0.794); 3D-printing vs. Angelalign (β = −61.62 μm, P = 0.041); Invisalign vs. Angelalign (β = −67.93 μm, P = 0.028). The likelihood ratio test for overall group effect was not significant (χ²(2) = 4.51, P = 0.105), corroborating the Kruskal-Wallis result. All comparisons with Angelalign remained non-significant after Bonferroni correction (P > 0.017).

Discussion

This pilot study achieved two primary objectives: the development and validation of an automated flood-fill algorithm for micro-CT gap quantification, and a preliminary comparison of fit accuracy across three aligner manufacturing processes. The automated algorithm showed promising performance with a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.87 ± 0.04, while comparative analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in fit accuracy between in-house 3D-printed and Invisalign thermoformed aligners, providing preliminary exploratory evidence for their potential viability as cost-effective alternatives in clinical practice.

Algorithm performance and methodological contributions

The automated flood-fill algorithm developed in this study represents a promising methodological contribution to micro-CT gap quantification. The average Dice similarity coefficient of 0.87 achieved in our validation study indicates good spatial agreement between automated and manual segmentation, exceeding the 0.70 threshold typically considered acceptable for medical image segmentation [20, 21]. This performance is consistent with validation metrics reported in other medical imaging applications employing similar region-growing algorithms [22, 23]. Recent advances in automated dental image segmentation have demonstrated that AI-driven approaches can achieve excellent accuracy while dramatically improving workflow efficiency [16, 17]. Cui et al. developed a fully automatic AI system for tooth and alveolar bone segmentation from CBCT images, demonstrating the feasibility of automated approaches in dental imaging applications [17]. Similarly, Dot et al. introduced DentalSegmentator, an open-source tool achieving robust multiclass segmentation on diverse CT and CBCT datasets [18]. The high sensitivity (0.89) and specificity (0.96) observed in our algorithm further confirm its reliability in correctly identifying gap regions while minimizing false positives.

Previous micro-CT studies of clear aligner fit have relied primarily on manual measurement protocols, which are inherently time-consuming and susceptible to inter-operator variability [13, 14]. Lombardo et al. performed 204 linear 2D measurements across six aligner systems, requiring substantial operator time and introducing potential measurement bias [13]. Similarly, Palone et al. employed manual 2D analysis on microtomographic images, acknowledging the labor-intensive nature of such approaches [14]. Our automated algorithm reduced processing time from approximately 45–60 min to 3.2 ± 0.8 min per complete aligner scan, representing a substantial improvement in workflow efficiency while maintaining measurement objectivity. This efficiency gain has important implications for potential quality control applications in aligner manufacturing, where high-throughput analysis is essential. Recent work by Fontenele et al. demonstrated that automated segmentation methods can be 116 times faster than manual approaches while maintaining excellent accuracy [19], supporting the clinical utility of automated image analysis pipelines.

The excellent interobserver reliability ICC = 0.94 between manual raters supports the objectivity of our reference standard and lends preliminary confidence to the algorithm validation results. The Dice similarity coefficient has been widely adopted as the most used metric in validating medical volume segmentations [21], and our findings demonstrate that flood-fill-based approaches can achieve performance comparable to more sophisticated deep learning methods for specific segmentation tasks. The combination of automated processing with validated accuracy metrics addresses a critical methodological gap in the clear aligner literature and provides a foundation for future large-scale comparative studies.

Comparative fit accuracy analysis

The central preliminary finding of this pilot study is that no statistically significant difference in fit accuracy was observed between in-house 3D-printed and Invisalign thermoformed aligners, with a negligible effect size (Cliff’s Delta = 0.005) and a mean difference of only 6.31 μm. This finding should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence, given the small number of aligners evaluated per group (n = 3), but provides exploratory support suggesting that direct 3D-printing warrants further investigation as a potentially viable manufacturing alternative that may offer economic and logistical advantages. Our results align with recent systematic reviews indicating that 3D-printed aligners possess potential advantages in accuracy over thermoformed systems [11, 12]. Torkomian et al. concluded that directly printed aligners demonstrate higher accuracy, trueness, and precision compared to thermoformed aligners, though they emphasized the need for more high-quality comparative studies [11]. Koenig et al. reported mean absolute discrepancies ranging from 0.076 to 0.260 mm for thermoformed aligners versus 0.079–0.224 mm for direct-printed aligners, demonstrating comparable or superior dimensional accuracy with 3D-printing approaches [10]. Niu et al. in a recent narrative review similarly concluded that printed aligners demonstrated superior fitting accuracy compared to commercially available thermoformed materials [24]. Our findings provide additional support for these conclusions using a distinct methodological approach focused specifically on gap quantification rather than overall dimensional accuracy. The mean gap widths observed in our study (193.27 μm for 3D-printing, 186.96 μm for Invisalign, 254.89 μm for Angelalign) fall within ranges reported in previous micro-CT investigations. Park et al. reported gap widths varying by tooth type and location for both thermoformed and 3D-printed aligners using TC-85 material [25]. Lombardo et al. observed mean gap widths ranging from approximately 50–300 μm across different commercial aligner systems, with considerable heterogeneity between brands [13]. The consistency of our measurements with established literature supports the validity of our automated quantification approach. The trend toward larger gaps observed in the Angelalign group, while not reaching statistical significance after Bonferroni correction, warrants consideration. The small effect sizes (Cliff’s Delta ≈ -0.23) suggest modest practical differences that may reflect manufacturing process variations, material properties, or quality control differences between manufacturers. However, the overlapping distributions and lack of statistical significance preclude definitive conclusions regarding systematic differences. These preliminary findings may inform hypotheses for future adequately-powered confirmatory studies. Clinical implications

The clinical significance of aligner-tooth gap dimensions relates directly to force transmission efficiency and treatment predictability. Mantovani et al. demonstrated that intimate contact between the aligner’s internal surface and tooth crown is essential for effective orthodontic force application [5]. Air spaces between contact surfaces cause applied forces to dissipate through the combined action of air within gaps and periodontal ligament elasticity, potentially compromising treatment outcomes [5]. Studies have reported that 70–80% of clear aligner cases require refinement or adjunctive fixed appliances, with inadequate fit accuracy being a significant contributing factor [4].

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have comprehensively evaluated the predictability of clear aligner therapy. Alwafi et al. conducted an overview of systematic reviews assessing CAT effectiveness and found that rotational movements, especially for canines, were not reliably predicted, while horizontal movements were more predictable [4]. Robertson et al. reported low to moderate certainty regarding specific tooth movement efficiency with clear aligners [2]. D’Antò et al. evaluated the predictability of tooth rotations and found accuracy ranging from 36% to 85%, averaging around 65% [26]. Castroflorio et al. demonstrated that treatment design significantly affects orthodontic tooth movement predictability with aligners [27]. These findings underscore the critical importance of optimizing aligner fit to maximize force transmission and treatment efficiency.

The lack of a significant difference observed between 3D-printed and Invisalign aligners suggests that in-house fabrication may provide comparable force transmission potential while offering several practical advantages. Direct 3D-printing eliminates intermediate manufacturing steps inherent in thermoforming, potentially reducing production costs and turnaround time [28]. The global clear aligner market was valued at over $5 billion in 2024 and is expected to grow significantly, with 3D printing technology transforming fabrication by reducing production time and costs [24]. For orthodontic practices seeking greater control over aligner production and reduced dependence on external manufacturers, our findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the clinical viability of this approach.

However, the substantial variability observed within all manufacturing groups (standard deviations ranging from 81.86 to 133.21 μm) highlights an important consideration for clinical practice. This variability, reflected in wide interquartile ranges and right-skewed distributions, indicates that all manufacturing processes produce aligners with regions of both excellent and suboptimal fit. The observation that anterior tooth regions tended to show larger gaps compared to posterior regions across all groups is consistent with previous findings by Palone et al., who reported that thermoformed samples displayed smaller gap widths at anterior teeth and gingival centers compared to posterior teeth and occlusal surfaces [14]. These regional variations have implications for force distribution and may partially explain the differential predictability of various tooth movements reported in clinical studies [1, 4, 26, 27].The gap widths observed across all three manufacturing groups in the present study (mean values: 193.27 μm, 186.96 μm, and 254.89 μm for 3D-printing, Invisalign, and Angelalign, respectively) warrant consideration in the context of established force transmission thresholds. Sever and Arslan reported that prior studies have identified 50 μm as a critical threshold for compromised force transmission, with gaps exceeding 100 μm associated with approximately 40% force reduction, and clinically significant gaps characterized as ranging from 50 to 200 μm [29]. Given that the mean gap widths in all three groups exceed the 100 μm threshold, some degree of force attenuation is expected across all manufacturing methods examined in this study. This observation underscores that gap quantification alone is insufficient as a quality metric; the absolute magnitude of gaps relative to established biomechanical thresholds must be considered when interpreting fit accuracy data. A further biomechanical consideration relates to the periodontal ligament’s inherent free movement space. Palone et al. noted that the periodontal ligament accommodates approximately 0.04 mm of free tooth movement before any mechanobiological response is initiated [14], suggesting that gap widths substantially exceeding this threshold may delay effective orthodontic loading at the start of each aligner stage. From a manufacturing perspective, recent evidence suggests that directly printed aligners demonstrate progressive improvement in fit with repeated insertion and removal cycles, whereas thermoformed aligners tend to show increasing gap widths over time, particularly in gingival regions [30]. This divergent trajectory implies that the static gap measurements obtained in the present in vitro study may underestimate the functional advantage of direct-printed aligners under clinical conditions. Future studies incorporating dynamic wear simulation would be valuable in quantifying this potential advantage.

Manufacturing process considerations

The comparable fit accuracy between 3D-printed and thermoformed aligners observed in this study may be attributed to fundamental differences in manufacturing processes and their effects on material properties. Thermoforming involves heating thermoplastic sheets and forming them over physical models, a process known to cause thickness reduction and material property alterations [7, 8]. Bucci et al. observed that 0.75 mm thermoplastic sheets measured between 0.38 and 0.69 mm across different regions after thermoforming [8], representing thickness reductions that may affect force delivery characteristics. Theoretical analyses suggest that a 10% reduction in aligner material thickness may result in up to 30% decrease in applied forces [9]. Elshazly et al. recently demonstrated that thermomechanical aging significantly affects the force system of orthodontic aligners made of different thermoformed materials [31].

In contrast, direct 3D-printing using DLP or SLA technologies produces aligners with potentially greater thickness consistency and geometric fidelity to the original digital design [10, 28]. Bandić et al. demonstrated that printed aligners had significantly lower deviations from planned thickness than thermoformed ones, suggesting more predictable manufacturing outcomes [32]. Park et al. similarly observed that thickness reduction occurred in thermoformed groups while thickness increase was noted in 3D-printed groups [25]. These manufacturing characteristics may contribute to the comparable or superior fit accuracy observed with 3D-printed aligners.

The emergence of shape memory polymers (SMPs) represents a significant advancement in 3D-printed aligner materials. Atta et al. conducted comprehensive physiochemical and mechanical characterization of 3D-printed aligner materials made of SMPs (4D aligner materials) and found that they exhibited mechanical properties more suitable for orthodontic treatment than thermoforming materials [33]. Can et al. reported that Tera Harz TC-85 is an aliphatic vinyl ester-polyurethane polymer functionalized with methacrylate with shape memory properties [34]. Sharif et al. evaluated the force system of 3D-printed orthodontic aligners made of SMPs and found that temperature significantly affected TC-85 material behavior, with the material applying consistent forces across different temperature ranges [35]. These advanced materials offer potential advantages including sustained force delivery and reduced number of aligners needed per treatment.

The use of LuxCreo direct-printed aligner material in our 3D-printing protocol follows established precedent in the literature. Jindal et al. reported that Dental LT clear resin demonstrates comparable mechanical properties to thermoformed materials under compressive forces equivalent to human bite cycles [6]. The continued evolution of printable materials specifically formulated for aligner applications will likely expand the capabilities and indications for in-house 3D-printing approaches. Recent clinical studies have begun validating directly printed aligner therapy with promising results [36, 37].

Implications for quality control and future manufacturing

The automated gap quantification approach developed in this study has important implications beyond comparative research. Quality control in aligner manufacturing remains a critical challenge, as evidenced by the substantial within-group variability observed across all manufacturing methods. Systematic quality assessment using objective metrics could enable manufacturers to identify and address sources of variability, ultimately improving treatment consistency and clinical outcomes.

The integration of artificial intelligence in clear aligner therapy is rapidly expanding. A recent scoping review by Morgan et al. identified AI applications across tooth segmentation (achieving 98% accuracy), digital measurement support, treatment outcome prediction, and remote monitoring [37]. The combination of automated micro-CT analysis with AI-driven treatment planning could enable comprehensive quality assurance workflows spanning from aligner fabrication through treatment monitoring.

For in-house fabrication specifically, our preliminary findings suggest that with appropriate equipment and protocols, orthodontic practices may achieve fit accuracy comparable to established commercial systems. However, the observed variability underscores the importance of implementing rigorous quality control measures regardless of manufacturing approach. Future work should explore whether automated gap quantification could serve as a practical quality control tool, enabling real-time assessment of manufacturing consistency and identification of substandard aligners before clinical deployment.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this pilot study warrant acknowledgment. First, regarding sample size and statistical design, our study employed three aligners per manufacturing group, yielding 120 gap measurements across multiple tooth positions. This methodological approach follows the precedent established by Lombardo et al.[16] and Palone et al.[17], who utilized single aligners per group with extensive measurement points to minimize model-to-model variation while isolating manufacturing effects. However, it must be critically acknowledged that since the primary research question concerns differences between manufacturing processes, the aligner itself represents the main experimental unit, and the true sample size at this level is only three per group. Multiple gap measurements obtained within the same aligner are clustered observations and cannot be treated as fully independent data points regardless of the number of measurement sites. Although sensitivity analyses using linear mixed-effects models with aligner as a random effect were conducted, and the intraclass correlation coefficient was relatively low (ICC = 0.179), this does not resolve the fundamental constraint imposed by the small number of experimental units. The statistical power of the present study to detect manufacturing differences at the aligner level is limited, and the absence of statistically significant differences between groups should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence. This study’s primary aims were methodological — validating the automated algorithm and generating preliminary parameter estimates to inform future research design. “It is noteworthy that prior micro-CT aligner fit studies have predominantly employed single specimens per group [13, 14], with Palone et al. explicitly acknowledging that a single specimen per brand may not be representative [14]. The largest comparable micro-CT study to date employed n = 10 aligners per group [38]. Consistent with this evidence base and the medium effect sizes observed in the present pilot study (Cohen’s d = 0.54–0.61 for comparisons involving the Angelalign group), future confirmatory studies should target a minimum of n = 10 aligners per group as a pragmatic starting point, with the aligner as the primary unit of analysis and formal a priori power calculations conducted prior to study initiation.

Second, the in vitro design does not account for factors present in the clinical environment, including saliva exposure, masticatory forces, and intraoral temperature variations. Aligners may expand differentially depending on material properties when exposed to oral fluids [39], and mechanical loading during function likely affects both fit and material thickness over time [8]. Dalaie et al. demonstrated that dynamic mechanical and thermal properties of clear aligners are altered after thermoforming and aging [7]. The absence of attachments in our passive aligner design also limits direct extrapolation to clinical scenarios where attachments significantly influence force delivery and aligner retention [5, 40].

Third, while our automated algorithm demonstrated good validation metrics and requires no manual parameter adjustment or operator intervention, future algorithm development could incorporate end-to-end deep learning-based segmentation networks, which have achieved DSC values exceeding 0.94 [17, 18, 41], to further enhance robustness across a wider range of scan quality and material combinations.

Fourth, our study employed a single standardized dental model, which eliminates inter-model variability but may not capture the range of anatomical variations encountered in clinical practice. The use of a standardized model allows isolation of manufacturing effects but limits assessment of how different dental morphologies might interact with various manufacturing processes. Garcés et al. demonstrated that expansion predictability with clear aligners varies based on arch location, planned expansion magnitude, and initial crossbite status [42], highlighting the importance of patient-specific factors in treatment outcomes.

Fifth, a critical limitation concerns the anatomical coverage of the Angelalign group. The Angelalign specimens consistently did not extend to the second molar region across all three aligners evaluated, resulting in the exclusion of tooth position 7 from this group. Consequently, comparisons between the Angelalign group and the other two groups were conducted across non-identical sets of tooth positions (6 vs. 7 positions), which may introduce positional sampling bias if second molar gap widths differ systematically from those of more anterior teeth. To explore the potential impact of this positional imbalance, a post-hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted restricting comparisons to the six tooth positions common to all groups. The results were consistent with the primary analysis: no significant difference was observed between 3D-printing and Invisalign (P = 0.477, Cliff’s Delta = − 0.122), and the comparison between 3D-printing and Angelalign remained significant (P = 0.005, Cliff’s Delta = − 0.479), supporting the robustness of the primary findings. Future studies should ensure equivalent anatomical coverage across all manufacturing groups to eliminate this potential source of bias.

Sixth, a potential source of variability relates to the aligner seating procedure. Although aligners were seated by a single operator using standardized finger pressure for 30 s to promote consistency, finger pressure cannot be precisely quantified or reproduced across specimens, and inter-specimen variability in seating force cannot be excluded. The use of a mechanical seating device with controlled and reproducible force application would represent a more rigorous approach. However, given the in vitro pilot nature of this study and the use of a single rigid dental model, this limitation is considered unlikely to have substantially affected the relative comparisons between manufacturing groups. Future studies should consider implementing standardized mechanical seating protocols to minimize this potential confound.

Seventh, the algorithm validation employed 2D slice-level segmentation overlap metrics (Dice similarity coefficient, sensitivity, and specificity) rather than direct three-dimensional validation of gap width measurements against a physical reference standard. However, it should be noted that the algorithm operates on the complete 3D CT volume, and gap widths are computed via 3D Euclidean distance transform across the entire reconstructed volume. Since gap width is mathematically derived from the segmentation boundary, the 2D validation of segmentation accuracy (DSC = 0.87 ± 0.04) provides an indirect but logically coherent basis for confidence in the gap width measurements. The systematic interval sampling strategy (every fifth slice, 1/5 of total slices per aligner) further ensures uniform spatial coverage across the full scanning extent. Nevertheless, direct validation against a physical reference standard of known geometry — such as calibrated phantom objects — was not performed in this pilot study and remains a direction for future methodological refinement.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this pilot study, the following conclusions can be drawn:

The automated flood-fill algorithm developed for micro-CT gap quantification showed promising performance with a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.87 ± 0.04, sensitivity of 0.89 ± 0.05, and specificity of 0.96 ± 0.03, suggesting its potential as a reproducible and efficient alternative to manual measurement protocols. The algorithm operates on the complete 3D CT volume with gap widths derived from 3D distance transform; validation was performed against 2D manual segmentation using systematic interval sampling (1/5 of total slices per aligner), which constitutes an indirect but logically coherent validation of the three-dimensional measurements. Direct validation against a physical reference standard was not conducted and remains a direction for future work.

No statistically significant difference in fit accuracy was observed between in-house 3D-printed and Invisalign thermoformed aligners (P = 0.974, Cliff’s Delta = 0.005, mean difference: 6.31 μm), nor among the three manufacturing groups overall (Kruskal-Wallis H = 4.396, P = 0.111). This absence of significant difference should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence, given the small number of aligners per group (n = 3) and the limited statistical power at the aligner level. Substantial within-group variability was observed across all manufacturing methods, underscoring the importance of quality control regardless of manufacturing approach.

As a pilot study, these findings are intended to inform the design of future confirmatory research rather than support definitive clinical recommendations. Adequately powered studies with larger aligner sample sizes, equivalent anatomical coverage across groups, and direct three-dimensional algorithm validation are needed before conclusions regarding manufacturing differences or clinical superiority can be drawn.
