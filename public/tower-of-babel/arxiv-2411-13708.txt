Comments on “ 𝒪 ⁡ ( m ⋅ n ) \mathcal{O}(m\cdot n) algorithms for the recognition and isomorphism problems on circular-arc graphs”
Tomasz Krawczyk

Abstract
. In the work [ 𝒪 ⁡ ( m ⋅ n ) \mathcal{O}(m\cdot n) algorithms for the recognition and isomorphism problems on circular-arc graphs , SIAM J. Comput. 24(3), 411–439, (1995) ], Wen-Lian Hsu claims three results concerning the class of circular-arc graphs: • the design of so-called decomposition trees that represent the structure of all normalized intersection models of circular-arc graphs, • an 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ m ) \mathcal{O}(nm) recognition algorithm for circular-arc graphs, • an 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ m ) \mathcal{O}(nm) isomorphism algorithm for circular-arc graphs. In [ Discrete Math. Theor. Comput. Sci., 15(1), 157–182, 2013 ] Curtis, Lin, McConnell, Nussbaum, Soulignac, Spinrad, and Szwarcfiter showed that Hsu’s isomorphism algorithm is incorrect. In this note, we show that the other two results – namely, the construction of decomposition trees and the recognition algorithm – are also flawed.



1.
Introduction

Given a family
\mathcal{F}
of sets, the
intersection graph
of
\mathcal{F}
is obtained by representing every set in
\mathcal{F}
by a vertex and connecting two vertices by an edge if and only if their corresponding sets have non-empty intersection.
A graph
G
is:

•

a
circular-arc graph
if
G
is the intersection graph of arcs of a circle,

•

a
circle graph
if
G
is the intersection graph of chords of a circle,

•

a
permutation graph
if
G
is the intersection graphs of chords
spanned between some two disjoint arcs of a circle.

In the work [
\mathcal{O}(m\cdot n)
algorithms
for the recognition and isomorphism problems on circular-arc graphs
,
SIAM J. Comput. 24(3), 411–439, (1995)
]
[
6
]
, Wen-Lian Hsu presents
three results concerning the class of circular-arc graphs.
The first of these results claims a construction of a
decomposition tree
of a circular-arc graph,
a data structure intended to represent the set of all its
normalized intersection models

(an analogue of the PQ-tree for an interval graph).

Normalized intersection models
(formally defined in Section
2
) of a circular-arc graph reflect the neighborhood relation between its vertices and can be seen as its canonical representations;
in particular, any intersection model can be made normalized by possibly extending some of its arcs.
Based on these decomposition trees, Hsu claims
\mathcal{O}(nm)
-algorithms for both the recognition and isomorphism problem for circular-arc graphs.

In this short note we show that the description of the normalized intersection models given by Hsu in
[
6
]
is not correct.
Specifically, we show the following:

Counterexample 1.1
.

There are circular-arc graphs whose all normalized models do not follow the description given by Hsu.

As a result, the decomposition trees developed by Hsu, along with his polynomial-time algorithms for recognizing and testing isomorphism of circular-arc graphs, are incorrect.
We note that Curtis, Lin, McConnell, Nussbaum, Soulignac, Spinrad, and Szwarcfiter
[
2
]
identified a different flaw in Hsu’s isomorphism algorithm already in 2013.
The approach used by Hsu (described below) determines somehow the main steps
that need to be taken to describe the structure of the normalized models of a circular-arc graph.
In this paper, we also identify several errors in Hsu’s work, showing that these steps were either partially or entirely incorrectly accomplished.

To describe the structure of the normalized models of a circular-arc graph Hsu uses a method that has its roots in the work of Gallai.
In his seminal paper
[
5
]
from 1967 Gallai introduced a data structure, called
modular decomposition tree
, which keeps a track of all
modules
of a simple graph
G
.
We recall that a set

is a

of

if every vertex from outside

is either adjacent to all vertices in

or non-adjacent to all

M

In the same work Gallai described the structure of all transitive orientations of
a comparability graphs.
In particular, Gallai proved that:

(G1):

If

V(G)
and

Otherwise, the structure of all transitive orientations of

The above-mentioned results of Gallai have turned out to be widely used to describe

Among them are, for example, permutation graphs

2.1.
The modular decomposition and the join decomposition

2.1.1.
Modular decomposition

Let
G
be a graph.
A
module

M
of
G
is a subset of
V(G)
such that between
M
and each vertex
v

in
V(G)\smallsetminus M
there are either no edges or all possible edges.
Module
M
is
connected
iff
G[M]

is connected.
Module
M
is
complement connected
iff the complement of
G[M]
is connected.
An unconnected module is called a
parallel module
.
A connected module whose complement is unconnected is called a
series module
.
A module that is both connected and complement connected is called a
neighborhood module
.
A module
M
of size greater than
1
is
nontrivial

if
M\neq V(G)
.
A graph
G
is
s-inseparable
if it does not contain any nontrivial module.
See page 416, lines 24 to 30.
A graph
G
which is s-inseparable is also called
prime
.

By definition, every module is exactly one of these three types.
The modular decomposition starts with the module
V=V(G)

and creates a corresponding root node in the decomposition tree labelled with S, P, or N,
depending on the type of
V
.
Then it decomposes this module into components

M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{k}
using one of the following rules:

•

if
V
is a series module, then
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
are the connected components of
\overline{G}
,

•

if
V
is a parallel module, then
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
are the connected components of
G
,

•

if
V
is a neighborhood module, then
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
are the maximal modules of
G
different than
V
.

For each
M_{i}
, find the modular decomposition tree of
G[M_{i}]
and make its root node a son of the root node representing
V(G)
.
The resulting tree is called a
modular decomposition tree
of
G
; it is proven in
[
5
]
that the modular decomposition tree of
G
is unique.
See page 416, lines -18 to -7.
