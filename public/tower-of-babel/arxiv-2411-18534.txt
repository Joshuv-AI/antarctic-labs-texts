On stabilizers in finite permutation groups
Luca Sabatini

Abstract
. Let G G be a permutation group on the finite set Ω \Omega . We prove various results about partitions of Ω \Omega whose stabilizers have good properties. In particular, we obtain the following three theorems: In every solvable permutation group, there is a subset whose stabilizer has all orbits of length at most 6 6 , which is best possible and answers two questions of Babai. Every solvable maximal subgroup of any almost simple group has derived length at most 10 10 , which is best possible. In every primitive group with solvable stabilizer, there are two points whose pointwise stabilizer has derived length bounded by an absolute constant.

1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a permutation group on the finite set
\Omega
.
One of the most studied topics in permutation group theory is finding a partition of
\Omega
whose stabilizer is trivial.
For example, a subset
\Delta\subseteq\Omega
is said to be in a regular orbit if its setwise stabilizer is trivial.
In general, the existence of such a
\Delta
is far from certain
[
Glu83
,
Ser97
]
, and it is necessary to stabilize many subsets.
On the other hand, the base size is the minimum number of points such that their pointwise stabilizer is trivial.
This corresponds to fixing a partition made by some single points and the complement.
Finding upper bounds for the base size is a difficult problem that has produced an immense volume of research,
and some of the most impressive results obtained are
[
Bab81
,
Pyb93
,
Ser96
,
DHM18
]
.
For more details on these topics, we refer the reader to the pleasant survey
[
BC11
]
.

In this paper, we are interested in a small variation on these questions.
More precisely, we fix only the bare minimum (so one subset, or a pair of points)
and we do not aim to find a trivial stabilizer, since this would be impossible in general,
but to find a stabilizer that has at least some good properties.
We will see that this can be done in a few interesting situations.
Regarding subsets, Babai
[
Bab22
]
has proved that in every solvable permutation group
there exists a set-stabilizer whose orbits have length bounded by a nonexplicit absolute constant.
Answering
[
Bab22
, Question 7a]
, our first main result provides the best possible constant:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(\Omega)
be a solvable permutation group.
Then there exists a subset of
\Omega
whose setwise stabilizer has all orbits of length at most
6
.
The constant
6
is the best possible.

The following consequence is immediate.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
G\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(\Omega)
be a solvable permutation group.
Then there exists a subset of
\Omega
whose setwise stabilizer has derived length at most
3
.

It happens that metabelian stabilizers need not to exist in general
(see Example
2.11
), so Corollary
1.2
answers
[
Bab22
, Question 7b]
.
Incidentally, the same example answers negatively a question in
[
Glu24
]
on the existence of supersolvable set-stabilizers.
We stress that the two constants
6
and
3
above turned out to be a little higher than what initially expected,
and better results can be obtained for primitive solvable groups.
As far as we know, Theorem
1.1
is the first statement concerning stabilizers of imprimitive groups
where the property involved is not preserved by a group extension,
and part of the difficulty of the proof is in finding the right statement to prove by induction.

With similar ideas, we are able to show that, in every solvable permutation group,
stabilizing two disjoint subsets can force the orbits to have length at most
2
.
This gives a strong positive answer to
[
Bab22
, Question 7c]
.
Moreover, we give some elementary proofs concerning nonsolvable groups:
we provide an easier proof of a theorem of Cameron
[
Cam86
]
, and answer
[
Bab22
, Question 6a]
.
In particular, we prove the following:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
G
be any permutation group on a finite set.
Then there exists a setwise stabilizer whose nonalternating composition factors have order bounded by an absolute constant.

This is new even using the Classification of Finite Simple Groups.

We come to the question of fixing two points.
Extending the work of Seress
[
Ser96
]
,
Burness
[
Bur21
]
showed that in a primitive group with solvable stabilizer,
there always exist
5
points whose pointwise stabilizer is trivial.
The hypothesis of having a solvable stabilizer is natural,
if we aim to structural results on the stabilizer of two points.
For primitive solvable groups, in
[
LS24
]
the authors observed
that there exist two points whose pointwise stabilizer has bounded derived length.
On the other hand, motivated by various applications,
Li and Zhang
[
LZ11
]
have classified the solvable maximal subgroups of the almost simple groups.
Combining their work with some properties of the finite simple groups, we remark the following:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
be an almost simple group, and let
H<G
be a maximal subgroup.
If
H
is solvable, then the derived length of
H
is at most
10
.
The constant
10
is attained only by the solvable maximal subgroups of the Fischer group
Fi_{{}_{23}}
.

Despite Theorem
1.4
,
it is easy to exhibit primitive nonsolvable groups of product type with solvable stabilizer of arbitrarily large derived length.
Dealing with the product type case, we are able to answer in the affirmative the Question at the end of
[
LS24
]
:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
G
be a primitive group with solvable stabilizer.
Then there exist two points whose pointwise stabilizer has derived length bounded by an absolute constant.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
is an application of Corollary
1.2
,
and it is perhaps surprising that, to answer a question about pointwise stabilizers of primitive nonsolvable groups,
one has to deal with set-stabilizers of arbitrary solvable permutation groups.
The idea, usually applied in the context of imprimitive linear groups,
is using a partition to construct a vector with small centralizer (see
[
MW04
, Sec. 4]
).
Our argument involves a double coset decomposition and a neat bound gives the constant
13
.
It is likely that a more in-depth study of almost simple groups leads to an improvement,
but the best possible constant is not even known for solvable groups
[
LS24
]
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we study set-stabilizers, proving Theorem
1.1
and the other related results.
In Section
3
, we prove Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
.
The methods are a mixture of group theory and combinatorics.

2.
Breaking symmetry with set-stabilizers

Let
G
be a finite group. The derived series is defined by
G^{(0)}=G
, and

G^{(n+1)}\>=\>[G^{(n)},G^{(n)}]

for all
n\geq 0
.
For a solvable group
G
, the
derived length

dl(G)
is the minimum
n
such that
G^{(n)}=1
.
For example,
dl(G)=1
if and only if
G
is abelian and
dl(G)\leq 2
if and only if
G
is metabelian.

When
G
acts on a finite set
\Omega
, then
G
acts naturally on the power set
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
.
If
\Delta\subseteq\Omega
,
we write
C_{G}(\Delta)
for the pointwise stabilizer, and
Stab_{G}(\Delta)
for the setwise stabilizer.
For each
g\in G
, it is easy to see that the stabilizers of
\Delta
and
\Delta^{g}
are conjugate subgroups.
As usual, we say that
\Delta
lies in a regular orbit (i.e., the orbit
\Delta^{G}
) if
Stab_{G}(\Delta)=1
.
Of course, as subgroups of
G
, the stabilizers still act on
\Omega
.

Definition
.

For
i\geq 1
,
we say that
\Delta\subseteq\Omega
lies in a
i
-regular
orbit if all the orbits of
Stab_{G}(\Delta)
on
\Omega
have length at most
i
.

The
1
-regular orbits are precisely the genuine regular orbits.
Moreover, unless explicitly stated otherwise, the
6
-regular orbits will also be called
good
orbits.
(This is motivated by Theorem
1.1
.)
We note that, by definition,

\mbox{ regular }\Rightarrow\mbox{ 2-regular }\Rightarrow\mbox{ 3-regular }\Rightarrow\mbox{ good }.

Remark 2.1
.

The total number of orbits of
G
on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is at least
|\Omega|+1
.

We now make a simple consideration.
If
G\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(\Omega)
and
\Delta=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{b}\}
is a base (i.e.,
C_{G}(\Delta)=1
),
then

Stab_{G}(\Delta)\cong Stab_{G}(\Delta)/C_{G}(\Delta)\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(b).

So, if
Stab_{G}(\Delta)
is solvable, then
dl(Stab_{G}(\Delta))\leq f(b)
, where

f(b)=\>\max\,\{dl(S):S\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(b),S\mbox{ solvable}\}.

We point out that
f(b)=O(\log b)

[
Dix68
]
.
When combined with
[
Ser96
]
, this gives that in every primitive solvable group
there exists a set-stabilizer of derived length at most
f(4)=3
.
We are going to see that a little more is true.

2.2.
Good colorings

The standard argument to deal with imprimitive groups is
[
Glu83
, Theorem 2]
,
which was used by Gluck to show that permutation groups of odd order have a regular orbit on the power set.
This does not work with a property such as “have all orbits of length at most
C
”, where
C
is a constant.
We use a different method based on good orbits and partitions that we are going to explain.
A key observation, already in the lines of the proof of
[
Bab22
, Theorem 12.1]
,
is that studying stabilizers of partitions
can also be useful to obtain results concerning stabilizers of a single subset.

We need some more definitions.
An
asymmetric coloring
of a permutation group
G
on
\Omega
is a partition of
\Omega

with the property that only the identity fixes every part.
We refer the reader to
[
Bab22
, Sec. 2]
for some history concerning breaking-symmetry results and some conflicting terminology.
We refine this notion as follows:

Definition
.

Let
G\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(\Omega)
, and
k\geq 2
.
A
k
-coloring
is a coloring of
\Omega
with at most
k
colors.
For
i\geq 1
, an
i
-asymmetric
k
-coloring
is a
k
-coloring
such that the stabilizer of the coloring in
G
has all orbits on
\Omega
of length at most
i
.

The
1
-asymmetric colorings are precisely the genuine asymmetric colorings.
Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the
6
-asymmetric colorings will also be called
good
colorings.
The total number of
k
-colorings of
\Omega
is
k^{|\Omega|}
,
but many of these are equivalent under the action of
G
.
Fixed
G\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(\Omega)
, for each
k\geq 2
we write

\ell_{k,1},\hskip 14.22636pt\ell_{k,2},\hskip 14.22636pt\ell_{k,3},\hskip 14.22636pt\ell_{k,6},

for the numbers of
G
-orbits of asymmetric,
2
-asymmetric,
3
-asymmetric and good
k
-colorings.
When necessary, we write
\ell_{k,i}(G)
to stress the group
G
we are referring to.
It is obvious that, for every
k
and
i\leq j
,

(2.1)

\ell_{k+1,i}\geq\ell_{k,i},\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 28.45274pt\ell_{k,j}\geq\ell_{k,i}.

We observe that describing a
2
-coloring is equivalent to describing a subset.
Therefore,
\ell_{2,1}
,
\ell_{2,2}
,
\ell_{2,3}
and
\ell_{2,6}
coincide respectively with
the numbers of regular,
2
-regular,
3
-regular and good orbits on the power set.
Restated in terms of colorings, Lemma
2.4
says that
\ell_{4,1}\geq 1
for every primitive solvable group
(one color is needed for the complement).

A portion of the proof of Theorem
1.1
relies
on showing that
\ell_{6-i,i}\geq 5
for all primitive solvable groups and each
i=1,2,3
.
The following combinatorial lemma is useful to estimate
\ell_{k,i}
for large
k
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Fix
k\geq 3
and
i\in\{1,2,3,6\}
.
If
\ell_{2,i}\geq 1
, then
\ell_{k,i}\geq{k\choose 2}
.
If
\ell_{j,i}>\ell_{j-1,i}
for some
3\leq j\leq k
, then
\ell_{k,i}\geq{k\choose j}
.

Proof.

The inequality
\ell_{j,i}>\ell_{j-1,i}
means that there exists a suitable coloring that involves precisely
j
colors.
Now suppose we have such a
j
-coloring.
For each subset of cardinality
j
of the
k
available colors, we obtain an inequivalent suitable
j
-coloring.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
A
be a primitive solvable group. Then
\ell_{5,1}(A)\geq 5
.

Proof.

Fix
A
. We have
\ell_{4,1}(A)\geq 1
by Lemma
2.4
.
This means that
\ell_{2,1}(A)\geq 1
, or the inequality
\ell_{j,1}(A)>\ell_{j-1,1}(A)
occurs at some index
j=3,4
.
Considering the worst case, we obtain

\ell_{5,1}(A)\geq\min_{j=2,3,4}{5\choose j}=5,

where we used Lemma
2.5
with
k=5
,
i=1
.
∎

The next result concerns
3
-colorings.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
A
be a primitive solvable group.
If
A\neq 1
, then
\ell_{3,2}(A)\geq 5
.

Proof.

Let
\Omega
be the base set, so that
|\Omega|\geq 2
.
By Lemma
2.2
and (
2.1
), we can assume
|\Omega|\leq 9
.
Since we are interested in a lower bound on the number of orbits, it is enough to deal with the solvable (over)groups

\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(2),\hskip 5.69046pt\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(3),\hskip 5.69046pt\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(4),\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{AGL}_{1}(5),\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{AGL}_{1}(7),\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{A\Gamma L}_{1}(8),\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{AGL}_{2}(3).

It is easy to see that
\ell_{3,2}
is at least
5
(actually
6
) in all these cases.
In particular, using
[
GAP4
]
, the command

OrbitsDomain( A , Tuples([1..k], NrMovedPoints(A)) , Permuted )

returns the orbits of

on the

-colorings of

.

2.3.
Imprimitive groups

We now explain the general strategy to deal with colorings of imprimitive groups.
A similar idea is contained in
[
Ser96
, Proof of Theorem 1.2]
, but we will go a little further.
Let
A
and
B
be permutation groups on the finite sets
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
respectively.
Let
G\leqslant B\wr_{|\Omega_{1}|}A
act imprimitively on
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
.
Let
x,y\geq 2
be integers.
Let
X=(X_{i})_{i=1}^{x}
be a
x
-coloring of
\Omega_{1}
, namely
\Omega_{1}=\sqcup_{i=1}^{x}X_{i}
.
Moreover, suppose we have
x
inequivalent
y
-colorings of
\Omega_{2}
, say
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{x}
.
In particular, for each
i=1,\ldots,x
, we have that
\Omega_{2}=\sqcup_{j=1}^{y}Y_{i,j}
.
The idea is to use the
y
-colorings of
\Omega_{2}
as colors for
\Omega_{1}
.
(In fact, we intentionally used the same index
i
to count the colors in
X
and the colorings of
\Omega_{2}
).
We will construct a new
y
-coloring of
\Omega
, say
Z=(Z_{j})_{j=1}^{y}
, as follows.
For each
j=1,\ldots y
, define

Z_{j}\>=\>\{(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\Omega:\omega_{1}\in X_{i}\mbox{ and }\omega_{2}\in Y_{i,j}\mbox{ for some fixed }i\}.

It is easy to see that
(Z_{j})_{j=1}^{y}
is actually a partition of
\Omega
.

Lemma 2.8
.

The stabilizer of the coloring
Z
in
G
can be embedded in the semidirect product
between the direct product of the stabilizers of the colorings
Y_{i}
’s in
B

and the stabilizer of the coloring
X
in
A
.

Proof.

First suppose
G=B\wr_{|\Omega_{1}|}A
.
Since the colorings of
\Omega_{2}
are inequivalent, the stabilizer of
Z
is precisely the above semidirect product.
The general case follows.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Inequivalent
x
-colorings of
\Omega_{1}
provide inequivalent
y
-colorings of
\Omega
.

Proof.

Fix
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{x}
colorings of
\Omega_{2}
, and let
X,X^{\prime}
be two colorings of
\Omega_{1}
.
Let
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
be the colorings of
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
obtained from
X
and
X^{\prime}
respectively.
If
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
lies in the same orbit of
B\wr_{|\Omega_{1}|}A
,
then the induced colorings of
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
on
\Omega_{1}
need to be in the same orbit of
A
.
But these restrictions are precisely
X
and
X^{\prime}
, and the proof follows.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.
We actually prove the following stronger statement, that lends itself to an inductive argument:

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
G\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(\Omega)
be a solvable permutation group.
If
|\Omega|\geq 2
, then there are at least
3
good orbits on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
.
If there are less than
5
good orbits on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, then
|\Omega|\leq 3
.

Proof.

We work by induction on the cardinality of
\Omega
.
Suppose that
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{t}
are the orbits of
G
on
\Omega,

and that we can find subsets
\Delta_{j}\subseteq\Omega_{j}
such that
Stab_{G}(\Delta_{j})

has orbits of length at most
6
on
\Omega_{j}
, for each
j=1,\ldots,t
.
Then
Stab_{G}(\sqcup_{j}\>\Delta_{j})
will surely work.
It follows that we can suppose that
G
is transitive.
Primitive groups can be handled with Lemma
2.2
and Table
1
.
Let
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
, with
|\Omega_{1}|\geq 2
.
Let
G\leqslant B\wr_{|\Omega_{1}|}A
, with
A
primitive on
\Omega_{1}
, and
B
transitive on
\Omega_{2}
.
By the inductive hypothesis applied to
\Omega_{2}
, we are in one of the following cases:

•

B
has at least
5
good orbits on

Primitive groups with solvable stabilizer

In this section, we move to consider pointwise stabilizers of very small sets.

In the case of primitive solvable groups, Theorem

Lemma 3.2

3.2.

Let

By

[

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let

H<G

Let

be the subgroup of

such that

Stabilizing two points corresponds to intersecting

To prove the theorem, we will find

The proof will follow, because
H\cap H^{v}\leqslant I_{v}
,
and the derived length of a subgroup is bounded by the derived length of the group.
Let
\pi\colon G\to S
be the natural projection, and let

.
Since

(3.1)

dl(I_{v})\>\leq\>dl(I_{v}\cap V)+dl(\pi(I_{v})).

We observe that

This gives

, and therefore we can write

dl(I_{v}\cap V)\leq dl(W)=dl(Y).

Now

by Theorem

, and the rest of the proof deals with

.
It is not hard to see that

\pi(I_{v})
can be quite bigger.
For every

,

and

(w,\sigma)^{v}=(v^{-1}wv^{\sigma},\sigma).

This implies that

\pi(I_{v})\>=\>\{\sigma\in S:\>W\cap v^{-1}Wv^{\sigma}\neq\emptyset\}.

Now let

\pi(I_{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})})=\{\sigma\in S:\>Y\cap x_{i}^{-1}Yx_{\sigma(i)}\neq\emptyset\mbox{ for each }i=1,\ldots,d\}.

Since

Y\cap x_{i}^{-1}Yx_{\sigma(i)}\neq\emptyset\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ if and only if }\hskip 28.45274ptx_{\sigma(i)}\in Yx_{i}Y,

we obtain the key equality

\pi(I_{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})})\>=\>\{\sigma\in S:\>x_{\sigma(i)}\in Yx_{i}Y\mbox{ for each }i=1,\ldots,d\}.

\delta\in\{2,3,4\}
.
Let
G\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(\Omega)
be a primitive group with solvable stabilizer.
Then there exists
\Delta\subseteq\Omega
,
|\Delta|=\delta
,
such that
Stab_{G}(\Delta)
is solvable of derived length bounded by an absolute constant.

Proof.

Let
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\subseteq\Omega
be as provided by Theorem
1.5
.
If
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\subseteq\Delta
, then
C_{G}(\Delta)
is solvable of bounded derived length.
Now
Stab_{G}(\Delta)/C_{G}(\Delta)\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(4)
,
and it follows that
Stab_{G}(\Delta)
is solvable of bounded derived lenght.
∎
