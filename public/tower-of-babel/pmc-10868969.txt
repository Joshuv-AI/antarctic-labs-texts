Variable Presentations of Photodermatoses - A Case Series
Authors: Sri Vaishnavi Renukuntla, Ketan K. Kolekar, Yoganand J. Phulari
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Photodermatoses represent a broad spectrum of skin diseases caused because of exposure to ultraviolet radiation. It is categorized mainly as idiopathic photosensitive disorders, drug or chemical induced photosensitivity reactions, DNA repair-deficiency photodermatoses and photoaggravated dermatoses. Despite being under the photodermatoses umbrella, the pathophysiology of each type of photodermatoses varies. We reported 4 cases of photodermatoses including azathioprine induced pellagra, adult onset actinic prurigo, and photoallergic contact dermatitis due to NSAIDs and cutting fluids. Photoprotection with the usage of photoprotective clothing, broad-spectrum sunscreen application and avoidance of photosensitizing drugs and chemicals are crucial.

Introduction

Photodermatoses is a term used to refer to skin diseases instigated/exaggerated due to exposure to ultraviolet (UV) rays. Photodermatoses is categorized into 4 classes such as “immunologically mediated photodermatoses, chemical- induced and drug-induced photosensitivity, photoaggravated dermatoses, and inherited disorders with defective DNA repair or with chromosomal instability.”[1] These photodermatoses involve different pathophysiology and clinical presentations.[2] In this case series, we presented 4 cases of photodermatoses due to various etiologies.

Case History

Case 1

Azathioprine induced pellagra in a case of pemphigus vulgaris

A 46-year-old female farmer, a known case of pemphigus vulgaris for one-and-half years, was being treated with oral azathioprine 50 mg once daily for 10 months along with oral corticosteroids. The lesions initially resembled a sunburn with minimal pain but later progressed to asymptomatic dark, dry scaly lesions confined to sun-exposed areas over both forearms, hands, and feet [Figure 1]. Associated complaints include loose stools (4–5 episodes per day) and insomnia. The patient had sun exposure of 2–3 hours per day. The patient was non-alcoholic.

Showing sharply demarcated scaly hyperpigmented plaques over both extensor aspects of forearms and dorsum of hands

On examination, bilaterally symmetrical, sharply demarcated scaly hyperpigmented plaques over both forearms, dorsum of hands, and feet were seen.

All routine investigations were within normal limits. The patient was clinically suspected of drug-induced pellagra secondary to Azathioprine, which was discontinued and substituted with cyclophosphamide 50 mg once daily. The patient was given Tab. Nicotinamide 250 mg twice daily with complete resolution of lesions on follow-up after 15 days [Figure 2] and resolution of diarrhea within a week.

Showing complete resolution of lesions

Case 2

Adult onset actinic prurigo

A 26-year-old male civil engineer presented with complaints of moderately itchy lesions mainly over sun-exposed areas for 5 years. History of average sun exposure of 3–4 hours per day. History of worsening during summer and remission during winter. The rash first involved the face and later progressed to the sun-exposed areas of the upper limbs and feet. There were no similar complaints in the family, no history of atopy, and no history of cheilitis and conjunctivitis.

On examination - bilaterally symmetrical, multiple, discrete, lichenified papules and nodules, few with excoriations present over the face, ear lobes, “V” region of the neck, scalp, extensors of forearms and dorsa of hands and feet [Figures 3 and 4].

Showing discrete excoriated papules over the face

Multiple, discrete, lichenified nodules, and papules seen over both forearms and hands

The biopsy showed mild superficial perivascular infiltrate of lymphocytes, eosinophils with mild to moderate irregular epidermal hyperplasia, thickened granular layer with stratum corneum showing marked compact orthokeratosis and focal parakeratosis. Thickened papillary dermis with papillomatosis and thickened collagen bundles in a vertical array. The capillaries in the papillary dermis are increased in number and are thick walled with an impression of actinic prurigo [Figure 5]. Laboratory tests excluded lupus erythematosus and porphyria. A diagnosis of adult-onset actinic prurigo was established based on history, clinical features, and histopathology.

Cases 3 and 4

Photoallergic contact dermatitis

Case 3: A 70-year-old male farmer had an itchy red rash over his face, neck, forearms, and hands for 9 months. History of topical diclofenac gel application over the forehead 24–48 hours before the appearance of the rash. The rash was initially confined to the area of application, later it spread to include the face, neck, and upper limbs over the span of the next 6 months [Figure 6]. The rash was initially more eczematous with oozing; later it became lichenified. History of worsening itching after sun exposure. On examination, bilaterally symmetrical, well-defined erythematous to hyperpigmented, scaly oozy plaques with lichenified changes and crusting at few sites were present over the face, “V” area of the neck, extensors of forearms, and dorsal aspects of hands, with sparing of nasolabial folds, eyelids, and retro auricular areas.

Showing well-defined, lichenified scaly plaques over sun-exposed areas

Case 4: A 43-year-old male, an auto mechanic, presented with a moderate to severely itchy red rash over his face for 6 months with aggravation after contact with cutting fluids and sun exposure. On examination, well-defined erythematous, slightly edematous, scaly plaques were present over the forehead, bilateral malar area, and nose with sparing of the retroauricular fold and eyelids [Figure 7].

Showing well-defined erythematous scaly plaques over the face

On histopathology, both the biopsies showed dense superficial and mid-perivascular lymphohistiocytic infiltrate with mild spongiotic psoriasiform findings in the epidermis which shows exocytosis of lymphocytes. The granular layer is thickened in places, and the stratum corneum displays compact orthokeratosis with focal parakeratosis and plasma globules. The papillary dermis is edematous with few eosinophils seen in the interstitium with the impression of photoallergic dermatitis [Figures 8 and 9].

Showing dense superficial and mid-perivascular lymphohistiocytic infiltrate with mild spongiotic psoriasiform changes in the epidermis, exocytosis of lymphocytes. The thickened granular layer in places, focal parakeratosis and plasma globules. Edematous papillary dermis with few eosinophils in the interstitium. (H and E, 10x)

Showing dense superficial and mid-perivascular infiltrate of lymphohistiocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils with mild spongiotic psoriasiform changes in the epidermis, exocytosis of lymphocytes. The thickened granular layer in places, focal parakeratosis. Edematous papillary dermis with few eosinophils in the interstitium. (H and E, 10x)

Both patients were diagnosed with photoallergic contact dermatitis based on history, clinical examination, and histopathology. The suspected topical photo allergen was discontinued and treated with an application of broad-spectrum sunscreen, usage of photoprotective wear and hat, topical steroids, and low-dose systemic steroids with significant improvement.

Discussion

Photodermatoses are a broad spectrum of disorders developed due to adverse reactions to solar UV radiation.[1]

A deficiency of niacin or tryptophan is responsible for pellagra. Azathioprine is one of the drugs responsible for pellagra.[2] Azathioprine is metabolized to 6-Mercaptopurine which causes reduction of NAD and NADP, essential for the niacin breakdown pathway or other metabolic pathways. Thus, decreased concentration of these coenzymes results in improper functioning of the organs that require high energy such as the brain, gut, and skin.[2] In the best of our known information only five cases of azathioprine-induced pellagra have been reported in the literature.[3456] We presented a similar case of azathioprine-caused pellagra, in a case of Pemphigus vulgaris.

“Actinic prurigo (AP) is a chronic, pruritic skin disease” that results due to an abnormal reaction to sunlight. The development of AP at ≥21 years of age is known as adult or old onset AP which is highly prevalent in Asians.

Adult-onset AP has a less satisfactory prognosis because of the perseverance of lesions. Ker et al. carried out a study for the duration of 9 years which suggested that the prevalence of AP is more in males than females (4.5:1) where the face, forearms, and hands were the most common sites.[7]

“Photoallergic contact dermatitis (PACD),” a sub-type of allergic contact dermatitis, results from a exposure to specific combination of topical chemicals and UV radiation. “PACD is a delayed type-IV hypersensitivity reaction”; it ensues when an exogenous chemical is used topically over the skin with associated exposure to UV rays, more specifically the UV-A range.

Topical NSAIDs such as ketoprofen, etofenamate, and diclofenac gel are known to cause PACD.[8] Topical diclofenac gel contain diclofenac sodium and other inactive ingredients together with PEG; PEG is a well-known allergen that can induce PACD.[9] Gulin SJ, and Chiriac A. reported four cases of diclofenac-induced PACD. In the series, patients presented with intense itchy eczematous lesions on the locations of drug application after numerous days of treatment.[10] Moreover, Sakakibara S. et al.[11] presented a case report of a 39-year-old male with PACD due to mineral oil (cutting fluids). These findings resembled our case presentations.
