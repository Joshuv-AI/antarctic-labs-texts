Abstract
. This work establishes a general stochastic maximum principle for partially observed optimal control of semi-linear stochastic partial differential equations in a nonconvex control domain. The state evolves in a Hilbert space driven by a cylindrical Wiener process and finitely many Brownian motions, while observations are in an Euclidean space having correlated noise. For convex control domain and diffusion coefficients in the state being control-independent, numerical algorithms are developed to solve the partially observed optimal control problems using stochastic gradient descent algorithm combined with finite element approximations and the branching filtering algorithm. Numerical experiments are conducted for demonstration.

Subsequently, the corresponding controlled stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs)
were considered; the adjoint states have been characterized either by operator-valued BSDEs
[

or by stochastic bilinear forms

The setting of our problem naturally arises in many real-world applications. In large-scale distributed systems, the full state of the system is often either physically inaccessible or of such
high dimensionality that direct monitoring becomes prohibitively and/or costly. However, partial observations are typically available through another dynamic process (such as a monitoring or observer system) whose state is fully accessible. Based on this available
possibly incomplete and imperfect, information, the objective is to optimally control the main system to achieve desired outcomes.

To further illustrate the motivation for studying the partially observed control problem in our setting, consider an aquatic ecosystem as an example. Aquatic environments such as the Great Lakes, are complex systems in which water quality and marine life depend on a delicate balance of organic and inorganic agents. The concentration of these agents, such as pollutants and nutrients, within the water body can be modeled by a partial differential equation (PDE) subject to noise, as follows

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}&\!\displaystyle\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}-D\Delta C+(\nabla C)v=b(C,u)+N_{C},\quad t\geq 0,\;\xi\in\mathcal{O}\\
&\!\displaystyle C|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0,\quad C(0,\xi)=C_{0}(\xi),\,\xi\in\mathcal{O},\end{array}\right.

where
\mathcal{O}
is assumed to be an open, connected, bounded domain representing the aquatic body. Here
C
is the concentration level of
m_{1}
different organic and inorganic agents such as pollutants and nutrients. The third term on the left of the first equation (
1.1
) represents the transport of
C
due to water movement, where
v
is the given velocity as a function of space-time. The function
b
represents the interactions between
m_{2}
different control agents

and
m_{1}
different pollutants and nutrients
C
. The

is the distributed noise that represents the additive effect such as land runoffs from surrounding farmlands, acid rain, accidental oil spills, summer cottages, etc.

The aquatic system supports diverse species, including micro-organism and fish populations, where the stock of fish is regulated by the Department of Fisheries. The biomass per unit volume of
m_{3}
different species of population

can be modeled by

(1.2)

dy=h(C,y)dt+N_{y},\quad y(0)=0,t\geq 0

with

being
the growth vector and

being
the noise vector. The

can be
the standard logistic growth function. The Department of Fisheries and Environments is interested in designing a control policy to promote marine life and water quality, such as the application of anti-pollutants, biological agents predating unwanted microorganisms, physical removal of solid water, algae, etc. They would like to minimize their cost to achieve their goals. We refer to

In addition to the

In the classical optimal control problem, there are two main frameworks to approximate optimal controls: (i) the method based on stochastic maximum principles, and (ii) the method based on the dynamical programming principles (DPPs). The well-established DPP-based methodology is the Markov chain approximation method; see

Note that there is a recent work using deep learning to solve nonlinear filtering problem

This method is seen

[

[

Let
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},{\mathbb{P}})
be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual condition, on which are defined a cylindrical Wiener process
W
in a Hilbert space
\Xi
and a

in

, which are assumed to be independent of each other. Consider the following stochastic partial differential equations

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!\!dx_{t}^{u}&\!\displaystyle\!\!\!\!=[\Delta x_{t}^{u}+b(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})]dt+\sigma(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})dW_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}g^{j}(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})dB_{t}^{j}\;\text{ on }[0,T]\times L^{2}(\Lambda),\\
\!\!x_{0}^{u}&\!\displaystyle\!\!\!\!=x_{0},\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Lambda).\end{array}\right.

Here
\Lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
is a bounded interval,
b
,
\sigma
, and
g^{j}
are Nemytskii operators. The control
u
takes values in the domain
U
, which is a nonempty subset of a separable Banach space
\mathcal{U}
. Furthermore,
W
is a cylindrical Wiener process in a Hilbert space
\Xi
. In what follows,
we use the Einstein summation convention by suppressing
the summation symbol
(e.g.,
\sum_{j=1}^{d}
) in (
1.3
).

Suppose that system state
x_{t}^{u}
is not completely observable, instead, one observes a function of
x_{t}^{u}
subject to some observation noise, giving rise to the following observation process:

(1.4)

dY_{t}=h(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})dt+dB_{t},

where
h
is a mapping from
L^{2}(\Lambda)\times U
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We remark that unlike classical partially observable system where the observation noise is modeled by a Wiener process
B
, we consider
Y

a priori
as a Wiener process under original measure
{\mathbb{P}}
on probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{F}}_{t},{\mathbb{P}})
. Imagine if we consider
B
being
the Wiener process in classical setting, applying the Girsanov theorem implies that
Y
will be the Wiener process under a new measure. Here we define
Y
as a Wiener process
at the beginning. This setting is standard in the literature and is appropriate from a modeling perspective that facilitates the
derivation of the SMP; see
[
38
, Remark 1]
.

Putting (
1.4
) in (
1.3
), we have

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dx_{t}^{u}&\!\displaystyle=[\Delta x_{t}^{u}+(b-g^{j}h^{j})(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})]dt+\sigma(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})dW_{t}+g^{j}(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})dY_{t}^{j}\\
x_{0}^{u}&\!\displaystyle=x_{0}\in L^{2}(\Lambda).\end{array}\right.

To deal with the partially observed optimal control nonlinear system, we use the measure transformation technique. Let us introduce

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle\rho_{t}^{u}:=\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t}h^{j,*}(x^{u}_{s},u_{s})dY_{s}^{j}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|h(x^{u}_{s},u_{s})|^{2}ds\Big\},\end{array}

where
h
is a bounded continuous function from
L^{2}(\Lambda)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then it can be shown that
z^{u}
satisfies the following stochastic differential equation

(1.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d\rho_{t}^{u}&\!\displaystyle=\rho_{t}^{u}h^{*}(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})dY_{t},\\
\rho_{0}^{u}&\!\displaystyle=1.\end{array}\right.

Define a new measure
{\mathbb{Q}}
by

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle\frac{d{\mathbb{Q}}}{d{\mathbb{P}}}\Big|_{{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}:=\rho_{T}^{u}.\end{array}

Since
\rho_{t}^{u}
is an exponential
martingale, we have
d{\mathbb{Q}}=\rho_{t}^{u}d{\mathbb{P}}
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
. Then the Girsanov theorem implies that
W
and
B

are independent cylindrical Wiener processes and finite-dimensional Brownian motions, respectively, in the new probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{F}}_{t},{\mathbb{Q}})
.

The cost functional we consider is given by

(1.7)

\begin{array}[]{ll}J(u(\cdot))&\!\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\ell(x_{t}^{u}(\lambda),u_{t})d\lambda dt+\int_{\Lambda}m(x^{u}_{T}(\lambda))d\lambda\Big]\\
&\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\rho_{t}^{u}\,\ell(x_{t}^{u}(\lambda),u_{t})d\lambda dt+\int_{\Lambda}\rho_{T}^{u}\,m(x_{T}^{u}(\lambda))d\lambda\Big],\end{array}

where
{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}
denotes the expectation with respect to probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{F}}_{t},{\mathbb{Q}})
and
{\mathbb{E}}:={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{P}}
denotes the expectation with respect to probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{F}}_{t},{\mathbb{P}})
.
Define
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}:=\sigma(Y_{s};s\leq t)
. Our partially observed control problem is to find
\widehat{u}
to minimize the cost functional (
1.7
) over
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{ad}}
, that is,

(1.8)

J(\widehat{u})=\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{ad}}}J(u(\cdot)),

where
\mathcal{U}_{\text{ad}}
is the admissible control set defined as

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\mathcal{U}_{\text{ad}}:=\{u:[0,T]\times\Omega\to U:u\text{ is }{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}\text{-adapted and }\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}[\|u_{t}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{k}]<\infty,\forall\,k\in{\mathbb{N}}\}.\end{array}

Let
\widehat{u}
be an optimal control of the partially observed problem (
1.8
), and let
\widehat{x}
be the associated optimal state. The goal of this paper is to establish
necessary conditions for the partially observed optimal control
\widehat{u}
and to develop efficient numerical algorithms to compute
\widehat{u}
. To the best of
our knowledge, this is the first study on a general SMP for partially observed optimal control of SPDEs with correlated state-observation noise, where the control affects all diffusion coefficients in a nonconvex domain.
In addition, we also develop efficient numerical algorithms.

The rest of the
paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the notation, assumptions, and main results, namely, Pontryagin’s maximum principle and Peng’s maximum principle under convex and nonconvex control domain, respectively. In Section
3
, we study the variational inequalities of the spike variation to prove Peng’s maximum principle. In Section
4
, we characterize the adjoint equation by backward SPDEs. Section
5
provides numerical algorithms to solve the partially observed optimal control of SPDEs. Finally,
an numerical example is presented in Section
6
for demonstration.

3.
Variational inequality

3.1.
Spike variation

Since the control domain is non-convex, we adopt the spike variation argument as in Peng
[
32
]
. Namely, fix any
v\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{ad}}
,
\tau\in(0,T)
, we define

(3.1)

u_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v,\quad\text{ if }\tau\leq t\leq\tau+\varepsilon,\\
\widehat{u}_{t},\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Let
x_{t}^{\varepsilon},\rho_{t}^{\varepsilon}
be the solutions of (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) corresponding to
u^{\varepsilon}
.

3.2.
Derivation of
variational inequality

We look for the cost variation of
J(u^{\varepsilon})-J(\widehat{u})
. It involves variations of
x^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{x}
and
\rho^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{\rho}
. To proceed, we introduce the following abbreviations:

\delta f(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t}):=f(\widehat{x}_{t},u^{\varepsilon}_{t})-f(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})
, and
\delta f(t,\lambda;u^{\varepsilon}_{t}):=f(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-f(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})
for
f=b,g^{j},h^{j},(b-g^{j}h^{j}),\ell,H
as well as their derivatives in
x
. We will use
\delta f(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})
(resp.
\delta f(t,\lambda;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})
) and
f(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-f(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})
(resp.
f(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-f(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})
) interchangeably for the rest of the paper.

Let
x^{1,\varepsilon}
be the solution of the SPDE

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dx_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle=[\Delta x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+(b-g^{j}h^{j})_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}]dt\\
&\displaystyle\quad+[\sigma_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\sigma(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\sigma(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})]dW_{t}\\
&\displaystyle\quad+[g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+g^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-g^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})]dY_{t}^{j},\\
x_{0}^{1,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle=0,\end{array}\right.

and
\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}
be the solution of SDE:

(3.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle=\{\rho^{1,\varepsilon}_{t}h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})+\widehat{\rho}_{t}[h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+(h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t}))]\}dY_{t}^{j},\\
\rho_{0}^{1,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle=0.\end{array}\right.

Equations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) are the first variational equations of
x^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{x}
and
\rho^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{\rho}
, respectively. The second variational equations are given by the solution of
(x_{t}^{2,\varepsilon},\rho_{t}^{2,\varepsilon})
satisfying:

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!\!\!dx_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!=[\Delta x_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}+(b-g^{j}h^{j})_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}]dt\\
&\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!+[(b-g^{j}h^{j})(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-(b-g^{j}h^{j})(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}(b-g^{j}h^{j})_{xx}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}]dt\\
&\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!+\big[\sigma_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}+(\sigma_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\sigma_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t}))x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{xx}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\big]dW_{t}\\
&\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!+\big[g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}+(g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t}))x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}g_{xx}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\big]dY_{t}^{j},\\
x_{0}^{2,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!=0,\end{array}\right.

and

(3.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d\rho_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle=[\rho_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})+\widehat{\rho}_{t}h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}+(h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t}))\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\\
&\displaystyle\quad+\widehat{\rho}_{t}(h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t}))x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\rho}_{t}h_{xx}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\\
&\displaystyle\quad+\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\big]dY_{t}^{j},\\
\rho_{0}^{2,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle=0.\end{array}\right.

Compared to classical optimal control without partial observations, we have two pairs
(x^{1,\varepsilon},x^{2,\varepsilon})
and
(\rho^{1,\varepsilon},\rho^{2,\varepsilon})
of variational equations. It is because our state process have two components
(x,\rho)
after using the Girsanov transformation. Following
[
36
]
and
[
26
]
, one can establish the following estimates

Lemma 3.1
.

We have

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\|x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}^{2k}]&\!\displaystyle\leq K\varepsilon^{k},\quad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\|x_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}^{k}\big]\leq K\varepsilon^{k},\\
\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}[|\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}|^{2k}]&\!\displaystyle\leq K\varepsilon^{k},\quad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}[|\rho_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}|^{k}]\leq K\varepsilon^{k},\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\|x_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{x}_{t}-x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}-x_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}^{2}\big]&\!\displaystyle\leq o(\varepsilon^{2}),\\
\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[|\rho_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{\rho}_{t}-\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}-\rho_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}|^{2}\big]&\!\displaystyle\leq o(\varepsilon^{2}).\end{array}

Proof.

The proof of the above inequalities can be found in
[
36
, Lemma 3.1, 3.2]
and
[
26
, Lemma 3.2]
. Thus, details are omitted.
∎

We are now in a position to establish the variational inequality for the cost functional.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have

(3.6)

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\!\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{P}}\Big\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\widehat{\rho}_{t}\big[\ell_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})(x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)+x_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}(\lambda))+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{xx}(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\ell(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\ell(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})\big]d\lambda dt\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\int_{\Lambda}\widehat{\rho}_{T}\Big[m_{x}(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))(x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)+x_{T}^{2,\varepsilon}(\lambda))+\frac{1}{2}m_{xx}(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)\Big]d\lambda\Big\}\\
&\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{P}}\Big\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\Big[(\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\rho_{t}^{2,\varepsilon})\ell(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})+\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\ell_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)\Big]d\lambda dt\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\int_{\Lambda}\big[(\rho_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}+\rho_{T}^{2,\varepsilon})m(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))+\rho_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}m_{x}(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)\big]d\lambda\Big\}\leq o(\varepsilon).\end{array}

Equivalently,

(3.7)

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\!\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\big[\ell_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})(x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)+x_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}(\lambda))+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{xx}(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\ell(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\ell(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})\big]d\lambda dt\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\int_{\Lambda}\Big[m_{x}(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))(x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)+x_{T}^{2,\varepsilon}(\lambda))+\frac{1}{2}m_{xx}(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)\Big]d\lambda\Big\}\\
&\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\widehat{\rho}^{-1}_{t}\big[(\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\rho_{t}^{2,\varepsilon})\ell(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})+\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\ell_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)\big]d\lambda dt\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\int_{\Lambda}\widehat{\rho}^{-1}_{T}\big[(\rho_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}+\rho_{T}^{2,\varepsilon})m(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))+\rho_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}m_{x}(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)\big]d\lambda\Big\}\\
&\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}_{1}^{\mathbb{Q}}+{\mathbb{E}}_{2}^{\mathbb{Q}}\leq o(\varepsilon).\end{array}

Proof.

The proof of this lemma can be obtained from
[
36
, Lemma 3.4]
and
[
26
, Lemma 3.3]
. The details are omitted.
∎

4.
Adjoint representation of variational inequality

4.1.
Rewrite of
{\mathbb{E}}_{1}^{\mathbb{Q}}+{\mathbb{E}}_{2}^{\mathbb{Q}}

The Itô formula implies that the solution of (
3.3
) and (
3.5
) have the following explicit expressions:

(4.1)

\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}=\widehat{\rho}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}[h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}+(h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})-h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s}))]dB_{s}^{j},

and

(4.2)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle=\widehat{\rho}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}[h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})x_{s}^{2,\varepsilon}+\delta h_{x}^{j}(s;u_{s}^{\varepsilon})x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}h_{xx}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}]\\
&\displaystyle\quad+\rho_{s}^{-1}\rho_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}[h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})-h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})+h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}]dB_{s}^{j}.\end{array}

Set

(4.3)

\Gamma_{t}:=(\widehat{\rho}_{t})^{-1}(\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\rho_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}).

Then (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) implies that

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{s}^{j}dB_{s}^{j},\end{array}

where
\gamma_{s}:=(\gamma_{s}^{1},\dots,\gamma_{s}^{d})
with
\gamma_{s}^{j}
defined as

(4.4)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\gamma_{s}^{j}&\!\displaystyle:=h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})(x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}+x_{s}^{2,\varepsilon})+[h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})-h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})]\\
&\displaystyle\quad+\delta h_{x}^{j}(s;u_{s}^{\varepsilon})x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}h_{xx}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}\\
&\displaystyle\quad+\widehat{\rho}_{s}^{-1}\rho_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}[h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})-h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{x}_{s})+h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}].\end{array}

We note that

(4.5)

\Gamma_{0}=0,\quad\Gamma_{T}=(\widehat{\rho}_{T})^{-1}(\rho_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}+\rho_{T}^{2,\varepsilon}).

Applying the Itô formula to
z_{t}\Gamma_{t}
, we have

(4.6)

{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}z_{T}\Gamma_{T}={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}z_{0}\Gamma_{0}+{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\big[\langle z_{2,s},\gamma_{s}\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}-\Gamma_{s}\ell(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})\big]ds.

Then plugging (
4.3
), (
4.4
), and (
4.5
) into (
4.6
), we obtain

(4.7)

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\!\displaystyle\!\!\!\!{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}(\widehat{\rho}_{t})^{-1}[\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\rho_{t}^{2,\varepsilon}]\ell(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t})d\lambda dt+\int_{\Lambda}(\widehat{\rho}_{T})^{-1}[\rho_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}+\rho_{T}^{2,\varepsilon}]m(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))d\lambda\Big\}\\
&\displaystyle\!\!\!\!={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle z_{2,s}^{j},h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})(x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}+x_{s}^{2,\varepsilon})+[h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})-h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})]\\
&\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\qquad\qquad\quad+\frac{1}{2}h_{xx}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}}ds\\
&\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\quad+{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle z_{2,s}^{j},h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})[(\widehat{\rho}_{s})^{-1}\rho_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}]\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}}dt+o(\varepsilon).\end{array}

In the above equality, from Lemma
3.1
, we used the fact that

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle z_{2,s}^{j},\delta h_{x}^{j}(s;u_{s}^{\varepsilon})x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}+(\widehat{\rho_{s}})^{-1}\rho_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}[h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})-h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})]\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}}ds=o(\varepsilon).\end{array}

Consider remaining terms in (
3.7
) of
{\mathbb{E}}_{2}^{\mathbb{Q}}
, we need to study
[(\widehat{\rho}_{s})^{-1}\rho_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}]x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}
. Define
X_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}:=[(\widehat{\rho}_{s})^{-1}\rho_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}]x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}
. From (
4.1
), we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle=x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}[h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})x_{s}^{1,\varepsilon}+h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})-h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s})]dB_{s}^{j}.\end{array}

The Itô formula for variational solutions for SPDEs in
[
28
, Theorem 4.2.5]
implies that the dynamics of
X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}
follows the equation:

\begin{array}[]{rl}dX_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}&\!\displaystyle=[\Delta X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+(b-g^{j}h^{j})_{x}X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}]dt\\
&\displaystyle+\Big\{g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta g^{j}(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t})h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})\Big\}dt\\
&\displaystyle+\Big\{\big(g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\delta g^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\big)\big(h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\delta h^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\big)\Big\}dt\\
&\displaystyle+\Big\{\sigma_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta\sigma(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\Big\}dW_{s}\\
&\displaystyle+\Big\{\big(g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta g^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\big)\\
&\displaystyle\qquad+x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\big(h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\delta h^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\big)\Big\}dB_{s}^{j}.\end{array}

We then apply the Itô formula to process
\langle p_{t},X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}
. It gives

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle d\langle p_{t},X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\\
&\!\displaystyle=\langle p_{t},dX_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}+\langle X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon},dp_{t}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}+d\langle p,X^{1,\varepsilon}\rangle_{t}\\
&\!\displaystyle=\big\langle p_{t},\Delta X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+(b-g^{j}h^{j})_{x}X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt\\
&\displaystyle+\big\langle p_{t},g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta g^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt\\
&\displaystyle+\big\langle p_{t},(g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\delta g^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon}))(h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\delta h^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon}))\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt\\
&\displaystyle+\big\langle p_{t},(\sigma_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta\sigma(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon}))dW_{t}\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\\
&\displaystyle+\big\langle p_{t},[g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta g^{j}(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t})\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\quad+x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta h^{j}(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t})]dB_{t}^{j}\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\\
&\displaystyle+\big\langle X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon},-\Delta p_{t}-b_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})p_{t}-\langle\sigma_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t}),q_{1,t}\rangle_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\Xi;L^{2}(\Lambda))}-g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})q^{j}_{2,t}\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-h_{x}^{j,*}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})z_{2,t}^{j}+g^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})p_{t}-\ell_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt\\
&\displaystyle+\langle X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon},q_{1,t}dW_{t}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}+\langle X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon},q_{2,t}dB_{t}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\\
&\displaystyle+\langle q_{1,t},\sigma_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta\sigma(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\Xi;L^{2}(\Lambda))}dt\\
&\displaystyle+\big\langle q_{2,t}^{j},g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta g^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\quad+x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta h^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt.\end{array}

Taking integration and expectation, and considering the initial and terminal conditions, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle\!\!\!\!{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\langle m_{x}(\widehat{x}_{T}),X_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\\
&\!\displaystyle\!\!\!\!={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\langle p_{T},X_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\\
&\!\displaystyle\!\!\!\!=-{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\big[z_{2,t}^{j}h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t}(\lambda))+\ell_{x}(\widehat{x}_{t}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{t}(\lambda))\big]X_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)d\lambda dt\\
&\displaystyle\!\!\!\!+{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle p_{t},\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta g^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt\\
&\displaystyle\!\!\!\!+{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle p_{t},(g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\delta g^{j}(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t}))(h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\delta h^{j}(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t}))\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt\\
&\displaystyle\!\!\!\!+{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle q_{1,t},\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta\sigma(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\Xi;L^{2}(\Lambda))}dt\\
&\displaystyle\!\!\!\!+{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle q_{2,t}^{j},\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta g^{j}(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t})+[h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}]x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}+\delta h^{j}(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t})x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt.\end{array}

From Lemma
3.1
, we further note
that

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle p_{t},\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta g^{j}(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t})h^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt=o(\varepsilon),\\
&\!\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle p_{t},g_{x}^{j}(t)x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta h(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t})+h_{x}^{j}(t)x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta g^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\delta g^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\delta h^{j}(t;u^{\varepsilon}_{t})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt=o(\varepsilon),\\
&\!\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle q_{1,t},\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta\sigma(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\rangle_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\Xi;L^{2}(\Lambda))}dt=o(\varepsilon),\\
&\!\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle q_{2,t}^{j},\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{-1}\rho_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta g^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})+x_{t}^{1,\varepsilon}\delta h^{j}(t;u_{t}^{\varepsilon})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}dt=o(\varepsilon),\end{array}

where
g_{x}^{j}(t):=g_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})
and
h_{x}^{j}(t):=h_{x}^{j}(\widehat{x}_{t},\widehat{u}_{t})
. Consequently, we conclude that

(4.8)

Lemma 4.3

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{T}e^{(T-s)\Delta}(\sigma(\widehat{x}_{s},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})-\sigma(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s}))dW_{s}\Big\|_{H_{0}^{\gamma}(\Lambda)}^{2}\Big]\\
&\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\varepsilon}\|e^{(T-s)\Delta}(\sigma(\widehat{x}_{s},v)-\sigma(\widehat{x}_{s},\widehat{u}_{s}))\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\Xi;H_{0}^{\gamma}(\Lambda))}^{2}ds\Big]\\
&\displaystyle\leq K\Big(1+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\|\widehat{x}_{t}\|_{H_{0}^{\gamma}(\Lambda)}^{2}\big]+\|v\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\|\widehat{u}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}\big]\Big)<\infty.\end{array}

For estimates of stochastic integrals w.r.t. Brownian motions
B
, we can apply the same argument as that of
W
. The proof of the lemma is then completed.
∎

We now proceed to prove our main result.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Recall
{\mathbb{E}}_{4}^{\mathbb{Q}}
in (
4.23
), we divide by
\varepsilon
to
{\mathbb{E}}_{4}^{\mathbb{Q}}
and let
\varepsilon\to 0
. Then (
3.1
) implies that as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{\mathbb{E}}_{4}^{\mathbb{Q}}&\!\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[H(\widehat{x}_{\tau},v,p_{\tau},q_{1,\tau},q_{2,\tau},z_{2,\tau}-\langle p_{\tau},g(\widehat{x}_{\tau},\widehat{u}_{\tau})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)})\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\quad-H(\widehat{x}_{\tau},\widehat{u}_{\tau},p_{\tau},q_{1,\tau},q_{2,\tau},z_{2,\tau}-\langle p_{\tau},g(\widehat{x}_{\tau},\widehat{u}_{\tau})\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)})\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau}^{Y}\Big]\\
&\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\langle P_{\tau}^{\eta}(\lambda,\mu),\big\langle\sigma(\widehat{x}_{\tau}(\lambda),v)-\sigma(\widehat{x}_{\tau}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{\tau}),\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\sigma(\widehat{x}_{\tau}(\mu),v)-\sigma(\widehat{x}_{\tau}(\mu),\widehat{u}_{\tau})\big\rangle_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\Xi;{\mathbb{R}})}\rangle\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau}^{Y}\Big]\\
&\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\langle P_{\tau}^{\eta}(\lambda,\mu),\big\langle g^{j}(\widehat{x}_{\tau}(\lambda),v)-g^{j}(\widehat{x}_{\tau}(\lambda),\widehat{u}_{\tau}),\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad g^{j}(\widehat{x}_{\tau}(\mu),v)-g^{j}(\widehat{x}_{\tau}(\mu),\widehat{u}_{\tau})\big\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}}\rangle\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau}^{Y}\Big].\end{array}

It remains to prove that

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to 0}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\int_{\Lambda}m_{xx}(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)d\lambda-\int_{\Lambda}m_{xx}^{\eta}(\lambda,\mu)X_{T}^{\varepsilon}(\lambda,\mu)d\lambda d\mu\Big]=0.\end{array}

By Lemma
4.3
and the compact embedding
H_{0}^{\gamma}(\Lambda)\subset\subset L^{2}(\Lambda),\gamma\in(0,1/2)
, we can extract a subsequence of
\widetilde{x}_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}
such that it converges to
\widetilde{x}_{T}^{1}\in L^{2}(\Lambda)
in
L^{2}(\Lambda)
. Thus, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to 0}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\int_{\Lambda}m_{xx}(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)x_{T}^{1,\varepsilon}(\lambda)d\lambda-\int_{\Lambda^{2}}m_{xx}^{\eta}(\lambda,\mu)X_{T}^{\varepsilon}(\lambda,\mu)d\lambda d\mu\Big]\\
&\displaystyle=\lim_{\eta\to 0}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\int_{\Lambda}m_{xx}(\widehat{x}_{T}(\lambda))\widetilde{x}_{T}^{1}(\lambda)\widetilde{x}_{T}^{1}(\lambda)d\lambda-\int_{\Lambda^{2}}m_{xx}^{\eta}(\lambda,\mu)\widetilde{x}_{T}^{1}(\lambda)\widetilde{x}_{T}^{1}(\mu)d\lambda d\mu\Big]=0.\end{array}

Consequently, we complete the proof.

5.
Numerical algorithm for solving partially observed optimal control

In this section, we develop a numerical algorithm to solve the partially observed optimal control for SPDEs where the system state and observation process have correlated noise. In practical applications, controlling volatility is difficult. Therefore, in this section, we consider that the diffusion coefficients in (
1.3
) are independent of the control variable. Moreover, we note that the equation (
2.3
) of
p_{t}
has a series term in the drift due to

(5.1)

\langle\sigma_{x}(x,u),q_{1,t}\rangle_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\Xi;L^{2}(\Lambda))}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}[\sigma(x,u)e_{i}]_{x}^{*}q_{1,t}e_{i}.

From computational point of view, it is best to consider the state to be driven either by the additive cylindrical Wiener process
W_{t}
or by finitely many Brownian motions
W_{t}^{i},i=1,\dots,N
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
with multiplicative coefficients. The correlated noise
B_{t}
stays the same. Consequently, (
5.1
) becomes a finite sum in the drift of
p_{t}
.

The state process we consider becomes

(5.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dx_{t}^{u}=[\Delta x_{t}^{u}+b(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})]dt+\sigma^{i}(x_{t}^{u})dW_{t}^{i}+g^{j}(x_{t}^{u})dB_{t}^{j}\\
x_{0}^{u}=x_{0}.\end{array}\right.

Again, we used the Einstein summation convention.
The observation process is

\begin{array}[]{rl}dY_{t}=h(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})dt+dB_{t},\end{array}

where
h
is a mapping from
L^{2}(\Lambda)\times U
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We assume that the control is in a convex set. Theorem
2.2
implies that the gradient of cost functional with respect to control variable becomes

(5.3)

\nabla_{u}J(u):={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[b_{u}^{*}(x_{t},u_{t})p_{t}+L_{u}(x_{t},u_{t})+h_{u}^{j,*}(x_{t},u_{t})z_{2,t}^{j}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}\big].

5.1.
Conditional SGD algorithm

We are interested in computing the optimal control
\widehat{u}_{t}
at time
t
given the available information
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}
. We use the gradient descent algorithm to achieve this purpose:

(5.4)

u_{t}^{\iota+1,Y}=u_{t}^{\iota,Y}-\alpha\nabla_{u}J(u_{t}^{\iota,Y}),\iota=0,1,2,\dots

where
\alpha
is learning rate or step size,
\iota
is the iteration number. We observe that computing
\nabla_{u}J(u_{t}^{\iota,Y})
in (
5.4
) requires the value
p_{t}
at time
t
. To obtain
p_{t}
, we need to solve the backward SPDE (
2.3
) and the backward SDE (
2.2
) from time
T
to
t
. However, solving (
2.3
) depends on the control values at time
r\in(t,T]
. In the SGD update (
5.4
) at time
t
, we only have access to the filtration
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}
, while
{\mathcal{F}}_{r}^{Y}
for
r\in(t,T]
remains unavailable. Hence, the control
u_{r}
cannot be used directly in solving (
2.3
) for
r\in[t,T]
given the available information
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}
.

To overcome this challenge, we replace
u_{r}^{\iota,Y},r\in(t,T]
with its conditional expectation given
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}
. This substitution is justified by the fact that the conditional expectation provides the best approximation of
u_{r}^{\iota,Y}
based on the available information
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}
. Define

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle u_{r}^{\iota,Y}|_{t}:={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}[u_{r}^{\iota,Y}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}].\end{array}

We then arrive at the following algorithm

(5.5)

u_{r}^{\iota+1,Y}|_{t}=u_{r}^{\iota,Y}|_{t}-\alpha\nabla_{u}J(u_{r}^{\iota,Y}|_{t}),\iota=0,1,2,\dots,

for
r\in[t,T]
and
\nabla_{u}J(u_{r}^{\iota,Y})
is calculated by (
5.6
).

In numerical approximations, all values in the Hilbert space, namely
x_{t},u_{t},p_{t},q_{1}^{i}
,
q_{2}^{j}
,
i=1,\dots,N,j=1,\dots,d
, will be projected to the finite-dimensional space
S_{h}
using the finite element approximation. To avoid introducing more notation, in the following, we regard them as
S_{h}
-valued variables with certain abuse of notation. We will apply the implicit Euler method for time discretization to avoid restrictions between time and space step sizes. For details on numerical schemes for solving forward-backward SPDEs of
x
and
p
using finite element approximations and those for solving the BSDE of
z
, we refer to
[
8
, Section 3,4]
.

Define

\begin{array}[]{rl}\psi(x,u,p,z_{2}):=b_{u}^{*}(x,u)p+L_{u}(x,u)+h_{u}^{j,*}(x,u)z_{2}^{j}.\end{array}

Then

(5.6)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla_{u}J(u_{r}^{\iota,Y})&\!\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\psi(x_{r},u_{r}^{\iota,Y}|_{t},p_{r},z_{2,r})|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}]\\
&\displaystyle=\int_{S_{h}}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\psi(x_{r},u_{r}^{\iota,Y}|_{t},p_{r},z_{2,r})\big|x_{t}=x\big]\cdot p\big(x|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}\big)dx,\end{array}

where
p(x_{t}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y})
is the probability density function (pdf) of the law
x_{t}
given the observation
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y}
. This is where nonlinear filtering plays a crucial role in computing partially observed optimal control. The pdf
p(x_{t}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y})
will be approximated by the empirical distributions
\pi(x_{t}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{Y})
using particles. We will use the Bayesian
framework and branching particle filtering algorithm to update
\pi
.

5.2.
The branching algorithm

In particle filtering algorithm, a well-known issue is the weight degeneracy problem. That is, after several time steps, the weights of most particles tend to zero, making the particles concentrate on a few samples. It reduces the effective particle size in the algorithm. To overcome this problem, several resampling strategies have been applied, such as systematic resampling, residual resampling, and stratified resampling. However, instead of using resampling strategies, we will use the branching algorithm in this paper because it produces fewer errors and variance; see
[
38
]
and
[
7
]
for comprehensive study on the branching algorithm.

To start, we divide the time interval
[0,T]
into equal lengths
[k\varrho,(k+1)\varrho)
, where
k>0
and
\varrho
is the time step size. At time
0
, the particle system consists of
S
particles of equal weight
1/S
distributed at locations
\{x_{0}^{\varsigma,S}\}_{\varsigma=1}^{S}
. The approximating measure at time
0
is then given by
\pi_{0}^{S}:=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{\varsigma=1}^{S}\delta_{x_{0}^{\varsigma,S}}
, where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac measure at
x
.

During the time interval
[k\varrho,(k+1)\varrho)
, the particles all move with the same law as the signal/state
x
. That is for
t\in[k\varrho,(k+1)\varrho)
,

(5.7)

\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{t}^{\varsigma,S}&\!\displaystyle=x_{k\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}+\int_{k\varrho}^{t}\big(\Delta x_{r}^{\varsigma,S}+b(x_{r}^{\varsigma,S},u_{r})\big)dr-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{k\varrho}^{t}g^{j}(x_{r}^{\varsigma,S})h^{j}(x_{r}^{\varsigma,S},u_{r})dr\\
&\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{k\varrho}^{t}\sigma^{i}(x_{r}^{\varsigma,S})dW_{r}^{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{k\varrho}^{t}g^{j}(x_{r}^{\varsigma,S})dY_{r}^{j},\end{array}

where
\{W_{r}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are mutually independent Brownian motions, independent of
Y
. Remember we will use finite element approximation for spatial discretization, (
5.7
) turns out to be a SDE. The weight
\overline{M}_{t}^{\varsigma,S}
is of the form

(5.8)

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\!\displaystyle\overline{M}_{t}^{\varsigma,S}=\frac{M_{t}^{\varsigma,S}}{\sum_{l=1}^{S}M_{t}^{l,S}},\end{array}

where

(5.9)

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\!\displaystyle M_{t}^{\varsigma,S}:=\exp\Big(\int_{k\varrho}^{t}h(x_{r}^{\varsigma,S},u_{r})dY_{r}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{k\varrho}^{t}|h(x_{r}^{\varsigma,S},u_{r})|^{2}dr\Big),\quad t\in[k\varrho,(k+1)\varrho).\end{array}

For
t\in[k\varrho,(k+1)\varrho)
, we define the empirical measure of particles at time
t
as

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle\pi_{t}^{S}=\sum_{\varsigma=1}^{S}\overline{M}_{t}^{\varsigma,S}\delta_{x_{t}^{\varsigma,S}}.\end{array}

At the end of the interval, each particle is branched into a random number
o_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}
of offsprings. Each offspring particle initially inherits the spatial position of its parent. Then
o_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}
is
{\mathcal{F}}_{(k+1)\varrho}
-adapted, and

(5.10)

o_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[S\overline{M}_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}\right],&\!\displaystyle\text{ with probability }1-\left\{S\overline{M}_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}\right\},\\
\left[S\overline{M}_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}\right]+1,&\!\displaystyle\text{ with probability }\left\{S\overline{M}_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}\right\},\end{array}\right.

where
[x]
is the largest integer smaller than
x
and
\{x\}
is the fractional part of
x
, that is,
\{x\}=x-[x]
, and

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle\overline{M}_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}:=\overline{M}_{(k+1)\varrho-}^{\varsigma,S}=\lim_{t\uparrow(k+1)\delta}\overline{M}_{t}^{\varsigma,S}.\end{array}

Then the direct computation from (
5.10
) implies

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[o_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{(k+1)\varrho-}\Big]=S\overline{M}_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}.\end{array}

Moreover, the minimal of conditional variance of the number of offspring is

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\!\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(o_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}\Big)^{2}\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{(k+1)\varrho-}\Big]-\Big({\mathbb{E}}\Big[o_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{(k+1)\varrho-}\Big]\Big)^{2}\\
&\displaystyle=\left\{S\overline{M}_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}\right\}\Big(1-\left\{S\overline{M}_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}\right\}\Big),\end{array}

see
[
7
, Exercise 9.1]
with its proof in
[
38
, Proposition 2]
. We will control the branching process such that the total number of particles in the system remains at
S
, that is

\sum_{\varsigma=1}^{S}o_{(k+1)\varrho}^{\varsigma,S}=S.

Consequently, combining the conditional SGD algorithm in (
5.5
), numerical approximation schemes to solve forward-backward SPDEs and BSDEs for (
5.2
), (
2.3
), and (
2.2
) in
[
8
, Section 3,4]
, as well as the branching particle filtering algorithm above, we obtain a full numerical algorithm to solve partially observed optimal control for SPDEs where the state and observation have finite-dimensional correlated noise. Moreover, we notice that in the computation of the gradient of the cost functional (
5.6
), BSPDEs (
2.3
), and BSDEs (
2.2
), one needs to compute conditional expectations. They can be approximated by the Monte-Carlo simulations using samples. In practice, this becomes very costly when the dimension of system state is high. Motivated by the stochastic approximation algorithm, specifically by the stochastic gradient descent algorithm, we utilize a single realization of trajectory to represent the conditional expectation; see details in
[
8
,
6
]
and
[
25
]
.

7.
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we establish Peng’s maximum principle for partially observed optimal control of SPDEs with finite-dimensional observations that have correlated noise with the state process. To approximate optimal control, we develop an efficient numerical algorithm that combines finite element approximations for solving forward-backward SPDEs, branching particle filtering for state estimation, and stochastic gradient descent algorithms for the optimization of the control.

The Laplacian
\Delta
in the state equation (
1.3
) can be replaced by a general operator
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subset L^{2}(\Lambda)\to L^{2}(\Lambda)
of the quadratic form

\int_{\Lambda}a(\lambda)(\partial_{\lambda}x)^{2}(\lambda)d\lambda,\;x\in H_{0}^{1}(\Lambda),

for some
a\in L^{\infty}(\Lambda)
with
a(\lambda)\geq a_{0}>0
. Moreover, the one-space dimension can be generalized to a higher-space dimension, but one needs a higher space regularity for the noise coefficients
\sigma
and
g
. They are required to guarantee that the operator
\pi
is a continuous operator; see details in
[
36
]
and
[
1
, Section 7.38]
.

Future research will focus on analyzing the algorithm’s convergence, establishing sufficient conditions for the existence of the partially observed optimal control problem, and exploring applications to more complex real-world models.
