Early Health Technology Assessment (HTA) of Olpasiran and Pelacarsen for Secondary Prevention of Coronary Heart Disease (CHD)

Published: 2026

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the primary cause of death globally, responsible for approximately one-third of annual mortality.1 Despite substantial advances in lipid-lowering therapies targeting low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), a significant residual cardiovascular risk persists, particularly among patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).1 One of the major contributors to this residual risk is elevated lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], a genetically determined lipoprotein particle structurally similar to LDL but containing an additional apolipoprotein(a) component covalently bound to apolipoprotein B-100.2 Plasma concentrations of Lp(a) are largely determined by genetic variation in the LPA gene, and unlike LDL-C, they are minimally influenced by environmental or lifestyle factors, including diet, exercise, or statin therapy.3

Elevated Lp(a) levels have been identified as an independent and causal risk factor for atherosclerosis and coronary heart disease (CHD).4,5 Mendelian randomization studies have confirmed a strong causal relationship between genetically elevated Lp(a) concentrations and increased risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and aortic valve stenosis.6–8

Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) is an autosomal dominant disorder most commonly resulting from mutations in the LDLR, APOB, or PCSK9 genes. It is characterized by lifelong exposure to elevated LDL-C and markedly increased susceptibility to premature ASCVD.9 Patients with FH frequently exhibit concomitantly elevated Lp(a) levels, which may further amplify their risk of premature CHD even when LDL-C is well controlled with statins and PCSK9 inhibitors. Therefore, the identification and treatment of elevated Lp(a) are particularly relevant in this high-risk population.10

The recognition of Lp(a) as an independent predictor of cardiovascular risk, distinct from traditional lipoproteins such as LDL-C, has recently intensified scientific interest in this molecule. Emerging evidence indicates that elevated Lp(a) levels confer substantial residual cardiovascular risk, even in patients undergoing standard lipid-lowering therapies. Consequently, therapeutic targeting of Lp(a) is increasingly viewed as a promising strategy for further reducing cardiovascular risk in affected individuals.11

Currently, there are no approved medications specifically indicated for lowering Lp(a) levels. Pelacarsen and olpasiran represent two of the most advanced gene-silencing therapies designed to reduce Lp(a) synthesis in the liver. Pelacarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) that inhibits apolipoprotein(a) mRNA translation, while olpasiran is a small interfering RNA (siRNA) that promotes degradation of LPA mRNA via the RNA-induced silencing complex. Both agents have demonstrated potent and durable reductions in circulating Lp(a) levels—up to 80–95% in Phase II clinical trials.12–14

The ongoing OCEAN(a) Outcomes trial (NCT05581303) is a Phase III study designed to evaluate the effects of olpasiran on cardiovascular outcomes in adults with established coronary heart disease and elevated Lp(a) levels (≥200 nmol/L or 90 mg/dL). The trial is expected to conclude in 2026. Similarly, the Lp(a) HORIZON trial (NCT04023552) with pelacarsen is a Phase III study enrolling patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and Lp(a) concentrations of ≥150 nmol/L (70 mg/dL), with expected completion in February 2026.15,16

Early health technology assessment (HTA) involves the use of early-phase health economic models to provide insights into the potential value of emerging medical interventions. These methods aim to support decision-making by industry and other stakeholders, particularly by quantifying expected outcomes and addressing associated uncertainties. Such models are designed to project long-term clinical benefits and to systematically compare the costs of a new intervention against those of existing standard-of-care treatments.17

Large Mendelian randomization studies have established a strong causal relationship between elevated Lp(a) levels and coronary heart disease. These studies minimize confounding and reverse causation compared with traditional observational research, providing the most robust genetic evidence linking Lp(a) to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Based on these findings, the clinical benefit of Lp(a) reduction was estimated using data from Mendelian randomization and Phase II trial results.5,6

This analysis focused on individuals with pre-existing coronary heart disease (CHD), familial hypercholesterolemia and markedly elevated Lp(a) levels, modeling the risk of recurrent CHD events, as this represents the target population and primary outcome of the OCEAN(a) Outcomes trial. The assessment was conducted from the perspective of the Bulgarian healthcare system and national decision-making in health policy.

This study aims to perform a comparative early health technology assessment (HTA) of olpasiran and pelacarsen for secondary prevention of coronary heart disease (CHD) in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, familial hypercholesterolemia, and elevated Lp(a), using data from clinical trials and Mendelian randomization studies to model expected outcomes and economic value from a healthcare system perspective.

Materials and Methods

Population

The cohort in the current analysis is profiled on a patient population examined at “Heart and Brain” Hospitals in Pleven and Burgas. Patients entered the model in the current analysis with elevated levels of Lp(a) and at mean age 55 years. Nearly all patients underwent moderate or high intensity statin therapy at baseline.

Model

In Pharmacoeconomics, simulating the progression of chronic diseases by use of decision tree can be difficult due to the complex potential pathways that might occur in the long-run. Markov models use disease states to emulate a disease course, particularly chronic diseases. In the current analysis, we developed a Markov state transition model to simulate the progression of the aforementioned cohort of 597 patients with established CHD in the treatment arms of OCEAN(a)-Outcomes trial (NCT05581303)16 and Lp(a) HORIZON trial (NCT04023552).15

The model compared two approaches: (1) screening patients for Lp(a) ≥200 nmol/L and treating with olpasiran or pelacarsen (150 mg every three months for olpasiran or 80 mg – monthly for pelacarsen) for a lifetime in addition to standard-of-care secondary prevention for CHD; and (2) not screening patients and continuing with standard-of-care. Standard of care therapy includes application of moderate or high intensity statin therapy. Moderate-intensity statin therapies include atorvastatin (10–20 mg), rosuvastatin (5–10 mg), simvastatin (20–40 mg), pravastatin (40–80 mg), pitavastatin (2–4 mg), according to the ACC/AHA guidelines 2013.18 High-intensity statin therapies include atorvastatin (40–80 mg), rosuvastatin (20–40 mg) as well as addition of ezetimibe 10 mg.

Figure 1 shows the health states and possible transitions between them. Our model uses annual cycles. Transition probabilities are used to determine movement between states and are accepted to be static in our model. The cohort was cycled through the model for 50 years or until death.

Health states and transitions in the Markov model. The circles represent health states, the boxes signify CHD events and the arrows represent transitions.

Effect of Treatment

Treatment effects for olpasiran and pelacarsen were modeled according to results from two phase II randomized trials: OCEAN(a)-DOSE trial19 and phase II pelacarsen trial.20 In OCEAN(a)-DOSE, patients had a 97.5% absolute reduction in baseline Lp(a) levels from 265.4 nmol/l with 225 mg olpasiran taken every 12 weeks. The absolute reduction in this case was 258.76 nmol/l.19 In the pelacarsen trial, Lp(a) levels were reduced by 80% with 20 mg pelacarsen received weekly. Lp(a) dropped by 187.8 nmol/l.20

In this model, the clinical benefit was estimated based on Mendelian randomization study data. One such study is the work of Lamina et al, who estimated that lipoprotein(a) would have to be lowered by 657 mg/dL to reach the same potential effect on clinical outcomes as lowering low-density lipoprotein cholesterol by 38.67 mg/dL. They postulated that this would correspond to a CHD risk reduction over the lifetime by 45% and by 22% over a short term.21

Transition Probabilities

Annual probability of CHD events was determined based on results from a population-based study of 58 527 individuals with a history of CVD (Copenhagen General Population Study; 2003–2015). Results show that in the group with the highest levels of Lp(a) ≥214 nmol/l there were 59 incidents of major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) out of 212 individuals over a median follow-up of 5 years. Major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) is the composite endpoint: myocardial infarction, percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass graft, stroke or cardiovascular death.22 We then applied Briggs et al formula to convert the 5-year probabilities into annual rates assuming the rate is constant over that period. The formula is as follows: annual rate = [ln(1-probability)]/time period.23

Further on, ratio between fatal and non-fatal CHD events was determined based on data from the Annual report on the state of health of citizens for 2023, published on the internet site of the Ministry of Health of Bulgaria in 2024.24

Non-CHD related death is estimated to be 2.2% in all groups based on data from the Annual report on the state of health of citizens for 2023.24

Probability of a recurrent CHD event after an initial one was determined based on the results from the study of Li et al who estimated cumulative recurrence rates of MI and ischemic stroke (IS) after hospital discharge and cumulative incidence of other cardiovascular events, all-cause death, and composite measures of multiple events after MI or IS over long-term follow-up in a 2008 cohort of older patients. According to them cumulative incidence of recurrence at 1 year was 7.7%.25 Death rate is estimated at 4.7% based on data from the Annual report on the state of health of citizens for 2023, published on the internet site of the Ministry of Health of Bulgaria in 2024.24

Utilities

All patients entered the analysis with a utility score of 0.83 based on the VALIANT trial cohort whose population had established CHD at baseline.26 Patients who had a non-fatal CHD event had a utility score of 0.71 derived from utility value estimates in CVD by Betts et al.27

Costs and Threshold

Prices of moderate and high intensity statin therapy have been taken from Annex 4 of the Positive Drug List issued by the National Council on prices and reimbursement of medicinal products in Bulgaria from 02.10.2025. We have built our estimates on prices of rosuvastatin in both cases of therapy. For moderate intensity statin therapy, rosuvastatin 10 mg was used. Its monthly price derived from the Annex 4 of the Positive Drug List is 43.31 BGN. Annual therapy would cost 519.72 BGN. For the high intensity statin therapy, we chose to work with rosuvastatin 20 mg with addition of ezetimibe 10 mg. Monthly costs for each are 64.64 BGN and 85.87 BGN, respectively, and annual costs are 775.68 BGN and 1080.44 BGN, respectively.28 Prices for laboratory investigation of Lp(a) and potential hospital stays have been considered equal for all participants and omitted in the study as it was our aim to directly compare treatment costs and make an estimate on cost effectiveness of olpasiran and pelacarsen.

The threshold we have applied has been based on the Methodological guidelines for the documents submitted for the assessment of the efficacy, therapeutic effectiveness, safety and pharmacoeconomic indicators of medicinal products when applying for maintenance of reimbursement status in the Positive Drug List issued by the National Council on prices and reimbursement of medicinal products in Bulgaria.28 According to this methodology, highly cost-effective products are those where the cost-effectiveness ratio is below the annual GDP per capita; cost-effective products are those where the cost-effectiveness ratio is between 1 and three times the annual GDP per capita; not cost-effective products are those where the cost-effectiveness ratio is over 3 times the annual GDP per capita. According to data from the National Statistical Institute (NSI), the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) of Bulgaria as of March 7, 2025 is BGN 202,861 million, and the GDP per capita is BGN 31,473.

Discounting

All future outcomes estimated were discounted by 3.5% according to the Regulation on the conditions, rules and procedures for regulation and registration of the prices of medicinal products by the National Council on prices and reimbursement of medicinal products in Bulgaria.29

Sensitivity Analysis

One-way sensitivity analysis was performed. Input pararmeters were varied by 95% confidence intervals where they were available or by ±25% of initial values. Changes were made in one parameter at a time leaving the rest unchanged.

With 3.5% annual discounting, there were 0.23 QALYs saved.

The treating strategy comprising olpasiran 150 mg every 3 months in addition to standard of care for patients with Lp(a) levels above 200 nmol/l was found highly cost-effective at the annual price 10,424.78 BGN for olpasiran, compared with standard-of-care alone. The ICER (Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio) for olpasiran at this case was 854.12 BGN, which places it well within the GDP per capita threshold. At the same time, this ICER was close to that of standard-of-care alone - 671.47 BGN. This means that the treating strategy comprising olpasiran 150 mg every 3 months in addition to standard of care provides considerably more in term of health benefits at publicly acceptable cost.

Analysis for Pelacarsen

Table 3 and Figures 2a and c show results from the analysis for pelacarsen. The treating strategy comprising pelacarsen 80 mg every month in addition to standard of care if Lp(a) levels are determined to be above 150 nmol/l saved 8.63 QALYs, compared with standard of-care alone (undiscounted), over a lifetime. With 3.5% annual discounting, there were 0.58 QALYs saved.

The treating strategy comprising pelacarsen 80 mg every month in addition to standard of care if Lp(a) levels are determined to be above 150 nmol/l was found highly cost-effective at the annual price 6105.99 BGN for pelacarsen, compared with standard-of-care alone. The ICER for pelacarsen at this case was 722.48 BGN, which places it well within the GDP per capita threshold. At the same time, this ICER was close to that of standard-of-care alone - 671.47 BGN. This means that the treating strategy comprising pelacarsen 80 mg every month in addition to standard of care provides considerably more in term of health benefits at publicly acceptable cost.

Sensitivity Analysis

One way sensitivity analysis showed that the prices of olpasiran and pelacarsen were susceptible to changes in base case values of some input parameters. The price of olpasiran was sensitive to changes in the risk for CHD event. When the risk was lowered by 25%, the price was 15,798.14 BGN, and when the risk was increased by 25% the price went to 27,152.93 BGN. Additionally, the price of olpasiran was sensitive to changes in Lpa reduction efficacy to −94.0% and −100%. Price was 14004.84 BGN and 24589.57 BGN, respectively (Figure 3a). The price of pelacarsen was sensitive to changes in Lpa reduction efficacy to −91% and −100% with values being 6831.95 BGN and 24589.57 BGN, respectively (Figure 3b).

Discussion

This study is a comparative early health technology assessment (HTA) of olpasiran and pelacarsen for secondary prevention of coronary heart disease (CHD) in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, familial hypercholesterolemia, and elevated Lp(a) levels. We found that olpasiran was potentially highly cost-effective at the annual price of 10,424.78 BGN, compared with standard-of-care alone. Pelacarsen had the potential to be highly cost-effective at the annual price of 6105.99 BGN. These results were true for the Bulgarian environment.

Other authors have also attempted to investigate the cost-effectiveness of gene-silencing therapies around the world. Burvill et al estimated prices at which olpasiran and pelacarsen, in addition to standard-of care, would be deemed cost-effective in reducing risk of recurrent coronary heart disease (CHD) events in the Australian healthcare system. Their results showed that the two therapeutic agents have the potential to be cost-effective at annual treatment prices of AU$1867 and AU$984, respectively. Olpasiran in addition to standard-of-care saved 0.87 discounted quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) per person according to Burvill et al.30

Passey et al review new RNA-based drugs, such as olpasiran, and conclude that they have a promising ability to significantly reduce Lp(a) levels, without major safety issues. Olpasiran may be cost-effective due to less frequent dosing. Other similar drugs, including pelacarsen, zerlasiran, and lepodisiran, also show potential in early trials for cardiovascular prevention.31

Marquina et al concluded that only 10% of the studies considered PCSK9 inhibitors cost effective compared with standard care from a healthcare perspective with ICERs ranging from US$231,119 to US$1,223,831 According to them, ezetimibe was cost effective in 50% of the studies, PCSK9 inhibitors in 33% and the polypill in 50% of the studies adopting a societal perspective.32

One of the main limitations of our early health technology assessment is that it is based on data from phase II trials since there were no published results from phase III trials at study conception phase. Therefore, we have leaned heavily on Mendelian randomization study data in our estimations of clinical benefits from Lp(a) lowering. Mendelian randomization has been successfully used in health evaluations before.33,34 The Markov model used is static and although it is less complex than other models, it is particularly helpful in decision analysis in healthcare. Other limitations of the current study are the assumed adherence to therapy and omission of prices for hospital stays and laboratory investigations.

One of the positive aspects of this study is that the model is based on a real cohort of patients rather than a hypothetical one. Another benefit is the 50 years perspective of the analysis as it encompasses a period much longer than typical clinical study data can provide. Finally, our analysis is preliminary and needs to be confirmed once more mature clinical and economic data become available before it becomes policy ready.

Conclusion

This early health technology assessment has shown that olpasiran and pelacarsen have the potential to be highly cost-effective in Bulgarian environment. Further evaluation incorporating data from phase III clinical trials is warranted to confirm these results.
