Integrated 3D modeling of train-induced ground vibrations considering track irregularities and wave impeding barrier performance under different subgrade stiffness conditions
Authors: Hussein Hadi Hussein, Mohannad Mhanna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study developed a three-dimensional (3D) numerical model to simulate the propagation of ground vibrations induced by high-speed trains (HSTs) traversing tracks with irregularities. The dynamic interaction between wheels and rails was first analyzed using a coupled multibody vehicle model integrated with randomly generated track irregularities defined by power spectral density (PSD) functions. The resulting dynamic loads were then incorporated into a validated 3D finite-difference model to simulate vibration propagation and assess mitigation measures. Unlike previous studies that considered vibration generation and mitigation separately, the proposed framework integrates stochastic track irregularities, vehicle–track interaction, and Wave Impeding Barrier (WIB) performance assessment within a single 3D time-domain model. The numerical framework was subsequently applied to evaluate the influence of track irregularity conditions, subgrade soil stiffness, and WIBs on vibration propagation characteristics. The results demonstrate a direct correlation between track unevenness and vibration intensity, with more pronounced irregularities leading to higher vibration levels. Increasing soil stiffness from 5 MPa to 50 MPa reduced vibration amplitudes by approximately 60%. Furthermore, the analysis confirms that a strategically placed WIB is a highly effective countermeasure for reducing ground vibrations induced in soft soils. A WIB with sufficient width larger than the wavelength (λ) can achieve isolation efficiency ranging from 50 to 70% up to 20 m from the track, bringing vibration levels closer to human comfort thresholds even under poor track conditions. The proposed framework provides a reliable computational tool for vibration assessment and mitigation in high-speed railway corridors.Keywords: Ground vibrations, Track irregularities, Train-induced vibration, 3D numerical model, Vibration isolation, Wave impeding barrierSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Despite the significant benefits of rail transportation, train-induced ground vibrations present notable environmental challenges, leading to human discomfort, disturbances to sensitive equipment, and even structural damage to nearby buildings1–3. These vibrations predominantly originate from the dynamic wheel–rail interaction forces, which are strongly influenced by track surface irregularities. Such forces excite the track structure, generating elastic waves that propagate through the soil, often resulting in oscillations within the audible frequency range4,5. To mitigate these adverse effects, several international standards define vibration threshold levels. For instance, DIN 4150-3 (1999) specifies a structural vibration limit of 2.5 mm/s for historic and sensitive structures, while NS8178 recommends 0.6 mm/s as a comfort threshold for infrequent vibrations in residential areas6. Previous studies on train-induced vibrations have utilized two main approaches: field measurements and numerical simulations. For field investigations, Connolly et al.7 assessed vibration levels on European railway lines, emphasizing the roles of distance and dominant frequency in predicting vibration attenuation. Dos Santos et al.8 examined vibrations from various train types (Intercity, Suburban, and Alfa Pendular) on Portuguese railways, identifying train speed and unsprung mass as key influencing factors. Celebi et al.9, through in-situ measurements on the Istanbul–Ankara HST line, highlighted the necessity of early-stage vibration assessments for residential projects near rail corridors. Likewise, Kioko et al.10 employed Arduino-based accelerometers in Nairobi, observing peak vibration amplitudes decreasing from 50.77 mm/s at the rail line to 1.05 mm/s at 16 m from the track.

Numerical modeling has been widely explored to predict and analyze vibration propagation. Lombaert and Degrande11,12 applied transfer function methods to evaluate traffic-induced vibrations, demonstrating that soil properties significantly affect attenuation characteristics. Bian et al.13 employed a 2.5D finite element method (FEM) to study track irregularities, concluding that short-wavelength irregularities generate higher vibration amplitudes and dominant frequencies. Germonpré et al.14 introduced a computationally efficient decoupled modeling strategy, combining train–track interaction models with free-field vibration predictions. Khan and Dasaka15 used ABAQUS-based FEM, revealing that higher train speeds intensify vibration amplitudes at greater distances. Nezhadshah et al.16 calibrated a 3D finite difference model (FLAC3D), reporting that peak ground velocities exceeded standard limits under Mohr–Coulomb failure criteria. Auersch17 found that the railway ground vibrations are frequency dependent in layered soils. Sites characterized by deep stiff strata exhibit diminished low-frequency vibration amplitudes, while shallow soft layers amplify high-frequency components. Numerical simulations and field measurements both reveal pronounced disparities between soft and stiff site responses. A hybrid prediction approach has been recently developed to evaluate the environmental impact of railway-induced vibration. This approach allows for a combination of analytical solution and numerical tool, providing increased flexibility and applicability18–20.

Regarding the screening of ground-borne vibrations, Wave Impeding Barriers (WIBs) represent a prominent geotechnical countermeasure for mitigating ground-borne vibrations induced by traffic loads, construction activities, and seismic events. Unlike vertical barriers such as open or in-filled trenches, a WIB consists of a horizontal high-stiffness layer installed at a shallow depth beneath the vibration source or the structure to be protected. The fundamental mechanism of vibration screening by WIBs relies on creating an impedance contrast between the barrier and the surrounding soil, which reflects and diffracts propagating waves, thereby reducing vibration amplitudes in the protected zone21.Previous research has established that WIB effectiveness depends critically on barrier dimensions relative to incident wavelength and the cutoff frequency of the topsoil layer22. Farahani and Dehkharghani23 demonstrated that 2D finite element simulations consistently overestimate wave barrier mitigation capacity compared to 3D analyses, leading to unconservative designs. This discrepancy stems from differing moving load assumptions and the inability of 2D models to represent finite-length barriers accurately. Cao et al.24 conducted field tests on WIBs constructed from deep mixed soil columns, demonstrating that even moderate stiffness increases can provide meaningful vibration attenuation when properly designed. Qu et al.25 used computational hybrid model to assess the impact of concrete foundations as wave impeding barriers. Embedded concrete foundations were found to generate impedance contrasts that reflect and diffract propagating waves.

Although significant progress has been achieved in the prediction and mitigation of railway-induced ground vibrations, previous studies have generally focused on either train–track interaction, free-field vibration propagation, or vibration mitigation measures as separate problems. Furthermore, most available investigations employ simplified representations of track irregularities or frequency-domain approaches that do not fully capture transient wheel–rail excitation mechanisms. The present study advances the state of knowledge by integrating: (i) a multibody vehicle–track interaction model subjected to PSD-based stochastic track irregularities, (ii) a validated three-dimensional time-domain finite-difference model for ground vibration propagation, and (iii) a systematic evaluation of Wave Impeding Barrier (WIB) performance under varying subgrade stiffness conditions within a unified computational framework. This integrated approach enables realistic simulation of dynamic wheel–rail loading and provides new insights into the combined influence of track condition, soil stiffness, and WIB geometry on railway vibration mitigation. The developed framework therefore offers a practical and computationally efficient tool for vibration assessment and mitigation in high-speed railway engineering.

Simulation of train dynamic load

To analyze ground-borne vibrations, the wheel–rail dynamic loads were first computed using a coupled vehicle–track interaction model, wherein random track irregularities were characterized by power spectral density (PSD) functions. This procedure has been shown to yield high-fidelity predictions of track response and radiated ground vibrations26. The resulting time histories of dynamic loads were subsequently imposed on a three-dimensional finite-difference (FDM) model to simulate wave propagation in the soil, and the numerical predictions were validated against in-situ measurements. In this study, the multibody vehicle model corresponds to a Thalys high-speed train (HST) representative of Western European operations. Exploiting symmetry in the direction of travel, the vehicle is idealized as a quarter-car model, with the carbody and bogies treated as rigid bodies interconnected via primary and secondary suspension systems, as shown in Fig. 1.

Following the extraction of the dynamic load, a MATLAB subroutine was implemented to automatically segment the load time history based on the length of each track element and the train’s velocity. To achieve this, the following discretization procedure was adopted. The dynamic load was computed with a time step of δt = 0.00004 s. The distance between consecutive sleepers was discretized into five nodes, yielding a rail node spacing of Δx = 0.1625 m. For a given train speed V, the travel time between adjacent nodes is ΔT = Δx /V, and the load time history was divided into intervals of width ΔT. For instance, at V = 300 km/h (83.33 m/s), ΔT = 0.00195 s, corresponding to n = ΔT/δt ≈ 48 loading points per rail element. Following this procedure, each rail element was progressively loaded and unloaded according to the train position, ensuring accurate representation of load magnitude and movement.

Numerical modeling approach

Modeling of track and subgrade soils

In this study, ground vibrations induced by high-speed trains (HSTs) were investigated using a finite difference method (FDM) model, which was validated against field measurements collected along the Brussels–Paris high-speed rail corridor27,28. The computational domain measured 150 m in length, 50 m in width, and 21 m in depth, with a mesh refinement concentrated near the model center and gradually coarsened toward the lateral boundaries, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The rail was represented by structural beam elements featuring a rectangular cross-section of 0.153 m × 0.078 m, positioned at the standard track gauge of 1.435 m. The model incorporated 231 concrete sleepers, each measuring 2.42 m × 0.242 m × 0.2 m, spaced at 0.65 m intervals. Each sleeper was discretized using five beam elements, connected to adjacent rail elements via node-to-node links equipped with vertical elastic springs characterized by a stiffness of (kv=120 MN/m)28.

Developed numerical model with adopted finite differences mesh.

The geotechnical properties of the track components and subgrade soil layers are depicted in Table 228. Each layer was discretized using eight-node brick elements to accurately capture the soil behavior. The rail and sleeper components were modeled as linear elastic (LE) materials, while the ballast and subgrade layers were represented using an elastoplastic Mohr–Coulomb (MC) constitutive model. This model was selected because it captures the key soil properties governing vibration propagation, including stiffness, density, and damping, while maintaining a reasonable computational cost. Railway-induced ground vibrations generally occur within the small-strain range, and therefore significant plastic deformation of the soil is not expected15. Consequently, the analysis focuses primarily on wave propagation and attenuation rather than on highly nonlinear soil behavior. A limitation of the MC formulation is that it does not explicitly account for strain-dependent stiffness degradation or damping variations at small strains. More advanced constitutive models, such as HS-small, can represent these effects more realistically. However, the primary objective of this study is to investigate the relative influence of track irregularities, subgrade stiffness, and WIB geometry on ground vibration response. The suitability of the adopted constitutive model is assessed through validation against field measurements presented in the following section.

In FLAC3D, these coefficients are automatically derived from two user-specified parameters: the minimum damping ratio (ξmin) and the corresponding center frequency (fmin) at which this ratio is achieved. The damping ratio varies with frequency according to ξ(f) = (α/f + βf)/2, attaining its minimum value ξmin at f = fmin. Thus, the target damping ratio was selected as ξmin = 5%, which falls within the range commonly reported for railway subgrade soils under small-strain dynamic loading conditions. Previous experimental and numerical investigations of train-induced ground vibrations have typically employed damping ratios between 2% and 7% for natural soils and compacted railway foundations13,16,32. Consequently, a value of 5% was considered representative of practical railway subgrade conditions. The center frequency was selected as 20 Hz because it lies within the dominant frequency range commonly observed in train-induced ground vibrations and corresponds closely to the frequency content identified in the validation results. By selecting the reference frequency within the dominant vibration range, the Rayleigh damping formulation provides an appropriate representation of energy dissipation for the vibration modes that contribute most significantly to the free-field response33. It should be noted that Rayleigh damping represents an approximation of the actual frequency-dependent damping behavior of soils. While more sophisticated damping formulations may provide a more detailed description of energy dissipation over a broader frequency spectrum, the adopted parameters were considered adequate for the objectives of the present study and yielded satisfactory agreement with the available field measurements.

Validation of the numerical model

The numerical results were validated against referential field data obtained from in-situ experiments27,28. The amplitudes of high-speed train (HST)-induced vibrations were evaluated at various offsets along a line perpendicular to the railway track, assuming a train speed of V = 300 km/h. The ground vibrations were analyzed in terms of vertical (Vz), lateral (Vx), and longitudinal (Vy) components, quantified using the peak particle velocity (PPV) in all three directions. Figure 4 illustrates the time history of vertical vibrations computed at different distances from the track centerline. A pronounced attenuation in vibration amplitude with increasing distance was observed, which can be attributed to both materials damping and geometric spreading effects. Moreover, the coherent time delays between signals at successive distances correspond well with the propagation velocities of the induced waves traveling through the railway track structure and subgrade soils.

Time history of vertical vibration (Vz) calculated at various distances from the centerline of track.

Figure 5 presents the peak particle velocities (PPVs) of ground vibrations in the vertical, lateral, and longitudinal directions. The results indicate that the vertical component (Vz) is the dominant motion at all measured offsets from the track, confirming that ground-borne vibrations are predominantly vertically oriented. Close to the railway (D = 3 m), the vertical PPV reached 2.61 mm/s and remained above the human perception threshold of 0.6 mm/s up to a distance of approximately 18 m. In contrast, both the lateral (Vx) and longitudinal (Vy) components fell below the human comfort threshold at distances exceeding 12 m, with the lateral component exhibiting the smallest amplitude. Consequently, only the vertical component Vz is analyzed in detail in the remainder of this study.

Parametric study

Several factors influence the magnitude and propagation characteristics of ground vibrations generated by high-speed trains (HSTs), among which rail surface irregularities and subgrade soil stiffness are particularly critical34,35. To investigate these effects, a parametric study was performed to assess ground vibrations induced by various classes of rail irregularities, as well as the impact of subgrade stiffness variations. Furthermore, the isolation efficiency of WIB as a mitigation measure was also discussed in details. In this analysis, the subgrade soil was idealized as a homogeneous, elastic–perfectly plastic material with the following properties: E = 50 MPa, υ = 0.3, ρ = 1700 kg/m3, c = 20 kPa, ϕ = 15°.

Effect of track irregularity

Track irregularities refer to deviations from the ideal or design geometry of the railway track. As the severity of these irregularities increases, the track quality deteriorates, resulting in reduced ride comfort and potentially higher maintenance demands. Over time, the rail surface experiences progressive wear and degradation due to the repeated dynamic loading imposed by wheel–rail interactions. These localized defects gradually evolve into global track irregularities, which serve as the primary excitation source of the vehicle–track dynamic system. To assess their influence on ground vibrations, four classes of track conditions categorized as good, moderate, poor, and very poor were examined in this study, as summarized in Table 4.

These findings highlight that poorly maintained tracks with significant irregularities can generate ground vibrations of magnitudes sufficient to compromise both human comfort and the structural integrity of nearby sensitive buildings.

The frequency content of ground vibrations was found to extend up to approximately 60 Hz at a distance of 3 m from the track, while it decreased to around 40 Hz at 9 m. This reduction in the frequency range with increasing distance highlights the filtering effect of the soil, where higher frequency components are attenuated more rapidly due to material damping and scattering. From an engineering perspective, these findings highlight the importance of track maintenance as a primary strategy for vibration control. The results indicate that deterioration of track geometry can significantly increase vibration amplitudes and enlarge the affected zone around the railway corridor. Consequently, regular monitoring and timely maintenance of track irregularities may provide a more cost-effective mitigation measure than implementing ground improvement or vibration barriers after vibration problems have developed.

Effect of soil stiffness

The influence of subgrade stiffness on free-field vibrations was assessed through numerical simulations for a train speed of 300 km/h over class D track irregularities. The soil was idealized as an elastic–perfectly plastic Mohr–Coulomb material with properties (c = 20 kPa, ϕ = 15°, ρ = 1700 kg/m³, υ = 0.3), while Young’s modulus was varied to represent a broad range of subgrade conditions encountered in railway engineering, ranging from very soft fine-grained soils to medium-stiff compacted subgrades. This range was chosen to evaluate vibration propagation under conditions where critical velocity effects may become significant and to assess the sensitivity of vibration attenuation to realistic variations in soil stiffness. Therefore, four values of Young’s modulus were selected: E1 = 5, E2 = 10, E3 = 25, and E4 = 50 MPa37. Corresponding Rayleigh wave velocities (CR) calculated from wave arrival times in the numerical model were 68, 87, 119, and 154 m/s, respectively.

Figures 12 and 13 show that increasing soil stiffness substantially reduces vibration amplitudes. Raising Young’s modulus from 5 to 50 MPa reduces PPVz by approximately 60% across all distances. At 21 m, vibrations attenuate to 18% and 15% of their 3 m values for E1 and E4, respectively. Softer subgrades (E1, E2) produce vibrations exceeding structural safety thresholds up to 21 m, whereas stiffer soils (E3, E4) limit this exceedance to 15 m, confirming stiffness as a critical factor in vibration attenuation.

At 300 km/h (83.3 m/s), train speed approaches or exceeds CR in soft soils: 68.9 m/s for E1 and 87.2 m/s for E2, triggering critical velocity effects that amplify dynamic responses as shown in Figs. 12 and 13. These effects accelerate track degradation, increase maintenance costs, and compromise ride quality, consistent with observations on high-speed rail networks worldwide38,39. The results also have implications for route planning and geotechnical design of high-speed railways. Railway alignments traversing soft subgrades are expected to exhibit greater vibration susceptibility due to lower wave velocities and the potential occurrence of critical velocity effects. In such locations, ground improvement techniques or vibration mitigation measures should be considered during the design stage, particularly when vibration-sensitive structures are situated close to the railway line.

Performance of wave impeding barrier (WIB)

As an alternative to modifying the bulk properties of the subgrade, the installation of a Wave Impeding Barrier (WIB) presents a practical geotechnical solution for mitigating train-induced ground vibrations. The geometric configuration of WIB, specifically its width and embedment depth, plays a pivotal role in the mitigation of train-induced ground vibrations, functioning primarily through the mechanism of dynamic impedance mismatch and wave screening. Regarding the influence of width, numerical and experimental studies consistently demonstrate a direct proportionality between the lateral extent of the WIB and its screening efficiency. Conversely, the embedment depth of the WIB governs the cut-off frequency of the soil-WIB system and dictates the interaction between the barrier and the vibration source40. Consequently, the optimization of WIB design necessitates a trade-off where the width is practically maximized to span the target wavelengths, while the depth is minimized to intercept surface waves efficiently, ensuring that the rigid barrier successfully reflects and refracts vibrational energy away from protected areas. Influence of WIB width

The efficacy of a WIB is fundamentally governed by its dimensions relative to the wavelength of the propagating waves it is intended to intercept41. In this study, the characteristic Rayleigh wavelength (λR), which dominates the vibration field generated by a surface source, was first estimated for the range of subgrade soils investigated. Using the shear wave velocities corresponding to the four soil stiffness values (E1 to E4), the Rayleigh wavelengths were calculated as 2.3, 2.9, 4.0, and 5.1 m, respectively. The range of WIB width (Bw) was selected to remain within practical construction limits for a continuous horizontal barrier installed beneath a railway embankment. Accordingly, three feasible widths were considered: 2 m, 3 m, and 4 m, with a fixed installation depth of Dw = 0.5 m. Figure 15 presents the attenuation of PPVz with distance for these WIB widths across different soil stiffnesses, benchmarked against the reference case without a barrier.

Effect of WIB width on ground vibration attenuation for different soil stiffness values (Dw=0.5 m).

The results demonstrate that the installation of a WIB non-uniformly reduces ground vibration amplitudes according to soil stiffness. For softer soils (E1, E2), installing a WIB consistently reduces PPVz across all distances. The most significant reduction occurs with the initial implementation of a 2 m-wide barrier. Increasing the width to 3 m and 4 m yields further attenuation, but the incremental benefit is less pronounced. This indicates that, in softer soils, a barrier of width equivalent to the wavelength interrupts a larger portion of the propagating wave-front, and provides a more effective “shadow zone” of reduced vibration. In contrast, for stiffer subgrades (E3, E4), the beneficial effect of the WIB is substantially diminished. While the installation of a WIB still provides a measurable reduction in PPVz in far field, the ground vibrations shows an amplification tendency in near field, and expanding the WIB width to 3–4 m yields only marginal additional attenuation. This diminished response is attributed to two primary factors. First, the inherently higher stiffness of these soils results in longer characteristic wavelengths (up to 6 m), meaning that the selected barrier widths constitute a smaller fraction of the wavelength, thereby reducing the efficiency of wave-barrier interaction. Second, the increased shear wave velocity in stiffer soils elevates the natural frequency of the soil stratum above the WIB. This shift may cause the fundamental resonance frequency of the trapped soil layer to approach or coincide with the dominant frequency components of the incident wavefield in the near-field region. Such resonance conditions can locally amplify ground vibrations immediately above the barrier, partially counteracting the screening effect of the WIB and further limiting the net reduction achieved by increasing its width. The localized amplification observed immediately above the WIB arises from the impedance contrast at the soil-barrier interface, which generates reflected and scattered waves that may constructively interfere with the incident wave field. Although the WIB increases the equivalent stiffness of the foundation beneath the track, the dynamic response under moving train loads is governed by wave propagation rather than static stiffness alone. These amplification effects are confined to the vicinity of the barrier and do not compromise the overall isolation performance, as demonstrated by the significant reduction in PPV beyond the WIB.

Figure 16 provides a quantitative synthesis through the isolation efficiency metric (Ef) for all different WIB width and soil stiffness. It reveals that the Ef is highest in the softest soil (E1) and decreases systematically as soil stiffness increases to E4. For instance, at Dw=0.5 m and Bw=4 m, Ef drops from approximately 80% in the softest soil to around 26% in the stiffest. For soft soils, where the dominant wavelengths are relatively short, the investigated barrier widths represent a significant fraction of the wavelength and therefore provide effective screening. In contrast, for stiffer soils, the longer wavelengths allow a larger portion of the wave energy to propagate beneath or around the barrier. Under such conditions, increasing the barrier width produces only limited additional attenuation, explaining the lower isolation efficiencies observed in Figs. 15 and 16. In this context, even though the absolute reduction in vibration levels remains beneficial in stiff soils, the design objective may shift from achieving a high percentage reduction to simply ensuring that the already-low PPVz values are pushed below a stringent regulatory threshold.

Influence of WIB installation depth

The influence of installation depth (Dw) on the isolation efficiency of the WIB is systematically examined using three different values of embedment depth Dw= 0.5, 1, and 2 m. The screening efficiency, shown in Fig. 17, reveal a nuanced interaction between barrier depth and subgrade stiffness. Contrary to the expectation that depth mainly governs performance, the data indicate that variations in installation depth from 0.5 m to 2.0 m produce only marginal changes in the isolation efficiency Ef for a given soil type. Instead, the dominant factor controlling Ef is the inherent stiffness of the subgrade soil, which dictates the baseline vibration amplitudes in the absence of mitigation. Shallower depths (Dw = 0.5 m) consistently provide good performance across all soil classes by intercepting surface-wave energy closer to its source. The mitigation efficiency exceeds 80% in soft soil (E1) and falls below 30%in stiffest soil (E4). The relatively small influence of WIB embedment depth reflects the dominant role of Rayleigh waves, which carry most of their energy within a shallow zone beneath the ground surface. Consequently, even the shallowest barrier configuration (Dw = 0.5 m) intercepts a significant portion of the transmitted vibration energy, while deeper installations provide only limited additional attenuation. This indicates that WIB performance is governed primarily by the impedance contrast introduced within the active wave-propagation zone rather than by deep penetration into the subgrade. From a practical perspective, these results suggest that relatively shallow WIB installations can achieve effective vibration mitigation while reducing construction requirements and associated costs.

The practical implications of the WIB analysis suggest that vibration mitigation strategies should be tailored to site-specific soil conditions. The results indicate that WIB installation is most beneficial in soft subgrades where significant vibration reductions can be achieved. In contrast, for relatively stiff soils, the additional construction cost associated with wider barriers may not be justified by the corresponding reduction in vibration levels. Furthermore, the limited influence of installation depth indicates that shallow WIB systems may offer an economically attractive solution while minimizing construction complexity and disruption to railway operations.

Limitations of the study

Several assumptions adopted in the present study should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. The subgrade was represented as a homogeneous elastoplastic medium using the Mohr–Coulomb constitutive model, whereas actual railway foundations often comprise layered soils with spatially varying stiffness and damping properties that may affect wave propagation and attenuation. In addition, only vertical track irregularities were considered in the generation of wheel–rail excitation loads. Although vertical irregularities are generally the dominant source of vertical ground vibrations, other geometric defects, including alignment, gauge, and cross-level irregularities, may also influence the dynamic response.

The WIB was modeled as a continuous linear-elastic barrier with perfect soil–structure contact. Consequently, potential effects associated with construction joints, interface degradation, material aging, and long-term service conditions were not addressed. Furthermore, the analysis was conducted for a single high-speed train configuration. While the proposed framework is readily applicable to other train types, differences in axle loads, suspension systems, vehicle geometry, and operating speed may alter both the amplitude and frequency content of the induced ground vibrations.

Future work should consider multiple train configurations, full multibody vehicle models, various directional irregularities, and advanced constitutive models capable of representing strain-dependent stiffness and damping characteristics under dynamic loading. In addition, the train speed was kept constant throughout the parametric analyses to isolate the effects of track irregularities, soil stiffness, and WIB characteristics. Future studies may investigate the combined influence of train speed and track irregularity on the frequency-domain characteristics of train-induced ground vibrations.

This study developed and validated a comprehensive three-dimensional framework that integrates a multibody vehicle-track model with stochastic track irregularities and time-domain FDM analysis. This approach enables, a high-fidelity simulation of HST-induced ground vibrations and a robust evaluation of WIB performance within a single, coupled model. The key findings are:
