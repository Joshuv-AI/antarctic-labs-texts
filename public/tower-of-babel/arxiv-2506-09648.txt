Scaling Laws for Uncertainty in Deep Learning
Mattia Rosso, Simone Rossi, Giulio Franzese, Markus Heinonen, Maurizio Filippone

Abstract
Deep learning has recently revealed the existence of scaling laws, demonstrating that model performance follows predictable trends based on dataset and model sizes. Inspired by these findings and fascinating phenomena emerging in the over-parameterized regime, we examine a parallel direction: do similar scaling laws govern predictive uncertainties in deep learning? In identifiable parametric models, such scaling laws can be derived in a straightforward manner by treating model parameters in a Bayesian way. In this case, for example, we obtain O ⁡ ( 1 / N ) O(1/N) contraction rates for epistemic uncertainty with respect to the number of data N N . However, in over-parameterized models, these guarantees do not hold, leading to largely unexplored behaviors. In this work, we empirically show the existence of scaling laws associated with various measures of predictive uncertainty with respect to dataset and model sizes. Through experiments on vision and language tasks, we observe such scaling laws for in- and out-of-distribution predictive uncertainty estimated through popular approximate Bayesian inference and ensemble methods. Besides the elegance of scaling laws and the practical utility of extrapolating uncertainties to larger data or models, this work provides strong evidence to dispel recurring skepticism against Bayesian approaches: “In many applications of deep learning we have so much data available: what do we need Bayes for?” Our findings show that “so much data” is typically not enough to make epistemic uncertainty negligible.

Introduction

Deep learning has recently revealed empirical scaling laws: test performance tends to scale by a power-law
f(x)\propto x^{-\gamma}
of the size of the function, data or compute
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
;
Hoffmann et al. 2022
)
. A related ‘double descent’ effect shows that increasing model capacity can surprisingly improve generalization
(
Belkin et al. 2019
)
. These findings suggest that bigger models often perform better. A parallel question is: do similar scaling laws govern uncertainty in deep learning?

Bayesian deep learning provides a principled framework for quantifying uncertainty in neural networks by marginalizing weights, rather than relying on point estimates, to obtain predictive uncertainties.

(
Neal 1996
;
MacKay 1995
)
.
Recent contributions show that, under rather simple conditions, inferring only a subset of model parameters can retain the capacity to represent any predictive distributions, at a significantly lower cost
(
Sharma et al. 2023
)

The ability to produce well-calibrated predictive distributions and uncertainties is essential in decision-making and safety-critical applications, such as medical diagnosis and autonomous driving, where sound quantification of uncertainty can guide downstream choices
(
Papamarkou et al. 2024
)
. However, exact Bayesian inference in large networks is intractable, which has motivated scalable approximations such as those based on the Laplace method
(
Ritter et al. 2018
;
Antoran et al. 2022
)
, variational inference
(
Graves 2011
)
,
Monte Carlo (
mc
)
dropout
(
Gal and Ghahramani 2016
)
, along with (non-Bayesian) Deep Ensembles
(
Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017
)
, which have proven effective and practical in obtaining sensible predictive uncertainties. Sampling-based methods, such as
Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (
sgld
)

(
Welling and Teh 2011
)
and
Stochastic Gradient Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (
sghmc
)

(
Chen et al. 2014
)
provide scalable
Markov chain Monte Carlo (
mcmc
)
techniques for posterior inference over neural network weights.
In the remainder of this paper, we will generally refer to
Uncertainty Quantification (
uq
)
methods to indicate Bayesian and non-Bayesian approaches designed to obtain predictive uncertainties.

Despite recent criticism
(
Wimmer et al. 2023
)
, current trends have focused on information-theoretic decomposition of predictive uncertainties into aleatoric and epistemic parts
(
Hüllermeier and Waegeman 2021
)
.
In this work, we study how
predictive uncertainties
evolves in deep models as training data size
N
grows. We evaluate a range of
uq
methods and architectures for both vision and language modalities. We systematically vary optimization settings, prior choices, stochasticity level, and inference techniques.
We demonstrate that different configurations exhibit a range of scaling behaviors across uncertainty metrics, but crucially all of them follow power-laws.

While classic results show
O(1/N)
posterior-variance contraction in identifiable parametric models
(
Cam 1953
)
, over-parameterized neural networks present challenges due to singular likelihoods, non-identifiability, and non-Gaussian posteriors.
Although empirical scaling laws apply to test performance
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
)
, a systematic understanding of how uncertainty scales with
N
, model size
P
, or optimization choices is lacking. Both Bayesian and non-Bayesian
uq
approaches yield ensembles, where
Epistemic Uncertainty (
eu
)
quantifies ensemble diversity in predictions. We are particularly interested in determining when
eu
vanishes leading to ensemble collapse;
this is where we can leverage the practical utility of scaling laws.

Several theories characterize neural networks in the large-data limit.
Singular Learning Theory (
slt
)
models deep networks as singular statistical manifolds, claiming that singularities of loss minima determine learning behavior, and that generalization follows a power-law
\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{0}=\frac{\lambda}{n^{\gamma}}

(
Watanabe 2009
)
. Other perspectives include the manifold dimensionality hypothesis, linking generalization to the intrinsic dimensionality of learned representations
(
Ansuini et al. 2019
)
, and mechanistic interpretability efforts explaining simple scaling phenomena via emergent internal representations
(
Nanda et al. 2023
)
.
We derive a formal connection of generalization and total uncertainty in the case of linear models. We believe that such a framework may help to explain the scaling behavior of uncertainty in deep models.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

(i)
Empirical study.
We provide a comprehensive evaluation of predictive uncertainties using a variety of
uq
methods across different architectures, modalities, and datasets. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to consider scaling laws associated with any form of uncertainty in deep learning.

(ii)
Scaling patterns.
We empirically demonstrate that predictive uncertainties evaluated on in- and out-of-distribution, follow power-law trends with the dataset size. This allows us to extrapolate to large dataset sizes and to identify data regimes where
uq
approaches remain relevant to characterize the diversity of the ensemble to a given numerical precision.

(iii)
Theoretical insights.
We derive a formal connection between generalization error in
slt
and total uncertainty in linear models. This novel analysis provides an interesting lead to explain the scaling laws observed in the experiments for over-parameterized models.

2
Background

2.1
Uncertainty Quantification

Predictive uncertainty refers to metrics associated with an ensemble of predictive distributions
and it can be decomposed into
Aleatoric Uncertainty (
au
)
, arising from intrinsic data variability, and
eu
, reflecting uncertainty due to limited data or model knowledge
(
Hüllermeier and Waegeman 2021
)
.

The
Total Uncertainty (
tu
)
is the entropy of the mean predictive distribution

\displaystyle\mathrm{TU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p\Big(y|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{(k)}\Big)\right],

(1)

where
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{(k)}
are the model parameters of the
k
’th ensemble member or stochastic pass, and
p(y|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{(k)})
is its predictive distribution. The irreducible uncertainty
au
is the average entropy of predictions:

\displaystyle\mathrm{AU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{H}\left[p\Big(y|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{(k)}\Big)\right].

(2)

Finally, the reducible uncertainty
eu
is their difference,

\displaystyle\mathrm{EU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{TU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})-\mathrm{AU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}).

(3)

In this work we study the power-law of test predictive uncertainty against training size
N
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{test}}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{test}}}\mathrm{Unc}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{n}),\qquad\mathrm{Unc}\in\{\mathrm{TU},\mathrm{AU},\mathrm{EU}\}.

(4)

While popular, these entropy-based metrics have limitations:
(i) The standard decomposition of
tu
assumes additive separation of
au
and
eu
, which
Wimmer et al. 2023
argues not to hold in complex deep models, and the difficulty of disentangling them hinders their interpretability
(
de Jong et al. 2024
)
;
(ii) Recent works highlight epistemic uncertainty collapse of large ensembles, leading to overly confident predictions
(
Kirsch 2024
;
Fellaji and Pennerath 2024
)
.

Despite these criticisms, these metrics remain useful and tractable in practice, especially when paired with typical
uq
methods. Their consistency across tasks such as active learning, out-of-distribution detection and model calibration makes them valuable diagnostic tools.

2.2
Scaling Laws and Generalization

Empirical studies in deep learning have demonstrated that performance metrics scale predictably in model size, dataset size and compute.
Initial findings by
Hestness et al. 2017
and
Kaplan et al. 2020
showed that test loss typically decreases following a power-law of the form

\mathcal{L}(x)=\mathcal{L}_{\infty}+\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{\alpha}\,,

(5)

where
x
is the resource under analysis (e.g., dataset size
N
, model size
P
, or compute budget
C
),

\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
is the irreducible loss, and
\alpha
is modality- and task-specific (
x_{0}
is a reference constant).
These relationships hold consistently across model architectures, tasks, and modalities, as empirically shown by
Henighan et al. 2020
, who demonstrated similar loss scaling for language, image, video and multimodal domains.
The scaling exponents differ across domains, but the functional form of the scaling law remains stable, indicating a general underlying behavior.

Theoretical explanations of scaling behaviors are only recently emerging: for example
Sharma and Kaplan 2022
;
Bahri et al. 2024
linked
\alpha
to the intrinsic dimension and spectral properties of the data, showing how the data geometry drives the reducible portion of the loss.
However, despite these empirical and theoretical advances, a comprehensive understanding of how epistemic and aleatoric uncertainties scales with model and data size remain an open question.
Indeed, a Bayesian perspective on uncertainty scaling laws is currently missing: it is unclear what kind of power laws uncertainty exhibits, if any. Understanding uncertainty scaling could significantly enhance the development and comprehension of Bayesian deep learning.

3
Methods

In this section, we describe the approximate Bayesian inference and ensemble methods used in our experiments. We hypothesize that if uncertainty scaling laws exist, they should emerge regardless of the
uq
method.

mc
Dropout.

MC dropout is a simple and common inference method, with connections to variational inference
(
Gal and Ghahramani 2016
)
. During training standard dropout is applied, while at test time dropout masks are resampled to produce stochastic forward passes, yielding ensemble predictions.
Due to its simplicity and universality, MC dropout is a good
uq
baseline.

Gaussian Approximations.

Gaussian are a common family of posterior approximations, and include Laplace approximations
(
Ritter et al. 2018
)
and Variational Inference
(
Graves 2011
)
.
In Laplace approximations the curvature of the log-posterior at a local optimum determines the covariance of the Gaussian approximation.
In variational inference, the optimization seeks an approximate distribution close to the true posterior in the
Kullback-Leibler divergence (
kl
)
sense.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo.

mcmc
is the classic method of obtaining samples from the posterior over model parameters
(
Neal 1996
;
MacKay 1995
)
. Gradient-based
mcmc
, such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, are some of the most effective samplers.
Mini-batch-based
sghmc

(
Chen et al. 2014
)
and Langevin dynamics
(
Welling and Teh 2011
)
have been successfully proposed to sample from the posterior over parameters of deep neural networks of moderate size
(
Tran et al. 2022
;
Izmailov et al. 2021
)
.
We consider parameter sampling with both global parameter priors and Gaussian process functional priors
(
Tran et al. 2022
)
.

Deep Ensembles.

Deep ensembles
(
Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017
)
are a popular technique to obtain predictive uncertainties, despite lacking a full Bayesian interpretation. Multiple networks are trained independently from different seeds and uncertainty is estimated from the ensemble of predictions. Deep ensembles tend to provide better uncertainty estimates and are more robust to model misspecification than MC dropout. We train ensembles of size
M\in\{5,10\}
.

Partially stochastic networks.

We consider partially stochastic networks where only few layers are inferred, while rest are optimized in a standard way
(
Sharma et al. 2023
)
.

5
Theoretical connections

The theory of identifiable models has extensively analyzed asymptotic behaviors, such as posterior contraction and convergence of test loss and generalization error. In this section, we recall how predictive uncertainty contracts with increasing data in Bayesian linear regression, highlight its connection to Watanabe’s Generalization Error, and outline links to Singular Learning Theory.

5.1
tu
scaling in Identifiable Parametric Models

We consider Bayesian linear regression
y={{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})+\epsilon
, where
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
are parameters of interest, and
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})=[\phi_{1}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}),\phi_{2}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}),\dotsc,\phi_{P}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})]^{\top}
are the basis functions, and assume zero-mean noise
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. We define the likelihood over
N
iid observations
\{{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}\}=\{({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
,

\displaystyle p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}},\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{N}\big(y_{n}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{n})^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}},\sigma^{2}\big).

(6)

By assuming a conjugate Gaussian prior
p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\mathcal{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}}_{0},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}}_{0})
the posterior is also Gaussian with mean
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}}_{N}={{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}}_{N}\left({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}}_{0}^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}}_{0}+\sigma^{-2}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}\right)
and covariance
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}}_{N}=\left(\sigma^{-2}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}+{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}}_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}
.
The predictive posterior for a new test points
({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*},y_{*})
is also Gaussian,

p(y_{*}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}})=\mathcal{N}\Big(y_{*}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{m}}}}_{N}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*}),\sigma^{2}_{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*})\Big)

with
\sigma^{2}_{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*})=\sigma^{2}+{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}(x_{*})^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}}_{N}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}(x_{*}).

The predictive variance
\mathrm{Var}[y_{*}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}]
decomposes into the uncertainty of data
\sigma^{2}
(
au
), and the uncertainty of parameters
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*})^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}}_{N}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*})
(
eu
). We stress that
eu
is made available only by taking the Bayesian approach of treating
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
as a random variable. It can be shown that
\sigma^{2}_{N+1}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*})\leq\sigma^{2}_{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*})
– the posterior distribution becomes narrower as additional data points are observed
(
Qazaz et al. 1997
)
. Moreover, in the limit of
N\rightarrow\infty
we get
\sigma^{2}_{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})\rightarrow\sigma^{2}
: in the infinite data regime, the predictive uncertainty converges to its irreducible
au
component.

5.2
Singular Learning Theory

Our speculative theoretical link is
slt
, which provides insights into the learning dynamics of highly overparameterized models. The Fisher information of deep neural networks is often singular at certain parameter values; despite forming a measure-zero subset, these singularities significantly influence learning.
slt
proves that the asymptotic properties of learning are shaped by the geometry near such degenerate points
(
Watanabe 2009
;
Watanabe 2018
)
. One main quantity of interest in this framework is the generalization error
G
. Here we limit ourself to connect this to the
tu
in Bayesian Linear Regression. Using Watanabe’s notation

F_{N}=-\log p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N})=-\log\int p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta})d\boldsymbol{\theta}

(7)

is the negative marginal log likelihood (NMLL) of the data
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N}
of size
N
. The generalization error
G_{N}=\mathbb{E}_{p(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},\hat{{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}})}[F_{N+1}]-F_{N}
measures the expected increase in marginal likelihood when training with one additional data point (See
Section

A.2
) and it can be rewritten as:

G_{N}=-\mathbb{E}_{\underbrace{\scriptstyle p(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{p(y)}}\Big[\log\underbrace{p\big(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}\big)}_{q_{N}(y)}\Big],

(8)

which reduces to the log posterior predictive distribution for the
(N+1)
’th datapoint given the first
N
datapoints, under the true process

\displaystyle y_{N+1}\sim\mathcal{N}\Big(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}_{\mathrm{true}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2}\Big).

(9)

By denoting the true process by
p(y)
and the posterior predictive from
N
data by
q_{N}(y)
we can manipulate the expression of the generalization error to obtain:

G_{N}=-\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\log{(2\pi e\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})}}_{\text{aleatoric uncertainty}}-\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big[p(y)||q_{N}(y)\big]}_{\text{epistemic uncertainty}},

(10)

where
\mathrm{KL}[p(y)||q_{N}(y)]
is the
kl
divergence between the true and the posterior predictive distribution, quantifying the epistemic uncertainty arising from limited knowledge of the true model parameters.
By looking at the asymptotic expression of
tu
as
N\rightarrow\infty
and by taking, without loss of generality, identity basis functions, we get:

\displaystyle\mathrm{TU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}[q_{N}(y)]

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})+\frac{1}{2(N+1)}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{X_{N+1}}^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1}+O\left(\frac{1}{(N+1)^{2}}\right).

(12)

As more data is collected, the predictive posterior
q_{N}(y)
converges toward the true predictive distribution
p(y)
. Consequently, the generalization error
G_{N}
asymptotically approaches the irreducible aleatoric uncertainty. Similarly, the
tu
converges to the aleatoric component as the epistemic part vanishes. In
Watanabe 1999
it’s proved an asymptotic expansion of
G_{N}
which is proportional to the effective dimensionality of the model.

We hypothesize that the theoretical tools provided by this framework, closely linked to concepts from Statistical Physics, may offer a promising foundation to explain some of the scaling behaviors we have observed. In particular, we find recent efforts to computationally characterize the effective dimensionality of deep learning models especially compelling
(
Lau et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
, and we aim to investigate formal connections in future extensions of this work.

6
Conclusions

Inspired by recent works on scaling laws in deep learning, we investigated whether these apply to predictive uncertainties as well.
Our empirical findings on computer vision and language tasks indicate that these scaling laws appear in a broad range of design choices, including architecture design, posterior approximation/ensembling approach, and hyper-parameters.

We set out to explain the emergence of such scaling laws and, inspired by
slt
, we provide novel theoretical insights on the connections between generalization error and predictive uncertainty.
This helps us in understanding the power-law behavior associated with the scaling of information-theoretic measures of uncertainty with respect to dataset size.

While the theory gives some indications along these lines, the experiments reveal that obtaining exact power-law coefficients from the theory is not easy, as choices of model architecture,
uq
method, and hyper-parameters affect such coefficients in unpredictable ways.
As a result, we believe that our work can be used to derive practical strategies to extrapolate uncertainties to large
N
, e.g., to predict how much data is needed to obtain ensemble predictions which are indistinguishable up to a given numerical precision.

In future works, we will investigate alternative theoretical angles to explain scaling laws on uncertainties in deep learning.
One approach could be to study the behavior of uncertainties in models with large number of units, such as WideResNets, and connect their uncertainties with those obtained by Gaussian processes

(
)

.

Limitations

Previous works on scaling laws for deep learning show clear trends of test performance with respect to model parameters; however, with our experimental setup, we did not observe the same for predictive uncertainties (see
Fig.

7
) and we were hoping to have more computational resources to investigate this aspect in more detail.
Another element characterizing scaling laws for test performance is the behavior with respect to computational budget, which we do not consider here, again for reasons related to limited computational resources.
Finally, it would have been great to study more applications where we could test the extrapolation of scaling laws to very large dataset sizes, in addition to the experiments on the GPT-2 language model.

Broader impacts.

This paper demonstrates the emergence of scaling laws of uncertainties in deep learning.
The results should motivate a wider adoption of
uq
methods for deep learning, even in the case of supposedly large datasets, in order to obtain uncertainties that can be used to assist decision-making.

A.1
Total Uncertainty in Bayesian Linear Regression

We consider a class of models defined as a linear combination of fixed nonlinear functions of the input variables of the form:

y({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\theta_{j}\phi_{j}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})={{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}),

(13)

where
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}=(\theta_{0},\dotsc,\theta_{M-1})^{\top}
are model parameters and
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}=(\phi_{0},\dotsc,\phi_{M-1})^{\top}
are known as
basis functions
which allow
y({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})
to be a non-linear function of the input
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}
.

We consider the target variable
y
that is given by a deterministic function
y({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})
plus additive Gaussian noise:

y={{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})+\epsilon\text{,}

(14)

where
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
is treated as a random vector and
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
, obtaining

p(y|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}},\sigma^{2})=\mathcal{N}(y|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}},\sigma^{2})\text{.}

(15)

We consider a supervised learning problem with
N
input-label training pairs
\{{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}\}=\{({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Assuming that the data points are drawn independently from
Eq.

15
, the likelihood becomes:

p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}},\sigma^{2})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{N}(y_{n}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{n})^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}},\sigma^{2})\text{.}

(16)

According to the transformation of the features introduced by the set of basis functions
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}
, we define the design matrix
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
with entries
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}_{nj}=\phi_{j}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{n})
. We assume a conjugate prior over
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}
:

p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})=\mathcal{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}}_{0},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{0})\text{.}

(17)

From Bayes’ theorem, the posterior can be derived in closed form leading to the following result:

\displaystyle p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}}_{N},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{N})\text{,}

(18)

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}}_{N}

\displaystyle={{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{N}\left({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{0}^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}}_{0}+\sigma^{-2}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}\right)\text{,}

(19)

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma^{-2}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}+{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\text{.}

(20)

We are interested in the predictive uncertainty for a new test point
({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*},y_{*})
so we inspect the predictive posterior and its properties:

\displaystyle p(y_{*}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}})

\displaystyle=\int p(y_{*}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}})\text{d}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(y_{*}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}}_{N}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*}),\underbrace{\sigma^{2}+{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*})^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{N}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*})}_{\mathbb{V}[y_{*}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}]})\text{,}

(21)

where
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}}_{N}
and
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{N}
are the posterior mean and covariance matrix. From the predictive variance
\mathbb{V}[y_{*}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}]
we identify two components: (i)
\sigma^{2}
which represents the uncertainty related to the data noise (
au
) and (ii)
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*})^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{N}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*})
which is the uncertainty associated with model parameters (
eu
). As we discuss in
Section

5.1
, by defining
\mathbb{V}[y_{*}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{*},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}]\coloneq\sigma^{2}_{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})
as the predictive uncertainty for a new test point
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}
when training on
N
input points, we have that
\sigma^{2}_{N+1}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})\leq\sigma^{2}_{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})
, meaning that the posterior distribution becomes narrower as additional data points are observed
(
Qazaz et al. 1997
)
. Moreover,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\sigma^{2}_{N}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})=\sigma^{2}
; that is, in the infinite data regime, the predictive uncertainty converges to its irreducible component,
au
.

A.1.1
From posterior predictive variance to Total Uncertainty

We now derive an expression for the (total) predictive uncertainty associated with a new data point
({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}},y)
, defined as the entropy of the predictive posterior:

\displaystyle\text{TU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}\left[p(y|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi e\sigma_{N}^{2}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi e\left(\sigma^{2}+{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})^{\top}\left(\sigma^{-2}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}+{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi e\sigma^{2}\left(1+\sigma^{-2}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})^{\top}\left(\sigma^{-2}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}+{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi e\sigma^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\sigma^{-2}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})^{\top}\left(\sigma^{-2}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}+{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi e\sigma^{2}\right)}_{\text{AU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2N}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{\phi}^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})}_{\text{EU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})}+\,O\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right)

(22)

In the previous derivation, we assume
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}=({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{1},\dotsc,{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N})
to be a collection of IID samples with
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}}}
mean and
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{x}
covariance matrix. The same holds for
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}
where samples
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})
are drawn independently from a distribution with
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}}}
mean and
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{\Phi}
covariance matrix. By the Law of Large Numbers
\frac{1}{N}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}[{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}){{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})^{\top}]={{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{\Phi}
as
N\rightarrow\infty
which implies
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Phi}}}}\rightarrow N{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{\Phi}
. In addition, for large
N
the
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{0}^{-1}
term vanishes. This shows that as
N\rightarrow\infty
,
tu
approaches
au
and the
eu
decays with rate
\frac{1}{N}
.

A.2
Total Uncertainty and Generalization Error for linear models

In this Section we formally derive a connection between the Watanabe generalization error
G_{N}
and the Total Uncertainty (
tu
) for Bayesian linear models. Using the notation from
Watanabe 2009
;
Watanabe 2018
the generalization error
G_{N}
and the Free Energy
F_{N}
are defined as:

\displaystyle G_{N}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}_{\mathrm{true}})}[F_{N+1}]-F_{N}

(23)

\displaystyle F_{N}

\displaystyle=-\log p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N})=-\log\int p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta})d\boldsymbol{\theta}.

(24)

We consider the same linear model of
Section

A.1
and without loss of generality we set the basis functions to be identity functions, i.e.,
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}})={{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}
. We also introduce
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}_{\mathrm{true}}
, the set of underlying true parameters. A new data point
({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},y_{N+1})
is generated according to the true process:

y_{N+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}_{\mathrm{true}})=\mathcal{N}(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}_{\mathrm{true}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2}).

(25)

The free energy
F_{N}
is defined as the negative log marginal likelihood:

\displaystyle F_{N}

\displaystyle=-\log{p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N})}

\displaystyle=-\log{\int{p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})d{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}}

\displaystyle=-\log{\int{\left(\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(y_{n}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{n},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})\right)p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})d{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}}.

(26)

We now relate
F_{N+1}
and
F_{N}
, the free energy for
N+1
and
N
input points, respectively:

\displaystyle F_{N+1}

\displaystyle=-\log p(Y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N+1})

\displaystyle=-\log p(y_{N+1},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N})

\displaystyle=-\log p(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},D_{N})p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N})

\displaystyle=-\log p(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},D_{N})-\log p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N})

\displaystyle=-\log p(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},D_{N})+F_{N}

(27)

where
D_{N}=\{{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}}}_{N},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}}_{N}\}
and
p(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},D_{N})
is the posterior predictive distribution for the
(N+1)
data point given the first
N
data points:

\displaystyle p(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},D_{N})

\displaystyle=\int{p(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}})p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}|D_{N})d{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(y_{N+1}|\mu_{\mathrm{pred}},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{pred}}),

(28)

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{pred}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},D_{N}]={{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}}}_{N}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1}

(29)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{pred}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{V}[y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},D_{N}]=\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2}+{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{N}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1}.

(30)

Let’s simplify the notation: we rename the new data point
y_{N+1}
simply
y
such that
p(y)\coloneq p(y_{N+1}|\mu_{\mathrm{true}},\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})
where
\mu_{\mathrm{true}}\coloneq{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}_{\mathrm{true}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1}
. We can now compute the generalization error:

\displaystyle G_{N}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(y)}[F_{N+1}]-F_{N}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(y)}[F_{N+1}-F_{N}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(y)}[-\log{p(y_{N+1}|{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1},D_{N})}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(y)}\left[\frac{1}{2}\log{(2\pi\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^{2})}+\frac{(y-\mu_{\mathrm{pred}})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log{(2\pi\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^{2})}+\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2}+(\mu_{\mathrm{true}}-\mu_{\mathrm{pred}})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{H}[\mathcal{N}(y|\mu_{\mathrm{true}},\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})]}_{\text{AU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1})}+\underbrace{\text{KL}\left[\mathcal{N}(y|\mu_{\mathrm{true}},\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})||\mathcal{N}(y|\mu_{\mathrm{pred}},\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^{2})\right]}_{\text{EU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1})}

(31)

where we used the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{p(y)}[(y-\mu_{\mathrm{pred}})^{2}]=\mathbb{V}_{p(y)}[y]+(\mu_{\mathrm{true}}-\mu_{\mathrm{pred}})^{2}=\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2}+(\mu_{\mathrm{true}}-\mu_{\mathrm{pred}})^{2}
and:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}[\mathcal{N}(y|\mu_{\mathrm{true}},\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})

(32)

\displaystyle\text{KL}\left[\mathcal{N}(y|\mu_{\mathrm{true}},\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})||\mathcal{N}(y|\mu_{\mathrm{pred}},\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2}}\right)+\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2}+(\mu_{\mathrm{true}}-\mu_{\mathrm{pred}})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}.

(33)

This is closely related to the asymptotic expression we derived for
tu
in
Section

A.1.1
where, if we now consider identity basis functions and a test point
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1}
, it becomes:

\displaystyle\text{TU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})}_{\text{AU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1})}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2(N+1)}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}}_{X}^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1}}_{\text{EU}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{N+1})}+O\left(\frac{1}{(N+1)^{2}}\right)

(34)

In terms of generalization error
G_{N}
, as the posterior predictive
q_{N}(y)
moves closer to the true data generating distribution
p(y)
, the
kl
term goes to zero and
G_{N}
converges to the aleatoric component
\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})
, i.e., if
\mu_{\mathrm{pred}}=\mu_{\mathrm{true}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^{2}=\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2}
then
\text{KL}\left[\mathcal{N}(y|\mu_{\mathrm{true}},\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}^{2})||\mathcal{N}(y|\mu_{\mathrm{pred}},\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^{2})\right]=0
.

A.2.1
Asymptotic behaviors

In
Watanabe 1999
;
Hayashi and Sawada 2025
it’s showed that the generalization error and the free energy have the asymptotic expansion:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}[G_{N}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{n}-\frac{m-1}{n\log n}+o\left(\frac{1}{n\log n}\right)

(35)

\displaystyle F_{N}

\displaystyle=nS_{n}+\lambda\log n-(m-1)\log\log n+O_{p}(1)

(36)

where
\lambda

a positive integer and

is the expectation operator the overall dataset. The constant

and
F_{n}-n
learning curves.
m
is called a multiplicity in algebraic geometry. In the case of regular models
\lambda=\frac{d}{2}
and
m=1
where
d
is the number of model parameters.
