Optimized YOLO architectures for efficient Kiwi detection in precision agriculture on embedded systems
Authors: Berat Karacaoglu, Muhammet Emin Sahin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate and efficient fruit detection is essential for precision agriculture, particularly in densely occluded crops such as kiwifruit. This study presents a comprehensive benchmarking and optimization framework covering YOLOv8–YOLOv11 architectures for kiwi detection, evaluated under both high-performance training conditions and embedded deployment on an NVIDIA Jetson TX2. A field-collected dataset containing 2,925 training and 1,936 test annotations was used to train five sub-models (n, s, m, l, x) per YOLO version under identical settings. To enhance efficiency for edge deployment, a structured hyperparameter optimization procedure was applied to all “s” models, yielding substantial performance gains without additional architectural modifications. Among all evaluated models, the optimized YOLOv11s achieved the best accuracy–efficiency trade-off, reaching mAP@0.5 = 0.956, precision = 0.868, recall = 0.918, and an embedded inference time of 3.33 s/image on Jetson TX2. While larger models (e.g., YOLOv8x, YOLOv11l) attained slightly higher raw accuracies (up to mAP@0.5 = 0.957), their latency rendered them unsuitable for edge deployment. The results demonstrate that lightweight YOLO architectures, when supported by targeted hyperparameter tuning, can be effectively adapted for resource-constrained agricultural systems. The proposed evaluation and optimization pipeline provides a transferable methodology for other fruit-detection tasks and supports future development of embedded vision solutions in precision agriculture.Keywords: Kiwi detection, YOLO, Object detection, Embedded systems, Precision agricultureSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing, Plant sciences

Introduction

Agriculture, one of humanity’s oldest economic pillars, remains vital for food security, ecological sustainability, and socioeconomic stability1,2. Despite technological progress, agriculture faces unprecedented challenges: rapid population growth increases food demand, while arable land continues to shrink due to urbanization and climate change2. Environmental stressors—such as drought, floods, extreme temperatures, and water scarcity—further disrupt production processes and expose the limitations of traditional farming practices3. These pressures have accelerated the transition toward smart, data-driven agriculture, where productivity is evaluated not only by yield but also by resource efficiency, environmental impact, product quality, and labor optimization4. Within this transformation, digitalization, automation, and artificial intelligence (AI) have become central. Especially in fruit production—where processes such as harvest timing, quality assessment, ripeness estimation, and early disease detection still rely heavily on human expertise—AI-driven systems offer crucial gains in speed and accuracy2. Manual observation is time-consuming, error-prone, and insufficient for the early detection of diseases or optimal harvest decisions, which may lead to reduced shelf life, lower quality, and economic loss. A core challenge in these environments is the reliable detection of fruits under natural conditions where foliage, occlusion, shadows, and varying illumination significantly complicate visual recognition. Kiwi fruit exemplifies these difficulties due to its dense canopy structure, overlapping growth patterns, and skin color that blends with the background5.

Deep learning–based computer vision methods, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have demonstrated strong performance in overcoming these challenges6. YOLO (You Only Look Once)–based detectors are particularly suited for real-time agricultural applications because they can localize multiple objects in a single inference pass7. Several studies confirm the robustness of YOLO variants for fruit detection, ripeness analysis, and yield estimation in complex outdoor environments. For instance, a YOLOv7-based plum detection system achieved high accuracy under occlusion and variable lighting9, while Chen et al. enhanced YOLOv8n with EMBC, DySample, and TADDH modules, producing EDT-YOLOv8n, which improved accuracy and reduced model size for embedded systems8. Similarly, Niu et al. redesigned YOLOv5 for small unripe plum detection, reaching 91.65% accuracy with only 7 ms inference time, outperforming YOLOv5, YOLOv7, and Faster R-CNN9. Studies across various crops highlight the comparative advantages of YOLO-based architectures. Li et al. presented YOLOv3 to provide the best balance of speed and accuracy for greenhouse detection tasks compared with Faster R-CNN and SSD10.

An et al. proposed SDNet (based on YOLOX), achieving 94.26% precision by integrating attention mechanisms and enhanced bounding-box optimization11. Xu12 applied the YOLOv3 detection algorithm to identify and classify tea buds across different growth stages and categories. Lightweight and attention-enhanced YOLO variants continue to expand capabilities for detecting small, overlapping fruits. Examples include GhostNet-YOLOv4 (Gao et al.) and YOLOv8n-CRS (Chen et al., 2024), both of which improve performance while reducing computational cost13,14. Tang et al. submitted 94.91% mAP with YOLOv7-plum by integrating CBAM attention15. Beyond fruit detection, YOLO-based systems facilitate wildfire monitoring16, plant disease recognition17, and other agricultural decision-support tasks.

Recent advancements in optimization and hybrid AI contribute to performance improvements across domains relevant to smart agriculture. Ghosh et al. proposed the MLOA algorithm, which overcame early-convergence issues and achieved high accuracy in medical image analysis18, while Chanda and Ghosh demonstrated how AI–blockchain integration can streamline HR processes19. Transformer–CNN hybrid models have also shown promise in physics-guided image analysis, reducing turbulence prediction errors by up to 40%20. Although these studies are outside agriculture, they exemplify methodological innovations—metaheuristics, hybrid architectures, and efficient optimization—that can inform future agricultural vision systems. Parallel to detection research, IoT-based smart agriculture systems are evolving rapidly. Embedded irrigation systems using real-time environmental data significantly reduce water waste, as demonstrated by Morchid et al.21. Fuzzy logic and IoT-based irrigation architectures have been shown to decrease water consumption by 11–28% and increase yields by up to 37%22. Drone technologies, highlighted by Chouhan et al., submitted fast, accurate data for disease detection, spraying, and growth monitoring, contributing to precision agriculture and sustainable farm management23,24. Chouhan et al. developed a Counter Propagation Network trained with Fuzzy Competitive Learning (FCPN) to handle uncertainties in natural scene images25. Using region growing for initial seed generation and Bacterial Foraging Optimization for weight tuning, the model automatically determined the optimal number of clusters and achieved high object-segmentation accuracy, outperforming FCM, SOM, CPN, and SEEFC. Upadhyay et al. proposed 3SW-Net, a lightweight architecture for early weed detection combining grayscale imagery, SLIC superpixel segmentation, and HOG-based texture features26. Evaluated on 4,000 images using five-fold cross-validation, it achieved 99.56% accuracy, 98.99% sensitivity, and 99.68% specificity. With only ~ 9 million parameters, 3SW-Net is well suited for embedded and real-time smart agriculture systems. Upadhyay and Gupta conducted a series of studies focusing on plant disease detection and severity assessment across multiple fruit species. In their first work, they introduced a modified ResNeXt-based framework that combines preprocessing, Fuzzy C-Means segmentation, and an enhanced ResNeXt classifier for identifying fungal diseases in heterogeneous fruit images, including apples, guavas, and custard apples. Evaluated on 14,408 images, the model achieved 98.92% accuracy and F1-scores exceeding 0.99, outperforming existing multi-crop disease detection methods27. In a subsequent study, the authors proposed a disease-severity assessment system that integrates a ResNet-50 classifier with SegLearner—an improved SegFormer-derived segmentation architecture featuring multi-scale feature aggregation, an efficient Transformer encoder, and semi-supervised mask generation. Tested on the Mask-LeafDataset, SegLearner achieved 91.4% mIoU, 95.4% accuracy, and a 94.7% F1-score, outperforming U-Net, SegFormer, and Mask R-CNN. By leveraging pixel-ratio calculations from segmentation masks, the system demonstrated reliable severity categorization and strong generalization on an external dataset28. Paul and Machavaram evaluated YOLOv9c-seg and YOLOv9e-seg for peduncle detection in nighttime greenhouse images, where YOLOv9c-seg delivered the best results with mAP@0.5 scores of 0.751 (boxes) and 0.725 (masks) and an F1-score of 0.89; Grounded SAM, operating without training, achieved only 59% confidence29. Their automated harvesting framework, based on YOLOv8s, achieved mAP@0.5 = 0.967 for fruit detection, box mAP = 0.790, and mask mAP = 0.771. Using a RealSense D455 camera, 3D cut-point localization errors were 8–12 mm, while the ByteTrack counting module reached 94.1% accuracy and the mobile app delivered real-time detection with IoU = 0.92 and 150 ms latency30. In a subsequent nighttime detection study, YOLOv9 again surpassed Grounding DINO with mAP = 0.947 and 26 ms inference, compared with Grounding DINO’s F1-score of 0.70. Yet, Grounding DINO showed greater flexibility in zero-shot tasks such as positional search and growth-stage estimation. Leveraging both strengths, the authors introduced YOLOVOVOD, which increased Grounding DINO’s positional-search confidence by 65.11% and achieved 88% confidence in growth-stage detection—offering a more accurate and adaptable framework for agricultural machine vision31.

Deep learning remains central in plant disease diagnosis, with numerous studies reporting near-perfect accuracies. Pacal et al. analyzed 160 studies from 2020 to 2024, demonstrating that CNN and Vision Transformer (ViT) models provide reliable automated disease detection32. EfficientNet, ResNet, Res2Net, and MaxViT-derived architectures have consistently achieved high accuracies in sugarcane33, tomato34, grapevine35, and apple36, disease classification37,38. These results support the broader argument that advanced CNN/ViT architectures significantly enhance agricultural decision-making. Other supporting studies include weed detection improvements via PSO-based YOLO optimization39, blockchain-based supply chain solutions40, cloud-enabled precision agriculture platforms41, and enhanced classification ensemble models for disease detection42. Furthermore, several works focusing on capsicum detection—YOLOv843, and YOLOv10 vs. SAM-DINO44 highlight the growing applicability of YOLO for real-time greenhouse monitoring. Beyond agriculture-specific applications, broader advances in digital ecosystems also inform the development of smart farming technologies. For example, Chanda et al. introduced a blockchain-based offboarding framework using the PBFT consensus mechanism, demonstrating secure, transparent, and high-throughput event management45. Similarly, Chanda and Singh conducted a bibliometric analysis of blockchain use in HR management, reviewing 124 studies and identifying key themes such as recruitment, talent governance, and data security46. Morchid et al. show that AI- and IoT-based systems improve early detection of agricultural and forest fires, increasing response speed and resource efficiency, and achieving higher accuracy than traditional methods47 Paul and Machavaram report that GenAI enhances efficiency, decision-making, and resource use in Indian agriculture within the Agriculture 4.0 framework, though infrastructural, data-governance, and ethical issues still limit broader adoption. Their study presents a feasible framework integrating multiple data sources with GenAI, supported by pilot project outcomes48. Although these works lie outside the agricultural domain, they highlight emerging trends in decentralized data management and trustworthy AI systems—concepts that increasingly shape next-generation smart agriculture infrastructures. In the context of a public kiwi fruit dataset used by Ulutaş and Altın49, Mask R-CNN with a ResNet50 backbone achieved a mAP of 98.48%, demonstrating the feasibility of deep learning–based kiwi detection despite occlusion and lighting variations.

Materials and methods

Dataset

The image dataset used in this study was collected in August 2021 at Güngör Farm, located in the Çarşamba Plain of Samsun, Turkey, during the pre-harvest period of kiwifruit under natural orchard conditions49. To ensure visual diversity and to reflect real-world agricultural scenarios, all images were captured on-site under naturally varying illumination, shadowing, canopy density, and branch complexity. Unlike laboratory-prepared datasets that often present controlled and homogeneous imaging conditions, this dataset incorporates the environmental variability inherent in open-field production systems, thereby increasing the model’s robustness and practical applicability. Image acquisition was carried out using a Samsung A51 smartphone mounted on a tripod-supported gimbal system fixed at approximately 1 m above ground level. The device’s 48-megapixel RGB main camera enabled the collection of high-resolution images under natural daylight without any artificial lighting. This setup was intentionally chosen to capture realistic orchard scenes where fruit visibility is affected by leaf occlusions, irregular branch structures, and heterogeneous light distribution. Such natural variability was included to enhance the dataset’s representativeness and improve the model’s ability to generalize under real operating conditions.

The dataset used in this study has been made publicly accessible on GitHub, allowing other researchers to utilize it for benchmarking and reproducibility purposes50. For annotation, rectangular bounding boxes were manually created exclusively for object detection tasks. Since the dataset contains only a single class (kiwifruit), all annotations were assigned the class label “0.” The dataset is divided into two subsets: the training set consists of 66 images containing a total of 2,925 bounding box annotations, while the test set includes 40 images with 1,936 bounding box annotations.

Representative raw images from the dataset are illustrated in Fig. 2. These images were captured directly in natural orchard conditions, exhibiting significant visual variability such as heterogeneous illumination, leaf density, branch occlusions, and irregular shadow patterns. This natural diversity creates a realistic and challenging learning environment that enhances the practical applicability of object detection models.

Manual annotation was performed using the Roboflow platform, where each kiwifruit instance was carefully enclosed within a rectangular bounding box. In deep learning–based object detection, particularly in single-stage architectures such as YOLO, model performance strongly depends on the quality and consistency of input images. Therefore, raw images underwent several preprocessing steps before being fed into the model to improve accuracy and stabilize training. All images, originally captured at 512 × 512 pixels, were resized to 640 × 640 pixels using the Roboflow platform to match the input size requirements of the YOLO architecture. EXIF metadata was used to automatically correct image orientation, ensuring consistent object alignment across the dataset.

Manual bounding boxes created using the Roboflow platform.

The YOLO model’s fixed input resolution facilitated standardized training and testing, enabling improved learning of objects at varying scales. Bounding box coordinates were normalized relative to image dimensions and stored in YOLO format as [x_center, y_center, width, height] along with class labels Since the dataset contains only a single class (kiwifruit), all annotations were assigned the class label “0.” During the annotation process, bounding boxes were drawn to tightly enclose each visible kiwifruit instance, regardless of variations in size, occlusion, or viewing angle. In cases where fruits overlapped, each fruit was annotated individually as long as its contour was partially identifiable. For partially occluded fruits, bounding boxes were created if at least 30–40% of the fruit surface was visible. Small and distant fruits were also annotated, provided that their circular shape remained distinguishable from the background. This consistent annotation policy ensured uniform bounding box placement across the dataset, particularly in visually complex orchard scenes with dense foliage and irregular branch structures. These preprocessing steps ensured more stable training and enhanced the model’s robustness against environmental variability commonly present in orchard conditions.

Object detection and YOLO models

Object detection, one of the most impactful applications of deep learning, aims not only to identify objects within an image but also to determine their precise locations51. Traditional image processing methods have shown limited capability in this domain, but the advent of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) brought a major breakthrough. In challenging scenarios with cluttered backgrounds or overlapping objects, deep learning models stand out by performing classification and localization simultaneously52. Region proposal–based methods, such as Faster R-CNN, achieve high accuracy but often struggle to meet the speed requirements of real-time applications10. Single-stage architectures, developed to optimize both speed and accuracy, address these limitations. Among them, YOLO introduced a paradigm shift by performing detection through a single neural network, delivering an effective balance between accuracy and real-time performance7. The evolution of the YOLO architecture began with YOLOv1, and each subsequent release has introduced more advanced network structures, improved training strategies, and higher detection performance. The early versions, from YOLOv1 to YOLOv3, primarily focused on speed and architectural simplicity. The intermediate versions, YOLOv4 through YOLOv7, integrated enhanced modules to optimize the trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency. With the release of YOLOv8 in 2023, the architecture adopted an anchor-free design, improving flexibility and reducing computational complexity. Subsequent versions—YOLOv9, YOLOv10, and YOLOv11—refined the architecture with advanced building blocks, gradient optimization techniques, and attention mechanisms. These updates led to notable improvements in both small-object detection and overall accuracy. Hyperparameters

Hyperparameters are user-defined variables that configure a machine learning algorithm prior to training and determine how it operates. Unlike model parameters, which are learned during training, hyperparameters are set in advance and exert a decisive influence on model accuracy, convergence speed, and generalization capacity. To identify the most appropriate configuration, the literature proposes various hyperparameter optimization strategies. One of the most widely used methods is Grid Search (GS), which systematically evaluates all possible combinations within predefined value ranges for each hyperparameter. Although GS is straightforward and easy to implement, its computational cost increases substantially as the number of hyperparameters grows, due to the exponential expansion of the search space. Consequently, more efficient alternatives—such as Random Search, Bayesian Optimization, and evolutionary algorithms—are increasingly preferred for high-dimensional problems. Random Search mitigates unnecessary evaluations by randomly sampling the search space and can outperform GS, particularly when certain hyperparameters have disproportionately greater influence on performance. Bayesian Optimization, on the other hand, leverages knowledge from prior evaluations to guide the search process, enabling near-optimal solutions with fewer iterations. Evolutionary algorithms draw on principles of biological evolution to offer strong exploratory capabilities within large and complex search spaces. The common advantage of these methods is their ability to reduce computational cost while improving model performance through more intelligent and adaptive search mechanisms. In this context, modern hyperparameter optimization approaches provide considerably more practical, effective, and sustainable solutions for high-dimensional and computationally expensive deep learning models compared with traditional techniques.

Evaluation metrics

In image-based object detection, the performance of deep learning architectures is assessed not only in terms of theoretical accuracy but also by their stability in real-world applications. This is particularly crucial in domains with low error tolerance—such as agriculture, healthcare, security, and industrial automation—where models must demonstrate not only the ability to make correct detections but also the capability to produce consistent and reliable results. Real-time object detection frameworks, such as YOLO, offer significant advantages in this regard7,53. To objectively evaluate and compare the performance of such algorithms, systematic evaluation metrics are essential. In object detection tasks, the most commonly adopted metrics include Intersection over Union (IoU), Precision, Recall, F1 Score, and mean Average Precision (mAP)54–57. These metrics provide quantitative insights into both the localization accuracy and the detection coverage of the model.

Intersection over Union (IoU) is a fundamental accuracy metric that measures the spatial overlap between the predicted object location and the ground truth bounding box. It quantifies how precisely the predicted bounding box aligns with the actual object54. In YOLO architectures, the input image is divided into grid cells, each responsible for predicting bounding boxes. The accuracy of these predictions is evaluated using the IoU score.

Embedded system

In this study, the NVIDIA Jetson TX2 was employed as a high-performance edge computing platform optimized for embedded artificial intelligence applications58. Equipped with a 256-core Pascal-based GPU and a heterogeneous six-core CPU architecture consisting of dual Denver 2 and quad ARM Cortex-A57 cores, the Jetson TX2 provides low-latency and energy-efficient processing for real-time image analysis and deep learning inference. Through the JetPack SDK, the platform offers CUDA, cuDNN, TensorRT, and OpenCV support, enabling hardware-accelerated and highly optimized execution of deep learning models. Experimental results

This section presents the experimental findings from the training and testing of the AI-based system developed for the real-time detection of kiwi fruit. The study evaluates different versions of the YOLO algorithm in terms of training configurations, performance analysis, hyperparameter optimization, and real-time deployment on embedded systems. The goal is to identify the most effective architectures for different operational scenarios by comparing models in terms of accuracy, recall, and inference time. In the experimental process, four versions of the YOLO algorithm—YOLOv8, YOLOv9, YOLOv10, and YOLOv11—were employed for kiwi detection. To ensure a fair comparison, a consistent and standardized training configuration was applied to all versions. This approach allowed architectural differences to be isolated as the primary factor influencing performance, while variability caused by training conditions was minimized. The results indicate that YOLOv8 models deliver high detection performance, with each variant exhibiting characteristic strengths across the evaluated metrics. The YOLOv8x model achieved the highest overall accuracy, obtaining an F1 score of 0.891, mAP@0.5 of 0.956, and mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.640, making it the most reliable architecture in terms of comprehensive detection capability. Among the lightweight variants, YOLOv8s recorded strong precision (0.875) and the highest recall (0.904) within the family, effectively reducing missed detections while maintaining computational efficiency. Although the YOLOv8n model produced slightly lower scores, its fast inference and minimal resource usage make it suitable for real-time, embedded deployments. Medium-scale models such as YOLOv8m and YOLOv8l provided balanced results, offering incremental gains in mAP values at the expense of higher computational load.

Regarding training duration, the YOLOv8n and YOLOv8s models converged more rapidly than the larger variants, representing the most efficient options in terms of training speed. In contrast, the YOLOv8l and YOLOv8x models required more iterations to achieve stable performance due to their increased parameter counts and architectural complexity. Despite this longer optimization process, their higher accuracy makes them preferable for high-precision detection systems without strict hardware limitations. Enhanced training process through hyperparameter optimization

In this study, hyperparameter optimization was conducted exclusively on the “s” variants of the YOLO models (YOLOv8s, YOLOv9s, YOLOv10s, and YOLOv11s). The primary motivation for this choice lies in the significantly shorter training times and lower computational requirements of these models, which enable more efficient use of available resources. Moreover, the satisfactory baseline performance results obtained in earlier sections demonstrate that these models are responsive to hyperparameter refinements and can achieve higher accuracy levels through optimization. Based on these considerations, the hyperparameter optimization process for each model was carefully planned, systematically executed, and comparatively evaluated.

The hyperparameter settings presented in Table 8 are optimized using the model.tune() function provided by the Ultralytics library, and the training process is conducted in accordance with these configurations. This function employs a hybrid and adaptive optimization strategy that combines Random Search, Bayesian Optimization, and Evolutionary (Genetic) search techniques to manage the hyperparameter exploration process. In this way, the broad and complex hyperparameter space is automatically scanned, the search is guided based on the outcomes of each trial, and parameter combinations yielding progressively higher performance are selected.

On embedded platforms such as the Jetson TX2, inference times below 5 s are frequently classified in the literature as “near real-time”59. In agricultural image processing applications, camera systems often operate at low frame rates (approximately 1 FPS or lower); therefore, an inference time of 3–4 s is generally considered sufficient for most use cases60. For instance, in a cotton detection study implemented on the Jetson TX2, an inference time of 2.5 s was reported, and the system was evaluated as near real-time61. Similarly, other works have demonstrated Jetson TX2-based systems achieving 13–14 FPS, confirming their suitability for time-critical agricultural applications61. These findings indicate that the inference times obtained in this study—ranging from 1.2 to 4.1 s—are consistent with the literature and confirm the practical applicability potential of the proposed models in embedded agricultural detection scenarios.

Attention-based variants (e.g., CBAM-YOLOv8 and YOLOv8 + SE) strengthen performance in crowded or small-object scenarios. Discussion

The findings of this study highlight the trade-offs between architectural complexity, detection accuracy, generalization capability, and computational efficiency when deploying YOLO-based models for fruit detection in real agricultural environments. A consistent pattern emerged across all YOLO families (v8–v11): while larger models (e.g., YOLOv8x, YOLOv9e, YOLOv10x, YOLOv11x) achieved slightly higher accuracy on the desktop training system, their inference times—often exceeding 20 s per image—render them unsuitable for deployment on constrained embedded platforms such as the Jetson TX2. In contrast, lightweight variants (n, s, and m) delivered a more favorable balance. Among these, the hyperparameter-optimized YOLOv11s model achieved the best overall performance with an mAP@0.5 of 0.956 and an average inference time of 3.33 s, aligning with definitions of near-real-time performance in agricultural monitoring applications. The hyperparameter optimization procedure played a significant role in improving the stability and robustness of the lightweight models. The optimization resulted in smoother convergence curves, reduced overfitting tendencies, and measurable gains in F1 score and mAP across all optimized models. This demonstrates that substantial performance improvements can be obtained through systematic tuning, even without architectural modification—an important insight for embedded AI deployments where model complexity must remain minimal. The YOLOv11s model benefited most from hyperparameter tuning, showing improvements across precision, recall, and mAP, confirming that lightweight architectures respond more sensitively to optimization strategies due to their narrower parameter space.

In addition to accuracy, inference latency remains a fundamental determinant of model suitability for field deployment. While robotics-driven agricultural systems generally require frame rates above 5 FPS, several studies in precision agriculture have classified inference times between 1 and 4 s as acceptable for static frame analysis, low-frequency monitoring, or stop-and-go operation. The inference times obtained in this study fall within this range, suggesting that optimized lightweight YOLO models can support practical near-real-time fruit detection tasks such as yield estimation, decision support, and orchard monitoring—even though they do not yet meet strict robotic real-time thresholds. Future hardware acceleration techniques such as TensorRT-based INT8 quantization or structured pruning are likely to reduce latency further, potentially enabling true real-time operation on the Jetson TX2. Despite the promising results, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the dataset—although collected under natural orchard conditions with significant variability in lighting, occlusion, and canopy density—remains relatively small.

This may limit generalization to orchards with different training systems, varieties, or environmental conditions. Expanding the dataset across multiple seasons, weather scenarios, and orchard structures would strengthen robustness. Second, the real-time embedded evaluation was performed on pre-collected test images rather than continuous video input. Although still valid for inference benchmarking, future work should test the models under online streaming conditions to more accurately assess end-to-end system latency. Third, while this study focused on YOLO architectures due to their popularity and efficiency, alternative models such as DETR variants, EfficientDet, MobileNet-SSD, or foundation models like Grounding DINO and SAM could provide valuable baselines for more comprehensive benchmarking. Another important observation concerns the computational trade-offs across YOLO versions. YOLOv11 consistently offered improved accuracy and efficiency over earlier architectures, confirming the advantages of its enhanced feature extraction blocks and optimization strategies. However, the performance differences between YOLOv10s, YOLOv11s, and YOLOv9s after hyperparameter optimization were relatively narrow, suggesting that architectural innovation alone is not always the primary determinant of embedded performance. Instead, a careful balance of architecture selection, dataset design, augmentation strategy, and hyperparameter tuning appears to have a greater cumulative effect. Finally, although this study focused on kiwi fruit detection, the methodology and optimization pipeline are highly transferrable to other horticultural crops with similar detection challenges—such as citrus, apples, plums, and peppers. Many agricultural fruits share structural similarities in terms of occlusion patterns, canopy density, and color blending with the surrounding foliage. Therefore, with appropriate retraining and dataset expansion, the optimized YOLO-based framework presented here has strong potential to support a wide range of orchard automation tasks including yield forecasting, selective harvesting, growth monitoring, and robotic perception.

Conclusions

This study presented a comprehensive evaluation of YOLOv8–YOLOv11 architectures for kiwi fruit detection under natural orchard conditions and on an embedded platform. By training all model variants under a uniform experimental setup and applying hyperparameter optimization to the lightweight “s” models, the research systematically examined the trade-offs between detection accuracy, model complexity, and embedded inference performance. The results demonstrated that while larger models achieved slightly higher accuracy on high-performance hardware, their latency on embedded devices makes them impractical for field deployment. In contrast, the optimized YOLOv11s model achieved the best overall balance, reaching an mAP@0.5 of 0.956 with an average inference time of 3.33 s on the Jetson TX2, establishing it as the most suitable near-real-time solution among all evaluated models. The findings emphasize the importance of targeted hyperparameter optimization, which significantly improved the performance and stability of the lightweight architectures without increasing computational cost. These results demonstrate that substantial gains can be achieved not only through architectural innovation but also through carefully designed training strategies—an especially valuable insight for embedded agricultural AI applications. The methodology and evaluation pipeline used in this study provide a transferable framework that can be applied to similar fruit detection problems across other horticultural domains. Despite the strong results, several limitations must be acknowledged. The dataset, although collected under realistic orchard conditions, remains limited in size and environmental diversity. Real-time inference tests were conducted on pre-collected images rather than continuous video streams, and the analysis focused primarily on YOLO-based architectures without benchmarking against alternative lightweight detectors. Addressing these limitations will strengthen system generalization and practical applicability. Future research will explore the integration of advanced optimization techniques, including TensorRT-based quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation, to reduce latency and enable true real-time performance on edge devices. Expanding the dataset across different orchards, seasons, and environmental conditions, as well as incorporating multi-modal sensing (e.g., depth, thermal imaging), will also enhance robustness. Additionally, evaluating non-YOLO models and transformer-based architectures may provide new insights into the performance boundaries of embedded agricultural vision systems. This study demonstrates that optimized lightweight YOLO architectures—particularly YOLOv11s—offer a viable and efficient solution for near-real-time kiwi detection in resource-constrained environments, supporting the broader adoption of AI-driven automation and intelligent decision-making in precision agriculture.
