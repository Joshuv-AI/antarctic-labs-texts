Anatomical location defines distinct molecular subtypes of mucosal melanoma
Authors: E.-R. Zhou, J. Rusheen, R. Baumann, S. Ariyan, H.M. Kluger, S. Gabizon-Peretz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMucosal melanoma (MM) is a rare and aggressive melanoma subtype that is understudied. The relationships between anatomical location, genomic alterations, stage at presentation, and survival remain incompletely characterized.MethodsWe carried out a retrospective single tertiary center study of 105 patients with histologically confirmed MM diagnosed between 1996 and 2025. Clinical and genomic data were analyzed to evaluate associations between anatomical location, mutational profile, stage at presentation, and survival outcomes, including melanoma-specific mortality.ResultsLower-body tumors arising in the anus or genital areas were enriched for KIT and splicing factor 3 subunit B1 alterations, whereas NRAS mutations were distributed across anatomical regions. Among the two most common mutated genes, NRAS-mutant tumors were more likely than KIT-mutant tumors to present with metastatic disease [53% versus 19%; P = 0.046, odds ratio (OR) 4.7, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.15-19.41]. Lower-body tumors were associated with worse overall survival (OS) than upper-body tumors (median 2.81 versus 8.40 years; OR = 0.05) and with higher melanoma-specific mortality. In multivariable analyses, upper-body location remained independently associated with improved OS (hazard ratio 0.14, 95% CI 0.05-0.36, P < 0.001).ConclusionsAnatomical location of MMs and genomic alterations define biologically and clinically distinct subtypes.

Introduction

Mucosal melanoma (MM) is a rare and aggressive subtype of melanoma, constituting ∼1% of all melanomas.1,2 Although arising from melanocytes, MMs are less responsive to immunotherapy than cutaneous melanoma and carry a poorer prognosis.3 This is partly attributable to their frequent diagnosis at advanced stages, as they arise in anatomically concealed sites.4 In addition, MMs typically harbor a lower mutational burden, reflecting their non-ultraviolet-driven pathogenesis.5

Although the genomic landscape of MM has been described in several moderate-sized sequencing studies and meta-analyses,5,6 how key genomic alterations vary across anatomical sites and relate to stage at presentation and clinical outcomes remains incompletely defined. Prior studies have reported variable associations between recurrent genomic alterations and clinical outcomes.7 A cohort of 46 MMs treated with immune checkpoint blockade did not identify clear associations between specific genomic alterations and response to immunotherapy, highlighting the need for further studies.8 Most clinically used sequencing panels for MMs include BRAF, KIT, and NRAS, given their potential implications for therapeutic decision-making, whereas splicing factor 3b subunit 1 (SF3B1) has recently emerged as a recurrent alteration with possible prognostic and therapeutic relevance.5,9 We therefore analyzed a single-center cohort of patients with MM to evaluate the association between tumor location, genomic alterations, stage at diagnosis, and melanoma-specific survival (MSS).

Methods

Study population and data collection

With Institutional Review Board approval, we retrospectively reviewed a prospectively maintained database of melanoma cases evaluated at Yale–New Haven Hospital between 1996 and 2025. Patients with histologically confirmed MM were identified. Clinical data included age at diagnosis, sex, primary tumor site, American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage, treatments received, cause of death, and survival status. Genomic data were obtained from clinical sequencing reports and included mutational status of BRAF, NRAS, and KIT, and SF3B1 when available.

Staging

Staging was assigned using site-specific AJCC eighth edition TNM10 (tumor–node–metastasis) systems where available (MM of the head and neck; conjunctival melanoma) and the cutaneous melanoma system for sites lacking dedicated staging criteria (vulvovaginal, anorectal, and urethral). Given the lack of a uniform staging system for all MMs, we classified patients into three groups based on extent of disease at diagnosis—localized, locally advanced (which included regional nodal involvement), and distant metastatic disease.

Statistical analysis

Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for selected 2 × 2 comparisons.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from histological diagnosis to death from any cause and depicted using Kaplan–Meier curves; groups were compared using the log-rank test. Median follow-up time was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method.

Melanoma-specific mortality was evaluated using cumulative incidence functions calculated via the Aalen–Johansen estimator, treating death from other causes as a competing risk. Groups were compared using the log-rank test on cause-specific hazards. Univariate and multivariate analyses used cause-specific Cox proportional hazards regression. Multivariable models including genomic variables were restricted to patients with available genomic data and limited to tumors with either KIT-only alterations or no identifiable driver [wild type (WT)] only, excluding codriver mutations.

All analyses were carried out using Python version 3.13.9 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE), using the pandas and lifelines packages. Two-sided P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Baseline characteristics of the cohort

Stage at diagnosis differed significantly by primary site (P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108528). Head and neck MMs more frequently presented with locally advanced or metastatic disease, whereas vulvovaginal and conjunctival melanomas were more often diagnosed at earlier stages.

Surgical and systemic treatments were received by 80.0% and 83.8% of patients, respectively, with no similar rates between upper- and lower-body tumors. As expected, surgical intervention was more common in localized compared with metastatic disease (89.8% versus 55.6%; P < 0.001). Radiotherapy was administered more frequently in upper-body tumors (46.3% versus 23.4%; P = 0.026). Among patients who received systemic therapy (n = 88), 65.9% received combination immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), 21.6% antiprogrammed cell death protein 1 monotherapy, and 12.5% other immunotherapy (interleukin-2 or ipilimumab), with no significant difference in distribution between body regions.

Driver genetic alterations were identified in a subset of tumors (Table 1), including KIT (20.0%), NRAS (18.1%), BRAF (4.8%), and SF3B1 (3.8%), whereas 44.8% of tumors lacked an identifiable driver mutation in any of these genes. Co-occurring mutations were observed in nine patients (8.6%), most frequently involving KIT or SF3B1. Among the two most common mutated genes, KIT and NRAS, NRAS-mutant tumors were associated with a greater likelihood of metastatic presentation than KIT-mutant tumors (53% versus 19%; P = 0.046, OR 4.7, 95% CI 1.15-19.41). The distribution of these recurrent driver mutations differed across anatomical regions (Figure 1A and Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108528). KIT and SF3B1 alterations were significantly enriched in tumors arising from lower-body mucosal sites compared with upper-body tumors (KIT, 39.7% versus 8.3%, P < 0.001; SF3B1, 45.8% versus 0%, P = 0.007). Conversely, NRAS alterations were detected in both regions with no significant difference in frequency (32.4% versus 22.6%, P = 0.331). BRAF mutations were rare overall and did not differ significantly between anatomical regions (P = 0.26). Given the relatively advanced age of the cohort, we assessed melanoma-specific mortality; melanoma-related death represented the predominant cause of death, exceeding other-cause mortality in cumulative incidence analysis (Figure 2C). Consistent with the OS analysis, lower-body tumors were associated with a higher cumulative incidence of melanoma-specific death than upper-body tumors (Figure 2D).

In patients who received systemic therapy (n = 88), lower-body tumors continued to demonstrate a higher cumulative incidence of melanoma-specific death (cause-specific log-rank P = 0.020), although the difference was attenuated, likely reflecting the smaller sample size (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108528).

By Cox univariable analyses, younger age, surgical treatment, and KIT mutations were associated with improved survival (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108528). In multivariable Cox regression analysis for OS (Table 2), restricted to patients with genomic data and excluding patients with co-occurring KIT and SF3B1 mutations, advanced disease at presentation, male sex, and age ≥65 years were independently associated with worse outcomes. Conversely, tumors arising from upper-body sites were associated with significantly improved OS compared with lower-body tumors (HR 0.14, 95% CI 0.05-0.36). In multivariable analyses of MSS (Table 2), only anatomical location remained independently associated with outcome, with upper-body tumors demonstrating a markedly lower risk of melanoma-specific death (HR 0.09, P < 0.001, 95% CI 0.02-0.37). There was a nonsignificant trend toward improved MSS for melanomas with KIT mutations (HR 0.44, P = 0.071, 95% CI 0.18-1.07), whereas age, sex, and disease stage were not significantly associated with MSS.

As surgery rates did not differ significantly between upper- and lower-body tumors (78.0% versus 81.2%; P = 0.881), it was not included in the primary multivariable models; in sensitivity analyses adding surgery to both models, it was not independently associated with OS or MSS and the main findings were preserved.

Discussion

In this relatively large, single tertiary institution cohort of patients with MM, the anatomical location of primary tumors was strongly associated with both distinct genomic alterations and survival. Tumors arising from lower-body sites were significantly enriched for KIT and SF3B1 mutations, whereas upper-body tumors displayed a different molecular distribution. Prior studies have described the mutational landscape of MM and our findings extend these observations by linking anatomical location to both specific mutational profiles and survival outcomes. These results support the concept that MMs arising from different anatomical sites may represent biologically distinct subgroups with divergent evolutionary trajectories and clinical behavior. Notably, conjunctival melanoma may share biological features with ultraviolet-associated cutaneous melanomas.

SF3B1 is the largest subunit of the SF3b multiprotein complex, involved in the earliest steps of RNA splicing in the spliceosome and mutations in it have been previously described.11 Aberrant spliceosome machinery may result in increased neoantigen presentation and an enhanced response to ICIs.12 Different mutations in this gene have been reported in up to 30% of myelodysplastic syndromes.11 However, SF3B1R625 mutations are more frequently observed in uveal melanoma and may be associated with improved prognosis.13 Data regarding the prognostic importance of SF3B1 mutations in MM remain inconsistent.14,15 In our cohort, SF3B1 mutational status was not clearly associated with OS, possibly reflecting the limited sample size and presence of co-occurring genomic alterations that could modify the clinical impact of SF3B1 mutations.

Our findings have important implications for surveillance and therapeutic interventions. Lower-body MMs may remain clinically occult for longer, often reaching greater tumor depth and an increased risk of metastatic spread at diagnosis. Accordingly, body region may help inform surveillance strategies and broader clinical management. In addition, due to the relatively lower response rates to immunotherapy in MMs, there is a substantial need for mutation-targeting therapies. For BRAF and KIT mutations, targeted therapies are approved, although response rates depend on the specific mutation subtype and are relatively low.16,17 For NRAS-mutant tumors, binimetinib - a mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) inhibitor - improved progression-free survival compared with chemotherapy and may be a salvage treatment option,18 and several clinical trials evaluating combinations with MEK inhibitors are ongoing.19 More recently, RAS-targeting strategies have entered clinical development and preliminary data suggest the potential activity of active-state RAS multiselective inhibitors in NRAS-mutant melanoma, although clinical experience remains limited.20 For SF3B1 mutations, several investigational approaches are being explored, including spliceosome modulators.21

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. This was a retrospective, single-center study and genomic data were derived from different clinically available sequencing panels, resulting in nonuniform gene coverage across the cohort. Due to the rarity of the disease, the cohort spans a prolonged study period during which systemic treatment approaches evolved; however, the comparable distribution of systemic therapy types across anatomical groups makes differential treatment exposure an unlikely sole explanation for the observed survival differences. This study did not focus on response to immunotherapy because some patients were diagnosed before the routine use of immunotherapy, although >90% of patients who developed metastasis eventually received immunotherapy. Nevertheless, the relatively large cohort size and long follow-up enabled integrated analysis of genomic alterations, clinical presentation, and survival outcomes.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that anatomical location in MM is associated with distinct molecular alterations and clinical outcomes. Lower-body tumors were enriched for KIT and SF3B1 mutations and were associated with inferior survival, supporting the concept that MMs arising from different anatomical sites represent biologically distinct subgroups. Improved molecular characterization and site-specific therapeutic strategies may therefore be important for optimizing the management of this rare melanoma subtype. Given the rarity of MM, further multi-institutional studies with comprehensive genomic profiling will be important to validate these observations and to better define the biological mechanisms underlying the anatomical heterogeneity of MM.
