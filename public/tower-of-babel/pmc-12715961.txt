Relationship between blood pressure and adverse clinical events in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI): a retrospective analysis
Authors: Zhaochong Tan, Ping Yuan, Ying Huang, Congcong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent guideline from the United States has a positive recommendation on lowering blood pressure (BP) target for patients with hypertension. However, excessively lowering BP might result in a possible deterioration of myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury, especially in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD).ObjectiveWe aimed to assess the impact of lowering BP on the risk for adverse clinical outcomes among CHD patients who have undergone percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).MethodsA total of 1670 consecutive patients with CHD from the Dryad database who have undergone PCI were enrolled in our study. The primary outcomes were defined as adverse clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death at the follow-up for a median of 29.8 months.ResultsAmong these patients, the rate of major clinical events in these CHD patients was 191 (11.4%). The all-cause death and cardiac death of these patients with clinical events was 120 (62.8%) and 80 (41.9%), respectively. A smooth curve from a real world showed a U-shaped correlation between systolic BP and the rate of primary outcomes including major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death, respectively. Diastolic BP tended to have a positive correlation with these outcomes. Further results did show a turning point in the associations of systolic BP with major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death, respectively. When the systolic BP level was at 95 mmHg, 105 mmHg and 107 mmHg, respectively, these CHD patients had the lowest risk for the major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death.ConclusionOur results demonstrated that accurately lowering systolic BP at a range of 95–107 mmHg may obtain benefits from reducing adverse outcomes including all-cause death and cardiac death for patients with CHD after undergoing PCI. However, further cohort study is needed to support our results.

Introduction

Hypertension has been considered a highly prevalent risk factor for involvement in the process of coronary heart disease (CHD) and other cardiovascular events [1–4]. The global prevalence of hypertension in 2019 has reached 1 billion, suggesting a twofold increase over the past three decades [1, 5]. Correspondingly, recent international guidelines have recommended that stricter BP targets after the 2015 SPRINT (Systolic BP Intervention Trial) have already formed the cornerstone of primary and secondary prevention among CHD patients [6–9]. Although there was a significant association between treating systolic BP and reduced risk of adverse cardiovascular events in patients with various cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [10, 11], it was still highly controversial regarding the optimal control range for diastolic BP, especially in patients with the presence of CHD where diastolic hypotension might reduce coronary perfusion [12, 13]. Previous clinical investigations have also shown that systolic BP < 120 mm Hg and diastolic BP < 70 mmHg had a J-curve association with an increased risk of adverse outcomes and mortality risk in patients with stable CHD [14]. Recently, the 2018 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines reported that antihypertensive therapy among individuals with coexistent CHD was recommended for systolic and diastolic BP targets of less than 130 and 80 mmHg, respectively [7]. However, the optimal control target for BP in patients who have undergone percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is still unclear. A low diastolic BP before PCI has also been found to contribute to a higher mortality risk previously [15]. Thus, we sought to further explore the association of a lowering BP target with adverse outcomes in patients with CHD after PCI.

We extracted raw data from the Dryad database where a cohort study reported long-term follow-up results in a Chinese population undergoing PCI with drug-eluting stents. Our study aimed to perform a secondary analysis to assess the impact of lowering BP on adverse outcomes (clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death) among these patients with PCI treatment.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a secondary analysis of an existing cohort with specified inclusion/exclusion criteria that provided the raw data stored on Dryad (10.5061/dryad.13d31) [16, 17]. This previous study was initially designed to explore the prognosis of participants with CHD after experiencing PCI. All included patients (n = 2533) have undergone PCI therapy with drug-eluting stents from July 2009 to August 2011, conducted by standard operating procedure and guidelines during hospitalization [17]. In addition to individuals who were ever administered antiplatelet therapy, all participants were treated with loading doses of clopidogrel and aspirin before PCI. Also, all individuals were administered dual antiplatelet medication (clopidogrel and aspirin) for at least 12 months after completing PCI therapy [16, 17]. For the purpose of our objective, a total of 1670 patients experiencing PCI were enrolled in our analysis after excluding missing data, as shown in Fig. 1. According to the Declaration of Helsinki, the Ethics Committee at Zhengzhou University’s First Affiliated Hospital has approved this study and the informed consent form was exempted. Because all patients in the dataset were anonymized, no additional ethical statement was required for our study based on the public policy statement from the Dryad database.Fig. 1Final participants for analysis

Clinical variables

Population characteristics, lifestyles, accompanying diseases, blood tests and others were collected on admission. Population characteristics included age and gender. Lifestyle included smoking. Accompanying diseases included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, previous acute myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), prior PCI and prior CABG. Blood tests on admission included glucose, lipid profile, kidney function and other indicators from fasting blood samples. Furthermore, medications containing dual antiplatelet medication (clopidogrel and aspirin), statins, beta (β) blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI) and others were collected. In order to achieve the purpose of the study, all cardiovascular-related risk factors were assessed upon admission.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were defined as major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death during the follow-up for a mean of 29.8 months. The follow-up information was obtained from telephone contact, readmission or outpatient clinic visits. The major clinical events included acute MI, repeat revascularization, all-cause death, cardiac death and stroke. All-cause death was defined as deaths caused by fatal cardiac and non-cardiac events. Cardiac death was defined as deaths caused by cardiac events such as sudden cardiac death, acute myocardial infarction, heart failure and other fatal cardiac outcomes.

Statistical analysis

The Kołmogorov–Smirnov test was used to evaluate the distribution of continuous data with a statistical description by mean ± standard deviation (SD) or inter-quartile range, and Mann–Whitney or independent T-test was performed for comparative analysis. Categorical data that were described as percentage (%) were compared by the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariate logistic regression was conducted to identify the association of systolic and diastolic BP with the risk of primary outcomes including major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death. Population characteristics, lifestyles and blood tests were entered into these adjusted models, which were described as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). The relationship between BP and primary outcomes was adjusted as follows: (1) adjusted I model for age and gender; (2) adjusted II model for age, gender, smoking, creatinine and uric acid. Also, threshold effect analysis was used to explore the non-linear correlation between BP and the risk of primary outcomes in a real world because previous studies have suggested a non-linear relationship between BP and poor prognosis [9–11]. The Empower Stats software 5.2 is a statistical tool based on R language for data analysis, which was used for all statistical analyses in our study. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariate analysis

We also performed a multivariate logistic regression analysis for the association of systolic and diastolic BP with the risk for primary outcomes among these patients with CHD after PCI. As expected, our results did not demonstrate a positive correlation of systolic BP with major clinical events [1.0 (1.0, 1.0) 0.430], all-cause death [1.0 (1.0, 1.0) 0.607] and cardiac death [1.0 (1.0, 1.0) 0.533], respectively, when age, gender, smoking, creatinine and uric acid were adjusted for (Table 2). The most reasonable explanation was the non-linear (U-shaped) correlation between systolic BP and these adverse outcomes in the real world. However, in addition to major clinical events [1.0 (1.0, 1.0) 0.071], diastolic BP posed a linear correlation with all-cause death [1.0 (1.0, 1.0) 0.002] and cardiac death [1.0 (1.0, 1.0) 0.009], respectively, when the same confounding factors were adjusted for (Table 2). Furthermore, sensitivity analysis for these associations between systolic and diastolic BP and the risk of primary outcomes was not almost changed, by considering “prior PCI and prior CABG” as the covariates (Table 3). Threshold effect analysis

Due to a non-linear correlation between BP and these primary outcomes, we further conducted a threshold effect analysis to explore a potential turning point. However, results did show an obvious turning point on the associations of systolic BP with major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death, respectively, after age, gender, smoking, creatinine and uric acid were controlled for (Table 4). When, systolic BP level was at 95 mmHg, 105 mmHg and 107 mmHg, respectively, CHD patients had the lowest risk for major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death (Table 4). Inconsistently, we have not found any threshold effect on the association between diastolic BP and primary outcomes (Table 5). Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the correlation of systolic and diastolic BP levels with primary outcomes including major clinical events, all-cause and cardiac death among CHD patients who have undergone PCI. These patients with a higher rate of adverse events were likely to be older and had a higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation, heart failure and diabetes mellitus. There was a U-shape relationship between systolic BP and major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death in the whole cohort using a smooth curve in a real world. The lowest risk of major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death was found at systolic BP levels of 95 mmHg, 105 mmHg and 107 mmHg, respectively.

The controversy for the “lower the better” hypothesis or “J-curve phenomenon” hypothesis has continuously sustained in setting BP control goals [18, 19]. Previous extensive clinical studies have demonstrated that adverse cardiovascular events and mortality rates were increased at high or low BP values [19–23]. However, the recent SPRINT study has suggested that intensive BP lowering with systolic BP less than 120 mmHg can significantly reduce mortality risk and other adverse outcomes [9], which has also affected the guideline published by the American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association in 2017 [6]. Obviously, the 2017 guideline tended to support “the lower the better” hypothesis. The results from our study showed a high prevalence of all-cause death and cardiac death in both the low and high systolic BP groups, indicating that there was a U-curve association between systolic BP level and adverse outcomes in CHD patients experiencing PCI. At the same time, the results in our study also showed that intensive BP lowering targeting a systolic BP range of 95–107 mmHg might obtain a survival benefit for CHD patients after PCI. Consistently, in a previous population-based retrospective cohort study from the national database of the Korean National Health Insurance System representing the entire Korean population, the baseline systolic BP and diastolic BP from 38,330 patients with a history of PCI for CHD had a J-curve relationship for all-cause mortality, with the nadir point at 119 mmHg [18]. As for the control target of diastolic BP, previous studies have suggested a positive or negative correlation between diastolic BP and adverse cardiovascular events, and a low diastolic BP before PCI has also been found to contribute to a higher mortality risk [15, 24]. In a prospective cohort study involving 1181 individuals aged 65 years or older with systolic BP < 130 mmHg, all patients were classified into low (< 70 mmHg), optimal (70 to < 80 mmHg), and high (80 to < 90 mmHg) diastolic BP groups [24]. Their multivariable Cox regression showed that no significant increase in cardiovascular risk was observed in the low diastolic BP group (hazard ratio [HR] 1.02, [95% CI 0.68–1.52], P = 0.93) [24]. Consistently, our study also suggested that keeping diastolic BP at a reasonable range did have no significant impact on the risk of adverse outcomes.

However, it should be considered that the effective control rate of hypertension is relatively low worldwide, especially in various populations from developing countries with or without antihypertensive medication [25–28]. Clinical physicians should decisively control the BP for patients after PCI. Although there was a U-curve association between systolic BP and adverse outcomes such as major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death, our results showed that patients after experiencing PCI can still benefit from intensive BP lowering.

There were several drawbacks for this study that need to be noted. Firstly, the present study only obtained the one-time measurement of BP from included participants. Thus, this could not reflect an accurate value due to the dynamic change of BP. Secondly, because these individuals had undergone a routine examination before and after undergoing PCI for CHD, which potentially caused a bias in selecting patients. Thirdly, the cause-specific mortality was not explicitly stated, although all-cause death and cardiac death were calculated as the adverse outcomes. Also, the specific cause of cardiac death has not been clearly identified. Fourthly, although our data came from a previous cohort study, logistic regression was used to analyze the causal relationship between BP and the risk of adverse outcomes due to the retrospective design which left us unable to obtain the specific time of endpoint occurrence. Finally, the retrospective, observational design might leave potential residual confounding, and the single‐center cohort of Chinese patients might restrict external generalizability to other ethnicities or care settings.

Conclusion

Our results suggested that the risk of primary outcomes including major clinical events, all-cause death and cardiac death was the lowest after lowering systolic BP in the range of 95–107 mmHg among CHD patients who have undergone PCI. Although there was a U-curve relationship between systolic BP and these adverse outcomes, the CHD patients after undergoing PCI can still benefit from intensive BP lowering. However, further cohort studies are needed to support our results.
