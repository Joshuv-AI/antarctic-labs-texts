Trichoderma longibrachiatum-based fertilizers promote Phoebe bournei seedling growth by regulating plant physiology, nutrients utilization, and the rhizosphere microbial community
Authors: Xuewen Wang, Xiaoli Wei, Cun Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhoebe bournei, a rare and endangered plant species in China, exhibits low seedling survival rates and slow growth. Although Trichoderma longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer is beneficial, it remains unclear whether it can reduce chemical fertilizer dependence while promoting P. bournei growth. Here, P. bournei seedlings were treated with a combination of T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer and reduced chemical fertilizer (designated as the HF treatment). This study was employed to investigate the synergistic mechanisms underlying growth promotion.ResultsPlant physiology, pedology, and amplicon sequencing were used to evaluate the growth of P. bournei seedlings. The HF treatment optimally promoted P. bournei seedling growth, with a 39.8% increase in seedling height compared to the control. Specifically, the treatment elevated the levels of auxin (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA), and jasmonic acid (JA) by 43.6, 50.2, and 63.1%, respectively, compared to the control. This treatment also enhanced plant nitrogen and phosphorus utilization. An increase in the relative abundances of potentially beneficial fungi (e.g., Trichoderma and Trichocladium) and bacteria (e.g., Gemmatimonas and Streptomyces) was observed under the HF treatment, which also suppressed fungal pathogens such as Fusarium.ConclusionsThis study revealed the synergistic mechanism by which T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer and chemical fertilizer co-promote P. bournei seedling growth. Our findings offer a practical strategy for sustainable forestry to enhance the productivity of this rare and endangered tree species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04597-x.

Introduction

Phoebe bournei is a subtropical evergreen tree endemic to China. It possesses high economic value owing to its superior wood quality and wide range of applications [15]. Nevertheless, its cultivation is hampered by low seedling survival rates and slow growth, presenting a major challenge for large-scale propagation [57]. Therefore, developing effective strategies to promote the growth of P. bournei seedling is crucial.

Soil fertility is a primary determinant of plant growth. Naturally limited soil fertility requires inputs like compost, chemical fertilizers, or bio-organic fertilizers for effective improvement [6, 36]. A growing body of evidence demonstrates that long-term chemical fertilizer use severely compromises soil ecological functions [10, 14]. In contrast, bio-organic fertilizers enriched with beneficial microbes provide a sustainable alternative to mitigate this detrimental effect [35, 38]. In addition to partially replacing chemical fertilizers, they also enhance plant tolerance to environmental stress [21, 30]. For instance, Bacillus-and Trichoderma-based organic fertilizers have demonstrated efficacy in promoting plant growth and suppressing disease incidence [28, 35]. However, most current research on bio-organic fertilization focuses on Poaceae species, whereas studies on P. bournei seedlings have been limited to chemical and Bacillus-based organic fertilizers [34, 56]. Therefore, developing new types of bio-organic fertilizers to promote the growth of P. bournei seedlings is of great significance.

Trichoderma species are known for their role as plant growth-promoting microorganisms [17, 25]. Nevertheless, when applied in aqueous formulations, their field performance is often inconsistent, which limits their practical use [40]. Recently, researchers have focused on the development and utilization of Trichoderma-based organic fertilizer [7, 44]. Studies have demonstrated that T. asperellum- and T. guizhouense-based organic fertilizers accelerate nutrient transformation and stimulate plant growth [29, 55]. In addition, formulations containing T. harzianum- or T. guizhouense-based efficiently modify the soil microbial community while also reducing dependence on chemical fertilizers for plant development [5, 42, 54]. Previously, we developed a T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer that effectively promoted P. bournei seedlings growth [23, 59]. However, no study has examined the interactive effects of T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer and reduced chemical fertilizer on P. bournei seedling physiology and rhizosphere microbiota.

In this study, we hypothesize that T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer can serve as an effective partial substitute for chemical fertilizers. We further hypothesize that it promotes the growth of P. bournei seedlings by modulating plant physiology and key rhizosphere microbiota. To verify this, P. bournei seedlings were treated with a combination of T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer and a 50% reduced dose of chemical fertilizer. We subsequently assessed seedlings growth physiology, soil nutrients, and the microbial community structure. Our goals were 1) to investigate the potential of partially replacing chemical fertilizers with a T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer, and 2) to elucidate the mechanisms through which it promotes the growth of P. bournei seedlings under reduced chemical fertilizer input. Overall, this study provides a foundational for developing and applying T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer and enhances our understanding of the mechanisms promoting P. bournei seedling growth.

Materials and methods

Strain

Trichoderma longibrachiatum (GenBank: MK284517.1) was isolated from the inner bark of P. bournei [58]. The strain was preserved in 25% glycerol at −80℃ and stored in the forest protection laboratory at Guizhou University, China.

Preparation of T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer

The production of T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer is similar to the methods of Wang et al. [51]. Briefly, T. longibrachiatum was cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 28 °C for approximately six days. Spores were then harvested by adding 10 mL of sterile water to the plate and gently scraping with a spreader rod. The suspension was filtered through four layers of sterilized lens paper to remove hyphal debris. The spore concentration was quantified with a hemacytometer and adjusted to 1.0 × 106 CFU/mL with sterile water. A solid medium containing wheat bran (3 g), sorghum distiller's grains (7 g), Mendel's nutrient solution (2 mL), and distilled water (8 mL) was adjusted to pH 7 and sterilized (121 °C, 103 kPa, 60 min). The composition of Mendel's nutrient solution per liter was: 1.4 g (NH4)2SO4, 0.3 g MgSO4, 2.0 g KH2PO4, 0.3 g CaCL2, 5.0 mg FeSO4, 1.6 mg MnSO4, 1.4 mg ZnSO4, and 2.0 mg CoCL2. Thereafter, the culture medium was cooled to room temperature and inoculated with 3 mL of T. longibrachiatum solution, placed in a shallow dish (10 cm × 10 cm × 5 cm). The vessel was sealed with cling film to minimize water evaporation, then five tiny openings were made above it to ensure sufficient aeration. All boxes were subsequently incubated at 28 °C. The concentration of spores was measured every day until it reached 5.31 × 1010 cfu/g. Finally, the T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer were stored at room temperature for subsequent use.

Seedlings, soil, and chemical fertilizer

P. bournei seeds were collected from Rongjiang County, in Guizhou Province (25°26′N, 108°13′E). Plump, uniformly sized P. bournei seeds were surface-sterilized in 0.5% KMnO₄ for 15 min, thoroughly rinsed five times with sterile water. Subsequently, they were soaked in warm water (30 °C) for 24 h in a large beaker. Following imbibition, the seeds were sown in plastic plugs on sterile vermiculite and cultivated at 28 °C for 30 days [26]. After germination, seedlings with two uniformly developed cotyledons were selected and transplanted into plastic pots (upper diameter: 12.5 cm, lower diameter: 9 cm, height: 10 cm), each filled with 500 g of sterilized soil (121 ℃ and 103 kPa for 2 h). All pots were placed in a greenhouse maintained at 25 °C with a 12-h photoperiod. The soil used in the experiment was a 1:1:1 mixture of peat soil, forest topsoil, and vermiculite, with the following properties: total nitrogen, 0.55 g/kg; available nitrogen, 180.83 mg/kg; total phosphorus, 5.17 g/kg; available phosphorus, 241.47 mg/kg; total potassium, 2.49 g/kg; available potassium, 18.71 mg/kg; pH, 4.51; and organic matter, 18.67 g/kg. Forest topsoil was collected from Huaxi District, Guiyang City, Guizhou Province (26°26′N, 106°39′E). Peat soil was purchased from Haoze Xingye Technology Co., Ltd., with a pH of 5.0. The chemical fertilizer used was Stanley's universal compound fertilizer for fruits and vegetables (N + P2O5 + K2O > 45%).

Experimental design

The experiment conducted from March to July 2021. A greenhouse experiment was conducted to assess the effects of T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer and chemical fertilizer on P. bournei seedlings growth. Similar with the method of Pang et al. [42], four fertilization regimes were established: 1) CK: 500 g of mixed soil only,2) CF: 500 g of mixed soil amended with 0.35 g chemical fertilizer; 3) TLOF: 500 g of mixed soil amended with 5 g T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer; 4) HF: 500 g of mixed soil amended with 0.175 g chemical fertilizer and 2.5 g T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer. Fertilizer application rates were as follows: CF according to manufacturer instructions; TLOF at 0.01 g soil−1; and HF at half the dosage of both CF and TLOF. The replication structure consisted of three independent biological replicates. Each biological replicate comprised ten pots (each pot containing one seedling), which were treated as technical replicates or sub-samples within the replicate. Consequently, each treatment had a total of 30 pots (experimental units). All pots were randomly placed in a greenhouse at 28 °C, 12 h photoperiod. After four months, the seedlings were harvested for the assessment of growth and physiological parameters. Rhizosphere soil from each pot was collected, thoroughly mixed, and subsampled to determine chemical properties, enzyme activities, and microbial community composition.

Growth and physiological parameters

The seedlings were removed from the soil, washed with water, and dried on filter paper. Thereafter, seedling height (cm), ground diameter (mm) and seedling biomass (g) were recorded. The physiological parameters of both aboveground and underground components of the seedlings were recorded, respectively. Chlorophyll content was determined using the acetone method [53]. Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were measured using a portable pulse-modulated fluorometer (FMS-2, Hansatech Instruments Ltd., UK). The parameters determined included the initial fluorescence (F0), maximum fluorescence (Fm), potential photosynthetic activity (Fv/F0), and maximum quantum yield of PSII (Fv/Fm). Furthermore, the photosynthetic parameters were recorded via a Li-COR 6800 photosynthesis system (H2O, 65%; CO2, 400 µmol; fan, 10000 rpm; temperature, 25 ℃; light, 1200 µmol · m⁻2 · s⁻1). Root activity was determined using the triphenyl tetrazolium chloride staining method [48]. The sulfuric acid anthrone method was used to measure soluble sugar content at 630 nm [11]. Soluble protein content was determined using Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 method [11]. Ultra-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UPLC) was used to determine the content of plant hormones (abscisic acid, gibberellin, auxin, and jasmonic acid). The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD) were measured using commercial assay kits (Solarbio, China).

Plant and soil nutrients

Total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), and total potassium (TK) contents in dried seedlings and rhizosphere soil were determined as follows: TN was measured by the semi-micro-Kjeldahl method, TP was quantified using molybdenum-antimony colorimetry, and TK was analyzed by flame photometry. Soil available nitrogen (AN) was determined by alkaline hydrolysis diffusion, available phosphorus (AP) was measured by colorimetrically, and available potassium (AK) was quantified by flame photometry. Additionally, the activities of urease, phosphatase, and sucrase in soil were determined by spectrophotometric colorimetry [24].

DNA extraction, high-throughput sequencing, and bioinformatics

Rhizosphere soil samples (n = 12) from the CK (control), CF, TLOF, and HF treatments roots (< 4 mm) were scrape with a sterile bristle brush for DNA extraction. A blank control was included in each batch of DNA extractions. This control was processed identically to the samples using the same kits and reagents, but with sterile water substituted for the soil sample. The V3-V4 region of bacteria and the ITS1-ITS2 region of fungi were amplified with universal primers [27]. For each PCR set-up, a negative control (sterile water) was included to detect any contamination in the PCR master mix or primers. Purified PCR products were sequenced with an Illumina MiSeq sequencing system (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The original amplicon sequencing data and complete metadata file are available for download from the [NCBI SRA] under the accession number PRJNA1299788. Raw sequences were processed and subjected to quality control using QIIME 2 (version 2023.5) [3]. The raw sequencing data from negative controls were processed using the same bioinformatic pipeline as the experimental samples. Potential contaminating sequences were identified using the decontam package (v1.x) in R [8]. All operational taxonomic units (OTUs) identified as contaminants with a probability > 0.5 were removed from the dataset prior to downstream analysis. The retained effective sequences were clustered into OTUs of ≥ 97% similarity with USEARCH 11.0.667 [12]. A representative sequence was selected with the highest frequency for each OTU. Differential abundance analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (version 1.38.0) in R [31]. Based on output-normalized data, alpha diversity was estimated using metrics of community diversity (Simpson and Shannon indices) and community richness (ACE and Chao1 indices), respectively [50]. The co-occurrence network was constructed based on statistically significant correlations (SparCC |r|> 0.7, p < 0.05; 100 bootstrap iterations). This matrix was then used to generate a weighted adjacency matrix [13]. The non-random nature of the network was further confirmed by comparing its topological properties to those of 1000 random networks (Erdős–Rényi model; p < 0.001). The global topological properties of the network were characterized by calculating the average degree, average path length, network diameter, graph density, clustering coefficient, and modularity.

Statistical analysis

The significance of treatment effects was determined using one-way/two-way ANOVA followed by a post-hoc test (Duncan's multiple range test). Prior to ANOVA, all data were confirmed to meet the assumptions of normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homoscedasticity (Levene's test). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Data visualization was conducted using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA), Gephi (version 0.10.1), and Adobe Photoshop 2018. The PLSPM (partial least squares path modeling) was established using the “plspm” package (version 0.5.0) [46]. Microbiome analysis was performed on the Maiorbio platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Each treatment contained at least three parallel trials, and the data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation.

Effect of fertilization on soil nutrients in P. bournei seedlings

The physicochemical properties of P. bournei seedlings' rhizosphere soil significantly altered by the different fertilization regimes (Table S2, p < 0.05). Soil pH in the TLOF and HF treatments were significantly greater than in the CK and CF treatments. Moreover, the contents of soil organic matter, total nitrogen (TN), and total phosphorus (TP) were significantly elevated in the HF treatment, by 8.4, 79.4, and 44.7% respectively, compared to the control. In contrast, the total potassium (TK) levels did not differ significantly among treatments. Compared to the control, the HF treatment increased soil alkaline nitrogen (AN), available phosphorus (AP), and available potassium (AK) by 17.1%, 73.3%, and 45.1%, respectively. Furthermore, the highest urease content was observed in the TLOF treatment. In contrast, the HF treatment enhanced both sucrase and phosphatase activities, with increases of 164.0 and 46.6%, respectively, relative to the control (Table S3, p < 0.05). These results suggest that the HF treatment more effectively promote soil nutrient dissolution.

Effect of fertilization on soil microbial community structure and composition

The negative controls yielded a very low number of sequences (mean < 200 reads per control) compared to the experimental samples (mean > 40,000 reads). Bioinformatic decontamination identified and removed 12 OTUs that were likely derived from kit or laboratory contaminants. These accounted for a negligible proportion (< 0.1%) of the total sequencing library. Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (ADONIS) showed significant differences in both fungal (R2 = 0.7704, p < 0.001) and bacterial (R2 = 0.7126, p < 0.001) community composition across the treatments (Fig. 1A–B). Furthermore, these treatments yielded a total of 173 fungal OTUs and 1066 bacterial OTUs (Fig. S2A–B). Although fungal diversity (Shannon and Simpson indexes) remained unchanged, community richness (Ace and Chao indexes) significantly decreased by 17.3% and 16.7%, respectively, compared to the control. In contrast, its diversity (Shannon and Simpson indexes) was not significantly affected (Table S4, p < 0.05). The Simpson index for bacteria increased significantly in the HF treatment, whereas the Shannon, Ace, and Chao indexes decreased (Table S4, p < 0.05). Ascomycota, Rozellomycota, and Chytridiomycota were the predominant fungal phylum in the rhizosphere soil. The relative abundances of Ascomycota, Rozellomycota, and Basidiomycota were significantly elevated in the HF treatment, by 13.4%, 147.8%, and 153.8%, respectively, compared to the control (Fig. 3 C, p < 0.05). Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, and Firmicutes were the predominant bacterial phylum. The TLOF and CF treatments had the highest relative abundance of Proteobacteria and Firmicutes, respectively. Compared to the control, the relative abundances of Actinobacteriota significantly increased by 11.1% in the HF treatment (Fig. 3E, p < 0.05).

At the fungal genus level, the relative abundance of Trichoderma was highest in the TLOF treatment, followed by the HF, CF, and control treatments (Fig. 3D, p < 0.05). Compared to the control, Penicillium increased significantly by 71.2 and 49.6% in the TLOF and HF treatments, respectively. Conversely, the relative abundance of the pathogen Fusarium was significantly reduced by 79.6, 98.2, and 78.8% in the CF, HF, and TLOF treatments, respectively. At the bacterial genus level, the CF treatment yielded the highest relative abundances of Bacillus and Tumebacillus. There overall abundance order being Bacillus: CF > CK > HF > TLOF and Tumebacillus: CF > HF > CK > TLOF (Fig. 3 F, p < 0.05). Compared to the control, the HF treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Streptomyces by 8.2%. The relative abundance of Gemmatimonas was significantly increased in the CF, TLOF, and HF treatments by 92.5%, 151.7%, and 98.5%, respectively. Moreover, we identified significant associations between these key microbial genera and the growth performance of P. bournei seedlings through correlation analysis (Fig. S3A–B, p < 0.05).

Co-occurrence network analysis revealed that fertilization regimes altered the interaction patterns among microbial genera. The key genera shaping the network structure were predominantly affiliated with the phyla Ascomycota and Basidiomycota (Fig. 2). Compared to the control, the CF, TLOF, and HF treatments all showed reduced network complexity. This simplification was evidenced by lower values of average degree, nodes, and edges, alongside an increase in the proportion of negative correlations (Table S5). Based on network degree, the top three fungal genus in each treatment were: control (Simplicillium, Apiotrichum, Chaetomiaceae); CF (Didymella, Rhinocladiella, Alternaria); TLOF (Cystobasidium, Umbelopsis, Arthrographiss); and HF (Mortierella, Spizellomyces, Chloridium). In addition, the degree of Trichoderma increased in the TLOF treatment. The dominant bacterial genus occurred within Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, and Acidobacteriota (Fig. 3). Furthermore, the TLOF and HF treatments developed networks with fewer edges than the control, although the number of nodes remained consistent across all treatments (Table S6). In contrast to the pattern observed in fungi, fertilization reduced the number of negative edges in the bacterial networks. Relationship between environmental factors and P. bournei seedlings growth

Mantel test revealed a significant overall correlation between environmental factors and the growth physiology of P. bournei seedlings (Fig. 6 A, p < 0.05). Specifically, soil nutrient levels were significantly positively correlated with taproot length, plant nutrient content, soluble sugars, SOD, POD, GA, and Fᵥ/F₀ (p < 0.01). They were also positively correlated with seedling height, dry weight, lateral root number, root activity, soluble proteins, CAT, and JA (p < 0.05), but negatively correlated with Fᵥ/Fₘ. Similarly, the dominant microorganisms in fertilized treatments were significantly positively correlated with seedling height, fresh weight, root activity, CAT, and JA (p < 0.01). Positive correlations were also observed with dry weight, lateral root number, TN, TK, and POD (p < 0.05), whereas negative correlations were found with TP and Fᵥ/Fₘ.

Partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM) was employed to quantify the effects of key factors on the growth of P. bournei seedlings (GOF = 0.7152; Fig. 4B). The model revealed that nutrient availability exerted a significant positive influence on both plant hormones and growth. Plant hormones, in turn, directly promoted photosynthetic performance (path coefficient = 0.641). Photosynthesis further exhibited a strong direct effect on microbial activity (path coefficient = 0.731), which subsequently enhanced plant growth directly (path coefficient = 0.703). In contrast, plant hormones showed a direct negative effect on growth (path coefficient = –0.516). Discussion

The ability of Trichoderma-based organic fertilizers to promote plant growth while reducing chemical fertilizer inputs has been widely studied [44, 54]. Our previous research demonstrated that a T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer effectively promotes P. bournei seedlings growth [23]. However, no study has examined the interactive effects of T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer and reduced chemical fertilizer on P. bournei seedling physiology and rhizosphere microbiota. This study aims to assess whether T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer can effectively replace chemical fertilizers for P. bournei seedling growth, with the goal of elucidating the associated biological mechanisms. We showed that the HF treatment more effectively promoted P. bournei seedlings growth by regulating plant physiological traits (photosynthesis and phytohormones), nutrient utilization, and enriching beneficial microbes.

Growth promotion mechanisms of T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer in P. bournei seedlings

Plant photosynthetic performance is closely linked to environmental adaptation, resource efficiency, and overall growth. In contrast to chemical fertilizer, T. longibrachiatum application resulted in lower transpiration and stomatal conductance alongside an improvement in water use efficiency in P. bournei seedlings (Table S1). These results suggest that seedlings treated with chemical fertilizer may be poorly adapted to stresses such as drought and high-temperature. Notably, P. bournei in the HF treatment had the highest levels of photosynthetic and intercellular CO2, aligning with reports in Rubio et al. [45]. Interestingly, Yan et al. Phytohormones are crucial in seed germination, hypocotyl and stalk elongation, and leaf extension [9, 22]. In this study, the HF treatment induced an accumulation of GA and JA in P. bournei seedlings (Fig. S1A). Our results can be compared with the results of Rubio et al. [45], who found that T. harzianum combined with chemical fertilization promoted tomato growth at the cost of phytohormone homeostasis. However, we found that a combination of T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer and a 50% reduced dose of chemical fertilizer was more effective in elevating phytohormone levels. It is noteworthy that their study employed a pure T. harzianum agent, not a compounded organic fertilizer. This difference in the carrier medium could significantly influence microbial activity and plant–microbe interactions, potentially leading to divergent physiological outcomes. Interestingly, seedlings in the HF treatment exhibited the highest levels of key stress-related biomarkers, including soluble substances and protective enzymes (Fig. 5D-H). Consistent with reports that Trichoderma (T. harziensis and T. guizhouense) and chemical fertilizer co-application improves stress resistance [43, 49]. These findings highlight a validated pathway for deploying T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizers in agricultural practice.

Nutrient dynamics of P. bournei seedlings under the action of T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer

Plant nutrient acquisition capacity is a key determinant of agroecosystem productivity, influencing not only crop yield but also resource use efficiency [18]. In this study, HF treatment resulted in the highest soil content of N, P, K, and organic matter (Table S2). This pattern was mirrored in plant tissue, where significant increases were detected specifically in N and P concentrations (Fig. 5B). Yan et al. [55] pointed out that T. asperellum-based organic fertilizer plus 75% chemical fertilizer were the best for improving the nitrogen use efficiency of Lycium chinense. A well-established mechanism for improved nutrient availability is the enzymatic mineralization of organic matter, as indicated by Nannipieri et al. [39]. Similarly, the work of Liu et al. [29] with T. guizhouense demonstrated enhanced enzyme activity promoting plant growth. Therefore, we hypothesize that the T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer in our study acted by analogous mechanisms, specifically by boosting urease, sucrase, and phosphatase activities (Table S2). Soil microorganisms are key drivers of soil nutrient dynamics. Specifically, genera such as Gemmatimonas and Trichoderma solubilize nutrients by secreting organic acids, thereby enhancing their bioavailability to plants [2, 32]. According to Pearson correlation analysis, numerous microbial genera exhibited significant correlations with soil nutrient levels (Fig. S3C–D). These results suggest that rhizosphere microbes contribute to plant growth by regulating soil nutrient dynamics. However, the specific mechanisms involved require further investigation to be fully elucidated.

T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer modulates plant–microbe interactions to promote P. bournei seedlings growth

Bio-organic fertilizer restructures the soil microbial community through a multi-level "resource-microbial-function" cascade [16, 52]. In this study, the TLOF and HF treatment reduced soil microbial diversity (Table S4). While a decline in diversity is conventionally viewed as detrimental to ecosystem stability and function, its ecological implications are in fact highly nuanced [19]. A comprehensive interpretation requires integrating these findings with concurrent shifts in community composition and function, as highlighted by Schlatter et al. [47] and Martins et al. [37]. Crucially, the TLOF and HF treatments enriched specific beneficial genera, such as Trichoderma, Bacillus, Paenibacillus, Ammoniphilus, and Aneurinibacillus (Figs. 2 and 3). Specifically, these taxa showed a significant increase in their degree and connectivity within the co-occurrence network, thereby potentially enhancing their functional influence. These findings indicate a selective enrichment of some specialized microbes consortium that benefits plant growth (Fig. 6A). The potential functional traits of microorganisms are commonly inferred at the taxonomic genus level. The plant growth-promoting functions of Trichoderma and Gemmatimonas are well-established [2, 32]. The HF treatment significantly increased the abundance of these taxa (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, their significant positive correlation with P. bournei growth (Fig. S3A-B). This finding corroborates the work of Tao et al. [49]. Whereas Luo et al. [34] reported that organic and chemical fertilizers could stimulate Fusarium in P. bournei, we found that all treatments in our study suppressed it. Specifically, the TLOF treatment resulted in the lowest Fusarium abundance. We observed a sharp increase in Bacillus abundance following chemical fertilizer treatment. This increase may contribute to the suppression of Fusarium, as Bacillus is known to inhibit soil-borne pathogens [20, 41]. Interestingly, although T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizers inhibited Fusarium, the relative abundance of Bacillus decreased specifically under the TLOF treatment. This inverse relationship suggests potential niche competition between Trichoderma and Bacillus in the P. bournei rhizosphere. Given that the cited study [34] was conducted in forests, the applicability of this interaction to our system requires verification. Collectively, these findings suggest that T. longibrachiatum-based organic fertilizer may enhance P. bournei disease resistance.

Soil nutrients, moisture, and temperature are key factors shaping soil microbial diversity and function [33]. Although this study maintained controlled conditions (25 °C, 60% soil moisture), it remains unclear how natural environmental fluctuations might influence these outcomes. Moreover, plant growth is influenced by multiple agricultural management factors, including soil type, cultivation environment, and climate [4]. The current study evaluated P. bournei seedling growth over a relatively short period (4 months) under specific culture conditions. The small sample size—three biological replicates per treatment—though revealing clear trends, may limit statistical power and generalizability. Consequently, the long-term efficacy of the HF treatment and its ability to improve soil nutrients under field conditions require further validation. Future work must be extended to a deeper investigation of the underlying mechanisms, such as the role of root exudates [34].

Conclusion

This research presents a viable alternative to address the urgent need for reducing chemical fertilizer reliance. We show that co-fertilization promotes P. bournei seedling growth by regulating photosynthesis, phytohormone, nutrient, and microbiome pathways. This synergy ultimately fosters a more resilient soil ecosystem. This work represents a paradigm shift from input-intensive fertilization to a biology-driven approach.
