Dual-targeted microspheres reshape the metabolic-immune microenvironment to reverse post-embolization dilemmas in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Nan Jiang, Zifan Pei, Fei Gong, Weihao Yang, Jiachen Xu, Jie Cao, Xiaoxiao Pan, Bin Yu, Jian Shen, Zhi Li, Binyan Zhong, Caifang Ni, Liang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTransarterial embolization (TAE) efficacy in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is limited by post-embolization hypoxia-driven angiogenesis and metabolic reprogramming. To address this, we develop pH-responsive gelatin microspheres (GMs) encapsulating zinc sulfide (ZnS) nanoparticles (ZnS-encapsulated gelatin microspheres [ZnS@GMs]) for the dual delivery of hydrogen sulfide (H2S) gas and Zn2+ ions. ZnS@GMs inhibit tumor growth through suppression of the hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α)/vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) axis and glycolytic metabolism, alongside promoting vascular normalization and immune activation. Mechanistically, H2S disrupts mitochondrial respiration, increasing oxygen levels and destabilizing HIF-1α, whereas Zn2+ inhibits hexokinase and lactate dehydrogenase, depleting ATP and biosynthetic intermediates. Furthermore, ZnS@GMs induce immunogenic cell death, which triggers dendritic cell maturation, cytotoxic T cell infiltration, and macrophage repolarization, thereby enhancing the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. In a rabbit orthotopic liver tumor model, intra-arterial infusion of ZnS@GMs confirms potent antitumor effects and effective embolic performance. This work establishes ZnS@GMs as a multifunctional platform to overcome TAE resistance in HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 Transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), the first-line treatment for intermediate- and advanced-stage HCC, exerts its anti-tumor effects by occluding the tumor blood supply and delivering localized chemotherapy.2 However, the complex and dynamic evolution of the tumor microenvironment (TME) post-TACE significantly limits its long-term efficacy.3 In particular, hypoxia within the TME activates the hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) signaling axis, driving the overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which promotes compensatory angiogenesis and increases the risk of distant metastasis.4 Concurrently, tumor cells adapt to energy crises by enhancing glycolytic metabolism through the Warburg effect.5 This metabolic reprogramming not only fuels tumor proliferation but also reshapes the immunosuppressive TME via lactate accumulation, thereby dampening antitumor immune responses.6 Consequently, disrupting the vicious cycle of “pro-angiogenic and metabolic imbalance” post-TACE is critical for overcoming clinical therapeutic bottlenecks.

Metal-based nanomaterials have been explored in oncology because of their physicochemical and biological properties.7 Metal ions play pivotal roles in cancer treatment by activating the STING pathway, modulating stem cell differentiation, and inducing ferroptosis or enhancing catalytic therapy.8,9,10 Among them, zinc ions (Zn2+), essential cofactors in cellular processes, play critical roles in energy metabolism, gene expression, and genomic stability.11 Zn2+ has been shown to irreversibly inhibit glycolysis, offering a potential alternative to tumor starvation therapy.12,13 Furthermore, Zn2+ promotes the phase separation of the cGAS protein, increasing its enzymatic activity.14 Zn2+ also acts as a potent inducer of pyroptosis, an immunogenic cell death (ICD) mechanism, by forming pores in tumor cells, leading to the release of inflammatory contents.15 Similarly, gas signaling molecules can regulate tumor progression by modulating intracellular signaling pathways and maintaining cellular homeostasis.16,17,18,19 In particular, hydrogen sulfide (H2S) has attracted significant attention in oncology as a gaseous signaling molecule with a dual role in tumor biology and therapeutic modulation.20 H2S has been reported to inhibit mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase), reducing oxygen consumption and increasing intracellular oxygen levels.21,22 This disrupts the hypoxia-HIF-1α-VEGF positive feedback loop.23 Additionally, H2S may induce the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, leading to the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytoplasm, which activates the cGAS-STING pathway and triggers innate immune responses.24 Both metal ion therapy and gas therapy have been integrated into embolization strategies.25,26 Engineering embolic materials for in situ Zn2+/H2S release to target angiogenesis, metabolism, and immune evasion offers a rational approach to break the post-TACE adaptation cycle.

Based on these insights, functionalized embolic microspheres (ZnS-encapsulated gelatin microspheres (GMs) [ZnS@GMs]) were proposed to synergize the anti-tumor effects of metal ions and gas, while enabling localized release to minimize systemic toxicity. The core design involved encapsulating zinc sulfide (ZnS) nanoparticles (NPs) within a degradable gelatin matrix, which, upon exposure to the acidic environment post-embolization, triggered the gradual release of H2S and Zn2+. H2S increased intracellular oxygen levels, inhibited the HIF-1α/VEGF axis, and suppressed tumor angiogenesis. Simultaneously, H2S-induced mtDNA release activated the cGAS-STING pathway and elicited innate immune responses. On the other hand, Zn2+ ions disrupted tumor metabolic reprogramming by inhibiting glycolytic rate-limiting enzymes and induced pyroptosis in tumor cells, which further promoted the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), activated antigen-presenting cells, and enhanced T cell infiltration. Our study introduces an “embolization-release-regulation” strategy by co-delivering H2S and Zn2+ via functionalized embolic materials to target the interconnected angiogenesis-metabolism-immunity network, providing a translational tool against post-TACE recurrence.

Results

Hypoxia-driven angiogenesis and metabolic reprogramming orchestrate TACE resistance

Transcriptomic integration of hepatocellular carcinoma cohorts (Database: The Cancer Genome Atlas [TCGA]-liver hepatocellular carcinoma [LIHC], n = 370; Database: Gene Expression Omnibus [GEO], GSE104580, n = 147) and rat transarterial embolization (TAE) models revealed angiogenesis and glycolysis as pivotal pathways in post-TACE adaptation (Figure 1A). Stratification of the TCGA-LIHC cohort by angiogenesis/glycolysis gene expression (Figures S1A–S1F) demonstrated differential survival outcomes. While high angiogenesis-related gene expression showed a non-significant trend toward poorer prognosis (median survival: 3.6 vs. 4.6 years, p = 0.601), low glycolysis-related gene expression strongly correlated with prolonged survival (5.8 vs. 2.1 years, p < 0.001) (Figures 1B and 1C). This angiogenesis paradox may stem from its dual roles in tumor progression and therapeutic delivery, such as immune cell infiltration and drug perfusion.27,28 Notably, pathological angiogenesis driven by post-TACE hypoxia is predominantly associated with adverse outcomes, as evidenced by significantly reduced survival in VEGFA-high patients (3.1 vs. 5.8 years, p < 0.010), establishing VEGFA as a key mediator of TACE resistance (Figures S1G and 1D). CIBERSORT analysis revealed distinct immune microenvironments between angiogenesis/glycolysis clusters. While angiogenesis stratification revealed no significant differences in immune infiltration (Figure S1H), high-glycolysis tumors exhibited the following immunosuppressive features: reduced numbers of naive B cells, memory CD4+ T cells, and M1 macrophages coupled with increased regulatory T cells (Figures 1E and S1I). Spearman correlation analysis revealed a modest but significant inverse correlation between VEGFA expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration (r = −0.156, p < 0.05; Figures 1F and S1J). To address clinical sample limitations, we established a rat TAE model via hepatic N1S1 cell implantation with arterial ligation (day 7 post-implantation, Figure 1H). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted on tumors 7 days after TAE treatment to delineate differential gene expression patterns (Figures S1K and S1L). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the upregulated genes highlighted extracellular matrix reorganization and angiogenesis potentiation (Figure 1I), alongside paradoxical immune modulation involving antigen presentation activation and concurrent suppression of lymphocyte proliferation. Conversely, the downregulated genes were enriched in oxidative metabolic processes and xenobiotic detoxification, with disrupted glucose homeostasis and interleukin-6 signaling, indicating metabolic-inflammatory decoupling (Figure 1J). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway integration confirmed this hypoxia-adaptive axis, showing coordinated upregulation of extracellular matrix-receptor interactions, angiogenesis-related signaling, and PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoints, alongside suppression of arachidonic acid metabolism and cytochrome P450-mediated detoxification (Figure 1K). These results collectively illustrated the complex interplay between hypoxia-driven angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, and immune modulation in the TME post-TAE, providing critical insights into the adaptive mechanisms of TACE resistance.

Dual-targeted modulation of the post-TAE tumor microenvironment via H2S and Zn2+ synergy

The above findings suggested the potential for developing a dual-targeted strategy to address abnormal angiogenesis and metabolic reprogramming post-TAE by simultaneously targeting VEGF signaling and glycolysis. Single-cell RNA-seq (GSE125449) identified tumor cells as the predominant VEGF source (Figures 1L–1N). Emerging evidence indicates that the gas transmitter H2S is a critical regulator of mitochondrial redox homeostasis and is capable of attenuating HIF-1α/VEGF signaling.23 As validated by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), H2S significantly reduced VEGF secretion in a dose-dependent manner under a hypoxic environment (Figure 1O).

To address the metabolic reprogramming in post-TAE tumors, we systematically screened metal ions for their ability to inhibit glycolysis. Among the tested ions, Zn2+ emerged as the most potent glycolysis inhibitor. Treatment with 150 μM of Zn2+ resulted in a 31.9% ± 3.2% reduction in glucose consumption and a 29.3% ± 2.9% decrease in lactate production (Figures 1P, 1Q, S1M, and S1N), demonstrating its efficacy in disrupting glycolytic flux. Overall, we proposed a dual-targeted therapeutic strategy to counteract post-TACE adaptive resistance. This approach integrated H2S-mediated blockade of the HIF-1α/VEGF axis and Zn2+-driven inhibition of glycolysis, addressing both the angiogenic and metabolic adaptations that contribute to resistance to tumor treatment (Figure 1R).

Synthesis and characterization of ZnS@GMs

To construct ZnS-engineered embolic materials, gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) was employed as a biodegradable carrier to prepare microspheres via a microfluidic strategy. ZnS@GMs were fabricated by ultraviolet-initiated crosslinking of GelMA droplets containing ZnS NPs (2 mg/mL) and lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (0.5%) in a water-oil emulsion (Figure 2J). Phase-contrast microscopy (Figures 2K and 2L) and scanning electron microscopy (Figures 2N and S2B) revealed a uniform diameter of the ZnS@GMs (45.5 ± 5.5 μm) (Figure 2M) with a porous architecture. EDS mapping further confirmed the co-existence of Zn and S within the microspheres (Figures 2O and 2P). The pH-responsive release behaviors of Zn2+ and H2S from ZnS@GMs were systematically evaluated under acidic conditions (pH 6.0) to simulate the post-TACE TME. For Zn2+, the cumulative release profiles revealed distinct kinetics between free ZnS NPs and ZnS@GMs (Figure 2Q). Free ZnS exhibited a rapid burst release within the first 3 days, followed by a sharp decline in the release rate, reaching near depletion by day 14. In contrast, ZnS@GMs demonstrated sustained Zn2+ release. H2S release kinetics were further quantified via a methylene blue (MB) colorimetric method (Figures 2R–2T). In the acidic system, free ZnS generated a transient H2S peak at day 1, with decreased residual release thereafter (Figures 2S and S2C). Conversely, ZnS@GMs maintained prolonged H2S release, sustaining detectable concentrations through day 14 (Figure 2T). All these results demonstrated that GMs enable the sustained co-release of Zn2+ and H2S within the acidic TME (Figure 2U).

Anti-angiogenic effects of ZnS@GMs

Owing to the sustained release of H2S, the anti-angiogenic potential of ZnS@GMs was systematically evaluated (Figure 3A). First, the cytotoxicity of ZnS@GMs was assessed across various cell lines under neutral (pH 7.4) and acidic (pH 6.0) conditions via the CCK-8 assay (Figures S2D–S2F). ZnS@GMs at relatively higher concentrations (≥1 mM) significantly inhibited the viability of H22 cells under acidic conditions (Figure S2D), while exhibiting negligible effects on 293T and HUVEC cells (Figures S2E and S2F). Based on these findings and the release kinetics of ZnS and ZnS@GMs, a concentration of 0.5 mM ZnS and ZnS@GMs was selected for the subsequent experiments, with Zn2+ and NaHS controls set at 0.125 mM. JC-1 staining was employed to assess the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) of H22 cells under various pH conditions. After 24 h of culture with ZnS or ZnS@GMs, a significant decrease in the MMP was observed (Figure S2G), accompanied by the release of mtDNA (Figure S2H). Using the oxygen-sensitive probe [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2, a significant increase in intracellular oxygen content within H22 cells treated with NaHS, ZnS, or ZnS@GMs was observed (Figures 3B and S2K). Concurrently, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were also elevated (Figures S2L and S2M). The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was further measured to confirm the inhibitory effects of H2S on mitochondrial respiration. All groups containing H2S demonstrated a good reduction in OCR (>60%) (Figures 3C and S2N), confirming H2S-mediated inhibition of mitochondrial respiration.

To investigate the inhibitory effects of ZnS@GMs on the HIF-1α/VEGF axis, HIF-1α protein levels in H22 cells were first examined. Western blot analysis confirmed that ZnS and ZnS@GMs significantly reduced HIF-1α stabilization under hypoxic conditions (Figures S2O and S2P) and consistently suppressed VEGF secretion (Figure S2Q). To establish causality, we introduced a specific HIF-1α inhibitor, LW6, which promotes HIF-1α degradation via the upregulation of VHL (von Hippel-Lindau) expression. LW6 treatment similarly suppressed VEGF secretion (Figure S2Q). To visualize the anti-angiogenic effects, a co-culture system in which HUVEC cells were seeded in the lower chamber of a transwell plate, and HepG2 cells were added to the upper chamber (HUVEC: HepG2 = 1:1), was established (Figure 3E). ZnS@GM treatment significantly reduced VEGF levels in the co-culture supernatant (Figure 3D) and strongly inhibited HUVEC tube formation (Figures S2U–S2W), while having minimal direct effect on HUVECs alone (Figures S2R–S2T). To further benchmark the anti-angiogenic effect and elucidate its mechanism, we performed a rescue experiment with quantitative VEGF analysis. Both ZnS@GMs and bevacizumab comparably inhibited HUVEC tube formation (Figure 3E) and reduced VEGF secretion from the co-cultured tumor cells (Figure 3F). Critically, the addition of exogenous recombinant VEGF restored both the VEGF level and the tube-forming capacity suppressed by ZnS@GMs (Figure 3E). These findings collectively demonstrated that ZnS@GM-mediated suppression of oxygen consumption and the HIF-1α/VEGF axis in tumor cells inhibited VEGF secretion and angiogenesis.

ZnS@GMs suppress tumor glycolysis

Zn2+ ions exhibit significant cytotoxicity toward tumor cells, prompting an initial evaluation of their impact on cell viability under acidic conditions across various concentrations and material formulations (Figure 3G). Consistent with the release kinetics of ZnS and ZnS@GMs, both ZnS at 1 mM and ZnCl2 at 0.25 mM demonstrated pronounced toxicity toward H22 cells after 24 h of co-incubation. The intracellular accumulation of Zn2+ in H22 cells under acidic conditions was further validated via Zinpyr-1 fluorescence staining (Figures S3A and S3B), confirming its efficient cellular uptake. Zn2+ significantly disrupted glycolytic metabolism, as evidenced by reductions in glucose consumption and lactate production, compared to untreated controls (Figures 3H and 3I). Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) assays further corroborated these findings, revealing significant suppression of glycolytic flux by ZnCl2, ZnS, and ZnS@GMs (Figures 3J and S3C), all of which indicated marked inhibition of the Warburg effect.

To identify the molecular targets of Zn2+, the activity of key glycolytic enzymes was analyzed. ZnCl2 and ZnS selectively inhibited hexokinase (HK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), with minimal effects on phosphofructokinase (PFK) or pyruvate kinase (PK) (Figure 3K). This enzymatic suppression correlated with diminished ATP levels (Figure 3L), confirming an energy crisis and implicating a broader metabolic collapse in pathways essential for tumor proliferation. Notably, ZnS and ZnS@GMs exhibited stronger HK inhibition than ZnCl2 treatment group (Figure 3K), suggesting regulatory mechanisms beyond direct Zn2+-enzyme interactions.

Pyroptosis and immunogenic cell death induced by ZnS@GMs

We next investigated whether the metabolic and mitochondrial stress converged on innate immune activation. Western blot analysis confirmed that ZnS@GM treatment robustly activated the cGAS-STING pathway in H22 cells, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of STING and its downstream effectors TBK1 and IRF3 (Figures 3M and S3D). This activation was associated with Zn2+-induced pyroptosis.15 Hallmark features included characteristic cellular swelling and membrane pore formation (Figure 3N), along with the proteolytic cleavage of caspase-1 and GSDMD (Figures 3O and S3E). Immunofluorescence confirmed that ZnS@GM treatment induced the formation of ASC-caspase-1 inflammasome specks, while assays demonstrated robust interleukin (IL)-1β secretion (Figures S3F and S3G). Both ASC speck assembly and IL-1β release were abolished by the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950. Concurrently, ZnS@GMs triggered ICD, evidenced by the surface exposure of calreticulin (CRT) and the release of ATP and HMGB1 (Figures 3P, S3H, and S3I). In co-culture experiments, ZnS@GM-treated tumor cells potently promoted the maturation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (DCs) (Figures S3J and S3K). This pro-maturational effect was significantly attenuated when the ecto-ATPase apyrase was added to hydrolyze extracellular ATP, or when a neutralizing anti-CRT antibody was applied to block the “eat-me” signal (Figures S3L and S3M), confirming that both released ATP and surface-exposed CRT are functionally required for DC activation. We then asked whether the observed DC maturation was ultimately driven by the activation of intracellular STING pathway within tumor cells. Strikingly, ZnS@GM was the most potent inducer of DC maturation among all treatments, and this effect was substantially reversed by the STING inhibitor C-176 (Figures 3Q and 3R). This positions cGAS-STING activation—driven by Zn2+-potentiated sensing of H2S-released mtDNA—as the central orchestrator. It links intracellular metabolic catastrophe to extracellular immunogenic signaling (pyroptosis, DAMP release), culminating in the priming of adaptive anti-tumor immunity (Figure 3S).

Anti-tumor efficacy of ZnS@GMs

In a subcutaneous H22 model (Figure 4A), ZnS@GMs sustained intratumoral H2S release, peaking at day 5 (Figure S4A). Spatial imaging confirmed H2S propagated throughout the tumor mass (Figure S4B). Consistent with this prolonged release, ZnS@GMs induced sustained alleviation of tumor hypoxia from days 3 to 14, as monitored by photoacoustic imaging, unlike the transient effect of ZnS or persistent hypoxia in controls (Figure 4B). To spatially visualize and quantify hypoxia alleviation, we performed pimonidazole immunostaining. ZnS@GMs induced a sustained and progressive reduction in both the hypoxic area (%) and the mean fluorescence intensity over the 14-day period, while ZnS only offered a transient effect during the first week (Figures S4C–S4E). This finding was corroborated by a dynamic reduction in HIF-1α expression and VEGF suppression (Figures 4C, S4F, and S4G). Beyond hypoxia relief, ZnS@GMs drove comprehensive vascular normalization. In vivo immune evaluation

Given the robust induction of ICD and significant immune activation by ZnS@GMs in vitro, we further evaluated changes in the tumor immune microenvironment following treatment with PBS (G1), GMs (G2), ZnS (G3), or ZnS@GMs (G4) using the same experimental groups as described previously. Two weeks post-administration, tumors and adjacent inguinal lymph nodes were harvested and subjected to flow cytometry analysis to assess immune cell populations (Figure S5I). The percentage of CD80+ CD86+ DCs among CD11c+ DCs increased in both tumor tissue and lymph nodes following ZnS@GM treatment compared with that in the PBS and GM groups and was also greater than that in the ZnS group (Figures 4M and 4T), suggesting that ZnS@GMs significantly promoted DC maturation via STING pathway activation. Mature DCs, in turn, facilitated the presentation of tumor-associated antigens, leading to a significant increase in the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (Figure 4N). Although the percentage of CD4+ T cells did not increase after ZnS or ZnS@GM treatment, a notable decrease in regulatory T cells (Tregs), which were known to exert pro-tumor immune responses and suppress CD8+ T cell activity (Figure 4O and 4P), was observed. The proportion of M1-type macrophages increased, while that of M2-type macrophages decreased following ZnS@GM treatment (Figures 4Q and 4R). Although the changes in individual macrophage subsets were moderate, the M1/M2 ratio dramatically increased (Figure 4S), indicating a shift toward anti-tumor macrophage polarization. These changes collectively depicted an immune microenvironment skewed toward anti-tumor activity.

To further evaluate the immune response, we analyzed multiple cytokines in tumor tissue and interferon (IFN)-β in lymph nodes. ZnS@GM-treated mice exhibited the highest levels of these cytokines, including IFN-β, IFN-γ, IL-1β, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and IL-6, in tumor tissue, as well as elevated IFN-β in the lymph nodes (Figures 4U–4Z). These findings underscored the systemic immune activation induced by ZnS@GMs. Overall, ZnS@GMs synergistically suppress tumor growth through dual VEGF-glycolysis inhibition, while reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME into an immunogenic niche (Figure 4AA).

Abscopal effect of ZnS@GMs combined with anti-PD-1 therapy

While localized therapies such as ZnS@GMs can effectively defeat primary tumors, their capacity to induce systemic immune responses capable of controlling distant, untreated lesions—termed the “abscopal effect”—remains a critical challenge. To evaluate the systemic anti-tumor immune response elicited by ZnS@GMs combined with PD-1 blockade, a bilateral H22 tumor model in BALB/c mice was established (Figure 5A). Tumor progression was monitored until day 14 or the endpoint (tumor volume ≥1,000 mm3). ZnS@GMs + anti-PD-1 (G4) exhibited profound control of both treated and distant tumors. By day 14, treated tumors in G4 presented a significant reduction in volume and lower mass without weight lost (Figures 5B–5E and S5J–S5O). In contrast, anti-PD-1 monotherapy (G2) and ZnS@GMs alone (G3) achieved moderate suppression with limited abscopal effects. In accordance with the results of tumor suppression and immune evaluation, histopathological analysis revealed that G4 induced more robust immunogenic reprogramming compared to other groups (Figures 5G and 5I). In both treated and untreated G4 tumors, H&E staining revealed extensive necrosis, which was consistent with the observed tumor regression (Figures 5F and 5H). Additionally, the number of Ki67+ proliferating cells was reduced, indicating significant inhibition of tumor growth (Figures 5G and 5I). Moreover, the CD8+ T cell density on both sides of the tumor increased, while the Foxp3+ Treg density decreased, reflecting a shift toward an anti-tumor immune phenotype. PD-1+ cells were also reduced, confirming effective blockade of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and restoration of T cell effector function. ZnS@GMs elicit STING-dependent systemic anti-tumor immunity

We hypothesized that ZnS@GMs prime cGAS-STING-dependent antigen-specific immunity, amplified by PD-1 blockade. IFN-γ enzyme-linked immunospot assay (ELISpot) confirmed robust antigen-specific T cell responses with ZnS@GM plus PD-1 blockade (Figures 5J–5M). This response was abrogated by co-treatment with the STING inhibitor C-176, confirming its dependence on cGAS-STING activation. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that p-STING and p-IRF3 signals were significantly upregulated by day 1, peaked around day 3, and remained elevated at day 7 (Figures S5P–S5R). Subsequently, we characterized the immune landscape within the TME (Figures 5J and 5K). ZnS@GM treatment elicited a broad immunogenic response: it enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration in tumors, promoted the differentiation of splenic CD8+ and CD4+ T cells into effector and central memory subsets, boosted DC maturation in tumors and draining lymph nodes, and increased IFN-β levels locally and systemically (Figures 5N–5W and S5S). Critically, STING inhibition substantially reversed each of these immunomodulatory effects, underscoring the central role of the cGAS-STING pathway in ZnS@GM-induced immune activation. The functional necessity of this STING-driven immunity for the abscopal effect was definitively proven in bilateral tumor model (Figure S5T). While ZnS@GMs alone or combined with anti-PD-1 controlled both primary and distant tumors, the concurrent administration of C-176 completely abolished this contralateral control (Figures S5U–S5W). Finally, to assess the durability of immunity, mice that exhibited primary tumor growth suppression after combination therapy successfully resisted a secondary tumor rechallenge, demonstrating the establishment of functional, long-term immunological memory (Figure 5X). Collectively, these data delineate a coherent mechanism: ZnS@GM activates the cGAS-STING pathway to drive a potent and systemic tumor-specific T cell response and memory formation. PD-1 blockade acts synergistically to amplify this response, leading to robust local and abscopal tumor control that is entirely dependent on STING signaling.

To complement the transcriptomic analysis and gain insight into the immediate functional state of the tumor metabolism, we performed untargeted metabolomics on the same set of orthotopic rat tumors harvested 7 days post-TAE (n = 5, Figure S6G). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the metabolomic data revealed a profound and coordinated suppression of core biosynthetic and energy-yielding modules in ZnS@GM-treated tumors (Figure S6H). The most significantly downregulated pathways included central carbon metabolism in cancer, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), the pentose phosphate pathway, and nucleotide metabolism. This pattern indicates a state of systemic anabolic collapse, where the tumor’s capacity to generate energy, reducing equivalents, and biosynthetic precursors is simultaneously compromised.

Immunofluorescence staining revealed diminished CD31+ vessels, increased αSMA+ mature vasculature, elevated STING expression, and increased infiltration of immune cells, including CD45+, CD3+, and CD8+ cells, in the ZnS@GM group (Figures 6I and S6I). ZnS@GMs achieve tumor suppression and TME modulation in a rabbit VX2 liver cancer model

To validate the therapeutic potential of ZnS@GMs for TAE, an auricular artery embolization experiment was performed and a VX2 liver cancer model in New Zealand white rabbits was established. Rabbit auricular artery embolization experiments revealed rapid occlusion of distal blood flow within 5 min (Figure 6K), with sustained ischemia confirmed by infrared thermography. Similar results were observed for the GMs (Figure S6J), confirming the mechanical reliability of both microspheres. For the VX2 liver cancer model, VX2 tumor tissue suspensions were percutaneously injected into the liver under computed tomography (CT) guidance, 14 days before TAE treatment (Figures 6J and 6L). The rabbits were randomized into three groups: control (no embolization), GMs (blank GMs), and ZnS@GMs (n = 5 per group). Tumor progression was monitored via contrast-enhanced CT scans pre- and post-TAE (days 7, 14, and 30). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed complete occlusion of tumor-feeding arteries post-TAE in ZnS@GM-treated rabbits, with preserved patency of the normal hepatic vasculature (Figure 6M). CT imaging revealed progressive tumor growth and lung metastases in the control, moderate growth in the GM, and significant suppression in the ZnS@GM treatment groups (Figures 6N–6P, S6K, and S6L). In rabbits subjected to hepatic artery embolization with ZnS@GMs, ICP-MS detected only transient elevations of zinc and sulfur in the plasma, which peaked at day 3 and returned to baseline after day 14 (Figures S6M–S6P). Concurrently, transient elevations in liver enzymes (AST and ALT) and bilirubin (TBIL) were observed on day 3 post-TAE in both the GM and ZnS@GM groups, likely reflecting acute embolic stress (Figure 6Q). However, the level of creatinine (Cr) in all groups remained stable after treatment, indicating negligible impact of ZnS on renal function. The control rabbits exhibited progressive liver dysfunction after day 14, likely due to the large tumor burden. Consistent with the tumor progression findings, H&E staining revealed broader necrotic areas in ZnS@GM-treated tumors and reduced Ki67 staining, indicating lower tumor activity (Figures 6R and S6Q). As evidenced by reduced intratumoral hemorrhage and decreased VEGF expression, ZnS@GMs also induced vascular normalization (Figures 6R and S6Q). Enhanced anti-tumor immunity was observed, with elevated CD45+ leukocyte infiltration and CD3+ T cell density in residual tumors (Figures 6R and S6Q). To specifically address the risk of innate immune-mediated liver injury, we performed histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis on peritumoral normal liver tissue. H&E staining showed no signs of sinusoidal endothelial cell injury, congestion, or Kupffer cell hyperplasia (Figure S6R). Furthermore, STING and its downstream cytokine IL-6 revealed negligible expression in the normal liver parenchyma of ZnS@GM-treated animals (Figure S6R). The rabbit VX2 model recapitulated key features of human HCC, including hypervascularity and metastatic potential. These findings underscored the potential of ZnS@GMs to achieve robust tumor suppression, vascular normalization, and immune activation, offering a promising strategy for clinical TAE therapy in HCC patients.

Discussion

HCC remains a global health challenge with limited durable responses to conventional locoregional therapies, primarily due to the adaptive resilience of the TME post-embolization. This study presents ZnS@GMs, a dual-functional embolic platform engineered to overcome this resistance by simultaneously targeting two interdependent pillars of tumor adaptation: hypoxia-driven angiogenesis and glycolytic metabolic reprogramming. In a subcutaneous H22 tumor model, ZnS@GMs significantly inhibited tumor growth and enhanced immune cell infiltration, while in an orthotopic HCC rat model, they suppressed angiogenesis and glycolysis, while promoting T cell-mediated immunity. The rabbit VX2 liver cancer model further confirmed the clinical translatability of ZnS@GMs, which demonstrated robust embolic efficacy, vascular normalization, and enhanced anti-tumor immunity with minimal systemic toxicity. Importantly, the combination of ZnS@GMs with anti-PD-1 therapy elicited a potent abscopal effect, controlling both treated and distant tumors, highlighting the potential of this approach to address metastatic disease.

Mechanistically, H2S released from ZnS@GMs inhibited mitochondrial complex IV, elevating intratumoral oxygen tension.21 This increase in oxygen availability enhanced oxygen-dependent prolyl hydroxylase activity, promoting the hydroxylation, VHL-mediated recognition, and subsequent proteasomal degradation of HIF-1α.29,30 The destabilization of HIF-1α led to a marked suppression of VEGF, a key driver of pathological angiogenesis and an established immunosuppressive factor.31,32 Concurrently, among various metal ions screened, Zn2+ emerged as the most effective glycolysis inhibitor. This ion-specific effect may stem from its unique ability to directly bind and inhibit enzymes, in contrast to other metals, which often serve as cofactors that promote metabolic flux or stabilize HIF-1α to upregulate glycolysis.33,34,35,36 Zn2+ directly targeted rate-limiting enzymes, selectively inhibiting HK and LDH, which catalyze the gateway step of glycolysis.37,38 This is supported by established mechanisms where zinc potently inhibits enzyme activity by binding to active-site residues, as demonstrated in various metabolic enzymes.35 This enzymatic blockade resulted in depleted ATP, reduced glucose consumption, and diminished lactate production, crippling the Warburg effect. Notably, the anti-glycolytic role of Zn2+ extends beyond direct enzyme inhibition. HK suppression is known to correlate with STING pathway activation, and Zn2+ can directly enhance cGAS enzymatic activity.39,40 Our data suggest that Zn2+-mediated metabolic stress synergizes with H2S-induced mitochondrial damage and mtDNA release to robustly activate the cGAS-STING axis, creating a feedforward loop that amplifies metabolic-immune crosstalk. The inhibition of glycolysis may be attributed to the following dual mechanism: (1) direct enzyme inhibition, wherein Zn2+ binds to HK and LDH active sites, disrupting substrate catalysis, and (2) indirect immune activation, wherein H2S-induced mtDNA release engages the cGAS-STING axis, downregulating HK expression, while synergizing with Zn2+-driven cGAS potentiation.

This integrated metabolic and gas therapy strategy did more than starve tumors; it actively ignited anti-tumor immunity. The ZnS@GM-induced dual stress (Zn2+ and H2S) disrupts the operational integrity of the metabolic network, leading to its coordinated dysfunction, as captured by transcriptomic downregulation of glycolytic and anabolic pathways. The subsequent metabolic crisis, coupled with mitochondrial perturbation, primed inflammasome activation and ROS accumulation,41 driving GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, a highly immunogenic form of cell death.42 This was evidenced by the cleavage of caspase-1 and GSDMD and crucially, by the robust surface exposure of CRT, a key hallmark of ICD,43,44 while HMGB1 remained sequestered in the nucleus.45,46 The release of TAAs and DAMPs from dying tumor cells, facilitated by this pyroptotic cascade, promoted DC maturation and antigen cross-presentation, leading to enhanced infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and a favorable shift in macrophage polarization toward an anti-tumor M1 phenotype.

The therapeutic promise of this immunomodulation was fully realized in combination with systemic immune checkpoint blockade. While ZnS@GMs alone effectively reprogrammed the local TME, their combination with anti-PD-1 therapy elicited a potent abscopal effect, controlling both treated and distant tumors. Locally, ZnS@GMs drive ICD and antigen release, while systemically, PD-1 blockade reverses T cell exhaustion, amplifying effector function. This synergy underscores the potential of integrating localized metabolic and gas modulation with systemic immunotherapy to achieve comprehensive anti-tumor immunity.

Validation across multiple preclinical models, from subcutaneous and orthotopic rodent models to a translational rabbit VX2 model, confirmed the robust efficacy, vascular normalization, and immune activation induced by ZnS@GMs, with favorable biocompatibility and no evidence of long-term heavy metal accumulation. Collectively, this work establishes ZnS@GMs as an effective “embolization-release-regulation” platform that disrupts the pro-angiogenic and metabolic vicious cycle, while converting immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones, offering a multifaceted strategy to overcome TAE resistance in HCC.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides comprehensive preclinical evidence, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, H22/N1S1 models may not fully recapitulate human HCC heterogeneity. Future studies employing patient-derived xenografts or genetically engineered mouse models would enhance translational relevance. Second, while we demonstrated the central role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in Zn2+-induced pyroptosis, the upstream sensors initiating this cascade warrant further delineation. Third, the direct structural interaction between Zn2+ and glycolytic enzymes (HK/LDH), although supported by functional inhibition data, requires validation through molecular docking or enzymatic kinetics studies. Finally, although our extended safety assessments up to 30 days showed no chronic toxicity, longer-term studies evaluating the complete degradation of microspheres and potential off-target effects in large animal models are necessary before clinical translation. Addressing these points in future work will further solidify the foundation for this promising therapeutic approach.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a controlled environment (temperature: 22 ± 2 °C; humidity: 50 ± 10%; 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to standard rodent chow and autoclaved water. For tumor studies, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence after tumor cell inoculation and confirmation of palpable tumor formation. All procedures involving mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Soochow University Laboratory Animal Center (Protocol Number: 202508A0704), and were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Rats

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (approximately 250 g body weight, 8 weeks old) were housed under standard laboratory conditions (temperature: 22 ± 2 °C; 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. Rats were randomly allocated to experimental groups after successful establishment of orthotopic liver tumors. Animal welfare and experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the IACUC of Soochow University Laboratory Animal Center (Protocol Number: 202505A1173).

Rabbits

Female New Zealand White rabbits (body weight 2.5–3.5 kg, 3 months old) were housed individually in standard rabbit cages under controlled environmental conditions. Rabbits were provided with a standard diet and water ad libitum. Rabbits bearing VX2 liver tumors were randomly divided into control, GMs, and ZnS@GMs treatment groups. All interventional procedures were performed under anesthesia, and all efforts were made to minimize suffering. These experiments were approved by the IACUC of Soochow University Laboratory Animal Center (Protocol Number: 202503A0626) and conformed to the relevant regulatory standards.

Note on sex of animals

This study utilized female mice and male rats for tumor models. The sex of the rabbits used was female. As single-sex cohorts were used for each animal model, a formal analysis of the influence of sex on the reported outcomes was not performed. This represents a limitation to the generalizability of the findings.

Cell lines

The H22 murine hepatocellular carcinoma cell line was used for in vitro and syngeneic in vivo studies. This cell line is derived from a male BALB/c mouse. Cells were obtained from Procell (Cat: CL-0341) and were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS).

The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2 was employed in co-culture angiogenesis assays. This cell line is of male origin and was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Cat: HB-8065). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and were authenticated by the provider using STR analysis.

The human umbilical vein endothelial cell line HUV-EC-C [HUVEC] was used for in vitro tube formation assays. This finite cell line was derived from a female newborn and was established in 1984. Cells were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Cat: GNHu23) and cultured in a specialized complete medium consisting of F-12K basal medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.1 mg/mL heparin, and 30 μg/mL endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS).

The 293T human embryonic kidney cell line, a female-derived cell line expressing the SV40 large T antigen, was used for general cytotoxicity assessment. Cells were obtained from ATCC (Cat: CRL-3216) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The cell line was authenticated by STR profiling by the provider.

The N1S1 rat hepatocellular carcinoma cell line was used to establish orthotopic liver tumors in rats. This cell line is derived from a male Sprague-Dawley rat. Cells were purchased from ATCC (Cat: CRL-1604) and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS.

All cell lines were cultured in a hypoxic incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2, 5% O2) to mimic the post-embolization tumor microenvironment and were routinely tested to confirm the absence of mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

Data acquisition from the database and the analysis

STAR-counts data and corresponding clinical information for liver tumors from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) were downloaded. Then, these data were extracted in TPM format and normalized using the log2(TPM+1) transformation. After retaining samples that included both RNAseq data and clinical information, 370 samples were ultimately selected for further analysis.

To functionally elucidate the biological characteristics of the angiogenesis and glycolysis regulators in HCC, the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package (1,000 iterations and resample rate of 80%, http://www.bioconductor.org/) was employed to classify the patients with HCC into different subtypes. PCA was performed to assess gene-expression patterns among distinct HCC subtypes.

The log-rank test was used to compare the survival differences between the two groups mentioned above in the KM survival analysis. For the Kaplan-Meier curve, the p-value and hazard ratio (HR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) were obtained through the long-rank test and univariate Cox regression.

GSE104580 dataset was sourced from the GEO database (Home - GEO DataSets - NCBI), with the downloaded data in MINiML format. Log2 transformation was uniformly performed to normalize the data, and the normalize.quantiles function in the preprocessCore package of R was used for data standardization. According to the corresponding platform annotation information, we converted probe IDs into gene symbols, excluded probes corresponding to multiple genes, and calculated the average value of genes corresponding to multiple probes.

The corresponding single-cell data (GSE125449) were download from the GEO database in.h5 format, and the annotation results from were obtained from TISCH. The R software tools MAESTRO and Seurat were used to process and analyze the single-cell data. The cells were reclustered via the t-SNE method.

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software, version v4.3.3. The results were considered statistically significant when the p-value was less than 0.05.

Synthesis of ZnS nanoparticles

ZnS nanoparticles were synthesized at room temperature using a chemical precipitation technique. Initially, 100 mg of polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) was dissolved in 6 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 1 mmol of Zinc acetate (Zn(CH3COO)2) was dissolved in water, stirred magnetically, and allowed to react for 30 min. Finally, 1.5 mmol of sodium hydrosulfide (NaHS) was dissolved and added gradually to the mixture. The immediate production of a turbid white mixture indicated the successful formation of a ZnS colloidal precipitate. The resulting ZnS was centrifuged, washed three times with deionized water, and kept at 4 °C for future use.

Synthesis of methacrylic acid-grafted gelatin (GelMA)

In 25 mL of Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), 2.5 g of gelatin was dissolved at 60°C for a duration of 5–10 min. Following complete dissolution, 2 mL of methacrylic anhydride (PMMA, Aladdin, China) was added into the reaction system for another duration of 3–5 h, and the resulting products were dialyzed (MWCO: 8000–14000 kDa) for 3 days at 50 °C. In the end, the solution was freeze-dried, and the resulting GelMA was kept at 4 °C.

Synthesis of the ZnS-encapsulated gelatin microspheres (abbr. ZnS@GMs)

The microspheres were prepared by a microfluidic method. Initially, ZnS (concentration: 2 mg/mL) nanoparticles that are soluble in water were mixed into a GelMA solution (with 10% GelMA) to create the water phase, followed by the addition of the LAP (lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate) initiator (at a concentration of 0.5%), which is an ultraviolet initiator, to this mixture. The oil phase consisted of mineral oil with 2% Span 80. Subsequently, the water and oil phases were separately injected into distinct channels of the microfluidic device using micro-infusion pumps. The formation of microdroplets in the outflow tract was followed by UV cross-linking to solidify them, producing ZnS-encapsulated gelatin microspheres, abbreviated as ZnS@GMs. Blank gelatin microspheres (GMs) were prepared via the same procedure but without incorporating ZnS into the water phase.

The encapsulation efficiency of ZnS nanoparticles within the gelatin microspheres was determined during the synthesis process. The encapsulation efficiency and drug loading were determined by directly analyzing the Zn content within the fabricated ZnS@GMs. The concentration of Zn was quantified via inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). The encapsulation efficiency and drug loading content were calculated via the following formulas:Encapsulationefficiency(EE,%)=(MassofZnmeasuredinZnS@GMs/TheoreticalmassofZnbasedontotalZnSaddedduringsynthesis)×100%Drugloadingcontent(DLC,μg/mg)=MassofZnSinthemicrospheres/totalmassoftheZnS@GMs

Based on this analysis, the encapsulation efficiency of ZnS nanoparticles in the ZnS@GMs was ∼70.2%, and the drug loading content was 20 μg ZnS per 1mg of microspheres.

Characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained using an FEI Tecnai F20 TEM. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted with a PHI Quantera SXM spectrometer equipped with an Al Ka monochromator. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra patterns were collected on a PANalytical X-ray diffractometer. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging and element-mapping were conducted with a ZEISS G500 SEM. The size of ZnS particles and ZnS@GMs was measured via an inverted phase contrast microscope and ImageJ software. The absolute zinc content was determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). UV-vis-NIR absorbance spectra were recorded with a PerkinElmer Lambda 750 UV-vis-NIR spectrophotometer. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) imaging was conducted with a Siemens DSA system.

Qualitative determination of H2S release

To detect H2S production, a WSP-1 probe was utilized. In brief, 10 mM, 5 mM, 2.5 mM, 1.25 mM and 0.625 mM of ZnS nanoparticles were dispersed in 2 mL of pH = 7.4 and pH = 6.0 buffers, and then was stored in a small Petri dish. WSP-1 (10 μM), which is a H2S-sensitive probe, was incubated with the reaction mixture for 30 min at 37 °C. The supernatant fluorescence was observed at Ex/Em 465/515 nm.

The MB colorimetric method, another detection method, was also used to determine the H2S content quantitatively. ZnS and ZnS@GMs (ZnS concentration: 4 mM) were incubated with pH 7.4 and pH 6.0 buffers at 37 °C for different time intervals, and the supernatant (solution A) was obtained through centrifugation. Solution B was created by combining 40 mM of zinc acetate and 10 mM of sodium acetate. Afterward, 1 mL of solution A was mixed with 1 mL of solution B and allowed to react for 30 min. In the final step, 40 μL of FeCl3 and DMPD·2HCl (0.15 M) were added to the system and incubated for another 30 min at 37 °C to generate blue MB, with the absorbance detected at 664 nm. A standard curve was created using various concentrations of NaHS solution to determine the linear correlation between concentration and absorbance.

Cellular experiments

All types of cells used in this study were cultured in the hypoxic incubator at 37 °C under 5% CO2 and 5% O2 to mimic the hypoxic environment after TAE. For the cytotoxicity test, 5 × 105 H22 cells/HUVEC cells/293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated overnight. Then, these cells were incubated with GMs (kept a same mass of gelatin with ZnS@GMs), ZnS nanoparticles, ZnS@GMs (kept a same concentration of ZnS with ZnS nanoparticles), ZnCl2 or NaHS at different concentrations for 12 h. An acidic environment was formed by adding hydrochloric acid (HCl) to the culture medium. The cell viability was measured via the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay.

Treatments included: NaHS, ZnCl2, ZnS nanoparticles, ZnS@GMs, Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF antibody) (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Catalog: IB6470), recombinant human VEGF protein (CUSABIO, Catalog: CSB-MP025833HU, https://www.cusabio.com/), C-176 (STING inhibitor) (Aladdin, Catalog: C288157), MCC950 (NLRP3 inhibitor) (TargetMol, USA, Catalog: T3701), LW6 (HIF-1α inhibitor) (TargetMol, USA, Catalog: T3494), anti-CRT (Invitrogen, Catalog: PA1-902A), apyrase (MCE, Catalog: HY-P2764).

Intracellular Zn2+, H2S, O2, and ROS analysis and mitochondrial function measurement

H22 cells (5 × 105) were seeded in 24-well plates for 12 h of stabilization. According to the CCK8 results, GMs (2.5 mg/mL), ZnS nanoparticles (50 μg/mL), ZnS@GMs (50 μg of ZnS in 2.5 mg of GMs per milliliter), ZnCl2 (0.125 mM) or NaHS (0.125 mM) were added and incubated for 12 h. An acidic environment was formed by adding HCl to the RPMI 1640. Zn2+, H2S and ROS were detected by staining with Zinpyr-1 (MCE., Cat: HY-D0155) (20 μM), WSP-1 (MKbio, Cat: MX5301) (50 μM), [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 (MKbio, Cat: MX4826) (10 μM) and DCFH-DA (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., D6470) (20 μM) at 37 °C for 30 min according to the manufacturers’ instructions via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). The mitochondria were stained with JC-1 (10 μM) for 30 min according to the manufacturers’ instructions and detected by flow cytometry.

Measurement of the extracellular oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)

The extracellular oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured via an OCR Assay Kit (Elabscience, E-BC-F068) and an ECAR Assay Kit (Elabscience, E-BC-F069), respectively, following the manufacturer’s protocols. To account for the release kinetics of ZnS@GMs, the concentration of ZnS@GMs was doubled. The treatment groups included GMs (5 mg/mL), ZnS nanoparticles (50 μg/mL), ZnS@GMs (100 μg ZnS in 5 mg of GMs per milliliter), ZnCl2 (0.125 mM), and NaHS (0.125 mM). Measurements were initiated immediately after drug administration.

mtDNA detection

H22 cells were seeded in 10 cm culture dishes and treated with GMs (2.5 mg/mL), ZnS nanoparticles (50 μg/mL) or ZnS@GMs (50 μg ZnS in 2.5 mg of GMs per milliliter) for 12 h. Then, the total DNA was extracted from cultured H22 cells using a commercial DNA extraction kit (Cowin Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) gene MT-ND1 was quantified using real-time PCR with SYBR Green chemistry (Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix, Yeasen) on a CFX-Connect 96 system (Bio-Rad). The reaction mixture included specific primers for MT-ND1 and β-ACTIN as an internal control. The primers used for MT-ND1 amplification were as follows:

MT-ND1-F: CTCAACCTAGCAGAAACAAACC.

HUVEC tube formation assay and co-culture system

To evaluate the angiogenic potential, 300 μL of Matrigel (BD Biocoat, Cat: 354230) was added to the bottom of 24-well plates and allowed to solidify. Subsequently, 2 × 105 HUVEC cells were seeded onto the Matrigel surface and cultured in medium supplemented with pure culture medium, NaHS (0.125 mM) or ZnS@GMs (50 μg ZnS in 2.5 mg of GMs per milliliter) or VEGF recombination protein (20 ng/ml) or Bevacizumab (0.5 μg/ml) for 12 h. Tube formation was observed and imaged using an inverted microscope. The capillary length and branch points were quantified via ImageJ software.

A Transwell co-culture system (pore size 0.4 μm) was employed to study cell-cell interactions. Briefly, 2 × 105 HepG2 cells were seeded in the upper chamber of a Transwell system and allowed to adhere overnight. In the lower chamber, Matrigel and HUVEC cells were added following the same procedure as described for the tube formation assay. The upper and lower chambers were then combined and co-cultured for 12 h to assess the effects of the co-culture on HUVEC tube formation.

The quantification of released VEGF was determined by ELISA kit (Boster, Cat: EK0539).

Western blotting

H22 cells were seeded in 10 cm culture dishes and treated with GMs (2.5 mg/mL), ZnS nanoparticles (50 μg/mL), ZnS@GMs (50 μg of ZnS in 2.5 mg of GMs per milliliter), ZnCl2 (0.125 mM) or NaHS (0.125 mM) for 24 h. Then, the proteins were collected with lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Following the addition of equal protein quantities and SDS-PAGE separation (GenScript, Catalog: M00928), the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. After the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and secondary antibodies for 2 h, the proteins were washed three times. Ultimately, the membranes were exposed after the addition of the electrochemiluminescence reagent.

Glucose consumption, lactate production, and glycolytic enzyme activity detection

For glucose consumption and lactate production assays, H22 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with different metal ions (0.15 mM) or GMs (2.5 mg/mL), ZnS nanoparticles (50 μg/mL), ZnS@GMs (50 μg ZnS in 2.5 mg of GMs per milliliter), ZnCl2 (0.125 mM), or NaHS (0.125 mM) for 12 h. After centrifugation, the cell culture supernatants were collected for analysis. For lactate measurement in tumor tissues, subcutaneous tumors were homogenized in 0.9% NaCl, and the supernatants were analyzed. Glucose and lactate levels were quantified using a glucose assay kit (Beyotime, S0201S) and a lactate assay kit (Elabscience, E-BC-K044-M), respectively. The blotting membranes with molecular weight markers are displayed in Figure S7A.

To assess the activity of key glycolytic enzymes, H22 cells (2 × 106 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with ZnS nanoparticles (50 μg/mL), ZnCl2 (0.125 mM), or NaHS (0.125 mM) for 6 h. The cells were then homogenized in 0.9% NaCl, and the supernatants were collected. For enzyme activity measurements in tumor tissues, subcutaneous tumor tissues were homogenized in 0.9% NaCl, and the supernatants were used for analysis. The activities of hexokinase (HK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), phosphofructokinase (PFK), and pyruvate kinase (PK) were determined via the respective assay kits (Elabscience, E-BC-K610-M, E-BC-K046-S, E-BC-K612-M, and E-BC-K611-M). The protein concentrations of all the samples were quantified via a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0012).

Detection of ATP

For the detection of released ATP, 12-well plate-seeded H22 cells were treated with different groups for 24 h. Subsequently, the supernatant or culture medium of the treated cells was collected directly, and the ATP content was assessed via a commercial kit (Elabscience, Cat: E-BC-F002) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Dendritic cell (DC) maturation

Bone marrow stem cells were obtained from the leg bones of 4-6-week-old C57 female mice and then stimulated with 20 ng/mL GM-CSF for 6 days to allow them to differentiate into myeloid DCs. Thereafter, the DCs were plated at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well, incubated with different groups for 24 h, and then centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 5 min (cells in GMs and ZnS@GMs groups needed to be filtration before centrifugation). Flow cytometry was used to stain DCs with antibodies to identify matured DCs (CD80+ CD86+ DCs within CD11c+ DCs).

Immunogenic cell death (ICD)

To investigate the SDT-induced ICD of cancer cells, the surface expression of calreticulin (CRT), and the extracellular release of high-mobility group box 1(HMGB1) were examined, respectively. H22 cells seeded in 24-well plates were incubated with GMs (2.5 mg/mL), ZnS nanoparticles (50 μg/mL), ZnS@GMs (50 μg ZnS in 2.5 mg of GMs per milliliter), ZnCl2 (0.125 mM), NaHS (0.125 mM), Apyrase (25 mUnit/ml) or anti-CRT (0.5 μg/mL) for 12 h. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed with PBS, fixed with paraformaldehyde, and stained with CRT (Beyotime, Cat: AF1666) or HMGB1 (Beyotime, Cat: AF0180) primary antibodies, followed by the appropriate Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies. After that, CRT expression and HMGB1 release were determined via confocal imaging. The quantification of released HMGB1 and IL-1β was determined by ELISA kit (Elabscience, Cat: E-EL-M0676; Boster, Cat: EK0394).

Tumor models

6-8 weeks old female BALB/c mice were purchased from Changzhou Cavins Biological Technology Co. Ltd. All animal experiments were conducted following protocols approved by the Soochow University Laboratory Animal Center. To establish the murine tumor model, H22 cells (2 × 106) in PBS (50 μL) were subcutaneously injected into the right side of the back of each mouse. For the bilateral tumor model, 2 × 106 H22 cells were injected on day −7 for the primary tumor, and another 2 × 106 cells were subsequently injected on day −3 for the distant tumor. A rat liver tumor model was constructed by laparotomy in male SD rats weighing around 250 g by injecting 2 × 106 N1S1 cells mixed with matrigel (50 μL) directly into the left lobe of liver. A rabbit liver tumor model was constructed in New Zealand white rabbits weighing 2.5–3.5 kg by directly injecting VX2 tumor tissue suspension into the liver under the guidance of CT imaging.

In vivo study

Once the H22 tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 5): (1) PBS; (2) GMs; (3) ZnS, and (4) ZnS@GMs. GMs, ZnS and ZnS@GMs were intratumorally injected into these tumors. The concentration was set to GMs (25 mg/mL), ZnS nanoparticles (0.5 mg/mL) or ZnS@GMs (0.5 mg ZnS in 25 mg of GMs per milliliter) per mouse. The injected volume of these agents was 50 μL. The tumor size and body weight of the mice were monitored every two days. The tumor volumes were calculated via the following formula: volume = length × width2/2, and the mice were considered dead once the tumor reached 1000 mm3.

In vivo measurement of H2S release kinetics and spatial distribution

Fluorescent probe Sulfidefluor 7 a.m. (SF7-AM) was employed in a subcutaneous H22 tumor model. Female BALB/c mice bearing subcutaneous H22 tumors (approximately 100 mm3 in volume) were used. Mice received a single intratumoral injection of ZnS@GMs (0.5 mg ZnS in 25 mg GMs per mouse, in 50 μL volume). For longitudinal quantification of total intratumoral H2S levels, mice (n = 3 per time point) were euthanized at serial time points post-injection (Days 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14). Tumors were immediately harvested, weighed, and homogenized in ice-cold PBS. Tissue homogenates were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected. A standardized aliquot of each supernatant was incubated with 10 μM SF7-AM in a black 96-well plate for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em: 490/525 nm) was measured using a microplate reader. To visualize the spatial diffusion of H2S within the tumor tissue, fresh tumor tissues were collected from separate cohorts of mice at key time points (Days 1, 3, 7, and 14 post-injection). Tissues were immediately cut and stained with 10 μM SF7-AM for 30 min at 37 °C in a humidified chamber protected from light. Fluorescence images were acquired under identical acquisition settings across all samples.

Pimonidazole staining and quantitative hypoxia analysis

Pimonidazole hydrochloride (Hypoxprobe-1 RED PE Kit, Hypoxyprobe, Inc, Catalog: HP11) was administered intravenously at a dosage of 60 mg/kg body weight 0.5 h before sacrifice. Subcutaneous tumors were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in OCT compound, and sectioned at 8 μm thickness. For immunostaining, frozen sections were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated with a RED PE-conjugated monoclonal anti-pimonidazole antibody (Clone 11.23.22.R, Hypoxyprobe, Inc., 1: 50 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

When the tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 5). Transarterial embolization therapy in a rat liver tumor model

To investigate the effects of TAE (TAE) on the tumor microenvironment, we established an orthotopic N1S1 liver tumor model in rats. For the first round of RNA sequencing, six tumor-bearing rats were divided into two groups (n = 3 per group): (1) control (no intervention) and (2) TAE (hepatic artery ligation only). This initial round aimed to mimic the post-embolization tumor environment without the influence of embolic agents. For the second round of RNA sequencing, another six tumor-bearing rats were divided into two TAE groups (n = 3 per group): (1) blank gelatin microspheres (GMs) and (2) ZnS-loaded gelatin microspheres (ZnS@GMs), to evaluate the specific effects of ZnS. Approximately 0.5 mg of GMs or ZnS@GMs was injected into the hepatic artery via a catheter.

Surgical procedures were performed as follows: Under anesthesia, the portal hepatis was dissected, and the junction of the common hepatic artery (CHA), proper hepatic artery (PHA), and gastroduodenal artery (GDA) was isolated. The GDA was cannulated in the direction of blood flow using a custom-made catheter with an inner diameter of 0.5 mm. The catheter was advanced through the junction until its tip was positioned in the CHA, allowing precise delivery of the embolic agents into the hepatic artery.

Transarterial embolization of the auricular ear artery in rabbits

The ears of New Zealand white rabbits were shaved. Approximately 50 mg of GMs or ZnS@GMs was injected with mixed PBS in the middle and posterior 1/3 of the auricular artery with a 1 mL syringe needle tip. Pictures of the rabbit auricular artery were taken before and 5, 20, 40, and 60 min after injection. The temperature changes of rabbit ears were recorded with an infrared thermal imaging camera before injection and at 20 and 60 min.

Transarterial embolization therapy in rabbit liver tumor model

2 weeks after VX2 tumor tissue suspension injection under CT scan. 15 rabbits bearing VX2 liver tumor with diameters of around 1.5 cm were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 5 per group), (1) Control, (2) embolized with GMs, and (3) embolized with ZnS@GMs. During the TAE procedure, the femoral artery of the rabbit was punctured using an 18-gauge sheath. A 2.7-F microcatheter and coaxial micro-guide wire were then inserted through the sheath, and fluoroscopy guidance was used to navigate its reach to the tumor supply vessel. Approximately 5 mg per rabbit of GMs or ZnS@GMs were infused into the liver tumor. Arteriography was performed to confirm the complete disappearance of hyper vascularity of hepatic tumors. After 7, 14 and 30 days, contrast-enhanced CT scan was performed to assess the blood supply of the liver tumors and lung metastasis. The tumor volumes were estimated on the basis of 3D reconstruction of the CT images.

The specific methods were summarized as follows

Randomization: Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated sequence after confirming successful tumor implantation by imaging.

Blinding: The researchers performing outcome assessments (tumor measurement on imaging) were blinded to the group allocation.

Sample Size Justification: The sample size (n = 5) was determined based on pilot data and previous publications using similar embolization models, ensuring sufficient power (β > 0.8) to detect a statistically significant difference (α = 0.05) in primary endpoints (tumor volume).

Inclusion criteria: 1. Successful tumor implantation confirmed by imaging (tumor diameter 1.2–1.8 cm for rabbits and 0.5–1.0 cm for rats); 2. Body weight 2.5–3.0 kg for rabbits and 250–300 g for rats. Exclusion criteria: 1. Significantly poor general condition before treatment, such as severely reduced food intake or diarrhea; 2. Hepatic arterial anatomic variations preventing superselective embolization; 3. Technical failure during the embolization procedure (e.g., catheter-related complication, non-target embolization).

Endpoint Definition: The technical endpoint for a successful TME procedure was defined as near-stasis of blood flow in the tumor-feeding artery, specifically observed under fluoroscopy as the clearance of the contrast agent mixed with embolic microspheres from the target vessel within 2–5 cardiac cycles.

RNA sequencing and metabolomics

mRNA sequencing analysis were performed on N1S1 liver tumor in rats from the (1) control group vs. TAE group (n = 3), and (2) GMs group vs. ZnS@GMs group (n = 3). Metabolomics analysis was performed from GMs group vs. ZnS@GMs group (n = 5). 7 days after treatment, the tumors were carefully isolated from normal tissue, excised, and promptly placed in liquid nitrogen prior to sampling. After verifying the integrity and purity of the RNA, high-throughput sequencing of the mRNAs was performed at GENEWIZ Co., Ltd. Suzhou, China. Further analysis of the RNA sequencing results was conducted on www.bioinformatics.com.cn. Metabolomics analysis was performed at Majorbio, Shanghai, China.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification methods

Band intensities in western blot were quantified via ImageJ software. Target protein signals were normalized to β-actin or β-tubulin loading controls from the same lane. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in fluorescence microscopy was measured from raw confocal images using ImageJ after background subtraction. Data in flow cytometry were analyzed with FlowJo (v10.8.1). Vessel assessments were conducted using the “Angiogenesis Analyzer” plugin for ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

All the results are presented as the means ± SEM. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (v10.4.1). Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi square test. For comparisons between two independent groups, unpaired Student’s t test was used for data passed normality and homogeneity of variance, and Mann-Whitney U test was used for data significantly deviated from normality. For comparisons among three or more groups, data were analyzed by Welch’s ANOVA followed by Games-Howell test, as the assumption of equal variances was violated (Bartlett’s test, p < 0.05). Normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical differences in tumor growth over time were determined by two-way repeated measures ANOVA with Geisser-Greenhouse correction. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
