MapAnything: Mapping Urban Assets using Single Street-View Images
Miriam Louise Carnot, Jonas Kunze, Erik Fastermann, Eric Peukert, André Ludwig, Bogdan Franczyk

Abstract
To maintain an overview of urban conditions, city administrations manage databases of objects like traffic signs and trees, complete with their geocoordinates. Incidents such as graffiti or road damage are also relevant. As digitization increases, so does the need for more data and up-to-date databases, requiring significant manual effort. This paper introduces MapAnything, a module that automatically determines the geocoordinates of objects using individual images. Utilizing advanced Metric Depth Estimation models, MapAnything calculates geocoordinates based on the object’s distance from the camera, geometric principles, and camera specifications. We detail and validate the module, providing recommendations for automating urban object and incident mapping. Our evaluation measures the accuracy of estimated distances against LiDAR point clouds in urban environments, analyzing performance across distance intervals and semantic areas like roads and vegetation. The module’s effectiveness is demonstrated through practical use cases involving traffic signs and road damage.



1
Introduction

Every city has countless objects that are provided by public funds for efficient, safe, and pleasant communal living. These include all traffic safety installations such as traffic lights, signs, and street lamps, but also recreational facilities such as benches, planting, bus stops, and bicycle stands.
City councils maintain databases for many of these objects, in which they list where the individual objects are located, what type they are, and, if applicable, what condition they are in. There are two fundamental challenges here:
1. The initial creation of the data record, and 2. keeping the database up to date. Many objects can break or even be stolen over time, thus, it is important to carry out regular checks. The opposite can also be the case, for example, when new traffic signs are placed on a construction site.
It may also be of interest to map incidents such as graffiti or road damage. By equipping vehicles like trams, taxis, or garbage trucks with cameras, the necessary images can be recorded to provide an up-to-date overview.

Current solutions for mapping objects have their pitfalls. Some use either additional images from the air or point clouds from LiDAR sensors
[
3
]
[
28
]
[
4
]
, which are not always available as such recordings are expensive and therefore cannot be taken regularly. Other works simply use the coordinates of the camera at the time of recording
[
22
]
, which is insufficient for accurate mapping. Yet other studies include knowledge of the actual size of the object in the calculation
[
6
]
. This approach assumes that the objects searched for have the same size and that this size is known. Finally, there are also approaches that analyze several consecutive images and then find the location using triangular relationships
[
13
]
[
16
]
[
15
]
[
7
]
. This requirement, that an object must be visible in several images, is not always given. We want to develop a universal method that does not assume any further knowledge about the objects and can be applied to any individual image.

Our developed approach is based on models that estimate the distance between the camera and object from a single image in meters, so-called Monocular Metric Depth Estimation Models. Many new models of this type have been developed in recent months and years, and we want to test their suitability for our practical urban application. We examine four state-of-the-art models that have been published in the last year: DepthAnything
[
29
]
, DepthPro
[
2
]
, Metric3D
[
30
]
[
14
]
, and UniDepth
[
23
]
. We compare the created depth images with projections of 3D LiDAR point clouds from the same scene, and especially evaluate errors in different semantic areas such as roads, vegetation, or buildings, and at several distance ranges. We aim to find out for which distances and backgrounds the models perform well and which areas cause difficulties. The inference time is also of interest if a large quantity of street images is to be analyzed.
With the estimated depth and information about the camera’s position and orientation, the actual coordinate of the object can be calculated using angular deviations from the center point and triangular relationships.
After, we will take a closer look at two practical use cases: traffic signs and road damage. For both cases, we assess the accuracy of the localization, i.e., how far the predicted coordinate is from the true coordinate. In the case of traffic signs, we also evaluate how many of the annotated signs were found and compare the predictions to the city council’s database.
The contributions include:

•

a plug-and-play module for estimating an object’s geo-location based on a single image of it,

•

an in-depth analysis of four SOTA monocular metric depth estimation models for their utility in urban road scenes,

•

an evaluation of estimated coordinates in two case studies.

2
Related Work

This Section discusses approaches for estimating the geo-location of urban objects and provides information on Monocular Depth Estimation as it constitutes a core part of MapAnything.

2.1
Mapping Occurrences or Objects

Several research papers have investigated geo-tagging objects through Street-View Imagery for urban applications. The most basic method is to use the geo-coordinate of the camera at the time of recording. For example, Novack et al.
[
22
]
took this approach when they detected graffiti on building façades.
Another possible solution is to include aerial images that provide additional information. Boller et al.
[
3
]
use high-quality aerial orthophotos to develop a mapping method for panorama images. They present a formula for calculating the geo-coordinates that also uses the distance between the object and the camera, but they do not reveal the origin of it. Their evaluation is only of a qualitative nature. Another study using street-level and aerial images tested their method on trees
[
28
]
. The same group performed a follow-up study where they also classified species, and tracked and classified changes
[
4
]
.
A third valid approach is combining the information from multiple images. These approaches assume that objects can be seen in several images and are therefore not suitable for every type of data collection.
One example of this line of research is the work by Hebbalaguppe et al.
[
13
]
. They detect telecommunication inventory in GSV image pairs of consecutive locations to perform triangulation and estimate coordinates.
One research group also used a depth estimation model to retrieve the distance to the object. They estimate the depth and then fuse the distance information from multiple images using Markov Random Fields for Triangulation. In their first work
[
16
]
, they detect traffic lights and telegraph poles in Google Street-View (GSV) images while they look into crowd-sourced image data (Mapillary) in their second study
[
15
]
.
With many available images, one may also use photogrammetry. Cheng et al.
[
7
]
retrieve many crowd-sourced images from one building and create a point cloud of it. The location and extension of the building can be determined from the created point cloud.

Some mapping approaches are object-specific, such as the one in the work of Campbell et al.
[
6
]
. They map traffic signs from GSV images using information about the size of the signs. They determine the distance between the sign and the camera by calculating the ratio between the sign size in physical space and the image space. Then they calculate the angular offsets to the object within the image to obtain the coordinate.
We found another work by Vishnani et al.
[
27
]
that uses a simple regression model and the Haversine formula to map manholes detected in GSV imagery. However, it is not further explained or evaluated.
In our work, we want to determine the geo-coordinate of an urban object without additional recordings or information about it. To the best of our knowledge, there have not been any papers with this objective.

In the fields of autonomous driving and robotics, similar problems occur, yet the objective is to perform 3D object detection from 2D images
[
25
]
[
18
]
. As their focus is on driving, they only train and test those networks for cars. Some of those models also use some form of depth estimation, but are interested in a precise box with correct corners and orientation, which is not that important for finding the geo-coordinate of urban objects. To the best of our knowledge, all these methods use more data than just a single image, such as LiDAR
[
31
]
, Radar
[
19
]
, RGB-D cameras
[
24
]
, multiple images taken simultaneously
[
17
]
or in sequence.

2.2
Monocular Depth Estimation

Depth estimation aims to infer the third dimension of a recorded scene, estimating the distance of objects from the camera. Monocular depth estimation performs this task using a single image, without stereo or multi-view information
[
20
]
.
Depth estimation can be categorized into relative and metric depth estimation. Relative depth estimation focuses on capturing depth relationships between objects in an image, ensuring correct ordering rather than absolute scale. Metric depth estimation, in contrast, aims to predict absolute distances between objects and the camera, requiring scale awareness through additional priors or estimations
[
32
]
.

Early monocular depth estimation models relied on supervised learning, using single-dataset training with consistent camera parameters and recording conditions (e.g., focal length, field of view, and lighting)
[
9
]
[
10
]
[
12
]
. These models often lacked generalization across diverse environments. Later, models such as MiDaS
[
26
]
introduced multi-dataset training, significantly improving cross-dataset performance.

Recently, zero-shot monocular depth models have demonstrated robust generalization across diverse images, handling varying domains without fine-tuning
[
1
]
[
2
]
.
Within the last year, several new models beating previous results were introduced to estimate metric depth: DepthAnything
[
29
]
, Depth Pro
[
2
]
, UniDepth
[
23
]
, and Metric3D
[
30
]
[
14
]
, among others. These models leverage additional constraints, such as learned geometric priors or scale normalization techniques, to predict absolute depth values more accurately. So far, these models have only been evaluated on known benchmarks, but not yet for concrete applicability to street-view images for practical urban use-cases.

Mapping Urban Assets using Street-View Imagery

In this Section, we present the three key steps to map any urban object or occurrence with a single street-view image:

in the image.

2.

Apply the
MapAnything Module

(a)

Estimate the metric depth for every pixel.

(b)

Calculate each object’s coordinate based on the predicted depth at this position and its angular offset from the image’s center.

Remove duplicates
of objects that were recognized in multiple images and/or match with database entries.

3.1
Detect/Segment the desired objects in the image

To map an object, the system needs to know where in the image the object occurs. These image areas can be annotated manually, or they can be predicted by a model. There are freely available models for detecting or segmenting different objects on platforms like HuggingFace. Of course, it is also possible to train a model from scratch or fine-tune it on specific data.
Valuable data sets containing large quantities of annotated urban images include Mapillary Vistas
[
21
]
, Cityscapes
[
8
]
, and A2D2
[
11
]
.

3.2
Our MapAnything Module

To perform the calculations to determine the geocoordinates of an object, two key pieces of information are required: the geocoordinates of the camera at the time of recording and the recording direction, i.e., the deviation from north (yaw). All other camera information is not strictly essential as it can be estimated.
The focal length of the camera can be predicted by models based on an image (like in DepthPro). And the pitch of the camera can be estimated based on visual features in the image (such as the horizon or buildings).
In our work, the distance to the object, measured in meters, is predicted by a depth estimation model. The distance to the object is derived from the center pixel of the bounding box or the weighted average of the depth of the image segment depicting the object. For larger objects or occurrences, a coordinate range can be determined between the outermost points, e.g., for a widespread graffiti painting.
Four recent Monocular Metric Depth Estimation models and their variations will be evaluated in Section
4
.

We set up the following equations following the pinhole camera model.
First, we normalize the pixel coordinates using the focal lengths
f_{x}
and
f_{y}
:

x_{norm}=\frac{x-W/2}{f_{x}},\quad y_{norm}=\frac{y-H/2}{f_{y}}

Next, we determine the angular offsets
\alpha_{x}
and
\alpha_{y}
relative to the camera’s optical axis, effectively converting the image-plane coordinates into real-world angles:

\alpha_{x}=\tan^{-1}(x_{norm}),\quad\alpha_{y}=\tan^{-1}(y_{norm})

We adjust the camera’s yaw and pitch angles by adding the calculated angles:

\theta_{yaw,eff}=\theta_{yaw}+\alpha_{x},\theta_{pitch,eff}=\theta_{pitch}+\alpha_{y}

We can then compute the horizontal distance and the differences in the x (eastward distance) and y (northward distance) direction:

d_{horizontal}=d\cos(\theta_{pitch,eff})

\Delta x=d_{horizontal}\cos(\theta_{yaw,eff})

\Delta y=d_{horizontal}\sin(\theta_{yaw,eff})

Finally, we convert the displacements into changes in latitude and longitude using the Earth’s radius
R
and add them to the car’s coordinates:

\Delta\text{lat}=\frac{\Delta y}{R},\quad\Delta\text{lon}=\frac{\Delta x}{R\cos(\text{lat}_{camera})}

\text{lat}_{object}=\text{lat}_{camera}+\left(\frac{180}{\pi}\right)\cdot\Delta\text{lat}

\text{lon}_{object}=\text{lon}_{camera}+\left(\frac{180}{\pi}\right)\cdot\Delta\text{lon}

Based on these calculations, there is a linear relationship between the deviation in the depth determination and the error of the calculated coordinate.

3.3
Remove duplicates or match with database entries

For each found object or database entry, we check whether there are objects of the same class within a given radius. For each object type, the radius size for the best trade-off between correct matches and error rate should be determined. A simple solution to determine the radius is to analyze the minimal distance between two distinct objects of the same class. When duplicates are found, we take the center of the predicted coordinates as the new prediction. To match predictions with database entries, we can use the calculated angular offsets (added to the recording direction) to find entries of the same class within a reasonable distance. Slight angle deviations should be taken into account.

4
Experiments

We analyze how far off the predicted depth is from the true distance measured with a LiDAR sensor. We test different models, distance ranges, and semantic segments in the images.
Then, we demonstrate the proposed pipeline on two use cases: traffic signs and damages in the road pavement, as they can be found on almost any street.

UniDepth (Version 2) also does not rely on external information and can estimate the camera intrinsic.
It has a ViT backbone and is also available in three sizes.
Metric3D was trained on 18 datasets, including over 16 million images from thousands of different camera types. It always requires the camera intrinsic, and there is a version with a ViT backbone and another with ConvNeXt.

To quantify the deviation between the measured and predicted distances, we calculate the mean absolute error (MAE) in meters (how far off is the estimation) and the absolute relative error (ARE):

AbsRel=\frac{\left|depth_{pred}-depth_{true}\right|}{depth_{true}}

(1)

The ARE normalizes the mean deviation. Large deviations in the background therefore do not influence the metric more than small deviations near the camera.
We do not calculate the errors for pixels of the image that were segmented as one of the people or vehicle classes, as moving objects were removed in the point clouds and would thus lead to errors in the calculations.

4.2
Case Study 1: Traffic Signs

We annotated an urban region including 4.74km of road with 261 signs (of which 23 were temporarily mounted) and evaluated our approach with high-resolution Cyclomedia images (786 images from early 2024) as well as crowd-sourced images from Mapillary (231 images from October 2023 to April 2024). The company Cyclomedia was commissioned for the task of street recording, the images cover 100% of the streets (according to OpenStreetMap) while the images from Mapillary only cover 28% in the same period. We analyzed, according to the driving direction, how many of the 261 signs should be theoretically visible, which yielded 258 for Cyclomedia (all of the temporary signs) and 86 for the Mapillary images (including 23 of the temporary signs). A key challenge for crowd-sourced data is GPS inaccuracy; camera coordinates deviate by several meters from actual coordinates, introducing significant location errors for objects. For twelve hand-measured images, we found a mean 5.5-meter deviation.

5
Discussion

We discussed the results with an employee of our local administration. We were told that it is not possible to assess the maximum deviation for all objects overall.
Instances of different object types have different distances between them and varying numbers of classes (e.g., signs have many classes and short distances, transmission masts have few classes but long distances). More classes and bigger distances make the distinction and the assignment to entries in a database easier.
However, our contact person confirmed that even measured object coordinates can deviate by up to five meters. Yet geolocations in current databases are very helpful. From this, we can conclude that our MapAnything module should be used for objects with an estimated distance of less than 20 meters, as the deviations in this range were always less than five meters in our evaluations.
Future work should assess how image resolution, lighting, and weather affect the accuracy of an object’s estimated geolocation.

The box plots in Fig.
10
display the error in predicted position (y-axis) as a function of distance to the camera (x-axis) for four depth estimation methods. The plots provide insights into the accuracy and variability of predictions across distance intervals.
The first plot (DepthAnything-B) shows a non-monotonic trend: errors start low, peak at mid-ranges ( 7m for 15-20m), and decrease for farther distances ( 4.5m at 25-30m).
The second plot (DepthPro) shows a consistent increase in median error with distance increasing, rising steadily from 2.3m (5-10m) to 5m (25-30m). Variability grows predictably with distance, reflecting stable performance.
The third plot (Metric3D) exhibits higher errors overall, with a median of 9m even at close ranges. Errors fluctuate across distances, peaking at 20-25m (9.5m median) before slightly decreasing at 25-30m. This irregular trend suggests challenges in specific ranges.
The fourth plot (UniDepth) resembles the plot for UniDepth, with errors starting at 2.3m for 5-10m and reaching 5.3m at 25-30m. The interquartile range (IQR) and whisker lengths also grow with distance, indicating increasing variability.
Overall, UniDepth and DepthPro demonstrate predictable behavior, while Metric3D and DepthAnything show irregularities.
