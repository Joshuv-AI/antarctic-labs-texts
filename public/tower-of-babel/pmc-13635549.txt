Evaluation of adherence to national regulations for documentation of limitations of life-sustaining treatments after a limited intervention at a Swedish hospital
Authors: Eva Piscator, Charlotte Ekestam, Lena Bremer, Umut Heilborn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimThe aim of this study was to improve adherence to the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare’s regulations for documenting decisions regarding Limitations of Life-Sustaining Treatments at Capio St. Göran’s Hospital in Stockholm following a limited intervention.MethodsThis was a before-and-after-study. The intervention took place in June 2023 and consisted of three parts: 1. Implementation of a modified digital medical record form for documentation of decisions regarding limitations of Life-Sustaining Treatments. 2. A brief educational intervention for physicians at the hospital. 3. Publication of an updated hospital protocol for decisions on limitations of Life-Sustaining Treatments. We analysed 100 randomly selected documentations of limitation of Life-Sustaining Treatments in adult patients before the intervention, and 98 after the intervention. Adherence to individual mandatory criteria and overall adherence to the regulatory requirements was evaluated.ResultsOverall adherence to all documentation regulations increased from 1% to 63% (p < 0.01). Grounds for the decision were well documented both pre- and post-intervention (99% and 97%, p > 0.99). All other individual criteria improved: consultation with another licenced caregiver (65–89%, p < 0.01), dialogue with the patient or motivation why not (24–89%, p < 0.01), information to the family or motivation why not (23–74%, p < 0.01), the view of the patient (14–86%, p < 0.01) and the view of the family (4–70%, p < 0.01).ConclusionA moderate intervention in terms of cost and work effort for the organisation can significantly improve adherence to regulations regarding documentation of limitations of Life-Sustaining Treatments, although further improvement is needed.

Introduction

The legislation and clinical practises regarding decisions for limitations of Life-Sustaining Treatments (LST) vary greatly worldwide.1, 2, 3 Previous publications have shown variable, but generally low, adherence to both ethical principles emphasising patient autonomy with shared decision making, as well as low adherence to regulatory requirements in several countries, including Sweden.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12

The Swedish regulations of the National Board of Health and Welfare13 require that the physician responsible for the limitation of LST decision, documents the following mandatory headings:

1. The grounds for the decision, 2. Consultation with another licenced healthcare professional, 3. Dialogue with the patient, or a motivation as to why a dialogue has not taken place, 4. Communication with the patient’s family, or a motivation as to why a dialogue has not taken place, 5. The view of the patient and 6. The view of the family in the matter. Documentation of a motivation as to why communication with the patient́s family did not take place is not mandatory by regulations but was considered significant and therefore added as mandatory to the modified medical record form.

In 2019, a pilot study was conducted at Capio St. Göran’s Hospital, Stockholm, Sweden showing poor adherence to the regulations (not published). The aim of this study, therefore, was to improve adherence to regulations for the documentation of limitation of LST decisions at this hospital, by implementing a modified medical record form, conducting a brief educational intervention, and updating the hospital protocol for decisions on limitation of LST.

Methods

Study design, population and data collection

This before-and-after study included adults with documentation of limitation of LST decisions at Capio St. Göran’s Hospital, Stockholm, Sweden. The hospital in central Stockholm is a general and emergency hospital with a broad spectrum of medical specialties providing both acute and elective care with public funding. It has a catchment area of approximately 420,000 inhabitants and had about 90,000 visits to the emergency department in 2025. It holds medical wards for Emergency medicine, Internal medicine, Neurology and Stroke, Cardiology, Oncology, Surgery, Urology, Orthopaedics, Anaesthesia-and Intensive care and Obstetrics and Neonatology.

Sample size calculation was based on a pilot study performed in 2019 with analysis of 50 retrospectively and randomly selected adult decisions regarding limitation of LST. Results showed variable but overall low adherence to the regulations. The power calculation was based on the assumption that the intervention would increase adherence to regulations by 20 percentage points. The study population was identified using the hospital’s IT systems (Insight and Power BI) by extracting all medical record forms for documentation of limitation of LST decisions. Inclusion criteria were patients ≥18 years receiving inpatient care in 2022 (pre-intervention), and in the last quarter (Q4) of 2023 (post-intervention). Information regarding age, sex, and the medical clinic responsible for the decision was retracted by the hospital’s quality controller based on the IT systems. Since analysis of the forms required manual interpretation, it was not feasible to include all limitation of LST decisions that were made. We therefore randomly selected and analysed 100 documentations pre-, and 100 post-intervention for analysis of adherence to the legislation in the documentation.

Intervention

The intervention consisted of three parts, all carried through during June 2023: modification of the digital medical record form for documentation of decisions regarding limitation of LST, a brief educational intervention for physicians at the hospital and, finally, the publication of an updated hospital protocol for decisions on limitation of LST.

The digital forms used before and after intervention are shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 2. The modified digital medical record form was designed to more clearly indicate which documentation is mandated by regulations. The headings of the form were based on the legal requirements. The main change was that the form could not be signed unless all mandatory fields we filled. Some headings had predefined selections combined with free-text areas, whereas others were free-text only. The form could not be signed unless all mandatory headings were completed. Mandatory free-text headings required the entry of at least one character. The modified medical form adapted to the physician’s input. For instance, when filling the field “has information been provided to the family”: checking “yes” opens a new field “to whom and when” while checking “no” opens the field “reason for not informing family”. The subsequent adaptive questions are mandatory according to regulations. The modified form also has the option “no limitation of LST”. This field is new and in accordance with the regulations that dictate that LST decisions must be reconsidered at every hospital admission and previous limitations can be revoked. The pre-intervention form contained only a few of the headings mandated by regulations, and the headings did not clearly indicate which documentation is required by law. The educational intervention consisted of a lecture (20–30 min) with physicians at staff meetings for each medical department separately. The lecture informed on the national regulations, the results of the pilot study, the modified medical record form and the updated hospital protocol. Attendance to the lectures was not monitored.

The updated hospital protocol for decisions on limitation of LST contained clinical practise, information about legislation and regulations for documentation, as opposed to the former protocol which focused on clinical practise only.

The content of free-text entries was analysed pre-intervention as the medical record form did not have clear headings related to what was obliged by law, and the information could be written in free text. As long as responses were relevant to the content of the regulations, they were considered valid. Post-intervention, free-text analysis was restricted to those headings demanding free-text. As long as the response was relevant to the heading, it was considered valid. Cases of uncertainty was discussed with a senior researcher until consensus was reached.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as the median and interquartile range. Proportions were reported with 95% confidence intervals calculated using the Wilson score method. Binary variables were compared using Chi-squared, or Fisher’s exact test in case of 5 or less values per cell. Significance tests were two-sided with a significance level of 0.05. Random selection of patients was conducted with Microsoft Excel®. Analyses were performed using Stata 13 for Windows (Stata Corp, College Station, TX).

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (2022-04985-01).

Adherence to regulations for the documentation of limitation of LST decisions

Complete adherence to all mandatory documentation requirements improved significantly from 1% pre- to 63% post-intervention (p < 0.01, 95% CI 0.2–5.4% and 53.2–71.8% respectively), as illustrated in Fig. 3. Grounds for the decision was well documented both pre-and post-intervention (99% (95% CI 94.6–99.8%) and 97% (95% CI 91.5–99%) respectively, p-value > 0.99). Documentation of consultation with another licenced caregiver increased from 65% to 89% (p < 0.01, 95% CI 55.3–73.6% and 81.4–93.7% respectively). Regarding the documentation of a dialogue with the patient, and information to the family, a more than three-fold improvement was seen (24% (95% CI 16.7–33.2%) to 89% (95% CI 81.4–93.7%) and 23% (95% CI 15.8–32.2%) to74% (95% CI 64.6–81.6%) respectively, p < 0.01 for both). Starting from lower baseline values, even higher improvements was seen for documentation of the view of the patient and family (14% (95% CI 8.5–22%) to 86% (95% CI 77.9–91.5%) and 4% (95% CI 1.6–9.8%) to 70% (95% CI 60.4–78.1%) respectively, p < 0.01 for both). Discussion

This study has shown that adherence to the National Board of Health and Welfare’s regulations regarding documentation of decisions to forgo life-sustaining treatment significantly improved with an intervention consisting of implementation of a modified medical record form for the documentation of decisions regarding limitation of LST, a brief educational intervention for physicians at the hospital and publication of an updated hospital protocol for limitation of LST decisions.

A moderate work effort was required by the IT department to modify the previous digital form within the already existing system in accordance with our instructions. In addition, the process of filling a digital form regarding LST was already familiar to the physicians of our hospital. We therefore conclude that a moderate intervention in terms of cost and labour can significantly improve adherence to regulations regarding limitations of LST.

Complete adherence to all documentation regulations was surprisingly low before the intervention (1%), even lower than 10% shown in a previous study from a University hospital in the same region.7 In view of that, post-intervention levels of 63% could be considered a substantial achievement. However, much improvement remains before adherence to the regulatory requirements can be considered fulfilled. The practise of documenting the grounds for limitation of LST decisions appears to be well established, and was higher than the 89% shown in the previously mentioned study.7 Documentation of consultation with another licenced caregiver increased from moderate (65%) to seemingly acceptable (89%). Fulfilment of these two parameters is important for the transparency of the decision as to communicating the medical rationale on which the decision is based. They provide relevant information for re-assessing the decision should the patient’s medical condition change and constitute a basis for renewed dialogue with the patient and family. As previous studies have shown that decisions on limitation of LSTs are complex and prediction of outcome is perceived as difficult,14, 15, 16 clarifying the medical ground for the decision and consultation with a licenced colleague serve as control functions of the individual physicians' assessments.

Swedish legislation emphases the patient’s right to autonomy and shared decision making.13, 17, 18, 19 In this study, all parts involving documentation of a dialogue with the patient and family increased significantly to levels that could seem acceptable, at least as a starting point. If this increase was an effect of an increase in high-quality dialogues being conducted, and not just that the documentation of the dialogue increased, it would represent a considerable positive impact for patients and families. Subsequent studies may investigate this issue further.

Limitations of the study include that we assessed the effect based on a small sample rather than all documentations and we did not statistically analyse representativity.

A further limitation of the study is that we cannot separate the effects of the three different components of the intervention. Surveys among Swedish physicians have shown a lack of knowledge about the National Board’s regulations, as well as lack of knowledge of the journal templates in their medical record systems.4, 6 It is possible that our interventions meet the knowledge gap in these topics. However, previous studies have shown conflicting results on the effectiveness of interventions, including education, on limitation of LST dialogues and their documentation.12, 20, 21, 22 Factors indicating that the educational intervention in our study was unlikely to have a major effect on the results were that only physicians on duty were present at the lecture, and that it was not repeated on several occasions. Neither do we believe that the publication of an updated and more informative hospital protocol for decisions on limitation of LST had a major effect, since the experience of the authors is that only a limited number of physicians read the local protocolss.

A systematic review conducted by Field et al.,22 revealed that with interventions that aimed at enhancing the appropriate use and outcomes of DNACPR decisions, standardised documentation contributed to improving both the frequency and quality of DNACPR decision documentation. Another study by Haire et al.,23 evaluated a national intervention in Wales with the aim of enhancing documentation of DNACPR decisions. The intervention consisted of a new DNACPR form (in paper) and a more extensive educational intervention. The new DNACPR form had mandatory text boxes for documentation of the grounds for the decision and discussions with patients and significant others. The study showed improved written documentation, and the authors attributed the results to the combined effect of the new mandatory form and the educational intervention. Our interpretation of the results in our study is that the major contributing factor was the modified medical record form, which was designed to clearly indicate which documentation is mandated by regulations and impossible to sign digitally without filling all mandatory sections. The educational effort and publication of a more informative hospital protocol possibly provided additional support.

Another limitation of this study was that before the intervention, a dialogue with the patient and family could have been documented in the medical record outside of the form (especially paragraphs without separate headings), resulting in falsely high improvement in adherence to regulations. This risk however is considered low, as the hospital, since many years, clearly has formulated that a limitation of LST is only valid if documented in the specific medical form designed for that purpose.

We did not follow up whether the improvement in adherence lasted over time. While the educational intervention may fade over time, the modification of the digital form, with mandatory entries, remain and we therefore speculate that the major part of the improvement persists, although this has to be further studied.

We hypothesise that the nature of this study makes it generalisable to other health systems using electronic medical record systems, and preferably those that enable forms with mandatory headings.

Conclusion

This study has shown that a limited intervention was associated with a significant improvement in adherence to the National Board of Health and Welfare’s regulations for the documentation of decisions to refrain life-sustaining treatments. Despite this, there is room for further improvement.
