Prognostic and clinicopathologic significance of FGFR mRNA and protein overexpression in colorectal cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xinke Chai, Changsheng Li, Yuchen Luo, Qian Shen, Shifeng Wu, Qiulin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide. Fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs), a family of receptor tyrosine kinases, regulate key cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, and angiogenesis. Although dysregulated FGFR signaling has been implicated in cancer progression, the clinical significance of FGFR overexpression and its association with prognosis in CRC remain unclear, with inconsistent findings across FGFR subtypes, detection methods, and cutoff thresholds. Therefore, this meta-analysis aimed to systematically evaluate the association between FGFR protein/mRNA overexpression and prognosis and clinicopathological characteristics in CRC patients.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA guidelines, we searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from inception through March 12, 2026, for observational cohort studies evaluating the association between FGFR overexpression and survival outcomes in histologically confirmed CRC patients. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Primary outcomes were overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free survival (PFS); secondary outcomes included vascular invasion, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis. Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed.ResultsThirteen studies involving 3,005 CRC patients were included, with a median NOS score of 7 (range, 5-8), indicating high methodological quality. FGFR overexpression was significantly associated with poorer OS (HR =1.95, 95% CI: 1.67–2.28) and DFS (HR =1.47, 95% CI: 1.05–2.07). Subgroup analyses showed that adverse prognostic associations were predominantly observed for FGFR2 (HR =2.79) and FGFR4 (HR =1.96) overexpression, whereas FGFR1 and FGFR3 overexpression showed no significant association with survival. Overall FGFR overexpression was significantly correlated with vascular invasion (OR =2.59, 95% CI: 1.12–5.97) but not with lymph node or distant metastasis.ConclusionsThe overall pooled analysis demonstrated that FGFR overexpression is significantly associated with poorer OS, DFS, and an increased risk of vascular invasion in CRC patients. Subgroup analyses further revealed that the adverse prognostic associations were particularly pronounced in cases of FGFR2 and FGFR4 overexpression, whereas FGFR1 and FGFR3 overexpression did not exhibit statistically significant associations with survival outcomes. These findings suggest that FGFR overexpression, especially involving specific FGFR subtypes, may represent a crucial molecular feature linked to CRC progression and prognosis. Further prospective studies are necessary to validate the prognostic relevance of FGFR subtype-specific overexpression and to explore its potential implications for individualized CRC management.Keywords: Colorectal cancer (CRC), fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR), prognosis, meta-analysis

Highlight box

Key findings

• This meta-analysis of 13 studies [3,005 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients] demonstrated that fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) overexpression was significantly associated with worse overall survival [OS, hazard ratio =1.95, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.67–2.28] and disease-free survival (DFS, HR =1.47, 95% CI: 1.05–2.07) in CRC.

• Subgroup analysis revealed that FGFR2 and FGFR4 overexpression exerted particularly prominent adverse prognostic effects on OS, while FGFR1 and FGFR3 showed no statistically significant prognostic value.

• FGFR overexpression was correlated with an increased risk of vascular invasion (odds ratio =2.59, 95% CI: 1.12–5.97), but not with lymph node or distant metastasis.

What is known and what is new?

• FGFR signaling is dysregulated in CRC and contributes to tumor progression, but the prognostic value of FGFR overexpression and its correlation with clinicopathological features remain inconsistent across individual studies.

• This study systematically synthesizes the available evidence to demonstrate that FGFR overexpression, particularly FGFR2 and FGFR4, independently predicts unfavorable survival outcomes in CRC. Notably, FGFR overexpression is also significantly correlated with vascular invasion, a key adverse pathological feature, thereby elucidating the subtype-specific biological and prognostic relevance of FGFR in CRC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• FGFR2/4 overexpression can serve as a reliable prognostic biomarker for risk stratification in CRC patients.

• These findings support the development of FGFR-targeted therapies, particularly for CRC patients with FGFR2/4 overexpression and vascular invasion, to improve clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates. It ranks as the third most commonly diagnosed malignant tumor and is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths. According to global statistics, over 1.9 million new CRC cases are diagnosed annually, resulting in approximately 930,000 deaths (1). Although advances in multimodal treatment strategies, including surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, have improved outcomes for some patients, significant heterogeneity in prognosis remains a major challenge in clinical practice. Even among patients with the same histological stage, survival outcomes can vary considerably due to differences in the molecular characteristics of tumors (2-4). Thus, conventional pathological staging alone may not fully capture the biological diversity of CRC or meet the increasing demands of precision medicine. Identifying and integrating clinically relevant molecular markers may provide additional insights for risk stratification and prognostic assessment in CRC patients.

Numerous molecular targets have been explored for CRC risk stratification, among which the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) family stands out due to its central roles in cell proliferation, differentiation, and angiogenesis (5). Basic research confirms that aberrant FGFR signaling drives CRC progression by remodeling the tumor microenvironment and rewiring cellular metabolism (6); however, its clinical prognostic value remains unclear (7).

Among various FGFR molecular alterations, mRNA and protein overexpression are the most frequently reported forms in clinical CRC samples. However, the prognostic significance of FGFR overexpression remains incompletely understood, with inconsistent findings reported across different clinical cohorts. For FGFR1, Kwak et al. (8) reported that FGFR1 mRNA overexpression was not significantly associated with overall survival (OS) in a cohort of 291 CRC patients, while an earlier study by Sato et al. (9) identified a significant association between increased FGFR1 expression, liver metastasis, and shorter survival outcomes. Similar discrepancies have been observed for FGFR4. Several independent cohorts have suggested that FGFR4 protein overexpression is associated with an unfavorable prognosis, whereas other studies have failed to demonstrate a significant relationship between FGFR4 expression and survival outcomes (10-12). The heterogeneity observed among these findings may be attributed to various clinical and methodological factors. These include variations in patient stages and treatment regimens, differences in detection methods and expression cutoff thresholds, inconsistent adjustment strategies for clinical covariates, disparities in patient populations, variable follow-up durations, and limited comparisons across FGFR subtypes within individual studies (11,12). Due to the inability of individual studies to independently isolate and quantify the impact of these potential confounding factors on prognostic estimates, a comprehensive understanding of the clinical significance of FGFR overexpression in CRC remains insufficient.

In summary, existing studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the prognostic significance of FGFR overexpression, and a comprehensive synthesis of available evidence remains lacking. To address this knowledge gap, this meta-analysis systematically integrates FGFR overexpression data across different subtypes and evaluates the association between overall FGFR overexpression and survival outcomes, including OS and disease-free survival (DFS), in CRC patients at the pan-subtype level. Furthermore, we investigate the relationship between overall FGFR overexpression and key clinicopathological features, including vascular invasion, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis, to further characterize its potential role in CRC progression. Subgroup analyses are subsequently performed to determine whether prognostic associations vary among individual FGFR subtypes. Through this comprehensive analysis, we aim to establish a clearer understanding of the prognostic relevance of FGFR overexpression in CRC, identify subtype-specific patterns underlying these associations, and provide a more comprehensive evidence framework for further investigation of FGFR overexpression and its clinical implications in CRC. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (13) (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0882/rc).

Methods

Study registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251011032).

Literature search and selection criteria

A systematic literature search was conducted across four major electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science Core Collection, covering the period from database inception to March 12, 2026. The search strategy was independently executed by two reviewers. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, database-specific controlled vocabulary, and free-text keywords were combined to identify studies examining the prognostic significance of FGFR expression in CRC. The search strategy comprised two primary concepts: colorectal cancer-related terms (e.g., “Colorectal Neoplasms”, “Colorectal Cancer”, and “Colorectal Carcinoma”) and FGFR-related terms (e.g., “Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor”, “FGFR”, and “FGF Receptor”), which were integrated using Boolean operators. Furthermore, the reference lists of all included studies were manually reviewed to identify additional eligible publications. The complete search strategies for each database are detailed in Table S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were formulated according to the PICOS framework (14).

Inclusion criteria comprised: (I) Population: adult patients with histologically confirmed CRC (colon or rectal cancer) were eligible regardless of disease stage or treatment modality. (II) Exposure: the exposure of interest was the overexpression of FGFR at either the mRNA or protein level, as assessed through validated detection methods such as immunohistochemistry, in situ hybridization, or quantitative molecular assays. FGFR gene amplification, activating mutations, fusion/rearrangement events, and other genomic alterations were regarded as biologically distinct entities and were excluded from consideration unless separate expression-level data were available. (III) Comparator: patients were categorized based on the expression criteria established in each original study, which included classifications of high versus low FGFR expression groups. (IV) Outcomes: the primary outcomes of this study were survival endpoints, specifically OS, DFS, disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free survival (PFS). The secondary outcomes encompassed various clinicopathological features, such as vascular invasion, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis.

Exclusion criteria comprised: (I) Review articles, conference abstracts, letters, commentaries, case reports, and single-arm studies without appropriate comparison groups were excluded. (II) Duplicate publications were excluded. When multiple studies reported data from the same cohort, only the study with the largest sample size, longest follow-up duration, or most comprehensive available information was retained. (III) Studies lacking prognostic outcomes or sufficient survival-related data were excluded. (IV) Non-English articles, animal studies, in vitro experiments, and other non-clinical studies were excluded.

Data extraction

Two investigators independently conducted study selection and data extraction utilizing a standardized dual-reviewer system. A predefined data extraction template was employed to gather the following variables from the included studies: first author, publication year, country of origin, sample size, tumor location, tumor differentiation grade, TNM staging, lymph node metastasis status, distant metastasis status, patient age and gender composition, adjuvant treatment regimens, detection assay methodology, definitions and cut-off criteria for FGFR overexpression, survival endpoints, effect estimates [hazard ratios (HRs) or odds ratios (ORs)] with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and other clinicopathological correlates. For studies that did not directly report effect estimates and 95% CIs, survival data were reconstructed using established Kaplan-Meier curve digitization methods as described by Tierney et al. (15). For each survival outcome, multivariable-adjusted effect estimates were preferentially extracted when available; otherwise, univariable effect estimates were utilized. When multiple time points were reported, the most recent follow-up data were selected. For other extracted variables, any missing or unclear information was recorded as ‘NR’ (not reported). All extracted data underwent cross-verification between reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through consensus or third-party adjudication.

Literature quality assessment

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was utilized to assess the methodological quality of the included cohort and case-control studies (16). In parallel, the revised Cochrane Risk-of-Bias Tool 2 (RoB 2) was employed to evaluate the risk of bias in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (17). Two independent researchers performed a quality evaluation of all eligible studies. Any discrepancies were resolved through group discussion, and unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third senior reviewer. Based on the NOS scores, studies were categorized into high-quality (NOS ≥7) and lower-quality (NOS <7) groups, following the criteria commonly adopted in previous meta-analyses (18,19). Detailed NOS scoring results for all included studies are provided in Table S2.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity among the included studies, subgroup analyses were performed based on relevant clinical and methodological characteristics when sufficient data were available. These analyses aimed to investigate whether differences in FGFR subtypes, detection methods, patient characteristics, study design, and methodological quality contributed to variations in the pooled estimates. When substantial heterogeneity (I2≥50%) remained and could not be adequately explained, the results were interpreted with consideration of potential clinical and methodological differences among studies rather than relying solely on a single pooled estimate. Furthermore, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding individual studies to evaluate the robustness and stability of the pooled results.

Statistical analysis

For each outcome, studies were included in the corresponding synthesis when extractable effect estimates were available. For survival outcomes, such as OS, DFS, PFS, DSS, HRs with corresponding 95% CIs were extracted. For dichotomous clinicopathological outcomes, including lymph node metastasis, vascular invasion, and distant metastasis, ORs with 95% CIs were calculated based on available event data. Pooled effect estimates along with their corresponding 95% CIs were computed using the inverse variance weighting method in Review Manager 5.3. Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. A fixed-effects model was employed when heterogeneity was deemed low (I2<50% and P>0.10), while a random-effects model was utilized when substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2≥50% or P≤0.10). The statistical significance of pooled effect estimates was evaluated using two-tailed Z-tests, with a significance threshold set at α=0.05. Publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry using Review Manager 5.3 and further assessed using Egger’s weighted regression test implemented in STATA 17.0. A P value <0.10 in Egger’s test was interpreted as indicative of statistically significant publication bias, in accordance with recommendations for meta-analyses involving a limited number of studies.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework (20,21). This assessment considered five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The certainty of evidence was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low according to the GRADE criteria. Two reviewers conducted the assessment independently, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

Results

Literature search and study selection

The systematic search initially identified 2,903 potentially relevant records, which were managed using EndNote X20.1. After removing 527 duplicates, 2,376 unique records were screened by title and abstract. This step excluded 2,318 articles that were either (I) irrelevant to the prognostic role of FGFR in CRC, or (II) restricted to preclinical models (cell lines or animal studies). Subsequently, 58 articles underwent full-text eligibility assessment. Following independent evaluation by two investigators, 45 studies were excluded, including several near-miss studies that met most inclusion criteria but were excluded for key reasons: (I) assessment of gene amplification or other genetic alterations rather than protein overexpression (22,23); (II) unavailable survival data that could not be retrieved from the corresponding authors despite at least two contact attempts (24,25). Finally, 13 studies were included for quantitative synthesis. The detailed literature selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Characteristics of included studies

Following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, 13 studies were deemed eligible for inclusion in this meta-analysis. All included studies were retrospective cohort studies; consequently, NOS was utilized for the assessment of methodological quality. Notably, 2 publications reported 2 independent cohorts each, resulting in a total of 3,005 CRC patients across various clinical stages and geographic regions. All included studies reported survival outcomes, including OS, DFS, DSS, and PFS, alongside clinicopathological parameters. Survival associations were evaluated using HRs, while clinicopathological correlations were assessed using ORs. The median NOS score of the included studies was 7 (range, 5–8), with 10 studies achieving a score ≥7, indicating generally good methodological quality. Survival outcome

Among the 13 eligible studies, 10 studies (including a study reporting two independent cohorts) investigated the association between FGFR overexpression and OS in CRC patients. Meta-analysis showed low heterogeneity among studies (I2=36%, P=0.11); therefore, a fixed-effects model was applied for pooled analysis. The pooled results demonstrated that FGFR overexpression was significantly associated with worse OS (HR =1.95, 95% CI: 1.67–2.28, P<0.001) (Figure 2A).

Prognostic impact of FGFR overexpression in colorectal cancer. (A) Combined HR for OS with corresponding 95% CI forest plot; (B) combined HR for DFS with associated 95% CI forest plot. CI, confidence interval; DFS, disease-free survival; FGFR, fibroblast growth factor receptor; HR, hazard ratio; OS, overall survival; SE, standard error.

3 of the 13 included studies evaluated the association between FGFR overexpression and DFS in CRC patients. No significant heterogeneity was observed among studies (I2=0%, P=0.61), and a fixed-effects model was subsequently applied. The pooled analysis indicated that patients with FGFR overexpression had significantly worse DFS compared with those with lower FGFR expression (HR =1.47, 95% CI: 1.05–2.07, P=0.03) (Figure 2B).

Among the included studies, only one study reported PFS and DSS as outcome measures. Due to the limited number of studies, quantitative synthesis was not performed for these endpoints, and descriptive analyses were therefore conducted. The PFS study included 382 surgically treated CRC patients and demonstrated that FGFR1 overexpression was associated with an increased risk of disease progression or mortality (HR =1.86, 95% CI: 1.19–2.90). The DSS study included 172 rectal cancer patients receiving neoadjuvant radiotherapy, with FGFR2 expression assessed in archival formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens. The results showed that FGFR2 overexpression was associated with increased disease-specific mortality (HR =4.99, 95% CI: 2.02–12.2).

Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis

Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method demonstrated the stability of the pooled estimates. Sequential exclusion of individual studies resulted in no substantial changes in the magnitude or direction of the pooled HRs for OS or DFS, indicating the robustness of the synthesized results.

To further evaluate the consistency of the primary findings, subgroup analyses were performed among studies investigating the association between FGFR overexpression and OS (Table 2). Stratification by FGFR subtype showed that FGFR1 overexpression (1 study: HR =1.86, P=0.08) and FGFR3 overexpression (1 study: HR =1.35, P=0.21) exhibited trends toward worse OS, although these associations did not reach statistical significance. FGFRL1 overexpression was significantly associated with worse OS in the single available study (HR =3.12, P=0.02). Analysis of FGFR2 (2 studies; fixed-effects model) demonstrated a significant association between FGFR2 overexpression and worse OS (HR =2.79, 95% CI: 1.44–5.41, P=0.002), with no observed heterogeneity (I2=0%). The FGFR4 subgroup (5 studies; random-effects model) also showed a significant association between FGFR4 overexpression and worse OS (HR =2.01, 95% CI: 1.68–2.41, P<0.001), although substantial heterogeneity was observed among studies (I2=63%). Further stratified analyses according to methodological quality and sample size demonstrated that FGFR overexpression remained significantly associated with worse OS across different NOS score categories and sample size strata. However, the association did not reach statistical significance among studies using ISH-based detection methods or among studies conducted in Japan. To assess the potential influence of different statistical adjustment strategies, additional subgroup analyses were performed according to the source of HR estimates. FGFR overexpression remained significantly associated with worse OS in both univariable analyses (HR =1.46, 95% CI: 1.08–1.99, P=0.015) and multivariable analyses (HR =2.33, 95% CI: 1.91–2.83, P<0.001). Notably, the association remained statistically significant after adjustment for clinicopathological factors, suggesting that the observed relationship between FGFR overexpression and OS was not fully explained by measured clinical characteristics.

Assessment of publication bias

For OS, Egger’s test suggested potential publication bias (t=2.48, df=9, P=0.02), which was accompanied by asymmetry in the funnel plot. In contrast, no apparent evidence of publication bias was observed for DFS (Egger’s test: t=0.16, df=2, P=0.88), lymph node metastasis (t=0.52, df=11, P=0.62), vascular invasion (t=-0.20, df=4, P=0.85), or distant metastasis (t=2.03, df=9, P=0.09) (Figure 4).

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE framework (21), considering the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. For OS, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate, with potential publication bias (Egger’s P=0.02) and some degree of heterogeneity (I2=36%) contributing to downgrading considerations. For DFS, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate due to the limited number of included studies (n=3) and imprecision of the estimates, despite low heterogeneity. For vascular invasion, the certainty of evidence was rated as low because of the limited number of events and wide CI (OR =2.59, 95% CI: 1.12–5.97).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the association between FGFR overexpression and clinical outcomes in CRC through a comprehensive meta-analysis. The pooled results demonstrated that FGFR overexpression was significantly associated with worse OS and DFS (HR =1.95, 95% CI: 1.67–2.28; HR =1.47, 95% CI: 1.05–2.07), suggesting that FGFR overexpression may represent a molecular feature associated with unfavorable disease outcomes in CRC. These findings are consistent with accumulating evidence from both basic and clinical studies indicating that dysregulated FGFR signaling may contribute to tumor progression. Previous studies have demonstrated that FGFR family members can activate downstream RAS-MAPK, PI3K-AKT, and STAT signaling pathways through multiple mechanisms, including gene amplification, activating mutations, and autocrine/paracrine ligand stimulation (26,29,36-38). At the metabolic level, FGFR signaling has been reported to promote aerobic glycolysis by enhancing the transcriptional activity of c-MYC and HIF-1α, thereby supporting the biosynthetic requirements of tumor cells (7,10,39,40). In terms of cellular stress adaptation, FGFR activation may inhibit ULK1 complex phosphorylation to suppress autophagy initiation and enhance BCL-2 binding to Beclin-1, thereby reducing autophagic cell death and contributing to chemotherapy resistance (41). Furthermore, downstream FGFR signaling components may enhance tumor cell resistance to ferroptosis through upregulation of GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression, providing survival advantages under oxidative stress conditions (42,43). Collectively, these molecular mechanisms may contribute to enhanced tumor cell proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, angiogenic activity, and metastatic potential, providing a biological rationale for the observed association between FGFR overexpression and adverse clinical outcomes in CRC.

Subgroup analysis revealed that FGFR2 (HR =2.79) and FGFR4 (HR =2.01) exhibited significantly higher prognostic value for poor outcomes compared to other subtypes, while the prognostic significance of FGFR1 and FGFR3 did not reach statistical significance. This discrepancy primarily arises from the biological differences among subtypes concerning signaling pathway preferences and microenvironment regulation. First, the FGF ligands bound by different subtypes vary, resulting in distinct intensity and functional emphasis within their activated downstream signaling networks (e.g., MAPK/ERK, PI3K/AKT, STAT3), ultimately influencing cell fate regulation (44). Second, FGFR2 can actively remodel the immune microenvironment by inducing PD-L1 expression and recruiting M2-type macrophages to establish an immunosuppressive state, whereas the immunomodulatory capabilities of other subtypes remain unclear (29,38). Furthermore, certain subtypes (e.g., FGFR2) play crucial roles throughout the entire tumor progression from primary to metastatic stages, while others, such as FGFR3, primarily exert key functions during late metastatic phases (31,45). Additionally, FGFR2 has been identified as a resistance driver gene in anti-EGFR therapy (46); however, the roles of other FGFR family members in this process remain to be elucidated, which also impacts their prognostic value in specific treatment populations.

Further subgroup analyses of the clinicopathological characteristics revealed that FGFR overexpression was significantly associated with vascular invasion (OR=2.59), but there was no statistically significant correlation with lymph node metastasis or distant metastasis. These findings are highly consistent with existing research conclusions. Studies indicate that the FGFR and VEGF pathways engage in multi-level crosstalk during tumor angiogenesis and progression, forming a synergistic pro-angiogenic network (47). Specifically, FGFR activation can upregulate VEGF expression, creating an autocrine amplification loop that preferentially mediates local tumor invasion and intravascular colonization by promoting endothelial cell activation and degradation of the basement membrane (48).

Despite corroborating previous findings, several unresolved controversies remain. For instance, while FGFR1 overexpression has been linked to increased distant metastasis risk and shortened OS in CRC across multiple investigations (9,49), Our meta-analysis did not confirm the independent prognostic significance of FGFR1 in predicting OS. Additionally, some investigations have demonstrated that FGFR4 overexpression correlates with improved survival outcomes in CRC (50), a finding that starkly contrasts with our results. Discrepancies observed in FGFR prognostic significance may arise from multiple interacting factors. Marked methodological heterogeneity exists in FGFR detection, with studies employing diverse platforms such as IHC semi-quantitative scoring systems and qRT-PCR. Inherent differences in sensitivity and quantification criteria across these methods lead to inconsistent threshold definitions between studies. Substantial variability also exists in study populations regarding ethnic composition, clinical staging, and treatment protocols—especially adjuvant therapy regimens, which may modulate FGFR pathway biology through mechanisms like epigenetic modifications (51). Importantly, subgroup analyses in our meta-analysis reflected this heterogeneity: FGFR expression detected by IHC showed more consistent prognostic correlations, whereas cohorts using ISH demonstrated inconsistent results. Geographic disparities further emerged, with Japanese CRC cohorts showing no significant association between FGFR and OS, potentially due to population-specific genetic and treatment differences.

The present findings provide additional evidence that FGFR overexpression is linked to unfavorable clinical outcomes in CRC, particularly for the FGFR2 and FGFR4 subtypes. These results imply that FGFR expression patterns may hold potential value for prognostic assessment and molecular characterization of CRC; however, their clinical applicability necessitates further validation. Given the methodological heterogeneity among current studies, including variations in detection platforms and cutoff definitions, standardized assessment approaches are essential for future investigations. Prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts are required to confirm the prognostic relevance of FGFR subtypes across diverse patient populations. Furthermore, additional research is necessary to ascertain whether FGFR overexpression can aid in identifying patients who may benefit from FGFR-targeted therapies or combination treatment strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. (I) Most included studies were retrospective observational cohorts, and the limited availability of specific FGFR subtype datasets, particularly for FGFR1 and FGFR3, may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. (II) Heterogeneity in FGFR detection methods, cutoff definitions, and statistical approaches, including differences between univariable and multivariable analyses, may affect the comparability and interpretation of pooled results. (III) The potential influence of different treatment regimens and pharmacogenomic factors could not be fully evaluated due to insufficient individual-level clinical data. (IV) Subgroup findings based on limited datasets, such as ISH-based detection and Japanese cohorts, should be interpreted cautiously and require further validation. (V) The GRADE assessment indicated that the certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to low across outcomes, reflecting limitations related to study design, imprecision, and potential bias. (VI) Potential publication bias in the OS analysis and limitations inherent to the systematic review process, including possible selection bias during literature identification, restriction to English-language publications, and potential subjectivity in data extraction and quality assessment despite independent reviewer procedures, should also be considered.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis demonstrated that FGFR overexpression is significantly associated with unfavorable outcomes in CRC patients, including shorter OS and DFS, as well as an increased risk of vascular invasion. Subgroup analyses further indicated that specific FGFR isoforms, particularly FGFR2 and FGFR4, correlate with poor OS outcomes, suggesting their potential relevance as prognostic markers. These findings support the role of FGFR expression as a prognostic stratification marker and highlight FGFR signaling as a promising therapeutic target in CRC. However, due to the observational nature of the available evidence, heterogeneity in FGFR detection methods and cutoff definitions, as well as potential publication bias, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future investigations should focus on establishing standardized protocols for FGFR assessment and evaluating the clinical efficacy of FGFR pathway-targeted therapies in prospective studies, especially in combination with conventional treatment strategies, to further define its role in precision medicine for CRC.
