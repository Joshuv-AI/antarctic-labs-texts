Bifrost: Steering Strategic Trajectories to Bridge Contextual Gaps for Self-Improving Agents
Quan M. Tran, Zhuo Huang, Wenbin Zhang, Bo Han, Koji Yatani, Masashi Sugiyama Tongliang Liu, [1ex] Sydney AI Centre, The University of Sydney; Florida International University

Abstract
Autonomous agents excel in self-improvement through reflection and iterative refinement, which reuse successful task trajectories as in-context examples to assist subsequent reasoning. However, shifting across tasks often introduces a context mismatch. Hence, existing approaches either discard the trajectories or manipulate them using heuristics, leading to a non-negligible fine-tuning cost or unguaranteed performance. To bridge this gap, we reveal a context-trajectory correlation, where shifts of context are highly parallel with shifts of trajectory. Based on this finding, we propose BrIdge contextual gap FoR imprOvised trajectory STeering (Bifrost), a training-free method that leverages context differences to precisely guide the adaptation of previously solved trajectories towards the target task, mitigating the misalignment caused by context shifts. Our trajectory adaptation is conducted at the representation level using agent hidden states, ensuring trajectory transformation accurately aligns with the target context in a shared space. Across diverse benchmarks, Bifrost consistently outperforms existing trajectory reuse and finetuned self-improvement methods, demonstrating that agents can effectively leverage past experiences despite substantial context shifts.

Methodology

Our work focuses on how an LLM Agent can effectively leverage its successful trajectories solved in the past to adapt to a target task under a substantial context shift.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a LLM that solved tasks under a previous context where successful trajectories are preserved as
\mathcal{C}=\{(q_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
. Here, each pair
(q_{i},a_{i})
denotes a trajectory consisting of a query and its corresponding reasoning process leading to the answer.
Our objective is to find an optimal reasoning strategy by leveraging previous trajectories that maximizes the log likelihood of the LLM agent on the target task
\hat{\mathcal{C}}=\{(\hat{q}_{i},\hat{a}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,

\mathcal{S^{*}}=\arg\max_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{(\hat{q},\hat{a})\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}}\left[\log\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}(\hat{a}\mid\hat{q})\right],

(1)

where
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{S}}
denotes that the model
\mathcal{M}
reasons following the reasoning strategy
\mathcal{S}
. Although the strategy
\mathcal{S}
succeeded under the previous context
\mathcal{C}
, it might not align with the target context
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
. Therefore, instead of fully fine-tuning the agent or naively reusing previous strategies, we aim to bridge the contextual gap between previous tasks and any target tasks by proposing Bifrost, which can improvise under any target contexts by properly steering the trajectory of an existing strategy. As a result, Bifrost improves in-context learning via an optimal reasoning strategy tailored for the target context, as shown in Fig.
1
.

To demonstrate Bifrost, we first reveal our findings of context-trajectory correlation in Sec.
2.1
. Further, based on this finding, we perform trajectory steering to adapt previous trajectories to the target task in Sec.
2.2
.

2.1
Context-Trajectory Correlation

To conduct proper reasoning under a target context, it is vital to employ an effective trajectory. Therefore, we investigate the correlation between trajectories and context under task shift. To accomplish this, we follow Linear Representation Hypothesis
47
by regarding LLMs as an embedding-unembedding process.

Particularly, the embedding encodes context inputs into hidden states,
e.g.
, embedding vectors, which contain essential concepts that enable knowledge generalization across tasks. For example, “king” and “queen” are conceptually related to “man” and “woman”, respectively. Formally, for a context text
x=(q_{i},a_{i})
, we assume the hidden state mapped by an LLM is
\mathbf{h}(x)
in a representation space
\Lambda
. Further, the model represents each word
y
in its vocabulary into an unembedding vector
\mathbf{g}(y)
in a separate space
\Gamma
with the same dimension. To this end, the probability distribution for next-word prediction is formulated as:

P(y\mid x)\propto\exp(\mathbf{h}(x)^{\top}\mathbf{g}(y)).

(2)

Intuitively, the reasoning process can be explained as two representation spaces that are connected via an inner product, where LLMs aim to find the best match between a context input and a reasoning output. By conducting such a process iteratively, the input-output logic chain formulates a strategic trajectory that is optimized for a certain context.

In agentic AI, it is common to apply agents across various tasks, thus encountering a novel context. To ensure the target task is solvable, we have the following assumption:

Assumption 1
.

For an LLM agent
\mathcal{M}
capable of solving task A and task B with contexts
\mathcal{C}^{A}
and
\mathcal{C}^{B}
, respectively, the concepts
\mathcal{W}^{A}=\{W_{i}^{A}\}_{i=1}^{k}
and
\mathcal{W}^{B}=\{W_{i}^{B}\}_{i=1}^{n}
extracted from task A and task B must satisfy:

•

The vocabulary space of
\mathcal{M}
covers
\mathcal{W}^{A}\cup\mathcal{W}^{B}
.

•

The shared concepts
\mathcal{W}^{A}\cap\mathcal{W}^{B}
are causally separable.

•

For any private concepts
W^{A}\in\mathcal{W}^{A}\setminus\mathcal{W}^{B}
and
W^{B}\in\mathcal{W}^{B}\setminus\mathcal{W}^{A}
, the embedding-unembedding vectors satisfy the subspace independence
\mathbf{h}_{W^{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{W^{B}}=0
.

To handle a target context
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
specific to concept
W
, the contextual gap can be captured by a steering vector
\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{W}
scaled by
\alpha
in the hidden states:

\hat{\mathbf{h}}=\mathbf{h}+\alpha\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{W}.

(3)

Considering a binary-value concept,
e.g.
, man and woman, the probability of the output
Y
taking value
Y(1)
versus its counterfactual
Y(0)
is measured by the logit. According to the measurement representation theorem in Park et al.
47
, such a logit is linear with respect to
\mathbf{h}
:

\!\!\!\text{logit\! }P(Y\!=\!Y(1)\mid\mathbf{h};Y\!\in\!\{Y(0),Y(1)\})=\beta\mathbf{h}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{W},\!\!

(4)

where
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{W}
is the unembedding representation (the output direction) of the task and
\beta>0
is a function of
\{Y(0),Y(1)\}
. The following theorem shows that output tokens change linearly with the contextual shift.

Theorem 2
.

Under the Linear Representation Hypothesis, for a concept
W
satisfying the conditions in Assumption
1
, for an embedding vector
\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{W}
and unembedding vector
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{W}
of
W
, the change of output logits is a linear function of the magnitude of the context shift
\beta\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{W}
.

Proof.

Given the optimal logit based on the target context hidden state
\hat{\mathbf{h}}
, we substitute the shift according to Eq. (
3
):

\displaystyle\text{logit }P(Y=Y(1)\mid\bar{\mathbf{h}})

\displaystyle=\beta(\mathbf{h}+\alpha\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{W})^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{W}

(5)

\displaystyle=\beta\mathbf{h}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{W}+\alpha(\beta\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{W}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{W}).

(6)

Note that
\beta
is fixed under a certain concept, and
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{W}
denotes the concept direction based on the model understanding,
e.g.,
, “king” to “queen” and “man” to “woman” have the same shifting direction when given a fixed model. Thus the term
\beta\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{W}
is a constant
K
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\!\!\!\text{logit\! }P(Y\!=\!Y(1)\mid\bar{\mathbf{h}})=\text{logit\! }P(Y\!=\!Y(1)\mid\mathbf{h})\!+\!\alpha K\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{W}.\!\!

(7)

By defining the shift of logit distribution under different context hidden states
\Delta_{P}=P(Y=Y(1)\mid\hat{\mathbf{h}})-P(Y=Y(1)\mid\mathbf{h})
, we have
\Delta_{P}\propto\alpha K\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{W}
.
∎

Intuitively, a trajectory is composed of output tokens, which are a sequence of actions
a
. This theorem shows that selecting an optimal trajectory based on an existing one can be guided by the context shift.

Discussion

The above results are focused on one concept, which can be generalized to complex tasks containing multiple concepts. For concepts that are not shared across tasks, the subspace independence ensures that the embedding space are irrelavant to the unembedding space. Thus, the corresponding prediction logits are not affected by the context shift. Moreover, for multiple shared concepts, the causal separability ensures that they are not confusing each other. For example, adapting the context “He speaks English” to a target context “She speaks French” must be separated from adapting to another target“He speaks French”.

2.2
Improvised Trajectory Steering for Target Context

Further, we build on the context-trajectory correlation and propose Bifrost, a training-free and context-guided strategy that enables agents to self-improve by employing prior successful task trajectories under substantial context shifts.

As demonstrated in Sec.
2.1
, trajectories exhibit behavioral adaptation in response to context shift toward a target task. This motivates a trajectory steering of the existing in-context learning mechanism using such trajectories.

Context-Guided Adaptation under In-Context Learning

Given a set of successful task trajectories from previous context
\mathcal{C}=\{(q_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
solved by a LLM agent
\mathcal{M}
.
Our goal is to solve a target task
\hat{q}\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}
from a different context by leveraging
\mathcal{C}
as in-context examples and steering the model toward the target context. The steered model then produces the output
\hat{a}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(p)
, where
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
denotes the contextually steered model, and
p=\mathcal{C}\circ\hat{q}
denotes the in-context learning input prompt with
\mathcal{C}
as demonstrations followed by the target question
\hat{q}
.

Contextual Representation Steering

Let
\mathbf{h}^{\ell}
be the last token hidden state of the original model
\mathcal{M}
, the trajectory steering can be applied across specific layers
\ell\in L
, yielding the steered model
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
with corresponding hidden state
\mathbf{h}^{\ell}_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathbf{h}^{\ell}+\alpha\Delta^{\ell}
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
controls the steering strength. The vector
\Delta^{\ell}
that represents the contextual direction from the previous trajectory to the target task is computed as

\Delta^{\ell}=\mathbf{h}^{\ell}(\hat{q})-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{h}^{\ell}(\mathcal{C}_{i}),

(8)

where
\mathbf{h}^{\ell}(\hat{q})
is the target question hidden state extracted by
\mathcal{M}(\hat{q})
, and
\mathbf{h}^{\ell}(\mathcal{C}_{i})
extracts trajectory hidden state when the agent solve the corresponding task
a_{i}=\mathcal{M}(q_{i})
.

The contextual direction
\Delta^{\ell}
represents a context-shift concept that appears as the difference between previous trajectory execution and the current task. Averaging the prior trajectory hidden states finds the shared patterns, while the steering model encourages such patterns adaptable to the target task, thereby enabling trajectory reuse under context shift and enhancing agent generalization.

Theoretical Analysis

We analyze the proposed method from a theoretical perspective, showing that Bifrost effectively leverages past trajectory experiences to adapt toward a target task. Our analysis is based on In-Context Learning (ICL) as Bayesian Inference
73
, demonstrating that Bifrost preserves prior knowledge induced by previous trajectories while reducing posterior uncertainty toward the target tasks. Furthermore, we exceed the risk bound of Bifrost compared with an ordinary LLM agent that solely solves the target task.
We begin by introducing In-Context Learning as Bayesian Inference. Proofs shown in Appendices
C.1
and
C.2
.

In-Context Learning as Bayesian Inference

The LLM agent
\mathcal{M}
generates a prediction for a given input
x
by implicitly inferring a latent concept
\phi
from a context
\mathcal{C}^{*}
represented by in-context examples,
\mathcal{M}(x)\approx\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C}^{*})}\left[f_{\phi}(x)\right]
, where
p(\phi|C^{*})
is the posterior distribution of concepts given a context. The effectiveness of agents stems from the latent concept inferred from the in-context examples. Thus, we focus on modeling the latent concept given previous trajectories.

In our case, the context is represented by previous trajectories,
\mathcal{C}^{*}=\mathcal{C}
. By the definition, the posterior probability of the concept given previous trajectories is defined as

\displaystyle p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C})\;\propto\;p(\mathcal{C}\mid\phi)p(\phi)\;\propto\;\exp\left(-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{L}(f_{\phi}(q_{i}),a_{i})\right)p(\phi),

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes the loss function.

Posterior Inference Steering Effect

The latent concept distribution can now be approximated by a Gaussian distribution over the context represented by previous context trajectories. Assuming
\mathcal{L}
is convex and
L
-Lipschitz in
f_{\phi}
, and
f_{\phi}
is locally smooth in
\phi
, we approximate the posterior of the latent concept via Laplace approximation,

p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C})\approx\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\phi}|\mu,\Sigma^{-1}),

(9)

where
\mathcal{N}(\phi|\mu,\Sigma^{-1})
denotes the Gaussian density with mean
\mu
and inverse covariance matrix
\Sigma^{-1}
, and
\Sigma=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{F}
, and
\mathbf{F}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the feature map.

When leveraging previous trajectories as in-context examples, the latent concept is additionally conditioned on the target task
\hat{q}\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}
. By further applying a trajectory steering
\Delta\in\Lambda
from Bifrost, the concept distribution mean shifts toward an ideal concept of such a target task accordingly.

Lemma 1
.

Let
p_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi\mid\mathcal{C},\hat{q})
be the latent concept posterior probability of the steered model, conditioned on previous context trajectories
\mathcal{C}
and the target task
\hat{q}\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}
.
The contextual shift
\Delta
shifts the concept distribution mean toward an ideal target task concept distribution by
\alpha P(\Delta)
where
P
is the projection, while preserving uncertainty reduction induced by previous context trajectories,

p_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi\mid\mathcal{C},\hat{q})\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\tilde{\phi}+\alpha P(\Delta)|\mu,\Sigma^{-1}\right)

Excess Risk Bound

We further analyze the risk bound of Bifrost compared to an ordinary agent that solely solves the target task without providing in-context examples from previous context tasks. Given that the target task shares a common representation with previous trajectories that are transferable, the following theorem shows that the bound of the proposed method reduces linearly with the number of induced previous trajectories.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
be the steered agent by Bifrost, and
\mathcal{M}

Assuming that the target task aligns with the previous trajectory context,
and the contextual shift

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{C}\circ\hat{q}))\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M}(\hat{q}))\right]-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).

5
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed Bifrost, a training-free method that enables self-improving agents to improvise their prior trajectories across tasks under substantial context shifts. We revealed a context-trajectory correlation that allows Bifrost to precisely steer any previous trajectories toward target tasks by exploiting context differences. Both theoretical analyses and empirical results demonstrate that Bifrost leverages latent concepts acquired from prior in-context examples to adapt to new tasks, consistently outperforming existing self-improvement methods across diverse domains. Despite these promising results, our theoretical analysis is grounded in the Linear Representation Hypothesis, which may limit its applicability where more expressive nonlinear representations are required in practice. In the future, we plan to extend Bifrost to multi-context adaptation, where multiple target contexts are presented. Such a direction introduces an intensified complexity, which is essential for general-purpose agentic AI under multi-task applications.

Appendix C
Theoretical Analysis

C.1
Proof of Lemma
1

After observing previous trajectories, we have approximated the latent concept posterior as a Gaussian

\displaystyle p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C})\approx\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\phi},\Sigma^{-1}),

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\tilde{\phi}=\arg\max_{\phi}(\log p(\mathcal{C}\mid\phi)+\log p(\phi)),\quad\Sigma=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{F},

where the mean
\tilde{\phi}
is usually centered at the mode of previous trajectories. When observing a new target query
\hat{q}\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}
, we update the posterior and its mean accordingly,

\displaystyle p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C},\hat{q})\propto p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)\cdot p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C}).

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}=\arg\max_{\phi}\ (p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)+p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C})).

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\phi}=p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)+p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C})
, we update the mean of new posterior by optimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
. To solve this, we will need to derive
p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)
and
p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C})
.

Deriving
\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)
by Taylor expansion to the new log-likelihood term,

\displaystyle\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)\approx\log p(\hat{q}\mid\tilde{\phi})+g^{\top}(\phi-\tilde{\phi})\;+\;\frac{1}{2}(\phi-\tilde{\phi})^{\top}Q(\phi-\tilde{\phi}),

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad g=\nabla_{\phi}\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)\Big|_{\phi=\tilde{\phi}},\quad Q=\nabla^{2}_{\phi}\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)\Big|_{\phi=\tilde{\phi}}.

Deriving
\log p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C})
as

\displaystyle\log p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C})\;\approx\;\text{const}-\frac{1}{2}(\phi-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\phi-\mu).

As a result,
\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}\approx-\frac{1}{2}(\phi-\tilde{\phi})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\phi-\tilde{\phi})+g^{\top}(\phi-\tilde{\phi})\;+\;\frac{1}{2}(\phi-\tilde{\phi})^{\top}Q(\phi-\tilde{\phi}).

To optimize
\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
, we take derivative w.r.t.
\phi
and set to zero

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\phi}=-\Sigma(\phi-\tilde{\phi})+g+Q(\phi-\tilde{\phi})=0.

Rearranging
and simplifying the single-step update by discarding
Q
, we have

\phi-\tilde{\phi}\approx\Sigma^{-1}g=\Sigma^{-1}\nabla_{\phi}\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)\Big|_{\phi=\tilde{\phi}}.

(13)

Therefore, adding the target query
\hat{q}\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}
will update the latent concept posterior as

p(\phi\mid\mathcal{C},\hat{q})\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\tilde{\phi}+\Sigma^{-1}\nabla_{\phi}\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi),\Sigma^{-1}\right).

(14)

Under the Linear Representation Hypothesis,
\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)
from Eq. (
13
) is generally a linear function of the hidden state expressed as

\displaystyle\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)\approx\mathbf{h}(\hat{q};\phi)\mathbf{W},

where
\mathbf{h}
is the hidden state of LLM
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathbf{W}
is a linear projection.

Treating
\mathbf{h}
as a function of
\phi
, derive
\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)=\frac{\partial\log p}{\partial\mathbf{h}}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}}{\partial\phi}.

The partial derivative of
\mathbf{h}
can be approximated by a first-order Taylor expansion, assuming that
\mathbf{h}
is locally smooth in
\phi
, and
\phi^{*}
is the ideal concept of the target task context
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}(\phi^{*})\approx\mathbf{h}(\phi)+\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}}{\partial\phi}(\phi^{*}-\phi).

Since
\Delta\approx\mathbf{h}(\phi^{*})-\mathbf{h}(\phi)
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}}{\partial\phi}(\phi^{*}-\phi).

(15)

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{J}\cdot\Sigma^{-1}\nabla_{\phi}\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)\quad\quad\text{(from Eq.~\eqref{eq:laplace_update_unsteer})}

(16)

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{J}\nabla_{\phi}\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi).

(17)

Rearranging,

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}\nabla_{\phi}\log p(\hat{q}\mid\phi)=\mathbf{J}^{\top}\Delta=P(\Delta).

(18)

Therefore, applying a trajectory steering
\Delta
shifts the mean toward an ideal concept of the target context, the steered concept posterior is expressed as

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi\mid\mathcal{C},\hat{q})\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\tilde{\phi}+\alpha P(\Delta),\Sigma^{-1}\right).

C.2
Proof of Theorem
3

Let

y^{*}=f_{\phi}(\hat{q})
be the true label of target query
\hat{q}
,

\hat{y}_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{C}\circ\hat{q})
be the prediction of the steered LLM agent,

p_{\mathrm{s}}=p_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi\mid\mathcal{C},\hat{q})
be the latent concept posterior of the steered model.
Our target bound is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{s}},y^{*})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim p_{\mathrm{s}}}\left[\mathcal{L}(f_{\phi}(\hat{q}),y^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim p_{\mathrm{s}}}\left[f_{\phi}(\hat{q})\right],y^{*}\right)+Var[f_{\phi}(\hat{q})]\quad\text{(Jensen inequality with smooth and convex $\mathcal{L}$)}

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{L}(f_{\mathbb{E}[\phi]}(\hat{q}),y^{*})+\mathcal{O}(Var[f_{\phi}(\hat{q})]).

The target bound consists of the bias and variance terms.
We first bound the variance term. From Lemma
1
we have
p_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi\mid\cdot)\approx\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma^{-1})
. Since
f_{\phi}
is locally Lipschitz in
\phi
with a constant
G
, we have

\displaystyle Var[f_{\phi}(\hat{q})]\;

\displaystyle\leq\;G^{2}\;\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma^{-1})\;=\;G^{2}\;\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k})\;\leq\;\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k}).

Next, we bound the bias term,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f_{\mathbb{E}[\phi]}(\hat{q}),y^{*})

\displaystyle\leq C\;||\mu-\phi^{*}||^{2}\quad\text{($\mathcal{L}$ is smooth)}

\displaystyle=||\alpha P(\Delta)-(\phi^{*}-\tilde{\phi})||^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(||\alpha P(\Delta)-(\phi^{*}-\tilde{\phi})||^{2}\right)

As a result, we obtain the bound of the steered model as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{s}},y^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k}+||\alpha P(\Delta)-(\phi^{*}-\tilde{\phi})||^{2}\right).

Since
\Delta\propto(\phi^{*}-\tilde{\phi})
, thus
\alpha P(\Delta)=c(\phi^{*}-\tilde{\phi})
for a scalar
c>0
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{s}},y^{*})\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k}).

For an ordinary model, its prediction
y=\mathcal{M}(\hat{q})
induces a broader pretrained latent concept, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(y,y^{*})]\leq\mathcal{O}(1).

Subtracting the bounds, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(y,y^{*})]-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{s}},y^{*})\right]\geq\mathcal{O}(1)-\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k}).

Rearranging

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{s}},y^{*})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(y,y^{*})]-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).

Appendix D
Experiments

D.1
Experimental Setup

Context Shift Experimental Setup

To evaluate trajectory leverage of self-improving agents under substantial context shifts, we setup source - target dataset pairs. In particular, a self-improving agent will solve tasks on a source dataset to collect successful trajectories (and/or unsuccessful ones depending on baselines), then leverage such trajectories to adapt toward the target task in the sense that there is no ground-truth available on such tasks of the target dataset. In this way, the self-improving agent has to employ their past experiences learned from previous tasks via such trajectories to solve the target tasks. We now present how this setup applies to Bifrost and the baselines in our work.

Bifrost Setup

Bifrost does not require any particular self-improving agent mechanism to solve tasks and collect corresponding trajectories. In our experiment, we use ordinary LLM agents as mentioned to solely solve source tasks and subsequently collect successful trajectories.

We let the agent solve a subset of 20 tasks when using Llama-3.1-8B, and 50 tasks when using Llama-3.2-3B and Llama-3.2-1B, for all source tasks in our experiments (AQUA, ARC-Easy, HumanEval). We subsequently collect corresponding successful trajectories and randomly pick a number of them as in-context examples to solve target tasks. In particular, we randomly pick 7 examples in
AQUA
\rightarrow
GSM8K
and
ARC-Easy

Baselines Setup

We compare Bifrost with the following baselines.

•

Chain-of-Though (CoT)

[
70
]
prompts an agent to perform step-by-step reasoning. We directly apply CoT to the target tasks.

•

In-Context Learning (ICL)

[
9
]
, we let an agent solve tasks from a source dataset to collect successful trajectories. We then leverage such trajectories as in-context examples in the prompts to solve target tasks. We use 7 in-context examples for both the math problem-solving task and the multiple-choice question answering task, and we use 5 in-context examples for the code generation task.

•

Reflexion

[
57
]
is able to iteratively refine its own trajectories given environment feedback. We directly apply Reflexion to the target tasks. We allow up to 5 attempts for math problem solving and code generation problems, as in their original work. For the multi-choice question answering problem, we allow 3 attempts since more attempts can enable the agent to easily get the correct answer with 4-choice questions, which is unfair to other methods.

•

Buffer of Thoughts (BoT)

[
74
]
creates thought templates when solving tasks as guidance in future tasks. We let BoT solve the source dataset tasks, subsequently creating corresponding though templates, which then serve as guidance in target tasks. Following their original work
[
74
]
, we let BoT continuously update its thought templates while solving the target tasks.

•

RISE

[
50
]
teaches an agent to self-improve by learning each fail-to-correct transition of trajectories via reinforcement learning. In our experiments, we let RISE solve source tasks and subsequently perform reinforcement learning on corresponding trajectories. We then leverage the finetuned agent to solve target tasks. We evaluate
RISE
within a single attempt and 5 attempts, respectively, as recommended by the original work
[
50
]
.

•

Diversity of Thought (DoT)

[
35
]
lets an agent generate diverse trajectories and self-reflect on given environment feedback. Successful trajectories are stored in a memory bank as is and subsequently retrieved as in-context examples when solving similar tasks. We let DoT solve source tasks, and employ it to solve the target tasks.

•

Paprika

[
60
]
learns strategic information by letting an agent self-explore and adjust its trajectories given environment feedback via a combined Supervised Finetuning and Direct
Preference Optimization. Such information is prone to out-of-domain as shown in their original paper
[
60
]
. Similar to RISE, we learn Paprika on source tasks and apply it to the target tasks.
