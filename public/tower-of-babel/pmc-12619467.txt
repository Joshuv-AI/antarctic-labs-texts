The association between plasma omega-3 fatty acids and suicidal ideation/self-harm in the United Kingdom Biobank
Authors: W Grant Franco, Nathan L Tintle, Jason Westra, Evan L O’Keefe, James H O’Keefe, William S Harris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-harm is a significant public health concern, with increasing prevalence globally. Omega-3 fatty acids (FAs), particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), are known for their health benefits, including potential mental health improvements. This study explores the association between omega-3 FAs and self-harm behaviors using data from the UK Biobank (UKBB).ObjectivesTo investigate the relationship between plasma levels of omega-3 FAs and various measures of self-harm, including passive suicidal ideation and deliberate self-harm, within a large cohort study.MethodsThis observational study analyzed data from a random subset of 258,012 participants with plasma omega-3 FA levels, covariate data, and self-harm records. Omega-3 levels were measured using Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) and are expressed as a percent of total FAs. Self-harm outcomes were assessed through self-reported questionnaires and medical records. Covariates included demographic, health, and lifestyle factors. Statistical analyses involved logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models, adjusting for relevant covariates. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) are presented and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsHigher levels of DHA, non-DHA (ALA+EPA+DPA) and total omega-3 were generally inversely associated with passive suicidal ideation, history of self-harm, and future self-harm, with both DHA and non-DHA showing some of the strongest associations. Participants in the highest quintile of non-DHA had a 14% lower risk of passive suicidal ideation in the last year (aOR = 0.86; 95% CI 0.75, 0.99), and the highest DHA levels were associated with a 33% lower odds of history of self-harm (aOR = 0.67; 95% CI 0.55, 0.83). These associations were generally stronger for medical record-based outcomes than for self-reported data.ConclusionsThis study provides evidence that higher omega-3 FA levels, both DHA and non-DHA, are associated with reduced risks of self-harm and suicidal ideation. These findings suggest that omega-3 FAs may play a protective role in mental health, highlighting the potential of dietary interventions to mitigate self-harm behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02689-1.Keywords: Omega-3; Self-harm, suicide; Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA); Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)

Introduction

The prevalence of self-harm, a significant public health concern globally, has increased over recent years. A recent report from England showed a two-fold increase in reported self-harm from 2.4% prevalence to 6.4% between 2000 and 2012 with a particularly notable increase among females aged 16–24 years, where prevalence jumped from 6.5 to 19.7% [1]. Deliberate self-harm, which includes non-suicidal self-injury involves behaviors such as cutting, burning, or self-poisoning and is often a manifestation of psychological distress [2–4]. Studies have reported varying rates of self-harm across different populations, with lifetime prevalence of this form of self-harm of 22% [5–8]. These findings underscore the urgency of addressing self-harm and its underlying causes to mitigate its impact on individuals and society.

Omega-3 fatty acids (FAs), in particular eicosapentaenoic (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acids (DHA), are bioactive constituents of certain fish and seafoods and have well-documented health benefits. These include protective effects against cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cognitive decline [9]. Recent evidence suggests that omega-3 FAs may also offer significant mental health benefits, potentially reducing the risk of depression, and anxiety, and possibly influencing self-harm behaviors [10]. The etiology of these benefits is believed to be linked to the anti-inflammatory properties of these FAs and to their crucial role in brain function [11]. Specifically, EPA and DHA are integral to maintaining neuronal cell membrane fluidity and function, which in turn supports neurotransmission and may mitigate mood disorders [12].

Supporting evidence for the impact of omega-3 FAs on mental health is growing, with studies linking higher levels of these nutrients to reduced symptoms of depression and anxiety [13–15]. For instance, the Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study found that higher omega-3 index levels (erythrocyte EPA+DHA) were associated with significantly lower risks of major depressive disorder (MDD) and other psychiatric conditions [16]. A recent report from the National Health And Nutrition Examination Survey found that higher intakes of omega-3 FAs (and of the omega-6 FA, linoleic acid) were linked to lower rates of suicidal ideation [17]. Plasma levels of omega-3 FAs were lower in US service personnel who ultimately committed suicide than in controls [18]. However, a study among individuals admitted to Emergency Department for attempted (or planned) suicide did not find an association with lower plasma omega-3 levels, but with lower arachidonic acid levels (another omega-6 FA) [19]. Beyond these observational studies, omega-3 supplementation has shown promise in reducing the severity of symptoms in individuals with clinical depression, suggesting a potential therapeutic role for these FAs in managing mental health conditions [13–15]. A meta-analysis of RCTs showed a possible use of omega-3 supplementation as adjuvant therapy in MDD [20]. There is evidence that omega-3 FA supplementation may provide benefit for patients with a history of self-injury and suicidal behavior [9, 18, 19, 21–27], however the evidence is unclear with other studies showing a lack of predictive power for self-harm or suicide [19, 28, 29]. Together, these findings suggest that omega-3 FAs may be beneficial across a range of mental health issues, including self-harm, but these relationships are still not widely accepted [30, 31]. Thus, in this study our goal is to determine the relationships between plasma levels of omega-3 FAs and measures of prevalent self-harm in the United Kingdom Biobank (UKBB). Our hypothesis was that higher omega-3 FA levels would be associated with lower risk for measures of self-harm including suicidal ideation.

Methods

Sample

This observational study was conducted within the UKBB, a prospective, population-based cohort of 502,366 individuals, 40–69 years of age, recruited in the UK between April 2007 and December 2010. UKBB has ethical approval (Ref. 11/NW/0382) from the Northwest Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee as a Research Tissue Bank. The University of South Dakota Institutional Review Board approved the use of these de-identified, publicly available data for research purposes (IRB-21-147). Within the cohort, a random subset of 258,012 participants had data on plasma omega-3 FA levels (exposures) and medical-record based information on self-harm or self-reported self-harm (outcomes) as well as complete data on relevant covariates and serves as our primary analytic sample. In secondary analyses, we also examined relationships in a subset of 80,745 individuals who answered more detailed questions on lifetime and 12-month outcome variables. The median follow-up time was 13.7 years (max = 15.8 years).

Plasma fatty acid exposures

In the UKBB, plasma omega-3 FA levels were measured by Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) (Nightingale Health Plc, Helsinki, Finland) and expressed as percent of total FAs [32]. The only omega-3 metrics available from the NMR analysis were total omega-3% and DHA%. We derived a third variable (alpha linolenic acid [ALA] + EPA + docosapentaenoic acid [DPA]%), by subtracting DHA% from total omega-3%. To confirm that the FA analysis by NMR correlated well with the more common, gold standard method, gas chromatography (GC), we performed an inter-laboratory experiment on 243 plasma samples [33]. As summarized in Supplementary Materials, there was good agreement for both plasma DHA and total omega-3 levels (r = 0.88 and 0.88, respectively), but the NMR-based non-DHA omega-3 (i.e., ALA + DPA + DPA) derived metric was somewhat less well correlated with the GC analysis (r = 0.75).

Self-harm and associated outcomes

We looked at 3 sets of outcomes related to suicidal ideation, self-harm and tendencies to these behaviors. The first set of variables were self-reported answers to questions about (1) life being worth living (passive suicidal ideation), (2) contemplating self-harm, either (a) ever (lifetime) or (b) in the previous year. The second set of variables were related to a history of actual self-harm from (1) self-reported history and (2) medical-record documentation, again (a) ever and (b) in the previous year. The third set of variables as derived from participant medical records and involved future events: (1) first time (after baseline) to a reporting of self-harm (just among people with no-history of self-harm), and (2) the same but in all subjects, regardless of history of self-harm. The exact verbiage of the questions, and ICD-10 codes used from the medical records are provided in Supplemental Table 1a. Loss to follow-up was censored at last data measurement.

Covariates

We included multiple covariates, the specifics of which are shown in Table 1. They included biological sex, age, BMI, marital status, employment status, race/ethnicity, physical activity, alcohol consumption, smoking, psychotropic medication use, history of depression, history of anxiety disorder, comorbid disorders and the Townsend Deprivation Index. (The latter is a measure of material deprivation in a geographical area. It uses four census variables to assess the level of deprivation: non-car ownership, overcrowding, non-home ownership, and unemployment. Higher scores indicate a greater degree of material deprivation). Additional details on variable definitions are provided in Supplemental Table 1b.

Statistical analyses

Sample characteristics are summarized using standard statistical methods, including means, standard deviations (SD), and percents. We used logistic regression models to generate odds ratios (ORs) for cross-sectional, lifetime and 12-month outcomes; and Cox proportional hazards models to generate hazard ratios (HRs) for future outcomes. Models were fit for continuous versions of the three omega-3 FA exposures per interquintile range (IQ5R, defined as the difference between the 10th and 90th percentiles). Separately, each FA was also modeled categorically with separate effect estimates for each quintile. Three polyunsaturated FA (PUFA) exposures (DHA%, ALA + EPA + DPA% and total omega-3%) and nine outcomes were considered, for a total of 27 models, all adjusted for the covariates described above. Cubic splines using 3 knots (at 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles), and after dropping the top/bottom 2.5% of the data were fit in all cases and then compared to continuous/linear models using Wald tests. Statistical significance was set to 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals (CI) are provided for most comparisons. Statistical significance for tests of non-linearity was set to 0.01 to account for multiple testing. R (www.r-project.org) was used for all analyses.

Results

The UKBB sample is slightly more female than male, primarily white and with a mean age of slightly over 56 years. Other characteristics of the sample are shown in Table 1. Although statistically significant, overall there were mimimal differences between the sample with only medical-record data and the smaller set of individuals who also had self-reported mental health/behavior data. The mean (SD) DHA % was slightly lower in the medical record sample [1.99% (0.66)] vs. the self-report sample [2.06% (0.66)], with a similar pattern for ALA + EPA + DPA [2.39 (0.99) vs. 2.43 (0.99)] and total omega-3 [4.39 (1.55) vs. 4.50 (1.56]). The estimated mean Omega-3 Index values [i.e., red blood cell EPA + DHA as a percent of total FAs; [34] computed from quintiles of plasma DHA were, for quintiles 1 through 5: 3.54%, 4.56%, 5.33%, 6.25% and > 7.96%, respectively, based on equations derived in Schuchardt et al. [33].

Past passive suicidal ideation and contemplating self-harm

The self-reported lifetime prevalence of having perceived one’s life as not worth living (passive suicidal ideation) was 30.5% (Table 2). DHA, total omega-3 and ALA + EPA + DPA levels all showed strong inverse associations with this metric. DHA showed the strongest evidence of inverse association with passive suicidal ideation with a 13% reduction in risk when comparing the highest to the lowest quintiles of DHA in the multivariable adjusted model (OR = 0.87; 95% CI 0.82, 0.92). DHA also showed significant evidence of non-linearity (p = 0.003). Subsequent cubic spline analysis of the DHA vs. prevalent passive suicidal ideation revealed an increase in risk below a DHA level of about 1.7% (Fig. 1). A similar association with this outcome was observed for total omega-3 PUFA levels (although the magnitude of association was less, [i.e., OR = 0.92 (0.87,0.97)], while inverse associations with ALA + EPA + DPA were even smaller [OR = 0.95 (0.90,1.00)], though still statistically significant, and did not have statistically significant evidence of non-linearity (p > 0.01). Lifetime and past 12-month associations with omega-3 levels and contemplations of self-harm also showed generally inverse relationships with a mix of statistically significant and non-significant findings, and risk reductions between 0 and 14% when comparing Q2-Q5 to Q1 (Table 2). None of these comparisons showed statistically significant evidence of non-linearity (p > 0.01 in all cases).

Lifetime self-reported/medical record inferred self-harm

Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 2a provide odds ratios for the relationship between a history of self-harm (self-reported and medical record inferred) and omega-3 PUFA levels. Overall, associations were statistically significant in most cases, with the strongest associations for history of self-harm as inferred from the medical record (e.g., DHA per IQ5R OR 0.71 [95% CI: 0.60, 0.83]). Associations were less strong, and periodically not statistically significant, for self-report history of self-harm. Twelve-month history of self-harm had generally low prevalence (Supplemental Table 2b) but did show trends towards inverse associations with omega-3 levels, though many of the aHRs were not statistically significant. Tests of nonlinearity were not statistically significant (p > 0.01).

Future self-harm

Associations between first time and any incident self-harm were also inversely associated with omega-3 FA levels in all cases. While overall prevalence was low (0.3–0.4% of the sample), comparisons between the highest and lowest quintiles of omega-3 levels showed HR between 0.71 and 0.83 showing a strong inverse relationship which was statistically significant (p < 0.05) in most cases (Table 3). As with other outcomes, these associations showed no evidence of non-linearity using cubic splines (p > 0.01 in all cases).

Discussion

This study examined the association between omega-3 FA levels and self-harm/suicidal ideation using data from the UKBB cohort. We found an inverse relationship between plasma omega-3 FA levels and both past and future metrics reflective of self-harm and/or suicidal ideation. Overall, the relationships, while favorable for all three omega-3 metrics (DHA, total omega-3, and non-DHA omega-3), tended to be stronger and more commonly statistically significant for the latter measure. As this metric combined ALA + EPA + DPA, we were unable to identify which of these FA(s) may have been most responsible for this finding. Previous meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials in depression have reported greater efficacy in treating the symptoms of MMD with EPA-rich products vs. those richer in DHA [35]. Thus, the omega-3 FA most likely to be favorably associated with lower risk for self-harm (to the extent that self-harm is a manifestation of depression), is EPA.

We used 3 different outcomes in this study. First was a history of thoughts related to self-harm (i.e., passive suicidal ideation), which was assessed through self-reporting of the extent to which life seemed to be “worth living.” We found that participants in the highest quintile of DHA levels were 13% less likely to have reported that life was not worth living compared to those in the lowest quintile. This supports a recent report from Lin et al. using the National Health And Nutrition Examination Survey [17] which reported an inverse association between the dietary intake of omega-3 FAs and the prevalence of passive suicidal ideation in about 28,000 individuals.

Our second outcome was a self-reported history of self-harming behaviors which was assessed through both self-reporting and medical records. The odds for the latter outcome were 26-33% lower (Q5 vs. Q1) across the plasma omega-3 FA exposures. Again, this is consistent with the results of a small randomized trial in which omega-3 supplementation reduced self-harming behaviors in a 12 week trial [21]. In a follow-up study, they reported that fish oil supplement users had significantly fewer self-harm episodes than non-consumers although the numbers were quite small (0 of 9 vs. 6 of 14, p = 0.048) [28].

The third outcome of interest was the medical records-based evidence of incident (future, after baseline) self-harm behaviors, both events in people with no history of self-harming and then in all participants, including those with a history of self-harm). Although sample sizes were small (~ 1000 out of 258,000), the hazard ratios (per IQ5R) were 18-26% lower across the three omega-3 FA exposures . These findings are consistent with several previous reports on omega-3 and risk for suicide. Sublette et al. reported in 2006 that plasma DHA levels were significantly lower in suicide attempters vs. non-attempters with among patients with a history of depression but who were not on antidepressants [24]. In a Chinese study, suicide attempters vs. matched controls had significantly lower red blood cell EPA levels [22]. In a case control study among US military service personnel, plasma DHA levels were significantly lower in the 800 who later committed suicide vs. 800 controls who had not [18]. The odds of being a suicide case were 14% lower for each SD increase in plasma DHA. At autopsy, prefrontal cortex levels of DHA were lower (p = 0.06) in suicide cases compared with non-suicide fatalities (without cardiovascular disease; those dying with cardiovascular disease had similar DHA levels as the suicide victims) [9].

The mechanisms underlying the associations observed here likely involve several factors. Omega-3, particularly DHA, is crucial for maintaining neuronal cell membrane fluidity, which is essential for proper neurotransmission and brain function [11, 36, 37]. Treating MDD with anti-inflammatory compounds has shown promise [38], and therefore, neuroinflammation is a likely component of the pathology of the disease. Thus, whatever benefits omega-3 FAs may play in diminishing MDD symptoms may derive from their well-documented anti-inflammatory properties [11]. Similarly, these anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects of omega-3s could contribute to the observed reductions in self-harm and suicidal ideation.

Our findings are consistent with the broader body of research highlighting the mental health benefits of omega-3 FA. Multiple studies have shown that omega-3 supplementation can be used as a supportive treatment for depression [13–15, 20]. For example, in the Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study there was a significant association between higher red blood cell omega-3 levels and lower risk of major depressive disorder [16]. A separate meta-analysis of randomized trials showed that omega-3 supplementation can be an effective treatment for bipolar depression [39]. However, a Cochrane review of the effects of omega-3 in MDD concluded that there was possibly a mild effect of uncertain clinical significance [40].

Given the associations observed in this study, increasing omega-3 intake through dietary sources or supplementation could be a valuable strategy in public health interventions aimed at reducing self-harm and suicidal behaviors. Despite the fact that two population-based cohort studies, one from the US [29] and the other from Japan [41], found no relationship between suicide risk and fish intake, it is possible that overall intakes were too low in the US study and already sufficiently high in the Japanese study. This is supported by the fact that in the latter study, women in the lowest decile of fish intake did have a significantly increased risk for suicide. Given the overall evidence, public health policies promoting the consumption of omega-3-rich foods may help mitigate the risks associated with self-harm and improve overall mental health, particularly in western countries where omega-3 levels are generally low [42].

Despite the consistency of our findings, this study has several limitations. The observational nature of the study naturally precludes causal inferences. However, we adjusted for numerous relevant covariates and saw similar patterns in retrospective, cross-sectional and prospective analyses. Additionally, self-reported data on self-harm and suicidal ideation may be subject to recall bias; even so, the strongest associations we identified were with medical record “hard” outcomes. The possibility of reverse causation must be kept in mind, i.e., that individuals of a stressed state of mind may consciously or subconsciously choose to consume diets of overall poorer quality which would likely be low in omega-3 fatty acids. The extent to which these findings can be generalized to other cultures or age groups is unclear. Future research should aim to establish causality through randomized controlled trials and explore the optimal dosage and duration of omega-3 supplementation for mental health benefits.

In conclusion, this study provides further evidence that higher levels of omega-3 FAs are associated with reduced risk of self-harm and suicidal ideation. These findings highlight the potential of omega-3 FA as part of nutritional strategies to improve mental health in general. Further research is warranted to confirm these associations and explore their practical applications in mental health interventions.
