Clarifying social undermining in nursing: a concept analysis
Authors: Hossein Ghasemi, Masoud Etedali, Nasrollah Alimohammadi, Mohsen Shahriari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial undermining is a subtle and covert form of negative interpersonal behavior that can disrupt professional relationships, undermine self-confidence, and negatively influence nursing practice. Despite its relevance, this concept has not been systematically clarified within the nursing literature.MethodsThis study employed Walker and Avant’s eight-step concept analysis method to clarify the meaning and dimensions of social undermining in nursing. A comprehensive review of English and Persian literature published between 2000 and 2025 was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Scientific Information Database.ResultsThe analysis identified social undermining as an intentional, covert, and repetitive process characterized by subtle behaviors that progressively erode professional trust and collaboration. Key antecedents included individual factors such as perceived competition and interpersonal insecurity, as well as organizational conditions such as workload pressure, perceived injustice, and inadequate managerial support. The consequences extended from emotional exhaustion and reduced job satisfaction at the individual level to disrupted teamwork, increased turnover intention, and potential risks to care processes at the organizational level. Social undermining was conceptually distinguished from incivility and bullying by its indirect nature, cumulative impact, and reliance on concealed behaviors rather than overt aggression.Conclusions and implications for nursing practiceSocial undermining represents a distinct and significant challenge in nursing work environments. Clarifying this concept provides a foundation for developing nursing-specific assessment tools and targeted organizational strategies aimed at fostering psychologically safe and supportive workplace cultures.Keywords: Social undermining, Nursing, Concept analysis, Workplace behavior, Psychological safety

Introduction

Social undermining is a covert and deliberate form of negative interpersonal behavior in the workplace, characterized by actions intended to hinder an individual’s professional reputation, interpersonal relationships, or ability to succeed [1]. Unlike overt aggression or physical conflict, it manifests through subtle behaviors such as gossip, exclusion, withholding information, and indirect derogatory remarks [2]. These behaviors not only harm the targeted individual but also disrupt team functioning and organizational performance.

Despite its relevance, the concept of social undermining remains insufficiently defined within the nursing literature and is often conflated with related constructs such as incivility, bullying, or interpersonal conflict [3, 4]. This lack of conceptual clarity limits the development of valid measurement tools and targeted interventions, thereby constraining empirical research and efforts to address psychosocial risks in nursing environments [5]. In nursing practice, effective communication, collaboration, and psychological safety are essential for both patient outcomes and staff well-being [6]. However, nurses frequently work in high-stress environments characterized by heavy workloads, interprofessional tensions, and emotional demands, which may facilitate the emergence of undermining behaviors [7]. Exposure to such behaviors has been associated with emotional exhaustion, reduced job satisfaction, diminished self-confidence, and impaired clinical decision-making [8, 9]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that social undermining negatively affects psychological empowerment and resilience, ultimately compromising motivation, innovation, and quality of care [10]. Given the growing recognition of workplace aggression as a critical factor influencing both staff well-being and patient safety, there is a clear need to delineate the unique characteristics of social undermining within nursing contexts. Concept analysis provides a systematic and rigorous approach to clarify ambiguous and inconsistently used concepts.

This study aims to clarify the concept of social undermining in nursing using Walker and Avant’s eight-step concept analysis method.

In this analysis, social undermining is conceptualized as a patterned and cumulative process that unfolds over time through repeated behaviors rather than isolated incidents. It is further distinguished from related constructs based on intentionality, covert versus overt expression, and the nature of interpersonal harm. While workplace incivility typically involves low-intensity and often unintentional disrespect, social undermining is characterized by intentional behaviors aimed at eroding professional relationships and work-related success over time. Although it may occur in both overt and covert forms, the present analysis emphasizes its predominantly covert nature within nursing environments, where professional norms and hierarchical structures often constrain overt expressions of hostility.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a conceptual analysis design based on the eight-step method developed by Walker and Avant [11], which is widely used in nursing theory development. Concept analysis is a systematic and rigorous method for clarifying ambiguous or inconsistently defined concepts, aiming to promote theoretical clarity and enhance research precision. Given the vague and overlapping use of the term social undermining in nursing literature, this method was selected as the most appropriate approach for the present study [6, 7].

This study adopted a theoretical, descriptive, and non-empirical approach to explore the nature, attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents of social undermining in nursing. The goal was to establish a clear, operational definition that distinguishes this concept from related terms such as bullying, incivility, and interpersonal conflict [4, 8].

Steps of concept analysis

The eight-step method of concept analysis proposed by Walker and Avant was applied in this study. First, the concept of social undermining was selected due to its increasing relevance in healthcare settings and the lack of a clear definition in nursing-specific literature [3]. Second, the aim of the analysis was to clarify the meaning and dimensions of social undermining among nurses and to distinguish it from similar concepts.

Third, Dictionary definitions emphasized covert weakening of another person’s position or effectiveness, while scientific literature highlighted intentional behaviors aimed at undermining professional relationships and success. All uses of the concept were identified through a comprehensive review of various sources. This review included English and Persian general and specialized dictionaries such as Cambridge, Merriam-Webster, Oxford, Haim, Moein, and Amid, as well as scholarly literature in the fields of nursing, organizational behavior, and psychology. In Persian dictionaries such as Moein and Amid, the term “undermining” is defined as weakening or incapacitating, often with a covert intent to harm an individual’s reputation or effectiveness [1]. English dictionary definitions were also reviewed to capture the general linguistic meaning of the term.

Fourth, defining attributes were identified through a structured analytical process. Relevant data were extracted from each included source and systematically coded. Codes with similar meanings were grouped into categories, and through iterative comparison across sources, core characteristics were identified and refined into defining attributes. This analytical process was conducted independently by the authors and finalized through discussion and consensus.

Fifth, a model case was developed based on real-life clinical experience in an intensive care unit (ICU) to illustrate all key features of social undermining as experienced by a nurse. Sixth, borderline, related, and contrary cases were examined to establish conceptual boundaries between social undermining and related phenomena such as incivility, bullying, and job burnout [2, 6]. Data sources and literature review

A comprehensive review of literature was conducted in both English and Persian, using peer-reviewed articles published between 2000 and 2025. Databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Scientific Information Database (SID). Inclusion criteria focused on articles addressing social undermining, workplace aggression, and nursing communication. In addition, classic and contemporary English and Persian dictionaries were consulted to explore linguistic and semantic dimensions of the concept. The search strategy included combinations of keywords such as social undermining, nursing, workplace behavior, incivility, and interpersonal conflict.

The initial database search yielded 320 records. After removal of duplicates (n = 85), 235 records remained and were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 190 records were excluded due to lack of relevance to the concept or nursing context. The full texts of the remaining 45 articles were assessed for eligibility, and all 45 were included in the final analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study was based solely on published literature and involved no human participants, data collection, or identifiable personal information; therefore, formal ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

Results

Overview of the concept

The findings of this concept analysis are presented in accordance with Walker and Avant’s eight-step method. The results were derived through systematic analysis and synthesis of the included literature. The findings include defining attributes, antecedents and consequences, empirical referents, conceptual distinctions from related constructs, and illustrative cases.

The following sections present these findings in a structured and sequential manner, providing a comprehensive understanding of how social undermining manifests in nursing practice.

Defining attributes

Social undermining is characterized by several defining attributes.

Initially, it involves intentionality, referring to deliberate actions aimed at harming a colleague’s professional image, relationships, or occupational success [1].

Next, these behaviors are typically covert and indirect, often manifesting in subtle forms such as withholding information, making sarcastic remarks, or excluding colleagues from team activities [5, 7].

Then, social undermining disrupts interpersonal trust and collaboration by weakening professional relationships and impairing communication in clinical settings [3].

Furthermore, it occurs in a patterned and repetitive manner, occurring over time and resulting in cumulative psychological and professional harm [6].

Finally, social undermining is often motivated by emotional or competitive factors such as envy, fear of losing power, or perceived threats [2, 9].

Although social undermining may manifest in both overt and covert forms, the present analysis emphasizes its predominantly covert nature in nursing contexts, where professional norms and hierarchical structures often constrain overt hostility.

Antecedents and consequences

The antecedents of social undermining in nursing include low self-esteem and interpersonal insecurity, perceived competition or threat among colleagues, organizational injustice or lack of managerial support, and workplace envy or hierarchical tension. These conditions create a work environment in which covert and intentional undermining behaviors are more likely to emerge and persist.

The consequences of social undermining are multifaceted and affect both individual nurses and healthcare organizations. Exposure to repeated undermining behaviors may lead to emotional exhaustion, anxiety, and psychological stress, as well as decreased job satisfaction and reduced organizational commitment. At the organizational and clinical levels, social undermining can disrupt teamwork, compromise the quality of patient care, increase withdrawal behaviors, and contribute to turnover intention and diminished professional motivation.

Empirical referents

Empirical referents of social undermining refer to observable indicators that signal the presence of this phenomenon in nursing practice, such as repeated exclusion from professional communication, systematic withholding of critical information, and erosion of interpersonal trust within nursing teams. To operationalize and assess these manifestations, several instruments have been developed. The Social Undermining Scale proposed by Duffy et al. (2002) is a validated instrument that assesses perceived undermining behaviors from supervisors and coworkers. Among available instruments, the Social Undermining Scale has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in nursing and healthcare-related samples, supporting its use as a reference framework for assessing social undermining behaviors.

The Social Undermining Scale was selected due to its conceptual alignment with the defining attributes identified in this analysis and its prior use in healthcare-related research settings.

Distinguishing social undermining from bullying

As presented in Table 1, social undermining is distinguished from several related constructs. The following section further elaborates its distinction from workplace bullying due to its conceptual relevance and overlap. Although social undermining and workplace bullying share certain overlapping features, such as negative interpersonal behaviors and harmful consequences, they represent conceptually distinct phenomena. Bullying is typically characterized by overt, aggressive, and often power-imbalanced behaviors that are visible and confrontational in nature. In contrast, social undermining in nursing is predominantly covert, indirect, and subtle, often occurring through behaviors such as withholding critical information, exclusion from professional interactions, or quietly damaging a colleague’s professional reputation. Furthermore, while bullying frequently involves explicit hostility and intimidation, social undermining may occur in the absence of open aggression and can be embedded within routine professional interactions. This distinction underscores social undermining as a unique form of workplace mistreatment that requires separate conceptualization and targeted interventions within nursing practice.

Model case

A model case represents an example that includes all the defining attributes of the concept.

Example

Sara, an experienced nurse in an intensive care unit, recently received praise from her manager for managing a complex case effectively. Since then, one of her colleagues, who feels threatened by Sara’s recognition, begins to exclude her from critical updates during team handovers, avoids eye contact, makes sarcastic comments about her capabilities in front of others, and withholds necessary patient information. Over time, Sara becomes increasingly anxious and isolated, begins to doubt her clinical decisions, and eventually avoids participating in discussions.

This case demonstrates all five defining attributes: the behaviors are intentional, covert, repetitive, damaging to professional relationships, and motivated by competition.

Discussion

This concept analysis clarified social undermining in nursing as a multidimensional phenomenon characterized by intentional, covert, and repetitive behaviors that negatively affect professional relationships and workplace functioning. By identifying defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents, this study provides a clearer conceptual foundation for understanding social undermining in nursing contexts.

Unlike overt workplace aggression or bullying, social undermining is often hidden yet equally harmful. Previous research in organizational psychology has conceptualized social undermining as intentional behaviors aimed at hindering others’ success and damaging interpersonal relationships over time [1]. Duffy et al. (2002) defined social undermining as behaviors intended to hinder, over time, another person’s ability to establish and maintain positive interpersonal relationships, work-related success, and favorable reputation. Subsequent studies have confirmed the cumulative and covert nature of such behaviors and their detrimental impact on employees’ psychological well-being and job performance [12, 13].

Although workplace bullying and social undermining share some overlapping characteristics, they differ in key aspects such as visibility, intensity, and intentional expression. Bullying is typically characterized by repeated, overt, and aggressive behaviors involving a power imbalance [14], whereas social undermining tends to be more covert, indirect, and strategically enacted over time.

In nursing contexts, workplace incivility has been widely studied as a form of low-intensity deviant behavior that violates norms of mutual respect. Andersson and Pearson described incivility as ambiguous behavior with unclear intent to harm, which distinguishes it from the intentional and strategic nature of social undermining [15]. In healthcare settings, incivility has been associated with decreased teamwork, increased stress, and compromised patient safety [16, 17]. Despite these insights, social undermining remains underdefined as a distinct construct in nursing, leading to gaps in measurement and intervention strategies. Beyond its conceptual distinction, the impact of social undermining on outcomes is particularly important.

In addition to its conceptual distinction, the practical impact of social undermining on individual and organizational outcomes is substantial. The cumulative effects of social undermining, particularly when repetitive and unaddressed, can lead to emotional exhaustion, job dissatisfaction, decreased collaboration, and poor patient outcomes [7, 16]. This underscores the need for nursing managers to foster psychologically safe work environments, as supported by previous studies highlighting the role of leadership in mitigating workplace mistreatment [16, 17].

Cultural and gender-related factors may influence the perception and impact of social undermining in nursing settings. In collectivist cultures, such as those prevalent in Persian-speaking contexts, the emphasis on group harmony and interdependence may amplify the emotional toll of undermining behaviors, as nurses may feel greater pressure to conform and avoid conflict. Similarly, gender dynamics, particularly in female-dominated professions like nursing, may exacerbate the effects of social undermining, as competitive or envious behaviors could be perceived as threats to professional identity. These observations are consistent with prior research highlighting the influence of cultural and gender dynamics on workplace interactions and perceptions of mistreatment [14, 18].

Nursing managers play a pivotal role in mitigating social undermining by fostering psychologically safe work environments. Implementing regular training programs to recognize and address covert undermining behaviors, such as gossip or exclusion, can enhance awareness and promote respectful communication. Additionally, establishing clear reporting mechanisms and policies against undermining behaviors can empower nurses to voice concerns without fear of retaliation. Managers should also cultivate a culture of trust and collaboration through team-building activities and open dialogue, ensuring that competitive or envious dynamics are minimized.

Finally, this analysis provides a foundational step toward establishing a standardized and context-specific understanding of social undermining in nursing. By enhancing conceptual clarity, this study supports the development of valid measurement tools, informs targeted interventions, and contributes to improving workplace environments and patient care outcomes. Future research should focus on developing and validating context-specific instruments, exploring cultural and gender-related differences, and evaluating the effectiveness of organizational strategies to reduce undermining behaviors.

Limitations

This study is limited by its theoretical nature, as it relies on existing literature without empirical data to validate the findings in real-world nursing settings. Concept analyses inherently depend on the quality and scope of available literature, which may introduce bias or incomplete representation of the phenomenon [11].

In addition, the inclusion of only English and Persian sources may have restricted the cultural breadth of the analysis, potentially overlooking context-specific manifestations of social undermining in other settings. Furthermore, as a non-empirical study, causal relationships between social undermining and its outcomes cannot be established. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as conceptual clarifications rather than evidence of direct effects.

Despite these limitations, this analysis provides a theoretically grounded framework that can inform future empirical research and support the development of context-specific measurement tools.

Conclusion

This concept analysis provides a clearer and more comprehensive understanding of social undermining in nursing by identifying its defining attributes, antecedents, and consequences, and by proposing a conceptual model to guide future research and practice.

A precise conceptualization of social undermining is essential for developing valid assessment tools and designing effective interventions aimed at promoting psychologically safe and respectful work environments. Addressing social undermining in nursing has the potential to enhance professional relationships, improve staff well-being, and ultimately contribute to better patient care outcomes.
