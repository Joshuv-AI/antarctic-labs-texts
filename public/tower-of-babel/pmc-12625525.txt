A cost-utility analysis of Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir versus Sofosbuvir/Daclatasvir and Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir for treatment of hepatitis C in Iran
Authors: Mojtaba Jafari, Parisa Mehdizadeh, Khosro Keshavarz, Ehsan Teymourzadeh, Mohammad Ali Abyazi, Seyed Moayed Alavian, Alijanpour Pouria
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesNew antiviral medications for hepatitis C can significantly reduce liver disease risk, and decrease mortality rates and associated costs. The cost-effectiveness of Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB) has not yet been compared with other treatments in Iran, although it has demonstrated effectiveness and cost-effectiveness in other countries such as Japan and Brazil. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the cost-effectiveness of Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir compared with Sofosbuvir/Daclatasvir (SOF/DCV) and Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir (SOF/VEL) in Iran.Matherial and methodsThe analysis was conducted using a Markov model with a one-year cycle in a lifetime horizon from the perspective of the Ministry of Health. Effectiveness was calculated based on Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALY). Costs were based on the direct medical costs (DMC) of Hepatitis C Virus treatment in Iran in 2024. The extraction of effectiveness was based on the results of published valid studies. The extraction of costs was done based on micro-costing and local costing. A cost-effectiveness comparison of the three investigated medication regimens was conducted through incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) and Incremental net benefit (INB). Finally, one-way sensitivity analysis and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) were used to evaluate the uncertainty of the model parameters.ResultsThe study showed that the direct medical costs (DMC) and Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) for GLE/PIB, SOF/DCV, and SOF/VEL were $7505, $5493, $5443, and 21.053, 20.806, and 20.898 QALYs, respectively. The ICER of GLE/PIB compared with SOF/DCV and SOF/VEL was $8138 and 13,282, respectively. The ICER was below the national willingness-to-pay threshold of 18,261 PPP$ (one time the GDP per capita for 2022), indicating that GLE/PIB was a cost-effective treatment. In the sensitivity analysis, the model was most sensitive to some parameters such as the cost of Chronic Hepatitis C (CHC) state for GLE/PIB, the cost of CHC for SOF/VEL, and the Utility of CHC for GLE/PIB and SOF/VEL. In the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, the probability of GLE/PIB being cost-effective compared to SOF/DCV was 56% and compared with SOF/VEL was 53.7%. The acceptability curve also showed that GLE/PIB was the superior choice in 40.6% of simulations based on differential willingness to pay.ConclusionThe results showed that GLE/PIB is cost-effective compared with the two common medication regimens in Iran, SOF/DCV and SOF/VEL, consistent with Iran’s national willingness-to-pay threshold based on one time the GDP per capita, making it a good treatment option for patients with hepatitis C.

Introduction

The Hepatitis C virus (HCV) is a significant public health issue that affects about 15–20% of acute hepatitis cases worldwide. It is primarily transmitted through blood transfusion and injection medication use. It is one of the leading causes of chronic liver diseases, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and liver cancer. According to the estimate of the World Health Organization in 2013, about 130–150 million people in the world have been infected with the hepatitis C virus. In some studies, this number has been reported to be up to 170 million people [1–3].

The prevalence of hepatitis C and its growth trend vary across countries. HCV is decreasing in developed countries and increasing in developing countries [1, 2, 4, 5]. Currently, HCV is classified into eleven different genotypes, of which the type 1 genotype is more common in Eastern societies and accounts for about 70% of all chronic infections [3, 6]. In Iran, the most prevalent HCV genotypes are primarily 1a and 3a, which influences the effectiveness of different treatment regimens (citations added as per domestic epidemiologic studies). Although acute infection with hepatitis C is mild, about 50% to 85% of these patients develop chronic infection [3, 7]. According to the available statistics in Iran, the prevalence of this disease in the general population is about 1–3%, while the latest national estimates in 2018 reported a prevalence rate of 0.3% among the general population [12]. It is higher in people with underlying diseases, whose prevalence is still increasing [8–12]. In addition, according to the studies conducted in Iran, the prevalence of HCV in some provinces is higher than the average worldwide, showing the necessity of investigating, diagnosing, and treating this disease even more [13–15]. Early diagnosis of hepatitis C disease, followed by the use of antiviral medications, can significantly reduce the risk of disease progression, and the development of acute liver diseases, and the mortality rate due to this disease. Therefore, the costs and the spread of this disease are reduced [16, 17].

Advancements in the treatment of hepatitis C have been remarkable in recent years. The new medication regimens, which are more effective, have fewer side effects, require a shorter treatment period, and represent a significant leap forward in patient care. These improvements often come at a higher cost than the older combinations [18, 19]. Among the new medications, are Direct-acting antiviral medications including single-dose and combination medications such as Sofosbuvir, Simeprevir, Sofosbuvir/Ledipasvir, Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir, Sofosbuvir/Daclatasvir, Daclatasvir/Asunaprevir, Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir, etc [20–22].

There have been various studies on the cost-effectiveness of using these medications in different countries that reported different results for this category of medications [7, 16, 23, 24]. In Iran, medication regimens of Sofosbuvir/Ledipasvir, Sofosbuvir/Daclatasvir, and Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir have been compared with each other in terms of cost-effectiveness in various studies [23, 25]. However, the medication regimen of Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir is not available in the Iranian pharmaceutical market despite its high effectiveness [26, 27]. While the cost-effectiveness of Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir has been evaluated in other countries, such as Japan and Brazil [24, 28], it has not yet been compared with other cost-effective treatment methods currently available in Iran. The choice of SOF/DCV and SOF/VEL as comparators is based on their status as the most commonly used regimens in Iran’s national hepatitis C treatment guidelines, reflecting real-world clinical practice and policy relevance.

This study aimed to assess the cost-effectiveness of Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir in comparison with Sofosbuvir/Daclatasvir and Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir in Iran. The documented results are intended to assist policymakers and healthcare decision-makers in determining the inclusion of this medication in Iran’s pharmaceutical market.

Materials and methods

Model Structure The analysis was conducted from the perspective of the Iranian Ministry of Health, focusing on direct medical costs. This cost-utility study aimed to compare three medication regimens, GLE/PIB, SOF/VEL, and SOF/DCV, in treating patients with HCV. Considering that HCV infection is chronic and recurring, a Markov model was developed and implemented for this study, adapted from the structure of a model described in a previous study [29]. Based on previous studies, the disease states chronic hepatitis C (CHC, F0-F3), compensated cirrhosis (CC, F4), decompensated cirrhosis (DC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), liver transplantation (LT), sustained virological response (SVR) and death were defined. A detailed schematic description of the Markov model and the patient pathway through these health states is shown in Fig. 1. Lifetime horizons and one-year Markov cycles were considered in the study to predict the costs and outcomes of the three medication strategies. This annual cycle length was chosen to align with typical clinical follow-up periods for chronic HCV and to provide a balance between model complexity and computational feasibility for the lifetime horizon. A continuity correction was not necessary Due to the one-year cycle length. The model was developed using TreeAge Pro 2020. The costs were presented in terms of PPP$ and utility in terms of QALYs. In the study, costs were discounted at an annual rate of 5.8% [30] and QALYs at 3% [16, 25, 31] to account for time horizons of more than one year. Study population characteristics

The base population in this study was patients with chronic HCV. In Iran, the prevalence rate of hepatitis C was 0.3% among the general population in 2018. It was estimated that about 240,000 people in the general population and about 147,000 people in the adult population were infected with hepatitis C [12]. Therefore, the model simulates a hypothetical cohort of patients entering the model at age 30, which is based on national epidemiologic data on the average age of diagnosis for chronic HCV infection in Iran. A meticulous simulation analysis was performed for a hypothetical cohort of 10,000 people, ensuring the accuracy of our findings. It is explicitly stated that the model simulates a hypothetical cohort of prevalent HCV patients; acute infection and disease transmission dynamics were not modeled due to data limitations and the defined scope focusing on treatment cost-effectiveness.

Model parameters transition probabilities

According to the results of the studies and the relevant expert’s opinions, the patient’s treatment period was considered one year. The transition probabilities between different health states were extracted from the results of internal and external published studies [23–25, 32, 33]. The efficacy of each treatment (SVR rates) was incorporated into these transition probabilities to reflect treatment-specific outcomes. In this model, patients who achieve SVR are assumed to remain in the SVR state for the remainder of their lifetime, with no possibility of relapse. When pooling data from multiple studies for transition probabilities, weighted averages were employed based on study sample size or relevance to the Iranian context. All-cause mortality probabilities based on age and gender were extracted from the life table of Iran in 2020. In addition, the probabilities related to mortality caused by hepatitis C were extracted from the studies [34–36].

Utility values

In this study, QALY was considered as a health outcome. The utility and transition probability of three medication regimens, GLE/PIB, SOF/VEL, and SOF/DCV, were extracted from the results of published studies [23–25, 32, 33]. Utility weights were obtained from published Iranian population studies and incorporated consistently across all treatment arms. According to the book"Relative Value of Health Services and Care in the Islamic Republic of Iran"and the latest document on health service benefits in Iran, the utilization rate of private and public sector services is 20% to 80% for inpatient care and 65% to 35% for outpatient care. Except for the price of GLE/PIB medication, other costs are calculated firstly based on the tariffs approved by the Ministry of Health, Treatment and Medical Education of Iran in 2024 and calculated in terms of Rials. Then based on the PPP (purchasing power parity) dollar rate (one dollar is equal to 64529 Rials) [37] converted and entered into the model. Due to the unavailability of GLE/PIB medication in Iran, the price of this medication is estimated based on the retail rate in Turkey plus a profit margin, as instructed by the Food and Drug Organization of Iran in 2024. This estimation method introduces a degree of uncertainty due to reliance on external pricing and markups, and its potential impact is further explored in the sensitivity analysis. The PPP$ calculations include notes on inflation adjustments to account for currency fluctuations over the lifetime horizon, ensuring costs are presented consistently.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

The modeling in this study was conducted using the Tree Age Pro 2020 software. A Markov model was designed based on the disease health states. The utility data, transition probabilities, and costs extracted in the previous steps were entered into the model. The costs were calculated in dollars (PPP$), and the effectiveness was measured in QALY. Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) and Incremental Net Benefit (INB) indexes were used to compare the cost-effectiveness of the three medication strategies. The formula for Incremental Net Benefit (INB) was calculated using the formula INB = K * ΔE - ΔC, where ΔE represented the incremental effectiveness, ΔC the incremental cost, and K the WTP threshold.

Sensitivity analysis

One-way sensitivity analysis and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) were used to evaluate the uncertainty of model parameters. In the one-way sensitivity analysis, key parameters of the model were changed, and the resulting analysis outcomes are shown in the tornado diagram. The cost variables were changed within ± 20%. The model’s utility, effectiveness, and transition probabilities were also changed in the confidence interval of ± 10% in the studies, and the corresponding ICER was calculated. These fixed ranges were applied to uniformly explore the robustness of the results to significant, predetermined variations in key parameters, allowing for a standardized assessment of parameter influence, complementing the PSA. Also, in the PSA according to the Gamma distribution for costs and Beta distribution for effectiveness and transition probabilities used in the model. A Monte Carlo simulation was performed using 10,000 iterations to investigate the effect of uncertainty of all variables on ICER per QALY. The PSA results were displayed based on the cost-effectiveness acceptability curve and scatter plot. Due to the lack of a specific willingness to pay (WTP) threshold for each QALY in Iran, according to the WHO recommendation for developing countries, the willingness to pay (WTP) threshold in this study is one time the GDP per capita. Iran’s GDP in 2022 was considered 18,261 PPP$, according to the latest information from the World Bank [38].

Results

Base-case results

Our cost-utility model analysis reveals that the average total costs of patients with hepatitis C under the SOF/DCV medication regimen amount to $5493 per patient during the treatment period. Similarly, the costs for the SOF/VEL medication regimen are $5443, showing a modest difference. In contrast, the GLE/PIB treatment regimen incurs significantly higher costs, averaging $7505 per patient during the treatment period, which is $2012 and $2062 more than the SOF/DCV and SOF/VEL medication regimens. The GLE/PIB medication regimen, showed the highest effectiveness with 0.247 more QALYs. The ICER related to SOF/VEL compared to SOF/DCV was negative and is estimated to be −543 PPP$. This negative ICER indicates that the SOF/VEL medication regimen is dominant, meaning it is both more effective and less costly compared with the SOF/DCV medication regimen. Therefore, to determine the cost-effectiveness status of these two interventions, there is no need to compare the ICER with the cost-effectiveness threshold. The GLE/PIB medication regimen has more cost and effectiveness than SOF/VEL, and the cost of incremental utility or the corresponding ICER is equal to 13282 PPP$. This value is compared with the national willingness-to-pay threshold of 18,261 PPP$ (one time the GDP per capita for 2022. Since the ICER value for the GLE/PIB medication regimen is less than this threshold, this treatment regimen is more cost-effective in patients with HCV than the SOF/VEL regimen. One-way sensitivity analysis

One-way sensitivity analysis was used to find the factors affecting the study results. In this method, the study variables are usually changed at a specific interval, and their effect on the study result is evaluated. The cost variables were changed within ± 20%. The model’s utility, effectiveness, and transition probabilities were also changed in the confidence interval of ± 10% in the studies, and the corresponding ICER was calculated. These fixed ranges were applied to uniformly explore the robustness of the results to significant, predetermined variations in key parameters, allowing for a standardized assessment of parameter influence, complementing the PSA. The changes in all variables from the study results in the Tornado diagram are shown in Figs. 3 and 4. Figure 3 shows that the model is indeed sensitive to specific parameters. Specifically, it shows the highest sensitivities to the cost of CHC state for GLE/PIB medication(cCHC_gs), the cost of CHC state for SOF/VEL (cCHC_vs) medication, and the utility of CHC state for GLE/PIB (effCHC_gs) and SOF/VEL (effCHC_vs) medication. Changing these parameters may change the study results and push the calculated ICER above the threshold. However, changing other model parameters and a 20% increase in the cost of other states, the ICER value stays below the threshold. Consequently, there will be no significant change in the study results.Fig. 3Tornado diagram of one-way sensitivity analysis for two medication regimens Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir and Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir in patients with hepatitis CFig. 4Tornado diagram of one-way sensitivity analysis for two medication regimens Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir and Sofosbuvir/Daclatasvir in patients with hepatitis C

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA)

This study also used Monte Carlo simulation (possible sensitivity analysis) to analyze the model results. Using this method, the incremental cost-effectiveness scatter plot and the cost-effectiveness acceptability curve could be drawn in different states of replacing the variables in the model. All cost variables were distributed in Gamma distribution with a standard deviation of 20%. Clinical variables and utility were estimated in the range (according to experts or treatment guidelines) or without an estimated range with a standard deviation of 10% and were distributed as a Beta distribution. In addition, the amount of data distribution of transition probabilities was determined as Beta distribution up to 10% (Figs. 5 and 6). This modeling is estimated for 10,000 iterations. Discussion

This study is the first cost-effectiveness analysis comparing the GLE/PIB medication regimen with two commonly used and nationally recognized hepatitis C treatment methods in Iran (SOF/VEL and SOF/DCV). Developed using a Markov model over a lifetime horizon from the perspective of the Ministry of Health, effectiveness was assessed using QALYs derived from valid studies. Direct costs of HCV disease treatment were calculated based on micro-costing and local costing in Iran in 2024. The cost-effectiveness of the three medication regimens was compared through ICER and INB, with one-way sensitivity analysis and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) used to evaluate model uncertainty. The results showed that for the CHC state, the medication price constituted a 44.11% share of the total cost for GLE/PIB, compared to 13.1% for SOF/VEL and 5.64% for SOF/DCV. The total prices for GLE/PIB, SOF/VEL, and SOF/DCV medications in a treatment period were approximately $6410, $1202, and $485, respectively. According to the analysis of the cost-utility model, the average total cost of hepatitis C treatment for SOF/DCV, SOF/VEL, and GLE/PIB was 5493 PPP$, 5443 PPP$, and 7505 PPP$ respectively. This is inconsistent with the results of the Ferreira 2020 study [28], in which GLE/PIB had lower treatment costs than other treatment methods. These differences in PPP$ values compared to Ferreira et al. (2020) are attributed to localized costing and drug pricing in Iran, as well as methodological differences. In addition, SOF/DCV has the lowest efficacy, followed by SOF/VEL with 0.092 more QALY and GLE/PIB with 0.247 more QALY, which is consistent with the results of Kawaguchi et al. 2020 [24]. Compared with SOF/VEL, the GLE/PIB medication regimen has more cost and QALY, and its incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) is 13,282 PPP$, which is below the WTP threshold (18261). In addition, the ICER for GLE/PIB compared with SOF/DCV was 8138 PPP$, making GLE/PIB a cost-effective treatment for HCV patients compared to SOF/DCV and SOF/VEL. In this sense, it is consistent with the results of the Ferreira 2020 [28] and Kawaguchi 2020 [24] studies, but not with the results of the Gordon 2020 study [42], in which SOF/VEL followed by SOF/VEL/VOX in the CHC State condition is cost-effectiveness option compared to GLE/PIB. Other cost-effectiveness studies conducted elsewhere compared GLE/PIB with other therapies such as PegIFN + RBV [33], Elbasvir/Grazoprevir [24, 32, 43], and SOF-based regimens including SOF/Ledipasvir [42]. They have reported different results for GLE/PIB. While SOF/Ledipasvir is also used in Iran, it was not included as a primary comparator in this study Due to a focus on the most commonly used regimens in national protocols. According to the analyses performed in the one-way and tornado diagram with changes of 20% of costs and 10% of utility and transition probabilities, in comparing GLE/PIB and SOF/VEL, the model was sensitive to some parameters, including CHC cost and Utility For the GLE/PIB and SOF/VEL medication. Changing these parameters may affect the results of the study and make the calculated ICER higher than the WTP threshold. However, the ICER value remained below the threshold by changing the other Parameters in the model. In addition, in the GLE/PIB and SOF/DCV comparisons, with changing cost, utility, and transition probabilities, ICER stays below the threshold. Then, the study result will remain the same. In the Monte Carlo analysis, the GLE/PIB treatment strategy compared with SOF/VEL and SOF/DCV was selected as the best option in 53.7% and 56% of simulation ICER results, respectively.

Study limitations

One of the study’s limitations was the reliance on external studies to determine the effectiveness of GLE/PIB medications compared to SOF/VEL and SOF/DCV, and to extract probabilities related to different disease states, due to a lack of sufficient internal studies. The unavailability of GLE/PIB medicine within the country significantly affected the study, as its price was calculated based on the retail price in Turkey plus a profit margin, introducing an element of uncertainty. Additionally, the study did not explicitly incorporate medication side effects into the model due to a lack of comprehensive localized information, and the assumption of full treatment adherence and clinical trial SVR rates may not perfectly reflect real-world Iranian settings.

Generalizability

In this study, the incidence and prevalence rate, payment system, localization of costs, and national willingness-to-pay threshold were based on internal information from Iran. The results of this study can be generalized to the treatment and health systems of Iran, as well as its policy. Health sector policymakers can use it to decide whether to enter GLE/PIB medicine into the Iranian pharmaceutical market or to compare or use existing treatment methods. Regarding the generalization of the results in other countries, the localized inputs (e.g., costs, genotypes, WTP thresholds) used in this study should be carefully considered before extrapolation.

Conclusions

The overall results showed that the GLE/PIB medication regimen, despite the high price compared with two medication regimens, SOF/DCV and SOF/VEL, can increase SVR and QALY, and reduce the probability of transition to a worse disease state and death. Moreover, it has become a practical option for the treatment of patients with hepatitis C.
