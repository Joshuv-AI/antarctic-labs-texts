Transcriptome analysis of different developmental stages of Tilletia controversa revealed Chitin synthases genes for growth
Authors: Zhaoyu Ren, Yan Wang, Huanyu Jia, Taiguo Liu, Wanquan Chen, Li Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This is the first study on the transcriptome signature of Tilletia controversa Kühn at different developmental stages. Results showed that the gene expression patterns differed at different stages of hyphal development and the number of gene expressions is continuously increasing. Chitin synthases genes TcChs1 and TcChs2 were continuously upregulated and the deletion of TcChs1 and TcChs2 lead to the decline of growth rate, ability to resist osmotic stress, peroxidase stress, and the cell wall inhibitor Congo red. Collectively, this research firstly advances the understanding of the hyphal development of T. controversa and provides reference information for the control.

Introduction

Wheat is an important food crop that is distributed across most agricultural areas and has a greater impact on global food security than any other crop (Chen et al., 2016; Patil et al., 2016; Wingen et al., 2017; Campos et al., 2018). Dwarf bunt of wheat (DB) is a devastating quarantine disease in wheat caused by Tilletia controversa Kühn, which seriously affects the yield and quality of wheat (Purdy et al., 1963). The disease is distributed throughout the winter wheat regions of the world and can be spread through soil as well as seeds (Trione, 1982). The wheat grains are replaced by numerous black teliospores and emit a fishy smell due to the presence of trimethylamine (Borgen, 2004; Gaudet et al., 2007). To date, research on this disease has mainly focused on detection of the pathogen, and no effective control method has been reported.

Transcriptomics focuses on the type, structure, function and regulation of all transcripts in the transcriptome of a specific organism (Lockhart and Winzeler, 2000; Wang et al., 2009). RNA-Seq has been widely used to discover the key pathways and development-related gene profile in different developmental stages of fungi. Kijpornyongpan and Aime (2021) compared transcriptomic profiles during both yeast and hyphal growth in Tilletiopsis washingtonensis, Meira miltonrushii and Ustolago maydis, results showed that the genes of cell energy production and transformation, amino acid transport and metabolism, and cytoskeleton were significantly enrich in the genes commonly expressed in the three species, and only the genes encoding cell terminal marker Tea4/TeaC and kinesin kinetin Kin3 were uniformly expressed in the three species, indicating that there were differences in the species specific mechanisms of two morphological transformations and mycelial growth. Li et al. (2019) analyzed the developmental transcriptome of Ophiocordycesia sinensis in six stages: hypha (HY), sclerotia (ST), primordium (PR), young fruiting body (YF), developmental fruiting body (DF) and mature fruiting body (MF), the results showed that more DEGs was found between ST and HY stages, and the modules of co-expressed genes and candidate central genes were determined for each development stage, which provided new information to existing models of development in fungi. Krizsán et al. (2019) constructed a reference atlas of mushroom formation based on developmental transcriptome data of six species to elucidate the core genetic program of complex multicellularity and fruiting body development in mushroom-forming fungi (Agaricomycetes), results showed that F-box protein, expansin like protein, protein kinase and transcription factor were amplified in Agaricomycetes, provides an entry point for the study of fungi development and the complex multicellular nature of eukaryotes. Kim et al. (2019) performed transcriptomic analysis across five stages of perithecium development in Fusarium neosmosporaellum to better understand the metabolic potential for sexual development and gain insight into its life history, results provided novel insights into the genomic underpinning of development in F. neocosmosporiellum, which may help elucidate its ability to occupy diverse ecological niches. Ren et al. (2020) compared the changes in gene expression of wheat after T. controversa infection, and the results showed that genes related to disease defense such as PR-related genes, WRKY transcription factors, and mitogen-activated protein kinase genes were upregulated after T. controversa infection. However, there is no report on the changes in gene expression in the key developmental stages of hyphal development of T. controversa promyceliu, primary sporidia, secondary sporidia production (Fig. 1). First stage, the teliospores germinate by extending a promyceliu, second stage, the promyceliu continue to develop and form a broom like primary sporidia, third stage, the hyphal produces crescent shaped secondary sporidia, which are considered to be fragile and short lived, causes disease in wheat (Purdy et al., 1963; Goates, 2010).Fig. 1Microscopic observation of hyphae at 3 developmental stages. For most fungal cells, chitin is the core structure of the cell wall and is widely distributed in filamentous fungi (Bulawa, 1993; Bernard and Latgé, 2001; Latgé, 2007). The biosynthesis of chitin is crucial for the survival and reproduction of pathogenic fungi (Chen et al., 2022). Studies on the chitin biosynthetic pathway can provide new targets for the green control of plant pathogens (Cortés et al., 2019). Chitin synthase, a member of the glycosyltransferase family 2 (GT2), is the core of the chitin biosynthetic machinery (Zhu et al., 2016). The number of chitin synthases varies significantly among different fungi. In yeasts, chitin synthases 1–3 have been identified, while filamentous fungi typically contain approximately 7 chitin synthases, and some species even possess >20 (Ruiz-Herrera and Ortiz-Castellanos, 2010). Weber et al. (2006) conducted functional studies on the eight chitin synthases (CHSs) from Ustilago maydis and reported that chs7 and chs5 are essential for morphogenesis ex planta, while Mcs1 acts as a key factor during plants infection. CHS5 and CHS7 from Fusarium verticillioides are crucial for the normal hyphal growth and pathogenicity, the pathogenicity of gene deletion mutants ΔCHS5 and ΔCHS7 strains is significantly reduced (Larson et al., 2011). In addition, in filamentous fungi such as Aspergillus nidulans, Colletrotrichum graminicola, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Neurospora crassa, it has been reported that CHSs can support hyphal tip growth and promote fungal morphogenesis (Takeshita et al., 2002; Amnuaykanjanasin and Epstein, 2003; Mellado et al., 2003; Fajardo-Somera et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2017).

In this study, RNA-Seq was performed to analyze the changes in gene expression between different developmental stages of T. controversa hyphae, and differentially expressed CHSs were identified, which provides a theoretical basis for the control of T. controversa.

Methods

Fungal materials and culture

The isolate of T. controversa was gifted from Blair Goates, the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), Agricultural Research Service (ARS), Aberdeen, Idaho, USA. Since T. controversa, T. laevis and T. caries were closely related, we used the specific SCAR primers TCKSF3/TCKSR3 of T. controversa (Gao et al., 2010), the specific SCAR primers L57F/L57R of T. laevis (Xu et al., 2020), and the specific SCAR primers Erc 19F/Erc 19R of T. caries (Ren et al., 2022) to identify the teliospores and then cultured in an incubator (MLR 352 H, Panasonic, USA) at 4 °C under a 24-h light cycle. After 3 weeks, teliospore germination and hyphal production were observed under an automatic inverted fluorescence microscope (IX83, Olympus, Japan). On days 30, 40, and 60, hyphae of 3 key stages of promycelia, primary sporidia, and secondary basidiomycetes were collected (Fig. 1). There were 3 sets of replicate samples for each stage (Table 3).

RNA extraction and strand-specific RNA library preparation

Total RNA was extracted using the mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit (Ambion) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7 were subjected to subsequent analysis. The libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. These libraries were then sequenced on the Illumina sequencing platform (HiSeq 2500 or Illumina HiSeq X Ten), and 125 bp/150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

Quality control and mapping

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw reads were processed using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al., 2014). The reads containing poly-N and low-quality reads were removed to obtain clean reads. The clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2015).

Gene-level and transcript-level quantification, analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), cluster analysis, GO and KEGG enrichment

The FPKM (Roberts et al., 2011) value of each gene was calculated using Cufflinks (http://cufflinks.cbcb.umd.edu/) (Trapnell et al., 2010), and the read counts of each gene were obtained using HTSeq-count (Anders et al., 2015). DEGs were identified using the DESeq (2012) functions estimateSizeFactors and nbinomTest (Anders and Huber, 2013). P-value < 0.05 and fold-change >2 or fold-change < 0.5 were set as the thresholds for significantly differential expression. A hierarchical cluster analysis of DEGs was performed to explore gene expression patterns. GO enrichment and KEGG (Kanehisa et al., 2008) pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using R based on the hypergeometric distribution. For transcript-level quantification, the FPKM and read count values of each transcript (protein coding) were calculated using bowtie2 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012) and eXpress (Roberts and Pachter, 2013).

Validation of RNA-Seq results by quantitative real‑time PCR analysis

Four transcripts with various expression levels demonstrated by RNA sequencing were randomly selected for qRT-PCR to varify RNA-Seq results. Total RNA was extracted and purified from three T. controversa developmental stages, respectively. First-strand cDNA was synthesized by using 1000 ng/µL total RNA, RT/RI enzyme and oligo (dT)18 primer (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) following the instructions of the manufacturer. The primers were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and listed in Table S1. qPCR was performed by the SLightCycler480 II (Roche, Switzerland) with a volume of 10 µL, including:5 µL 2 × SybrGreen qPCR Master Mix, 0.2 µL of each primers, 1 µL cDNA and 3.6 µL ddH2O. Three technical replicates were employed for every gene. The program settings were as follows:3 min 95 °C followed by 45 cycles of 5 s 95 °C and 30 s 60 °C.

Fungal gene deletion

The hyphae of T. controversa was hydrolyzed by enzymatic hydrolysis mixture (1.5 % Driselase, 1.5 % Snailase and 1.5 % Lywallzyme in 1.2 mol/L KCl) at 28 °C, 180 rpm for 2.5 h to obtain protoplasts (Shen et al., 2018). ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China) was used to ligate the upstream and downstream flanking sequences of TcChs1 and TcCHs2 with hygromycin phosphotransferase (hph) fragments in pUC19 vector. The recombinant fragments were then co-introduced into protoplasts of T. controversa and screen on hygromycin-resistant plates.

Results

Identification of T. controversa

The specific SCAR primers TCKSF3/TCKSR3 of T. controversa (Gao et al., 2010), the specific SCAR primers L57F/L57R of T. laevis (Xu et al., 2020), and the specific SCAR primers Erc 19F/Erc 19R of T. caries (Ren et al., 2022) was used to identify the teliospores samples. Results showed that primers TCKSF3/TCKSR3 amplified a 419 bp DNA fragment from teliospores samples, while no products amplified by SCAR primers L57F/L57R and Erc 19F/Erc 19R (Fig. S1), indicating the teliospores samples were T. controversa.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

DESeq2 software was used to standardize the counts of each sample gene (the expression level was estimated by BaseMean), the fold difference was calculated, and the significance of the difference was tested by an NB (negative binomial distribution test). Finally, the DEGs were screened according to the fold difference and the results of the significance test (Table S3). There were 980 DEGs (515 upregulated and 465 downregulated) between S2 and S1, whereas there were 1908 DEGs (1284 upregulated and 624 downregulated) between S3 and S2 (Fig. 2D). The number of differentially expressed genes increased, and the number of upregulated genes was greater than the number of downregulated genes.

Hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) was performed to elucidate gene expression changes at different hyphal developmental stages (Fig. 3). The results showed that gene expression patterns differed at different stages of hyphal development and the differences increased with the continuous development of hyphae, whereas the expression patterns of biological replicates from the same developmental period were the same.Fig. 3Hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) of DEGs between the three developmental stages of T. controversa. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs

To describe the functions of DEGs, GO enrichment analysis was performed for three main domains:biological processes, cellular components and molecular functions (Fig. 4). In a comparison of S2 and S1, DEGs enriched in positive regulation of helicase activity, pyrimidine dimer repair, mismatch repair complex and DNA-dependent ATPase activity related to DNA damage repair and maintenance of genomic stability, suggesting S2 stage undergo DNA replication and recombination events, leading to extensive DNA damage and structural changes; DEGs enriched in four-way junction DNA binding and heteroduplex DNA loop binding associated with the meiotic process, indicating that the S2 stage may be undergoing meiosis; chronological cell aging associated with fungal differentiation and developmental transitions, ensuring the progression of developmental processes. These metabolic pathways collectively reflect that the S2 stage ensures the accurate transmission and diversity of genetic material, while completing the differentiation and renewal of the cell itself, thus laying the foundation for subsequent life cycles. In a comparison of S3 and S2, DEGs enriched in pathogenesis indicating a large number of genes associated with host invasion and disease causation are significantly activated; To counteract pathogen infection, plants activate their defense systems and release reactive oxygen species (ROS) as well as toxic aldehydes to attack the pathogens, DEGs enriched in cellular detoxification of aldehyde suggesting the pathogen has prepared a detoxification system in advance to counteract the host immune system; DEGs enriched in Hexose transmembrane transport, glucose transmembrane transport, and carbohydrate:proton symporter activity indicate that the pathogen has prepared for the rapid uptake of carbon sources and energy from the environment during the S3 stage; The enrichment of cellular components such as the extracellular region, plasma membrane, and integral components of the membrane suggests that the pathogen begins to secrete effectors in S3 stages. Phylogenetic analysis of TcChs1 and TcCHs2

Chitin, which accounts for 10 % to 20 % of fungal cell wall components, is a backbone component that covalently cross-links glucans and glycoproteins together to form a complex network structure. For most fungal cells, chitin is the core structure of the cell wall and is widely distributed in filamentous fungi (Bulawa, 1993; Bernard and Latgé, 2001; Latgé, 2007; Bowman et al., 2010). Chitin synthase (CHS) plays an important role in maintaining the normal growth and development of strains, sporulation, cell wall integrity and other physiological and biochemical properties and also has a certain impact on the pathogenicity of pathogenic fungi (Roncero, 2002; Liu et al., 2004; Ruiz-Herrera et al., 2002; Lee et al., 2004). In this study, TcChs1 (A4 × 06_0g6402) and TcChs2 (A4 × 06_0g7099) were upregulated with the development of T. controversa (Table S3). Phylogenetic analysis showed that the homologous proteins of TcChs1 and TcChs2 widely exist in many Tilletia spp. and could be clustered together, indicating that the functions of these two proteins are conserved in Tilletia spp. (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Phylogenetic analysis of TcChs1 and TcChs2. A4 × 06_0g6402: TcChs1; A4 × 06_0g7099: TcChs2.Fig 6

In order to investigate the effect of TcChs1 and TcChs2 on the growth of T. controversa, ΔTcChs1 and ΔTcChs2 were generated (Fig. S2). The △TcChs1 and △TcChs2 strains were then cultured in CM medium, and the colony diameter was counted after 7 d of dark culture. The wild type was used as the control (Fig. 7A). Discussion

In this study, we performed RNA-Seq for 3 developmental stages in T. controversa hyphae, and a total of 59.69 Gb of clean data were obtained (Table 1). The expression levels of genes at different developmental stages of T. controversa hyphae were analyzed, and the results demonstrated that significantly differentially expressed genes existed among the different developmental stages of T. controversa hyphae (Table S3). Because there were differences in the number of genes expressed and the distribution of gene expression values in the samples, we divided the sample expression value (FPKM) into multiple intervals and calculated the number of genes expressed in the samples for the different expression intervals, which are shown in Fig. 2A. The DEGs had different distribution patterns at different hyphal developmental stages. Sample to sample distances analysis indicating the good repeatability of our date (Fig. 2B). PCA was performed on all samples, and the results showed that the samples of the 3 different stages were well separated, indicating that genes have different expression patterns at different developmental stages (Fig. 2C). Statistical analysis of the DEGs found that the number of DEGs increased with hyphal development, and the number of upregulated genes was greater than the number of downregulated genes in the third stage (Fig. 2D), indicating more genes are involved in the production of secondary sporidia. HCA analysis indicating the gene expression changes at different hyphal developmental stages and more genes upregulated in the third stages (Fig. 3). GO enrichment and KEGG analysis showed that indicates the transition of the pathogen from the vegetative growth stage to the mycelial infection stage (Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

CHS has an essential role in fungi (Henar Valdivieso, 1999; Schmidt et al., 2002; Schmidt, 2004). In this study, 2 DEGs of CHS were identified to have continuously upregulated expression patterns. Phylogenetic tree analysis showed that TcChs1 and TcChs2 were conserved in Tilletia spp (Fig. 6), indicating the two genes were closely related to the haphal development of Tilletia spp.. Zhang et al. (2016) characterized the function of FgCHS8 in Fusarium graminearum and found that the deletion of the FgCHS8 resulted in inhibited growth of the pathogen and pathogenicity. Silencing of chitin synthase 7 (Chs7) in F. graminearum results in inhibition of mycelial growth (Yang et al., 2021). In this study, we also found that the deletion of TcChs1 and TcChs2 led to a decline in growth rate and the ability to resist congo-red, reactive oxygen and osmotic stress (Fig. 7). Xu et al. (2010) reported that deletion of CHS1 in F. asiaticum resulted in a significant reduction in pathogenicity. Zhao et al. (2018) use the CHS1 gene of Sitobion avenae as the target, significantly enhanced wheat resistance to S. avenae. In the future, we will also use CHS as a target to provide reference information for achieving effective prevention and control of T. controversa.

In conclusion, we analyzed the transcriptome data of T. controversa at three key stages of growth and development and identified differentially expressed genes, which facilitates an understanding of the developmental process of T. controversa and provides a theoretical basis for control of the disease.
