Betulinic acid as a novel AT1R inhibitor: attenuation of liver fibrosis via modulation of endothelial–mesenchymal transition in chronic hepatic injury
Authors: Bingwen Zhu, Xiyu Dai, Chunyan Liu, Tao Bi, Shenglu Liu, Lei Chen, Ting Wang, Qixin Zhao, Xinyue Liu, Qin Sun, Yingcheng Yang, Zengjin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) has emerged as a pivotal pathological process during liver fibrosis. Angiotensin II receptor type 1 (AT1R) has been implicated in the regulation of EndMT and is considered a promising target for therapeutic intervention. Consequently, the identification of specific AT1R inhibitors may offer a novel approach to alleviate the liver fibrosis.PurposeThis study aims to evaluate the potential of Betulinic Acid (BA) as a natural AT1R inhibitor and investigate its underlying mechanisms in the context of EndMT and liver fibrosis treatment.MethodThe binding interaction between BA and AT1R was evaluated using AlphaFold 3 (AF3) predictions, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and in vivo validation through At1r gene knockout in animals. A mouse model of liver fibrosis, induced by a Western diet combined with CCl4, along with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) treated with angiotensin II (Ang II), were utilized to assess the therapeutic efficacy of BA in the context of liver fibrosis and EndMT. To elucidate the pharmacological mechanisms underlying BA’s inhibition of EndMT, analyses including RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), hepatic endothelial cell magnetic bead sorting, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence staining were employed.ResultAF3 and MD simulations revealed stable binding between BA and AT1R, which was further confirmed in the At1r knockout mouse model. Pharmacodynamic studies indicated that long-term oral administration of BA did not induce significant pathological alterations in liver and kidney functions, blood routine, or major organs in mice. However, notable improvements in liver fibrosis-related pathological indicators were observed. Further pharmacological analysis demonstrated that BA treatment substantially inhibited EndMT in the context of liver fibrosis, with the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway in endothelial cells identified as a potential mechanism.ConclusionThis study presents the first evidence that BA alleviates liver fibrosis by acting as an AT1R antagonist to inhibit EndMT. These findings underscore the potential therapeutic application of BA as an AT1R inhibitor in the management of liver fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07362-w.Keywords: Betulinic acid, AT1R, EndMT, Liver fibrosis, Chronic hepatic injury

Introduction

Liver fibrosis is a pathological process intrinsically linked to tissue repair and wound healing, representing the liver’s response to a wide array of chronic insults, including viral hepatitis infection, alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), cholestatic disorders, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) [1–3]. Among patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), liver fibrosis is particularly prevalent and may progress to MASH, a more severe and fibrogenic subtype [4, 5]. Currently, the global prevalence of MASLD is estimated at approximately 32.4% and is steadily increasing [6]. This epidemiological shift underscores MASLD as an emerging contributor to the global burden of chronic liver disease [7]. MASH is regarded as the progressive phenotype of MASLD and is histopathologically characterized by macrovesicular steatosis, lobular inflammation, hepatocellular injury—manifested notably as ballooning degeneration—and varying degrees of fibrosis [8–10]. The pathogenesis of liver fibrosis is primarily driven by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) components and subsequent scar tissue formation [11]. Dysregulated ECM accumulation disrupts hepatic architecture and function, and is recognized as a pivotal mediator of disease progression [12]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at inhibiting ECM production and remodeling have garnered significant attention as potential interventions for the prevention and treatment of liver fibrosis.

During liver fibrosis, myofibroblasts serve as the principal source of ECM deposition [13, 14]. However, the precise cellular origin of these activated myofibroblasts remains a subject of ongoing debate [15, 16]. Emerging evidence suggests that endothelial cells (ECs) contribute to fibrogenesis through a process known as endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), wherein ECs acquire mesenchymal characteristics and transdifferentiate into myofibroblast-like cells [17–19]. EndMT is a dynamic and tightly regulated biological process in which endothelial cells, in response to pathological stimuli, lose their endothelial identity—characterized by the downregulation of markers such as platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1/CD31)—and acquire mesenchymal traits, including enhanced contractility and increased collagen synthesis [20, 21]. This process has been implicated in the pathogenesis of fibrosis across multiple organs, including the liver, kidneys, lungs, and heart [22, 23]. Notably, in addition to undergoing complete mesenchymal conversion, a subset of ECs can persist in a plastic, reversible transitional state termed partial EndMT [24]. The reversibility of this intermediate phenotype under specific pathological or therapeutic conditions highlights EndMT as a promising therapeutic target in fibrotic disease. In our recent study, we identified angiotensin II as a pivotal upstream regulator of EndMT in liver fibrosis [25]. Pharmacological inhibition of its type I receptor (AT1R) with a selective small-molecule antagonist effectively suppressed EndMT-associated signaling pathways, leading to a marked attenuation of fibrotic progression [25]. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting the angiotensin II–AT1R axis to mitigate EndMT-mediated fibrogenesis and offer novel insights into anti-fibrotic intervention strategies.

Angiotensin II (Ang II), a pivotal effector peptide of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), exerts critical regulatory functions in the modulation of systemic blood pressure, electrolyte homeostasis, and the pathogenesis of various disease states [26, 27]. Accumulating evidence indicates that elevated circulating or tissue levels of Ang II markedly accelerate the progression of hepatic fibrosis by orchestrating pro-inflammatory signaling cascades, augmenting oxidative stress, and inducing endothelial dysfunction [28–30]. The biological activity of Ang II is primarily mediated through its interaction with two distinct G protein-coupled receptors: AT1R and type 2 receptor (AT2R) [31, 32]. Among these, AT1R is predominantly implicated in the fibrogenic response within hepatic tissue [32, 33]. Mechanistically, the engagement of Ang II with AT1R initiates the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), promoting their transdifferentiation into a myofibroblast-like phenotype, which subsequently contributes to inflammatory milieu amplification and excessive ECM deposition, including collagen fibrils—hallmarks of hepatic fibrogenesis [30, 34]. Furthermore, emerging studies have elucidated that the Ang II–AT1R signaling axis exacerbates liver fibrosis not only by direct HSC activation but also through the induction of EndMT [25]. This multifaceted involvement of AT1R underscores its centrality in the fibrotic cascade. Clinically, several small-molecule AT1R antagonists—such as losartan, valsartan, and olmesartan—have demonstrated favorable therapeutic outcomes in the management of hypertension and cardiovascular disorders [35–37]. Nonetheless, their pharmacokinetic limitations, particularly suboptimal hepatic bioavailability and rapid systemic metabolism, constrain their applicability in targeting liver-specific pathologies [38]. Consequently, the rational design and development of novel AT1R-targeted small-molecule inhibitors with better therapeutic effects on liver fibrosis. Such advancements could pave the way for a paradigm shift toward AT1R-centered therapeutic strategies for chronic liver diseases.

Betulinic acid (BA) is a naturally occurring triterpenoid compound widely distributed across the plant kingdom, primarily isolated from traditional Chinese medicinal herbs such as birch bark and Ziziphus jujuba seeds [39, 40]. BA exhibits a broad spectrum of pharmacological properties, including anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory, antiretroviral, and antimalarial activities [41–43]. Moreover, it demonstrates potential anti-obesity and anti-diabetic effects and has been reported to confer hepatoprotective benefits [44]. Previous studies have shown that BA mitigates liver fibrosis by attenuating ethanol-induced HSC activation through the suppression of ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokine production [45]. Additionally, BA exerts anti-fibrotic effects by inhibiting the nuclear translocation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) [46]. However, the underlying mechanisms of BA in liver fibrosis remain incompletely understood, particularly with respect to its regulatory role in AT1R-mediated EndMT, which warrants further investigation. This study aims to evaluate the therapeutic potential of BA as a novel small-molecule inhibitor targeting AT1R and to elucidate its role in modulating EndMT and ameliorating the progression of liver fibrosis.

Analytical workflow for single-cell transcriptomic data

Following quality control procedures, single-cell transcriptomic data (scRNA-seq) were processed and analyzed using the Seurat R package (version 4.1.1). Data normalization was performed using the NormalizeData function with default parameters. Highly variable genes were identified via the FindVariableFeatures function. Cell type annotation was conducted manually based on canonical markers curated from the CellMarker 2.0 database. Gene expression patterns were visualized using dimensionality reduction through EigenPlot.

Structural preparation of proteins and ligands

The primary amino acid sequence of angiotensin II receptor type 1 (AGTR1) was retrieved from the UniProt database (UniProt ID: P30556) and utilized as the input for subsequent structural modeling and molecular docking analyses. The two-dimensional (2D) chemical structure of the small-molecule ligand Betulinic acid was obtained from the PubChem database (Compound CID: 64971). This structure was further translated into its corresponding SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) notation: OC([C@@]12CC[C@@]3(C@@(CC[C@@]4([C@@]5(C@@(C(C)(C@HO)C)[H])C)[H])[H])C) = O, using cheminformatics tools to facilitate downstream applications including molecular construction, conformational ensemble generation, and structure-based virtual screening.

Structure prediction of the protein–ligand complex using alphafold 3

To obtain the initial binding conformation of AGTR1 with betulinic acid, we employed the state-of-the-art deep learning–based structural prediction framework AlphaFold 3 (AF3) to achieve high-accuracy modeling of protein–small molecule complexes. AF3 is capable of directly inferring putative binding sites and three-dimensional interaction patterns by leveraging the primary structural information of proteins (i.e., amino acid sequences) in conjunction with the chemical descriptors of ligands, such as their SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) representations. In this study, the complete amino acid sequence of AGTR1 (UniProt ID: P30556) and the SMILES string of betulinic acid were simultaneously input into the AF3 platform. The model subsequently generated an ensemble of plausible protein–ligand complex conformations. Among these, the conformation exhibiting the highest predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score—indicating optimal structural confidence—was selected as the initial structure for downstream molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. This approach ensured both the physicochemical plausibility and the biological relevance of the modeled complex, thereby enhancing the reliability of subsequent dynamic and energetic analyses.

Molecular dynamics simulation

To comprehensively investigate the conformational stability and dynamic interaction mechanism of the AGTR1–Betulinic acid complex under near-physiological conditions, a 500-nanosecond (ns) all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was performed using the GROMACS 2021.2 software suite.

System parameterization and preparation

The protein component was parameterized using the Amber99SB-ILDN force field, which offers refined accuracy in describing protein secondary structures and side-chain torsional preferences. Ligand parameters for Betulinic acid were generated using the General Amber Force Field (GAFF), with atomic partial charges computed via the AM1-BCC method implemented in the Antechamber toolkit. The resulting topology and coordinate files in Amber format were converted into GROMACS-compatible input files using ACPYPE (AnteChamber PYthon Parser interfacE). The AGTR1–Betulinic acid complex was positioned at the geometric center of a triclinic simulation box, ensuring a minimum distance of 1.2 nm between any solute atom and the box boundary to eliminate artifacts from periodic boundary interactions. The system was solvated using the TIP3P explicit water model, and counterions (Na⁺ and Cl⁻) were added to neutralize the net system charge. Additionally, the ionic strength was adjusted to 0.15 M to mimic physiological saline conditions.

Energy minimization and equilibration

Prior to production dynamics, the system underwent a two-stage energy minimization to eliminate steric clashes and unfavorable contacts: Stage I: Heavy atoms of the protein and ligand were position-restrained, and only solvent molecules and ions were relaxed. Stage II: All positional restraints were removed, and the entire system was subjected to unconstrained minimization until the maximum residual force converged below 1000 kJ/mol/nm. Following minimization, the system was equilibrated in two canonical ensembles: NVT Ensemble: A 100-ps simulation under constant volume and temperature (310 K) was carried out using the V-rescale thermostat, while applying position restraints to heavy atoms. NPT Ensemble: An additional 100-ps simulation under constant pressure (1 bar) was conducted using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat, allowing the system to reach appropriate density and volume equilibrium.

Production MD simulation

After releasing all restraints, a 500-ns production MD simulation was performed. The simulation employed the Leap-frog integration algorithm with a 2-femtosecond (fs) time step to ensure numerical stability. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated with the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, while short-range electrostatic and van der Waals interactions were calculated using a cutoff radius of 1.0 nm. Covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, and the geometry of water molecules was preserved using the SETTLE algorithm. The simulation was executed in a GPU-accelerated environment, with checkpoint files generated at regular intervals to ensure reproducibility and facilitate recovery in case of interruption.

Simulation trajectory analysis

To systematically assess the conformational stability and interaction dynamics of the AGTR1–Betulinic acid complex throughout the molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, trajectory frames were extracted at 100-picosecond (ps) intervals for comprehensive structural and interaction analyses.

Conformational stability assessment

Global structural stability was evaluated by calculating the Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) of protein backbone atoms with respect to the initial equilibrated conformation. This metric provides a time-resolved measure of the system’s overall deviation from its reference structure. Additionally, local flexibility and residue-level fluctuations were quantified by computing the Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) of the Cα atoms across all residues, offering insights into the dynamic behavior of specific structural regions.

Interaction profiling and contact analysis

To quantitatively describe the temporal stability of protein–ligand interactions, we employed the Interaction Fraction, defined as the percentage of simulation time during which a specific non-covalent interaction persists. For in-depth characterization of hydrogen bonding patterns, two distinct hydrogen bond types were analyzed using the getcontacts toolkit: Hydrogen Bond Ligand–Sidechain (HBL–S): Hydrogen bonds formed between the ligand and amino acid side chains; Hydrogen Bond Ligand–Backbone (HBL–B): Hydrogen bonds established between the ligand and backbone atoms of protein residues. These metrics enabled a detailed comparison of the respective contributions of side-chain and backbone interactions to ligand binding stability.

Identification of key binding residues

To pinpoint residues critical for ligand recognition and retention, the minimum distance between the ligand and each protein residue was calculated throughout the simulation. A contact threshold of 0.35 nm was employed to define meaningful interactions. Based on this criterion, contact fractions—i.e., the percentage of simulation time during which each residue remained within the contact threshold—were computed. Residues exhibiting high contact persistence were identified as key binding residues, likely to contribute significantly to molecular recognition and binding specificity. These analyses collectively provide a structural and dynamic framework for understanding the interaction landscape of the AGTR1–Betulinic acid complex and establish a rational basis for pharmacophore modeling, binding hotspot identification, and site-directed mutagenesis in downstream drug discovery efforts.

Mice

All animal experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH), and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southwest Medical University, Luzhou, China (Approval No. SWMU20240084). Male C57BL/6J mice (18–20 g, 6–8 weeks old) and at1r knockout (At1r-/-, Strain NO. T027916) mice, supplied by GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Chengdu, China), were housed in individually ventilated cages under controlled conditions (22 ± 0.5 °C, 55% ± 5% relative humidity) with a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on from 06:00 to 18:00), simulating natural circadian rhythms. The gene knockout strategy for at1r knockout (At1r-/-, Strain NO. T027916) mice is described in detail on the company’s official website. Prior to experimentation, all animals underwent a 7-day acclimatization period to ensure physiological stabilization. Throughout the study, mice were maintained under standardized laboratory conditions with consistent nutritional support, ensuring both animal welfare and experimental data integrity.

Human samples

Liver biopsy samples were collected from three patients diagnosed with advanced clinical fibrosis in the MASH context and three control subjects without MASH. All sample collection procedures adhered strictly to the pre-established protocol, and the liver transplant tissue specimens were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) medium for subsequent immunofluorescence analysis. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Affiliated Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital of Southwest Medical University (Ethics Approval No.: KY2020025-XZ01), ensuring adherence to relevant ethical standards.

Establishment of MASH model and treatment of BA for MASH

To establish a murine model of metabolic associated steatohepatitis (MASH), mice were subjected to a high-fat, high-sucrose diet for 28 consecutive days. The diet formulation comprised 21.1% fat, 41% sucrose, and 1.25% cholesterol (w/w), accompanied by ad libitum access to a sugar-enriched solution containing 18.9 g/L D-glucose and 23.1 g/L D-fructose. Concurrently, CCl₄, diluted to 20% in corn oil, was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 0.32 g/kg every three days, totaling seven injections. Control animals received equivalent volumes of corn oil via the same regimen to serve as vehicle controls. A total of 40 mice were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 10 per group) and treated as follows: (1) corn oil control; (2) CCl₄-induced MASH model; (3) CCl₄ combined with 20 mg/kg BA; and (4) CCl₄ combined with 50 mg/kg BA. BA was administered via intragastric gavage every three days, with dosing initiated on the day following each CCl₄ injection. In At1r-/- mice, BA intervention followed the same induction and administration protocol. All animals were euthanized humanely on the second day after the final BA administration, and comprehensive liver tissue samples were harvested for subsequent histological, molecular, and biochemical analyses.

Isolation of mouse liver ECs

To achieve optimal purification of hepatic endothelial cells (ECs), monoclonal antibodies specific to CD31 (Invitrogen, 13-0319-82) or CD45 (Invitrogen, 13-0459-82) were thoroughly conjugated to Dynabeads™ Sheep Anti-Mouse IgG magnetic beads (Invitrogen, 11031) via overnight incubation at 4 °C, thereby ensuring maximal antibody-bead coupling efficiency. Freshly excised liver tissues were immediately rinsed with ice-cold Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) to eliminate residual blood and minimize enzymatic degradation. Subsequently, the liver samples were subjected to a combined mechanical and enzymatic dissociation protocol, following the manufacturer’s instructions, utilizing a high-efficiency liver dissociation kit (DHGT-5004, RWD, China) in conjunction with an automated single-cell suspension system (DSC-400, RWD), resulting in the generation of a viable single-cell suspension. To deplete hematopoietic lineage cells, the resulting suspension was incubated with Dynabeads pre-coated with anti-CD45 antibodies, thereby selectively removing CD45⁺ cell populations. Enrichment of liver ECs was then accomplished via positive selection using CD31 antibody-conjugated Dynabeads. Throughout the magnetic sorting and washing procedures, MACS buffer was employed to preserve cellular viability and ensure sample integrity. The buffer was composed of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with 2 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 1% antibiotic cocktail (penicillin/streptomycin/anti-mitotic agents). The purified ECs were immediately processed for RNA extraction to maintain transcriptomic fidelity and biological relevance. The transcriptomic data utilized in the present study were obtained from the same batch of normal control samples and RNA sequencing datasets as those employed in our previous research. This consistency in data sourcing is stated here to ensure methodological clarity and reproducibility.

Histological assessment of liver fibrosis

Liver tissues were initially subjected to post-fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde solution diluted in PBS, thereby preserving tissue architecture and maintaining antigenic integrity. Upon completion of fixation, samples underwent graded ethanol dehydration through a sequential series of increasing concentrations to effectively eliminate residual moisture and facilitate optimal paraffin infiltration. Following dehydration, tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices using a precision microtome to ensure uniform morphology across samples. Prior to histological staining, paraffin-embedded sections were rehydrated through a descending ethanol gradient to restore hydrophilicity, enabling efficient penetration of staining reagents. Histological assessments were conducted using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and Sirius Red staining, all of which were performed in accordance with standardized protocols to evaluate hepatic architecture and the extent of fibrotic remodeling. For quantitative analysis, stained sections were imaged and assessed using the Image-Pro Plus software (version 6.0), which enabled precise and reproducible measurement of collagen deposition, thereby providing robust histopathological evidence for liver fibrosis assessment.

Immunofluorescence staining of liver tissue slides

In immunofluorescence staining assays, frozen liver tissue sections with a thickness of 4 μm were initially fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution to preserve cellular architecture and maintain antigenicity. Following fixation, tissue sections were incubated at room temperature with 5% goat serum blocking buffer to minimize non-specific antibody binding and reduce background fluorescence. After blocking, the sections were incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C for an extended period to ensure optimal specificity and stable antigen–antibody interactions. Upon completion of primary antibody incubation, unbound antibodies were thoroughly removed by repeated washes with PBS. Subsequently, sections were incubated at room temperature for 1 h with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies, including goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Abcam, ab150081) and goat anti-mouse IgG (Abcam, ab150114), to enable multiplexed detection of target antigens. To visualize nuclei, the tissue sections were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), followed by PBS washes to remove excess dye. Finally, the stained sections were mounted and preserved in an anti-fade mounting medium containing 20% glycerol to prevent fluorescence quenching and ensure optimal conditions for subsequent microscopic imaging.

Cell culture and in vitro stimulation of EndMT

Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs) and the Human Microvascular Endothelial Cell Line-1 (HMEC-1) were obtained from iCell Bioscience (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in a proprietary complete endothelial growth medium (catalog number: iCell-h110-001b), specifically formulated by the manufacturer to meet the distinct metabolic and proliferative requirements of endothelial cells. Both HUVECs and HMEC-1 were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ to closely mimic physiological conditions and ensure optimal cell viability. During the proliferation phase, cell seeding density was carefully adjusted to achieve approximately 50% confluency, thereby generating a uniform monolayer suitable for subsequent experimental manipulations.

To synchronize the cell cycle and minimize the influence of exogenous growth factors, cells were subjected to serum deprivation by culturing in basal medium supplemented with 3% fetal bovine serum (FBS) for 12 h. Following this pretreatment, HUVECs were exposed to 3.0 µM angiotensin II (Ang II) for 48 h to induce endothelial dysfunction and simulate pathophysiological conditions. At 24 h post-Ang II stimulation, the experimental intervention group received an additional treatment with the candidate pharmacological agent BA, aiming to evaluate its potential protective or restorative effects under Ang II-induced endothelial injury.

Western blot analysis

To extract total protein, cultured cells were first thoroughly rinsed with ice-cold PBS to eliminate extracellular debris and residual culture medium. Subsequently, cell lysis was performed using a pre-chilled lysis buffer supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (1%) to prevent proteolytic degradation and preserve phosphorylation states, thereby maintaining protein structural and functional integrity. The resulting lysates were subjected to vigorous vortexing to ensure complete cellular disruption, followed by centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble debris. The clarified supernatant containing total cellular proteins was collected, and protein concentrations were quantified using the Bradford assay (Solarbio, China) to ensure consistent protein loading in downstream applications. Equal amounts of protein samples were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) on a 4–12% Bis-Tris gradient SurePAGE gel (Shanghai Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd.). Following electrophoretic separation, proteins were electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (pore size: 0.25 μm; Merck Millipore) for immunoblotting analysis. Membranes were blocked with QuickBlock™ Western blocking buffer (Beyotime, China) for 20 min at room temperature to suppress non-specific antibody binding. Subsequently, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies under gentle agitation to promote specific antigen–antibody interactions. On the following day, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat secondary antibodies, directed against the appropriate host species of the primary antibodies, for 1 h at room temperature with mild shaking to enhance signal development. After multiple washes with TBST buffer to eliminate unbound antibodies, chemiluminescent signals were visualized using the ChemiScope 6200 Touch imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized against β-actin as a loading control. All data were derived from three independent biological replicates and are presented as mean values to ensure statistical robustness.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were subjected to statistical analysis using GraphPad Prism 9 software, and the results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Statistical comparisons between the two groups were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. In hypothesis testing, a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while a P-value of less than 0.01 was considered highly statistically significant. The statistical significance of the results is indicated in the figures as follows: * represents P < 0.05, and ** represents P < 0.01.

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling reveals a association between AT1R expression and endothelial cell functional dynamics during chronic hepatic injury

To elucidate the functional involvement of At1r in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis associated with steatosis, we analyzed the liver single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE182365 derived from a murine model of fatty liver. In this model, mice were subjected to a high-sucrose, high-fat diet for 17 weeks. Initially, unsupervised clustering analysis was performed on the single-cell transcriptomic data to delineate distinct cellular populations (Fig. 1A). Cell type annotation was subsequently conducted based on canonical marker genes, enabling classification of the clusters into hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, endothelial cells, and immune cells (Fig. 1B). Representative marker genes were used to identify specific cell populations, including Alb for hepatocytes, Cd79a for B lymphocytes, Cd68 for Kupffer cells, Clec4g for endothelial cells, and Krt19 for cholangiocytes (Fig. 1C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the endothelial cell-specific transcriptomic signatures revealed significant association with biological processes such as cell migration, regulation of cell migration, and localization dynamics (Fig. 1D). Further investigation demonstrated that At1r expression was absent in hepatocytes but predominantly localized in endothelial cells and Kupffer cells. Notably, the spatial expression profile of At1r exhibited a high degree of concordance with the endothelial cell marker Cdh5 (Fig. 1E). Additionally, differential expression analysis of external datasets, including the human NASH cohort (GSE135251) and a murine NASH model (GSE65220), confirmed a marked upregulation of At1r expression in the context of steatohepatitis (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, to further elucidate the role of AT1R in endothelial cells during liver fibrosis, we conducted immunofluorescence staining on liver tissues from both normal and diseased individuals to assess the expression changes of AT1R in endothelial cells. The immunofluorescence results revealed a significant upregulation of AT1R expression in endothelial cells during liver fibrosis, thereby providing additional evidence to support the potential functional involvement of AT1R in endothelial cell alterations associated with liver fibrosis (Fig. S1). Collectively, these findings underscore a pivotal role for AT1R in modulating endothelial cell function during chronic liver injury.

Molecular docking, MM-GBSA calculations, and molecular dynamics simulations collectively confirm the stable binding of betulinic acid to AT1R

Betulinic acid (BA), a classic pentacyclic triterpenoid, has been extensively investigated for its therapeutic potential in various diseases. However, its role as a specific small molecule inhibitor of AT1R remains unexplored. To assess its potential as an AT1R inhibitor, we employed molecular docking to compare the binding affinities of BA with those of several clinically used AT1R inhibitors, including Eprosartan, Valsartan, Losartan, and Olmesartan. The results revealed that BA exhibited the lowest binding energy (-9.65 kcal·mol⁻¹), suggesting its strongest interaction with AT1R (Fig. S1). Additionally, we utilized the advanced AF3 model to predict the complex structure of BA in complex with AT1R. The prediction yielded an overall confidence score (ranking_score) of 0.92, an interface template modeling score (ipTM) of 0.88, and a predicted template modeling score (pTM) of 0.78, collectively indicating that the AF3-generated AT1R- BA complex possesses high structural reliability and stability, providing a solid foundation for further detailed analyses (Fig. 2A).

The darker the color (e.g., deep orange), the more stable the interaction between the residue and the ligand

To evaluate the dynamic behavior and structural stability of the complex under physiologically relevant conditions, we performed a 500 ns full-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. Initially, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the protein backbone atoms was calculated to monitor the overall stability of the system (Fig. 2B). The simulation results revealed that the RMSD of the complex rapidly converged in the early phase and subsequently stabilized within the range of 0.2–0.4 nm, indicating system equilibrium and confirming that the binding of BA did not induce significant structural alterations in the protein.

To further elucidate the molecular underpinnings of complex stability, we conducted a comprehensive interaction analysis of the MD simulation trajectory (Fig. 2A, D, E). The results demonstrated that BA is deeply embedded within the binding pocket of AT1R, which is composed of both hydrophobic and polar residues. Throughout the simulation, hydrogen bonding was identified as the primary stabilizing force maintaining the integrity of the complex. Interaction analysis (Fig. 2A, D) revealed several stable, high-frequency hydrogen bonds. Notably, the functional groups of BA formed persistent hydrogen bonds with the side chains of SER35 and ARG92, with interaction frequencies exceeding 60%, suggesting that these residues are crucial in anchoring the ligand.

To further illustrate the dynamics of these interactions, we plotted a timeline of the binding events (Fig. 2E). The lower panel displays dark bands corresponding to residues such as ALA84, SER108, and CYS288, clearly indicating sustained interaction with the ligand over the majority of the simulation. The upper panel shows that once equilibrium was reached, the total number of interactions between the ligand and protein remained relatively stable, further reinforcing the stability of the AT1R-BA complex. To further elucidate the binding interplay between BA and AT1R, we investigated the regulatory influence of BA treatment on the principal downstream signaling pathways of AT1R. Our findings demonstrated that BA markedly attenuated Ang II–induced signaling cascade activation. Specifically, BA treatment significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of ERK1/2 and ELK1 triggered by Ang II (Fig. S3), thereby functionally corroborating a direct interaction between BA and AT1R. In parallel, we performed molecular dynamics simulations to systematically assess the alterations in binding affinity between BA and AT1R upon mutagenesis at key binding residues. Using the Mutagenesis module in PyMOL, the predicted critical residues SER35, ARG92, and PHE109 were individually substituted with Ala (to abolish side-chain interaction capacity), and a triple mutant (SER35A/ARG92A/PHE109A) was subsequently constructed. Simulation analyses revealed that the wild-type system preserved a relatively high global contact number throughout the production phase, averaging 65.37, whereas the mutant system exhibited a pronounced reduction, averaging 58.42 (a decline of approximately 10.63%) (Fig. S4), thereby indicating a significant loss of binding stability.

Based on the above analysis, the results demonstrate that BA binds stably to the active pocket of AT1R. This stable interaction primarily hinges on the multi-point hydrogen bond network formed between betulinic acid and key residues such as SER35, ARG92, and PHE109, while the surrounding hydrophobic environment further stabilizes this binding. These findings provide compelling evidence for the molecular basis of BA’s interaction with AT1R and establish a theoretical foundation for its potential as a small molecule inhibitor of AT1R.

BA demonstrates favorable biocompatibility and minimal toxicological liabilities within the defined therapeutic dosage range

Although AT1R has been recognized as a promising therapeutic target for a variety of hepatic disorders [47], its long-term application in managing MASH remains limited due to the intrinsic pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic constraints associated with small-molecule AT1R antagonists. Consequently, a comprehensive and systematic evaluation of the chronic oral administration of BA (the candidate compound) and its effects on vital organ systems and hematological parameters in murine models is essential for assessing its translational potential as a therapeutic agent for MASH. In light of these considerations, we implemented a 30-day pharmacological intervention study using both female and male C57BL/6J mice. BA was administered via oral gavage at a fixed daily dose of 50 mg/kg, consistently delivered at 10:00 a.m. Post-intervention, tissue samples from multiple organ systems were meticulously harvested for subsequent histological and biochemical analyses.

Preliminary investigations centered on the morphological integrity of major organs. H&E staining revealed that, following 30 consecutive days of BA exposure, the architectural integrity of key organs—including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys—was well preserved. No apparent histopathological abnormalities or inflammatory cell infiltrates were observed, indicating the absence of overt organotoxic effects (Fig. 3A). In addition, hematological profiling demonstrated that red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (HGB) concentration, white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil (NEUT#) and lymphocyte (LYMPH#) counts, platelet (PLT) count, and plateletcrit (PCT) levels all remained within established physiological reference ranges (Fig. 3B). The absence of significant deviations in these indices suggests that prolonged BA administration does not elicit hematological toxicity. Complementary serum biochemical assessments further corroborated the favorable safety profile of BA. Key indicators of hepatic and renal function—including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (UREA), creatinine (CREA), and uric acid (UA)—remained within homeostatic limits throughout the study duration (Fig. 3B), thereby excluding the likelihood of BA-induced hepatic or nephrotoxicity. Collectively, these findings underscore the biocompatibility and systemic safety of BA in vivo, thereby establishing a robust preclinical foundation for its advancement as a candidate for early therapeutic intervention in MASH.

Assessment of the anti-fibrotic efficacy of BA in a murine model of MASH

To further elucidate the therapeutic potential of BA in ameliorating hepatic fibrosis during the progression of MASH, we conducted a comprehensive in vivo investigation utilizing a well-established MASH murine model (Fig. 4A). Our preceding studies have substantiated that chronic oral administration of BA is associated with minimal systemic toxicity (Fig. 3), thereby establishing a solid safety profile and supporting its candidacy for MASH pharmacotherapy. In the present study, we systematically evaluated the anti-fibrotic efficacy of BA in MASH-induced mice. Histological and biochemical analyses revealed a marked attenuation of hepatic steatosis following BA intervention, with a discernible dose-dependent response (Fig. 4B and C). This attenuation was substantiated by histopathological assessments: H&E staining demonstrated substantial restoration of hepatic architecture, characterized by organized hepatocyte cord formation and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, Sirius Red and Masson’s trichrome staining exhibited a significant reduction in hepatic collagen fiber deposition post-treatment, reinforcing the anti-fibrotic capacity of BA in a dose-responsive manner (Fig. 4C and D). Biochemical assays assessing hepatic function further corroborated these findings. Serum levels of ALT and AST, commonly regarded as clinical biomarkers of hepatocellular injury, were significantly reduced in BA-treated groups, indicating improved hepatic integrity and function (Fig. 4E). To further delineate the molecular mechanisms underpinning BA-mediated fibrosis mitigation, immunofluorescence staining was performed on cryosections of liver tissue. Quantitative fluorescence imaging revealed pronounced downregulation of key pro-fibrotic markers, including type I collagen (Collagen-I), type III collagen (Collagen-III), and fibronectin, following BA administration (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these findings provide compelling evidence that BA exerts robust anti-fibrotic effects in the context of MASH. The data underscore the therapeutic promise of BA and support its further development as a viable candidate for clinical intervention in MASH-associated liver fibrosis.

BA effectively attenuates the onset of EndMT both in vitro and in vivo

Preliminary experiments have validated the safety and efficacy of BA in the treatment of MASH. However, the specific impact of BA on pro-fibrotic biological processes during MASH remains unclear. To address this gap, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on mouse liver tissue to investigate the underlying mechanisms. The experimental design involved a comparative analysis between the MASH and NC control groups, as well as between the BA treatment and MASH groups. Differentially expressed genes were analyzed, followed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. The results revealed that BA intervention markedly suppressed cell cycle-related processes during MASH (Fig. 5A). Building on our previous work on the regulation of EndMT by AT1R, we further investigated EndMT-associated markers through heat map visualization (Fig. 5B). The findings indicated that BA treatment significantly upregulated endothelial cell markers while downregulating mesenchymal markers. Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis corroborated these results, supporting the notion that BA treatment alleviates liver fibrosis (Fig. 5C).

Subsequently, we evaluated the impact of BA on the EndMT process through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Initially, we performed immunofluorescence staining on liver tissues from each group of mice, focusing on co-staining for α-SMA with CD31 and FSP1 with CD31—widely recognized as the gold standard for assessing EndMT. The immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant reduction in the area of co-localization of positive regions in the BA treatment group, indicating that BA effectively inhibited EndMT (Fig. 5D and E). Furthermore, we also evaluated the expression of EndMT markers in endothelial cells isolated from mouse liver tissue (Fig. S5). In addition, we induced an EndMT model in the human umbilical vein endothelial cell line (HUVEC) using Ang II, followed by BA treatment to evaluate its potential inhibitory effects on EndMT. The results showed that Ang II induction led to a marked downregulation of endothelial markers and an upregulation of mesenchymal markers; however, after BA intervention, endothelial markers were upregulated and mesenchymal marker expression was suppressed, further substantiating BA’s ability to inhibit EndMT in vitro (Fig. 5F). In conclusion, these data comprehensively demonstrate that BA significantly inhibits EndMT both in vitro and in vivo, and its anti-fibrotic effects are likely mediated through the suppression of EndMT.

The anti-fibrotic and EndMT inhibitory properties of BA during the progression of MASH are mediated by its specific targeting of AT1R

We previously established that BA exerts prominent antifibrotic effects and suppresses EndMT during the progression of MASH. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether these beneficial outcomes are contingent upon the direct targeting of the AT1R by BA. To elucidate whether BA’s therapeutic efficacy in the context of MASH is reliant on its AT1R-targeting capability, we employed a genetically engineered C57BL/6J mouse model with global At1r gene knockout and induced MASH pathology via combined administration of a high-fat diet and CCl4. Subsequently, BA intervention was administered to assess its therapeutic impact under conditions of At1r deficiency, followed by collection and analysis of hepatic tissue samples through conventional histopathological and immunofluorescence staining techniques.

Histological assessment using H&E staining revealed that mice deficient in the At1r gene alone exhibited significant attenuation of MASH-related hepatic lesions. However, administration of BA in the context of At1r knockout failed to further ameliorate MASH pathology. Specifically, despite observable improvements in hepatic architecture and marked reduction in inflammatory cell infiltration, additional BA treatment did not yield statistically significant enhancement beyond that achieved by At1r knockout alone (Fig. 6A). Further analyses using Sirius Red, Masson’s trichrome, and Collagen-I immunofluorescence staining confirmed that collagen fiber deposition, significantly diminished by At1r knockout alone, remained essentially unchanged following subsequent BA intervention in At1r-deficient mice (Fig. 6A). Moreover, the serological analysis of liver function further corroborated these experimental findings (Fig. S6). Consistently, immunofluorescence assessment of EndMT markers corroborated these findings, demonstrating pronounced suppression of EndMT following At1r deletion alone, yet no additional significant improvement was observed upon BA treatment (Fig. 6B and C). Collectively, our findings indicate that the antifibrotic and anti-EndMT effects of BA in MASH are mediated primarily through its interaction with AT1R, underscoring the receptor’s essential role in the therapeutic efficacy of BA.

BA significantly suppressed the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway during the process of EndMT amelioration

The preliminary experimental results have unequivocally demonstrated that BA can significantly ameliorate liver fibrosis during the progression of MASH and inhibit the occurrence of EndMT. However, the precise key signaling nodes through which BA exerts its inhibitory effects on EndMT via targeting AT1R remain unclear. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying BA’s inhibition of EndMT through AT1R targeting in MASH, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on liver endothelial cells isolated from control, MASH, and BA treatment groups of mice (Fig. 7A). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the samples from each group exhibited high reproducibility, with marked differences observed between the BA and control groups, as well as between the BA and MASH groups (Fig. 7B). To further explore the role of BA in endothelial cells (ECs) during MASH, we conducted Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The KEGG analysis revealed that the “PI3K-AKT signaling pathway” showed the most prominent alterations following BA intervention, suggesting that BA may exert its effects in mitigating EndMT and improving MASH primarily through the inhibition of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 7C and D). Furthermore, we employed immunofluorescence techniques to assess the phosphorylation levels of key signaling factors, including mTOR, AKT, and PI3K, within the PI3K-AKT pathway in liver tissue from mice. The results indicated that BA intervention significantly suppressed the phosphorylation of mTOR, AKT, and PI3K (Fig. 7E). In addition, similar findings were obtained in the Ang II-induced HUVEC cell line model of EndMT, where Ang II activated the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, but BA treatment notably suppressed the activation of these signaling molecules (Fig. 7F and H). To further elucidate the mechanistic basis of BA’s inhibitory effect on EndMT and its reliance on AT1R and PI3K/AKT signaling, we incorporated the AT1R-selective antagonist olmesartan and the PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitor GDC-0980 into our experimental design. Specifically, the study included the following treatment groups: NC control group, Ang II-induced model, Ang II + BA, Ang II + olmesartan, Ang II + BA + olmesartan, Ang II + GDC-0980, and Ang II + BA + GDC-0980. The concentration of Ang II was 3 µM, with olmesartan and GDC-0980 administered at 1 µM, and BA at 5 µM. In the mixed treatment groups, cells were pretreated with olmesartan or GDC-0980 for 24 h prior to BA intervention. The experimental results revealed that BA, olmesartan, and GDC-0980 each exhibited significant inhibitory effects on EndMT (Fig. S7). However, when cells were pretreated with olmesartan or GDC-0980 before BA treatment, the inhibitory effect of BA on EndMT was markedly diminished or even abolished. In conclusion, the above findings suggest that the inhibitory effect of BA on EndMT may be attributed to its repression of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway.

Discussion

MASH has emerged as a critical global public health concern, imposing substantial burdens on healthcare systems worldwide [4]. Current therapeutic strategies for MASH predominantly aim to mitigate hepatocellular injury, suppress inflammatory responses, and decelerate the progression of hepatic fibrosis [48]. Nevertheless, particularly during the early stages of disease onset, therapeutic options remain markedly limited, with a conspicuous absence of efficacious and targeted pharmacological interventions [4]. Accumulating evidence underscores the pivotal role of the AT1R in modulating the pathophysiological progression of MASH [49]. Hepatic fibrosis, a hallmark of MASH, is characterized by excessive deposition of collagen fibers and ECM, culminating in progressive disruption of liver architecture and function [11]. This fibrogenic process is primarily orchestrated by activated myofibroblasts, which originate predominantly from HSCs and LSECs through transdifferentiation [50]. Notably, AT1R signaling is intricately involved in the activation and phenotypic conversion of these cell populations, thereby serving as a key regulatory node in fibrosis pathogenesis. Although several AT1R antagonists have been explored for the management of hepatic disorders, their clinical translation has been hampered by suboptimal efficacy, adverse drug reactions, and safety concerns. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel AT1R inhibitors with improved therapeutic profiles for the effective management of MASH. In this context, bioactive small molecules derived from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) present a promising avenue, owing to their structural diversity, multitarget potential, and favorable safety margins.

BA, a naturally occurring triterpenoid compound, has been extensively recognized for its ability to inhibit multiple therapeutic targets in liver fibrosis and demonstrates considerable efficacy in the treatment of liver fibrosis-related diseases [51]. Nevertheless, its role as an inhibitor of AT1R remains poorly defined. In this study, we employed AF3 and molecular dynamics simulations to investigate the structural properties and binding affinity of the complex formed between BA and the AT1R target. The computational results revealed the formation of a stable complex with strong binding affinity between BA and AT1R. To further substantiate the AT1R targeting activity of BA, we generated an At1r gene knockout mouse model. The subsequent findings indicated that the therapeutic effect of BA on liver fibrosis was markedly diminished in the absence of the At1r gene. These animal model experiments provide robust evidence supporting the direct targeting of AT1R by BA. Collectively, our data underscore the significant potential of BA as an AT1R inhibitor, offering a solid theoretical foundation for its application in AT1R-targeted therapeutic interventions for liver fibrosis.

The critical determinants for the potential use of BA as a long-term oral intervention for liver fibrosis are its minimal organ toxicity and its pronounced anti-fibrotic therapeutic efficacy. In this study, we systematically evaluated the impact of oral BA administration on hematological parameters, liver and kidney function, as well as the histological integrity of major organ tissues in C57BL/6 mice over an extended feeding period. The results indicated no significant alterations in these clinical parameters, suggesting that BA, when administered orally as a long-term therapeutic agent, demonstrates a favorable safety profile. Subsequently, we assessed the therapeutic efficacy of BA in a murine model of MASH induced liver fibrosis. The experimental data revealed a significant amelioration of liver fibrosis-related symptoms following BA intervention. These comprehensive in vivo safety and efficacy studies substantiate the safety and therapeutic potential of BA as an AT1R inhibitor in the management of liver fibrosis, reinforcing its viability for long-term clinical application.

In this study, we not only assessed the targeting efficacy of BA as an AT1R inhibitor and its safety and therapeutic potential in ameliorating liver fibrosis, but also investigated the underlying pharmacological mechanisms by which BA exerts its anti-fibrotic effects. Building upon our previous research highlighting the role of AT1R in EndMT during liver fibrosis, we evaluated the impact of BA on EndMT both in vitro and in vivo. The experimental data demonstrated that BA effectively inhibited the progression of EndMT during liver fibrosis in both models. Further mechanistic analysis revealed that the inhibitory action of BA on EndMT may be mediated through its suppression of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Pharmacological experiments confirmed that BA inhibits AT1R, thereby attenuating the PI3K-AKT pathway and subsequently blocking EndMT progression in liver fibrosis. This cascade of events ultimately culminates in the anti-fibrotic effects of BA, elucidating its potential as a therapeutic agent for liver fibrosis (Fig. 8).

BA exerts a significant therapeutic effect on liver fibrosis, primarily through its inhibition of the AT1R, which subsequently prevents the activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and suppresses the occurrence of EndMT

We conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the safety, efficacy, and underlying pharmacological mechanisms of BA as an AT1R inhibitor in the treatment of liver fibrosis. The experimental data support the potential therapeutic application of BA in liver fibrosis, providing a solid foundation for the clinical implementation of AT1R-targeted interventions for liver fibrosis. However, this study did not encompass the pharmacokinetic profiling of BA in the context of liver fibrosis alleviation, and it remains unclear whether BA exerts its anti-fibrotic effects through additional molecular mechanisms. Future studies will aim to address these gaps by conducting thorough pharmacokinetic assessments and exploring deeper insights into the pharmacological mechanisms, thereby reinforcing the preclinical applicability of BA for liver fibrosis management.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that BA exhibits high binding affinity toward AT1R and possesses favorable biocompatibility. As an orally administrable AT1R-targeted therapeutic, BA effectively suppresses the initiation of EndMT by modulating the PI3K-AKT signaling cascade, thereby markedly attenuating fibrosis-associated pathological alterations in the livers of MASH mice. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic efficacy of BA in mitigating EndMT, substantiate its potential as a selective AT1R modulator, and position it as a promising lead compound for the development of EndMT-centric anti-fibrotic strategies.
