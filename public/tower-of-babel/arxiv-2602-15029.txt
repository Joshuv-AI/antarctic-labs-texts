Symmetry in language statistics shapes the geometry of model representations
Dhruva Karkada, Daniel J. Korchinski, Andres Nava, Matthieu Wyart, Yasaman Bahri

Abstract
The internal representations learned by language models consistently exhibit striking geometric structure: calendar months organize into a circle, historical years form a smooth one-dimensional manifold, and cities’ latitudes and longitudes can be decoded using a linear probe. To explain this neural code, we first show that language statistics exhibit translation symmetry (for example, the frequency with which any two months co-occur in text depends only on the time interval between them). We prove that this symmetry governs these geometric structures in high-dimensional word embedding models, and we analytically derive the manifold geometry of word representations. These predictions empirically match large text embedding models and large language models. Moreover, the representational geometry persists at moderate embedding dimension even when the relevant statistics are perturbed (e.g., by removing all sentences in which two months co-occur). We prove that this robustness emerges naturally when the co-occurrence statistics are controlled by an underlying latent variable. These results suggest that representational manifolds have a universal origin: symmetry in the statistics of natural data.

The semantic information encoded in the trained parameters of an LLM is reflected in the vector geometry of its internal representations.
A growing body of empirical work has documented a number of intriguing geometric structures in this neural code, including prisms
(
21
;
27
;
20

, loops
(
8

22
)
, and continuous 1D manifolds
(

.
Notably, these structures emerge consistently across a range of model architectures and tasks.
Despite this apparent universality, we lack an organizing principle that explains why these patterns arise.

In this work, we provide such an organizing principle: that representational geometry reflects pairwise co-occurrence statistics between words.
In particular, we give evidence that
symmetry governs these statistics and drives the formation of representational manifolds
. This hypothesis underpins our theory and explains the following phenomena:

•

Circles in representation space for cyclical concepts.

(
8
;
22
)
find that LLMs encode concepts such as days of the week, months of the year, or hues on the color wheel with circular geometry.

(
12
)
and related work suggest that circular representations may enable LLMs to efficiently compute modular addition tasks (“Five months after November is:”).

•

“Rippled” 1D manifolds for continuous sequences.

(
8
;
13
)
find that LLM representations corresponding to continuums (e.g., years of history or number lines) are organized in compact 1D manifolds with so-called “ripples,” or extrinsic curvature.
The LLM exploits this manifold geometry to compute increments and decrements.

•

Linear decoding of spatiotemporal coordinates.

(
14
)
find that LLM representations of geographical locations and historical events
linearly
encode their spatial and temporal coordinates, respectively.
Thus, LLMs may straightforwardly compute maps and timelines by linear transformations.

To test this hypothesis, we first examine word embedding models, where representation learning is driven exclusively by pairwise co-occurrence statistics.
Surprisingly, we find that each of these geometries arise in this simple setting.
We develop a mathematical theory that unifies and explains these seemingly disparate observations, deriving the representation geometries from translation symmetry in the pairwise co-occurrence statistics of words.
For example, when the co-occurrence probability of two events or places depends only on their temporal or spatial distance, these models spontaneously learn Fourier representations, i.e., the embedding vectors vary sinusoidally along each principal component.
We analytically predict both the amplitude and the frequency of each component, showing that loops and circles in the manifold geometry correspond to the long-wavelength modes, while ripples are the higher harmonics.

Importantly, even if the co-occurrence statistics of a given set of words is perturbed (e.g. removing all sentences where two months co-occur), their representational geometry is preserved at intermediate
embedding dimension. One of our central results is to explain this surprising robustness: it emerges because the co-occurrence of many words in the vocabulary are affected by the corresponding latent variable (e.g., the season/time of year), leading to large eigenvalues in the pairwise statistics of words whose eigenvectors are insensitive to noise or perturbation.

2.1
Predicting word embedding geometry

Two-token co-occurrences are arguably the simplest nontrivial statistic of natural language.
Word embedding models such as
word2vec
and GloVe learn exclusively from this statistic
(
21
;
28
)
.
These models learn a word embedding matrix
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}
whose
i^{\text{th}}
row
{\bm{w}}_{i}
is the
d
-dimensional embedding vector for word
i
.
In recent work,
(
16
)
showed that such models learn to represent the top eigenmodes of a
normalized co-occurrence matrix
, approximately equal to the pointwise mutual information (PMI) matrix:

{\bm{M}}^{\star}_{(ij)}\coloneqq\frac{P_{ij}-P_{i}P_{j}}{\frac{1}{2}(P_{ij}+P_{i}P_{j})}\approx\log\left(\frac{P_{ij}}{P_{i}P_{j}}\right).

(1)

In terms of the eigendecomposition
{\bm{M}}^{\star}={\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\star}{\bm{\Phi}}^{\top}
, the learned embeddings are

{\bm{W}}_{(i\mu)}={\bm{\Phi}}_{(i\mu)}\sqrt{|{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{(\mu\mu)}|}.

(2)

Clearly, the
\mu^{\text{th}}
principal component of the embeddings encodes the
\mu^{\text{th}}
-largest eigenmode of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
,
{\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot\mu)}
.

This key result underlies our theoretical analysis, since it provides an analytical link between co-occurrence statistics and learned embedding geometry.
In
Appendix

B
we review this result in detail, including a complete discussion of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
, its interpretation, and its relation to the PMI matrix.

Although this result gives exact expressions for the learned embeddings, it requires numerically diagonalizing
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
in general. In certain cases, however,
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
may be dominated by a simple structure that is amenable to analytic diagonalization. A recent example of this is the work of
(
17
)
, which shows that the parallelogram-like geometry underlying linear analogies can be understood as a consequence of Kronecker structure in
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
. In particular, when the co-occurrence statistics are driven by weakly correlated discrete latent variables, analogy completion by vector addition follows naturally.

We take a similar approach. We show that the manifold geometry of word embeddings can be understood as a consequence of
translation symmetry
in
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
. In particular, when the co-occurrence statistics are driven by
continuous
latent variables, the observed low-dimensional representation manifolds follow naturally.

To fully describe the mutual word embedding geometry for some subset
\mathcal{S}
of the vocabulary, it is sufficient to specify their PCA coordinates.
Let
{\bm{W}}_{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq{\bm{P}}{\bm{W}}_{([\mathcal{S}],\cdot)}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times d}
be the centered embeddings for
\mathcal{S}
, where
{\bm{P}}={\bm{I}}-|\mathcal{S}|^{-1}\bm{1}{\bm{1}}^{\top}
is the mean-centering matrix.
Let its compact SVD be
{\bm{W}}_{\mathcal{S}}={\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{1/2}{\bm{U}}^{\top}
.
Then to describe its PCA geometry, it suffices to predict the elements of
\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq{\bm{W}}_{\mathcal{S}}{\bm{U}}={\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{1/2}
.
The projected embeddings
\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times\min(|\mathcal{S}|,d)}
retain all information contained in the subspace spanned by
{\bm{W}}_{\mathcal{S}}
, thus fully recovering the Gram matrix:
{\bm{W}}_{\mathcal{S}}{\bm{W}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}=\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}
.

Note that if we approximate
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
as positive semi-definite and if the embedding dimension satisfies
d\geq\rank{\bm{M}}^{\star}
, then the uncentered Gram matrix must exactly recover the relevant submatrix of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
, denoted
{\bm{M}}^{\star}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
Therefore
\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}={\bm{P}}{\bm{M}}^{\star}_{\mathcal{S}}{{\bm{P}}}^{\top}
.
The theoretical results in
Section

3
follow from this key fact.

We go beyond this work by 1) providing an explicit mechanism connecting symmetry to representational geometry; 2) demonstrating the phenomenon in word embedding models, arguably the simplest models of language; 3) broadening the scope by handling open boundary conditions and 2-dimensional lattices; 4) giving a detailed mathematical description of the representation manifolds; and 5) importantly, identifying and characterizing the collective nature of the phenomenon.

3
Co-occurrence model with symmetry

We aim to describe the representational geometry of words and phrases that share an underlying continuous concept such as the time of year, historical dates, or geography.
We denote by
D
the dimension of that sematic continuum, and characterize it by “periodic BC” if it displays periodic boundary conditions and by “open BC” otherwise.
As examples, calendar time and the color wheel have
D=1
with periodic BC; historical time and number lines have
D=1
with open BC; and geography of some sub-region of the globe has
D=2
with open BC. We denote by
\mathcal{S}
a subset of the vocabulary representative of a given concept; for example,
\mathcal{S}
may be the 12 months of the year or one hundred US cities.

We endow such a concept continuum with a coordinate system, defining
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
as the coordinate of word
i\in\mathcal{S}
.
For example,
x_{\text{april}}=4
, and
{\bm{x}}_{\text{paris}}=[48.86,2.35]
using geographic coordinates.
We then define
\mathrm{dist}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
as the minimum Euclidean distance between
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
, giving a single notion of distance for both open BC and periodic BC.

Intuitively, we expect that
in the absence of other strongly discriminating features, no particular location on a semantic continuum is distinguished over any other. Hence, we model this translation symmetry in the co-occurrence statistics as follows:

Assumption 3.1
.

Translation symmetry with kernel
C(\cdot)
.
Let
\tilde{C}(\cdot)
be some well-behaved function. For any two words
i,j\in\mathcal{S}
, assume
P_{ij}=P_{i}P_{j}\tilde{C}(\mathrm{dist}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j}))
.
It follows that the co-occurrence matrix
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
(defined in
Equation

1
) inherits the translation symmetry:

{\bm{M}}^{\star}_{(ij)}=f(\tilde{C}(\mathrm{dist}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j})))\eqcolon C(\mathrm{dist}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j})),

(3)

where
f(y)=2(y-1)/(y+1)
. Thus, the
co-occurrence convolution kernel

C
is simply a reparameterization of
\tilde{C}
.
Furthermore, assume that
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
is positive semi-definite.

3.1
states that the co-occurrence probability of any two words in
\mathcal{S}
depends only on their distance on the semantic continuum.
This translation symmetry is a strong constraint on the co-occurrence statistics; nonetheless, we show in
Figures

5
and
6
that it accurately describes real data.
We assume
{\bm{M}}^{\star}\succeq\bm{0}
simply for ease of presentation; in
Section

B.3
we relax this assumption and show that our predictions still hold.

2

In
Section

4
, we show that the continuum coordinates constitute a continuous latent variable that underlies and modulates the co-occurrence statistics. In this model, the symmetry condition hold by construction, not by assumption.

Under
3.1
, rigid transformations of the latent coordinates leave
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
invariant, and isotropic rescaling induces only a scalar reparameterization of
C
. Since our theory depends only on the structure of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
, we may (and do) freely choose a coordinate system satisfying
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in[-1,1]^{D}
.

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\cos(\vk_1^\top\vx_i),\;\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\sin(\vk_1^\top\vx_i),\;

Corollary 2

(Periodized exponential kernel (simplified))
.

Let

Then the PCA-projected embeddings for the words

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{w}}}_{i}=\sqrt{2/|\mathcal{S}|}\Big[

\displaystyle a_{1}\cos(k_1x_i),\;a_{1}\sin(k_1x_i),\;

(5)

\displaystyle a_{2}\cos(k_2x_i),\;a_{2}\sin(k_2x_i),\;

\displaystyle a_{3}\cos(k_3x_i),\;\dots\Big]

3.3
New predictions of the co-occurrence model

Propositions

3

(x(t),y(t))=(A\sin(at),B\sin(bt+\delta))

In

This explains the origin of the “ripples” displayed in 3D visualizations of representation manifolds

translation symmetry in the statistics of language.

A second prediction concerns how models may exploit the Fourier embedding geometry to do useful computations.
We consider the coordinate decoding task in which one aims to predict

from the representation vector
{\bm{w}}_{i}
using a linear probe.
For example,

3.4

{\bm{M}}^{\star}

Figure

4
Collective effects control the embedding of space and time

Thus far, our analysis has focused on embeddings of maximal dimension
d=V
. In that case, the scalar product of two embedding vectors simply corresponds to the associated elements of the co-occurrence matrix. Our assumption above is that if the co-occurrence is restricted to the subset of words considered, e.g. the months of the year, it is a circulant matrix.

Figure

4
(Left) shows however that the circular embedding of the months can be recovered if these two assumptions are broken simultaneously, by removing explicitly the co-occurrences of all the months in the PMI matrix, and by considering smaller embedding dimensions. In that new set-up, the Gram matrix of the month embeddings very nearly matches the raw co-occurrence statistics of the months, despite this reconstruction task utilizing only a small fraction of the eigenspectrum.

Such robustness of embedding properties to perturbation has already been observed in the context of analogical reasoning, e.g.
\text{King}-\text{Queen}+\text{Woman}=\text{Man}

(
21
)
. Remarkably, accurate analogy completions persist even when all sentences with a pair of words involved in the analogy (e.g. King, Queen) are explicitly removed from the corpus
(
4
)
. It was argued that this robustness is a consequence of a low-rank structure of the PMI matrix
(
17
)
. We hypothesize that a similar effect is at play here and provide a mechanism for the emergence of an effectively low-rank structure.

Consider, as an example, the embedding of seasonality, i.e. period of the year (a similar argument can be made e.g. for geography). We argue that many words are “seasonal,” such that they occur more often when certain periods of the year are considered. For example, “ski” is associated to winter and will thus co-occur more frequently with e.g. “December” than “August”; and the reverse can be said of “beach” or “hurricane.” In this view, the time of the year
t
is a latent variable that characterizes many words, which will globally affect the PMI co-occurrence matrix, leading to some large eigenvalues – a structure that is very robust to perturbations. This view is tested empirically in
Figure

4
(Right), where we show that if the months of the year are complemented with highly seasonal words, the modified PMI in which all co-occurrences between months are removed still leads to circular months embeddings of some embedding dimension. This can be contrasted with highly
non-seasonal
words, like the numbers “one” through “seventeen,” which do not enable seasonal reconstruction, as shown in
Figure

14
.

4.1
Co-occurrence arising from a shared continuous latent variable

To illustrate how recovery of the geometry is possible from co-occurrences with other seasonal words, we consider a simple setting where the collection of all seasonal words is governed by a periodic latent variable
t
in the domain
[-T/2,T/2]
, which describes the time of year. Suppose each word
i
has an intrinsic seasonal center
t_{i}
, uniformly distributed in
[-T/2,T/2]
.
t_{i}
is a
D=1
version of the semantic lattice coordinates
{\bm{x}}_{i}
introduced in
Section

3
, but we now consider the larger set - beyond e.g. the calendar months - of seasonal words, which share statistical structure.

For word
i
, we assume its conditional probability
of occurring at season
t
is modulated by

P(i\mid t)=P(i)\bigl(1+g(t-t_{i})\bigr),

(12)

where
g(t)
is a symmetric, zero-mean function peaked at
t=0
that monotonically decreases towards
\pm T/2
. Normalization implies that
\sum_{i}P(i\mid t)=1
. For a vocabulary of
N
words taken to be equispaced in time,
t_{i}=\left(\frac{i}{N}-\frac{1}{2}\right)T
, normalization is obtained in the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
e.g. if the
P(i)
’s are iid variables of finite variance and mean
1/N
.

Averaging over seasons, and assuming conditional independence, yields the joint marginal

\displaystyle P(i,j)

\displaystyle=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\frac{dt}{T}\;P(i\mid t)P(j\mid t)

\displaystyle=P(i)P(j)\Bigl[1+\tilde{K}(t_{i}-t_{j})\Bigr],

(13)

where
\tilde{K}
is the circular autocorrelation

\tilde{K}(\Delta)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}g(u)\,g(u+\Delta)\,du.

(14)

Thus the PMI between two words depends only on their seasonal separation:

\displaystyle\mathrm{PMI}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{P(i,j)}{P(i)P(j)}=\log\!\bigl(1+\tilde{K}(t_{i}-t_{j})\bigr)

(15)

\displaystyle\eqcolon K(t_{i}-t_{j}),

(16)

which defines the kernel
K
(approximately equal to
C(\cdot)
in (
3
)).
\mathrm{PMI}={\bm{K}}
is a circulant matrix.

4.1.1
Diagonalization of the PMI

Circulant matrices are diagonalized by discrete Fourier modes

\displaystyle{\bm{\phi}}_{k(i)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\,e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}kt_{i}/T},\qquad{\bm{K}}{\bm{\phi}}_{k}=\mu_{k}{\bm{\phi}}_{k},

(17)

where
\mu_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}kj/N}K(t_{j})
is the discrete Fourier transform of
K
, and
k=0,\pm 1,...,\pm(N-1)/2
. For large
N
we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{k}/N=\int e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}kt/T}K(t)dt.

(18)

Because
K
is smooth and even,
its Fourier coefficients
\mu_{k}
are real and decay rapidly with
|k|
.
The eigenvector associated with
\mu_{0}
is the constant mode, representing
the global seasonal “mean.”
The next two eigenvectors,
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{-1}=\phi_{1}^{*}
, form a cosine–sine pair corresponding to a once-per-year oscillation.
Subsequent eigenvectors encode higher-frequency seasonal variations.

Robustness of embedding to removal of the PMI elements:

Consider a fixed embedding dimension
d
, in the limit of large
N
. Because eigenvalues and the gap between them (or more precisely between degenerate pairs of eigenvalues) are proportional to
N
, any pertubation that affects a fixed number of entries of the PMI (such as the
12^{2}
co-occurrence between months) will be much smaller than these gaps. Using the Weyl or Davis-Kahan theorem, we obtain that the top
d
eigenvectors and thus the word embeddings are not perturbed in this limit.

4.2
Joint continuous and binary model

In the collective model just described, the only signal assumed to be present in the PMI matrix is “seasonality.” In real data, there are many more signals present; e.g. signals that relate to other continuous latent variables such as “geography” or signals that lead to linear analogies of the type
\text{King}-\text{Man}=\text{Queen}-\text{Woman}
. In
Appendix

D
, we build on
(
17
)
to develop a model where both seasonality and correlations inducing linear analogies are present. We show that circular embedding geometry still emerges in this more realistic framework.

5
Discussion

Word embedding models, text embedding models, and LLMs all learn to represent time and space in a remarkable fashion: cyclic time exhibits circular geometry, absolute time is represented as open one-dimensional manifolds, and geographical location can be decoded by linear probes. We have shown that this apparently universal behavior emerges from a single unifying principle: pairs of words that correspond to similar time or space co-occur more frequently in text. This effect is very much collective, since many words are e.g. seasonal or geographical. As a consequence, learned representational geometry is remarkably robust to local perturbations to the statistics.

An intriguing question is if such arguments play a role in neuroscience as well. In this respect it is worth noting that grid cells, which encode two-dimensional space in the mammalian entorhinal cortex, display a firing pattern that can be interpreted as the interference of a small number of Fourier modes
(
15
;
6
;
34
)
. Our work illustrates that seeking to predict the next position of the animal from a dataset of past trajectories (e.g. characterized as a succession of landmarks as in
(
30
)
) will spontaneously create such representations, in either shallow or deep networks.

Limitations

Our theory is derived in the setting of word embedding algorithms. Remarkably, we demonstrated that it also captures many aspects of the geometry of representations in LLMs. The latter, however, have the ability to adapt to context; for example, ambiguities may be removed. A very illustrative example is shown in
Figure

13
: if a prompt containing “May” provides disambiguating context, the representations of months become increasingly circular as they propagate through the forward pass. Building a theory to explain such phenomena would be very interesting.

In this work we are considering the fact that words can be characterized by continuous attributes. This extends the previous work of
(
17
)
that analyzed binary attributes and the associated emergence of parallelograms in representation space.
(
26
)
have identified the emergence of remarkable geometric structure for hierarchical attributes that currently remains unexplained - providing a global framework for all these properties would be desirable.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine
Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none
which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Appendix B
Review of word embedding algorithms

In this appendix we review recent results showing that word embedding algorithms are well-approximated by spectral methods.
We first review the mechanism by which symmetric word embeddings trained via self-supervised gradient-based algorithms learn the spectral decomposition of the positive part of the co-occurrence matrix
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
.
We then discuss how the PCA geometry of
asymmetric
word embeddings recovers instead the matrix absolute value
|{\bm{M}}^{\star}|
.
Finally, we discuss the relation between
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
and the well-known pointwise mutual information (PMI) matrix.

B.1
Symmetric word embeddings from
{\bm{M}}^{\star}

In the main text, we use
{\bm{W}}
to denote the trained embeddings. Since we are now discussing optimization, we change notation, using
{\bm{W}}
to instead refer to the variable being optimized, and
\hat{\bm{W}}
to denote the optimal value.

Let the co-occurrence (skip-gram) distribution
P_{ij}
and the unigram distribution be defined as in
Section

A.2
.
Let
V
be the number of words in the vocabulary, and let
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}
be a trainable weight matrix whose
i^{\text{th}}
row is the
d
-dimensional embedding vector for word
i
.
Word embedding algorithms such as
word2vec
aim to imbue
{\bm{W}}
with semantic structure so that the inner products between word embeddings capture semantic similarity.
In particular, these self-supervised algorithms iterate over the text corpus, aligning the embeddings of frequently co-occurring words and repelling unrelated words.

(
16
)
show that the following symmetric matrix factorization problem well-approximates
word2vec
, producing word embeddings of comparable linear algebraic structure and semantic quality:

\hat{\bm{W}}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{{\bm{W}}}\|{\bm{W}}{{\bm{W}}}^{\top}-{\bm{M}}^{\star}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

(20)

where the target matrix
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
is defined as in
Equation

1
:

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}^{\star}_{(ij)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{P_{ij}-P_{i}P_{j}}{\frac{1}{2}(P_{ij}+P_{i}P_{j})}

(21)

\displaystyle=2\frac{\frac{P_{ij}}{P_{i}P_{j}}-1}{\frac{P_{ij}}{P_{i}P_{j}}+1}

(22)

\displaystyle\approx\log\left(\frac{P_{ij}}{P_{i}P_{j}}\right).

(23)

Intuitively,
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
encodes the normalized excess co-occurrences of word pairs;
{\bm{M}}^{\star}_{(ij)}>0
when words
i
and
j
co-occur more frequently than if the words were drawn independently, and vice versa.
Some additional properties of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
include that its elements are bounded (
-2\leq{\bm{M}}^{\star}_{(ij)}\leq 2
) and that it is real symmetric (and therefore has a real-valued spectral decomposition
{\bm{M}}^{\star}={\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{\Lambda}}{{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}
).
The approximation in the last line shows that
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
is approximately equal to the
pointwise mutual information
(PMI) matrix, elementwise; we derive and discuss it in
Section

B.4
.

(
16
)
show that running the
word2vec
algorithm is approximately equivalent to running gradient descent on the objective in
Equation

20
, which is known to converge to
\hat{\bm{W}}
with high probability. By the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem, the trained embeddings must have the structure

\hat{\bm{W}}={\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot,:d)}\sqrt{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{(:d,:d)}}

(24)

up to semantically-irrelevant right orthogonal transformations. In other words,
{\bm{W}}
learns to model the
d
largest positive eigenmodes of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
.

A limitation of this analysis is that it is restricted to symmetric factorizations. This prevents
{\bm{W}}
from ever learning eigenmodes of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
with negative eigenvalues. To be precise, let us define the positive semidefinite and negative semidefinite components of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
,
{\bm{M}}^{+}
and
{\bm{M}}^{-}
respectively, to be the unique matrices satisfying

{\bm{M}}^{\star}={\bm{M}}^{+}-{\bm{M}}^{-}\qquad\qquad{\bm{M}}^{+}\succeq\bm{0}\qquad\qquad{\bm{M}}^{-}\succeq\bm{0}\qquad\qquad{\bm{M}}^{+}{\bm{M}}^{-}=\bm{0}.

(25)

Then, even if
{\bm{W}}{{\bm{W}}}^{\top}
has no rank constraint, one may only achieve
{\bm{W}}{{\bm{W}}}^{\top}={\bm{M}}^{+}\neq{\bm{M}}^{\star}
in general.

B.2
Asymmetric word embeddings from
{\bm{M}}^{\star}

To circumvent this limitation, we consider the asymmetric setting

(\hat{\bm{W}},\hat{\bm{W}}^{\prime})=\mathop{\arg\min}_{{\bm{W}},{\bm{W}}^{\prime}}\|{\bm{W}}{{\bm{W}}^{\prime}}^{\top}-{\bm{M}}^{\star}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

(26)

where
{\bm{W}}
is the word embedding matrix and
{\bm{W}}^{\prime}
is known as the context embedding matrix. Unlike the case of symmetric factorization, where global minimizers are all related by right orthogonal transformations, the asymmetric objective has a substantially larger invariance:
(\hat{\bm{W}},\hat{\bm{W}}^{\prime})\equiv(\hat{\bm{W}}{\bm{A}},\hat{\bm{W}}^{\prime}{\bm{A}}^{-\top})
for any invertible matrix
{\bm{A}}
. Therefore the geometric structure of the learned word embeddings
\hat{\bm{W}}
is identifiable only up to arbitrary linear transformations.

This is a highly underdetermined problem. To simplify the analysis of the training dynamics, we consider a special initialization scheme:
{\bm{W}}(0)
initialized with i.i.d. Gaussian elements as usual, and
{\bm{W}}^{\prime}(0)={\bm{Q}}{\bm{W}}(0)
with random orthogonal matrix
{\bm{Q}}
. Then, exploiting the well-known conservation law of gradient flow on deep linear networks, i.e.,
\frac{d}{dt}({{\bm{W}}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}-{{\bm{W}}^{\prime}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}^{\prime})=0
, we conclude that the right singular vectors of
{\bm{W}}
and
{\bm{W}}^{\prime}
must agree throughout training (and likewise for the singular values). Consequently, let us denote the SVD of the optimal
{\bm{W}}
as
\hat{\bm{W}}=\hat{\bm{\Phi}}\hat{\bm{S}}
and the SVD of the optimal
{\bm{W}}^{\prime}
as
\hat{\bm{W}}^{\prime}=\hat{\bm{\Phi}}^{\prime}\hat{\bm{S}}
, where without loss of generality we assume the right singular vectors are simply identity. Invoking Eckart-Mirsky-Young again, and re-indexing the modes of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
to be non-increasing in the
magnitude
of its eigenvalues, we find

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{W}}{\hat{\bm{W}}}^{\prime}{}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\Phi}}\hat{\bm{S}}^{2}{\hat{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\prime}{}^{\top}

(27)

\displaystyle={\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot,:d)}{\bm{\Lambda}}_{(:d,:d)}{{\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot,:d)}}^{\top}

(28)

\displaystyle={\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot,:d)}\sqrt{|{\bm{\Lambda}}_{(:d,:d)}|}\sqrt{|{\bm{\Lambda}}_{(:d,:d)}|}{\bm{D}}{{\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot,:d)}}^{\top}

(29)

where
{\bm{D}}\coloneqq\mathrm{sign}({\bm{\Lambda}})
is the diagonal matrix containing the signs of the modes of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
. Clearly, the learned solutions are

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{W}}

\displaystyle={\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot,:d)}\sqrt{|{\bm{\Lambda}}_{(:d,:d)}|}

(30)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{W}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left({\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot,:d)}{\bm{D}}\right)\sqrt{|{\bm{\Lambda}}_{(:d,:d)}|}.

(31)

Since we will no longer discuss optimization, we revert to our previous notation where
{\bm{W}}
and
{\bm{W}}^{\prime}
refer to the optima. This yields the expression given in
Equation

2
. However, the PCA geometry of the word embeddings is given by the gram matrix
{\bm{W}}{{\bm{W}}}^{\top}
, not the model matrix
{\bm{W}}{{\bm{W}}^{\prime}}^{\top}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}{{\bm{W}}}^{\top}

\displaystyle={\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot,:d)}|{\bm{\Lambda}}_{(:d,:d)}|{{\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot,:d)}}^{\top}

(32)

Indeed, if the embedding dimension is sufficiently large,
d\geq\rank{\bm{M}}^{\star}
, the gram matrix factors the matrix absolute value of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
, defined
|{\bm{M}}^{\star}|\coloneqq{\bm{M}}^{+}+{\bm{M}}^{-}
:

{\bm{W}}{{\bm{W}}}^{\top}=|{\bm{M}}^{\star}|.

(33)

B.3
Weaker version of
3.1

Note that if
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
is positive semi-definite, then
{\bm{M}}^{-}=\bm{0}
, and the result

32
is exactly the same as in the symmetric case. In particular, the gram matrix exactly recovers
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
. This convenience is why we include the PSD assumption in
3.1
.

However, this assumption is violated in real data. Indeed, the empirical spectrum of
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
on Wikipedia has a unimodal distribution peaked near
\lambda=0
, and
\rank{\bm{M}}^{+}\approx\rank{\bm{M}}^{-}
(
Figure

7
). Therefore, in
Figures

1
and
2

Assumption B.1
.

Translation symmetry of

B.4
Relation to PMI matrix

Early word embedding algorithms obtained low-dimensional embeddings by explicitly constructing some target matrix and employing a dimensionality reduction algorithm. One popular choice was the
pointwise mutual information
(PMI) matrix
(
5
)
, defined

\mathrm{PMI}_{(ij)}=\log\frac{P_{ij}}{P_{i}P_{j}}.

(37)

Later,
(
19
)
showed that
\mathrm{PMI}
is the rank-unconstrained minimizer of the
word2vec
objective. To see the relation between
\mathrm{PMI}
and the normalized co-occurrence
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
, let us first write

\log\left(\frac{P_{ij}}{P_{i}P_{j}}\right)=\log(1+\Delta(x_{ij})),

(38)

where the function
\Delta(x)
represents the fractional deviation away from independent word statistics (
P_{ij}=P_{i}P_{j}
), in terms of some small parameter
x
of our choosing (so that
\Delta(0)=0
). This setup allows us to Taylor expand quantities of interest around
x=0
.
A judicious choice will produce terms that cancel the
-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2}
that arises from the Taylor expansion of
\log(1+\Delta)
, leaving only third-order corrections. One such example is
\Delta(x)=2x/(2-x)
, which yields

\mathrm{PMI}=\log\left(1+\frac{2x}{2-x}\right)=x+\frac{x^{3}}{12}+\frac{x^{5}}{80}+\cdots

(39)

and has the solution

x_{ij}=\frac{P_{ij}-P_{i}P_{j}}{\frac{1}{2}(P_{ij}+P_{i}P_{j})}={\bm{M}}^{\star}_{(ij)}.

(40)

This calculation reveals that
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
is a very close approximation to the PMI matrix; the leading correction is third order.
For this reason, we use the PMI matrix in the theoretical analysis of
Section

4
.
However,
{\bm{M}}^{\star}
is empirically much friendlier to least squares approximation because its elements are bounded (
-2\leq{\bm{M}}^{\star}_{(ij)}\leq 2
). Indeed,
(
18
)
observed that word embeddings obtained by least squares factorization of the PMI matrix perform poorly on downstream tasks. One can mitigate this problem by instead factorizing a regularized variant of the PMI matrix:

\mathrm{PMI}_{\epsilon}=\log\left(\frac{P_{ij}}{P_{i}P_{j}}+\epsilon\right)

(41)

for some hyperparameter
\epsilon
.

Appendix D
Combined Model of Seasonality and Binary Semantic Attributes

In Section 4.1, we consider words sharing a continuous latent attribute, but lacking any other additional semantic information. This is an unrealistic assumption, and predicts that the PCA dimensions are strictly Fourier. Here, we now enrich the vocabulary by assigning to each word both a seasonal phase

x\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
and a binary attribute vector

{\bm{a}}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
, where the binary attributes encode additional semantic information (e.g. gender, singular vs. plural, royal vs non-royal, etc…), as in
(
17
)
.
The total vocabulary size is
N2^{d}
.

Multiplicative generative model.

Seasonality and semantic attributes are assumed to influence co-occurrence
independently.
The seasonal factor is the same as in
Section

4
:

F_{\rm time}(x,y)=1+C(t_{x}-t_{y}),

where
C
is the circular autocorrelation of a unimodal kernel
g
.

For semantic attributes we use a fully factorized multiplicative model:

F_{\rm attr}({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})=\prod_{r=1}^{d}(1+s_{r}a_{r}b_{r}),

(42)

where
s_{r}
measures the strength of attribute
r
.

Thus the generative probability of observing
(x,{\bm{a}})
and
(y,{\bm{b}})
jointly is

P((x,{\bm{a}}),(y,{\bm{b}}))=P(x,{\bm{a}})P(y,{\bm{b}})\,F_{\rm time}(x,y)\,F_{\rm attr}({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}).

(43)

Exact PMI and decomposition into constant and structured terms

The PMI is

\mathrm{PMI}((x,{\bm{a}}),(y,{\bm{b}}))=\log F_{\rm time}(x,y)+\log F_{\rm attr}({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}).

The semantic factor contributes

\displaystyle\log F_{\rm attr}({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{d}\log(1+s_r a_r b_r).

(44)

Because
a_{r}b_{r}\in\{\pm 1\}
, each term admits the exact decomposition

\log(1+s_r a_r b_r)=\alpha_{r}+\beta_{r}\,a_{r}b_{r},

with

\alpha_{r}=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[\log(1+s_r)+\log(1-s_r)\right],\qquad\beta_{r}=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[\log(1+s_r)-\log(1-s_r)\right].

Hence

\log F_{\rm attr}(a,b)=A+\sum_{r=1}^{d}\beta_{r}\,a_{r}b_{r},\qquad A:=\sum_{r=1}^{d}\alpha_{r}.

(45)

The constant
A
affects all pairs equally, whereas the second term encodes the
semantic structure.

For the seasonal part we define:

\log F_{\rm time}(x,y)=K_{t}(t_{x}-t_{y}).

Combining these contributions yields the
exact
PMI up to seasonal
linearization:

\boxed{\mathrm{PMI}((x,{\bm{a}}),(y,{\bm{b}}))=K_{t}(t_{x}-t_{y})+A+\sum_{r=1}^{d}\beta_{r}\,a_{r}b_{r}.}

(E.1)

Matrix structure on the product space

Let
{\bm{J}}_{\rm time}
and
{\bm{J}}_{\rm attr}
denote the all-ones matrices of sizes

N
and
2^{d}
, respectively.
Define

K(x,y)=K_{t}(t_{x}-t_{y}),\qquad K_{\rm attr}({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})=\sum_{r=1}^{d}\beta_{r}a_{r}b_{r}.

Then (E.1) takes the exact Kronecker form

\boxed{\mathbf{PMI}={\bm{K}}_{t}\otimes{\bm{J}}_{\rm attr}\;+\;{\bm{J}}_{\rm time}\otimes{\bm{K}}_{\rm attr}\;+\;A\,{\bm{J}}_{\rm time}\otimes{\bm{J}}_{\rm attr}.}

(E.2)

Each term acts on a distinct tensor component:

•

{\bm{K}}_{t}
and
{\bm{J}}_{\rm time}
act on seasonal indices,

•

{\bm{K}}_{\rm attr}
and
{\bm{J}}_{\rm attr}
act on attribute indices.

Spectral decomposition

We diagonalize the PMI in the product basis of: (i) Fourier modes
\phi_{k}
on the seasonal circle, and (ii) Walsh characters
\psi_{S}
, which serve as the equivalent Fourier basis on the hypercube. For any subset of attribute indices
S\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,d\}

\Psi_{S}({\bm{a}})
is defined
\Psi_{S}({\bm{a}})\equiv\prod_{r\in S}{\bm{a}}_{r}
.

Seasonal operators.

As before,

{\bm{K}}_{t}\phi_{k}=\mu_{k}\,\phi_{k},\qquad{\bm{J}}_{\rm time}\phi_{0}=N\,\phi_{0},\qquad{\bm{J}}_{\rm time}\phi_{k\neq 0}=0.

where the relation on

Attribute operators.

Walsh characters satisfy

{\bm{J}}_{\rm attr}\psi_{\varnothing}=2^{d}\psi_{\varnothing},\qquad{\bm{J}}_{\rm attr}\psi_{S\neq\varnothing}=0,

and

{\bm{K}}_{\rm attr}\psi_{\{r\}}=2^{d}\beta_{r}\,\psi_{\{r\}},\qquad{\bm{K}}_{\rm attr}\psi_{S}=0\text{ if }S=\varnothing\text{ or }|S|>1.

which follows from the fact that
{\bm{K}}({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})=\sum_{r}^{d}\beta_{r}\psi_{\{r\}}({\bm{a}})\psi_{\{r\}}({\bm{b}})

d

Joint eigenbasis.

All terms in (E.2) are diagonal in the product basis

\Phi_{k,S}(x,{\bm{a}})=\phi_{k}(x)\psi_{S}({\bm{a}}).

The three components contribute:

({\bm{K}}_{t}\otimes J_{\rm attr}):\qquad 2^{d}\,\mu_{k}\ \text{on }S=\varnothing;

({\bm{J}}_{\rm time}\otimes{\bm{K}}_{\rm attr}):\qquad N\,2^{d}\beta_{r}\ \text{on }(k=0,S=\{r\});

Thus:

(Spectrum of the combined seasonal–attribute PMI)

For the PMI defined in

E.1
Latent semantic lattice

Let
\mathcal{S}
be any subset of the vocabulary with cardinality
|\mathcal{S}|=L^{D}
, for positive integers

and

\mathcal{N}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\{-\tfrac{L}{2},-\tfrac{L}{2}+1,\dots,\tfrac{L}{2}-1\}^{D},&L\text{ even},\\[4.0pt]

(46)

This defines a simple integer lattice in

L

|\mathcal{N}|=L^{D}
.

For each word

, let

be a bijective map from a word to its lattice index.
Define the latent semantic continuum coordinate for word

.
Thus the latent coordinates constitute a lattice on the interval
[-1,1]^{D}
.

E.1.1
Wavevectors for periodic BC

With periodic boundary conditions, we may define the associated reciprocal lattice. We begin with the auxiliary notion of a positive half-space: we write
{\bm{n}}\succ\bm{0}
if the first nonzero component of
{\bm{n}}
is positive, i.e.,

{\bm{n}}\succ\bm{0}\iff\exists j\in\{1,\dots,D\}\text{ such that }n_{1}=\cdots=n_{j-1}=0\text{ and }n_{j}>0.

(47)

Next, for each
{\bm{n}}\in\mathcal{N}
we define the periodized negation
\ominus{\bm{n}}\in\mathcal{N}
to be the unique element satisfying

\ominus{\bm{n}}\equiv-{\bm{n}}\pmod{L}\quad\text{(componentwise)}.

(48)

Then we may define the following disjoint sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{+}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\pi{\bm{n}}:{\bm{n}}\in\mathcal{N}\text{ and }\ominus{\bm{n}}\neq{\bm{n}}\text{ and }{\bm{n}}\succ\bm{0}\},

(49)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{-}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\ominus{\bm{k}}:{\bm{k}}\in\mathcal{K}_{+}\},

(50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sc}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\pi{\bm{n}}:{\bm{n}}\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}\text{ and }\ominus{\bm{n}}={\bm{n}}\},

(51)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\bm{0}\}.

(52)

Their union comprises the set of wavevectors allowed by the periodic boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathcal{K}_{+}\;\cup\;\mathcal{K}_{-}\;\cup\;\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sc}}\;\cup\;\mathcal{K}_{0}

(53)

\displaystyle=\{\pi{\bm{n}}_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\}.

(54)

Despite being constructed from the same index set, the lattice coordinates
\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i}
and the wavevectors
\{{\bm{k}}_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
are not commensurate. The coordinates occupy a lattice in real space whereas the wavevectors live on a lattice in reciprocal space.

Each wavevector
{\bm{k}}\in\mathcal{K}
is associated with the plane wave mode
\exp(\iu\vk^\top\vx)
.

\mathcal{K}_{+}
and
\mathcal{K}_{-}
correspond to
\pm
mode pairs,
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sc}}
corresponds to nonzero self-conjugate modes, and
\mathcal{K}_{0}
corresponds to the constant (DC) mode. When
L
is odd, the only self-conjugate wavevector is
{\bm{k}}=\bm{0}
, so
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sc}}=\varnothing
; when
L
is even there are

nonzero self-conjugate modes, which lie on the faces of the Brillouin zone and correspond to plane waves at the Nyquist frequency of the lattice.

Define
P\coloneqq|\mathcal{K}_{+}|
and
S\coloneqq|\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sc}}|
. We choose an ordering
\mu\mapsto{\bm{k}}_{\mu}
of the wavevectors
\mathcal{K}
such that

\mathcal{K}=\{{\bm{k}}_{2p-1},{\bm{k}}_{2p}\}_{p=1}^{P}\;\cup\;\{{\bm{k}}_{2P+s}\}_{s=1}^{S}\;\cup\;\{{\bm{k}}_{2P+S+1}\},

(55)

with

{\bm{k}}_{2p-1}\in\mathcal{K}_{+},\qquad{\bm{k}}_{2p}=\ominus{\bm{k}}_{2p-1}\in\mathcal{K}_{-},\qquad{\bm{k}}_{2P+s}\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sc}},\qquad{\bm{k}}_{2P+S+1}=\bm{0}.

(56)

Thus
\mathcal{K}
contains first the conjugate pairs (in arbitrary order), followed by the nonzero self-conjugate modes (arbitrary order), followed by the zero mode.

B.2
that
{\bm{W}}{{\bm{W}}}^{\top}=|{\bm{M}}^{\star}|
. Therefore the PCA-projected embeddings
\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}
factorize the centered submatrix of
|{\bm{M}}^{\star}|
:

\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}{\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}}^{\top}={\bm{P}}{\bm{H}}{{\bm{P}}}^{\top}

(58)

and the PCA geometry of the projected embeddings can be directly obtained from the spectral decomposition of
{\bm{P}}{\bm{H}}{{\bm{P}}}^{\top}
.

In general, knowing the spectral decomposition of
{\bm{H}}
does not allow one to directly infer the spectral decomposition of its centered version. However, in this case, we will find that the constant mode
\bm{1}
is an eigenvector of
{\bm{H}}
, so centering simply removes this mode without mixing eigenvectors, allowing the remaining spectrum to be read off directly.
With this knowledge, we may begin by explicitly diagonalizing
{\bm{H}}
.

We will follow the standard argument for diagonalizing block-circulant matrices with circulant blocks. Let
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
be a candidate eigenvector of
{\bm{H}}
.
We define
v({\bm{x}}_{i})\coloneqq{\bm{v}}_{(i)}
and use a plane wave ansatz:
v({\bm{x}}_{i})=\exp(\iu\T\vk\vx_i)
, where
\mathrm{i}
is the imaginary unit and
{\bm{k}}\in\mathcal{K}
.
Then the
j
-th coordinate of the image of
{\bm{v}}
is

\displaystyle[{\bm{H}}{\bm{v}}]_{(j)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}C\!\left(\mathrm{dist}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j})\right)v({\bm{x}}_{i})

(59)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}}C\!\left(\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{a}})\right)\exp(\iu\T\vk(\vx_j+\va))

(60)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}}C\!\left(\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{a}})\right)\exp(\iu\T\vk\va)\right){\bm{v}}_{{(j)}}

(61)

\displaystyle=\tilde{m}({\bm{k}}){\bm{v}}_{{(j)}},

(62)

where in the second step we use the periodic lattice symmetry and perform the change of variables
{\bm{a}}\coloneqq{\bm{x}}_{p}-{\bm{x}}_{q}
, and the final step simply recognizes the discrete Fourier transform on the lattice. This confirms that
{\bm{v}}
is a (complex-valued) eigenvector of
{\bm{H}}
with eigenvalue
\tilde{m}({\bm{k}})
. The eigenvalue is real, by the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices.

The function
C(\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{x}}))
is real-valued and even in
{\bm{x}}
, implying conjugate symmetry in Fourier space:
\tilde{m}({\bm{k}})=\tilde{m}(\ominus{\bm{k}})
. Therefore the eigenvectors come in degenerate pairs, and we may superpose them in the standard way to obtain real-valued Fourier modes. These Fourier eigenvectors, considered over all lattice wavevectors
{\bm{k}}
, form the standard orthonormal
D
-dimensional discrete Fourier basis and hence span
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
. Thus, we have fully diagonalized
{\bm{H}}
:

\displaystyle{{\bm{H}}}_{(ij)}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\text{odd }\mu\leq 2P}\tilde{m}({\bm{k}}_{\mu})\frac{\sin(\T\vk_\mu\vx_i)\sin(\T\vk_\mu\vx_j)}{|\mathcal{S}|/2}

(63)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\text{even }\mu\leq 2P}\tilde{m}({\bm{k}}_{\mu})\frac{\cos(\T\vk_\mu\vx_i)\cos(\T\vk_\mu\vx_j)}{|\mathcal{S}|/2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{2P<\mu\leq 2P+S}\tilde{m}({\bm{k}}_{\mu})\frac{\cos(\T\vk_\mu\vx_i)\cos(\T\vk_\mu\vx_j)}{|\mathcal{S}|}

\displaystyle+\tilde{m}(\bm{0})\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}.

The third sum is over nonzero self-conjugate modes; since these modes are at the Nyquist frequency, their values simply alternate
\pm 1
on the lattice. Therefore the eigenvector normalization is
\sqrt{1/|\mathcal{S}|}
rather than
\sqrt{2/|\mathcal{S}|}
. The same holds for the constant mode (final term).

It is easy to verify that centering annihilates the constant mode. Therefore the Gram matrix of the centered embeddings satisfy

\displaystyle\left[\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}{\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}}^{\top}\right]_{(ij)}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\text{odd }\mu\leq 2P}\tilde{m}({\bm{k}}_{\mu})\frac{\sin(\T\vk_\mu\vx_i)\sin(\T\vk_\mu\vx_j)}{|\mathcal{S}|/2}

(64)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\text{even }\mu\leq 2P}\tilde{m}({\bm{k}}_{\mu})\frac{\cos(\T\vk_\mu\vx_i)\cos(\T\vk_\mu\vx_j)}{|\mathcal{S}|/2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{2P<\mu\leq 2P+S}\tilde{m}({\bm{k}}_{\mu})\frac{\cos(\T\vk_\mu\vx_i)\cos(\T\vk_\mu\vx_j)}{|\mathcal{S}|}

Since the sinusoidal modes are mutually orthogonal, the Gram matrix is already expressed in the diagonalized form
\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}{\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}}^{\top}={\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{\Lambda}}{{\bm{\Phi}}}^{\top}
,
where the columns
{\bm{\Phi}}_{(\cdot\mu)}
encode the normalized sinusoids,
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{(\mu\mu)}=\tilde{m}({\bm{k}}_{\mu})
, and
{\bm{D}}\coloneqq\mathrm{sign}({\bm{\Lambda}})
.
Recalling the definition of PCA projection given in
Section

2.1
, we recognize that this immediately yields PCA coordinates of
{\bm{W}}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
Proposition

1
follows immediately.

\blacksquare

E.3
Proof of Fourier representation geometry under exponential kernel (periodic BC)

Corollary

2
, precise statement.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be any subset of the vocabulary.
Using the notation defined in
Section

E.1
, we define the associated 1
D
latent semantic lattice
\{x_{i}\}
with length
L
, as well as the associated allowed wavenumbers
\mathcal{K}
with enumeration
\{k_{\mu}\}
and partition indices
P,S
.
Let
{\bm{M}}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
denote the normalized co-occurrence matrix.
Let
\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}
denote the PCA-projected embeddings for
\mathcal{S}
as specified in
Section

B.2
and
Section

2.1
.

Assume
B.1
holds on
\mathcal{S}
.
Assume the co-occurrence convolution kernel has the functional form
C(\Delta x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(-|\Delta x+2n|/\sigma)
, where
\sigma
is a free parameter.
Assume the embedding dimension satisfies
d\geq\rank{\bm{M}}^{\star}
.

Then, up to orthogonal transformations within degenerate subspaces, and up to permutations of the principal directions, the PCA-projected embeddings are given by

\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}{(i\mu)}}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}a_{\mu}\sin(k_\mu x_i)&\text{ if }\mu\leq 2P\text{ and }\mu\text{ is odd, }\\[12.0pt]
\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}a_{\mu}\cos(k_\mu x_i)&\text{ if }\mu\leq 2P\text{ and }\mu\text{ is even, }\\[12.0pt]
\sqrt{\frac{1}{L}}a_{\mu}\cos(k_\mu x_i)&\text{ if }2P<\mu\leq(2P+S),\\[12.0pt]
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(65)

where

a_{\mu}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}\;\frac{1-q^{2}}{1-2q\cos(2 k_\mu/ L)+q^{2}}},\qquad q\coloneqq e^{-2/(\sigma L)}

(66)

which has the continuum limit

\lim_{L\to\infty}a_{\mu}=\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma}{1+\sigma^{2}k_{\mu}^{2}}}.

(67)

Proof.

Applying
Proposition

1
, we see that
Corollary

2
holds if we equate
a_{\mu}=\sqrt{|\tilde{m}(k_{\mu})|}
. We define for convenience
q\coloneqq e^{-2/(\sigma L)}
. Under the periodized exponential kernel, the transfer function is

\displaystyle\tilde{m}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{L}\sum_{x\in\{x_{i}\}}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x+2n|}{\sigma}\right)\right)e^{-\mathrm{i}kx}

(68)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{L}\Big(1+2\sum_{r\geq 1}q^{r}\cos(2kr/L)\Big)

(69)

using a coordinate relabeling and standard trigonometric identities. Now, using the fact
|q|<1
, we apply the standard geometric series identity
\sum_{r\geq 1}q^{r}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta r}=qe^{\mathrm{i}\theta}/(1-qe^{\mathrm{i}\theta})
, take the real part, and obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{m}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{L}\left(1+2\real\!\left(\frac{qe^{2\mathrm{i}k/L}}{1-qe^{2\mathrm{i}k/L}}\right)\right)

(70)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{L}\;\frac{1-q^{2}}{1-2q\cos(2k/L)+q^{2}}.

(71)

The
L\to\infty
limit can be found by direct computation.

E.4
Proof of Fourier representation geometry under exponential kernel (open BC)

Proposition

3
, precise statement.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be any subset of the vocabulary.
Using the notation defined in
Section

E.1
, we define the associated 1
D
latent semantic lattice
\{x_{i}\}
with length
L
with open boundary conditions.
Let
{\bm{M}}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
denote the normalized co-occurrence matrix.
Let
\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}
denote the PCA-projected embeddings for
\mathcal{S}
as specified in
Section

B.2
and
Section

2.1
.

Assume
3.1
holds on
\mathcal{S}
.
Assume the co-occurrence convolution kernel has the functional form
C(\Delta x)=\exp(-|\Delta x|/\sigma)
, where
\sigma
is a free parameter.
Assume the embedding dimension satisfies
d\geq\rank{\bm{M}}^{\star}
.

Then in the continuum limit the PCA-aligned embeddings are given by

\lim_{L\to\infty}\sqrt{L}\;\;\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}{(i\mu)}}=\begin{cases}a_{\mu}\dfrac{\sin(k_\mu x_i)}{N_{\mu}}&\text{ if }\mu\text{ is odd, }\\[12.0pt]
a_{\mu}\dfrac{\cos(k_{\mu}x_i)-\frac{\sin k_{\mu}}{k_{\mu}}}{N_{\mu}}&\text{ if }\mu\text{ is even,}\end{cases}

(72)

where for each mode index
\mu
, the wavenumber
k_{\mu}>0
, the normalization
N_{\mu}>0
, and the singular value
a_{\mu}>0
are given as follows.
k_{\mu}
is the unique solution to the self-consistent quantization condition

k_{\mu}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{(\mu+1)\pi}{2}-\tan^{-1}(\sigma k_{\mu}),&\mu\text{ odd},\\
\dfrac{\mu\pi}{2}+\tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{k_{\mu}}{1+\sigma(1+\sigma)k_{\mu}^{2}}\right),&\mu\text{ even}.\end{cases}

(73)

It follows that
k_{\mu}<k_{\mu+1}
. The normalization is

N_{\mu}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sin(2k_{\mu})}{4k_{\mu}}}&\mu\text{ odd},\\
\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sin(2k_{\mu})}{4k_{\mu}}-\left(\frac{\sin k_{\mu}}{k_{\mu}}\right)^{2}}&\mu\text{ even}.\end{cases}

(74)

The singular values are

a_{\mu}=\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma}{1+\sigma^{2}k_{\mu}^{2}}}.

(75)

Proof.

In the continuum limit, we update our variables as follows. The lattice
\{x_{i}\}
becomes the continuum
x\in[-1,1]
with uniform measure. Vectors become functions, and in particular, eigenmodes
{\bm{\phi}}
become eigenfunctions denoted
\phi(x)
.
The submatrix of
|{\bm{M}}^{\star}|
corresponding to words in
\mathcal{S}
becomes the kernel operator denoted
H
, whose action on a function
f
can be expressed as the integral transform

(Hf)(x):=\int_{-1}^{1}C(|x-y|)\,f(y)\,dy,

(76)

where
C(|x-y|)=e^{-|x-y|/\sigma}
is the co-occurrence kernel. The mean-centering matrix
{\bm{P}}
becomes the self-adjoint operator
P
that projects out the constant function:

(Pf)(x)\coloneqq((I-\Pi)f)(x)=f(x)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}f(y)\,dy.

(77)

where
I
is identity and
\Pi
is the rank-one projector onto the constant mode.

Warm-up.

To obtain the embedding geometry, we must diagonalize the operator
PHP
. As a warm-up, let us first diagonalize the uncentered co-occurrence operator
H
.
A general solution strategy is given by
(
36
)
; here we specialize and simplify the solution for our setup.
We begin by obtaining endpoint identities for functions in the image of
H
, e.g.,
u=Hf
, by differentiating the integral representation of
H
and evaluating at the endpoints:

u^{\prime}(1)=-\frac{1}{\sigma}u(1),\qquad u^{\prime}(-1)=\frac{1}{\sigma}u(-1).

(78)

We then notice that
C
satisfies the identity

\left(1-\sigma^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\right)e^{-|x|/\sigma}=2\sigma\,\delta(x).

(79)

This convenient identity states that
H
is the Green’s function of the linear differential operator

L\coloneqq\frac{1-\sigma^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma}

(80)

on
[-1,1]
with the boundary conditions given in
Equation

78
. Thus, diagonalizing
H=L^{-1}
amounts to diagonalizing
L
, i.e., if
H\phi=\lambda\phi
then
L\phi=\lambda^{-1}\phi
. We begin by evaluating the latter equation, yielding

\phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{2\sigma}{\lambda}-1\right)\phi=0.

(81)

Associating
k^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{2\sigma}{\lambda}-1\right)
, we see that this is simply the homogeneous Helmholtz equation, i.e., the harmonic oscillator ODE. This implies that the eigenfunctions must be sinusoids with wavenumber
k
. Solving
\lambda
in terms of
k
, we find that the eigenvalues of the exponential kernel
H
are

\lambda=\frac{2\sigma}{1+\sigma^{2}k^{2}}.

(82)

Since
H
and its domain have parity symmetry around the origin, if
\phi(x)
is an eigenfunction then so must be
\phi(-x)
with the same eigenvalue. Furthermore, since
L
defines a regular Sturm–Liouville problem on a finite interval with separated boundary conditions, we may use the known fact that such operators have non-degenerate spectra. This implies that
\phi(x)=\pm\phi(-x)
. This constraint precludes both complex plane wave solutions and sinusoids with arbitrary phase shifts; each eigenfunction must be either
\sin(kx)
or
\cos(kx)
.
Plugging these into the boundary conditions (
Equation

78
) yields constraints on the possible values of
k
.

With mean-centering.

We now aim to obtain the spectral decomposition of the mean-centered operator
H_{c}\coloneqq PHP
. We first observe that since
Pf=f
for any odd function
f
, the odd sector solutions obtained above are unaffected. Let us now consider how the even modes change under mean-centering.

First we observe that since
P
projects out the DC mode,
H_{c}\phi
must have no DC component. If
\phi
is an eigenfunction, then
\phi
can have no DC component, and
P\phi=\phi
. Therefore the eigenproblem for
H_{c}
becomes

\displaystyle\lambda\phi

\displaystyle=PHP\phi

(83)

\displaystyle=PH\phi

(84)

\displaystyle=H\phi-\Pi H\phi.

(85)

Note that the last term is a constant function (whose amplitude depends on
\phi
and
\lambda
). Denoting
\alpha\coloneqq\Pi H\phi
, we obtain the integral equation

H\phi=\lambda\phi+\alpha.

(86)

Acting on both sides with
L
, we obtain

\displaystyle LH\phi

\displaystyle=\lambda L\phi+L\alpha

(87)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(\frac{\phi-\sigma^{2}\phi^{\prime\prime}}{2\sigma}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{2\sigma}

(88)

which simplifies to

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{2\sigma}{\lambda}-1\right)\phi=\frac{\alpha}{\lambda\sigma^{2}}.

(89)

This is simply the
inhomogeneous
Helmholtz equation with constant driving. Since
\phi=\mathrm{const.}
is a particular solution, and since the Helmholtz equation is a linear ODE, the general solutions must be sinusoids offset by constants. We have already seen that the constant offset for the odd solutions is zero (since there we have
\alpha=0
). Additionally, we have already discussed that the constant offset must be exactly such that the DC component of
\phi
vanishes. Therefore the even solutions take the form

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\cos(kx)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\cos(ky)\;dy

(90)

\displaystyle=\cos(kx)-\frac{\sin k}{k}

(91)

where, just as before,
k^{2}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{2\sigma}{\lambda}-1\right)
. Therefore the eigenvalue
\lambda
is unaffected by centering.

Now we impose the boundary conditions to constrain the possible values of
k
.
Differentiating (
86
) and evaluating at
x=\pm 1
yields the centered boundary conditions

\phi^{\prime}(1)=-\frac{1}{\sigma}\phi(1)-\frac{\alpha}{\lambda\sigma},\qquad\phi^{\prime}(-1)=+\frac{1}{\sigma}\phi(-1)+\frac{\alpha}{\lambda\sigma}.

(92)

In the odd sector,
\alpha=0
and the boundary conditions reduce to those in the warm-up. Plugging in the candidate eigenfunction
\phi(x)=\sin(kx)
, we find the quantization condition

k\cos k=-\frac{1}{\sigma}\sin k\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\tan k=-\sigma k.

(93)

In the even sector, we plug
Equation

91
into the inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation and obtain

\frac{\alpha}{\lambda\sigma^{2}}=-k\sin k.

(94)

Evaluating the boundary conditions
Equation

92
yields the quantization condition

\cos k=\sin k\left(\frac{1}{k}+\sigma(1+\sigma)k\right)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\tan k=\frac{k}{1+\sigma(1+\sigma)k^{2}}.

(95)

Combining the odd-sector quantization (
93
) with the centered-even
quantization (
95
), we may write the self-consistent enumerated
forms (with
k_{\mu}>0
the unique solution in the appropriate interval for each
\mu
):

k_{\mu}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{(\mu+1)\pi}{2}-\tan^{-1}(\sigma k_{\mu}),&\mu\text{ odd},\\[10.0pt]
\dfrac{\mu\pi}{2}+\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{k_{\mu}}{1+\sigma(1+\sigma)k_{\mu}^{2}}\right),&\mu\text{ even}.\end{cases}

(96)

Since the argument of each
\tan^{-1}(\cdot)
is strictly positive, those terms are strictly in the interval
(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Therefore
k_{\mu}<k_{\mu+1}
.

Finally, we compute the normalizing constants for the eigenfunctions. For odd modes
\phi_{\mu}(x)=\sin(k_\mu x)
,

N_{\mu}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\sin^{2}(k_{\mu}x)\,dx=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sin(2k_\mu)}{4k_{\mu}}.

(97)

For even modes
\phi_{\mu}(x)=\cos(k_\mu x)-\sin k_{\mu}/k_{\mu}
,

N_{\mu}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sin(2k_\mu)}{4k_{\mu}}-\left(\frac{\sin k_{\mu}}{k_{\mu}}\right)^{2}.

(98)

Since the centered operator
H_{c}
is diagonalized by the above odd and centered-even
eigenfunctions, with eigenvalues
\lambda_{\mu}=2\sigma/(1+\sigma^{2}k_{\mu}^{2})
, the coefficients of the word embeddings are simply the singular values
a_{\mu}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\mu}}
. This yields the stated PCA-aligned embeddings.

\blacksquare

E.5
Proof of lattice coordinate decoding

Proposition

4
, precise statement.

We work in the setting of
Proposition

1
, which defines the vocabulary subset
\mathcal{S}
; the lattice coordinates
\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}
and lattice length
L
; the wavevectors
\{{\bm{k}}_{\mu}\}
and their partition indices
P,S
; the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{\mu}\}
; and the PCA projection of the embeddings
\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{S}|}
.

Assume
\lambda_{\mu}
is monotone non-increasing with
{\bm{k}}_{\mu}
. For simplicity, we assume
L
is odd; the analysis and result is almost identical for the even case.

Let
r\leq|\mathcal{S}|
be the embedding rank, and define
{\bm{W}}_{r}
to be the Eckart-Young-Mirsky rank-
r
approximation of
\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}
. We assume this approximation is unique, i.e.,
r
is chosen such that there is a spectral gap at the truncation rank. (In other words,
r
is chosen so that the truncation does not select only a strict subset of a degenerate singular subspace.)

Let
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times D}
be the matrix of lattice coordinates.
Let
{\bm{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times D}
be a linear probe.
Define the decoding error as

\varepsilon^{2}({\bm{\Omega}};r)\coloneqq\frac{\|{\bm{W}}{\bm{\Omega}}-{\bm{X}}\|_{F}^{2}}{\|{\bm{X}}\|_{F}^{2}}.

(99)

Then

\min_{\bm{\Omega}}\varepsilon^{2}({\bm{\Omega}};r)\leq\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\frac{L^{2}}{L^{2}-1}\left(\left(\frac{r}{\mathrm{Vol}_{D}}\right)^{1/D}-\frac{\sqrt{D}}{2}\right)^{-1}.

(100)

Proof.

We recall from
Proposition

1
that
\overline{{\bm{W}}}_{\mathcal{S}}={\bm{F}}{\bm{A}}
, where the eigenmodes
{\bm{F}}_{(\cdot,\mu)}
are normalized, discrete, real-valued Fourier modes, and
{\bm{A}}
is the diagonal matrix of amplitudes whose elements are given by the square root of the eigenvalues of
|{\bm{M}}^{\star}|_{\mathcal{S}}
.
It follows that
{\bm{W}}_{r}={\bm{F}}_{r}{\bm{A}}_{r}
.

The mean-squared-error optimal
{\bm{\Omega}}
is simply the OLS solution,
\hat{\bm{\Omega}}=({{\bm{W}}}^{\top}_{r}{\bm{W}}_{r})^{-1}{{\bm{W}}}^{\top}_{r}{\bm{X}}
. Therefore

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{r}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}_{r}({{\bm{W}}_{r}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}_{r})^{-1}{{\bm{W}}_{r}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}

(101)

\displaystyle={\bm{F}}_{r}{\bm{A}}_{r}\left({\bm{A}}_{r}{{\bm{F}}_{r}}^{\top}{\bm{F}}_{r}{\bm{A}}_{r}\right)^{-1}{\bm{A}}_{r}{{\bm{F}}_{r}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}

(102)

\displaystyle={\bm{F}}_{r}{{\bm{F}}_{r}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}

(103)

where in the last step we use the semi-orthogonality of
{\bm{F}}_{r}
. Note that
{\bm{F}}_{r}{{\bm{F}}}^{\top}_{r}
is an orthogonal projection matrix; it projects
{\bm{X}}
into its top-
r
Fourier approximation. The remainder of the proof is in simply computing the resulting error.

Let
{\bm{k}}_{n}
denote the
n
-th smallest wavevector (by magnitude), and let
{\bm{\phi}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
be the corresponding lattice Fourier mode. The ordering of wavevectors sharing the same magnitude may be arbitrary.
Additionally, since
\varepsilon^{2}
is invariant to rescaling of the coordinates, let us rescale
{\bm{X}}\mapsto(L/2){\bm{X}}
so that the lattice coordinates are integers. The allowed wavevectors are rescaled commensurately:
{\bm{k}}\mapsto(2/L){\bm{k}}
.

Then the numerator of the minimal error is

\displaystyle\|{\bm{W}}_{r}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}-{\bm{X}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|({\bm{I}}_{|\mathcal{S}|}-{\bm{F}}_{r}{{\bm{F}}_{r}}^{\top}){\bm{X}}\|_{F}^{2}

(104)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{D}\sum_{n={r+1}}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\left({{\bm{\phi}}_{n}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{(\cdot\ell)}\right)^{2}.

(105)

This equation states that the coordinate decoding error decomposes into overlaps between the lattice coordinates and the short-wavelength Fourier modes not realized in the embedding vectors.
To make further progress, let us unroll one of the overlap terms. WLOG let us examine the case
\ell=1
:

{{\bm{\phi}}_{n}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{(\cdot,1)}=\sum_{x_{1}=-M}^{M}x_{1}\sum_{x_{2}=-M}^{M}\cdots\sum_{x_{D}=-M}^{M}\sqrt{\frac{2}{|\mathcal{S}|}}\exp(\iu\sum_{p=1}^D (\vk_n)_\idx{p} x_p)

(106)

where we use the fact the lattice length is odd,
L=2M+1
. We implicitly understand that this equation takes the real or imaginary parts as prescribed by
{\bm{\phi}}_{n}
.

One may verify that this inner product vanishes if, for any
p\neq\ell
, the corresponding wavevector coordinate
({\bm{k}}_{n})_{(p)}\neq 0
. Thus, the only inner products that survive are the ones for which the wavevector is perfectly aligned with the lattice coordinate in question, i.e.,
\hat{\bm{e}}_{\ell}^{\top}{\bm{k}}_{n}=|{\bm{k}}_{n}|
.
Furthermore, the real part also vanishes since
f(x_{\ell})=x_{\ell}
is odd while
\cos(kx_\ell)
is even. Together, this gives

\displaystyle{{\bm{\phi}}_{n}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{(\cdot\ell)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{|\mathcal{S}|}}\sum_{x_{1}=-M}^{M}x_{1}\sin(|\vk_n| x_1)\sum_{x_{2}=-M}^{M}\cdots\sum_{x_{D}=-M}^{M}1

(107)

\displaystyle=L^{D-1}\sqrt{\frac{2}{L^{D}}}\sum_{x_{1}=-M}^{M}x_{1}\sin(|\vk| x_1)

(108)

if
\hat{\bm{e}}_{\ell}^{\top}{\bm{k}}_{n}=|{\bm{k}}_{n}|
and
{\bm{\phi}}_{n}
odd, and 0 otherwise. Therefore the squared overlap is

\displaystyle\left({{\bm{\phi}}_{n}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{(\cdot\ell)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2L^{D-1}}{L}\left(\sum_{x=-M}^{M}x\sin(|\vk_n| x)\right)^{2}.

(109)

Note that this does not depend on
\ell
. Thus, the
D
-dimensional lattice problem decouples into independent 1D problems:

\displaystyle\|{\bm{W}}_{r}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}-{\bm{X}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{D}\sum_{n={r+1}}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\left({{\bm{\phi}}_{n}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{\ell}\right)^{2}

(110)

\displaystyle=DL^{D-1}\sum_{n=R+1}^{M}\frac{2}{L}\left(\sum_{x=-M}^{M}x\sin(2\pi nx/L)\right)^{2}

(111)

where we define
R
such that
2\pi R/L
is the maximal wavenumber magnitude encoded in the rank
r
embeddings. Our spectral gap condition guarantees that
R
is well-defined. We will later find a bound for
R
in terms of
r
.

We now evaluate the trigonometric sum:

\displaystyle\sum_{x=-M}^{M}x\sin(2\pi n x/L)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{x=1}^{M}x\sin(2\pi n x/L)

(112)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sin(\pi n (L+1)/L)}{\sin^{2}(\pi n/L)}-\frac{(L+1)\cos(\pi n)}{\sin(\pi n/L)}\right)

(113)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n+1}\frac{L}{2\sin(\pi n / L)}

(114)

where the sum formula can be found in
(
11
)
Eq. 1.352.1 and the final step follows from a few lines of algebra. Thus the numerator simplifies further:

\|{\bm{W}}_{r}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}-{\bm{X}}\|_{F}^{2}=DL^{D-1}\sum_{n=R+1}^{M}\frac{L}{2}\csc^{2}(\pi n/L).

(115)

It is now useful to compute the denominator of the error. Using a very similar decoupling argument, we find

\displaystyle\|{\bm{X}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{D}L^{D-1}\sum_{x=-M}^{M}x^{2}

(116)

\displaystyle=DL^{D-1}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{M(M+1)(2M+1)}{6}

(117)

\displaystyle=DL^{D-1}\frac{L(L^{2}-1)}{12}

(118)

using a well-known formula for the sum of a sequence of squares. Combining with the numerator, we can now obtain an expression for the relative error:

\min_{\bm{\Omega}}\varepsilon^{2}({\bm{\Omega}};r)=\frac{6}{L^{2}-1}\sum_{n=R+1}^{M}\csc^{2}(\pi n/L).

(119)

Since
\csc
is positive and decreasing on this interval, we may bound the sum as

\displaystyle\sum_{n=R+1}^{M}\csc^{2}(\pi n/L)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{R}^{M}\differential t\csc^{2}(\pi t/L)

(120)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L}{\pi}\cot\frac{\pi R}{L}-\cot\frac{\pi M}{L}

(121)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L}{\pi}\cot\frac{\pi R}{L}

(122)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L^{2}}{\pi^{2}R}

(123)

where the fourth step follows from
\cot(x)\leq 1/x
on this interval. This gives an upper bound in terms of
R
:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\Omega}}\varepsilon^{2}({\bm{\Omega}};r)\leq\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\frac{L^{2}}{L^{2}-1}\frac{1}{R}.

(125)

Now we must relate
1/R
to the embedding rank
r
. Since the wavenumbers are monotonic decreasing with the eigenvalues, the top
r
embedding directions contain the Fourier modes with the
r
shortest wavevectors. Therefore, in the so-called
reciprocal lattice
, we identify
r
as the number of integer lattice sites enclosed by a
D
-sphere of radius
R
.
Using the standard volume argument for counting integer points, we recognize that

\displaystyle r\leq\mathrm{Vol}_{D}\cdot\left(R+\frac{\sqrt{D}}{2}\right)^{D}

(126)

where
\mathrm{Vol}_{D}
is the volume of the unit
D
-sphere.
Rearranging and substituting, we obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\leq\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\frac{L^{2}}{L^{2}-1}\left(\left(\frac{r}{\mathrm{Vol}_{D}}\right)^{1/D}-\frac{\sqrt{D}}{2}\right)^{-1}.

(127)

Note that when the lattice dimension
D=1
we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\leq\frac{12}{\pi^{2}}\frac{L^{2}}{L^{2}-1}\left(\frac{1}{r-1}\right)\sim\frac{1}{r}

(128)

and when
D=2
we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\leq\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\frac{L^{2}}{L^{2}-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{r}{\pi}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{-1}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}.

(129)

\blacksquare
