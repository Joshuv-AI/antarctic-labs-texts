Rotator Cuff Reconstruction Using Biceps Tendon Autograft Versus Acellular Dermal Allograft: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Clinical Outcomes
Authors: Ahmed H. Alhussain, Mohammed T. Alzahrani, Ziad A. Aljaafri, Abullah M. Alanazi, Abdulrahman H. Almalki, Khalid A. Alsheikh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Massive rotator cuff tears impose a challenge in shoulder surgery because of high failure rates and poor tendon healing. Augmentation using biological grafts emerged as a solution to enhance surgical outcomes; they provide structural reinforcement, while biceps tendon autografts offer native biological integration. The comparative effectiveness of these 2 techniques remains unclear.Hypothesis/Purpose:We hypothesized that both techniques would provide comparable clinical outcomes, but differences may exist in graft integrity and complication rates. The purpose was to evaluate clinical outcomes, functional improvement, and graft healing rates after rotator cuff repair using human dermal (HD) allograft versus biceps tendon autograft augmentation.Study Design:Meta-analysis and systematic review; Level of evidence, 4.Methods:A meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines. Studies were identified through a comprehensive search of the PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane Library databases. Studies comparing HD allografts and biceps tendon autografts for rotator cuff repair were included. Outcome measures included the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, visual analog scale (VAS), range of motion (ROM), acromiohumeral distance (AHD), and retear rates. A random-effects model was used to analyze pooled data, and heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic.Results:Seventeen observational studies involving 613 patients were included, comparing long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) autograft (n = 322) and acellular HD allograft (n = 291). Both techniques demonstrated substantial improvements across functional, pain, and radiological outcomes. For functional recovery, the pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) in ASES scores was higher in the LHBT autograft group (SMD, 2.35 [1.63 to 3.07]) compared with the HD allograft group (SMD, 1.65 [1.40 to 1.90]). However, subgroup differences were not statistically significant (P = .07). Pain outcomes showed marked improvement in both groups, with LHBT autograft demonstrating a greater reduction (VAS SMD, −2.25 [−2.89 to −1.61]) compared with HD allograft (SMD, −1.89 [−2.22 to −1.56]; P = .32). ROM also improved, with forward elevation SMDs of 0.92 (LHBT) and 0.65 (HD), and external rotation SMDs of 1.08 and 0.22, respectively. AHD increased in both groups (LHBT SMD, 1.38; HD SMD, 1.13), with no significant intergroup differences observed.Conclusion:Both LHBT autograft and acellular HD allograft augmentation for massive rotator cuff repair showed improvements in function, pain, ROM, and AHD, with comparable retear rates. Despite heterogeneity among studies, HD allograft procedures appear to yield predictable clinical outcomes, although comparisons with the unaugmented rotator cuff repair literature remain necessary.

Rotator cuff tear (RCT) is a common shoulder disorder, especially among older adults and those who perform repetitive overhead activities. Massive RCTs pose significant challenges for surgeons due to their high retear rates and limited potential for tendon healing. Although conventional repair methods show variable success, biological augmentation has gained attention for its potential to promote tendon healing and improve postoperative function. Human dermal (HD) allografts and biceps tendon autografts emerged as augmentation options aiming at strengthening repairs and minimizing retear rates. 2

The primary objective of the rotator cuff repair is to restore shoulder mobility, relieve pain, and prevent progressive glenohumeral joint degeneration. However, there are massive tears that are often present with compromised tendon quality and limited healing capacity. It is influenced by factors such as fatty degeneration, tendon retraction, and inadequate vascularity. To address these challenges, biological graft augmentation techniques are employed to enhance mechanical strength and promote tissue regeneration. HD allografts are derived from cadaveric tissue and are widely used because of their favorable biocompatibility and structural properties reported in previous studies. In contrast, biceps tendon autografts use the patient's own tissue, which may reduce the risk of immune-related complications and provide a biologically active scaffold to support tendon healing. 1 These characteristics have been described in the literature and are presented here to highlight the biological rationale for the use of graft augmentation techniques, although these specific biological properties were not directly evaluated in the present study.

HD allografts are used for augmentation because they provide a supportive scaffold for tendon regeneration. These grafts are decellularized to retain the extracellular matrix, which facilitates fibroblast activity and collagen synthesis. Evidence suggests that dermal allografts enhance repair strength and tendon integrity, thereby lowering the risk of retear. Nonetheless, the uncertainties about graft integration, immune reactions, and long-term outcomes persist. 20

A biceps tendon autograft uses the patient's own tissue to strengthen a rotator cuff repair. The long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT), commonly involved in shoulder disorders and often released during repair, serves as a convenient and structurally suitable graft source. This autologous option integrates effectively with adjacent tissues, and evidence suggests it may promote improved healing by preserving native vascularity and cellular components, thereby supporting enhanced tendon remodeling and tendon-to-bone integration. 8

Despite the increasing use of both methods, there remains uncertainty about the relative effectiveness in optimizing patient outcomes. Although HD allografts and biceps tendon autografts show potential in promoting tendon healing, variations in biomechanical strength, biological incorporation, and clinical success warrant deeper evaluation.5,22,24 Moreover, outcomes are influenced by factors such as patient characteristics, tear severity, surgical approach, and postoperative rehabilitation, all of which should be considered when comparing the efficacy of these 2 techniques. 36

This study aimed to compare and assess the outcomes and differences in functional improvement, pain relief, retear rates, and overall patient satisfaction between the acellular HD allograft and LHBT autograft after repair of massive RCTs.

Methods

This study is a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing outcomes after the repair of massive RCTs augmented with HD allograft versus LHBT autograft. A comprehensive literature review was conducted to identify relevant studies that met the inclusion criteria. The study followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines to ensure a transparent and replicable methodology.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

A systematic search of electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane Library, was conducted. Keywords and MeSH terms, including rotator cuff tear, patch augmentation, HD allograft, biceps tendon autograft, and surgical outcomes, were used to identify relevant literature. The reference lists of included studies were also reviewed to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Study Selection Process

Two independent reviewers (Z.A.A., A.M.A.) screened titles and abstracts for relevance. Full-text articles were retrieved for studies meeting the initial eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (A.H.Alhussain). Figure 1 shows the PRISMA flow diagram for the study selection process.

The risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies, evaluating selection bias, comparability, and outcome assessment.

Statistical Analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using the Review Manager (RevMan) software. Continuous outcomes (ASES scores, VAS pain scores, and ROM) were reported as mean differences with 95% CIs. The chi-square test and the I2 statistic were used to assess heterogeneity among studies. A random-effects model was applied when heterogeneity was significant (I2 >50%), and a fixed-effects model was used otherwise. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of findings.

Results

Study Characteristics and Methodologies

Our review included 17 observational studies comparing the outcomes of massive RCT repair using acellular HD allograft versus LHBT autograft (Table 1). A total of 613 patients were included, of whom 322 underwent LHBT autograft, and 291 received acellular HD allograft. Studies consisted of a mixed design, which included retrospective, prospective, and comparative designs. The sample size ranged from 9 patients in Hirahara et al 15 to 59 patients in Denard et al. 10 The follow-up durations showed a considerable heterogeneity, with the longest extending to 45.56 ± 2.72 months in the study by Kim et al. 21 Most of the studies followed patients for at least 24 months, which ensured the adequate assessment of tendon healing and functional recovery.

Demographic and Baseline Characteristics

Rhee et al 31 reported the use of arthroscopic interposition reconstruction with either LHBT autograft or HD allograft in patients with mean ages of 66.9 and 63.3 years, respectively. Lädermann et al 26 similarly compared LHBT autograft and HD allograft SCR techniques in cohorts with comparable mean ages of approximately 59 years. Several studies utilized HD allograft SCR techniques, including Pennington et al, 30 Denard et al, 10 Hirahara et al, 15 Dykhouse et al, 12 Lacheta et al, 25 and Pashuck et al, 29 with reported mean patient ages ranging from 56 to 67 years. LHBT autograft techniques varied and included SCR-type reconstructions, augmentation procedures, and interposition grafting as described by Kim and Han, 19 Ji et al, 17 Kim and Han, 19 Cho et al, 7 Jeon et al, 16 Kocaoglu et al, 23 Park et al, 28 and Rhee et al. 32 Details included in Table 2.

Clinical Outcomes

Table 3 presents the pre- and postoperative clinical outcomes, measured primarily using the ASES and VAS. Both graft types demonstrated significant postoperative improvements in functional and pain outcomes. Among the LHBT autograft studies, the lowest preoperative ASES score was 35.5 ± 19.4 in the study by Ji et al. 17 The highest postoperative ASES score was 91.4 ± 6.3 in the study by Kim et al, 22 signifying marked recovery in shoulder function. Figure 2 shows that in the LHBT autograft group, the standardized mean difference (SMD) for the ASES was 2.35 [1.63-3.07], while in the acellular HD allograft group, the SMD was 1.65 [1.40-1.90], demonstrating substantial improvement. The test for subgroup differences showed no statistically significant difference between the 2 graft types (P = .07).

ROM Outcomes

Both groups achieved substantial postoperative improvements in ROM across most parameters (Table 4). In forward elevation (FE), the LHBT autograft group improved from a pooled mean of 126.8° preoperatively to 157.4° postoperatively, while the HD allograft group increased from 118.4° to 152°, demonstrating a mean gain of approximately 30° to 34°. Several studies, including those of Rhee et al 10 and Denard et al, 13 reported statistically significant improvements (P < .05). Figure 4 shows that the pooled analysis of FE revealed postoperative improvement in both groups. For the LHBT autograft, the pooled SMD was 0.92 [0.34-1.49]. Similarly, the acellular HD allograft group demonstrated a pooled SMD of 0.65 [0.42-0.87]. The subgroup differences were not statistically significant (P = .39).

AHD and Retear Rate

AHD and retear rate varied between the LHBT autograft and acellular HD allograft groups (Table 5). Improvements in the AHD were observed with both techniques. Quality Assessment of Included Studies

Most of the studies demonstrated a low risk of bias across all domains. Discussion

Massive RCTs are tears involving at least 2 tendons, or those measuring >5 cm, thus affecting normal shoulder biomechanics, which leads to subsequent upward subluxation of the humeral head and tear propagation.9,14 These tears continue to impose a significant challenge because of their high degree of muscle atrophy, fatty infiltration, high retear rates, tenuous blood supply, and poor healing potential. 34

An attempt at repair is the mainstay of treatment. In cases of severe fatty infiltration, massive tendon retraction, or insufficient tendon length, repair might not be feasible, and other options must be considered. 37 Those options include partial repair with or without medial foot-print medialization, interposition graft, and SCR have shown great potential in terms of pain and functional restoration. 11 Moreover, tendon transfer and SCR have both been proven useful in younger patients with intact or repairable subscapularis tendons and functional deltoids. 6 However, they are contraindicated where there is evidence of moderate rotator cuff arthropathy, bone defect, or stiffness; in such situations, reverse shoulder arthroplasty is an appropriate option.3,13

Graft augmentation is a highly effective adjunct to RCT repairs. A graft can be used to augment a repairable tear or act as an interposition graft, connecting the 2 ends of an irreparable tear. 33 LHBT, as well as HD allografts, are among the most widely used. 27

Our studies showed that both the LHBT autograft and the HD allograft are excellent augmentation options for massive irreparable RCTs. Both techniques showed a significant functional improvement, and increased patient satisfaction as reflected by ASES scores, with allografts demonstrating slightly greater mean improvements compared with autografts, although the difference was not statistically significant, as similarly reported by Kim et al. 18

Both groups experienced substantial postoperative pain relief, with VAS scores decreasing by a comparable magnitude. Interestingly, some studies reported slightly lower postoperative pain levels in the allograft group, possibly due to the biological properties of the dermal scaffold, including its ability to reduce inflammation, and no donor-site morbidity. 15 However, this advantage was not consistent across all studies, suggesting that differences in surgical technique and graft construct may influence outcomes.

ROM is a vital parameter in assessing the success of rotator cuff repair. Forward elevation improved significantly in both groups, with autografts demonstrating slightly superior outcomes. In a previous study, autografts showed a significantly greater mean gain in forward elevation (48.7) compared with allografts. 18 The external rotation showed comparable improvement in both groups. Moreover, allografts provided more consistent outcomes across studies, which indicated that they may be a more predictable option for patients with severe tendon degeneration or poor tissue quality. Moreover, previous literature also showed that allografts improved internal and external rotation. 18 The decision to use either graft should, therefore, be based on patient-specific needs rather than a generalized assumption of superiority.

AHD is an indicator of superior humeral head migration and joint stability. Both augmentation techniques led to significant improvements in AHD, reflecting adequate stabilization of the shoulder joint and a reduced risk of postoperative dysfunction. The preservation of AHD is crucial for preventing secondary degenerative changes.

Key factors influencing patients’ recovery and long-term functional success include tendon healing and retear rates. Our analysis revealed that the allograft had significantly lower retear rates compared with the autograft. This suggests that an allograft may have consistent biological and biomechanical properties. The higher retear rates observed in the autograft group may be related to variability in graft preparation techniques, structural properties of the graft construct, and differences in surgical techniques across studies, which may influence graft incorporation and overall repair stability. These findings align with a previous study by Villatte et al, 35 which showed that there was no significant difference between the 2 types of graft, with a retear rate of 27% (15.4-40.2) and 20.9% (9.9-34.2) with autograft and allograft, respectively.

Despite the positive outcomes observed with both techniques, variability in study methodologies and surgical techniques presents a challenge in drawing definitive conclusions. Differences in patient characteristics, rehabilitation protocols, and surgeon experience may have influenced the reported outcomes. Future research should focus on randomized controlled trials with standardized surgical approaches and long-term follow-up to provide more definitive evidence on the superiority of one technique over the other. Additionally, advancements in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine may lead to the development of enhanced graft materials that further improve tendon healing and functional recovery.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity exists among the included studies in terms of surgical techniques, graft types, and patient characteristics. Both HD allograft and LHBT autograft procedures encompassed different operative strategies, including superior capsular reconstruction, graft augmentation, and gap-spanning interposition techniques, which may have distinct biomechanical and clinical implications. Second, several potentially important prognostic factors could not be evaluated because they were either inconsistently reported or unavailable in the included studies. These include graft thickness, biologic augmentation, fatty infiltration of the rotator cuff muscles, tendon retraction, subscapularis integrity, and whether the procedure was performed as a primary or revision surgery. Similarly, details regarding concomitant procedures, postoperative rehabilitation protocols, and biceps management techniques were variably reported, limiting subgroup analysis.

Third, definitions of massive RCTs were not uniform across studies, with some defining massive tears based on tear size (≥3 or ≥5 cm) and others based on involvement of ≥2 tendons. Fourth, the methods used to diagnose graft failure or retear varied, with most studies using MRI while others reported ultrasound or clinical evaluation, which may introduce detection bias given the limitations of ultrasound in evaluating subacromial structures and graft-native tendon interfaces. Finally, most included studies were retrospective and involved relatively small sample sizes, which limits the overall level of evidence and increases the risk of selection bias. Collectively, these factors contribute to methodological heterogeneity and should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review.

Conclusion

This study shows that both acellular HD allograft and LHBT autograft techniques demonstrated postoperative improvements in shoulder function, pain reduction, and ROM after repair of massive RCTs. Overall clinical and radiologic outcomes were comparable between the 2 approaches. Although HD allografts showed a trend toward lower retear rates and complications, the heterogeneity among studies limits definitive superiority. Nevertheless, HD allograft augmentation may appear to provide a predictable and reliable option for tendon reconstruction in complex rotator cuff repairs.
