Intraocular lens exchange for vision-dependent vestibular disorder after cataract surgery: A case report
Authors: Kaori Morii, Azusa Akashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDissatisfaction after cataract surgery is often attributed to psychosomatic factors when no ophthalmologic abnormalities are detected, and visually induced vestibular dysfunction is rarely considered. We report a rare case in which such dysfunction was identified and successfully treated by restoring the preoperative refractive state through intraocular lens (IOL) exchange.ObservationsA 79-year-old woman who had undergone bilateral cataract surgery at another hospital presented with persistent blurred vision, dizziness, and a floating sensation despite good postoperative acuity. She had mild preoperative myopia (spherical equivalent approximately −2.0 D) and no history of spectacle use. Stabilometry revealed abnormal postural sway under rubber-load conditions, indicating strong visual dependence. To simulate her preoperative state, +3.5 D contact lenses were trialed bilaterally, resulting in marked symptom relief. Five months after the original surgery, bilateral IOL exchange was performed (+23.0 D NX70S), targeting her habitual refractive state. Postoperative corrected distance visual acuity was 1.2 in the right eye (sph −2.0; cyl −1.0 × 96°) and 1.2 in the left eye (sph −1.75; cyl −0.75 × 87°) (all visual acuity values reported in decimal), with marked resolution of subjective symptoms. Follow-up stabilometry showed improvement: locus length per unit area (eyes open), 44.73; Romberg ratio for area, 0.61; and velocity Romberg ratio under rubber-load, 1.65. No symptom recurrence was observed at 26 months.Conclusions and importanceIn visually dependent patients, substantial changes in habitual refractive state may exacerbate vestibular symptoms. In such cases, IOL exchange may alleviate visually induced vestibular dysfunction by restoring the preoperative refractive error.Keywords: Intraocular lens exchange, Visually induced vestibular disorder, Visual dependence, Stabilometry, Refractive planning

Introduction

Cataract surgery is one of the most commonly performed ophthalmic procedure worldwide. Advances in surgical techniques and intraocular lens (IOL) technology have led to excellent visual outcomes in most patients.1 Nevertheless, a subset of patients experiences postoperative complications, including posterior capsule opacification, residual refractive error, dysphotopsia, and ocular surface disease, which may impair visual satisfaction.2, 3, 4 Beyond these identifiable complications, it is common in clinical practice to encounter patients who express dissatisfaction with their vision or report uncomfortable symptoms despite the absence of detectable ophthalmological abnormalities. In such cases, complaints are sometimes attributed to psychosomatic factors and managed as non-specific subjective symptoms.5 This case report illustrates an unusual situation in which postoperative visual dissatisfaction was not linked to ocular pathology, but instead to vestibular dysfunction exacerbated by a change in the refractive state.6 In this case, although the patient's symptoms were initially regarded as unsatisfactory outcomes after cataract surgery without ophthalmologic abnormalities, stabilometry revealed visually induced vestibular dysfunction. Visual dependence is a recognized risk factor for balance disorders,6,7 yet its manifestation after cataract surgery has rarely been documented. In clinical practice, persistent dizziness or visual discomfort after an otherwise successful surgery is often misattributed to psychosomatic causes.8 This report is novel in demonstrating that restoration of the preoperative myopic refractive state through IOL exchange alleviated vestibular dysfunction and improved symptoms. To our knowledge, only a few case reports have directly linked postoperative refractive changes to destabilization of vestibular compensation. These findings underscore the importance of considering visual-vestibular interactions in patients with unexplained postoperative dissatisfaction.

Case presentation (Revised with Patient-Centered Narrative)

Patient information and history of present illness

A 79-year-old woman with mild preoperative myopia (approximately −2.0 D) presented for evaluation. Preoperatively, her corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA, decimal) was 0.4 in the right eye (RE) (sph −0.5; cyl −3.0 × 90°) and 0.3 in the left eye (LE) (sph −1.0; cyl −1.5 × 70°), and she had no history of spectacle use. She underwent bilateral cataract surgery in another hospital five months prior to presentation. Monofocal IOLs (ZCB00V, Johnson & Johnson) were implanted targeting distance vision: +18.5 D in the RE (target refraction −0.24 D) and +18.0 D in the LE (target refraction −0.16 D). Prior to the initial cataract surgery at the previous hospital, she had been verbally informed that targeting distance vision with monofocal IOLs would necessitate the use of reading glasses for near tasks. She provided informed consent based on this explanation. Postoperatively, her visual acuity was objectively good, with uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) of 1.2 in both eyes. However, despite her prior agreement, she persistently complained of blurred vision and a pronounced floating sensation. She reported that these symptoms interfered with her ability to walk confidently outdoors, caused dizziness when reading or using stairs, and generated considerable anxiety because she expected cataract surgery to “restore clear vision without discomfort.” Since the symptoms were regarded as non-specific, she was referred to Asagiri Hospital for further evaluation.

Initial ophthalmic evaluation

In Asagiri Hospital, her UDVA was 0.6 in the RE and 0.9 in the LE. CDVA was 1.2 in both eyes, with refraction of +0.5 sph and −1.0 cyl × 100° in the RE and +0.5 sph and −0.5 cyl × 95° in the LE.

Slit-lamp, fundus, and ocular motility examinations were unremarkable, and no nystagmus was observed. Nevertheless, she emphasized that her floating sensations were “constant and exhausting,” making her reluctant to go out alone. Given the absence of an ocular pathology, she was referred to the Department of Otolaryngology at Kobe University.

Otolaryngology findings

Stabilometry revealed abnormal postural sway patterns, particularly under rubber-load conditions, consistent with strong visual dependence contributing to vestibular dysfunction9, 10, 11, 12 (Fig. 1). A neural network analysis supported the presence of labyrinthine disorders. Additional vestibular tests showed reduced vestibular-evoked myogenic potentials (VEMP) on the left side and decreased function of the left anterior semicircular canal on video head impulse testing (vHIT).5,13 To rule out other causes of dizziness, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and neurological examinations were performed, and were unremarkable. Psychogenic dizziness was considered unlikely, as stabilometry demonstrated objective patterns of vestibular dysfunction rather than erratic functional patterns, and the symptoms were consistently relieved by refractive correction, as described below. Collectively, these findings led to the diagnosis of left-sided vestibular dysfunction with strong visual dependence (Fig. 2). Diagnostic reasoning and refractive trial

It was hypothesized that the abrupt shift from long-standing myopia to near emmetropia disrupted visual-vestibular compensation. When offered spectacles, the patient explained that she had “never been able to get used to spectacles” and found them intolerable.

As a diagnostic trial, daily disposable contact lenses (+3.5 D OU, approximating −2.0 D bilaterally) were prescribed. The patient reported functional stability without spectacles before surgery and had been adapted to a spherical equivalent of approximately −2.0 D. Therefore, a +3.5 D contact lens trial was performed to replicate this habitual refractive state. The preoperative against-the-rule astigmatism was primarily lenticular; thus, residual corneal astigmatism after cataract extraction was lower, making full astigmatic simulation unnecessary. Although the RE was dominant, the patient experienced the greatest relief with bilateral correction during the trial.

Within 1 week, she reported striking improvement, stating that “for the first time since surgery, walking feels stable and I can read without dizziness.” This experience reassured her that her symptoms were not psychosomatic but were related to refractive change.

Treatment options, including continued contact lens use, LASIK, and add-on IOL implantation, were discussed. She declined contact lenses due to handling concerns related to her age. Regarding LASIK, she expressed concern about potential reduction in contrast sensitivity from increased spherical aberrations and the unpredictability of hyperopic correction. She also worried about the risk of chronic iridocyclitis with add-on IOLs. Ultimately, she requested a definitive IOL exchange.14, 15, 16, 17.

IOL exchange surgery

Five months after the initial cataract surgery, a bilateral IOL exchange was performed via a temporal corneal incision. The ZCB00V lenses were removed uneventfully, and NX70S lenses (Santen Pharmaceutical) were implanted in the capsular bag: +23.0 D in both eyes (target refraction [approximately spherical equivalent]: −2.61 D in the RE and −2.05 D in the LE).

Postoperative course and follow-up

Ten months after the IOL exchange, repeat stabilometry demonstrated substantial improvement compared with preoperative findings. Neural network analysis indicated normalization, and the indices of visual dependence were reduced to the normal range. Clinically, the patient remained free of recurrent symptoms, reported stable vision, and showed improved confidence in performing daily activities. Discussion

This case highlights that postoperative dissatisfaction after cataract surgery, often dismissed as non-specific or psychosomatic, may reflect underlying vestibular dysfunction with strong visual dependence. Despite good visual acuity, persistent dizziness and visual discomfort present diagnostic challenges in ophthalmology. This case demonstrates that stabilometry can reveal vestibular dysfunction with marked visual dependence, providing an objective explanation for symptoms that may otherwise be attributed to subjective or psychiatric causes.

Clinical reasoning and differential diagnosis

Following the principle that visually induced vestibular dysfunction is a diagnosis of exclusion, we conducted a rigorous diagnostic workup to rule out other potential etiologies. The patient's blurred vision and floating sensations persisted despite uneventful surgery; however, detailed ophthalmic examinations excluded common postoperative complications such as posterior capsule opacification, cystoid macular edema, and other surgical sequelae.18 Furthermore, unremarkable findings on brain MRI and neurological assessments helped rule out central nervous system pathology. Although psychogenic dizziness was considered, stabilometry revealed patterns inconsistent with functional dizziness.5,13 Ultimately, specialized vestibular testing (VEMP and vHIT) localized the deficit without evidence of acute otological disease. This systematic approach indicated that the symptoms were specifically triggered by the postoperative refractive shift in a patient with strong visual dependence.17

Comparison of treatment options

In addressing residual refractive error, options such as LASIK, PRK, or add-on IOLs are often considered less invasive than IOL exchange. However, in this case, IOL exchange was prioritized to restore the patient's habitual intraocular optics as closely as possible to her preoperative state. LASIK for hyperopic correction can increase corneal spherical aberrations, potentially reducing contrast sensitivity, which could further destabilize a visually dependent patient.14,18 Furthermore, although add-on IOLs can be effective, they carry long-term risks such as chronic iridocyclitis or pigment dispersion.15 By performing a definitive IOL exchange,16 we achieved a stable, high-quality myopic refractive state, which was crucial for reintegration of visual and vestibular inputs.

Relationship to existing literature

Humans can maintain a stable posture and walk smoothly without consciously regulating their body balance, owing to the integrated function of the vestibular system, which operates involuntarily to preserve body stability. This equilibrium function is maintained through the coordinated interaction of four major components of the central nervous system: the visual system; vestibular apparatus, including the semicircular canals; somatosensory system, comprising deep receptors in muscles and tendons; and higher neural centers such as the brainstem, cerebellum, and cerebrum (Fig. 4). Impairment in any of these components can lead to balance disorders, manifesting as dizziness, postural instability, or unsteadiness. Among these, visual input plays a critical role in the regulation of postural control. Visual dependence is a recognized contributor to balance disorders and dizziness.6,7 In otolaryngology, persistent postural-perceptual dizziness is a condition in which visual dependence persists even after the removal of the precipitating cause.8 However, the ophthalmic literature rarely addresses visual dependence as a factor in postoperative dissatisfaction after cataract surgery. Although refractive error and residual astigmatism are commonly implicated,2, 3, 4 few reports have linked postoperative refractive changes to destabilization of vestibular compensation. To our knowledge, no prior case reports have demonstrated that IOL exchange, performed to restore the preoperative refractive state, can alleviate vestibular dysfunction symptoms. Therefore, this case provides novel evidence that postoperative refractive planning should consider not only the visual acuity but also the patient's habitual visual environment and potential vestibular dependence.

Clinical implications

This case underscores the importance of considering stabilometry or other vestibular assessments in patients without detectable ocular abnormalities who report disabling dizziness or visual discomfort after cataract surgery. Preoperative postural sway testing, including the rubber-load test, may help identify patients with high visual dependence and prevent postoperative exacerbation of vestibular symptoms.6, 7, 8,17,18 Moreover, this case illustrates that IOL exchange, although typically performed for refractive surprise or dysphotopsia, may have therapeutic value in selected patients with vestibular dysfunction.

Limitations

This case highlights that postoperative dissatisfaction after cataract surgery, often dismissed as non-specific or psychosomatic, may reflect underlying vestibular dysfunction with strong visual dependence. Even when postoperative visual acuity is excellent, persistent dizziness poses significant diagnostic challenges. Our findings demonstrate that stabilometry can reveal objective patterns of vestibular dysfunction and marked visual dependence, providing a physical explanation for symptoms that might otherwise be attributed to subjective or psychiatric causes.

However, this report describes a single patient; therefore, a causal relationship between refractive changes and vestibular symptoms cannot be definitively established. The generalizability of the stabilometry findings is limited, and long-term outcomes beyond 26 months require further follow-up. Despite these limitations, this case suggests that, for a specific subset of visually dependent patients, IOL exchange to restore the habitual refractive state may be a transformative therapeutic option. Larger studies are needed to confirm the broader effectiveness of this approach.

Conclusion

A patient initially thought to have non-specific complaints after cataract surgery was found to have vestibular dysfunction with strong visual dependence on stabilometry. Restoring the preoperative myopic refraction with IOL exchange alleviated her symptoms and improved balance. This case emphasizes that in visually dependent patients, altering the habitual visual environment through cataract surgery may exacerbate vestibular dysfunction, and careful attention to the preoperative refractive status is warranted.
