Abstract
. We settle a conjecture proposed by B. Maji and T. Sarkar regarding the location of zeros of a two-parameter family of reciprocal polynomials, R k , ℓ ​ ( z ) R_{k,\ell}(z) for positive integers k k and ℓ \ell . These polynomials are generalizations of Ramanujan polynomials studied by M. R. Murty, C. Smyth, and R. Wang. More specifically, we show that except for two real zeros, all other zeros of R k , ℓ ​ ( z ) R_{k,\ell}(z) lie on the unit circle.

1.
Introduction

The study of polynomials and their zeros is an important theme in mathematics, with relevance to a wide variety of areas. We call a polynomial
P(x)
of degree
d
to be
reciprocal
if
x^{d}\,P(1/x)=\pm P(x)
. The main object of investigation in this paper is a two-parameter family of reciprocal polynomials related to values of the Riemann zeta-function at even positive integer arguments that arises as a generalization of Ramanujan polynomials introduced by M. R. Murty, C.
Smyth, and R. Wang in
[
9
]
.

Recall that the Riemann zeta function is defined by

\zeta(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{s}}\qquad\text{for}\qquad\text{Re}(s)>1,

and for
n\geq 0
, the Bernoulli numbers are given by

\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{B_{n}}{n!}\,t^{n}\qquad|t|<2\pi.

From this, we have that
B_{0}=1
,
B_{1}=-1/2
,
B_{2k+1}=0
for
k\geq 1
and
B_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}
. In particular, we have Euler’s formula, namely,

(1)

\zeta(2k)=-\,\frac{{(2\pi i)}^{2k}\,B_{2k}}{2\,(2k)!}\qquad k\geq 1.

Thus, since
\pi
is transcendental, it is evident that the even zeta-values are transcendental. The transcendental nature of odd zeta-values however, remains unknown.

An attempt towards this study is Ramanujan’s formula for odd zeta-values (see
[
10
]
), that is, for positive real numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
with
\alpha\beta=\pi^{2}
and any positive integer
k
,

\displaystyle\alpha^{-k}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\,\zeta(2k+1)\,+\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-2k-1}}{e^{2\alpha n}-1}\right\}\,

\displaystyle-\,{\left(-\beta\right)}^{-k}\,\left\{\frac{1}{2}\,\zeta(2k+1)\,+\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-2k-1}}{e^{2\beta n}-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip-99.58464pt=\,-\,2^{2k}\,\alpha^{k+1}\,\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}{(-1)}^{j}\,\frac{B_{2j}\,B_{2k+2-2j}}{(2j)!\,(2k+2-2j)!}\,{\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)}^{j}.

A more general version of this formula was independently derived by E. Grosswald
[
5
]
in 1970. Let
\mathbb{H}
denote the upper half plane, that is the set of complex numbers with strictly positive imaginary part. For any positive integer
k
, set

(2)

R_{2k+1}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\frac{B_{2j}\,B_{2k+2-2j}}{(2j)!\,(2k+2-2j)!}\,x^{2j}

Then we have the following.

Theorem

(Grosswald)
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer and
\sigma_{k}(n):=\sum_{d\mid n}d^{k}
. For
z\in\mathbb{H}
, define

\mathcal{F}_{k}(z):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{k}(n)}{n^{k}}\,e^{2\pi inz}.

Then

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2k+1}(z)-z^{2k}\,\mathcal{F}_{2k+1}\left(-\frac{1}{z}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,\zeta(2k+1)\,\left(z^{2k}-1\right)+\frac{(2\pi i)^{2k+1}}{2\,z}R_{2k+1}(z).

One can recover Ramanujan’s formula from the above identity by specializing at
z=i\alpha/\pi
,
-1/z=i\beta/\pi
and noting that

\mathcal{F}_{k}(z)=-\zeta(k)-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-k}}{e^{-2\pi inz}-1}.

The function
\mathcal{F}_{2k+1}(z)
is an Eichler integral of the second kind of the standard Eisenstein series of weight
2k+2
for
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and has been well-studied in the literature. From (
3
), it is clear that if
z_{0}
is a zero of
R_{2k+1}(x)
, which is not a
2k
-th root of unity, then we obtain an expression for the odd zeta value
\zeta(2k+1)
in terms of special values of the Eichler integral
\mathcal{F}_{2k+1}(z_{0})
.

With this motivation, in 2011, M. R. Murty, C. Smyth, and R. Wang isolated the polynomials
R_{2k+1}(x)
for independent study and called them
‘Ramanujan polynomials’
. It is not difficult to see that the Ramanujan polynomials are reciprocal polynomials, that is,
R_{2k+1}(x)=x^{2k+2}R_{2k+1}(1/x)
. In the same paper, they observed that most of the zeros of these polynomials lie on the unit circle.

Theorem

(M. R. Murty, C. Smyth, and R. Wang)
.

For
k\geq 0
, all the non-real zeros of
R_{2k+1}(x)
are simple and lie on the unit circle. For
k\geq 1
,
R_{2k+1}(x)
has exactly four distinct real zeros.
These real zeros of
R_{2k+1}(x)
can be expressed as
\alpha_{k}
,
-\alpha_{k}
,
1/\alpha_{k}
and
-1/\alpha_{k}
for a real number
\alpha_{k}>1
. Moreover,
\alpha_{k}\in(2,2.2)
and
\alpha_{k}\rightarrow 2
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

One may recognize the Ramanujan polynomial
R_{2k+1}(x)
as essentially the odd-part of the period polynomial associated to the Eisenstein series of weight
2k+2
for
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. This suggests that we can study period polynomials associated to modular forms and in particular to Hecke eigenforms. With this context, the above result gave rise to a flurry of activity in determining the location of zeros of period polynomials of Hecke eigenforms, and their odd parts. This theme is now referred to as the
‘Riemann hypothesis for period polynomials’
. The interested reader can refer to the excellent survey
[
3
]
for further details.

Recently, several generalizations of Ramanujan’s formula have appeared in the literature. In particular, A. A. Dixit and R. Gupta
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
obtained an analogous formula for the square of odd zeta values.

Theorem

(A. Dixit, R. Gupta)
.

Let
\tau(r)=\#
divisors of
r
,
\epsilon=e^{i\pi/4}
,
\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}(n):=\sum_{d\mid n}\tau(d)\,\tau(n/d)\,d^{s},
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2k+1}(x):=\zeta^{2}(2k+1)\bigg(\gamma+

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{x}{\pi}\right)-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(2k+1)}{\zeta(2k+1)}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{2k+1}(n)}{n^{2k+1}}\,\bigg(K_{0}(4\epsilon\sqrt{n}x)+K_{0}(4\overline{\epsilon}\sqrt{n}x)\bigg),\qquad x>0,\qquad

where
K_{z}(x):=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{I_{-z}(x)-I_{z}(x)}{\sin\pi z}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order
z
, and
I_{z}
is that of the first kind. Then, for positive real number
\alpha
and
\beta
with
\alpha\beta=\pi^{2}
,

\displaystyle\alpha^{-2k}\,\mathcal{G}_{2k+1}(\alpha)-{(-1)}^{k}

\displaystyle\beta^{-2k}\,\mathcal{G}_{2k+1}(\beta)

\displaystyle=-\pi\,2^{4k}\,\beta^{2k+2}\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}{(-1)}^{j}{\left(\frac{B_{2j}}{(2j)!}\,\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}}{(2k+2-2j)!}\right)}^{2}\,{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)}^{2j}.

This was further generalized to any positive integer power of odd zeta-values by S. Banerjee and V. Sahani
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
in 2023.

Motivated by the analogy of the sums in terms of Bernoulli numbers appearing above with Ramanujan polynomials, and supported by numerical evidence, B. Maji and T. Sarkar in
[
8
]
proposed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1

(Maji-Sarkar)
.

For positive integers
k
and
\ell
, set

R_{k,\ell}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}{\left(-1\right)}^{(\ell+1)j}{\left(\frac{B_{2j}}{(2j)!}\,\frac{B_{2k+2-2j}}{(2k+2-2j)!}\right)}^{\ell}\,x^{j}.

Then all the non-real zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(x)
lie on the unit circle.

Note that
R_{k,1}(x^{2})=R_{2k+1}(x)
, the Ramanujan polynomial. Thus, for
\ell=1
, the conjecture follows from the theorem of M. R. Murty, C. Smyth, and R. Wang
[
9
]
.

It is easy to see that
R_{k,\ell}(x)
is a reciprocal polynomial, that is
x^{k+1}R_{k,\ell}(1/x)=\pm R_{k,\ell}(x)
.
In this paper, we study the zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(x)
and show that the above conjecture is true. Our main theorems can be summarized as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

For positive integers
k
and
\ell
, Conjecture
1.1
is true. More specifically, except for two real zeros of the form

\alpha_{k,\ell}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\frac{1}{\alpha_{k,\ell}}

with
\alpha_{k,\ell}>1
, the remaining zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(x)
lie on the unit circle. Moreover, all the zeros are simple.

When
\ell
is odd, we also determine the location of the largest real zero.

Theorem 1.2
.

For positive integers
k
and
\ell
, with
\ell
- odd and
k\geq 3
, let
\alpha_{k,\ell}
denote the largest real zero of
R_{k,\ell}(x)
. Then

\alpha_{k,\ell}\in\bigg(\,\,\left(\frac{2\,\zeta(2)\,\zeta(2k)}{\zeta(2k+2)}\right)^{\ell},\,\,\big(2\zeta(2)\big)^{\ell}\,\bigg({\frac{2}{\zeta(2)}\left(1+\frac{3\,d_{\ell}}{4^{k}}\right)}\bigg)\,\,\bigg),

where
d_{\ell}=\frac{(1.75)^{\ell}-1}{0.75}.

A few remarks are in order. First of all, it must be noted that when
\ell
is even, we can consider the related family of polynomials
\widetilde{R}_{k,\ell}(x):=R_{k,\ell}(-x)
. Theorem
1.1
immediately implies that all the non-real zeros of these polynomials, namely,

\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}{\left(\frac{B_{2j}\,B_{2k+2-2j}}{(2j)!\,(2k+2-2j)!}\right)}^{\ell}\,x^{j},

also lie on the unit circle, for
k\geq 3
and
\ell\geq 1
. However, for technical reasons that will be evident in the proof, it is more convenient to prove Theorem
1.1
for
R_{k,\ell}(x)
than for
\widetilde{R}_{k,\ell}(x)
. The method of proof of Theorem
1.1
is inspired by that in
[
9
]
. However, the corresponding estimates in our case require to ascertain the dependence on
\ell
, which adds an extra layer of difficulty.

The questions regarding the location of the largest real zero of
R_{k,\ell}(x)
when
\ell
is even, as well as the roots of unity which may be zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(x)
appear to be very subtle and involved in this generality. We relegate these investigations to future research.

2.
Preliminaries

This section is dedicated to deriving the estimates that will be crucial in our proofs.

Instead of the polynomial
R_{k,\ell}(z)
, we work with its monic, even counterpart,

M_{k,\ell}(z)=\frac{{\left(-1\right)}^{(\ell+1)(k+1)}}{{\left(B_{2k+2}/(2k+2)!\right)}^{\ell}}\,R_{k,\ell}(z^{2}).

Using (
1
),
M_{k,\ell}(z)
can be rewritten as

(4)

M_{k,\ell}(z)=z^{2k+2}+{(-1)}^{(\ell+1)(k+1)}-2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{(\ell+1)(k+j)}\,{\left(\frac{\zeta(2j)\,\zeta(2k+2-2j)}{\zeta(2k+2)}\right)}^{\ell}\,z^{2j}.

For simplicity, let

q_{j}:=\frac{\zeta(2j)\,\zeta(2k+2-2j)}{\zeta(2k+2)}{\qquad j=1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,k},

with the dependence on
k
being implicit. Thus,

M_{k,\ell}(z)=z^{2k+2}+(-1)^{(\ell+1)(k+1)}-2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{(\ell+1)(k+j)}\,q_{j}^{\ell}\,z^{2j}.

The zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(z)
are squares of the zeros of
M_{k,\ell}(z)
.

Recall the following preliminary lemmas from
[
9
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have the following inequalities for values of the Riemann zeta-function.

(a)

For
n\geq 2
,

(5)

1+2^{-n}<\zeta(n)<1\,+\,\left(\frac{n+1}{n-1}\right)\,2^{-n}.

(b)

For
k\geq 1
and
j=1,\ldots,k
,

(6)

\frac{\zeta(2k+2-2j)}{\zeta(2k+2)}-1<3\cdot 4^{j-(k+1)}.

(c)

For
k\geq 3
and
2\leq j\leq k-1
,

(7)

\frac{(2j+1)(2k+3-2j)}{(2j-1)(2k+1-2j)}<25/9.

(d)

We have

(8)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta(2j)}{4^{j}}=\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

For proofs of parts (a), (b), and (d), see
[
9
, Lemma 4.4, Lemma 4.5 and Lemma 4.6]
. The proof of part (c) follows along the same lines as that of
[
9
, Lemma 5.2]
, but for
k\geq 3
instead of
k\geq 5
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

For
k\geq 2
and
1\leq j\leq\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor
,
q_{j}=\frac{\zeta(2j)\zeta(2k+2-2j)}{\zeta(2k+2)}
is decreasing.

Proof.

For
k\geq 2
, let

f(x)=\zeta(2x)\zeta(2k+2-2x).

Now

f^{\prime}(x)=2f(x)\Bigg(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(2x)}{\zeta(2x)}-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(2k+2-2x)}{\zeta(2k+2-2x)}\Bigg)>0\;\;\text{for}\;\;x\in\left[\frac{k+1}{2},k\right],

as
\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(x)}{\zeta(x)}
is increasing for
x>1
. This follows from the well-known Dirichlet series expansion

\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(x)}{\zeta(x)}=\,-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{x}},

where
\Lambda(n)
is the von Mangoldt function. Therefore,
f(x)
is increasing in
\left[\frac{k+1}{2},k\right]
. Hence, the result follows using
q_{j}=q_{k+1-j}
and
\zeta(2k+2)>1
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
x\in[0,b]
and
\ell\geq 1
, we have

(1+x)^{\ell}\leq 1\,+\,c_{\ell}(b)\,x\qquad\text{ with }\qquad c_{\ell}(b)=\frac{(1+b)^{\ell}-1}{b}.

Proof.

Consider the function
f(x)=\frac{(1+x)^{\ell}-1}{x}
on
(0,b]
. Using calculus, it is easy to prove that
f(x)
is a strictly increasing function on
(0,b]
. The result follows by taking
c_{\ell}(b)=f(b)
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

For each
k\geq 3
and
\ell\geq 1
, let
2\leq j\leq k-1
and

(9)

\varepsilon_{k,j}=\left(\frac{2j+1}{2j-1}\right)\,4^{-j}+\left(\frac{2k+3-2j}{2k+1-2j}\right)\,4^{-k-1+j}+\left(\frac{(2j+1)(2k+3-2j)}{(2j-1)(2k+1-2j)}\right)\,4^{-k-1}.

Then we have

(1+\varepsilon_{k,j})^{\ell}\leq 1\,+\,c_{\ell}\,\varepsilon_{k,j}\quad\text{ where }\quad c_{\ell}=\frac{(1.306)^{\ell}-1}{0.306}.

Proof.

Treating this as a calculus problem involving a function of
j
, one can prove that

\max_{2\leq j\leq k-1}\varepsilon_{k,j}=\varepsilon_{k,2}.

For
k\geq 3
, we have
\varepsilon_{k,2}\leq 0.306
. Thus, the result follows from Lemma
2.3
by putting
{b=0.306}
.
∎

3.
Real zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(z)

We focus on understanding the properties of the real zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(z)
in this section.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
k
and
\ell
be positive integers with
k
-even. Then
R_{k,\ell}(1)=0
if
\ell
is even and
R_{k,\ell}(-1)=0
if
\ell
is odd.

Proof.

From (
4
), we see that

R_{k,\ell}(z)=z^{k+1}+{(-1)}^{(k+1)(\ell+1)}-2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}{(-1)}^{(\ell+1)(k+j)}\,q_{j}^{\ell}\,z^{j}.

The claim is now evident since
q_{j}=q_{k+1-j}
.
∎

We gain further understanding about the zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(z)
by considering the polynomial
M_{k,\ell}(z)
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
k
and
\ell
be positive integers with
\ell
odd. Then,
M_{k,\ell}(z)
has exactly four real zeros, of the form
\beta_{k,\ell}
,
-\beta_{k,\ell}
,
1/\beta_{k,\ell}
and
-1/\beta_{k,\ell}
with
\beta_{k,\ell}>1
.

Proof.

For odd positive integer
\ell
, we have

M_{k,\ell}(z)=z^{2k+2}+1-2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}q_{j}^{\ell}z^{2j}.

Therefore
M_{k,\ell}(1)=2-2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}q_{j}^{\ell}<0
, as
q_{j}>1
for
j=1,\ldots,k
. Also,
M_{k,\ell}(z)
is positive for
z
real, positive, and sufficiently large. Thus, by the intermediate value theorem, it has a real zero,
\beta_{k,\ell}
say, greater than
1
. Since
M_{k,\ell}(z)
is an even reciprocal polynomial,
-\beta_{k,\ell}
,
1/\beta_{k,\ell}
and
-1/\beta_{k,\ell}
are also zeros of
M_{k,\ell}(z)
, and all of them are distinct. On the other hand, by Descartes’ Rule of Signs, we see that
M_{k,\ell}(z)
can have at most
2
positive zeros. This completes the proof.
∎

In the next proposition, we study the real zeros of
M_{k,\ell}(z)
when
\ell
is even.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
k
and
\ell
be positive integers with
\ell
even.

(a)

If
k
is odd, then
M_{k,\ell}(z)
has at least four real zeros of the form
\beta_{k,\ell}
,
-\beta_{k,\ell}
,
1/\beta_{k,\ell}
and
-1/\beta_{k,\ell}
with
\beta_{k,\ell}>1
.

(b)

If
k
is even, then
M_{k,\ell}(z)
has at least six real zeros of the form
-1
,
1
,
\beta_{k,\ell}
,
-\beta_{k,\ell}
,
1/\beta_{k,\ell}
and
-1/\beta_{k,\ell}
with
\beta_{k,\ell}>1
.

Proof.

For
k
odd and
\ell
even,

M_{k,\ell}(z)=z^{2k+2}+1+2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}q_{j}^{\ell}z^{2j},

which gives

M_{k,\ell}(1)=2+2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}q_{j}^{\ell}.

Let

G_{k,\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}q_{j}^{\ell}.

As
k
is odd and
q_{j}=q_{k+1-j}
, we get

G_{k,\ell}=2\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{k-1}{2}}(-1)^{j}q_{j}^{\ell}\right)+(-1)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}\,q_{\frac{k+1}{2}}^{\ell}.

If
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, we have

G_{k,\ell}=\,-2\left[\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{k-1}{4}}\bigg(q_{2j-1}^{\ell}-q_{2j}^{\ell}\bigg)\right]-q_{\frac{k+1}{2}}^{\ell},

and if
k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, we have

G_{k,\ell}=\,-2\left[\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{k-3}{4}}\bigg(q_{2j-1}^{\ell}-q_{2j}^{\ell}\bigg)\right]-\bigg(q_{\frac{k-1}{2}}^{\ell}-q_{\frac{k+1}{2}}^{\ell}\bigg)-q_{\frac{k-1}{2}}^{\ell}.

In both the above cases, using Lemma
2.2
and the fact that
q_{j}>1
for all
j=1,\ldots,k
, we obtain that
G_{k,\ell}<-1
. Therefore

M_{k,\ell}(1)=2+2^{\ell}G_{k,\ell}<0

as
\ell\geq 2
. Now
M_{k,\ell}(z)
is positive for
z
real, positive, and sufficiently large. Thus, by the intermediate value theorem, it has a real zero,
\beta_{k,\ell}
say, greater than
1
. As
M_{k,\ell}(z)
is an even reciprocal polynomial,
-\beta_{k,\ell}
and
\pm 1/\beta_{k,\ell}
are also distinct real zeros of
M_{k,\ell}(z)
. This proves part (a).

Now assume that
k
is even. In this case, the polynomial under consideration becomes

M_{k,\ell}(z)=z^{2k+2}-1-2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}q_{j}^{\ell}z^{2j},

which implies that

M_{k,\ell}(\pm 1)=0
,
by the symmetry
q_{j}=q_{k+1-j}
. Thus, we need to study the sign of the derivative of
M_{k,\ell}(z)
at
z=1
.

From the above expression, we have

M_{k,\ell}^{\prime}(z)=(2k+2)z^{2k+1}-2^{\ell+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}\,j\,q_{j}^{\ell}\,z^{2j-1},

which gives

(10)

M_{k,\ell}^{\prime}(1)=(2k+2)(1-H_{k,\ell}),

where

H_{k,\ell}=\frac{2^{\ell}}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}\,j\,q_{j}^{\ell}.

As
k
is even and
q_{j}=q_{k+1-j}
, we can rewrite this as

H_{k,\ell}=\frac{2^{\ell}}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k/2}(-1)^{j+1}\,(k+1-2j)\,q_{j}^{\ell}.

If
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, we have

\begin{split}H_{k,\ell}=\frac{2^{\ell}}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k/4}\Bigg((k+3-4j)\,q_{2j-1}^{\ell}-(k+1-4j)\,q_{2j}^{\ell}\Bigg)\end{split}

and if
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, we have

H_{k,\ell}=\frac{2^{\ell}}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{(k-2)/4}\Big((k+3-4j)\,q_{2j-1}^{\ell}-(k+1-4j)\,q_{2j}^{\ell}\Big)+q_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\ell}.

In both cases, using Lemma
2.2
and the fact that
q_{\frac{k}{2}}>1
, we deduce that

(11)

H_{k,\ell}>{2}^{\ell}\,\left(\frac{k-3}{k+1}\right)\,\left(q_{1}^{\ell}-q_{2}^{\ell}\right)\,\geq\,4\,q_{2}^{2}\,\left(\frac{k-3}{k+1}\right)\,\left(\left(\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}\right)^{2}-1\right)

as
\ell\geq 2
and
q_{1}/q_{2}>1
. Using Lemma
2.1
, equation (
5
), we have

(12)

\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}}=\frac{\zeta(2)\zeta(2k)}{\zeta(4)\zeta(2k-2)}\geq\left(\frac{\zeta(2)}{\zeta(4)}\right)\,\left(\frac{1+4^{-k}}{1+\left(\frac{2k-1}{2k-3}\right)\,4^{-k+1}}\right)=\frac{15}{\pi^{2}}\,\times\,\left(\frac{1+4^{-k}}{1+\left(\frac{2k-1}{2k-3}\right)4^{-k+1}}\right).

Using (
12
) in (
11
), we get

\begin{split}H_{k,\ell}&>\,4\,q_{2}^{2}\,\left(\frac{k-3}{k+1}\right)\,\left[\left(\frac{15}{\pi^{2}}\,\times\,\frac{1+4^{-k}}{1+\left(\frac{2k-1}{2k-3}\right)4^{-k+1}}\right)^{2}-1\right]\\
&>\,4\,\zeta(4)^{2}\,\left(\frac{k-3}{k+1}\right)\,\left[\left(\frac{15}{\pi^{2}}\,\times\,\frac{1+4^{-k}}{1+\left(\frac{2k-1}{2k-3}\right)4^{-k+1}}\right)^{2}-1\right]\\
&>1.5\end{split}

for
k\geq 4
, using the facts that

\frac{k-3}{k+1}

and

\frac{1+4^{-k}}{1+\left(\frac{2k-1}{2k-3}\right)4^{-k+1}}

are increasing functions of
k
. For
k=2
,

H_{2,\ell}=\frac{(2\zeta(2)\zeta(4))^{\ell}}{3\zeta(6)^{\ell}}>1

for all
\ell
. Together with (
10
), this implies that

M_{k,\ell}^{\prime}(1)<0
for all
k
even and
\ell
even.

Now, let
P_{k,\ell}(z)=\frac{M_{k,\ell}(z)}{z^{2}-1}
. Then all the zeros of
M_{k,\ell}(z)
other than
\pm 1
are the zeros of
P_{k,\ell}(z)
. Also,
P_{k,\ell}(1)=M_{k,\ell}^{\prime}(1)<0
and
P_{k,\ell}(z)
is positive for
z
real, positive, and sufficiently large. Thus, by the intermediate value theorem,
P_{k,\ell}(z)
(and hence,
M_{k,\ell}(z)
) has a real zero,
\beta_{k,\ell}
say, greater than 1. As
M_{k,\ell}(z)
is an even reciprocal polynomial,
-\beta_{k,\ell}
and
\pm 1/\beta_{k,\ell}
are also zeros of
M_{k,\ell}(z)
. This completes the proof.
∎

In order to show that these are the only real zeros of
M_{k,\ell}(z)
when
\ell
is even, we will prove that the remaining zeros must lie on the unit circle. This is achieved in the next section.

We now give the proof of Theorem
1.2
. This is equivalent to proving the following statement for the polynomial
M_{k,\ell}(z)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For positive integers
k
and
\ell
with
\ell
odd and
k\geq 2
, let
\beta_{k,\ell}
denote the largest real zero of
M_{k,\ell}(z)
. Then

\beta_{k,\ell}\in\Bigg(\left(\frac{2\,\zeta(2)\,\zeta(2k)}{\zeta(2k+2)}\right)^{\ell/2},\;\Big(2\zeta(2)\Big)^{\ell/2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\zeta(2)}\Big(1+\frac{3\,d_{\ell}}{4^{k}}}\Big)\Bigg),

where

d_{\ell}=\frac{(1.75)^{\ell}-1}{0.75}.

Proof.

For
\ell
odd and
q_{1}=\frac{\zeta(2)\,\zeta(2k)}{\zeta(2k+2)}
, we get

M_{k,\ell}\Big(\Big(2q_{1}\Big)^{\ell/2}\Big)=\Big(2q_{1}\Big)^{\ell(k+1)}+1-2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}q_{j}^{\ell}\,\Big(2q_{1}\Big)^{\ell j}<0,

as
q_{k}=q_{1}
and
q_{j}>1
for
j=1,\ldots,k
. On the other hand,

\frac{M_{k,\ell}(\sqrt{4+t})}{(4+t)^{k+1}}=1+\frac{1}{(4+t)^{k+1}}-2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}q_{j}^{\ell}\,(4+t)^{j-k-1}.

Changing the variable
j
to
k+1-j
in the sum and using
q_{j}=q_{k+1-j}
, we get

\frac{M_{k,\ell}(\sqrt{4+t})}{(4+t)^{k+1}}=1+\frac{1}{(4+t)^{k+1}}-2^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{q_{j}^{\ell}}{(4+t)^{j}}.

Now replacing
1
by
2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\zeta(2j)}/{4^{j}}
using Lemma
2.1
, equation (
8
), the above expression becomes

\frac{M_{k,\ell}(\sqrt{4+t})}{(4+t)^{k+1}}=2\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta(2j)}{4^{j}}+\frac{1}{(4+t)^{k+1}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Bigg[\frac{2\zeta(2j)}{4^{j}}-\frac{(2q_{j})^{\ell}}{(4+t)^{j}}\Bigg].

We want to find a
t
for which
M_{k,\ell}(\sqrt{4+t})>0
. Thus, it is sufficient to find
t
for which

\begin{split}&\frac{2\zeta(2j)}{4^{j}}>\frac{(2q_{j})^{\ell}}{(4+t)^{j}}=\frac{(2\zeta(2j))^{\ell}}{(4+t)^{j}}\Bigg(\frac{\zeta(2k+2-2j)}{\zeta(2k+2)}\Bigg)^{\ell}\;\;\text{for}\;\;{j=1,\ldots,k}.\end{split}

Using the upper bound in Lemma
2.1
, equation (
6
), the above inequality reduces to

\Bigg(\frac{4+t}{4}\Bigg)^{j}>\left(2\zeta(2)\right)^{\ell-1}\Bigg(1+3\cdot 4^{j-(k+1)}\Bigg)^{\ell},\;\;\text{as}\;\;\zeta(2)>\zeta(2j)\;\;\text{for}\;\;{j=2,\ldots,k},

or equivalently

\Bigg(\frac{4+t}{4}\Bigg)^{j}>\left(2\zeta(2)\right)^{\ell-1}\Bigg(1+3\,d_{\ell}\cdot 4^{j-(k+1)}\Bigg),

where
d_{\ell}=\frac{(1.75)^{\ell}-1}{0.75}
follows from Lemma
2.3
by putting
b=0.75
. Thus,

\frac{4+t}{4}>\left(2\zeta(2)\right)^{\frac{\ell-1}{j}}\Bigg(1+3\,d_{\ell}\cdot 4^{j-(k+1)}\Bigg)^{1/j}.

For
a\geq 0
and
0<\delta\leq 1
, we have
(1+a)^{\delta}\leq 1+\delta a
. we replace the above condition by

\frac{4+t}{4}>\left(2\zeta(2)\right)^{\frac{\ell-1}{j}}\Bigg(1+\frac{3\,d_{\ell}\cdot 4^{j-(k+1)}}{j}\Bigg).

The above lower bound is attained at
j=1
. Hence we obtain our final sufficient condition for
M_{k,\ell}(\sqrt{4+t})
to be positive, namely that

4+t>2^{\ell+1}\zeta(2)^{\ell-1}\Bigg(1+\frac{3\,d_{\ell}}{4^{k}}\Bigg).

This proves the theorem.
∎

Note that in order to prove the upper bound on

, we did not use the parity of

. This bound also holds when
\ell
is even.

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Delta_{k,\ell}(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert-2^{\ell}\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}(-1)^{(\ell+1)(k+j)}(q_{j}^{\ell}-1)z^{2j}\right\rvert

, equation (

(15)

\begin{split}q_{j}&<\zeta(2j)\zeta(2k+2-2j)\\

where

, we get that for

\begin{split}\left\lvert\Delta_{k,\ell}(z)\right\rvert&<2^{\ell}c_{\ell}\,\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}\varepsilon_{k,j}\\

\begin{split}\left\lvert\Delta_{k,\ell}(z)\right\rvert&<2^{\ell}c_{\ell}\,\left(2\left[\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\Bigg(1+\frac{2}{2j-1}\Bigg)4^{-j}\right]+\frac{25\,(k-2)\,4^{-k-1}}{9}\right)\\

since

The claim follows by taking

is equivalent to the statement that

and

5.
Concluding remarks

Let
f(x)=a_{0}\,x^{m}+a_{1}\,x^{m-1}+\cdots+a_{m}\in\mathbb{C}[x]
with
a_{0}\neq 0
. Then the discriminant of
f
is
\text{Disc}(f):=a_{0}^{2m-2}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}{\left(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}\right)}^{2}
, where
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
,
\ldots
,
\alpha_{m}
are all the zeros of
f(x)
. A related quantity is the Mahler measure of
f
and is given by

M(f):=|a_{0}|\,\prod_{j=1}^{m}\max\left\{1,\,|\alpha_{j}|\right\}.

Then Mahler
[
7
]
showed that

|\text{Disc}(f)|\leq m^{m}\,M(f)^{2m-2}.

In case of the polynomials
R_{k,\ell}(x)
, Theorem
1.1
implies that

M\left(R_{k,\ell}\right)=|a_{0,k,\ell}|\,|\alpha_{k,\ell}|,

where
a_{0,k,\ell}
is the leading coefficient of
R_{k,\ell}(x)
and
\alpha_{k,\ell}
is its largest real zero. Therefore, we deduce that

{\left({(k+1)}^{-(k+1)}\,\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k+1}{\left(\alpha_{i,k,\ell}-\alpha_{j,k,\ell}\right)}^{2}\right)}^{1/2k}\leq|\alpha_{k,\ell}|.

Thus, using the remark after Theorem
3.1
, we obtain that the largest real zero
\alpha_{k,\ell}
always lies in the interval

\left(\ {\left({(k+1)}^{-(k+1)}\,\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k+1}{\left(\alpha_{i,k,\ell}-\alpha_{j,k,\ell}\right)}^{2}\right)}^{1/2k},\,\,\,2^{\ell+1}\,\zeta(2)^{\ell-1}\,\left(1+\frac{3\,d_{\ell}}{4^{k}}\right)\right),

where

d_{\ell}=\frac{(1.75)^{\ell}-1}{0.75}.

Here
\alpha_{j,k,\ell}
denote the zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(x)
. However, determining the size of the lower bound above does not appear straightforward.

On another note, since the Bernoulli numbers are rational, the zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(z)
are algebraic numbers, and their algebraic properties warrant further study. For instance, the irreducibility of
R_{k,\ell}(z)
over
\mathbb{Q}
when
k
is odd and that of
R_{k,\ell}(z)/(z-{(-1)}^{\ell})
when
k
is even, is a natural question. Numerical evidence suggests that this is indeed the case. Note that a proof of such a fact would entail the irrationality of the real zero
\alpha_{k,\ell}
of
R_{k,\ell}(z)
. A related problem is to identify whether any of the zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(z)
are roots of unity. Preliminary computations suggest that except for
\pm 1
, the other zeros of
R_{k,\ell}(z)
are not roots of unity for
\ell>1
, but a proof of this fact seems elusive.

A real algebraic integer
\alpha>1
, all of whose Galois conjugates except
\alpha
lie inside
|z|\leq 1
, with at least one on the boundary
|z|=1
, is called a
Salem number
. These numbers are well-studied in the context of Diophantine approximation and related areas in number theory (see the survey
[
11
]
). If we assume the irreducibility of
R_{k,\ell}(z)
alluded to earlier, then for
k\geq 4
, the real zero
\alpha_{k,\ell}
