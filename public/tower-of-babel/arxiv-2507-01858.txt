Infinite Dimensional Topological-Holomorphic Symmetry in Three-Dimensions
Hank Chen, Joaquin Liniado

Abstract
. We introduce a three-dimensional quantum field theory with an infinite-dimensional symmetry, realized explicitly through a centrally extended affine graded Lie algebra. This symmetry is a direct three-dimensional generalization of the chiral symmetry in the Wess-Zumino-Witten model. Accordingly, this setup provides a framework for extending the methods of two-dimensional conformal field theory to three dimensions, and we expect it to lay the groundwork for exact methods in three-dimensional quantum field theory.



1.
Introduction

Two-dimensional conformal field theories have become a foundational framework for both physics and mathematics. The modern formulation of conformal invariance in two dimensions began with the seminal work of Belavin, Polyakov, and Zamolodchikov
[
1
]
. Building on the representation theory of the Virasoro algebra, they introduced the concept of an algebra of local operators and used it to construct exactly solvable conformal theories, known as minimal models. This breakthrough sparked intense activity at the intersection of mathematical physics and statistical mechanics, as minimal models were soon identified with a variety of two-dimensional statistical systems at criticality.

In view of these developments, many efforts were devoted to extending the powerful tools of two-dimensional conformal field theory to higher dimensions. These attempts, however, encountered fundamental obstacles. The most immediate is Liouville’s rigidity theorem, which implies that for
d\geq 3
, all local conformal transformations extend to global ones, and the global conformal group is finite-dimensional. This rules out the existence of local infinite-dimensional conformal symmetry algebras in higher dimensions.

Given these obstructions, a prevailing strategy has been to isolate 2d chiral subalgebras within higher-dimensional theories, where infinite-dimensional symmetries can still be realized. A prominent example is provided by
4
d
\mathcal{N}=2
superconformal field theories, where a protected sector of local operators was shown in
[
2
]
to organize into a two-dimensional vertex operator algebra, enabling the use of chiral algebra techniques to obtain exact results. More recently, the celestial holography program has proposed that
4
d scattering amplitudes can be reinterpreted as correlators in a
2
d conformal field theory on the celestial sphere
[
3
,
4
]
, where the hope is that this perspective may allow the application of chiral algebra techniques to the problem of flat space holography
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

The aim of this work is to revisit the pursuit of infinite-dimensional symmetry algebras in three-dimensional quantum field theories, but with a shift in perspective. Rather than looking for a subsector whose dynamics can be captured by a two-dimensional chiral algebra, the strategy is to generalize the very notion of a chiral algebra into an infinite-dimensional symmetry structure suited to describe three-dimensional dynamics. While these algebras are not chiral in the usual sense, they remain infinite-dimensional and thus preserve many of the powerful features typically associated with chiral algebras.

The three-dimensional theories that we will consider are said to be partially topological and partially holomorphic. They first appeared in the context of twisted 3d
\mathcal{N}=2

These theories are defined on three-dimensional manifolds equipped with a transverse holomorphic foliation (THF)

To recover an analogue of this structure in three dimensions, one must impose a stronger constraint: a compatibility condition that ties holomorphicity to independence along the topological direction. But how exactly?

2.
WZW model and the Kac-Moody Algebra

Given a compact Lie group
G
and a two-dimensional Riemann surface
\Sigma
, the Wess-Zumino-Witten model describes a sigma model with target space
G
, with an additional topological term. The action is given by
[
23
]

(2.0.1)

S[g]=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Sigma}\langle g^{-1}{\mathrm{d}}g,\star g^{-1}{\mathrm{d}}g\rangle+\frac{ik}{12\pi}\int_{B}\langle\tilde{g}^{-1}{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{g},[\tilde{g}^{-1}{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{g},\tilde{g}^{-1}{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{g}]\rangle\,,

where
g:\Sigma\rightarrow G
is a smooth field, and
\tilde{g}:B\rightarrow G
is an extension to a three-manifold
B
with boundary
\partial B=\Sigma
. The integer
k\in\mathbb{Z}
is known as the
level
of the theory. The first term is the standard sigma model kinetic term, while the second—known as the Wess–Zumino term—depends only on the homotopy class of the extension
\tilde{g}
.

A remarkable feature of this model is its invariance under the transformations

(2.0.2)

g(z,\bar{z})\mapsto\ell^{-1}(\bar{z})\,g(z,\bar{z})\,r(z)\,,

where
(z,\bar{z})
are local complex coordinates on
\Sigma
, and
\ell(\bar{z})
,
r(z)
are smooth maps valued in
G
. It’s invariance can be verified directly using infinitesimal expressions or the Polyakov-Wiegmann identity
[
24
]
. In particular, the existence of these symmetries imply the existence of conserved currents, which are given by

(2.0.3)

J(z)=kg^{-1}\partial_{z}g\,,\quad\bar{J}(\bar{z})=k\partial_{\bar{z}}gg^{-1}\,.

The conservation of these currents follows from the equations of motion. Indeed, under the infinitesimal variation
g\mapsto g+g\tilde{\alpha}
with arbitrary
\tilde{\alpha}
, one obtains the equation

(2.0.4)

\partial_{\bar{z}}(g^{-1}\partial_{z}g)=0\,,

while the variation
g\mapsto g-\alpha g
yields the complementary equation

(2.0.5)

\partial_{z}(\partial_{\bar{z}}gg^{-1})=0\,.

The significance of these equations was first recognized in Witten’s seminal work
[
23
]
, where he realized that the modes of these currents generate the Kac-Moody algebra at level
k
. Let us review this statement explicitly for the holomorphic current
J(z)
; everything we say will apply equally to
\bar{J}(\bar{z})
.

Since
J(z)=k\,g^{-1}\partial_{z}g
, it takes values in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{Lie}(G)
. Choosing a basis
\{t_{a}\}
of
\mathfrak{g}
, we can expand

(2.0.6)

J(z)=\sum_{a=1}^{\dim\mathfrak{g}}J^{a}(z)\,t_{a}\,.

Moreover, the holomorphicity of
J(z)
implies that each component
J^{a}(z)
is a holomorphic function, and thus admits a Laurent expansion on
\mathbb{C}^{\times}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
of the form

(2.0.7)

J^{a}(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}J^{a}_{n}\,z^{-n-1}\,,

where the choice of powers matches the standard conventions in the literature. We have the following proposition

2.0.1 Proposition.

The modes
J^{a}_{n}
satisfy the current algebra

(2.0.8)

\{J^{a}_{n},J^{b}_{m}\}=f^{ab}{}_{c}\,J^{c}_{n+m}+kn\,\kappa^{ab}\delta_{n+m,0}\,,

which are precisely the commutation relations of the Kac-Moody algebra at level
k
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha(z)
and
\alpha^{\prime}(z)
be two infinitesimal right acting holomorphic transformations. The
conserved Noether charge aspects
associated to these transformations are given by

(2.0.9)

Q_{\alpha}=\langle\alpha,J\rangle\,,\quad Q_{\alpha^{\prime}}=\langle\alpha^{\prime},J\rangle\,,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is the bilinear form. The Poisson brackets between these charges can be obtained from the formula

(2.0.10)

\{Q_{\alpha},Q_{\alpha^{\prime}}\}_{\rm P.B}=\delta_{\alpha}Q_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,.

The right hand side of the above equation can be computed as

(2.0.11)

\delta_{\alpha}Q_{\alpha^{\prime}}=\langle\alpha^{\prime},\delta_{\alpha}J\rangle=\langle\alpha^{\prime},-[\alpha,J]+k\partial_{z}\alpha\rangle=Q_{[\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}]}+k\langle\alpha^{\prime},\partial_{z}\alpha\rangle\,.

To compute the Poisson brackets of the currents, we begin by expanding both the holomorphic current
J
and the symmetry transformation parameters
\alpha
,
\alpha^{\prime}
into Laurent modes:

(2.0.12)

J(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}J_{n}z^{-n-1}\,,\qquad\alpha(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{n}z^{n}\,,\qquad\alpha^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha^{\prime}_{n}z^{n}\,,

and consider the integrated Noether charge obtained by integrating over the circle:

(2.0.13)

q_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{S^{1}}\mathrm{d}z\,Q_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\oint_{S^{1}}\mathrm{d}z\,\langle\alpha_{n},J_{m}\rangle z^{n-m-1}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle\alpha_{n},J_{n}\rangle\,.

Applying this procedure to equations (
2.0.10
) and (
2.0.11
) and using the notation
\langle t_{a},t_{b}\rangle=\kappa_{ab}
for the invariant bilinear form, we find

(2.0.14)

\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{n}^{a}\alpha^{\prime\,c}_{m}\{J_{n}^{b},J_{m}^{d}\}\kappa_{ab}\kappa_{cd}=\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{n}^{a}\alpha_{m}^{\prime\,c}(J_{n+m}^{d}f^{b}_{ac}\kappa_{bd}+k\kappa_{ac}\delta_{m+n,0})\,.

Rearranging and contracting indices appropriately, the desired result follows

∎

The fact that the modes of the chiral currents
J
and
\bar{J}
satisfy a Kac–Moody algebra has a number of significant consequences; among them is the fact that it makes the WZW model exactly solvable at the quantum level. Indeed, the chiral currents
J
and
\bar{J}
allow for the construction of the stress-energy tensor via the Sugawara construction
[
25
,
26
]
. This tensor is conserved and traceless as an operator, ensuring full quantum conformal invariance. The combination of affine Kac–Moody and Virasoro symmetries imposes strong constraints on the theory. In particular, the Ward identities associated with the Kac–Moody symmetry lead to the Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov equation, which governs the correlation functions of primary fields
[
27
]
. In many cases, this equation can be solved exactly, yielding explicit expressions for these correlators — these are the
conformal blocks
. Since all other fields in the theory can be written in terms of primaries, this gives a complete solution for the WZW model.

2.1.
4d Chern-Simons Theory

The WZW model is closely related to 3d Chern Simons theory. This connection was first pointed out by Witten in
[
28

Notably, the full (non-chiral) WZW model can also be obtained from four-dimensional Chern–Simons theory when the meromorphic 1-form is chosen such that the action takes the form
[

(2.1.1)

S_{\mathrm{CS_{4}}}(A)=\int_{\Sigma\times\mathbb{C}P^{1}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}z}{z}\wedge\langle A,{\mathrm{d}}A+\tfrac{2}{3}A\wedge A\rangle\,,

where
\Sigma
is a two-dimensional manifold and
z
is a coordinate on
\mathbb{C}P^{1}
. For an introduction to four-dimensional Chern–Simons theory, the reader may consult
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
.

What makes this construction of the WZW model particularly interesting is that it provides a natural origin for the left- and right-acting symmetries described in equation (
2.0.2
). Indeed, the gauge symmetries of the gauge field
A
at the four-dimensional level become, after localisation, the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic symmetries of the two-dimensional field
g
. For a detailed explanation of this mechanism, we refer the reader to §5.1 of
[
18
]
. As we shall discuss in the next section, a similar analysis will allow us to identify the symmetries of the three-dimensional topological-holomorphic field theory.

3.
3d Topological-Holomorphic Theory

In this section, we introduce the main features of the three-dimensional theory constructed in
[
18
]
, following the same analysis than in the previous section. Our goal is to show that the Raviolo formalism is not merely a convenient tool, but in fact the natural framework for understanding the infinite dimensional symmetry structure of this theory, and more generally, of topological holomorphic field theories in three-dimensions.

Let
\mathfrak{G}=(\mathfrak{g}\xrightarrow{\mu_{1}}\mathfrak{h},\mu_{2})
denote a Lie 2-algebra and let
\mathbb{G}=(\mathsf{H}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{t}}G,\rhd)
the corresponding Lie 2-group. We consider a three-dimensional manifold
M
, which we take for now to be
M=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}
. The three-dimensional action is given by
[
18
]

(3.0.1)

S[g,\Theta]=\int_{M}\operatorname{vol}_{M}\Big(\langle\partial_{z}(\partial_{\bar{z}}gg^{-1}),g\rhd\Theta_{\tau}\rangle-\langle\partial_{z}(\partial_{\tau}gg^{-1}),g\rhd\Theta_{\bar{z}}\rangle\\
+\langle\mu_{1}\big(\partial_{z}(g\rhd\Theta_{\bar{z}})\big),g\rhd\Theta_{\tau}\rangle\Big)\,,

where
g\in C^{\infty}(M,G)
is a
G
-valued smooth field,
\Theta\in\Omega^{1}(M,\mathfrak{h})
an
\mathfrak{h}
-valued
1
-form, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{h}\to\mathbb{R}
a degree
-1
, non degenerate, invariant bilinear form. For an introduction to Lie
2
-groups and Lie
2
-algebras, we refer the reader to
[
34
,
35
,
36
]

The appearance of the 1-form field
\Theta
, not as a gauge connection but rather as a matter field playing a role analogous to that of
g
, is closely tied to the higher categorical nature of the action. This reflects the categorical ladder = dimensional ladder principle
[
37
,
38
]
, which underlies the construction of the three-dimensional action (
3.0.1
). As we shall see, the presence of this higher-form field, together with the graded structure provided by the Lie 2-algebra, integrates seamlessly with the Raviolo formalism developed in
[
21
]
.

In complete analogy with the WZW model, the action (
3.0.1
) is invariant under both left- and right-acting symmetries. A direct computation shows that it is invariant under the transformations

(3.0.2)

g\mapsto g(1+\tilde{\alpha})\,,\quad\Theta\mapsto\Theta-\tilde{\alpha}\rhd\Theta+\tilde{\Gamma}\,,

where the infinitesimal parameters
\tilde{\alpha}\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathfrak{g})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}\in\Omega^{1}(M,\mathfrak{h})
satisfy the constraints

(3.0.3)

\partial_{\bar{z}}\tilde{\alpha}+\mu_{1}(\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{z}})=0\,,\quad\partial_{\tau}\tilde{\alpha}+\mu_{1}(\tilde{\Gamma}_{\tau})=0\,,\quad\partial_{\tau}\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{z}}-\partial_{\bar{z}}\tilde{\Gamma}_{\tau}=0\,.

These define the right-acting symmetries of the model. The left-acting symmetries take the form

(3.0.4)

g\mapsto(1-\alpha)g\,,\quad\Theta\mapsto\Theta-g^{-1}(1+\alpha)\rhd\Gamma\,,

where the infinitesimal parameters
\alpha\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathfrak{g})
and
\Gamma\in\Omega^{1}(M,\mathfrak{h})
satisfy

(3.0.5)

\partial_{z}\alpha=0\,,\quad\partial_{z}\Gamma_{\tau}=0\,,\quad\partial_{z}\Gamma_{\bar{z}}=0\,.

Let us pause to highlight a few important observations. First, just like the fields in the theory, the symmetry transformations also come in pairs, once again reflecting the underlying categorical structure. Second, the left-acting symmetry pair is anti-holomorphic, exactly as in the WZW model. In contrast, the right-acting symmetry satisfies a more intricate condition: it involves derivatives with respect to
\bar{z}
and
\tau
, along with a nontrivial appearance of the map
\mu_{1}
. This marks an important difference from the two-dimensional case. In particular, since the spacetime is now odd-dimensional, there is no natural chiral–anti-chiral decomposition, and we must carefully identify which of these symmetries plays the role of the chiral symmetry familiar from two dimensions—that is, which one gives rise to the infinite-dimensional symmetry algebra.

The symmetry transformations (
3.0.2
) and (
3.0.4
) give rise to associated conserved currents. In line with the higher categorical structure of the theory, these currents will also be a pair, in this case, a
\mathfrak{g}
-valued 1-form and an
\mathfrak{h}
-valued 2-form. This structure is consistent with the higher-form symmetries of the action. With this in mind, these currents can be derived using
Noether’s trick
as in the two-dimensional case. Specifically, for the right-acting symmetry, one considers a variation of the action under
g\mapsto g(1+\tilde{\alpha})
and
\Theta\mapsto\Theta-\tilde{\alpha}\rhd\Theta+\tilde{\Gamma}
with arbitrary infinitesimal parameters
\tilde{\alpha}\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathfrak{g})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}\in\Omega^{1}(M,\mathfrak{h})
. Requiring the action to be stationary under such variations, i.e.,
\delta S=0
, leads to

(3.0.6)

\partial_{\bar{z}}\tilde{L}_{z}-\mu_{1}\tilde{H}_{{\bar{z}}z}=0,\qquad\partial_{\tau}\tilde{L}_{z}-\mu_{1}\tilde{H}_{\tau z}=0,\qquad\partial_{\tau}H_{\bar{z}z}+\partial_{\bar{z}}H_{\tau z}=0\,.

where we have introduced the currents

(3.0.7)

\tilde{L}_{z}=g^{-1}\partial_{z}g\,,\quad\tilde{H}_{z{\bar{z}}}=\partial_{z}\Theta_{\bar{z}}+\mu_{2}(L_{z},\Theta_{\bar{z}}),\quad\tilde{H}_{z\tau}=\partial_{z}\Theta_{\tau}+\mu_{2}(L_{z},\Theta_{\tau})\,.

In a completely analogous manner, we can compute the currents associated to the left symmetry, given by

(3.0.8)

\displaystyle L_{\bar{z}}=-\partial_{\bar{z}}gg^{-1}-\mu_{1}(g\rhd\Theta_{\bar{z}})\,,\quad L_{\tau}=-\partial_{\tau}gg^{-1}-\mu_{1}(g\rhd\Theta_{\tau})\,,

\displaystyle H_{{\bar{z}}\tau}=g\rhd(\partial_{\bar{z}}\Theta_{\tau}-\partial_{\tau}\Theta_{\bar{z}}-[\Theta_{\bar{z}},\Theta_{\tau}])\,,

which satisfy the conservation equations

(3.0.9)

\partial_{z}L_{\bar{z}}=0\,,\quad\partial_{z}L_{\tau}=0\,,\quad\partial_{z}H_{\bar{z}\tau}=0\,.

The attentive reader may have noticed a slight abuse in our presentation: although we stated that the conserved currents are given by a
\mathfrak{g}
-valued 1-form and an
\mathfrak{h}
-valued 2-form, we have so far only written down specific components of these objects. Writing the currents fully as differential forms is, in fact, central to the Raviolo construction, so we now turn to this point more carefully.

3.1.
Higher-currents as differential forms

The expressions we have obtained suggest a natural splitting of coordinates, treating
(\bar{z},\tau)
and
z
separately. More precisely, we can introduce the differential

(3.1.1)

\mathrm{d}^{\prime}=\bar{\partial}+\mathrm{d}_{\tau}\,,

and decompose differential forms into components along
\mathrm{d}^{\prime}
and along
\partial
. With this in mind, the currents associated with the right-acting symmetry can be written as

(3.1.2)

\tilde{L}=g^{-1}\partial g\,,\quad\tilde{H}=\partial\Theta^{\prime}+\mu_{2}(g^{-1}\partial g,\Theta^{\prime})\,,

and those associated with the left-acting symmetry as

(3.1.3)

L=-\mathrm{d}^{\prime}g\,g^{-1}-\mu_{1}(\Theta^{\prime})\,,\quad H=g\rhd\left(\mathrm{d}^{\prime}\Theta-\tfrac{1}{2}[\Theta^{\prime},\Theta^{\prime}]\right)\,.

In terms of this decomposition, the conservation equation (
3.0.6
) takes the form

(3.1.4)

\mathrm{d}^{\prime}\tilde{L}-\mu_{1}(\tilde{H})=0\,,\quad\mathrm{d}^{\prime}\tilde{H}=0\,,

and the differential constraints (
3.0.5
) satisfied by the symmetry parameters can be written as

(3.1.5)

{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\tilde{\alpha}+\mu_{1}(\tilde{\Gamma})=0\,,\quad{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\tilde{\Gamma}=0\,.

Similarly, the conservation equation (
3.0.9
) can be written using the remaining differential as

(3.1.6)

\partial L=0\,,\quad\partial H=0\,,

and the differential constraints (
3.0.5
) satisfied by the symmetry transformation parameters take the form

(3.1.7)

\partial\alpha=0\,,\quad\partial\Gamma=0\,.

Let us note that this runs in complete parallel to what happens in the two dimensional case. Indeed, there, one can use the Dolbeault decomposition
{\mathrm{d}}=\partial+\bar{\partial}
to define

(3.1.8)

J=g^{-1}\partial g\,,\quad\bar{J}=\bar{\partial}gg^{-1}

such that the conservation equations become

(3.1.9)

\bar{\partial}J=0\,,\quad\partial\bar{J}=0\,,

and the symmetry transformation parameters satisfy

(3.1.10)

\bar{\partial}\tilde{\alpha}=0\,,\quad\partial\alpha=0\,.

At first glance, one might think this is just a convenient way to package our expressions. However, there is more to it: the existence of Laurent expansions for both the symmetry transformation parameter
\tilde{\alpha}
and the holomorphic current
J
—which was crucial for realizing the symmetry algebra of the WZW model as the Kac-Moody algebra in
Proposition

2.0.1
—can instead be understood from a cohomological perspective.

Indeed, the condition
\bar{\partial}\tilde{\alpha}=0
implies that

(3.1.11)

\tilde{\alpha}\in H^{(0,0)}_{\bar{\partial}}((\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})\otimes\mathfrak{g})={\mathbb{C}}[z,z^{-1}]\otimes\mathfrak{g}\,\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\tilde{\alpha}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\alpha}_{n}z^{n}\,.

Furthermore, using the linear isomorphism

(3.1.12)

{\mathrm{d}}z\wedge-:H^{(0,0)}_{\bar{\partial}}((\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})\otimes\mathfrak{g})\to H^{(1,0)}_{\bar{\partial}}((\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})\otimes\mathfrak{g})\,,

one can also write a Laurent expansion for the current

\bar{\partial}J=0

(3.1.13)

J\in H^{(1,0)}_{\bar{\partial}}((\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})\otimes\mathfrak{g})\,.

In other words, the mode expansions of the current and symmetry parameters, can be understood as arising from their realization as nontrivial Dolbeault cohomology classes.

Turning back to our theory, our goal is to find mode expansions for the conserved currents
(\tilde{L},\tilde{H})
,
(L,H)

(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\Gamma})
,
(\alpha,\Gamma)
, in order to explicitly construct an infinite-dimensional symmetry algebra analogous to the Kac–Moody algebra. Based on the preceding argument, a natural strategy is to work directly at the level of cohomology.

Crucially, to study the cohomology of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
using the splitting along
(\bar{z},\tau)
and
z
, we must understand the decomposition of the entire complex
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})
. While the Dolbeault decomposition for
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})
is a well established tool in complex geometry, the study of
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})
using the splitting defined by the differentials
\mathrm{d}^{\prime}
and
\partial
lies at the core of the Raviolo formalism
[
21
]
. This is precisely why the Raviolo framework provides the appropriate structure for analyzing our theory, as we shall discuss in detail in §
4
.

3.2.
Transverse Holomorphic Foliation

In general, the theory (
3.0.1
) can be defined on any oriented 3-manifold
M
equipped with local coordinate charts of the form
(\tau,z,\bar{z})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}
, such that the transition functions are given by

(\tau,z,\bar{z})\mapsto(\tau^{\prime}(\tau,z,\bar{z}),z(z),\bar{z}(\bar{z}))\,.

Such 3-manifolds are said to be equipped with a
transverse holomorphic foliation

\mathcal{F}
. More precisely, we have

Definition 3.2.1
.

Let
M
denote a smooth manifold. A
transverse holomorphic foliation (THF)
on
M
is a smooth foliation
\mathcal{F}
of even codimension, whose leaves are equipped with a structure of a complex manifold. In other words, the foliation atlas of
(M,\mathcal{F})
has equipped transition functions which are biholomorphic on the leaves. Vector fields in the tangent bundle
T\mathcal{F}\subset TM
along the leaves are called the
topological directions
.

A field theory is said to be
topological-holomorphic
if its fields are defined on a smooth manifold
M
equipped with a THF structure
\mathcal{F}
.
It is clear that the kinematical and dynamical properties of a topological-holomorphic field theory depend crucially on the underlying THF structure. As we shall see in the upcoming section, the Raviolo construction is done for general 3-manifolds which admit a THF.

4.
The Raviolo

In this section, we review the Raviolo formalism developed in
[
21
]
. As previously noted, this framework can be understood as the study of the cohomology of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, using the natural splitting of coordinates into
(\bar{z},\tau)
and
z
. The core idea behind the Raviolo formalism is that it provides a direct mechanism for constructing higher-dimensional vertex algebras (see also
[
39
]
)—an algebraic structure that has proven remarkably powerful in the context of two-dimensional conformal field theory.

At times, the discussion will necessarily become technical in order to maintain precision. However, we will aim to motivate each step and provide clear explanations for the objects we introduce, helping the reader follow the structure of the construction, understand where we are headed, and see why each ingredient is introduced and how it fits into the overall picture.

Recall that in the previous section, we motivated the need to construct the cohomology of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
in order to obtain an object analogous to

(4.0.1)

H^{(0,0)}_{\bar{\partial}}\big((\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})\otimes\mathfrak{g}\big)={\mathbb{C}}[z,z^{-1}]\otimes\mathfrak{g}\,,

which underlies the mode expansions of the Kac-Moody currents. In our case, however, we no longer work with the differential
\bar{\partial}
, but with a modified differential
\mathrm{d}^{\prime}=\bar{\partial}+\mathrm{d}_{\tau}
. Accordingly, the object we want to study is schematically of the form

(4.0.2)

H_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}\big((\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})\otimes\mathfrak{G}\big)\,,

where
\mathfrak{G}=(\mathfrak{g}\xrightarrow{\mu_{1}}\mathfrak{h},\mu_{2})
is the Lie 2-algebra used to define our three-dimensional theory (
3.0.1
). To construct such an object, we must first understand the structure of the differential complex defined by
\mathrm{d}^{\prime}
.

4.1.
The mixed de Rham-Dolbeault complexes

Let
M
denote a 3d manifold equipped with a THF
\mathcal{F}
. Locally, there are coordinate charts
(\tau,z,\bar{z})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{C}}
on
M
which fixes a canonical basis
\partial_{\tau}
spanning the topological directions
T_{(\tau,z,\bar{z})}\mathcal{F}
. The complexified quotient bundle

(4.1.1)

Q_{\mathbb{C}}=TM/T\mathcal{F}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\cong Q^{(1,0)}\otimes Q^{(0,1)}\,,

then determines a projection
TM\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow Q^{(1,0)}
whose kernel

V_{\mathcal{F}}=\operatorname{ker}(TM\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow Q^{(1,0)})\subset TM\otimes{\mathbb{C}}

defines a subbundle spanned locally by the vector fields
\partial_{\tau},\partial_{\bar{z}}
. In other words, we have at each point
(\tau,z,\bar{z})\in M
the tangent spaces

(4.1.2)

V_{\mathcal{F}}|_{(\tau,z,\bar{z})}=\mathrm{span}(\partial_{\tau},\partial_{\bar{z}})\,,\quad Q^{(1,0)}|_{(\tau,z,\bar{z})}=\mathrm{span}(\partial_{z})\,.

The space
V_{\mathcal{F}}
plays a crucial role in the characterization of manifolds with THFs
(M,\mathcal{F})

[
19
]
. Crucially, it also gives rise to a local decomposition of the complexified de Rham complex
\Omega_{\mathrm{dR}}^{\bullet}(M)
on
M
, such that the exterior derivative splits

(4.1.3)

{\mathrm{d}}={\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}+\partial\quad\text{on}\quad\mathcal{A}^{p,q}=\wedge^{p}(V_{\mathcal{F}}^{*})\otimes\wedge^{q}(Q^{(1,0)})^{*},

into the holomorphic Dolbeault differential
\partial
plus the differential
{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}
on the so-called
\mathcal{F}
-basic holomorpic forms
4
4

4

More precisely, these are sheaves of holomorphic functions
f
on
M
such that
L_{X}f=0
for all
X\in V_{F}
.
. In this decomposition
V_{\mathcal{F}}^{*}
is locally spanned by
{\mathrm{d}}\bar{z},{\mathrm{d}}\tau
, while
\big(Q^{(1,0)}\big)^{*}
is locally spanned by
{\mathrm{d}}z
. Note, for reference, that
\mathcal{A}^{p,q}
is what we usually denote by
\Omega^{(p,q)}(M)
. Since the space
\mathcal{A}^{p,q}
will play an important role, let us provide explicit examples of its elements for the lowest degrees.

The space
\mathcal{A}^{0,0}
consists of smooth complex-valued functions. An element of
\mathcal{A}^{1,0}
can be expressed locally as

(4.1.4)

L_{\bar{z}}(z,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\bar{z}+L_{\tau}(z,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,.

At degree 2, elements of
\mathcal{A}^{1,1}
are locally given by

(4.1.5)

\tilde{H}_{z\bar{z}}(z,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}z\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar{z}+\tilde{H}_{z\tau}(z,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}z\wedge\mathrm{d}\tau\,,

while elements of
\mathcal{A}^{2,0}
look like

(4.1.6)

H_{\tau\bar{z}}(z,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar{z}\,.

In particular, the differential
{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}
raises the
p
-degree by 1, while
\partial
raises the
q
-degree by 1.

Remark 1
:

If
M
is
split
, that is, if
M\cong\Sigma\times S
with
\Sigma
a Riemann surface and
S
a
1

(4.1.7)

\left(\mathcal{A}^{\bullet,q},{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\right)\cong\left(\Omega^{(q,\bullet)}_{\mathrm{Dol}}(\Sigma)\otimes\Omega^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{dR}}(S),\bar{\partial}\otimes\mathbb{1}+\mathbb{1}\otimes{\mathrm{d}}_{\tau}\right)

for

, where
\Omega^{(q,\bullet)}_{\mathrm{Dol}}(\Sigma)
is the Dolbeault complex and
\Omega^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{dR}}(S)
the de Rham complex. In this case, we can also define a different complex

(4.1.8)

(\mathcal{B}^{\bullet,q},\partial+\bar{\partial})=\left(\Omega^{(\bullet,\bullet)}_{\mathrm{Dol}}(\Sigma)\otimes\Omega^{q}_{\mathrm{dR}}(S),(\partial+\bar{\partial})\otimes\mathbb{1}\right)

for
q=0,1
. In other words, the idea is that the differential in
\mathcal{A}
can be thought of as
split
into
\mathrm{d}=\mathrm{d}^{\prime}+\partial
, while in
\mathcal{B}
it splits as
\mathrm{d}=(\partial+\bar{\partial})+\mathrm{d}_{\tau}
. Crucially, the complexes
\mathcal{A}^{\bullet,q}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\bullet,q}
are not quasi-isomorphic, however, we do have the following isomorphisms

(4.1.9)

\left(\bigoplus_{q=0,1}\left(\mathcal{A}^{\bullet,q},{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\right),\partial\right)\cong\Omega_{\mathbb{C}}^{\bullet}(M)\cong\left(\bigoplus_{q=0,1}\left(\mathcal{B}^{\bullet,q},(\partial+\bar{\partial})\right),{\mathrm{d}}_{\tau}\right)\,,

when we add back in the ”missing” differentials in
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
. This gives a precise notion of the
chirality vector

\ell

[
18

: it specifies which differential is “missing”.

4.1.1.
Tensoring with
\mathfrak{G}

At the beginning of this section, we stated that our goal was to compute the cohomology of
M\otimes\mathfrak{G}
. However, unlike the 2d case,
\mathfrak{G}=(\mathfrak{g}\xrightarrow{\mu_{1}}\mathfrak{h},\mu_{2})
itself is a chain complex with differential
\mu_{1}
. Consequently, we are actually dealing with a tensor product complex of the form

(4.1.10)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{0,q}\otimes\mathfrak{h}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{1,q}\otimes\mathfrak{h}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{0,q}\otimes\mathfrak{g}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{1,q}\otimes\mathfrak{g}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}

for
q=0,1
. As a tensor product complex
\mathcal{A}^{\bullet,q}\otimes\mathfrak{G}
is equipped with the differential

(4.1.11)

\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}={\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\otimes\mathbb{1}-(-1)^{\text{deg}}\mathbb{1}\otimes\mu_{1}\,,

where “
deg
” denotes the homogeneous degree of the first entry. More explicitly, for each
\alpha\otimes\mathsf{x}\in\mathcal{A}^{\bullet,q}\otimes\mathfrak{G}
we have

(4.1.12)

\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\alpha\otimes\mathsf{x})={\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\alpha\otimes\mathsf{x}-(-1)^{|\alpha|}\alpha\otimes\mu_{1}(\mathsf{x})\,,

where
|\alpha|
is the homogeneous degree of
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{\bullet,q}
.

The crucial observation is then that, as we shall now discuss, the currents associated to the symmetry of the three-dimensional theory determine precisely the cohomology classes in the tensor product complex
\mathcal{A}^{\bullet,q}\otimes\mathfrak{G}
!

4.2.
Revisiting the Three-Dimensional Theory

Let us now apply the framework developed above to reformulate our 3d topological-holomorphic field theory
S[g,\Theta]
in terms of the tensor product complex
\mathcal{A}^{p,q}\otimes\mathfrak{G}
.

We begin by noting that, since the component
\Theta_{z}
is missing from the action (
3.0.1
), we can view the fundamental fields of
S[g,\Theta]
as elements

g\in\mathcal{A}^{0,0}\otimes G,\quad\Theta\in\mathcal{A}^{1,0}\otimes\mathfrak{h}\,.

This then allows us to write
S[g,\Theta]
in a very compact form

(4.2.1)

S[g,\Theta]=\int_{M}\langle\partial({\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}gg^{-1}),g\rhd\Theta\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\mu_{1}(\partial(g\rhd\Theta)),g\rhd\Theta\rangle\,.

The symmetry transformation parameters of (
3.0.2
) and (
3.0.4
) can also be identified with elements of the complex as

(4.2.2)

\alpha,\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}^{0,0}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\,,\quad\Gamma,\tilde{\Gamma}\in\mathcal{A}^{1,0}\otimes\mathfrak{h}\,,

as well as the currents

(4.2.3)

\tilde{L}=g^{-1}\partial g\,,\quad\tilde{H}=\partial\Theta^{\prime}+\mu_{2}(g^{-1}\partial g,\Theta^{\prime})\,,

and

(4.2.4)

L=-\mathrm{d}^{\prime}g\,g^{-1}-\mu_{1}(\Theta^{\prime})\,,\quad H=g\rhd\left(\mathrm{d}^{\prime}\Theta-\tfrac{1}{2}[\Theta^{\prime},\Theta^{\prime}]\right)\,,

which can be written as

(4.2.5)

\displaystyle L\in\mathcal{A}^{1,0}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\,,\quad H\in\mathcal{A}^{2,0}\otimes\mathfrak{h}

(4.2.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}\in\mathcal{A}^{0,1}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\,,\quad\tilde{H}\in\mathcal{A}^{1,1}\otimes\mathfrak{h}\,.

We are now in a position to formulate a pair of conservation conditions for our three-dimensional theory in a form analogous to the 2d WZW case
\bar{\partial}J=0
and
\partial\bar{J}=0
.

The conservation equations for the currents
(\tilde{L},\tilde{H})
given in (
3.1.4
), associated with the right symmetry of (
4.2.1
), are given by

(4.2.7)

\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\tilde{L},\tilde{H})=({\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\tilde{L}-\mu_{1}(\tilde{H}),{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\tilde{H})=0\,,

so that
(\tilde{L},\tilde{H})\in H_{\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}^{(\bullet,1)}(M\otimes\mathfrak{G})
5
5

5

We refer the reader to appendix
A
for our conventions on cohomological notation.
. Note that
\mu_{1}
does not act on the second factor
\tilde{H}\in\mathcal{A}^{1,1}\otimes\mathfrak{h}
as it is already valued in
\mathfrak{h}
. Similarly, the differential constraints (
3.0.3
) satisfied by the symmetry transformation parameters corresponding to the right symmetry can be written as

(4.2.8)

\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\Gamma})=({\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\tilde{\alpha}+\mu_{1}(\tilde{\Gamma}),{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\tilde{\Gamma})=0\,,

so that
(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\Gamma})\in H^{(\bullet,0)}_{\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}(M\otimes\mathfrak{G})
.

Conversely, the conservation equations for the currents
(L,H)
given in (
3.0.2
) associated to the left symmetry of (
4.2.1
) are given by

(4.2.9)

\partial(L,H)=(\partial L,\partial H)=0\,,

so that
(L,H)\in H^{(\bullet,1)}_{\partial}(M\otimes\mathfrak{G})
, whereas the differential constraints (
3.0.5
) take the form

(4.2.10)

\partial(\alpha,\Gamma)=(\partial\alpha,\partial\Gamma)=0\,,

so that
(\alpha,\Gamma)\in H^{(\bullet,0)}_{\partial}(M\otimes\mathfrak{G})

It thus follows that the higher-currents of our 3d theory admit a natural decomposition, captured by the mixed de Rham–Dolbeault complex
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathfrak{G}
. Put differently, starting from the theory (
4.2.1
) and seeking to define a cohomology theory for the symmetry algebras would naturally lead to the very partition underlying the Raviolo construction. We shall call
H_{\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}^{(\bullet,\bullet)}
the

, and
H_{\partial}^{(\bullet,\bullet)}
the
anti-chiral sector
so that
(\tilde{L},\tilde{H})
are chiral whereas
(L,H)
are anti-chiral.

It is important to note that in this case, the chiral and anti-chiral sectors are not “symmetric” to one another. In particular, as we will see, only the chiral sector will give rise to the infinite dimensional symmetry algebra. Consequently, we will focus on the cohomology group

(4.2.11)

H_{\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}^{(\bullet,q)}(M\otimes\mathfrak{G})\,,

for
q=0,1
, with the objective of finding a mode expansion for both our higher current
(\tilde{L},\tilde{H})
and the gauge transformation parameters
(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\Gamma})
.

Remark 2

4.3.
Mode expansion of the currents

We have seen that the differential constraints for
(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\Gamma})
and the conservation equations for
(\tilde{L},\tilde{H})
are related to the cohomology group

(4.3.1)

H_{\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}^{(\bullet,q)}(M\otimes\mathfrak{G})\,,

with
q=0,1
. Our next goal is to obtain an explicit expression for this cohomology group.
We will proceed in two steps: first, we will describe

H^{(\bullet,q)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
following
[
21
]
,
and then we will apply the Künneth formula to compute the cohomology of
the tensor product complex.

Similarly than in the two-dimensional pure Dolbeault case, there is a linear isomorphism

(4.3.2)

{\mathrm{d}}z\wedge-:H^{(\bullet,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)\to H^{(\bullet,1)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)\,.

Hence, it suffices to find an explicit realization of
H^{(\bullet,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
, since elements in
H^{(\bullet,1)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
can be obtained by wedging elements of the former with
\mathrm{d}z
. The cohomology of
(\mathcal{A}^{\bullet,0},\mathrm{d}^{\prime})
was completely characterized in
[
21
]
and is concentrated in degrees
zero and one. The zeroth cohomology consists of polynomials in the holomorphic
variable
z
, that is,

(4.3.3)

H^{(0,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)=\mathbb{C}[z]\,.

Unlike in the Dolbeault complex, no negative powers of
z
appear here.
Consequently, any element
\tilde{\alpha}\in H^{(0,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
can be
expanded in modes as

(4.3.4)

\tilde{L}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{n}z^{n}\,.

The degree-one cohomology
H^{(1,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
is more subtle and
requires introducing additional structures. Since the negative powers of

z
that appear in the holomorphic setting are absent from

H^{(0,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
, the idea is that in the Raviolo framework,
the role of
z^{-1}
is played by a different object with analogous properties,
which appears in
H^{(1,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
. Concretely, this is given
by the
(1,0)
-form

(4.3.5)

\omega=\frac{\tau\,{\mathrm{d}}\bar{z}-2\bar{z}\,{\mathrm{d}}\tau}{(|z|^{2}+\tau^{2})^{3}}\in V^{\ast}_{{\mathcal{F}}}\,.

It is straightforward to verify that
{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\omega=0
; however, it is not

{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}
-exact and thus defines a non-trivial cohomology class. The key property
of
\omega
is an analogue of Cauchy’s residue theorem: its integral
over a two-sphere centered at the origin is given by

(4.3.6)

\oint_{S^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}z\wedge\omega=8\pi i\,.

Moreover, just as
z^{-1}
is the Green’s function for
\partial
on

\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, the
(1,0)
-form
\omega
serves as the
Green’s function for
{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
Consequently, they both act as propagators in field theories with the corresponding kinetic operators.

We can now use
\omega
to give an explicit description of

H^{(1,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
. First, we introduce the so-called degree-one

raviolo differential forms
, which are obtained by taking derivatives of

\omega
with respect to
z
. Specifically, we define

(4.3.7)

\Omega^{m}=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!}\partial_{z}^{m}\omega\,.

Each
\Omega^{m}
is a one-form, and it can be shown that they satisfy

(4.3.8)

z^{n}\Omega^{m}=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }n>m\\
\Omega^{m-n}&\text{for }n\leq m\end{cases}\,.

In other words, the raviolo differential forms serve a role analogous to that of negative modes in
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
, but in the form of
1-forms
with non-trivial degree. This is consistent with the expectation from Hartog’s theorem.

The degree-one cohomology is then given by
[
21
]

(4.3.9)

H^{(1,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\Omega^{0},\Omega^{1},\Omega^{2},\dots\}\,,

so that any element
\tilde{\Gamma}\in H^{(1,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
can be
expanded in modes as

(4.3.10)

\tilde{\Gamma}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tilde{\Gamma}_{m}\Omega^{m}\,.

We will denote the full cohomology by

(4.3.11)

\mathcal{K}^{\bullet}_{\text{poly}}:=H^{(\bullet,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)\,,

and refer to it as the space of polynomials on the raviolo. This space is the
analogue of the Laurent polynomials
\mathbb{C}[z,z^{-1}]
on

\mathbb{C}^{\times}
and provides the appropriate algebraic structure for
constructing the mode expansions, ultimately leading to the centrally extended affine graded Lie algebra.

Having found an explicit realization for
H^{(\bullet,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
, we can use the linear isomorphism in (
4.3.2
) to construct elements in
H^{(\bullet,1)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
. The existence of this isomorphism implies that any element
\tilde{L}\in H^{(0,1)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
can be written as
\tilde{L}=\tilde{L}_{z}\mathrm{d}z
for some
\tilde{L}_{z}\in H^{(0,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
and similarly with elements in
H^{(1,1)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
. With a slight abuse of notation we will write

(4.3.12)

\mathrm{d}z\wedge\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{\bullet}:=H^{(\bullet,1)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)\,.

With an explicit expression for both
H^{(\bullet,0)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
and
H^{(\bullet,1)}_{\mathrm{d}^{\prime}}(M)
at hand, we can proceed to determine the cohomology of the tensor product complex
H_{\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}^{(\bullet,q)}(M\otimes\mathfrak{G})
. To do so, we make use of the
Künneth formula
which relates the cohomology of the tensor product with the tensor product of the cohomologies

(4.3.13)

H_{\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}^{(0,q)}(M\otimes\mathfrak{G})=\bigoplus_{0=m+k}H^{(m,q)}_{{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}}(M)\otimes H^{k}_{\mu_{1}}(\mathfrak{G})\,,

where the ”correction” to the Künneth formula, which are the derived Ext-groups, do not appear here as both of our complexes
{\mathcal{A}},\mathfrak{G}
are free.

Since
\mathfrak{G}
is concentrated in degrees
-1
and
0
, its cohomology will also be concentrated in these degrees. Let
H^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{G})=V\oplus\mathfrak{n}

denote the cohomology of the Lie 2-algebra
\mathfrak{G}
, with

(4.3.14)

V=\ker\mu_{1}=H^{-1}(\mathfrak{G}),\qquad\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{g}/\mathrm{im}(\mu_{1})=H^{0}(\mathfrak{G})\,,

where
V
is an Abelian
\mathfrak{n}
-module. By a theorem of Gerstenhaber, the cohomology
H^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{G})
is part of the data which characterizes Lie 2-algebras
\mathfrak{G}
up to equivalence
[
41
]
. In the special case where
\mu_{1}=0
, namely when
\mathfrak{G}
is called
skeletal
, then
\mathfrak{G}=H^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{G})
is its own cohomology. On the other hand, if
\mu_{1}=\mathbb{1}
is the identity, then
H^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{G})=0
is trivial.

We then have the following theorem.

4.3.1 Theorem.

The symmetry transformation parameters
(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\Gamma})
corresponding to the right symmetry of the action (
4.2.1

5.
The Poisson Brackets

With the mode expansions of the currents and transformation parameters at hand, the idea is to proceed exactly as in
Proposition

2.0.1
to compute the symmetry algebra of the three-dimensional theory. Let us briefly recall how that argument was carried out. We defined the smeared Noether charges
Q_{\alpha}=\langle\alpha,J\rangle
, and used the relation
\{Q_{\alpha},Q_{\alpha^{\prime}}\}_{\mathrm{P.B.}}=\delta_{\alpha}Q_{\alpha^{\prime}}
to compute their Poisson brackets. To isolate the brackets between individual modes, we expanded both the currents and the transformation parameters in Laurent series and applied Cauchy’s residue theorem to extract the mode algebra by integrating out the
z
-dependence. The resulting Poisson brackets between the modes
J_{n}
precisely matched those of the Kac–Moody algebra.

In the present case, the currents and transformation parameters are valued in the cohomology
H^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{G})=V\oplus\mathfrak{n}
, where, recall,

(5.0.1)

V=\ker(\mu_{1})\subset\mathfrak{h}\,,\qquad\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{g}/\operatorname{im}(\mu_{1})\,.

To write expressions like
\langle\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{H}\rangle
or
[\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\alpha}^{\prime}]
, as in the two-dimensional case, it is necessary to verify that these operations descend to cohomology. We include a proof of this fact for completeness; readers who are happy to take it as given may proceed directly to the Poisson bracket computation.

5.0.1 Proposition.

The following properties hold

(1)

The induced graded brackets on
V\oplus\mathfrak{n}
satisfy the graded Jacobi identity.

(2)

The non-degenerate bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{h}\to{\mathbb{C}}
induces a non-degenerate bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathfrak{n}\times V\to{\mathbb{C}}
.

Note that the first property guarantees that
\mathfrak{n}
inherits a Lie bracket from
\mathfrak{g}
, and that
\mu_{2}
induces a well-defined derivation on
V
.

Proof.

There are natural projection and inclusion maps

p=(p_{-1},p_{0}):\mathfrak{G}\to H^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{G}),\qquad\iota=(\iota_{-1},\iota_{0}):H^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{G})\to\mathfrak{G},

which satisfy
p\circ\iota=\mathbb{1}_{H^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{G})}
. The equation
\iota\circ p\simeq\mathbb{1}_{\mathfrak{G}}
, on the other hand, only holds
up to homotopy

[
42
]
(see also
[
43
]
).

The latter condition implies that there exists a linear map
h:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}
, called the
chain homotopy
, which satisfies

(5.0.2)

h\circ\mu_{1}=\iota_{-1}\circ p_{-1}-\mathrm{id}_{\mathfrak{h}}\,,\qquad\mu_{1}\circ h=\iota_{0}\circ p_{0}-\mathrm{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}\,.

To prove (1) we can use this chain homotopy to show that the canonically induced Lie bracket on
\mathfrak{n}
and the induced action on
V

(5.0.3)

[\cdot,\cdot]^{\prime}=p_{0}\circ[\cdot,\cdot]\circ(\iota_{0}\otimes\iota_{0})\qquad\mu_{2}^{\prime}=p_{-1}\circ\mu_{2}\circ(\iota_{0}\otimes\iota_{-1})

satisfy the graded Jacobi identity.

Indeed, to see this, we compute for each
\mathsf{x},\mathsf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{n}
and
\mathsf{y}\in V
that

\begin{split}\mu_{2}^{\prime}(\mathsf{x},\mu_{2}^{\prime}(\mathsf{x}^{\prime},\mathsf{y}))&=p_{-1}\Big(\mu_{2}\big(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x},(\iota_{-1}p_{-1})\mu_{2}(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}^{\prime},\iota_{-1}\mathsf{y})\big)\Big)\\
&=p_{-1}\Big(\mu_{2}\big(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x},h\mu_{1}\mu_{2}(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}^{\prime},\iota_{-1}\mathsf{y})\big)\Big)+p_{-1}\Big(\mu_{2}\big(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x},\mu_{2}(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}^{\prime},\iota_{-1}\mathsf{y})\big)\Big)\\
&=p_{-1}\Big(\mu_{2}\big(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x},h[\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}^{\prime},\mu_{1}\iota_{-1}\mathsf{y}]\big)\Big)+p_{-1}\Big(\mu_{2}\big(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x},\mu_{2}(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}^{\prime},\iota_{-1}\mathsf{y})\big)\Big)\\
&=0+p_{-1}\Big(\mu_{2}\big(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x},\mu_{2}(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}^{\prime},\iota_{-1}\mathsf{y})\big)\Big)\,,\end{split}

where we have used the identity
\mu_{1}\mu_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)=[\cdot,\mu_{1}\cdot]
, and the first term vanishes in the last line due to the definition of
\iota_{-1}:V=\operatorname{ker}(\mu_{1})\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
.

Similarly, we compute for
\mathsf{x}_{1},\mathsf{x}_{2},\mathsf{x}_{3}\in\mathfrak{n}
,

\begin{split}[\mathsf{x}_{1},[\mathsf{x}_{2},\mathsf{x}_{3}]^{\prime}]^{\prime}&=p_{0}\Big([\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{1},\iota_{0}p_{0}\big([\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{2},\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{3}]\big)]\Big)\\
&=p_{0}\Big([\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{1},\mu_{1}h\big([\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{2},\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{3}]\big)]\Big)-p_{0}\Big([\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{1},\big([\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{2},\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{3}]\big)]\Big)\\
&=p_{0}\Big(\mu_{1}\big(\mu_{2}(\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{1},h[\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{2},\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{3}])\big)\Big)-p_{0}\Big([\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{1},\big([\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{2},\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{3}]\big)]\Big)\\
&=0-p_{0}\Big([\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{1},\big([\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{2},\iota_{0}\mathsf{x}_{3}]\big)]\Big)\,,\end{split}

where the first term vanishes in the last line due to the definition
p_{0}:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{g}/\operatorname{im}(\mu_{1})
. By summing over cyclic permutations, the remaining quantities in both of the above equations vanish by the graded Jacobi identity satisfied by
\mathfrak{G}
. Thus, property (1) holds.

To prove (2), we must show that the induced bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is both well defined and non-degenerate. First, we verify that it is independent of the choice of representatives. Let
\mathsf{x},\mathsf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{g}
be representatives of the same class in
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{g}/\mathrm{im}(\mu_{1})
, so that
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}=\mathsf{x}+\mu_{1}(\theta)
for some
\theta\in\mathfrak{h}
. Then, for any
\mathsf{y}\in V=\ker(\mu_{1})
, we have

(5.0.4)

\langle\mathsf{x}^{\prime},\mathsf{y}\rangle=\langle\mathsf{x}+\mu_{1}(\theta),\mathsf{y}\rangle=\langle\mathsf{x},\mathsf{y}\rangle+\langle\mu_{1}(\theta),\mathsf{y}\rangle\,.

The second term vanishes due to the compatibility of the bilinear form with
\mu_{1}
, namely,

(5.0.5)

\langle\mu_{1}(\theta),\mathsf{y}\rangle=\langle\theta,\mu_{1}(\mathsf{y})\rangle=0\,,

since
\mathsf{y}\in\ker(\mu_{1})
. Therefore, the pairing is well defined on cohomology classes.

To show that the induced form is non-degenerate, we apply the rank-nullity theorem. Since
\mu_{1}:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{g}
is linear, we have:

(5.0.6)

\displaystyle\dim\ker(\mu_{1})+\dim\mathrm{im}(\mu_{1})

\displaystyle=\dim\mathfrak{h}\,,

(5.0.7)

\displaystyle\dim\mathrm{coker}(\mu_{1})+\dim\mathrm{im}(\mu_{1})

\displaystyle=\dim\mathfrak{g}\,.

Since the original bilinear form is invertible we have that
\dim\mathfrak{h}=\dim\mathfrak{g}
so that subtracting these equations gives

(5.0.8)

\dim V=\dim\ker(\mu_{1})=\dim\mathrm{coker}(\mu_{1})=\dim\mathfrak{n}\,,

which implies that the restriction of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to
V\oplus\mathfrak{n}
is non-degenerate.

∎

6.
The Centrally Extended Affine Graded Lie Algebra

In the previous section, we computed the Poisson brackets of the currents associated with the infinite-dimensional symmetry of the three-dimensional action (
4.2.1
). In this section, we show that these commutation relations define a centrally extended affine graded Lie algebra, thereby completing the picture and demonstrating that the symmetry of the three-dimensional theory is indeed captured by a centrally extended affine graded Lie algebra.

The Kac-Moody Algebra

We begin by recalling the analogous construction in two dimensions, where the Poisson brackets of the WZW model give rise to a centrally extended affine Lie algebra. This infinite-dimensional algebra, known as the Kac–Moody algebra at level
k
, is defined as

(6.0.1)

\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{k}:=\mathfrak{g}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}[z,z^{-1}]\oplus k\mathbb{C}\,,

with Lie bracket

(6.0.2)

[X\otimes f(z),Y\otimes g(z)]=[X,Y]\otimes(fg)(z)+k\langle X,Y\rangle\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}(f\partial_{z}g)\,,

for
X\otimes f(z),Y\otimes g(z)\in\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{k}
. The second term defines a nontrivial
2
-cocycle,

(6.0.3)

\omega(X\otimes f(z),Y\otimes g(z)):=k\langle X,Y\rangle\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}(f\partial_{z}g)\,,

which satisfies the 2-cocycle condition

(6.0.4)

\omega\big([X\otimes f,Y\otimes g],Z\otimes h\big)+\omega\big([Y\otimes g,Z\otimes h],X\otimes f\big)\\
+\omega\big([Z\otimes h,X\otimes f],Y\otimes g\big)=0\,,

for arbitrary elements
X\otimes f,Y\otimes g,Z\otimes h\in\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{k}
. This relation holds provided that the bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathfrak{g}
is invariant. The cocycle ensures that the central extension is compatible with the Lie algebra structure, guaranteeing that
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{k}
satisfies the Jacobi identity and is thus a well-defined Lie algebra.

The identification between this realization of the Kac–Moody algebra and the algebra generated by the modes of the symmetry currents in the WZW model is given by

(6.0.5)

J_{n}^{a}\mapsto t_{a}\otimes z^{n}\in\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathbb{C}[z,z^{-1}]\,,

where
\{t_{a}\}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{g}
. A direct computation then shows that the Poisson brackets of the currents in the WZW model

(6.0.6)

\{J_{n}^{a},J_{m}^{b}\}=f_{c}^{ab}J_{n+m}^{c}+k\,\kappa^{ab}\delta_{n+m,0}

match the Lie bracket (
6.0.2
) under the identification (
6.0.5
).

6.1.
The Affine Graded Lie Algebra

The affine graded Lie algebra is constructed in close analogy with its classical counterpart, ensuring that its commutation relations reproduce the Poisson brackets of our three-dimensional theory.

, using the non-degenerate (invertible) bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, as follows:

(6.1.2)

t^{a}=(\kappa^{-1})^{ab}\langle-,s_{b}\rangle\,,\qquad s^{a}=(\kappa^{-1})^{ab}\langle t_{a},-\rangle\,.

By construction, these satisfy the duality conditions:

(6.1.3)

t^{a}(t_{b})=\delta^{a}_{b}\,,\qquad s^{a}(s_{b})=\delta^{a}_{b}\,.

We endow the graded vector space
\mathfrak{G}^{\vee}=\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\oplus V^{\vee}
with the following brackets:

(6.1.4)

[t^{a},t^{b}]^{\vee}=\tilde{f}^{ab}_{c}\,t^{c}\in V^{\vee},\qquad\mu_{2}^{\vee}(t^{a},s^{b})=(\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{ab}_{c}\,s^{c}\in\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\,,

where the structure constants are defined by

(6.1.5)

\tilde{f}^{ab}_{c}=(\kappa^{-1})^{ax}(\kappa^{-1})^{by}f_{xy}^{z}\kappa_{zc},\qquad(\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{ab}_{c}=(\kappa^{-1})^{ax}\kappa_{cz}(\mu_{2})_{xy}^{z}(\kappa^{-1})^{yb}\,,

as in equation (
5.1.17
). We assign degree 0 to
V^{\vee}
and degree
-1
to
\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}
. With these conventions, we have the following:

6.1.1 Proposition.

The brackets defined in (
6.1.4
) satisfy the graded Jacobi identity. Hence, they endow
\mathfrak{G}^{\vee}
with the structure of a graded Lie algebra.

Proof.

We consider

(6.1.6)

\begin{split}[t^{a},[t^{b},t^{c}]^{\vee}]^{\vee}&=\tilde{f}^{ad}_{e}\tilde{f}^{bc}_{d}t^{e}\\
&=(\kappa^{-1})^{ar}(\kappa^{-1})^{bs}(\kappa^{-1})^{ct}\big(f_{ry}^{z}f_{st}^{y}\big)\kappa_{ze}t^{e}\,,\end{split}

where we used the definition of the dual structure constants given in (
6.1.5
). Summing the three terms that appear in the graded Jacobi identity, we obtain

(6.1.7)

[t^{a},[t^{b},t^{c}]^{\vee}]^{\vee}+[t^{c},[t^{a},t^{b}]^{\vee}]^{\vee}+[t^{b},[t^{c},t^{a}]^{\vee}]^{\vee}=\\
(\kappa^{-1})^{ar}(\kappa^{-1})^{bs}(\kappa^{-1})^{ct}\big(f_{ry}^{z}f_{st}^{y}+f_{ty}^{z}f_{rs}^{y}+f_{sy}^{z}f_{tr}^{y}\big)\kappa_{ze}t^{e}=0

The expression in parentheses vanishes by the Jacobi identity for the Lie bracket of
\mathfrak{n}
, whose structure constants are given by
f_{ab}^{c}
. Next, for the mixed brackets involving both
t^{a}
and
s^{b}
, we compute:

(6.1.8)

\begin{split}\mu_{2}^{\vee}(t^{a},\mu_{2}^{\vee}(t^{b},s^{c}))&=(\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{ad}_{e}(\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{bc}_{d}s^{e}\\
&=\kappa_{ez}\big((\mu_{2})_{xy}^{z}(\mu_{2})_{pq}^{y}\big)(\kappa^{-1})^{ax}(\kappa^{-1})^{bp}(\kappa^{-1})^{qc}s^{e}\,.\end{split}

As in the previous case, this vanishes due to the fact that
\mu_{2}
descends to a derivation on
V
, as shown in Proposition
5.0.1
.
∎

With this at hand, we define the higher affine graded Lie algebra
\hat{\mathfrak{G}}
as

(6.1.9)

\hat{\mathfrak{G}}=\left(V^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{0}\right)\oplus\left(\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{1}\right),

where
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{0}={\mathbb{C}}[z]
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{1}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\Omega^{0},\Omega^{1},\dots\}
denote the two graded components of the raviolo polynomials (
4.3.11
). The bracket on
\hat{\mathfrak{G}}
is defined as follows:
For
X\otimes f(z),Y\otimes g(z)\in V^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{0}
, we set

(6.1.10)

[X\otimes f(z),Y\otimes g(z)]=[X,Y]^{\vee}\otimes f(z)g(z)\in V^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{0}\,.

For
X\otimes f(z)\in V^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{0}
and
A\otimes k(\Omega)\in\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{1}
, the bracket is

(6.1.11)

[X\otimes f(z),A\otimes k(\Omega)]=\mu_{2}^{\vee}(X,A)\otimes f(z)k(\Omega)\in\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{1}\,.

Finally, for two elements
A\otimes k(\Omega),B\otimes\ell(\Omega)\in\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{1}
, the bracket vanishes:

(6.1.12)

[A\otimes k(\Omega),B\otimes\ell(\Omega)]=0\,.

This bracket structure is consistent with the relations

(6.1.13)

z^{n}\Omega^{m}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }n>m\,,\\
\Omega^{m-n}&\text{if }n\leq m\,,\end{cases}\qquad\Omega^{n}\Omega^{m}=0\,.

With this definition at hand, we can show that the graded Lie brackets reproduce the Poisson brackets of the higher currents, up to the central term. Specifically, consider the bijective graded linear map

(6.1.14)

H_{n}^{a}\mapsto t^{a}\otimes z^{n}\,,\qquad L_{m}^{a}\mapsto s^{a}\otimes\Omega^{m}\,,

which identifies the modes of the currents with the generators of the affine graded Lie algebra. Using the relations in equation (
6.1.13
), one readily verifies that the non-central contributions to the Poisson brackets in equations (
5.1.15
) and (
5.1.16
) coincide precisely with the graded Lie bracket on
\hat{\mathfrak{G}}
.

6.1.1.
The Central Extension

The final ingredient in the construction is the central extension. In the case of the Kac–Moody algebra, the central term arises from the
2
-cocycle (
6.0.3
), built from the invariant bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}
and the residue pairing defined by integration over
S^{1}
. In our setting, a similar construction yields a central extension of the affine graded Lie algebra
\hat{\mathfrak{G}}
. To define the corresponding
2
-cocycle, we must first introduce the appropriate invariant bilinear form and an analogue of the residue pairing.

For the residue pairing, we will use the integration along
S^{2}
introduced in the previous sections which satisfies

(6.1.15)

\frac{1}{8\pi i}\oint_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}z\wedge z^{n}\Omega^{m}=\delta_{n,m}\,.

With this, we can define a residue pairing as a map

\operatorname{Res}:\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{\bullet}\to\mathbb{C}[-1]

which acts on the raviolo polynomial generators as

(6.1.16)

\operatorname{Res}(z^{n})=0\,,\quad\operatorname{Res}(z^{n}\Omega^{m})=\frac{1}{8\pi i}\oint_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}z\wedge z^{n}\Omega^{m}=\delta_{n,m}\,.

The notation
{\mathbb{C}}[-1]
reflects the fact that we are working with chain complexes. Concretely, it indicates that
\operatorname{Res}
vanishes on
(0,0)
-forms, while its action on
(1,0)
-forms yields values in
{\mathbb{C}}
, as explicitly shown in (
6.1.16
).

On the other hand, to define a bilinear form on
\mathfrak{G}^{\vee}=\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\oplus V^{\vee}
, we make use of the bilinear pairing on
H^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{G})=V\oplus\mathfrak{n}
, which we established to be non-degenerate and invariant in
Proposition

5.0.1
. Recall that, for a basis
\{t_{a}\}
of
\mathfrak{n}
and
\{s_{b}\}
of
V
, this pairing is defined by

\langle t_{a},s_{b}\rangle=\kappa_{ab}
where
\kappa_{ab}
is an invertible matrix due to the non-degeneracy of the form. Taking the basis
\{s^{a}\}
of
\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}
and
\{t^{b}\}
of
V^{\vee}
we define the bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{\vee}:V^{\vee}\times\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\to{\mathbb{C}}
by

(6.1.17)

\langle t^{a},s^{b}\rangle^{\vee}=\big(\kappa^{-1}\big)^{ab}\,.

In the Kac–Moody case, the fact that
\omega
defines a 2-cocycle relies crucially on the invariance of the bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}
. The same principle applies here, so we now proceed to show that our bilinear pairing satisfies the required invariance condition.

6.1.2 Proposition.

The pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{\vee}:V^{\vee}\times\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is invariant.

Proof.

We aim to show that the bilinear pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{\vee}
on
\hat{\mathfrak{G}}
is invariant under the dual Lie bracket structure, specifically that

(6.1.18)

\langle[t^{a},t^{b}]^{\vee},s^{c}\rangle^{\vee}=\langle t^{a},\mu_{2}^{\vee}(t^{b},s^{c})\rangle^{\vee}\,.

Using the explicit expressions for the dual brackets from (
6.1.4
) and the dual bilinear form from (
6.1.17
), this identity becomes

(6.1.19)

(\kappa^{-1})^{dc}\,\tilde{f}^{ab}_{d}=(\kappa^{-1})^{ad}\,(\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{bc}_{d}\,.

Substituting the expressions for the dual structure constants in terms of the original ones as in (
6.1.5
), we find the condition

(6.1.20)

(\kappa^{-1})^{ax}(\kappa^{-1})^{by}f_{xy}^{c}=(\kappa^{-1})^{bx}(\mu_{2})^{a}_{xy}(\kappa^{-1})^{yc}\,.

Now, recall that the invariance of the original bilinear pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathfrak{n}\times V\to\mathbb{C}
implies

(6.1.21)

\langle[t_{a},t_{b}],s_{c}\rangle=\langle t_{a},\mu_{2}(t_{b},s_{c})\rangle\,.

Writing this in components we obtain

(6.1.22)

f_{ab}^{d}\,\kappa_{dc}=\kappa_{ad}\,(\mu_{2})_{bc}^{d}\,,

which is precisely equivalent to (
6.1.20
) upon multiplying both sides by the inverse of
\kappa
. This completes the proof.

∎

With both the residue pairing and the bilinear form in place, we are now ready to define the
2
-cocycle that determines the central extension of the affine graded Lie algebra. We consider, for
X\otimes f(z)\in V^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{0}
and
A\otimes k(\Omega)\in\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{1}

(6.1.23)

\mathfrak{w}(X\otimes f(z),A\otimes k(\Omega))=\langle X,A\rangle^{\vee}\,\operatorname{Res}\left(\partial_{z}f(z)\,k(\Omega)\right),

and extend it to be zero on pairs of elements both in
V^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{0}
and in
\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{1}
. Let us show that
\mathfrak{w}
is indeed a
2
-cocycle

6.1.3 Proposition.

The bilinear map
\mathfrak{w}:\hat{\mathfrak{G}}\times\hat{\mathfrak{G}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
determines a
graded Lie algebra 2-cocycle
.

Proof.

To verify that
\mathfrak{w}
defines a graded Lie algebra
2
-cocycle, we must check that

(6.1.24)

\mathfrak{w}\big([t^{a}\otimes z^{n},\,t^{b}\otimes z^{m}]^{\vee},\,s^{c}\otimes\Omega^{k}\big)+\mathfrak{w}\big(\mu_{2}^{\vee}(s^{c}\otimes\Omega^{k},\,t^{a}\otimes z^{n}),\,t^{b}\otimes z^{m}\big)\\
+\mathfrak{w}\big(\mu_{2}^{\vee}(t^{b}\otimes z^{m},\,s^{c}\otimes\Omega^{k}),\,t^{a}\otimes z^{n}\big)=0\,.

Using the structure constants and the identification
z^{n}\Omega^{m}=\Omega^{m-n}
, this becomes

(6.1.25)

\mathfrak{w}\big(\tilde{f}^{ab}_{d}\,t^{d}\otimes z^{n+m},\,s^{c}\otimes\Omega^{k}\big)+\mathfrak{w}\big((\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{ca}_{d}\,s^{d}\otimes\Omega^{k-n},\,t^{b}\otimes z^{m}\big)\\
+\mathfrak{w}\big((\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{bc}_{d}\,s^{d}\otimes\Omega^{k-m},\,t^{a}\otimes z^{n}\big)\,.

Applying the definition of
\mathfrak{w}
and the identity
\partial_{z}\Omega^{m}=-(m+1)\Omega^{m+1}
, we find

(6.1.26)

\tilde{f}^{ab}_{d}\,(\kappa^{-1})^{dc}(n+m)\,\delta_{n+m-1,k}-(\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{ca}_{d}\,(\kappa^{-1})^{bd}(k-n+1)\,\delta_{m,k-n+1}\\
-(\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{bc}_{d}\,(\kappa^{-1})^{da}(k-m+1)\,\delta_{n,k-m+1}\,.

Next, we use the invariance of the bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{\vee}
, which implies

(6.1.27)

\tilde{f}^{ab}_{d}\,(\kappa^{-1})^{dc}=(\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{bc}_{d}\,(\kappa^{-1})^{da}=(\tilde{\mu}_{2})^{ca}_{d}\,(\kappa^{-1})^{bd}\,.

Substituting this into the previous expression, we obtain

(6.1.28)

\tilde{f}^{ab}_{d}\,(\kappa^{-1})^{dc}\left[(n+m)\,\delta_{n+m-1,k}-(k-n+1)\,\delta_{m,k-n+1}-(k-m+1)\,\delta_{n,k-m+1}\right]\\
=2\,\tilde{f}^{ab}_{d}\,(\kappa^{-1})^{dc}\,(n+m-k-1)\,\delta_{n+m-k-1,0}=0\,,

which proves the 2-cocycle condition.
∎

In particular, it is straightforward to verify that this
2
-cocycle precisely reproduces the central term that appears in the Poisson brackets of the higher currents. Consider

(6.1.29)

\mathfrak{w}(t^{a}\otimes z^{n},s^{b}\otimes\Omega^{m})=\langle t^{a},s^{b}\rangle^{\vee}\mathrm{Res}(nz^{n-1}\Omega^{m})=n\big(\kappa^{-1}\big)^{ab}\delta_{n-1,m}\,.

Using the identification (
6.1.14
), we see that this expression precisely matches the central term in the Poisson brackets given in (
5.1.16
). We have thus shown

6.1.4 Theorem.

The Poisson algebra of the currents of the 3d theory (
4.2.1
) defines a centrally extended affine Lie algebra
\hat{\mathfrak{G}}_{\mathfrak{w}}
fitting into the central extension sequence

(6.1.30)

0\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}[-1]\rightarrow\hat{\mathfrak{G}}_{\mathfrak{w}}\rightarrow\hat{\mathfrak{G}}\rightarrow 0\,,

where the graded Lie bracket is given defined as follows:
For
X\otimes f(z),Y\otimes g(z)\in V^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{0}
, we have

(6.1.31)

[X\otimes f(z),Y\otimes g(z)]=[X,Y]^{\vee}\otimes f(z)g(z)\,.

For
X\otimes f(z)\in V^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{0}
and
A\otimes k(\Omega)\in\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{1}
, the bracket is

(6.1.32)

[X\otimes f(z),A\otimes k(\Omega)]=\mu_{2}^{\vee}(X,A)\otimes f(z)k(\Omega)+\langle X,A\rangle^{\vee}\mathrm{Res}(\partial_{z}f(z)k\Omega)\,.

Finally, for two elements
A\otimes k(\Omega),B\otimes\ell(\Omega)\in\mathfrak{n}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{1}
, the bracket vanishes:

(6.1.33)

[A\otimes k(\Omega),B\otimes\ell(\Omega)]=0\,.

Proof.

The proof follows from the identification

(6.1.34)

{\tilde{H}}_{n}^{a}\mapsto t^{a}\otimes z^{n}\,,\quad{\tilde{L}}_{m}^{a}\mapsto s^{a}\otimes\Omega^{m}\,.

∎

This is the main result of the article: it establishes that the symmetry algebra of the three-dimensional theory (
4.2.1
) is infinite-dimensional and fits naturally within the framework of Raviolo vertex algebras. In forthcoming work
[
44
]
, we will explore the associated Raviolo vertex algebra in detail, including its operator product expansions and other quantum features of the theory.
