Affine Equivalence of Subsets of 𝔽 2 n \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} via Venn Diagrams and Applications to Sidon Sets
Kariane Calta, Sarah Covey, Timothy E. Goldberg, Lauren L. Rose, Daniel Rose-Levine

Abstract
. Two subsets S S and T T of 𝔽 2 n \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} are affinely equivalent if there is an affine automorphism of 𝔽 2 n \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} taking S S to T T . Given a basis of the affine span of S S , we can construct a Venn diagram whose regions partition S S . We prove that any two bases of aff ⁡ ( S ) \aff(S) will have the same Venn diagram up to a linear permutation of the Venn regions. Moreover, we prove that two sets are affinely equivalent if and only if there is a cardinality-preserving linear permutation from the Venn regions of S S to the Venn regions of T T . We use these results to classify certain Sidon sets up to affine equivalence.



1.
Introduction

The work described in this paper was inspired by studying the card game
Quads
, created by Rose and Pereira as
SuperSET
in 2013,
[
9
]
, and published by the Association for Women in Mathematics as
EvenQuads
,
[
14
]
.
By coding the 64 cards as binary vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{6}
, the game can be viewed as a model for
\operatorname{AG}(6,2)
, the finite affine geometry of order
2
and dimension
6
. This can be expanded to a game with
2^{n}
cards for any positive
n
, which corresponds to
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and models
\operatorname{AG}(n,2)
. The goal of the game is to identify four cards fitting a particular pattern, which corresponds to four vectors
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that
\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{b}+\boldsymbol{c}+\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{0}
. A set of four such cards or vectors is called a
quad
, and the quads are exactly the affine planes in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. For a detailed description, see
[
4
]
.

Of particular interest are quad-free collections of cards (or vectors), called
quad caps
,
Sidon sets
, or
2-caps
(
[
1
]
). Sidon sets have many applications, for example in cryptography for designing secure systems, in coding theory for constructing error-correcting codes, and in signal processing for creating efficient frequency-hopping sequences. See for example,
[
13
]
,
[
8
]
, and
[
6
]
. An active research area in cryptography and coding theory is the study and construction of maximal Sidon sets in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and in particular determining the maximum size of a Sidon set in each dimension. This is the analog of the famous
Cap Set problem
for the card game SET,
which remains unsolved in dimensions
n\geq 7
. Although asymptotic bounds are known for maximum size Sidon sets,
[
12
]
, explicit constructions of all possible caps are only known for
n\leq 7
,
[
2
]
, and exact maximum sizes are unknown for dimensions
n>10
. A subsidiary question is to determine when two Sidon sets of the same size are affinely equivalent. Recent papers on this and related topics include
[
5
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
3
]
, and
[
11
]
.

For the remainder of this paper, we will refer to Sidon sets in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
as
quad caps
or just
caps
.
Although each quad cap has a well-defined cardinality and dimension, these two numbers do not characterize the set, even up to affine equivalence. Quad caps generally do not have any apparent algebraic or geometric structure, and so determining when they are affinely equivalent can be challenging. In this paper, we develop a
Venn diagram method
for determining whether any two subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, not just quad caps, are affinely equivalent.

In order to do this, we make use of the fact that the power set
\mathcal{P}(S)
of a set
S
is a vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
under the operation of symmetric difference of sets. In Section
2
, we recall the key ideas from affine linear algebra over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, and define the
even zero-sum sets
of
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, which form a linear subspace
E(S)
of
\mathcal{P}(S)
. This subspace captures the affine geometric structure of
S
, and a bijection between subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is an affine equivalence if and only if it induces a bijection of the even zero-sums sets, (Theorem
5.6
).

In Section
3
, we examine linear subspaces
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(S)
in general, and define a partition of
S
into
Venn regions
, which are the connected components of the Venn diagram of sets in
\mathcal{V}
. The collection
\venn(S,\mathcal{V})
of these Venn regions is also a vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
which encodes information about
\mathcal{W}
. In Section
4
, we define isomorphisms between pairs
(S,\mathcal{V})
and
(T,\mathcal{W})
, where
S,T\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(S)
are linear subspaces. In particular, we show that these pairs are isomorphic if and only if there is a cardinality-preserving, linear bijection
\venn(S,\mathcal{V})\to\venn(T,\mathcal{W})
.

In Section
5
, we study
E(S)
in more depth, and apply the results of previous sections to obtain our main theorem, that subsets
S,T\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
are affinely equivalent if and only if there is a cardinality-preserving, linear bijection
\venn(S,E(S))\to\venn(T,E(T))
.

In the remaining sections of the paper, we apply the tools developed in Sections 3–5 to study quad caps. In Section
6
we obtain a complete characterization of caps of size
k
and dimension
d=k-3
. In Section
7
, we consider the templates introduced in
[
2
]
for
7
-dimensional caps of sizes 10–12, and verify which templates are affinely equivalent using the Venn diagram method.

2.
Affine linear algebra in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

The set
\AG(n,q)
is the finite affine geometry of dimension
n
and order
q
, where
q
is a power of a prime. A standard model for
\AG(n,q)
consists of vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, where
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the field of order
q
. In this model the affine subspaces (called
flats
) are just translates of linear subspaces. A detailed description of the geometry of
\AG(n,q)
, and in particular
\AG(n,2)
, can be found in
[
4
]
. In particular, we assume the reader is familiar with the definitions and basic properties of affine combination, affine independence and dependence, affine span, affine subspace (also called a
flat
), affine basis and dimension, affine map, and affine isomorphism and automorphism.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
V
be a vector space over a field
\mathbb{F}
, and let
S\subseteq V
. A
basis
for
S
is a basis for the flat
\aff(S)
, and the dimension of
S
is
\dim(S)=\dim\aff(S)
.
Subsets
S,T\subseteq V
are
affinely equivalent
if there is an affine automorphism
f
of
V
such that
f(S)=T
, or equivalently if there is a bijection
{g}\colon{S}\to{T}
that can be extended to an affine automorphism of
V
.

For vector spaces over the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}=\{0,1\}
, the standard definitions take on a somewhat peculiar character. In particular, the affine combinations are precisely sums of an odd number of elements, and so
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is affinely dependent if and only if there is a nonempty even subset of
S
whose elements sum to
\boldsymbol{0}
. Moroever, if
F\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a flat of dimension
d
, then
\lvert{F}\rvert=2^{d+1}
.

Inspired by this characterization of affinely dependent sets, we make the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

A subset
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}\}
of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a
zero-sum set
if

\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{x}_{l}=\boldsymbol{0}.

An
even zero-sum set
is a zero-sum set of even cardinality. We define the empty set
\varnothing
to be an even zero-sum set. We denote the collection of even zero-sum subsets of a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
by
E(S)
.

Note that we are not concerned with odd zero-sum sets, because those do not pertain to the affine geometric structure of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Throughout the remainder of this paper, we will regularly write a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
as the disjoint union
S=B\sqcup D
, where
B
is a basis for
S
and
D=S-B
.

Recall that any map
{f}\colon{X}\to{Y}
between sets induces a map
{f_{\ast}}\colon{\mathcal{P}(X)}\to{\mathcal{P}(Y)}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

A function
{f}\colon{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}}
is affine if and only if
f_{\ast}(E(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}))\subseteq E(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m})
.

Proof.

Let
{f}\colon{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}}
. Note that
f_{\ast}(\varnothing)=\varnothing\in E(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m})
. By definition
f
is affine if and only if it preserves affine combinations. Recall that the affine combinations in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
are precisely the sums of an odd number of points.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2\ell+1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be distinct, and let
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{x}_{2\ell+1}
. Observe that
X=\{\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2\ell+1}\}\in E(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})
.
Then

\begin{array}[]{l}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{x}_{2\ell+1})=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+\cdots+f(\boldsymbol{x}_{2\ell+1})\\[4.30554pt]
\phantom{mmm}\iff f(\boldsymbol{y})=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+\cdots+f(\boldsymbol{x}_{2\ell+1})\\[4.30554pt]
\phantom{mmm}\iff f_{\ast}(X)=\left\{f(\boldsymbol{y}),f(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}),\ldots,f(\boldsymbol{x}_{2\ell+1})\right\}\in E(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}).\end{array}

Since every nonempty element of
E(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})
corresponds to an affine combination, this proves the proposition.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

Two sets
S,T\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
are affinely equivalent if and only if there is a bijection
{g}\colon{S}\to{T}
such that
g_{\ast}|_{E(S)}
is a bijection onto
E(T)
.

Proof.

Let
S,T\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

Suppose
S
and
T
are affinely equivalent. Then there is an affine automorphism
f
of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that
f(S)=T
. Define
{g}\colon{S}\to{T}
by setting
g=f|_{S}
. Since
f
is injective, we know
g
is also injective, and because
g(S)=f(S)=T
we also know
g
is surjective. Hence
g
is bijective. Note that
g^{-1}=(f^{-1})|_{T}
.

By Proposition
2.5
, we know that
f
and
f^{-1}
preserve even zero-sums, and hence so do
g
and
g^{-1}
. Therefore
g_{\ast}|_{E(S)}
is bijective onto
E(T)
.

Suppose there is a bijective function
{g}\colon{S}\to{T}
such that
g_{\ast}|_{E(S)}
is a bijection onto
E(T)
. Let
B=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}\}\subseteq S
be a basis for
S
. We will show that
g_{\ast}(B)\subseteq T
is affinely independent.

Let
X
be an even zero-sum subset of
g_{\ast}(B)
. By assumption we have
g_{\ast}^{-1}(X)\in E(S)
, so
g_{\ast}^{-1}(X)
is an even zero-sum set in the independent set
B
, so we know that
g_{\ast}^{-1}(X)=\emptyset
. Since
g
is bijective, this implies that
X=\emptyset
. Therefore
g_{\ast}(B)
contains no nonempty even zero-sum sets, so by the same theorem just cited, it is affinely independent.

Let
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=g(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
for
0\leq i\leq d
. The affinely independent sets
B
and
g_{\ast}(B)
can each be extended to affine bases

\{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{d},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{d},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\}

for
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
Then the function
{f}\colon{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}
defined by setting
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq n
and extending affine linearly is an affine automorphism.

We now show that
f(S)=T
, which will complete the proof that
S
and
T
are affinely equivalent. Let
\boldsymbol{x}\in S
. Since
B
is a basis for
S
, there are
\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{2j+1}}\in B
whose sum is
\boldsymbol{x}
. Then

X=\{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{2j+1}}\}\in E(S).

By assumption

g_{\ast}(X)=\{g(\boldsymbol{x}),g(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}}),\ldots,g(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{2j+1}})\}\in E(T),

so

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=g(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}})+\cdots+g(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{2j+1}})

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}})+\cdots+f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{2j+1}})

\displaystyle(\text{since $f|_{B}=g|_{B}$})

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{2j+1}})

\displaystyle(\text{since $f$ is affine})

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{s}).

Hence
f|_{S}=g|_{S}
, so
f(S)=g(S)=T
.

∎

3.
Venn diagrams and regions

Let
S
be any set. It is well-known that the power set
\mathcal{P}(S)
of
S
has the structure of a
Boolean ring
with addition given by the
symmetric difference

\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}
, defined by

X\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}Y=(X\cup Y)-(X\cap Y)

for
X,Y\in\mathcal{P}(S)
, and multiplication given by set intersection. The zero element is the empty set and the unity element is
S
. The symmetric difference is both commutative and associative, and intersection distributes over symmetric difference.

The following two lemmas are standard results about
\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S
be a set, and let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}
be a finite collection of subsets of
S
. For any
x\in S
,
x\in X_{1}\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\cdots\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}X_{r}
if and only if
x\in X_{i}
for exactly an odd number of
i
,
1\leq i\leq r
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
{f}\colon{S}\to{T}
be an injective function between sets
S
and
T
. Then
f(A\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}B)=f(A)\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}f(B)
for all
A,B\subseteq S
.

In this paper, we focus on
\mathcal{P}(S)
as a vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
S
be a nonempty set of size
k
. Then the singleton subsets of
S
form a basis for
\mathcal{P}(S)
and hence
\dim\mathcal{P}(S)=k
.

Proof.

The singleton subsets of
S
span
\mathcal{P}(S)
, because for any
X\in\mathcal{P}(S)
we have

X=\mathop{{{{\bigtriangleup}}}}\displaylimits_{x\in X}\{x\}.

Also, for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\in S
we have

\mathop{{{{\bigtriangleup}}}}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r}\{x_{i}\}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}\neq\varnothing,

so the singleton subsets are linearly independent.
Thus the singleton subsets form a basis for
\mathcal{P}(S)
, so
\dim\mathcal{P}(S)=|S|=k
.
∎

Definition 3.4
.

Let
S
be a finite set and let
\mathcal{V}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(S)
. The
Venn regions
of
S
with respect to
\mathcal{V}
are the sets

R_{I}=\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}X_{i}\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\notin I}X_{i}^{\mathsf{c}}\right)

for
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}
, where
X_{i}^{\mathsf{c}}=S-X_{i}
.
The set

R_{\varnothing}=X_{1}^{\mathsf{c}}\cap\cdots\cap X_{r}^{\mathsf{c}}

is the
isolated point set
for
\mathcal{V}
.

If
x,y\in S
are contained in the same Venn region, then we write
x\sim_{\mathcal{V}}y
.

Remark 3.5
.

Observe that the Venn regions defined above are exactly the connected components of a Venn diagram for the sets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}
. The isolated point set is the region outside of all of these sets,
X_{1}^{\mathsf{c}}\cap\cdots X_{r}^{\mathsf{c}}
.
The nonempty Venn regions are pairwise disjoint and partition
S
, and thus
\sim_{\mathcal{V}}
is an equivalence relation.
Hence the quotient set
S/{\sim_{\mathcal{V}}}
is the set of nonempty Venn regions.
Note also that
x\sim_{\mathcal{V}}y
if and only if
x
and
y
are contained in exactly the same elements of
\mathcal{V}
.

In the following lemma, we show that each set in
\mathcal{V}
is a union of Venn regions. Later, in Proposition
3.15
, we will also prove that if
\mathcal{V}
is a linear subspace, then each Venn region is an intersection of sets in
\mathcal{V}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
S
be a finite set and let
\mathcal{V}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(S)
.

(1)

For any
X_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
and Venn region
R
from
\mathcal{V}
, either
R\cap X_{i}=\varnothing
or
R\subseteq X_{i}
.

(2)

Each
X_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
is a union of Venn regions from
\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

Let
X_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
and let
R
be a Venn region from
\mathcal{V}
. Then either
R\cap X_{i}=\varnothing
or
R\cap X_{i}\neq\varnothing
. Assume the latter, so there exists
\boldsymbol{x}\in R\cap X_{i}
. Then
\boldsymbol{x}\in R
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in X_{i}
. Then for any
\boldsymbol{r}\in R
we have
\boldsymbol{x}\sim_{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{r}
, so
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
are contained in exactly the same elements of
\mathcal{V}
. Since
\boldsymbol{x}\in X_{i}
, it follows that
\boldsymbol{r}\in X_{i}
. Therefore
R\subseteq X_{i}
.

That each
X_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
is a union of Venn regions follows from the facts that each
\boldsymbol{x}\in X_{i}
is in some Venn region, and if a Venn region intersects
X_{i}
at all, then it must be contained in
X_{i}
.
∎

. The set

is called the

Remark 3.7
.

For the remainder of this paper, we will focus on the case where
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(S)
is a linear subspace. We will denote this condition by
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
S
be a finite set, let
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
, and let
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
be a spanning set for
\mathcal{V}
. Then

S/{\sim_{\mathcal{U}}}=S/{\sim_{\mathcal{V}}}.

Proof.

We will show that
\sim_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\sim_{\mathcal{V}}
are the same relation.
Let
x,y\in S
.

Suppose
x\sim_{\mathcal{V}}y
. Then
x
and
y
are contained in precisely the same elements of
\mathcal{V}
. Since
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, it follows that
x\sim_{\mathcal{U}}y
.

Suppose
x\sim_{\mathcal{U}}y
. Let
A\in\mathcal{V}
. Since
\mathcal{U}
spans
\mathcal{V}
, there are sets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\in\mathcal{U}
such that

A=X_{1}\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\cdots\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}X_{r}.

Since
x\sim_{\mathcal{U}}y
, we know that
x\in X_{i}
if and only if
y\in X_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq r
. This implies that
x\in A
if and only if
y\in A
. Since this statement holds for all
A\in\mathcal{V}
, it follows that

.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
S
be a set, let
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
, and let
\mathscr{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}
be a basis for
\mathcal{V}
.
The
coordinate function for
\mathcal{V}
with respect to
\mathscr{X}
is
{z_{\mathscr{X}}}\colon{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\to{\mathcal{V}}
defined by

z_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a})=\mathop{{{{\bigtriangleup}}}}\displaylimits_{i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}}}X_{i}

for
\boldsymbol{a}\in Z_{2}^{r}
, where
I_{\boldsymbol{a}}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}
is the support of
\boldsymbol{a}
.

The set of
Venn regions of
S
with respect to
\mathscr{X}
is denoted
\venn_{\mathscr{X}}(S,\mathcal{V})
. The
coordinate function for
\venn_{\mathscr{X}}(S,\mathcal{V})
is
{v_{\mathscr{X}}}\colon{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\to{\venn_{\mathscr{X}}(S,\mathcal{V})}
defined by

v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a})=\left(\bigcap_{i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}}}X_{i}\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\notin I_{\boldsymbol{a}}}X_{i}^{\mathsf{c}}\right)

for
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
.

Remark 3.11
.

(1)

The function
{z_{\mathscr{X}}}\colon{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\to{\mathcal{V}}
defined above is the inverse of the map assigning each element of
\mathcal{V}
to its vector expression with respect to the basis
\mathscr{X}
, so it is a linear bijection.

(2)

The function
{v_{\mathscr{X}}}\colon{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\to{\venn_{\mathscr{X}}(S,\mathcal{V})}
is bijective and makes
\venn_{\mathscr{X}}(S,\mathcal{V})
into a vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
of dimension
r
with addition defined by

v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a})+v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{b}):=v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{b})

for all
v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a}),v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{b})\in\venn_{\mathscr{X}}(S,\mathcal{V})
.

(3)

The Venn region
v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{0})
is the set of isolated points for
\mathcal{V}
, so
v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{0})=R_{\varnothing}
.

(4)

The set
\venn_{\mathscr{X}}(S,\mathcal{V})
, considered as a collection of sets, may contain multiple copies of the empty set, but since these Venn regions are indexed by vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
, they are distinguishable.

(5)

Lemma
3.8
implies that the elements of
\venn_{\mathscr{X}}(S,\mathcal{V})
and those of
\venn_{\mathscr{Y}}(S,\mathcal{V})
are the same for any bases
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{Y}
for
\mathcal{V}
, so we will denote this basis-independent collection of Venn regions by
\venn_{\mathcal{V}}(S)
. In Corollary
3.17
, we will show that the vector space structure on
\venn_{\mathcal{V}}(S)
is also basis-independent.

By Lemma
3.6
, each set in
\mathcal{V}
is a union of Venn regions. In the next example, we examine the coordinates for a set and its constituent Venn regions.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
S
be a finite set and let
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
have basis
\mathscr{X}
. Let
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
be such that
v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a})\in\venn_{\mathscr{X}}(S,\mathcal{V})
is nonempty.
Then

v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a})\subseteq z_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{b})\iff\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}=1.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r})\neq\boldsymbol{0}
have support
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{s}\}
of
\boldsymbol{b}
.
The elements of
z(\boldsymbol{b})=X_{i_{1}}\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\cdots\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}X_{i_{s}}
are those contained in exactly an odd number of the sets
X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{s}}
. We will determine which Venn regions consist of points with that property.

By definition
v(\boldsymbol{a})\subseteq X_{i_{j}}
if and only if
a_{i_{j}}=1
, for
1\leq j\leq s
. Therefore
v(\boldsymbol{a})
is contained in an odd number of the sets
X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{s}}
if and only if the entries
a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{s}}
have an odd number of ones. Now consider the dot product
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}
. Considering which entries of
\boldsymbol{b}
are zero and which are one, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}

\displaystyle=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\cdot(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r})

\displaystyle=a_{1}b_{1}+\cdots+a_{r}b_{r}

\displaystyle=a_{i_{1}}+\cdots+a_{i_{s}}.

Since these are elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, this sum equals one if and only if the entries
a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{s}}
have an odd number of ones. Therefore
v(\boldsymbol{a})\subseteq z(\boldsymbol{b})
if and only if
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}=1
.
∎

We have the following useful corollary.

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
S
be a finite set, and let
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
with
\dim(\mathcal{V})=r
. Each nonempty Venn region
R
is contained in either none or exactly half,
2^{r-1}
, of the sets in
\mathcal{V}
.
Hence each point in
S
is contained in none or exactly
2^{r-1}
of the sets in
\mathcal{V}
.
Also each nonempty element of
\mathcal{V}
contains exactly
2^{r-1}
nonempty Venn regions.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a basis for
\mathcal{V}
, let
R\in\venn_{\mathscr{X}}(S,\mathcal{V})
be nonempty, and let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
be such that
R=v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a})
. If
\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{0}
then
v(\boldsymbol{a})=R_{\varnothing}
, the isolated point set, so
R
does not intersect any sets in
\mathcal{V}
. Suppose
\boldsymbol{a}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
.
Lemma
3.13
implies that

\lvert{\left\{\,{A\in\mathcal{V}}\;\middle|\;{R\subseteq A}\,\right\}}\rvert=\lvert{\left\{\,{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\;\middle|\;{\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}=1}\,\right\}}\rvert.

The linear equation
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}=1
defines a hyperplane of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
, which is an
(r-1)
-flat and hence contains
2^{r-1}
points.
Therefore

\lvert{\left\{\,{A\in\mathcal{V}}\;\middle|\;{R\subseteq A}\,\right\}}\rvert=\lvert{\left\{\,{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\;\middle|\;{\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}=1}\,\right\}}\rvert=2^{r-1}.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}\in S
and let
R_{\boldsymbol{x}}
be the Venn region containing
\boldsymbol{x}
. By Lemma
3.6
, we know
R_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is contained in a set in
\mathcal{V}
if and only if it intersects that set. It follows that
\boldsymbol{x}
is contained in the same number of sets in
\mathcal{V}
, which we just proved is
0
or
2^{r-1}
.

Now let
X\in\mathcal{V}
be nonempty, and let
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
be such that
z_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{b})=X
, and note that
\boldsymbol{b}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
. By Lemma
3.6
, the number of nonempty Venn regions contained in
X
is

\lvert{\left\{\,{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\;\middle|\;{\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}=1}\,\right\}}\rvert=2^{r-1}.

∎

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
S
be a set, let
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
, let
A\in\mathcal{V}
, and let
R\in S/{\sim_{\mathcal{V}}}-\{R_{\varnothing}\}
.
Then

A=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}R^{\prime}\in S/{\sim_{\mathcal{V}}},\\
R^{\prime}\cap A\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}R^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad R=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V},\\
R\cap A^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}A^{\prime}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.6
, we know that
A
is the union of the Venn regions that intersect it, so

A=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}R^{\prime}\in S/{\sim_{\mathcal{V}}},\\
R^{\prime}\cap A\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}R^{\prime}.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{R}=\left\{\,{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}\;\middle|\;{R\cap A^{\prime}\neq\varnothing}\,\right\}
. Corollary
3.14
implies that
\lvert{\mathcal{V}_{R}}\rvert=2^{r-1}
, where
r=\dim(\mathcal{V})
.
By the definition of Venn regions we have

R=\bigcap_{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}}A^{\prime}-\bigcap_{A^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{V}_{R})^{\mathsf{c}}}A^{\prime}\subseteq\bigcap_{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}}A^{\prime}.

On the other hand, let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{V}_{R}}A^{\prime}
. Since
\lvert{\mathcal{V}_{R}}\rvert=2^{r-1}
and by Corollary
3.14
we know
\boldsymbol{x}
is contained in exactly
2^{r-1}
elements of
\mathcal{V}
, it follows that
\boldsymbol{x}\notin A^{\prime}
for all
A^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{V}_{R})^{\mathsf{c}}
. Thus

\boldsymbol{x}\in\bigcap_{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}}A^{\prime}-\bigcap_{A^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{V}_{R})^{\mathsf{c}}}A^{\prime}=R.

Therefore
R=\bigcap_{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}}A^{\prime}
.
∎

Corollary 3.17
.

Let
S
be a finite set and let
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
be a linear subspace. Then the addition operation on
\venn_{\mathcal{V}}(S)
is independent of the choice of basis for
\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

Let
\dim(\mathcal{V})=r
, and let
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{Y}
be bases for
\mathcal{V}
. By Theorem
3.16
there is an
(r\times r)
-matrix
N
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
such that
v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a})=v_{\mathscr{Y}}(N\boldsymbol{a})
for all
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
. Denote the addition of Venn regions induced by
\mathscr{X}
by
+_{\mathscr{X}}
, and that induced by
\mathscr{Y}
by
+_{\mathscr{Y}}
.

Let
R_{1},R_{2}\in\venn_{\mathcal{V}}(S)
. Then there are
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
such that
R_{1}=v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a})=v_{\mathscr{Y}}(N\boldsymbol{a})
and
R_{2}=v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{b})=v_{\mathscr{Y}}(N\boldsymbol{b})
.
Then

\displaystyle R_{1}+_{\mathscr{X}}R_{2}

\displaystyle=v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a})+_{\mathscr{X}}v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle=v_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle=v_{\mathscr{Y}}(N(\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{b}))

\displaystyle=v_{\mathscr{Y}}(N\boldsymbol{a}+N\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle=v_{\mathscr{Y}}(N\boldsymbol{a})+_{\mathscr{Y}}v_{\mathscr{Y}}(N\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle=R_{1}+_{\mathscr{Y}}R_{2}.

∎

4.
Maps between Venn regions

Any map
{g}\colon{S}\to{T}
between sets
S
and
T
induces a map
{g_{\ast}}\colon{\mathcal{P}(S)}\to{\mathcal{P}(T)}
given by the image of each subset. By Lemma
3.2
, if
g
is injective then
g
preserves the symmetric difference, so
g_{\ast}
is linear.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
S
and
T
be sets, and let
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
and
\mathcal{W}\leq\mathcal{P}(T)
. A function
{g}\colon{S}\to{T}
is a
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-isomorphism
if
g
is bijective and
g_{\ast}(\mathcal{V})=\mathcal{W}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that if
{g}\colon{S}\to{T}
is a
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-isomorphism, then the restriction
g_{\ast}|_{\mathcal{V}}
is an injective linear map with image
\mathcal{W}
, so induces a linear bijection

{g_{\mathcal{V}\mathcal{W}}}\colon{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathcal{W}}.

Furthermore
g_{\mathcal{V}\mathcal{W}}
is
cardinality-preserving
, in the sense that

\lvert{g_{\mathcal{V}\mathcal{W}}(X)}\rvert=\lvert{X}\rvert

for all
X\in\mathcal{V}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
S
and
T
be sets, let
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
and
\mathcal{W}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
, and let
{g}\colon{S}\to{T}
be a
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-isomorphism.
Then
g_{\ast}|_{\venn(S,T)}
is a cardinality-preserving linear bijection
\venn(S,\mathcal{V})\to\venn(T,\mathcal{W})
.

Proof.

Since
g
is bijective, we know that
g_{\ast}
and hence
g_{\ast}|_{\venn(S,\mathcal{V})}
is cardinality-preserving. We now prove that the latter map is linear and injective with image
\venn(T,\mathcal{W})
.

Let
\mathscr{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}
be a basis for
\mathcal{V}
, and let
Y_{i}=g_{\mathcal{V}\mathcal{W}}(X_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq r
. Because
{g_{\mathcal{V}\mathcal{W}}}\colon{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathcal{W}}
is a linear bijection, the set
\mathscr{Y}=\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{r}\}
is a basis for
\mathcal{W}
. Since
g
is bijective and hence preserves set intersection and complement, for any
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
with support
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}
we have

\displaystyle(g_{\ast}\circ v_{\mathscr{X}})(\boldsymbol{a})

\displaystyle=g_{\ast}\left[\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}X_{i}\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\notin I}X_{i}^{\mathsf{c}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}g_{\ast}(X_{i})\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\notin I}[g_{\ast}(X_{i})]^{\mathsf{c}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}Y_{i}\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\notin I}Y_{i}^{\mathsf{c}}\right)

\displaystyle=v_{\mathscr{Y}}(\boldsymbol{a}).

Hence
g_{\ast}\circ v_{\mathscr{X}}=v_{\mathscr{Y}}
, i.e. the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\venn(S,\mathcal{V})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\venn(T,\mathcal{W})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces v_{\mathscr{Y}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces v_{\mathscr{X}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g_{\ast}}

Therefore
g_{\ast}|_{\venn(S,\mathcal{V})}=v_{\mathscr{Y}}\circ v_{\mathscr{X}}^{-1}
. Since
{v_{\mathscr{X}}}\colon{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\to{\venn(S,\mathcal{V})}
and
{v_{\mathscr{Y}}}\colon{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\to{\venn(T,\mathcal{W})}
are linear bijections, it follows that
g_{\ast}|_{\venn(S,\mathcal{V})}
defines a linear bijection
\venn(S,\mathcal{V})\to\venn(T,\mathcal{W})
.

∎

Proposition
4.3
has the immediate following corollary.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
S
and
T
be finite sets, and let
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
and
\mathcal{W}\leq\mathcal{P}(T)
. If there exists a
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-isomorphism
S\to T
, then the multisets of Venn region cardinalities for
\venn(S,\mathcal{V})
and
\venn(T,\mathcal{W})
must be the same. Hence, if the multisets of Venn region cardinalities are different, there is no
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-isomorphism.

Not only does a
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-isomorphism between sets induce a cardinality-preserving isomorphism between Venn region sets, but in fact the converse is true.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
S
and
T
be finite sets, and let
\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
and
\mathcal{W}\leq\mathcal{P}(T)
. There exists a
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-isomorphism
S\to T
if and only if there is a cardinality-preserving linear bijection
\venn(S,\mathcal{V})\to\venn(T,\mathcal{W})
.

Proof.

Suppose
{g}\colon{S}\to{T}
is a
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-isomorphism.
By Proposition
4.3
, the map
{g_{\ast}|_{\venn(S,\mathcal{V})}}\colon{\venn(S,\mathcal{V})}\to{\venn(T,\mathcal{W})}

Suppose there is a cardinality-preserving linear bijection

{h}\colon{\venn(S,\mathcal{V})}\to{\venn(T,\mathcal{W})}.

For each
R\in\venn(S,\mathcal{V})
, since
h(R)
has the same cardinality as
R
, we can define a bijection
R\to h(R)
. Doing so for each Venn region of
S
, since
S
and
T
are each the disjoint unions of their respective Venn regions, we obtain a bijective function
{g}\colon{S}\to{T}
such that
g_{\ast}(R)=h(R)
for each
R\in\venn(S,\mathcal{V})
. We will show that
g
is a
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-isomorphism.

Let
r=\dim\venn(S,\mathcal{V})=\dim\venn(T,\mathcal{W})
, and recall that this means
r=\dim(\mathcal{V})=\dim(\mathcal{W})
, as well. Let
\mathscr{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}
and
\mathscr{Y}=\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{r}\}
be bases for
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
, respectively. Let
N
be the matrix representation of
{h}\colon{\venn(S,\mathcal{V})}\to{\venn(T,\mathcal{W})}
with respect to the coordinates
v_{\mathscr{X}}
and
v_{\mathscr{Y}}
, so that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\venn(S,\mathcal{V})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\venn(T,\mathcal{W})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces v_{\mathscr{X}}}
mult. by
\scriptstyle N
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces v_{\mathscr{Y}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}

Let
M=(N^{-1})^{T}

be the linear bijection represented by the matrix
M
with respect to the bases
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{Y}
, so that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{V}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{W}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z_{\mathscr{X}}}

\scriptstyle M
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z_{\mathscr{Y}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma}

\gamma(X)=g_{\ast}(X)
for all

, so that

defines a linear bijection
\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{W}
, so

(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
-isomorphism.

Let
X\in\mathcal{V}
. Then

for some

3.13

\gamma(X)=\gamma(z_{\mathscr{X}}(\boldsymbol{a}))=z_{\mathscr{Y}}(M\boldsymbol{a})=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r},\\

\displaystyle\left\{\,{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\;\middle|\;{(M\boldsymbol{a})\cdot\boldsymbol{b}=1}\,\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\,{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}}\;\middle|\;{\boldsymbol{a}\cdot(N^{-1}\boldsymbol{b})=1}\,\right\}.

\displaystyle=\left\{\,{N\boldsymbol{c}}\;\middle|\;{\text{$\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}$ and $\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}=1$}}\,\right\}.

5.
Zero-sum sets

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. In this section we will prove that the even zero-sum set
E(S)
of
S
is a linear subspace of
\mathcal{P}(S)
, describe how an affine basis for
S
induces a linear basis for
E(S)
, and then apply the results of the preceding section to this context.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Then
E(S)\leq\mathcal{P}(S)
.

Proof.

By definition
\varnothing\in E(S)
, so
E(S)
contains the zero vector of
\mathcal{P}(S)
. It remains to show that
E(S)
is closed under the symmetric difference.

Let
X,Y\in E(S)
. Let
A=X-Y
,
B=Y-X
, and
C=X\cap Y
. Then
X\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}Y=A\sqcup B
. Since
\lvert{X}\rvert=\lvert{A}\rvert+\lvert{C}\rvert
and
\lvert{Y}\rvert=\lvert{B}\rvert+\lvert{C}\rvert
are even, it follows that
\lvert{A}\rvert
and
\lvert{B}\rvert
have the same parity, so

\lvert{X\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}Y}\rvert=\lvert{A\sqcup B}\rvert=\lvert{A}\rvert+\lvert{B}\rvert

must be even.
Also

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in X\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}Y}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in A}\boldsymbol{x}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in B}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in A}\boldsymbol{x}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in B}\boldsymbol{x}+2\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in C}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in A}\boldsymbol{x}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in C}\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in B}\boldsymbol{x}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in C}\boldsymbol{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in X}\boldsymbol{x}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in Y}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=0+0=0,

where the last line follows from the fact that
X,Y\in E(S)
.
Therefore
X\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}Y\in E(S)
.
∎

We now describe how to construct a basis for
E(S)
from a basis for
S
, which will allow us to determine the dimension of
E(S)
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
S=B\sqcup D\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
have affine basis
B=\{\boldsymbol{b}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{d}\}
. Let
\boldsymbol{w}\in D
, and let
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{b}_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{b}_{i_{j}}
be the unique expression of
\boldsymbol{w}
as an odd sum of basis elements.
define

X_{\boldsymbol{w}}=\{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{b}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{i_{j}}\}\in E(S).

The
sumset map induced by
B
is the map
{\varphi}\colon{\mathcal{P}(D)}\to{E(S)}
defined by
\varphi(\varnothing)=\varnothing
and

\varphi(D^{\prime})=\mathop{{{{\bigtriangleup}}}}\displaylimits_{\boldsymbol{w}\in D^{\prime}}X_{\boldsymbol{w}}

for nonempty
D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(D)
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
S=B\sqcup D\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
have affine basis
B
, and let
{\varphi}\colon{\mathcal{P}(D)}\to{E(S)}
be the sumset map induced by
B
. Let
D^{\prime},D_{1},D_{2}\subseteq D
. Then

(1)

D\cap\varphi(D^{\prime})=D^{\prime}
;

(2)

if
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\varnothing
then
\varphi(D_{1}\cup D_{2})=\varphi(D_{1})\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D_{2})
;

(3)

\varphi
is bijective and linear.

Proof.

Note that
D\cap X_{\boldsymbol{w}}=\boldsymbol{w}
for each
\boldsymbol{w}\in D
. Since intersection distributes over symmetric difference, it follows that

D\cap\varphi(D^{\prime})=D\cap\left(\mathop{{{{\bigtriangleup}}}}\displaylimits_{\boldsymbol{w}\in D^{\prime}}X_{\boldsymbol{w}}\right)=\mathop{{{{\bigtriangleup}}}}\displaylimits_{\boldsymbol{w}\in D^{\prime}}\left(D\cap X_{\boldsymbol{w}}\right)=\mathop{{{{\bigtriangleup}}}}\displaylimits_{\boldsymbol{w}\in D^{\prime}}\{\boldsymbol{w}\}=D^{\prime}.

Suppose that
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\varnothing
. Then

\displaystyle\varphi(D_{1}\cup D_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathop{{{{\bigtriangleup}}}}\displaylimits_{\boldsymbol{w}\in D_{1}\cup D_{2}}X_{\boldsymbol{w}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathop{{{{\bigtriangleup}}}}\displaylimits_{\boldsymbol{w}\in D_{1}}X_{\boldsymbol{w}}\right)\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\left(\mathop{{{{\bigtriangleup}}}}\displaylimits_{\boldsymbol{w}\in D_{2}}X_{\boldsymbol{w}}\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi(D_{1})\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D_{2}).

Suppose that
\varphi(D_{1})=\varphi(D_{2})
. By part 1 of this lemma, it follows that

D_{1}=\varphi(D_{1})\cap D=\varphi(D_{2})\cap D=D_{2}.

Hence
\varphi
is injective.

Let
Z\in E(S)
and set
D^{\prime}=Z\cap D
. Then

[Z\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D^{\prime})]\cap D=(Z\cap D)\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}[\varphi(D^{\prime})\cap D]=D^{\prime}\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}D^{\prime}=\varnothing.

Since
S=B\sqcup D
it follows that
Z\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D^{\prime})\subseteq B
. Since
Z\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D^{\prime})\in E(S)
and
E(S)\cap\mathcal{P}(B)=\varnothing
, this implies that
Z\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D^{\prime})=\varnothing
.
Thus
\varphi(D^{\prime})=Z
. Therefore
\varphi
is surjective.

Finally, it follows from part 2 of this lemma that

\displaystyle\varphi(D_{1})\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad=\varphi\left[(D_{1}-D_{2})\sqcup(D_{1}\cap D_{2})\right]\,\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\,\varphi\left[(D_{2}-D_{1})\sqcup(D_{1}\cap D_{2})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\varphi(D_{1}-D_{2})\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D_{1}\cap D_{2})\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D_{2}-D_{1})\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D_{1}\cap D_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad=\varphi(D_{1}-D_{2})\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\varphi(D_{2}-D_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad=\varphi\left((D_{1}-D_{2})\cup(D_{2}-D_{1})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\varphi(D_{1}\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}D_{2}).

Hence
\varphi
is linear.
∎

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
S=B\sqcup D\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
have affine basis
B
, and let
\lvert{S}\rvert=k
and
\dim(S)=d
.
Then

\left\{\,{X_{\boldsymbol{w}}}\;\middle|\;{\boldsymbol{w}\in D}\,\right\}

is a basis for
E(S)
and
\dim E(S)=k-(d+1)
.

Proof.

Let
{\varphi}\colon{\mathcal{P}(D)}\to{E(S)}
be the sumset map of
E(S)
with respect to
B
.
By Lemma
5.3
we know that
\varphi
is a linear bijection. By Proposition
3.3
, the set

\left\{\,{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\;\middle|\;{\boldsymbol{w}\in D}\,\right\}

is a basis for
\mathcal{P}(D)
, and hence

\varphi(\left\{\,{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\;\middle|\;{\boldsymbol{w}\in D}\,\right\})=\left\{\,{X_{\boldsymbol{w}}}\;\middle|\;{\boldsymbol{w}\in D}\,\right\}

is a basis for
E(S)
. Since
\lvert{B}\rvert=d+1
, it follows that

\dim E(S)=\lvert{D}\rvert=\lvert{S}\rvert-\lvert{B}\rvert=k-(d+1).

∎

6.
Caps with Size-Dimension Difference 3

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
S,T\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be sets with size
k
and dimension
d=k-3
. Then
S
and
T
are affinely equivalent if and only if
S
and
T
have the same multisets of Venn region cardinalities and the same number of isolated points.

Proof.

If
S
and
T
are affinely equivalent, then Theorem
5.7
implies that the multisets of cardinalities of elements of
\venn(S,E(S))
and
\venn(T,E(T))
are identical, and that
\lvert{V_{0}(S)}\rvert=\lvert{V_{0}(T)}\rvert
, because these correspond to the zero vector in
\venn(S,\mathcal{V})
and
\venn(T,\mathcal{W})
, respectively.

Now, suppose that
S
and
T
have the same multisets of Venn region cardinalities and the same number of isolated points. Then there exists a cardinality-preserving bijection
{f}\colon{\venn(S,E(S))}\to{\venn(T,E(T))}
that maps the isolated point set of
S
to that of
T
. Both
\venn(S,E(S))
and
\venn(T,E(T))
have dimension
k-(d+1)=2
, so are isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}
. Since every permutation of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}
that fixes the origin is linear, it follows that the function
f
must be linear, so
f
is a cardinality-preserving linear bijection. By Theorem
5.7
, this implies that
S
and
T
are affinely equivalent.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
have
\lvert{S}\rvert=k
and
\dim(S)=d=k-3
, and let
a\leq b\leq c
be the Venn region cardinalities of
S
, not including
V_{0}(S)
. Then
S
is a cap if and only if the numbers
a+b
,
b+c
, and
a+c
are all greater than or equal to
6
.

Proof.

Observe that the nonempty even zero-sum sets of
S
have sizes
a+b
,
b+c
, and
a+c
. These numbers must be even, and none can equal
0
or
2
, because these are nonempty sets consisting of distinct points. Recall that
S
is a cap if and only if no four elements of
S
sum to
0
, which is equivalent to there being no even zero-sum sets of size
4
. Therefore
S
is a cap if and only if the even numbers
a+b
,
b+c
, and
a+c
are greater than

, which means they are greater than or equal to
6
.
∎

Suppose

,

, and

.

Let

\displaystyle=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{b-1}+z_{1}+\cdots+z_{a},

\displaystyle y_{c}

\displaystyle=y_{1}+\cdots+y_{c-1}+z_{1}+\cdots+z_{a}.

Let
V_{b}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{b}\}
, and
V_{c}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{c}\}
, and
V_{a}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{a}\}
, and set
C=V_{a}\cup V_{b}\cup V_{c}\cup\{\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{k-(a+b+c)}\}
.
Then
V_{a}\cup V_{b}
,
V_{a}\cup V_{c}
, and
V_{b}\cup V_{c}
are the nonempty even zero-sum sets of
C
. Since these have cardinality
a+b
,
a+c
, and
b+c
, respectively, and these numbers are all
\geq 6
, we conclude that the set
C
is a cap.
