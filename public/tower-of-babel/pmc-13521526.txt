Antagonistic co-evolution throughout the herpesvirus life cycle
Authors: Thomas Höfler, Dina Aly Abdulrahman, Jana Reich, Lisa Kossak, Jenny Ospina-Garcia, Dilan Gün Serdar, Mariana Mara Nascimento, Yingnan Cheng, Benedikt Bertold Kaufer, Jakob Trimpert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerpesviruses are a widespread and diverse family of large double-stranded DNA viruses. Narrow host ranges maintained over long evolutionary times have fostered intimate host adaptations. However, such close virus–host associations inevitably invite conflict. Genetic conflict describes antagonistic interactions between two individuals that drive genetic changes to overcome said conflict. During the herpesvirus infectious cycle, genetic conflict—and specifically antagonistic co-evolution—can occur on multiple levels: between host and virus, between co-infecting viruses, or between genes within the same genome. This review highlights the many ways such conflict occurs and how it shapes herpesvirus evolution. Starting with attachment and entry, and continuing through gene expression, DNA metabolism, packaging, and egress, we examine how antagonistic interactions manifest at each stage of the viral life cycle. Latency and immunity receive special consideration as their inherent biology makes them focal points of sustained antagonism. Since genetic conflict is a major force in evolutionary ecology, we aim to emphasize its central role in herpesvirus biology and encourage future studies to explicitly consider the antagonistic forces shaping their evolution.

Introduction

Genetic conflict is a fundamental yet often neglected evolutionary force that drives adaptive change in all living and evolving organisms (Rice 2013). Resolving conflict requires multiple adaptations that tip the scale and consequently forces similar changes on the other conflicting party (McLaughlin Jr and Malik 2017).

Early definitions of genetic conflict restricted the concept to genes that primarily influenced their own transmission and triggered the spread of modifying genes, all expressed within a single individual (Hurst et al. 1996). This framework focused exclusively on selfish genetic elements (SGEs) (Burt and Trivers 2006), thereby excluding all conflict between different individuals, such as host–pathogen interactions (Daugherty and Malik 2012), within population competition (Díaz-Muñoz et al. 2017), predator–prey interactions (Delong and Vasseur 2013), or parent–offspring relationships (Kölliker et al. 2015). In contemporary usage, however, genetic conflict encompasses all these scenarios, often described as Red Queen conflicts, evolutionary arms races, competitive interactions, or antagonistic co-evolution (Werren 2011).

Viruses evolve at high rates, particularly in traits related to immune evasion and interactions with host factors. Herpesviruses especially are highly diverse (Brito et al. 2021). Virtually every mammalian, avian, or reptilian species serves as a herpesvirus host, frequently harbouring multiple distinct host-specific species (i.e. human herpesviruses are highly specific for human hosts, whereas animal herpesviruses do not infect humans). This diverse family of large double-stranded (ds)DNA viruses is divided into subfamilies of alpha, beta, and gamma herpesviruses (Gatherer et al. 2021).

Despite their diversity, all herpesviruses share a conserved infection cycle comprising distinct stages: attachment and entry, gene expression, DNA metabolism, virion assembly, and egress as well as lifelong latency (Flint et al. 2015, Howley et al. 2021). Antagonistic interactions occur not only at all these stages but also while interacting with the host immune response. Throughout, conflict emerges between host and virus, co-infecting viruses, and genes within the same viral genomes (Fig. 1). This review examines antagonistic co-evolution on multiple levels throughout the herpesvirus infectious cycle, with special focus on human herpesviruses as those are studied in great detail. For clarity and consistency, we structured the following sections, subsections, and paragraphs to discuss alpha, beta, and gamma herpesviruses sequentially wherever possible. Additionally, for Figs 2–4, we used a colour scheme denoting different subfamilies (alpha yellow, beta purple, and gamma green). While providing a broad conceptual overview, we also highlight specific examples of conflict and speculate on avenues for future investigation. Our goal is to stimulate curiosity and encourage new lines of research into the evolutionary dynamics of herpesviruses.

Genetic diversity

Compared to RNA viruses, herpesviruses mutate and evolve relatively slowly (Sanjuan et al. 2010). However, recent advances in sequencing technologies have demonstrated that although per-base mutation rates are comparably low (~10 − 6–10−7 substitutions per base per infectious cycle), the large genome and population size combined with latent reservoirs do compensate for otherwise little genetic diversity (Kuny and Szpara 2021, Ortigas-Vasquez and Szpara 2024). Comprehensive sequencing of clinical samples, as well as experimental evolution studies, elucidates the remarkable diversity of herpesviruses, within and between subpopulations (Szpara et al. 2014, Kuny et al. 2020, López-Muñoz et al. 2021, Hayes et al. 2022). Modern long-read sequencing methods are enhancing our understanding of herpesvirus diversity even more, as haplotypes and copy-number variants can be accurately determined (Ortigas-Vasquez et al. 2025).

Especially, human cytomegalovirus (HCMV; formally known as human beta herpesvirus 5; see Table 1) exhibits levels of nucleotide diversity comparable to highly diverse RNA viruses such as hepatitis B and C (HBV, HCV) or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (Renzette et al. 2015, Cudini et al. 2019). Chronic infection combined with extensive antiviral treatment can select for exonuclease-impaired viral polymerases, thereby increasing mutation rates (Chen et al. 2014). Recent studies have confirmed that such exonuclease-impaired mutants are viable in other herpesviruses, such as herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1; formally known as human alpha herpesvirus 1; see Table 1) or Marek’s disease virus (MDV; formally known as gallid alpha herpesvirus 2; see Table 1) (Trimpert et al. 2019, Xing et al. 2022, Brunialti et al. 2023, Höfler et al. 2024). In vivo selection for hypermutator phenotypes could temporarily boost nucleotide diversity. In addition, recombination rates of up to 25% in HSV-1 can effectively purge deleterious mutations from growing populations (Ralph-Altman et al. 2025). Together, de novo generation of variants and rearrangement via recombination provide the genetic diversity required to overcome host–virus, virus–virus, or intra-genomic conflicts.

Attachment and entry

The initial step of any viral life cycle is the identification of susceptible host cells, as progression of the infectious cycle requires access to the intracellular environment. Consequently, the cell surface presents the first line of defence that viruses must overcome. At this interface, conflict arises through variations in receptor availability and usage, mechanisms of superinfection exclusion, and modulation of immune recognition. Glycoproteins, which mediate host cell attachment and entry in herpesviruses, are the key factors engaged at the cell surface and represent focal points of evolutionary interaction.

Modular glycoproteins

Herpesviruses encode for a multitude of glycoproteins ranging from 8 to 18. However, the core complex (formed by gH/L and gB) is conserved across all three subfamilies (Möhl et al. 2019, Nishimura and Mori 2019, Vallbracht et al. 2019). Receptor recognition is either mediated by a single glycoprotein, by the conserved gH/L core complex alone or in concert with other viral factors (Connolly et al. 2021). The emergence of distinct viral receptor recognition proteins (or the loss thereof) might already be a direct consequence of antagonism between co-infecting viruses or between virus and host. Primarily, envelope proteins in herpesviruses are extraordinarily diverse (Akhtar et al. 2019). Among herpesvirus genes, glycoproteins exhibit the highest rates of gene gain, duplication, and loss compared to other functional groups such as capsid components, DNA metabolism factors, or modulatory proteins (Brito and Pinney 2020). Glycoproteins, like gD and gG in HSV-1, illustrate how functional constraints drive duplication events and lead to sustained alterations in viral attachment (Brito and Pinney 2020). Initially unfavourable protein–protein interactions were overcome by gene duplication and subsequent decoupling of mutation and selection. In turn, gD and gG developed high-affinity for nectin and chemokine binding, respectively (Bryant et al. 2003). Opposing selective pressures to ensure receptor recognition and viral entry, as well as evading humoral immunity, probably contributed to this functional diversity and modularity of herpesvirus glycoproteins (Fig. 2; see also the Immunity and immune evasion section).

Receptor interactions

HSV-1 engages three specific receptors: herpesvirus entry mediator (HVEM), nectin-1, and 3-O-sulfated heparan sulfate (Petermann et al. 2015). HVEM, a member of the tumour necrosis factor (TNF) receptor family, is predominantly expressed on lymphoid cells such as T cells, B cells, and monocytes, with lower expression on fibroblasts and endothelial cells. Although HVEM supports HSV-1 entry into activated T cells, viral replication is typically arrested prior to DNA replication in this cell type (Hayward et al. 1988). These apparent evolutionary dead-ends invite speculation about potential localized sabotage or diversionary effects. It is conceivable that HSV-1 entry into nonpermissive T cells might aid undetected propagation of virus in oral or genital mucosa cells and keratinocytes by interfering with cellular surveillance.

In addition to binding gD, HVEM interacts with four other ligands including the B and T lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA) (Liu et al. 2019), a member of the immunoglobulin (Ig) superfamily expressed on immune cells (Watanabe et al. 2003). Although, gD and BTLA bind HVEM in different manners, the binding sites overlap (notably at cysteine-rich domain 1), suggesting potential ligand competition and immune modulatory effects (Carfi et al. 2001). Indeed, gD’s binding to HVEM induces NF-κB, thus enhancing both virus replication and lymphocyte responses (Sciortino et al. 2008). This immune enhancement has been proposed to mitigate early host mortality and may contribute to latency maintenance and reactivation dynamics (Knickelbein et al. 2008). However, further investigation is necessary to clarify how gD affects immune modulation during infection.

HCMV utilizes two distinct envelope glycoprotein complexes for cell attachment and entry: a trimeric (gH/gL/gO) as well as a pentameric (gH/gL/UL128/130/131A) complex (Sinzger 2008). The trimeric complex specifically targets platelet-derived growth factor receptor-α (PDGFR-α), a member of the receptor tyrosine kinase 3 (RTK3) family (Kabanova et al. 2016). Entry into fibroblasts is facilitated by this trimer, which also promotes cell-free and cell-associated viral spread (Wu et al. 2017). The trimer is hypothesized to compete with the platelet derived growth factor (PDGF) as ligand, suggested by its comparatively higher binding affinity to PDGFR-α (Kschonsak et al. 2021). Interestingly, unlike many receptor-virus interactions that activate signalling pathways, HCMV engagement of PDGFR-α does not activate downstream signalling. Instead, HCMV binding inhibits PDGFR-α signalling, disrupting the normal cellular pathway as indicated by reduced phosphorylation of PDGFR-α monomers and downstream AKT in infected MRC-5 fibroblasts.

Human herpesvirus 6 A (HHV-6A; formally known as human beta herpesvirus 6A; see Table 1) exploits CD46 as its primary entry receptor. Successful binding requires a four-component complex (gH/gL/gQ1/gQ2), as neither the individual components nor the sub-complexes are sufficient for cellular attachment (Tang et al. 2011). CD46 is a type-1 glycoprotein found on the surface of all nucleated human cells identified (Liszewski et al. 1991). The use of this widely expressed receptor by HHV-6A may account for its capability to infect a variety of cell types in vitro and its association with various clinical manifestations (Santoro et al. 1999). CD46 belongs to the regulator of complement activation (RCA) protein family and typically acts as an inhibitor of the complement system by deactivating C3b and C4b, thereby protecting host cells from immune-mediated injury (Liszewski et al. 1991) (see the Immunity and immune evasion section).

Kaposi’s sarcoma–associated herpesvirus (KSHV: formally known as human gamma herpesvirus 8; see Table 1) attaches to the ephrin receptor tyrosine kinase A2 (EphA2) via its gH/gL glycoproteins, triggering the receptor’s phosphorylation and subsequent viral endocytosis (Hahn and Desrosiers 2014). Pretreating epithelial or endothelial cells with soluble ephrinA4, a natural ligand for EphA2, markedly reduces KSHV infection, highlighting ligand competition (Hahn et al. 2012). EphA2 plays a crucial role in angiogenesis; binding to ephrin-A1 and subsequent phosphorylation activates the MAPK and PI3K/Akt pathways, promoting endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and survival while also working in concert with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) to stimulate new blood vessel formation (Nehal et al. 2024). This suggests that KSHV hijacks EphA2 for pathogenesis and chronic infection. By hijacking EphA2, KSHV co-opts these signalling pathways, enhancing VEGF expression and driving the tumour-like angiogenesis characteristics which is a hallmark of Kaposi’s sarcoma (KS) (Kerr et al. 2017).

Superinfection exclusion

HSV-1’s gD can trigger the turnover of HVEM on primary human T cells, leading to receptor downregulation (Stiles et al. 2010). Reduced HVEM expression significantly impacts immune regulation and the responses to HSV. Paradoxically, decreased receptor availability may also limit viral spread between cells; however, this tradeoff may be outweighed by the importance of this mechanism for superinfection exclusion (SIE) (Perdoncini Carvalho et al. 2022). Downregulation of other HSV receptors such as nectin-1 (Stiles and Krummenacher 2010) suggests protection from superinfection by other, less-related viruses. Indeed, co-expression of the shared receptor and gD from either HSV-1, bovine herpesvirus 1 (BHV-1; formally known as bovine alpha herpesvirus 1; see Table 1) or pseudorabies virus (PRV; formally known as suid alpha herpesvirus 1; see Table 1) protects against superinfection from the other viruses (Geraghty et al. 2000), underscoring the evolutionary advantage of SIE.

gD-mediated superinfection exclusion by both homologous and heterologous viruses has been well documented for several alpha herpesviruses (Dasika and Letchworth 2000). In HSV, gD-dependent SIE is attributed to a specific domain near leucine-25 and is typically observed between 4 and 8 h postinfection (Campadelli-Fiume et al. 1990). Additionally, gD leucine-50 and glutamine-52 also seem play a role as changes in both positions allow for more superinfection (Höfler et al. 2025). However, Criddle et al. demonstrated that SIE in both PRV and HSV-1 can occur as early as 2 h postinfection, suggesting a mechanism independent of gD expression (Criddle et al. 2016). Such early exclusion implies the involvement of immediate-early viral genes and/or tegument proteins. Additionally, it seems plausible that herpesviruses may exploit host cellular factors to mediate SIE at this early stage, though this hypothesis remains to be tested.

Despite the common outcome of SIE across alpha herpesviruses, the underlying mechanisms of gD-independent SIE appear to differ between HSV-1 and PRV (Cwick et al. 2022). In PRV, SIE leads to reduced capsid internalization, with virions accumulating at the cell periphery and inhibition occurring at both direct fusion and low-endocytic entry pathways. In contrast, HSV-1 SIE does not significantly impact capsid internalization, despite a similar pattern of peripheral accumulation. This implies that HSV-1 employs postentry mechanisms to suppress the replication of superinfecting viruses, potentially through host or viral factors that act downstream of entry (e.g. virus host shut-off protein, Vhs; see gene expression chapter). Recently, the role of syncytia formation by gK has been found to enhance SIE (Höfler et al. 2025). Further research is needed to delineate the molecular basis of these differences and determine the exact stage at which secondary infection is blocked and to elucidate underlying mechanisms.

HHV-6A infection also leads to a significant downregulation of CD46 expression not only in productively infected cells but also in the uninfected bystander cells (Grivel et al. 2003). This reduction compromises CD46-mediated protection against autologous complement activation, which may contribute to extensive complement-driven damage within infected lymphoid tissue, while also providing substantial superinfection exclusion (Lusso 2006) (see the Immunity and immune evasion section).

Gene expression

Gene products are essential in the production of virions. However, host gene expression entails a delicate trade-off. Replication and reproduction promoting host factors are essential while antiviral restriction factors are to be avoided through multiple mechanisms. Conversely, host cells aim to suppress viral transcription to limit spread and pathogenesis. Thus, herpesvirus infection represents a molecular tug-of-war over transcriptional and post-transcriptional control.

Competition over transcriptional control

Herpesviruses actively promote their own gene expression while simultaneously repressing host transcription. For example, the HSV-1 transactivator ICP4 directs preferential recruitment of RNA-polymerase (Pol) II to viral promoters (Dremel and DeLuca 2019). In addition, HSV also actively undermines host gene transcription by post-translational modification of Pol II and utilizes a plethora of other mechanisms to repress host gene expression (Hennig et al. 2021). Among them are reduction of promoter-proximal pausing (Birkenheuer and Baines 2020), repression of transcription termination (Wang et al. 2020), alternative polyadenylation (Wang et al. 2021), and altering splice sites (Tang et al. 2019). Beyond transcriptional regulation, herpesviruses also employ endonucleases to restrict host gene expression by mRNA degradation (e.g. Vhs in HSV-1) and translational arrest (Eke et al. 2024).

Host cells, however, possess countermeasures to restrict viral transcription. The interferon-stimulated host factor Schlafen 5 (SLFN5), for example, interferes with Pol II binding to viral promoters (Kim et al. 2021), whereas other antiviral proteins like IFI16, PML, or Daxx epigenetically silence HSV-1 genes (Merkl et al. 2018). Similarly, ATF1 antagonizes HHV-6A lytic gene expression via an interferon beta–mediated mechanism (Aktar et al. 2022). Gene expression in KSHV is restricted by CTCF and cohesin DNA binding, by m6A mRNA methylation and by regulating RNA granules (Li et al. 2014, Hesser et al. 2018).

Noncoding RNAs

Herpesviruses utilize noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNAs (miRNAs) and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), to influence transcriptional regulation, immune modulation, and pathogenesis. In all three herpesvirus subfamilies, miRNAs play an important role in modulating viral and host gene expression by interfering with mRNA translation (Sorel and Dewals 2016). Similarly, lncRNAs modulate gene expression by interacting with proteins, RNA, and DNA at multiple stages (Ahmed and Liu 2018).

In addition to encoding their own ncRNAs, herpesviruses modulate host miRNAs (Cokarić Brdovčak et al. 2018). These include miRNAs that regulate antiviral immunity, apoptosis, and viral replication. For example, HSV-1’s ICP0 promotes polyubiquitination and degradation of ZEB, thereby lifting transcriptional repression of the miR-183 cluster and promoting upregulation of several miRNAs (Lutz et al. 2017). Viral miRNAs are particularly important in early stages of infection; however, after viral DNA replication, their nuclear export is blocked in an ICP27-dependent manner (Pan et al. 2019). Furthermore, viral miRNAs are post-transcriptionally edited, especially in latently infected cells (Zubković et al. 2023). Hyperediting, however, is highly species specific, arguing for distinct selection pressures to gain regulatory control.

Host-encoded lncRNAs further regulate innate antiviral responses in infected cells, by binding DNA and modifying chromatin states (Ouyang et al. 2016). Such lncRNAs (like NARV and BIC) respond to herpesvirus infection and mediate various immune functions (Ahmed and Liu 2018). Viral lncRNAs include latency-associated transcript (LAT) in HSV, β2.7, 5, and 7.2 in HCMV or Epstein–Barr Virus (EBV; formally known as human gamma herpesvirus 4; see Table 1)–encoded RNAs (EBERs). Many of those are required for effective lytic gene silencing during latency as described later; however, β2.7 is highly expressed during lytic infection (Gatherer et al. 2011) and involved in preventing apoptosis and continuous ATP production (Reeves et al. 2007).

Telomerase and transformation

Another important noncoding RNA is telomerase RNA (TR). Certain herpesviruses, such as MDV, encode their own copy of viral telomerase RNA (vTR). Both telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and vTR significantly contribute to the development and malignancy of MDV-induced lymphomas. This has been demonstrated by deletion of both vTR copies in the highly oncogenic MDV strain RB-1B, which resulted in significantly reduced tumour incidence, without affecting lytic or latent replication (Kheimar et al. 2017). vTR is regulated by the oncoprotein c-Myc, whose expression is, in turn, induced by Meq, MDV’s major oncoprotein (Shkreli et al. 2007). Meq is a basic leucine zipper DNA binding protein, which can repress or promote viral growth depending on its interaction partners (Reinke et al. 2010). This points towards competition for cellular leucine zippers, leading to manipulation of host gene expression, transformation, and virulence. Notably, vTR-driven oncogenesis does not appear to depend on TERT activity, as replacement of vTR with EBV’s noncoding RNAs EBER-1 and 2 (non-TRs) restored tumorigenesis (Kheimar and Kaufer 2018). Furthermore, TRs exhibit anti-apoptotic functions in a highly species-dependent manner, highlighting an evolutionary arms race between host and virus over apoptotic control (Kheimar et al. 2023).

Other herpesviruses also employ strategies to modulate host telomeres. HCMV induces hTERT expression, through histone modifications mediated by immediate early proteins (Strååt et al. 2009, Cavanaugh et al. 2025). This may promote lytic infection over latency and might play a role in HCMV-mediated oncogenesis. In EBV, however, hTERT inhibits lytic replication, pushing the virus towards a mostly latent lifestyle (Terrin et al. 2007). Since hTERT expression prolongs the survival of B lymphocytes, this likely represents a viral strategy to sustain long-term latency (Bechter et al. 2002, Tchirkov et al. 2004). Indeed, latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1) drives telomerase activation via NF- κB and ERK1/2 signalling pathways, independently of c-Myc, Akt, or mTOR involvement (Terrin et al. 2008). Similarly, KSHV regulates hTERT expression via direct interaction of latency associated nuclear antigen (LANA) with SP1 (Verma et al. 2004).

DNA metabolism

Mobile genetic elements

Viral infections induce transposable elements (TEs) (Macchietto et al. 2020). As SGEs, TEs propagate by inserting themselves into host genomes, thereby enhancing their own evolutionary success, often at the expense of host fitness (Hurst et al. 1996, Burt and Trivers 2006). Consequently, TEs are mostly considered genetic parasites; however, some have been domesticated by their hosts to fulfil molecular purposes (Cosby et al. 2021, Hale 2022). Their presence is a constant part of genome evolution, whether it is to defend against them or to use them to obtain new genetic material.

The effects of TEs on herpesvirus replication and reproduction are manifold with sometimes opposing effects. Induction of endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), for example, protects from vaginal HSV-2 infection (Jayewickreme et al. 2021), yet their activation in HSV-1 brain infections has been linked to dementia and Alzheimer’s disease (Licastro and Porcellini 2021). Furthermore, herpesvirus-induced ERV activity has been implicated in autoimmune disorders such as multiple sclerosis (Meier et al. 2021). In HCMV infection, LINE-1 elements are co-opted to facilitate viral DNA replication (Hwang et al. 2024). LINE-1 expression there is induced by YY1 and RUNX3-mediated transcription. Direct interaction of UL44, HCMV’s DNA-polymerase processivity factor, with the LINE-1 ribonucleoprotein complex accelerates viral DNA replication by increased DNA-damage responses in replication compartments. Induction of LINE-1 elements in KSHV lymphoma has been connected to oncogenesis (Ahuja et al. 2021). Due to their structural similarity to viruses, expression of TEs can induce robust antiviral responses, suggesting that their activation is tightly linked to innate immune sensing mechanisms (Jayewickreme et al. 2021).

A key regulator of TE activation is DUX4, a germline transcription factor (Hendrickson et al. 2017). Herpesviruses employ protein kinases such as US3 to inactivate KAP1, leading to reduced histone methylation and transcriptional activation of DUX4 (Tan et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the HSV-1 immediate early transactivators ICP0 and ICP4 are sufficient to induce TEs (Neugebauer et al. 2025). This epigenetic remodelling occurs within 4–8 h of infection, and depletion of DUX4 abolishes viral replication (Neugebauer et al. 2025, Tan et al. 2024).

Beyond their direct impact on viral replication, TEs are recognized as major drivers of evolutionary innovation, shaping antiviral immune defences (Rheinemann et al. 2021, Hale 2022). Other DNA viruses, like poxviruses, exploit LINE-1-mediated retro-transposition to capture host genes (Fixsen et al. 2022). As herpesviruses are also known to capture immune modulatory genes, retro-transposition likely represents a key mechanism underlying this process (Schönrich et al. 2017).

Cell cycle control

Herpesviruses rely on their host cells’ machinery to support productive viral genome replication (Packard and Dembowski 2021). On the other hand, hosts actively try to prevent viral usage of the DNA replication and repair machinery, resulting in a finely balanced conflict over replicative resources. Consequently, lytic replication of herpesviruses is nearly exclusively occurring in growth-arrested cells, with the viruses encoding multiple factors to manipulate cell cycle regulation at different checkpoints.

The phenomenon of host DNA synthesis inhibition during HSV-1 infection was discovered in 1988, identifying a virion-associated factor as responsible for cell cycle arrest (de Bruyn Kops and Knipe 1988). Subsequent work attributed this effect to ICP0 (Hobbs and DeLuca 1999). However, ICP0 deletion mutants were also found to induce the arrest, likely via an ICP27-mediated mechanism (Flemington 2001).

Similar to HSV-1, HCMV induces G1/S arrest (Dittmer and Mocarski 1997), largely mediated by UL69 and IE2 (Hayashi et al. 2000). Additionally, UL97, a viral cyclin-dependent-kinase (CDK) homologue, modulates interactions with cyclin H, influencing viral replication, host regulation, and drug resistance (Marschall et al. 2024). CDK homologues are functionally diverse but conserved across all three subfamilies of herpesviruses (Fig. 3), with beta herpesviruses uniquely featuring NLS and RXL domains (Bogdanow et al. 2020). HCMV also promotes re-entry into S phase by multiple mechanisms, potentially to sustain latent reservoirs (Bogdanow et al. 2021). This re-entry is not only facilitated by UL97 but also by UL82 and subsequent retinoblastoma protein family degradation/inactivation (Kalejta and Shenk 2003). The fact that S phase re-entry is mediated by two distinct mechanisms in HCMV highlights the evolutionary significance of this control point. Other beta herpesviruses like HHV-6A/B induce cell cycle arrests not just at the G1/S but also the G2/M transition point (Øster et al. 2005), likely as a control point to ensure genome integration (see the Latency and reactivation section). HHV-6B infection also induces p53 expression, G2/M arrest, however, seems to be U14 and EDD mediated, which could promote replication (Mori et al. 2015). Cyclin A sequestration by mouse cytomegalovirus (MCMV; formally known as murid beta herpesvirus 1; see Table 1) CDK facilitates host DNA replication shutoff while supporting pro-viral cell signalling (Bogdanow et al. 2020), whereas in HCMV, UL21 targets Cyclin A for degradation (Caffarelli et al. 2013).

Gamma herpesviruses also induce G1/S arrests. In EBV, immediate early proteins BZLF1 and BRLF1, along with late protein BGLF2, contribute to arrest via tumour suppressor p21, p53, and p27 pathways (Huang et al. 2012). Additionally, BORF2 sequesters p53 and deprives its activity; however, APOBEC3B binding engages BORF2, triggering the release of p53 to initiate cell cycle arrest (Yockteng-Melgar et al. 2022). Furthermore, host DNA synthesis and S progression are further delayed by BGLF4 (Chang et al. 2012). Similarly, KSHV utilizes its replication associated protein (RAP) to stall the cell cycle in G1 phase via induction and stabilization of p21 and C/EBPα (Wu et al. 2003). In latency, however, KSHV’s LANA induces S phase by lifting BRD4 and BRD2/RING3 mediated G1 arrest (Ottinger et al. 2006). Notably, KSHV also encodes a cyclin homologue (K-cyclin) capable of either promoting G1/S progression or inducing arrest, depending on its interaction partners (Chang and Li 2008).

Genetic stability & defective viral genomes

Cellular mechanisms such as DNA-damage response proteins, cell cycle control elements, tumour suppressors, shelterins, and recombination machineries collectively safeguard genomic integrity. Herpesviruses engage with these systems to exert their oncogenic potential, accelerate their replication dynamics, and promote long-term persistence in infected hosts (Weitzman et al. 2010).

Herpesviruses rely on host factors to maintain viral genome integrity. For instance, HCMV recruits host translesion polymerases to replication compartments to aid in replication, maintain genome integrity, and limit defective viral genome (DVG) production (Zeltzer et al. 2022). In Burkitt’s lymphoma (BL) cells, EBV drives chromosomal aberrations, including dicentric chromosomes and telomere fusions, through mechanisms involving telomere dysfunction and DNA damage, thereby facilitating transformation and tumour progression (Kamranvar et al. 2007). While BGLF4 promotes DNA damage, chromosomal aberrations, and condensation (Chang et al. 2012), EBV nuclear antigen 1 (EBNA1) induces oxidative stress that displaces the telomere-protective protein TRF2, enhancing genomic instability and potentially triggering alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) (Kamranvar and Masucci 2011). KSHV infection similarly induces ALT, possibly by destabilizing telomere-associated genomes or hijacking ALT-linked break-induced repair (BIR) pathways for viral replication (Lippert et al. 2021). Additionally, LANA interacts with host proteins involved in telomere maintenance, chromatin remodelling, and DNA replication, driving chromosomal instability, telomer shortenings, and ultimately malignancies (Shamay et al. 2012).

DVGs are replication-defective versions of parental genomes that carry mutations, truncations, or rearrangements that prevent independent propagation (Brennan and Sun 2024). Initially described for RNA viruses and mostly considered artefacts of in vitro infections, DVGs are now known to also arise in vivo, influencing viral replication, immune modulation, and virulence (Vignuzzi and López 2019). Depending on whether these genomes contain origins of replication (oriS/L) and packaging sites (pac1/2), DVGs may form defective particles (DPs), a transmissible subset with potential evolutionary significance (Brennan and Sun 2024). DVGs and DPs compete with parental virus for cellular and viral resources, such as polymerases and terminases. Since DVGs are usually shorter and contain nonfunctional proteins that uncouple them from selection, co-infection often favours them (Leeks et al. 2021). Paradoxically, DVGs interfere with parental viral replication but need parental gene products to replicate. This dynamic underlies the Von Magnus effect, where DVG abundance oscillates with parental virus replication (Marriott and Dimmock 2010).

Herpesviruses are known to produce DVGs. For example, equine herpesvirus 1 (EHV-1; formally known as equid alpha herpesvirus 1; see Table 1) DVGs have been described and studied since the 1970s (Ebner and O’Callaghan 2006). These consist of a substantially shortened genome of ~6–7.5 kb (~5% of the parental genome) containing the UL3 and UL4 genes, and one of two new hybrid ORFs containing fragments of early regulatory genes IR4 fused to fragments of UL5 but retaining oriS/L and pac1/2 sequences important for initiation of replication and genome maturation. Importantly, those DVGs are found in Syrian hamster models of EHV-1 infection and recombinant forms were shown to persist longitudinally in vitro while still interfering with parental replication (Ebner et al. 2008).

HSV-1 DVGs have been studied for a long time and are reported to feature large deletions, some retaining only ~10% of the parental genome and others forming numerous head-to-tail sequence reiterations (Frenkel et al. 1980). Sequencing data confirm the presence of defective genomes in experimental and clinical samples alike (Szpara et al. 2014, Shitrit et al. 2023).

KSHV’s defective genome KV-1 (found in KSs and PEL cells) lacks 82 kb of lytic genes; however, using parental virus, this variant was nonetheless packaged into infectious virions (Deng et al. 2004). Enhanced cell proliferation and transformation point to a possible role of KV-1 in establishing harsher malignancies.

How herpesvirus DVGs impact the viral life cycle is not fully understood. Presence of DVGs, particularly those that get packaged (i.e. DPs), effectively reduce the overall productive offspring of the growing population (Shitrit et al. 2023). Therefore, they might provide a source of antagonism that selects for adaptive countermeasures such as extensive SIE, privatization of gene products, or compartmentalized replication sites. Furthermore, DVGs might play a role for persistence and latency, as well as for oncogenesis (Deng et al. 2004, Ebner et al. 2008). However, there might be beneficial aspects of DVGs, such as antigenic decoys, reservoirs of genetic diversity, or novel hybrid proteins. As of now, more work is necessary to fully appreciate the multiple roles DVGs play during the herpesvirus life cycle.

Detection and recovery of DVGs can be challenging as biophysical methods are labour-intensive and ineffective (Marriott and Dimmock 2010). However, as nucleic acid sequencing became more widely available, bioinformatic tools can readily detect genomic rearrangements (Sotcheff et al. 2023). Furthermore, long-read sequencing and especially direct DNA sequencing can greatly ease the task of characterizing viral populations in a quantifiable manner. Future applications of single-cell sequencing promise even greater resolution, enabling spatial and temporal mapping of DVGs within complex viral populations (Vignuzzi and López 2019).

Packaging and egress

Herpesvirus particles share a conserved architecture: linear dsDNA packaged into a pseudo-icosahedral nucleocapsid, encased in a proteinaceous tegument layer (comparable to an extended form of matrix known for other viruses), and enclosed within a lipid envelope containing viral glyco- and membrane proteins. These particles—or substructures thereof—require transport from the nucleus, cytoplasm, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the cell surface, where mature virions are budding. Given the complexity of this process, it is tightly regulated but also a frequent site of antagonism.

Capsid assembly and DNA packaging

High-resolution structural studies have revealed conserved features across herpesvirus capsids (Yu et al. 2017, Dai et al. 2018), including major and small capsid proteins, triplexes as well as one vertical portal. Herpesvirus capsids occur in three forms: A, B, and C-capsids: A-capsids are empty, B-capsids contain scaffolding proteins, and C-capsids are filled with DNA (Tandon et al. 2015). While the exact function of these respective forms is unknown, all three can acquire an envelope (Tandon et al. 2015). However, only C capsids do successfully associate with tegument proteins (Wang et al. 2022), arguing for a quality control mechanism to ensure structural maturity (Toropova et al. 2011). An intriguing speculation might be that immature capsids may act as molecular decoys that adsorb antibodies, similar to hepatitis B virus like particles (Rydell et al. 2017).

DNA packaging may represent another quality control step that invites competition among viral genomes. Assembly of the terminase complex and recognition of pac1 and pac2 sites are crucial for packaging (Heming et al. 2017). DVGs exploit this step by rapid duplication of packaging sites, enabling preferential encapsidation (Shitrit et al. 2023). This resembles meiotic drive phenomena in eukaryotes, where centromeric satellite DNA and histones bias inheritance during female meiosis, often contributing to genetic incompatibilities and speciation events (Akera et al. 2019). Analogously, packaging competition might contribute to the extensive intrahost speciation observed across herpesvirus lineages (Brito et al. 2021).

Tegument

Viral tegument proteins are crucial for rapid viral replication. For example, in HSV-1, VP22 interacts with the TATA box binding protein–associated factor (TAF-I) to disrupt nucleosome assembly on viral DNA (van Leeuwen et al. 2003). Furthermore, VP22 has been shown to inhibit inflammasome formation, effectively promoting viral spread (Maruzuru et al. 2018). Additionally, Vhs selectively modulates host mRNA stability (Strom and Frenkel 1987). In concert with the translational factor eIF4H (Feng et al. 2001), Vhs interferes with cell survival, apoptosis, and immune responses (Esclatine et al. 2004). Similarly, ICP0, a multifunctional viral protein highlighted throughout this review, also antagonizes host processes by selective protein degradation (Smith et al. 2011).

Apart from viral factors, herpesviruses also incorporate and repurpose cellular machinery. Among the key host factors are Ras-related proteins in brain (Rab GTPases), a family of proteins that regulate intracellular membrane trafficking (Spearman 2018). These proteins play crucial roles in glycoprotein transport and virion morphogenesis in HSV-1 (Raza et al. 2018, Spearman 2018), while EBV exploits Rab8a, Rab10, and Rab11a during egress (Nanbo 2020).

A comprehensive proteomic analysis detected 10 members of this family in extracellular HSV-1 virions, including Rab5, Rab6, and Rab11 (Loret et al. 2008). Moreover, knockdown of Rab5a, Rab6a, and Rab10 in the mature virions reduced virus production by at least 50% (Stegen et al. 2013). Similarly, kinesin motor proteins are packaged to facilitate intracellular transport and egress (Pegg et al. 2021), highlighting the importance of packaged host factors for viral egress and transmission.

Yet, host derived cargo can also mediate antiviral defence. Cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate dinucleotide (cGAMP) has been detected in sucrose-purified MCMV virions (Bridgeman et al. 2015, Gentili et al. 2015). Upon recognizing cytosolic DNA, cGAMP synthase (cGAS) produces cGAMP, which subsequently activates the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway (Deng et al. 2022). Virion-associated cGAMP can be transferred to neighbouring cells, to rapidly amplify the immune response and to limit viral spread (Bridgeman et al. 2015, Gentili et al. 2015). It is highly likely that more antiviral signalling or restriction factors are packaged into budding virions as a defensive strategy. Especially, protein–protein interaction studies of mature herpesvirus virions might shed some light the mobility of antiviral host factors (Bogdanow et al. 2023).

Egress

For herpesviruses to spread, nucleocapsids must exit the nucleus, acquire tegument and envelope, and ultimately bud from the plasma membrane. The viral nuclear egress complex (NEC) disrupts the nuclear lamina, a network of intermediate filament proteins, to permit capsid translocation across the nuclear envelope (Mettenleiter 2016). HSV-1 employs multiple strategies to induce lamina disassembly: UL34 recruits protein kinase C (PKC) to phosphorylate lamin B (Muranyi et al. 2002, Park and Baines 2006), while viral kinases US3 and UL13 further contribute to lamina breakdown (Mou et al. 2007, Cano-Monreal et al. 2009). ICP34.5 also enhances lamina disassembly by recruiting PKC to the nuclear membrane via the cellular protein p32 (Wang et al. 2014). In response, host cells activate Tripartite Motif Containing (TRIM) 43, which is upregulated during infection, to degrade pericentrin (Neugebauer et al. 2019). This suppresses transcriptionally active viral chromatin and hinders replication, pointing towards a co-dependency of nuclear structure, viral gene expression, and nuclear egress. Furthermore, transmembrane protein 140 (TMEM140) interacts with UL31 to disrupt NEC function and restrict viral spread (Guan et al. 2014).

Additionally, tetherin, an interferon-inducible restriction factor that physically tethers budding virions to the cell surface presents a barrier to viral spread (Neil et al. 2008). Herpesviruses have evolved multiple strategies to neutralize tetherin’s effects. In HSV-1, Vhs acts by reducing tetherin mRNA levels (Zenner et al. 2013), while gM supports the removal of tetherin from the membrane (Blondeau et al. 2013). Similarly, HSV-2 (formally known as human alpha herpesvirus 2; see Table 1) employs gB, gD, gH, and gL to counteract tetherin, with gD specifically targeting tetherin’s coiled-coil domain, facilitating lysosomal degradation (Liu et al. 2016a). KSHV relies on K5, which mediates ubiquitin-dependent tetherin degradation (Pardieu et al. 2010). EBV employs its BFRF1 protein to recruit the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) machinery to the nucleus-associated membrane (Bigalke and Heldwein 2016). The ESCRT machinery is responsible for membrane remodelling and vesicle formation in cellular processes like endocytosis and cytokinesis. While many enveloped viruses exploit ESCRT to exit the cell (Ahmad and Wilson 2020), some hosts (e.g. mice and new world monkeys) developed ESCRT-derived restriction factors, underscoring the antagonistic co-evolution in this pathway (Rheinemann et al. 2021). Together, these strategies exemplify the evolutionary arms race shaping viral egress mechanisms.

Latency and reactivation

A defining feature of herpesviruses is their ability to persist in infected cells through latency, a state that enables long-term survival and immune evasion, and ultimately maximizes transmission potential.

Latency entails conflict at all possible levels. At the host–virus level, immune surveillance exerts strong selective pressure for escape and stealth. However, conflict between viruses is just as prominent, as latency reduces opportunities for superinfection while reactivation can be triggered by heterologous viral infections. At the intragenomic level, the tension between lytic and latent expression programs lies at the heart of herpesvirus biology. While latency secures persistence, it prevents replication and horizontal spread; lytic reactivation ensures transmission but exposes the virus to immune clearance. Navigating this balance requires finely tuned strategies that integrate viral programs with host and environmental cues.

Establishing latency

Genomic maintenance is required for effective persistence. For herpesviruses, such as HSV-1 or VZV (both alpha herpesviruses), which typically establish latency in terminally differentiated, nondividing cells, genome replication is unnecessary. In contrast, herpesviruses like EBV, KSHV, HHV-6, or MDV (gamma, beta, and alpha herpesviruses) establish latency in actively dividing cells, where genome loss poses a significant threat. These viruses rely on cellular as well as viral proteins for genomic maintenance (Fig. 4). For example, EBV utilizes EBNA1 to bind its genome (oriP), mediate low levels of replication and tether it to the host chromosome (Hammerschmidt and Sugden 2013), while KSHV utilizes LANA in a similar fashion (Juillard et al. 2019). An alternative strategy observed in HHV-6A/B, HHV-7, and MDV is the integration of the viral genome into the host’s telomeres via telomeric repeat arrays, bypassing the need for active genomic maintenance (Kheimar et al. 2017, Aimola et al. 2020, Cheng et al. 2026).

The struggle between expression programs is marked by reciprocal repression. Latent herpesviruses suppress lytic gene expression by multiple means. The major latent transcript in HSV-1 is LAT, an lncRNA that is involved in genome silencing and transcriptional repression (Nicoll et al. 2016). Other ncRNAs involved in suppressing lytic gene expression include host-encoded miRNAs in HSV-1-infected neurons (Pan et al. 2014), miR-U86 in HHV-6 (Nukui et al. 2015), and miUL112-1 in HCMV (Grey et al. 2007). Latency is further stabilized by viral proteins that repress lytic gene expression, including ORF63 in VZV (Cohen et al. 2004) and U94 in HHV-6 (Caselli et al. 2006).

Long-term persistence additionally requires that infected cells evade cell death. Herpesviruses deploy multiple anti-apoptotic factors, including miRNAs derived from LAT in HSV-1 and 2 (Liu et al. 2016b) and vTR in MDV (Kheimar et al. 2023). HCMV utilizes a wide repertoire of anti-apoptotic factors (Collins-McMillen et al. 2018), while EBV prevents apoptosis via LMP1 (Zeng et al. 2020) and KSHV via miR-K12-9 (Suffert et al. 2011).

Dynamic latency

Herpesvirus infection was traditionally considered to exist in a binary state—lytic or latent—with gene expression and resulting protein production either fully active or entirely suppressed (Howley et al. 2021). It was assumed that viral gene expression is limited to specific RNAs that support latency or suppress replication. However, emerging research across herpesvirus families challenges this model. Increasing evidence suggests that latency is a dynamic and heterogeneous state, characterized by low-level or intermittent expression of lytic genes, influenced by cell type and microenvironment (Collins-McMillen and Goodrum 2017).

In HSV-1 animal models, for example, latent genomes occasionally express the immediate-early transactivator ICP4 (Ma et al. 2014). Expression of other lytic transcripts, such as ICP0, may paradoxically inhibit reactivation and stabilize latency (Raja et al. 2016). Similar expression profiles have been found in other herpesviruses (Krishnan et al. 2004).

Dynamic latency is heavily influenced by epigenetic modifications, where the chromatin state of viral genes plays a crucial role. Recent evidence suggests that chromatin states remain dynamic, allowing for reversing silencing and enabling ‘leaky’ gene expression (Cliffe et al. 2009). A more relaxed chromatin state allows for occasional gene expression, which can lead to sporadic reactivation events, while the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) helps to reinforce latency by catalysing the methylation of histone H3K27, a modification that tightly represses gene expression (Toth et al. 2013). Global transcriptional activation, e.g. through histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors can further shift this balance towards reactivation (Ghosh et al. 2025). Other host-mediated suppressors of reactivation include transcription factor YY1 (Chang et al. 2011) or nuclear bodies (Jan Fada et al. 2021).

Reactivation

Reactivation represents the virus’s mechanism to overcome lytic repression and reassert transcriptional control. In HSV-1, this requires the coordinated action of key transactivators like ICP0, ICP4, and VP16 (Halford et al. 2001). Interestingly, the MDV key regulator Meq is acting both, as an activator and repressor of viral and host gene expression, underscoring the complexity of this regulatory switch (Qian et al. 1996).Analogous regulators are found in other herpesviruses, including IE1/2 in HCMV (Korioth et al. 1996) and switch genes like RTA in KSHV (Toth et al. 2010). As RTA is antagonized by LANA and PRC2, reactivation in KSHV entails a constant fight over the chromatin state (Toth et al. 2016). The outcome is a biphasic process: an initial, nonsequential burst of gene expression to accumulate sufficient quantities of transactivators (Cliffe et al. 2015), followed by a second, more orderly cascade that produces viral progeny.

This interplay of repressive complexes, chromatin state modifications, and selective viral gene expression enables herpesviruses to maintain latency while remaining receptive for reactivation under favourable conditions, highlighting an intricate regulatory network. Environmental sensing is particularly important for herpesvirus persistence: pathways culminating in mTOR integrate signals of nutrient status, energy levels, oxygen availability, and stress, directly influencing reactivation (Moorman and Shenk 2010). Hypoxia appears to be a particularly important trigger of reactivation and promotes lytic infection in MDV, HHV-6A, EBV, and KSHV (Reich et al. 2024). Viruses exploit the host’s HIF-1 signalling cascade and often incorporate hypoxia response elements into their promoters, thereby tuning replication to oxygen levels (Haque et al. 2003, Kraus et al. 2017). Other reactivation triggers include immune suppression, stress responses (e.g. molecular, physical, or physiological), superinfection, hormonal changes, and cell differentiation (Tang et al. 2012, Cliffe et al. 2015, Reich et al. 2024).

How did latency evolve?

Despite being a hallmark of herpesvirus biology, the origins of latency remain elusive. Because latency strategies align more closely with infected cell types than with viral lineages, they appear to be products of convergent evolution rather than ancestral inheritance. Intricate antagonistic interactions, either between co- and superinfecting viruses or between host and virus, might have selected for persistence. Yet, many aspects of latency and especially their evolutionary consequences remain elusive. If latency evolved primarily as an immune evasion strategy, why have so few viral families adopted it? True latency requires a complex regulatory network that balances persistence with reactivation, ensuring that infection does not terminate in an evolutionary dead end. Reactivation itself hinges on the capacity to sense host signals and time replication to maximize transmission while minimizing immune clearance. This delicate balance reflects a complex evolutionary trade-off beyond straightforward immune evasion. Herpesviruses have fine-tuned latency and reactivation through extended co-evolution with their hosts, while other viruses favour simpler strategies centered on rapid replication and spread.

One hypothesis proposes that latency may occur by default when immediate-early gene expression fails to initiate, or when metabolic conditions cannot support lytic replication (Marshall et al. 2000). However, other studies suggest active promotion of latency by specific viral proteins, indicating that latency is not merely a fallback but a regulated program (Goodrum et al. 2007). Latency is thus a contested battlefield where viral and host factors, as well as competing expression programs, determine the outcome (Fig. 4). Genes that support latency must continually suppress lytic replication to remain evolutionarily relevant. Lytic genes, however, are less easily disrupted due to their essential function. This asymmetry may explain why latency manifests in diverse forms across herpesviruses, whereas the lytic program remains comparatively conserved. Elucidating the specific conflicts that select for latency might lead to a more complete understanding of herpesvirus evolution.

Immunity and immune evasion

Virtually every stage of the herpesvirus life cycle intersects with the host’s immune system. Because herpesviruses co-evolve with their hosts, immune defence and viral countermeasures form the central axis of their evolutionary conflict. Vertebrate immune genes rank among the most rapidly evolving loci, reflecting ongoing arms races that shape pathogen recognition, innate defence, and adaptive responses. Unsurprisingly, herpesviruses have evolved an extensive arsenal of strategies to evade or manipulate both innate and adaptive immunity.

Innate immune effectors

The first line of host defence relies on the detection of viral pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) or RIG-I-like receptors. These activate interferon (IFN) and NF-κB pathways, culminating in type I IFN production and the induction of hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) via JAK/STAT signalling (Murphy et al. 2017). Many ISGs function as restriction factors, directly targeting viral replication steps. Viral ligands potently stimulate TLR2 signalling, eliciting proinflammatory and antiviral responses. To counter this, HSV-1 deploys ICP0, which degrades TLR adaptors, blocks TLR2–MyD88 complex assembly, and recruits USP7 to the cytoplasm to inhibit TRAF6 and IKKγ activation, thereby dampening IFN production and inflammation (Kurt-Jones et al. 2010).

Restriction factors are the cornerstone of antiviral defence (see earlier sections for examples). They target different steps of the viral replication cycle to prevent viral reproduction and spread. Among others, herpesvirus E3 ubiquitin ligases, like HSV-1’s ICP0, target restriction factors—such as SLFN5, IFI16, and ZAP—for degradation (Rodríguez et al. 2020). US11 shields viral RNA from recognition by PKR, while viral kinases interfere with interferon signalling at multiple steps (Xing et al. 2012). Tegument protein UL36 further suppresses innate signalling by blocking IRF-3 activation, thereby preventing ISG induction in HSV-1- and HCMV-infected cells (Wang et al. 2013). HHV-6B mitigates STAT2 signalling via IE1, thus preventing binding of ISGF3 to IFN-responsive promoter elements (Jaworska et al. 2010), while KSHV suppresses type I and III IFN signalling by viral (v)IRFs (Fuld et al. 2006).

Interaction with lymphocytes

Cytotoxic T-lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells monitor intracellular infections through MHC I–peptide presentation (Murphy et al. 2017). A common viral strategy is therefore to inhibit MHC I antigen presentation. Herpesviruses achieve this by various means. Simplexviruses use ICP47 to block TAP transporters, whereas MDV and other mardiviruses deploy MDV012 to block MHC I presentation (Hearn et al. 2015, Mozzi et al. 2022). In the case of HCMV, antigen presentation is prevented by multiple steps (Ahn et al. 1996), e.g. by blocking TAP via US6 (Ahn et al. 1997) or MHC degradation facilitated by US2 and US11 (Wiertz et al. 1996). Additionally, the MHC I heavy chain can also bind to UL18 (Browne et al. 1990), therefore preventing antigen presentation but still sending inhibitory NK cell signals (Kim et al. 2008). Yet, this only inhibits a subset of NK cells (Prod'homme et al. 2007), driving the second round of the host–pathogen arms race. KSHV and gamma herpesvirus 68 (MHV68; formally known as murid gamma herpesvirus 4; see Table 1) target mature MHC I for proteasomal degradation via K3 (Stevenson et al. 2000). EBVs’ EBNA-1 protein prevents self-peptide presentation by suppressing initiation of translation via a Gly-Ala repeat stretch (Apcher et al. 2010).

The host counters such strategies with NK cell surveillance, which targets cells lacking MHC I. In turn, herpesviruses evolved countermeasures. HCMV UL16 retains NKG2D ligands (MICB, ULBP1, ULBP2) in the ER/Golgi, preventing their presentation and thereby blunting NK activation (Cosman et al. 2001). Interestingly, UL16 appears to have no host homologue, suggesting de novo gene birth. UL40 and UL141 further manipulate NK recognition by stabilizing HLA-E and blocking death receptor presentation (Smith et al. 2013).

Similarly to MHC I, herpesviruses also downregulate MHC II presentation. Compared to the ubiquitously expressed MHC I, MHC II expression is specific for antigen-presenting cells to help mount a tailored adaptive immune response (Murphy et al. 2017). HSV-1 impairs MHC II presentation using Vhs, ICP34.5, and gB-mediated trafficking disruption (Trgovcich et al. 2002, Temme et al. 2009). Additionally, the release of exosomes containing gB and DR from infected cells may play a role in transferring immune information between cells, affecting their response to HSV-1 (Temme et al. 2009). HHV-6 induces the formation of multivesicular bodies, where HHV-6’s glycoproteins B and M are detected, manipulating the host immune response (Mori et al. 2008). HCMV represses MHC II expression by manipulating the JAK/STAT pathway (Sandhu and Buchkovich 2020), while KSHV LANA blocks transactivator binding (Thakker et al. 2015). Finally, cytokines are utilized to circumvent MHC II presentation, as exemplified by MCMV (Redpath et al. 1999). Other herpesviruses repurpose cytokines to manipulate host immune responses; those include viral (v)IL-10 in HCMV (Kotenko et al. 2000) and EBV (Moore et al. 2001) or vIL6 in KSHV (Nicholas et al. 1997).

Contrarily, cell-associated, lymphotropic MDV (Sharma and Witter 1975), while still preventing MHC I presentation, upregulates MHC II to effectively spread to CD4+ lymphocytes (Niikura et al. 2007). Furthermore, upregulation of PD-1 in T-cells delays immune responsiveness and promotes MDV-mediated transformation (Parvizi et al. 2010). In concert, this facilitates cell-to-cell contact between macrophages and T-cells, allowing MDV to progress to their favourable replication site. Additionally, vIL8 also plays a role in lymphocyte attraction (Parcells et al. 2001).

Humoral immunity

Herpesviruses also antagonize antibody and complement responses. In HSV-1, gI, and gE form a complex acting as a viral Fc receptor on infected cells (vFcR). Antibody bipolar bridging, allows degradation of host IgG antibodies specific to HSV that bind simultaneously to the gI/E complex and viral antigens, such as gC, gD, or gB, via their Fc and Fab arms, respectively (Ndjamen et al. 2014). vFcR can even be recycled due to pH-dependent binding (Lubinski et al. 2011). Rapid evolution of vFcR upon mouse anti-gD antibody selection suggests remarkable evolutionary plasticity and highlights the host–pathogen conflict (Höfler et al. 2025). VZV utilizes gI/E homologues for antibody evasion in a similar fashion (Litwin and Grose 1992). HCMV encodes four glycoproteins (gp68, gp34, TLR12, and TLR13) that act as a vFcR (Lilley et al. 2001, Atalay et al. 2002). Unlike the HSV-1’s vFcR, the gp68/antibody complex remains stable across various pH levels, leading to the degradation of the gp68 alongside the antibody and antigen (Corrales-Aguilar et al. 2014).

Complement evasion is equally diverse. In HSV-1 gC binds complement factor C3b, effectively inhibiting opsonization and formation of the membrane attacking complex (Friedman et al. 1984). HCMV evades the complement system by inducing expression and embedding host proteins CD46, CD55, and CD59 into the viral envelope (Lujan et al. 2024). Strikingly, interaction between HHV-6A and its cell receptor, CD46, impacts complement regulation differently. Infection leads to downregulation of CD46 (Lusso 2006), which, in turn, increases complement activation and leads to immunopathology. Age-related macular degeneration (AMD), a leading cause of vision loss in older adults, has been increasingly linked to complement system dysregulation. Emerging evidence indicates that HHV-6A infection may contribute to AMD by reducing CD46 levels in retinal pigment epithelial cells, leading to excessive complement activation (Fierz 2017). Complement activation is also the proposed mechanism for HHV-6’s role in the pathology of MS. Studies showed that a subset of MS patients experiences reactivation of the virus, potentially triggering autoimmunity and the subsequent tissue damage (Fotheringham and Jacobson 2005).

KSHV encodes a complement control protein (KCP/ORF4), a CD55 mimic regulating C3 convertase (Spiller et al. 2006). EBV cleaves C3b and C4b and even exploits complement receptors CD21 and CD35 for entry (Ogembo et al. 2013).

Viral chemokines and receptors

Immune modulatory genes, such as chemokines (ChKs), their receptors (ChKRs), or binding proteins (ChKBPs), are frequently adopted by herpes- and poxviruses (Hernaez and Alcamí 2020). Many of those genes have been captured by retro-transposition (Schönrich et al. 2017, Fixsen et al. 2022); however, there is also some evidence for viral gene duplication and diversification (Brito and Pinney 2020), highlighting the immense pressure to manipulate immune signalling during herpesvirus infections.

Alpha herpesviruses encode ChKBP and ChK analogs (Pontejo et al. 2018). Among HSV glycoproteins the ChKBP gG is particularly diverse (<14% amino acid identity), indicative of extensive diversification. As mentioned above, gG likely originated from a gene duplication of the gD/G precursor (see Attachment and Entry). The ChKBP gC in VZV is unrelated to gG; however, it can bind 27 different ChK with high affinity (González-Motos et al. 2017). Together, these observations suggest strong selective pressures favouring, either extensive diversification or broad-spectrum ChK binding activity. Notably, rather than neutralizing ChKs, gG and gC potentiate their function (Viejo-Borbolla et al. 2012, González-Motos et al. 2017). To date, the biological significance of this phenomenon remains incompletely understood. One prevailing hypothesis suggests that binding and potentiating ChK allows alpha herpesviruses to reach their preferred site of infection. In addition, gL in both HSV and VZV adopts ChK-like folds, suggesting a potential role in chemotactic modulation.

Human beta herpesviruses encode an extensive repertoire of ChKBPs, ChKs, and ChKRs (Pontejo et al. 2018). HCMV blocks CCL5 binding by UL21.5 and utilizes vCXCL1, vCXCL2, and possibly UL128 and UL130 as viral ChKs. Encoded within HCMV’s highly variable region UL/b’ (Prichard et al. 2001), both vCXCL1 and vCXCL2 are related to human CXC ChKs, consistent with ancient host gene capture events. UL128 and UL130 are part of the pentameric entry complex and, like alpha herpesvirus gL, feature ChK folds (Connolly et al. 2021). HHV-6 encodes vCCL4, a viral ChK capable of signalling through multiple chemokine receptors (Dewin et al. 2006). Alternative splicing of vCCL4 allows for further fine-tuning of host cell attraction. Beta herpesviruses also encode also encode numerous viral ChKRs, including US27, US28, UL33, and UL78 in HCMV, as well as U12 and U51 in both HHV-6 and -7 (Pontejo et al. 2018),

Gamma herpesviruses engage in immune modulation with specialized viral ChKs and ChKRs (Pontejo et al. 2018). KSHV encodes vCCL1, vCCL2, and vCCL3, which attract distinct immune cell populations, while gamma herpesvirus gL proteins adopt ChK-like folds. Whereas vCCL1 and vCCL3 are highly homologous to human ChKs, vCCL2 displays exceptionally broad receptor usage, suggesting extensive diversification to engage structurally distinct ChKRs (Lüttichau et al. 2007). Gamma herpesviruses also encode viral ChKRs such as ORF74 in KSHV and BILF1 in EBV. Both receptors are thought to originate from captured host genes (CXCR2 and CXCR4, respectively) and show low amino acid identity (<27%), relative to their cellular counterparts (Pontejo et al. 2018), consistent with substantial evolutionary divergence.

Gene capture of host-derived immune modulatory genes to manipulate the immediate environment of infected cells is a textbook example of antagonistic co-evolution (Rheinemann et al. 2021, Fixsen et al. 2022). Hypervariable genomic regions (e.g. US4 in HSV-1 or UL/b’ in HCMV) are hotspots for captured genes with subverted functions (Prichard et al. 2001). Evolutionary arms-races in which viruses aim to overcome the host’s immune response likely promote gene captures, duplications, deletions, and rearrangements. This results in ongoing diversification and large-scale restructuring of associated genetic material. Immune modulation by ChKs in particular is only possible in pathogens closely aligned with their hosts, highlighting long-lasting conflict (McGeoch et al. 2006, Brito et al. 2021).

Conclusions

Antagonistic co-evolution in herpesvirus infections is manifold and diverse (see Fig. 1). Every stage of the infectious cycle is to some degree shaped by antagonistic interactions between virus and host or within viral populations. While many of these relations are readily apparent and well recognized, others are more subtle and often overlooked. The goal of this review was to draw attention to the role antagonistic interactions play in herpesvirus biology and how it shapes their evolution.

Early in the infectious cycle, conflict occurs mainly at the cell surface, particularly during interaction with cellular receptors (Connolly et al. 2021). Competition with other ligands, activation of signal transduction pathways, or downregulation of receptors to prevent viral superinfection are well-established examples of viral engagement. The modulatory design of herpesvirus entry complexes (see Fig. 2), facilitates the acquisition of novel receptor-binding proteins through mechanisms such as retro-transposition or recombination, allowing viruses to alter cell tropism without compromising the essential fusiogenic core. As intrahost speciation occurs more frequently than previously appreciated (Brito et al. 2021), adaptive radiation of herpesviruses within individual hosts seems plausible.

Intracellularly, herpesviruses deploy a broad arsenal of factors to promote their own gene expression, genome replication, packaging, and egress. Central for effective infection and transmission are tegument elements (Maruzuru et al. 2018). In particular, viral kinases (see Fig. 3) play key roles in rapidly establishing a permissive intracellular environment (Li et al. 2011). The success of the tegument as a reservoir of viral effectors, however, creates vulnerabilities: host antiviral factors can be incorporated into virions as well, exemplified by the packaging of innate immune signalling molecules such as cGAMP (Bridgeman et al. 2015, Gentili et al. 2015).

As the above discussed examples illustrate, antagonism is an inevitable feature of herpesvirus infections. How, then, does this persistent antagonism influence viral evolution? One line of evidence lies in the modulatory organization of herpesvirus glycoproteins. Utilizing multiple different glycoproteins for cell entry and immune engagement effectively decouples the selection pressure acting on receptor recognition and immune evasion (Fig. 2) (Höfler et al. 2025). Similarly, the diverse latency strategies employed by herpesviruses reflect adaptations shaped by conflict. A striking example is MDV, the latent genome of which integrates into host telomeres (You et al. 2021). So far, this strategy is only known for beta herpesviruses (e.g. HHV-6 and 7) (Aimola et al. 2020). Since MDV is an alpha herpesvirus, this form of latency is unlikely to be an ancestral trait of the lineage or subfamily. All three viruses (MDV, HHV-6, and 7) replicate in T-lymphocytes, suggesting that features of this cellular environment, rather than shared ancestry, favoured the evolution of this latency mechanism (Fig. 4). It is conceivable that antiviral pressure and rapid genome loss in those fast-dividing cells pushed these viruses to develop a new way of genome maintenance. These examples only scratch the surface of how genetic conflict has influenced herpesvirus evolution, leaving many antagonistic mechanisms yet to be discovered.

The long co-existence of herpesviruses with their respective hosts has resulted in deeply intertwined evolutionary relationships. In contrast to many viruses encountered through zoonotic spillover, herpesviruses show a strong tendency towards host co-speciation (McGeoch et al. 2006). Persistence over the evolutionary lifetime of a host species permits highly nuanced adaptations, particularly in the context of the relatively low mutation rates in herpesviruses (Sanjuan et al. 2010). These features make herpesviruses excellent models to study host adaptation, immune evasion, and genetic conflict.

Overall, this review brings together a broad body of primary literature demonstrating that antagonistic co-evolution is a defining feature of herpesvirus infections. Although genetic conflict was not the explicit focus of many of the studies discussed, we highlight antagonistic interactions wherever they are evident and speculate on others to stimulate a debate on antagonistic co-evolution in virology. We hope that this conceptual framework encourages future research to explicitly consider genetic conflict as a driving force in herpesvirus biology, the perspective of conflict may reveal new insights and alternative interpretations of existing data. The lifestyle of a pathogen is inherently antagonistic, so why not start from there?
