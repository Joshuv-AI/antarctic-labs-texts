Towards A Unified PAC-Bayesian Framework for Norm-based Generalization Bounds
Xinping Yi, Gaojie Jin, Xiaowei Huang, Shi Jin

Abstract
Understanding the generalization behavior of deep neural networks remains a fundamental challenge in modern statistical learning theory. Among existing approaches, PAC-Bayesian norm-based bounds have demonstrated particular promise due to their data-dependent nature and their ability to capture algorithmic and geometric properties of learned models. However, most existing results rely on isotropic Gaussian posteriors, heavy use of spectral-norm concentration for weight perturbations, and largely architecture-agnostic analyses, which together limit both the tightness and practical relevance of the resulting bounds. To address these limitations, in this work, we propose a unified framework for PAC-Bayesian norm-based generalization by reformulating the derivation of generalization bounds as a stochastic optimization problem over anisotropic Gaussian posteriors. The key to our approach is a sensitivity matrix that quantifies the network outputs with respect to structured weight perturbations, enabling the explicit incorporation of heterogeneous parameter sensitivities and architectural structures. By imposing different structural assumptions on this sensitivity matrix, we derive a family of generalization bounds that recover several existing PAC-Bayesian results as special cases, while yielding bounds that are comparable to or tighter than state-of-the-art approaches. Such a unified framework provides a principled and flexible way for geometry-/structure-aware and interpretable generalization analysis in deep learning.

Introduction

Lying at the heart of statistical learning theory, generalization remains a central challenge in understanding the empirical success of deep learning models, whose expressive power with over-parameterization often defies classical intuition.
A generalization error bound quantitatively characterizes generalization performance by measuring, with high probability, the discrepancy between a model’s empirical performance on the training data and its expected performance on unseen test data drawn from the underlying data distribution.

The PAC-Bayesian framework combines probably approximately correct (PAC) learning and Bayesian statistics. The former focuses on providing theoretical guarantees about the generalization performance of a learning algorithm based on the amount of observed (cf. training) data, while the latter provides reasoning about uncertainty by incorporating prior beliefs and their posteriors updated with observed data using Bayes’ theorem.
With respect to the PAC-Bayesian generalization, upper bounds on the expected generalization error of a learning algorithm are usually given as the sum of the empirical error on the training data and a complexity measure over the hypothesis space of the learning algorithm. These bounds are typically in the form of high probability, meaning that with high probability (e.g., at least

The complexity measure is usually expressed in terms of KL divergence, which measures the divergence between the distribution induced by the learned model (cf. the posterior) and a predetermined distribution irrelevant to models (cf. the prior).

I-B

Motivation and Contributions

Motivation.

Despite the substantial progress of PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds for deep networks, existing results still exhibit several limitations that motivate the development of a unified and more interpretable framework. First, most analyses adopt isotropic Gaussian posteriors for mathematical convenience, assuming homogeneous uncertainty across all parameters. Nevertheless, such an isotropy is rarely consistent with the highly anisotropic geometry of trained deep models observed in practice. Second, many bounds depend crucially on controlling the spectral norm of weight perturbations, where the use of matrix concentration inequalities may yield loose mean or variance estimates, particularly for deep or structured architectures, thereby limiting both tightness and interpretability. Third, current approaches typically treat networks in a largely architecture-agnostic manner, overlooking the rich structural properties of modern models and the heterogeneous sensitivity of the loss landscape with respect to different layers, modules, or parameter subspaces. These limitations suggest that a unified PAC-Bayesian norm-based framework—capable of accommodating anisotropic posteriors, refined perturbation control beyond spectral norms, and explicit architectural and sensitivity-aware structures—is necessary to more faithfully capture the generalization behavior of deep learning models.

Contributions.
Motivated by the aforementioned limitations, this work makes the following contributions toward a unified and practically relevant framework of PAC-Bayesian norm-based generalization for deep learning:

1.

Unified PAC-Bayesian framework from an optimization perspective.
We establish a unified framework for PAC-Bayesian norm-based generalization bounds, in which the derivation of the generalization bound is reformulated as a stochastic optimization problem in a layer-wise manner. In contrast to conventional analyses with isotropic Gaussian posteriors fixed
a priori
, our framework allows for fully anisotropic Gaussian posteriors and treats them as optimization variables. By minimizing the KL divergence term, these posterior covariances can then be identified in a closed-form with respect to a properly designed, structure-aware sensitivity matrix. This perspective explicitly couples the network sensitivity to weight perturbations and the KL complexity term through the sensitivity matrix, providing a principled mechanism to adapt the posterior geometry to the loss landscape of the trained network.

2.

Sensitivity-aware and structure-exploiting bounds.
The sensitivity matrix characterizes the difference of network outputs to weight perturbations, in such a way that the required perturbation condition in the margin-based bound can be satisfied by explicit designs of sensitivity matrices. This also avoids the spectral norm concentration in the previous works, but instead employs the more flexible concentration inequalities for vector norms. By accommodating various structural properties, the sensitivity matrix allows for heterogeneous weight perturbations to fit diverse loss landscapes of different networks. Imposing different properties on the sensitivity matrix—such as diagonal, residual, low-rank, circulant, and Toeplitz structures—leads to a family of PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds that recover, unify, and extend several existing results as special cases. This approach enables fine-grained control of weight perturbation effects without relying solely on spectral-norm concentration.

3.

Tighter and more interpretable generalization guarantees.
Thanks to the unified framework with flexible designs of sensitivity matrices, the resulting PAC-Bayesian norm-based generalization bounds are shown to be comparable to, and strictly tighter than, existing ones (e.g., fully-connected, residual, and convolutional networks) that rely on isotropic perturbations and/or restrictive spectral-norm concentrations. By aligning posterior anisotropy with network architecture and heterogeneous parameter sensitivity, the proposed framework yields theoretically sharper and more interpretable bounds, offering new insights into the interplay between weight norms, loss landscape, and network structure in generalization. That is, network architectures do not merely matter parameter counts — they refine the spectral complexity with shared weights and restrict the admissible weight sensitivity to loss landscapes. As such, generalization is governed by both the compact spectral complexity with refined weight norms and the weight perturbation geometry allowed by the loss landscape, both due to network architecture.

In the next section, we provide some preliminaries in statistical learning theory, especially the norm-based bound derived in the literature, together with the main proof techniques. Section III presents the proposed unified framework and summarizes the resulting generalization bounds, along with specific designs of sensitivity matrices. This is followed by Section IV, which provides detailed generalization bounds for those specific designs of sensitivity matrices. Section V concludes the paper with some detailed proofs relegated to the appendix.

On the contrary, the margin measures the “robustness” or “confidence” of the model’s predictions on the data, which can usually be ensured by, e.g., max-margin training and label smoothing. The training process is usually trying to find a good tradeoff between them, aligning perfectly with the norm-based generalization bound.

Proof Techniques

Spectral normalization.

In the above spectrally normalized generalization error bound, the spectral norms of
\{{\bm{W}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}

\beta=\sqrt[d]{\prod_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}}

[
13
]
. Given that the weight normalization does not change the spectral complexity, i.e.,
\Phi({\bm{w}})=\Phi(\tilde{{\bm{w}}})
, it is assumed in
[
13
]
to consider the same spectral norm across layers, i.e.,
\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}=\beta
for all
l
, without loss of generality.

The key gradients of the spectrally-normalized bounds consist of the following steps:

1.

Given any fixed
\hat{\beta}

\beta
such that
|\hat{\beta}-\beta|\leq\frac{1}{d}\beta
, choose a proper

as a function of

to satisfy the perturbation condition based on the concentration inequality of spectral norm

and the perturbation bound Eq-(
6
);

2.

Given the choice of

, compute the KL divergence and bound the difference between expected and empirical margin loss for the fixed
\hat{\beta}
;

3.

Given the bound for a fixed
\hat{\beta}
, derive the final generalization error bound for all possible
\hat{\beta}
by taking a union bound over all chosen
\hat{\beta}
so that every
\beta
can be covered.

The norm-based generalization bound in

Limitations.
Albeit a promising approach, there are two limitations in existing works. First, most existing works consider the isotropically Gaussian distributed weight perturbation, i.e.,
{\bm{u}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})
, assuming that the weight perturbations in different directions are identically uncertain and independently play the same role. In reality, some weight patterns (e.g., convolutional filters or graph diffusion matrices) or weight directions (in the parameter space) are more sensitive to the perturbations, and therefore hurt the network’s margin much more than others. The isotropic perturbation treats every weight as equally sensitive, and thus cannot capture the “sharpness” of weight directions or patterns.
Second, although the perturbation bound Eq-(
6
) appears elegant and tight, it renders the choices of
\sigma^{2}
reliance on the concentration inequality of matrix spectral norm, resulting in high inflexibility to take into account any potential structure of the model weights. This motivates us to reconsider the necessity of the perturbation bound for the sake of flexible and unifying designs.

III
A Unified Framework

To address the above issues, we propose a unified framework by introducing two auxiliary variables.

1.

Consider an anisotropic weight perturbation, e.g.,
{\bm{u}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}{\bm{R}})
to capture different sensitivities to weight perturbations, where
{\bm{R}}
will be optimized to minimize the KL divergence and, in turn, the complexity measure;

2.

Introduce a sensitivity matrix
{\bm{A}}
to encode different network structures, so as to impose different sensitivities on the perturbation to the output, and enable the flexible designs of perturbation bounds.

Therefore, the derivation of the generalization bound can be done by solving the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma^{2},{\bm{R}},{\bm{A}}}\quad

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}({\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}||P)

(8a)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{u}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2}{\bm{R}})}(\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}<\frac{\gamma}{4})\geq\frac{1}{2},

(8b)

\displaystyle\|f_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({\bm{x}})-f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})\|_{\infty}\leq\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2},

(8c)

where the
\ell_{\infty}
norm is upper-bounded by the
\ell_{2}
norm in such a way that proper concentration inequalities of the
\ell_{2}
norm ensure a closed-form solution
{\bm{R}}
with respect to
{\bm{A}}
. Here
{\bm{A}}
is referred to as a Jacobian-like sensitivity matrix, which is a function of the weights and the inputs. The choices of
{\bm{A}}
play a key role in tightening the PAC-Bayes generalization bounds through
{\bm{R}}
. When it makes
{\bm{R}}
aligning with flat directions of the loss landscape, the expected margin loss drift can be minimized, resulting in a lower empirical loss under weight perturbation. When suppressing the variance of
{\bm{R}}
in sensitive directions, it avoids large KL divergence, giving us a lower model complexity penalty.

However, directly optimizing Eq-(
8
) is intractable, as it involves the perturbation condition in a probabilistic form.
As such, it enables us to employ the concentration inequality of the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the vector
{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}
, rather than the spectral norm of
{\bm{U}}_{l}
, to derive more flexible perturbation bounds, ultimately yielding more adaptable bounds.

III-A

The General Framework

III-A
1
Anisotropic weight perturbation

To capture the diverse sensitivities of the model’s outputs to weight perturbations, we relax the commonly used assumption of the isotropic Gaussian distribution to the anisotropic settings such that
{\bm{u}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},\sigma^{2}{\bm{R}})
where
\sigma^{2}
is a tunable variable shared by both prior and posterior, and
{\bm{R}}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{T}]
is the positive definite covariance matrix of weight perturbations across the
d
-th layer.
This allows us to encode in
{\bm{R}}
different sensitivities and any correlations between weight perturbations.
With a general
{\bm{R}}\neq{\bm{I}}
, we can down-weight the “sharp” directions where a tiny perturbation could collapse the margin, and up-weight the “flat” directions when there is some slack, in such a way that the resulting PAC-Bayesian norm-based bound could reflect the actual geometry of the loss landscape.

Moreover, by optimizing
{\bm{R}}
towards the KL minimization, we unlock the potential of achieving much tighter norm-based bounds.
A larger variance
{\bm{R}}
implies more stochasticity, helping reduce margin loss under perturbation (cf. a flatter loss landscape) at the cost of increased KL. On the contrary, reducing the variance can decrease the KL divergence, but it also increases the sensitivity.
As such, the PAC-Bayesian bound encourages a structured design of
{\bm{R}}
to put variance in directions where the loss is stable, and suppress variance where the margin is sensitive.

There is also a tradeoff between
\sigma^{2}
and
{\bm{R}}
due to the perturbation condition. Too large
\sigma^{2}
leads to excessive perturbations that make the condition fail, while a poorly structured
{\bm{R}}
, e.g., large variances in sensitive directions, may also break the condition. In reality,
\sigma^{2}
should be as large as possible to minimize the KL divergence while satisfying the condition, and
{\bm{R}}
should align with the model sensitivity to avoid perturbing unstable directions.

2

With the consideration of anisotropic Gaussian distributed weight perturbations, we need an upgraded concentration inequality for the spectral norm of correlated weight perturbation

39

. Nevertheless, we can circumvent such a requirement by constraining the perturbation bound with the following alternative, i.e.,

\displaystyle\|f_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({\bm{x}})-f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})\|_{\infty}\leq\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}

(9)

for any
{\bm{w}}
and
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,
where
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a sensitivity matrix, serving as a proxy for a global Jacobian. As such, the original perturbation condition is enforced by bounding the change in the network’s output using a Jacobian-type matrix
{\bm{A}}
.
By such an updated perturbation condition, the original weight-space perturbation can be translated to function-space stability.
Thus, to satisfy the perturbation condition, we need to guarantee that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{u}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},\sigma^{2}{\bm{R}})}\big[\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}<\frac{\gamma}{4}\big]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(10)

III-A
3
Concentration inequality of the
\ell_{2}
vector norm

Let
{\bm{u}}=\sigma{\bm{R}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{z}}
with
{\bm{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}})
and
{\bm{M}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{R}}{\bm{A}}^{T}
positive semi-definite, we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}^{2}=\sigma^{2}{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}{\bm{z}},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}\Tr({\bm{M}}).

(11)

By
[
40
, Proposition 1.1]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}(\Tr({\bm{M}})+\sqrt{4t}\|{\bm{M}}\|_{F}+2t\|{\bm{M}}\|_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp(-t)

(12)

for all
t>0
.
This can be similarly done by the Hanson-Wright inequality
[
41
]
that bounds the quadratic form of sub-Gaussian random vectors, such as
\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}^{2}
. It also concentrates the quadratic form on its mean
\Tr({\bm{M}})
with a deviation term controlled via
\|{\bm{M}}\|_{F}
and
\|{\bm{M}}\|_{2}
.

Let
1-\exp(-t)=\frac{1}{2}
, we have
t=\ln 2
.
Thus, we conclude that, with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
, it holds

\displaystyle\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}(\underbrace{\Tr({\bm{M}})+\sqrt{4\ln 2}\|{\bm{M}}\|_{F}+2\ln 2\|{\bm{M}}\|_{2}}_{\triangleq\;\Gamma({\bm{M}})})

(13)

It is worth noting here that the above concentration inequality works directly on the
\ell_{2}
vector norm of
{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}
, rather than the spectral norm of
{\bm{U}}_{l}
as those in
[
13
,
29
,
38
,
39
,
35
]
. This brings in two benefits: 1) it is more flexible to consider anisotropic Gaussian distributions for the vector norm than the spectral norm as in
[
39
]
; and 2) this avoids layer-wise consideration of spectral norms and the need of a union bound over layers of concentration inequalities, therefore eliminating a
\ln(dh)
factor in the choices of
\sigma^{2}
as in
[
13
,
29
,
38
,
39
,
35
]
and many others.

By letting
\sigma^{2}\Gamma({\bm{M}})=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{16}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{16\Gamma({\bm{M}})}{\gamma^{2}}

(14)

to satisfy the perturbation condition. Note here that the choice of
\sigma^{2}
scales as
\gamma^{2}
, and the perturbation condition still holds if some upper bounds of
\Gamma({\bm{M}})
are in place in Eq-(
14
).

Note also here that the choice of
\sigma^{2}
is also used for the isotropic Gaussian prior, which usually cannot depend on the learned weights
\{{{\bm{W}}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
that may be a part of
{\bm{M}}
through the flexible design of
{\bm{A}}

[

that has been widely accepted in the follow-up works in e.g.,
[

38

39

35
]
, to add some adjustment on the choice of
\sigma^{2}
according to the approximated weights
\{\hat{{\bm{W}}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
.

III-A
4
KL divergence minimization

Given the Gaussian prior
P={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})
and posterior
Q={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{w}},\sigma^{2}{\bm{R}})
with the same
\sigma
, the KL divergence can be written by
[
42
]

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||P)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}+\Tr({\bm{R}})-\log\det{\bm{R}}-\dim({\bm{R}})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{16\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\Gamma({\bm{A}}{\bm{R}}{\bm{A}}^{T})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\Tr({\bm{R}})-\log\det{\bm{R}}-\sum_{l=1}^{d}h_{l}h_{l-1}\right)

(15)

due to the choice of
\sigma^{2}
.
Note that
\frac{\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}
penalizes the large weights related to posterior uncertainty,
\Tr({\bm{R}})
is the total variance budget across weight directions,
\log\det{\bm{R}}
is the volume of correlation for anisotropic uncertainty, and
\dim({\bm{R}})
is the dimension of the Gaussian distributed vector
{\bm{u}}
.
To tighten the generalization error bound, we can minimize
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||P)
by choosing the proper covariance matrix
{\bm{R}}
. As such, we formulate an optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{R}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{R}})\triangleq\frac{16\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\Gamma({\bm{A}}{\bm{R}}{\bm{A}}^{T})+\Tr({\bm{R}})-\log\det{\bm{R}}

(16)

where
{\mathcal{S}}_{+}
is the space of symmetric positive semi-definite matrices. By
\min_{{\bm{R}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{R}})
, we end up with
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||P)
as a function of
{\bm{A}}
that is carefully designed to satisfy the perturbation condition in Eq-(
9
), followed by the final generalization error bound with each specific design of
{\bm{A}}
. Although
{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{R}})
is convex on
{\bm{R}}
, it is still challenging to find a neat closed-form solution using, e.g., KKT conditions, due to the non-differentiability of the spectral norm. Therefore, for simplicity and traceability, we instead minimize a tractable upper bound of
{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{R}})
to have a closed-form solution to
{\bm{R}}
.

For the positive demi-definite matrix
{\bm{M}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{R}}{\bm{A}}^{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{M}}\|_{2}\leq\|{\bm{M}}\|_{F}\leq\Tr({\bm{M}})

(17)

where the equalities hold when
{\bm{M}}
has rank-1.
Therefore, we set
\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{16\kappa}{\gamma^{2}}\Tr({\bm{M}})
to satisfy the perturbation bound, where

\displaystyle\kappa=1+2\ln 2+\sqrt{4\ln 2}.

(18)

As such, we end up with an upper bound of
{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{R}})
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}({\bm{R}})\triangleq\frac{16\kappa\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\Tr({\bm{A}}{\bm{R}}{\bm{A}}^{T})+\Tr({\bm{R}})-\log\det{\bm{R}}.

Since
{\mathcal{D}}({\bm{R}})
is convex over
{\bm{R}}
, the minimization of
{\mathcal{D}}({\bm{R}})
using KKT conditions yields a closed-form solution, denoted by
{\bm{R}}^{*}=\arg\min_{{\bm{R}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}}{\mathcal{D}}({\bm{R}})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\bm{R}}^{*}=\left({\bm{I}}+\frac{16\kappa\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{A}}\right)^{-1},

(19)

which shrinks variance along sensitive directions with large
{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{A}}
.
If
{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{A}}
is diagonal, then
{\bm{R}}^{*}
is diagonal, recovering the i.i.d. case; If
{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{A}}
is low-rank or with spectral decay, then
{\bm{R}}^{*}
effectively allocates less variance to sensitive directions, tightening the KL bound.
Note also that
{\bm{R}}^{*}
is also dependent on the margin
\gamma
. When
\gamma
is small,
{\bm{R}}^{*}
relies on the choice of
{\bm{A}}
so that the directional sensitivity dominates; when
\gamma
is large,
{\bm{R}}^{*}
reduces to the isotropic case so that the sharpness of the loss landscape does matter.

Plugging
{\bm{R}}^{*}
in the KL divergence, we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||P)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}+\Tr({\bm{R}}^{*})-\log\det{\bm{R}}^{*}-N\right).

(20)

Together with the proper choice of
\sigma^{2}
and the design of
{\bm{A}}
to satisfy the perturbation condition, we end up with a new form of PAC-Bayesian generalization error bounds.

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma^{2},{\bm{R}}_{l},{\bm{A}}_{l}}\quad

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\sigma^{2},{\bm{R}}_{l})\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}+\Tr({\bm{R}}_{l})-\log\det{\bm{R}}_{l}-\dim({\bm{R}}_{l})

(21a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{u}}_{l}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},\sigma^{2}{\bm{R}}_{l})}\Big[\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}<\frac{\gamma^{2}}{16}\Big]\geq\frac{1}{2},

(21b)

\displaystyle\|f_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({\bm{x}})-f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2},

(21c)

III-B

Block-diagonal Simplification

For simplicity, we assume there are no correlations of weight perturbations across layers, where the covariance matrix
{\bm{R}}
of weight perturbation possesses a block-diagonal structure, i.e.,
{\bm{R}}=\mathrm{blkdiag}({\bm{R}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{R}}_{d})
with each diagonal block
{\bm{R}}_{l}
up to optimization.
Similarly, we let
{\bm{A}}=\mathrm{blkdiag}({\bm{A}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{A}}_{d})
with
{\bm{A}}_{l}
being carefully designed for each layer
l
, as a global measure of sensitivity without respecting inter-layer structures.

With the block-diagonal simplification, we advocate a unified framework to derive the norm-based PAC-Bayesian generalization error bounds by solving the optimization problem in Eq-(
21
) on the top of the next page. Note that the objective Eq-(
21a
) is to find the minimized KL divergence by choosing the proper
{\bm{R}}_{l}
and
\sigma^{2}
to tighten the upper bound, the first constraint Eq-(
21b
) comes from the perturbation condition with properly chosen
\sigma^{2}
, and the second constraint Eq-(
21c
) is to guide the choices of
{\bm{A}}_{l}
.

In general, it is challenging to solve the problem directly. Instead, we propose the following route to find some feasible solutions, which end up with new generalization error bounds for different designs of
\{{\bm{A}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
. The key is to make a good tradeoff between
{\bm{A}}_{l}
and
{\bm{R}}_{l}
, where the former captures weight perturbation as much as possible to satisfy the perturbation condition, and the latter aligns sensitivity to reduce the influence of weight perturbation as much as possible for tighter bounds.

III-B
1
Figure out the prior variance
\sigma^{2}
to satisfy the perturbation condition

In general, we need to choose a proper
\sigma^{2}
as a function of
\gamma
,
{\bm{A}}_{l}
and
{\bm{R}}_{l}
to satisfy the perturbation condition Eq-(
21b
) with the following chain of inequalities

\displaystyle\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}\Gamma({\bm{M}})

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\Gamma({\bm{M}}_{l})\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{16}

(22)

where
{\bm{M}}_{l}={\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{R}}_{l}{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}
and the second inequality is due to

\displaystyle\|{\bm{M}}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{l}\|{\bm{M}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2}}\leq\sum_{l}\|{\bm{M}}_{l}\|_{F},

(23)

\displaystyle\|{\bm{M}}\|_{2}=\max_{l}\|{\bm{M}}_{l}\|_{2}\leq\sum_{l}\|{\bm{M}}_{l}\|_{2},

(24)

for block diagonal matrix
{\bm{M}}=\mathrm{blkdiag}({\bm{M}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{M}}_{d})
.
As
{\bm{M}}_{l}
is positive semi-definite, we have
\|{\bm{M}}_{l}\|_{2}\leq\|{\bm{M}}_{l}\|_{F}\leq\Tr({\bm{M}}_{l})
, and therefore the perturbation condition can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\Gamma({\bm{M}}_{l})

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}\kappa\sum_{l=1}^{d}\Tr({\bm{M}}_{l})\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{16}.

(25)

By setting

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{16\kappa}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\Tr({{{\bm{M}}}}_{l}),

(26)

the original perturbation condition can be satisfied with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
.
As said earlier,
\sigma^{2}
should be independent of the learned weight
\{{\bm{W}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
, thus

\sigma^{2}
should be adjusted later, when specifying
{\bm{A}}_{l}
as a function of
\{\hat{{\bm{W}}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
, yet to obtain the optimized
{\bm{R}}_{l}
we still proceed with the
\sigma^{2}
in Eq-(
26
) for convenience.

III-B
2
Minimize KL

Given the choice of
\sigma^{2}
, we have the upper bound
{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{R}})\leq\sum_{l}{\mathcal{D}}({\bm{R}}_{l})
where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}({\bm{R}}_{l})=\frac{16\kappa\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\Tr({\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{R}}_{l}{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T})+\Tr({\bm{R}}_{l})-\log\det{\bm{R}}_{l}.

(27)

To figure out the optimal
{\bm{R}}_{l}^{*}
as a function of
\gamma
and
{\bm{A}}_{l}
, by KKT conditions, we have

\displaystyle{\bm{R}}_{l}^{*}=\left({\bm{I}}+\frac{16\kappa\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{l}\right)^{-1}.

(28)

Note that the design of
{\bm{R}}_{l}^{*}
shrinks variance along sensitive directions (those with large
{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{l}
), which aligns with the idea of robust weight perturbation. When
{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{l}
is diagonal with independent perturbation across dimensions,
{\bm{R}}_{l}^{*}
is diagonal, recovering somehow the i.i.d. setting. When
{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{l}
is of low-rank or has a spectrum with decay,
{\bm{R}}_{l}^{*}
allocates less variance to sensitive directions, which may tighten the KL divergence.

III-B
3
Design sensitivity matrices

The goal of designing
{\bm{A}}_{l}
is to capture the local Lipschitz sensitivity of the network output to the weight perturbation
{\bm{u}}_{l}
. It turns out to be finding a surrogate Jacobian-like matrix that captures the layer-wise or neuron-wise impact of perturbations.
with certain imposed structures to satisfy the perturbation bound Eq-(
21c
), such as diagonal, low-rank, and Toeplitz structures. Some possible designs are as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{Diagonal:}&{\bm{A}}_{l}=e\sqrt{d}B\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{W}}_{i}\|_{2}{\bm{I}}\\
\text{Residual:}&{\bm{A}}_{l}=e\sqrt{d}B\prod_{i\neq l}(\|{\bm{W}}_{i}\|_{2}+1){\bm{I}}\\
\text{Low-rank:}&{\bm{A}}_{l}=\sqrt{d}{\bm{V}}_{l}\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{l,1},\dots,\sigma_{l,K},0,\dots,0){\bm{V}}_{l}^{T},\\
&\text{with }\sigma_{l,k}=B\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{W}}_{i}\|_{2}\\
\text{Circulant:}&{\bm{A}}_{l}=\sqrt{d}B\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{V}}^{H}{\bm{w}}_{i}\|_{\infty}{\bm{V}}_{[1:K]}{\bm{V}}_{[1:K]}^{H}\\
&\text{with ${\bm{w}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}$ is the vectorized convolutional}\\
&\text{filter of $i$-th layer with circulant padding.}\\
\text{Toeplitz:}&{{\bm{A}}}_{l}=\frac{e\sqrt{d}B\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{W}}_{i}\|_{2}}{\min_{\omega}\psi(\omega))}{\bm{T}}{\bm{P}}\\
&\text{with ${\bm{T}}$ a Toeplitz matrix with symbol $\psi(\omega)$}\\
&\text{and ${\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{2}\times k}$ for kernel-Toeplitz mapping.}\end{array}

where
{\bm{V}}_{l}
is the collection of the right singular vectors of the Jacobian matrix of
l
-th layer,
{\bm{V}}_{[1:K]}
is the collection of
K
columns of the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) matrix, and the additional factor
e
is due to the consideration of the weights in choosing
\sigma^{2}
in the prior.

For the diagonal
{\bm{A}}_{l}
, we aim to explore the worst-case sensitivity of all directions in each layer with an equal strength, yet with maybe distinct strengths across layers. By setting
{\bm{A}}_{l}
to be a low-rank matrix, we wish to explore some typical directions extensively while ignoring other directions. In contrast, with
{\bm{A}}_{l}
being circulant matrices, we want to mimic the behavior of convolutional layers, and explore the impact of parameter sharing in generalization. Finally, by considering
{\bm{A}}
being graph Laplacian matrices, we investigate the influence of graph structures in generalization. In fact,
{\bm{A}}_{l}
could be also data-dependent as a function of
{\bm{x}}
, yet for simplicity, we employ
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}\leq B
instead for any input
{\bm{x}}
.

Different choices of sensitivity structures encode distinct inductive biases. The diagonal sensitivity corresponds to the worst-case and parameter-wise robustness, whilst the low-rank sensitivity reflects the output-driven or task-aligned complexity. In contrast, the circulant structure models global and periodic correlations across parameters, while the Toeplitz structure captures smoothness and locality induced by weight sharing.

III-B
4
Obtain final bounds

With the adjusted
{\sigma}^{2}

and the optimized
{\bm{R}}_{l}^{*}
in Eq-(
28
), we can obtain the resulting KL divergence in Eq-(
21a
) or its upper bounds, i.e.,

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\{{\bm{A}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2}}{{\sigma}^{2}}+\Tr({\bm{R}}_{l}^{*})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\log\det{\bm{R}}_{l}^{*}-h_{l}h_{l-1}

(39)

by which we end up with new generalization error bounds

\displaystyle L_{0}(f_{{\bm{w}}})\leq\hat{L}_{\gamma}(f_{{\bm{w}}})+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{B^{2}\Delta(d,h,{\bm{w}})+\ln\frac{dm}{\delta}}{\gamma^{2}m}}\right)

(40)

where

\displaystyle\Delta(d,h,{\bm{w}})=\left\{\begin{matrix}d^{2}h^{2}\Phi({\bm{w}}),&\text{${\bm{A}}_{l}$ diagonal}\\

(41)

with

\displaystyle\Phi({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2}}{\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}},

(42)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\mathrm{rn}}({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{d}(\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}+1)^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2}}{(\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}+1)^{2}},

(43)

\displaystyle{\Phi}^{\mathrm{circ}}({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{V}}^{H}{\bm{w}}_{i}\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2},

(44)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\mathrm{toep}}({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi_{\max}^{2}}{\psi_{\min}^{2}}\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{w}}_{i}\|_{1}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}

(45)

with the variables specified later.
It is worth noting that, each choice of sensitivity matrix
{\bm{A}}_{l}
defines a PAC-Bayes bound that is valid for networks whose perturbation geometry is compatible with
{\bm{A}}_{l}
. With certain structured choices (e.g., residual, low-rank, circulant, and Toeplitz) of
{\bm{A}}_{l}
, we end up with strictly sharper bounds when the network architecture enforces the corresponding weight sharing or spectral constraints. When the architecture does not enforce such a structure, the bound remains valid only after relaxing
{\bm{A}}_{l}
to a worst-case (diagonal) form, leading to looser generalization guarantees.

IV-A

Diagonal Matrices

By letting
{\bm{A}}_{l}
be a diagonal matrix, we intend to assume the perturbations are independent per-layer (or even per-parameter), each layer contributes separately to output sensitivity, and there’s no cross-talk or interaction between different parameter blocks. This matches the layerwise Lipschitz structure, where the change in output is dominated by per-layer norms and no mixing across dimensions is modeled.

Remark 1
.

In contrast to the isotropic Gaussian posterior in
[
13
]
, the diagonal sensitivity matrix design gives rise to a diagonal optimized posterior covariance matrix
{\bm{R}}_{l}^{*}
as in Eq-(
80
), where the intra- and inter-layer correlation of model weights are fully decoupled with only different scales across layers. Hence, the obtained generalization error bounds are also similar yet with different scaling on
d
and
h
. In fact, the choice of
{\bm{A}}_{l}
in Eq-(
46
) would end up with a looser bound because the perturbation condition Eq-(
21c
) is validated by the perturbation bound in Eq-(
6
). Nevertheless, with the optimized choice of
{\bm{R}}_{l}
in the unified framework, we end up with a generalization error bound comparable to that in Eq-(
5
).
Compared with the bound in
[
13
]
, it appears our bound scales similarly as
\Phi({\bm{w}})
, being tighter for deep networks with
d^{2}
versus
d^{2}\ln d
, yet looser for wide networks with
h^{2}
versus
h\ln h
.
Although it is simple and always valid, it incurs the worst scaling of
h^{2}
, ignoring structure, correlations, and weight sharing.

With such a diagonal (or isotropic) sensitivity matrix in Eq-(
46
), weight perturbations are applied independently and equally in all parameter directions. This corresponds to enforcing uniform flatness of the loss landscape in every coordinate direction, i.e., the loss must not increase significantly under small independent perturbations of each parameter. Such a uniform flatness favors globally flat minima in the standard Euclidean sense, which matches the classical “flat minima” intuition, i.e., small Hessian eigenvalues in all directions. However, it would be overly conservative, as it penalizes sharpness even in directions that have little effect on the output, and ignores correlations and task-relevant subspaces.

Corollary 1
.

Consider a residually connected feedforward neural network
f_{{\bm{w}}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
with ReLU activations, depth
d
, width
h
, and bounded inputs
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}\leq B
, where the

Remark 2
.

For residual networks, the resulting sensitivity matrix takes a form closely related to the diagonal case, with the key difference that each layer contributes a factor of

\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}
to the spectral complexity term. Consequently, the generalization bound differs from that of standard feedforward networks only through the replacement of the spectral norm by its shifted counterpart, reflecting the presence of identity skip connections. This structure reveals several properties: (i) perturbations propagate additively along residual paths instead of being purely multiplicative, preventing exponential amplification across depth; (ii) each residual branch contributes largely independently to the overall sensitivity, consistent with the modular design of residual blocks; and (iii) cross-layer amplification of perturbations is effectively suppressed by the identity mapping, yielding improved stability without requiring explicit spectral compression of weights. Notably, this bound is architecture-aware—it exploits the intrinsic modularity and skip-connectivity of residual networks. Thus, the bound provides a theoretical explanation for the empirical robustness and favorable generalization of residual architectures. That is, stability is achieved structurally, through additive identity pathways, rather than solely through norm control of individual layers.

Low-Rank Matrices

The motivation for designing a low-rank sensitivity matrix stems from the observation that, for most neural networks, the output dimension
K
is much smaller than the hidden dimension

h^{2}

[

Remark 3
.

By explicitly leveraging the output bottleneck, a low-rank sensitivity design of
{\bm{A}}_{l}
in Eq-(

K

K

h^{2}

[
13

with
K\leq h
for most practical networks.

The design of low-rank

{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}})

l

identify the directions in parameter space along which perturbations most strongly influence the output. By aligning the sensitivity matrix

with the principal subspace of

h^{2}
in the model complexity, achieving tighter, task-aligned generalization bounds.

Low-rank sensitivity restricts perturbations to a subspace aligned with the row space of the Jacobian, where only the directions that actually affect the network outputs count. It enforces flatness only along output-relevant directions, allowing for possible sharp curvature in null directions that do not affect predictions.
From a loss landscape viewpoint, the generalization can still be good as long as the loss is flat in the prediction-sensitive subspace, even if the entire model may lie in a sharp basin globally. This aligns with the understanding that not all sharpness is harmful—only the output-relevant sharpness is.

IV-C

Circulant Matrices

For fully convolutional models, parameters exhibit strong global structure induced by weight sharing and local translation invariance, which cannot be faithfully captured by diagonal or low-rank sensitivity designs. By choosing the sensitivity matrix
{\bm{A}}_{l}
to be circulant, we explicitly model correlations across adjacent parameters and emulate convolution-like interactions in the parameter space. More interestingly, by taking the Fourier transform of the weight perturbations, we can conduct perturbation analysis in the frequency domain.

Remark 4
.

For the convolutional layers in practical convolutional neural networks (CNNs), the weight matrix

is a circulant matrix, i.e.,

As

is the DFT matrix, the sensitivity matrix

{\bm{A}}_{l}

{\bm{A}}_{l}
correspond to directions where the network output is more sensitive, and the posterior variance
{\bm{R}}_{l}
is therefore shrunk more aggressively to suppress harmful perturbations. This yields an anisotropic and frequency-selective perturbation model, so that stability can be enforced where it matters most, while ignoring insensitive frequency bands.

With the circulant sensitivity, weight perturbation favors flatness per frequency band rather than per parameter.
It enforces flatness in frequency bands where sensitivity is high, and suppresses harmful high-frequency perturbations.
Notably, the loss landscape is encouraged to be flat with respect to structured, convolution-like perturbations rather than arbitrary ones. This connects nicely to observations that CNNs generalize by suppressing high-frequency filters and noise.

IV-D

Toeplitz Matrices

To model convolutional weight sharing without imposing artificial periodicity (i.e., circulant padding), we consider Toeplitz sensitivity matrices, which naturally encode locality and distance-dependent correlations among parameters, reflecting the fact that perturbations of a single kernel coefficient affect many spatial locations.

.

Remark 5
.

For the linear convolutional layers, the efficient weight matrix
{\bm{W}}_{l}
is a Toeplitz matrix consisting of repeated Toeplitz kernels without circulant padding
[
45
,
46
]
.
Similar to the circulant case, we have a newly defined “kernel” spectral complexity
{\Phi}^{\mathrm{toep}}({\bm{w}})
, which is a function of the Toeplitz kernel, i.e.,
{\bm{w}}_{l}
.
Nevertheless, unlike circulant matrices, Toeplitz sensitivity captures convolution with finite support and no wrap-around effects, making it more faithful to practical convolutional architectures. In this setting, weight perturbations
\{{\bm{u}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
, added directly to weight kernels
\{{\bm{w}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
, are correlated across parameters through
{\bm{P}}
according to spatial proximity, enforcing smoothness and locality in the posterior geometry. As a result, the resulting PAC-Bayesian bound replaces weight matrices with the Toeplitz kernel vectors, adjusted by the dynamic range of the filter’s frequency response, i.e.,
\frac{\psi_{\max}^{2}}{\psi_{\min}^{2}}
.
This change reflects that the effective complexity is governed by the bandwidth
k
and shape of the convolutional kernel, rather than by the total parameter dimension.

The Toeplitz sensitivity design enables a precise spectral alignment between the posterior covariance
{\bm{R}}_{l}
, the intrinsic sensitivity structure
{\bm{A}}_{l}
, and the PAC-Bayesian KL geometry. In contrast to diagonal sensitivity, which enforces isotropic shrinkage in the parameter space, Toeplitz structure induces anisotropic and frequency-dependent shrinkage through its symbol
\psi(\omega)
with
\omega\in[0,2\pi]
, allowing the posterior
{\bm{R}}_{l}
as in Eq-(
169
) to contract more strongly in spectrally sensitive directions while preserving stable, low-frequency components.
Toeplitz sensitivity naturally encodes weight sharing and locality priors, effectively reducing the relevant spectral dimension even when the parameter dimension remains unchanged. As a result, the induced posterior becomes frequency-selective, leading to interpretable smoothness regularization that suppresses high-frequency, spatially incoherent perturbations while retaining low-frequency, structure-preserving variations.

Toeplitz sensitivity enforces flatness with respect to local, smooth deformations of filters. It encourages flatness under spatially local and coherent perturbations, while allowing for sharpness under highly unstructured perturbations.
In other words, Toeplitz sensitivity prefers minima that are flat with respect to local correlated directions, ignoring possibly sharp in highly irregular directions that violate convolutional structures.

V
Conclusion

This work presented a unified PAC-Bayesian norm-based generalization framework for deep learning by reformulating the derivation of generalization bounds as a stochastic optimization problem over anisotropic Gaussian posteriors. The proposed framework explicitly accounts for the anisotropic posterior covariances and network architectural structures, unifying and extending a broad class of existing PAC-Bayesian results that were derived under restrictive isotropic posterior assumptions. The key novelty was the introduction of structure-aware sensitivity matrices with e.g., diagonal, low-rank, circulant, and Toeplitz structures, which enable fine-grained control of weight perturbation effects, leading to theoretically sharper and more interpretable generalization bounds.
Such a framework is unified in the sense that it can be extended naturally to different settings, e.g., the adversarial settings, where the interaction between input perturbations and weight perturbations poses additional challenges for robust generalization, and graph neural networks, where weight sharing and graph operators call for structure-aware and geometry-sensitive designs. The proposed unified framework could bring in different perspectives for addressing these challenges and yield tighter and more interpretable PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds.

\displaystyle\geq 1

(91)

(92)

\beta
.

VI-C

Proof of Theorem
2

The procedure is similar to that in the diagonal case, while the difference lies in the following two points:

•

Without relying on the perturbation bound in Eq-(
6
), the difference of the network’s output due to weight perturbation is approximated by its first-order Taylor expansion with the Jacobian
{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}})
.

•

The sensitivity matrix
{\bm{A}}_{l}
captures the directions aligning with the right singular vectors of
{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}})
, so that the scaling factor of generalization bound reduces from
h^{2}
to
K
.

VI-C
1
Perturbation condition

The perturbation difference is the change in the output
f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})
when the weights are perturbed by
{\bm{u}}
. It is influenced by the Jacobian matrix
{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}})
at each layer, which determines how sensitive the output is to perturbations in the weights.

By the first-order Taylor expansion of
f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})
with a small perturbation
{\bm{u}}
, we have

\displaystyle f_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({\bm{x}})-f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{l=1}^{d}{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}_{l}+o(\|{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2})

(101)

where
{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}})=\frac{\partial f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})}{\partial\mathrm{vec}({\bm{W}}_{l})}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times h_{l}h_{l-1}}
is the Jacobian matrix corresponding to the
l
-th layer.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\|f_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({\bm{x}})-f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\sum_{l=1}^{d}{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}+o(\|{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2})

(102)

\displaystyle\leq d\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}.

(103)

When considering ReLU networks with bounded inputs (i.e.,
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}\leq B
), we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{l,\max}=\|{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\leq B\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}}{\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}}.

(104)

This is because the Jacobian
{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}})
is influenced by both the forward pass, i.e., from the input to layer-
l
, and the backward pass, i.e., from layer-
l
to the output, as well as the fact that the ReLU activation function is 1-Lipschitz. Therefore, the spectral norm of
{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}})
is upper-bounded by the product of the spectral norms of the weight matrices of other layers, multiplied by the upper bound of the input norm.

Let

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}_{l}=\sqrt{d}{\bm{V}}_{l}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{l}{\bm{V}}_{l}^{T}

(105)

where
{\bm{V}}_{l}
is the collections of corresponding right singular vectors of
{\bm{J}}_{l}
, and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{l}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{l,1},\dots,\sigma_{l,K},0,\dots,0)
with

\displaystyle\sigma_{l,k}=B\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}}{\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}},\quad\forall\;k=1,\dots,K.

(106)

It is clear that

\displaystyle{\bm{J}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{J}}_{l}\preceq{\bm{V}}_{l}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{l}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{l}^{T}=\frac{1}{d}{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{l}.

(107)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{d}{\bm{u}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}

(108)

\displaystyle=d\sum_{l=1}^{d}{\bm{u}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{J}}_{l}^{T}({\bm{x}}){\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}_{l}=d\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{J}}_{l}({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|f_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({\bm{x}})-f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}

(109)

\displaystyle\geq\|f_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({\bm{x}})-f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(110)

for any
{\bm{u}}_{l}
, so that the perturbation condition Eq-(
9
) is satisfied.

of a fixed
\hat{\beta}

As it holds

VI-D

Proof of Theorem
3

Let us consider the convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with ReLU activation functions, where
\{{\bm{W}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times h}
are (block) circulant matrices such that
{\bm{W}}_{l}=\mathrm{circ}({\bm{w}}_{l})
with
{\bm{w}}_{l}
being the vectorized convolutional filter (a.k.a. kernel). Each row of
{\bm{W}}_{l}
consists of the shifted version of
{\bm{w}}_{l}
with circulant padding. Thus, the size of
{\bm{w}}_{l}
is at most
h
, and usually much smaller than
h
. For simplicity, we consider
{\bm{w}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
and the corresponding weight perturbation
{\bm{u}}_{l}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}{\bm{R}}_{l})
with
{\bm{R}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times h}
added onto
{\bm{w}}_{l}
.

Given the fact that the (block) circulant matrices can be (block)-diagonalized by discrete Fourier transform (DFT) matrices, i.e.,
{\bm{V}}={\bm{F}}
for 1-dim and
{\bm{V}}={\bm{F}}\otimes{\bm{F}}
for 2-dim circular convolution with
{\bm{F}}
being DFT matrix, we can design the sensitivity matrices
{\bm{A}}_{l}
in the frequency domain.

Given the circulant structure of
{\bm{W}}_{l}
, we have

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{l}={\bm{V}}\tilde{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{l}{\bm{V}}^{H}

(128)

where
\tilde{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{l}
is a diagonal matrix containing eigenvalues of
{\bm{W}}_{l}
. When
{\bm{W}}_{l}
is a block circulant matrix (resp. tensor convolutional filters),
\tilde{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{l}
is a block diagonal matrix.

For simplicity, we focus on the scalar case with
\tilde{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{l}
being a diagonal matrix, and the extension to the tensor case can be straightforwardly done according to
[
45
]
.
In the frequency domain, the inputs turns to be
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}={\bm{V}}^{H}{\bm{x}}
, and the convolutional filter at the
l
-th layer is
\tilde{{\bm{w}}}_{l}={\bm{V}}^{H}{\bm{w}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
, where
{\bm{w}}_{l}
is the convolutional filter. For circulant matrices, it is readily verified that
\tilde{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{l}=\mathrm{diag}(\tilde{{\bm{w}}})
. Therefore, we have the output in the frequency domain
\tilde{f}_{\tilde{{\bm{w}}}}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}})={\bm{V}}^{H}f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\tilde{{\bm{w}}}}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}})

\displaystyle={\bm{V}}^{H}{\bm{W}}_{d}\phi({\bm{W}}_{d-1}\phi(\dots\phi({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{V}}\tilde{{\bm{x}}})))

(129)

\displaystyle\approx\tilde{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{d}\phi(\tilde{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{d-1}\phi(\dots\phi(\tilde{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{1}\tilde{{\bm{x}}}))),

(130)

due to the ReLU activation function
\phi(\cdot)
.
It looks as if we have a feedforward neural network with diagonal weight matrices
\{\tilde{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
, so that the weight perturbation in the frequency domain works as
\tilde{{\bm{w}}}_{l}+\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}
in a layer-wise manner, where the compact form of weight perturbation in the frequency domain is
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}={\bm{V}}^{H}{\bm{u}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
due to parameter sharing introduced by circular convolution.
For the spatial- and frequency-domain neural networks, we have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}_{\tilde{{\bm{w}}}+\tilde{{\bm{u}}}}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}})-\tilde{f}_{\tilde{{\bm{w}}}}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|{\bm{V}}^{H}({f}_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({{\bm{x}}})-{f}_{{\bm{w}}}({{\bm{x}}}))\|_{2}

(131)

\displaystyle=\|{f}_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({{\bm{x}}})-{f}_{{\bm{w}}}({{\bm{x}}})\|_{2}

(132)

because
{\bm{V}}
is unitary and the rotation with unitary matrices does not change the
\ell_{2}
norm.

In what follows, we consider in a layer-wise manner to figure out proper choices of the sensitivity matrices
\{{\bm{A}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
and the corresponding generalization bounds.

VI-D

We treat CNNs in the frequency domain as feedforward neural networks with diagonal weight matrices

, where the number of weights is reduced from

to
h
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}_{\tilde{{\bm{w}}}+\tilde{{\bm{u}}}}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}})-\tilde{f}_{\tilde{{\bm{w}}}}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8(e^{4}+1)\kappa K\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\lambda_{l}^{2}

(150)

\displaystyle\lesssim{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{B^{2}d^{2}K\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{V}}^{H}{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)

(151)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{B^{2}d^{2}K}{\gamma^{2}}{\Phi}^{\mathrm{circ}}({\bm{w}})\right)

(152)

where where
\delta(\cdot)
is defined in Eq-(
87
) and
{\Phi}^{\mathrm{circ}}({\bm{w}})=\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{V}}^{H}{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}
.

Note here that in CNNs
{\Phi}^{\mathrm{circ}}({\bm{w}})
with convolutional filters
\{{\bm{w}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
could be
h
times smaller than
{\Phi}({\bm{w}})
with weight matrices
\{{\bm{W}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}
due the the parameter sharing induced by convolutional layers, i.e.,
{\bm{W}}_{l}=\mathrm{circ}({\bm{w}}_{l})
.
Hence, for a fixed
\hat{\beta}
, the generalization error bound can be given by

\displaystyle L_{0}(f_{{\bm{w}}})\leq\hat{L}_{\gamma}(f_{{\bm{w}}})+\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{B^{2}d^{2}K{\Phi}^{\mathrm{circ}}({\bm{w}})+\ln\frac{m}{\delta}}{\gamma^{2}m}}\right).

(153)

Following the same footsteps as in
[
13
]
and those in the previous sections, we end up with the final
generalization bound as stated in the theorem for any
\beta
.

VI-E

Proof of Theorem
4

Let us consider neural networks with certain weight sharing, where
\{{\bm{W}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times h}
are banded Toeplitz matrices such that
{\bm{W}}_{l}=\mathrm{toep}({\bm{w}}_{l})
with
{\bm{w}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
being the Toeplitz kernel.
Specifically, for a vector
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
[\mathrm{toep}({\bm{v}})]_{i,j}=v_{j-i}
for
0\leq j-i\leq k-1
and 0 otherwise.
Usually
k\ll h
in practice.
As Toeplitz matrices are a general case of circulant matrices, the corresponding neural networks typically feature linear convolutional layers. Therefore, each row of
{\bm{W}}_{l}
is actually the shifted Toeplitz kernel with zero padding. The corresponding weight perturbation
{\bm{u}}_{l}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}{\bm{R}}_{l})
with
{\bm{R}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
. Denote by
{\bm{w}}=({\bm{w}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{w}}_{d})
and
{\bm{u}}=({\bm{u}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{u}}_{d})
as the concatenated weight and perturbation vectors, respectively.

Similar to the convolution kernels, the Toeplitz structure implies local, translation-invariant sensitivity.
Unlike the circulant case, here we consider the Toeplitz matrix from a spectral perspective, where the perturbation bound does not rely on a Taylor approximation. We still consider the expanded weight matrices
{\bm{W}}_{l}=\mathrm{toep}({\bm{w}}_{l})
and its corresponding perturbation
{\bm{U}}_{l}=\mathrm{toep}({\bm{u}}_{l})
.
This corresponds to weight sharing in sensitivity, not in the network itself.
The Toeplitz sensitivity matrix enforces correlations among parameter perturbations. That is,
the nearby parameters (with respect to the indices) have correlated influence on the output, and sensitivity depends on relative offsets, rather than the absolute indices.

VI-E
1
Perturbation bound

Let
{\bm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{2}\times h^{2}}
be a banded Toeplitz matrix with bandwidth
k
and the generating sequence
(\{t_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k-1})
up to design, and
\psi_{\min}=\min_{\omega}|\psi(\omega)|>0
with
\psi(\omega)
being
the spectral symbol (a.k.a. generating function) of
{\bm{T}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\psi(\omega)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}t_{i}e^{-\jmath i\omega}

(154)

with
\omega\in[0,2\pi]
. The eigenvalues of
{\bm{T}}
are concentrated around the samples of
\psi(\omega)
.

Let
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}=\mathrm{vec}({\bm{U}}_{l})={\bm{P}}{\bm{u}}_{l}
, where
{\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{2}\times k}
is a structured sparse binary matrix, such that for each column
j
(
j=0,1,\dots,k-1
), there is a 1 at row index
ih^{2}+q
whenever
q-i=j
with
0\leq i,q<h^{2}
.
Given that
{\bm{u}}_{l}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}{\bm{R}}_{l})
, it follows that
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}{\bm{P}}{\bm{R}}_{l}{\bm{P}}^{T})
.
With the properties of Toeplitz matrices, we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{T}}\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{\min}({\bm{T}})\|\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\|_{2}\geq\psi_{\min}\|\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\|_{2}

(155)

where
\sigma_{\min}({\bm{T}})
is the smallest singular value of
{\bm{T}}
, and the last inequality is due to the Szegö limit theorem
[
44
]
.

Define an auxiliary matrix
\tilde{{\bm{A}}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{h^{2}\times h^{2}}
be a Toeplitz matrix with

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{A}}}_{l}=\frac{e\sqrt{d}B\prod_{l}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}}{\psi_{\min}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}}{\bm{T}},\ \forall~l,

(156)

so that we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|\tilde{{\bm{A}}}_{l}\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}B^{2}d\prod_{l}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}}{\psi_{\min}^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{\|{\bm{T}}\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}}

(157)

\displaystyle\geq e^{2}B^{2}d\prod_{l}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{\|{\bm{U}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2}}{\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}}

(158)

\displaystyle\geq e^{2}B^{2}\prod_{l}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{\|{\bm{U}}_{l}\|_{2}}{\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}}\right)^{2}

(159)

\displaystyle\geq\|f_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({\bm{x}})-f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}

(160)

where the first inequality is due to Eq-(
155
) and
\|\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}=\|{\bm{U}}_{l}\|_{F}^{2}
. The rest follows similarly to the diagonal case, where the last inequality holds regardless of the structure of
{\bm{W}}_{l}
. In fact, the Toeplitz case yields a looser perturbation bound than the diagonal case, yet its underlying weight sharing would lead to a tighter generalization bound.

Note here that we could either apply the concentration inequality to
\|\tilde{{\bm{A}}}_{l}\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\|_{2}
with
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}{\bm{P}}{\bm{R}}_{l}{\bm{P}}^{T})
containing repeated elements in
{\bm{u}}_{l}
due to the Toeplitz structure, or to
\|{{\bm{A}}}_{l}{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\|_{2}
with
{\bm{u}}_{l}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}{\bm{R}}_{l})
directly with a refined sensitivity matrix
{\bm{A}}_{l}
, such that

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}_{l}=\tilde{{\bm{A}}}_{l}{\bm{P}}.

(161)

Since

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}^{T}\tilde{{\bm{A}}}_{l}^{T}\tilde{{\bm{A}}}_{l}\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{l}={\bm{u}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l},

(162)

we have the perturbation bound holds, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{{\bm{A}}}_{l}{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\|f_{{\bm{w}}+{\bm{u}}}({\bm{x}})-f_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{x}})\|_{\infty}^{2}

(163)

with a
h^{2}\times k
sensitivity matrix
{\bm{A}}_{l}
.
In doing so, the concentration inequality of
\|{\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{u}}_{l}\|_{2}
follows as in the previous cases without loss of rigor.
This demonstrates that the perturbation bounds do not actually require Jacobian domination, but energy domination in parameter space.

VI-E
2
The choice of
\sigma
for a fixed
\hat{\beta}

Similarly to the diagonal case, for a fixed
\hat{\beta}
, we introduce the approximation of
{\bm{A}}_{l}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{A}}}_{l}=\frac{e\sqrt{d}B\prod_{l}\|\hat{{\bm{W}}}_{l}\|_{2}}{\psi_{\min}\|\hat{{\bm{W}}}_{l}\|_{2}}{\bm{T}}{\bm{P}},\ \forall~l.

(164)

Then it follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{e^{2}}{\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}\preceq\hat{{\bm{A}}}_{l}\hat{{\bm{A}}}_{l}^{T}\preceq e^{2}{\bm{A}}_{l}{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}.

(165)

By enforcing

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{{\bm{A}}}_{l}{{\bm{u}}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}\kappa\sum_{l=1}^{d}\Tr({{\bm{A}}}_{l}{{\bm{R}}}_{l}{{\bm{A}}}_{l}^{T})

(166)

\displaystyle\leq e^{2}\sigma^{2}\kappa\sum_{l=1}^{d}\Tr(\hat{{\bm{A}}}_{l}\hat{{\bm{A}}}_{l}^{T})\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{16}

(167)

with
{{\bm{R}}}_{l}\preceq{\bm{I}}
, we set

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\sigma}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{16e^{2}\kappa}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\Tr(\hat{{\bm{A}}}_{l}\hat{{\bm{A}}}_{l}^{T}).

(168)

VI-E
3
PAC-Bayesian bound for a fixed
\hat{\beta}

Due to weight sharing, the weights of interest in the KL term reduce to
{\bm{w}}=({\bm{w}}_{1},{\bm{w}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{w}}_{d})
.
Next, according to Eq-(
28
), we have the optimized
{{\bm{R}}}_{l}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{{\bm{R}}}_{l}^{*}=({\bm{I}}+{\eta}^{2}{\bm{A}}_{l}^{T}{\bm{A}}_{l})^{-1}=({\bm{I}}+{\eta}^{2}{\lambda}_{l}^{2}{\bm{P}}^{T}{\bm{T}}^{T}{\bm{T}}{\bm{P}})^{-1}

(169)

where
\eta^{2}=\frac{16\kappa\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}
and

\displaystyle\lambda_{l}=\frac{e\sqrt{d}B\prod_{l}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}}{\psi_{\min}\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}}.

(170)

To make the optimized
{{\bm{R}}}_{l}^{*}
tractable, we consider the asymptotic analysis.
Due to the Szegö limit theorem with respect to the eigenvalue distribution, where
g({\bm{T}})\leftrightarrow g(\psi(\omega))
for any continuous function
g
, in the sense that
[
44
]

\displaystyle\lim_{h^{2}\to\infty}\frac{1}{h^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{h^{2}}g(\sigma_{j}({\bm{T}}))=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}g(\psi(\omega))\mathrm{d}\omega

(171)

we have, as
h^{2}\to\infty
, that

\displaystyle\Tr({{\bm{A}}}_{l}{{\bm{A}}}_{l}^{T})

\displaystyle\sim\frac{k}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lambda_{l}^{2}|\psi(\omega)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\omega

(172)

\displaystyle\Tr({\bm{R}}_{l}^{*})-\log\det{\bm{R}}_{l}^{*}-\dim({\bm{R}}_{l}^{*})

\displaystyle\sim\frac{k}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\delta({\eta}{\lambda}_{l}|\psi(\omega)|)\mathrm{d}\omega

(173)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\eta}^{2}{\lambda}_{l}^{2}|\psi(\omega)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\omega

(174)

with
\delta(x)=\log(1+x^{2})-\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}\leq x^{2}
as defined before.

Through
{\bm{R}}_{l}
, Toeplitz suppresses structured directions, keeps sensitivity only where the symbol is large, corresponding to smoothness in parameter sensitivity.
With the choice of
\sigma^{2}
, the KL divergence can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8e^{2}\kappa\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\Tr(\hat{{\bm{A}}}_{l}\hat{{\bm{A}}}_{l}^{T})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left(\Tr({\bm{R}}_{l}^{*})-\log\det{\bm{R}}_{l}^{*}-\dim({\bm{R}}_{l}^{*})\right)

(175)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{8e^{4}\kappa\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{k}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lambda_{l}^{2}|\psi(\omega)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\omega

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{k}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\delta({\eta}{\lambda}_{l}|\psi(\omega)|)\mathrm{d}\omega

(176)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{8(e^{4}+1)\kappa\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{k}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lambda_{l}^{2}|\psi(\omega)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\omega

(177)

\displaystyle\lesssim{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{B^{2}d^{2}k\beta^{2d-2}}{\psi_{\min}^{2}\gamma^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\psi(\omega)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\omega\right)

(178)

\displaystyle\lesssim{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{B^{2}d^{2}k}{\gamma^{2}}\frac{\psi_{\max}^{2}}{\psi_{\min}^{2}}\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{w}}_{i}\|_{1}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}\right)

(179)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{B^{2}d^{2}k}{\gamma^{2}}\Phi^{\mathrm{toep}}({\bm{w}})\right)

(180)

where
\psi_{\max}=\max_{\omega}|\psi(\omega)|
,
\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}\leq\beta=\|{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{2}\leq\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{1}
for Toeplitz matrices, and the spectral complexity becomes

\displaystyle\Phi^{\mathrm{toep}}({\bm{w}})=\frac{\psi_{\max}^{2}}{\psi_{\min}^{2}}\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{w}}_{i}\|_{1}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}.

(181)

With the same arguments as the previous, we end up with the final generalization bound in the theorem

\displaystyle L_{0}(f_{{\bm{w}}})\leq\hat{L}_{\gamma}(f_{{\bm{w}}})+\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{B^{2}d^{2}k\Phi^{\mathrm{toep}}({\bm{w}})+\ln\frac{md}{\delta}}{\gamma^{2}m}}\right)

(182)

for any
{\beta}
. As the “kernel” spectral complexity
\Phi^{\mathrm{toep}}({\bm{w}})
is usually much smaller than that of the spectral complexity of entire weight matrices, this bound is tighter than the previous one
[
13
]
, when network structures follow the Toeplitz style.

In what follows, we consider some special designs of
{\bm{T}}
.

Orthogonal circulant matrix.
Let
{\bm{T}}
be an orthogonal circulant matrix, i.e.,
{\bm{T}}^{T}{\bm{T}}={\bm{I}}
, which is also Toeplitz. So,
\psi_{\max}=\psi_{\min}=1
.
As such, the scaling factor reduces to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{B^{2}d^{2}k}{\gamma^{2}}\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{w}}_{i}\|_{1}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)

(183)

which is comparable with that in Theorem
3
, because
\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}\leq\|{\bm{V}}^{H}{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{\infty}\leq\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{1}
, yet is tighter than others.

Geometric kernel with exponential decay.

Let
{\bm{T}}
be a banded symmetric Toeplitz matrix with exponential decay, i.e.,
t_{i}=\rho^{|i|}
, with
\rho\in(0,1)
.
The exponentially decaying entries encode smooth attenuation of perturbation effects, mimicking spatial or channel locality. The design avoids unnecessary coupling of unrelated coordinates (unlike full matrices) while still being richer than diagonal.

In this case, we have

\displaystyle\psi(\omega)=\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{1-2\rho\cos\omega+\rho^{2}}

(184)

which yields

\displaystyle\frac{\psi_{\max}}{\psi_{\min}}=\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}.

(185)

The diagonal case is a special Toeplitz when
\rho=0
, where the spectral symbol is flat, and all frequencies are penalized equally. The symbol
\psi(\omega)
is a frequency filter, where the low-frequency components are penalized more. Thus, the resulting scaling factor with
\rho=0
reduces to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{B^{2}d^{2}k}{\gamma^{2}}\prod_{i\neq l}\|{\bm{w}}_{i}\|_{1}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\|{\bm{w}}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)

(186)

which is strictly tighter than those in Theorem
1
and in
[
13
]
.
