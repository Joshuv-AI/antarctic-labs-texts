A sustainability-oriented supply chain supplier selection framework using resilience and HSE performance metrics in the automotive industry
Authors: Saeid Maddah, Amin Padash, Mostafa Hajiaghaei-Keshteli, Gholam Reza Nabi Bidhendi, Ata Allah Taleizadeh, Faezeh Borhani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Occupational Safety and Health (OSH) represents a core element of the social dimension of sustainability, working in tandem with environmental performance to support sustainable development. Resilience Engineering complements this perspective by focusing on organisational adaptability and the ability to maintain safety in dynamic, uncertain environments. The objective of this study is to develop an integrated framework for sustainable supplier selection that incorporates both resilience and Health, Safety, and Environment (HSE) performance as strategic evaluation criteria. This approach aims to guide organisations in aligning procurement practices with broader sustainability and safety goals. To construct and validate the proposed framework, expert input was gathered and synthesised using the Delphi method, ensuring consensus on critical evaluation factors. The Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was applied to assess the clarity and relevance of the selected criteria. For prioritisation, the Best-Worst Method (BWM) was used to determine the weight of each criterion. The multi-attributive Border Approximation Area Comparison (MABAC) method was then employed to rank supplier performance across the established criteria. The resulting framework consists of seven main categories and 26 sub-criteria, representing a holistic view of supplier sustainability, resilience, and HSE performance. The model was applied to assess four suppliers, demonstrating its effectiveness in identifying variances in safety practices, adaptability, and environmental responsibility. It provided clear, actionable insights into the comparative strengths and weaknesses of each supplier. The integrated framework offers a practical and strategic tool for enhancing supplier selection decisions. By embedding resilience and HSE into evaluation processes, organizations can support long-term goals related to safety, adaptability, and sustainability. This structured approach facilitates the development of robust and adaptive supply chains, reinforcing the alignment between operational practices and sustainable development principles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30055-9.

Introduction

Supply Chain Management (SCM) has evolved from a purely logistical function to a strategic capability enabling competitiveness, adaptability, and innovation across global networks1,2. Modern supply chains are no longer linear transactional systems but complex, interconnected ecosystems that integrate digitalization, sustainability, and agility. In the Industry 4.0 era, technologies such as cyber-physical systems, data analytics, and automation have transformed how organizations design, monitor, and coordinate their value networks3. Consequently, supplier management has become central to organizational performance, as suppliers determine the quality, cost, and environmental footprint of final products4,5. Sustainability has become an imperative strategic dimension of SCM, emphasizing not only financial efficiency but also environmental and social responsibility. The concept of Sustainable Supply Chain Management (SSCM) integrates economic viability with ecological preservation and social well-being6,7. Within SSCM, supplier selection plays a pivotal role, as suppliers’ practices directly influence the overall sustainability performance of the supply chain. Accordingly, supplier evaluation criteria have expanded beyond cost, delivery, and quality to include eco-innovation, waste reduction, carbon emissions, and occupational health and safety8.

However, contemporary supply chains face unprecedented disruptions caused by geopolitical conflicts, pandemics, resource shortages, and climate-related disasters. These challenges have revealed the fragility of globalized production systems and underscored the need for resilience—the capacity to anticipate, absorb, and recover from disturbances while maintaining functionality9. Resilience has therefore become a complementary dimension of sustainability, ensuring that environmental and social commitments remain viable under conditions of uncertainty10,11.

Empirical motivation and industry statistics

Recent global data reveal that the supply chain sector still faces major sustainability and resilience challenges. According to the World Economic Forum12, more than $1.6 trillion in annual losses across global manufacturing stem from supply chain disruptions related to climate events, geopolitical instability, and pandemics. Despite growing awareness, only 28% of manufacturing firms have fully implemented sustainability metrics within supplier evaluation13, and fewer than 15% systematically assess suppliers’ occupational health and safety performance14. The automotive industry is particularly exposed: a single supply disruption can halt assembly lines within 72 h, while environmental non-compliance among Tier-2 suppliers remains above 30% in emerging economies15. Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), which constitute nearly 90% of automotive suppliers, experience up to three times more workplace accidents than large firms16 and account for roughly 30% of industrial pollution17. These statistics clearly demonstrate that although sustainability and resilience are widely promoted, their practical integration—especially incorporating HSE performance—remains limited, underscoring the need for an integrated evaluation framework such as the one proposed in this study.

Linking resilience, sustainability, and HSE performance

A growing body of literature connects resilience and sustainability with Health, Safety, and Environment (HSE) performance18,19. Workplace safety incidents and environmental failures can trigger cascading operational disruptions, resulting in production stoppages, quality degradation, and reputational harm. Conversely, strong HSE systems strengthen resilience by stabilizing operations and improving workforce well-being20,21. The integration of HSE management systems with sustainability programs is thus essential for achieving both short-term reliability and long-term adaptability22,23. Recent studies in Green Lean Six Sigma (GLSS) further reinforce this argument. GLSS integrates lean efficiency, six sigma quality, and green sustainability objectives, thereby enhancing operational performance while reducing environmental impact24–26. Yet, despite its synergies, GLSS applications have predominantly focused on manufacturing efficiency or environmental metrics, with limited attention to occupational safety and systemic resilience. Studies such as3,27 show that organizational agility and readiness are critical for Industry 4.0 transformation, but they do not explicitly operationalize HSE performance or resilience within supplier selection frameworks.

Fragmented treatment of sustainability, resilience, and HSE dimensions. Existing models often analyze these three areas independently. Sustainability studies focus on green procurement and carbon efficiency6, resilience research emphasizes agility and redundancy11, and HSE studies target workplace safety without linking it to operational continuity21. There is no integrated framework that unifies these dimensions into a comprehensive evaluation system.

Research objectives and innovation

To address these limitations, this study develops a Sustainability-Oriented Supplier Selection Framework that integrates Resilience and HSE performance metrics. A major innovation is the Resilient, Green, and Healthy (RGH) Index, a new composite tool conceptually inspired by prior sustainability and safety models but fully developed and validated within this research. The RGH Index combines resilience engineering principles, environmental indicators, and HSE metrics through a hybrid Best–Worst Method (BWM) and Multi-Attributive Border Approximation Area Comparison (MABAC) model.

Significance of the study

This research is important because it addresses a critical and long-standing gap in supply chain management by integrating sustainability, resilience, and Health, Safety, and Environment (HSE) performance into a single, unified evaluation framework. While previous studies have advanced green and resilient supplier selection independently, they have failed to capture the interdependent nature of environmental responsibility, operational adaptability, and human safety that defines modern, sustainable enterprises. The proposed Resilient, Green, and Healthy (RGH) Index offers an innovative, data-driven tool that quantitatively measures these three pillars through a hybrid Best–Worst Method (BWM) and MABAC approach. Theoretically, this integration contributes to the development of a new multidimensional perspective in supply chain sustainability research, combining the principles of the Triple Bottom Line and Resilience Engineering. Practically, it provides decision-makers—particularly within automotive SMEs—with a comprehensive, evidence-based mechanism for supplier assessment, enabling them to enhance competitiveness, reduce risks, and ensure long-term sustainability and worker well-being in the industry 4.0 era.

Literature review

Green supply chain management and supplier selection

Environmental consciousness, regulatory pressures, and corporate social responsibility have led to the widespread adoption of Green Supply Chain Management (GSCM). Green supplier selection, a key component of GSCM, involves multiple qualitative and quantitative parameters, making it a Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) problem29–31. Classical MCDM methods (AHP, ANP, DEMATEL, TOPSIS) and hybrid or fuzzy extensions have been widely used32,33, Fattahi et al. 2021. However, these studies largely focus on environmental criteria.

Integration of green, resilient, and HSE approaches

A limited number of studies combine green and resilient supplier selection56,57,60, yet none fully integrate HSE performance—a critical enabler of both sustainability and resilience. Resilience engineering principles (anticipation, learning, flexibility) align with occupational safety frameworks61, but their joint operationalization in supplier evaluation remains absent. Moreover, research largely overlooks SMEs, which are more disruption-prone and safety-sensitive than large firms.

Research gap and theoretical foundation

A critical review of existing literature reveals that despite significant progress in sustainable and resilient supply chain research, the integration of sustainability, resilience, and HSE (Health, Safety, and Environment) remains fragmented and conceptually inconsistent. The majority of studies have developed frameworks that either emphasize environmental efficiency (green supply chain management) or focus on system adaptability and risk mitigation (resilient supply chain management). However, these domains are rarely combined within a unified evaluation model. As a result, the complex interdependence between operational safety, environmental stewardship, and supply continuity is not adequately captured in current supplier selection practices3,24,26.

First, there exists a conceptual gap in linking resilience engineering principles—such as anticipation, flexibility, and learning—to sustainability and HSE performance. Most green supplier selection frameworks lack indicators that measure organizational capacity to respond to disruptions or prevent safety incidents. Similarly, resilience-oriented studies focus primarily on system recovery and redundancy without embedding environmental or occupational safety considerations. This separation leads to suboptimal decision-making where suppliers may perform well in one dimension but poorly in others, undermining overall supply chain stability and sustainability.

Second, there is a methodological gap in developing quantitative composite indices that simultaneously evaluate the three dimensions. Existing Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) approaches, while effective for isolated criteria, often rely on subjective weighting or incomplete indicator sets that fail to reflect multi-dimensional interrelationships. Few models employ hybrid or integrated techniques—such as Best–Worst Method (BWM) combined with Multi-Attributive Border Approximation Area Comparison (MABAC)—to objectively assess supplier performance across sustainability, resilience, and HSE domains.

Third, a contextual gap is evident in the limited empirical validation of these integrated frameworks within small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), particularly in the automotive sector. SMEs form the backbone of global supply chains yet face greater operational vulnerabilities, resource constraints, and safety challenges. Most prior studies focus on large corporations with mature management systems, leaving SMEs underrepresented in the literature16,28.

Theoretically, the integration of sustainability, resilience, and HSE can be supported by the Resilience Engineering Theory62 and the Triple Bottom Line (TBL) framework. Resilience Engineering emphasizes proactive safety and adaptive capacity in socio-technical systems, while TBL highlights the balanced pursuit of economic, environmental, and social goals. Combining these perspectives provides a holistic foundation for developing decision models that promote eco-efficiency, operational robustness, and human well-being.

Addressing the above deficiencies, this study proposes the Resilient, Green, and Healthy (RGH) Index—a novel, integrated, and quantifiable framework that unites sustainability, resilience, and HSE performance under a single decision-support mechanism. The RGH Index operationalizes theoretical insights from both resilience engineering and sustainability management, enabling supply chain practitioners to make evidence-based, balanced, and adaptive supplier decisions aligned with Industry 4.0 requirements.

Phase 1 Criteria identification and development.

Phase 1 establishes the conceptual groundwork for the entire research by connecting contextual understanding, theoretical reasoning, and systematic literature synthesis to generate the initial set of evaluation criteria. It begins with defining the context and objectives—clarifying the industrial setting (automotive SMEs), the relevance of Industry 4.0, and the overarching aim of integrating sustainability, resilience, and HSE dimensions into supplier evaluation. The phase then proceeds to a literature synthesis, systematically reviewing scholarly works, standards, and industry reports to identify existing frameworks, methodological trends, and knowledge gaps in green, resilient, and safety-oriented supply chain management. Building on these insights, a theoretical integration is developed by aligning the Triple Bottom Line and Resilience Engineering perspectives, establishing the conceptual logic that sustainable performance, operational adaptability, and worker safety are mutually reinforcing. Finally, the candidate criteria extraction step translates these theoretical constructs into measurable factors—grouped under the domains of Resilience, Green, and HSE (R–G–H)—forming the preliminary foundation for subsequent phases of indicator operationalization and model development.

By bridging these dimensions within the context of automotive SMEs, the study not only advances theoretical understanding but also offers a practical decision-support pathway for organizations aiming to build supply chains that are environmentally responsible, disruption-resilient, and safe for their workforce. Phase 2 Selecting experts and decision-makers.

Panelist selection is the most important methodological step for a Delphi panel63. In this phase, appropriate experts are selected by nonprobability and judgmental sampling techniques to ask their opinions to define and weighting the criteria. Two groups of participants, namely, Group A and Group B, were established. Group A consisted of five experts whose opinions were asked to determine and weighting the criteria in the evaluation. Group B consisted of 15 participants whose opinions were asked to measure the content validity of the designed tool and criteria. The members of groups A and B were selected from the following categories:

Managers and decision-makers of the company that is engaged in supply chain management of the automobile industry and is studied in this research. These people are responsible for assessing, auditing, and ranking suppliers (automobile parts manufacturers), and companies’ assessing the criteria are finalized and applied according to their opinion. They can properly include the company’s policies in grading these criteria.

Experts responsible for health, safety, and environment (HSE) unit with long work experience in automobile parts manufacturing companies in addition to companies active in the car industry, so that they can assess and grade the criteria concerning existing issues and challenges in the industry.

University professors specializing in environment, safety management, and occupational health with experience in different HSE projects and management systems auditing can grade the criteria both due to their knowledge of scientific literature on the subject and the real atmosphere of industry in the country.

Phase 3 Developing the evaluation framework (RGH index).

This phase includes defining the resilience and HSE performance evaluation criteria and sub-criteria for evaluating supplier companies in terms of resiliency, greenness, and occupational safety and health (OHS) concepts. By integrating these concepts, the criteria are categorized and RGH index as the evaluation framework is developed. Firstly, the criteria were collected through an extant literature review that was carried out by keywords such as occupational health and safety (OHS) performance evaluation indicators, HSE performance evaluation indicators/criteria, resilience criteria, green supplier selection, sustainable supplier selection, and resilient supplier selection. Then, the criteria which have been applied for small and medium-sized enterprises were chosen and categorized.

Next, they were discussed and finalized using the three-round expert panel process of Delphi method. Panelists of group A, engaged in this structured consensus process. The first round involves reading the objectives of the study and a list of criteria and indices that were collected through a literature review. The second round includes participation in an expert panel meeting to integrate the concepts, and the third round involves a consensus of opinions on categorizing the criteria and sub-criteria and making the final framework of evaluation. The Delphi method, first introduced by the RAND Corporation, is a structured process that uses a series of questionnaires or rounds to emerge a consensus of opinions and to provide information64. Delphi method depends on requesting individual answers to written questions by survey or other types of communication which, in this case, is organizing expert panel meetings.

Phase 4 Validity test of the designed framework.

In statistics, validity is the extent to which a question or measurement criteria is justifiable and consistent accurately with real-world conditions. There are different types of validities such as construct validity, face validity, and content validity. In this study, Content Validity Ratio (CVR) that is proposed by65, was used to test the content validity of criteria and sub-criteria in the designed framework. In the method, each expert from group B answers the following question for each item: Is the knowledge or skill measured by this item ‘’not essential”, ‘’useful, but not necessary” or ‘’essential” to the performance of the construct? The content validity ratio varies between 1 and − 1. The higher score indicates further agreement of experts of a panel on the necessity of an item in an evaluation framework66. Phase 5 Weighting the Criteria using BWM

Hierarchy Design and Criteria Integration

In traditional MCDM applications, criteria are assumed to be independent; however, sustainability-related performance dimensions often exhibit interdependence. In this study, the hierarchical structure was formulated based on a systemic integration approach, combining Health, Safety, and Environment (HSE), Green, and Resilience aspects to capture their mutual influence on supplier performance. This design reflects findings from prior research that have integrated multiple sustainability domains into unified MCDM hierarchies. For example24,26, developed Green–Lean–Six Sigma and Green–Lean–Resilient frameworks, while54–57 incorporated green and resilient supplier evaluation factors within a common multi-layered model. Consistent with these approaches, the present hierarchy recognizes that supplier safety and environmental practices directly enhance resilience, justifying a cross-domain sub-criteria structure under the overarching RGH framework.

In this phase weights of each criterion and sub-criterion are calculated using Best-Worst multi-criteria Method (BWM) according to the group A experts’ opinion67. established Best Worst Method which is a new MCDM method to minimize the instability incorporated in calculating criteria weights. This is realized by modifying the necessities of enormous pairwise comparisons among criteria, as typically observed and confronted in Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and Analytic Network Process (ANP) methods-based computations. As described by68,69 different steps in the implementation of BWM are discussed below:

Step 1: Finalization of decision criteria. Decision criteria that were finalized in the first phase of the research will be taken. These criteria are symbolized as {c1, c2…, cn} for n main criteria.

Step 2: specification of the best and the worst criteria among the main ones, in addition to sub-criteria.

Step 3: Determination of the preference of the best criteria over all other criteria on a scale of 1 to 9. The vector of 10 best criteria can be represented as: AB= (αB1, αB2, …, αBn) where αBj displays the rating of best-selected criteria B over any other criteria j. In this case, αBB = 1. Several experts reached a consensus to finalize the preference ratings.

Phase 6 Supplier ranking and selection.

The final phase includes assessing the premier suppliers of auto parts and ranking them according to the designed framework discussed in the previous step. After calculating the weights of criteria and HSE performance evaluation indicators concerning the resilience engineering approach, four premier suppliers of auto parts were selected for assessment. These four companies which stand at the highest levels of grade A among other suppliers are selected according to performance indicators like price, quality, technical capacity, on-time delivery of products and so on. The data used in this phase were collected through auditing, visiting the companies, and grading the indicators in a 7-point Likert scale. Data analysis and ranking of the companies were done using MABAK multi-criteria decision-making method. MABAK method or Multi-Attributive Border Approximation area Comparison, one of the newest multi-criteria decision-making methods, includes six steps as follows70:

Formation of primary decision matrix.

In this method, the decision matrix is a criterion alternative. In other words, it consists of a matrix in which its columns are criteria/indices of the problem and its rows are alternatives. Case study.

This section will briefly explain the context of the company in which the case study was performed. The application of the five-step methodology is carried out on the case company to develop a robust model of supplier selection for them among multiple suppliers. The application of this methodology in real-world examples signifies its validity and robustness. The supply chain management of one of the biggest automobile manufacturers in Iran is chosen as the case to select resilient, green, and healthy suppliers. Selecting and ranking suppliers for grading in this company is mainly based on criteria such as quality, prices, delivery, and technical capacity and, in a lower degree of importance, also on environmental criteria. Thus, due to the environmental protection strategy of the company, in addition to the importance of social responsibility criteria such as occupational health and safety situation of employees, and to help have a resilient supply chain, in this study RGH index was defined to develop a framework for the company to rank and select the most resilient, green and healthy suppliers from among other suppliers. There are an extensive number of suppliers that are ranked as grade A, but due to limitations on data gathering, only the four top suppliers are selected as the volunteer companies for being evaluated and ranked.

Results and discussion

The results are presented as follows, in the order mentioned in the methodology:

Identifying decision-makers

A panel of experts including five participants was organized to finalize proper and relevant criteria and to weigh them. Experts from Group A were considered the decision-makers in this study, which includes the supplier evaluation project manager in supply chain management of automobile manufacturer as Decision-Maker 1 (DM1), department manager of supplier selection and evaluation in supply chain management of automobile manufacturer as DM2, HSE department manager in supply chain management of automobile manufacturer as DM3, an auditor of management systems with vast experience of auditing ISO 45,001, ISO1 4001 and ISO9001 in a variety of enterprises as DM4, and an academia expert in HSE and crisis management as DM5. All the experts are chosen concerning their vast experience (10 years at least) in supplier selection and auditing their expertise in terms of HSE and crisis management.

Finalization of the selection criteria and developing RGH index

After conducting basic studies and field visits and surveying documents, the applied criteria to assess the resilience and performance of HSE for small and medium-sized enterprises were collected and categorized and also discussed in the expert panel. After this step, 15 experts (Group B) were given the criteria and indices to comment on the content validity of the designed assessing tool. After calculating the content validity ratio (CVR) for sub-criteria, 9 indices from the total 35 primary indices did not earn an acceptable minimum value of 0.49 according to the Lawshe table and were excluded. Ranking the alternatives

After reviewing company documents, conducting field visits, and auditing the four automobile part suppliers (abbreviated as S1, S2, S3, and S4), each supplier was graded on a 7-point Likert scale (from 1 = very poor to 7 = excellent) across 26 sub-criteria. The scoring process was carried out by a panel of HSE and supply chain experts based on observed evidence—including audit results, interviews, and document reviews—and comparative assessment among the four suppliers.

It is important to note that these scores were relative evaluations rather than absolute quantitative measures. For example, the sub-criterion C1 (budget allocation for HSE measures) received a score of 2 for Supplier S1, reflecting expert judgment that its HSE investment level was lower relative to the other suppliers in the study. The main objective of this evaluation was to demonstrate the practical applicability of the proposed RGH framework and its ability to differentiate suppliers within a real industrial context. For large-scale or independent assessments, the same framework could be expanded by defining quantitative thresholds (e.g., percentage of turnover devoted to HSE, average training hours per employee, or incident rates) to ensure cross-organizational comparability and reduce subjectivity.

Data Reliability and Validation.

Given that the case study involved small and medium-sized enterprises, data reliability was carefully considered during the evaluation process. Information used for scoring was triangulated through multiple sources, including field audits, interviews, document reviews, and confirmation with the supply management company responsible for contracting and supervising the suppliers. Where possible, third-party verified documents (such as ISO certifications and quality assurance records) were reviewed to strengthen confidence in the reported data. However, as the aim of this research was primarily to develop and validate the RGH evaluation model, not to audit data accuracy, the results should be interpreted within this methodological context.

Alignment with SAQ 5.0 and Practical Input Collection.

To strengthen the practical applicability of the proposed RGH framework, the process of data collection and evidence gathering can be aligned with the Sustainability Assessment Questionnaire (SAQ 5.0) developed by Drive Sustainability, which is widely used across the global automotive industry for supplier sustainability evaluation71. The SAQ 5.0 defines standardised evidence types for verifying environmental, health, safety, and ethical performance, including documented management systems (e.g., ISO 14001 or ISO 45001 certifications), company policies, performance data (e.g., emissions, energy, waste, and training records), and audit reports.

In practice, companies applying the RGH model can use similar evidence categories to collect and validate inputs for each sub-criterion. For example, budget allocation for HSE can be verified through financial statements or management review reports; training and awareness through employee training logs and attendance sheets; and emergency preparedness through documented drills or certification audits. This alignment ensures that supplier assessments conducted using the RGH framework are both transparent and consistent with globally recognized sustainability evaluation systems.

Criteria and indices of assessment tool

Due to the different terms of resilience and the lack of consensus among all researchers regarding a set of criteria or principles regarding this concept, in this study criteria were chosen to evaluate resilience, which are the most frequent compared to previous researches in terms of occupational health, safety and environmental management, and dealing with HSE risks and practically, following the results of the research, these criteria can be implemented from the point of view of experts in organizations to evaluate the competence of contractors.

So, four criteria, i.e. top management commitment, learning, awareness, and flexibility, which were defined as principles for building resilience in a variety of studies and were used and assessed in other selected researches.

Moreover, criteria like monitoring, responding, and predicting, which along with learning are introduced by Hollnagel as four key principles for building resilience, were cited in these research criteria72.

As observed in results, top management commitment was recognized as the most important criterion from experts’ viewpoint and was assigned the highest weight and impact in selecting the premier supplier. Management commitment is one of the tools for assessing resilience of a system for creating and keeping balance between production pressures in one hand, and safety and occupational health of system in another hand. Managers’ desire to invest in safety and occupational health and allocating resources to improve safety in an on-time and precautionary way is one of the key factors for building resilience in a system. Indeed, commitment have to be made in both individual and group level73.

Literature review on indices of assessing performance of safety and occupational health demonstrates that management commitment is recognized as the most important factor in most of related works74–77 which is consistent with the results of the present paper. In another work done by78, senior management commitment was determined as one of the effective criteria in resilience of a system and one of the main factors in success of safety planning.

Overall, the ranking and weight results obtained through the hybrid BWM–MABAC approach are consistent with earlier MCDM-based evaluations48, which demonstrated the robustness of combining structured weighting and distance-based ranking in complex supplier assessments. Compared to previous models that primarily considered cost, quality, or environmental performance, the proposed RGH framework introduces a broader evaluation logic that captures safety, resilience, and greenness simultaneously—responding to the multidimensional challenges of modern supply chains.

This finding reinforces the pivotal role of leadership commitment in ensuring the success of integrated sustainability and HSE programs. Similar to24 and26, strong managerial support enables resource allocation, policy enforcement, and continuous improvement culture—all essential for embedding resilience and environmental responsibility. For example, in automotive SMEs, management-driven initiatives such as periodic safety audits, supplier development programs, and green procurement incentives directly translate these commitments into operational outcomes, reducing workplace accidents and environmental non-compliance.

Awareness was recognized as the second most important criterion which is considered as one of the main indices of resilience of an organization. Resilient organizations are aware of boundaries of their area and know how to handle risks79. Awareness is critical for two situations: one to predict the changing nature of risk and the other to assess exchanges between safety and production. Resilient systems have to know what to search for and how to predict future risks72.

Comparable patterns have been reported in studies linking employee awareness and training to enhanced safety culture and resilience27,61,80. also observed that training and awareness are foundational readiness factors for Lean Six Sigma adoption in SMEs, suggesting that awareness not only promotes safe behavior but also operational efficiency and waste reduction. This reinforces the integrative role of human awareness as a bridge between sustainability and resilience dimensions.

This result suggests that organizational awareness and workforce education serve as a bridge between sustainability and resilience outcomes. Consistent with27,61, training programs and participatory workshops enhance employees’ ability to identify risks early and respond proactively to environmental and safety challenges. In practical terms, suppliers with structured HSE and sustainability awareness programs exhibit lower incident rates and faster recovery times after disruptions, confirming that awareness is not only a social factor but a measurable resilience enabler.

Responding was identified as the third most important criterion, which means organization’s preparedness in responding to HSE risks and also reacting against critical conditions. So, principles like preparedness, flexibility, team work, redundancy, and self-regulation which are introduced by81 as distinct factors for building resilience in an organization can be regarded as parts of responding. In terms of results of weighting sub-criteria (indices) in this study, the sub-criteria (index) “Budget allocation for HSE measures” obtained the highest weight from among the defined sub-criteria for management commitment. This index got the highest level of importance in comparison with all the indices of other criteria. Expenditures and allocated budget for items such as staff training, control measures to reduce risks, improving environment, providing safety equipment and personal protective equipment, controlling environmental pollutants and so on are examples of used measures to grade these indices.

The strong weight of the responding criterion confirms the findings of54,55,60, who emphasized absorptive and adaptive capacities as key resilience enablers in green supply chain networks. Yet, the inclusion of HSE-oriented response actions—such as emergency planning and self-organization—extends their scope by embedding human safety behavior within resilience modeling, an innovation not covered in prior frameworks.

This highlights the importance of adaptive response mechanisms—such as emergency planning, redundancy, and rapid communication systems—in strengthening overall resilience54,55. similarly identified adaptive capacity as a critical success factor in resilient-green supplier selection. In the context of automotive SMEs, this finding implies that developing flexible logistics contracts, supplier redundancy, and worker-level safety response training can significantly mitigate disruption risks, ensuring both safety and operational continuity.

“Building management systems like ISO 45001, ISO14001, and HSE-MS” obtained the third position in terms of the total weight, in comparison with all the 26 indices. Padash and Rajabzadeh20demonstrated that in organizations that have built such systems and have a strategic approach to green management issues and components, they have a more ready and flexible structure compared to organizations that lack this insight. It is obvious that the commitment of management at the top of these criteria, along with adherence to safety rules and procedures, leads to safer behaviors of employees, which in turn leads to the prevention of risky behaviors and reduction of accidents. And finally it will bring business stability82. also showed that communication level among staff of those enterprises which have such systems is higher than those without them.

Furthermore, staff training in enterprises which have obtained certificates of building such systems is higher than those without them20,83,84. So, it can be concluded that in the defined framework of the present study, this index not only directly influences on performance level of HSE, but also has an indirect effect on it through influencing on other indices like training.

Implications and practical significance

The results of this study have both theoretical and managerial implications. Theoretically, they confirm the interdependence between sustainability, resilience, and HSE performance, reinforcing the argument that safety-oriented practices contribute directly to adaptive capacity and environmental compliance. This aligns with resilience engineering and the Triple Bottom Line framework, offering a new integrated perspective for supplier evaluation research. Practically, the findings highlight actionable pathways for managers—particularly in automotive SMEs—to operationalize the RGH framework. For instance, top management commitment, awareness, and adaptive capacity were found to be the most influential criteria. These correspond to leadership initiatives, workforce training, and emergency planning, respectively. Implementing these priorities helps organizations enhance supplier reliability, minimize workplace risks, and improve sustainability performance across the supply chain.

Limitations and contextual boundaries

While this study provides an integrated understanding of sustainability, resilience, and HSE performance within supplier evaluation, several contextual limitations should be acknowledged to ensure a balanced interpretation of the findings. First, the empirical validation of the proposed Resilient, Green, and Healthy (RGH) Index was conducted within the context of automotive SMEs, which may limit the generalizability of the results to large corporations or other industrial sectors with different operational characteristics and resource profiles. Future research should therefore test and calibrate the RGH framework across diverse sectors such as electronics, food manufacturing, or logistics to examine its cross-industry applicability.

Second, although the study used a hybrid Best–Worst Method (BWM) and MABAC approach for robust decision-making, the weighting process still depends on expert judgment, which may introduce subjective bias. Complementary methods such as entropy weighting or Bayesian updating could strengthen the objectivity of future analyses.

Finally, the implications drawn from this research should be interpreted within the evolving technological and regulatory landscape of Industry 4.0. The RGH framework is particularly suitable for contexts where digital monitoring, supplier data transparency, and safety culture maturity are achievable. In regions or sectors where these enablers are absent, additional capacity-building efforts may be required before full adoption.

From a theoretical perspective, these limitations offer future research opportunities to extend the RGH model by integrating social sustainability, digital resilience, and circular economy principles. From a managerial perspective, the framework provides actionable guidance to practitioners seeking to balance sustainability, safety, and operational continuity under resource constraints, while recognizing that contextual adaptation is essential for successful implementation.

The more comprehensive criteria and components are considered for the resilience of the organization, the more flexible the organizations will be in dealing with future risks or megatrends.

Managerial implication:

Senior leadership must lead a visible, sustained shift in attitudes toward health, safety, environment (HSE) and organizational resilience by treating them as core strategic priorities rather than compliance tasks. Concretely, executives should set explicit HSE-resilience objectives tied to business outcomes, cascade them into performance metrics and incentives for all managers, and allocate dedicated resources for proactive risk identification, training, and continuous improvement. Managers should model desired behaviors in daily operations (e.g., stop-work authority, open reporting without blame), integrate HSE/resilience criteria into investment and operational decisions, and embed learning loops that convert incidents and near-misses into standardized process improvements. Regularly measure leading indicators (training completion, hazard remediation cycle time, safety observations) alongside lagging ones, and communicate progress transparently. By aligning tone at the top, governance, incentives, and routines, the organization can normalize safety-first decision-making, strengthen resilience capabilities, and improve overall performance and stakeholder trust.

Limitations and recommendations for future research

While this study provides a robust and practical model for integrating resilience, sustainability, and HSE performance in supplier evaluation, certain limitations should be recognized to provide a balanced interpretation of the findings. First, the empirical analysis was limited to four automotive supplier companies due to time and access constraints. Although this provided in-depth insight into the SME context, a broader sample across different manufacturing sectors would enhance generalizability. Future studies should therefore validate the RGH Index using larger and more diverse datasets.

Second, despite using a hybrid Best–Worst Method (BWM) and MABAC approach to improve reliability, the weighting process inherently relies on expert judgment, which may introduce bias. Integrating data-driven or machine learning techniques—such as fuzzy inference systems or entropy-based weighting—could strengthen objectivity in future applications.

Third, the model focused primarily on operational and environmental dimensions. Expanding it to include social sustainability, digital resilience, or circular economy factors would further enhance its comprehensiveness and relevance in Industry 5.0 contexts.

Finally, from a practical perspective, future research could develop decision-support software based on the RGH framework to assist real-time supplier evaluation and scenario analysis. Such extensions would help translate this study’s theoretical contributions into digital tools for industrial use.
