Preclinical and clinical evaluation of a new flexible high pressure injection knife for endoscopic submucosal dissection
Authors: Raphael Olivier, Jérémie Albouys, Clement Juglard, Ludovico Alfarone, Raouf Boubekeur, Rémi Collin, Véronique Van der Voort, Hugo Lepetit, Romain Legros, Jérémie Jacques, Marion Schaefer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe studied a newly developed high-pressure injection flexible knife, to offer potential procedural simplification in colorectal Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection (ESD). Animal study: open randomized controlled study using an anesthetized porcine model, five ESD novices alternated between the flexible high-pressure injection Knife (HPK) and a Standard Knife (SK). Clinical study: a prospective cohort study conducted in an expert center analyzed colorectal ESD performed with HPK or SK, with propensity score matching. Animal study: 41 gastric and 59 colorectal ESDs were performed. No significant differences were observed between HPK and SK. Operator satisfaction was significantly better with HPK. Clinical study: 379 colorectal ESDs were performed including 70 with HPK. After propensity score matching based on predictors of technical difficulty, 69 lesions per group (HPK vs. SK) were compared. Histological R0 resection rate was significantly higher with HPK than with SK (n = 64 (95.52%) vs. n = 56 (82.35%) p = 0.026), as was the curative resection rate (n = 62 (93.94%) vs. n = 53 (77.94%), p = 0.012). No significant differences were noted regarding procedural speed or perforation rates. HPK improves trainee satisfaction during animal ESD and yields superior histological and curative resection rates in complex colorectal ESD cases when used by experts, without increasing risks.Subject terms: Colonoscopy, Gastroenterology, Gastrointestinal models, Thermoelectric devices and materials

Introduction

Endoscopic Sub-mucosal Dissection (ESD) allows for en-bloc and R0 resection of gastrointestinal (GI) lesions larger than 2 cm in diameter, using dedicated ESD knives1. ESD is the gold standard all over the world to treat early neoplasia in the upper GI tract. However, in the lower GI tract, given the technical challenges2–4, including the thinness of the submucosa and muscularis propria, difficulty maneuvering the endoscope due to loops and folds, and significant intestinal contraction, piece-meal endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) remains the preferred technique. A recent randomized trial demonstrated the superiority of ESD over EMR in endoscopic colon adenoma removal by significantly reducing neoplasia recurrence rates5. Despite this, there remains a need to simplify ESD through advanced technological tools.

Effective exposure of the submucosal layer is critical for quick and safe ESD. Previous generation high-pressure injection knives (Hybridknife ®, Erbe Medical, Germany) have shown promise in animal studies6,7. However, their adoption for colonic ESD in humans has been limited due to design limitations, such as the wide electrode and rigid structure, which are ill-suited to the specific challenges of colorectal ESD.

A next-generation high-pressure flexible knife (Hybridknife Flex®, ERBE Medical, Germany) has been developed in partnership with endoscopists with expertise in ESD and offers a smaller cutting probe and increased flexibility, while retaining the well-known efficacy of the high-pressure injection technology.

Although this device has recently become available worldwide, animal data on its potential benefits are limited8,9. This study reports the results of a randomized trial involving trainees operating on pigs, and a comparative prospective study in humans, focusing exclusively on complex colorectal ESD cases.

Materials and methods

Part 1: animal study

This prospective randomized open-label trial was conducted at the EMIS platform (Experimental Medicine, Imaging and Surgery), of Limoges University Hospital, a research unit dedicated to animal experimentation for clinical and medical applications. Five ESD trainees performed successive gastric or colorectal ESDs on anesthetized pigs. Operators were randomized to start the experiment with either the high-pressure flexible hybrid knife with a small platter cutting probe (HKT-2) or a standard pressure knife (SK) with a small platter cutting probe (the Dualknife-J®, Olympus medical) and by location (stomach, colon or rectum) using block randomization of two or three endoscopists. The SK allowed conventional injections, through the knife with water pump, but not under high pressure as the HKT-2, which, combined with its maneuverability, should improve the performance of trainees. Knives were alternated for each procedure to mitigate learning-curve bias. The imposed resection strategy was circumferential incision followed by systematic application of traction. Saline solution was used for all submucosal injections and all the different knives.

Inclusion criteria & procedures

Operators were ESD trainees, with more than two years of general endoscopy experience, including endoscopic mucosal resection, and no more than 16 animal ESD procedures (ex-vivo or in-vivo), including traction-assisted ESDs. Clinical experience was limited to fewer than 10 ESD cases. They were supervised by two experienced endoscopists with over 100 colorectal ESDs each.

The animal models were Sus Scorfa Domesticus pigs, aged 2 to 6 months and weighing 15 to 40 kg. The pigs were prepared for colonoscopy with the ingestion of a dedicated polyethylene glycol (PEG) solution. Anesthesia was induced with intramuscular ketamine and intravenous propofol (2 mg/kg), followed by continuous infusion of propofol (6 mg/kg/min) and inhaled sevoflurane. Our study protocol followed the ARRIVE guidelines (PLoS Bio 86, e1000412,2010). On each model, four gastric ESDs and four colorectal ESDs were performed (rectal and left colon). Standardized lesions were created by using a forced coagulation current applied through an open 25-mm diameter resection snare (Fig. 1a). Both types of knives were used with ERBE VIO3 electosurgical units. Operators could use Endocut I, SwiftCoag and/or PreciseSect modes for dissection. SoftCoag or SwiftCoag modes could be used for hemostasis.

(a) Standardized lesion, marked with application of forced coagulation current through an open 25-mm diameter resection snare. (b) Endoscopic macroscopic R0 resection, standardized lesion completely removed.

A time-limit for each procedure was set at 60 min, then operators were asked to stop the procedure.

The following procedure details were prospectively recorded: duration of each step, perforations (wall-defect with visualization of mesorectal or peritoneal space), muscle layer injury (non-transmural injury), use of hemostatic forceps, electrosurgical settings, and the size of the resected specimen.

Outcomes

For comparisons of the HKT-2 and SK groups, the primary outcome was procedure duration, measured from the first submucosal injection to the last cut of submucosal fiber.

Secondary outcomes included: intraoperative perforations or muscle layer injury, speed, complete resection rate (defined as endoscopic macroscopic en-bloc resection (Fig. 1b) within 60 min) and the performance of the two knives, assessed using a 5-class Likert Scale, according to injection quality, knife stability in the operative channel, length stability of the knife outside the operative channel, placement accuracy, incision sharpness, dissection sharpness and coagulation efficacy.

Sample size calculation

The expected duration of ESD for experts, based on a first preclinical study, was 35 ± 11 min with the HKT-2 and 34 ± 8.7 min with the SK9. Assuming a pooled standard deviation of 9.9 min and an assumed acceleration of 5 min with HKT-2, 35 procedures per group were required to detect a 3-minute reduction in SK duration (power of 80% (1 β) and a first-species risk α = 5%). Adding a 10% safety margin, 39 lesions per group were planned. In order to meet ESGE training guidelines for the five trainees10, the sample size was increased to 100 (20 ESDs per participant).

Ethical considerations

The animals were cared for in compliance with French legislation and the principles of replacement, reduction and refinement. Ethical approval was granted by the ethics committee and, institutional review board of Limoges University Hospital.”.

The study was funded by Erbe Medical Company. Participants, study designers and writers were not compensated.

Part 2: clinical study

Patients referred to our center for colorectal ESD were enrolled in the prospective, French Colorectal ESD Cohort (NCT04592003). Our center was involved in the User acceptance testing of the new HybridKnife Flex after CE marking of the European Medicines Agency and approval from the Food and Drug Administration in the United States of America. All ESDs performed using this knife were included in the prospective cohort.

Patients provided written informed consent for the endoscopic procedure and data collection. Institutional ethics committee approval was obtained for the prospective cohort (Institutional Review Board of Limoges University Hospital approval #87RI20-0021_FECCo, NCT04592003). All authors had access to the anonymized study data and approved the final manuscript. Research on the participants has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Comparisons between ESDs performed with new HybridKnife Flex and other knives were conducted on the full cohort and after propensity score matching to mitigate selection bias.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All patients treated with Colorectal ESD in Limoges University Hospital between January 2023 and August 2024 were enrolled. Patients with lesions suggesting deep submucosal cancer (JNET III, KUDO Vn, CONNECT III, and JNET III)11–15, sessile serrated lesions or submucosal lesions were excluded.

Procedures

All ESDs were performed by three expert gastroenterologists with 7 to 10 years expertise in ESD acquired through a structured training program in animals and with experience of more than 400 ESD each.

All the procedures were performed under general anesthesia. The choice of endoscope and knife was at the operator’s discretion. Electrosurgical units (VIO200D and VIO3, Erbe Medical, Tubingen, Germany) were used, with mucosal incisions performed in the Endocut I mode. Submucosal dissection was performed using SwiftCoag, PreciseSect, or Endocut I modes according to the physician’s preference. The resection strategy (e.g. conventional, tunneling, and traction assisted ESD) was also chosen by the physician. Prophylactic coagulation of visibly bleeding vessels was systematically performed using hemostatic forceps at the end of the procedure.

Definitions

En-bloc resection was defined as one-piece resection.

R0 resection was defined by a disease-free vertical and lateral margin confirmed by pathological analysis.

In cases of cancer, curative resection was defined as R0 resection without high-risk features (submucosal invasion > 1,000 mm, lymphovascular invasion, poor differentiation, and significant budding)16.The specimen’s surface area was calculated as area (mm2) = (smaller diameter (mm)/2) x (larger diameter (mm)/2) xπ. The procedure duration (min) was calculated as the time between the first submucosal injection and the dissection of the last submucosal fiber. The resection speed (mm2/min) was calculated as the specimen area divided by the procedure duration.

Outcomes

The primary objective was the histological R0 resection rate, given its clinical significance, achieving an R0 resection obviates the need for the early 6-month surveillance, recommended following a piecemeal resection. Secondary outcomes included the curative resection rate, en-bloc resection rate, procedure duration, resection speed, perforation rate, post-procedural bleeding and secondary surgery rate.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means (± SD) and discrete outcomes as absolute and relative (%) frequencies. For the animal study, two groups were established according to the type of knife. The learning curve was analyzed in three stages (1st to 8th procedure, 9th to 15th procedure and 16th to 22nd procedure). The normality and heteroskedasticity of continuous data were assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene test, respectively. Continuous outcomes were compared using the unpaired Student t-test, Welch t-test or Mann-Whitney U test according to data distribution. Discrete outcomes were compared using the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test accordingly. The alpha risk was set at 5% and two tailed tests were used. For clinical study, two groups were created based on the type of knife used. The comparability of the groups was assessed by comparing baseline demographic data and follow-up duration between groups. Propensity scores were calculated using a logistic regression model that included the following standardized covariates already proven to be associated with lower rates of R0 resection: lesion type, previously treated lesions (recurrence), dentate line involvement and endoscope maneuverability. Imputations of means and frequencies were used in cases of missing covariate data. Patients in the control group (SK) were matched to patients in the treatment group (HKT-2) by nearest-neighbor matching with a bracket value of 0.2 of the pooled standard deviation of the logarithms of the propensity score.

Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to compare baseline characteristics after matching. A post matching SMD < 0.1 was considered an acceptable difference. This statistical analysis was performed using EasyMedStat (version 3.30; www.easymedstat.com). Our study followed the STROBE statement about Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology.

No statistical difference was observed in the primary outcome, as the mean procedure duration was slightly shorter in the HKT-2 group (38.71 ± 18.95 min) than in the SK group (41.54 min ± 19.79 min; p = 0.327). Among secondary outcomes, no significant differences were found regarding the speed of resection, slightly shorter in the HKT-2 group 22.12 (± 12.7) vs. 25.01 mm2/min (± 13.36) p = 0.235, or regarding the complete resection rate within 60 min, obtained in 38/49 HKT-2 cases (77.5%) vs. 33/51 SK cases (64.71%); p = 0.232. However, HKT-2 was associated with a reduced need for injection needles at the start of the procedure (30.61% vs. 66.67%; p = 0.001).). Physicians expressed a preference for the HKT-2 knife regarding injection quality and dissection sharpness, with no significant differences in other criteria, such as knife stability, incision sharpness, or placement accuracy. A full comparison of the knives’ characteristics according to operator satisfaction is shown on a Likert scale in Fig. 2. On average, operators used as many SK as HKT-2: 10 of each (and an average of 20 procedures per operator).

Part 2: clinical study

Baseline characteristics

Between January 2023 and August 2024, 410 ESDs were performed. After excluding serrated sessile lesions, submucosal tumors and lesions classified as JNET III, 379 colorectal ESDs were eligible. Of these, 70 ESDs were performed using the HKT-2 knife and 309 with other knives. The distribution was similar between the three experts in the use of SK and HKT-2 without significative differences (notably a mean of 23 procedures with HKT-2 by expert). Patients’ characteristics are presented in Table 3. The mean age of patients was 68 ± 10.94 years, and 59.3% were men. Most Lesions were Lateral Spreading tumor Granular (LST G) homogenous (32.6%) and nodular mixed (28.6%), with 44.6% located in the proximal colon. Lesions had previously been treated in 5.8% of cases (recurrence), 13.0% of the patients received anticoagulant therapy and 14.9% antiplatelets. The R0 resection rate was 92.0%. About ten different types of dissecting knives were used in this cohort, all of the different models available being used routinely, with a majority of Dual Knife-J® (Olympus, Japan) (252/309 (82%)). Saline combined with glycerol solution was used for all submucosal injections and all the different knives.

Concerning the primary objective, we found a significantly higher R0 resection rate in the HKT-2 group (95.52% vs. 82.35%; p = 0.026). Discussion

Initially pioneered in Japan as a method for the endoscopic treatment of superficial gastric neoplasms, endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has become the preferred therapeutic approach for superficial colorectal lesions in Asia. Unlike gastric ESD, which is widely practiced globally, colorectal ESD presents significant technical challenges, limiting its widespread adoption outside Japan despite a recent randomized trial reporting several advantages over piece-meal EMR5.

The complexity of colorectal ESD arises from factors such as bowel peristalsis, the thinness of the submucosa and muscularis propria, limited endoscope access, frequent fibrosis, and the intricate fold anatomy17. In Western countries, ESD for rectal lesions has gained popularity, as it is technically less demanding than colon ESD. However, there is an ongoing debate concerning the advantages of colon ESD versus piecemeal EMR, particularly with regard to procedural difficulty, safety, and the relatively favorable outcomes of piecemeal EMR18. Research efforts are increasingly focused on enhancing ESD techniques and developing improved devices to address these challenges19–21. Our study evaluates a new flexible high pressure injection knife to facilitate colorectal ESD. This is the only knife that injects under pressure using a dedicated pump, capable of increasing pressure up to 60 bar, whereas other knives inject using a conventional washing pump, at a pressure of between 1 and 2 bar.

The first randomized part of the study involving trainees operating on pigs showed no statistically significant superiority of this new knife over a standard knife. However, the new knife proved to be numerically superior for all outcomes in colorectal lesions. Trainees felt significantly more comfortable with this new knife, as assessed by the Likert scale evaluation. This new generation knife seems to be superior to the first model, which was less maneuverable due to its rigid structure and poor maneuverability. One limitation of this animal trial was the low power due to the small sample size compounded by the inclusion of gastric lesions. Indeed, gastric lesions in pigs are technically easier to resect (due to the thicker submucosa, and the rarity of bleeding vessels), potentially masking the benefits of the device. The systematic use of traction may have further reduced the observable impact of the knife’s injection capabilities. However, subgroup analyses of colorectal lesions showed trends favoring the new knife, despite the systematic use of traction, with improved complete resection rates and shorter procedure durations, though these differences were not statistically significant. A more targeted study focusing solely on colorectal lesions, which are more challenging in anesthetized pigs, might have better demonstrated the knife’s advantages, as it was specifically designed for colorectal ESD.

Regarding the human study, which involved only expert endoscopists, the new flexible high-pressure knife significantly improved R0 resection rates. Interestingly, during the study (User acceptance test for the company), physicians probably understood the advantage of this knife as they appeared to select it for more challenging lesions. Indeed, lesions were larger (mean size close to 7 cm), more frequently located in the proximal colon (47%) and had large macronodules (50%). All these factors are known to increase procedural difficulty, decrease R0 resection rates and increase the risk of perforation22. Despite these challenges, the HKT-2 group achieved a significantly higher R0 resection (95%) compared with the standard knife group (85%) after propensity score matching. Interestingly, this difference was achieved even with the almost systematic use of traction-assisted ESD, which typically reduces the need for through-the-knife injections by improving exposure. We believe that all the available strategies are complementary and not competitive and that the evident advantages of high-pressure injections improve every step of the procedure, in particular by facilitating lateral margin resection, meaning that wider margins can be obtained without increasing procedure complexity. However, only the R0 resection rate and the lateral margins can be meaningfully interpreted, and not the curative resection rate, since there were more deeply invasive submucosal cancers in the standard knife group.

This superior R0 resection rate is the most important outcome as it is the goal of ESD for benign lesions, for which complete resection decreases recurrence rates and eliminates the need for costly, stressful and carbon footprint impacted follow-up colonoscopies. High-pressure injection comes as a complement to the set of tools available for ESD, especially the different traction modalities that have greatly improved the quality and resection speed of ESD23,24 and the various resection strategies such as tunnels, pockets and, more recently, underwater resections25–27, to make the R0 objective ever more reachable.

This study has several strengths. By combining results from both animal and human studies, it offers a comprehensive perspective on the performance of the new device in different settings. High-pressure injection could be useful for operators beginning their specialized training and for experienced endoscopists facing challenging lesions. Importantly, this is the first prospective human study to report results in a large patient cohort. It should be noted that the literature contains few studies testing ESD devices in animals or ex vivo19,20,28., and that very few studies have compared different types of knives for ESD in human colorectal lesions, and such studies had small numbers of patients. Moreover, the short inclusion period ensured that ESD practices were unmodified during the study, thereby reinforcing the validity of the results. The favorable outcomes obtained in this study could encourage the wider adoption of colorectal ESD.

However, this study is not without limitations. The animal trial was underpowered. In addition, gastric lesions in pigs are easier to resect than colorectal lesions, and this may have masked the benefits of the new device in more challenging cases. However, this allowed us to adhere to ethical guidelines by minimizing the number of animals needed for the trial while ensuring that trainees achieved the number of animal procedures required in the ESD training protocol. The human clinical study had a non-randomized design, which is an inherent limitation. Nonetheless, the use of propensity score matching in a large prospective cohort effectively mitigated selection bias, ensuring a fair comparison between the two groups.

In conclusion, a new flexible high-pressure knife increased the comfort of trainees in a randomized animal trial, and in expert hands improved R0 resection rates for large complex colorectal ESDs. This knife represents a significant advance in the pursuit of simplifying colorectal ESD and its implementation as an alternative for piecemeal EMR in the treatment of large superficial colorectal lesions25.
