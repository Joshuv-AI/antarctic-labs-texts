Abstract
. In this article we classify totally geodesic submanifolds of homogeneous nearly Kähler 6 6 -manifolds, and of the 𝖦 2 \mathsf{G}_{2} -cones over these 6 6 -manifolds. To this end, we develop new techniques for the study of totally geodesic submanifolds of analytic Riemannian manifolds, naturally reductive homogeneous spaces, and Riemannian cones. In particular, we obtain an example of a totally geodesic submanifold with self-intersections in a simply connected homogeneous space.

Introduction

An almost Hermitian structure on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
is a
(1,1)
tensor field
J
preserving the Riemannian metric
g
and satisfying the identity
J^{2}=-\operatorname{Id}
. Gray and Hervella
[
24
]
showed that there are 16 natural classes of almost Hermitian structures.
A nice example of those are Kähler structures, which are characterized by the equation
\nabla J=0
, where
\nabla
denotes the Levi-Civita connection of
(M,g)
. Among the non-integrable Hermitian structures, the nearly Kähler structures are particularly noteworthy.
An almost Hermitian structure
J
on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
is
nearly Kähler
if it satisfies

(\nabla_{X}J)(Y)=-(\nabla_{Y}J)(X)\quad\textrm{for all vector fields $X$ and $Y$ of $M$}.

The study of nearly Kähler geometry is particularly interesting in dimension
6
, as strictly nearly Kähler
6
-manifolds (that is, those which are not Kähler) are automatically Einstein, and their Riemannian cones are 7-manifolds with holonomy groups contained in
\mathsf{G}_{2}
, see
[
5
]
. Indeed, in
[
11
]
, Bryant constructed the first examples of manifolds with holonomy exactly equal to
\mathsf{G}_{2}
, one of which is a Riemannian cone over the flag manifold
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})

equipped with its homogeneous nearly Kähler metric.

Although investigations about nearly Kähler manifolds began in the 1950s, significant progress has been made in recent decades.
In 2005, Butruille
[
12
]
classified all simply connected, homogeneous, strictly nearly Kähler manifolds of dimension six. These are:

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}^{6}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{G}_{2}/\mathsf{SU}(3),

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Sp}(2)/(\mathsf{U}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1)),

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{SU}(3)/\mathsf{T}^{2},

\displaystyle\qquad\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{SU}(2)^{3}/\Delta\mathsf{SU}(2).

More recently, in 2017, Foscolo and Haskins
[
19
]
produced the first inhomogeneous nearly Kähler structures on
\mathsf{S}^{6}
and
\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
.
These structures are of cohomogeneity one and are
\mathsf{SU}(2)\times\mathsf{SU}(2)
-invariant.
The aforementioned examples constitute all the known simply connected strictly nearly Kähler
6
-manifolds so far.

The main goal of this article is to classify totally geodesic submanifolds in homogeneous strictly nearly Kähler
6
-manifolds. For this purpose, we develop some general tools for the study of totally geodesic submanifolds of naturally reductive homogeneous spaces.
Moreover, we also classify maximal totally geodesic submanifolds of the
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-cones over homogeneous strictly nearly Kähler
6
-manifolds.

The theory of totally geodesic submanifolds in homogeneous spaces is especially fruitful due to the large isometry groups of these spaces. Symmetric spaces are a particular type of homogeneous spaces with an isometric geodesic reflection at each point.
Totally geodesic submanifolds of symmetric spaces have been extensively studied, see e.g.
[
7
,
13
,
14
,
34
]
.
In this setting, it turns out that totally geodesic submanifolds arise as orbits of isometric actions and can be characterized algebraically by means of the so-called Lie triple systems.
That being said, the classification of totally geodesic submanifolds in classical symmetric spaces of rank greater than two remains widely open.
In contrast, in a non-symmetric Riemannian homogeneous space we lack a nice algebraic characterization of totally geodesic submanifolds.
Furthermore, although these submanifolds are intrinsically homogeneous, they are not necessarily orbits of Lie groups acting isometrically, see
[
32
,
43
]
.

Another relevant aspect is that in the (non-symmetric) homogeneous setting, it is natural to consider totally geodesic immersions that are not necessarily injective.
The theory of totally geodesic immersions, developed in
[
53
]
and
[
27
]
, is central to this work, and in this article we aim to further build upon it, see Section
3
.
It is well known that in homogeneous spaces, every one-dimensional totally geodesic submanifold is injectively immersed (in other words, every geodesic loop is a closed geodesic), and in symmetric spaces, all totally geodesic submanifolds are injectively immersed.
In this work, we present what is, up to the best of our knowledge, the first example of a totally geodesic submanifold in a simply connected homogeneous space with self-intersections, see Subsection
5.2.6
.

In this article, we introduce the class of
D
-invariant totally geodesic submanifolds
of a reductive homogeneous space, where
D
denotes the difference between the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
and the canonical connection
\nabla^{c}
, see Subsection
2.1
.
In the setting of naturally reductive homogeneous spaces, which includes symmetric spaces,
D
-invariant totally geodesic submanifolds are orbits of Lie groups acting isometrically, and they admit a nice algebraic description similar to that of Lie triple systems in symmetric spaces.
Furthermore, totally geodesic submanifolds of symmetric spaces are trivially
D
-invariant.
Thus, the class of
D
-invariant totally geodesic submanifolds seems to be a natural generalization of totally geodesic submanifolds of symmetric spaces.
As we will see, not all totally geodesic submanifolds in a naturally reductive homogeneous space are
D
-invariant.
It will follow from our classification that all maximal totally geodesic submanifolds—that is, those that are not contained in any other proper totally geodesic submanifold—in the homogeneous nearly Kähler
6
-manifolds are
D
-invariant.

As we have seen, there are exactly four examples of simply connected homogeneous strictly nearly Kähler
6
-manifolds.
The sphere
\mathsf{S}^{6}=\mathsf{G}_{2}/\mathsf{SU}(3)
, whose nearly Kähler structure is induced by octonionic multiplication, was the first one to appear.
Since
\mathsf{S}^{6}=\mathsf{G}_{2}/\mathsf{SU}(3)
is isotropy irreducible, it carries a unique
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-invariant metric up to scaling, which is precisely the round one.
Thus, its totally geodesic submanifolds are open parts of intersections of vector subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{7}
passing through the center of the unit sphere
\mathsf{S}^{6}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{7}
.
Each of the remaining examples appears as the total space of a homogeneous fibration

F=\mathsf{K}/\mathsf{H}\rightarrow M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{H}\rightarrow B=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}

induced by a triple of compact Lie groups
\mathsf{H}\subseteq\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathsf{G}
.
In the cases of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
and
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
, these fibrations are also examples of twistor fibrations.
A
twistor fibration
of an oriented Riemannian
4
-manifold
N
is a fiber bundle
\pi\colon M\rightarrow N
, where each fiber over
p\in N
is equal to the set of complex structures of
T_{p}N
which preserve the orientation and the Riemannian metric of
N
. It turns out that
\pi
is an
\mathsf{S}^{2}
-bundle over
N
, and the twistor space
M
admits two different natural almost Hermitian structures. One of them is called the Atiyah–Hitchin–Singer structure and it is integrable if and only if the
4
-manifold
N
is self-dual, see
[
4
]
. The other one is the Eells–Salamon structure, which can be obtained from the Atiyah–Hitchin–Singer structure by changing the sign only in the fibers. The nearly Kähler structures of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
and
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
that we are considering are precisely the Eells–Salamon structures that we get when
N
is the round sphere
\mathsf{S}^{4}
and the complex projective plane
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{2}
, respectively. Moreover, the celebrated Eells–Salamon correspondence, see
[
18
]
, states a one-to-one correspondence between (branched) minimal surfaces in
N
, and non-vertical
J
-holomorphic curves
in
M
, i.e.
J
-invariant immersions of
2
-manifolds in
M
.

When studying totally geodesic submanifolds of the total space
M
of a Riemannian submersion
F\rightarrow M\rightarrow B
, it is also relevant to consider their behavior with respect to the underlying Riemannian submersion. Following
[
43
]
, we say that a totally geodesic submanifold
\Sigma
of the total space of a Riemannian submersion
M
is
well-positioned
if

T_{p}\Sigma=(\mathcal{V}_{p}\cap T_{p}\Sigma)\oplus(\mathcal{H}_{p}\cap T_{p}\Sigma)\quad\text{for all $p\in\Sigma$},

where
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{H}
denote the vertical and horizontal distributions associated with the Riemannian submersion
F\rightarrow M\rightarrow B
. It turns out that if a totally geodesic submanifold is well-positioned, the metric of the total space can be rescaled in the direction of the fibers while preserving the totally geodesic property for all these new metrics, see
[
16
, Lemma 3.12]
. In this work, we find several examples of not well-positioned totally geodesic submanifolds.

We are also interested in understanding the interaction between totally geodesic
submanifolds of the nearly Kähler spaces under investigation and their ambient almost
complex structure.
A way to measure how a submanifold fails to be complex is by using the notion of Kähler angle, see e.g.
[
9
]
. We say that a submanifold
\Sigma
of an almost Hermitian manifold
M
has constant Kähler angle
\Phi(\Sigma)=\varphi\in[0,\pi/2]
if

\lvert\lvert\mathrm{proj}_{T_{p}\Sigma}Jv\rvert\rvert^{2}=\cos^{2}(\varphi)\lvert\lvert v\rvert\rvert^{2}\quad\text{for all $v\in T_{p}\Sigma$ and every $p\in M$},

where
\mathrm{proj}_{T_{p}\Sigma}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
T_{p}\Sigma
.
The submanifolds satisfying
\Phi(\Sigma)=0
or
\Phi(\Sigma)=\pi/2
are exactly those which are almost complex or totally real, respectively.
An interesting question is to determine the possible constant Kähler angles of the totally geodesic submanifolds of an almost Hermitian manifold.
Of course, this question is only interesting for spaces with non-constant curvature, since in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
every number in
[0,\pi/2]
can be realized as the constant Kähler angle of a totally geodesic submanifold.
In the setting of Hermitian symmetric spaces, not all totally geodesic submanifolds
\Sigma
satisfy
\Phi(\Sigma)\in\{0,\pi/2\}
.
For instance, Klein realized in
[
33
]
that there is a totally geodesic
2
-sphere in the Hermitian symmetric space
\mathsf{G}^{+}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{5})=\mathsf{SO}(5)/(\mathsf{SO}(3)\times\mathsf{SO}(2))
with constant Kähler angle
\arccos(1/5)
.
Even more, the second author of this article proved in
[
45
]
that every rational number between
[0,1]
can be realized as the arccosine of the Kähler angle of a totally geodesic submanifold embedded in a Hermitian symmetric space of large enough rank.

There is a relatively large number of articles focusing on the investigation of totally geodesic submanifolds of nearly Kähler homogeneous
6
-manifolds under strong assumptions.
In these works the authors use special frames to carry out the classification for Lagrangian totally geodesic submanifolds, or totally geodesic
J
-holomorphic curves.
In
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
, the Lagrangian totally geodesic submanifolds were classified in
[
3
,
40
]
.
In the flag manifold
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
the Lagrangian totally geodesic submanifolds were classified in
[
50
]
, and the totally geodesic
J
-holomorphic curves were classified in
[
15
]
.
In
\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
, the totally geodesic Lagrangian submanifolds were classified in
[
57
]
, and the totally geodesic
J
-holomorphic curves were classified in
[
8
]
.
Although they listed six congruence classes of totally geodesic submanifolds in
\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
, there are just two different ones: either a round sphere or a Berger sphere, where the latter was first constructed in
[
41
]
.
It is important to remark that there are no known results obstructing the existence of totally geodesic submanifolds
\Sigma
of nearly Kähler
6
-manifolds when:
\Sigma^{3}
is not Lagrangian,
\Sigma^{2}
is not
J
-holomorphic, or
\Sigma
has dimension
4
.
In this article, we generalize the aforementioned partial classifications following an entirely different approach.
By employing tools from the theory of Riemannian homogeneous spaces, we address the classification problem of totally geodesic submanifolds in its full generality.

\mathsf{S}^{n}(r)

\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{n}(r)

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\mathsf{S}^{3}

, Theorem

Corollary D
.

Let
\Sigma
be a maximal totally geodesic submanifold of a homogeneous nearly Kähler
6
-manifold of non-constant curvature.
Then the following statements hold:

(i)

if
\Sigma
has dimension two, then
\Sigma
is a
J
-holomorphic curve.

(ii)

if
\Sigma
has dimension three, then
\Sigma
is a Lagrangian submanifold.

Thus, every totally geodesic submanifold
\Sigma
of a homogeneous strictly nearly Kähler
6
-manifold of non-constant curvature satisfies
\Phi(\Sigma)\in\{0,\pi/2\}
.
This raises the question whether this also holds for (not necessarily homogeneous) irreducible strictly nearly Kähler manifolds.

In this article, we also study totally geodesic submanifolds of Riemannian cones.
It can be checked that for every totally geodesic submanifold
\Sigma
of a Riemannian manifold
M
, the cone over
\Sigma
is a totally geodesic submanifold of the cone over
M
, see Lemma
6.6
.
However, there might be totally geodesic submanifolds of a Riemannian cone that do not arise as cones, see Examples
6.13
and
6.15
.
In Section
6
, we prove a structure result for totally geodesic submanifolds in cones, see Theorem
6.9
.
As a consequence of this, we deduce that maximal totally geodesic submanifolds of Riemannian cones are either cones over a totally geodesic submanifold or totally geodesic hypersurfaces, see Corollary
6.10
.
It was observed in
[
5
]
that Riemannian cones are intimately linked to special holonomy.
For instance, a special class of Sasakian manifolds is that of Sasakian–Einstein manifolds, whose investigation has led to the construction of many inhomogeneous Einstein metrics on spheres, see
[
10
]
.
It turns out that the holonomy of the cone over a Sasakian–Einstein manifold is contained in
\mathsf{SU}(n)
.
Similarly, Riemannian cones over strictly nearly Kähler
6
-manifolds have its holonomy contained in
\mathsf{G}_{2}
.
This holonomy reduction is equivalent to the existence of a parallel
3
-form
\phi
that is locally modeled on the associative
3
-form on
\mathbb{R}^{7}
, or alternatively, the existence of a torsion-free
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-structure.
Another class of
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-structures defined on
7
-dimensional manifolds is that of nearly parallel
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-structures.
A
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-structure
\phi
is
nearly parallel
if it satisfies
\star d\phi=c\phi
, for
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, where
\star
denotes the Hodge star operator. It can also be proved that cones over nearly parallel
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-manifolds have its holonomy contained in
\mathsf{Spin}(7)
.
The first example of a manifold with exceptional holonomy
\mathsf{Spin}(7)
was constructed in
[
11
]
, and it is the cone over the homogeneous nearly parallel
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-manifold
\mathsf{B}^{7}=\mathsf{SO}(5)/\mathsf{SO}(3)
.
We prove that Sasakian–Einstein, strictly nearly Kähler
6
-manifolds, and nearly parallel
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-manifolds do not admit totally geodesic hypersurfaces, see Theorem
6.7
.

Moreover, we consider the classification problem of totally geodesic submanifolds in cones with holonomy
\mathsf{G}_{2}
over homogeneous nearly Kähler manifolds.
As a consequence of Corollary
6.4
, these are the only Riemannian cones with holonomy equal to
\mathsf{G}_{2}
equipped with a metric of cohomogeneity one, and thus with the highest possible degree of symmetry, contributing to a rich presence of totally geodesic submanifolds.

Theorem E
.

Let
M
be a homogeneous nearly Kähler
6
-manifold of non-constant curvature and let
\Sigma
be a maximal totally geodesic submanifold of the
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-cone
\widehat{M}
over
M
of dimension greater than one. Then
\Sigma
is the Riemannian cone of a maximal totally geodesic submanifold
S
of
M
.

Notice that combining Theorem
E
with the classification of totally geodesic submanifolds in cones over space forms (see Proposition
6.12
) and three-dimensional Berger spheres (see Proposition
6.14
), one can list all totally geodesic submanifolds in cohomogeneity one
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-cones and thus obtain the full classification.

Moreover, Riemannian cones over
J
-holomorphic curves or Lagrangian submanifolds of a nearly Kähler
6
-manifold give rise to associative or coassociative manifolds of the corresponding
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-cone over
M
, respectively.
By definition,
associative
and
coassociative
submanifolds are the submanifolds of a
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-manifold calibrated by
\phi
and the Hodge dual of
\phi
, respectively; see
[
26
]
and
[
31
, Chapters 4 and 12]
for an introduction to calibrated geometry.
As a consequence of Corollary
D
and Theorem
E
, one has the following:

Corollary F
.

Let
\Sigma
be a maximal totally geodesic submanifold of the
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-cone over a homogeneous nearly Kähler
6
-manifold of non-constant curvature.
Then the following statements hold:

(i)

if
\Sigma
has dimension three, then
\Sigma
is an associative submanifold.

(ii)

if
\Sigma
has dimension four, then
\Sigma
is a coassociative submanifold.

Both Corollaries
D
and
F
seem to indicate that for totally geodesic submanifolds of nearly Kähler
6
-manifolds and their
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-cones, there is a strong link between this purely Riemannian property and the underlying nearly Kähler and
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-structures, respectively.
Consequently, we find that it would be very interesting to investigate whether both corollaries hold true without the homogeneity assumptions.

Organization of the paper

In particular, in Subsection

.
In Subsection
4.2
, we derive a useful criterion for the existence of totally geodesic surfaces in naturally reductive homogeneous spaces, see Proposition
4.8
.
Also, in Subsection
4.3
, we characterize when a totally geodesic submanifold of the total space of a homogeneous fibration is well-positioned, see Lemma
4.9
.
In Section
5
, we exhibit and discuss the properties of the totally geodesic submanifolds of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
, the flag manifold
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})

\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
.
In Section

In Subsection

.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Riemannian homogeneous spaces

Let
M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
be a Riemannian homogeneous space and
o=e\mathsf{K}
.
We denote by
\mathsf{G}_{p}
the isotropy subgroup of
p
, that is, the set of all elements of
\mathsf{G}
that fix
p
.
For our purposes we assume that
\mathsf{G}
is connected and the action of
\mathsf{G}
on
M
is almost effective, in the sense that
\bigcap_{p\in M}\mathsf{G}_{p}
is a discrete subgroup of
\mathsf{G}
.
The (Lie) group of isometries of
M
is written as
I(M)
, and
I^{0}(M)\subseteq I(M)
denotes its identity component.
Throughout this article, Lie groups are denoted by uppercase letters and their Lie algebras are denoted by their corresponding lowercase gothic letters.

To each
X\in\mathfrak{g}
we can associate its corresponding fundamental vector field
X^{*}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
defined via
X^{*}_{p}=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}\operatorname{Exp}(tX)\cdot p
for all
p\in M
.
Clearly,
X^{*}
is a Killing vector field.
Moreover, one can see that the map
X\in\mathfrak{g}\mapsto X^{*}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
is a Lie algebra anti-homomorphism.
We may identify the quotient
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{k}
with the tangent space
T_{o}M
via the isomorphism
X+\mathfrak{k}\mapsto X^{*}_{o}
, which also establishes an equivalence of representations between the isotropy representation
\mathsf{K}\to\mathsf{GL}(T_{o}M)
and the adjoint representation
\mathsf{K}\to\mathsf{GL}(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{k})
.

We say that
M
is
reductive
if there exists a vector subspace
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
and
\operatorname{Ad}(\mathsf{K})\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}
.
The subspace
\mathfrak{p}
is called a
reductive complement
and the decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
is known as a
reductive decomposition
.
It can be shown that every Riemannian homogeneous space is reductive.
As a consequence, we may identify
\mathfrak{p}
with
T_{o}M
and the isotropy representation is equivalent to the adjoint representation
\mathsf{K}\to\mathsf{GL}(\mathfrak{p})
.
In particular, we may endow
\mathfrak{p}
with the scalar product induced by the metric on
M
, and one sees that it is
\mathsf{K}
-invariant.
We denote both the metric on
M
and the inner product on
\mathfrak{p}
by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
If
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, we denote by
X_{\mathfrak{k}}
and
X_{\mathfrak{p}}
the unique vectors in
\mathfrak{k}
and
\mathfrak{p}
respectively such that
X=X_{\mathfrak{k}}+X_{\mathfrak{p}}
.
Furthermore, if
V
is a Euclidean space and
W\subseteq V
is a subspace, we denote by
v_{W}
the orthogonal projection of
v\in V
onto
W
. We define the symmetric bilinear map
U\colon\mathfrak{p}\times\mathfrak{p}\rightarrow\mathfrak{p}
by

(1)

2\langle U(X,Y),Z\rangle=\langle[Z,X]_{\mathfrak{p}},Y\rangle+\langle X,[Z,Y]_{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle,\quad X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{p}.

The reductive decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
is
naturally reductive
if
U
is identically zero.
In that case, one sees that the curves
\operatorname{Exp}(tX)\cdot o
(where
X\in\mathfrak{p}
) are geodesics.
Suppose that
\mathfrak{g}
admits an
\operatorname{Ad}(\mathsf{G})
-invariant inner product
q
and consider
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{k}^{\perp}
, where
\mathfrak{k}^{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal complement of
\mathfrak{k}
in
\mathfrak{g}
with respect to
q
.
Then, we say that the reductive decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
is
normal homogeneous
. In particular, normal homogeneous reductive decompositions are naturally reductive.

Every reductive homogeneous space
M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
admits two natural connections.
On the one hand, we may consider the canonical connection
\nabla^{c}
, which is characterized by the equation

(\nabla^{c}_{X^{*}}Y^{*})_{o}=(-[X,Y]_{\mathfrak{p}})^{*}_{o},\quad X,Y\in\mathfrak{p}.

On the other hand, we have the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
. We can also consider the tensor
D:=\nabla-\nabla^{c}
. It turns out that
D
is a
\mathsf{G}
-invariant tensor defined on
M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
called the
difference tensor
. Notice that
U=0
holds if and only if the operators
A_{X}\colon Y\in\mathfrak{p}\to[X,Y]_{\mathfrak{p}}
are skew-symmetric for all
X\in\mathfrak{p}
.
In particular, if
D
is an element of
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathsf{K}}\bigl(\bigwedge^{2}\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}\bigr)
, then the reductive decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
is naturally reductive.

Following
[
36
, Chapter X]
, we can express
\nabla
and
D
at the base point
o\in M
in terms of the Lie bracket of
\mathfrak{g}
and the metric of
M
in the following way:

(\nabla_{X^{*}}Y^{*})_{o}=\left(-\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]_{\mathfrak{p}}+U(X,Y)\right)^{*}_{o},\quad D_{X}Y=\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]_{\mathfrak{p}}+U(X,Y),

where
X,Y\in\mathfrak{p}
.
Moreover, the curvature tensor associated with the canonical connection and the Levi-Civita connection can be written, respectively, as

R^{c}(X,Y)Z=-[[X,Y]_{\mathfrak{k}},Z],\qquad R(X,Y)Z=D_{X}D_{Y}Z-D_{Y}D_{X}Z-[[X,Y]_{\mathfrak{k}},Z]-D_{[X,Y]_{\mathfrak{p}}}Z,

where
X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{p}
.
We also need to compute the covariant derivative
\nabla R
of the curvature tensor.
As shown in
[
43
, Remark 2.5]
, the tensor
\nabla R
is given at
o
by

\nabla_{V}R(X,Y,Z)=D_{V}(R(X,Y)Z)-R(D_{V}X,Y)Z-R(X,D_{V}Y)Z-R(X,Y)D_{V}Z

for all
X,Y,Z,V\in\mathfrak{p}
.

An important class of Riemannian submersions involving homogeneous spaces is that of homogeneous fibrations, which we briefly describe.
The main reference is
[
25
]
.
Let
\mathsf{H}\subseteq\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathsf{G}
be a chain of inclusions of compact connected subgroups of the connected Lie group
\mathsf{G}
, and endow
\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
with a
\mathsf{G}
-invariant Riemannian metric.
Then there exists a left-invariant metric on
\mathsf{G}
that is also right
\mathsf{K}
-invariant, and a
\mathsf{G}
-invariant metric on
\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{H}
that makes the canonical projection
\pi\colon\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{H}\to\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
a Riemannian submersion with totally geodesic fibers isometric to
\mathsf{K}/\mathsf{H}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{H}
the vertical and horizontal distributions associated with the Riemannian submersion
\pi
.
We are concerned with the case that
\mathsf{G}
is a compact group with a bi-invariant metric and the homogeneous spaces
F=\mathsf{K}/\mathsf{H}
,
M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{H}
and
B=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
are endowed with the corresponding normal homogeneous metrics.
If
V
is a Euclidean vector space and
W\subseteq V
is a vector subspace, we denote by
V\ominus W
the orthogonal complement of
W
in
V
.
In this case, the tangent space
T_{o}M
at
o=e\mathsf{H}
is identified with
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{g}\ominus\mathfrak{h}
, so the vertical and horizontal subspaces at
o
are
\mathcal{V}_{o}=\mathfrak{k}\ominus\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathcal{H}_{o}=\mathfrak{p}\ominus\mathfrak{k}
.
It turns out that the distributions
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{H}
are
\mathsf{G}
-invariant in the sense that for every
p\in M
and
g\in\mathsf{G}
, we have
g_{*p}\mathcal{V}_{p}=\mathcal{V}_{g\cdot p}
and
g_{*p}\mathcal{H}_{p}=\mathcal{H}_{g\cdot p}
.

A submanifold
N\subseteq M
is said to be
extrinsically homogeneous with respect to the presentation

M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
if there exists a Lie subgroup
\mathsf{S}\subseteq\mathsf{G}
such that
N
is an orbit of
\mathsf{S}
.
More generally, a submanifold
N
of a Riemannian manifold
M
is
extrinsically homogeneous
if it is an orbit of a Lie subgroup
\mathsf{S}\subseteq I(M)
.
In the following lemma, we provide a formula for the second fundamental form for an extrinsically homogeneous submanifold of a homogeneous space.
This formula was originally derived by Solonenko in the case of Riemannian symmetric spaces, see
[
49
, Proposition 2.2.43]
.
See also
[
2
, Proposition 2.2]
for an alternative expression.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
be a Riemannian homogeneous space equipped with an arbitrary
\mathsf{G}
-invariant metric and reductive decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
.
Assume that
\mathsf{S}
is a Lie subgroup of
\mathsf{G}
, and let
\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\perp}
be the tangent and normal spaces to
\mathsf{S}\cdot o
at
o
(regarded as subspaces of
\mathfrak{p}
).
Let
V_{\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\perp}}
denote the orthogonal projection of
V\in\mathfrak{p}
onto
\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\perp}
.
Then the second fundamental form of
\mathsf{S}\cdot o
at
o
is given by

(2)

\mathbb{II}(X_{\mathfrak{p}},Y)=([X_{\mathfrak{k}},Y]+D_{X_{\mathfrak{p}}}Y)_{\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\perp}}

for all
X\in\mathfrak{s}
and
Y\in\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}
.
In particular,
\mathsf{S}\cdot o
is totally geodesic if and only if
[X_{\mathfrak{k}},Y]+D_{X_{\mathfrak{p}}}Y\in\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all
X\in\mathfrak{s}
and
Y\in\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

Proof.

Choose arbitrary elements
X
,
Y\in\mathfrak{s}
, so that the vector fields
X^{*}
and
Y^{*}
are tangent to
\mathsf{S}\cdot o
and their values at
o
are
X_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
Y_{\mathfrak{p}}
respectively.
We evaluate the covariant derivative
\nabla_{Y^{*}}X^{*}
at
o
.
We see that

\displaystyle\nabla_{Y^{*}}X^{*}={}

\displaystyle\nabla_{Y_{\mathfrak{p}}^{*}}X^{*}=\nabla_{X^{*}}Y_{\mathfrak{p}}^{*}+[Y_{\mathfrak{p}}^{*},X^{*}]=\nabla_{X_{\mathfrak{p}}^{*}}Y_{\mathfrak{p}}^{*}+[X,Y_{\mathfrak{p}}]^{*}=\nabla_{X_{\mathfrak{p}}^{*}}Y_{\mathfrak{p}}^{*}+[X_{\mathfrak{k}},Y_{\mathfrak{p}}]^{*}+[X_{\mathfrak{p}},Y_{\mathfrak{p}}]^{*}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}[X_{\mathfrak{p}},Y_{\mathfrak{p}}]_{\mathfrak{p}}+U(X_{\mathfrak{p}},Y_{\mathfrak{p}})+[X_{\mathfrak{k}},Y_{\mathfrak{p}}]+[X_{\mathfrak{p}},Y_{\mathfrak{p}}]_{\mathfrak{p}}=[X_{\mathfrak{k}},Y_{\mathfrak{p}}]+D_{X_{\mathfrak{p}}}Y_{\mathfrak{p}}.

Thus, projecting onto the normal space and using the fact that the second fundamental form is symmetric, we obtain that

\mathbb{II}(X_{\mathfrak{p}},Y_{\mathfrak{p}})=(\nabla_{Y^{*}}X^{*})_{\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\perp}}=([X_{\mathfrak{k}},Y_{\mathfrak{p}}]+D_{X_{\mathfrak{p}}}Y_{\mathfrak{p}})_{\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\perp}},

as desired. ∎

2.2.
Homogeneous nearly Kähler manifolds

In this section we present the ambient spaces that we work with throughout the rest of this article.
The classification of homogeneous nearly Kähler manifolds in dimension six was done by Butruille
[
12
]
.
Indeed, every simply connected Riemannian manifold satisfying the previous conditions is homothetic to either the sphere
\mathsf{S}^{6}
, the complex projective space
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
, the flag manifold
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
, and
\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
.
Since
\mathsf{S}^{6}
carries its natural round metric, its totally geodesic submanifolds are well-known, so we only need to focus on the other three spaces.
It turns out that these manifolds are examples of
3
-symmetric spaces.
The main reference for the description of
3
-symmetric spaces is
[
56
]
.

Let
(M,J)
be an almost Hermitian manifold.
Recall that
M
is
nearly Kähler
if for every vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
we have
(\nabla_{X}J)X=0
, and that it is
strictly nearly Kähler
if it is nearly Kähler and
\nabla_{X}J\neq 0
for all nonzero
X\in TM
.
If
\dim M=6
, this condition is equivalent to
\nabla J\neq 0
.
On the other hand, a
3
-symmetric space

M
is a Riemannian manifold
M
together with a family of isometries
s_{p}\colon M\to M
, where
p\in M
, satisfying
s_{p}^{3}=\operatorname{Id}_{M}
for all
p\in M
,
p
is an isolated fixed point of
s_{p}
, and each
s_{p}
is holomorphic with respect to the so-called
canonical almost complex structure

J
defined via

(3)

(s_{p})_{*p}=-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}_{T_{p}M}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}J_{p},\quad p\in M.

Any connected 3-symmetric space is automatically homogeneous
[
22
, Theorem 4.8]
.
Conversely, one can construct a
3
-symmetric space in terms of algebraic data.
Indeed, let
\mathsf{G}
be a connected Lie group and
\mathsf{K}
a closed subgroup of
\mathsf{G}
.
Assume that there exists an automorphism
\Theta\colon\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{G}
of order three such that
\mathsf{G}^{\Theta}_{0}\subseteq\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathsf{G}^{\Theta}
, where
\mathsf{G}^{\Theta}
is the fixed point set of
\Theta
and
\mathsf{G}^{\Theta}_{0}
is its identity component.
It turns out that
M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
is a reductive homogeneous space in such a way that
\theta=\Theta_{*}
preserves the reductive complement.
Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
be a reductive decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
satisfying
\theta\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}
.
Then, any inner product on
\mathfrak{p}
that is invariant under
\operatorname{Ad}(\mathsf{K})
and
\theta
gives rise to a
\mathsf{G}
-invariant metric on
M
that turns
M
into a
3
-symmetric space, where the isometry of order three at
o=e\mathsf{K}
is given by
s_{o}(x\mathsf{K})=\Theta(x)\mathsf{K}
.
We say that
(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{K},\Theta)
is the
triple
associated with the
3
-symmetric space
M
.
The corresponding almost complex structure is the
\mathsf{G}
-invariant tensor field
J
defined at
o
by (
3
).
By
[
22
, Proposition 5.6]
, the almost Hermitian manifold
(M,J)
is nearly Kähler if and only if the decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
is naturally reductive.

We now proceed to describe our six-dimensional examples, exhibiting them as
3
-symmetric spaces.

2.2.1.
The complex projective space
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}

Consider
\mathbb{H}^{2}
as a right
\mathbb{C}
-vector space, so that the projective space
\mathsf{P}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
is exactly
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
.
The natural action of
\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{Sp}(2)
on
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
is transitive, and the isotropy subgroup at
o=[1:0]
is
\mathsf{K}=\mathsf{U}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1)
, so that
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
can be viewed as the quotient
\mathsf{Sp}(2)/\mathsf{U}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1)
.
The Killing form of
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sp}(2)
is
\mathcal{B}(X,Y)=12\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}_{\mathbb{H}}(XY)
, so
-\mathcal{B}
is an
\operatorname{Ad}(\mathsf{Sp}(2))
-invariant inner product in
\mathfrak{g}
,
but we renormalize it so that the inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
is
\langle X,Y\rangle=-2\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}_{\mathbb{H}}(XY)
.
Let
\mathfrak{p}
be the orthogonal complement of
\mathfrak{u}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{sp}(1)
in
\mathfrak{sp}(2)
.
We endow
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
with the homogeneous metric induced by the restriction of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to
\mathfrak{p}
.
We also consider the element
\omega=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(e^{\frac{2\pi i}{3}},1\bigr)\in\mathsf{K}
.
Then the conjugation
\Theta=I_{\omega}
defines an inner automorphism of order three in
\mathsf{G}
, whose fixed point set is
\mathsf{Sp}(2)^{\Theta}=\mathsf{U}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1)
, and
(\mathsf{Sp}(2),\mathsf{U}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1),\Theta)
is the triple associated with the
3
-symmetric space
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
.
The nearly Kähler complex structure
J
is defined as
J=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\Theta_{*}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\operatorname{Id}_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

Let
E_{ij}\in\mathfrak{gl}(2,\mathbb{H})
be the elementary matrix which has all components equal to zero except for the
(i,j)
component, which is one.
We use the orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{6}\}
of
\mathfrak{p}
defined by

\displaystyle e_{1}=

\displaystyle~\frac{j}{\sqrt{2}}E_{11},

\displaystyle e_{2}=

\displaystyle~\frac{k}{\sqrt{2}}E_{11},

\displaystyle e_{3}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2}(E_{21}-E_{12}),

\displaystyle e_{4}=

\displaystyle~\frac{i}{2}(E_{12}+E_{21}),

\displaystyle e_{5}=

\displaystyle~\frac{j}{2}(E_{12}+E_{21}),

\displaystyle e_{6}=

\displaystyle~\frac{k}{2}(E_{12}+E_{21}).

The isotropy representation allows us to decompose
\mathfrak{p}
as the direct sum of two irreducible submodules
\mathfrak{p}_{1}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{2}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{3},\dots,e_{6}\}
.
Indeed, the subrepresentation
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
of
\mathsf{U}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1)
is isomorphic to the representation
\mathbb{C}
with the action given by
(\lambda,\mu)\cdot z=\lambda^{2}z
, whereas
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
is isomorphic to the representation
\mathbb{R}^{4}
of
\mathsf{U}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1)
under
(\lambda,\mu)\cdot x=\mu x\bar{\lambda}
.
In particular, the group
\mathsf{U}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1)
acts transitively on the unit sphere of each
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
.

The isometry group of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
is
I(\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3})=(\mathsf{Sp}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, where the outer
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is generated by conjugation by
\operatorname{diag}(j,1)\in\mathsf{Sp}(2)
(see for example
[
48
]
).

Now, consider the chain of subgroups
\mathsf{U}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1)\subseteq\mathsf{Sp}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1)\subseteq\mathsf{Sp}(2)
.
This gives rise to the homogeneous fibration
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}\to\mathbb{H}\mathsf{P}^{1}=\mathsf{S}^{4}
, which is precisely the twistor fibration, the fiber of which is a totally geodesic
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{1}=\mathsf{S}^{2}
.
The decomposition of
\mathfrak{p}
into the vertical and horizontal subspaces of this submersion is given by
\mathcal{V}_{o}=\mathfrak{p}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{o}=\mathfrak{p}_{2}
.

2.2.2.
The flag manifold
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})

Recall that a full flag in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
is a chain
0=V_{0}\subseteq V_{1}\subseteq V_{2}\subseteq V_{3}=\mathbb{C}^{3}
(also denoted by
(V_{1},V_{2})
) of subspaces such that
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{k}=k
for each
k
.
We denote by
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
the space of all flags in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, which is naturally identified with the quotient of the Stiefel manifold of orthonormal bases of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
under the standard action of
\mathsf{U}(1)^{3}
.
The group
\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{SU}(3)
acts transitively on
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
, and if
o
is the standard flag
0\subseteq\mathbb{C}e_{1}\subseteq\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{2}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{3}
, its isotropy subgroup is the maximal torus
\mathsf{K}=\mathsf{T}^{2}
of diagonal matrices in
\mathsf{SU}(3)
, so we have
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})=\mathsf{SU}(3)/\mathsf{T}^{2}
.

Let us endow
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
with a reductive decomposition and a Riemannian metric.
Note that the Killing form of
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{su}(3)
satisfies
\mathcal{B}(X,Y)=6\operatorname{tr}(XY)
for all
X
,
Y\in\mathfrak{g}
.
As a consequence, the negative Killing form gives a bi-invariant metric on
\mathsf{SU}(3)
.
However, for the sake of convenience, we rescale this metric so that the inner product in
\mathfrak{g}
is
\langle X,Y\rangle=-\operatorname{tr}(XY)
for all
X
,
Y\in\mathfrak{su}(3)
.
Let
\mathfrak{p}
be the orthogonal complement of
\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{u}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{u}(1)
in
\mathfrak{g}
.
Then, the restriction of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to
\mathfrak{p}
induces an
\operatorname{Ad}(\mathsf{T}^{2})
-invariant inner product on
\mathfrak{p}
, that is, a
\mathsf{G}
-invariant metric on
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
.
This metric is homothetic to the standard homogeneous metric on
M
.
We also consider the automorphism
\Theta=I_{\omega}\colon\mathsf{SU}(3)\to\mathsf{SU}(3)
, where
\omega=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(e^{\frac{2\pi i}{3}},1,e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{3}}\bigr)\in\mathsf{T}^{2}
.
Then
\Theta
is an automorphism of order three whose fixed point set is precisely
\mathsf{SU}(3)^{\Theta}=\mathsf{T}^{2}
, so
(\mathsf{SU}(3),\mathsf{T}^{2},\Theta)
is the triple associated with the
3
-symmetric space
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
.
The corresponding almost complex structure
J
at
\mathfrak{p}
is determined by
J=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\Theta_{*}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\operatorname{Id}_{\mathfrak{p}}
.
We consider the orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{6}\}
of
\mathfrak{p}
, where

\displaystyle e_{1}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{12}-E_{21}),

\displaystyle e_{2}=

\displaystyle~\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{12}+E_{21}),

\displaystyle e_{3}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{23}-E_{32}),

\displaystyle e_{4}=

\displaystyle~\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{23}+E_{32}),

\displaystyle e_{5}=

3.
Totally geodesic immersions in real analytic Riemannian manifolds

In this section we develop some new tools in the theory of totally geodesic immersions in analytic Riemannian manifolds.

To conduct the global study of totally geodesic submanifolds, it is natural to assume that the ambient space is real analytic, since homogeneous spaces are examples of real analytic manifolds
[
35
, Proposition 4.2]
.
As we will see, it is also necessary to allow self-intersections in our submanifolds, leading to the study of totally geodesic immersions in analytic Riemannian manifolds.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold and
f\colon\Sigma\to M
an isometric immersion of a connected Riemannian manifold
\Sigma
to
M
.
We say that
f
is a
totally geodesic
immersion (and
\Sigma
is a
totally geodesic submanifold
of
M
) if for every geodesic
\gamma
of
\Sigma
the composition
f\circ\gamma
is also a geodesic of
M
.
This condition is equivalent to the vanishing of the second fundamental form
\mathbb{II}
of
\Sigma
.
We refer the reader to
[
27
, Appendix A]
and
[
6
, Section 10.3]
for more details on totally geodesic immersions.
A similar treatment can be found in
[
53
]
.

When studying general totally geodesic immersions, certain redundancies arise that need to be avoided.
The first one is due to reparametrizations of the domain, which is handled by introducing the notion of equivalence.
We say that two totally geodesic immersions
f_{i}\colon\Sigma_{i}\to M
are
equivalent
if there exists an isometry
h\colon\Sigma_{1}\to\Sigma_{2}
such that
f_{1}=f_{2}\circ h
.
The second redundancy occurs when considering surjective local isometries.
For instance, a totally geodesic embedding of
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}
yields the same information as its composition
\mathsf{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}
with the universal cover
\mathsf{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}
, though the latter is not an embedding.
We can deal with this issue by working exclusively with compatible immersions.
Let
f\colon\Sigma^{k}\to M
be totally geodesic, and consider the Grassmann bundle
\mathsf{G}_{k}(TM)
of
k
-planes in
TM
.
Then
f
induces a smooth map
\widetilde{f}\colon\Sigma\to\mathsf{G}_{k}(TM)
by letting
\widetilde{f}(p)=(f(p),f_{*p}(T_{p}\Sigma))
(for ease of notation, we simply write
\widetilde{f}(p)=f_{*p}(T_{p}\Sigma)
).
The map
f
is said to be
compatible
if
\widetilde{f}
is injective (this is the case, for example, if
f
is injective).
In other words, we allow
\Sigma
to have self-intersections in
M
, but at each point of self-intersection the corresponding tangent spaces must be different.
It turns out that any totally geodesic immersion can be factored through a compatible one
[
6
, p. 272]
, so imposing this condition does not cause us to lose information.
We note that in general, given compatible totally geodesic immersions
f_{1}\colon\Sigma_{1}\to\Sigma_{2}
and
f_{2}\colon\Sigma_{2}\to M
, the composition
f_{2}\circ f_{1}
may not be compatible.
A counterexample will be provided in Remark
5.4
.

One sees that a compatible immersion
f\colon\Sigma\to M
is determined up to equivalence by the image
\widetilde{f}(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathsf{G}_{k}(TM)
.
Given two compatible totally geodesic immersions
f_{i}\colon\Sigma_{i}\to M
, we say that
f_{2}\colon\Sigma_{2}\to M

extends

f_{1}\colon\Sigma_{1}\to M
if there exists an injective local isometry
j\colon\Sigma_{1}\to\Sigma_{2}
satisfying
f_{1}=f_{2}\circ j
, or equivalently, if
\widetilde{f}_{1}(\Sigma_{1})\subseteq\widetilde{f}_{2}(\Sigma_{2})
.
This defines a partial ordering on any set of equivalence classes of compatible totally geodesic immersions.
By
[
6
, Proposition 10.3.2]
, every compatible totally geodesic immersion can be extended to an
inextendable
one (that is, an immersion which is maximal with respect to this partial ordering), and this extension is unique up to equivalence (we say in this case that the submanifold is
inextendable
). Note that Heintze, Liu and Olmos
[
27
]
refer to these submanifolds as
maximal
totally geodesic submanifolds, whereas we prefer to reserve this term for another concept.
We thus aim to classify inextendable totally geodesic submanifolds up to congruence.

Moreover, given
p\in\Sigma
and a vector subspace
V\subseteq T_{p}M
, if there exists an inextendable totally geodesic immersion
f\colon\Sigma\to M
with such that
(p,V)\in\widetilde{f}(\Sigma)
, then
f
is unique up to equivalence.
In other words, there exists at most one inextendable totally geodesic submanifold passing through
p
with tangent space
V
.
Because of this, we are interested in determining which subspaces
V
of
T_{p}M
are tangent to a totally geodesic submanifold.
The answer to this question is given by means of the following result, see
[
6
, Section 10.3.2]
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
M
be a real analytic Riemannian manifold,
p\in M
and
V\subseteq T_{p}M
a vector subspace.
Then there exists a totally geodesic submanifold
\Sigma
of
M
whose tangent space at
p
is
V
if and only if
(\nabla^{k}R)_{p}
preserves
V
for all
k\geq 0
.

Motivated by the previous theorem, we introduce the following definition.
A vector subspace
V\subseteq T_{p}M
is
totally geodesic
if there exists a totally geodesic immersion
f\colon\Sigma\to M
such that
V\in\widetilde{f}(\Sigma)
.

Remark 3.2
.

For the sake of convenience, we refer to the totally geodesic submanifold
f\colon\Sigma\to M
as
\Sigma
during the rest of this paper unless we need to specify the immersion.
In most cases, the totally geodesic immersions that will appear are inclusions of embedded submanifolds
\Sigma\subseteq M
, so in this context there is no ambiguity in omitting the immersion.

For each
X\in T_{p}M
we can define its corresponding
Jacobi operator

R_{X}\colon T_{p}M\to T_{p}M
via the equation
R_{X}Y=R(Y,X)X
.
Moreover, we may also consider the
Cartan operator

C_{X}\colon T_{p}M\to T_{p}M
given by
C_{X}Y=\nabla R(X,X,Y,X)
.
Both linear maps are symmetric with respect to the inner product on
T_{p}M
, so we may decompose
T_{p}M
as the orthogonal direct sum of the eigenspaces of
R_{X}
, as well as the orthogonal direct sum of the eigenspaces of
C_{X}
.

Remark 3.3
.

If
V\subseteq T_{p}M
is a totally geodesic subspace and
X\in T_{p}M
is any vector, it follows from Theorem
3.1
that
V
is an invariant subspace for
R_{X}
and
C_{X}
.
In particular, if
T_{p}M=(T_{p}M)_{\lambda_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus(T_{p}M)_{\lambda_{r}}
is the eigenspace decomposition of
T_{p}M
with respect to
R_{X}
, we have
V=(V\cap(T_{p}M)_{\lambda_{1}})\oplus\dots\oplus(V\cap(T_{p}M)_{\lambda_{r}})
.
The same argument can be applied for the operator
C_{X}
.

We also establish the following notation.
Given a smooth curve
\alpha\colon I\to P
, where
I
is an interval and
P
is a Riemannian manifold, the parallel transport map from
\alpha(t_{1})\in P
to
\alpha(t_{2})\in P
along
\alpha
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{t_{1},t_{2}}^{\alpha}
.

In this paper we focus on real analytic manifolds which are not necessarily complete (see Section
6
).
Because the exponential map of a real analytic Riemannian manifold is also real analytic, one sees that for any compatible totally geodesic immersion
f\colon\Sigma\to M
, the domain
\Sigma
is also a real analytic manifold and
f
is a real analytic map.

We now provide a criterion that allows us to determine whether a totally geodesic submanifold
\Sigma
of
M
is inextendable in terms of the maximal geodesics of
\Sigma
and
M
.
The key observation that makes use of real analyticity is the following:
if
p\in M
and
V\subseteq T_{p}M
is a totally geodesic subspace, then for every geodesic
\gamma\colon I\subseteq\mathbb{R}\to M
satisfying
\gamma(0)=p
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)\in V
, the parallel translates
\mathcal{P}_{0,t}^{\gamma}V
are also totally geodesic subspaces of
T_{\gamma(t)}M
for all
t\in I
(see for example the proof in
[
53
, Corollary 3.7]
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
M
be a connected real analytic Riemannian manifold and
f\colon\Sigma\to M
a compatible totally geodesic immersion.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

The immersion
f
is inextendable.

(ii)

For every maximal geodesic
\gamma\colon I\subseteq\mathbb{R}\to\Sigma
, the composition
f\circ\gamma\colon I\to M
is also a maximal geodesic of
M
.

Proof.

Firstly, suppose that (
i
) holds.
If (
ii
) is not satisfied, then there exists a geodesic
\gamma\colon[0,1)\to\Sigma
with
\gamma(0)=x
that is not extendable to the right, while the composition
f\circ\gamma
admits an extension
\sigma\colon[0,1]\to M
.
We consider
q=\sigma(1)
.
The subspace
W=\mathcal{P}_{0,1}^{\sigma}V\subseteq T_{q}M
, where
V=\widetilde{f}(x)
, is therefore totally geodesic.

We show that
W\notin\widetilde{f}(\Sigma)
.
Indeed, suppose that for a certain
y\in\Sigma
we have
W=\widetilde{f}(y)
.
Since
-\sigma^{\prime}(1)=-\mathcal{P}_{0,1}^{\sigma}\sigma^{\prime}(0)\in W=f_{*y}(T_{y}\Sigma)
, there exists a geodesic
\alpha\colon[0,\delta)\to\Sigma
satisfying
\alpha(0)=y
and
\alpha^{\prime}(0)=-(f_{*y})^{-1}\sigma^{\prime}(1)
.
Suppose without loss of generality that
\delta<1
.
The composition
f\circ\alpha
satisfies
f(\alpha(0))=q
and
(f\circ\alpha)^{\prime}(0)=-\sigma^{\prime}(1)
, so
f(\alpha(t))=\sigma(1-t)
for all
t\in[0,\delta)
.
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(\alpha(t))={}

\displaystyle f_{*\alpha(t)}(T_{\alpha(t)}\Sigma)=f_{*\alpha(t)}(\mathcal{P}_{0,t}^{\alpha}\Sigma)=\mathcal{P}_{0,t}^{f\circ\alpha}f_{*y}(T_{y}\Sigma)=\mathcal{P}_{1,1-t}^{\sigma}W=\mathcal{P}_{0,1-t}^{\sigma}V

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{0,1-t}^{f\circ\gamma}V=\mathcal{P}_{0,1-t}^{f\circ\gamma}f_{*x}(T_{x}\Sigma)=f_{*\gamma(1-t)}(T_{\gamma(1-t)}\Sigma)=\widetilde{f}(\gamma(1-t)),

so using that
\widetilde{f}
is injective we see that
\alpha(t)=\gamma(1-t)
for all
t\in[0,\delta)
.
Because
\alpha
is continuous at
0
, we obtain that the limit
\lim_{t\to 1^{-}}\gamma(t)
exists and coincides with
y
, but this contradicts the fact that
\gamma
is not extendable to the right.
We deduce that
W
is not in the image of
\widetilde{f}
.

We now consider an
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small so that
\exp_{q}\colon B_{T_{q}M}(0,\varepsilon)\to M
is a diffeomorphism onto its image and
S=\exp_{q}(B_{T_{q}M}(0,\varepsilon)\cap W)
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
M
.
As
-\sigma^{\prime}(1)\in W
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that
\sigma(t)\in S
for all
t\in(1-\delta,1]
.
In particular, we have for all
t\in(1-\delta,1)
that

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(\gamma(t))={}

\displaystyle f_{*\gamma(t)}(T_{\gamma(t)}\Sigma)=\mathcal{P}_{0,t}^{f\circ\gamma}V=\mathcal{P}_{0,t}^{\sigma}\mathcal{P}_{1,0}^{\sigma}V=\mathcal{P}_{1,t}^{\sigma}V=\widetilde{i}(\sigma(t)),

where
i\colon S\hookrightarrow M
is the inclusion map.
This means that
\widetilde{f}(\Sigma)\cap\widetilde{i}(S)\neq\varnothing
, so
[
6
, Lemma 10.3.1]
allows us to construct a compatible totally geodesic immersion that strictly extends
f
and
i
, as
W=T_{q}S
is not contained in
\widetilde{f}(\Sigma)
, contradicting the fact that
f
is inextendable.

Conversely, suppose that
f
satisfies (
ii
) and let
g\colon E\to M
be an extension of
f
.
By definition, we can find an injective local isometry
\phi\colon\Sigma\to E
satisfying
f=g\circ\phi
.
Replacing
\Sigma
by
\phi(\Sigma)
, we may suppose directly that
\Sigma\subseteq E
is an open subset and
f=g|_{\Sigma}
.
If we show that
\Sigma
is also closed, then we may conclude that
\Sigma=E
and
f=g
.

If
\Sigma
is not closed in
E
, then we can find a geodesic
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to E
such that
\gamma(t)\in\Sigma
for all
t\in[0,1)
and
\gamma(1)\in E\setminus\Sigma
.
Write
x=\gamma(0)\in\Sigma
.
The composition
f\circ\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
is also a geodesic with
f(\gamma(0))=f(x)
and
(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(0)\in\widetilde{f}(x)
, so by (

) we may find a geodesic
\beta\colon[0,1]\to\Sigma
satisfying
\beta(0)=x
and
f(\beta(t))=f(\gamma(t))
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
By uniqueness of
E
-geodesics, we have
\beta=\gamma
, so
\gamma(1)=\beta(1)\in\Sigma
, which gives a contradiction.
We conclude that
\Sigma=E
and
f=g
, and because the choice of
g
is arbitrary we obtain that
\Sigma
does not admit a proper extension, thus showing (

). ∎

Thus, as a consequence of the Hopf–Rinow theorem and Proposition
3.4
, one obtains the following corollary which generalizes a result by Hermann
[
28
]
to the case of non-complete ambient manifolds.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
M
be a connected real analytic Riemannian manifold,
p\in M
and
V
a totally geodesic subspace of
T_{p}M
.
If
f\colon\Sigma\to M
is the inextendable compatible totally geodesic immersion associated with the subspace
V
, then
\Sigma
is complete if and only if the exponential map
\exp_{p}
is defined on all
V
.

3.1.
Maximal totally geodesic submanifolds in analytic Riemannian manifolds

We now concern ourselves with defining a notion of maximality for totally geodesic submanifolds.
Indeed, if
M
is a real analytic Riemannian manifold and
\Sigma_{1}
,
\Sigma_{2}\subseteq M
are two inextendable and embedded totally geodesic submanifolds, one can wonder if
\Sigma_{1}\subseteq\Sigma_{2}
.
In this case, it is easy to see that

if and only if there exists a point

such that

.
Therefore, the study of inclusions between embedded totally geodesic submanifolds of

.
For general totally geodesic immersions, the situation is more involved, and one needs to introduce the following “pullback-type” construction to make sense of the inclusion relationship.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let

be a connected real analytic Riemannian manifold,

and

For each

, consider the inextendable compatible totally geodesic immersion

be the unique point such that

.

(i)

.

(ii)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{2}}

Now, suppose (

Motivated by the previous proposition, we say that an inextendable compatible totally geodesic immersion

(or simply,

•

is maximal.

•

For all

,

is a maximal totally geodesic subspace of

.

•

.

4.
Totally geodesic submanifolds in naturally reductive homogeneous spaces

In this section, we introduce new techniques for studying totally geodesic submanifolds in naturally reductive homogeneous spaces.
Because homogeneous spaces are complete and real analytic, by Corollary
3.5
their inextendable totally geodesic submanifolds are complete.
These submanifolds are also homogeneous as Riemannian manifolds as a consequence of
[
36
, Theorem 8.9]
, but they need not be extrinsically homogeneous.

We denote by
M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
a naturally reductive homogeneous space endowed with a reductive decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
.
Since
M
is homogeneous, we may only consider totally geodesic submanifolds passing through
o=e\mathsf{K}
, which is equivalent to studying totally geodesic subspaces of

.
In this setting, we have the following characterization of these subspaces due to Tojo:

Theorem 4.1

(Tojo’s criterion
[
51
]
)
.

Let

be a naturally reductive homogeneous space with reductive decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
.
Assume

is a vector subspace and consider for each

the operator

Then, the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

of

\mathfrak{v}
.

(ii)

(iii)

For each

, the subspace

is
R

We now give a geometric interpretation of the subspace

Consider the geodesic

Then there are two vector space isomorphisms that we can establish between

Both maps are related by

(5)

Suppose that

4.1.
Totally geodesic submanifolds invariant under
D

We now study a particular class of totally geodesic submanifolds of
M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
.
Consider the canonical connection
\nabla^{c}
associated with the reductive decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
and the difference tensor

.
We say that an immersion

is

-

(and

.
It is immediate that a

-invariant submanifold is totally geodesic if and only if for every
X
,

the covariant derivative

\Sigma
.

These submanifolds are related to certain subalgebras of

.
We say that a Lie subalgebra

is

if it splits with respect to the reductive decomposition, that is,

\mathfrak{s}=(\mathfrak{s}\cap\mathfrak{k})\oplus(\mathfrak{s}\cap\mathfrak{p})=\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{k}}\oplus\mathfrak{s}_{\mathfrak{p}}.

The following theorem gives an algebraic characterization of
D
-invariant totally geodesic submanifolds passing through the origin.
Furthermore, it gives an explicit method to construct them from their tangent space at
o
.
The proof can be obtained by combining the theorem in
[
46
, p. 11]
and the first result in
[
29
, §2]

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

be a naturally reductive homogeneous space with reductive decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
a vector subspace.

(i)

The subspace

and

(ii)

Corollary 4.4
.

Every complete
D
-invariant totally geodesic submanifold of a naturally reductive homogeneous space
M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{K}
is extrinsically homogeneous with respect to the given presentation of

, and thus an injectively immersed submanifold.

Remark 4.5
.

Theorem
4.3
is also a refinement of
[
43
, Lemma 3.1]
, which states that for a general reductive homogeneous space a subspace

and

is tangent to a complete totally geodesic submanifold. Also, notice that the class of

-invariant totally geodesic submanifolds includes all totally geodesic submanifolds of symmetric spaces, since in a symmetric space

.
Thus, in the symmetric setting the subspaces

appearing in the previous theorem are the Lie triple systems (see
[
6
, §11.1]
).

Remark 4.6
.

The case

in Theorem

is part of a result by Kostant
[
37
]
, which implies in particular that the connected (normal) subgroup with Lie algebra

acts transitively on

.

Remark 4.7
.

It is worth noting that in the irreducible setting the conditions of Theorem

do not depend on the naturally reductive decomposition that we choose.
Indeed, from
[
42

, we see that if

is a simply connected irreducible naturally reductive space which is not symmetric, then the canonical connection

is unique.
Therefore, given any naturally reductive decomposition

of

that are invariant under

Similarly, if

4.2.
Totally geodesic surfaces

As an application of Corollary

Let
\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
be a
2

4.3.
Well-positioned totally geodesic submanifolds and homogeneous fibrations

We now study the case that
M=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{H}
is also the total space of the homogeneous fibration induced by the inclusions
\mathsf{H}\subseteq\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathsf{G}
(observe the change of notation).
Let

be the base space and

be the fiber of the given submersion.
Consider a totally geodesic immersion

.
We say that

is

(with respect to the fibration

Furthermore,
\Sigma
is said to be

if it is well-positioned at every point

.
The next result allows us to give an algebraic characterization for a totally geodesic submanifold to be well-positioned.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let

be the homogeneous fibration induced by the chain of inclusions
\mathsf{H}\subseteq\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathsf{G}

\mathsf{H}
,
\mathsf{K}
and

are compact and the Riemannian metrics on

and

are induced by a bi-invariant metric on
\mathsf{G}
.
Let

be a complete totally geodesic immersion passing through the point

.
Then, the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\Sigma
is well-positioned with respect to the submersion

,

(ii)

for all
X\in\mathfrak{v}
, the subspace

splits with respect to the decomposition
\mathfrak{p}=\mathcal{V}_{o}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{o}
.

Proof.

Let

be such that
\widetilde{f}(p)=\mathfrak{v}
.
Since we are assuming that
\Sigma
is connected and complete, every point of

is of the form

for a certain

.
Consider the geodesic

(where

), which connects

and

.
Then, we have

.
As

and

and

Therefore, using (

.
As

Corollary 4.10
.

Let

. ∎

5.
The examples

In this section we will describe the totally geodesic submanifolds of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
,
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
and
\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
that appear in the classification and determine their isometry type.
We indicate if the examples are well-positioned with respect to the homogeneous fibrations given in Subsection
2.2
.

Let us recall some definitions about special submanifolds of almost Hermitian manifolds. If
(M^{2n},J)
is an almost Hermitian manifold and
f\colon\Sigma\to M
is an immersion, we say that
f
(and
\Sigma
) is
totally real
if for all
p\in\Sigma
the subspaces
f_{*p}(T_{p}\Sigma)
and
Jf_{*p}(T_{p}\Sigma)
of
T_{f(p)}M
are orthogonal.
If we also have
T_{p}M=f_{*p}(T_{p}\Sigma)\oplus Jf_{*p}(T_{p}\Sigma)
(that is, if
\dim\Sigma=n
), then
\Sigma
is a
Lagrangian
submanifold.
Separately,
f
(and
\Sigma
) is
almost complex
(or
J
-holomorphic
) if
f_{*p}(T_{p}\Sigma)
is invariant under
J
for all
p\in\Sigma
.
Furthermore, if
\Sigma
is a surface, we will refer to it as an
almost complex surface
or a
J
-holomorphic curve
.

Remark 5.1
.

Many of the totally geodesic submanifolds that appear in this section are isometric to a sphere with a round or complex Berger metric.
We can compute the radius
r
of the sphere
\mathsf{S}^{n}(r)
, as well as the parameters of the Berger sphere
\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},\tau}(r)
from its sectional curvature.
Indeed, it is well known that the sectional curvature of
\mathsf{S}^{n}(r)
is equal to
1/r^{2}
.
In the case of
\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},\tau}(r)
, the parameters
r
and
\tau
can be obtained from the equations
\tau=r^{2}\sec(U,X)
and

4-3\tau=r^{2}\sec(X,Y)
,
where
U
is a vertical vector and
X
,
Y
are horizontal vectors with respect to the Hopf fibration (see
[
21
]
).

5.1.
The complex projective space

We describe the totally geodesic examples of the nearly Kähler complex projective space
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
.

5.1.1.
The real projective space
[
3
, Example 3.9]

Consider the subgroup
\mathsf{U}(2)^{j}\subseteq\mathsf{Sp}(2)
whose Lie algebra is given by

\mathfrak{u}(2)^{j}=\operatorname{span}\bigl\{jE_{11},jE_{22},E_{21}-E_{12},j(E_{12}+E_{21})\bigr\}=(\mathfrak{u}(2)^{j}\cap\mathfrak{k})\oplus(\mathfrak{u}(2)^{j}\cap\mathfrak{p}).

Then,
\mathfrak{u}(2)^{j}
is canonically embedded in
\mathfrak{sp}(2)
, so the orbit
\mathsf{U}(2)^{j}\cdot o
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
whose tangent space is
\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{3},e_{5}\}
.
The isotropy subgroup
\mathsf{U}(2)^{j}\cdot o
is equal to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathsf{U}(1)
, so
\mathsf{U}(2)^{j}\cdot o
is diffeomorphic to a real projective space
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}
.
The induced metric is Berger-like.
Indeed, this totally geodesic submanifold is isometric to
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/2}(2)
.
Let us write
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/2}(2)}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{3},e_{5}\}
.
A computation gives
J(\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/2}(2)})=\mathfrak{p}\ominus\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/2}(2)}
, and since
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/2}(2)
is extrinsically homogeneous we see that
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/2}(2)
is a Lagrangian submanifold.
Finally, note that
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/2}(2)
is well-positioned at
o
, so by Corollary
4.10
, it is well-positioned.

5.1.2.
The fiber of the twistor fibration

Recall that the fibers of the twistor fibration are totally geodesic surfaces in
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
.
In particular, the orbit through
o
is
\left(\mathsf{Sp}(1)\times\mathsf{Sp}(1)\right)\cdot o=\mathsf{Sp}(1)_{f}\cdot o
, where
\mathsf{Sp}(1)_{f}
denotes the image of the standard embedding of
\mathsf{Sp}(1)
in
\mathsf{Sp}(2)
in the first block.
The isotropy subgroup
(\mathsf{Sp}(1)_{f})_{o}
coincides with
\mathsf{U}(1)
, so
\mathsf{Sp}(1)_{f}\cdot o
is diffeomorphic to a sphere.
Its tangent space at
o
is
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{Sp}(1)_{f}\cdot o}=\mathfrak{p}_{1}
.
The sectional curvature in this case is
\operatorname{sec}(\mathfrak{p}_{1})=2
, so
\mathsf{Sp}(1)_{f}\cdot o
is a round sphere of radius
1/\sqrt{2}
.
Furthermore, the fact that
J(\mathfrak{p}_{1})=\mathfrak{p}_{1}
implies that
\mathsf{Sp}(1)_{f}\cdot o
is an almost complex surface in
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
.
By definition,
\mathsf{Sp}(1)_{f}\cdot o
is well-positioned.

5.1.3.
The horizontal sphere
\mathsf{SU}(2)\cdot o

Consider the standard embedding of
\mathsf{SU}(2)
in
\mathsf{Sp}(2)
.
Since the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
is given by

\mathfrak{su}(2)=\operatorname{span}\bigl\{i(E_{11}-E_{22}),E_{21}-E_{12},i(E_{12}+E_{21})\bigr\}=(\mathfrak{su}(2)\cap\mathfrak{k})\oplus(\mathfrak{su}(2)\cap\mathfrak{p}),

it follows that
\mathfrak{su}(2)
is canonically embedded, and the orbit
\mathsf{SU}(2)\cdot o
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
with tangent space
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{SU}(2)\cdot o}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{3},e_{4}\}
.
The isotropy subgroup of
\mathsf{SU}(2)
at
o
is the canonical
\mathsf{U}(1)
, so
\mathsf{SU}(2)\cdot o
is diffeomorphic to a sphere.
Furthermore, its sectional curvature is given by
\operatorname{sec}(\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{SU}(2)\cdot o})=1
, so this submanifold is a round sphere of radius
1
.
Finally, note that
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{SU}(2)\cdot o}
is
J
-invariant, so this sphere is also an almost complex surface in
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
.
Note that
\mathsf{SU}(2)\cdot o
is well-positioned by Corollary
4.10
.
Indeed, its tangent space at every point is always contained in the horizontal subspace of the twistor fibration.

5.1.4.
The sphere
\mathsf{SU}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}\cdot o

Consider the unique complex irreducible representation
\Lambda_{3}
of
\mathsf{SU}(2)
of dimension four.
Since this representation is unitary and of symplectic type, it restricts to a homomorphism
\mathsf{SU}(2)\to\mathsf{Sp}(2)
.
To get an explicit description of this map at the Lie algebra level (which is enough for our purposes), it suffices to see that the linear map
\mathfrak{su}(2)\to\mathfrak{sp}(2)
defined via

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\mapsto i(E_{11}+3E_{22}),

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\mapsto\sqrt{3}(E_{21}-E_{12})+2jE_{11},

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle\mapsto-2kE_{11}-i\sqrt{3}(E_{12}+E_{21}),

is a Lie algebra homomorphism which is also irreducible as a representation, so by uniqueness it must be equal to
\Lambda_{3}
.

We denote by
\mathsf{SU}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}
the image of the previous homomorphism.
The Lie algebra of this group satisfies

\mathfrak{su}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}=\operatorname{span}\Bigl\{i(E_{11}+3E_{22}),\sqrt{2}e_{1}+\sqrt{3}e_{3},\sqrt{2}e_{2}+\sqrt{3}e_{4}\Bigr\}=(\mathfrak{su}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}\cap\mathfrak{k})\oplus(\mathfrak{su}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}\cap\mathfrak{p}),

so it is canonically embedded in
\mathfrak{sp}(2)
.
As a consequence, the orbit
\mathsf{SU}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}\cdot o
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
with tangent space
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{SU}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}\cdot o}=\operatorname{span}\left\{\sqrt{2}e_{1}+\sqrt{3}e_{3},\sqrt{2}e_{2}+\sqrt{3}e_{4}\right\}
.
The isotropy subgroup at
o
is the
\mathsf{U}(1)
subgroup with Lie algebra generated by
\operatorname{diag}(i,3i)
, so this orbit is actually a sphere.
Since the sectional curvature of
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{SU}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}\cdot o}
is
1/5
, we see that
\mathsf{SU}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}\cdot o
is a sphere of radius
\sqrt{5}
.
One sees that
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{SU}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}\cdot o}
is
J
-invariant, and by homogeneity it follows that
\mathsf{SU}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}\cdot o
is an almost complex submanifold of
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
.
Clearly,
\mathsf{SU}(2)_{\Lambda_{3}}\cdot o
is not well-positioned at
o
, so it is not well-positioned.

5.2.
The flag manifold

We describe the totally geodesic examples of the nearly Kähler flag manifold
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
.

5.2.1.
The real flag manifold
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

[
50
, Example 3.1]

There is a natural embedding of the real flag manifold
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
in
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
which is induced by the usual inclusion of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
.
This submanifold can also be seen as the orbit
\mathsf{SO}(3)\cdot o
of the standard
\mathsf{SO}(3)\subseteq\mathsf{SU}(3)
, and the corresponding isotropy subgroup is
\mathsf{SO}(3)_{o}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, so we get
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})=\mathsf{SO}(3)/(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2})=\mathsf{Sp}(1)/\mathsf{Q}_{8}
, where
\mathsf{Q}_{8}=\{\pm 1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}
.
Observe that
\mathfrak{so}(3)
is canonically embedded in
\mathfrak{su}(3)
, since
\mathfrak{so}(3)\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
. Thus, Theorem
4.3
allows us to conclude that
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is totally geodesic in
M
, and its tangent space is precisely
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\mathfrak{so}(3)=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{3},e_{5}\}
.
A direct computation shows that
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
has constant curvature equal to
1/8
.
Furthermore, we have the equality
J(\mathfrak{so}(3))=\operatorname{span}\{e_{2},e_{4},e_{6}\}
, implying that the inclusion
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\subseteq\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
is Lagrangian.
Finally, note that
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is well-positioned at
o
, so
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is well-positioned by Corollary
4.10
.

5.2.2.
The Berger sphere
[
50
, Example 3.2]

Let
\mathsf{SU}(2)_{(1,0,1)}
denote the subgroup of
\mathsf{SU}(3)
that fixes
(1,0,1)\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
.
This subgroup is conjugate to the standard
\mathsf{SU}(2)
inside
\mathsf{SU}(3)
.
The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)_{(1,0,1)}
is the set of all
X\in\mathfrak{su}(3)
such that
X(1,0,1)=0
, and its projection to
\mathfrak{p}
is
spanned by
\{e_{1}+e_{3},e_{2}-e_{4},e_{6}\}
.
It is easy to check that the isotropy subgroup of
\mathsf{SU}(2)_{(1,0,1)}
at
o
is trivial, so the corresponding orbit
\mathsf{SU}(2)_{(1,0,1)}\cdot o
is diffeomorphic to a
3
-sphere.
A direct application of (
2
) yields that
\mathsf{SU}(2)_{(1,0,1)}\cdot o
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
isometric to
\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/4}(\sqrt{2})
and whose tangent space is given by
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/4}(\sqrt{2})}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1}+e_{3},e_{2}-e_{4},e_{6}\}
.
The subspace
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/4}(\sqrt{2})}
is also invariant under
D
.
However, the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)_{(1,0,1)}
is not canonically embedded in
\mathfrak{su}(3)
.
In this case, the connected subgroup given by Theorem
4.3
is actually the subgroup
\mathsf{U}(2)_{(1,0,1)}
that fixes the complex line generated by
(1,0,1)
.
A direct calculation shows that
J(\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/4}(\sqrt{2})})=\mathfrak{p}\ominus\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/4}(\sqrt{2})}
, so the Berger sphere is Lagrangian.
Note from the expression of
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/4}(\sqrt{2})}
that
\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/4}(\sqrt{2})
is not well-positioned.

5.2.3.
The torus
[
15
, Example 3.3]

Consider the maximal torus
\mathsf{H}\subseteq\mathsf{SU}(3)
whose Lie algebra is given by
\mathfrak{h}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1}+e_{3}+e_{5},e_{2}+e_{4}-e_{6}\}
.
Observe that
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
, so it is a canonically embedded subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, and the orbit
\mathsf{H}\cdot o
is a totally geodesic surface.
Since
\mathsf{H}\cdot o
is a quotient of
\mathsf{H}
by a finite group, it is a compact abelian Lie group itself and hence diffeomorphic to a torus.
However, we see that
\mathsf{H}\cdot o
is not isometric to the standard flat torus.
Indeed, in order to determine the isometry type of
\mathsf{H}\cdot o
, we compute the preimage
\exp_{o}^{-1}(o)
.
For this, we need a description of the Riemannian exponential map
\exp_{o}\colon\mathfrak{h}\to\mathsf{H}\cdot o\subseteq M
, which is merely the restriction of the Riemannian exponential map of
M
.
To this end, we define the following orthonormal vectors
X=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(e_{1}+e_{3}+e_{5})
,
Y=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(e_{2}+e_{4}-e_{6})
of
\mathfrak{h}
.
Then the exponential map of
\mathfrak{h}
satisfies
\exp_{o}(uX+vY)=e^{uX}e^{vY}\cdot o
, and this element is equal to
o
if and only if
e^{uX}e^{vY}
is a diagonal matrix.
The solutions to
\exp_{o}(uX+vY)=o
are given by the lattice
\Lambda=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\left\{\left(\sqrt{2}\pi,\sqrt{2}\pi/\sqrt{3}\right),\left(0,2\sqrt{2}\pi/\sqrt{3}\right)\right\}
.
Since
\exp_{o}
is
\mathfrak{h}
-equivariant, in the sense that it satisfies the equation

\exp_{o}(T+S)=\operatorname{Exp}(T)\cdot\exp_{o}(S),

it follows that
\exp_{o}
is actually a Riemannian covering map, so
\mathsf{H}\cdot o
is isometric to the quotient
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\Lambda
.
We will refer to this orbit as
\mathsf{T}^{2}_{\Lambda}=\mathsf{H}\cdot o=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\Lambda
.
Note that
\mathsf{H}\cdot o
is not a product
\mathsf{S}^{1}(r_{1})\times\mathsf{S}^{1}(r_{2})
, since the closest points in
\Lambda\setminus\{(0,0)\}
to the origin are those in

\left\{\left(\pm\sqrt{2}\pi,\pm\sqrt{6}\pi/3\right),\left(\pm\sqrt{2}\pi,\mp\sqrt{6}\pi/3\right),\left(0,\pm 2\sqrt{6}\pi/3\right)\right\},

and thus there exist three different closed geodesics of minimum length passing through
o
, as opposed to two in the case of
\mathsf{S}^{1}(r)\times\mathsf{S}^{1}(r)
or one in the case of
\mathsf{S}^{1}(r_{1})\times\mathsf{S}^{1}(r_{2})
with
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
.
Let
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{T}^{2}_{\Lambda}}=\mathfrak{h}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1}+e_{3}+e_{5},e_{2}+e_{4}-e_{6}\}

be the tangent space of this surface, then
J(\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{T}^{2}_{\Lambda}})=\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{T}^{2}_{\Lambda}}
, and by homogeneity it follows that
\mathsf{T}^{2}_{\Lambda}
is an almost complex surface in
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
.
However, it is clear from the expression of
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{T}^{2}_{\Lambda}}
that
\mathsf{T}^{2}_{\Lambda}
is not well-positioned.

5.2.4.
The fiber of the submersion
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})\to\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{2}

[
15
, Example 3.1]

Recall that the fibers of the Riemannian submersion
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})\to\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{2}
are totally geodesic.
The fiber through
o
is
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{1}=\mathsf{U}(2)\cdot o=\mathsf{SU}(2)\cdot o
, where the isotropy subgroup of
\mathsf{SU}(2)
at
o
is
\mathsf{U}(1)
.
The tangent space, as said before, is
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{1}}=\mathfrak{p}_{1}
.
Since the sectional curvature of
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
is
2
, it follows that
\mathsf{SU}(2)\cdot o
is isometric to the round sphere of radius
1/\sqrt{2}
.
Furthermore,
J(\mathfrak{p}_{1})=\mathfrak{p}_{1}
, so
\mathsf{SU}(2)\cdot o
is an almost complex surface in
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
.
Clearly,
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{1}
is well-positioned as it is a fiber itself.

5.2.5.
The sphere
[
15
, Example 3.2]

Consider
E=\operatorname{span}\{(0,1,0),(1,0,-1),(i,0,i)\}
, which is a real form of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, and let
\sigma\colon\mathbb{C}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}^{3}
be the associated real structure.
Then the normalizer

\mathsf{SO}(3)^{\sigma}=\{g\in\mathsf{SU}(3)\colon g(E)=E\}=\{g\in\mathsf{SU}(3)\colon g\sigma=\sigma g\}

is a subgroup of
\mathsf{SU}(3)
conjugate to the standard
\mathsf{SO}(3)
.
The corresponding Lie algebra is given by
\mathfrak{so}(3)^{\sigma}=\operatorname{span}\{\operatorname{diag}(i,0,-i),e_{1}+e_{3},e_{2}+e_{4}\}
, and in particular it is canonically embedded in
\mathfrak{su}(3)
.
One sees that the isotropy subgroup
\mathsf{SO}(3)^{\sigma}_{o}
is the
\mathsf{U}(1)
subgroup generated by
\mathfrak{so}(3)^{\sigma}\cap\mathfrak{k}
, so we obtain that
\mathsf{SO}(3)^{\sigma}\cdot o
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
that is diffeomorphic to a sphere.
Its tangent space at
o
is

\mathfrak{p}_{\mathfrak{so}(3)^{\sigma}}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1}+e_{3},e_{2}+e_{4}\},

and this plane has sectional curvature
1/2
, so
\mathsf{SO}(3)^{\sigma}\cdot o
is isometric to a two-dimensional sphere of radius
\sqrt{2}
.
The equality
J(\mathfrak{p}_{\mathfrak{so}(3)^{\sigma}})=\mathfrak{p}_{\mathfrak{so}(3)^{\sigma}}
implies that
\mathsf{SO}(3)^{\sigma}\cdot o
is an almost complex surface in
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C})^{3}
.
Since
\mathsf{SO}(3)^{\sigma}\cdot o
is not well-positioned at
o
, it is not well-positioned.

5.2.6.
Real projective planes inside
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

Recall that
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is a Lagrangian submanifold with constant sectional curvature.
In particular, every
2
-plane inside
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}
will give rise to a totally geodesic surface inside
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
(hence inside
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
). We describe these examples.

As we saw earlier,
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
can be regarded as the Lie group quotient
\mathsf{Sp}(1)/\mathsf{Q}_{8}
with a metric of constant curvature equal to
1/8
.
As a consequence,
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is isometric to the quotient
\mathsf{S}^{3}(2\sqrt{2})/\mathsf{Q}_{8}
, and the projection map
\pi\colon\mathsf{S}^{3}(2\sqrt{2})\to\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is a Riemannian covering map.
This projection is equivariant with respect to the double cover
\mathsf{Sp}(1)\to\mathsf{SO}(3)
.

We view
\mathbb{H}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
Consider the totally geodesic embedding
h\colon\mathsf{S}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})\hookrightarrow\mathsf{S}^{3}(2\sqrt{2})
defined by
h(x,y,z)=xi+yj+zk
.
Then
\pi\circ h
is also a totally geodesic immersion of the
2
-sphere satisfying
\pi\circ h(-x,-y,-z)=\pi\circ h(x,y,z)
for all
(x,y,z)\in\mathsf{S}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})
, so it factors through an isometric immersion
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})\to\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
defined via

(6)

\phi([x:y:z])=(xi+yj+zk)\mathsf{Q}_{8},\quad(x,y,z)\in\mathsf{S}^{2}(2\sqrt{2}).

As the projection
\mathsf{S}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})\to\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})
is also a covering map, we deduce that
\phi
is a totally geodesic immersion.
Note that
\phi
is not injective, as the points
[2\sqrt{2}:0:0]
,
[0:2\sqrt{2}:0]
and
[0:0:2\sqrt{2}]
have the same image.

Proposition 5.2
.

The map
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})\to\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
defined by (
6
) is a non-injective inextendable compatible totally geodesic immersion.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})
is complete, we only need to show that
\phi
is compatible.
This is equivalent to proving the following: for every pair of different points
p=[x:y:z]
and
q=[x^{\prime}:y^{\prime}:z^{\prime}]\in\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})
such that
\phi(p)=\phi(q)
, the images
\phi_{*p}(T_{p}\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2}))
and
\phi_{*q}(T_{q}\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2}))
are different subspaces of
T_{\phi(p)}\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Let
p=[x:y:z]
and
q=[x^{\prime}:y^{\prime}:z^{\prime}]
be as above.
Then
\phi(p)=\phi(q)
implies that there is an element
\lambda\in\mathsf{Q}_{8}
such that
x^{\prime}i+y^{\prime}j+z^{\prime}k=(xi+yj+zk)\lambda
.
Changing the sign of the homogeneous coordinates of
q
if necessary, we may assume that
\lambda\in\{i,j,k\}
.
We will deal with the case
\lambda=i
, as the other two cases can be treated in an analogous manner.
In this setting, we obtain that
x^{\prime}i+y^{\prime}j+z^{\prime}k=(xi+yj+zk)i=-x-yk+zj
, which yields
x=x^{\prime}=0
,
y^{\prime}=z
and
z^{\prime}=-y
, so
p=[0:y:z]
and
q=[0:z:-y]
.

Let us compute
\phi_{*p}(T_{p}\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2}))
.
On the one hand, we can identify the tangent space of
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})
at
p=[0:y:z]
with the tangent space
T_{(0,y,z)}\mathsf{S}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})\equiv\mathbb{R}(0,y,z)^{\perp}=\operatorname{span}\{(1,0,0),(0,-z,y)\}
.
Moreover, we can also view
T_{(yj+zk)\mathsf{Q}_{8}}\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
as
T_{(yj+zk)}\mathsf{S}^{3}(2\sqrt{2})\equiv\mathbb{R}(yj+zk)^{\perp}
.
Under these identifications,
\phi_{*p}(T_{p}\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2}))
is spanned by
\phi_{*p}(1,0,0)=i
, and
\phi_{*p}(0,-z,y)=-zj+yk
. We now determine
\phi_{*q}(T_{q}\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2}))
.
For this, we have identifications
T_{q}\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})\equiv\mathbb{R}(0,z,-y)^{\perp}=\operatorname{span}\{(1,0,0),(0,y,z)\}
and

We obtain that
\phi_{*q}(T_{q}\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2}))
is generated by
\phi_{*q}(1,0,0)=i
, and
\phi_{*q}(0,y,z)=yj+zk

In order to finish, observe that the composition of the isomorphisms

\mathbb{R}(yj+zk)^{\perp}\to T_{(yj+zk)\mathsf{Q}_{8}}\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})=T_{(zj-yk)\mathsf{Q}_{8}}\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\to\mathbb{R}(zj-yk)^{\perp}

is simply right multiplication by

\phi_{*p}(T_{p}\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2}))

\mathbb{R}(zj-yk)^{\perp}

.
Thus, we obtain that the images of
\phi_{*p}
and
\phi_{*q}
are different, and therefore
\phi
is a compatible immersion. ∎

The next lemma shows that, up to congruence,
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}(2\sqrt{2})
is the unique totally geodesic surface of
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let

be a compatible totally geodesic immersion of a complete two-dimensional Riemannian manifold.
Then
\psi
is congruent to
\phi
under an element of
\mathsf{SO}(3)
.

and

Proof.

Let

.
Since left multiplication by

\phi_{a}
is also a compatible totally geodesic immersion of
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{2}
congruent to
\phi
.
We show that all totally geodesic surfaces arise in this manner.

Let

Let

6.
Riemannian cones and totally geodesic submanifolds

In this section we start by recalling the definition and the basic properties of Riemannian cones.
After that we prove a structure result for totally geodesic submanifolds of Riemannian cones.
We refer the reader to
[
39
]
for a detailed account on semi-Riemannian cones.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold, which for our purposes is assumed to be real analytic and complete.
We define its
Riemannian cone
as the warped product
\widehat{M}=\mathbb{R}^{+}\times_{f}M
, where
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the identity map.
More explicitly, if
g=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the metric on
M
and
r\colon\widehat{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the projection on the first factor, the inner product on
\widehat{M}
is given by
\widehat{g}=\mathrm{d}r^{2}+r^{2}g
.

Every vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
(respectively,
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
) admits a natural extension to
\widehat{M}
, which we will also denote by
X
.
In particular, if
\partial_{r}
is the unit radial vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then its natural extension to
\widehat{M}
is called the
cone vector field
or the
radial vector field
.
Note that at every point
(\tau,p)
of
\widehat{M}
we have the orthogonal decomposition
T_{(\tau,p)}\widehat{M}=\mathbb{R}(\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)}\oplus T_{p}M
.
The Levi-Civita connection
\widehat{\nabla}
of
\widehat{M}
is characterized by the following equations for
X
,
Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
:

(8)

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}_{\partial_{r}}\partial_{r}={}

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}_{X}\partial_{r}={}

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}_{\partial_{r}}X=\frac{1}{r}X,

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}_{X}Y{}={}

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}Y-r\langle X,Y\rangle\partial_{r}.

As a consequence, the curvature tensor
\widehat{R}
is determined by the conditions

(9)

\displaystyle\widehat{R}(\partial_{r},\cdot)\cdot={}

\displaystyle\widehat{R}(\cdot,\partial_{r})\cdot=\widehat{R}(\cdot,\cdot)\partial_{r}=0,

\displaystyle\widehat{R}(u,v)w={}

\displaystyle R(u,v)w-\langle v,w\rangle u+\langle u,w\rangle v,

\displaystyle u,v,w\in T_{p}M.

Let
X=a\partial_{r}+v\in T_{(\tau,p)}\widehat{M}
be arbitrary.
From the equations above we see that the Jacobi operator associated with
X
satisfies

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{X}(b\partial_{r}+w)={}

\displaystyle\widehat{R}(b\partial_{r}+w,a\partial_{r}+v)(a\partial_{r}+v)=\widehat{R}(w,v)v={}R_{v}w-\lvert v\rvert^{2}w+\langle v,w\rangle v.

Although the process is more tedious, it is possible to compute the covariant derivatives of the curvature tensor from (
8
) and (
9
).
For instance, one can show that

(10)

(\widehat{\nabla}_{x}\widehat{R})(u,v,w)=(\nabla_{x}R)(u,v,w)-\langle x,\widehat{R}(u,v)w\rangle\tau\partial_{r}

for all
(\tau,p)\in\widehat{M}
and

,
u
,
v
,
w\in T_{p}M
.
We will make use of this formula later on.

Remark 6.1
.

Let

We can also describe the geodesics of

\left(-\frac{\tau}{a},\infty\right),&a>0,\\

Observe that
\widehat{M}
is never complete.
However, it is clear that it is an analytic Riemannian manifold. The following lemma shows that actually the only incomplete geodesics are those tangent to the cone vector field.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
M
be a complete Riemannian manifold,
(\tau,p)\in\widehat{M}
a point in its Riemannian cone and
w=a\partial_{r}+v\in T_{(\tau,p)}M
a unit vector, where
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\in T_{p}M
.
Consider the maximal geodesic
\widehat{\gamma}(t)
such that
\widehat{\gamma}(0)=(\tau,p)
and
\widehat{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)=w
.
The following statements hold:

(i)

If
v=0
, then the maximal interval of definition of
\widehat{\gamma}(t)
is precisely
I_{a}
.

(ii)

If
v\neq 0
, then
\widehat{\gamma}(t)
is defined on all
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Firstly, assume that
v=0
.
Without loss of generality, we may also suppose that
a
is positive, so
a=1
.
From (
11
), we see that the curve
\beta\colon(-\tau,\infty)\to\widehat{M}
defined by
\beta(t)=(t+\tau,p)
is a geodesic of
\widehat{M}
with initial conditions
\beta(0)=(\tau,p)
,
\beta^{\prime}(0)=\partial_{r}
.
Since
t+\tau
converges to zero as
t
converges to
-\tau
, the curve
\beta
is a maximal geodesic, thus proving (
i
).

Now, assume that
v\neq 0
, so that the curve
\beta\colon I_{a}\to\widehat{M}
,
\beta(t)=(\rho(t),\alpha(t))
defined by (
11
) is a geodesic of
\widehat{M}
with
\beta(0)=p
,
\beta^{\prime}(0)=w
.
Note that the derivative

\rho^{\prime}(t)=\frac{2a(at+\tau)+2\lvert v\rvert^{2}\tau^{2}t}{2\rho(t)}=\frac{a\tau+(a^{2}+\lvert v\rvert^{2}\tau^{2})t}{\sqrt{(at+\tau)^{2}+\lvert v\rvert^{2}\tau^{2}t^{2}}}=\frac{a\tau+t}{\sqrt{(at+\tau)^{2}+\lvert v\rvert^{2}\tau^{2}t^{2}}}

vanishes at
t_{0}=-a\tau\in I_{a}
(this last inclusion holds because
\lvert a\rvert\in(0,1)
and
\tau>0
).
As a consequence,
\beta^{\prime}(t_{0})=\alpha^{\prime}(t_{0})\in T_{\alpha(t_{0})}M
.
Looking at (
12
), we obtain that the geodesic
\widehat{\exp}_{\beta(t_{0})}(\beta^{\prime}(t_{0}))
is defined on all of
\mathbb{R}
, and by uniqueness it must coincide with the curve
\beta(t+t_{0})
.
Thus,
\beta
can be extended to all
\mathbb{R}
, so (
ii
) holds.∎

Suppose that
f\colon M\to N
is a smooth map.
We define its associated
cone map
as the map
\widehat{f}\colon\widehat{M}\to\widehat{N}
given by
\widehat{f}(\tau,p)=(\tau,f(p))
.
It can be easily checked that
\widehat{f}
is an isometric immersion (respectively, an isometry) if and only if
f
is an isometric immersion (respectively, an isometry). In the following, we provide some information about the isometry group of Riemannian cones.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
M
and
N
be two complete Riemannian manifolds. Every isometry
f\colon\widehat{M}\to\widehat{N}
is the cone map of an isometry
g\colon M\to N
. In particular, if the cones
\widehat{M}
and
\widehat{N}
are isometric, then
M
and
N
are also isometric.

Proof.

We take the subset
\mathcal{C}_{(\tau,p)}=\{X\in T_{(\tau,p)}\widehat{M}\colon\widehat{\exp}_{(\tau,p)}(tX)\text{ is defined on }\mathbb{R}\}
of
T_{(\tau,p)}\widehat{M}
for each
(\tau,p)\in\widehat{M}
.
It is clear from Lemma
6.2
that
\mathcal{C}_{(\tau,p)}=T_{(\tau,p)}\widehat{M}\setminus\mathbb{R}(\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)}
.

Now, let
f\colon\widehat{M}\to\widehat{N}
be an isometry and fix
(\tau,p)\in\widehat{M}
with image
(s,q)=f(\tau,p)
.
Since
f
is an isometry, it sends
\mathcal{C}_{(\tau,p)}
to
\mathcal{C}_{(s,q)}
, and thus we have
f_{*(\tau,p)}((\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)})=\pm(\partial_{r})_{(s,q)}
.
The equality
f_{*(\tau,p)}((\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)})=~-(\partial_{r})_{(s,q)}
is not possible, because in that case the maximal geodesic
\gamma(t)=\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}_{(\tau,p)}(t\partial_{r})
would be mapped to the maximal geodesic
\beta(t)=\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}_{(s,q)}(-t\partial_{r})
, which is not possible because the first geodesic is defined for all
t\geq 0
, while the second one is not.
We deduce that
f_{*(\tau,p)}((\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)})=~(\partial_{r})_{f(\tau,p)}
.
In addition, the maximal geodesic
\gamma(t)=\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}_{(\tau,p)}(t\partial_{r})
is mapped to the maximal geodesic
\beta(t)=~\widehat{\operatorname{exp}}_{(s,q)}(t\partial_{r})
, and their corresponding intervals of definition are
(-\tau,0)
and
(-s,0)
.
Hence,
\tau=s
.
All in all, we have seen that
f
sends the link
\{\tau\}\times M
to
\{\tau\}\times N
for each
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, so
f
takes the form
f(\tau,p)=(\tau,h(\tau,p))
for a map
h\colon\widehat{M}\to N
.
Furthermore, at each
(\tau,p)\in\widehat{M}
we have

\displaystyle(\partial_{r})_{f(\tau,p)}={}

\displaystyle f_{*(\tau,p)}((\partial_{r})_{\tau,p})=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}(\tau+t,h(\tau+t,p))=(\partial_{r})_{f(\tau,p)}+h_{*(\tau,p)}((\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)}),

which means that
h_{*(\tau,p)}((\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)})=0
, so
h
does not depend on
\tau
.
In other words, there exists a map
g\colon M\to N
such that
h(\tau,p)=g(p)
for all
(\tau,p)\in\widehat{M}
.
The fact that
f
is an isometry readily implies that
g
is also an isometry. ∎

Corollary 6.4
.

For a complete Riemannian manifold
M
, the map
f\in I(M)\mapsto\widehat{f}\in I(\widehat{M})
is a Lie group isomorphism.

Many geometric properties of Riemannian manifolds can be translated into geometric properties of their cones.
For instance, as a consequence of (
9
),
M
is an Einstein manifold with
\operatorname{Ric}=(n-1)\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
if and only if
\widehat{M}
is Ricci-flat.
It turns out that nearly Kähler structures on six-dimensional manifolds are related to
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-structures on their cones.
We briefly describe this relationship, see
[
5
]
for details.
For the general theory of
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-manifolds, we refer the reader to
[
31
, Chapter 10]
.

Let
M
be a six-dimensional strictly nearly Kähler manifold.
Then
M
is Einstein with positive Ricci curvature
[
23
, Theorem 5.2]
and after rescaling the metric we may assume that the Einstein constant of
M
is
\lambda=5
.
In that case, one defines a three-form
\phi\in\Omega^{3}(\widehat{M})
via the equations (for
X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
)

\displaystyle\phi(X,Y,Z)={}

\displaystyle r^{3}\langle Y,(\nabla_{X}J)Z\rangle,

\displaystyle\phi(\partial_{r},X,Y)=

\displaystyle-\phi(X,\partial_{r},Y)=\phi(X,Y,\partial_{r})=r^{2}\langle X,JY\rangle,

and checks that
\phi
is a parallel three-form inducing a
\mathsf{G}_{2}
structure on
\widehat{M}
.
In addition, the restricted holonomy group of
\widehat{M}
is precisely
\mathsf{G}_{2}
whenever
M
is not locally isometric to
\mathsf{S}^{6}
.
Conversely, suppose that
\widehat{M}
is a
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-manifold whose structure is given by the parallel three-form
\phi
.
Then the almost complex structure
J
defined on
M
by

\langle X,JY\rangle=\phi(\partial_{r},X,Y)

is strictly nearly Kähler and
M
is an Einstein manifold with
\operatorname{Ric}=5\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Remark 6.5
.

Notice that the metrics of the nearly Kähler manifolds
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3},\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3}),\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
that we are considering have Einstein constants
5/2
,
5/2
and
5/3
, respectively. Therefore, one would have to rescale these metrics by
1/2
,
1/2
, and
1/3
to obtain the
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-cones over them. However, for our purposes this is not a problem, since the totally geodesic property is preserved under rescalings of the ambient manifold and, as we will see, the maximal totally geodesic submanifolds of these
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-cones are cones over the totally geodesic submanifold of a homogeneous nearly Kähler
6
-manifold.

There is also a relationship between submanifolds of
M
that have a nice interaction with
J
and calibrated cones inside

\widehat{M}
.
The notions of calibrated geometry were introduced in the seminal paper
[
26
]
by Harvey and Lawson.
We remind that a
calibration
on a Riemannian manifold
N
is a closed differential form
\omega\in\Omega^{k}(M)
satisfying

\omega(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\leq 1

whenever
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
are unit vectors in
TN
.
A
k
-dimensional oriented submanifold
S
of
N
is
calibrated
if the restriction of
\omega
to
S
is equal to the Riemannian volume form of
S
, and it follows that
S
is a minimal submanifold.
It can be shown that for the case of a
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-manifold
(N,\phi)
, both
\phi
and its Hodge dual
\star\phi
are calibrations (
[
26
, Theorem 1.4 and Theorem 1.16]
).
We say in this case that
S
is
associative
(respectively,
coassociative
) if it is calibrated with respect to
\phi
(respectively,
\star\phi
).
Coming back to the case that
N=\widehat{M}
is the cone of a six-dimensional strict nearly Kähler manifold, it is known that the cone of a
J
-holomorphic curve is an associative submanifold, whereas the cone of a Lagrangian submanifold is a coassociative submanifold.

6.1.
Totally geodesic submanifolds of Riemannian cones

We now discuss the relationship between the totally geodesic submanifolds of a Riemannian cone (over a complete real analytic manifold) and those of its base.
We are interested in determining the maximal totally geodesic submanifolds of the cone
\widehat{M}
over
M
.

This first result shows that every totally geodesic submanifold of the base induces a totally geodesic submanifold of the cone by means of the cone map.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold and
\phi\colon S\to M
an isometric immersion of a
k
-dimensional submanifold
S
. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

The immersion
\phi
is totally geodesic if and only if the cone map
\widehat{\phi}\colon\widehat{S}\to\widehat{M}
is totally geodesic.

(ii)

The totally geodesic immersion
\phi
is compatible if and only if the cone map
\widehat{\phi}\colon\widehat{S}\to\widehat{M}
is compatible.

Proof.

First of all, as being totally geodesic is a local property, we may suppose that
S\subseteq M
is embedded and
\phi
is the inclusion map.
As a consequence,
\widehat{S}=\mathbb{R}^{+}\times S
as a subset of
\widehat{M}
.

Firstly, assume that
S
is totally geodesic in
M
.
Given
(\tau,p)\in\widehat{S}
and
w=a\partial_{r}+v\in T_{(\tau,p)}\widehat{S}
(so
v\in T_{p}S
), we know by (
11
) that the geodesic
\widehat{\gamma}(t)=\widehat{\exp}_{(\tau,p)}(tw)
is of the form
\widehat{\gamma}(t)=(\rho(t),\beta(t))
, where
\beta(t)
is a pregeodesic of
M
such that
\beta^{\prime}(0)=v
.
Since
S
is totally geodesic, there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
\beta(t)\in S
for all
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, so
\widehat{\gamma}(t)\in\widehat{S}
for all
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
.
Therefore,
\widehat{S}
is totally geodesic in
\widehat{M}
.

Conversely, suppose that
\widehat{S}
is totally geodesic, and let
p\in S
,
v\in T_{p}S
.
The geodesic
\widehat{\gamma}(t)=\widehat{\exp}_{(1,p)}(tw)
is locally of the form
(\rho(t),\beta(t))
, where
\beta(t)=\exp_{p}(f(t)v)
for a diffeomorphism
f(t)
such that
f(0)=0
and
f^{\prime}(0)=1
.
Thus, as
\widehat{S}
is totally geodesic, there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
\widehat{\gamma}(t)\in\widehat{S}
for
\lvert t\rvert<\varepsilon
, which means that
\exp_{p}(f(t)v)\in S
for the same values of
t
.
As
f^{-1}
is continuous at
t=0
, it follows that there exists
\delta>0
such that
\exp_{p}(sv)\in S
for
\lvert s\rvert<\delta
.
We conclude that
S
is totally geodesic in
M
, proving (
i
).

Finally, observe that since
\widehat{\phi}_{*(\tau,p)}(T_{(\tau,p)}\widehat{S})=\mathbb{R}(\partial_{r})_{\phi(\tau,p)}\oplus\phi_{*p}(T_{p}S)
for all
(\tau,p)\in\widehat{S}
, it follows that the induced map of
\widehat{\phi}
is an injection of
\widehat{S}
to
\mathsf{G}_{k+1}(T\widehat{M})
if and only if the induced map of
\phi
is an injection of
S
to
\mathsf{G}_{k}(TM)
. This yields (
ii
).
∎

It was shown in
[
30
]
that certain types of Riemannian manifolds with special holonomy do not admit totally geodesic hypersurfaces.
We deduce that the same result holds for Sasakian–Einstein,
6
-dimensional nearly Kähler and nearly parallel
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-manifolds.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
M
be a complete Riemannian manifold with non-constant sectional curvature. Assume that
M
satisfies one of the following conditions:

(i)

M^{2n+1}
is Sasakian–Einstein,

(ii)

M^{6}
is a
6
-dimensional strictly nearly Kähler manifold, or

(iii)

M^{7}
is a nearly parallel
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-manifold.

Then,
M
does not admit a totally geodesic hypersurface.

Proof.

Observe that in all three cases
M
is an Einstein manifold with positive Ricci curvature.
As the (non)existence of totally geodesic hypersurfaces is a purely local question, we may suppose that
M
is simply connected.
Furthermore, as their existence is also independent of rescalings of the metric, we may also suppose that the Einstein constant of
M
is equal to
\dim M-1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a totally geodesic hypersurface of
M
. By Lemma
6.6
,
\widehat{\Sigma}
is a totally geodesic hypersurface of
\widehat{M}
. By Gallot’s theorem (see
[
20
]
),
\widehat{M}
is locally irreducible since
M
has non-constant sectional curvature. Moreover, by
[
5
]
, we know that:

(i)

If
M^{2n+1}
is a Sasakian–Einstein manifold, then the restricted holonomy of
\widehat{M}
is contained in
\mathsf{SU}(n+1)
.

(ii)

If
M^{6}
is a strictly nearly Kähler manifold, then the restricted holonomy of
\widehat{M}
is contained in
\mathsf{G}_{2}
.

(iii)

If
M^{7}
is a nearly parallel
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-manifold, then the restricted holonomy of
\widehat{M}
is contained in
\mathsf{Spin}(7)
.

Now, since
\widehat{M}
is Einstein (indeed Ricci-flat), by
[
30
, Theorem 4.3]
the restricted holonomy of the cone
\widehat{M}
is
\mathsf{SO}(T_{p}\widehat{M})
. This contradicts the fact that the holonomy of
\widehat{M}
is contained in one of three aforementioned groups, yielding the result.
∎

The following result is concerned with the extendability of cones over totally geodesic submanifolds of the base.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
M
be a complete real analytic Riemannian manifold and
\phi\colon S\to M
a compatible totally geodesic immersion of a
k
-dimensional complete submanifold
S
.
Then
\widehat{\phi}\colon\widehat{S}\to\widehat{M}
is an inextendable compatible totally geodesic immersion.

Proof.

We have already seen in Lemma
6.6
that
\widehat{\phi}\colon\widehat{S}\to\widehat{M}
is compatible, so we only need to show inextendability.
Let
(\tau,p)\in\widehat{S}
be arbitrary and
w=a(\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)}+v
be a nonzero tangent vector, where
v\in T_{p}M
.
We consider the
\widehat{S}
-geodesic
\gamma(t)=\exp_{(\tau,p)}(tw)
.
If
v\neq 0
, then
\gamma
is defined on all
\mathbb{R}
due to Lemma
6.2
, so
\widehat{\phi}\circ\gamma
is also globally defined.
Otherwise, we have
v=a(\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)}
, and Lemma
6.2
implies that
\gamma
is defined precisely on
I_{a}
.
Because
\widehat{\phi}_{*(\tau,p)}((\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)})=(\partial_{r})_{(\tau,\phi(p))}
, the maximal
\widehat{M}
-geodesic
\exp_{(\tau,\phi(p))}(t\widehat{\phi}_{*(\tau,p)}(w))
is also defined exactly on
I_{a}
, so it coincides with
\widehat{\phi}\circ\gamma
.
We conclude that
\widehat{\phi}
sends maximal geodesics of
\widehat{S}
to maximal geodesics of
\widehat{M}
, so it is inextendable by Proposition
3.4
. ∎

We can now prove the following characterization of totally geodesic submanifolds in cones:

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
M
be a connected
n
-dimensional complete real analytic Riemannian manifold and consider its Riemannian cone
\widehat{M}
.
Suppose
\Sigma
is a
k
-dimensional manifold with

,
(\tau,p)=f(x)

(i)

(\partial_{r})_{(\tau,p)}

.

•

•

and a surjective local isometry

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widehat{S}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widehat{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}

We work with the natural projection

.

Then the cone map

is the inextendable compatible totally geodesic immersion associated with

.
Because

to conclude.
∎

Corollary 6.10
.

Let
M
be an analytic Riemannian manifold and
\widehat{M}
its Riemannian cone.
If
\Sigma
is a maximal totally geodesic submanifold of
\widehat{M}
, then
\Sigma
is either a hypersurface of
\widehat{M}
or the cone over a maximal totally geodesic submanifold
S
of
M
.

Theorem
6.9
reduces the classification of (maximal) totally geodesic submanifolds of cones to that of totally geodesic submanifolds of the base manifold, and separately to that of totally geodesic hypersurfaces.
We note that these hypersurfaces may not arise as cones over totally geodesic hypersurfaces in the base space, as we will see in Example
6.13
and Example
6.15
.

Remark 6.11
.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold and suppose that
\Sigma
is a totally geodesic hypersurface of
M
that is not tangent to the cone vector field
\partial_{r}
.
We may assume without loss of generality that
\Sigma\subseteq\widehat{M}
is embedded, and we choose a point
(\tau,p)\in\Sigma
.
The tangent space
V=T_{(\tau,p)}\Sigma\subseteq T_{(\tau,p)}\widehat{M}
is a totally geodesic hyperplane satisfying
\partial_{r}\notin V
.
This means that there exists a unique (possibly zero) vector
\eta\in T_{p}M
such that
T_{(\tau,p)}\widehat{M}\ominus V=\mathbb{R}(\partial_{r}+\eta)
.
Let
X=a\partial_{r}+v\in V
be arbitrary, where
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\in V
.
Then, since
V
is
\widehat{R}_{X}
-invariant and
\widehat{R}_{X}
is symmetric, it follows that
\partial_{r}+\eta
is an eigenvector of
\widehat{R}_{X}
.
However, by (
9
) the image of
\widehat{R}_{X}
is contained in
T_{p}M
, and because
\partial_{r}+\eta
is not tangent to the link we must have
\widehat{R}_{X}(\partial_{r}+\eta)=0
, so
\widehat{R}(\eta,v)v=\widehat{R}(\partial_{r}+\eta,X)X=0
for all
X\in V
.
As the orthogonal projection of
V
onto
T_{p}M
is a linear isomorphism, we deduce that if
V=\mathbb{R}(\partial_{r}+\eta)^{\perp}
is a totally geodesic hyperplane, then
\widehat{R}(\eta,v)v=0
for all
v\in T_{p}M
.

Proposition 6.12
.

Let
M
be a space of constant sectional curvature
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, every totally geodesic submanifold of dimension
d\geq 2
of the Riemannian cone
\widehat{M}
is a cone over a totally geodesic submanifold
S
of
M
if and only if
\kappa\neq 1
.

Proof.

Due to Theorem
6.9
, we may focus only on totally geodesic hypersurfaces.
Let
M
be a connected complete Riemannian manifold of constant sectional curvature
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and dimension
n\geq 2
, and suppose that
\widehat{M}
admits a totally geodesic hypersurface
\Sigma
that is not tangent to the cone vector field.
By shrinking
\Sigma
if necessary, we can assume that
\Sigma\subseteq\widehat{M}
is embedded and every
(\tau,p)\in\Sigma
is such that
V=T_{(\tau,p)}\Sigma
does not contain
\partial_{r}
, so its orthogonal complement must be generated by a vector of the form
\partial_{r}+\eta
for a certain
\eta\in T_{p}M
.
Now, from Remark
6.11
and (
9
) we deduce that

0=\widehat{R}(\eta,v)v=(\kappa-1)(\lvert v\rvert^{2}\eta-\langle\eta,v\rangle v)

for all
v\in T_{p}M
, which means that either
\kappa=1
or
\eta=0
.
If
\kappa\neq 1
, we obtain that
V=T_{(\tau,p)}M
for all

, so
\Sigma
is an integral manifold of the distribution
\mathcal{D}=\partial_{r}^{\perp}
on
\widehat{M}
.
The maximal integral manifolds of
\mathcal{D}
are precisely the links
\{\tau\}\times M
for each

, so

is actually an open subset of a leaf

for a certain

.
However, the last equation in (

) shows that the leaves are never totally geodesic, so we arrive at a contradiction.
We conclude that if

, the inextendable totally geodesic hypersurfaces of

are precisely the cones over the complete totally geodesic hypersurfaces of

.

If

has constant sectional curvature equal to

, then

is flat by (

), so for every point

such that

and

.
In particular,

is not (contained in) a cone over a totally geodesic hypersurface of

if and only if

.
∎

Example 6.13
.

Let us assume that
M=\mathsf{S}^{n}(1)
.
Then
\widehat{M}
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
in such a way that the cones over the totally geodesic submanifolds of

are of the form

, where

In particular, any affine hyperplane

.

Proposition 6.14
.

Let
M
be either
\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},\tau}(r)
or
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},\tau}(r)
and let
\widehat{M}
denote the Riemannian cone over

. Then,
\widehat{M}
admits a totally geodesic hypersurface if and only if
\tau=r=1
.

Proof.

Let
M=\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},\tau}(r)
be a three-dimensional Berger sphere of radius
r
and deformation parameter
\tau
.
We show that
\widehat{M}
does not admit totally geodesic hypersurfaces unless
r=\tau=1
(that is,
M
is the unit round sphere).

We first establish some notation.
Recall that
\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},\tau}(r)=\mathsf{U}(2)/\mathsf{U}(1)
as a homogeneous space, and we have a reductive decomposition
\mathfrak{u}(2)=\mathfrak{u}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{p}
, where
\mathfrak{u}(1)=\mathbb{R}K
and
\mathfrak{p}=\operatorname{span}\{E,X,Y\}
for the matrices

K=iE_{11},\quad E=\frac{i}{r\sqrt{\tau}}E_{22},\quad X=\frac{1}{r}(E_{21}-E_{12}),\quad Y=\frac{i}{r}(E_{21}+E_{12}).

Furthermore, if
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product on
\mathfrak{p}
induced by the Berger metric on
M
, then
E
,
X
and
Y
are orthonormal vectors with respect to this metric.
In addition, the vertical and horizontal subspaces at
o=e\mathsf{U}(1)
with respect to the Hopf fibration are
\mathcal{V}_{o}=\mathbb{R}E
and
\mathcal{H}_{o}=\operatorname{span}\{X,Y\}
.

Let us suppose that
M\neq\mathsf{S}^{3}(1)
and
\Sigma
is a totally geodesic hypersurface of
\widehat{M}
.
We may assume that
\Sigma
is embedded in
M
.
Because
M
is homogeneous, Corollary
6.4
allows us to suppose that
\Sigma
passes through a point of the form
(t,o)
with tangent space
V\subseteq T_{(t,o)}\widehat{M}\equiv\mathbb{R}\partial_{r}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
.
As
M
does not admit totally geodesic hypersurfaces
[
43
, Theorem A]
, we have that
\partial_{r}\notin V
, so
V^{\perp}
must be spanned by a vector of the form
\partial_{r}+\eta
, where
\eta\in\mathfrak{p}
.
We may write
\eta=a_{1}E+a_{2}X+a_{3}Y
for some constants
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,
a_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
.
From Remark
6.11
we also know that
\widehat{R}(\eta,Z)Z=0
for all
Z\in\mathfrak{p}
.
A polarization argument shows that the previous condition is equivalent to
\widehat{R}(\eta,Z)W+\widehat{R}(\eta,W)Z=0
for all
Z
,
W\in\mathfrak{p}
.

Firstly, suppose that
\tau\neq r^{2}
.
Then the equations

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\widehat{R}(\eta,E)Y+\widehat{R}(\eta,Y)E=\left(1-\frac{\tau}{r^{2}}\right)(a_{3}E+a_{1}Y),

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\widehat{R}(\eta,E)X+\widehat{R}(\eta,X)E=\left(1-\frac{\tau}{r^{2}}\right)(a_{2}E+a_{1}X),

imply that
a_{1}=a_{2}=a_{3}=0
, so
\eta=0
and
V=\mathfrak{p}
.
However, from (
10
) and the fact that
\widehat{R}
is not identically zero on
\mathfrak{p}
we deduce that
\mathfrak{p}
is not a totally geodesic subspace, giving us a contradiction.

Secondly, suppose that
\tau=r^{2}
and
r\neq 1
.
Since
0=\widehat{R}(\eta,X)Y+\widehat{R}(\eta,Y)X=\frac{4(r^{2}-1)}{r^{2}}(a_{3}X+a_{2}Y)
, we obtain
a_{2}=a_{3}=0
.
As a consequence,
\eta\in\mathcal{V}_{o}
, and
V
contains the horizontal subspace
\mathcal{H}_{o}
.
Using (
10

\left(4-\frac{4}{r^{2}}\right)t\partial_{r}=(\widehat{\nabla}_{X}\widehat{R})(X,Y,Y)\in V
,
so
\partial_{r}\in V

All in all, we have shown that
\widehat{M}
does not admit totally geodesic hypersurfaces except in the case
M=\mathsf{S}^{3}(1)
.
Since the natural projection
\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},\tau}(r)\to\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},\tau}(r)
is a Riemannian covering map, the same result holds for the three-dimensional Berger projective space.
∎

In view of the latter results, one could think that totally geodesic hypersurfaces nowhere tangent to the radial vector can only appear if the base is a sphere of radius one.
However, the following construction shows that there are many nontrivial local examples of totally geodesic hypersurfaces satisfying this condition.

Example 6.15
.

Consider two smooth positive functions
p,q\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.
We consider the Riemannian manifold
M=(0,\pi/2)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
(endowed with Cartesian coordinates
x
,
y
,
z
) with the metric

g=dx^{2}+(\sin x)^{2}p(y,z)^{2}dy^{2}+(\sin x)^{2}q(y,z)^{2}dz^{2}.

We see that the metric on the cone
\widehat{M}=(0,\infty)\times(0,\pi/2)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
is now given by

\widehat{g}=dr^{2}+r^{2}dx^{2}+r^{2}(\sin x)^{2}p(y,z)^{2}dy^{2}+r^{2}(\sin x)^{2}q(y,z)^{2}dz^{2}

with respect to the product coordinates
(r,x,y,z)
.
A calculation shows that the hypersurface

\Sigma=\left\{\left(\frac{1}{\cos x},x,y,z\right)\colon(x,y,z)\in M\right\}\subseteq\widehat{M}

is totally geodesic in
\widehat{M}
.
Note that
\Sigma
does not arise as a cone over a totally geodesic hypersurface of
M
because the cone vector field is nowhere tangent to it.

The abundance of examples highlights the appropriateness of further exploring this class of totally geodesic hypersurfaces in cones.

7.1.
The complex projective space

Lemma 7.1
.

If
\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
is a totally geodesic subspace and
\mathfrak{v}
contains a vertical or a horizontal vector, then
\mathfrak{v}
is well-positioned.

Proof.

If
X\in\mathfrak{v}
is a unit vertical vector, we may assume by means of the isotropy representation that
X=e_{1}
.
Since the spectrum of the Jacobi operator
R_{e_{1}}\colon\mathfrak{p}\ominus\mathbb{R}e_{1}\to\mathfrak{p}\ominus\mathbb{R}e_{1}
consists of the eigenvalues
2
, with eigenspace
\mathbb{R}e_{2}
, and
1/8
, with eigenspace
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
, the claim follows from Remark
3.3
.
Similarly, if
X
is horizontal we may suppose that
X=e_{3}
, and in this case the eigenvalues of
R_{e_{3}}\colon\mathfrak{p}\ominus\mathbb{R}e_{3}\to\mathfrak{p}\ominus\mathbb{R}e_{3}
are
1/8
, with eigenspace
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
,
1
, with eigenspace
\mathbb{R}e_{4}
, and
5/8
, with eigenspace
\operatorname{span}\{e_{5},e_{6}\}
, so the result also holds in this case.
∎

Proposition 7.2
.

There are no dimension four totally geodesic submanifolds in
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
.

Proof.

Assume, on the contrary, that there exists a totally geodesic submanifold
\Sigma
of
M
of dimension four passing through
o
, and let
\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
be its corresponding totally geodesic subspace.
From Lemma
7.1
and by dimension reasons, we know that
\mathfrak{v}
is well-positioned.
We will distinguish three possibilities according to the dimension of
\mathfrak{v}\cap\mathfrak{p}_{1}
.

If
\mathfrak{v}\cap\mathfrak{p}_{1}=\mathfrak{p}_{1}
, then
\mathfrak{v}\cap\mathfrak{p}_{2}
is two-dimensional.
By using the isotropy representation if necessary, we may suppose that
e_{3}\in\mathfrak{v}
.
We can therefore consider a basis of
\mathfrak{v}
of the form
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},a_{4}e_{4}+a_{5}e_{5}+a_{6}e_{6}\}
.
In particular,
a_{6}e_{5}-a_{5}e_{6}
is orthogonal to
\mathfrak{v}
, and the equality

0=\langle R(e_{1},e_{2})(a_{4}e_{4}+a_{5}e_{5}+a_{6}e_{6}),a_{6}e_{5}-a_{5}e_{6}\rangle=\frac{3(a_{5}^{2}+a_{6}^{2})}{4}

yields
a_{5}=a_{6}=0
, so actually
\mathfrak{v}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\}
.
This is a contradiction due to the fact that
(\nabla_{e_{1}}R)(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})=-\frac{3}{4\sqrt{2}}e_{6}\notin\mathfrak{v}
,
so this case is not possible.

If
\mathfrak{v}\cap\mathfrak{p}_{1}
is one-dimensional (which forces
\dim\mathfrak{v}\cap\mathfrak{p}_{2}=3
), we may use the isotropy representation to assume that
\mathfrak{v}
contains
e_{1}
and
e_{3}
.
In particular,
e_{5}=4\sqrt{2}(\nabla_{e_{3}}R)(e_{3},e_{1},e_{3})
also belongs to
\mathfrak{v}
.
As a consequence,
\mathfrak{v}
admits a basis of the form
\{e_{1},e_{3},e_{5},a_{4}e_{4}+a_{6}e_{6}\}
, which means that
a_{6}e_{4}-a_{4}e_{6}\in\mathfrak{p}\ominus\mathfrak{v}
, and

0=\langle R(e_{3},e_{5})(a_{4}e_{4}+a_{6}e_{6}),a_{6}e_{4}-a_{4}e_{6}\rangle=-\frac{1}{8}(a_{4}^{2}+a_{6}^{2}),

so
a_{4}=a_{6}=0
, another contradiction.

If
\mathfrak{v}\cap\mathfrak{p}_{1}=0
, then
\mathfrak{v}=\mathfrak{p}_{2}
, which is also not possible, since
(\nabla_{e_{3}}R)(e_{3},e_{4},e_{6})=-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}e_{1}
is not in
\mathfrak{v}
. In conclusion, no such
\mathfrak{v}
can exist, and the claim follows.∎

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a complete totally geodesic submanifold of

Let

Once again, by dimension reasons we see that

We consider three cases according to the dimension of

\mathfrak{v}\cap\mathfrak{p}_{1}=0

\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}_{2}

i\in\{1,2,3\}
.
In particular, we have that the vector
-c_{2}e_{1}+c_{1}e_{2}+c_{2}e_{3}+c_{1}e_{4}

\mathfrak{v}

0=2\langle R(X,Z)U,-c_{2}e_{1}+c_{1}e_{2}+c_{2}e_{3}+c_{1}e_{4}\rangle=-9(c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2})

Therefore
\mathfrak{v}=\operatorname{span}\{X,Z,e_{5},e_{6}\}
.
However, we have
8R(X,e_{5})e_{6}=3(e_{2}+e_{4})\notin\mathfrak{v}

From all of the above, we see that
\mathfrak{v}
must coincide with
\ker C_{X}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{3},e_{5},\lambda e_{2}+e_{4}\}

However, this is also not possible, since
\frac{8}{3}R(e_{1},e_{3})(\lambda e_{2}+e_{4})=-e_{2}+\lambda e_{4}\notin\mathfrak{v}

Thus, the case

is not possible.

Now, suppose that

e_{1}\in\mathfrak{v}
.
Since the intersection of
\mathfrak{v}
with
\mathfrak{p}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{p}_{3}

\mu\in\mathbb{R}

However,
\mu=0

\mu\neq 0

\mathfrak{v}

Now this yields that

is also in
\mathfrak{v}

We conclude that this case is also not possible, and therefore there are no codimension two totally geodesic subspaces in
\mathfrak{p}
. ∎

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
\Sigma
be a complete three-dimensional totally geodesic submanifold of the flag manifold
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
.
Then

\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/4}(\sqrt{2})

We need to classify three-dimensional totally geodesic subspaces of
\mathfrak{p}

\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
be such a subspace, and
\Sigma
the corresponding complete totally geodesic submanifold.
Then we know that there exists a vector

The Cartan operator

\frac{3\lambda\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}{2\sqrt{2}}

-\frac{3\lambda\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}{2\sqrt{2}}

First, assume that

We prove in this case that either
\Sigma=\mathsf{SU}(2)_{(1,0,1)}\cdot o

\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\ker C_{X}

.

.

4R(X,Y)X=-13\varepsilon e_{2}+13\varepsilon e_{4}-\sqrt{2}e_{6}\in\mathfrak{v}

\mathfrak{v}

e_{2}-e_{4}

\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\ker C_{X}

\mathfrak{v}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{3},e_{5}\}

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla_{X}R)(X,Y,Y),e_{6}\rangle={}

\displaystyle\frac{3(a_{3}-a_{1})}{4\sqrt{2}},

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla_{X}R)(X,Y,Y),e_{2}-e_{4}\rangle={}

\displaystyle\frac{-3a_{5}}{2\sqrt{2}},

Proposition 7.10

Y=e_{1}+\lambda e_{4}\in\mathfrak{v}
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
The Jacobi operator
R_{Y}\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{p}\ominus\mathbb{R}Y)
has three different eigenvalues
0
,
\frac{1+\lambda^{2}}{12}
and
\frac{3(1+\lambda^{2})}{4}
, with corresponding eigenspaces
\mathbb{R}(\lambda e_{4}-e_{1})
,
\operatorname{span}\{\lambda e_{2}-e_{5},\lambda e_{3}-e_{6}\}
and
\operatorname{span}\{e_{2}+\lambda e_{5},e_{3}+\lambda e_{6}\}
.
Note that the isotropy subgroup
\mathsf{K}_{e_{1}}
fixes
Y
and acts transitively on the set of lines in both
\operatorname{span}\{\lambda e_{2}-e_{5},\lambda e_{3}-e_{6}\}
and
\operatorname{span}\{e_{2}+\lambda e_{5},e_{3}+\lambda e_{6}\}
.
Now, the fact that
\dim\mathfrak{v}=3
implies that
\mathfrak{v}
must contain a nonzero eigenvector from one of the last two eigenspaces.
Our next step is to prove that
\lambda^{2}=3
or
\lambda^{2}=1/3
.

If
\mathfrak{v}\cap\operatorname{span}\{\lambda e_{2}-e_{5},\lambda e_{3}-e_{6}\}\neq 0
, we can conjugate by an element of
\mathsf{K}_{e_{1}}
to assume that
Z=\lambda e_{2}-e_{5}\in\mathfrak{v}
is tangent to
\Sigma
.
If we suppose that
\lambda^{2}\notin\left\{3,1/3\right\}
, then we can give a basis of
\mathfrak{v}
with the vectors

Y=e_{1}+\lambda e_{4},\quad Z=\lambda e_{2}-e_{5},\quad T=3\sqrt{3}(\nabla_{Z}R)(Z,Y,Z)=\lambda(1-3\lambda^{2})e_{3}+(3\lambda^{2}-1)e_{6}.

As a consequence,
e_{3}+\lambda e_{6}
is orthogonal to
\mathfrak{v}
, and we deduce that

0={3\sqrt{3}}\langle(\nabla_{Y}R)(Y,Z,Y),e_{3}+\lambda e_{6}\rangle=\lambda\left(3-\lambda^{2}\right)\left(\lambda^{2}+1\right),

a contradiction, so we obtain that
\lambda^{2}\in\left\{3,1/3\right\}
.

Similarly, in the case that
\mathfrak{v}\cap\operatorname{span}\{e_{2}+\lambda e_{5},e_{3}+\lambda e_{6}\}\neq 0
, we can use an element of
\mathsf{K}_{e_{1}}
to assume that
Z=e_{2}+\lambda e_{5}
also belongs to
\mathfrak{v}
.
Here, if
\lambda^{2}\notin\{3,1/3\}
, then we can construct a basis of
\mathfrak{v}
with the vectors

\displaystyle Y=e_{1}+\lambda e_{4},\quad Z=e_{2}+\lambda e_{5},\quad T=\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{\lambda}(\nabla_{Y}R)(Y,Z,Y)=\lambda(\lambda^{2}-3)e_{3}+(3-\lambda^{2})e_{6}.

In particular,
e_{3}+\lambda e_{6}\in\mathfrak{p}\ominus\mathfrak{v}
, and we have

0=\langle(\nabla_{T}R)(Y,Z,T),e_{3}+\lambda e_{6}\rangle=\frac{\left(\lambda^{2}-3\right)^{2}\left(\lambda^{2}+1\right)\left(3\lambda^{2}-1\right)}{6\sqrt{3}},

contradicting our assumption.

All in all, we have proved that the totally geodesic subspace
\mathfrak{v}
contains
Y=e_{1}+\lambda e_{4}
, and
\lambda^{2}=3
or
\lambda^{2}=1/3
.
Now, consider the isometry
s_{o}\colon\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}\to\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
defined in Subsection
2.2.3
.
One sees that the differential
(s_{o})_{*o}
satisfies the identities

\displaystyle(s_{o})_{*o}(e_{1})={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+\sqrt{3}e_{4})

\displaystyle(s_{o})_{*o}^{-1}(e_{1})={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}-\sqrt{3}e_{4}),

\displaystyle(s_{o})_{*o}(e_{4})={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{3}e_{1}-e_{4}),

\displaystyle(s_{o})_{*o}^{-1}(e_{4})={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{3}e_{1}+e_{4}).

Using these equations, we deduce that either
(s_{o})_{*o}(\mathfrak{v})
or
(s_{o})_{*o}^{2}(\mathfrak{v})
is a totally geodesic subspace that contains either
e_{1}
or
e_{4}
, so by Lemma
7.8
it is well-positioned, and thus
\Sigma
is congruent to
\mathsf{S}^{2}(2/\sqrt{3})
or
\mathsf{S}^{3}_{\mathbb{C},1/3}(2)
. ∎

Proposition 7.11
.

Let
\Sigma\to\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
be a complete totally geodesic surface.
Then, either
\Sigma
is congruent to either
\mathsf{T}^{2}_{\Gamma}
, the not well-positioned
\mathsf{S}^{3}(\sqrt{3/2})
, or a great sphere inside the round
\mathsf{S}^{3}\left(2/\sqrt{3}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be a complete totally geodesic surface, and assume without loss of generality that it passes through
o
with tangent space
\mathfrak{v}
.
We distinguish two situations for
\mathfrak{v}
according to whether it is well-positioned or not.

First, suppose that
\mathfrak{v}
is well-positioned.
If
\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}_{1}
, then
\Sigma
is merely a round sphere inside the round
\mathsf{S}^{3}
.
If
\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}_{2}
, we may suppose that
\mathfrak{v}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{4},e_{5}\}
, but the equation
3\sqrt{3}(\nabla_{e_{4}}R)(e_{4},e_{5},e_{4})=e_{3}
implies that
\mathfrak{v}
is not
\nabla R
-invariant, a contradiction.
Now, suppose that
\mathfrak{v}\cap\mathfrak{p}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{v}\cap\mathfrak{p}_{2}
are both one-dimensional.
By using the isotropy representation, we may suppose that
\mathfrak{v}\cap\mathfrak{p}_{1}=\mathbb{R}e_{1}
.
The Jacobi operator
R_{e_{1}}
preserves
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
and its restriction to
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
has eigenvalues
0
(with eigenspace
\mathbb{R}e_{4}
) and
\frac{1}{12}
(with eigenspace
\operatorname{span}\{e_{5},e_{6}\}
).
Thus, we can further assume (up to the action of
\mathsf{K}
) that either
\mathfrak{v}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{4}\}
or
\mathfrak{v}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{5}\}
.
The first case simply yields
\Sigma=\mathsf{T}^{2}_{\Gamma}
, while the second case gives a contradiction because
3\sqrt{3}(\nabla_{e_{5}}R)(e_{5},e_{1},e_{5})=e_{6}
.
This completes the case that
\mathfrak{v}
is well-positioned.

Suppose
\mathfrak{v}
is not well-positioned.
In particular
\mathfrak{v}_{\mathfrak{p}_{1}}\neq 0
, and this combined with the isotropy representation lets us assume that
\mathfrak{v}
contains a vector of the form
X=e_{1}+\rho\cos\theta e_{4}+\rho\sin\theta e_{5}
, where
\rho>0
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{v}
is invariant under
D
, which yields
\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\ker D_{X}
as it is two-dimensional.
If
\theta\in\{0,\pi\}
, then
\ker D_{X}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{4}\}
, which forces
\mathfrak{v}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{4}\}
, contradicting our hypothesis that
\mathfrak{v}
is not well-positioned.
If
\theta\notin\{0,\pi\}
, the kernel of
D_{X}
is spanned by
X
and
Y=\rho\cos\theta e_{1}+\rho\sin\theta e_{2}-e_{4}
, so
\mathfrak{v}=\operatorname{span}\{X,Y\}
.
As a consequence, we see that the vector
Z=~-\rho\sin\theta e_{1}+\rho\cos\theta e_{2}+e_{5}
is orthogonal to
\mathfrak{v}
, and thus

0=\langle R(X,Y)X,Z\rangle=\frac{4}{3}\rho^{2}\sin 2\theta
,
which forces
\theta=\pi/2
or
\theta=3\pi/2
.
We group these cases together by writing
X=e_{1}+te_{5}
and
Y=te_{2}-e_{4}
for a nonzero
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
As a consequence,
e_{2}+te_{4}
is orthogonal to
\mathfrak{v}
, and
0=\langle R(X,Y)X,e_{2}+te_{4}\rangle=\frac{4}{3}t(t^{2}-1)
,
so
t=\pm 1
.
The cases
t=1
and
t=-1
give rise to congruent submanifolds.
Indeed, the element

k=(\operatorname{diag}(i,-i),\operatorname{diag}(i,-i),\operatorname{diag}(i,-i))\in\mathsf{K}

satisfies
\operatorname{Ad}(k)(e_{1}+e_{5})=e_{1}-e_{5}
and
\operatorname{Ad}(k)(e_{2}-e_{4})=-e_{2}-e_{4}
.
As a consequence, we see that
\mathfrak{v}
is conjugate to
\operatorname{span}\{e_{1}+e_{5},e_{2}-e_{4}\}
, so
\Sigma
is congruent to the not well-positioned
\mathsf{S}^{2}(\sqrt{3/2})
.

Now, assume that
\mathfrak{v}
is not
D
-invariant.
Arguing as before, we may suppose that
\mathfrak{v}
contains a vector of the form
X=e_{1}+\rho\cos\theta e_{4}+\rho\sin\theta e_{5}
for
\rho>0
and
0\leq\theta<2\pi
.
On the one hand, one sees that
C_{X}=0
if
\rho=\sqrt{3}
and
\theta\in\{0,\pi\}
, but in this case
\mathfrak{v}
is congruent to a well-positioned example.
Indeed, we argue as in the end of Proposition
7.10
.
Consider the isometry
s_{o}\colon\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}\to\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
defined as in Subsection
2.2.3
.
Then, if
\theta=0
we have
X=-2(s_{o})_{*o}(e_{1})
, so
\mathfrak{v}
is congruent to
(s_{o})_{*o}^{-1}(\mathfrak{v})
, and this subspace is well-positioned by Lemma
7.8
, so we may apply the conclusions from the previous case.
Similarly, if
\theta=\pi
, then
X=-2(s_{o})^{-1}_{*o}(e_{1})
, and
(s_{o})_{*o}(\mathfrak{v})
is well-positioned.
On the other hand, if
\rho\neq\sqrt{3}
or
\theta\neq\pi
, we have

\displaystyle C_{X}e_{1}={}

\displaystyle\frac{\rho^{2}\sin 2\theta}{6\sqrt{3}}e_{3}+\frac{\rho^{3}\sin\theta}{3\sqrt{3}}e_{6},

\displaystyle C_{X}e_{2}={}

\displaystyle\frac{\rho^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{3\sqrt{3}}e_{3}-\frac{\rho\left(\rho^{2}-3\right)\cos\theta}{3\sqrt{3}}e_{6},

\displaystyle C_{X}e_{4}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho\left(\rho^{2}-2\right)\sin\theta}{3\sqrt{3}}e_{3}-\frac{\rho^{2}\sin 2\theta}{6\sqrt{3}}e_{6},

\displaystyle C_{X}e_{5}={}

\displaystyle\frac{\rho\left(\rho^{2}-3\right)\cos\theta}{3\sqrt{3}}e_{3}-\frac{\rho^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{3\sqrt{3}}e_{6}.

A straightforward computation gives that these four vectors span the subspace
\operatorname{span}\{e_{3},e_{6}\}
, so we have
\operatorname{span}\{e_{3},e_{6}\}\subseteq\operatorname{im}C_{X}
.
In particular, since
C_{X}
is a symmetric endomorphism, we have
\mathfrak{p}=\ker C_{X}\oplus\operatorname{im}C_{X}
orthogonally, so
\ker C_{X}
is orthogonal to
e_{3}
and
e_{6}
.
As a consequence,
\mathfrak{v}
is spanned by
X
and a vector of the form
Y=a_{1}e_{1}+a_{2}e_{2}+a_{4}e_{4}+a_{5}e_{5}
,
where
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,
a_{4}
,
a_{5}\in\mathbb{R}
.
However,

D_{X}Y=\frac{a_{4}\rho\sin\theta-a_{5}\rho\cos\theta+a_{2}}{2\sqrt{3}}e_{3}+\frac{a_{1}\rho\sin\theta-a_{2}\rho\cos\theta-a_{5}}{2\sqrt{3}}e_{6}

is in
\ker C_{X}
by Proposition
4.8
and in
\operatorname{span}\{e_{3},e_{6}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}\ominus\ker C_{X}
, which forces
D_{X}Y=0
, and thus
\mathfrak{v}
is
D
-invariant, contradicting our assumption.
This finishes the proof. ∎

Proof of Theorem
C
.

The result follows from combining Theorem
6.7
, Proposition
7.9
, Proposition
7.10
, and Proposition
7.11
.
∎

E
.

Proof of Theorem
E
.

Let
M
be a homogeneous nearly Kähler
6
-manifold with non-constant curvature.
Since totally geodesic submanifolds are preserved under Riemannian coverings and the universal cover of
\widehat{M}
is the cone of the universal cover of
M
, we may assume that
M
is simply connected, so
M
is either
\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3}
,
\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
or
\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}
.

Let
\Sigma
be a complete totally geodesic submanifold of the
\mathsf{G}_{2}
-cone
\widehat{M}
.
By Corollary
6.10
,
\Sigma
is either a hypersurface of
\widehat{M}
or the cone of a maximal totally geodesic submanifold of
M
.
The first case is not possible due to
[
30
, Theorem 1.2]
, so we conclude that
\Sigma=\widehat{S}
for a maximal totally geodesic submanifold
S\subseteq M
, which yields the desired result.
∎

Remark 7.12
.

Let
M\in\{\mathbb{C}\mathsf{P}^{3},\mathsf{F}(\mathbb{C}^{3}),\mathsf{S}^{3}\times\mathsf{S}^{3}\}
.
A look at Tables
1
,
2
and
3
shows that two (complete) totally geodesic submanifolds of
M
are congruent if and only if they are isometric.
Combining this with Proposition
6.3
, we deduce that the congruence classes of maximal totally geodesic submanifolds of
\widehat{M}
are in a bijective correspondence with the congruence classes of maximal totally geodesic submanifolds of
M
.

We also note that in order to obtain the classification of all totally geodesic submanifolds of
\widehat{M}
, it suffices to iterate Corollary
6.10
and take into account Propositions
6.12
and
6.14
.
