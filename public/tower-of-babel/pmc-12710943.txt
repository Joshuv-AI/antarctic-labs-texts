Unraveling alfalfa molecular adaptation to salinity and Ascochyta medicaginicola infection: insights from differential physiological traits and proteomics profiling
Authors: Wiem Mnafgui, Nawres Zaidi, Ali Elnaeim Elbasheir Ali, Cheima Jabri, Gerhard Basson, Musa Oyebowale Akanbi, Mohsen Hanana, Bettina Hause, Hatem Boubakri, Ndiko Ludidi, Mounawer Badri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn real-life scenarios, plants are often exposed to biotic and abiotic stresses simultaneously; it is crucial to understand how they respond to single and combined stresses. This study explored the physiological and molecular responses of two contrasting alfalfa varieties, Gabes-2353 (tolerant) and Magna-601 (sensitive), to salinity and/or Ascochyta medicaginicola infection. Both varieties exhibited reductions in stem length, leaf relative water content, and chlorophyll content, with a greater decrease in Magna-601. Label-free LC‒MS/MS analysis identified 128 differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) between the two varieties, of which 14, 60, and 70 proteins were differentially regulated under salinity, Pm8 infection, and combined stresses, respectively. Gene Ontology analyses revealed that Gabes-2353 activated photosynthesis, metabolism, redox balance, and immune pathways, while Magna-601 disrupted carbohydrate metabolism. Pm8 infection induced immune pathways in both varieties, with antioxidant and metabolic proteins expressed in Gabes-2353, while protease inhibitors and defense proteins were expressed in Magna-601. Under combined stress, Gabes-2353 upregulated proteins associated with synergistic integration of metabolic and immune pathways. In contrast, Magna-601 activated broad stress-signaling responses but inhibited primary metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, and vitamin metabolism. The PPI and KEGG analyses identified 7-O-methyltransferase as a central hub in Gabes-2353, while Magna-601’s PPI network focused on starch mobilization, reflecting differential adaptation strategies.Keywords: Medicago sativa, biotic and abiotic stresses, label-free proteomics, salinity, Ascochyta medicaginicola

Introduction

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is a vital forage crop that plays a crucial role in livestock feed because of its high levels of micronutrients, macronutrients, and proteins.1,2 It is a moderately salt-tolerant crop that is widely cultivated in arid and semi-arid Tunisian climates, which are characterized by high levels of salinity.2,3 However, the increasing impact of climate change on global ecosystems is increasing the exposure of plants to various abiotic and biotic stresses simultaneously, and thus poses a significant threat to crop production.4,5 Over the past decade, Tunisia's livestock have suffered from decreased forage crop productivity due to the combined effects of abiotic and biotic constraints exacerbated by climate change.2 Soil salinization is a significant factor that limits the productivity of Medicago species.2,3,6 Salinity stress increases the levels of Na+ and Cl- ions within plant tissues, leading to a biological imbalance in plant cells. This imbalance may lead to a profound disruption in the structural and functional integrity of plant cell membranes. In addition, salinity-induced osmotic stress triggers the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative damage and disruption of photosynthesis, ultimately affecting plant growth and development.5,7,8

Black stem disease caused by Ascochyta medicaginicola (strain Pm8) is recognized as a significant threat to Medicago species in many regions, including North America, South Africa, Europe, Australia, and particularly Tunisia.1,5,9,10 Studies on the pathogenicity of isolates originating from annual Medicago species have demonstrated that their virulence is comparatively lower on alfalfa (Medicago sativa) than on their native hosts.5,11 Previous studies have identified spring black stem and leaf spot, caused by the soil- and seed-borne pathogen Ascochyta medicaginicola, as a globally distributed disease that significantly reduces the yield and nutritional quality of alfalfa (Medicago sativa).5,12 Strain Pm8 can cause significant defoliation and premature death in highly susceptible Medicago plants.11 Under greenhouse conditions, strain Pm8 infection affected traits related to plant size and vigor, such as a decreased number of stems, smaller crown diameter, lower stem and root dry weight, and fewer axillary buds, compared to control plants.13 Recent investigations into the mechanisms of pathogenesis and disease resistance have revealed that strain Pm8 reduces the transfer rate and capture efficiency of photosynthetic electrons10 and carboxylation efficiency (CE) in alfalfa leaves.8 Therefore, the combined effect of salinity and strain Pm8 infection may be more detrimental than the effects of each stress individually, as studies have shown that combined stresses may not be an additive response but a unique response.14 The disease process and pathogenic mechanisms of strain Pm8 in alfalfa grown under salt stress are currently not well understood. Such knowledge is crucial for developing innovative control strategies and breeding programs that aim to produce resistant varieties.13 Several research studies have outlined the importance of multi-omics technologies for identifying the molecular mechanisms that underlie plant tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses.15,16 Proteomic approaches have recently emerged as powerful strategies for comprehensively analyzing the differentially expressed proteins involved in plant responses to abiotic and biotic stresses.16 In this context, several studies have highlighted some protein markers involved in alfalfa adaptation under individual salinity17 or fungi infection18,19 stress. For example, under salt stress, the salt-tolerant alfalfa cultivar presented greater ROS scavenging, greater energy availability, and stronger photosynthetic performance compared to the salt-sensitive cultivar.20 Similarly, proteomic data from alfalfa shoots showed that infected plants maintained higher levels of pathogen defense, photosynthesis, carbon fixation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, anion transport, and cell wall remodeling under fungi and bacterial stresses.12,19 Currently, there is a lack of information on the molecular mechanisms in alfalfa leaves in response to salinity, and A. medicaginicola infection. This study investigated how different types of stress affect growth parameters, photosynthetic responses, and the proteomic profiles in the leaves of two contrasting M. sativa varieties.

Materials and methods

Source of A. medicaginicola strain (Pm8) and inoculum preparation

The A. medicaginicola (Pm8) strain was isolated from M. truncatula in Bulla Regia in the North of Tunisia9 and has been maintained by Prof. Naceur Djebali's team at the Centre of Biotechnology of Borj-Cedria, Tunisia. This Pm8 strain was confirmed to be the main causal agent of Medicago species disease.

The A. medicaginicola (Pm8) inoculum was prepared using the methods outlined by Zaidi et al.5 and Mnafgui et al.21 The following steps were taken: (i) Conidia were harvested by incubating plates (1-month-old cultures) of the Pm8 strain with 10 mL of autoclaved, sterilized water for 20 min, (ii) the suspension was filtered through a sterilized compress, and the inoculum concentration was adjusted to 3 × 106 spores/mL. Tween 20 was added to a final concentration of 0.05%, (iii) two months after planting, the M. sativa varieties were inoculated by spraying the inoculum onto the plants using a glass spray bottle until runoff, to ensure even distribution over the plant surfaces, (iv) to maintain high humidity and stimulate conidia germination, the plants were placed under a sealed plastic bag for 14 d, and (v) infection progression was monitored daily.

Plant material

This study selected two contrasting alfalfa varieties, Gabes2353 and Magna-601 based on findings from our previous varieties screening study.2,3 Our researchers identified these two different varieties of M. sativa with contrasting responses to soil salinity stress and A. medicaginicola infection. The Gabes2353 variety demonstrated greater tolerance or resistance to these two stressors, while the Magna-601 variety was more susceptible.2,3,5

Plant growth and stress treatments

The seeds of two contrasting alfalfa varieties, Gabes-2353 and Magna-601, were surface sterilized by immersion in a 0.35% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution (v/v) on a shaker for 10 min, followed by a 30-s incubation in 70% ethanol. Seeds were washed three times with autoclaved distilled water after each treatment. After sterilization, the seeds were placed on sterile paper towels in Petri dishes in the dark and left to germinate at 21 °C. The germinated seedlings were then transferred to 1-liter pots containing an autoclaved mixture of sand and peat (3:1, v/v). Seedlings were grown in a greenhouse with a temperature regime of 23/18 °C (day/night hours), a relative humidity of 60%–80%, and a photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h dark. The plants were subjected to four different treatments: (i) control (no stress applied), (ii) soil salinity stress (150 mM NaCl),1,5 (iii) infection with the Pm8 strain, or (iv) combined salinity-Pm8 infection stress. Sixteen biological replicates were used for each variety under each condition according to methods described by.5,11,21

Measured traits

At harvest, we evaluated four traits in line with our previous study (Mnafgui et al. 2024), including stem length (SL, cm), leaf fresh weight (LFW, g), percentage of healthy leaves (PHL, %), and relative water content (RWC). The relative water content (RWC) was determined using the third youngest trifoliate leaf from three randomly selected plants.22 The leaf samples were ground into a powder using liquid nitrogen, then freeze-dried and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Photosynthetic pigment concentration analysis

Photosynthetic pigments were extracted using the method described by.10 Fresh leaf discs (50 mg) were immersed in 3 ml of 80% acetone and stored in darkness at 4 °C for seven days. The absorbance was measured at wavelengths of 663, 646, and 470 nm for chlorophyll a (Chl a), chlorophyll b (Chl b), and total carotenoids, respectively, using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Specord 210 Plus, Analytik Jena, Germany).

Label-free liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry proteomic analysis

Four biological replicates of leaf tissue from each alfalfa variety and treatment were used for proteomics analysis. Each replicate consisted of leaf tissue pooled from four different plants. Proteins were extracted for LC‒MS/MS analysis using the SDS/phenol method as described by Ali et al.15 LC‒MS/MS analysis was performed using a Q-Exactive quadrupole-orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), which was coupled with a Dionex Ultimate 3000 nano-UPLC system. Data acquisition was conducted using Xcalibur v4.1.31.9, Chromeleon v6.8 (SR13), Orbitrap MS v2.9 (build 2926), and Thermo Foundations 3.1 (SP4). The parameters for peptide separation using liquid chromatography are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. The parameters for data acquisition are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Protein identification and quantification

Peptide and protein identification was conducted using database interrogation with Byonic Software v3.8.13 (Protein Metrics, USA). The raw data were searched against the Medicago truncatula reference proteome (M.truncatula 57066prot_UP000002051_010422.fasta), which was sourced from UniProt (www.uniprot.org) on April 1 , 2022. The search parameters are shown in Supplementary Table 3. Data processing involved peak picking, run alignment, and normalization, with singly charged spectra excluded from the processing pipeline. Peptide quantification was performed with Progenesis QI for Proteomics v2.0.5556.29015 (Nonlinear Dynamics, UK). Relative protein quantification was determined using the “relative quantification using non-conflicting peptides” method. Only proteins containing at least two unique peptides were considered valid after the results were filtered to exclude reverse hits, common contaminants, and singly charged ions.

Identification of differentially abundant proteins and functional annotation

Differential expression analyses and functional annotation were performed as described by Ali et al.15 An R script was used to identify differentially abundant proteins between the two alfalfa varieties under three different treatment conditions. Two-way ANOVA with Benjamini–Hochberg multiple-testing correction was performed on the two varieties under each stress condition. We then computed the log2-fold change of proteins for each variety in response to each treatment to determine the proteins that were differentially abundant within both varieties in response to the respective treatment. Proteins exhibiting log2-fold changes >1 with a q ≤0.05 were considered to be differentially upregulated in response to stress within varieties, while proteins exhibiting log2-fold changes <−1 with a q ≤0.05 were considered to be differentially downregulated in response to stress within varieties. A Venn diagram was produced using FunRich23 to illustrate common and unique differentially regulated proteins between the two varieties among treatments, as well as to show the unique and common upregulated and downregulated proteins between the two varieties under each treatment. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted using OmicsBox software version 3.0.30 (BioBam Bioinformatics 2019). FASTA sequences of the differentially abundant proteins were obtained from the UniProtKB database.

Heat map visualization and protein–protein interaction network analysis

The “TBtools” software (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools/releases) was used to generate a heat map representation with hierarchical classification using log2-transformed values corresponding to the rates of change of differentially abundant proteins (DAP) for each genotype in stressed samples compared to the controls (P < 0.05). The abundance ratio of the identified proteins was examined by Euclidean distances and Ward’s minimum criteria.

The protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were generated using the STRING v11.0 database (https://string-db.org). The key candidate proteins selected based on the hierarchical classification were used to construct PPI networks for both varieties in response to combined stress. The proteins selected with the highest bit scores, resulting from the similarity search against the Medicago truncatula proteome at STRING v11.0 database, were considered for the analysis.

Statistical analysis of physiological parameters

Physiological parameters were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA in the SPSS software (version 20.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Tukey's multiple range test was employed at a 5% significance level to compare the means of the measured data. The results are expressed as the means ± SD.

Results

Assessment of the individual and combined effects of salinity and strain Pm8 infection on the agronomic traits of two contrasting alfalfa varieties

As shown in Figure 1a, both salinity and infection with strain Pm8 had similar impact on the stem length (SL) of the Gabes-2353 variety, resulting in a 21.5% reduction compared to the control. However, combined stress caused a more pronounced reduction (by 41%) compared to the control. By contrast, the Magna-601 variety was affected primarily by salinity, which experienced a 38% decrease in SL. A 28% reduction in SL was observed in Magna-601 during infection with strain Pm8. Compared with salinity alone, combined stress resulted in a 44% reduction in the SL of Magna-601 (Figure 1a).

Effects of salinity and/or Pm8 infection on stem length (a), leaf fresh weight (b), relative water content (c), and percentage of healthy leaves (d) of Medicago sativa varieties (Gabes-2353 and Magna-601). Different letters (a, b, ab, d, e, f, and g) indicate a significant difference between treatments according to Tukey’s test at P ≤ 0.05. Means denoted by the same letter(s) are not significantly different among the studied varieties for each trait, based on Tukey’s P > 0.05.

Compared to salinity alone, Pm8 infection caused a greater reduction in leaf fresh weight (LFW) in both varieties. The decrease in LFW was even more pronounced under combined stress (Figure 1b). However, the tolerant Gabes-2353 variety exhibited a lower LFW reduction under all stress conditions than the sensitive Magna-601 variety. Under salinity stress, Gabes-2353 showed a 23% decrease in LFW, while Magna-601 experienced a greater reduction of 47%. Infection with Pm8 reduced LFW by 50% in Gabes-2353 and by 54% in Magna-601. The combined effects of salinity and Pm8 infection led to a 64% reduction in LFW in Gabes-2353 and a 73% reduction in LFW in Magna-601.

A similar trend was observed for the relative water content (RWC), where the combined stress resulted in a greater reduction in RWC for both varieties than single stresses, with Magna-601 showing a greater decrease than Gabes-2353 (Figure 1c). Salinity stress reduced RWC by 22% in the Gabes-2353 variety and by 25% in Magna-601. Infection with strain Pm8 decreased RWC by 5% in Gabes-2353 and by 7% in Magna-601. Under combined stresses, Gabes-2353 exhibited a 29% decrease in RWC, while Magna-601 showed a 32% decrease in RWC compared to the controls.

In response to combined stresses, both varieties exhibited a greater reduction in the percentage of healthy leaves (PHL) compared to single stresses, with a higher decrease observed in the Magna-601 variety under all three stress treatments (Figure 1d). Gabes-2353 exhibited a decrease of 25%, 49% and 62% in PHL under salinity, Pm8 infection, and the combined stress, respectively. A greater reduction in PHL was observed in Magna-601, with reductions of 30%, 58%, and 69% under salinity, Pm8 infection, and combined stress, respectively.

The results presented in Figure 2 indicate the effects of different stress conditions (salinity, Pm8 infection, and salinity-Pm8 infection stresses) on chlorophyll pigment production, where a significant reduction in these pigments was observed in Gabes-2353. Indeed, salinity had the most pronounced effects on chlorophyll pigment contents, compared to Pm8 infection. Magna-601 showed a different trend: individual stresses had similar effects, but the combined stress led to a greater increase than individual stresses did.

Specifically, the Chl a content in Gabes-2353 decreased by 13% under salinity, by 31% under Pm8 infection, and by 55% under the combined stress (Figure 2a). For the Magna-601 variety, the greatest reduction in Chl a was observed under salinity (30%), whereas both Pm8 infection and combined stress resulted in reductions of a similar magnitude (21%).

The reductions in Chl b under salinity, Pm8 infection, and combined stress were 19%, 40%, and 84%, respectively, in Gabes-2353. For the Magna-601 variety, individual stresses reduced Chl b by 60%, whereas under combined stress, Chl b decreased by 31% compared to the controls (Figure 2b).

The carotenoid content in Gabes-2353 significantly changed, with salinity resulting in the smallest reduction (22%), while the combined stress had the greatest impact. In Magna-601, the variation in carotenoid content decreased by 36% under salinity and by 26% under combined stress, while Pm8 infection caused the greatest reduction (61%) (Figure 2c).

Identification of differentially expressed proteins and gene ontology (GO) under the three stress treatments

A total of 2397 proteins were identified by LC‒MS/MS in the leaves of the two alfalfa varieties subjected to the different stress treatments. Analysis of differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) revealed that a total of 128 proteins exhibited differential abundance between the two contrasting alfalfa varieties under the three stress conditions (Figure 3). Among these proteins, 14 were differentially regulated between the two varieties in response to salinity, 60 proteins were differentially regulated in response to Pm8 infection, and 70 were differentially regulated in response to the combined salinity-Pm8 infection stress (Figure 3). Among the 14 DAPs identified between Gabes-2353 and Magna-601 under salt stress, 3 proteins were uniquely upregulated and 6 were uniquely downregulated in Gabes-2353 (supplementary Tables 4 and 5), while 2 proteins were exclusively upregulated and 2 were downregulated in Magna-601 (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). In response to Pm8 infection, 60 proteins showed differential abundance between the two varieties, where 1 protein was uniquely upregulated and 1 was downregulated in Gabes2353 (Supplementary Tables 8, 9) while 27 were upregulated and 14 were downregulated in Magna-601 (Supplementary Tables 10 and 11). Under combined stresses, 70 proteins were differentially regulated between the varieties, with 4 uniquely upregulated and 3 downregulated in Gabes2353 (Supplementary Tables 12 and 13) and 29 upregulated and 12 downregulated exclusively in Magna-601 (Supplementary Tables 14 and 15). The heatmap presentation associated with the hierarchical clustering revealed contrasting adaptation strategies between Gabes-2353 and Magna-601 varieties under salinity, Pm8 infection, and their combination (Figure 4). Under salinity, Gabes-2353 exhibited upregulation of proteins involved in biological processes related to photosynthesis and carbohydrates (SPP, PAR, IPS), as well as secondary metabolite biosynthesis (7-OMT), protein ubiquitination (UBA1HH), amino acid metabolism and osmoprotection (GAB, GLU, PLP), redox regulation (TRX), and carbohydrate/starch metabolism (AGPS2), reflecting coordinated metabolic adjustment, antioxidant defense, and energy management. In contrast, the salt-sensitive Magna-601 displayed a limited response. Only HP2 and SBE were upregulated, which is associated with carbohydrate metabolism and starch biosynthesis, indicating partial attempts to maintain energy balance. However, stress-related proteins such as GTP and GLUT were upregulated, affecting energy metabolism and amino acid homeostasis in Magna-601.

Under Pm8 infection, both varieties upregulated defense- and detoxification-related proteins. In Gabes-2353, IRK and SBE1 were downregulated (stress response, carbohydrate metabolism),while 7-OMT (secondary metabolism), GST (ROS scavenging), CAB6 (photosynthesis), ACC (hormone metabolism), and PLP (amino acid metabolism) were upregulated, indicating a coordinated antioxidant and metabolic response. In Magna-601, protease inhibitors and defense proteins (SCPL25, PGIP2, and SBT) were upregulated. Notably, immune signaling regulators (RhiR, UBA1A, and COPA2) were upregulated in both varieties, highlighting the shared activation of immune pathways.

When exposed to combined salinity and Pm8 infection, Gabes-2353 demonstrated the upregulation of UBA1A, COPA2, RhiR (immune signaling), DHPS, PLP, and IPS (metabolic flexibility). The metabolism-related proteins, including CAB6, CAB-AB80, NDH (involved in photosynthesis), and ASP, PII, and SLP (involved in amino acid and nitrogen metabolism), along with defense-related proteins, were upregulated in Magna-601.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks and KEGG under the single effect of salinity, Pm8 infection, and the combined stress

The protein-protein interaction analyses of upregulated proteins Gabes-2353 (Figure 5a) showed a dense central cluster of highly interconnected nodes, with Mt7-OMT (O-methyltransferase) acting as a hub that interacts with CADs (cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenases), PER (peroxidases), UGT72E1-3 (UDP-glycosyltransferases), CYP84A4 (cytochrome P450 monooxygenase), and MEE23 (methyl esterase 23), the network highlights its central role in regulating secondary metabolism, particularly phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthesis. The core modules of CADs and PERs are likely responsible for lignin polymerization and the detoxification of ROS. In contrast, peripheral clusters, such as F24B9.23-MtBGlu (β-glucosidase) and Q9LRP8_ARATH-T9L24.40-MtPAR (phenylalanine ammonia-lyase-related protein), suggest specialized roles in cell wall remodeling and stress signaling. In Gabes-2353, downregulated proteins and their predicted partners clustered primarily around three hubs: Q9LHC4_ARATH (light-harvesting/photosynthesis), MtRHD11 (redox regulation and RNA binding), and MtERD15 (early stress response and transcriptional regulation). These hubs are connected to functional modules related to chloroplast activity, RNA/protein processing, and stress adaptation.

Stress-specific KEGG pathway analyses (Figure 5b) revealed that, in response to salinity, the secondary metabolite production (phenylpropanoid biosynthesis) and the osmotic stress pathway (cyanoamino acid metabolism) were upregulated, while starch and sucrose metabolism were downregulated. Under Pm8 infection, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis was downregulated. Under combined stress, Gabes2533 exhibited an upregulation of stress signaling pathways (MAPK) and antimicrobial pathways (monobactam biosynthesis). Meanwhile, pathways such as NF-kappa B signaling and lysine biosynthesis were downregulated, indicating a trade-off between growth, immune signaling, and stress adaptation mechanisms.

The PPI network of upregulated proteins in Magna-601 (Figure 6) responds to individual and combined stresses by forming a dense, carbohydrate-centered network dominated by amylases (AMY1-3), β-amylases (BAM2-3), isoamylases (ISA1-2), disproportioning enzymes (DPE1-2), glucan water dikinase (GWD1), and the central hub protein MAAP2, all of which are tightly connected, with the maltose transporter MEX1 playing a crucial role in moving sugars out of the chloroplast. These proteins are highly connected, reflecting the heavy mobilization of starch reserves, which are likely to provide soluble sugars for osmoprotection, ROS detoxification, and metabolic fuel during stress. In addition to this carbohydrate hub, peripheral nodes composed of proteins such as MtGLP, MtGLM, NUDF1, CSP41, SVR3, various mitochondrial proteins, and transcription factors such as BHLH52 form satellite networks that contribute to plastid and mitochondrial integrity, redox regulation, and transcriptional reprogramming, indicating that Magna-601 also invests in maintaining organelle stability and energy production under stress. However, the PPI network (Figure 6a) formed by a central cluster of NAPRT1/MnAPRT2 and NIC1-3, associated with nicotinate and NAD metabolism, as well as defense-related secondary metabolism, also includes a predicted function related to a stress‒response module centered on MHSP90-5, which is linked to protein folding, chaperone activity, and degradation. In the salt-sensitive Magna-601, no annotated proteins were assigned to any up- or downregulated KEGG pathway in response to salinity treatment. Magna-601 responded to Pm8 infection by activating defense-related secondary metabolism and upregulating DAPs related to signaling pathways (mTOR, PI3K-Akt), reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification (glutathione), and energy production (oxidative phosphorylation) (Figure 6b). Conversely, pathways related to primary metabolism (amino sugar, nucleotide sugar, purine, and starch metabolism) were generally suppressed. Under combined stress, Magna-601 maintained the upregulation of photosynthesis – antenna proteins and stress signaling (cGMP-PKG, calcium signaling). Additionally, proteins involved in pathways of detoxification balance (biosynthesis of cofactors and alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism) were activated. Notably, Magna-601 activated pathways of cell death control (necroptosis, cellular senescence) under the combined stress. Moreover, primary metabolism (protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, and vitamin metabolism) is suppressed.

Discussion

Soil salinity and Ascochyta medicaginicola significantly limit the growth and productivity of Medicago species around the world, most especially in Tunisia.6,11,21 Consequently, it is crucial to understand how alfalfa responds to the combined effects of salinity and Pm8 infection to improve its stress tolerance and ensure sustainable production. In this study, we analyzed the physiological responses of two contrasting alfalfa varieties to the individual and combined effects of salinity and Pm8 infection stresses. Our findings demonstrated that salinity reduced stem length, RWC, and LFW in both varieties because of osmotic imbalance caused by toxic levels of the induced salt, and this effect was more pronounced in Magna-601. Additionally, combined salt-Pm8 infection further exacerbated the reduction in these parameters. This observation is consistent with previous studies on other legumes, such as Vicia faba, where combining salinity and fungal infection stresses caused a more severe reduction in growth compared to each stress alone.24 Suzuki et al.25 explained that combined stresses elicit complex responses governed by distinct, often conflicting, signaling pathways, which can interact and hinder each other. Similarly, Maiza et al.10 found that the combined Pm8 infection and drought stress significantly reduced the leaf fresh weight and growth parameters of two contrasting Medicago truncatula lines more than individual stress.26 The RWC is one of the most reliable biomarkers to measure the plant leaf water status.27 Our findings demonstrated that salinity led to a higher decrease in RWC than Pm8 infection in both alfalfa varieties. The accumulation of salt in the soil lowers the water potential and creates osmotic stress that forces plants to use more energy to draw water into the cell,26 a mechanism better achieved by Gabes-2353, which maintains its leaf water status better than Magna-601. A reduction in RWC was observed in both alfalfa when exposed to Pm8 infection. When exposed to infection such as A. medicaginicola, plants’ xylem vessels that transport water from roots become impaired, and this leads to increased transpiration and stomatal closure.26,28 Moreover, the ability of Gabes-2353 to sustain higher shoot biomass could be linked to its ability to maintain a higher percentage of healthy leaves under these stresses.3,21 Similar observations were reported by Zaidi et al.5 where a lower percentage of cell death in Gabes-2353 leaves was associated with its ability to maintain higher leaf biomass when compared to maize grown under water deficit stress. Both varieties experienced a reduction in chlorophyll content under the three types of stress. Interestingly, compared to the individual stresses, the chlorophyll a and b contents under the combined stress were greater. Metwally and Abdelhameed et al.28 explained that under combined salinity with fungi stress, cellular components, such as mitochondria and the nucleus, are compromised and affected, whereas the chloroplast system remains intact and capable of sustaining photosynthetic activity. A similar trend was reported by Maiza et al.10 where a sensitive line of M. truncatula displayed elevated chlorophyll amounts under the combined drought-Pm8 infection stress compared to single stresses. This suggests that the tolerant variety Gabes-2353 may activate synergistic defense pathways under combined stress, leading to a more significant physiological cost, whereas the sensitive variety Magna-601 might experience a saturation of stress signals, leading to a different prioritization of resources.

Under salinity, Gabes-2353 upregulated proteins involved in biological processes related to carbohydrate metabolism (IPS, AGPS2), secondary metabolism (7-OMT), amino acid/osmoprotection (GAB, GLU, PLP), and redox regulation (TRX), reflecting coordinated metabolic and antioxidant adjustments. Magna-601 showed only limited activation of carbohydrate/starch metabolism (SBE) and (GLUT). Hang et al.20 revealed that GLU and SBE, which are related to starch and sucrose metabolism, were upregulated in salt-stressed alfalfa. The expression of these proteins promotes the accumulation of soluble sugars, which provide substrates and energy for orderly metabolism in the salt environment. Similar results were demonstrated in sorghum,29 cotton,30 and durum wheat.31 During Pm8 infection, both varieties induced immune regulators (RhiR, UBA1A, COPA2)32 which had also been reported in alfalfa under Pseudomonas syringae infection,33 and in barley under Ramularia collo-cygni.34 However, Gabes-2353 reinforced its response with secondary metabolism (7-OMT) and ROS scavenging (GST), photosynthesis (CAB6), which has been proven as one of the main proteins to regulate the photosynthetic process,35 while Magna-601 mainly relied on protease inhibitors/defense proteins (SCPL25, PGIP2, SBT). Our results agreed with previous reports by Cong et al.36 where an important increase in protein abundance involved in regulating plant redox homeostasis, such as glutathione transferases (GSTs) and secondary metabolites (7-OMTs), has been noted in alfalfa during Fusarium proliferum infection. The tolerance of Gabes-2353 under combined stress resulted from an overabundance of proteins involved in the integration of immune signaling (UBA1A, COPA2, RhiR), and metabolic flexibility (DHPS, 7-OMTs). Mnafgui et al.21 demonstrated that alfalfa tolerance under combined stresses was controlled by ubiquitin pathways as well as pathways involved in metabolite production. Khashin et al.37 demonstrated that the expression level of 7-OMT in sorghum was greater under Fusarium Thapsinum stress than under drought stress, while the combination of these stresses increased its expression.

The PPI network and KEGG pathway analyses showed contrasting strategies between the salt-tolerant Gabes-2353 and the salt-sensitive Magna-601 under single and combined stresses. In Gabes-2353, the upregulated networks are dominated by a central hub, Mt7-OMT, which interacts with CADs, PERs, UGTs, CYP84A4, and MEE23, underscoring its pivotal role in secondary metabolism, particularly phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthesis. The phenylpropanoid pathway produces a wide range of secondary metabolites that serve as antioxidants, signaling molecules, and structural components. Under salt stress, plants upregulate phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, increasing the production of phenolic compounds that scavenge ROS, protect cellular components from oxidative damage, and regulate salt stress signaling pathways. Studies in common bean and perennial ryegrass showed that phenylpropanoid metabolism, including enzymes such as 4-coumarate-CoA ligase and caffeoyl-CoA O-methyltransferase, was activated to mitigate salt stress effects.38 Additionally, lignin was found to strengthen cell walls, making them more rigid and less permeable. This structural reinforcement is essential under salinity conditions to prevent cellular damage from ionic and osmotic stress. Increased lignin accumulation and the upregulation of lignin biosynthesis genes are documented as key adaptations in salt-tolerant plants such as Arabidopsis and birch. This leads to enhanced mechanical support, reduces water loss, and contributes to maintaining ion homeostasis under saline environments.19,21,39

Peripheral clusters suggested to have specialized functions in cell wall remodeling (β-glucosidases) and stress signaling (phenylalanine ammonia-lyase-related proteins). The cluster hubs of the downregulated proteins were involved in photosynthesis (Q9LHC4_ARATH), redox regulation (MtRHD11), and the early stress response (MtERD15). This reflects a coordinated suppression of growth-related functions while prioritizing stress adaptation. Gabes-2353 demonstrated superior tolerance to salinity stress compared to Magna-601, as evidenced by a lower reduction in growth parameters and an increased shoot biomass. These phenotypic advantages may be attributed to the upregulation of key KEGG pathways in Gabes-2353, including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and cyanoamino acid metabolism, while 2DEPs related to starch and sucrose metabolism were downregulated. The induction of the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, which is responsible for synthesizing antioxidants such as flavonoids and structural compounds such as lignin, may enhance ROS scavenging and structural reinforcement of cell walls, supporting better stress mitigation. Additionally, the activation of cyanoamino acid metabolism, which leads to the production of cyanogenic glycosides, may enhance osmoprotection.28,38 Starch and sucrose are complex sugars; this downregulation is attributed to the fact that there are enough simple sugars such as glucose and fructose and that there is no need for the Gabes-2353 cells to start breaking down starch and sucrose to produce energy. Under Pm8 infection, Gabes-2353 exhibited a focused upregulation of ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, indicating activation of the protein quality control system, which is responsible for maintaining cellular homeostasis by eliminating misfolded or damaged proteins and modulating immune signaling.39,40 Magna-601 was unable to express the ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, and this response may have enhanced immune regulation and allowed Gabes-2353 to mount a more effective defense against the fungal pathogen. Under combined stresses, Gabes-2553 upregulates MAPK signaling, while no expression was observed in Magna-601. MAPK, a protein kinase, receives pathogen receptors and relays the signal to downstream molecules to initiate key pathways involved in stress signal transduction and defense activation, such as initiating ROS generation and cell death to limit pathogen spread.41 Additionally, the induction of monobactam biosynthesis may represent a defense-like response, as similar enrichments have been observed in other stress-adapted species like peanut and Gliricidia sepium.42,43 The upregulation of lysine biosynthesis further suggests a role in systemic acquired resistance via pipecolic acid production,44 as well as support for osmoprotection through the synthesis of protective amino acids such as proline and glutamate.45

In contrast, Magna-601 exhibited a stress response that is centered on carbohydrate metabolism. Upregulated networks form a dense hub of amylases, β-amylases, isoamylases, DPEs, GWD1, and the central MAAP2 protein, with MEX1 facilitating sugar transport from chloroplasts. These findings suggest that Magna-601 relies mainly on starch mobilization to provide energy, osmoprotectants, and substrates for ROS detoxification. The peripheral nodes include proteins that support plastid and mitochondrial integrity, redox regulation, and transcriptional reprogramming. Additional modules involved nicotinate/NAD metabolism (NAPRT1, MnAPRT2, NIC1-3) and chaperone-mediated stress responses (MHSP90-5, MtPDI). Nicotinate and the NAD biosynthesis pathway, involving enzymes such as NAPRT1, MnAPRT2, and NIC1-3, are crucial in plant immunity. NAD and its metabolites act as cofactors in redox reactions and as signaling molecules that activate defense responses. Elevated NAD levels can induce the expression of pathogenesis-related (PR) genes and increase disease resistance by modulating immune signaling pathways. For example, NAD precursors such as nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) have been shown to activate resistance against fungal pathogens and bacterial phytopathogens by controlling defense signaling and the oxidative stress balance. This metabolism helps regulate reactive oxygen species and the cellular energy status, which are critical for pathogen defense.43,46

Magna-601 showed a more pronounced response to Pm8 infection. Defense-related secondary metabolism was activated, with an upregulation of proteins involved in signaling pathways such as mTOR and PI3K-Akt, both of which are known to regulate cell growth, survival, and stress adaptation.19,36,47 Furthermore, an upregulation of glutathione metabolism (central for ROS detoxification) and oxidative phosphorylation (energy production) was observed,18,29 indicating an energy-intensive defense response.37,48 The increased accumulation of proteins linked to photosynthesis and phenylpropanoid/flavonoid biosynthesis further supports the Magna-601's attempt to maintain energy balance and reinforce its antioxidant and pathogen defense systems. Interestingly, this defense activation comes at the cost of suppressing primary metabolic pathways, including amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, purine metabolism, and starch metabolism. Consistent with these pathway-level changes under the combined salinity-Pm8 infection stress, KEGG enrichment under Pm8 infection and the combined stresses in Magna-601 showed that pathways associated with energy metabolism, amino acid and carbohydrate metabolism, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis (especially phenylpropanoids and flavonoids) were upregulated. These pathways are involved in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism,49 which is essential for plant defense mechanisms,15,50 and essential for photosynthesis regulation.12,51 Notably, pathways related to cell death regulation, including necroptosis and cellular senescence, were also activated under the combined stress, indicating that Magna-601 might approach the threshold of stress tolerance as a damage limitation response. However, the observed downregulation of vitamin (thiamine) metabolism and nucleotide biosynthesis is concerning, given their critical roles in cell division, metabolic balance, and defense gene expression.37,52 This imbalance could reduce Magna-601's immune efficiency, especially under fungal attack.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the combined effect of soil salinity and Pm8 infection had a more detrimental impact on the growth of two contrasting alfalfa varieties than the individual effects. However, the tolerant variety Gabes-2353 was less affected by these stresses than Magna-601. Proteomics analyses showed the upregulation of proteins such as UBA1A, COPA2, and 7-OMTs, highlighting the critical role of ubiquitination, secondary metabolism, and immune signaling in conferring stress resilience. In addition, KEGG annotation indicated that the greater tolerance to salinity in Gabes-2353 was linked to the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, starch and sucrose metabolism, and cyanoamino acid metabolism pathways. In contrast, Magna-601 relies largely on carbohydrate metabolism and energy-intensive defense activation (Figure 7). These findings highlight that the complex interplay between abiotic and biotic stresses requires not only strong defense activation but also the capacity for metabolic adjustment and signaling integration. Key proteins could be valuable candidates for alfalfa breeding selection programs.
