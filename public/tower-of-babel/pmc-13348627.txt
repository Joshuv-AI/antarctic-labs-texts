Parental satisfaction with neonatal care in tertiary hospitals in Nigeria: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Michael Abel Alao, Rasaki Aliu, Jacinta Chinyere Elo-Ilo, Chisom Adaobi Nri-Ezedi, Ayodeji Mathew Borokinni, Hassan Kamiludeen Shina, John Adeboye Oyeboade, Olukemi Oluwatoyin Tongo

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParental satisfaction serves as a vital indicator of quality in neonatal wards and reflects the effectiveness of care delivery. Quality improvement studies generate evidence that can shape best practices and strengthen neonatal services within specific settings. This study examined parental satisfaction with neonatal care and explored service delivery gaps to inform targeted interventions to improve outcomes.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study that evaluated parent–neonate dyads in three tertiary hospitals in Nigeria. Parental experiences were assessed using a modified EMPATHIC-30 questionnaire. Optimal satisfaction was defined as a mean score ≥ 4/5 (80%). Bivariate and multivariable analyses identified predictors of overall satisfaction, reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsA total of 503 mother–baby dyads were enrolled, with a male predominance (311; 61.8%). 64.6% of mothers were primary caregivers, and 76.7% of families were of middle socioeconomic status. Environment and infrastructure had the lowest satisfaction scores (47.1%). Background

Parental satisfaction is a well-established and credible measure of healthcare quality, including in neonatal care [1–3]. In the drive to enhance neonatal services and reduce neonatal mortality, fostering high levels of parental satisfaction is not only essential but also a proven pathway to better care outcomes [1].

Parents are both recipients of care and primary advocates for their newborns. Their active participation in decision-making and their feedback to caregivers are integral to delivering high-quality care. The gold standard in neonatal intensive care units is family-centred care, which enhances care quality by recognising parents and caregivers as the newborn’s central support system [1, 4]. Achieving optimal newborn care requires accurate and timely communication of information [5], as well as consistent physical and emotional support for parents. Actively involving parents in their newborn’s care, including shared decision making, strengthens both care outcomes and parental confidence.

Neonatal care in low and middle-income countries faces persistent challenges, including late presentation, high rates of home deliveries, and an increasing burden of preterm births that often require prolonged or repeated hospitalisation [6]. These pressures are compounded by shortages in the healthcare workforce, driven in part by migration of skilled professionals, and by inadequate infrastructure and technology. Consequently, neonatal services are overstretched, leading to poor adherence to medical advice, underutilisation of healthcare facilities, increased discharge against medical advice, and higher rates of neonatal morbidity and mortality. Many newborns, particularly those discharged against medical advice, require readmission soon after discharge [1, 3].

Based on these challenges, it is critical to ensure that parents remain engaged in the process of caring for their infant and that their experience throughout this process is satisfactory. Parental satisfaction is shaped by multiple domains, including communication, family-centred care, emotional support, clinical competence, professionalism, quality of treatment, infrastructure, and administrative efficiency [7]. Understanding caregiver experiences across these domains helps identify service gaps and guides interventions to improve neonatal outcomes [8].

Currently, evidence-based data on parental satisfaction with newborn care, assessed using validated instruments such as the EMPATHIC framework, remain limited, particularly in relation to its thematic structure of communication with healthcare providers, parental involvement in care, accessibility of caregivers, and confidence in providers’ competence to deliver effective care. The influence of the care environment, which often varies by unit, country setting and income level, has been less explored as a key determinant of parental satisfaction. In Nigeria, most available studies are single-centre reports, underscoring the need for comprehensive research on parental satisfaction with neonatal care in tertiary hospitals.

This study assessed parental satisfaction with neonatal care and sought to identify gaps in service delivery to inform targeted interventions to improve neonatal outcomes. It examined overall satisfaction levels and the socio-demographic factors shaping parents’ experiences. Particular attention was given to parents’ perceptions of communication with healthcare providers, their involvement in decision making, and their views on the strengths and weaknesses of neonatal services. The study also placed significant focus on the influence of the neonatal unit’s physical environment, including cleanliness, comfort, privacy, and organisation, on parental satisfaction. In addition, it explored the extent to which expectations were met and the role of parental participation in shaping overall satisfaction.

Subjects and methods

Study design

This study adopted a cross-sectional design and involved parents or caregivers of neonates admitted to level III neonatal units in Nigeria.

Study sites

The study was conducted across three tertiary hospitals in three of Nigeria’s six geopolitical zones. These included University College Hospital, Ibadan (South West), Federal Teaching Hospital, Gombe (North East), and Nnamdi Azikiwe University Teaching Hospital, Nnewi (South East).

Study population

The study involved caregivers of neonates at newborn wards of selected hospitals in Nigeria.

Data collection instrument and scoring

Parental perceptions and experiences in the newborn unit were evaluated using a modified version of the EMPATHIC-30 Questionnaire [10]. The modified tool comprised 39 items, along with a section capturing the sociodemographic characteristics of the parent–infant dyad. All 30 original items in Emphatic 30 were retained, with some shortened or rephrased, and nine new items (6, 12, 19, 28, 35–39) were added. These changes were made to capture cultural nuances in communication and address specific logistical challenges in Nigerian neonatal units. The questionnaire has been validated in the published literature and has demonstrated good internal consistency, with a reliability index (Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.78–0.93. The updated version of the instrument also has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.98.

Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale. For items 1 to 35, responses ranged from 1 (Extremely Dissatisfied) to 5 (Extremely Satisfied). Items 36 and 37 were scored from 1 (Definitely No) to 5 (Definitely Yes), reflecting parents’ agreement with statements about involvement in care and satisfaction with overall services. Item 38 assessed satisfaction with nurses’ performance, while item 39 evaluated satisfaction with doctors’ performance, both using the 1-to-5 scale described. Overall satisfaction was assessed using a single global question, independent of the responses to the specific domain items.

Data processing and scoring of questionnaire responses

Responses to the 39 evaluative items were collected using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from “extremely dissatisfied” to “extremely satisfied” (or equivalents), scored from 1 to 5. Item scores were converted to percentages by multiplying by 20.

Scores of 80% or higher were deemed optimal or satisfactory, whereas scores below 80% were categorised as suboptimal or unsatisfactory. This cut-off is pragmatic and conforms to best practices in service evaluation and quality improvement processes, where thresholds around 80% are widely used to delineate acceptable or high performance [11–14]. This dichotomisation was applied at the participant level.

The items were grouped into eight domains according to the adapted EMPATHIC framework:

A mean domain score was calculated for each participant by summing all domain scores and dividing by the number of items. Scores ≥ 4 (≥ 80% of the maximum possible) were dichotomised into optimal/satisfied, while scores < 4 were suboptimal/unsatisfied for ease of statistical analysis. A common threshold of 80% was selected as a pragmatic benchmark for high performance. This is consistent with its use in several published quality improvement initiatives, service evaluation audits, and hospital performance metric studies [11, 15].

Reliability of the parental satisfaction scale

The reliability of the instrument was assessed using the Cronbach’s alpha (α) coefficient for each domain (Supplementary Table S3). The analysis showed good internal consistency across the domains as follows: Communication (α = 0.83); Family centered care (α = 0.89); Emotional support and compassion (α = 0.92); Clinical care and professionalism (α = 0.86); Quality of care and treatment (α = 0.92); Environment and infrastructure (α = 0.83); Organization (α = 0.71); Administrative processes (α = 0.83); General satisfaction and recommendation (α = 0.82); and Acceptance of readmission (α = 0.78).

Overall, the scale assessing parental satisfaction with neonatal care demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.98.

Validation of the modified instrument

After the incorporation of nine further items to the EMPATHIC-30 instrument, to accurately represent contextual circumstances. A panel of three neonatologists and two quality improvement specialists assessed the instrument for its relevance, clarity, and simplicity. The aggregated mean scores were 4, 3, and 3, respectively, on a four-point scale for the items, subsequent to preliminary face validation by the expert team. The questionnaire was later pilot tested with 20 parents who were excluded from the final study. Insights garnered through the pilot phase were utilised to enhance and revise the instrument.

Data management and analysis

Data was entered into a Microsoft Access file and analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23. Categorical variables were reported as proportions, presented in frequency tables. Normally distributed continuous variables were summarised as means and standard deviation, while those that are non-Gaussian were summarised as medians and their corresponding interquartile ranges. Differences in proportion were further analysed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable regression analysis was performed using a forced-entry method, with all prespecified covariates entered simultaneously into the model. Factors with p-values less than 0.20 in bivariate analysis, together with variables identified in the literature as associated with parental satisfaction, were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. The variance inflation factor was used to assess multicollinearity and ensure model stability. An approximate ratio of 15 outcome events per predictor variable was applied to minimise the risk of model overfitting. Results were reported as coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Missing data handling

Data were collected through in-person interviews requiring completion of all items. The few missing values observed were not associated with the outcome variables and were excluded from the analysis.

Study reporting

This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies. A completed STROBE checklist has also been provided as a supplementary file.

Results

Social demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 503 baby-mother dyads were recruited from the study. Only 99 neonates (19.7%) presented during the early neonatal period, while the majority (80.3%) presented in the late neonatal period, with a notable male predominance (311; 61.8%). The caregivers were primarily in the early phase of middle adulthood, aged 30 to 36 years. The primary caregivers were mostly mothers (325; 64.6%), followed by both parents (151; 30.0%). Most neonates were referred from the obstetrics department at the admitting tertiary hospitals. In terms of socioeconomic status, most neonates belonged to the middle social class (386; 76.7%); see Table 1.

Parents reported their most positive experiences in the domain of emotional support and compassion. Among them, 139 (27.7%) were satisfied, and 309 (61.7%) were extremely satisfied with the emotional support provided by doctors. Regarding recommendations, 162 parents (32.3%) said they would recommend the newborn wards to others, while 258 (51.4%) stated they would definitely recommend the facility to parents whose neonates might require similar care. The satisfaction with doctors was 161 (32.1%), and 294 (58.6%) were extremely satisfied. For nurses, 172 (34.2%) were satisfied, and 273 (54.3%) were extremely satisfied.

Although satisfaction with both nurses and doctors measured by the adapted EMPATHIC scale was linked to parental satisfaction, satisfaction with nurses’ performance was the strongest independent predictor of overall satisfaction with newborn care in tertiary facilities (aOR = 28.495; 95% CI: 9.234 to 87.931; p < 0.001) after adjusting for confounders (Table 8).

Discussion

African newborns face higher rates of hospital admission and often endure prolonged or repeated stays in the first month of life. Caregivers’ feedback on care quality and satisfaction is rarely collected systematically. Routine collection of this feedback is essential for monitoring, evaluation, and improving service delivery, ultimately improving health outcomes.

Caregivers generally expressed satisfaction with the care provided in this study; several key domains that predicted overall satisfaction received relatively low scores. These important areas, including emotional support, communication, family-centred care, and the admission process, were rated lower compared to other domains such as organisation, clinical care, professionalism, the quality of care and treatment.

The lowest ratings were observed in environmental and infrastructural support, highlighting significant gaps in the provision of comprehensive neonatal care, particularly with regard to funding for healthcare facilities. Infrastructural facilities have been shown to contribute to the satisfaction of parents who attend the hospitals [16–18].

We also found that most admissions occurred during the late neonatal period, with a predominance of male infants. Parental satisfaction was significantly associated with maternal employment status and the newborn’s sex.

The findings indicate that parents and caregivers rated several aspects of the neonatal care environment, including noise levels, space, cleanliness, hygiene, and access to basic amenities, among the least satisfying components of their experience. This is significant given the crucial role the care environment plays in shaping both newborn outcomes and caregiver perceptions. For first-time parents, the neonatal unit is not only a medical space but also an emotional one, where early bonds with their infant are nurtured or hindered by stress, discomfort, and unmet needs. The environment influences how caregiving roles develop, affecting parental confidence, engagement, and the overall quality of newborn care [16–18].

High noise levels have been linked to sleep disturbances and physiological instability in neonates, particularly preterm infants who are more vulnerable to environmental stress [19, 20]. Inadequate space and limited access to essential facilities can hinder parental involvement, increase stress, and contribute to feelings of exclusion. Parents in this study may have perceived such limitations as barriers to participating meaningfully in their infant’s care. Prior research shows that clean, quiet, and well-organised spaces support caregiver engagement and satisfaction, while lapses in hygiene increase the risk of nosocomial infections, a known challenge in neonatal units [21]. These low ratings reflect not only environmental shortcomings but also missed opportunities to strengthen family-centred care and improve both clinical and emotional outcomes for infants and their families.

Growing evidence supports the adoption of individualised neonatal care spaces, which have been associated with reduced cross-infection risk, improved privacy, better spatial organisation, and a greater sense of control for caregivers and families [16]. Our study also found suboptimal family-centred care practices, particularly in parental participation, shared decision-making, and opportunities to remain close to infants. Although family presence was generally encouraged, many caregivers reported limited involvement in clinical discussions or infant care tasks. This disconnect mirrors findings from a 2024 Egyptian study, which showed that family-centred care reduces parental stress only when parents are truly engaged through shared responsibility and active communication [22]. A recent meta-analysis further confirmed that structured family-integrated care models enhance both infant outcomes and parental confidence and well-being [23].

These findings underscore the need to move beyond passive support toward an intentional inclusion of families as partners in care, facilitated by clear policies and staff training. Addressing environmental and infrastructural challenges, as well as gaps in family-centred care, is crucial to fostering a supportive, safe, and empowering neonatal experience for both infants and their caregivers.

Parents and caregivers in our study reported that admission and discharge processes involving Doctors and Nurses require improvement. They described challenges similar to those documented in other low‑ and middle‑income settings, including unclear admission pathways, multiple uncoordinated points for registration and payment, and inadequate discharge planning, which contribute to confusion and fragmented care [24]. These concerns align with recent findings from Montefiore–Weiler Children’s Hospital, where the introduction of structured discharge protocols improved task completion and clarity without compromising caregiver readiness [25]. Similarly, caregivers in other settings have voiced concerns about emotional preparedness and insufficient guidance prior to discharge [26]. These parallels suggest that the challenges identified in our context are not isolated but part of a broader pattern that calls for structured, team-based communication and tailored support for families during transitions of care.

The finding of disparity in satisfaction with newborn care by mothers who were employed compared with those who were unemployed further emphasises the strong role of women’s empowerment in modelling total wellbeing. The observed disparity in satisfaction with newborn care between employed and unemployed mothers in our study underscores the complex role of women’s empowerment in shaping perceptions of care and overall well-being. Employed mothers reported higher satisfaction, which may reflect greater confidence, autonomy, and access to resources that support informed engagement with the healthcare system. This aligns with recent findings from a multicountry analysis, which demonstrated that maternal empowerment, including financial independence and decision-making capacity, was strongly associated with improved maternal and newborn healthcare experiences [27]. Similarly, a 2024 study in sub-Saharan Africa found that employed women were more likely to advocate for their preferences, participate in care decisions, and report respectful treatment in healthcare settings [28, 29]. These findings suggest that enhancing women’s socioeconomic status not only contributes to their personal well-being but also positively influences their interactions with health systems and their satisfaction with neonatal care. Strengthening policies and programs that support women’s employment, and agency could therefore have far-reaching benefits for maternal and newborn health outcomes.

The association between the birth of a female infant and lower parental satisfaction observed in this study is noteworthy and warrants careful consideration. Despite increasing advocacy and public campaigns promoting gender equality, this finding suggests that sociocultural preferences related to child sex may still influence parental perceptions [30]. Several studies have shown that parental sex preferences and prevailing cultural norms can shape attitudes toward childbirth outcomes in parts of Africa and the Middle East [11, 31–33]. Our finding is consistent with the report from Turkey, where having a child of the desired sex was associated with higher parental satisfaction with the birth experience [31]. This observation highlights how gender preferences may influence subjective evaluations of care and outcomes. Similarly, evidence from Nigeria indicates that cultural expectations and family structure continue to shape parental preferences for the sex of children, with a greater proportion of men expressing a preference for male offspring [34]. Such patterns reflect enduring social norms that associate male children with lineage continuity and social security in some communities. These sociocultural expectations may subtly influence how parents interpret their experiences within healthcare settings, even when the objective quality of care is comparable. At the same time, the observed association may also reflect unmeasured contextual factors, including variations in provider–family interactions, family dynamics, or external social pressures. The findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Further research is needed to clarify the extent to which gender-related expectations influence parental perceptions and satisfaction with neonatal care.

The study’s findings of heightened satisfaction with nurses’ performance among parents/caregivers, exceeding that with doctors, may indicate nurses’ superior communication skills, as reported in the literature [35, 36]. The elevated scores may also have resulted from the intersection of related domains, such as emotional support and communication. Emotional support and communication are intricately connected, as effective communication frequently underpins both the provision and the interpretation of successful emotional support [36]. The evaluation of multicollinearity revealed no evidence of problematic correlation among predictors, thereby supporting their concurrent inclusion in the model rather than being a statistical artefact. Consequently, emotional and communicative support mechanisms function synergistically to affect the outcome. Nonetheless, more sophisticated modelling techniques, such as structural equation modelling, may prove beneficial in future studies to further investigate these intricate interrelationships and potential interactions.

Study limitations

This study has some limitations. Its cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference. Parental experiences may have been underestimated due to courtesy bias in healthcare settings. Modifying the primary instrument to capture cultural nuances and logistical challenges in Nigerian neonatal units, as well as the use of consecutive sampling, may have influenced the assessment. Nevertheless, the cordial relationship established with participants and clear explanations emphasising honest responses helped elicit pragmatic feedback. The instrument showed excellent internal consistency, with a reliability index of 0.96, supporting the robustness of the findings.

Conclusion

Parental satisfaction with newborn care in tertiary hospitals in Nigeria was generally high, although meaningful variation was observed across care domains. Lower satisfaction was associated with the physical environment, admission and discharge processes, and key elements of family-centred care, emotional support, communication, and shared decision-making. These findings highlight priority areas for targeted quality improvement to enhance the caregiver experience within neonatal services.
