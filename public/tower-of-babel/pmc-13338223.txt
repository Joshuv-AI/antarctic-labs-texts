The association between food group-based inflammatory score and cardiometabolic disorders in Ganzhou City of China
Authors: Yixing Guo, Yating Luo, Xia Liu, Huan Lai, Yisheng Liu, Xiaomei Cui, Minghua Dong, Xiaoting Luo, Chunmei Wu, Xi Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study systematically evaluated the association between the Food Group-Based Inflammatory Score (FGBIS) and cardiometabolic disorders among adults in Ganzhou City, China. The results demonstrated a significant correlation between FGBIS levels and metabolic abnormalities, with differential distribution patterns observed across sex (P = 0.044) and marital status (P = 0.008) groups. Regression analyses revealed a significant positive association between age and FGBIS, while hypertension status showed an inverse relationship with FGBIS. Gender-stratified analyses further indicated that higher FGBIS levels in males were significantly associated with greater age (β = 0.027), and smoking initiation among non-smoking males substantially associated with higher FGBIS (β = 0.500). In contrast, female hypertensive patients exhibited lower FGBIS levels (β=-0.268). These findings not only reveal distinct demographic patterns in dietary inflammatory effects but also provide important implications for public health practice: targeted anti-inflammatory dietary interventions may effectively mitigate the development of metabolic disorders, and incorporating FGBIS assessment into chronic disease prevention systems could facilitate the development of personalized dietary guidelines, thereby offering scientific evidence for improving population cardiometabolic health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-35645-9.

Introduction

Dietary habits play a pivotal role in shaping an individual’s overall health and quality of life1. A balanced diet, characterized by a diverse array of foods providing adequate energy and essential nutrients, is crucial for maintaining optimal health2. Conversely, an unhealthy diet not only jeopardizes human health but also contributes to malnutrition and other complications, serving as a significant risk factor for noncommunicable chronic diseases3. Consequently, adopting a balanced diet has emerged as a critical dietary practice for promoting well-being and has garnered global attention as a pressing public health concern. China’s dietary landscape is guided by the “Oriental Dietary Pattern,” as outlined in the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (2022). This pattern emphasizes a light and low-salt diet; diverse food sources; abundant consumption of vegetables, fruits, soy products, fish, shrimp, and aquatic products; and daily milk intake4. Notably, the health implications of dietary habits vary significantly, with many diets exhibiting either anti-inflammatory or proinflammatory properties. These effects are largely mediated by specific dietary components that either mitigate or exacerbate inflammation5.

Diet is a well-established modulator of chronic inflammation, a key driver of many non-communicable diseases. To quantify the overall inflammatory potential of an individual’s diet, the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) was developed and validated based on a global literature review of the effects of specific nutrients and food constituents on inflammatory biomarkers6. However, the computation of the standard DII requires detailed nutrient intake data, which is not always available from food frequency questionnaires (FFQs) that primarily collect data on food group consumption. To address this limitation within the context of our study and to tailor the assessment to our specific dietary data, we constructed a Food Group-Based Inflammatory Score (FGBIS). This score serves as a practical and validated proxy measure, adapting the conceptual framework of the DII to operate on the level of whole food groups commonly consumed by our study population. The FGBIS was specifically designed to capture the collective inflammatory potential of the overall diet pattern, providing a holistic tool for investigating diet-inflammation relationships in scenarios where nutrient-based profiling is not feasible. FGBIS provides a comprehensive assessment of dietary inflammation by integrating multiple food variables and utilizing advanced scoring algorithms to generate individualized scores. This method classifies inflammation levels on a continuum from low to high, offering a nuanced understanding of dietary influences on inflammatory responses. The primary goal of the FGBIS is to inform dietary interventions aimed at reducing inflammation, thereby preventing and managing chronic diseases associated with inflammatory processes7. Numerous studies have highlighted the critical role of diet in modulating chronic inflammation. Research has demonstrated strong correlations between dietary patterns and inflammatory biomarkers, indicating that the FGBIS is closely associated with both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors. The regular consumption of anti-inflammatory foods has been shown to reduce the secretion of inflammatory markers, underscoring the potential of dietary strategies to mitigate chronic inflammatory responses8. The validity of the FGBIS has been further supported by studies examining its associations with serologic markers and hyperlipidemia across diverse populations. These studies consistently revealed a significant positive correlation between higher FGBIS and elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), a key marker of systemic inflammation. Additionally, specific dietary components, such as fish and omega-3 fatty acids, have garnered attention for their anti-inflammatory properties and potential to alleviate symptoms of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC)9.

Metabolic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and diabetes, are characterized by insidious onset and progressive trajectories, with shared risk factors such as dyslipidemia, hypertension, and systemic inflammation10. Growing evidence highlights dietary inflammation, quantified via the FGBIS, as a modifiable driver of metabolic disease pathogenesis. Epidemiological studies across diverse populations link proinflammatory diets (elevated FGBIS) to increased risks of type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and subclinical atherosclerosis markers, such as carotid plaque formation11. Mechanistically, inflammatory dietary components disrupt metabolic homeostasis by impairing insulin sensitivity and lipid regulation12. Conversely, anti-inflammatory dietary interventions show promise in mitigating these risks, underscoring the utility of FGBIS-guided strategies for metabolic disease prevention13. This evidence positions dietary inflammation as a critical target for reducing the global burden of metabolic disorders.

Emerging evidence underscores the critical association between pro-inflammatory diets and metabolic disorders, with Food Group-Based Inflammatory Score (FGBIS) increasingly recognized as a valuable tool for evaluating dietary inflammatory potential in chronic diseases such as hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes. However, current FGBIS research demonstrates significant geographical imbalance, with most evidence derived from developed countries while economically underdeveloped regions with distinctive dietary cultures like Ganzhou, China remain understudied. As Jiangxi Province’s largest prefecture-level city with a substantial rural population (52.3% in 2022), Ganzhou exhibits unique dietary characteristics including prevalent consumption of spicy foods, high sodium intake, and traditional fermented products - a dietary pattern that may uniquely influence metabolic health through inflammatory mechanisms. Crucially, residents in such underdeveloped areas generally lack awareness of dietary inflammatory risks, and community-based health education remains inadequate. This study systematically examines the relationship between dietary inflammatory potential and metabolic diseases in Ganzhou, not only addressing a research gap regarding region-specific dietary cultures in China but also providing localized evidence to inform culturally appropriate nutritional interventions, offering important implications for chronic disease prevention in underdeveloped regions with large rural populations.

This study addresses these limitations by pioneering an investigation into FGBIS-associated risks of metabolic diseases (hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes) within southern Jiangxi’s unique dietary context. Our findings hold dual significance: (1) Mechanistically, they clarify how localized dietary inflammation drives metabolic dysregulation, offering novel insights into diet‒pathology interactions. (2) Practically, they provide actionable data to refine culturally adapted dietary guidelines, empowering at-risk populations to mitigate disease through targeted nutritional interventions. By bridging the critical gap between regional dietary practices and metabolic health outcomes, this work advances precision nutrition paradigms and informs public health policies aimed at curbing the escalating burden of metabolic disorders.

Materials and methods

Study population

The survey was conducted through standardized questionnaires and on-site physical examinations, yielding a final valid sample size of 468 participants. We employed a multistage stratified random sampling approach, first categorizing all 18 districts/counties of Ganzhou, Jiangxi Province into three strata based on economic development levels. From each stratum, we randomly selected one district/county. Subsequently, within each chosen district/county, we further stratified all streets/townships into three economic tiers and randomly selected one village/neighborhood committee from each tier. The study population comprised permanent local residents aged 18 years above, excluding individuals with mental illness and pregnant women (Fig. 1).

Information and biomarkers collection

Biomarkers collection

This study was conducted through questionnaire surveys, physical examinations, and biochemical analyses. Face-to-face interviews were performed using a self-designed questionnaire covering health status, smoking and alcohol consumption habits, and related factors. Physical examinations focused on measuring height, weight, and waist circumference. After 8–12 h of fasting, blood samples were collected between 7:00 and 10:00 am and delivered to the laboratory within 12 h for analysis. Biochemical parameters including hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were measured using a Beckman Coulter AU5800 automated biochemical analyzer with the following methods: latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetry, hexokinase method, GPO-PAP method, CHOD-PAP method, catalase elimination method, and surfactant elimination method. All assay kits were provided by medical system companies. Uniformly trained investigators conducted the questionnaire administration and physical measurements, while all laboratory analyses were performed by professional technicians in the clinical laboratory department14.

Height measurement

Height was measured using a stadiometer placed vertically against a wall on a flat surface. Participants were instructed to remove shoes and hats prior to measurement. During measurement, participants stood erect with back straight, looking forward, and with shoulder blades, buttocks, and heels touching the wall. The measurer ensured participants’ eyes were level with the measuring tape for accurate reading, recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Two measurements were taken with ≤ 0.5 cm difference required between readings.

Weight measurement

Weight was measured using an electronic scale placed on a hard, level surface. Participants removed outerwear and shoes before stepping onto the scale. Measurements were recorded after stabilization, repeated twice with ≤ 0.2 kg difference required, recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg.

Waist circumference

A flexible tape measure was used at the midpoint between the lower rib margin and iliac crest. Participants lifted clothing to expose the abdomen. The tape was placed horizontally, snug but not compressing the skin. Measurements were recorded in cm to the nearest mm, with 2 mm subtracted if undergarments were worn.

Hip circumference

A non-stretch tape measured the maximal hip protrusion while participants stood with feet together. The horizontal tape was snug without compression, measured at end expiration. Two measurements were averaged (cm), with participants wearing light clothing. The tape remained parallel to the floor throughout.

Blood pressure

After 15 min seated rest (no caffeine/smoking for 30 min prior), BP was measured using a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer on the right arm. The cuff was placed 2–3 cm above the antecubital fossa at heart level. Korotkoff phases I and V defined SBP and DBP. Two measurements (1–2 min apart) were averaged; if difference > 5 mmHg, a third was taken for final average.

Assessment of the food group-based inflammatory score (FGBIS)

Since nutrient data were not available to calculate the standard Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), we constructed a proxy measure, the Food Group-Based Inflammatory Score (FGBIS), from 14 food groups derived from the food frequency questionnaire to investigate the dietary intake of the subjects. The FGBIS is a method of evaluating the effect of diet on the body’s inflammatory response, aiming to reduce the level of inflammation by adjusting the dietary structure, thus preventing and controlling inflammation-related chronic diseases.

The investigators were trained to investigate the types and amounts of food recently consumed by the subjects. The intake of a food group or nutrient was calculated according to the Chinese Standard Version of the Food Composition Table (6th Edition), and the total FGBIS was calculated on the basis of the average daily intake of nutrients. The FGBIS was calculated according to the algorithm developed by Nitin Shivappa et al. at the University of South Carolina, USA, which summarizes dietary data from representative populations in 11 countries (Shivappa et al., 2018). A total of 45 food parameters were assigned a FGBIS of 1 to 1 on the basis of the potential to alter serum inflammatory markers. On the basis of the potential to alter serum inflammatory markers, 45 food parameters were assigned a FGBIS, with negative FGBIS values indicating anti-inflammatory potential and positive FGBIS values indicating proinflammatory potential15.

Hyperlipidemia

Hyperlipidemia, also known as hyperlipoproteinemia, is a common type of dyslipidemia that manifests as elevated levels of one or more lipids and/or lipoproteins in the blood.

In this study, blood biochemical tests were conducted to diagnose hyperlipidemia among participants. In accordance with the diagnostic criteria of the 11th edition of practical internal medicine (edited by Chen Haozhu), fasting serum total cholesterol (TC) ≥ 5.20 mmol/L, triglyceride (TG) ≥ 1.70 mmol/L, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (HDL-C) ≤ 1.04 mmol/L, and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥ 3.12 mmol/L were used. Hyperlipidemia can be diagnosed when any one of the four criteria is exceeded17.

Hypertension

In this study, blood pressure was measured on-site for all participants. Participants were classified into the hypertension group if they met any of the following criteria: (1) systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg, (2) diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, or (3) current use of antihypertensive medications. The rest were included in the non-hypertensive group18.

Diabetes mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disease that is characterized, among other things, by persistent high blood sugar (hyperglycemia) levels. Normally, insulin is a hormone secreted by the pancreas that contributes to the uptake and utilization of glucose in cells to maintain blood glucose levels within the normal range. In diabetic patients, either because of insufficient insulin secretion (type 1 diabetes) or insufficient cellular response to insulin (type 2 diabetes), blood glucose control is malfunctioning, resulting in hyperglycemia.

In this study, blood biochemical tests were conducted to diagnose diabetes mellitus among participants. The diagnostic criteria published by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) in 2010 were used. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (fasting is defined as no caloric intake for at least 8 h); glycated hemoglobin (A1c) ≥ 6.5% 19.

Glycated hemoglobin

Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) is a product of hemoglobin in red blood cells combined with glucose in the blood. HbA1c is a product of the combination of hemoglobin in red blood cells and glucose in the blood. It can directly and effectively reflect the average situation of diabetic patients’ blood glucose control over the past three months, is not easily affected by fluctuations in blood glucose, and does not need to be taken on a fasting schedule.

HbA1c was ultimately included in the diagnostic criteria for diabetes mellitus in the publication of the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Control of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (2020 Edition) and has become an important indicator of glycemic control, with HbA1c ≥ 6.5% as the cutoff point to assist in the diagnosis of diabetes mellitus20.

Statistical analysis

EXCEL and SPSS27.0 software were used for data collation and analysis. Missing data are replaced by averages. This investigation used chi-square test to compare the differences of various factors in FGBIS quartile grouping. Because FGBIS does not satisfy the normal distribution, and the independent variables contain both continuous variables and categorical variables, generalized linear regression is used for multivariate analysis. In order to evaluate the robustness of the regression results, a number of sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, BMI is adjusted from a categorical variable to a continuous variable. Secondly, the four categorical variables of Marriage, Education, Smoke and Drink are regrouped respectively. Finally, because there are only two types of Occupation groups, the analysis is carried out again after eliminating the confounding factor of Occupation. At the same time, a subgroup analysis of the Gender variable was performed. P < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

G * Power 3.1.9.7 software was used to analyze the post-test efficiency of this study, and the minimum detectable effect size (Effect size f2) was calculated to evaluate the reliability of the overall regression model and subgroup analysis results. The test type was ' Linear multiple regression : Fixed model, R 2 increase ‘, and the significance level was set to α = 0.05 ( two-sided test ).

In the post-effect analysis of the overall model, the Effect size f2 = 0.15, the total sample size of 468 cases, the total number of predictors = 11, and the number of predicted variables tested = 6 were set. The results showed that the actual test efficiency of this study(1-β)≈1, indicating that the overall model has a strong effect detection ability.

In the calculation of the minimum detectable effect size of the overall model, set the test efficiency(1-β)=0.95, input the total sample size of 468 cases, the total number of predictors = 11, the number of tested predictors = 6, and the results show that Effect size f2 = 0.045.

In the calculation of the minimum detectable effect size of the subgroup analysis, the sample size ( male : 138, female : 330 ) was input according to the number of male and female students, the total number of predictive variables = 10, and the number of tested predictive variables = 5. The results showed that the Effect size f2 of the male group was 0.061, and the female group was 0.150.

Results

Basic situation of the survey population: The total number of people included in this survey was 474. After excluding 6 people with incomplete data, a total of 468 people were included in this study, of which 330 were females and 138 were males. The average age was 54.4 ± 10.19 years, with most of them aged 45–65 years. Obese subjects with a BMI ≥ 28.0 accounted for 8.9% of the respondents. The basic information of the population was divided into the above table according to the quartiles of the inflammatory effect index. By chi-square test, there were significant differences among different FGBIS groups in terms of gender (P = 0.044) and marital status (P = 0.008). There were no significant differences among different FGBIS groups in terms of age, weight status, employment status, smoking status and drinking status (all P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study investigated the association between the Food Group-Based Inflammatory Score (FGBIS) and cardiometabolic disorders among adults in Ganzhou City, China, demonstrating a significant relationship between FGBIS and metabolic disorders. Using FGBIS as an evaluation metric, we categorized the local adult population into distinct groups to examine how varying FGBIS levels correlate with the risk of developing lipid profile abnormalities, hypertension, and other metabolic parameters. The results demonstrated statistically significant differences in FGBIS distribution across sex (P = 0.044) and marital status (P = 0.008) groups through chi-square analysis. Regression analysis revealed that hypertension was inversely associated with FGBIS levels in non-hypertensive individuals, while age showed a positive correlation with FGBIS, indicating higher dietary inflammatory potential in older adults. Furthermore, gender-stratified analyses identified that older age was associated with significantly higher FGBIS levels in males (β = 0.027, P = 0.006), with smoking status substantially elevating FGBIS among non-smoking males (β = 0.500, P = 0.008). In contrast, female hypertensive patients exhibited significantly lower FGBIS levels compared to their normotensive counterparts (β=-0.268, P = 0.025).

Gender and marital status effects on dietary inflammation: mechanisms and interventions

The chi-square test results revealed statistically significant differences in FGBIS distribution by gender (P = 0.044) and marital status (P = 0.008), a phenomenon likely attributable to multiple interacting factors. Regarding gender differences, males may exhibit higher pro-inflammatory dietary tendencies due to factors like social drinking and greater red meat consumption, while females may consume more anti-inflammatory foods due to greater health consciousness. The marital status effect may stem from post-marriage lifestyle changes, including regular meal schedules, modifications in home cooking methods, and spousal influences on dietary choices21. These findings suggest several important implications: First, clinical nutrition guidance should be personalized, with particular emphasis on anti-inflammatory diet education for unmarried individuals and male populations. Second, public health departments should consider implementing family-based healthy eating promotion programs to reduce dietary inflammation risks by modifying household food purchasing and cooking habits. Finally, these results highlight the importance of individuals consciously evaluating the inflammatory potential of their dietary patterns - unmarried persons can compensate for lack of spousal supervision by acquiring nutritional knowledge, while married couples can mutually encourage healthier family eating patterns22.

Subgroup analysis demonstrated significant sex-specific differences in factors influencing FGBIS: Among males, advancing age substantially increased FGBIS levels (β = 0.027, P = 0.006), while smoking initiation among non-smoking males dramatically elevated FGBIS (β = 0.500, P = 0.008). These associations may stem from male-specific dietary patterns (e.g., higher red meat and alcohol consumption) combined with smoking’s dual effects on taste perception and food preferences. In contrast, female hypertensive patients showed significant FGBIS suppression (β=-0.268, P = 0.025), potentially attributable to better treatment adherence, more frequent healthy food choices, and estrogen’s anti-inflammatory properties. These findings underscore the necessity for sex-tailored strategies in chronic disease management: males - particularly older smokers - require integrated “smoking cessation plus anti-inflammatory diet” interventions, while female hypertensive patients may benefit from optimized nutritional guidance to enhance treatment efficacy. Furthermore, these results highlight the need for future research to investigate interactions among sex hormones, gender roles, and dietary behaviors to establish a theoretical foundation for precision nutrition interventions23.

Future research should further investigate the specific mechanisms through which these sociodemographic factors influence dietary inflammation, to provide evidence for developing more targeted interventions.

The differential effects of age and hypertension status on FGBIS and the exploration of their mechanisms

This generalized linear regression analysis, adjusted for education level and lifestyle factors, revealed a significant positive association between advancing age and higher FGBIS levels (β = 0.013). Three potential mechanisms may explain this finding: (1) age-related chronic low-grade inflammation promoting pro-inflammatory dietary choices; (2) decreased dietary quality in older adults, such as reduced antioxidant intake; (3) gut microbiota aging affecting nutrient metabolism. Conversely, the observed FGBIS suppression in hypertensive patients (particularly significant in non-hypertensive individuals) may reflect clinical interventions, including: (1) common dietary guidance (e.g., low-sodium, high-fiber diets) for hypertension management; (2) potential anti-inflammatory effects of antihypertensive medications (e.g., ACE inhibitors); (3) survival bias eliminating high-FGBIS hypertensive patients24. Notably, sex, BMI, and dyslipidemia showed no significant associations, suggesting these factors may influence dietary inflammatory potential through more complex pathways (e.g., hormonal levels or insulin resistance) or require larger samples to detect potential nonlinear relationships25. These findings inform targeted nutritional interventions: older adults should be prioritized for anti-inflammatory dietary education, healthcare institutions should strengthen dietary monitoring in hypertensive patients to consolidate treatment effects, and future research should employ longitudinal designs to establish causality and investigate drug-diet interactions26.

Sensitivity analyses confirm robustness of core findings

To evaluate the robustness of regression results, this study conducted multiple sensitivity analyses through various statistical approaches: (1) converting BMI from categorical to continuous variable, (2) recategorizing marital status, education, smoking, and drinking variables, (3) excluding the dichotomous occupational variable, and (4) performing gender-stratified analyses27. The results demonstrated consistent statistical significance and effect directions across all sensitivity analyses for both the positive age-FGBIS association (β = 0.013–0.027) and the inverse hypertension-FGBIS relationship (β=-0.151 to -0.268), confirming the reliability of core findings. This robustness carries three key implications: first, the stable age-FGBIS association across models suggests aging may directly influence dietary choices through mechanisms like chronic low-grade inflammation. Secondly, the persistent FGBIS reduction in hypertensive patients supports the effectiveness of dietary interventions in hypertension management. Finally, gender-stratified consistency provides evidence for developing universal nutritional strategies. These analyses also highlight methodological limitations regarding dichotomous variables like occupational status. Overall, the sensitivity analyses strengthen conclusion validity and provide robust evidence for relationships between age/hypertension status and dietary inflammatory potential.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional study used in this study explored the current status and characterization of cardiovascular chronic diseases among the population in Gannan on the basis of specific time points and time periods, but it was not possible to determine the causal relationship. Additionally, information bias may exist. Participants may be biased in their ability to recall or report their behavior, health status, or exposure. This bias may affect the accuracy and reliability of the data. Third, this condition also fails to capture comorbidities with all other metabolic diseases and is prone to confounding factors.

Notably, our sample covered 18 counties and districts in the Ganzhou city of China, and the sample selection was relatively reasonable. We plan to arrange regular follow-up visits to the survey population in subsequent studies to monitor dietary changes and the status of cardiovascular chronic diseases in the population, which will provide clues to further explore the biological mechanisms involved in FGBIS and chronic cardiovascular diseases. The results of this study may provide new evidence to explain their interrelationships and add new research results to the occurrence and development of FGBIS and chronic cardiovascular diseases.

A key limitation of this study pertains to the measurement of dietary inflammatory potential. It is important to explicitly acknowledge that the Food Group-Based Inflammatory Score (FGBIS) employed here is a proxy measure derived from food groups, not the validated, nutrient-based Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII).While its construction was informed by the conceptual framework of the DII, this methodological adaptation means that our findings are not directly comparable with studies utilizing the standard DII algorithm. Whilst the FGBIS used is a proxy measure and not directly analogous to the comprehensive, nutrient-based DII, it was developed to serve a specific purpose. It is important to note that this food-group-based approach provides a practical and epidemiologically relevant metric for capturing the inflammatory potential of habitual dietary patterns within our cohort, despite precluding direct comparisons with the standard DII.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study investigated the association between the Food Group-Based Inflammatory Score (FGBIS) and cardiometabolic disorders among adults in Ganzhou City, China. The results demonstrated a significant correlation between FGBIS and metabolic abnormalities, with notable distributional differences across sex and marital status groups. The analysis revealed a positive association between age and FGBIS, while hypertension status showed an inverse relationship with FGBIS. Notably, age significantly increased FGBIS levels in males, and smoking substantially elevated FGBIS among non-smoking males. These findings suggest that anti-inflammatory dietary interventions should be prioritized for elderly populations and high-risk males (particularly smokers), integrating nutritional guidance into chronic disease prevention systems. Additionally, the observed FGBIS reduction in female hypertensive patients indicates the potential effectiveness of current dietary interventions in hypertension management, warranting broader implementation in community health programs. Collectively, these results provide scientific evidence for developing differentiated nutrition policies and emphasize the need for precision dietary interventions tailored to demographic characteristics in public health practice.
