Quantization of bounded symplectic domains associated with compact Lie groups


Abstract
We present a systematic quantization scheme for bounded symplectic domains of the form D × G ⊂ T ∗ ​ G D\times G\subset T^{\ast}G , where D ⊂ 𝔤 ∗ D\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\ast} is a bounded region defined by algebraic inequalities and G G is a compact Lie group with Lie algebra 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} . The finiteness of the symplectic volume implies that quantization yields a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, with observables represented by Hermitian matrices, for which we provide an explicit realization. Boundary effects necessitate modifications of the standard von Neumann and Dirac conditions, which usually underlie the correspondence principle. Physically, the compact group G G plays the role of momentum space, while 𝔤 ∗ \mathfrak{g}^{\ast} corresponds to the (noncommutative) position space of a particle. The assumption of compact momentum space has profound physical consequences, including the supertunneling phenomenon and the emergence of a maximal fermion density.

Introduction

Quantization of symplectic manifolds is a central and recurring theme in mathematical physics. Such manifolds naturally serve as phase spaces for classical mechanical systems, and their quantization provides a bridge from classical to quantum mechanics. The most familiar examples arise when the phase space is the cotangent bundle
T^{\ast}M
of a configuration space
M
, whose topology may already be nontrivial. An even richer class of examples comes from constrained Hamiltonian dynamics, where the reduced (physical) phase spaces often exhibit nontrivial global geometry. The multitude of approaches to the quantization problem – commonly labeled as
geometric
,
deformation
,
strict
,
Berezin–Toeplitz
, etc. – makes any comprehensive review impossible here; we therefore refer the reader to the standard monographs
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

The present work is motivated by a different class of examples, where the nontrivial topology of the phase space originates in momentum rather than configuration space. This shift of perspective introduces new features in the quantization problem and reveals novel connections between geometry and quantum theory. Historically, the hypothesis of curved momentum space was first put forward by Born
[
6
,
7
]
and subsequently explored by many authors (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
and references therein). One of the original motivations was the idea that a nontrivial momentum space geometry could act as an effective cut-off, mitigating or even eliminating ultraviolet divergences in quantum field theory.
For instance, if momentum space is compact, then its finite volume provides a natural ultraviolet cut-off.

It was soon realized, however, that the hypothesis of curved momentum space is closely related to the idea of noncommutative spacetime, where particle positions
x^{\mu}
, represented by noncommuting operators, cannot be measured with arbitrary precision. In this picture, spacetime itself acquires a noncommutative structure, replacing sharp localization with a “smeared” notion of a point.
The proposal to suppress ultraviolet divergences through spacetime noncommutativity was first formulated by Snyder
[
13
,
14
]
.

While these early ideas laid the groundwork, renewed interest emerged around the turn of the millennium with the work of Seiberg and Witten
[
15
]
, who studied open strings ending on
D
-branes in a constant
B
-field background. They demonstrated that the low-energy effective theory on a single
D
-brane admits a consistent formulation as a noncommutative gauge theory. This insight sparked an extensive body of research on noncommutative field theory (see, e.g.,
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
) and was accompanied by parallel developments in mathematics, including the theory of deformation quantization, quantum groups, and the broader framework of noncommutative geometry.

By analogy with conventional quantum mechanics, one expects that spacetime noncommutativity in the semiclassical limit can be captured by suitable Poisson brackets.
Applying this semiclassical approximation to noncommutative gauge theories naturally leads to what is known as Poisson gauge theory
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
, bridging classical geometry and its quantum deformations.
Once the spacetime manifold
M
is endowed with a Poisson structure, it is natural to ask about the phase space of a point particle moving in
M
. Following the correspondence principle, this phase space should be a symplectic manifold of dimension
2\dim M
, with Poisson brackets extending those on
M
.

A careful analysis of the minimal coupling of a charged point particle to gauge fields was presented in Refs.
[
23
,
24
]
. This work shows that the natural candidate for the phase space is not an ordinary cotangent bundle but a
symplectic groupoid

\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows M

integrating the Poisson manifold
M
. As a concrete example, let
M=\mathbb{R}^{d-1,1}
be Minkowski space endowed with linear Poisson brackets

\{x^{\mu},x^{\nu}\}=f^{\mu\nu}{}_{\lambda}\,x^{\lambda}\,,

(1.1)

so that the dual space naturally acquires the structure of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with structure constants
f^{\mu\nu}{}_{\lambda}
. The corresponding symplectic groupoid
\mathcal{G}
over
M
is then given by the cotangent bundle

T^{\ast}G
,
where
G
is a Lie group integrating the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. In this construction, the Poisson brackets arise from the canonical symplectic structure on
T^{\ast}G
, thereby providing a
symplectic realization
of the linear Poisson structure on
M
. Using left-invariant vector fields, one identifies
T^{\ast}G\simeq\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\times G
, where the first factor
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
corresponds to the configuration space
M
, while
G
represents the momentum space.
This example explicitly illustrates how spacetime noncommutativity induces a nontrivial geometry on momentum space, curving it according to the underlying Poisson structure. If
\mathfrak{g}
is compact semisimple, the associated Lie group
G
and thus the momentum space are compact, in contrast with conventional classical mechanics, where the momentum space
T^{\ast}_{p}M
at
p\in M
is always linear.

Several mechanical systems on symplectic groupoids
T^{\ast}G\rightrightarrows\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
have been analyzed in recent works
[
25
,
26
]
. These studies indicate that the compactness of momentum space can lead to notable physical consequences. For instance, the velocity of a free (nonrelativistic) particle is bounded from above, provided that the kinetic energy is a smooth function of the momenta. Furthermore, when a particle is subjected to a central potential, it cannot fall to the center, regardless of the strength of the attractive force. Conversely, for any repulsive central force, there exists a finite domain of bounded motion. These results challenge conventional intuition based on flat momentum spaces.

Taking noncommutative spacetime as a working hypothesis, one is naturally led to consider the quantization of the classical phase space given by the symplectic groupoid
\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows M
.
In this work, we focus on a particular instance of this general problem, where the noncommutative configuration space
M
is modeled by a bounded domain
D\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}

in the dual of a compact Lie algebra, endowed with the linear Poisson bracket (
1.1
). Equivalently, we consider a nonrelativistic particle confined to a finite region of the noncommutative space
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. The corresponding momentum space is the compact Lie group
G
, and the resulting phase space is diffeomorphic to the direct product

D\times G
,
carrying the symplectic structure induced by the embedding

D\times G\hookrightarrow T^{\ast}G
.
Since this phase space has finite symplectic volume, any consistent quantization must yield a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, where observables are represented by finite Hermitian matrices. This leads to discrete spectra and bounded physical quantities, in sharp contrast with those of standard quantum mechanics.

In the following sections, we develop the necessary mathematical framework for this quantization and discuss
some of its elementary physical consequences.

2
Quantization on a rectangle and a bounded cylinder

To explain the basic idea, let us start with a simple but instructive example of a rectangular domain on the phase plane with canonical coordinates
x
and
p
. The domain
\mathcal{R}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
is defined by the inequalities:

0<x\leq L\,,\qquad 0\leq p\leq M\,.

(2.1)

We interpret
x
as a position coordinate and
p
as the conjugate momentum. Thus, both the position and momentum spaces are bounded, and the canonical volume (area) of the domain is
LM
.
Classical observables are identified with smooth complex-valued functions on
\mathcal{R}
. These form a Poisson algebra with respect to the canonical Poisson brackets
~{\{p,x\}=1}
. The Poisson algebra
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{R},\mathbb{C})
contains a dense subalgebra
\mathfrak{A}
, multiplicatively generated by the functions
x
and
z=e^{\frac{2\pi ip}{M}}
. As a linear space,
\mathfrak{A}
is spanned by the monomials

x^{n}z^{m}\,,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

(2.2)

By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, every continuous (and hence smooth) function on
\mathcal{R}
can be uniformly approximated by finite linear combinations of monomials (
2.2
).

Upon quantization, the state space of the system, denoted
\mathcal{H}_{N}
, must be finite-dimensional. Indeed, the Bohr–Sommerfeld
quantization rule implies that in the semiclassical limit,

N:=\dim\mathcal{H}_{N}\sim\left[\frac{LM}{2\pi\hbar}\right]\,,\qquad LM\gg\hbar\,.

(2.3)

Here, the square brackets stand for the floor function.
For our purposes, it is convenient to work in the momentum representation and realize
\mathcal{H}_{N}
as a subspace of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of complex-valued functions
\psi(p)
. The Hermitian inner product is defined in the standard way:

(\psi,\psi^{\prime})=\frac{1}{M}\int_{0}^{M}dp\bar{\psi}(p)\psi^{\prime}(p)\qquad\forall\psi,\psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}\,.

(2.4)

Then the functions
\psi_{n}=z^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, form an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
:

(\psi_{n},\psi_{m})=\delta_{nm}\,.

(2.5)

By analogy with canonical quantization, we set

\hat{x}=-i\hbar\frac{d}{dp}\,.

(2.6)

Integration by parts shows that
\hat{x}
defines a self-adjoint operator provided that the wave functions satisfy the periodic boundary condition

\psi(0)=\psi(M)\,.

(2.7)

However, this is not the only condition we need to impose on admissible states. Indeed, applying the operator
\hat{x}
to the basis functions
\psi_{n}
, we find

\hat{x}\psi_{n}=x_{n}\psi_{n}\,,\qquad x_{n}=\frac{2\pi n\hbar}{M}\,,

(2.8)

meaning that
\psi_{n}
is an eigenstate of the position operator with the definite coordinate value
x_{n}
. Since the motion of the particle is confined to the interval
(0,L]
, we arrive at the inequalities

1\leq n\leq N\,,

(2.9)

where
N
is given by Eq. (
2.3
), together with

\qquad\frac{LM}{2\pi(N+1)}\leq\hbar\leq\frac{LM}{2\pi N}\,.

(2.10)

Hence, only the eigenstates
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\ldots,\psi_{N}
are admissible. By the superposition principle, these span a state space
\mathcal{H}_{N}
of dimension
N
, in agreement with Eq. (
2.3
).

Instead of quantizing momentum coordinate
p
, we may consider the quantization problem for Laurent polynomials
f(z)
, which are just specific functions of
p
. If the state space were the whole
\mathcal{H}
, we could apply the recipe of canonical quantization and define the action of
\hat{f}
on
\psi(p)
as multiplication by
f(z)
. This definition, however, would generally yield a function
f(z)\psi(p)\notin\mathcal{H}_{N}
. Therefore, we introduce the Hermitian projector
P_{N}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{N}
and define the operator
\hat{f}
by the rule:

\hat{f}\psi=P_{N}f(z)\psi\,.

(2.11)

It is easy to see that

\hat{f}^{\dagger}=\hat{\bar{f}}
and the operator
\hat{f}
is Hermitian for real-valued functions
f(z)
. It is convenient to introduce the indicator function

\chi_{N}(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1\,,&1\leq n\leq N;\\
0\,,&\mbox{otherwise}\,.\end{array}\right.

(2.12)

Then

\widehat{z^{m}}\psi_{n}=\chi_{N}(n+m)\psi_{n+m}\,.

(2.13)

Because of the projector in the definition (
2.11
),

\widehat{z^{n}}\widehat{z^{m}}\neq\widehat{z^{n+m}}\neq\widehat{z^{m}}\widehat{z^{n}}

(2.14)

in general. However, these equalities hold whenever
nm\geq 0
. In particular,

\widehat{z^{n}}=\hat{z}{}^{n}
.
We can now extend this quantization rule to general monomials in the
x
’s and
z
’s as

\widehat{x^{n}z^{m}}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\hat{x}^{n}\widehat{z^{m}}+\widehat{z^{m}}\hat{x}^{n}\big)

(2.15)

and then to arbitrary elements of
\mathfrak{A}
by linearity. It follows from the definition that

[\hat{x},\hat{z}]=i\hbar\widehat{\{x,z\}}\,,

(2.16)

which is a manifestation of the
correspondence principle
in Dirac’s sense. However,
~{[\hat{z},\hat{\bar{z}}]\neq 0}
, in contrast to the Poisson brackets
\{z,\bar{z}\}=0
.
Formula (
2.15
) defines a map

\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{\mathcal{R}}:\mathfrak{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{N})

(2.17)

from the Poisson algebra of classical observables to the algebra of operators on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. We will refer to
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{\mathcal{R}}
as a
quantization map
. Since the algebra
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
is finite-dimensional, the quantization map is far from being injective and much information about
\mathfrak{A}
is lost on the operator side. This information can hopefully be restored in the classical limit as
\hbar\to 0
and
N\to\infty
.
To trace the classical limit carefully, we make
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
into normed spaces. In both cases, the norm is introduced through a positive-definite inner product. For classical observables, we can use the canonical measure on the rectangle
\mathcal{R}
to define the inner product

(a,b)_{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{1}{LM}\int_{\mathcal{R}}\overline{a(x,p)}b(x,p)dxdp\,,\qquad\forall a,b\in\mathfrak{A}\,,

(2.18)

and for the operator algebra, we take the
normalized
Frobenius inner product

(A,B)_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)\,,\qquad\forall A,B\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{N})\,.

(2.19)

The corresponding norms are defined as

\|a\|_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}=(a,a)_{\mathcal{R}}\,,\qquad\|A\|^{2}_{N}=({A},{A})_{N}\,.

(2.20)

They satisfy all standard properties, including positive-definiteness

not
submultiplicative; rather, it satisfies the inequality
\|AB\|_{N}\leq\sqrt{N}\|A\|_{N}\|B\|_{N}
. Consequently,
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
is not a Banach algebra unless
N=1
. Nonetheless, this norm proves to be extremely useful for studying the asymptotic behaviour of large matrices

]
,

.
. The normalization is chosen such that
\|1\|_{\mathcal{R}}=1
and
\|1\!\!1\|_{N}=1
. Using an orthonormal basis
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\in\mathcal{H}_{N}
, one can also express the normalized Frobenius norm in terms of the usual operator norm:

\|A\|^{2}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|A\psi_{k}\|^{2}\,.

(2.21)

Proposition 1
.

The quantization map (
2.17
) satisfies the following properties:

i)

Normalization:

\hat{1}=1\!\!1\qquad(\mbox{\it identity operator on }\mathcal{H}_{N})\,,

ii)

Reality:

\widehat{\bar{a}}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\,,

iii)

Separability:

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\|\hat{a}\|_{N}=\|a\|_{\mathcal{R}}\,,

iv)

von Neumann’s condition:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|\hat{a}\hat{b}-\widehat{ab}\|_{N}=0\,,

v)

Dirac’s condition: for any pair
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}
, there exists a subspace
\mathcal{H}^{a,b}\subset\mathcal{H}_{N}
such that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\dim\mathcal{H}^{a,b}}{N}=1

and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\big\|\big((i\hbar)^{-1}[\hat{a},\hat{b}]-\widehat{\{a,b\}}\big)\psi\big\|=0\qquad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{a,b}\,.

Properties (
i
) and (
ii
) follow immediately from the definition of the quantization map.
The proof of (
iii
-
v
), which involves more technical arguments, is deferred to Appendix A. Several remarks are now in order.

Remark 2.22
.

Properties (
i
-
iv
) resemble the defining conditions of
strict quantization

[
29
,
30
,
4
]
, but they are not quite the same. The lack of submultiplicativity for the norm (
2.19
) prevents us from treating
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
as a
C^{\ast}
-algebra (or even as a Banach algebra). Moreover, replacing the normalized Frobenius norm with the operator norm
\|\cdot\|
would violate the von Neumann condition, as shown in Appendix
A
.

Remark 2.23
.

Condition (
iii
) implies that the quantization map
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{\mathcal{R}}
is asymptotically injective and induces a family of seminorms
|a|_{N}:=\|\hat{a}\|_{N}
on
\mathfrak{A}
that separates points. It also ensures that, in the classical limit, the root-mean-square values of a physical observable coincide whether evaluated on the classical phase space or on the quantum spectrum. This is a natural and desirable feature from a physical point of view. Since the Hermitian inner product is completely determined by the associated norm, we further obtain

\lim_{N\to\infty}(\hat{a},\hat{b})_{N}=(a,b)_{\mathcal{R}}\,,\qquad\forall a,b\in\mathfrak{A}\,.

(2.24)

Remark 2.25
.

Compared to strict deformation quantization and related approaches, Dirac’s condition (
v
) holds in a weaker sense – namely, for “almost all” states in the sense of dimension. The “exceptional” states are represented by the elements of the quotient space
~{\mathcal{H}_{N}/\mathcal{H}^{a,b}}
, whose dimension becomes negligible compared to
\mathcal{H}^{a,b}
in the classical limit
\hbar\rightarrow 0
.

Remark 2.26
.

By applying the triangle inequality to the normalized Frobenius norm, one finds that the quantum observables asymptotically commute:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|\hat{a}\hat{b}-\hat{b}\hat{a}\|_{N}=0\,.

(2.27)

Remark 2.28
.

The periodic boundary condition (
2.7
) imposed on the wave functions allows us to interpret the momentum space as a circle of circumference
M
. Consequently, all formulas above remain valid and lead to a quantization on the bounded cylinder
S^{1}\times I
.

Observe that the von Neumann and Dirac conditions of Proposition
1

\mathcal{H}_{N}=P_{N}\mathcal{H}
not as separate Hilbert spaces, but rather as a family of nested subspaces of a single Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

The choice of ambient space
\mathcal{H}
for the family
\{\mathcal{H}_{N}\}

L^{2}(S^{1})

C^{k}(S^{1},\mathbb{C})

\psi(p)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{m}e^{\frac{2\pi imp}{M}}

with

.

Within this framework, the following result provides additional insight into how operator products and commutators behave in the classical limit.

Proposition 2
.

Let the position space be the interval
[u,v]
with
u,v\neq 0
. Then,
for any

and

, we have the following limits:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\big\|(\hat{a}\hat{b}-\widehat{ab})P_{N}\psi\big\|=0\,,\qquad\lim_{N\to\infty}\big\|\big({(i\hbar)^{-1}}[\hat{a},\hat{b}]-\widehat{\{a,b\}}\big)P_{N}\psi\big\|=0\,.

(2.29)

Example 2.30
.

The first nontrivial case of quantization corresponds to the two-level system with

~{\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}}
and

\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\,,\qquad\widehat{z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\,,\qquad\widehat{\bar{z}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\,,\\[19.91692pt]
\widehat{z^{n}}=0\,,\qquad n\neq\pm 1\,,\qquad\displaystyle\hat{x}=\frac{2\pi\hbar}{M}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&2\end{array}\right)\,.\end{array}

(2.31)

The quantization map
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{\mathcal{R}}:\mathfrak{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
is surjective and the operators (
2.31
) span the entire algebra of complex
2\times 2
matrices
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
.
In contrast to the classical Poisson brackets
\{z,\bar{z}\}=0
,

[\widehat{z},\widehat{\bar{z}}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right)\neq 0\,.

(2.32)

We see that the compactness of the momentum space has far-reaching physical implications. Most importantly, the Hilbert space of states becomes finite-dimensional whenever the particle is confined to a bounded interval. Consequently, the spectrum of the position operator is finite and equidistant: there are a finite number of spatial points where the particle can be observed through measurements. Below, we consider two more interesting consequences of compactness.

2.1
Supertunneling

So far we have discussed a quantum particle confined within a connected, bounded interval. Now, let us consider a configuration space consisting of two disjoint segments,
I_{1}=[0,x_{1})
and
I_{2}=(x_{2},x_{3}]
, separated by the intermediate segment
I=[x_{1},x_{2}]
. The corresponding phase space is given by the disjoint union

I_{1}\times[0,M]\bigsqcup I_{2}\times[0,M]\,.

Suppose that, initially, the particle is located in
I_{1}
with probability
1
. What, then, is the probability of finding the particle in the segment
I_{2}
after some time
t
?

To simplify the analysis, we assume that the segments
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are sufficiently small to support only a single quantum state, i.e.,

x_{1}=x_{3}-x_{2}=\frac{2\pi\hbar}{M}\,.

Choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2\pi\hbar n/M\in I_{2}
. By construction, we have
n\geq|I|M/2\pi\hbar
. The initial state is then described by the wave function
\psi_{0}(p)=1
, and the wave function
~{\psi_{n}(p)=e^{\frac{2\pi inp}{M}}}
corresponds to the particle being located at
I_{2}
. These wave functions span the two-dimensional state space of the system. The classical Hamiltonian of a free particle is defined by its kinetic energy, which is assumed to be a smooth function of the particle’s momentum:

H(p)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}H_{k}z^{k}\,,\qquad z=e^{\frac{2\pi ip}{M}}\,.

(2.33)

Since

\|H\|^{2}_{\mathcal{R}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|H_{k}|^{2}<\infty\,,

(2.34)

it follows that
H_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\pm\infty
. For the two-state system under consideration, the quantum Hamiltonian is given by the operator

\hat{H}=H_{0}+H_{n}\widehat{z^{n}}+\bar{H}_{n}\widehat{z^{-n}}\,,

(2.35)

and the evolution operator takes the form

\hat{U}(t)=e^{-\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}}=e^{-\frac{itH_{0}}{\hbar}}\left(\cos(\omega_{n}t)1\!\!1-i\frac{\sin(\omega_{n}t)}{|H_{n}|}(\hat{H}-H_{0})\right)\,,\qquad\omega_{n}=\frac{|H_{n}|}{\hbar}\,.

(2.36)

The transition probability is given by the standard formula

P_{0\rightarrow n}(t)=|\langle\psi_{n}|\hat{U}(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle|^{2}=\sin^{2}(\omega_{n}t)\,.

(2.37)

During time evolution, the system oscillates between the two possible states, with the probability oscillating accordingly.
If the distance
|I|
between the segments
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
remains fixed, then in the decompactification limit
M\rightarrow\infty
, it follows that
n\rightarrow\infty
and
\omega_{n}\rightarrow 0
. As a result, no transition between the two states is possible (
P_{0\rightarrow n}=0
) unless the momentum space is compact. This result is consistent with physical intuition: neither classical nor quantum particles can penetrate an infinitely high potential barrier of finite thickness. Such tunneling, however, becomes possible for a quantum particle with compact momentum space. This explains why we refer to this phenomenon – a nonzero transition amplitude – as
supertunneling
. The qualitative picture does not change if we broaden the intervals
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
to accommodate additional particle states. This situation bears some resemblance to relativistic quantum field theory. The inability to localize a relativistic particle within a finite region of space ultimately stems from the existence of a maximal velocity, just as the supertunneling phenomenon arises from the upper bound on the particle’s momentum.

It is also instructive to compare this result with the transition amplitude between the states
\psi_{0}
and
\psi_{n}
, assuming that the configuration space is the entire real line
\mathbb{R}
, with no gaps. For simplicity, we focus on a particular Hamiltonian of the form (
2.33
), namely,

H=E\left(1-\frac{z+\bar{z}}{2}\right)\,,\qquad z=e^{\frac{2\pi ip}{M}}\,,

(2.38)

where
E
is a constant with units of energy. On the entire real line, the evolution operator is expressed as

\hat{U}(t)=e^{-\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}}=e^{-it\omega}\Big[J_{0}(t\omega)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}i^{k}J_{k}(t\omega)\big({z^{k}}+{z^{-k}}\big)\Big]\cdot\,,

(2.39)

where
\omega=E/\hbar
and
J_{k}(w)
are the Bessel functions of the first kind. The probability of transition is given by

P_{0\rightarrow n}(t)=J^{2}_{n}(t\omega)\,.

(2.40)

Using the asymptotic form of the Bessel functions, we find

P_{0\rightarrow n}(t)\sim\frac{2}{\pi t\omega}\cos^{2}\left(t\omega-\frac{n\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\,,\qquad t\omega\gg n^{2}\,.

(2.41)

As before, the probability oscillates, but its amplitude gradually decreases over time, and the oscillation frequency
\omega
is independent of the spatial separation between
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. Thus, whenever the momentum space is compact, the particle has a nonzero probability of jumping from the origin
x=0
to a point in
I_{2}
, except at certain times.
To highlight the contrast with conventional quantum mechanics, let us set
E=M^{2}/4\pi^{2}m
, where
m
is the mass of the particle. Then

H=\frac{M^{2}}{4\pi^{2}m}\Big(1-\cos\Big(\frac{2\pi p}{M}\Big)\Big)=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{M^{2}}\Big)\,.

(2.42)

For sufficiently small momenta (
|p|\ll M
), the particle obeys the standard quadratic energy-momentum relation, which becomes exact in the decompactification limit
M\rightarrow\infty
. In this limit,
\omega\rightarrow\infty
and
P_{0\rightarrow n}\rightarrow 0
. Again, this agrees with the conventional quantum picture: the probability of a particle transitioning from a given point to any finite interval vanishes, since the wave function becomes spread uniformly along the real line
[
31
]
.

2.2
Degenerate Fermi gas

As another example, consider the gas of free Fermi particles confined to the interval
(0,L]
at zero temperature. All particles are assumed to be scalar, noninteracting, and indistinguishable. The dynamics of each particle are governed by the Hamiltonian (
2.42
). As before, let
N
denote the dimension of the single-fermion state space
\mathcal{H}_{N}
, and let
\mathcal{N}
represent the total number of fermions. In the basis
\big\{\psi_{n}(p)=z^{n}\big\}_{n=1}^{N}
, the quantum Hamiltonian takes the form of a tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix
3
3

3

Curiously, the matrix is proportional to the Cartan matrix of type
A_{N}
.
:

\hat{H}=\frac{E}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}2&-1&0&\cdots&0\\
-1&2&-1&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&-1&2&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&-1\\
0&\cdots&0&-1&2\end{array}\right)\,,\qquad E=\frac{M^{2}}{4\pi^{2}m}\,.

(2.43)

The eigenvalues of this matrix determine the energy spectrum of a single fermion. To compute the spectrum, we introduce the
N\times N
matrix
\hat{T}
via

\hat{H}=E\big(1\!\!1-\frac{1}{2}\hat{T}\big)

(2.44)

and set
P_{N}(\lambda):=(-1)^{N}\det(\hat{T}-\lambda 1\!\!1)
. Expanding the last determinant along the first row yields the relation

P_{N+1}(\lambda)=\lambda P_{N}(\lambda)-P_{N-1}(\lambda)\,,

(2.45)

in which one may recognize the recurrence relation for the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind on the interval
[-2,2]
. An alternative definition is

P_{n}(2\cos\phi)=\frac{\sin\,(n+1)\phi}{\sin\,\phi}\,.

(2.46)

In particular,

P_{0}=1\,,\qquad P_{1}=\lambda\,,\qquad P_{2}=\lambda^{2}-1\,,\qquad P_{3}=\lambda^{3}-2\lambda\,,\qquad P_{4}=\lambda^{4}-3\lambda^{2}+1\,.

(2.47)

The roots of the
n
-th Chebyshev polynomial follow directly from Eq. (
2.46
):

\lambda_{k}=2\cos\,\frac{k\pi}{n+1}\,\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,n\,.

(2.48)

This leads to the expression

P_{n}(z)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Big(z-2\cos\,\frac{k\pi}{n+1}\Big)\,.

(2.49)

Using Rel. (
2.44
), it is easy to express the energy levels of a free particle through the roots (
2.48
) of Chebyshev’s polynomials
4
4

4

An interesting discussion of Cartan matrices and Chebyshev polynomials can be found in
[
32
]
.
:

E_{k}=E\big(1-\cos k\theta_{N}\big)\,,\qquad\theta_{N}=\frac{\pi}{N+1}\,,\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,N\,.

(2.50)

The Pauli exclusion principle sets an upper bound on the number of identical fermions that can be accommodated within a segment
(0,L]
, namely

\mathcal{N}\leq N=LM/2\pi\hbar
. This gives rise to the
maximal density

n_{\mathrm{max}}
: if
n=\mathcal{N}/L
denotes the density of the 1D Fermi gas, then

n\leq n_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{N}{L}=\frac{M}{2\pi\hbar}\,,\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\frac{n}{n_{\mathrm{max}}}=\frac{\mathcal{N}}{N}\leq 1\,.

(2.51)

The total energy of the degenerate Fermi gas is given by the sum

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle U(\mathcal{N})=\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{N}}E_{k}=E\left(\mathcal{N}+1-\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k=0}^{\mathcal{N}}e^{ik\theta_{N}}\right)=E\left(\mathcal{N}+1-\mathrm{Re}\frac{1-e^{i(\mathcal{N}+1)\theta_{N}}}{1-e^{i\theta_{N}}}\right)\\[19.91692pt]
\displaystyle=E(\mathcal{N}+1)-\frac{E}{2}\left(1+\frac{\cos{\mathcal{N}\theta_{N}}-\cos(\mathcal{N}+1)\theta_{N}}{1-\cos{\theta_{N}}}\right)\\[19.91692pt]
\displaystyle=E\left(\mathcal{N}+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\frac{E}{2}\left(\cos{\mathcal{N}\theta_{N}}+\sin\mathcal{N}\theta_{N}\,\mathrm{ctg}\left(\frac{\theta_{N}}{2}\right)\right)\,.\end{array}

(2.52)

In particular, the maximum energy that can be stored in the Fermi gas is equal to

U_{\mathrm{max}}=U({N})=E{N}\,.

For the case

N\gg\mathcal{N}\gg 1
, the energy can be approximated as

U(\mathcal{N})\approx E\mathcal{N}+\frac{E}{4}\left(\frac{\mathcal{N}}{N}\right)^{2}-\frac{EN}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi\mathcal{N}}{N}\approx\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\frac{E\mathcal{N}^{3}}{N^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\frac{\hbar^{2}\mathcal{N}^{3}}{mL^{2}}\,,

(2.53)

from which the degeneracy pressure of the dilute fermion gas follows as

P=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial L}\approx\frac{\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}{3m}n^{3}\,.

(2.54)

Thus, the pressure scales as the cube of density, as expected for the 1D Fermi gas with an approximately quadratic dispersion relation (
2.42
). In the general case,
P
is a function of both the concentration
n
and the number of available states
N
(or equivalently, the system size
L
). In the thermodynamic limit
N\rightarrow\infty
, however, the explicit dependence on
L
disappears, and the pressure reaches its maximum at the highest possible concentration (see Eq. (
2.58
) below).

It is instructive to compare these results with semiclassical computations based on the Hamiltonian (
2.42
). In the semiclassical approximation, the total energy of the Fermi gas is given by the integral

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle U=\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int^{p_{F}}_{-p_{F}}H(p)dpdx=\frac{L}{2\pi\hbar}\int_{-p_{F}}^{p_{F}}\frac{M^{2}}{4\pi^{2}m}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi p}{M}\right)\right)dp\\[19.91692pt]
\displaystyle=\frac{M^{2}L}{4\pi^{3}\hbar m}\left(p_{F}-\frac{M}{2\pi}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi p_{F}}{M}\right)\right)\,,\end{array}

(2.55)

where the Fermi momentum
p_{F}
is determined by the Bohr–Sommerfeld condition:

\mathcal{N}=\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int_{-p_{F}}^{p_{F}}dpdx=\frac{p_{F}L}{\pi\hbar}\,.

(2.56)

Substituting this into (
2.55
), we get

U=\frac{M^{2}\mathcal{N}}{4\pi^{2}m}-\frac{LM^{3}}{8\pi^{4}\hbar m}\sin\left(\frac{\pi\mathcal{N}}{N}\right)\,,

(2.57)

from which the degeneracy pressure follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}P=\displaystyle-\frac{\partial U}{\partial L}&=&\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial L}\left[\frac{LM^{3}}{8\pi^{4}\hbar m}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi^{2}\hbar\mathcal{N}}{LM}\right)\right]\\[14.22636pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{M^{3}}{8\pi^{4}\hbar m}\left[\sin\Big(\frac{\pi\mathcal{N}}{N}\Big)-\Big(\frac{\pi\mathcal{N}}{N}\Big)\cos\Big(\frac{\pi\mathcal{N}}{N}\Big)\right]\,.\end{array}

(2.58)

Setting
\mathcal{N}=N
yields the maximum pressure
P_{\mathrm{max}}=M^{3}/8\pi^{3}\hbar m
. For
\mathcal{N}\ll N
or in the decompactification limit
M\to\infty
, we recover the cubic law (
2.54
).

3
More general symplectic domains

A bounded cylinder
S^{1}\times I
, discussed in Sec.
2
, may be viewed as a bounded domain in the cotangent bundle of the abelian group
U(1)
. Using elementary harmonic analysis on
U(1)
, we constructed a quantization of the phase space
S^{1}\times I
, leading to a family of finite-dimensional algebras of Hermitian operators. In this section, we generalize this quantization technique to bounded domains associated with arbitrary compact Lie groups.

3.1
Classical observables

Let
T^{\ast}G\simeq G\times\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
be the cotangent bundle of a compact Lie group
G
and let
D
be an open domain in
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. If
\dim G=d
, then
G\times D\subset T^{\ast}G
is an open submanifold of dimension
2d
with symplectic structure obtained by restricting the canonical symplectic form on
T^{\ast}G
. We regard the direct product
M=G\times D
as the phase space of a classical mechanical system, with
D
interpreted as the position space and
G
as the momentum space. The corresponding Poisson brackets are defined by

\{f,h\}=0\,,\qquad\{f,x_{a}\}=\ell_{a}f\,,\qquad\{x_{a},x_{b}\}=-f_{ab}^{c}x_{c}\,.

(3.1)

Here
f,h\in C^{\infty}(G)
,
x_{a}
are Cartesian coordinates on
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, and
\ell_{a}
form the dual basis of left-invariant vector fields on
G
, satisfying the commutation relations
[\ell_{a},\ell_{b}]=f_{ab}^{c}\ell_{c}
. In the case where
G
is semisimple, we choose the basis
\ell_{a}
in such a way that the Cartan–Killing metric
g_{ab}=f^{c}_{ad}f^{d}_{bc}
is given by the negative Kronecker’s
-\delta_{ab}
.

Denote by
\mathcal{F}(G)
the algebra of representative functions on
G
. By definition, this algebra is spanned by the matrix elements of unitary irreducible representations of
G
. If
\hat{G}
denotes the set of all equivalence classes of irreducible representations of
G
, then a generic element of
\mathcal{F}(G)
can be written as

f(g)=\sum_{[\pi]\in\hat{G}}f_{\pi}^{ij}\pi_{ij}(g)\,,

(3.2)

where
\big(\pi_{ij}(g)\big)
are the matrix elements of a representation
\pi:G\rightarrow GL(V)
and only finitely many coefficients
f_{\pi}^{ij}\in\mathbb{C}
are nonzero. Let
\mathrm{Pol}(\mathfrak{g}^{\ast})
denote the algebra of polynomial functions on
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, and
\mathrm{Pol}(D)
its restriction to the domain
D\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
.
The Poisson algebra of smooth complex-valued functions on
M
contains a subalgebra
\mathfrak{A}=\mathcal{F}(G)\otimes\mathrm{Pol}(D)
, whose elements are given by finite linear combinations of monomials

f(g)x_{a_{1}}\cdots x_{a_{n}}\,,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,,\quad f\in\mathcal{F}(G)\,.

(3.3)

We identify
\mathfrak{A}
with the
Poisson algebra of classical observables
.

By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem,
\mathfrak{A}
is dense in the space
C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{C})
with respect to the supremum norm
|\cdot|_{\infty}
. For our subsequent calculations, it will be more convenient to introduce another norm on

\mathfrak{A}
, which we call the
R
-norm
. It is defined as follows. Using multi-index notation, we can write each element of
\mathfrak{A}
as

a=\sum_{\bf{n}}a_{\bf n}(g)x^{\bf{n}}\,,

(3.4)

where
{\bf n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})
and
x^{\bf n}:=x_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots x^{n_{d}}_{d}
. Given
R>0
, we define

|a|_{R}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}|a_{\mathbf{n}}|_{\infty}R^{|\mathbf{n}|}\,,

(3.5)

where
|\mathbf{n}|:=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d}
. The
R
-norm satisfies all the necessary properties (positivity, homogeneity, subadditivity, and submultiplicativity), thereby turning
\mathfrak{A}
into a normed commutative algebra. Suppose the domain
D\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
is bounded and entirely contained in the ball of radius
R
centered at
0\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. The minimal
R
with this property will be called the
radius of the domain
D
. Clearly, for any such domain,
|a|_{\infty}\leq|a|_{R}
.

In addition to the norm topology, the algebra
\mathfrak{A}
enjoys a bifiltration determined by the natural filtrations of
\mathrm{Pol}(D)
and
\mathcal{F}(G)
. The filtration
in
\mathrm{Pol}(D)
is the standard filtration by monomial degree:

\mathrm{Pol}^{n}(D)\subset\mathrm{Pol}^{n+1}(D)\,,\qquad\mathrm{Pol}^{n}(D)\cdot\mathrm{Pol}^{m}(D)\subset\mathrm{Pol}^{n+m}(D)\,,

(3.6)

where the subspace
\mathrm{Pol}^{n}(D)
is spanned by all polynomials of degree less than or equal to
n
.

To introduce the second filtration, we need some additional terminology. Recall that all finite-dimensional (not necessarily irreducible) representations of a compact Lie group
G
(considered up to equivalence) form a semiring
\mathfrak{R}(G)
, with direct sum and tensor product as addition and multiplication, respectively.
If
G
is simply connected (and hence semisimple), then each representation
\pi\in\mathfrak{R}(G)
both defines and is defined by a finite-dimensional representation
\dot{\pi}
of the corresponding Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. The latter, in turn, is completely determined by its character:

\mathrm{ch}(\dot{\pi})=\sum_{\lambda\in P}m_{\lambda}e^{\lambda}\,.

(3.7)

Here
P=P(\dot{\pi})
is the system of weights of the representation
\dot{\pi}
, and
m_{\lambda}
is the multiplicity of the weight
\lambda\in P
. Recall that the weights of
\mathfrak{g}
form a lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
in the dual space to the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
, and that the dominant weights constitute a sublattice
\Lambda^{+}\subset\Lambda
. The additive and multiplicative properties of the character,

\mathrm{ch}(\dot{\pi}_{1}\oplus\dot{\pi}_{2})=\mathrm{ch}(\dot{\pi}_{1})+\mathrm{ch}(\dot{\pi}_{2})\,,\qquad\mathrm{ch}(\dot{\pi}_{1}\otimes\dot{\pi}_{2})=\mathrm{ch}(\dot{\pi}_{1})\cdot\mathrm{ch}(\dot{\pi}_{2})\,,

(3.8)

allow us to regard the character as a homomorphism from the semiring
\mathfrak{R}(G)
to the group ring
\mathbb{Z}[\Lambda]
of the free abelian group
\Lambda
.
Define the
Casimir length

|\pi|\geq 0
of a representation
\pi\in\mathfrak{R}(G)
by the formula

|\pi|^{2}=\max_{\lambda\in P(\dot{\pi})}\langle\lambda,\lambda+2\rho\rangle\,.

(3.9)

Here, the angle brackets denote the inner product in
\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
induced by the Killing form, and
\rho
is the sum of the fundamental weights (the generators of the lattice
\Lambda^{+}
), often called the Weyl vector. It is known that the set of weights
P(\dot{\pi})
is invariant under the action of the Weyl group
W
and that each weight of
\Lambda
is conjugate to a dominant weight from
\Lambda^{+}
. Since
\langle\lambda,\rho\rangle>0
for any
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
, the maximum in (
3.9
) is attained on dominant weights. By restricting to the dominant weights, one can easily see that

|\pi_{1}\otimes\pi_{2}|\leq|\pi_{1}|+|\pi_{2}|\,.

(3.10)

This induces a filtration on the semiring
\mathfrak{R}(G)
defined by the family of additive subgroups

\mathfrak{R}_{l}(G)=\big\{\pi\in\mathfrak{R}(G)\;\big|\;|\pi|\leq l\;\big\}\,,\qquad l\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,.

(3.11)

By definition,

\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1}}(G)\subset\mathfrak{R}_{l_{2}}(G)\quad\mbox{whenever}\quad l_{1}\leq l_{2}\,,

and

\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1}}(G)\,\mathfrak{R}_{l_{2}}(G)_{l_{2}}\subset\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1}+l_{2}}(G)\,,\qquad\mathfrak{R}(G)=\bigcup_{l\geq 0}\mathfrak{R}_{l}(G)\,.

Thus, the family
\mathfrak{R}(G)_{l}
defines an exhaustive multiplicative filtration on
\mathfrak{R}(G)
, indexed by the abelian semigroup
(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},+)
.

This filtration induces a multiplicative filtration on the algebra of representative functions
\mathcal{F}(G)
. Define the subspace of
Casimir length at most

l
as

\mathcal{F}_{l}(G)=\mathrm{span}\big\{\pi_{ij}(g)\in\mathcal{F}(G)\;\big|\;\pi\in\mathfrak{R}_{l}(G)\;\big\}\,.

(3.12)

Then

\mathcal{F}_{l_{1}}(G)\,\mathcal{F}_{l_{2}}(G)\subset\mathcal{F}_{l_{1}+l_{2}}(G)\,,\qquad\bigcup_{l\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{l}(G)=\mathcal{F}(G)\,.

Associated with the quadratic Casimir element
C_{2}=-\delta^{ab}t_{a}t_{b}
is the Laplace operator

\mathbb{L}=-\delta^{ab}\ell_{a}\ell_{b}

(3.13)

on scalar functions. It is well known that the eigenfunctions of
\mathbb{L}
are precisely the matrix elements of irreducible representations of
G
. Furthermore,

\mathbb{L}\,\pi_{ij}=|\pi|^{2}\pi_{ij}\,,\qquad\forall\,[\pi]\in\hat{G}\,.

(3.14)

This explains the terminology “Casimir length” for
|\pi|
. Each eigenvalue
|\pi|^{2}
has finite multiplicity, and the classical Weyl law states that

\dim\mathcal{F}_{l}(G)=\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(G)}{(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}l^{d}+\mathcal{O}(l^{d-1})\,,

(3.15)

where
d=\dim G
(see
[
33
]
). Thus, Weyl’s law shows that the filtration grows polynomially in
l
, which will provide effective control over the size and complexity of
\mathcal{F}(G)
in the process of quantization.

Let us denote

\mathfrak{A}^{n}_{l}=\mathcal{F}_{l}(G)\otimes\mathrm{Pol}^{n}(D)\,,\qquad\mathfrak{A}^{n}=\mathcal{F}(G)\otimes\mathrm{Pol}^{n}(D)\,,\qquad\mathfrak{A}_{l}=\mathcal{F}_{l}(G)\otimes\mathrm{Pol}(D)\,.

Combining both filtrations, we introduce the
total filtration
:

\mathfrak{A}^{(t)}=\bigoplus_{n+l=t}\mathfrak{A}_{l}^{n}\,,\qquad\mathfrak{A}^{(t)}\subset\mathfrak{A}^{(t^{\prime})}\quad\mbox{whenever}\quad t\leq t^{\prime}\,.

(3.16)

This total filtration will be especially useful in our subsequent constructions.

3.2
Prequantization

Consider the pre-Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{F}(G)
of representative functions equipped with the Hermitian inner product

(f,h)=\int_{G}\overline{f(g)}h(g)dg\qquad\forall f,g\in\mathcal{H}\,,

(3.17)

where
dg
denotes the normalized Haar measure on
G
. By the Peter–Weyl theorem,
\mathcal{H}
is dense in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(G,dg)
. Let
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H})
denote the algebra of operators on
\mathcal{H}
. For any real number
\hbar\geq 0
, define a
\mathbb{C}
-linear map

\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}:\mathfrak{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H})
by the relations:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)\psi=f\cdot\psi\,,\qquad\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a})\psi=-i\hbar\ell_{a}\psi\,,\qquad\forall f\in\mathcal{F}(G)\,,\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{H}\,,\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a_{1}}\cdots x_{a_{n}})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\sigma\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a_{1}})\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a_{2}})\cdots\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a_{n}})\big)\,,\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(fx_{a_{1}}\cdots x_{a_{n}})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a_{1}}\cdots x_{a_{n}})+\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a_{1}}\cdots x_{a_{n}})\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)\Big)\,,\end{array}

(3.18)

where the summation runs over all permutations of the symbols
x_{a_{1}},\ldots,x_{a_{n}}
. We will refer to
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}
as the
prequantization map
. It follows immediately from the definition that

[\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a}),\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{b})]=-i\hbar f_{ab}^{c}\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{c}),\quad[\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a}),\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)]=-i\hbar\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(\ell_{a}f)\,,\ \quad[\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f),\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(h)]=0

(3.19)

for all
f,h\in\mathcal{F}(G)
. The first commutator shows that the operators
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a})
form a Lie algebra which is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}
for
\hbar>0
. The second and third commutators then define a trivial extension of
\mathfrak{g}
by the
\mathfrak{g}
-module
\mathcal{F}(G)
. Note that the action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\mathcal{F}(G)
respects the filtration by the Casimir length, so that we can speak of an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra
~{\mathcal{L}=\mathfrak{g}\subset\hskip-10.00002pt+\mathcal{F}(G)}
filtered by the family of finite-dimensional subalgebras
~{\mathcal{L}_{l}=\mathfrak{g}\subset\hskip-10.00002pt+\mathcal{F}_{l}(G)}
. The prequantization map
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}
defines a unitary representation of the universal enveloping algebra
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{L})
in the pre-Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. This allows us to identify the space of prequantum observables
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(\mathfrak{A})
with the quotient algebra
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{L})/\mathcal{J}
, where the two-sided ideal
\mathcal{J}
is generated by the relations

\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(fh)-\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(h)=0\,,\qquad\forall f,h\in\mathcal{F}(G)\,.

The next statement, for which the proof is left to the reader, represents a form of the correspondence principle.

Proposition 3
.

The following statements hold:

1.

The prequantization map is Hermitian, meaning that
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(\bar{a})=\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(a)\big)^{\dagger}
.

2.

The prequantization map is injective, i.e.,
\ker(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar})=0
.

3.

The image of the prequantization map,
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(\mathfrak{A})
, is a subalgebra of
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H})
.

4.

For any
a\in\mathfrak{A}^{n}
and
b\in\mathfrak{A}^{m}
, the following
\ast
-product is defined:

a\ast b:=\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{-1}\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(a)\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(b)\big)=\sum_{k=0}^{n+m}\hbar^{k}D_{k}(a,b)\,,

(3.20)

where

D_{0}(a,b)=ab\,,\qquad D_{1}(a,b)=\frac{i}{2}\{a,b\}\,,\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad D_{k}(a,b)\in\mathfrak{A}^{n+m-k}\,.

If
a
and
b
do not depend on
\hbar
, then neither do the elements
D_{k}(a,b)
.

5.

The
\ast
-product respects the total filtration (
3.16
), that is,

\mathfrak{A}^{(t)}\ast\mathfrak{A}^{(s)}\subset\mathfrak{A}^{(t+s)}
.

Thus, the star-product (
3.20
) defines a deformation quantization of the dense Poisson subalgebra
\mathfrak{A}\subset C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{C})
.

Deformation quantization on the cotangent bundles of Lie groups has been studied by many authors; see, for example,
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
.

Using the commutation relations (
3.19
) one can bring prequantum observables (
3.18
) into the form where all operators
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a})
appear to the right of operators
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)
. In the multi-index notation (
3.4
), this reordering of factors takes the form

\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}\big(a_{\bf n}x^{\bf n}\big)=\sum_{|{\bf m}|\leq|{\bf n}|}\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}\big(a^{\hbar}_{\bf m}\big)\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}\big(x^{\bf m}\big)

(3.21)

for some
a^{\hbar}_{\bf m}\in\mathcal{F}(G)
.

The operators
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(a)
are generally unbounded unless
a\in\mathcal{F}(G)
. A more detailed estimate can be obtained as follows. The filtration by the Casimir length (
3.12
) extends naturally to the pre-Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\bigcup_{l\geq 0}\mathcal{H}^{l}
. For
\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{l}
, we have

\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a})\psi\|^{2}\leq\sum_{b=1}^{d}\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{b})\psi\|^{2}=\delta^{ab}(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{a})\psi,\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(x_{b})\psi)=\hbar^{2}(\psi,\mathbb{L}\psi)\leq\hbar^{2}l^{2}\|\psi\|^{2}\,.

(3.22)

If
f\in\mathcal{F}(G)
, then

\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)\psi\|^{2}=\|f\psi\|^{2}=\int_{G}|f(g)|^{2}|\psi(g)|^{2}dg\leq|f|_{\infty}^{2}\|\psi\|^{2}\,.

(3.23)

Using the definition of
R
-norm (
3.5
) with
R=\hbar l
, we finally obtain

\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(a)\psi\|\leq|a|_{R}\cdot\|\psi\|\,,\qquad\forall a\in\mathfrak{A}\,,\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{l}\,.

(3.24)

This shows that, when restricted to the subspace
\mathcal{H}^{l}
, the prequantization map is controlled by the
R
-norm, providing a concrete estimate of the operator size.

3.3
Quantazable semialgebraic domains

We consider open domains
D\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
that are defined by a finite set of inequalities

d_{A}(x)>0\,,

(3.25)

where
d_{A}
are polynomials. Such domains are called
semialgebraic
or, more precisely,
basic (open) semialgebraic sets

[
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
. To indicate the set of polynomials used to define a basic semialgebraic set
D
, we write
D=D(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
. Replacing the strict equality
d_{A}>0
with the non-strict inequality
d_{A}\geq 0
defines a
basic closed semialgebraic set
. More general semialgebraic sets are obtained by applying the set-theoretic operations of intersection, union, and complement. For example, if
D(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
and
D^{\prime}(d^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,d_{m}^{\prime})
, then

(D\cap D^{\prime})(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n},d^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,d_{m}^{\prime})\,.

Clearly, any finite union of open intervals on
\mathbb{R}
is semialgebraic . The open ball
B^{d}_{R}
:

R^{2}-(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2})>0

is a semialgebraic set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that a semialgebraic set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
bounded
if there exists a ball
B^{d}_{R}
such that
D\cap B^{d}_{R}=D
. The minimal
R
satisfying this property is called the
radius of the domain

D
.

According to the definition, a basic semialgebraic domain
D=D(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
is not just an open set in
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
– it is an open set defined by a specific collection of polynomials
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
. If two different collections of polynomials define the same domain
D\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, we regard them as defining different semialgebraic sets. For example, the inequalities

d=1-x^{2}-y^{2}>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad d^{\prime}=(x^{2}+3y^{2}+1)(1-x^{2}-y^{2})>0

both define the unit disc in the plane, but
D(d)\neq D(d^{\prime})
. Given a basic semialgebraic set
D(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
, one can introduce the following semirings
5
5

5

A semiring is a generalization of a ring in which additive inverses are not required.

•

– the semiring of polynomials strictly positive on the topological closure
\bar{D}

d_{A}(x)\geq 0
;

•

\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n},\mathbb{R}_{+}\rangle
– the semiring generated by the defining polynomials

together with positive reals;

•

\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n},\mathrm{Pol}(\mathfrak{g}^{\ast})^{2}\rangle
– the semiring generated by the

It is clear that

\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n},\mathbb{R}_{+}\rangle\subset\langle d_{1},\ldots,d_{n},\mathrm{Pol}(\mathfrak{g}^{\ast})^{2}\rangle\subset S(D)\,.

In general, both inclusions are proper. Consequently, neither the polynomials

nor the semiring they generate are uniquely determined by an open set

S(B^{d}_{R})=\langle R^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-\cdots-x^{2}_{d}\,,\,\mathrm{Pol}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}\,\rangle

and

S([a,b]^{d})=\langle b-x_{1},\ldots,b-x_{d},x_{1}-a,\ldots,x_{d}-a,\mathbb{R}_{+}\rangle\,.

3.4
Quantization

We continue with the notation and definitions of Sec.
3.2
. Suppose we are given a symplectic domain

G\times D\subset T^{\ast}G
, where
D=D(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
is a bounded and quantizable semialgebraic set in
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
of radius
R
. As the first step, we associate to
D
a subspace
\mathcal{H}^{D}
of the pre-Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{F}(G)
. This is defined as follows. Using the prequantization map (
3.18
), we first define the operators
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{A})
. Among these operators we have
R^{2}-\hbar^{2}\mathbb{L}
.
By definition, the operators
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{A})
are Hermitian and commute with each other. Therefore, we can speak of their common eigenfunctions defined by the equations:

\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{A})\psi=\lambda_{A}\psi\,,\qquad A=1,\ldots,n\,.

The eigenfunctions that correspond to the positive spectrum of the
\lambda_{A}
’s span a subspace in
\mathcal{H}
, which we denote
\mathcal{H}^{D}
. In other words,

\mathcal{H}^{D}=\mathrm{span}\big\{\psi\in\mathcal{H}\;|\;\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{A})\psi=\lambda_{A}\psi\,,\;\lambda_{A}>0\,,\;A=1,\ldots,n\;\big\}\,.

(3.31)

The presence of the Laplace operator implies that
\dim\mathcal{H}^{D}\leq\infty
. The Weyl law (
3.15
) approximates the dimension of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
for
R/\hbar\gg 1
. By abuse of language, we will refer to
\psi
and
\mathcal{H}^{D}
from (
3.31
) as the eigenstate and eigenspace of the domain
D
. If
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are two quantizable basic domains that are compatible with each other, then
D_{1}\cup D_{1}
is quantizable and we define

\mathcal{H}^{D_{1}\cup D_{2}}:=\mathcal{H}^{D_{1}}+\mathcal{H}^{D_{2}}\,.

(3.32)

Since all the Hermitian operators
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{A})
associated with the compatible domains
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are pairwise commuting, we can choose an orthonormal basis in
\mathcal{H}^{D_{1}\cup D_{2}}
by combining orthonormal bases in
\mathcal{H}^{D_{1}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{D_{2}}
. Using this diagonalizing basis shows that

\mathcal{H}^{D_{1}\cap D_{2}}=\mathcal{H}^{D_{1}}\cap\mathcal{H}^{D_{2}}

and

\mathcal{H}^{D_{1}}\cap\mathcal{H}^{D_{2}}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{H}^{D_{1}}\perp\mathcal{H}^{D_{2}}\,.

In the last case, we say that the domains
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are
\hbar
-disconnected. Conversely, the domains
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are said to be
\hbar
-connected if they share a common eigenstate. Let us emphasize that the topological connectedness of the domains (
D_{1}\cap D_{2}\neq\varnothing
) does not necessarily imply their
\hbar
-connectedness and two compatible domains may well be
\hbar
-connected for one value of
\hbar
and
\hbar
-disconnected for another. For
\hbar
-disconnected domains
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, the sum in (
3.32
) becomes direct.

The space
\mathcal{H}^{D}
is identified with the state space of a quantum-mechanical system whose classical phase space is the
G\times D
. Since the factor
G
represents the space of momenta, we can speak of a momentum representation for the space of states. Let
P_{D}
denote the Hermitian projector onto the subspace
\mathcal{H}^{D}\subset\mathcal{H}
. By definition,

P_{D}^{\dagger}=P_{D}\,,\qquad P_{D}^{2}=P_{D}\,.

(3.33)

Given a classical observable
a\in\mathfrak{A}
, we set

\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a)=P_{D}\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(a)P_{D}\,.

(3.34)

Since
\mathrm{Im}\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a)\big)\subset\mathcal{H}^{D}
, we can think of
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a)
as an operator on the space
\mathcal{H}^{D}
.
This defines a linear map
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}:\mathfrak{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}^{D})
from the space of classical observables to the algebra of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}^{D}
, which we call the
quantization map
. If
\dim\mathcal{H}^{D}=N
, then we will write
\mathcal{H}^{D}_{N}
to indicate the dimension of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
. Following the pattern of the cylinder from Sec.
2
, we endow the finite-dimensional space
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}^{D}_{N})
with the normalized Frobenius norm:

||A||_{N}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}{\mathrm{Tr}}(A^{\dagger}A)\,,\qquad\forall A\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}^{D}_{N})\,.

(3.35)

Definition 3.36

(Bulk and Boundary States)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{D}
be the state space associated with a quantizable domain
D
, and let

\mathcal{H}^{D}_{(t)}\supset\mathcal{H}_{(t^{\prime})}^{D}\,,\qquad t\leq t^{\prime},\qquad\mathcal{H}^{D}_{(t)}=\left\{\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{D}\ \middle|\ \mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(a)\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{D}\quad\forall\,a\in\mathfrak{A}^{(t)}\right\}

be the descending filtration induced by the total filtration
\mathfrak{A}^{(t)}
in the space of classical observables.
The elements of
\mathcal{H}^{D}_{(t)}
are called bulk states of filtration degree
t
, by analogy with the interior points of
D
.
Correspondingly, the elements of the orthogonal complement
(\mathcal{H}^{D}_{(t)})^{\perp}
are referred to as boundary states.

Definition 3.37

(Thick Domain)
.

A quantizable domain
D
is called thick if, for any finite
t
,

\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\frac{\dim\mathcal{H}^{D}_{(t)}}{\dim\mathcal{H}^{D}}=1\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\frac{\dim(\mathcal{H}^{D}_{(t)})^{\perp}}{\dim\mathcal{H}^{D}}=0\,.

In other words, in the classical limit
\hbar\to 0
, the proportion of boundary states with any given filtration degree becomes negligible compared to the bulk states of that filtration degree.

Consider the Lie algebra

N=\sum_{j=m+1/2}^{s}(2j+1)(2j-2m)=\frac{2}{3}{(s-m)(2s-2m+1)\left(2s+m+2\right)}\,.

Theorem 2
.

For any quantizable domain
D
the following properties hold:

i)

normalization:

\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(1)=1\!\!1\,,

ii)

reality:

\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a)\big)^{\dagger}=\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(\bar{a})\,.

If the domain
D
is thick, then one additionally has

iii)

von Neumann’s condition:

\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\big\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a)\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(b)-\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(ab)\big\|_{N}=0\,.

iv)

Dirac’s condition:

\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\big\|\big((i\hbar)^{-1}[\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a),\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(b)]-\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(\{a,b\})\big)\psi\big\|=0\qquad\forall a,b\in\mathfrak{A}_{(t)},\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{D}_{(2t)}\,.

Proof.

Let us denote

A:=\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a)\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(b)-\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(ab)\,,

for some
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}_{(t)}
. We need to evaluate the classical limit of
\|A\|_{N}
. Let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\ldots,\psi_{n}
be a basis in the subspace
\mathcal{H}^{D}_{(2t)}
of bulk states of filtration degree
2t
. We complete this basis to a full basis
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}
in
\mathcal{H}^{D}
, where the additional
N-n
basis vectors represent the boundary states. Then

\|A\|^{2}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|A\psi_{k}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|A\psi_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=n+1}^{N}\|A\psi_{k}\|^{2}\,.

(3.45)

Since
a,b,ab\in\mathfrak{A}_{(2t)}
, we have

A\psi_{k}=\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(a)\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(b)-\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(ab)\big)\psi_{k}\,,\qquad k=1,\ldots,n\,.

(3.46)

By Proposition
3
, the first sum on the right of (
3.45
) is evaluated as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|A\psi_{k}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big\|\sum_{m=1}^{[2t]}\hbar^{m}\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}\big(D_{m}(a,b)\big)\psi_{k}\Big\|^{2}\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{N}\Big(\sum_{m=1}^{[2t]}\hbar^{m}\big\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}\big(D_{m}(a,b)\big)\big\|\Big)^{2}\leq\frac{n}{N}\Big(\sum_{m=1}^{[2t]}\hbar^{m}\big|D_{m}(a,b)\big|_{R}\Big)^{2}\quad\longrightarrow 0\end{array}

(3.47)

as
\hbar\to 0
. For the second sum, we have

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=n+1}^{N}\|A\psi_{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{N-n}{N}\|A\|^{2}\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\leq\frac{N-n}{N}\big(\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(a)\|\cdot\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(b)\|+\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(ab)\|\big)^{2}\leq 4|a|^{2}_{R}|b|^{2}_{R}\frac{N-n}{N}\,.\end{array}

(3.48)

By the thickness property,
(N-n)/N\rightarrow 0
in the classical limit.

To prove the Dirac condition, we introduce the operator

B=(i\hbar)^{-1}[\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a),\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(b)]-\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(\{a,b\})\,.

(3.49)

For bulk states, we have

\|B\psi\|=\big\|\big((i\hbar)^{-1}[\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(a),\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(b)]-\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(\{a,b\})\big)\psi\big\|\,,\qquad\forall a,b\in\mathfrak{A}_{(t)}\,,\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{D}_{(2t)}\,.

(3.50)

Applying then Proposition
3
yields

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\|B\psi\|=\sum_{m=2}^{[2t]}\hbar^{m-1}\big\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}\big(D_{m}(a,b)-D_{m}(b,a)\big)\psi\big\|\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=2}^{[2t]}\hbar^{m-1}\big\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}\big(D_{m}(a,b)-D_{m}(b,a)\big)\big\|\leq\sum_{m=2}^{[2t]}\hbar^{m-1}\big|D_{m}(a,b)-D_{m}(b,a)\big|_{R}\quad\longrightarrow 0\end{array}

(3.51)

as
\hbar\to 0
, so the Dirac condition follows.

As is seen, the thickness property is essential here. For thick quantizable domains, the quantum operators

\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a)
correctly reproduce the classical algebra in the semiclassical limit: products and commutators converge to their classical counterparts. This ensures that the quantization is consistent and physically meaningful, with boundary effects vanishing in the classical regime.

Then for any

Taking into account the Casimir length of

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|\pi|<l}(\ldots)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|\pi|<l-t}(\ldots)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l-t\leq|\pi|<l}(\ldots)\,.

(3.56)

The classical limit of the first sum is computed as

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|\pi|<l-t}\dim\pi\sum_{i,j=1}^{\dim\pi}\int_{G}dg\,\bar{\pi}_{ij}(g)\bar{f}(g)f(g)\pi_{ij}(g)\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle=\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|\pi|<l-t}(\dim\pi)^{2}\int_{G}dg|f(g)|^{2}=\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\frac{\sum_{|\pi|<R/\hbar-t}(\dim\pi)^{2}}{\sum_{|\pi|<R/\hbar}(\dim\pi)^{2}}\int_{G}dg|f(g)|^{2}\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle=\int_{G}dg|f(g)|^{2}=\|f\|^{2}_{D}\,,\end{array}

(3.57)

since the ratio tends to
1
by the Weyl law (
3.15
).
Here we also used the fact that
\pi
is a unitary representation of
G
, so that
\bar{\pi}_{ij}(g)=\pi_{ji}(g^{-1})
and

\sum_{i,j=1}^{\dim\pi}\bar{\pi}_{ij}(g)\pi_{ij}(g)=\dim\pi\,.

(3.58)

As to the second sum in (
3.56
), it vanishes in the classical limit due to the thickness of the ball domain. Indeed,

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l-t\leq|\pi|<l}\dim\pi\sum_{i,j=1}^{\dim\pi}\int_{G}dg\,\bar{\pi}_{ij}(g)\bar{f}(g)P_{D}f(g)\pi_{ij}(g)\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle=\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l-t\leq|\pi|<l}\dim\pi\sum_{i,j=1}^{\dim\pi}\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(f)\pi_{ij}\|^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)\|^{2}\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{R/\hbar-t\leq|\pi|<R/\hbar}(\dim\pi)^{2}\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\leq|f|_{\infty}^{2}\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\frac{(R-\hbar t)^{d}-R^{d}}{R^{d}}=0\,.\end{array}

(3.59)

In the last step, we used (
3.55
) together with the Weyl law (
3.15
). Thus,

\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(f)\|^{2}_{N}=\|f\|^{2}_{D}

(3.60)

for
D=B_{R}^{d}
and
f\in\mathcal{F}(G)
.

From a physical viewpoint, one can regard
f
as the Hamiltonian of a free particle whose phase space is
B_{R}^{d}\times G
. In this interpretation, equality (
3.60
) states that the classical limit of the root mean square value of the quantum energy spectrum coincides with that of the classical Hamiltonian
f(g)
. This shows that the quantization scheme respects the correspondence principle: the statistical properties of the spectrum approach those of the underlying classical system once quantum fluctuations are suppressed.

Using the concept of bulk and boundary states, it is not hard to generalize Proposition
2
from one-dimensional intervals to arbitrary balls in higher dimensions.

Proposition 4
.

Let
D=B^{d}_{R}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
be an open ball and let
\mathcal{H}
be the space of representative functions on
G
, or its
L^{2}
-completion. Then for any

a,b\in\mathfrak{A}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
, the quantization map asymptotically reproduces classical multiplication and Poisson brackets in the sense that the following limits hold:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\big\|\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a)\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(b)-\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(ab)\big)\psi\big\|=0\,,\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\big\|\big((i\hbar)^{-1}[\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(a),\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(b)]-\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(\{a,b\})\big)\psi\big\|=0\,.\end{array}

(3.61)

The proof exploits the Casimir filtration in
\mathcal{F}(G)
, which allows one to represent each function of
\mathcal{H}
as a generalized Fourier series

\psi(g)=\sum_{l}\sum_{|\pi|=l}\psi_{\pi}^{ij}\sqrt{\dim\pi}\,\pi_{ij}(g)\,,

(3.62)

where the inner sum runs over all (equivalence classes of) representations
[\pi]\in\hat{G}
of Casimir length
l
. Square summability,

\|\psi\|^{2}=\sum_{l}\sum_{|\pi|=l}\sum_{i,j}|\psi_{\pi}^{ij}|^{2}<\infty\,,

(3.63)

implies that
|\psi_{\pi}^{ij}|\to 0
as
|\pi|\to\infty
. This decay condition suppresses the contribution of boundary states to the classical limits (
3.61
).

Theorem 3
.

Let
D(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
be a bounded and quantizable semialgebraic domain and let
f\in\mathrm{Pol}(\mathfrak{g}^{\ast})
be a polynomial strictly positive on the topological closure of

D
, i.e.,
f|_{\bar{D}}>0
. Then, for each
\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{D}
and each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\big(\psi,\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(f)\psi\big)>-\varepsilon

for all
\hbar<c
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1
, any polynomial
f(x)
positive on
\bar{D}
belongs to the semiring
S(D)
, meaning that

f=\sum_{e_{k}\in\{0,1\}}f^{2}_{e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{d}}d_{1}^{e_{1}}d_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots d_{n}^{e_{n}}

(3.64)

for some polynomials
f_{e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{k}}(x)
. Applying the prequantization map, we can bring the operator
\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)
into the form

\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f)=\sum_{e_{k}\in\{0,1\}}\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f_{e_{1}\cdots e_{n}})\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{1})\big)^{e_{1}}\cdots\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{n})\big)^{e_{n}}\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f_{e_{1}\cdots e_{n}})+\hbar\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(r_{\hbar})\,,

where
r_{\hbar}
is some polynomial in the
x
’s and
\hbar
. Then, for any normalized state
\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{D}
,

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\big(\psi,\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}^{D}(f)\psi\big)=\sum_{e_{k}\in\{0,1\}}\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f_{e_{1}\cdots e_{n}})\psi,\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{1})\big)^{e_{1}}\cdots\big(\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{n})\big)^{e_{n}}\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(f_{e_{1}\cdots e_{n}})\psi\big)\\[14.22636pt]
+\hbar\big(\psi,\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(r_{\hbar})\psi\big)\,.\end{array}

(3.65)

By the Schwarz inequality and Rel. (
3.24
),

\big|\big(\psi,\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(r_{\hbar})\psi\big)\big|^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(r_{\hbar})\|^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(r_{\hbar})\|^{2}\leq|r_{\hbar}|^{2}_{R}\,,

(3.66)

where
R
is the radius of the domain
D
. Since the operators
\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{1})\big)^{e_{1}}\cdots\big(\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\hbar}(d_{n})\big)^{e_{n}}
are positive semidefinite on
\mathcal{H}^{D}
, the sum over the
e_{k}
’s in (
3.65
) is non-negative, while the last term tends to zero as
\hbar\rightarrow 0
. Therefore, we can take
c=\varepsilon/(b+\varepsilon)\leq 1
, where

b=\max_{0\leq\hbar\leq 1}|r_{\hbar}|_{R}\,.

∎

Heuristically, the theorem shows that a quantum particle cannot be detected far beyond the domain
D
, where the observable
f
may take large negative values. In the classical limit, the particle becomes increasingly confined within

D
. Of course, since the position operators do not commute, the particle’s location in space is intrinsically uncertain. Consistently, inequality (
3.22
) indicates that the possible measurement outcomes of the particle’s coordinates

x^{a}
do not exceed the radius of the domain
D
.

4
Discussion

Let us briefly summarize the main results and indicate possible directions for future work. In this paper, we developed a quantization scheme for bounded symplectic domains associated with compact Lie groups. Specifically, we studied domains of the form
D\times G\hookrightarrow T^{\ast}G
, where
G
is a compact Lie group and
D
is a bounded region in the dual space
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
of the corresponding Lie algebra. The finiteness of the symplectic volume implies that the resulting quantum theory is described by a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, with observables represented by finite Hermitian matrices.
Particular attention was devoted to a detailed understanding of the classical limit.

It is worth emphasizing that most of the existing literature on quantization focuses on smooth symplectic manifolds, whether compact or noncompact. In contrast, in our setting, the presence of the boundary
\partial\bar{D}\times G
gives rise to specific boundary effects that cannot be ignored. These effects require a careful reconsideration – and indeed a modification – of the standard von Neumann and Dirac conditions that usually guide the quantization procedure.
As a consequence, the quantization scheme developed here does not coincide with any single known approach. Rather, it combines elements of several: deformation quantization at the prequantization stage, projection onto the state space as in Berezin–Toeplitz quantization, and the construction of the operator norm in the spirit of strict quantization. Moreover, it reveals noteworthy links with semialgebraic geometry. This hybrid character highlights both the novelty of the framework and the richness of the underlying mathematical structures.

A natural direction for further research is to extend the present scheme by analyzing the separability condition from Proposition
1
in the context of more general quantizable domains and observables. In particular, a systematic analysis of the instances and forms in which separability holds would further clarify the nature of the classical limit. Equally intriguing would be to explore possible connections with
noncommutative
semialgebraic geometry, where related structural phenomena may appear in a broader algebraic framework
[
41
,
42
]
.

Finally, the proposed quantization scheme appears promising from the perspective of physical applications, as it provides a consistent framework for quantizing systems with compact momentum
and
configuration spaces. In this setting, the phenomenon of supertunneling, identified here, appears to be new and merits further investigation. Equally significant is the emergence of a maximal density of the fermionic gas, pointing to novel physical effects that may arise in systems with compact momentum space. In particular, the concept of a maximal fermion density could offer a natural resolution of the singularity problem in the collapse of massive stars and other compact astrophysical objects; in this scenario, the singularity is avoided not through speculative quantum gravity effects (for which no complete theory yet exists), but rather through the model-independent Pauli exclusion principle built into quantum mechanics itself. The limiting volume is determined by the product of the minimal spatial cell,
(2\pi\hbar)^{3}/\mathrm{Vol}(G)
, and the number
N
of fermions involved in the collapse. We intend to explore these questions in future work.

For example,

(A.2)

(A.3)

Both relations (

Thus, the operators

and

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\|f(\hat{x})\|=|f|_{\infty}\,.

(A.5)

The operator and normalized Frobenius norms are related by the inequality

\|A\|_{N}\leq\|A\|\,,\qquad\forall{A\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}_{N})}\,.

(A.6)

As mentioned in footnote

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|AB\|_{N}\leq\|A\|\cdot\|B\|_{N}\,,&\qquad\|AB\|_{N}\leq\|B\|\cdot\|A\|_{N}\,,\\[14.22636pt]

(A.7)

With these preliminaries, we are ready to prove Propositions

and

.

Separability condition.

Consider first the case where the classical observable is given by a polynomial

. Using the orthonormal basis
\big\{\psi_{k}(p)=e^{\frac{2\pi ikp}{M}}\big\}_{k=1}^{N}
in

\|\hat{f}\|^{2}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(f(\hat{x})\psi_{k},f(\hat{x})\psi_{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(f(x_{k})\psi_{k},f(x_{k})\psi_{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|f(x_{k})|^{2}\,,

(A.8)

whence

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\|\hat{f}\|^{2}_{N}=\frac{1}{L}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{L}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|f(x_{k})|^{2}=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}|f(x)|^{2}dx=\|f\|^{2}_{D}\,.

A general classical observable of

(A.10)

where only finitely many polynomials

(A.13)

Clearly,

and

as

On the other hand,

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\|\hat{a}\|^{2}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\hat{a}\psi_{k},\hat{a}\psi_{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\big(\hat{z}^{n}a^{\hbar}_{n}(x_{k})\psi_{k},\hat{z}^{m}a^{\hbar}_{m}(x_{k})\psi_{k}\big)\\[19.91692pt]

\hbar\to 0
and
N\to\infty
. Hence, for any pair of classical observables (
A.21
) with
n,m\leq l
, choosing
\mathcal{H}^{a,b}_{N}=\mathcal{H}^{l}_{N}
ensures the Dirac condition.

Turning to Proposition
2
, we consider an interval
[u,v]
with the boundary points
u
and
v
distinct from zero.
The finite-dimensional state space
\mathcal{H}_{N}\subset\mathcal{H}
is spanned by the Fourier harmonics

\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{n}(p)=e^{\frac{2\pi inp}{M}}\,,\qquad N_{1}\leq n\leq N_{2}\,,\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle N_{1}=\left[\frac{uM}{2\pi\hbar}\right]\,,\qquad N_{2}=\left[\frac{vM}{2\pi\hbar}\right]\,,\qquad N=N_{2}-N_{1}\,.\end{array}

(A.29)

A general state of
\mathcal{H}
is represented by a Fourier series

\psi=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{k}\psi_{k}\,,

(A.30)

with
c_{k}\to 0
as
|k|\to\infty
. Define the operator measuring the deviation from classical multiplication:

A:=(\widehat{z^{n}a_{n}})(\widehat{z^{m}b_{m}})-(\widehat{z^{n+m}a_{n}b_{m}})\,.

To control the “edge effects,” we orthogonally decompose
\mathcal{H}_{N}
into the edge and bulk states:

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{N}\psi=\psi^{\prime}+\psi^{\prime\prime}\,,\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}=\sum_{k=N_{1}}^{N_{1}+m}c_{k}\psi_{k}+\sum_{k=N_{2}-m}^{N_{2}}c_{k}\psi_{k}\,,\qquad\psi^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{k=N_{1}+m+1}^{N_{2}-m-1}c_{k}\psi_{k}\,.\end{array}

(A.31)

Applying the triangle inequality, we find

\|AP_{N}\psi\big\|\leq\sum_{k=N_{1}}^{N_{1}+m}|c_{k}|\cdot\|A\psi_{k}\big\|+\sum_{k=N_{2}-m}^{N_{2}}|c_{k}|\cdot\|A\psi_{k}\big\|+\|A\psi^{\prime\prime}\big\|\,,

(A.32)

which can be further bounded as

\|AP_{N}\psi\big\|\leq 2|a_{n}|_{\infty}|b_{m}|_{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=N_{1}}^{N_{1}+m}|c_{k}|+\sum_{k=N_{2}-m}^{N_{2}}|c_{k}|\right)+\|A\psi^{\prime\prime}\big\|\,.

(A.33)

The edge contributions vanish in the classical limit, since
|c_{k}|\rightarrow 0
as
|k|\rightarrow\infty
. The bulk term
\|A\psi^{\prime\prime}\|

is estimated as in the above derivation of Dirac’s condition. Using Eq. (
A.23
), we obtain:

\begin{array}[]{c}\|A\psi^{\prime\prime}\|=\big\|\hat{z}^{n+m}a^{\hbar}_{n}(\hat{x}+m\Delta x)\big[b_{m}^{\hbar}(\hat{x})-b_{m}^{\hbar}(\hat{x}+n\Delta x)\big]\psi^{\prime\prime}\big\|\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\leq\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|\cdot\big\|a^{\hbar}_{n}(\hat{x}+m\Delta x)\big\|\cdot\left\|\frac{b_{m}^{\hbar}(\hat{x})-b_{m}^{\hbar}(\hat{x}+n\Delta x)}{\Delta x}\right\|\cdot|\Delta x|\,,\end{array}

(A.34)

so that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|A\psi^{\prime\prime}\|\leq\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|\cdot|a_{n}|_{\infty}|nb^{\prime}_{m}|_{\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}|\Delta x|=0\,.

(A.35)

Thus, combining the edge and bulk contributions, we obtain

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|AP_{N}\psi\|=0
,
which establishes the first limit in Proposition
2
.
The second limit follows by the same argument using decomposition
A.31
.
