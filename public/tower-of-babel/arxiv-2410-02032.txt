On the Factor Complexity Associated with a Family of Multidimensional Continued Fraction Algorithms
Thomas Garrity, Otto Vaughn Osterman

Abstract
We study the complexity of S S -adic sequences corresponding to a family of 216 multidimensional continued fractions maps, called Triangle Partition maps (TRIP maps), with an emphasis on those with low upper bounds on complexity. Our main result is to prove that the complexity of S S -adic sequences corresponding to the triangle map (called the ( e , e , e ) (e,e,e) -TRIP map in this paper) has upper bound at most 3 ​ n 3n . Our second main result is to prove an upper bound of 2 ​ n + 1 2n+1 on complexity for another TRIP map. We discuss a dynamical phenomenon, which we call “hidden ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} behavior,” that occurs in this map and its relationship to complexity. Combining this with previously known results and a list of counter-examples, we provide a complete list of the TRIP maps which have upper bounds on complexity of at most 3 ​ n 3n , except for one remaining case for which we conjecture such an upper bound to hold.

Introduction

1.1
Overview

There is a remarkable correspondence between infinite words with factor complexity
n+1
(the number of subwords of length
n
is exactly
n+1
) and continued fraction expansions of irrational numbers. (See Coven and Hedlund
[
19
]
and also the work of Morse and Hedlund from the early 1940s
[
35
,
36
]
. For a good expository introduction, see Chapter 19 in Goodson’s textbook
[
29
]
.)
Given an irrational number
a\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, we can produce an infinite word on two letters with letter frequencies
(a,1-a)
by associating the continued fraction expansion to a sequence of substitutions (for background on this see Chapter 6 in
[
24
]
or in
[
40
]
).
It is well-known that such infinite words are
Sturmian
(meaning that their factor compexity is
n+1
, the lowest possible complexity for aperiodic infinite binary strings, as seen in the above references).

Over the years, there have been many generalizations of continued fractions, often called multidimensional continued fractions. Such generalizations often arise from the desire to find best simultaneous Diophantine approximations or to solve the Hermite problem, which asks for methods of writing a real number
\alpha
as a sequence of integers so that the sequence is eventually periodic if and only if
\alpha
is a cubic irrational. Just as continued fractions can be interpreted as a dynamical system of the Gauss map on the interval, multidimensional continued fractions can be interpreted as dynamical systems on a higher-dimensional simplex.

For higher-dimensional continued fractions, there is a straightforward technique mimicking the classical case which produces sequences on larger alphabets encoding orbits of the dynamical system. It is natural to wonder about the typical complexities of these sequences, whether there is any general framework for understanding them, and whether the correspondence between Sturmian words and words of low complexity can be generalized.

Arnoux-Rauzy words were among the first such sequences studied; these have factor complexity
2n+1
and correspond to the application of the Arnoux-Rauzy multidimensional continued fraction algorithm in a set of Lebesgue measure zero, the so-called Rauzy Gasket
[
5
]
. Additionally, in
[
12
]
it was shown that generically the algorithm produces sequences of complexity less than or equal to
\frac{5}{2}n+1
. Later, Cassaigne, Labbé and Leroy
[
17
]
showed that Cassaigne’s multidimensional continued fraction algorithm produces words of factor complexity
2n+1
almost everywhere. Given these two examples, it might be reasonable to wonder what the “generic” complexity is for continued fraction algorithms.

All of these words belong to a more general class of words called
S
-adic sequences
, which are derived via a finite family of substitutions. It is known that any uniformly recurrent infinite word with at most linear factor complexity is
S
-adic
[
23
,
32
]
. However, the converse is not true in general
[
24
]
. The
S
-adic conjecture
asks what additional conditions can be placed on
S
-adic sequences to ensure at most linear complexity. (For remarkable progress on this conjecture, see the work of Espinoza
[
22
]
.)

There is a framework (the triangle partition family, or TRIP family for short) that captures almost all known multidimensional continued fraction algorithms. This framework is generated by 216 different multidimensional continued fraction algorithms, indexed by a triple of permutations in
S_{3}\times S_{3}\times S_{3}
. Though
[
17
]
did not use the rhetoric of TRIP maps, the Cassaigne algorithm is in this list (it is the TRIP map
(e,23,23)
)
[
20
]
.

Our goal is to understand the complexity for all of the triangle partition maps with an emphasis on those with low upper bounds. Our main technical result is that the triangle map (called the
(e,e,e)
-TRIP map in this rhetoric) has linear complexity bounded between
2n+1
and
3n
. While other work on this subject has often focused on other properties of such sequences such as balance (as seen for example in
[
17
,
18
]
), we do not address these topics here. Also, while the Cassaigne map was created in large part to find a multi-dimensional continued fraction with low linear complexity, the triangle map has independent interest, as reviewed in the beginning of Section
6
.

We review the basics of multidimensional continued fractions in Section
2
and the basics of
S
-adic sequences and factor complexity in Section
3
.
We discuss the link between multidimensional continued fractions and
S
-adic sequences in Section
4
. In Section
5
, we review the common techniques used to determine factor complexity of
S
-adic sequences, which we make use of throughout the rest of the paper.

The new work begins in Section
6
. This will be the longest and most technical section of this paper, as we give the full proof that the linear complexity for the
S
-adic sequences corresponding to the
(e,e,e)
-TRIP map has lower bound
2n+1
and upper bound
3n
.

In Section
7
we return to a discussion of more general TRIP maps and reduce the number of different TRIP maps and classes of
S
-adic sequences to consider from 216 to 21 through equivalence relations.

In Section
8
, we give an overview of each of these 21 classes, specifying those for which are known or for which we conjecture to have an upper bound on complexity of at most
3n
and providing counterexamples for the others. Three of them are what we call
degenerate TRIP maps
as they can be reduced to maps in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and their corresponding
S
-adic sequences are Sturmian sequences. We find through computational experiments that two others are candidates for an upper bound of
2n+1
and two are candidates for an upper bound of
3n
(one of which is the triangle map).

In Section
9
, we discuss a new phenomenon for some multidimensional continued fraction algorithms, which we call
hidden
\mathbb{R}^{2}
maps
, whereby they can be reduced to continued fraction maps in two dimensions on a positively invariant subset, with respect to Lebesgue measure, of
\triangle
but not on all of
\triangle
. We discuss the
(e,13,e)
TRIP map as an example of this and show how the complexity of its corresponding
S
-adic sequences relate to this phenomenon, with a full proof that the complexity has upper bound
2n+1
.

In Section
10
, we explore the other candidate for an upper bound of
3n
, the
(e,23,e)
TRIP map, and give an outline of a possible proof for this upper bound similar to that in Section
6
.

We close with some natural questions in Section
11
.

For finite words
v=v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{n-1}
and
w=w_{0}w_{1}\cdots w_{m-1}
we define the
concatenation
of
v
and

to be
vw:=v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{n-1}w_{0}w_{1}\cdots w_{m-1}
.
Concatenation of finite words is associative but not commutative.

The following definitions are adopted from Berthé and Labbé
[
11
]
, Berthé and Rigo
[
13
]
, and Fogg
[
24
]
:

Definition 2
.

A
factor
or
subword
of an infinite word
\mathbf{u}
is a finite word

that appears exactly as some sequence of consecutive characters in
\mathbf{u}
. Specifically, this means that if
\mathbf{u}=a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
and
\left|w\right|=n
, then
w
is a factor if for some non-negative integer
m
, we have
w=a_{m}a_{m+1}\cdots a_{m+n-1}
.
Factors of a finite word are defined similarly.

Definition 3
.

A
language
is a subset
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{*}
that is factorially closed, that is, if
w\in\mathcal{L}
and
v
is a factor of
w
, then
v\in\mathcal{L}
.
We say that a language
\mathcal{L}
is
left extendable
(respectively
right extendable
) if for all
w\in\mathcal{L}
, there is some letter
a\in\mathcal{A}
such that
aw\in\mathcal{L}
(respectively
wa\in\mathcal{L}
).
If
\mathcal{L}
is both left extendable and right extendable, then we say
\mathcal{L}
is
extendable
.

Note that our definition of a language and extendability differs slightly from that in Berthé and Rigo
[
13
]
.

Definition 4
.

The
factor complexity
of a language
\mathcal{L}
is the function
p_{\mathcal{L}}
that maps each positive integer
n
to the number of distinct words of length
n
contained in
\mathcal{L}
.

While there many different types of complexity, overwhelmingly in this paper we when we say “complexity,” we will mean factor complexity.

Definition 5
.

A
prefix
of a finite or infinite word
w
is a word
a
that appears at the beginning of
w
, that is,
w=av
for some finite or infinite word
v
.
Similarly, a
suffix
of a finite word
w
is a word
b
that appears at the end of
w
, that is,
w=vb
for some finite word
v
.
We call a prefix or suffix of a word
w
that is not equal to
w
a
proper prefix
or
proper suffix
, respectively.

Definition 6
.

An infinite word
\mathbf{u}
is
recurrent
if all of its factors occur infinitely often in
\mathbf{u}
.
If for any factor
w
of
\mathbf{u}
there exists
N
such that every subword of length
N
in
\mathbf{u}
contains
w
as a factor, then
\mathbf{u}
is
uniformly recurrent
.

Definition 7
.

The
language
of an infinite word
\mathbf{u}
, denoted
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u})
, is the set of all factors of
\mathbf{u}
.
The
complexity function
of
\mathbf{u}
, denoted
p_{\mathbf{u}}
,
p
,
p_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u})}
, or even
p_{\mathcal{L}}
, is simply the complexity of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u})
, so that
p_{\mathbf{u}}(n)
is the number of distinct factors of
\mathbf{u}
of length
n
.

For example, for the word
\mathbf{u}=123123123\ldots
, the set of factors of length
3
is
\left\{123,231,312\right\}
, so
p_{\mathbf{u}}(3)=3
.

A
substitution
is a function
\sigma:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{*}
. A substitution can be extended to an operation on finite words through the operation of concatenation,

\sigma(u_{0}u_{1}u_{2}\ldots u_{n-1})=\sigma(u_{0})\sigma(u_{1})\sigma(u_{2})\ldots\sigma(u_{n-1})

(5)

and to infinite words

\sigma(u_{0}u_{1}u_{2}\ldots)=\sigma(u_{0})\sigma(u_{1})\sigma(u_{2})\ldots,

(6)

We give the following definition, as in Definition 1.4.1 of
[
40
]
:

Definition 8
.

Let
S
be a finite set of substitutions on
\mathcal{A}
. An infinite word
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is
S
-adic
if there is a sequence
\left\{\mathbf{u}^{({m})}\right\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
of infinite words and a sequence
\left\{\sigma_{m}\right\}_{m=0}^{\infty}\subseteq S
of substitutions such that
\mathbf{u}^{({0})}=\mathbf{u}
and for all
m
,
\mathbf{u}^{({m})}=\sigma_{m}(\mathbf{u}^{({m+1})})
.
In this case,
\left\{\sigma_{m}\right\}
is the
coding sequence
of
\mathbf{u}
.

The classical case of
S
-adic words are the Sturmian words, which are infinite words on two letters with complexity
n+1
. Let
\mathcal{A}=\left\{1,2\right\}
and
S=\left\{S_{0},S_{1}\right\}
with:

•

S_{0}:\quad 1\mapsto 1,\quad 2\mapsto 21
,

•

S_{1}:\quad 1\mapsto 12,\quad 2\mapsto 2
.

It is known that an infinite word on two letters is Sturmian if and only if it has the above coding sequence containing
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
infinitely often. See Chapter 2 in Lothaire
[
33
]
, Chapter 6 in Fogg
[
24
]
or Chapter 9 in Allouche and Shallit
[
2
]
.

The
S
-adic conjecture asks to what extent this can be generalized to a relationship between
S
-adic words and words of at most linear complexity.
In one direction, it was proven by Ferenczi
[
23
]
that any uniformly recurrent word
\mathbf{u}
with linear complexity has the same language as an
S
-adic word. Leroy
[
32
]
strengthened this result and showed that
\mathbf{u}
itself is
S
-adic.
However, there exists
S
-adic words with only two different substitutions that have superlinear complexity (see, for example,
[
24
]
). (For recent work on the converse, see Espinoza
[
22
]
.)

As mentioned in the introduction, recent work has explored classes of
S
-adic words on three letters with low complexity. For example, classes of such infinite words with upper bounds
2n+1
were found by Arnoux and Rauzy
[
5
]
and by Cassaigne, Labbé, and Leroy
[
17
]
, and a class of such infinite words
\mathbf{u}
with
2n+1\leq p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)\leq\frac{5}{2}n
was found by Berthé and Labbé
[
11
]
.

Correspondence between Multi-dimensional Continued Fractions and
S
-adic sequences

Connections between three-dimensional continued fraction algorithms and classes of

-adic sequences have been explored by many others in recent years. For an excellent general background, see Fogg
[
24
]
, Berthé and Delecroix
[
10
]
and Thuswaldner’s recent survey
[
40
]
. We will be following, to some extent, chapter 3 of
[
40
]
. In this section, we present a way to associate a TRIP map
T(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
with a set
S(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})=\left\{S_{0}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}),S_{1}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})\right\}

For the rest of the paper, we restrict our alphabet to

4.1
Abelianizations

We start with seeing how any substitution can be associated to a matrix via abelianization. While all of this works for any finite alphabet, we will restrict attention to the three letter alphabet
\mathcal{A}=\left\{1,2,3\right\}
for ease of notation.

Definition 9
.

The
abelianization
of a finite word
w
is defined as

\mathbf{\ell}(w)=\begin{bmatrix}\left|w\right|_{1}\\
\left|w\right|_{2}\\
\left|w\right|_{3}\end{bmatrix},

(7)

and the
frequency vector
is
\frac{1}{\left|w\right|}\mathbf{\ell}(w)
, where
\left|w\right|_{i}
is the number of times the character
i
appears in
w
.
We define the frequency vector of an infinite word
\mathbf{u}
to be
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})=\left(f_{1}(\mathbf{u}),f_{2}(\mathbf{u}),f_{3}(\mathbf{u})\right)
with

f_{i}(\mathbf{u})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\left|u_{0}u_{1}u_{2}\ldots u_{n-1}\right|_{i}}{n},

(8)

provided that this limit exists.

Definition 10
.

The abelianization of a substitution
\sigma
is a
3\times 3
matrix
\ell(\sigma)
defined by

\ell(\sigma)=\left\{a_{ij}\right\},\qquad a_{ij}=\left|\sigma(j)\right|_{i},

(9)

where
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}.

For example, the abelianization of the substitution

\sigma:\quad 1\rightarrow 2,\quad 2\rightarrow 3,\quad 3\rightarrow 13

is the matrix

\ell(\sigma)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\end{bmatrix}.

Note that two distinct substitutions can have the same abelianization, as seen for the substitution

\widehat{\sigma}:\quad 1\mapsto 2,\quad 2\mapsto 3,\quad 3\mapsto 31,

we have
\ell(\sigma)=\ell(\widehat{\sigma})
.
An important property is that under abelianization, the action of a substitution becomes matrix multiplication,

\ell(\sigma(w))=\ell(\sigma)\ell(w).

(10)

Note that this means
\ell(\sigma^{2})=\ell(\sigma)^{2}.

While two different substitutions can have the same matrix, we can always go from a
3\times 3
matrix with non-negative entries to a substitution. For example, given any matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}a&b&c\\
d&e&f\\
g&h&k\end{bmatrix},

the substitution

\sigma_{M}:\quad 1\mapsto 1^{a}2^{d}3^{g},\quad 2\mapsto 1^{b}2^{e}3^{h},\quad 3\mapsto 1^{c}2^{f}3^{k}

will have abelianization
M
.

4.2
Substitutions and TRIP Maps

We aim to define substitutions
S_{0}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
and
S_{1}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
whose abelianizations are
F_{0}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
and
F_{1}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
, respectively.
To do so, we first define the substitutions of the TRIP map
T(e,e,e)
as

•

S_{0}(e,e,e):\quad 1\mapsto 2,\quad 2\mapsto 3,\quad 3\mapsto 13
,

•

S_{1}(e,e,e):\quad 1\mapsto 1,\quad 2\mapsto 2,\quad 3\mapsto 13
.

Then, for each of the other 215 TRIP maps, we define

\displaystyle S_{i}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})=\sigma\circ S_{i}(e,e,e)\circ\tau_{i}

for
i\in\left\{0,1\right\}
. Here we consider the permutations
\sigma
and
\tau_{i}
on
\mathcal{A}
as substitutions which map each letter to a word of length
1
.
We define the
S(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
-adic sequences using Definition
8
with
S=\left\{S_{0}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}),S_{1}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})\right\}
.
When the choice of the permutations is clear from context, we will often refer to the substitutions
S_{i}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
simply by
S_{i}
.

We will routinely refer to the substitutions
S_{k}^{G}:=S_{1}^{k}\circ S_{0}
as “Gauss substitutions.”
Any coding sequence over
\left\{S_{0},S_{1}\right\}
containing
S_{0}
infinitely often can be expressed as a coding sequence over
\left\{S_{k}^{G}\right\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
simply by replacing each group of
k\geq 0
occurrences of
S_{1}
followed by an
S_{0}
by
S_{k}^{G}
.

We can associate points in
\triangle
with
S(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
-adic sequences
\mathbf{u}
according to their frequency ratios, and the Farey sequences of such points to the coding sequence of
\mathbf{u}
. The following proposition (which is a special case of Theorem 5.7 in Berthé and Vincent Delecroix
[
10
]
, which provides a proof)
determines when this is possible:

Proposition 1
.

For any
\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}\in S_{3}
, let
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
be the corresponding Farey matrices, and
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
the corresponding substitutions. Let
(i_{0},i_{1},i_{2},\cdots)
be the Farey sequence of some point
(x,y,z)\in\triangle
. If

\bigcap_{m=0}^{\infty}\triangle_{i_{0},i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{m-1}}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})=\left\{(x,y,z)\right\},

(11)

then any
S(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
-adic sequence
\mathbf{u}
with coding sequence
(S_{i_{0}},S_{i_{1}},S_{i_{2}},\cdots)
will have frequency vector
(x,y,z)
.

We give the following definition of primitivity from page 29 of Thuswaldner
[
40
]
:

Definition 11
.

An
S
-adic word
\mathbf{u}
with coding sequence
\left\{\sigma_{m}\right\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
is
primitive
if for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}^{0}}
, there exists
n>m
such that
M_{m}M_{m+1}M_{m+2}\cdots M_{n-1}
has all entries strictly positive, where each
M_{i}
is the abelianization matrix of the substitution
\sigma_{i}
.

Lemma 1.5.10 of
[
40
]
implies, in particular, that condition (
11
) is true for primitive
S
-adic words that are also recurrent.

Our ultimate goal is to find bounds on the complexity of
S
-adic sequences associated with various TRIP maps. Specifically, for any
\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1}\in S_{3}
, we would like to find an upper bound for
p
for any
F(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
-adic sequences. We are particularly interested in cases with low upper bounds such as
2n+1
or
3n
.

Remark 1
.

Our definition of
S_{i}
substitutions can differ from what one would get by taking the
F_{i}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
matrix as defined in
[
27
]
and defining an associated substitution to this matrix as was done in the previous subsection. For example, we have

S_{0}(13,e,e):\quad 1\mapsto 2,\quad 2\mapsto 1,\quad 3\mapsto 31,

but the substitution we would derive from the matrix
F_{0}(13,e,e)
in the previous subsection is

1\mapsto 2,\quad 2\mapsto 1,\quad 3\mapsto 13.

The definitions given above allow for equivalence relations between various
S
-adic sequences that correspond to certain equivalence relations between various TRIP maps which lead to the same complexities, as we will see in Section
7
.
There is, however, one source of freedom that we do have in choosing the substitutions from the Farey matrices, which we will explain in Section
8
.

4.3
Removing the Requirement of Primitivity

In most cases throughout the literature, complexity corresponding to multidimensional continued fraction maps is viewed through the lens of infinite
S
-adic words. However, it is possible to define a language directly from a coding sequence without a particular
S
-adic word using the following definition from
[
40
]
:

Definition 12
.

The
language associated with
a coding sequence
\mathbf{\sigma}=(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots)
is the smallest possible language (by inclusion) containing
(\sigma_{0}\circ\sigma_{1}\circ\ldots\circ\sigma_{n-1})(a)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
Equivalently,

\mathcal{L}(\sigma)=\left\{w:w\text{ is a factor of }(\sigma_{0}\circ\sigma_{1}\circ\ldots\circ\sigma_{n-1})(c)\text{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N},\;c\in\mathcal{A}\right\}.

(12)

If the coding sequence
\sigma=\left\{\sigma_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is primitive, as the vast majority of cases occurring in the literature are, then it makes no difference whether we consider the complexity of
S
-adic words with coding sequence
\sigma
or the complexity of the language
L(\sigma)
as defined in Definition
12
.
Indeed, assuming primitivity, all
S
-adic words
\mathbf{u}
with coding sequence
\sigma
satisfy
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{L}(\sigma)
, and such an
S
-adic word always exists as one can be found by taking a limit point of the sequence

u_{n}:=\left(\sigma_{0}\circ\sigma_{1}\circ\ldots\circ\sigma_{n-1}\right)(a)

for any
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

However, many of the
S
-adic languages that we consider in this paper are not primitive, so the distinction between these two perspectives is substantial.
For example, consider the substitutions associated to the
T(e,13,e)
TRIP map,

•

S_{0}:\quad 1\mapsto 13,\quad 2\mapsto 3,\quad 3\mapsto 2
,

•

S_{1}:\quad 1\mapsto 1,\quad 2\mapsto 2,\quad 3\mapsto 13
.

The
S
-adic word
\mathbf{u}=1323232323\ldots
with coding sequence
(S_{0},S_{0},S_{0},\ldots)
has complexity
n+2
, but binary sequence of
2
s and
3
s is also
S
-adic with the same coding sequence. Such words have complexity up to
2^{n}
, but they do not properly reflect the substitutions as each character is its own “
S
-adic component,” meaning it can be de-substituted an arbitrarily large number of times independently of the other characters.

We believe a reasonable condition to impose on
S
-adic words is that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a single character
c\in\mathcal{A}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u_{0}u_{1}u_{2}\ldots u_{n-1}
is a prefix of
\left(\sigma_{0}\circ\sigma_{1}\circ\ldots\circ\sigma_{m-1}\right)(c)
. We call such sequences
singly-generated
.
It can be shown that the only singly-generated
S
-adic sequence with this coding sequence is
1323232323\ldots
, so in a sense the true upper bound is
n+2
.

Now consider the substitutions associated with the TRIP map
T(13,12,132)
,

•

S_{0}:\quad 1\mapsto 1,\quad 2\mapsto 2,\quad 3\mapsto 31
,

•

S_{1}:\quad 1\mapsto 31,\quad 2\mapsto 3,\quad 3\mapsto 2
.

We would expect the complexity properties of
S(13,12,132)
-adic words with coding sequence
(S_{1},S_{1},S_{1},\ldots)
to be the same as those of
S(e,13,e)
-adic words with coding sequence
(S_{0},S_{0},S_{0},\ldots)
, as the corresponding substitutions are exactly the same with the order of characters reversed (see Section
7
for a more detailed discussion). However, an infinite singly-generated
S
-adic word with this coding sequence does not exist, as can be seen in

1\xmapsto{S_{1}}31\xmapsto{S_{1}}231\xmapsto{S_{1}}3231\xmapsto{S_{1}}\cdots,

giving us that the first term quickly starts to oscillate between
3
and
2
.

If we consider instead the complexity of the languages of coding sequences as defined in Definition
12
as opposed to the complexity of
S
-adic sequences, we have
n+2
for both cases and these issues are resolved.
For this reason, we believe considering complexity from the perspective of languages defined directly from coding sequences is more natural for our work.
Our complexity bounds immediately imply the same upper bounds on complexity for singly-generated
S
-adic words via the following:

Proposition 2
.

For any singly-generated
S
-adic word
\mathbf{u}
with coding sequence
\sigma
,
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u})\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\sigma)
, with equality if
\sigma
is primitive.

Certainly
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u})\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\sigma)
is always the case. The assumption of primitivity of
\sigma
, which means that eventually all the entries in the corresponding matrices are positive, will imply that no matter what is our initial “seed” for
\mathbf{u}
, all terms will eventually appear.

5
Proof Techniques for Complexity of
S
-Adic Sequences

Our goal is to find bounds for the complexity functions for various
S
-adic sequences. For example, in Theorem
2
, we will see that an
S
-adic sequence corresponding to the TRIP map
T(e,e,e)
(also called the triangle map) will satisfy the bound
p(n)\leq 3n
.
In order to find such bounds, we will need to relate the factors of length
n+1
or
n+2
to factors of length
n
.

The tools described in this section are the primary methods of determining complexities of
S
-adic words (see Cassaigne and Nicolas’s “Factor Complexity” in
[
13
]
and also
[
5
,
11
,
13
,
31
]
). However, we reformulate them to describe the complexity of languages in general rather than the language of a specific infinite word.

Definition 13
.

For
w\in\mathcal{L}
, we define the
left extension set
of
w
to be

E^{-}(w):=\left\{a\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}:aw\in\mathcal{L}\right\},

and the
right extension set
of
w
to be

E^{+}(w):=\left\{b\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}:wb\in\mathcal{L}\right\}.

Finally, we set

E(w):=\left\{(a,b)\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}\times\left\{1,2,3\right\}:awb\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u})\right\}.

We say
w
is
left special
if
|E^{-}(w)|\geq 2
,
right special
if
|E^{+}(w)|\geq 2
, and
bispecial
if it is both left special and right special.

Theorem 4.5.4 in Cassaigne and Nicolas’s “Factor Complexity” in
[
13
]
states

Theorem 1
.

Letting
\mathcal{L}_{n}
denotes the set of words of length
n
in any language
\mathcal{L}
, we have

1.

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)-p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}\left(|E^{-}(w)|-1\right)

2.

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)-p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}\left(|E^{+}(w)|-1\right)

3.

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+2)-2p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)+p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}m(w),

where

m(w):=|E(w)|-|E^{-}(w)|-|E^{+}(w)|+1.

(The first two formulas follows directly from the fact that there is a bijection from
\mathcal{L}_{n+1}
to pairs
(a,w)
with
w\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
a\in E^{-}(w)
, and similarly for
E^{+}(w)
.
We may restrict the summations in the above theorem to be only over the left (respectively right) special factors and the factors which have no left (respectively right) extensions since all other terms are zero. The third follows from the first two and the fact that there is a bijection from
\mathcal{L}_{n+2}
to triples
(a,w,b)
with
w\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
(a,b)\in E(w)
.)

We may then compute complexity by the following formulas:

Corollary 1
.

For any language
\mathcal{L}
,

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)-p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)=\left|\mathcal{L}_{1}\right|-1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{L}\\
\left|w\right|\leq n-1\end{subarray}}m(w).

(13)

In particular,

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)-p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)\leq\left|\mathcal{L}_{1}\right|-1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{L}\\
\left|w\right|\leq n-1\\
w\text{ bispecial}\end{subarray}}m(w),

(14)

where equality holds if
\mathcal{L}
is extendable.

Adopting the convention of Cassaigne and Nicolas from
[
13
]
, we call a bispecial factor
w

neutral
if
m(w)=0
.

6
Factor Complexity for
(e,e,e)
TRIP map: The Triangle Map

We are now done with the preliminaries. In this section, we prove the main technical result of this paper:

Theorem 2
.

The complexity function of any
S(e,e,e)
-adic language
\mathcal{L}
with rationally independent frequency vectors satisfies
2n+1\leq p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)\leq 3n
for all
n\geq 1
.

The
(e,e,e)
TRIP map has independent interest. It is usually called the triangle map and was originally created in
[
26
,
7
]
for number theoretic reasons, namely in an attempt to solve the Hermite Problem. Messaoudi, Nogueira, and Schweiger, in the beginning of
[
34
]

where they showed that the fast map is ergodic, discuss why this map is interesting dynamically. Copying from
[
9
]
,
we know that further dynamical properties were discovered by Berthé, Steiner and Thuswaldner
[
14
]
and by Fougeron and Skripchenko
[
25
]
. Bonanno, Del Vigna and Munday
[
16
]
and Bonanno and Del Vigna
[
15
]
recently used the
\mathbb{R}^{3}
slow triangle map to develop a tree structure of rational pairs in the plane. In a recent preprint, Ito
[
30
]
showed that the fast map is self-dual (in section three of that paper).

The upper bound is what is new. The lower bound stems from quite general principles, as shown by R. Tijdeman
[
41
]
. Also see the more recent work of Andrieu and Vivion
[
4
]

Finally, the

6.1
Preliminaries on
S
-adic Languages for the Triangle map

We begin by noting the following:

Lemma 1
.

The Farey sequence associated to any point in
\triangle
with rationally independent entries in the TRIP map
T(e,e,e)
has infinitely many
0
s.

Proof.

The proof is relatively straightforward. As discussed in Section
2
, we start with the cone spanned by the basis vectors
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
. As we apply various
F_{i}(e,e,e)
, we are shrinking the initial cone, but the edges are images of the
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
by the product of the
F_{i}(e,e,e)
. As each of these
F_{i}(e,e,e)
are in the special linear group, the cone will be spanned by vectors with integer entries. This means that after a finite number of applications of the
F_{i}(e,e,e)
, we have a cone
\triangle=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
, where each
v_{i}
has integer entries. Now let us suppose we simply keep applying the map
F_{1}(e,e,e)
over and over again. Then we get

F_{1}^{k}(e,e,e)(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})=(v_{1},v_{2},kv_{1}+v_{3}).
The only points that will be in
F_{1}^{k}(e,e,e)(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
for all
k
are those points in the plane spanned by
v_{1}
and
v_{2}.
But any point in this plane will have entries that are rationally related, as desired.
∎

This allows us to transform the coding sequence
\left\{S_{i_{m}}\right\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
into a Gauss coding sequence
\left\{G_{k_{m}}\right\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
, where

G_{k}:=S_{1}^{k}\circ S_{0}:\quad 1\mapsto 2,\quad 2\mapsto 1^{k}3,\quad 3\mapsto 1^{k+1}3.

(15)

(The adjective “Gauss” is being used in analog of the difference between the traditional Farey map and the traditional Gauss map for continued fractions; hence we are using Gauss to refer to concatenating sequences of the
F_{1}
map.)

We have the following proposition:

Proposition 3
.

Any Gauss coding sequence is primitive.

Proof.

The matrix whose abelianization gives us
G_{k}
is

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&k&k+1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\\
\end{array}\right)

Composing three Gauss maps, say
G_{k_{0}},G_{k_{1}}
and
G_{k_{2}}
will yield the product of the three matrices

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&k_{0}&k_{0}+1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&k_{1}&k_{1}+1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&k_{2}&k_{2}+1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\\
\end{array}\right)

which is

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}k_{0}+1&k_{0}k_{2}+k_{0}+1&k_{0}(k_{2}+1)+k_{0}+1\\
k_{1}&k_{1}+1&k_{1}+1\\
1&k_{2}+1&k_{2}+2\\
\end{array}\right).

As this matrix has all non-zero entries, we are done.
∎

From Proposition
2
and the above proposition, we have

Corollary 2
.

Any
S
-adic word
\mathbf{u}
with coding sequence
\sigma
and rationally independent frequency vectors has
L(\mathbf{u})=L(\sigma)
.

Thus, for the rest of this section, we will compute the complexity of such a sequence
\sigma
by computing the complexity of a corresponding
S
-adic word
\mathbf{u}
.

We define a sequence of de-substituted languages via preimages,
\left\{\mathcal{L}^{(n)}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, by
\mathcal{L}^{(0)}=\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{(n+1)}=G_{k_{n}}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(n)}\right)
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}
is the language of the coding sequence
\left\{G_{k_{n}},G_{k_{n+1}},G_{k_{nS+2}},\ldots\right\}
.

To control the linear complexity of
\mathbf{u}
, as we saw in Section
5
, we need to understand the non-neutral bispecial factors of
\mathbf{u}
. These are the finite subwords
w
with both the left extension set
E^{L}(w)

and the right extension set
E^{R}(w)
having at least two elements and with

0\neq m(w)=|E(w)|-|E^{L}(w)|-|E^{R}(w)|+1.

To prove Theorem
2
, we show that for all
n
, the summation in (
34
) is either
0
or
1
. To do so, it suffices to find an explicit sequence of bispecial factors

\left\{w_{0}^{+},w_{0}^{-},w_{1}^{+},w_{1}^{-},w_{2}^{+},w_{2}^{-},\ldots\right\}

such that:

1.

Every non-neutral bispecial element of
\mathcal{L}
is in this sequence (although it might also contain neutral bispecial factors)

2.

The lengths of the factors satisfy

|w_{0}^{+}|\leq|w_{0}^{-}|<|w_{1}^{+}|\leq|w_{1}^{-}|<|w_{2}^{+}|\leq|w_{2}^{-}|<\ldots.

3.

For all
n
,
0\leq m(w_{n}^{+})\leq 1
.

4.

For all
n
,
m(w_{n}^{+})=-m(w_{n}^{-})
.

To do this, we need to consider not just the bispecial elements of
\mathcal{L}
but also the bispecial elements of
\mathcal{L}^{(n)}
for all non-negative integers
n
.
Let
\text{Bispecial}(\mathcal{L})
denote the set of bispecial factors of
\mathcal{L}
. We will construct a map

A:\{\text{Bispecial}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(n)}\right)\setminus 1^{j}\}\rightarrow\text{Bispecial}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(n+1)}\right)

satisfying the following properties:

1.

Lengths decrease, that is, for all
w\in\{\text{Bispecial}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(m)}\right)\setminus 1^{j}\}
,

|A(w)|\leq|w|.

2.

For all
w\in\{\text{Bispecial}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(n)}\right)\setminus 1^{j}\}
, there is some
N
such that
A^{N}(w)
is either the empty word
\varepsilon
or a word of the form
1^{j}
. We call

the

of

.

Note that our definition of the term

is analogous to that in
[
11
]
.
We will show that for all

,
\mathcal{L}
has at most two non-neutral bispecial words of age

. If there is only one such word, we label it

, which we will show is neutral.
If there are two such words, we label one of them
w_{N}^{+}
and the other

and show that properties (c) and (d) above are satisfied.
All of this will take work, though almost all of the individual steps are fairly straightforward calculations. The difficulty lies in the amount of such calculations that are required.

6.2
General Techniques for Relating
\mathcal{L}
to
\mathcal{L}^{(m)}
and Allowed Words of length
2

At the forefront of our analysis is relating elements of
\mathcal{L}
to elements of
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
. In other words, given some finite word
w
, we want to find some finite word
\hat{v}
such that
w\in\mathcal{L}
if and only if
\hat{v}\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
. Recall as discussed in subsection
6.1
that we are given a Gauss coding sequence
G_{k_{0}}=S_{1}^{k_{0}}\circ S_{0},G_{k_{1}}=S_{1}^{k_{1}}\circ S_{0},\ldots,
that
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{(0)}
is our initial language and
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}=G_{k_{0}}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(0)}\right)
.

It follows from Definition
12
that for
\left|w\right|\geq 2
,

if and only if there is some
v\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
such that
w
is a factor of
G_{k_{0}}(v)
.
We want to find
v
by “de-substituting”
w
, which requires decomposing
w
into substitutions of single characters.
To do so, notice that in view of the Gauss substitutions (
15
), the last character of
G_{k_{0}}(c)
for any
c\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}
is either a
2
or a
3
, while all characters before the last character, if they exist, are
1
s. Thus, we simply insert a break after every

or
3
and de-substitute each block to uniquely write
w=aG_{k_{0}}(v)b
where
a
is a proper suffix of
G_{k_{0}}(c)
for some single character
c
and
b
is a proper prefix of
G_{k_{0}}(d)
for some single character
d
.
Below are some examples:

•

If
k_{0}=2
and
w=132111311131132111
, then we may write

w=13\;2\;1113\;1113\;113\;2\;111=13G_{k_{0}}(13321)111,

so
a=13
,
v=13321
, and
b=111
. The word
a
can be a suffix of either
G_{k_{0}}(2)
or
G_{k_{0}}(3)
, while
b
must be a prefix of
G_{k_{0}}(3)
, so we know that
w\in\mathcal{L}
implies
2v3\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
or
3v3\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
. Conversely, since
w
is a factor of both
G_{k_{0}}(2v1)
and
G_{k_{0}}(3v1)
, if
2v3\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
or
3v3\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
, then
w\in\mathcal{L}
.

•

If
k_{0}=1
and
w=22113131
, then we may write

w=2\;2\;113\;13\;1=G_{k_{0}}(1132)1,

so
a=\varepsilon
,
v=1132
, and
b=1
. The word

can be a prefix of either
G_{k_{0}}(2)
or
G_{k_{0}}(3)
, so

if and only if
v2\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
or
v3\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
.

Remark 2
.

Notice that this technique is specific to our particular TRIP substitution. In general, arbitrary choices of substitution will require individual analysis to determine how to, if possible, develop conditions on
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
for when a given word is in
\mathcal{L}
.

As an application of these observations, we now determine the set of allowed factors of length
2
in
\mathcal{L}
for different choices of
k_{0}
and
k_{1}
. We know that
\varepsilon
as well as all single characters occur in any

, but this is certainly not true for words of length

, and in fact the set of length

words in

depend on the coding sequence. This is the content of the following proposition.

The two-letter words in
\mathcal{L}
are also the extension set for
\varepsilon
. The two letter words that occur are said to be

and those that do not occur are said to be

.

Proposition 4
.

The set of allowed two letter words is determined by

and

as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}k_{0},k_{1}&\mbox{Allowed $2$-words}&\mbox{Forbidden $2$-words}\\
\hline\cr\hline\cr k_{0}=0,k_{1}=0&13,21,31,32,33&11,12,22,23\\

\end{array}

This implies that the empty word
\varepsilon
is always bispecial, but it is non-neutral only if both

and

with

.

In the rhetoric of
[

, we can also describe this result in terms of extension diagrams. These are

tables where the rows represent left extensions and the columns represent right extensions. By Proposition

, the extension diagrams of

are:

\begin{array}[]{cc||cc}k_{0}=0,k_{1}=0&\begin{tabular}[]{c|ccc}$\varepsilon$&1&2&3\\
\hline\cr 1&&&$\times$\\
2&$\times$&&\\
3&$\times$&$\times$&$\times$\end{tabular}&k_{0}=0,k_{1}\geq 1&\begin{tabular}[]{c|ccc}$\varepsilon$&1&2&3\\
\hline\cr 1&&&$\times$\\
2&$\times$&$\times$&\\
3&&$\times$&$\times$\end{tabular}\\

\hline\cr 1&$\times$&&$\times$\\
2&$\times$&&\\
3&$\times$&$\times$&\end{tabular}&k_{0}\geq 1,k_{1}\geq 1&\begin{tabular}[]{c|ccc}$\varepsilon$&1&2&3\\
\hline\cr 1&$\times$&&$\times$\\

Proof of Proposition

.

As with almost all of the results in this section, the proof is not deep but is instead simply keeping track of the notation and the maps.

First, in all four cases we are claiming that the words
12
and
23
are forbidden. In (
15
), we see that any occurrence of a
1
must be followed by a
1
or by a
3
, but not a
2
, so
12
is always forbidden.
Also, by the above procedure, we may uniquely de-substitute
23
into
12
and conclude that
23\in\mathcal{L}
if and only if

, so
23
must also be forbidden.
To see that
13
,
21
, and
32
are always allowed, simply notice that all are factors of
\left(G_{k_{0}}\circ G_{k_{1}}\circ G_{k_{2}}\right)(3)=\left(1^{k_{0}}3\right)^{k_{2}+1}2^{k_{1}+1}1^{k_{0}+1}3
.

The four remaining cases,
11
,
22
,
31
, and
33
, depend on the particular values of
k_{0}
and
k_{1}
.

We start with

. For any finite word

, every occurrence of the character

in

must occur in groups of either
k_{0}
or
k_{0}+1

2
or
3
, respectively. Furthermore, these groups of
1
s must be either at the beginning of
G_{k_{0}}(v)
or be immediately preceeded by the last character of

for some

, which must be either
2
or
3
. Hence, if
k_{0}=0
, then

cannot be a factor of

and is therefore forbidden, but if
k_{0}\geq 1
, then

is a factor of

, so

is allowed.

By the above procedure,
22
can be uniquely de-substituted into

, so

if and only if

. Hence,
22
is allowed if and only if
11
is allowed in
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
, or if
k_{1}\geq 1
.

Now consider

. If
k_{0}\geq 1
, then any occurrence of a
3
in

for some finite word

must be immediately preceded by a

, so

is forbidden. If

, then since

is a subword of

and

is allowed in

,

is allowed in
\mathcal{L}
.

Finally, we consider the word

. By the above procedure, we have that

if and only if
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
contains

,
23
,
32
, or
33
. We see that

is a subword of

and
G_{k_{0}}(32)=1^{k_{0}+1}31^{k_{0}}3
if and only if

, while

is always a subword of

is forbidden in

Therefore, it holds that

if and only if it is true that both

and

is a factor of

for some
v\in\left\{22,32,33\right\}
. By the cases completed above, we find that this is true for all cases except that where

and
k_{1}\geq 1
.
∎

w\in\mathcal{L}
to
\hat{v}\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}

, we were able to identify words

for which

As an illustration of the ambiguity that may occur, suppose we have

,

, and

.

or

,

, and

We have the following sequence, letting

\displaystyle w_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}G_{k_{0}}(w_{1})b_{0}

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{1}G_{k_{1}}(w_{2})b_{1}

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle=

6.8
Characterizing Extended Images of Bispecial Words

First to recap the notation and the goals. We have an
S
-adic
word
{\bf u}
described via a Gauss coding sequence
\{G_{k_{0}},G_{k_{1}},G_{k_{2}},\ldots\}
. Our language
\mathcal{L}
is all subwords of
{\bf u}
. We need to understand all of the bispecial factors in
{\bf u}
. We do this in part by looking at the bispecial factors in
\mathcal{L}^{1}
, which is the language of the Gauss coding sequence of
\{G_{k_{1}},G_{k_{2}},G_{k_{3}},\ldots\}
, etc.

In this subsection, we aim to characterize all possible extended images
w\in\mathcal{L}
of a bispecial word
v\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
based on its extension set
E(v)
and the value of
k_{0}
.

Proposition 12
.

Suppose
v\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
has age at least
1
. Then, for all extended images
w=aG_{k_{0}}(v)b
:

•

If
E^{-}(v)=\left\{1,3\right\}
, then
a=\varepsilon
and
E^{-}(w)=\left\{2,3\right\}
.

•

If
E^{-}(v)=\left\{2,3\right\}
, then
a=1^{k_{0}}3
and
E^{-}(w)=\left\{1,3\right\}
.

Furthermore:

•

If
E^{+}(v)=\left\{1,2\right\}
or
\left\{1,3\right\}
, then
w=aG_{k_{0}}(v)
is the unique extended image.

•

If
E^{+}(v)=\left\{2,3\right\}
, then
w=aG_{k_{0}}(v)1^{k_{0}}
is the unique extended image.

•

If
E^{+}(v)=\left\{1,2,3\right\}
and
k_{0}=0
, then
w=aG_{k_{0}}(v)
is the unique extended image and
E^{+}(w)=\left\{1,2,3\right\}
.

•

If
E^{+}(v)=\left\{1,2,3\right\}
and
k_{0}\geq 1
, then there are two extended images:
w_{+}=aG_{k_{0}}(v)
with
E^{+}(w_{+})=\left\{1,2\right\}
, and
w_{-}=aG_{k_{0}}(v)1^{k_{0}}
with
E^{+}(w_{-})=\left\{1,3\right\}
.

Proof.

These results are all direct applications of Proposition
7
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{L}
be bispecial. If the age of
w
is odd, then
E^{-}(w)=\left\{1,3\right\}
, while if the age of
w
is even and nonzero, then
E^{-}(w)=\left\{2,3\right\}
.

Proof.

By the results of Subsection
6.7
,
E^{-}(w)=\left\{1,3\right\}
for all bispecial words of age
1
. The lemma can be proven inductively by applying Proposition
12
, using this as a base case.
∎

Owing to Lemma
2
and Proposition
8
, we know all the bispecial factors of age
\geq 1
have left extension set
\left\{1,3\right\}
or left extension set
\left\{2,3\right\}
, so we can restrict our attention to these.

We proceed with our goal of characterizing all non-neutral bispecial
w\in\mathcal{L}
and the values of
m(w)
.
We begin with the case where the extended image is unique.

Proposition 13
.

Suppose that
v\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
is bispecial with age at least
1
. If
\left|E^{+}(v)\right|=2
or
k_{0}=0
, then there is only one extended image
w=aG_{k_{0}}(v)b
of
v
and the function
\alpha_{b,k_{0}}^{R}
is a bijection from
E^{+}(v)
to
E^{+}(w)
. Consequently,
\left|E^{+}(v)\right|=\left|E^{+}(w)\right|
and
m(v)=m(w)
.

For foreshadowing purposes, we will see in subsection
6.9
that this allows us to define various
A^{-1}(w)
.

Proof.

First notice that Lemma
2
and Proposition
12
guarantee that either
E^{-}(v)=\left\{1,3\right\}
or
E^{-}(v)=\left\{2,3\right\}
and that
\alpha_{a}^{L}
is a bijection from
E^{-}(v)
to
E^{-}(w)
.
It can then be verified by applying Proposition
7
on all cases that if
\left|E^{+}(v)\right|=2
or
k_{0}=0
, only one choice of
b
makes
w
right special, and for this
b
,
\alpha_{b,k_{0}}^{R}
is injective when restricted to
E^{R}(v)
, meaning it is a bijection from
E^{+}(v)
to
E^{+}(w)
.
Hence,
E(w)
can be derived from

by applying a permutation to both the left extensions and the right extensions. Since permutations preserve
\left|E\right|
,
\left|E^{-}\right|
, and
\left|E^{+}\right|
, this means
\left|E^{+}(v)\right|=\left|E^{+}(w)\right|
and

.
∎

In the case where
\left|E^{+}(v)\right|=2
for some bispecial
v\in\mathcal{L}^{(k)}
, for any

, the above results imply that
v
has a unique descendant

of order

and that

. Therefore, no further investigation is required on bispecial words with only two right extensions.
In fact, based on the results of Subsection
6.7
, we may conclude the following:

Corollary 5
.

Every non-neutral bispecial word with age at least
1
is a descendant of
1^{k_{0}}3
or
1^{k_{0}}32^{k_{1}}
.

Proof.

Proposition
10
gives an exclusive list of the possible bispecial words of age
1
. However, by Proposition
11
, for all of the words
w
in this list other than
1^{k_{0}}3
and
1^{k_{0}}32^{k_{1}}
,
\left|E^{-}(w)\right|=\left|E^{-}(w)\right|=2
and
m(w)=0
whenever
w
is bispecial.
∎

There is only one case where a bispecial word

can have two extended images
w_{+}
and
w_{-}
: that where
\left|E^{+}(v)\right|=3
and

. In this case, to determine the values

and

This requires characterizing the possible extension sets of all bispecial factors with three right extensions.

Proposition 14

Let

be bispecial with age at least

and
\left|E^{+}(w)\right|=3
. The extension set of

\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&1&2&3\\
\hline\cr i&\times&\times&\times\\
3&&\times&\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&1&2&3\\
\hline\cr i&\times&\times&\times\\
3&&&\times\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&1&2&3\\
\hline\cr i&\times&\times&\times\\

3&&\times&\times\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&1&2&3\\
\hline\cr i&\times&\times&\\

By the results of Subsection

, the only possible age

Suppose that

is if

. In this case, by Proposition

, the extension diagram of

can be derived from that of

In the case where the extended image of a bispecial word is not unique, both extended images only have two right extensions, so the following is all that remains to characterize all non-neutral bispecial words
w
and the values of

:

Proposition 15
.

Suppose that
v\in\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
is bispecial with
\left|E^{+}(v)\right|=3

. Let

to determine

and

∎

Note that it is not always true that

6.9
Construction of a sequence containing all non-neutral bispecial words

Proposition 16
.

There is a sequence

w_{0}^{+},w_{0}^{-},w_{1}^{+},w_{1}^{-},\ldots,w_{m}^{+},w_{m}^{-},\ldots.

of words in
\mathcal{L}
containing all non-neutral bispecial words such that:

1.

For all
m
,

0\leq m(w_{m}^{+})=-m(w_{m}^{-})\leq 1

2.

\left|w_{0}^{+}\right|\leq\left|w_{0}^{-}\right|<\left|w_{1}^{+}\right|\leq\left|w_{1}^{-}\right|<\left|w_{2}^{+}\right|\leq\left|w_{2}^{-}\right|<\cdots

In this section we will define the sequence and prove the first part.
In the process, we will also show the following:

•

If
w_{m}^{+}=w_{m}^{-}
, then
\left|E^{+}(w_{m}^{+})\right|=\left|E^{+}(w_{m}^{-})\right|=3
.

•

If
w_{m}^{+}\neq w_{m}^{-}
, then
\left|E^{+}(w_{m}^{+})\right|=\left|E^{+}(w_{m}^{-})\right|=2
.

We will prove the increase in the lengths of the elements of the sequence in the next section.

The various
w_{k}^{+}
and
w_{k}^{-}
are subwords of the initial language
\mathcal{L}
. There are analogous subwords in each of the languages
\mathcal{L}^{(k)}
. We will write these subwords as

w_{k}^{+}(\mathcal{L}^{(k)})
and
w_{k}^{-}(\mathcal{L}^{(k)})
when it is important to specify the language.

Start by setting

w_{0}^{+}=\varepsilon,\qquad w_{0}^{-}=1^{k_{0}}.

These are the same when
k_{0}=0.

Set

w_{1}^{+}=1^{k_{0}}3,\qquad w_{1}^{-}=1^{k_{0}}32^{k_{1}}.

These are equal precisely when
k_{1}=0.

We know that
\left\{w_{0}^{+},w_{0}^{-},w_{1}^{+},w_{1}^{-}\right\}
includes all non-neutral bispecial words in
\mathcal{L}
with age
0
or
1
, and by Corollary
5
, all non-neutral bispecial words with age
n
are descendents of
w_{1}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(n-1)}\right)
or
w_{1}^{-}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(n-1)}\right)
.

Now assume that we know how to construct
w_{0}^{+},w_{0}^{-},w_{1}^{+},w_{1}^{-},\ldots,w_{m}^{+},w_{m}^{-}
and that
w_{m}^{+}\neq w_{m}^{-}
implies
\left|E^{+}(w_{m}^{+})\right|=\left|E^{-}(w_{m}^{-})\right|=2
. We must show how to construct the next two terms
w_{m+1}^{+},w_{m+1}^{-}
.

We first look at the corresponding sequence for
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
,

w_{0}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right),w_{0}^{-}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right),w_{1}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right),w_{1}^{-}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right),\ldots,w_{m}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right),w_{m}^{-}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right),\ldots.

We will now find
w_{m+1}^{+},w_{m+1}^{-}
from knowing
w_{m}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right),w_{m}^{-}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right).

There are the following cases.
If
w_{m}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right)\neq w_{m}^{-}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right),
then we know that
\left|E^{+}(w_{m}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right)\right|=\left|E^{+}(w_{m}^{-}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right)\right|=2
, which means that both
A^{-1}(w_{m}^{+})
and
A^{-1}(w_{m}^{-})
are unique. Thus we set

w_{m+1}^{+}=A^{-1}(w_{m}^{+}),\;w_{m+1}^{-}=A^{-1}(w_{m}^{-}).

Next suppose
w_{m}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right)=w_{m}^{-}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right)
. If
A^{-1}(w_{m}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right))
is unique, then we again define
w_{m+1}^{+}=A^{-1}(w_{m}^{+})
and
w_{m+1}^{-}=A^{-1}(w_{m}^{-})
. If
A^{-1}(w_{m}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right))
is not unique, then by the results of Subsection
6.8
, we know
E^{R}\left(w_{m}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right)\right)=\{1,2,3\}
,
k_{0}=0
, and
A^{-1}\left(w_{m}^{+}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}\right)\right)
has two elements, differing only by a suffix of
1^{k_{0}}.

We set
w_{m+1}^{+}
and
w_{m+1}^{-}
to be these two elements, with

w_{m+1}^{-}=w_{m+1}^{+}1^{k_{0}}.

All our work so far has shown that in all cases, for all
m
,

0\leq m(w_{m}^{+})=-m(w_{m}^{-})\leq 1.

Given any bispecial
w\in\mathcal{L}
, we know that by repeatedly applying the map
A
to
w
, we will eventually get to
\varepsilon
or some
1^{k}
. All of the above work produces all possible non-neutral descendents of
\varepsilon
and of
1^{k}
. In particular,
w
must be in this sequence.

6.10
Showing
|w_{m}^{+}|\leq|w_{m}^{-}|<|w_{m+1}^{+}|

This section has a different feel than the earlier sections. Our goal is to prove the following:

Proposition 17
.

We have

\left|w_{0}^{+}(\mathcal{L})\right|\leq\left|w_{0}^{-}(\mathcal{L})\right|<\left|w_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{L})\right|\leq\left|w_{1}^{-}(\mathcal{L})\right|<\left|w_{2}^{+}(\mathcal{L})\right|\leq\left|w_{2}^{-}(\mathcal{L})\right|<\cdots.

We will do this through using linear algebra on the abelianizations of these finite words, as well as the results of the extension diagrams.

Before giving the proof, which is at the end of this section after the proof of Lemma
3
,
we need to set some notation. As defined in subsection
4.1
, for a factor
w
, we write the “abelianization” of
w
as

\ell(w)=\begin{bmatrix}\left|w\right|_{1}\\
\left|w\right|_{2}\\
\left|w\right|_{3}\end{bmatrix},

where
\left|w\right|_{i}
is the number of
i
’s in
w
.

Now we define
a_{m}^{+},a_{m}^{-},b_{m}^{+},b_{m}^{-}
so that

w_{m}^{+}(\mathcal{L})=a_{m}^{+}w_{m-1}^{+}(\mathcal{L}^{(1)})b_{m}^{+},\qquad w_{m}^{-}(\mathcal{L})=a_{m}^{-}w_{m-1}^{-}(\mathcal{L}^{(1)})b_{m}^{-}.

The incidence matrix of the substitution
G_{k_{0}}
is

M_{k_{0}}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&k_{0}&1+k_{0}\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\end{bmatrix}.

(18)

Therefore, we have

\mathbf{\ell}(w_{m}^{+}(\mathcal{L}))=M_{k_{0}}\mathbf{\ell}(w_{m-1}^{+}(\mathcal{L}^{(1)}))+\mathbf{\ell}(a_{m}^{+})+\mathbf{\ell}(b_{m}^{+}).

(19)

Since
a_{m}^{+}
and
b_{m}^{+}
may take only one of two possible values, by defining

A_{m}^{+}(\mathcal{L}):=\begin{cases}1,&a_{m}^{+}=1^{k_{0}}3\\
0,&a_{m}^{+}=\varepsilon,\end{cases}\qquad B_{m}^{+}(\mathcal{L}):=\begin{cases}1,&b_{m}^{+}=1^{k_{0}}\\
0,&b_{m}^{+}=\varepsilon,\end{cases}

6.11
Proving Theorem
2
, the

We are finally ready to prove that the factor complexity
p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)
is bounded above by

(Theorem
2
), the whole goal of this long section. (As mentioned at the beginning of Section
6
, the lower bound of
p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)\geq 2n+1

stems from quite general principles, as shown by R. Tijdeman
[
41
]
.)

We need one more lemma:

Lemma 4
.

We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{L}\\
\left|w\right|\leq n-1\\
w\text{ non-neutral}\end{subarray}}m(w)\in\left\{0,1\right\}

Proof.

Our sequence
w_{0}^{+},w_{0}^{-},w_{1}^{+},w_{1}^{-},\ldots,w_{n}^{+},w_{n}^{-},\ldots
was created precisely to prove this. The terms in the sequence have non-decreasing lengths, and each time some
w_{n}^{+}
has multiplicity
m(w_{n}^{+})\neq 0
, we know not only that
m(w_{n}^{+})=1
but also that for the very next term
w_{n}^{-}
we have
m(w_{n}^{-})=-1
.
This means that the above sum will always alternate between
0
and
1
, giving us our result.
∎

Now for the grand finale:

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Refer back to Corollary
1
, which states that for any language

\mathcal{L}
,

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)-p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)=\left|\mathcal{L}_{1}\right|-1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{L}\\
\left|w\right|\leq n-1\end{subarray}}m(w).

(33)

In particular,

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)-p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)\leq\left|\mathcal{L}_{1}\right|-1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{L}\\
\left|w\right|\leq n-1\\
w\text{ bispecial}\end{subarray}}m(w),

(34)

where equality holds if
\mathcal{L}
is extendable.

For our
(eee)
language, this means that

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)\leq p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)+|\mathcal{L}_{1}|-1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{L}\\
\left|w\right|\leq n-1\end{subarray}}m(w).

By the above lemma, this means that for all
n
we have

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)\leq p_{\mathcal{L}}+|\mathcal{L}_{1}|.

In our language, we know that all three subwords of length one, namely
1,2,3,
can appear. Thus

|\mathcal{L}_{1}|=3,

giving us

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)\leq p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)+3.

We now simply induct over
n
. As
p_{\mathcal{L}}(0)=0
, the base case simply is stating that
p_{\mathcal{L}}(1)\leq 3
, which of course we know to be true since
p_{\mathcal{L}}(1)=|\mathcal{L}_{1}|=3.

Then if
p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)\leq 3n,
we have

p_{\mathcal{L}}(n+1)\leq p_{\mathcal{L}}(n)+3\leq 3n+3=3(n+1),

as desired.

∎

7
Equivalence Classes of TRIP Maps

The work in finding the complexity bounds for the
T(e,e,e)
TRIP map was detailed and technical. It is hard for us to imagine that such work would be worthwhile to imitate for the other 215 TRIP maps. However, following the procedure in Section 8.2 of
[
27
]
, certain TRIP maps are equivalent via transformations which preserve the complexities of the associated
S
-adic sequences, reducing the 216 TRIP maps to 21 equivalence classes.

7.1
Reduction to 36 Maps through Conjugacy

Each permutation
\rho\in S^{3}
is a bijection on
\mathcal{A}
, so given an infinite sequence
\mathbf{u}=u_{0}u_{1}u_{2}\ldots
in
\mathcal{A}
, we may apply
\rho
as a substitution to yield

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{u}):=\rho(u_{0})\rho(u_{1})\rho(u_{2})\cdots,

and similarly for finite words.
For any language
\mathcal{L}
, we can define the language
\rho(\mathcal{L}):=\left\{\rho(w):w\in\mathcal{L}\right\}
, whose complexity will be identical to that of
\mathcal{L}
.
This motivates the following proposition:

Proposition 18
.

If
\mathcal{L}
is an
S(\rho,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
-adic language with coding sequence
\left\{S_{i_{n}}(\rho,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})\right\}
, then
\rho(\mathcal{L})
is
S(e,\tau_{0}\rho,\tau_{1}\rho)
-adic with coding sequence
\left\{S_{i_{n}}(e,\tau_{0}\rho,\tau_{1}\rho)\right\}
.

This reduces the number of classes of
S
-adic languages to consider from 216 to 36.

7.2

In this section, we show how under certain conditions the 36 distinct classes of
S
-adic sequences can be reduced further through twinning, or interchanging the roles of the
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
substitutions as well as the

and
F_{1}

[

]
.

The dynamics of the TRIP maps and the substitutions will in many ways remain unchanged, and factors of the
S
-adic sequences will be related to factors of their twins by the reversal operation:

Definition 15

For any finite word

R(v)=v_{n-1}\cdots v_{2}v_{1}v_{0}

\sigma

R(\sigma)(i)=R(\sigma(i))
, and the reversal of a language
\mathcal{L}
by
R(\mathcal{L}):=\left\{R(w):w\in\mathcal{L}\right\}

Clearly, the complexities of
\mathcal{L}
and

are identical.

The

The associated substitutions have the property that

S_{1}(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})=R\left(S_{0}(\sigma(13),(12)\tau_{0},(12)\tau_{1})\right)

Proposition 19

Let
\mathcal{L}
be an
S(\sigma,\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
-adic language with coding sequence

The remainder of this paper is dedicated to exploring classes of

By the results of the previous section, it suffices to consider only 21 cases, explicitly listed in Subsection

Theorem 3

The top row in the above are all of the TRIP maps conjugate to

Our second class of TRIP maps are all of the conjugates and twins of the TRIP map

∎

We now turn to the maps that are called “degenerate” in

Theorem 5

Proof of Theorem
5

By direct calculation, we have that

•

S_{0}(e,12,e):\quad 1\mapsto 3,\quad 2\mapsto 2,\quad 3\mapsto 13

•

S_{0}(e,12,13):\quad 1\mapsto 3,\quad 2\mapsto 2,\quad 3\mapsto 13

All of the above pairs of substitutions, when restricted to

Conclusion and Future Directions

Factor complexity is of course only one of many ways to understand
S
-adic sequences. Others are seen, for example, in the recent survey by Thuswaldner
[
40
]
. The extent to which other properties, for example, balance, hold for TRIP maps is mostly unknown. There are many different multi-dimensional continued fraction algorithms that are not in the family of 216 TRIP maps. As shown in
[
20
]
, almost all of them are combination triangle partition maps, and hence various combinations of the maps considered in this paper. How to take known complexity bounds for, say, two multidimensional continued fraction algorithms and then determine the complexity bounds for some combination of the two would be important to know. Possibly the recent techniques developed by Berthé, Steiner and Thuswaldner
[
14
]
could be used.
