Selected biomarkers of coagulation, inflammation, and vascular tone in young women with normal weight obesity
Authors: Waldemar Pluta, Anna Lubkowska, Wioleta Dudzińska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndividuals with the Normal Weight Obesity (NWO) phenotype are characterized by excessive body fat despite normal Body Mass Index (BMI), often leading to underestimated cardiovascular risk. The mechanisms linking this specific body composition to early vascular damage remain unclear. The aim of this study was to evaluate endothelial dysfunction biomarkers in young women with NWO, and to identify the main axes of vascular impairment. The study included 176 young women (median age 20) with normal BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m²). Based on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), participants were classified into an NWO group (n = 88; percent body fat (PBF) ≥ 35.8%) and a healthy control group (NW; n = 88). Serum levels of tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA), von Willebrand factor (vWF), Angiopoietin-1 (Ang-1), Nitric Oxide (NO), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) were analyzed. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to identify latent patterns of endothelial function. Women with NWO exhibited significantly higher levels of tPA (1.86 vs. 1.48 ng/ml; p = 0.038), vWF (72.24 vs. 63.62 ng/ml; p = 0.038), and Ang-1 (515.3 vs. 491.63 pg/ml; p = 0.008) compared to controls. PCA identified three principal components explaining 71.68% of the total variance: Coagulation (tPA, vWF), Inflammation/Angiogenesis (hs-CRP, Ang-1), and Vasodilation (NO). Young women with NWO exhibit subclinical endothelial dysfunction, which is characterized primarily by a pro-thrombotic state and disturbed angio-inflammatory balance. These changes occur even before the onset of overt metabolic or cardiovascular disease.Subject terms: Preclinical research, Preventive medicine

Introduction

Obesity and its associated clinical, economic, and epidemiological burdens have been the subject of extensive research [1, 2]. Excessive adiposity causes cardiometabolic dysregulation through complex, interconnected pathways. Key mechanisms include endothelial dysfunction, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation, which together contribute to coronary heart disease, hypertension, and even stroke [3]. Currently, the primary measure of obesity is body mass index (BMI), which unfortunately does not provide sufficient information about an individual’s phenotype (e.g., by failing to differentiate muscle from fat tissue). Although obesity remains a major problem and a topic of scientific interest, increasing attention is being paid to “hidden obesity,” also known in the literature as normal-weight obesity (NWO). Individuals with NWO have a normal body mass index (BMI) and do not exhibit phenotypic signs of obesity. Consequently, they are often overlooked in standard risk assessments, despite the presence of significant metabolic abnormalities [4].

This study builds on our previous research [5], which involved 330 young women from north-western Poland with a normal BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m²). Using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), we identified the NWO phenotype in 27.3% of the cohort and established a percent body fat (PBF) cut-off of 35.8%. Importantly, despite being of normal weight, these women exhibited an unfavorable metabolic profile, including insulin resistance and dyslipidaemia. This raises questions about the early onset of vascular complications in this group.

These metabolic disturbances prompted us to investigate the mechanistic link between occult adiposity and cardiovascular risk. Therefore, we focused on a key link in this process - vascular endothelial dysfunction. The endothelium, as the active layer of cells lining blood vessels, plays an important role in regulating inflammation, angiogenesis, coagulation, vascular tone, and the exchange of fluids and solutes [6]. Endothelial dysfunction is considered an early marker of the development of cardiovascular diseases [7] and may be a mechanism linking the metabolic abnormalities observed in the NWO phenotype with increased cardiovascular risk.

The aim of this study was to evaluate selected biochemical markers of endothelial dysfunction in young women with the NWO phenotype and to identify the main axes of vascular impairment using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The study focused on biomarkers reflecting distinct pathophysiological pathways: tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA; fibrinolysis), von Willebrand factor (vWF; thrombosis), nitric oxide (NO; vasodilation), angiopoietin-1 (Ang-1; regulation of angiogenesis), and C-reactive protein (CRP; inflammation).

We hypothesise that women with the NWO phenotype (PBF ≥ 35.8%) will exhibit specific features of vascular endothelial dysfunction. Our study fills a gap in current knowledge by providing a comprehensive assessment of biomarkers reflecting different aspects of endothelial function. To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate DXA-derived body composition data with multivariate analysis of endothelial biomarkers in young women with NWO, addressing the critical need for early risk stratification.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Pomeranian Medical University in Szczecin (no. KB-0012/13/2021). Prior to inclusion, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Volunteers

This study is a continuation of our previous work [5], which examined 330 women aged 18–24 with a normal BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), residing in an urban area in western Poland, and attending a university. Women were excluded from the study if they smoked, had metal implants, were physically active (athletes or regularly engaged in competitive exercise), reported taking vitamin and/or mineral supplements, reported acute and/or chronic illnesses, used hypolipidemic, antihypertensive, antiglycemic, or insulin medications, were pregnant, breastfeeding, or taking hormone therapy, were undergoing nutritional monitoring, and/or had changed their usual diet in the past 6 months.

In the previous phase of the study [5], we established a new cutoff point for PBF using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to identify women at increased cardiometabolic risk. This specific threshold was identified by the Youden index (0.71) as the most effective value for predicting a metabolically unhealthy phenotype in young women, providing a sensitivity of 0.949 and a specificity of 0.763. Applying a PBF threshold of 35.8%, we established an NWO group (n = 88; PBF ≥ 35.8%) and a corresponding matched control group (NW; n = 88; PBF < 35.8%). Both groups were drawn from the same population of young women with normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m²). To minimize confounding and selection bias, a 1:1 case-control matching procedure was employed, in which control subjects (NW) were paired with NWO participants based on the closest available BMI and age.

Research procedures

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric data collected during the first stage of the study were used [5].

Blood collection and analysis of hematological parameters

Biological materials obtained in the first stage of the study were used. Detailed protocols for blood collection, processing, and storage were described in our previous report [5].

Biochemical analysis

The determination of tPA, vWF, and Ang-1 levels was performed using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits from EIAab (Wuhan EIAab Science Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). The measurement ranges and precision parameters were as follows:

PA: 0.156–10 ng/mL (intra-assay CV ≤ 5.6%, inter-assay CV ≤ 8.1%)

vWF: 3.12–200 ng/mL (intra-assay CV ≤ 4.4%, inter-assay CV ≤ 7.9%)

Ang-1: 7.8–500 pg/mL (intra-assay CV ≤ 4.6%, inter-assay CV ≤ 6.9%)

For Ang-1, samples with concentrations exceeding the upper limit of the standard curve (500 pg/ml) were diluted with the appropriate sample diluent and re-tested to ensure all measurements were accurately quantified within the linear dynamic range of the assay.

NO concentration was determined by colorimetric method using Nitrate/Nitrite Colorimetric Assay Kit from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA), with a measurement range of 2–35 µM and precision of ≤2.7% (intra-assay) and ≤3.4% (inter-assay).

All determinations were performed in duplicate, maintaining the quality control standards recommended by the manufacturers.

A spectrophotometric method was used to determine the concentration of high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP). The analyses were performed in the accredited Medical Diagnostic Laboratory of the Laboratory Medicine Team of the 109th Military Specialist Hospital of the Independent Public Health Care Facility with an Outpatient Clinic in Szczecin.

Statistical analysis

To estimate the minimal size of the study sample, the G*Power 3.1.9.4 software was used. The following parameters were set: an effect size of 0.5, an error probability of 0.05, and a power of 0.95. The non-central parameter δ was 3.317. The total sample size was 176, with an actual power of 0.951.

Statistica 13.3 (Statistica PL, StatSoft, Krakow, Poland) software was used for statistical analysis. The distribution’s normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The data distribution deviates from the normal distribution, therefore detailed analyses were carried out using non-parametric tests. The results are presented as the median and the interquartile range (Q25–Q75). The significance of the differences was demonstrated using the Mann–Whitney test. Correlation analysis was performed using Spearman correlation.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to reduce the dimensionality of the data and identify hidden patterns in the structure of relationships between variables. Bartlett’s sphericity test and Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) sampling adequacy measure were performed to verify the validity of PCA. Principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1 (Kaiser criterion) were extracted. The analysis was based on the correlation matrix, and the results were subjected to Varimax rotation to obtain a simpler structure of components.

To further investigate whether the observed differences were independent of body mass, an Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was performed for key vascular and metabolic parameters, with BMI included as a continuous covariate.

The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Group characteristics

Detailed group characteristics and analysis of body composition were described in the previous article [8]. Briefly, participants were young women with a median age of 20 years. The NWO group was characterized by statistically significantly higher PBF (37.9 vs. 32.1%, p < 0.0001) and BMI (22.7 vs. 20.4 kg/m2, p < 0.0001). Detailed characteristics of the study group are presented in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Table S1–S3).

The analysis of markers of endothelial function and inflammation parameters showed significantly higher values in the NWO group compared to the control group were observed for tPA (1.86 vs. 1.48 ng/ml; p = 0.038), vWF (72.24 vs. 63.62 ng/ml; p = 0.038), Ang-1 (515.3 vs. 491.63 pg/ml; p = 0.008) and hs-CRP (0.63 vs. 0.6 mg/l; p = 0.014). Higher NO values were shown in the NW group (7.72 vs. 6.44 µM), but this was not a statistically significant difference (p = 0.479). Discussion

Main findings of the study

The presented study showed that young women with the metabolic obesity phenotype at normal body weight (NWO) are characterized by significantly altered markers of vascular endothelial dysfunction compared to women with normal body weight and normal metabolic profile (NW). This is a continuation of our two previous studies. In the first study, we identified the NWO phenotype in 27.3% of young women from northwestern Poland, establishing a threshold of body fat percentage (PBF ≥ 35.8%) as a potential critical marker for cardiometabolic risk [5]. In the second study, we observed that the NWO phenotype is characterized by a subclinical pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic hematological profile, manifested by elevated leukocyte and platelet indices [8]. The key findings of this part of the study include: (1) identification of a specific profile of endothelial dysfunction markers in women with NWO, with angiopoietin-1, tissue-type plasminogen activator, and von Willebrand factor as the most diagnostically relevant, (2) separation of three major components of endothelial function by PCA: coagulation and fibrinolysis (30.64% of variance), inflammation and angiogenesis (23.09% of variance), and vasodilatory function (17.95% of variance).

Indicators of the coagulation and fibrinolysis process

In PCA, principal component 1 (PC1)—the coagulation and fibrinolysis axis—explained 30.64% of the total variance in the biomarkers and was defined by strongly negative loadings for tPA (−0.82) and vWF (−0.86). Low PC1 values therefore correspond to a simultaneous increase in the concentrations of both markers, indicating early activation of prothrombotic mechanisms in the NWO phenotype.

Among the volunteers in our study, women with NWO had significantly higher tPA concentrations (1.86 vs. 1.48 ng/ml; p = 0.038) compared with the control group. tPA, released primarily by endothelial cells, is a key enzyme initiating fibrinolysis by converting plasminogen to plasmin, which ultimately degrades cross-linked fibrin with the release of D-dimers. Although endogenous tPA stimulates fibrinolysis, paradoxically, many prospective epidemiological studies have shown that higher plasma tPA antigen concentrations are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events in healthy individuals [9] and in individuals with established coronary artery disease [10, 11]. This occurs because tPA released into the blood immediately forms a complex with circulating plasminogen activator inhibitor (PAI-1) [12]. Therefore, tPA antigen detection assays primarily measure inactive tPA/PAI-1 complexes, which are strongly correlated with tPA antigen levels and PAI-1 activity. Hence, higher tPA antigen concentrations may paradoxically reflect greater antifibrinolytic capacity [13], which in turn promotes atherothrombotic coronary occlusion and recurrent cardiovascular events. Elevated plasma tPA concentrations have been reported to be associated with insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and the presence of obesity, and an increased risk of coronary artery disease [14, 15].

The literature describes that both tPA and its inhibitor PAI-1 are elevated in individuals with obesity [16, 17], with the predominance of PAI-1 leading to hypofibrinolysis and increased thrombotic risk [18]. In patients with metabolic syndrome, impaired fibrinolysis was observed despite high levels of tPA and PAI-1 [19]. Basurto et al. demonstrated significantly higher PAI-1 in a group with metabolically unhealthy normal body weight, emphasizing the need for diagnostics in individuals with apparently normal weight [20]. Zhang et al. demonstrated that tPA concentration is sensitive to changes in adipose tissue – strong correlations were demonstrated between changes in visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue and changes in tPA levels in both sexes after weight loss [21].

The second component of PC1 was vWF, the concentration of which was significantly higher in the NWO group compared to the control group (72.24 vs. 63.62 ng/ml; p = 0.038). This is consistent with literature data, according to which increased vWF concentration is observed in individuals with obesity [22]. vWF plays a key role in thrombosis, it is responsible for mediating the adhesion of platelets to the damaged vascular subendothelium and then their aggregation [23]. Elevated vWF concentration is widely recognized as a marker of early endothelial activation and prothrombotic vascular state [4, 24].

Our results indicate the potential association between observed vWF elevations and prothrombotic mechanisms occurring early in the course of subclinical endothelial dysfunction in young women with NWO. Recent literature has shown that chronic low-grade inflammation and abnormal adipokine profiles in obesity lead to increased synthesis and release of vWF by endothelial cells and perivascular adipose tissue, which enhances platelet and leukocyte adhesion to the vessel wall [25, 26]. These data indicate that in the NWO phenotype, subclinical endothelial dysfunction includes increased synthesis and release of tPA and vWF. Circulating t-PA [27] and vWF [28] levels are associated with the risk of coronary heart disease, and their elevated levels in individuals with NWO may therefore promote atherothrombotic complications.

Inflammation and angiogenesis

Inflammation is a fundamental immunological process that, under physiological conditions, constitutes the body’s defense mechanism. However, chronic low-grade inflammation, characteristic of obesity, can lead to endothelial dysfunction and the development of cardiovascular disease [29, 30]. In our PCA, the second principal component (PC2) explained 23.09% of the total variance of the biomarkers and was defined by the opposite loadings for hs-CRP (+0.71) and Ang-1 (−0.74). The antagonistic pattern of these markers reflects the balance between proinflammatory processes and vascular repair mechanisms, suggesting a disruption of vascular homeostasis in the NWO phenotype.

In our cohort a statistically significantly higher hs-CRP concentration was observed in the NWO group (0.65 vs. 0.60 mg/l; p = 0.014). A similar upward trend in hs-CRP in the NWO group, although without statistical significance, was described by Ashtary-Larky et al. [31] and Kang et al. [32]. This was also confirmed by a systematic review and meta-analysis (SMD 0.60; 95% CI: 0.30–0.91) [29]. On the other hand, Amani et al. [33] found lower hs-CRP concentrations in the NWO group than in the control group (also statistically insignificant).

hs-CRP, synthesized by hepatocytes in response to IL-6 and other proinflammatory cytokines, is the best-known marker of chronic low-grade inflammation [34, 35]. It also directly affects endothelial function – studies have shown that CRP induces endothelial dysfunction by reducing eNOS activity in endothelial cells and inhibiting endothelium-dependent vasodilation [36]. Furthermore, elevated CRP levels are associated with increased cardiovascular risk in adults, as well as with an increased risk of diabetes and insulin resistance in adults, children, and adolescents [37]. In our study, hs-CRP levels were significantly higher in the NWO group, although both groups remained within the low-risk clinical range (<1.0 mg/l). This indicates that the NWO phenotype coexists with a subtle but detectable state of low-grade inflammation, potentially reflecting an early metabolic shift even in early adulthood.

The second component of PC2 is Ang-1, which in our cohort had higher concentrations in the NWO group (515.3 vs. 491.63 pg/ml; p = 0.008) compared to the control group. Cullberg et al. demonstrated that Ang-1 concentration decreases with weight loss [38]. We theorize that the elevated Ang-1 levels in women with NWO may result from an early compensatory mechanism aimed at counteracting vascular destabilization in the face of incipient inflammation. The protective mechanism of Ang-1 involves activation of the Tie2 receptor and the PI3K/Akt pathway, which inhibits the transcription of proinflammatory genes by inhibiting the transcription factor FKHR (FOXO1) [39]. Studies have shown that Ang-1 downregulates the expression of adhesion molecules (ICAM-1, VCAM-1) and inhibits leukocyte adhesion to the endothelium, confirming its anti-inflammatory effect [40]. Ang-1 has a powerful vascular protective effect: it inhibits plasma leakage, stops vascular inflammation and prevents endothelial cell death [41].

The antagonistic pattern of Ang-1 and hs-CRP in PC2 could potentially reflect a dynamic balance between repair and inflammation in the NWO phenotype. Our findings suggest that high Ang-1 levels, coupled with moderate hs-CRP elevations, might indicate an early compensatory phase. In this stage, vascular protective mechanisms appear to remain active while proinflammatory processes are simultaneously initiated. This delicate balance may represent a theoretical tipping point in the progression toward overt endothelial dysfunction and future cardiovascular disease.

Regulation of vascular tone

The regulation of vascular tone is based on a dynamic balance between vasodilatory signals – mainly transmitted by NO – and vasoconstrictor factors [42]. In our PCA, the third component (PC3) explained 17.95% of the total variance and was defined almost exclusively by the strongly positive loading for NO (0.83), identifying it as the vasodilatory axis.

In our cohort, women with NWO had statistically insignificantly lower plasma NO concentrations compared to the control group (6.44 vs. 7.72 µM; p = 0.479), which, despite the lack of statistical significance, is consistent with the extensive literature describing reduced NO bioavailability in individuals with obesity [43–46]. Furthermore, microcirculation studies confirm that obesity is associated with a reduction in NO due to increased oxidative stress [47]. The mechanisms underlying reduced NO bioavailability in obesity are multifactorial and include: (1) impaired L-arginine transport, (2) eNOS uncoupling, (3) increased arginase activity, and (4) tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) deficiency [48–50].

Clinical significance

The results of our cross-sectional study may have important clinical implications for the early identification and prevention of cardiovascular disease in young women with apparently normal body weight; however, their full value needs to be confirmed in longitudinal studies. Most importantly, PCA suggested that the PC1-PC3 components better discriminated women with NWO from controls (combined variance of 71.68%) than single markers, suggesting their potential as multivariate risk indicators. Our results suggest the potential of using PC1 (the coagulation axis) as an exploratory early marker of thrombotic risk and PC2 (the inflammation-angiogenesis axis) to identify women potentially in the critical phase of the inflammation-repair balance. The identification of specific axes of endothelial dysfunction provides a theoretical framework for future research into targeted preventive strategies. For instance, the coagulation axis (PC1) could be investigated as a potential target for early antithrombotic monitoring, while the inflammation-angiogenic axis (PC2) may help identify individuals who might benefit from intensified lifestyle-based anti-inflammatory approaches. Furthermore, the PC3 axis underscores the importance of maintaining NO bioavailability. However, the efficacy of specific pharmacological or supplemental interventions in the NWO phenotype requires validation through rigorous longitudinal and interventional clinical trials.

Limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, participants were recruited from a well-characterized ethnic group aged 18–24 years. The narrow age range is advantageous because it eliminates variability related to age and other related factors that could confound the results of the analysis. Second, we used a broad panel of biomarkers (tPA, vWF, hs-CRP, Ang-1, NO), providing comprehensive insight into the mechanisms of hemostasis, inflammation, and regulation of vascular tone. Third, principal component analysis identified three biologically consistent axes of endothelial dysfunction, which together explain over 70% of the variability in the data. Furthermore, supplementing intergroup comparisons with correlation analyses and Fisher’s exact test allowed us to capture subtle differences in the patterns of associations between biomarkers and hematological parameters. These strengths provide a solid foundation for further discussion and interpretation of our results.

The authors are also aware of the weaknesses of the study. The major limitation is the cross-sectional nature of the study, which prevents establishing causal relationships between adipose tissue distribution and endothelial dysfunction. This limitation necessitates the conduct of rigorous cohort studies to confirm our current findings and determine the direction of potential causal relationships. The lack of direct PAI-1 measurement means that hypofibrinolysis cannot be confirmed directly, although tPA antigen is a well-established surrogate marker.

In the case of PCA, although a KMO index of 0.52 is acceptable for exploratory principal components analysis, it indicates moderate adequacy of variable selection, which may limit the stability and repeatability of the extracted components. The low KMO value may be due to relatively weak correlations between the markers themselves in the entire population (as seen in the correlation matrix) and suggests that the extracted “axes” should be treated as a preliminary/exploratory model, requiring confirmation in a larger group. Another limitation is the limitation of biomarker measurement to a single time point, which does not capture their diurnal variability or the influence of menstrual cycle phase in young women. Cross-sectional studies are inherently susceptible to the influence of confounding variables, which could potentially introduce bias into our results.

We also acknowledge that our assessment was limited to specific biomarkers for each pathway: vWF for coagulation, tPA for fibrinolysis, hs-CRP for inflammation, and NO for vascular tone. Given that these processes are governed by multiple complex and interrelated pathways, our results should be interpreted as an exploratory insight into these physiological axes rather than a comprehensive evaluation of the entire systems.

Furthermore, although we excluded professional athletes and individuals participating in competitive sports to maintain group homogeneity, we did not employ a standardized tool (such as the International Physical Activity Questionnaire, IPAQ) to precisely quantify daily physical activity levels or sedentary behavior. This may limit our ability to fully account for the influence of lifestyle-related physical activity on the observed endothelial markers.

Although patients with overt chronic diseases were excluded, the influence of subclinical inflammation on the levels of the assessed endothelial markers cannot be completely eliminated.

Finally, while participants with diagnosed hormonal disorders and those using exogenous hormone therapy were excluded, the specific phase of the menstrual cycle was not standardized during blood collection. This is a limitation because it has been shown that fluctuations in endogenous estrogens and progesterone during the menstrual cycle may have a vasoprotective effect on the endothelium [51].

Future research directions

Future studies should focus on: (1) prospective cohort analyses that will allow to assess the prognostic value of the distinguished endothelial dysfunction axes (PC1–PC3) in predicting the development of hypertension, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular events, (2) incorporating genomic, transcriptomic and metabolomic data that can enrich the PCA model and reveal new pathophysiological mechanisms, (3) extending the studies to diverse populations – different ethnic groups, ages and males – will be crucial to confirm the universality and general usefulness of the distinguished endothelial dysfunction axes.

Conclusion

This study is the first to use principal component analysis to comprehensively assess endothelial dysfunction in young women with a normal weight obesity phenotype. The three biological axes identified—coagulation and fibrinolysis (PC1), inflammation-angiogenesis balance (PC2), and vasodilation (PC3)—together explain more than 70% of the biomarker variance and better discriminate women with NWO from controls than single markers.

Key findings indicate that in the NWO phenotype, prothrombotic mechanisms are simultaneously activated (increased tPA and vWF), the balance between inflammatory and repair processes is disturbed (antagonism of hs-CRP and Ang-1), and the regulation of vascular tone shows a tendency towards impairment (reduced NO bioavailability).

These results have significant clinical implications, suggesting the need to transition from BMI-based assessment alone to comprehensive body composition analysis using biomarkers of endothelial dysfunction. PC1-PC3 components may serve as novel tools for early cardiovascular risk stratification and targeted preventive interventions in young women with apparently normal body weight.

Future research should focus on prospective validation of these biomarkers, assessment of their prognostic value, and development of personalized therapeutic strategies. Integrating multivariate biomarker analysis with omics technologies and artificial intelligence may open new avenues for precision preventive medicine in women with hidden metabolic obesity.
