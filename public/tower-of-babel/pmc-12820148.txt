Paramicrosphaeropsis eriobotryae as an emerging canker pathogen in pomegranate trees and the susceptibility of various cultivars
Authors: Pouria Sekandarpour, Hamed Negahban, Maryam Salami, Reza Mostowfizadeh-Ghalamfarsa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The pomegranate (Punica granatum L.) is highly valued for its significant health benefits, adaptability, cold resistance, and ability to thrive in low-fertility soils. Iran, a major producer, contributes nearly one-fifth of global pomegranate production, with Fars Province being the leading region. Recently, there has been a notable increase in pomegranate diseases, including decline, dieback, and canker. This investigation aimed to identify the fungal pathogens responsible for these diseases in Fars Province. Morphological and molecular analyses identified several fungal isolates belonging to Paramicrosphaeropsis eriobotryae, an emerging canker-associated ascomycete pathogen. The most aggressive isolate was identified through inoculation on detached branches of the ‘Rabab-e-Neyriz’ cultivar and subsequently used in pathogenicity assessments on five commercially important cultivars. The ‘Malas-e-Saveh’ and ‘Wonderful’ cultivars exhibited higher susceptibility, while ‘Malas-e-Danesiyah-e-Esfahani’ cultivar demonstrated relative resistance. Cross-pathogenicity assessments with isolates from pomegranate and other natural hosts, including fig, loquat, almond, and olive indicated diverse levels of host susceptibility and isolate aggressiveness. This study represents the first global report of P. eriobotryae causing branch canker and dieback on pomegranate trees.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32295-1.

Introduction

Pomegranate (Punica granatum L.), a member of the Lythraceae family, is cultivated commercially across a broad geographical range, including the Middle East, the Caucasus, North and tropical Africa, the Indian subcontinent, Central and Southeast Asia, the Mediterranean Basin, and extends to the Americas and Australia1. This species is indigenous to Iran and its adjacent regions, where the pomegranate exhibits a rich assortment of genetic resources and genotypes2, with a production of 1,100,000 tons in 2022, accounting for 18% of the world’s total production3. The pomegranate tree, with its notable resilience, is well-adapted to arid and semiarid environments, flourishing in conditions characterized by minimal moisture and soils of low fertility, evidence of its remarkable drought tolerance2,4. Despite this hardiness, the tree’s productivity and longevity are compromised by several fungal diseases, including black heart rot, fruit rot, dieback, and canker, which are prevalent across various pomegranate-producing regions1.

Recent investigations have identified diverse fungal taxa associated with canker and necrotic wood tissues of the pomegranate, implicating them in the manifestation of these diseases5. Notably, fungal species from the Botryosphaeriaceae4,6–8, Cytosporaceae9,10, Diatrypaceae11,12, and Togniniaceae5,13 families have been recurrently associated with symptomatic pomegranate trunks and branches. Previous investigations conducted in Iran have elucidated the association of a spectrum of fungal pathogens with the canker and dieback diseases in pomegranate trees including Botryosphaeria dothidea (Moug.) Ces. & De Not.14, Neofusicoccum parvum (Pennycook & Samuels) Crous, Slippers & A.J.L. Phillips2, Nattrassia mangiferae (Syd. & P. Syd.) B. Sutton & Dyko (now, Neoscytalidium dimidiatum (Penz.) Crous & Slippers)5,15, and different species of Phaeoacremonium5,16. Moreover, the latest research by Kadkhoda-Hematabadi et al. [5] has brought to light the involvement of Diatrype whitmanensis J.D. Rogers & Glawe, Kalmusia variispora (Verkley, Göker & Stielow) Ariyaw. & K.D. Hyde, Neomicrosphaeropsis italica Thambug., Camporesi & K.D. Hyde, and Stilbocrea walteri Voglmayr & Jaklitsch, further expanding the list of fungi associated with necrotic wood tissues of pomegranate trees in Iran. However, pathogenicity assessments have been performed exclusively on some species, utilizing detached shoots from pomegranate trees5.

In a recent survey conducted to identify fungal pathogens responsible for canker and dieback diseases in pomegranate trees in central regions of Fars Province, Iran, isolates of a Paramicrosphaeropsis species were recovered from symptomatic tissues of mature branches. Molecular identification revealed that the recovered isolates belong to the newly described species, Paramicrosphaeropsis eriobotryae Tavakolian, Mostowf. & Crous (Didymellaceae). This species was originally identified as a canker-causing agent in loquat trees and has been associated with infected almond trees17. Shortly after its initial report, this fungal species was also found to be associated with canker and dieback diseases in edible fig (H. Negahban & R. Mostowfizadeh-Ghalamfarsa, unpublished data) and olive (F. Moradi & R. Mostowfizadeh-Ghalamfarsa, unpublished data) trees in Fars Province. Furthermore, recent investigations into the potential host range of P. eriobotryae have demonstrated that this pathogen, in addition to its natural hosts, including almond, fig, loquat, and olive, has a broader potential host range, including pomegranate (H. Negahban & R. Mostowfizadeh-Ghalamfarsa, unpublished data), and was soon found in the pomegranate orchards. The primary objectives of this research were: (i) to identify the recovered isolates from infected pomegranate trees through morpho-molecular investigations, (ii) to evaluate the aggressiveness and pathogenicity of the isolates on detached shoots and one-year-old pomegranate saplings, respectively (iii) to evaluate the susceptibility of different pomegranate cultivars to this newly emergent pathogen, using the most aggressive isolate, and (iv) to assess the cross-pathogenicity and aggressiveness of the P. eriobotryae isolates recovered from the identified natural hosts.

Results

Field surveys, disease symptoms, and fungal isolates

Extensive field assessments conducted within the pomegranate orchards in Fars Province have disclosed a pervasive manifestation of disease symptoms, including canker, dieback, internal wood discoloration, and a general decline in trees of different ages. These symptoms were notably more pronounced in orchards suffering from suboptimal maintenance practices and in trees of advanced age compared to their well-maintained, and young counterparts. Initial disease stages were characterized by the emergence of diminutive cankers on both young and mature branches, as well as on the trunks, followed by dieback. Progressive stages of the infection led to foliar yellowing, culminating in defoliation and, ultimately, an overall decline of infected trees. Morphological identification and phylogenetic analysis

The fungal colonies on potato dextrose agar (PDA) exhibited a cotton-like texture, with aerial mycelium initially appearing white. After 10 days, the color transitioned to brick pink, and eventually, the edges turned brown to dark green while the central ring displayed a pinkish-white buff hue. Additionally, the colonies’ reverse side grew pinkish and darkened in the edges over time (Fig. 2). On oatmeal agar (OA), the surface colony was initially white and after seven days, it began turning olivaceous, eventually becoming dark olivaceous to brown at the edges. Concurrently, the reverse side transitioned from white to pinkish, darkening to olivaceous at the edges over time (Fig. 2). Chlamydospores were observed in chains, globose to ellipsoid, smooth, and thick-walled, measuring 7.65–16.29 µm in length and 6.81–15.37 µm in width, with an average size of 10.31 ± 0.25 × 8.24 ± 0.2 µm. Pycnidia appeared after 16 days on pine needle agar (PNA), being globose to subglobose, mostly solitary, sometimes confluent, pale brown, and semi-immersed, measuring 321.52–388.12 µm in length and 211.23–296.21 µm in width, with an average size of 342.28 ± 0.8 × 235.31 ± 0.2 µm. Conidia were solitary, variable in shape, ranging from globose to subcylindrical, straight to slightly curved, and greenish-brown, measuring 7.74–10.82 µm in length and 3.64–5.77 µm in width, with an average size of 9.41 ± 0.3 × 4.33 ± 0.2 µm (Fig. 2). The analysis of the tub2 and rpb2 loci from three distinct isolates, each representing a different County within Fars Province, revealed a close genetic relationship with three Paramicrosphaeropsis eriobotryae isolates, specifically CBS 149,854 (GenBank accession no. OP871278 and OP856724), CBS 149,853 (GenBank accession no. OP856722 and OP856725), and CBS 149,856 (GenBank accession no. OP871281 and OP893804). Our isolates shared 99.51% to 100% nucleotide sequence identity with these isolates (Supplementary Table S1). Pathogenicity assessments

Examination of pathogenicity in 23 P. eriobotryae isolates revealed their ability to cause brown spots around the inoculation points and wood discoloration in detached shoots within 8–10 days. Further analysis of aggressiveness showed variation in lesion sizes. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed significant differences among isolates (P < 0.0001) across three pathogenicity indicators (Supplementary Table S2). Tukey’s HSD test indicated that isolate S21-4 had the most extensive upward lesion length (ULL; 71.98 ± 0.51 mm), significantly larger than that of all other isolates, while isolate A8-31 had the smallest ULL (34.86 ± 0.31 mm). Isolate S21-4 also exhibited the largest downward lesion length (DLL; 63.78 ± 1.31 mm), while isolate S8-31 had the smallest (32.13 ± 0.53 mm). Additionally, isolates S21-4 (8.46 ± 0.15 mm) and S31-3 (7.79 ± 0.11 mm) showed the greatest lesion width (LW), compared to the smallest LW observed in isolate A7-22 (4.56 ± 0.23 mm) (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Pathogenicity traits assessed on pomegranate detached shoots inoculated with 23 isolates of Paramicrosphaeropsis eriobotryae, 10 days post inoculation. Discussion

The findings of this study represent the first global validation of the association between P. eriobotryae, an emerging pathogen responsible for canker in fruit trees, and the infection identified in pomegranate trees in southern Iran. Isolates of P. eriobotryae were successfully recovered from mature and one-year-old branches displaying a range of symptoms, including canker, dieback, trunk and branch flaking, as well as internal wood discoloration. The morpho-molecular assessment demonstrated significant similarity between the isolates derived from pomegranate trees and the type strain isolates previously identified in the loquat and almond, as documented by Tavakolian et al. [17].

In our preliminary comprehensive evaluation of pathogenicity on detached pomegranate shoots, we observed that all 23 recovered P. eriobotryae isolates were capable of eliciting brown lesions and wood discoloration in varying degrees of severity. This severity of the symptoms correlated with the aggressiveness of each isolate, as determined by three distinct pathogenicity traits. The employment of these traits to assess aggressiveness on artificially infected detached shoots has been validated as a swift and dependable technique, supported by prior research18–21. Principal Components Analysis (PCA) of the aggressiveness data from these tests divided the isolates into two distinct groups based on aggressiveness. Isolates S31-3, S21-4, and A11-22, from different locations within Fars Province, exhibited a higher degree of aggressiveness compared to the other 20 isolates. Moreover, among the three pathogenicity traits, ULL and DLL were identified as the most critical traits in the evaluation of isolate aggressiveness, and were the primary contributors to the variance explained by PC1 (96.1%). Additionally, pathogenicity trials conducted on one-year-old pomegranate saplings under greenhouse conditions reinforced the pathogenicity results from the detached shoot tests. The saplings exhibited symptoms of wood necrosis around the inoculation site, along with internal wood discoloration. Eventually, these symptoms progressed to yellowing and defoliation in the commercially significant ‘Rabab-e-Neyriz’ cultivar saplings. In this study, mycelial plugs were used as the inoculum source to enable a more comprehensive assessment of pathogenicity. Unlike spore suspensions, which restricted the quantification of external lesion dimensions, mycelial plugs facilitated the evaluation of a broader spectrum of pathogenic traits, thereby enhancing the accuracy of cultivar susceptibility and pathogen aggressiveness analyses. This method is widely recognized and has been validated in fungal pathogenicity research, as demonstrated in prior studies18,19,21. Moreover, empirical evidence from Antony et al.22 supports the use of mycelial plugs, showing their superior infection efficiency compared to conidial inoculations.

In this investigation, one-year-old pomegranate sapling cultivars were grouped according to eight distinct pathogenicity traits, revealing varying degrees of susceptibility. The findings indicated that the five cultivars under study were categorized into three susceptibility levels. Specifically, ‘Malas-e-Saveh’ and ‘Wonderful’ were classified as susceptible, while ‘Malas-e-Danesiyah-e-Esfahani’ exhibited the lowest susceptibility to P. eriobotryae based on the assessment of the eight pathogenicity traits. In this experiment, we selected pomegranate cultivars based on their commercial significance. The ‘Malas-e-Saveh’ and ‘Rabab-e-Neyriz’ cultivars are highly valued in Iran for their flavor, fruit size, and yield23. Additionally, the ‘Wonderful’ cultivar is the most extensively cultivated variety worldwide, particularly in the United States, due to its high yield and consumer preference24. Using PCA, a statistical method designed to discern patterns within datasets containing different variables, we aimed to explore the relationship between pathogenicity traits and the cultivars’ susceptibility to P. eriobotryae20,21,25,26. Among the eight pathogenicity traits, DILL and LW were identified as the most critical traits in the cultivar susceptibility assessments and were the key contributors to the variance explained by PC1 (62.6%). The duration of symptom manifestation in a two-month timeframe (60 days—latent phase period) was used to establish a positive correlation between all evaluated pathogenicity traits and the susceptibility degrees for PCA.

In this study, cross-pathogenicity assessments showed that five distinct P. eriobotryae isolates from each natural host species were able to elicit symptoms on the detached shoots of other natural hosts. These isolates obtained from recent investigations into the etiology of canker and dieback diseases in southern Iran’s fruit tree orchards such as loquat and almond trees17, as well as commercially important species of fig (H. Negahban & R. Mostowfizadeh-Ghalamfarsa, unpublished data) and olive (F. Moradi & R. Mostowfizadeh-Ghalamfarsa, unpublished data). A comparative assessment of the induced symptoms, guided by pathogenicity traits of lesion length, lesion width, and lesion depth, indicated a spectrum of host susceptibilities. Almond, loquat, and pomegranate shoots exhibited a higher susceptibility relative to those of fig and olive. While pathogenicity assessments of fungal isolates on detached shoots offer a reliable, cost-effective, and rapid method, as indicated by several studies18–21,27, complementary cross-pathogenicity testing of P. eriobotryae isolates on saplings of natural hosts is recommended to deepen our understanding of host susceptibility and potential infection dynamics under more natural conditions. Furthermore, an analysis comparing the aggressiveness of isolates from various hosts identified the almond (KhR155-4), fig (GhFsa11-8), and pomegranate (S21-4) isolates as the most aggressive, surpassing those from olive (ShE02-2) and loquat (KhR158-4). The findings suggest that the pathogen in question does not exhibit host preference. Evidence shows that an isolate derived from one host species is capable of inducing significant pathogenic effects on a variety of other hosts, aligning with the results of cross-pathogenicity evaluations of our prior studies on other emerging fruit canker-causing ascomycetes in Fars Province, such as Stilbocrea banihashemiana Bolboli, Tavakolian & Mostowf.27. This lack of specificity in host preference underscores the potential for widespread impact across diverse plant populations, necessitating further investigation into its transmission dynamics and host–pathogen interactions to develop effective management strategies.

Recent research indicates that P. eriobotryae has been isolated from symptomless wood tissue in loquat trees17. These findings imply the presence of latent infections, supporting the hypothesis that this pathogen might reside within asymptomatic wood tissues of its natural hosts, such as pomegranate trees, without producing visible symptoms. It is hypothesized that abiotic stressors, including drought conditions, could activate these dormant infections in various tree species. The concept of latent infection stages in canker-causing agents has been well-documented across multiple fruit tree species28–31. Notably, latent infections originating from nurseries have been associated with significant losses in newly established orchards [32]. Observations indicate that Middle Eastern countries, particularly Iran, are increasingly vulnerable to arid climates and extreme weather patterns such as prolonged droughts and heatwaves33,34. Given the nonspecific host preference exhibited by P. eriobotryae and its potential for latent infection coupled with ongoing climate change challenges, this pathogen poses a considerable threat to agricultural systems in the region moving forward.

To the best of our knowledge, the present study represents the first documentation of a strong association between P. eriobotryae and the canker and dieback in pomegranate trees, both in Iran and globally. The presence of P. eriobotryae poses a significant threat to economically important pomegranate cultivation areas globally, particularly affecting the commercially significant susceptible cultivars ‘Malas-e-Saveh’ in Iran and ‘Wonderful’ worldwide. In addition, research into cultivar susceptibility has identified the ‘Malas-e-Danesiyah-e-Esfahani’ cultivar as exhibiting low susceptibility to the canker caused by P. eriobotryae, suggesting its potential role as a progenitor or rootstock in future breeding or horticultural endeavors35,36. Given the possibility of latent infections, it is critical to closely monitor this disease and establish molecular diagnostic tools for its early and accurate detection in both symptomatic and asymptomatic trees. These measures are vital due to the significant phytosanitary implications for the global trade of live plants. With the ongoing shifts in climate patterns, there is an urgent need for in-depth research to fully understand the prevalence of this disease across different agricultural environments and to devise robust management tactics.

Material and methods

Sampling and fungal isolation

Pomegranate orchards in the central regions of Fars Province, Iran were surveyed over a two-year period (2023–2024). Samples were obtained from trunks, mature branches, and juvenile shoots showing symptoms of cankers, dieback, and internal wood discoloration across various pomegranate cultivars, with a focus on the commercially important ‘Rabab-e-Neyriz’ cultivar. The symptomatic wood tissues were bisected transversely to expose the pith and inspected for internal discoloration. Sections were taken from the edge of the necrotic tissue, resulting in 25 mm2 fragments. These wood pieces were subsequently washed extensively with flowing tap water. To surface disinfect, the samples were immersed in solutions of 70% ethanol and 0.5% sodium hypochlorite solution for one minute each, then rinsed twice with sterile distilled water to eliminate any remaining disinfectant. After being surface disinfected, the samples were left to dry on a sterile paper towel for 15 min in a laminar flow hood. The prepared wood samples were transferred onto the potato dextrose agar (PDA; extract of 300 g/L boiled potato, 20 g/L dextrose, 15 g/L agar, and distilled water) supplemented with 1 mg/L of tetracycline to prevent bacterial contamination. The Petri dishes were incubated at 25 °C for a duration of seven days. After the fungal isolates had formed, single hyphal tips were transferred onto water agar (WA; 20 g/L agar, and distilled water) to obtain pure cultures. Following three days of incubation at 25 °C, single hyphal tips were meticulously chosen and transferred onto the PDA to acquire pure cultures20. The pure cultures were maintained in the culture collection of the Department of Plant Protection at Shiraz University for further investigations.

Morphological investigations

Pure isolates obtained from the necrotic pomegranate wood tissues were cultured on PDA and oatmeal agar (OA; extract of 300 g/L boiled oatmeal, 15 g/L agar, and distilled water) plates and incubated at 25 °C under a 12-h light/dark cycle for 21 days. The morphological characteristics of the colonies, including the texture of the mycelial surface and pigmentation on both the obverse and reverse sides, were documented using the color chart of Rayner37. To induce sporulation, a 0.5 mm diameter mycelial plug of a two-week-old fungal culture was positioned on pine needle agar (PNA; 2% WA with sterile pine needle fragments)38 and incubated at 25 °C under fluorescent white light (12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod)39. Isolates were assessed weekly for the development of pycnidia. Microscopic structures, including the pycnidia, conidia, and chlamydospores, were evaluated for their shapes and average dimensions (length and width) using a light microscope in select isolates (n = 30)17. Slide preparations were mounted in distilled water and lactic acid to investigate the micromorphological structures.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and phylogenetic analysis

To extract the genomic fungal DNA, the isolates were cultured in potato broth (PB; extract of 300 g/L boiled potato) for 14 days40. The DNG-PLUS extraction kit (CinnaGen, Tehran, Iran) was used to extract total DNA from freeze-dried mycelium of select isolates. The extraction procedure followed the directions provided by the manufacturer. The fungal genomic DNA was assessed for both its quality and quantity using the MD-1000 Nanodrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA). The primer pairs of Btub2Fd/Btub4Rd and fRPB2-5F/fRPB2-7cR were utilized to amplify a partial sequence of the β-tubulin gene (tub2) and RNA polymerase II second-largest subunit (rpb2) in the representative isolates41,42. The thermal cycling program for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the tub2 and rpb2 genes followed Tavakolian et al.17. The PCR mixtures were prepared in quantities of 25 µL, containing 1 µL of total fungal DNA (~ 100 ng), 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 pM), 12.5 µL of Taq DNA polymerase 2 × Master Mix RED (Amplicon, Odense, Denmark), and 9.5 µL of PCR-quality water. PCR amplifications were carried out using a Peltier Thermal Cycler (Techne, Germany). The PCR products were subjected to sequencing using the identical primer pairs employed for amplification through a dye terminator cycle at the Cardiogenetic Research Center in Tehran, Iran. The resulting sequences were edited by BioEdit v.7.0.9.1[43]. The BLASTn tool (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) was used to identify sequences with the highest similarity to the query sequences. Gene multiple alignment was conducted using MAFFT version 744. Furthermore, phylogenetic analyses were conducted using Bayesian inference and Maximum Likelihood analysis for individual and concatenated tub2 and rpb2 sequences within the TrEase platform following the directions provided by Salmaninezhad et al. 45. Displaying and final editing of the phylogenetic trees were conducted using MEGA 11 software46.

Pathogenicity and aggressiveness of isolates on detached shoots

To assess the pathogenic potential and the severity of symptoms elicited by Paramicrosphaeropsis sp. isolates from diseased pomegranate trees, an inoculation test was performed on detached shoots using mycelial plugs. For the experiment, symptomless and healthy one-year-old shoots of the commercially significant ‘Rabab-e-Neyriz’ pomegranate cultivar were utilized. This method allowed for a controlled evaluation of the isolate’s aggressiveness in inducing disease symptoms on woody plant tissue. Detached shoots were collected and cut into segments measuring 25–35 cm in length with an approximate diameter of 10 mm. The inoculation process commenced with a superficial disinfection using 98% ethanol applied via the flaming method20. A 5 × 5 × 2 mm colonized PDA plug was excised from the periphery of a two-week-old culture and inserted into an incision (5 × 5 mm) made by a sterile scalpel. To prevent contamination, the inoculation site was sealed with Parafilm (Bemis Packaging, USA). Sterile PDA discs served as negative controls during the inoculation of the shoots47. The upper end of the inoculated shoots was coated with molten paraffin, while the opposite end was placed into an Erlenmeyer flask filled with 300 mL of sterile distilled water. Four detached shoots, each with one inoculation site, were used for the inoculation of each Paramicrosphaeropsis sp. isolate. The inoculated pomegranate shoots were kept in a germinator using a Completely Randomized Design (CRD). The germinator was maintained at 25 ± 2 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. After ten days, the bark was removed from the inoculated shoots. Upward lesion length (ULL), downward lesion length (DLL), and lesion width (LW), were measured utilizing a digital caliper. To satisfy Koch’s postulates, wood tissue fragments from the lesion edges were cultured on PDA augmented with 1 mg/L tetracycline. The fungal isolates were subsequently identified by their unique cultural morphologies17.

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test48 in IBM SPSS Statistics 25. To address the skewed distribution of ULP, DLP, and LW, a square root transformation was applied. One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) using a General Linear Model (GLM) in SAS (v.9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), analyzed the effect of isolates on these three pathogenicity traits. Distinctive differences among the means were determined using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test (P ≤ 0.05). Furthermore, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted to elucidate the data relationships and to comprehend the variance in aggressiveness across the 23 isolates based on the three pathogenicity traits, using R software v. 3.4.0 (http://www.r-project.org).

Pathogenicity test on one-year-old pomegranate saplings

The pathogenic potential of the most aggressive isolate (identified previously on detached shoots) was further investigated on one-year-old ‘Rabab-e-Neyriz’ saplings. A 5 × 5 × 2 mm mycelial plug from the actively growing margin of a nine-day-old fungal culture was implanted into a 5 × 5 mm incision made in the sapling stems with a sterile scalpel after bark removal. The inoculation site was then wrapped with Parafilm26. Non-colonized mycelial plugs were used as negative controls. Three saplings, each with two inoculation points, were used as biological replicates. Saplings were monitored every other day for wood discoloration and necrosis. Koch’s postulate was fulfilled by reisolating and identifying the inoculated isolate from infected wood tissues on tetracycline-supplemented PDA.

Assessing the susceptibility of different pomegranate cultivars to the isolates

The susceptibility of pomegranate cultivars (‘Malas-e-Danesiyah-e-Esfahani’, ‘Rabab-e-Neyriz’, ‘Malas-e-Saveh’, ‘Shirin-e-Shahvar’, and ‘Wonderful’)49 to the pathogen was assessed using one-year-old saplings. Saplings were inoculated as described in the previous section (Material and Methods; Pathogenicity test on one-year-old pomegranate saplings), with non-colonized mycelial plugs serving as negative controls. This experiment was structured as a completely randomized design with three biological replicates for each pomegranate cultivar, with two inoculation sites per replicate. Two months post-inoculation, eight pathogenicity traits were measured: bark lesion length (LL) and width (LW); upward (UILL) and downward (DILL) internal lesion length; internal lesion width (ILW) after bark removal; lesion depth (LD); and vascular progression (VP) following a longitudinal section. Additionally, the incubation period (IP), defined as the number of days until symptom appearance post-inoculation was recorded for each pomegranate cultivar20.

The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test48. To address data skewness, a square root transformation was performed on the pathogenicity traits, which included LW, UILL, DILL, VP, and IP. To analyze the impact of different pomegranate cultivars on pathogenicity traits, a one-way ANOVA was conducted, followed by Tukey’s HSD test for post-hoc analysis. Statistical analysis was performed with SAS software version 9.4, with a significance level set at P ≤ 0.05. PCA was carried out using R software v. 3.4.0 to further explore susceptibility among inoculated cultivar saplings, based on eight pathogenicity traits. To ensure a positive correlation between all pathogenicity traits and the overall classification of cultivars in the PCA, the number of days with symptoms within the 60-day incubation period was used instead of the incubation period itself.

Evaluating the susceptibility of natural hosts to different isolates of P. eriobotryae

The pathogenic capabilities of P. eriobotryae isolates, sourced from diverse natural arboreal hosts, were evaluated through cross-pathogenicity tests on one-year-old detached shoots of almond, fig, loquat, olive, and pomegranate trees. Five unique isolates were selected, each representing a different host: KhR158-4 (CBS 149,856) from almond, KhR155-4 (CBS 148,866) from loquat [17]; GhFsa11-8 (identified via tub2) from fig (H. Negahban & R. Mostowfizadeh-Ghalamfarsa, unpublished data), ShE02-2 (identified via rpb2) from olive (F. Moradi & R. Mostowfizadeh-Ghalamfarsa, unpublished data); and S21-4 from pomegranate. Following inoculation using the protocol outlined in the previous section (Materials and Methods; Pathogenicity and aggressiveness of isolates on detached shoots), the shoots were monitored for 14 days. The experiment consisted of 25 treatments, each with three replicates. Post-inoculation, pathogenicity was quantified by measuring LL, LW, and LD with a digital caliper. Additionally, to satisfy Koch’s postulates, necrotic tissue fragments from lesion edges were cultured on tetracycline-treated PDA.

Additionally, to assess the susceptibility of natural tree hosts to P. eriobotryae isolates, we examined the normal distribution of three pathogenicity traits using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test48. To correct for skewness in the distribution of the data, a square root transformation was applied to LL, LW, and LD. The factorial experiment was arranged in a CRD with two factors: fungal isolates and host species. A two-way ANOVA was then carried out on the transformed data to determine the main effects and interactions between these factors on pathogenicity traits. This analysis was conducted using the GLM procedure in SAS version 9.4, and significant differences between means were identified using Tukey’s HSD test at a significance level of P ≤ 0.05.
