Global burden and trends of oral disorders in adults aged 60 + (1990–2021) with projections to 2040
Authors: Xingzhu Dai, Xiaoyu Li, Manqiong Dai, Jun Li, Wanghong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aims to provide a comprehensive assessment of the global burden of oral disorders in individuals aged 60 + , from 1990 to 2021, with projections through 2040.MethodsData on the annual prevalence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for oral disorders were sourced from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. Temporal trends in age-standardized prevalence and DALYs rates, by age, sex, and Socio-Demographic Index (SDI), were analyzed using estimated annual percentage changes. Projections to 2040 were made using the Bayesian age-period-cohort model.ResultsIn 2021, there were 675.8 million cases and 9.4 million DALYs due to oral disorders in older adults. While age-standardized prevalence and DALYs rates declined globally from 1990 to 2021, significant regional disparities remained. High burdens were observed in Andean Latin America and East Asia, while increasing trends were seen in Tropical Latin America and Eastern Europe. Dental caries and periodontitis were the most common disorders, with edentulism contributing substantially to DALYs. Middle-SDI countries faced the highest burdens. Projections suggest declines in dental caries and periodontitis but an increase in edentulism.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the need for targeted public health policies focusing on prevention, treatment, and equitable access to oral care for aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07021-y.

Introduction

Oral diseases rank among the most prevalent health conditions globally, causing pain, functional limitations, and a significant decline in quality of life [1]. Beyond their health impact, these conditions pose substantial economic challenges, disproportionately affecting vulnerable groups, particularly older adults [2, 3]. According to the World Health Organization, the global population aged 60 and above is growing at an unprecedented rate, with the proportion expected to nearly double from 12% in 2015 to 22% by 2050 [4]. This rapid demographic shift highlights the critical need to prioritize oral health care for aging populations.

With the aging global population, the prevalence and impact of oral diseases are steadily increasing, placing the oral health of older adults at the forefront of public health priorities [5]. It has been reported that 28.02% of the increase in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for oral conditions between 1990 and 2010 can be attributed solely to population aging [6]. This growing burden is aggravated by factors such as socio-economic disparities and reduced access to oral care [7]. Moreover, oral diseases are closely linked to a wide range of systemic conditions that become more prevalent with age [8]. In addition to cardiovascular disease and diabetes, oral disorders such as periodontitis and edentulism have been associated with respiratory infections, neurodegenerative disorders including dementia and Alzheimer’s disease, chronic kidney disease, and certain cancers [9]. Poor oral health also contributes to malnutrition and frailty through impaired chewing ability, chronic pain, and systemic inflammation [10]. These complex interconnections highlight the importance of oral health as an integral component of healthy aging and make its management in older adults particularly challenging [11].

Global oral health inequality remains a significant global challenge, with unmet dental care needs on the rise, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [2]. In 2021, the World Health Assembly adopted a resolution emphasizing the importance of incorporating oral health into the global health agenda, particularly within the context of universal health coverage (UHC) [12]. However, oral health remains a critical yet often neglected component of healthy aging, with limited research assessing the burden of oral diseases among older adults [13, 14]. Closing this knowledge gap is essential for guiding the development of targeted public health policies.

The Global Burden of Diseases Study (GBD) offers reliable and internally consistent estimates of the burden of oral disorders, including dental caries in permanent teeth, periodontitis, edentulism, and other oral conditions not classified under the definitions of permanent or deciduous caries, periodontal disease, or severe tooth loss [15]. These estimates are derived by synthesizing global epidemiological data and utilizing specialized modeling techniques. This study aims to fill the existing gap by systematically evaluating the global burden of oral diseases in individuals aged 60 and older, using data from the GBD 2021 [15]. Our analysis examines the composition of various oral diseases and offers international comparisons. By highlighting these critical issues, we seek to inform public health strategies and interventions that can shape more targeted oral health policies for older adults, ultimately improving oral health outcomes, enhancing the quality of life for older populations, and advancing health equity across regions.

Methods

Data extraction

We retrieved the annual prevalence and DALYs estimates for oral disorders among adults aged 60 and above from 1990 to 2021, by sex, age group (in 5-year intervals), and location, from the GBD 2021 via the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) results tool, following the data extraction approach described in our previous study [16]. Specifically: (1) Untreated dental caries: Any tooth with a clear coronal cavity at the dentin level or a root cavity in cementum that feels soft or leathery upon probing. (2) Severe periodontitis: Pocket depth ≥ 6 mm or attachment loss > 6 mm. (3) Edentulism: Complete loss of permanent teeth. (4) Other oral disorders: Other dental, tongue, and jaw disorders and malformations not included in the above definitions [17]. The detailed case definitions and the mapping of International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes to oral disorders are provided in Appendix 1 of the Supplementary Materials. Although oral disorders rarely contribute to mortality, we used DALYs as the primary measure of health burden for consistency with the GBD framework [17]. In this context, DALYs for oral disorders are effectively equivalent to years lived with disability (YLDs), as mortality (YLL) is negligible [18]. Using DALYs ensures comparability with other GBD studies and facilitates integration into global health policy frameworks. The data, covering 204 countries and territories, were categorized into 21 GBD regions based on geographical proximity and into 5 sociodemographic index (SDI) regions [15]. Detailed descriptions of data source, data processing, and modeling methods are presented in the Supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

The age-standardized rates (ASR) per 100,000 population for the prevalence and DALYs of oral disorders among older adults were calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{ASR}=\frac{{\sum }_{i=1}^{A}{a}_{i}{w}_{i}}{{\sum }_{i=1}^{A}{w}_{i}} \times \text{100,000}$$\end{document}

In this equation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${a}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the age-specific rate for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${i}^{th}$$\end{document} age subgroup, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{i}$$\end{document} is the population count for that age subgroup, based on the GBD 2021 standard population [19]. N denotes the total number of age groups. Changes in ASR are assumed to follow a linear trend over time, with the natural logarithm represented by the equation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y=\alpha +\beta x+\varepsilon$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y=ln(ASR)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x$$\end{document} is the calendar year, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document} is the error term. To evaluate the average trends over a specified period, the estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) in ASRs were calculated using the formula \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$100\times\left(\text{exp}\left(\beta\right)-1\right)$$\end{document} [20]. A positive EAPC reflects an average annual increase, whereas a negative EAPC denotes a decrease. Values close to zero suggest relative stability over the study period. When the 95% CI includes 0, the trend is interpreted as stable. If the CI does not cross 0, the trend is classified as increasing (EAPC > 0) or decreasing (EAPC < 0).

The age-standardized prevalence and DALYs rates of oral disorders from 2022 to 2040 as projected using the Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model with nested Laplace approximations [21]. Briefly, the BAPC model is a log-linear Poisson model:

In the model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document} denotes the intercept, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{i}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta }_{j}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\gamma }_{k}$$\end{document} represent the effects of age, period, and cohort, respectively. Additionally, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${z}_{ij}$$\end{document} represents parameters for other unstructured heterogeneity.

In addition, a Locally Weighted Scatterplot Smoothing (LOWESS) model was applied to analyze the correlation between the burden of oral disorders and the SDI across 21 regions and 204 countries and territories [22]. Pearson correlation analysis was then conducted to calculate the ρ indices and p values, assessing the relationship between the burden of oral disorders and the SDI.

All statistical analyses and mapping were performed using R software, version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Overall burden of oral disorders

In 2021, there were 675.8 million prevalent cases of oral disorders and 9.4 million DALYs associated with oral disorders among adults aged 60 and above globally (Table 1). The global age-standardized prevalence rate and age-standardized DALYs rate for oral disorders in 2021 were 61,987 (95% uncertainty interval [UI] 61,982.3 to 61,991.6) and 861.1 (95% UI 860.5 to 861.6) per 100,000 population, respectively (Table 1). From 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) of oral disorders declined at an average annual rate of –0.12% (95% CI –0.14 to –0.1), while the age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR) decreased by an average of –0.32% (95% CI –0.39 to –0.25) per year globally (Table 1). In 1990, the number of prevalent cases and DALYs due to oral disorders increased with higher SDI levels (Table 1). However, by 2021, middle-SDI regions had surpassed high-SDI regions in terms of the largest number of prevalent cases and DALYs, while low-SDI regions continued to report the lowest numbers. Burden of specific types of oral disorders

In 2021, among older adults globally, an estimated 339.2 million cases of dental caries, 285.7 million cases of periodontitis, 247.5 million cases of edentulism, and 2.4 million cases of other oral disorders were reported, resulting in 0.3 million, 1.8 million, 6.6 million, and 0.7 million DALYs, respectively (Table 1). From 1990 to 2019, both edentulism and dental caries experienced larger reductions in ASPR and ASDR compared to other oral disorders, while the rates for periodontitis remained stable during this period (Table 1).

The ASPR and ASDR for specific oral disorders among older adults varied by geographical region in 2021 (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table S2-S5). The highest ASPR of dental caries occurred in Andean Latin America (50,914.3 per 100,000 population), of periodontitis occurred in South Asia (32,825.5), of edentulism occurred in Andean Latin America (50,047.3), and of other oral disorders occurred in Eastern Europe (2,253.8). In terms of the distribution of prevalent oral disorders in 2021, periodontitis represented the largest proportion of cases in High-income Asia Pacific (45.2%), East Asia (39.5%), and High-income North America (35.6%). Edentulism made up the highest proportion of cases in Tropical Latin America (44.3%), Australasia (38.3%), and Eastern Europe (36.2%), while dental caries was the most prevalent in the remaining 14 regions, particularly in Oceania (61.7%). However, edentulism accounted for the highest proportion of DALYs due to oral disorders across all regions (Fig. 2C-D).

In 2021, at the country level, Switzerland reported the highest both ASPR and ASDR for dental caries (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B and Supplementary Table S6). Gambia and Cabo Verde had the highest ASPR and ASDR for periodontitis (Supplementary Fig. 2A-B and Supplementary Table S7), while Bolivia and Peru reported the highest ASPR and ASDR for edentulism (Supplementary Fig. 3A-B and Supplementary Table S8). For other oral disorders, Russia had the highest ASPR and North Korea the highest ASDR (Supplementary Fig. 4A-B and Supplementary Table S9). Between 1990 and 2021, Sweden and Colombia saw the fastest increases in both the ASPR and ASDR for dental caries (Supplementary Fig. 1C-D). Vietnam experienced the greatest rise in both the ASPR and ASDR for periodontitis (Supplementary Fig. 2C-D), while Palau had the largest increase in both the ASPR and ASDR for edentulism (Supplementary Fig. 3C-D).

Correlations of ASPR and ASDR With SDI

At the regional level, an inverted "U"-shaped relationship was observed between the ASPR and ASDR for overall oral disorders and the SDI in 2021 (Supplementary Fig. 4 A and 4 F). Similarly, both ASPR and ASDR for dental caries and edentulism initially increased with rising SDI, then declined around an SDI value of 50 (Supplementary Fig. 4B, 4D, 4G, and 4I). In contrast, the ASPR and ASDR for periodontitis decreased exponentially as SDI increased (Supplementary Fig. 4 C and 4H). Interestingly, a significant positive correlation was found between the EAPC of ASPR and ASDR for periodontitis and SDI in 2021, while a significant negative correlation was detected between the EAPC of ASPR and ASDR for edentulism and SDI in the same year (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Age- and sex-related burden of oral disorders

Although the ASPR for oral disorders did not show a significant gender difference, it was higher in females than in males (62,666.9 vs. 61,192.4 per 100,000), as was the ASDR (907.1 vs. 807.6 per 100,000; Table 1). The number of prevalent cases and DALYs for each specific type of oral disorder decreased with age, and similarly, the ASPR and ASDR for oral disorders generally declined with increasing age, except for edentulism (Supplementary Fig. 6A-B). From 1990 to 2021, the ASPR and ASDR for overall oral disorders, dental caries, edentulism, and other oral disorders declined across all eight age groups (Supplementary Fig. 6C-D). However, the ASPR and ASDR for periodontitis increased during the same period, except in the 60–64-year age group.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive assessment of the global burden, long-term trends, and regional disparities in the prevalence and DALYs of oral disorders among individuals aged 60 and older, along with their correlation with socioeconomic status at both the regional and national levels, using data from the GBD 2021. Our findings indicate that the ASPR of oral disorders has declined globally by an average annual rate of − 0.12% from 1990 to 2021, while the ASDR decreased more notably by − 0.32%. Despite these reductions, the absolute number of prevalent cases and DALYs has increased globally, underscoring the growing burden of oral disorders driven by demographic changes, particularly the aging population [23]. In 2021, approximately 675.8 million prevalent cases and 9.4 million DALYs were attributable to oral disorders in this age group, highlighting the substantial global burden of oral health issues in older adults.

There was notable geographic variation in both the prevalence and DALYs of oral disorders. In terms of age-standardized rates, Andean Latin America had the highest figures for both prevalence and DALYs, while East Asia accounted for the largest absolute number of cases and DALYs. The increasing burden in East Asia can likely be attributed to the region’s aging population, along with lifestyle changes such as altered dietary habits and reduced awareness of oral hygiene [24, 25]. The growing burden of oral disorders in middle-income regions reflects broader global health transitions, where rising life expectancy has outpaced the development of comprehensive healthcare systems to address age-related health issues, including oral diseases [19]. Countries like Bolivia and Peru reported the highest rates of oral disorders, particularly edentulism, highlighting broader health disparities in lower-income regions [26]. In contrast, countries such as Sweden and Colombia experienced the most rapid increases in the prevalence and burden of dental caries, which may point to emerging risk factors or gaps in preventive measures [27, 28]. These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions at both the national and regional levels to address the growing burden of oral disorders in older populations. Our results also reveal a complex relationship between the SDI and the burden of oral disorders. In 1990, high-SDI regions had the highest burden, but by 2021, middle-SDI regions surpassed high-SDI regions in terms of both the total number of prevalent cases and DALYs. This shift may reflect improvements in dental care and hygiene in high-SDI regions, leading to lower prevalence, while middle-SDI regions face increasing challenges due to limited access to oral healthcare services [29]. Low-SDI regions, though reporting the lowest burdens of oral disease, continue to struggle with insufficient healthcare infrastructure to manage these conditions effectively [30].

These findings reflect two complementary dynamics. First, high-SDI regions bore the largest absolute numbers of cases and DALYs due to larger aging populations and longer life expectancy. By 2021, however, middle-SDI regions had surpassed high-SDI regions in absolute burden, reflecting rapid demographic growth and aging in settings where health systems are less equipped to manage oral health demands [31]. Second, when examined on an age-standardized scale, ASPR and ASDR for dental caries and edentulism followed an inverted U-shaped association with SDI, peaking around a value of 50 before declining in high-SDI regions. This indicates that middle-SDI regions currently face the greatest relative burden, likely due to increasing exposure to cariogenic diets and uneven access to preventive and restorative oral healthcare, while high-SDI regions have benefited from established preventive programs, advanced services, and greater health literacy [16, 32, 33]. These findings are consistent with genuine health transition dynamics rather than limitations of data or assumptions.

This study also examined the burden of specific oral disorders, including dental caries, periodontitis, edentulism, and other oral conditions. Among these, dental caries, periodontitis, and edentulism were the most prevalent, contributing significantly to the overall burden. Of these, edentulism was responsible for the highest number of DALYs, reflecting its long-term impact on the quality of life and functional health of older adults. Edentulism remains a major issue, particularly in regions such as Tropical Latin America, Australasia, and Eastern Europe, where tooth loss rates are disproportionately high. Dental caries and periodontitis displayed varying trends over time. While dental caries and edentulism saw substantial declines in age-standardized prevalence and DALYs between 1990 and 2019, periodontitis remained relatively stable, suggesting that persistent risk factors have not been adequately addressed. The prevalence and DALYs associated with periodontitis remained high in regions like South Asia and East Asia, highlighting the ongoing need for targeted prevention and treatment strategies. Notably, the increasing burden of periodontitis in some regions, such as Eastern Europe, and the stable trends in others, like High-income North America, may reflect differences in oral healthcare practices, aging populations, and socioeconomic factors. These variations emphasize the necessity for region-specific interventions to address the underlying risk factors for periodontal disease, such as tobacco use, poor dietary habits, and limited access to oral healthcare services [34].

Our analysis did not reveal significant gender differences in the age-standardized prevalence of oral disorders, although females exhibited a slightly higher burden in both prevalence and DALYs compared to males. This may be attributed to women’s longer life expectancy, which increases the likelihood of living with oral diseases as they age [35]. Notably, the burden of specific oral disorders, such as periodontitis, showed age-related variations. Younger age groups experienced a greater decline in prevalence and DALYs, while older adults faced a significant increase in edentulism. This underscores the importance of maintaining good oral health throughout life to prevent or delay the onset of edentulism and other severe oral conditions.

Maintaining oral health in older adults presents unique challenges. Physiological changes of aging, including reduced salivary flow, weakened immunity, and functional decline, can accelerate the progression of oral diseases [36, 37]. Multimorbidity and polypharmacy may further exacerbate oral conditions, with medications commonly used in older populations contributing to xerostomia, taste alterations, and periodontal vulnerability [38, 39]. Beyond biological factors, barriers such as financial constraints, limited mobility, and reduced access to integrated dental care often hinder timely treatment [40]. These challenges are compounded by social determinants of health, with older adults from disadvantaged backgrounds being disproportionately affected [41]. Addressing these issues requires a shift toward integrated, multidisciplinary approaches that recognize oral health as inseparable from overall health in later life, and that ensure equitable access to preventive and rehabilitative oral healthcare [42].

Looking ahead, the global prevalence of oral disorders among older adults is projected to rise, driven by demographic changes and an aging population. While the prevalence of dental caries and periodontitis is expected to decline slightly, the prevalence of edentulism is anticipated to increase. This projection suggests that, despite advances in dental care, tooth loss will remain a significant challenge in older populations, particularly in regions with limited access to dental services [43]. The growing burden of edentulism in high-SDI countries, such as Switzerland, highlights the need for comprehensive policies aimed at preventing tooth loss and ensuring effective rehabilitation for older adults [44]. These projections emphasize the importance of preparing healthcare systems to meet the increasing demand for oral health services [5]. Policymakers and health practitioners must focus on enhancing prevention strategies for dental caries and periodontitis, while also addressing the growing need for prosthetic and rehabilitative dental care for older adults, particularly those experiencing edentulism [45]. We also advocate for stronger regional collaborations to promote oral health globally, with a focus on expanding access to dental care in underserved areas. Addressing these challenges will be critical in mitigating the future burden of oral disorders and ensuring that older adults experience better oral health as they age.

This study has several limitations. First, while the GBD provides reliable estimates, the quality and accuracy of the data depend on the availability and reliability of primary data sources, which can vary across regions. Second, although we analyzed trends over time, the cross-sectional nature of the data for specific years limits our ability to explore the underlying mechanisms behind the observed trends. Third, the projected future burden of oral disorders, especially edentulism, relies on demographic trends and assumptions that may evolve over time. These projections are subject to uncertainty due to potential shifts in population health behaviors, healthcare interventions, and public health policies, which could impact the future prevalence of oral diseases among older populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study demonstrates that while age-standardized prevalence and DALY rates for oral disorders among older adults have slightly declined since 1990, the absolute number of cases and associated burden has continued to rise, driven largely by population growth and aging. This trend underscores the urgent need for comprehensive, region-specific strategies that integrate oral health into broader aging and health policies. Public health interventions should prioritize preventive care, timely treatment, and health education to address the growing demands of older populations. Improving accessibility and affordability of dental services can enhance oral health outcomes, improve quality of life, and contribute to health equity for aging societies worldwide.
