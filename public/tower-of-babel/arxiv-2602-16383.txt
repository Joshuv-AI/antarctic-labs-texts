Abstract
This paper investigates the design of integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) systems assisted by simultaneously transmitting and reflecting reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (STAR-RISs), which act as multi-functional programmable metasurfaces capable of supporting concurrent communication and sensing within a unified architecture. We propose a two-stage ISAC protocol, in which the preparation phase performs direction estimation for outdoor users located in the reflection space, while maintaining communication with both outdoor and indoor users in the transmission space. The subsequent communication phase exploits the estimated directions to enhance information transfer. The directions of outdoor users are modeled as Gaussian random variables to capture estimation uncertainty, and the corresponding average communication performance is incorporated into the design. Building on this framework, we formulate a performance-balanced optimization problem that maximizes the communication sum-rate while guaranteeing the required sensing accuracy, jointly determining the beamforming vectors at the base station (BS), the STAR-RIS transmission and reflection coefficients, and the metasurface partition between energy-splitting and transmit-only modes. The physical constraints of STAR-RIS elements and the required sensing performance are explicitly enforced. To address the non-convex nature of the problem, we combine fractional programming, Lagrangian dual reformulation, and successive convex approximation. The binary metasurface partition is ultimately recovered via continuous relaxation followed by projection-based binarization. Numerical results demonstrate that the proposed design achieves an effective trade-off between sensing accuracy and communication throughput, by significantly outperforming conventional STAR-RIS-aided ISAC schemes. Such characteristics make the proposed architecture well suited for dense Internet-of-Things deployments in smart environments, where energy-efficient connectivity and device localization must be jointly supported.



I
Introduction

Metasurfaces are artificial electromagnetic (EM) structures composed of subwavelength elements (“meta-atoms”) possibly
integrated with tunable microelectronic components, such as diodes and varactors, which enable programmable
control over the amplitude, phase, and polarization of incident waves
[
1
]
.
When empowered with reconfigurability, these structures give rise to
reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RISs)
,
which have emerged as a key enabler for next-generation wireless networks due to their ability to reshape
the propagation environment in a software-defined manner
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
By dynamically manipulating the reflected or
transmitted wavefronts, RISs allow the establishment of favorable communication links, mitigation of blockages,
and enhancement of coverage in complex propagation scenarios
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. Owing to their low power consumption,
compact form factor, and deployment flexibility, RISs are particularly attractive for integration into
urban infrastructures, vehicles, and buildings, thus supporting the vision of environment-aware
and user-centric wireless networks
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
.

Despite their advantages, conventional RIS architectures typically rely on reflective elements,
which inherently limit their angular coverage to a single half-space. This constraint restricts their
applicability in scenarios where users are distributed on both sides of the surface.
To overcome this limitation,
simultaneously transmitting and reflecting reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (STAR-RISs)

have been recently proposed
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. By leveraging advanced meta-atom designs and interlayer structures, STAR-RISs are capable of
concurrently reflecting and transmitting incident signals, thereby enabling full-space EM control
[
15
,
16
]
.
This unique capability makes STAR-RISs a promising solution for supporting heterogeneous users located
in distinct spatial regions, such as indoor and outdoor environments separated by a building facade.

STAR-RISs can operate under different working
modes
, including energy splitting (ES), mode switching (MS), time division (TD),
polarization division (PD), and frequency division (FD)
[
17
,
18
]
.
Among these, the ES mode has received the most attention in the
literature due to its ability to simultaneously support transmission and reflection while respecting energy conservation principles

[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.
In this mode, the transmitted and reflected signals are intrinsically coupled through both amplitude and phase constraints. Other modes, such as MS and TD
[
26
]
, can be interpreted as special cases or extensions of the ES mode with additional constraints, while PD and FD impose stringent requirements on signal polarization or frequency selectivity
[
27
,
28
]
,
and have therefore received limited attention in the literature.

In parallel with the development of programmable metasurfaces,
integrated sensing and communication (ISAC)
has emerged as a fundamental paradigm for sixth-generation (6G) wireless networks
[
29
,
30
]
. ISAC aims to unify sensing and communication functionalities within a single system, enabling spectrum- and hardware-efficient operation. STAR-RISs are particularly well suited for ISAC applications, as their full-space control capability allows simultaneous information delivery and environmental sensing in different spatial regions. Recent works have investigated STAR-RIS-assisted ISAC systems under various assumptions
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
.
However, most existing designs rely on idealized knowledge of user locations or directions and do not explicitly account for the intrinsic uncertainty
arising from sensing and estimation processes. Indeed,
in practical ISAC deployments, especially in dynamic outdoor environments, user positions or directions are not perfectly known and must be
inferred through sensing mechanisms. Prior studies
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]

have shown that direction-of-arrival (DOA) estimation accuracy in metasurface-assisted
systems is fundamentally limited by noise, propagation conditions, and hardware constraints.
Moreover, most existing works focus on optimizing beamforming and STAR-RIS coefficients within a single transmission stage.
In contrast, the protocol-level design (i.e., the joint optimization of system parameters across multiple phases within
a slot) accounting for
the distinct roles of sensing- and communication-oriented signaling
has received comparatively limited attention.

I-A

Contribution

This paper investigates a STAR-RIS-aided ISAC system deployed on the
façade of a smart building (see Fig.
1
), where a base station (BS)
serves both indoor and outdoor users while sensing the directions of outdoor users through dedicated sensors mounted on the STAR-RIS.
Beyond conventional broadband services, the proposed STAR-RIS-assisted ISAC framework is particularly well suited to support Internet-of-Things (IoT)
ecosystems in smart cities
[
39
]
. Its low-power, compact metasurface deployment on building facades enables wide-area, environment-aware connectivity
and accurate device localization - supporting IoT use cases such as large-scale sensor networks, crowd and traffic monitoring,
asset tracking, and energy-efficient massive access in dense urban scenarios.

We propose a two-stage ISAC protocol operating on a slot-by-slot basis. In the preparation phase, the system performs DOA estimation
for outdoor users while simultaneously supporting downlink communication to all users. In the subsequent communication phase, the estimated
angular information is exploited to refine the transmission strategy and enhance sum-rate communication performance. In oder to explicitly capture the effect
of sensing uncertainty, the directions of outdoor users are modeled as Gaussian random variables whose variances are determined by
estimation accuracy, and the average communication and sensing performance is incorporated into the system design.

Building on this framework, we formulate a performance-balanced
optimization problem that jointly designs the base station beamforming vectors,
the STAR-RIS transmission and reflection coefficients in both stages, and
the metasurface partition between ES and transmit-only (TO) modes, where
each element fully transmits the incident signal without power splitting.
The physical feasibility constraints of STAR-RIS elements, including
energy conservation and phase coupling, are explicitly enforced, together with sensing-quality requirements. To tackle the resulting non-convex and mixed discrete–continuous optimization problem, we derive an efficient solution based on fractional programming, Lagrangian dual reformulation, successive convex approximation, and semidefinite relaxation. The binary metasurface partition is ultimately recovered through a continuous relaxation followed by projection-based binarization.
Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed design achieves a favorable trade-off between sensing accuracy and communication throughput, while exhibiting robustness against direction estimation errors. Compared with conventional STAR-RIS-aided ISAC schemes that neglect uncertainty or multi-functional operation, the proposed framework yields significant performance gains under both ideal and imperfect sensing conditions.

System model and basic assumptions

As shown in Fig.
1

Smart buildings already integrate multiple technologies (e.g., heating,

In accordance with the emerging paradigm of user-centric and

[
39
]

Sensors on the wall have direct visibility

such as, e.g., crowd monitoring, pedestrian flow analysis,
public transportation systems monitoring

41

The considered ISAC system consists of a BS,

The STAR-RIS is composed by a uniform planar array (UPA) of

The impinging EM signals can be
reflected from the STAR-RIS for outdoor sensing and communication,

We assume that
K_{\text{R}}
single-antenna users reside in the
outdoor reflection side (
reflection space
)
and
K_{\text{T}}
single-antenna users lie in

Hereinafter, for the sake of conciseness, the set

collects the indexes of both indoor and outdoor receivers:

Similarly to
[
31

where a dedicated low-cost sensor with a ULA consisting of

The direct BS–to-user links are assumed negligible (in number)

urban environments.
Such an assumption is supported by standardized models and field measurements

The observation interval is divided into time slots of
duration
T

of the users are assumed to be constant.
All the relevant channels are modeled as frequency-flat
(i.e., the channel delay spread is small relative to the inverse signal bandwidth),
as detailed in Subsection
II-C
.
During the
t
-th slot, with
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
the direction of the
k
-th user is described by

{\mathbf{d}}_{k}(t)\triangleq[\phi_{k}(t),\varphi_{k}(t)]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbf{R}^{2}

where
\phi_{k}(t)
and
\varphi_{k}(t)
are the elevation
and azimuth angles between the
k
-th user and the STAR-RIS,
respectively, for
k\in\mathcal{K}
.
We assume perfectly

45
]

i.e.,

Such an assumption is justified by the

Moreover, since the indoor propagation environment is quasi-static, the directions

Conversely, the directions of outdoor users cannot be assumed as perfectly known,

Channel model

For all wireless links, we employ

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{\text{in}}^{-1/2}\left({\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{\text{2,in}}}{1+\mu_{\text{2,in}}}}}{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,in}}^{\text{LoS}}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{1+\mu_{\text{2,in}}}}{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,in}}^{\text{NLoS}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{\text{out}}^{-1/2}\left({\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{\text{2,out}}}{1+\mu_{\text{2,out}}}}}{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,out}}^{\text{LoS}}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{1+\mu_{\text{2,out}}}}{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,out}}^{\text{NLoS}}\right)

and the indoor users,
\mathbf{P}_{\text{out}}\triangleq{\text{diag}}(\varsigma_{\text{out},1},\varsigma_{\text{out},2},\ldots,\varsigma_{\text{out},K_{\text{R}}})

gathers the path losses of the channels between the STAR-RIS
and the outdoor users.
For the generic
\ell
-th link, we adopt as path-loss model

\varsigma_{\ell}=\varsigma_{0}\left({d_{\ell}}/{d_{0}}\right)^{-\kappa_{\ell}}
, with

{d_{\ell}}
denoting the propagation distance,

d_{0}
being the reference distance (typically
1
m),

\varsigma_{0}
representing the reference path loss at
d_{0}
,
and
\kappa_{\ell}
indicating the path loss exponent.
Moreover, in (
14
), (
15
),
and (
16
)
\mu_{1},\mu_{\text{2,in}},\mu_{\text{2,out}}>0

are the Rice factors of the corresponding channels, whereas

\{{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,in}}^{\text{LoS}},{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,out}}^{\text{LoS}}\}
and

\{{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,in}}^{\text{NLoS}},{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,out}}^{\text{NLoS}}\}

denote the (deterministic) line-of-sight (LoS) component
and the (random) nonline-of-sight (NLoS) components.

The NLoS channel entries of the matrices
{\mathbf{H}}_{1}^{\text{NLoS}}
and

{\mathbf{H}}_{2}^{\text{NLoS}}\triangleq[\{{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,in}}^{\text{NLoS}}\}^{\text{T}},\{{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,out}}^{\text{NLoS}}\}^{\text{T}}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times N}

are mutually independent, modeled as independent identically-distributed (i.i.d.)
complex circularly-symmetric zero-mean unit-variance Gaussian random variables.
For the LoS components, we express them using the array steering vectors of the BS and STAR-RIS.
Specifically, under the assumption of half-wavelength inter-element spacing, one has
(see Fig.
3
)

{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{BS}}(\phi,\varphi)\triangleq\left[1,e^{-j\pi\cos\phi\,\cos\varphi},\cdots,e^{-j\pi(M-1)\cos\phi\,\cos\varphi}\right]^{\text{T}}

(17)

and

{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{STAR}}(\phi,\varphi)=\left[1,e^{-j\pi\sin\phi\,\cos\varphi},\right.\\
\left.\cdots,e^{-j\pi(N_{x}-1)\sin\phi\,\cos\varphi}\right]^{\text{T}}\\
\otimes\left[1,e^{-j\pi\sin\varphi},\cdots,e^{-j\pi(N_{z}-1)\sin\varphi}\right]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}

(18)

respectively, with
\phi
and
\varphi
denoting the elevation and azimuth angles.
Consequently, it turns out that

{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{LoS}}={\mathbf{a}}_{\text{STAR}}(\phi_{\text{BS}},\varphi_{\text{BS}})\,{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{BS}}^{\text{H}}(\phi_{\text{BS}},\varphi_{\text{BS}})

(19)

with
\phi_{\text{BS}}
and
\varphi_{\text{BS}}

(see Fig.
3
)
denoting the elevation and azimuth angles
between the BS and the STAR-RIS.
Moreover, the
k
-th row of

{\mathbf{H}}_{2}^{\text{LoS}}\triangleq[\{{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,in}}^{\text{LoS}}\}^{\text{T}},\{{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,out}}^{\text{LoS}}\}^{\text{T}}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times N}
is given by

{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{STAR}}^{\text{H}}(\phi_{k},\varphi_{k})
,
where we recall (see Fig.
3
)
that
\phi_{k}
and
\varphi_{k}
are the elevation and azimuth angle
between the
k
-th user and the STAR-RIS.

In this work, the channel between the BS and the STAR-RIS (i.e., the matrix
{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}
) is assumed to be perfectly known.
This assumption is reasonable and widely adopted in the literature on RIS- and STAR-RIS-assisted
ISAC systems (see e.g.,
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
) for several practical and theoretical reasons.
First, the BS-to-STAR-RIS link is typically quasi-static and highly deterministic, as the STAR-RIS is deployed at a fixed and
known location with a stable orientation relative to the BS. This link primarily consists of a LoS or
dominant specular component, which can be accurately characterized during installation or calibration
using standard site-survey or backhaul measurements. Second, in ISAC systems, the BS transmits known pilot or sensing waveforms,
enabling precise self-sounding of the BS-to-STAR-RIS channel prior to or during regular operation. Since the channel matrix
{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}
changes
much more slowly than the dynamic STAR-RIS–to-user channels, its estimation overhead is negligible
with respect to the overall system timescale.

Regarding the links among the STAR-RIS and the indoor users,
the matrix
{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,in}}
is assumed to be perfectly known
due to the assumption stated above that the directions
of indoor users are given and the fact that the rich but wide-sense stationary
NLoS multipath of indoor users can be reliably estimated and tracked with
negligible training overhead relative to the coherence time.
On the other hand, as discussed earlier, the angles of outdoor users relative
to the STAR-RIS are generally unknown and must be estimated
[
46
]
.
Therefore, the LoS components of the STAR-RIS–to–outdoor-user channels
(i.e., the matrix
{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,out}}^{\text{LoS}}
)
inherently depend on imperfect directional information. Conversely, the NLoS components
(i.e., the matrix
{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2,out}}^{\text{NLoS}}
)
are treated as known, as their small-scale variations can be learned through standard pilot-based channel acquisition within each coherence interval and typically remain quasi-static over short durations. This modeling approach reflects practical ISAC operation
[
49
,
50
]
,
in which geometric parameters such as DOAs are inferred through sensing, while small-scale fading is estimated from communication signaling and updated sufficiently often to maintain reliable channel knowledge.

k

Consequently, it follows that

, where

Let
{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}^{H}
denote the
k

, during a given time slot,

for

and

, where

Thus, the

for
k\in\mathcal{K}
and

\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.

Assuming that the BS encodes the information for

\mathcal{R}=\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{\text{p}}+(1-\eta)\,\mathcal{R}^{\text{c}}

In what follows, the total

II-E

Signal received by the sensor

For analytical tractability, it is assumed that the sensor mounted on the STAR-RIS can
perfectly separate the echo signals corresponding to the different targets (i.e.,
outdoor users).
This is consistent with recent studies on ISAC systems
[
51
,
31
,
33
]
,
which employ target-specific signaling or structured processing to make multi-target discrimination feasible.
In particular, the echo signals can be processed independently if the targets are sufficiently separated in
angle, range, or Doppler domain
[
51
,
31
]
, or when orthogonal modulation
sequences are employed
[
33
]
.
Such an assumption is widely adopted in ISAC literature and can be relaxed in future work to incorporate
partial or imperfect target separability.

During the preparation phase of a given time slot, under the assumption of perfect echo separation,
the received echo signal at the sensor due to
the
k
-th target can be written as

{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{s},k}(i)=\alpha_{k}\,{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{S}}(\phi_{k},\varphi_{k})\,{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{STAR}}^{\text{H}}(\phi_{k},\varphi_{k})\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{x}}(i)+{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{s}}(i)

(26)

for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
and

i\in\{0,1,\ldots,I_{\text{p}}-1\}
,
where
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
is the known complex amplitude determined
by the round-trip path-loss and the radar cross section (RCS)
of the target, whereas

\mathbf{a}_{\rm{S}}({\phi_{k}},{\varphi_{k}})=\left[1,e^{-j\pi\cos{\phi_{k}}\cos{\varphi_{k}}},\right.\\
\left.\cdots,e^{-j\pi\left({{N_{\rm{s}}}-1}\right)\cos{\phi_{k}}\cos{\varphi_{k}}}\right]^{T}

(27)

represents the steering
vector of the sensor with half-wavelength inter-element spacing,
and
{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{s}}(i)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{s}}}
is zero-mean complex circular
AWGN having covariance
matrix
{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{s}}(i)\,{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}(i)]=\sigma_{n_{\text{s}}}^{2}\,{\mathbf{I}}_{N_{\text{s}}}
,
which is statistically
independent of noise samples in other symbol intervals and slots.

The overall data block used by the sensor to estimate
the direction
{\mathbf{d}}_{k}=[\phi_{k},\varphi_{k}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

of the
k
-th outdoor user during the current time slot is given by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{s},k}

\displaystyle\triangleq[{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{s},k}(0),{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{s},k}(1),\ldots,{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{s},k}(I_{\text{p}}-1)]\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{s}}\times I_{\text{p}}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\,{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{S}}(\phi_{k},\varphi_{k})\,{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{STAR}}^{\text{H}}(\phi_{k},\varphi_{k})\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{W}}\,{\mathbf{S}}+{\mathbf{N}}_{\text{s}}

(28)

with

{\mathbf{S}}\triangleq[{\mathbf{s}}(0),{\mathbf{s}}(1),\ldots,{\mathbf{s}}(I_{\text{p}}-1)]\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times I_{\text{p}}}
collecting the symbols
transmitted during the preparation stage
and the matrix

{\mathbf{N}}_{\text{s}}\triangleq[{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{s}}(0),{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{s}}(1),\ldots,{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{s}}(I_{\text{p}}-1)]\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{s}}\times I_{\text{p}}}

gathering the corresponding noise samples.
With reference to the
k
-th target, the
sensing signal-to-noise ratio
(SSNR)
is defined as

\text{SSNR}_{k}=\frac{|\alpha_{k}|^{2}\,\left\|{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{S}}(\phi_{k},\varphi_{k})\,{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{STAR}}^{\text{H}}(\phi_{k},\varphi_{k})\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{W}}\right\|^{2}}{N_{\text{s}}\,\sigma_{n_{\text{s}}}^{2}}

(29)

for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
.
On the basis of the observations
{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{s},k}
,
the task of the sensor is to provide an estimate

\widehat{{\mathbf{d}}}_{k}=[\widehat{\phi}_{k},\widehat{\varphi}_{k}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

of the
k
-th outdoor direction during the current slot, which
can be obtained by the classic maximum
likelihood estimation (MLE)
[
31
,
52
]
or
subspace-based algorithms
[
53
,
54
,
55
]
. The mean square errors (MSEs)

\sigma^{2}_{\phi_{k}}\triangleq{\mathbb{E}}[(\phi_{k}-\widehat{\phi}_{k})^{2}]

and
\sigma^{2}_{\varphi_{k}}\triangleq{\mathbb{E}}[(\varphi_{k}-\widehat{\varphi}_{k})^{2}]

are commonly used to evaluate the estimation performance.
The posterior Cramér–Rao bound (PCRB)
[
56
]
establishes a lower bound on

\sigma^{2}_{\phi_{k}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\varphi_{k}}
and, hence, it is a rigorous benchmark
for direction estimation accuracy. However, in STAR-RIS-enabled ISAC systems, the PCRB
typically leads to highly intricate mathematical expressions
[
31
]
. Therefore,
using the PCRB as performance metric significantly complicates the optimization problem
and limits analytical tractability. To maintain a good balance between accuracy
and computational simplicity, we adopt the SSNR as the primary performance indicator for
the estimation of outdoor users’ directions.
This approach allows us to formulate the problem in a more
tractable manner, while still capturing the essential impact of beamforming,
propagation conditions, and STAR-RIS configuration on the achievable sensing performance.
However, replacing the PCRB with the SSNR is meaningful primarily in the high-SSNR regime
[
56
]
.
For this reason, we impose the sensing constraint

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{SSNR}_{k}]\geq\delta_{\text{sens}}
,
with
\delta_{\text{sens}}>0
being a given threshold,
which ensures operation in the high-SSNR
region and makes the SSNR-based uncertainty model a reliable approximation.

III
Optimization framework

We formulate an optimization problem, aimed at
maximizing
the achievable communication rate with respect to
{\mathbf{W}}
,

{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}
(encompassing
{\mathbf{b}}
too),

{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{p}}
,

{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}}
, and
{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{c}}
,
while ensuring that both the BS transmit power
constraint and the target detection performance requirement are satisfied.
The considered optimization also takes into account
the intrinsic physical limitations of the STAR-RIS elements, such as energy conservation,
amplitude–phase coupling, and power-splitting constraints between transmission and reflection modes.

According to (
11
), the matrix

{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}
depends on both the metasurface
partition vector
{\mathbf{b}}
and the STAR-RIS reflection
coefficients during the preparation phase.
For optimization purposes,
we exploit the factorization
{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}={\mathbf{B}}\,\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}
,
where
{\mathbf{B}}={\text{diag}}({\mathbf{b}})
encodes the metasurface partition, while

\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}\triangleq{\text{diag}}(\widetilde{\beta}_{\text{R},1}^{\text{p}}\,e^{j\theta_{\text{R},1}^{\text{p}}},\widetilde{\beta}_{\text{R},2}^{\text{p}}\,e^{j\theta_{\text{R},2}^{\text{p}}},\cdots,\widetilde{\beta}_{\text{R},N}^{\text{p}}\,e^{j\theta_{\text{R},N}^{\text{p}}})
contains only the reflection amplitudes
and phases of the STAR-RIS during the preparation stage.

A key observation is that, for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
and

\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
, the SINR in (
23
) depends on the direction
{\mathbf{d}}_{k}

of the outdoor users and, thus, knowledge of
{\mathbf{d}}_{k}

is required to jointly design the transmit beamforming at the BS and the
transmission/reflection coefficients at the STAR-RIS during both the
preparation and communication phases.
In practice, the directions
of outdoor users typically vary in a very slow manner
compared to the slot duration
T
, as their spatial positions
change only slightly over short time intervals. This is especially true in scenarios with moderate user
mobility or quasi-static targets. As a result, the outdoor directions remain approximately constant between two
adjacent time slots and, consequently, their estimates during a slot can be
reliably reused for system design in the preparation phase of the subsequent slot.

Hereinafter, for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
, we denote with
\phi_{k}^{\text{p}}
and
\varphi_{k}^{\text{p}}

the angles of the outdoor users used to optimize the system during
the preparation phase: they correspond to
the direction estimates of the outdoor users obtained in the previous slot.
On the other hand, for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
, the system design during the communication
phase relies on the outdoor user angles
\phi_{k}^{\text{c}}
and
\varphi_{k}^{\text{c}}
, estimated
in the preparation stage of the same slot. Specifically, with reference to the

t
-th slot, we set
\phi_{k}^{\text{p}}=\widehat{\phi}_{k}(t-1)
and
\varphi_{k}^{\text{p}}=\widehat{\varphi}_{k}(t-1)

in the preparation phase, and
\phi_{k}^{\text{c}}=\widehat{\phi}_{k}(t)
and
\varphi_{k}^{\text{c}}=\widehat{\varphi}_{k}(t)

in the communication stage.

To enhance robustness against estimation errors of the outdoor users’ directions, we average
the achievable rate and the SSNR with respect to
the random variables
\epsilon_{\phi_{k}}^{\text{v}}\triangleq\phi_{k}-\phi_{k}^{\text{v}}
and

\epsilon_{\varphi_{k}}^{\text{v}}\triangleq\varphi_{k}-\varphi_{k}^{\text{v}}
,
for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
and
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.
The optimization problem can thus be formulated as

(P1)

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathbf{W}},\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{c}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{R}]\quad

(30a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\phi_{k}=\phi_{k}^{\text{p}}+\epsilon_{\phi_{k}}^{\text{p}},\,\varphi_{k}=\varphi_{k}^{\text{p}}+\epsilon_{\varphi_{k}}^{\text{p}}

for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
and
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,I_{\text{p}}-1\}

(30b)

\displaystyle\phi_{k}=\phi_{k}^{\text{c}}+\epsilon_{\phi_{k}}^{\text{c}},\,\varphi_{k}=\varphi_{k}^{\text{c}}+\epsilon_{\varphi_{k}}^{\text{c}}

for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
and
i\in\{I_{\text{p}},I_{\text{p}}+1,\ldots,I-1\}

(30c)

\displaystyle\theta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}},\theta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\in\left[0,2\pi\right),\,\cos\left(\theta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}-\theta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)=0

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}},\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\in\left[0,1\right],\,\left(\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\right)^{2}+\left(\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)^{2}=1

for
n\in\mathcal{N}
and
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}

(30d)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\text{SSNR}({\mathbf{d}}_{k})]\geq\delta_{\text{sens}},\,\text{for $k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}$}

(30e)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\leq\mathcal{P}_{\text{max}}

(30f)

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{b}}\right\|_{1}=N_{\text{part}}\leq N

(30g)

where (
30b
) and (
30c
) account for the fact that the optimization relies on the
estimates of the outdoor users’ directions, with the expectation taken with respect to the
estimation errors
\epsilon_{\phi_{k}}^{\text{v}}
and
\epsilon_{\varphi_{k}}^{\text{v}}
,
for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
and
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.
Constraint (
30d
) ensures compliance with the physical operating conditions of the STAR-RIS,
constraint (
30e
) guarantees
that the average SSNR (ASSNR) of the targets remains above a given threshold
\delta_{\text{sens}}
,
and is active only during the preparation phase,
constraint (
30f
) ensures that the BS transmission power does not exceed its maximum
limit
\mathcal{P}_{\text{max}}>0
,
and, finally, constraint (
30g
) partitions the STAR-RIS during the preparation phase, with

N_{\text{part}}
elements operating in ES mode
and the others in transmit-only mode.

III-A

Evaluation of the average performance metrics

To calculate the average achievable rate and the average sensing SNR, we
assume that the random pairs
(\epsilon_{\phi_{K_{\text{T}}+1}}^{\text{v}},\epsilon_{\varphi_{K_{\text{T}}+1}}^{\text{v}}),(\epsilon_{\phi_{K_{\text{T}}+2}}^{\text{v}},\epsilon_{\varphi_{K_{\text{T}}+2}}^{\text{v}}),\ldots,(\epsilon_{\phi_{K}}^{\text{v}},\epsilon_{\varphi_{K}}^{\text{v}})
are statistically independent,
with
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.
For high SNR and large samples, DOA estimates from MLE and
subspace-based algorithms are asymptotically normal with variance approaching
the PCRB
[
56
,
57
]
. Therefore,
for each
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
, the estimation errors

\epsilon_{\phi_{k}}^{\text{v}}
and
\epsilon_{\varphi_{k}}^{\text{v}}
are modeled as independent
zero-mean Gaussian random variables with variance

\sigma^{2}_{\phi_{k}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\varphi_{k}}
given by the corresponding
PCRB, whose expression can be found in
[
31
]
.

It is worth noting that the estimates
\phi_{k}^{\text{p}}
and

\varphi_{k}^{\text{p}}
used in the current slot were obtained during the
previous slot. Consequently, they are treated as deterministic parameters
in the optimization performed during the preparation stage.
Likewise,
\phi_{k}^{\text{c}}
and

\varphi_{k}^{\text{c}}
have been acquired in the preparation phase
of the current slot and they are therefore regarded as deterministic
quantities in the subsequent communication-stage optimization.
Consequently, the angles
\phi_{k}
and
\varphi_{k}

of the outdoor users in (
30b
) and (
30c
) are independent Gaussian
random variables with mean
\phi_{k}^{\text{v}}
and

\varphi_{k}^{\text{v}}
, and variance

\sigma^{2}_{\phi_{k}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\varphi_{k}}
, respectively,
for all
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
and
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.

It comes from (
24
) that

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{R}]=\eta\,{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{R}^{\text{p}}]+(1-\eta)\,{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{R}^{\text{c}}]

(31)

where, by virtue of (
23
) and (
25
), one obtains

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{R}^{\text{v}}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\text{T}}}\log_{2}\left(1+\gamma_{k}^{\text{v}}\right)\\
+\sum_{k=K_{\text{T}}+1}^{K}{\mathbb{E}}[\log_{2}\left(1+\gamma_{k}^{\text{v}}\right)]\leq\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{max}}

(32)

with

\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{max}}\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\text{T}}}\log_{2}\left(1+\gamma_{k}^{\text{v}}\right)+\sum_{k=K_{\text{T}}+1}^{K}\log_{2}\left(1+{\mathbb{E}}[\gamma_{k}^{\text{v}}]\right)

(33)

for
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
, where the upper bound follows from the
application of Jensen’s inequality
[
52
]
.
The exact expression of
{\mathbb{E}}[\gamma_{k}^{\text{v}}]
is intractable due to the nonlinear
ratio of random functions. To obtain a tractable yet accurate characterization,
we employ a first-order approximation
[
58
]
by replacing the random ratio with
the ratio of the corresponding expectations:

{\mathbb{E}}[\gamma_{k}^{\text{v}}]\approx\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}^{\text{v}}\}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\right|^{2}\right]}{\displaystyle\sum_{\shortstack{\footnotesize$h=1$ \\
\footnotesize$h\neq k$}}^{K}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}^{\text{v}}\}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{h}\right|^{2}\right]+\sigma_{n_{\text{u}}}^{2}}\\

\footnotesize$h\neq k$}}^{K}{\mathbf{w}}_{h}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{H}}\left\{{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\right\}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}}^{\text{v}}\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{h}+\sigma_{n_{\text{u}}}^{2}}

(34)

for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
, where
{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}}^{\text{v}}\triangleq{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}^{\text{v}}\,\{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}^{\text{v}}\}^{\text{H}}\right]

and, according to (

\left\{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}^{\text{v}}\right\}^{\text{H}}=\sqrt{\varsigma_{\text{out},k}}\left({\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{\text{2,out}}}{1+\mu_{\text{2,out}}}}}\right.\\
\left.\cdot\,{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{STAR}}^{\text{H}}(\phi_{k}^{\text{v}}+\epsilon_{\phi_{k}}^{\text{v}},\varphi_{k}^{\text{v}}+\epsilon_{\varphi_{k}}^{\text{v}})\right.\\
\left.+\sqrt{\frac{1}{1+\mu_{\text{2,out}}}}\left\{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}^{\text{NLoS}}\right\}^{\text{H}}\right)

(35)

with
\{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}^{\text{NLoS}}\}^{\text{H}}
being the
k
-th row of the matrix

{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{2}}^{\text{NLoS}}
, for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
and
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.
The approximation in (

) is accurate when the fluctuations of

Generally, the correlation matrix
{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}}^{\text{v}}
does not

In the simulations of Section

,
\varphi_{k}^{\text{v}}
,

\sigma^{2}_{\phi_{k}}
, and
\sigma^{2}_{\varphi_{k}}
,
the matrix

The ASSNR comes from averaging (

) with respect to
\epsilon_{\phi_{k}}^{\text{p}}
and
\epsilon_{\varphi_{k}}^{\text{p}}
,
thus yielding

{\mathbb{E}}[\text{SSNR}({\mathbf{d}}_{k})]=\frac{|\alpha_{k}|^{2}}{N_{\text{s}}\,\sigma_{n_{\text{s}}}^{2}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{S}}(\phi_{k}^{\text{p}}+\epsilon_{\phi_{k}}^{\text{p}},\varphi_{k}^{\text{p}}+\epsilon_{\varphi_{k}}^{\text{p}})\right.\right.\\

for

, with

under (

) and (

.

III-B

Closed-form fractional programming solution

In the sequel, we consider a modification of problem
(P1)
,
which is obtained by replacing
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{R}^{\text{v}}]
in (
31
) with

\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{max}}
given by (
33
), where

{\mathbb{E}}[\gamma_{k}^{\text{v}}]
is approximated as in (
34
),
for
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
and
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
.
In a unified form, we rewrite
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{max}}
as

\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{max}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log_{2}\left(1+\bar{\gamma}_{k}^{\text{v}}\right)

(38)

where

\bar{\gamma}_{k}^{\text{v}}\triangleq\frac{{\mathbf{w}}_{k}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{H}}\left\{{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\right\}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{k}}{\displaystyle\sum_{\shortstack{\footnotesize$h=1$ \\
\footnotesize$h\neq k$}}^{K}{\mathbf{w}}_{h}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{H}}\left\{{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\right\}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{h}+\sigma_{n_{\text{u}}}^{2}}

(39)

with

{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{v}}(k)\triangleq\begin{cases}{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}^{\text{v}}\,\{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}^{\text{v}}\}^{\text{H}}\>,&\text{for $k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{T}}$}\\
{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{2},k}}^{\text{v}}\>,&\text{for $k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}$}\>.\end{cases}

(40)

We refer to such an optimization problem as
(P1)-mod
.
This problem is difficult to solve because it is non-convex
due to the coupling between the variables,
as well as to the non-linear sensing constraint.
Further, the optimization variables are mixed discrete-continuous.

Herein, we apply fractional programming (FP) theory
[
59
,
60
]
to derive an
equivalent yet more tractable fractional formulation.
More precisely, we resort to closed-form FP.
Such an approach relies on a Lagrangian dual reformulation of the original problem, enabling an
algorithm where each iteration admits a closed-form update instead of requiring the
numerical solution of a convex subproblem as in the direct FP alternative
[
59
,
60
]
.
The Lagrangian dual transform is capable of converting the cost function of
problem
(P1)-mod
to a sum-of-ratio form. Specifically,
let
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{v}}\triangleq[\bar{\tau}_{1}^{\text{v}},\bar{\tau}_{2}^{\text{v}},\ldots,\bar{\tau}_{K}^{\text{v}}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
be a vector of auxiliary variables, for
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
,
problem
(P1)-mod
is equivalent to maximizing
(see
[
60
]
for a constructive proof)

\bar{\mathcal{R}}=\eta\,\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{p}}+(1-\eta)\,\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{c}}

(41)

with respect to (w.r.t.)

{\mathbf{W}},\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{c}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{p}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{c}}
, s.t. (
30b
), (
30c
),
(
30d
), (
30e
), (
30f
), and (
30g
), where

\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}
is reported in (
42
)
at the top of the next page,

\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}\triangleq\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\log_{2}\left(1+\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}\right)-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}+\underbrace{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\left(1+\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}\right){\mathbf{w}}_{k}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{H}}\left\{{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\right\}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{k}}{\sum_{h=1}^{K}{\mathbf{w}}_{h}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{H}}\left\{{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\right\}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{h}+\sigma_{n_{\text{u}}}^{2}}}_{\text{Sum-of-ratio term}}

(42)

for
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.
The equivalence between the two problems stems from the fact
that
\bar{\mathcal{R}}
is a concave differentiable
function over
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{p}}
and

\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{c}}
, when

{\mathbf{W}},\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{c}}

are held fixed. In this case, the optimal value of
\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}
is obtained
by setting
\partial\bar{\mathcal{R}}/\partial\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}=0
, thus yielding

\bar{\tau}_{k,\text{opt}}^{\text{v}}=\bar{\gamma}_{k}^{\text{v}}

(43)

for
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
and
k\in\mathcal{K}
.
It is worth noticing that the optimal
\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}

is equal to the downlink SINR of the
k
-th user.
On the other hand, when
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{v}}
is kept fixed, only the last term
of
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}
in (
42
), which has
a sum-of-ratio form, is involved in the optimization of
the variables
{\mathbf{W}},\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{c}}
.
By the quadratic transform
[
59
,
60
]
on the fractional term
in (
42
), we may further recast
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}
as reported in
(
44
) at the top of the next page,

\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{q}}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\log_{2}\left(1+\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}\right)-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}2\,\bar{\rho}_{k}^{\text{v}}\sqrt{\left(1+\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}\right){\mathbf{w}}_{k}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{H}}\left\{{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\right\}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{k}}\\
-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}(\bar{\rho}_{k}^{\text{v}})^{2}\left(\sum_{h=1}^{K}{\mathbf{w}}_{h}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{H}}\left\{{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\right\}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{h}+\sigma_{n_{\text{u}}}^{2}\right)

(44)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{v}}\triangleq[\bar{\rho}_{1}^{\text{v}},\bar{\rho}_{2}^{\text{v}},\ldots,\bar{\rho}_{K}^{\text{v}}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
is a vector of auxiliary variables.
Hence, to optimize the variables
{\mathbf{W}},\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{c}}
,
we can equivalently maximize (see
[
60
, Corollary 1]
for the formal proof)

\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{q}}=\eta\,\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{p}}_{\text{q}}+(1-\eta)\,\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{c}}_{\text{q}}

(45)

w.r.t.
{\mathbf{W}},\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{c}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{p}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{c}}
,
s.t. (
30b
), (
30c
),
(
30d
), (
30e
), (
30f
), and (
30g
),
where the entries of
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{v}}

are iteratively optimized according to (
43
), for
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.
When all the other variables are fixed, the
optimal value of
\bar{\rho}_{k}^{\text{v}}

can be obtained by setting

\partial\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{q}}/\partial\bar{\rho}_{k}^{\text{v}}=0
, i.e.,

\bar{\rho}_{k,\text{opt}}^{\text{v}}=\frac{\sqrt{\left(1+\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}\right){\mathbf{w}}_{k}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{H}}\left\{{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\right\}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{k}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{K}{\mathbf{w}}_{h}^{\text{H}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{H}}\left\{{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\right\}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{\text{v}}(k)\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\,{\mathbf{w}}_{h}+\sigma_{n_{\text{u}}}^{2}}\>.

(46)

In the forthcoming subsections, we develop the optimization of
the variables
{\mathbf{W}},\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{c}}
in the
preparation and communication phases, by
fixing
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{v}}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{v}}
,
for
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.

III-C

Optimization of the beamforming matrix at the BS

This subsection focuses on optimizing the BS beamformer, while keeping

{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{v}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{v}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{v}}
fixed,
for a given system’s stage
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
. The problem to be solved reads as

\displaystyle\textbf{(P2)}:\quad

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{W}}\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{q}}\quad

(47a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;{\mathbb{E}}[\text{SSNR}({\mathbf{d}}_{k})]\geq\delta_{\text{sens}},\,\text{for $k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}$}

(47b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\leq\mathcal{P}_{\text{max}}

(47c)

where the transformed objective
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{q}}
is concave in
\mathbf{W}
.
According to (
2
),
the convex constraint (
47c
) is explicated as

\sum_{h=1}^{K}\mathbf{w}_{h}^{\text{H}}\,\mathbf{w}_{h}\leq\mathcal{P}_{\text{max}}\>.

(48)

It is worth noting that the beamforming matrix has to be optimized in both stages
of the protocol: during the preparation phase, where it jointly supports communication
and direction estimation, and again in the communication phase, where the updated angular
information is exploited to refine the communication process.

During the communication phase, constraint (
47b
) is removed
and, thus, problem
(P2)
reduces to a communication-only beamforming design under
a sum transmit power constraint
[
61
]
. In this stage, the resulting problem is convex
and satisfies Slater’s condition
[
58
,
62
]
. Consequently, strong duality holds and
the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions are necessary and sufficient for optimality.

The solution of problem
(P2)
is more challenging during the
preparation phase, when constraint (
47b
) is active. In this stage,
relying on (
36
), constraint (
47b
) can be rewritten as

f_{k}(\mathbf{W})\triangleq\sum_{h=1}^{K}\mathbf{w}_{h}^{\text{H}}\,\mathbf{P}_{k}\,\mathbf{w}_{h}\geq\frac{\delta_{\text{sens}}\,N_{\text{s}}\,\sigma_{n_{\text{s}}}^{2}}{|\alpha_{k}|^{2}}

(49)

with
\mathbf{P}_{k}\triangleq{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}^{\text{H}}\,\{{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}\}^{*}\,{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{S}}{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{STAR}},k}^{\text{p}}\,{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}\,{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{1}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
,
which is active only during the preparation phase.
The sensing constraint in (
49
) is non-convex because it
involves a super-level set of a convex quadratic form.
To handle this, we adopt a successive convex approximation (SCA) strategy
[
63
]
.

At iteration
\imath\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let

\mathbf{W}^{\imath}\triangleq[\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\imath},\mathbf{w}_{2}^{\imath},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{K}^{\imath}]

be the current beamformer matrix. The first-order Taylor expansion of
f_{k}(\mathbf{W})

around
\mathbf{W}^{\imath}
gives a global affine lower bound

f_{k}(\mathbf{W})\geq\sum_{h=1}^{K}2\,\Re\left\{[\mathbf{w}_{h}^{\imath}]^{\text{H}}\,\mathbf{P}_{k}\,\mathbf{w}_{h}\right\}-\sum_{h=1}^{K}[\mathbf{w}_{h}^{\imath}]^{\text{H}}\,\mathbf{P}_{k}\,\mathbf{w}_{h}^{\imath}\>.

(50)

Therefore, at iteration
\imath+1
, we may replace (
49
)
with its affine inner approximation

\sum_{h=1}^{K}2\,\Re\left\{[\mathbf{w}_{h}^{\imath}]^{\text{H}}\,\mathbf{P}_{k}\,\mathbf{w}_{h}\right\}-\sum_{h=1}^{K}[\mathbf{w}_{h}^{\imath}]^{\text{H}}\,\mathbf{P}_{k}\,\mathbf{w}_{h}^{\imath}\geq\frac{\delta_{\text{sens}}\,N_{\text{s}}\,\sigma_{n_{\text{s}}}^{2}}{|\alpha_{k}|^{2}}

(51)

for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}
. Such a set of constraints is convex and still inner-approximates
the original feasible set, ensuring monotonic improvement under standard SCA conditions
[
63
]
.
Consequently, at iteration
\imath+1
, the maximization of
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{p}}_{\text{q}}

w.r.t.
\mathbf{W}
,
s.t. (
48
) and (
51
), is
a convex quadratically constrained quadratic program (QCQP), which
can be handled with standard solvers
[
58
]
.
The corresponding solution
\mathbf{W}^{\imath+1}
is then updated and
the algorithm is repeated until convergence.

III-D

Optimization of the STAR-RIS partition during the preparation phase

In this subsection, we tackle the optimization of the
binary selection vector
\mathbf{b}
during
the preparation phase, when

\mathbf{W},\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{p}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{p}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{p}}

are kept fixed.
We recall that, during the communication phase, all the elements of
the STAR-RIS work in ES mode, i.e.,

\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{1}_{N}
.
In the preparation stage, the optimization problem assumes the form

\displaystyle\textbf{(P3)}:\quad

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{p}}_{\text{q}}\quad

(52a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\left\|{\mathbf{b}}\right\|_{1}=N_{\text{part}}\leq N\>.

(52b)

Optimization
(P3)
is a purely combinatorial problem and, therefore,
SCA cannot be applied
directly
in its original form.
To circumvent such a problem, we propose to first relax the binary constraint
by replacing
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
with its continuous approximation

\mathbf{b}\in[0,1]^{N}
and, then, enforce a
“binary-like” behavior via a penalty term

\chi\triangleq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(b_{n}-b_{n}^{2}\right)

(53)

which is maximized when
b_{n}=0
or
b_{n}=1
.
Specifically, we consider the penalized objective

\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{p}}_{\text{q},\text{pen}}\triangleq\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{p}}_{\text{q}}-\kappa\,\chi
,
which is maximized w.r.t.
\mathbf{b}\in[0,1]^{N}
, where
\kappa
is the regularization parameter.
Such an optimization problem is non-convex, since
\chi

is the difference of convex mappings
(a linear function minus a convex quadratic one)
and it can be now solved by leveraging SCA.
At iteration
\imath+1
, by resorting to first-order Taylor expansion,
we linearize the concave part
-b_{n}^{2}
of
\chi

around the current point
b_{n}^{\imath}
, thus obtaining

-b_{n}^{2}\approx-(b_{n}^{\imath})^{2}-2\,b_{n}^{\imath}\,(b_{n}-b_{n}^{\imath})
,
which yields the affine approximation of the penalty term

\chi\approx\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[b_{n}-(b_{n}^{\imath})^{2}-2\,b_{n}^{\imath}\,(b_{n}-b_{n}^{\imath})\right]\>.

(54)

With this first-order linearization, the subproblem at iteration
\imath+1
, which amounts to maximize

\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{p}}_{\text{q},\text{pen}}
w.r.t.
\mathbf{b}
with the approximation
(
54
), becomes convex and can be tackled
with a standard convex solver
[
58
]
.
The resulting solution
{\mathbf{b}}^{\imath+1}\triangleq[b_{1}^{\imath+1},b_{2}^{\imath+1},\ldots,b_{N}^{\imath+1}]^{\text{T}}\in[0,1]^{N}
is then updated and the
process is repeated until convergence.

Finally, to enforce the constraint (
52b
) and binarize the vector obtained
after convergence of the SCA algorithm, the binary partition vector

\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
is obtained by
selecting the
N_{\text{part}}
entries of largest magnitude and setting them to one,
while the remaining elements are set to zero. This operation constitutes the
Euclidean projection onto the feasible binary set with fixed cardinality.

III-E

Optimization of the STAR-RIS coefficients

In this subsection, we tackle the optimization of the STAR-RIS parameters,
while maintaining

\mathbf{W}
,
{\mathbf{b}}
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{v}}
, and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{v}}
fixed,
for a given system’s stage
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.
In this case, we have to solve the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\textbf{(P4)}:\quad

\displaystyle\max_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{v}},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}}}\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{q}}\quad

(55a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\theta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}},\theta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\in\left[0,2\pi\right),\,\cos\left(\theta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}-\theta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)=0

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}},\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\in\left[0,1\right],\,\left(\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\right)^{2}+\left(\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)^{2}=1\>,\forall n\in\mathcal{N}

(55b)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\text{SSNR}({\mathbf{d}}_{k})]\geq\delta_{\text{sens}},\,\text{for $k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}$}\>.

(55c)

with
\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Phi}}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}}={\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}}

as defined in (
13
).
In this case, the transformed objective function
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{q}}

in (
44
) depends on the STAR-RIS coefficients only through quadratic forms of the reflection and transmission coefficient vectors.
Specifically, let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}}

To convexify the problem, we follow a semidefinite relaxation (SDR) approach

\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}}

The STAR-RIS physical constraints can be translated into affine constraints in the lifted variables.
Indeed, it is verified that the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}}

represent squared amplitudes

Since the equality constraint

\left(\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\right)^{2}+\left(\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)^{2}\leq 1

\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}

{\text{diag}}\left(\mathbf{V}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}}\right)+{\text{diag}}\left(\mathbf{V}_{\text{R}}^{\text{v}}\right)\leq\mathbf{1}_{N}

(61)

where the inequality is understood element-wise.

Since the term
{\text{tr}}(\mathbf{E}_{k,k}^{\text{v}}\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}})

\text{a}\in\{\text{T},\text{R}\}
and
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}

we introduce the auxiliary variables

(z_{\text{a},k}^{\text{v}})^{2}\leq{\text{tr}}(\mathbf{E}_{k,k}^{\text{v}}\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}})
,
which is a rotated

constraint
[
58
]

So doing, the objective (
59

z_{\text{a},k}^{\text{v}}
and trace-linear functions
of the lifted matrix
\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}}
, i.e.,

\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{q}}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K_{\text{T}}}2\,\bar{\rho}_{k}^{\text{v}}\sqrt{1+\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}}\,z_{\text{T},k}^{\text{v}}+\sum\limits_{k=K_{\text{T}}+1}^{K}2\,\bar{\rho}_{k}^{\text{v}}\sqrt{1+\bar{\tau}_{k}^{\text{v}}}\,z_{\text{R},k}^{\text{v}}\\
-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K_{\text{T}}}{\text{tr}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{k}^{\text{v}}\,\mathbf{V}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}}\right)-\sum\limits_{k=K_{\text{T}}+1}^{K}{\text{tr}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{k}^{\text{v}}\,\mathbf{V}_{\text{R}}^{\text{v}}\right)\\
+\text{const}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{v}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{v}})

(62)

where

\mathbf{C}_{k}^{\text{v}}\triangleq(\bar{\rho}_{k}^{\text{v}})^{2}\sum_{h=1}^{K}\mathbf{E}_{k,h}^{\text{v}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}

(63)

and
\text{const}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{v}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{v}})
refers to a
constant term when
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{v}}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{v}}
are fixed.
By virtue of the Schur complement
[
64
]
, the SOC constraint is guaranteed
by the linear matrix inequality (LMI)

\begin{bmatrix}{\text{tr}}(\mathbf{E}_{k,k}^{\text{v}}\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}})&z_{\text{a},k}^{\text{v}}\\[2.0pt]
z_{\text{a},k}^{\text{v}}&1\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0\>,\quad\text{with $z_{\text{a},k}^{\text{v}}\geq 0$}\>.

(64)

By relaxing the rank-one constraints on
\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}}

while preserving the PSD condition
\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}}\succeq 0
,
we obtain the SDR problem

\displaystyle\textbf{(P4)-sdr}:\quad

\displaystyle\max_{\shortstack{\footnotesize$\{z_{\text{T},k}^{\text{v}}\}_{k=1}^{K},\{z_{\text{R},k}^{\text{v}}\}_{k=1}^{K}$
\\
\footnotesize$\mathbf{V}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}},\mathbf{V}_{\text{R}}^{\text{v}}$}}\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{v}}_{\text{q}}\quad

(65a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\mathbf{V}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}}\succeq 0,\mathbf{V}_{\text{R}}^{\text{v}}\succeq 0

(65b)

\displaystyle{\text{diag}}\left(\mathbf{V}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}}\right)+{\text{diag}}\left(\mathbf{V}_{\text{R}}^{\text{v}}\right)\leq\mathbf{1}_{N}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\text{tr}}(\mathbf{E}_{k,k}^{\text{v}}\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}})&z_{\text{a},k}^{\text{v}}\\[2.0pt]
z_{\text{a},k}^{\text{v}}&1\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0\>,\,\text{with $z_{\text{a},k}^{\text{v}}\geq 0$}

(65c)

\displaystyle{\text{tr}}(\mathbf{D}_{k}^{\text{p}}\,\mathbf{V}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}})\geq\frac{\delta_{\text{sens}}\,N_{\text{s}}\,\sigma_{n_{\text{s}}}^{2}}{|\alpha_{k}|^{2}}\>,\,\text{for $k\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{R}}$}\>.

(65d)

This problem is a standard SDP with LMI and affine constraints and can be solved efficiently
via interior-point methods with polynomial complexity
[
58
]
.
Let
\{z_{\text{a},k}^{\text{v}\star}\}_{k=1}^{K}

and
\mathbf{V}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}\star}

denote a solution of
(P4)-sdr
,
for
\text{a}\in\{\text{T},\text{R}\}
and
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
,
two cases may occur.
If
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}\star})=1
, then

\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}\star}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}\star}\,\{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}\star}\}^{\text{H}}
,
and the optimal STAR-RIS coefficients are obtained directly
from the principal eigenvector of
\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}\star}
.
If
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{V}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}\star})>1
,
the relaxation is not tight. A feasible rank-one approximation
can be constructed via principal eigenvector extraction,
followed by normalization, or Gaussian randomization with
projection onto the feasible STAR-RIS set.

After recovery of
the STAR-RIS parameters, the phase-coupling constraint

\cos\left(\theta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}-\theta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)=0

can be enforced by projecting the obtained phases onto orthogonal pairs,
as explained in the forthcoming subsection.

III-F

Phase-coupling restoration via Euclidean projection

Since the optimal solution of
(P4)-sdr
does not necessarily preserve
the phase-coupling structure, we enforce it by projecting the obtained solution
onto the feasible STAR-RIS set. This is achieved by minimizing the Euclidean
distance between the normalized reference vector and the feasible coefficients.

\displaystyle\textbf{(P5)}:\quad

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{v}},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}}}\;\left\|{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{v}\star}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{\text{R}}^{\text{v}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}\star}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{\text{T}}^{\text{v}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(66a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\theta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}},\theta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\in\left[0,2\pi\right),\,\cos\left(\theta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}-\theta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)=0

(66b)

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}},\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\in\left[0,1\right],\,\left(\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\right)^{2}+\left(\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)^{2}=1

(66c)

\displaystyle\forall n\in\mathcal{N}

where the diagonal matrix

{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}\star}={\text{diag}}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\text{a}}^{\text{v}\star})

is obtained from the solution of
(P4)-sdr
.
Constraint (
66b
) enforces a
\pi/2
phase difference between transmission and reflection
coefficients and is non-convex due to its discrete nature.
However, since (
66b
) and (
III-F
) independently govern phase and amplitude,
the problem can be solved via alternating optimization.

Let the amplitudes
\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}
and
\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}

be fixed,
\forall n\in\mathcal{N}
, problem
(P4)-sdr
reduces to a projection onto the phase-coupled feasible set.
The following Lagrangian formulation addresses this phase-only subproblem.
Defining the unit-modulus complex variable

u_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}}\triangleq e^{j\theta_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}}}

and the unit-modulus complex parameter
u_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}\star}\triangleq e^{j\theta_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}\star}}

(given by the solution of
(P4)-sdr
),
for
\text{a}\in\{\text{T},\text{R}\}
and
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
,
constraint (
66b
) is equivalent to the orthogonality condition

\Re\left\{u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\,(u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}})^{*}\right\}=0
.
Introducing a real Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
, the Lagrangian
of the phase-only subproblem can be written
\forall n\in\mathcal{N}
as

\mathcal{L}(u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}},u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}})\triangleq\left|u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}\star}-u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\right|^{2}+\left|u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}\star}-u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right|^{2}\\
+\lambda\,\Re\left\{u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\,(u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}})^{*}\right\}\>.

(67)

Using Wirtinger calculus
[
65
]
, the stationary conditions are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}})^{*}}

\displaystyle=\left(u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}\star}-u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\,u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}=0

(68)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}})^{*}}

\displaystyle=\left(u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}\star}-u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\,u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}=0\>.

(69)

Let
\mu=-\lambda/2\in\mathbb{R}
. For
\mu^{2}\neq 1
, the solution of
the system of equations (
68
)-(
69
) is given by

u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}=\frac{u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}\star}-\mu\,u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}\star}}{1-\mu^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}=\frac{u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}\star}-\mu\,u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}\star}}{1-\mu^{2}}\>.

(70)

Substituting (
70
) into the orthogonality constraint yields

\Re\!\left\{\frac{(u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}\star}-\mu\,u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}\star})(u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}\star}-\mu\,u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}\star})^{*}}{(1-\mu^{2})^{2}}\right\}=0

(71)

which leads to the second-order equation

c_{1}\,\mu^{2}-c_{2}\,\mu+c_{1}=0
, with
c_{1}\triangleq\Re\{u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}\star}\,(u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}\star})^{*}\}

and
c_{2}\triangleq|u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}\star}|^{2}+|u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}\star}|^{2}
, whose solutions can be expressed
in closed-form as

\mu=\frac{c_{2}\pm\sqrt{c_{2}^{2}-4\,c_{1}^{2}}}{2\,c_{1}}.

(72)

For each
n\in\mathcal{N}
, the optimal phases are then obtained as

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}

\displaystyle=\arg(u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}\star}-\mu\,u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}\star})

(73)

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}

\displaystyle=\arg(u_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}\star}-\mu\,u_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}\star})

(74)

where the root in (
72
) minimizing the objective is selected.
If
c_{1}=0
, then
\mu=0
and the phases are already orthogonal.

Let us now consider the derivation of the optimal amplitudes, for a given value
of the phases
\theta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}
and
\theta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}
,
\forall n\in\mathcal{N}
.
Problem
(P4)-sdr
ends up to

\displaystyle\max_{\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}},\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\in\left[0,1\right]}\;\left(\chi_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\,\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}+\chi_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\,\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)

(75)

s.t.

\displaystyle\left(\beta_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\right)^{2}+\left(\beta_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)^{2}=1

where
\chi_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}}\triangleq\beta_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}\star}\,\cos(\theta_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}\star}-\theta_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}})
,
with
\beta_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}\star}\,e^{j\theta_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}\star}}

obtained by solving
(P4)-sdr
,
for
\text{a}\in\{\text{T},\text{R}\}
and
\text{v}\in\{\text{p,c}\}
.
Such an amplitude-only subproblem is the maximization of a linear function over the unit circle,
which admits the following closed-form solutions:

•

If
\chi_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\geq 0
and
\chi_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\geq 0
, then

\widehat{\beta}_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}}=\frac{\chi_{\text{a},n}^{\text{v}}}{\sqrt{(\chi_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}})^{2}+(\chi_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}})^{2}}}\quad\text{for $\text{a}\in\{\text{T},\text{R}\}$}\>.

•

If
\chi_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\geq 0
and
\chi_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}<0
, then

\widehat{\beta}_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\beta}_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}=0\>.

•

If
\chi_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}<0
and
\chi_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\geq 0
, then

\widehat{\beta}_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\beta}_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}=1\>.

•

If
\chi_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}<0
and
\chi_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}<0
, then

\left(\widehat{\beta}_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}},\widehat{\beta}_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\right)=\begin{cases}(0,1),&\chi_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\geq\chi_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}\\
(1,0),&\chi_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}>\chi_{\text{T},n}^{\text{v}}\end{cases}.

We note that, when both

\chi_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}
and
\chi_{\text{R},n}^{\text{v}}

are positive, the optimum lies in the interior of the
first quadrant. Otherwise, the solution occurs at the
boundary of the feasible set.

III-G

Overall alternating optimization procedure

The optimization problems addressed in
Subsections
III-C
,
III-D
,

III-E
, and
III-F

provide update rules and tractable subroutines for each variable block. In this subsection we
describe how these blocks are coordinated to solve the original problem
(P1)-mod
.
We adopt a block coordinate descent (BCD) framework
[
66
]
, where at each
iteration one block of variables is updated while keeping the others fixed.
More specifically, we resort to a penalty parameter updating strategy, widely used
in augmented-Lagrangian and constrained non-convex optimization

[
67
,
68
]
, which enables the BCD algorithm to first locate
a reliable operating point in a relaxed feasible region and subsequently enforce
the physical STAR-RIS constraints. This continuation mechanism mitigates poor
stationary points commonly encountered in tightly coupled non-convex beamforming problems.

During the preparation stage, the algorithm updates
the variables sequentially as follows:

1.

Update the variables
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{p}}

and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{p}}

using (
43
) and (
46
).

2.

Update the BS beamforming matrix
\mathbf{W}
by solving problem
(P2)

in (
47a
)–(
47c
) via SCA.

3.

Update the STAR-RIS partition vector
\mathbf{b}
using the
penalized relaxation and projection in Subsection
III-D
.

4.

Update the STAR-RIS coefficients

(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\text{T}}^{\text{p}},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\text{R}}^{\text{p}})

by solving the SDR problem
(P4)-sdr

in (
65a
)–(
65d
) and applying rank-one recovery.

5.

Recover feasible element coefficients via the element-wise projection in
Subsection
III-F
.

During the communication stage, sensing constraints
are inactive and all elements operate in ES mode
(
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{1}_{N}
). The updates reduce to the following steps:

1.

Update
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\text{c}}
and

\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{c}}
using (
43
) and (
46
).

2.

Update
\mathbf{W}
by solving the convex communication-only
beamforming problem
[
61
,
58
,
62
]
.

3.

Update
(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\text{T}}^{\text{c}},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\text{R}}^{\text{c}})

by solving the SDR problem
(P4)-sdr

in (
65a
)–(
65d
) and applying rank-one recovery.

4.

Recover feasible element coefficients via the element-wise projection in
Subsection
III-F
.

Each block update either (i) maximizes the objective with the remaining
variables fixed, or (ii) maximizes a tight surrogate (SCA) that lower-bounds
the original cost. Hence, the sequence of objective values is monotonically
non-decreasing and upper bounded due to the finite transmit power. By
standard results for block-coordinate methods
(see, e.g.,
[
66
,
67
,
68
]
),
the procedure converges to a stationary point of the original problem
under mild regularity conditions.

In practice we terminate the outer loop when the relative improvement of the
objective between two successive outer iterations is below a threshold

\epsilon
(e.g.,
10^{-3}
) or when a maximum number of iterations
I_{\max}

is reached. Inner subproblems (SDPs/SCA) are solved to moderate tolerances to
balance accuracy and runtime.
The computational bottleneck is represented by the SDP
solvers in problem
(P4)-sdr

(Section
III-E
). Their complexity scales polynomially with the STAR-RIS
dimension
N
(roughly cubic for interior-point solvers). The SCA and
projection steps are comparatively inexpensive. For large-scale arrays,
first-order SDP solvers or low-rank approximations can be employed. However, this
is outside the current paper’s scope and is left for future work.

Numerical results

This section evaluates the performance of the proposed STAR-RIS-assisted ISAC framework
through Monte Carlo numerical simulations. The objective is to assess the achievable communication-sensing
tradeoff and to quantify the benefits of jointly optimizing
beamforming weights, metasurface coefficients,
and element partitioning. To this end, the proposed design is compared with several benchmark schemes
representing different modeling assumptions and levels of optimization capability.
Specifically, the following benchmark schemes are considered for performance evaluation.

1.

Coupled phase-shift STAR-RIS (CPS-STAR)
:
This scheme follows the coupled phase model introduced in
[
31
]
, where the
transmission and reflection coefficients satisfy a fixed phase-difference constraint.
It serves as a reference implementation of STAR-RIS designs explicitly accounting for phase coupling.

2.

Phase-independent STAR-RIS (IPS-STAR)
:
Based on
[
32
,
7
]
, this approach optimizes the communication sum-rate while
ignoring the phase-coupling relation between transmission and reflection coefficients;
it enforces a minimum user-rate constraint.

3.

Proposed STAR-RIS without statistical information (NoStat-STAR)
:
This scheme relies on the proposed optimization framework but neglects the spatial statistical information used in the main design.
It is introduced to isolate the performance gain obtained from exploiting error estimation statistics.

4.

Proposed STAR-RIS with fixed manifold (Fixed-STAR)
:
Here, the metasurface configuration is predetermined and not optimized.
Specifically, half of the elements operate in transmission-only mode, while the
remaining elements operate in reflection-only mode. This scheme evaluates the
benefit of adaptive element partitioning.

V
Conclusions

This paper investigated the design of a STAR-RIS-assisted
ISAC systems from a protocol-level perspective.
By leveraging the full-space transmission and reflection capabilities of STAR-RISs,
a two-stage ISAC protocol was proposed, in which a preparation phase supports direction
estimation and communication, while a subsequent communication phase exploits the
acquired angular information to enhance information transfer. Differently from
most existing works, the system design explicitly takes into account
the distinct roles
of the two stages and jointly optimizes the corresponding transmission strategies.

To capture the impact of sensing uncertainty on communication performance, the directions
of outdoor users were modeled as random variables, and the average achievable rate was
incorporated into the system design. A performance-balanced optimization problem was
formulated, jointly optimizing the BS beamforming weights,
the STAR-RIS transmission and reflection
coefficients in both stages, and the metasurface partition between ES and
TO modes, while explicitly enforcing STAR-RIS physical feasibility
constraints and sensing-quality requirements. To tackle the resulting non-convex and
mixed discrete–continuous problem, an efficient solution framework based on fractional
programming, Lagrangian dual reformulation, successive convex approximation,
and semidefinite relaxation was developed.

Simulation results have demonstrated that the proposed design achieves a favorable
trade-off between sensing accuracy and communication throughput,
exhibiting a remarkable robustness against DOA
estimation errors. Compared with conventional
STAR-RIS-aided ISAC schemes that neglect protocol-level
optimization or sensing uncertainties, the proposed framework
provides consistent performance
gains under both ideal and imperfect sensing conditions.

Future deployments could exploit the low-power, facade-mounted STAR-RIS ISAC architecture
to deliver scalable, spectrum-efficient IoT connectivity across urban infrastructures,
bridging communication and localization needs for emerging smart-city services.
