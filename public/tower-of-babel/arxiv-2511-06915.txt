The noiseless limit and improved-prior limit of the maximum entropy method and their implications for the analytic continuation problem
Thomas Chuna, Nicholas Barnfield, Paul Hamann, Sebastian Schwalbe, Michael P. Friedlander, Tobias Dornheim

Abstract
Quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) methods are uniquely capable of providing exact simulations of quantum many-body systems. Unfortunately, the applications of a QMC simulation are limited because extracting dynamic properties requires solving the analytic continuation (AC) problem. Across the many fields that use QMC methods, there is no universally accepted analytic continuation algorithm for extracting dynamic properties, but many publications compare to the maximum entropy method. We investigate when entropy maximization is an acceptable approach. We show that stochastic sampling algorithms reduce to entropy maximization when the Bayesian prior is near to the true solution. We investigate when is Bryan’s controversial optimization algorithm [Bryan, Eur. Biophys. J. 18 , 165–174 (1990)] for entropy maximization (sometimes known as the maximum entropy method) appropriate to use. We show that Bryan’s algorithm is appropriate when the noise is near zero or when the Bayesian prior is near to the true solution. We also investigate the mean squared error, finding a better scaling when the Bayesian prior is near the true solution than when the noise is near zero. We point to examples of improved data-driven Bayesian priors that have already leveraged this advantage. We support these results by solving the double Gaussian problem using both Bryan’s algorithm and the newly formulated dual approach to entropy maximization [Chuna et al., J. Phys. A: Math. Theor. 58 , 335203 (2025)].

II
The mean squared error of the maximum entropy estimate

II.1
Definition of the mean squared error in terms of the bias and variance

The mean squared error (MSE) expresses, on average, the 2-norm distance of your estimator from the true solution. The MSE can be expressed as a bias-variance decomposition and, for a one-dimensional estimator, derivations are commonplace in introductory textbooks
40
. A multi-variate estimator
\hat{x}
has a bias-variance decomposition given by summing over its dimensions. This is expressed as

\displaystyle\text{MSE}\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-\hat{x}\|^{2}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\|\text{Bias}(\hat{x})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\text{Tr}\left\{\text{Cov}(\hat{x})\right\}.

(3a)

The first term is essentially sum of squares over the component-wise bias,

\displaystyle\text{Bias}(x_{i})=\mathbb{E}\left[(x_{0})_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}\right],

(3b)

where
x_{0}
is the true solution. The second term is the variance, given by the trace of the covariance matrix,

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\hat{x})_{ij}=\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{x}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{x}_{i}\right])(\hat{x}_{j}-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{x}_{j}\right])\right].

(3c)

The bias-variance decomposition breaks down the MSE into two components: the systematic error and the statistical error. Some additional commentary may be found online
69
;
47
.

To use expressions (
3b
) and (
3c
), we must have a closed form of the estimator
\hat{x}
. However, the optimum of the entropic regularized cost function (
2
) is given by
7
;
38

\displaystyle A^{\top}C^{-1}A\,\hat{x}-A^{\top}C^{-1}b+\alpha\ln\hat{x}/\mu=0

(4)

and, as pointed out by Rothkopf
56
, the term
\ln\hat{x}/\mu
prevents us from isolating the
\hat{x}
and linearly parameterizing the solution; expansions are needed. Previous investigations of the MSE expanded the solution
\hat{x}=x_{0}+\Delta\hat{x}
about the true solution
x_{0}

38
; we do not take that approach here. Instead we consider, for fixed regularization weight
\alpha
, the noiseless limit of uncorrelated data with equal variance,
i.e.

C^{-1}\approx\sigma^{-2}I
, where

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\ll 1

(5)

and the limit that the Bayesian prior is near the true solution, which we refer to as the “improved-prior” limit

\displaystyle\frac{|\hat{x}_{i}-\mu_{i}|}{\mu_{i}}\ll 1.

(6)

II.2
MSE in the noiseless limit

To compute the MSE, we insert (
5
) into (
4
) yielding

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(A^{\top}A\,\hat{x}-A^{\top}b)+\alpha\ln\hat{x}/\mu=0.

(7)

In the noiseless limit, the first term dominates. We drop the logarithmic term to recover the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimate. By the Gauss-Markov theorem, this is the best linear un-biased estimator (BLUE), with variance

\displaystyle\,\text{Cov}(\hat{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}(A^{\top}A)^{-1}.

(8)

We use the singular value decomposition,
A=U\Sigma V^{\top}
and cyclicity of the trace to arrive at

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\,\text{Cov}(\hat{\bm{x}})=\sigma^{2}\,\text{Tr}\left\{\Sigma^{-2}\right\},

(9)

which exposes how the singular values affect the MSE.

II.3
MSE in the improved-prior limit

Evaluating (
3
) for an improved-prior (
6
) requires a more detailed treatment. To start, we must compute
\hat{x}
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{x}\right]
. Beginning from (
4
), we rewrite
\hat{x}/\mu=1+(\hat{x}-\mu)/\mu
and linearize the logarithm by Taylor expanding

\displaystyle A^{\top}C^{-1}A\,\hat{x}-A^{\top}C^{-1}b+\alpha\frac{\hat{x}-\mu}{\mu}\approx 0.

(10)

This estimator resembles the estimator arising from the common
L_{2}
-distance regularization, but with
x-\mu
weighted by
1/\mu
. Next we substitute
b=b_{0}+\varepsilon=Ax_{0}+\varepsilon
and insert
0=A^{\top}C^{-1}A(\mu-\mu)
. After simplification, this produces

\displaystyle\hat{x}=\mu+M\,A(x_{0}-\mu)+M\varepsilon,

(11a)

where

\displaystyle M=\left(A^{\top}C^{-1}A+\alpha\,\text{diag}(\frac{1}{\mu})\right)^{-1}A^{\top}C^{-1}.

(11b)

Assuming
\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon\right]=0
and taking the expectation value yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{x}\right]=\mu+M\,A(x_{0}-\mu).

(12)

We may now compute the bias (
3b
) by substituting equations (
11
) and (
12
). We arrive at the expression

\displaystyle\text{Bias}\left[\hat{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(I-M\,A\right)(x_{0}-\mu)\right\|^{2}_{2}.

(13)

Grouping
H=M\,A
and distributing terms yields

\displaystyle\text{Bias}\left[\hat{x}\right]=\left\|x_{0}-\mu\right\|^{2}_{2}-2\left\|x_{0}-\mu\right\|^{2}_{H}+\left\|x_{0}-\mu\right\|^{2}_{H^{\top}H}.

(14)

To expose how the singular values affect the bias, we assume
C=\sigma^{2}I
and simplify via the SVD
A=U\Sigma V^{\top}
:

\displaystyle H=

\displaystyle\,\sigma^{-2}V\left(\sigma^{-2}\Sigma^{2}+\alpha\,V^{\top}\text{diag}(\frac{1}{\mu})V\right)^{-1}\Sigma^{2}V^{\top},

(15)

\displaystyle H^{\top}H=

\displaystyle\,\sigma^{-2}V\Sigma^{2}\left(\sigma^{-2}\Sigma^{2}+\alpha\,V^{\top}\text{diag}(\frac{1}{\mu})V\right)^{-2}\Sigma^{2}V^{\top}.

(16)

Notice that for
\sigma\ll 1
we can drop the
\alpha
terms, yielding
H=H^{\top}H=I
and
\text{Bias}\left[\hat{x}\right]=0
as is expected.

Next we compute the trace of the covariance. First, we substitute (
11
) into (
3c
) and cancel terms, which leaves behind only the noise term:

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\,\text{Cov}\left(\hat{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left\{\text{Cov}[M\varepsilon]\right\}\ .

(17)

It is interesting to assume
C=\sigma^{2}I
and simplify via the SVD to expose how the singular values affect the statistical error. We pass
M
through the Cov operator and use cyclity of the trace to produce

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\,\text{Cov}\left(\hat{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\text{Tr}\left\{\Sigma^{2}\left(\Sigma^{2}+\sigma^{2}\alpha\,V^{\top}\text{diag}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\right)V\right)^{-2}\right\}\ .

(18)

Notice in the limit that
\sigma\ll 1
we recover (
9
), which is a nice sanity check.

III
Identifying MEM as a special case of stochastic methods in the improved-prior limit

Finally, we revisit Beach’s seminal work
7
to show that the improved-prior-limit satisfies Beach’s mean-field-approximation.
Beach shows that the MEM cost function is recovered from the mean-field (
i.e.
, saddle point) approximation. In the language of this paper, this amounts to assuming that the covariance (
3c
) scaled by the default model is negligible

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[x_{i}]}{\mu_{i}}-\frac{x_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\right)\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[x_{j}]}{\mu_{j}}-\frac{x_{j}}{\mu_{j}}\right)\right]\approx 0.

(23)

Equation (
23
) is essentially the Ginzburg criterion
(
46
, Section 2)
and the LHS can be rewritten in the typical representation of the covariance as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu_{i}\mu_{j}}\text{Cov}(x_{i},x_{j})\ .

(24)

Following Asakawa, Hatsuda, Nakahara’s monkey argument
(
4
, appendix A)
, we assume the covariance between
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
is described by a multinomial distribution. Essentially, in fixed-grid stochastic sampling there are
M
blocks to be distributed into
N
bins. For a particular arrangement of blocks
x
, where
x_{i}
is the number of blocks in bin
i
, the probability of a block being in bin
i
is
p_{i}=\hat{x}_{i}/M
(
\mathbb{E}[x]=\hat{x}
) and the probability that the
i
-th bin receives
x_{i}
blocks is given by the multinomial distribution. Thus, the covariance between bins is
\text{Cov}(x_{i},x_{j})=-Mp_{i}p_{j}
, inserting this into (
24
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{\mu_{i}\mu_{j}}M^{-1}\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\,.

(25)

Notice now that Beach’s mean field approximation (
23
) is satisfied by assuming
M\rightarrow\infty
. However, we do not need this assumption in the improved-prior limit, where
\hat{x}/\mu\approx 1
and (
25
) is reduced to
M^{-1}
. So that even small
M
values (
\mathcal{O}[10^{2}]
) satisfy the Ginzberg criterion (
23
).

Intuitively, we may have expected that the improved-prior limit reduces stochastic sampling to the MEM. In statistical mechanics textbooks, the mean-field approximation is the assumption that the variable of interest has only small perturbations about the mean (for example see Tuckerman
(
63
, Section 16)
). We express this as

\displaystyle\frac{x}{\mu}=\frac{\hat{x}}{\mu}+\frac{\delta x}{\mu}.

(26)

This would precisely be the case if you initialize your stochastic sampling algorithm to a prior
\mu
near the true solution. Essentially, the samples of
x
will only be small perturbations about
\mu
because the sampler will reject large jumps away from this already small
\chi^{2}
location.

Finally, we suggest that the noiseless limit does not satisfy Beach’s mean-field approximation. Notice in the noiseless limit that, because the least squares estimate is the BLUE then
\hat{x}=x_{0}
, which does not immediately reduce (
25
) to
M^{-1}
. To add strength to the suggestion, the free term and the interaction term in Beach’s seminal paper
7
(equations 23, 24, and 25) are both scaled by
1/\sigma^{2}
. Thus, the noiseless limit would not uniquely affect the interaction term and thus would not facilitate the expansion.

IV
Conclusions

Our three major conclusions are: (a) if the Bayesian prior is near the true solution then stochastic sampling methods reduce to solving the entropy regularized least squares problem (
2
); (b) if the Bayesian prior is near the true solution, then the estimator reduces to a linear estimate and thus Bryan’s algorithm becomes valid; (c) the best way to reduce the mean squared error of the estimate is to improve the Bayesian prior.

(a), in Section
III
, we demonstrated that the assumption of an improved-prior (
6
) is sufficient to reduce stochastic methods to the MEM. Essentially, the improved-prior limit implies Beach’s mean-field approximation. This result provides an intuitive situation in which the Beach’s assumption is valid. Additionally, we suggest that the noise-less limit does not imply Beach’s mean-field approximation.

(b), in Section
II
, we demonstrated that the noiseless and improved-prior limits reduced the MEM estimator
\hat{x}
to a linear estimator. As per Rothkopf
56
, this implies that Bryan’s null-space trick is valid in these limits. We also observed this to be the case in our numeric investigation, see Figure
3
. This result explains a host of results in the literature where MEM performs equally well as other methods in the noiseless limit. The key takeaway being that other algorithms are
expected
to outperform the MEM when the signal-to-noise ratio is large because Bryan’s algorithm is not valid in that limit.

(c), in Section
II
, we have shown that in the noiseless limit, the MSE is
\sigma^{2}\,\text{Tr}\,\Sigma^{-2}
. Thus, quadratic improvements may be found with vanishing noise, but for the Laplace kernel
\text{Tr}\,\Sigma^{-2}
is numerically infinite. Given the extreme cost of quantum Monte Carlo simulations this is not an appealing approach. By comparison, in the improved-prior-limit the bias (
13
) and the variance (
18
) contain quadratically vanishing terms that are scaled by matrix
M
, which is windowed by
\alpha\,\text{diag}(1/\mu)
(
i.e.
small singular values do not lead to numerical infinity). As a result, you can reap the benefits of the quadratically diminishing terms. The MSE was estimated numerically for the double Gaussian problem to verify these claims, see Figure
2
.
