Retraction Analysis in Social Media Research: A Scopus Based Data From 2011 to 2025
Authors: Burhan Fatih Kocyigit, Maidan Mukhamediyarov, Ainur B Qumar, Yuliya Fedorchenko, Olena Zimba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial media has significantly impacted communication and information flow, making it a key academic topic today. As the field grows, the number of retractions has increased. This study aims to identify trends in research ethics by examining the bibliometric characteristics of retracted social media-related papers.MethodsThis descriptive study examined publications in the Scopus database labeled with “social media” and categorized under the “Retracted” document category. The study language was restricted to English, no time constraints were imposed, and all records from the initial retracted publication through 2025 were included. Distributions by country, institution, journal, author, keyword, year, and reasons for retraction were evaluated.ResultsA total of 141 retracted papers were detected. No significant increase in publication counts was detected from 2011 to 2025 (P = 0.053); however, a statistically significant rise in retraction numbers was identified (P = 0.008). The majority of retractions originated predominantly in Asia, particularly in China and India. The predominant retraction reason was “Investigation by Journal/Publisher” (n = 92). This was followed by “Unreliable Results and/or Conclusions” (n = 65) and “Concerns/Issues Regarding Referencing/Attributions” (n = 61).ConclusionThe findings reveal a significant increase in retractions within social media research, particularly in the 2020s. Retractions concentrated in certain countries and journals, primarily due to editorial investigation, suspicious results, and concerns related to referencing. The results underscore the field’s persistent methodological and ethical challenges. Enhancing research integrity, editorial oversight, and transparency is crucial for mitigating ethical risks and improving the scientific reliability of future social media research.Keywords: Social Media, Internet, Bibliometrics, Information Science, Retraction of Publication as Topic, Retraction Notice

INTRODUCTION

Social media has evolved into a worldwide ecosystem that has fundamentally altered communication, information availability, and the dynamics of digital engagement.1 With the rise of platforms such as Facebook, Twitter (X), Instagram, YouTube, and TikTok, social media has transcended simple daily activity; it has emerged as a significant domain of inquiry for scientific research, social behaviors, political discussions, and public health practices.2,3 In this context, the volume of academic papers on social media has surged significantly over the past decade; subjects such as social media analytics, user behavior, misinformation, and public health implications have attained prominence in the academic literature.4,5

A growing focus accompanies the increase in academic publications on retraction processes, which play a vital role in maintaining scientific integrity and upholding research ethics.6 Retraction refers to the official removal of a paper from the literature owing to significant errors in publishing, methodological inadequacies, ethical breaches, data manipulation, or discrepancies in the publication process.7 Examining retracted publications is essential for understanding issues in scientific publication processes, ensuring research integrity, and reinforcing publication ethics. In recent years, the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted manuscripts, manipulation of peer review protocols, paper milling activities, and a boost in editorial inspections have considerably influenced the number of retractions.8,9

The current phase of expanding social media research requires assessing the bibliometric characteristics of retracted articles in the field, thereby addressing a literature gap and enhancing understanding of research moral trends.10 This study aims to deliver an in-depth bibliometric examination of retracted publications concerning social media. An analysis of retracted papers in the Scopus database was conducted, focusing on indicators including trends in publication and retraction over the years, contributions from different countries, diversity among sources, distribution of institutions and authors, noteworthy keywords, citation patterns, and the Altmetric Attention Score (AAS). Additionally, an examination of the retraction reasons was conducted to uncover significant ethical and methodological challenges confronting the field.

METHODS

Search setting

This descriptive study used the Scopus database to examine publications retracted in the context of social media. The list of publications was derived from an inquiry performed on November 7, 2025. The search approach utilized the phrase “social media” and integrated it within the title, abstract, and/or keywords field.11

Only papers labeled “Retracted” in the document type were included in the study. No timeframe constraints were imposed, and all publications from the first retracted paper in the database through 2025 were included in the analysis. The original publication year and the retraction year were retrieved for each retracted document. The original publication year and the retraction year were retrieved for each retracted document. The publication year indicates when the paper was first published in the journal, while the retraction year denotes the date the official retraction notice was issued. The earliest retracted paper detected was published in 2011, consequently establishing the research period as 2011–2025. Only papers published in English were considered in the analysis.

AAS functions as a distinctive metric for measuring the degree of online engagement that an academic publication receives. The score is derived from a weighted algorithm that assesses interactions across various digital platforms, including social media posts, news sites, blog entries, policy documents, and online reference managers. AAS distinguishes itself from traditional citation metrics by focusing on evaluating a publication’s online presence, social influence, and dissemination through social media channels. Consequently, it serves as an auxiliary measure that embodies both academic engagement and social impact.13,14 AAS values were obtained on November 7, 2025, the same date on which all other bibliometric data were collected.

Analyses and data presentation

Data are represented in numbers. Data representations and visualizations were crafted utilizing Microsoft Excel. The analysis of trends in the number of publications and retractions over the years was conducted using linear regression in SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The threshold for statistical significance was established at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Selection procedure

Following the search approach outlined in the METHODS section, articles related to “social media” were listed in Scopus, with “Retracted” selected as the document type. Moreover, the research was restricted to papers published exclusively in English. After applying all filters and review procedures, a total of 141 retracted articles were included in the study.

Publication trend

The earliest social media-related paper analyzed in this study was released in 2011, with the examination period extending from 2011 to 2025. The peak number of publications transpired in 2022 (n = 51). The linear regression analysis conducted to evaluate the annual variation in the number of publications revealed no statistically significant upward trend between years and the count of papers (P = 0.053) (Fig. 1).

Retraction trend

The first retraction process documented in this study occurred in 2011, and the analysis period spanned 2011 to 2025. An analysis of annual retractions revealed that the peak number occurred in 2023 (n = 56). The linear regression analysis performed to assess the yearly fluctuations revealed a statistically significant upward trend in the number of retractions from 2011 to 2025 (P = 0.008) (Fig. 2).

Journal data

Source analysis showed that retractions were notably concentrated in a few specific journals. The journal with the highest number of retractions among social media-related publications recorded 20 cases. This was followed by two other prominent journals, each with 10 retractions. Overall, the top ten sources accounted for a substantial portion of the retracted publications, highlighting a clustering effect in certain publishing platforms.

Author data

According to the author analysis, a concentration of retractions among a few specific researchers was observed. Four authors were identified as having the highest number of retracted papers, with three retractions each. Additionally, it was determined that 12 authors contributed to two retracted publications each.

Keyword data

The keyword analysis revealed that the term “Social Media” (n = 98) was the most prominent among retracted papers. This was followed by “Human” (n = 60) and “Social Networking (online)” (n = 57). Furthermore, “Humans” (n = 54) and “Article” (n = 25) were identified as being extensively utilized. “Deep Learning” (n = 18) and “Machine Learning” (n = 17) were the most common keywords, while “Female” (n = 16), linked to individual characteristics, was also widely used. Furthermore, methodological and analytical frameworks such as “Sentiment Analysis” (n = 15) and “Controlled Study” (n = 15) were prevalent in the retracted papers (Table 1).

Institutional data

The outcomes of the institutional analysis showed that the institution with the highest number of retractions recorded 5 cases, followed by another institution with 4 cases. Several other prominent institutions in the top 10 were involved in 3 or 2 retractions each. Notably, the majority of these top-ranking institutions are located in Asia, which closely aligns with the findings of the country-level data (Fig. 3). A total of 27 institutions were identified with two retracted articles each.

Retraction reasons

An examination of the retraction notices disclosed a variety of reasons for the retraction of papers regarding social media. The predominant reason mentioned was “Investigation by Journal/Publisher” (n = 92). This was succeeded by “Unreliable Results and/or Conclusions” (n = 65) and “Concerns/Issues Regarding Referencing/Attributions” (n = 61). Additional frequent reasons were “Investigation by Third Party” (n = 52), “Concerns/Issues with Peer Review” (n = 51), and “Concerns/Issues About Data” (n = 47). Moreover, concerns over computer-generated or AI-assisted material (n = 45) and concerns about results and/or conclusions (n = 45) were significant contributors (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

This bibliometric analysis uncovered 141 retracted papers in the social media domain from 2011 to 2025, indicating a substantial rise in retraction numbers over the years, with a notable peak in 2023. The majority of retractions originated predominantly in Asia, particularly in China and India. The predominant reasons for retraction include investigation by the journal/publisher, suspicious results or conclusions, citation and reference discrepancies, and third-party reviews. Additionally, 45 articles exhibited potential AI-related concerns, highlighting the growing ethical risks in current discourse. The observation that certain retracted papers maintained high citation and AAS values indicates that flawed or problematic research can persist in influencing both scientific discourse and online presence.

An integrated evaluation of publication and retraction trends over the years reveals an imbalance between the volume of production and the retraction processes. The study revealed no statistically significant upward trend in the number of publications from 2011 to 2025 (P = 0.053). However, the notable increase in retraction numbers during this timeframe (P = 0.008) merits attention. The peak in retractions, particularly in 2023, could be attributed to the growing skepticism surrounding AI-generated content in recent years, alongside the adoption of more stringent screening and investigative processes by publishing entities and editorial teams.15,16 It is also probable that, as social media research has become increasingly popular, low-quality studies have gravitated toward this field, leading to a higher number of retractions over time.17

The country distribution suggests that social media retractions were primarily concentrated in Asian countries, with China and India leading by a wide margin. The obvious lead of these countries might be attributed to the rapid rise in academic output in recent years, increased publishing pressure, and identified methodological flaws across several fields.18,19 Publication approaches, expectations for swift production, increased publication pressure, and disparities in institutional or editorial oversight may also account for the elevated retraction rates observed in these locations.

Retractions were notably concentrated in certain journals. For instance, high numbers of retractions in some journals may be due to enhanced editorial monitoring in areas where artificial intelligence and computational approaches are spreading. Furthermore, elevated retraction rates in other journals indicate potential misuse of special issues.The retraction numbers across different journals reflect the multifaceted nature of social media research, which may introduce additional challenges regarding data precision, methodological suitability, and editorial oversight.

The prominence of the phrase “Social Media” in the keyword analysis indicates that the retracted studies were explicitly focused on social media-based research. The frequent use of terms such as “Human/Humans” and “Social Networking (online)” suggests that these papers are primarily concerned with human behavior, online interaction, and social network dynamics. Furthermore, the prominence of methodological terms such as “Deep Learning” and “Machine Learning” suggests that methodological and verifiability difficulties in research involving AI-based models may have led to the retraction.

Findings from the institutional analysis indicate that retractions are associated with higher retraction rates in Asia. The high number of retracted papers in institutions primarily located in countries such as China, India, and Saudi Arabia corroborates the elevated retraction rates at the national level with institutional data. The data suggest that retraction processes exhibit clustering in specific regions and that the country data show strong parallelism with the institutional distribution.

The predominant reason was “Investigation by Journal/Publisher,” reflecting the heightened scrutiny of editorial procedures and the reinforcement of publication ethical norms in recent years. Additional widely reported reasons were “Unreliable Results/Conclusions” and “Referencing/Attribution Issues,” suggesting that methodological discrepancies, erroneous analyses, and concerns over academic integrity are pivotal in retraction procedures.20,21 Moreover, the relevance of factors such as “third-party investigation,” “peer-review concerns,” and “data issues” underscores that systemic challenges associated with peer-review manipulation, data verifiability, and transparent reporting continue to pose a considerable risk. Finally, it is worth noting that concerns about computer-generated or AI-enabled content are increasingly cited as reasons for retraction; this illustrates that AI-based text generation has recently introduced new ethical issues in scientific publication.22

The study revealed that the most-cited papers focused on prominent subjects, including social media, misinformation, AI-based analysis, and public health. The fact that the most cited article, which focused on the anti-vaccination infodemic on social media, illustrates that misinformation is a critical issue that attracts intense attention in both the scientific and social arenas. The substantial level of online engagement with particular papers in the AAS analysis suggests that, even after retraction, this research remains widely disseminated on social media. This indicates a significant risk of misinformation and deception due to the ongoing presence of articles derived from the scientific literature in broad digital contexts.23

This study has certain limitations. The analyses are entirely based on the Scopus database. Therefore, the findings may not entirely capture the overall trends of retractions. The study investigated retraction records published in English. Publications circulated in languages other than English were omitted, potentially introducing a linguistic bias. Our search approach was based solely on the term ‘social media,’ potentially excluding papers that address analogous themes using other terminology. The publication counts, citation metrics, and AAS values used in the bibliometric analysis are dynamic indicators that fluctuate over time. Consequently, the findings are valid as of the date the analysis was executed. Although our outcomes demonstrate a significant rise in retraction numbers over time, the current bibliometric data do not allow us to determine whether this increase is due to higher publication volume, greater editorial scrutiny, or underlying research misconduct. The reliance on absolute retraction numbers, coupled with the lack of statistics on overall publishing volume, constrains our capacity to assess proportional retraction rates across countries and journals.

This study addresses retractions in the expanding field of social media research by delineating publishing trends, the most frequent reasons for retraction, and the recent increase in retracted papers. These findings are relevant to public health, as studies on social media often inform health communication and provide insights into population-level behaviors. The existence of inaccurate or retracted publications in this domain can foster disinformation and diminish trust in scientific findings. Enhancing research integrity and implementing more stringent editing processes are essential for providing accurate and reliable public health information.

In conclusion, this study highlighted a significant increase in retracted publications regarding social media from 2011 to 2025, with a notable acceleration in retraction dynamics in recent years. The outcomes revealed that retractions are concentrated in certain countries and institutions, with a higher frequency observed in particular journals. The main reasons for retraction include editorial investigations, unreliable results, citation and reference discrepancies, and irregularities in data and peer-review procedures. Concerns over AI-generated material are increasingly leading to retractions.

The analysis revealed that several retracted papers had increased citation and AAS values, indicating that scientifically inaccurate publications might persist in impacting both the academic domain and the digital landscape, hence facilitating potential misinterpretation.

The methodological, ethical, and editorial issues identified in social media-based research directly influence retraction processes, requiring enhanced evaluation, oversight, and transparency measures to safeguard the scientific integrity of the field.
