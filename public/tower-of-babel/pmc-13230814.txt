Target trial emulation of early acetaminophen use and mortality in critically ill stroke patients
Authors: Xinyan Wang, Kangda Zhang, Youxuan Wu, Zihui Zhang, Yang Li, Ruquan Han, Fa Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The role of acetaminophen in managing critically ill patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is debated. While it is commonly used for fever and pain, its direct impact on mortality in the intensive care unit (ICU) setting is unclear. This study aims to estimate the causal effect of early acetaminophen administration on mortality in critically ill adults with AIS. This study employed a target trial using retrospective data from the MIMIC-IV database (2008–2019, 1779 patients) and an external validation cohort in the eICU database (1182 patients). We compared acetaminophen (ACE) use within 48 h of ICU admission with non-ACE use within 48 h and used clone-censor-weight methods for the main analysis. The primary outcomes were 30-day and 90-day all-cause mortality. Furthermore, the findings were substantiated by sensitivity analyses, competing risk models, and subgroup analyses. In the primary CCW-weighted analysis, early acetaminophen administration was associated with a statistically significant reduction in 90-day all-cause mortality (HR, 0.70; 95% CI 0.58–0.84; P < 0.001). Similarly, the 30-day mortality risk was significantly lower in the ACE group (HR, 0.66; 95% CI 0.54–0.81). Early acetaminophen administration was associated with reduced mortality in critically ill AIS patients, particularly those without diabetes. Despite the variability in external validation, these findings suggest a phenotype-specific survival benefit warranting confirmation in precision-based randomized trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46381-5.

Introduction

As a primary contributor to global mortality and enduring disability, acute ischemic stroke (AIS) imposes a formidable burden on public health systems1,2. A significant cohort of AIS patients, particularly those exhibiting rapid neurological decline or critical systemic instability, warrants management within an intensive care unit (ICU)3,4. Within the neurocritical care environment, pyrexia frequently emerges as a metabolic sequela to severe stroke and is an independent predictor of worsened functional outcomes and elevated mortality risk5,6. The poststroke inflammatory cascade, involving microglial activation and cytokine release, significantly contributes to secondary brain injury, edema, and neuronal apoptosis7,8. Consequently, management strategies often include antipyretic and analgesic agents to mitigate these secondary injuries and reduce cerebral metabolic demand9,10.

Acetaminophen is among the most frequently administered medications in the ICU because of its antipyretic and analgesic properties. Beyond its canonical role in pyrexia management, an expanding corpus of preclinical evidence indicates that acetaminophen may have pleiotropic effects that are integral to stroke pathophysiology11,12. These include antioxidant properties via the scavenging of free radicals, modulation of the endocannabinoid system13, and potential inhibition of a centrally mediated cyclooxygenase (COX) variant, termed COX-314. Furthermore, emerging data suggest that acetaminophen may exert neuroprotective effects by preserving blood–brain barrier integrity and ameliorating microcirculatory dysfunction during the critical reperfusion phase, which are mechanisms that are distinct from its antipyretic action15,16. These mechanisms suggest that the benefits of acetaminophen might extend beyond simple fever reduction.

However, the clinical evidence regarding its impact on mortality in critically ill stroke patients is sparse and conflicting. The large Paracetamol (acetaminophen) in Stroke (PAIS) trial did not find a benefit of high-dose acetaminophen on functional outcomes17. It was not specifically powered to detect a difference in mortality in a high-acuity ICU population and included a broader stroke cohort17. Furthermore, clinical trials have generally shown that the effect of acetaminophen on temperature reduction in stroke patients is modest at best17,18. The routine use of acetaminophen in this population thus remains debated, with major clinical guidelines offering varied recommendations19.

Given the challenges of conducting large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for ubiquitous and low-cost interventions, robust observational studies are needed20. The target trial emulation (TTE) framework provides a structured approach to designing and analyzing observational data to minimize common biases and strengthen causal inference21. By explicitly specifying the protocol of a hypothetical pragmatic RCT and emulating it with real-world data, we can more reliably estimate an intervention’s effects22. This study applies the TTE framework to an extensive, high-resolution critical care database to estimate the causal effect of early acetaminophen administration on mortality in ICU patients with AIS.

Methods

Study design and data source

To approximate a pragmatic randomized trial, we designed and emulated a target trial protocol and applied it to retrospectively collected data. Our data were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV, version 2.2), a comprehensive, high-granularity database of deidentified electronic health records from patients admitted to the intensive care units (ICUs) at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, MA, between 2008 and 201923. External validation was also performed using the eICU Collaborative Research Database24. The design and reporting of this study rigorously adhere to the principles outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement and were structured to align with the Transparent Reporting of a Target Trial Emulation (TARGET) checklist22. One author has passed the CITI program exam and has been granted authorization to use the database for research purposes. Individual patient permission (from MIMIC and eICU) is not needed; access to the database is awarded upon completion of the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) assessment.

Target trial protocol and emulation

To mitigate biases common to observational research, such as immortal time bias and confounding by indication, we explicitly defined and emulated the protocol of a hypothetical pragmatic randomized trial using the Clone-Censor-Weight (CCW) framework. The key components were specified as follows:

Eligibility criteria: The study cohort included adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with a primary diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) at the time of their first documented ICU admission. To ensure a population of new users and minimize baseline heterogeneity, patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) received acetaminophen before ICU admission; (2) had a preexisting diagnosis of severe active liver disease or acute liver failure; or (3) did not resuscitate/not intubate (DNR/DNI) order was established within the first 24 h of their ICU stay. No minimum length of stay was required for inclusion to avoid selection bias and to ensure that all person-time postadmission was accounted for.

Treatment strategies and assignment: We defined two strategies within a 48-hour grace period following ICU admission (time zero). At time zero, each patient was assigned to one of the following groups: (1) the “treatment group” (or early acetaminophen use), which received treatment within 48 h of ICU admission; and (2) the “control group” (or not-early acetaminophen use), which avoided acetaminophen throughout the grace period. This design allows participants to contribute person-time to both strategies until their actual clinical trajectory deviates from the assigned protocol, effectively eliminating immortal time bias.

Outcomes and Follow-up: The primary endpoints were all-cause mortality at 30 and 90 days after the index ICU admission. A key secondary endpoint was in-hospital mortality. Patient follow-up commenced at time zero and continued until the first occurrence of one of the following events: death, hospital discharge, or 90-day administrative censoring.

Statistical analysis

Primary analysis in the MIMIC-IV cohort

All patient characteristics used for confounding adjustment were extracted from the initial 24 h of ICU admission. To handle missing data, we adopted a principled approach: variables with more than 30% missing data were omitted from modeling. In comparison, those with less than 30% confidence were multiply imputed using chained equations (MICE), with the assumption that missing data were missing at random. We generated 10 imputed datasets and pooled the results using Rubin’s rules.

The primary analysis estimated the per-protocol effect using the CCW framework. Clones were artificially censored when their real-world treatment deviated from the assigned strategy during the 48-hour grace period. To mitigate the potential selection bias and informative censoring introduced by this process, we applied inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW). Propensity scores (PSs) for the remaining uncensored patients were estimated via multivariable logistic regression. The selection of covariates for the PS model was strictly informed by a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to identify potential confounders and avoid bias from adjusting for mediators or colliders (Supplementary Fig. 4). The final model incorporated demographics (age, sex, race), clinical severity scores (SOFA, SAPS II, GCS, Charlson Comorbidity Index), physiological or laboratory parameters (heart rate, SBP, creatinine, glucose), and WBC max, use of antibiotics, sedatives, and mechanical ventilation for 48 h during the ICU stay. Stabilized weights were calculated and truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

Covariate balance was assessed via an SMD < 0.10. To evaluate the robustness of our primary CCW findings, we conducted three sensitivity analyses: (1) inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW); (2) 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) with a caliper of 0.2; and (3) a complete-case CCW analysis (CC-CCW), restricted to patients with complete records for all propensity score covariates, treatment initiation timing, and primary outcomes.

Causal effects were estimated within the weighted pseudopopulation. For 30-day and 90-day mortality, absolute risks were derived from standardized Kaplan‒Meier curves, and relative effects were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) from weighted Cox proportional hazards models. For in-hospital mortality, absolute measures are presented as weighted proportions; relative effects are reported as risk ratios (RRs) to avoid informative censoring bias arising from hospital discharge. Absolute differences were noted as risk differences (RDs) for all mortality outcomes. Length of stay (LOS) was analyzed as a weighted mean, with effects reported as the meanmean differences (MD). Exploratory subgroup analyses with interaction tests were performed for 90-day mortality across age, sex, comorbidities, and severity scores (GCS/SOFA). E-values were calculated to quantify the potential impact of unmeasured confounding for the primary analysis25.

Validation analysis in the eICU

The eICU-CRD validation strictly mirrored the primary CCW/IPCW methodology with database-specific adaptations. The PS model was recalibrated to 13 covariates: systolic blood pressure was omitted (> 80% missing), and the baseline white blood cell count replaced the 48-hour maximum. GCS scores were retained despite 32.0% missingness for prognostic necessity. Missing therapeutic indicators were zero-imputed. Other quantitative data followed the independent 10-dataset MICE protocol to ensure methodological consistency.

All procedures were executed using R software, version 4.4.2 (https://www.r-project.org).

Results

Primary analysis in the MIMIC-IV cohort

Cohort characteristics

Among the 1,966 patients initially identified with acute ischemic stroke upon their first ICU admission, a final cohort of 1,779 patients met the full eligibility criteria for this target trial emulation (Fig. 1). Within this cohort, 1,150 individuals (64.6%) were assigned to the early acetaminophen strategy (the ACE group), while 629 (35.4%) were assigned to the control strategy (the control group) (Table 1). The distribution and patterns of missing data for baseline variables are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. To address this, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was performed, and the stability of clinical characteristics before and after imputation was verified to ensure data integrity (Supplementary Table 2).

Before statistical adjustment, significant baseline differences were observed between the two groups, as detailed in Table 1. The control group had a greater median age and a greater burden of comorbidities. Following the application of the primary clone-censor-weighting (CCW) framework, nearly all measured baseline covariates were well balanced, with absolute standardized mean differences (SMDs) falling below the 0.10 threshold. Diagnostic density plots of the estimated propensity scores demonstrated substantial common support. They achieved a high degree of distributional balance between the two strategies after weighting (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). A similar degree of balance was achieved in the PSM and IPTW analyses. (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 5)

Association with mortality outcomes

In the primary CCW-weighted analysis, early acetaminophen administration was associated with a statistically significant reduction in 90-day all-cause mortality (HR, 0.70; 95% CI 0.58–0.84; P < 0.001; E-value = 2.21) (Table 2; Fig. 2a). This corresponded to an absolute risk difference (RD) of -7.82% (95% CI -12.18% to -3.45%) (Table 2). Similarly, the 30-day mortality risk was significantly lower in the ACE group (HR, 0.66; 95% CI 0.54–0.81; E value = 2.40) (Table 2).

These findings were highly consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses (Table 2). Specifically, complete-case CCW analysis (CC-CCW) confirmed a significant survival benefit (HR for 90-day mortality, 0.82; 95% CI 0.68–0.99) (Table 2) (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The results for 90-day mortality from the IPTW (HR, 0.70; 95% CI 0.58–0.85) and 1:1 PSM (HR, 0.65; 95% CI 0.52–0.80) models further reinforced the primary conclusions (Table 2).

Competing risk analysis for in-hospital outcomes

When live discharge was treated as a competing event, the weighted absolute risk of in-hospital mortality in the MIMIC-IV cohort was 15.0% for the ACE group versus 21.7% for the control group, yielding a significant absolute risk reduction (risk difference, RD) of -6.70% (95% CI -10.56% to -2.84%). To mitigate the information-based censoring bias inherent in ICU discharge, the relative effect was estimated as a risk ratio (RR) of 0.69 (95% CI 0.56–0.85; P < 0.001; E value = 2.26) (Table 2). Cumulative incidence function (CIF) curves revealed that the ACE group had a consistently lower cumulative incidence of in-hospital death (Fig. 2c). Moreover, the ACE group tended to have a higher probability of live discharge (CS-HR, 1.08; 95% CI 0.97–1.21; P = 0.159) (Fig. 2d), indicating that the observed survival benefit did not lead to a prolonged hospital stay.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses for 90-day mortality revealed a directionally consistent protective effect of acetaminophen across most strata. A significant interaction was observed for diabetes status (P-interaction = 0.013), with more pronounced benefits in nondiabetic patients (HR, 0.60; 95% CI 0.48–0.74) than in those with diabetes (HR, 0.99; 95% CI 0.72–1.38) (Fig. 3a). No significant interactions were found for age, SOFA score, or GCS score (all P-interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 3a). Owing to the lack of multiplicity adjustment, these findings remain exploratory.

External validation in the eICU cohort

A total of 1,182 patients from the eICU-CRD database met the eligibility criteria (Fig. 1; Table 1). The baseline missingness and MICE diagnostic comparisons for this cohort are detailed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 3. Within this cohort, 433 patients (36.6%) were assigned to the ACE group, whereas 749 (63.4%) were not (control group) (Supplementary Table 3). Consistent with the primary analysis, the PS model for the eICU cohort was recalibrated on the basis of the same DAG-based strategy (Supplementary Figure S4) and achieved satisfactory balance (Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. 2c, d).

Unlike the primary cohort, early acetaminophen use was not associated with reduced mortality in the validation set (Table 2; Fig. 2). No significant difference was found between the results of the exploratory subgroup analyses and the complete-case sensitivity analysis (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

In this target trial emulation of critically ill patients with acute ischemic stroke, we reported that early administration of acetaminophen was associated with a significant reduction in both 30- and 90-day all-cause mortality. This protective association remained stable across two distinct, rigorous analytical approaches and was supported by competing risk models that accounted for live discharge.

External validation (eICU cohort) revealed significant heterogeneity in treatment effects. Unlike the primary cohort, early acetaminophen use was not associated with reduced mortality in the validation set. Crucially, our primary findings failed to replicate in this larger, geographically diverse cohort, a discrepancy that warrants caution in interpreting the treatment’s generalizability. These results should be interpreted with caution. While it may reflect context heterogeneity, it could also stem from lower measurement precision. Specifically, we could not capture dynamic 48-hour physiological trajectories in the eICU cohort as accurately as in the high-granularity MIMIC-IV database. This difference likely stems from differences in patient populations and clinical settings between tertiary academic and community hospitals. Variations in prescribing patterns (70.9% vs. 38%), stroke severity, and institutional protocols may explain these discrepancies. Rather than invalidating the primary findings, this heterogeneity highlights the context-dependent nature of treatment effects. This emphasizes the need to consider local factors when applying observational evidence to clinical practice.

The contrast between our findings and the neutral results of the landmark PAIS trial is notable and likely reflects critical distinctions in both the study population and the primary endpoint17. Whereas the PAIS trial evaluated functional outcomes in a heterogeneous cohort of both hemorrhagic and less acute ischemic stroke patients17, our investigation was confined to a homogenous, high-acuity cohort of critically ill ischemic stroke patients, where all-cause mortality served as the primary outcome. Although we could not formally model the fever burden because of the high rate of missing temperature records, our results suggest that the role of acetaminophen might extend beyond simple pyrexia management. One hypothesis is that acetaminophen modulates peroxidase-mediated oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory pathways, which are often elevated in critically ill patients15,16,26–28. However, these nonantipyretic mechanisms remain theoretical in the context of this study and warrant confirmation in trials that include granular physiological monitoring.

An exploratory finding of our study was potential heterogeneity in treatment effects across diabetes statuses. The protective effect of acetaminophen was most pronounced among patients without diabetes. This observation is biologically plausible. Diabetes and associated hyperglycemia are known to amplify oxidative stress after stroke7,29. We postulate that the baseline oxidative and inflammatory burden in diabetic patients may be too profound for the modest antioxidant effects of acetaminophen to overcome, thereby negating its potential benefit. These findings suggest that the underlying metabolic state could be a crucial determinant of the efficacy of acetaminophen and highlight a possible direction for personalized therapeutic strategies in neurocritical care. Furthermore, the therapeutic effect of acetaminophen appears to be context dependent when it is viewed in the broader landscape of critical illness. For instance, major trials of acetaminophen in septic shock, such as the ASTER trial and PRECIOUS trial, failed to demonstrate a mortality benefit in the general sepsis population18,30.

While our findings are consistent with those of Piao et al.31, our study design used a target trial emulation (TTE) approach to address competing events. The results of the competing-risk analysis indicate that the survival benefit is not an artifact of informative censoring due to live discharge. To confirm this causal relationship, our team has initiated a prospective, randomized controlled trial to validate the effect of acetaminophen on neurological outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke receiving endovascular therapy (NCT06767969).

Nevertheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged. As an observational study, it remains susceptible to residual confounding from unmeasured variables. However, the E value suggests robustness against moderate unmeasured confounders25. Crucially, we lacked data on specific dosages, administration routes, and clinical indications. Furthermore, we were unable to formally analyze the temperature response or fever burden because temperature records were missing for more than 30% of the study population within the 48-hour window. The inclusion of these sparse data would have introduced significant selection bias. Consequently, the relative contribution of antipyretic versus nonantipyretic pathways cannot be definitively elucidated.

From a clinical perspective, these hypothesis-generating findings suggest a potential role for acetaminophen as a low-cost protective agent rather than solely an antipyretic. However, these results should not guide definitive changes in practice at this time. Instead, they support a shift toward precision medicine in neurocritical care, in which future trials might stratify patients on the basis of metabolic phenotypes or inflammatory signatures.

Conclusions

Early acetaminophen treatment may improve survival in critically ill AIS patients, although the findings vary across cohorts, potentially reflecting differences in data granularity. Treatment effects demonstrated significant heterogeneity across clinical settings and patient subgroups, particularly by diabetes status. These hypothesis-generating findings warrant multicenter randomized trials to establish causality and identify optimal candidates definitively.
