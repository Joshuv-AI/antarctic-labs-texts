A linear programming framework for optimizing molecular combinations of asphalt four components
Authors: Jie Zhu, Chuyuan Wen, Sijie Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo enhance the efficiency and accuracy of asphalt molecular modeling, this investigation proposes a four-component molecular combination optimization method for asphalt based on linear programming. This method automatically selects the optimal representative molecular combination while satisfying multiple constraints, such as component ratios, element mass fractions, and hydrogen-to-carbon atomic ratio. Additionally, this investigation has developed a supporting graphical modeling system based on the PuLP library and Streamlit framework, featuring visual operations, flexible parameter configuration, and result export. Case studies demonstrate that the model output is highly consistent with experimental values, with all index errors within ± 2%. The system demonstrates well-fitting ability, solution stability, and engineering practicality, providing an efficient and intelligent solution for asphalt molecular simulation and formulation optimization.Keywords: Asphalt molecular model, Linear programming, Multi-objective optimization, Molecular screening, Automatic modeling systemSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Asphalt is a complex multi-component material widely used in road engineering, waterproofing and other applications1,2. Based on its compositional characteristics, asphalt is typically divided into four main components: asphaltene, resin, aromatic, and saturate3,4. The proportions of these components significantly affect key characteristics of asphalt, such as its cohesiveness, ductility, thermal stability, and aging resistance.

In recent years, with the increasingly complex road service conditions, the traditional experience-based component blending methods have been unable to meet the demands of refined performance design. Consequently, researchers have gradually turned to molecular dynamics (MD) simulation technology. By simulating the microstructure and interaction mechanisms of asphalt molecules, this technology reveals the microscopic origins of their macroscopic properties, thereby providing theoretical support for material design5,6. However, to conduct instructive MD simulations, it is essential to construct a representative molecular model that is consistent with experimental data. This process requires the reasonable selection of the types and quantities of molecules, ensuring that the simulation model closely matches experimental parameters in terms of element composition, hydrogen-to-carbon atomic ratio (H/C), and the proportions of four components.

Currently, there are primarily two methods for constructing asphalt molecular models: (1) Manual selection: Researchers typically select and combine molecules from a molecular library based on experience, adjusting the total mass and ratio to approximate experimental values7,8. This method is inefficient and has poor reproducibility. Once experimental conditions change, it requires re-selection, greatly affecting modeling efficiency and reliability. (2) Numerical solution using mathematical software: Optimization tools such as Linear Interactive and General Optimizer (Lingo) can solve for optimal combinations by setting a large number of linear or nonlinear equations9,10. However, this method demands high mathematical modeling and programming skills from users, lacks visual operations, and has a high threshold for use, limiting its widespread adoption. To date, some literature in various fields has attempted to use optimization algorithms for model construction. Li et al.11 developed an automatic generation program for constructing hydrated calcium silicate-aluminate structures, which can efficiently construct atomic structure models of hydrated calcium silicate-aluminate with different calcium-to-silicon and aluminum-to-silicon ratios. You et al.12 proposed a high-throughput molecular simulation framework for asphalt combined with open-source software tools, which can simultaneously create multiple asphalt molecular models with different properties. Although some optimization algorithms are currently used to assist in the construction of asphalt molecular models, due to the complexity of the models, its extension to additional molecular types and complex constraints is challenging. Consequently, an urgent need exists for an automated, visual, and low-threshold intelligent method to help researchers quickly select appropriate molecular types and quantities based on set parameters such as component ratios, H/C ratios, and element compositions, to achieve efficient and accurate model construction.

In fields such as crude oil or asphalt, Linear Programming (LP) is widely applied to problems like component optimization and resource allocation. Kwame et al.13 proposed an LP model to minimize the aggregate cost of hot-mix asphalt while satisfying constraints such as gradation and volumetric properties. The results showed that LP can effectively reduce costs and optimize inventory management, making it suitable for the rapid design of on-site modified asphalt mixtures. Gomes et al.14 employed LP methods to address the maintenance and management of asphalt pavements. The core objective was to maximize the overall condition of the entire road network within the planning period, while ensuring that each road section maintains a predetermined level of condition, and also subject to the corresponding annual budget constraints. Kayode Coker et al.15 elucidated the application of LP in crude oil fractionation and product blending (such as gasoline octane number optimization), and demonstrated through case studies the flexibility of LP in multi-objective optimization (cost minimization, environmental compliance). The aforementioned studies indicate that the LP method possesses strong practicality and flexibility when facing complex constraints and multi-objective optimization problems. Nevertheless, the application of LP in asphalt model construction remains relatively limited. The main challenges include how to convert complex parameters such as molecular composition, component ratios, and element mass fractions into linear constraints, and how to balance target accuracy and operability while ensuring optimization efficiency.

Despite the increasing use of MD simulations in asphalt research, a critical gap remains at the molecular model construction stage. Most existing studies emphasize the analysis of binder properties based on predefined molecular models, while the systematic selection and quantitative combination of representative molecules are often conducted in an experience driven method. As a result, different molecular assemblies that satisfy similar macroscopic composition requirements may exhibit substantially different microscopic structures, leading to uncertainty in simulation-based performance analyses. This lack of a unified and quantitative molecular combination framework limits the comparability of asphalt molecular simulations. To address this gap, this investigation proposes an optimization-based framework for the quantitative construction of asphalt molecular models. By simultaneously constraining component ratios, elemental mass fractions, and H/C ratios, the proposed method generates molecular assemblies that are highly consistent with experiment data. Although the framework does not directly predict macroscopic binder performance, it establishes a more reliable and controllable microscopic foundation for subsequent MD simulations. Consequently, the proposed approach enhances the credibility of performance investigations by reducing uncertainties in the molecular model construction stage.

Method principle and model design

The molecular combination optimization method proposed in this investigation aims to automatically screen and combine a set of representative molecules from a molecular library based on preset target ratios and element parameters, so that the overall properties can approach the experimental target as closely as possible. This problem is essentially a combinatorial optimization problem with constraints, which can be formalized as a linear programming model.

Modeling objective of the problem

Given a set of candidate molecules , select several different types and quantities of molecules from this set such that the resulting mixed system meets the following requirements: the proportions of the four components are within the specified range; the total molecular weight falls within a given interval; the total number of molecules complies with the interval limit; the element mass fractions (C, H, O, N, S) comply with the requirements; and the H/C ratio of the overall system or its components meets the target value. The optimization objective is to minimize the sum of weighted absolute deviations between the simulated values and the target values.

To balance physical rationality and experimental data consistency when constructing a microscopic model of asphalt molecules, this investigation established an optimization model for molecular component selection based on linear programming. The core objective of the model is to determine the selection quantity of each molecule from a given candidate molecule library, so that the constructed set can be as close as possible to the experimental values of the target asphalt sample in multiple indicators such as the proportion of four components, H/C ratio, and element mass fraction.

It should be noted that although several target properties of asphalt, such as component ratios, element mass fractions, and the H/C ratio, are typically expressed in nonlinear fractional forms, the optimization model constructed in this study does not directly use these nonlinear expressions. To ensure that the overall formulation remains linear, all ratio-type requirements are reformulated through deviation variables. In practice, instead of constraining a ratio itself, the model constrains the difference between two linear quantities, and the deviation between them is represented by a non-negative error variable. This approach avoids the appearance of fractional or nonlinear expressions in the constraints, and all relationships in the model become linear combinations of decision variables and deviation variables.

The theoretical basis of this transformation is that representing the deviation from a ratio-type requirement through linear inequalities is a standard and widely used linearization technique in mathematical programming16. Because the relevant physical quantities in this problem, such as molecular mass, and element mass, are inherently linear functions of the decision variables, expressing the target-matching requirement through deviation variables maintains the linear structure of the model without altering the feasible solution space in a physically meaningful way. This linearization strategy is also suitable for the asphalt molecular combination problem, whose objective is to obtain a representative set of molecules whose aggregate properties approximate the experimental targets within acceptable tolerance. Therefore, the use of deviation variables not only preserves the physical consistency of the problem but also enables the entire optimization model to be formulated as a Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) problem, allowing for stable and efficient solution by branch-and-bound-based solvers.

Solution method

The optimization problem is a linear programming problem with integer variables and multi-objective error terms, belonging to the MILP model. To efficiently select a set of molecules that meet the constraints from a candidate molecule library, the aforementioned MILP model needs to be transformed into a standard form that can be solved by an optimizer, and parameters need to be configured to improve the efficiency and stability of the solution.

This investigation employs the Coin-or Branch and Cut (CBC) solver to solve the MILP model. CBC is an open-source linear programming solver that supports advanced features such as integer variables, branch and bound strategies, and flexible constraint objective functions. It is suitable for the complex constraint structure and combinatorial optimization properties of this model. The solver is invoked through the PuLP interface in Python. Considering the measurement errors present in actual experimental data and the variability of asphalt component characteristics, the model adopts a “flexible constraint” strategy in constraint design, which involves introducing error variables to replace strict hard constraints, thereby enhancing the feasibility and stability of the model’s solution. The specific approach involves not setting target values such as component mass fraction, H/C ratio, and element mass fraction as equal constraints that must be met; instead, absolute error variables (such as emass,g, eHC, eelem,e, etc.) are introduced, and relaxation constraints in the form of upper and lower bounds are established. All error variables are incorporated into the objective function, and the solution is guided towards the target by minimizing the sum of errors.

System design and implementation

To facilitate the engineering application of asphalt molecular model construction, this investigation has developed an auxiliary modeling system equipped with a graphical user interface. The system is built on the Python programming language, utilizing the Streamlit framework for front-end display and interactive logic, and incorporating the PuLP library for the construction and solving of optimization models. The overall design of the system emphasizes user-friendliness, parameter flexibility, and visualization of computational results, making it convenient for researchers and engineers to quickly apply it in asphalt molecular modeling tasks.

System architecture

The system comprises five core functional modules (as shown in Fig. 1): data loading and parsing module, molecule screening module, parameter setting module, calculation and optimization module, and result display and export module.

Core functional modules of the system.

The system architecture is designed to both facilitate user interaction and enable construction, solution, and verification of the MILP optimization model. Beyond basic interface functionalities, each module integrates dedicated algorithmic logic. The data loading and parsing module conducts automated structural validation of the uploaded molecular library, ensuring completeness of all required fields (e.g., molecular weight, elemental composition, component classification) to enable unambiguous formulation of the optimization model. The molecule screening module implements logical preprocessing for the subsequent optimization model by differentiating between mandatory molecules and optional candidates, and this distinction is directly incorporated into the decision-variable definition during MILP model construction. The parameter setting module acts as the interface connecting users with the optimization engine. Rather than simply recording inputs, it dynamically updates internal symbolic expressions for objective terms, deviation variables, and constraint structures in response to user-defined targets. This real-time reconstruction ensures the solver consistently receives a complete, well-defined MILP model.

During the calculation phase, the system communicates with the CBC solver via a structured model—solver protocol. Once all decision variables, linear constraints, and objective components are assembled, the system converts them into a format readable by the solver and monitors the branch-and-cut solution process. If infeasibility is detected (e.g., due to overly strict component/elemental constraints), the system activates a diagnostic module to identify potential constraint conflicts and offer users adjustment recommendations. This mechanism enhances the system’s robustness and ensures the optimization model’s applicability to a wide range of asphalt compositions. The result display and export module not only visualizes molecular selection results but also assesses solver performance by reporting convergence status, deviation distribution, and constraint satisfaction levels.

From the system level perspective, the data flow across modules follows a sequential and closed loop structure. The molecular library and target compositional data are first processed by the data loading and parsing module to ensure structural completeness and consistency. The molecule screening module then classifies candidate molecules and defines the corresponding decision variable domains. Based on the defined targets, the parameter setting module constructs the optimization model by assembling decision variables, constraints, and objective terms. The calculation and optimization module interacts with the MILP solver to obtain feasible solutions, while solver feedback is further analyzed to detect potential infeasibility. Finally, the result display and export module organizes the optimized molecular combinations and diagnostic information for subsequent analysis. This modular data flow ensures both methodological transparency and computational robustness.

User interface design

The interface is clean and intuitive, utilizing a folding panel structure to facilitate step-by-step configuration and quick positioning for users, as shown in Fig. 2.

User interface of the system.

The entire system possesses the following primary characteristics: (1) Modular structure: Each functional area is reasonably distributed, with clear operational logic, reducing the cognitive burden on users; (2) Parameter synchronous response: Parameters take effect immediately after being set, ensuring consistency between the solving logic and input; (3) Progress feedback mechanism: Display real-time progress bars and solving status during the solving process, enhancing operability; (4) Result visualization: Display through charts and tables, enabling users to intuitively understand the optimization results; (5) Cross-platform deployment: Built on the Streamlit framework, supporting local operation and remote Web publishing, with flexible deployment. Through this system, users can complete the configuration and optimization solving of complex models without directly writing code, significantly lowering the barrier to algorithm adoption and enhancing research efficiency and practical operability.

Case analysis

To verify the practicality and effectiveness of the proposed optimization model and system platform, this investigation selected experimental data from a typical asphalt as the simulation target. This investigation utilized a constructed candidate molecular database, and by setting reasonable component constraints and physical property indicators, automatic optimization generation of simulated molecular combinations was executed in the system platform. The differences between the simulation results and the target values were then compared.

It should be clarified that the accuracy of the proposed optimization method in this study is defined in terms of compositional consistency and modeling reliability, rather than the prediction accuracy of macroscopic performance. The aim of the optimization framework is to construct molecular assemblies whose aggregated properties closely match experimental compositional indicators, including component proportions, elemental mass fractions, and H/C ratios. These indicators represent necessary constraints for a physically reasonable asphalt molecular model, although they may not uniquely determine a single molecular arrangement. Indeed, different molecular combinations may satisfy similar compositional indicators, which reflects the intrinsic complexity of asphalt binders rather than a limitation of the proposed method. In this context, the rationality of the optimization framework lies in its ability to systematically and reproducibly identify feasible molecular assemblies within a constrained solution space, while minimizing deviations from experimental targets. The proposed method is not intended to establish a one-to-one correspondence between molecular structure and performance results. Instead, it provides a controllable and quantitative basis, enabling exploration of the relationship between this structure and performance in further studies.

In addition to the simulation results presented above, it is also meaningful to compare the proposed framework with commonly adopted molecular model construction approaches from a practical perspective. In traditional manual construction, molecular combinations are typically adjusted through repeated trial-and-error processes to satisfy compositional requirements. In practice, modifying a single target parameter often requires reconstructing the entire molecular assembly, which makes the process time consuming. Moreover, the lack of a unified quantitative criterion reduces reproducibility. Compared with existing algorithm-based approaches, the proposed method does not aim to achieve a significant advantage in computational speed, as most optimization-based strategies exhibit similar solver level efficiency. Instead, the primary advantage of the proposed framework lies in its usability and operational transparency. By integrating model construction, constraint configuration, solver interaction, and result visualization into a unified system, the proposed approach significantly lowers the technical barrier for practical application. This feature makes the framework particularly suitable for engineering-oriented studies, where rapid model adjustment are required. Overall, the proposed method improves modeling efficiency relative to manual approaches while offering enhanced accessibility compared with existing algorithmic solutions.

Sensitivity tests

To further verify the robustness of the proposed model and solving process, sensitivity tests were designed by perturbing key target parameters. Each test was repeated 10 times with different initial conditions (e.g., random selection of mandatory molecules, slight adjustments to constraint bounds), and indicators such as feasibility rate and maximum error of key parameters were recorded to quantify the model’s adaptability.

The target parameter perturbation was conducted based on the original simulation parameters described in Sect. “Simulation parameter settings”. Specifically, three types of adjustments were applied. The component proportion was perturbed by ± 5% for each component. For example, the asphaltene proportion was adjusted from 16.20% to either 11.2% or 21.2%, while the resin proportion was adjusted from 37.30% to 32.30% or 42.30%. The overall H/C ratio target was varied by ± 0.1, changing from 1.49 to either 1.39 or 1.59. The elemental mass fractions were perturbed by ± 1% for each element. As an example, the target mass fraction of carbon was adjusted from 83.90% to 82.9% or 84.9%. The quantitative results of the sensitivity tests are shown in Table 3.

It should be noted that the aim of this study is to establish an efficient and systematic framework for the quantitative construction of asphalt molecular models, rather than to directly investigate the relationship between molecular counterparts and macroscopic or simulated performance indicators. Although molecular structural features are commonly regarded as the microscopic basis of asphalt binder behavior, a definitive correlation between specific molecular assemblies and performance simulation results requires multiscale analysis and extensive parametric studies. Such correlation analysis is beyond the scope of the present work and will be addressed in future studies. Based on the molecular models generated by the proposed framework, subsequent research will focus on linking molecular composition and structure with performance related simulation outcomes, such as rheological behavior, aging characteristics, and transport properties. The proposed method serves as a foundational step that enables more reliable and reproducible investigations into molecular-performance relationships.
