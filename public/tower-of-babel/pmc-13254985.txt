Enhancing the regenerative potential of MSC-derived exosomes via 3D microenvironment modulation for cartilage repair
Authors: Hao Wang, Jie Wu, Guangzhao Tian, Zhijie Zhao, Mingxue Chen, Quanyi Guo, Yayi Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Articular cartilage defects remain a major clinical challenge due to their poor self-repair capacity. Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived exosomes have emerged as promising cell-free therapeutics; however, conventional two-dimensional (2D) cultures yield exosomes with limited bioactivity. Here, we engineered hierarchical macro–microporous scaffolds using gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel and cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM) to establish biomimetic three-dimensional (3D) microenvironments for MSC culture. This approach yielded three distinct exosome types—2D-Exo, GelMA-derived exosomes (G-Exo), and ECM-derived exosomes (E-Exo). Compared with 2D-Exo, 3D-derived exosomes significantly enhanced MSC proliferation, migration, chondrogenic differentiation, immunomodulation, and chondrocyte protection under inflammatory conditions, with E-Exo exhibiting the most potent effects. In vivo, E-Exo combined with a decellularized cartilage ECM (DCM) scaffold promoted robust hyaline cartilage regeneration in a rat model. Mechanistically, we identify a key pathway by which E-Exo drives chondrogenesis: they are enriched in miR-503-5p, which suppresses Smad7 to enhance TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling. These findings highlight ECM-based 3D culture as an effective strategy to optimize exosome bioactivity and provide a clinically translatable approach for cell-free cartilage regeneration.

Introduction

Articular cartilage has a limited intrinsic repair capacity owing to its avascular and aneural nature, making cartilage defects a persistent clinical challenge [1]. Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-based therapies have been widely explored for tissue regeneration, but their clinical translation remains hindered by immune rejection, potential tumorigenicity, risk of pathogen transmission, and logistical difficulties in cell storage and delivery [2]. Increasing evidence suggests that MSCs exert their therapeutic effects largely through paracrine mechanisms, particularly by secreting exosomes that transfer bioactive molecules (e.g., miRNAs, proteins, lncRNAs) to recipient cells and thereby regulate inflammation, survival, and differentiation [3]. Compared with direct MSC transplantation, exosome-based “cell-free” approaches circumvent many of the limitations of cell therapy and are emerging as a promising strategy for cartilage tissue engineering.

Despite their therapeutic promise, the clinical translation of exosomes is hampered by insufficient bioactivity, intrinsic heterogeneity, and difficulties in large-scale production [[4], [5], [6]]. To address these limitations, diverse strategies have been investigated. Notably, emerging strategies such as gene-activated matrices and bio-active therapies—including the use of therapeutic stem cell-derived exosomes—have demonstrated significant potential in modulating the local microenvironment for enhanced tissue repair [7,8]. In parallel, strategies for exosome optimization such as chemical induction [9], hypoxic preconditioning [10], and physical stimulation (e.g., mechanical loading or electromagnetic fields) [6] have been widely explored. Although these approaches can enhance exosome function, they also present notable drawbacks: chemical interventions raise safety and dose-dependence concerns, while physical methods, despite being relatively safe, often require complex instrumentation and high costs, thereby restricting scalability and translational feasibility [11]. These challenges underscore the need for alternative approaches that can more effectively and reliably enhance exosome bioactivity for regenerative applications.

Conventional exosomes are usually harvested from cells maintained in two-dimensional (2D) cultures, which poorly recapitulate the complex three-dimensional (3D) microenvironment encountered in vivo [12]. Within native tissues, cells are embedded in a 3D matrix, where continuous cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions are critical for maintaining their phenotype and secretory profile. As a result, exosomes derived from 2D cultures differ substantially in composition and function from those generated under physiologically relevant 3D conditions. Increasing evidence suggests that 3D culture can restore a more authentic cellular state and thereby improve the biological activity and therapeutic efficacy of MSC-derived exosomes. However, further optimization of the 3D microenvironment—particularly the design and composition of scaffolds that support cell growth—remains essential to fully realize the regenerative potential of exosomes.

In contrast to scaffold-free 3D systems such as hanging drop or spinner flask cultures [13,14], scaffold-based approaches better replicate the cell-matrix interactions that are fundamental in vivo [12]. Engineered scaffolds offer precise control over geometry, pore architecture, stiffness, ligand density, and degradability, thereby recreating the mechanical and adhesive environment of cartilage while supporting stable MSC phenotypes and efficient mass transfer. A hierarchical macro-microporous design is particularly advantageous: macropores enable oxygen and nutrient diffusion as well as waste removal, whereas micropores increase surface area and cell-matrix contact, reducing shear stress-induced alterations in the secretory profile [15]. Moreover, 3D printing provides high reproducibility and scalability, ensuring consistent quality and compatibility with good manufacturing practice (GMP) standards for translational use [16]. Collectively, scaffold-based 3D culture offers both biological relevance and manufacturing feasibility, making it a promising strategy to regulate MSC behavior and fine-tune the bioactive properties of derived exosomes.

Scaffold composition is a critical determinant of cell behavior and secretome profiles, and thus represents a key axis in regulating exosome bioactivity [17,18]. In cartilage tissue engineering, gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) hydrogel has been widely adopted owing to its biocompatibility, tunable mechanical properties, printability, degradability, and suitability for standardized manufacturing [19]. In contrast, cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM) scaffolds retain native structural and biochemical cues that can directly instruct chondrocyte-related functions and provide tissue-specific signals [15,19,20]. To capture both design paradigms, we selected GelMA as a chemically defined, broadly applicable hydrogel and ECM as a biologically complex, cartilage-specific material. Importantly, both scaffolds were fabricated using the same 3D printing parameters to ensure consistent macroscopic architecture. However, we acknowledge that at the microscopic level, due to their distinct intrinsic material properties, these scaffolds inherently differ in biophysical properties (e.g., pore structure). Therefore, our study aims to evaluate how these comprehensive microenvironmental cues—rather than biochemical composition alone—drive the functional programming of MSC-derived exosomes.

In this study, we established three culture microenvironments defined by spatial dimensionality and material composition: conventional planar flasks (2D), GelMA-based 3D scaffolds, and ECM-based 3D scaffolds. Bone marrow MSCs were cultured under each condition to generate exosomes derived from 2D cultures (2D-Exo) and from 3D scaffolds (3D-Exo), the latter including GelMA-derived exosomes (G-Exo) and ECM-derived exosomes (E-Exo). All exosome types were systematically compared for their effects on proliferation, migration, chondrogenic differentiation, immunomodulation, and chondrocyte protection. Differential miRNA profiling was performed to elucidate underlying mechanisms, with a particular focus on factors regulating chondrogenesis. To assess translational relevance, the most potent exosome subtype was combined with a decellularized cartilage ECM (DCM) scaffold for cartilage defect repair in rats. Through this design, our study moves beyond generic 3D culture strategies. We specifically aimed to clarify how cartilage ECM–derived biochemical cues, in synergy with 3D structural architecture, functionally ‘program’ exosomal cargo and generate superior regenerative potency compared with structurally similar but compositionally generic hydrogels (e.g., GelMA). Furthermore, we sought to identify the specific miRNA mechanism underlying this ECM-driven enhancement. Materials and methods

Isolation, culture and identification of BMSCs, chondrocytes and BMDMs

The isolation and culture of bone marrow MSCs (BMSCs), chondrocytes and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were carried out according to a previous study [21]. Detailed experimental procedures are available in Supplementary Section 1.

Synthesis and characterization of 3D GelMA and ECM scaffolds

Hierarchical macro-microporous GelMA and ECM scaffolds were fabricated as previously described [15]. Briefly, GelMA hydrogel (EFL GM 60) was prepared at 10% (w/v) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and porcine articular cartilage was processed to yield a 5% (w/v) ECM suspension. Each bioink was printed into scaffolds using a water-based low temperature deposition manufacturing (LDM) system. The physicochemical properties and morphology of the scaffolds were comprehensively characterized. Specifically, the biomechanical properties were evaluated via compressive mechanical testing. Subsequently, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to visualize surface topography, and ImageJ software was employed to analyze pore size distribution. Additionally, samples were characterized after cell seeding using confocal microscopy. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in Supplementary Section 2.

Isolation and characterization of BMSC-derived exosomes

BMSCs were cultured on culture flasks, GelMA scaffolds, or ECM scaffolds. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with 10% exosome-depleted FBS-supplemented medium and incubated for another 48 h. Conditioned media were collected, and exosomes were isolated via differential ultracentrifugation (Fig. 3A). Exosome morphology was assessed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Hitachi, Japan), surface markers (CD9, CD81, TSG101) and negative marker (Calnexin) by Western blot, and size distribution by NTA (NanoSight NS500). To assess secretion efficiency, exosome yield was normalized to the number of producing cells (particles/106 cells). Furthermore, sample purity was evaluated by calculating the particle-to-protein ratio, for which total protein content was quantified using a BCA assay. Detailed procedures are in Supplementary Section 3.

Labeling and uptake of exosomes

BMSCs, chondrocytes, and BMDMs were seeded at 2 × 104 cells per well on cell slides in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. Exosomes were labeled using an exosome membrane labeling kit (Dojindo Biotech Co., Ltd.) following the manufacturer's instructions. The labeled exosomes were then resuspended in an exosome-free complete medium to a concentration of 1.5 × 108 particles/mL. Subsequently, 300 μL of the medium containing labeled exosomes was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 12 h. Following three washes with PBS, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and subsequently stained with FITC-labeled phalloidin (Solarbio, China) to visualize the cytoskeleton and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (10 μg/mL, Solarbio) to label nuclei. The stained cells were observed under a confocal laser microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Effects of different exosomes on the proliferation and migration of chondrocytes and BMSCs in vitro

CCK-8 assay, Edu staining, and cell cycle analysis

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay, Edu staining, and cell cycle analysis. For the CCK-8 assay, BMSCs (3 × 103 cells) were seeded in 96-well plates and then incubated in exosome-free serum medium containing different exosomes (2D-Exo, G-Exo, and E-Exo) (1.0 × 109 particles/mL). Cells cultured without exosomes were used as the control group. On days 1, 3, and 5, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using the CCK-8 assay kit (Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

DAPI staining was used to quantify cell nuclei. BMSCs (1 × 104/well) were seeded in 24-well plates containing cell slides and incubated with different exosomes. On days 1, 3, and 5, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a microscope (Nikon, Japan) and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

For EdU staining (Abbkine, USA), BMSCs (2 × 104/well) on cell slides were treated with exosomes for 24 h, followed by incubation with 10 μM EdU at 37 °C for 2–4 h. Cells were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 15 min), permeabilized (0.5% Triton X-100, 30 min), and stained with a Click-iT™ reaction. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired by fluorescence microscopy and analyzed using ImageJ.

For cell cycle analysis, exosome-treated cells were fixed in 70% ethanol, stained with propidium iodide (PI), and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCelesta, USA). Cell cycle distribution (G1, S, G2) was calculated using ModFit LT software (Verity Software House, Topsham, ME).

Scratch and Transwell assays

Scratch and Transwell assays were performed to assess cell migration. In the scratch assay, BMSCs (2 × 105/well) were seeded in 6-well plates. At 80–90% confluency, a scratch was made with a 200 μL sterile pipette tip. After washing with PBS, cells were cultured in medium containing 2% FBS with or without exosomes. Images were acquired at 0 and 24 h, and wound closure was analyzed with ImageJ.

For Transwell assays, 2 × 104 BMSCs in serum-free α-MEM were added to the upper chamber (Corning, USA), with 600 μL α-MEM + 2% FBS and PBS or exosomes in the lower chamber. After 24 h, cells on the upper side were removed. Migrated cells were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde) and stained with 1% crystal violet (Solarbio). Images were captured by light microscopy, and migration was quantified with ImageJ.

Effects of different exosomes on macrophage polarization in vitro

Immunofluorescence staining

To examine exosome-induced macrophage polarization under inflammatory conditions, BMDMs (1 × 106 cells/mL) were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured in macrophage medium containing 100 ng/mL LPS for 24 h, followed by treatment with PBS (control), 2D-Exo, G-Exo, or E-Exo (1.0 × 109 particles/mL) for another 24 h. Macrophage polarization was assessed by immunofluorescence staining using CD206 (1:400, Cell Signaling, USA) and Arg-1 (1:100, Cell Signaling, USA) for M2 markers, and CD86 (1:100, Novus, USA) and iNOS (1:200, Abcam, UK) for M1 markers. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000, Abcam, UK) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope and subsequently quantified using ImageJ.

Flow cytometry assay

BMDMs (2 × 106 cells/mL) were seeded in 6-well plates and treated as above. After fixation in 4% formaldehyde and permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100, cells were washed with 1× elution buffer (BioLegend, USA), stained with APC-CD163 or FITC-CD86 for 30 min, then analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCelesta). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, USA).

RT-qPCR

BMDMs (2 × 106 cells/mL) were treated as above. RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, China) and reverse-transcribed with the HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme). Inflammation-related gene expression was analyzed by qPCR with 2×RealStar Fast SYBR qPCR Mix (GenStar, China). Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Effect of different exosomes on the protection of chondrocytes under inflammatory conditions

TUNEL staining and flow cytometry assay

Chondrocyte apoptosis was assessed using TUNEL staining and flow cytometry. Chondrocytes (2 × 104) were seeded in a 24-well plate and treated with PBS or different exosomes (1.0 × 109 particles/mL) for 24 h followed by IL-1β (10 ng/mL; PeproTech, USA) stimulation for another 24 h. TUNEL staining was performed using a commercial kit (MedChemExpress LLC, USA), and images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

For flow cytometry, chondrocytes (1 × 105) were seeded into 6-well plates and similarly treated. Cells were collected, stained with Annexin V and 7-AAD (BD, USA), and analyzed using a flow cytometer. Data were processed using FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Chondrocytes (2 × 104) were treated with exosomes and IL-1β as above. After 24 h, cells were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 20 min), permeabilized (0.5% Triton X-100, 30 min), and blocked (10% goat serum, 30 min). Immunofluorescence staining was performed using primary antibodies against type II collagen (COL2, 1:200; DSHB, USA), aggrecan (ACAN, 1:500; Thermo Fisher, USA), MMP13 (1:500; Abcam, UK), and ADAMTS4 (1:500; Proteintech, China). Cytoskeleton and nuclei were stained with FITC-phalloidin and DAPI, respectively. Images were captured and quantified using ImageJ.

RT-qPCR and ELISA

Chondrocytes (1 × 105) in 6-well plates were treated as above. Total RNA was extracted, and expression of IL-6, TNF-α, ADAMTS4, and COL2 was measured by RT-qPCR (see Section 2.6). Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. Culture supernatants were collected post-treatment. Levels of TNF-α, PGE2, and IL-6 were measured by ELISA (Meimian, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Effects of different exosomes regulate the joint cavity inflammation

Animal grouping and surgical procedures followed previously described methods [22]. Briefly, twelve male Sprague–Dawley rats (250 ± 50 g) were randomly divided into four groups: PBS, 2D-Exo, G-Exo, and E-Exo. Under anesthesia, a medial parapatellar incision was made to expose the right femoral trochlea, where a 2 mm-diameter, ∼1.5 mm-deep cartilage defect was created using a corneal ring drill until mild bleeding appeared. Postoperatively, rats received intra-articular injections of 50 μL PBS (control) or exosome solution (1 × 108 particles/mL) once weekly. On day 14, animals were euthanized, and synovial tissues were harvested, frozen, and sectioned (7 μm thickness). To assess joint cavity inflammation, sections were stained for IL-10 (1:200; Cell Signaling, USA), IL-1β (1:50; Abcam, UK), TNF-α (1:500; Abcam, UK), CD68 (1:50; Abcam, UK), CD206 (1:400; Cell Signaling, USA), and CD86 (1:100; Novus, USA). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. Inflammatory gene expression in synovial tissue was analyzed by RT–qPCR, following the protocol described in Section 2.6.

Preparation and characterization of DCM scaffolds

The DCM scaffolds (2 mm in diameter, 1.5 mm in thickness) were fabricated as previously described [23]. Experimental protocols are described in Supplementary Section 4.

Effects of different exosomes on the recruitment of endogenous MSCs in vivo

The experimental method is the same as in Section 2.8. It is noteworthy that all rats received implantation of DCM scaffolds. Two weeks later, frozen sections of the regenerated tissue were prepared and subsequently subjected to immunofluorescence staining using antibodies against the MSC surface markers CD73 (1:100; Novus, USA) and CD105 (1:100; Abcam, UK). Quantitative image analysis was performed using ImageJ.

Effects of different exosomes on promoting BMSC chondrogenic differentiation in vitro

To evaluate the chondrogenic potential of different exosomes, both short- and long-term in vitro assays were performed using BMSCs. To assess the short-term effects, BMSCs (2 × 104) were seeded in 24-well plates. At ∼80% confluency, the medium was replaced with chondrogenic induction medium (Cyagen, RAXMX-90041, China) containing PBS (control) or different exosomes (1 × 109 particles/mL). The medium was changed every other day. After 7 days, immunofluorescence staining for COL2 (1:200; DSHB, USA), ACAN (1:500; Thermo Fisher, USA), and SOX9 (1:300, Abcam, UK) was performed as described in Section 2.6.

BMSC pellets were used to evaluate the long-term effects of different exosomes on chondrogenic differentiation. 3 × 105 BMSCs were pelleted by sequential centrifugation (250 × g, then 150 × g, 5 min each), and cultured in chondrogenic induction medium with PBS or exosomes. After 21 days, pellets were frozen sectioned for H&E, Alcian blue, Safranin O, and COL2 immunohistochemical staining. Chondrogenic-related gene expression of pellets was assessed by qRT–PCR. The experimental procedure according to Section 2.6. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

MicroRNA sequencing analysis

MicroRNA (miRNA) sequencing of G-Exo and E-Exo was conducted by Majorbio (Beijing, China). Total RNA was extracted using the QIAzol Lysis Reagent (Qiagen, Germany). RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). For library construction, 1 μg of total RNA per sample was used with the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer's protocol. Differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs) were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of DEMs were performed using the clusterProfiler R package.

Target gene prediction and validation

Putative target genes of miR-503-5p were predicted using miRTargetLink 2.0, DIANA-microT-CDS, miRDB, miRWalk, and RNAInter. To validate Smad7 as a direct target of miR-503-5p, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed. Briefly, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with luciferase reporter vectors and either miR-503-5p mimics or negative control. After 24 h, luciferase activity was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Beyotime, China), and results were expressed as the normalized Firefly/Renilla ratio.

Transfection of miRNA mimics or inhibitors

BMSCs were transfected with rno-miR-503-5p mimics, inhibitors, or negative controls (GenePharma, China) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Life Technologies, USA), following the manufacturer's protocol. Transfection efficiency was assessed 24 h post-transfection by fluorescence microscopy and qRT–PCR analysis of extracted RNA. BMSCs were immediately cultured in chondrogenic induction medium (Cyagen, China) upon initiation of transfection with miRNA, following the manufacturer's protocol. Medium was changed every two days. The transfection procedure was repeated twice at three-day intervals. Additionally, BMSCs were cultured in chondrogenic induction medium supplemented with either PBS or exosomes (1.0 × 109 particles/mL). Cells were collected on day 7 for Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses. On day 21, cell spheroid samples were harvested for immunofluorescence staining. Detailed procedures are provided in Supplementary Section 5.

In vivo animal experiment

This study was conducted in full compliance with the ethical principles outlined by the Ethics Committee of the Beijing Jishuitan Hospital.

E-Exo combined with DCM scaffolds promote articular cartilage endogenous regeneration

In vitro studies have shown that 3D-Exo consistently outperforms 2D-Exo in enhancing MSC proliferation, migration, chondrogenic differentiation, immunomodulation, and chondrocyte protection. Therefore, we used two types of 3D-Exo to promote cartilage repair in vivo. A total of fifty 6-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (250 ± 50 g) were randomly assigned to five groups: control, scaffold only, scaffold/G-Exo, scaffold/E-Exo, and sham (n = 5 per time point per group). Details of the surgical procedure in Section 2.8 (Fig. 11B). In the sham group, the joint cavity was exposed without defect creation. Control and scaffold-only groups received intra-articular injections of 50 μL PBS, while the scaffold/G-Exo and scaffold/E-Exo groups were administered 50 μL PBS containing G-Exo or E-Exo (1.0 × 108 particles/mL), respectively. Injections were performed once every seven days for a total of five doses postoperatively, ceasing after the fourth week (Fig. 11C) [22]. Postoperative activity and food intake were not restricted. Rats were euthanized at 6 and 12 weeks following surgery for subsequent joint tissue analysis.

Macroscopic assessment

At 6 and 12 weeks postoperatively, images of the defect area were captured using a stereomicroscope (Nikon, Japan) for macroscopic observation. The International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) macroscopic evaluation criteria for cartilage repair were used for scoring (Table S2) [15].

Microcomputed tomography (micro-CT)

Micro-CT was used to assess subchondral bone regeneration at 6 and 12 weeks post-knee surgery. Regions of interest were selected in the subchondral bone of each defect area (diameter = 2.0 mm, thickness = 1 mm). Software was used to calculate parameters such as total volume (TV), bone volume (BV), volume ratio (BV/TV), and bone mineral density (BMD).

Histomorphological evaluation

For histological analysis, samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 3 days and decalcified in 10% EDTA. Following paraffin embedding, sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm and stained with H&E, safranin O/fast green, toluidine blue, and sirius red. For COL2 immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody (1:200; DSHB) in the dark. After three washes with PBS solution, the sections were incubated sequentially with the secondary antibody solution and hematoxylin solution. Quantification of COL2 expression was performed using ImageJ software. Sections were scored using the modified O'Driscoll histological scoring system (MODHS) to assess cartilage repair [24] (Table S3).

To evaluate the potential vascularization risk in the regenerated tissue, immunofluorescence staining was performed. Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking with 10% goat serum to prevent non-specific binding, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mixture of primary antibodies against CD31 (endothelial marker; 1:3000; Servicebio, China) and α-SMA (vascular smooth muscle/myofibroblast marker; 1:3000; Servicebio, China). Following three washes with PBS, sections were incubated with corresponding fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio, China). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan), where CD31 (red), α-SMA (green), and nuclei (blue) were visualized to assess the presence of vascular endothelial and smooth muscle cells.

Biochemical assay

Collagen and glycosaminoglycan (GAG) contents were quantified using hydroxyproline and 1,9-dimethylmethylene blue (DMMB) assay kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, and Genmed Scientific Inc., Shanghai, China, respectively), according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were analyzed and graphed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The assumptions of normal distribution and homogeneity of variances were verified prior to parametric testing. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired Student's t-test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. All experiments were performed with a minimum of three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Characterization of 3D GelMA and ECM scaffolds

To comprehensively characterize the physicochemical microenvironment, we evaluated the mechanical properties, morphology, and pore structure of the scaffolds. First, mechanical testing revealed that the compressive modulus of GelMA scaffolds (80.59 ± 7.11 kPa) was statistically comparable to that of ECM scaffolds (67.50 ± 5.57 kPa) (Fig. S1), thereby minimizing stiffness as a primary confounding factor. Subsequently, SEM imaging revealed distinct structural features of the two scaffolds (Fig. 2C). ECM scaffolds displayed a uniform, multilayered macro-microporous architecture with abundant surface micropores, whereas GelMA scaffolds exhibited a smoother surface with irregularly distributed pores. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that GelMA scaffolds possessed significantly larger micropores (58.62 ± 32.48 μm) compared to ECM scaffolds (19.79 ± 3.58 μm) (Figs. S2–S3).

Cytoskeletal staining showed that BMSCs adhered and spread well on both 2D and 3D substrates (Fig. 2D). On 3D scaffolds, cells exhibited a more elongated spindle-like morphology compared with 2D culture. Notably, ECM scaffolds supported more uniform spreading, well-defined spindle-shaped morphology, and pronounced cell clustering, suggesting superior support for cell organization and cytoskeletal integrity relative to GelMA scaffolds.

Live/Dead staining confirmed high cell viability under both 2D and 3D conditions, with strong green (live) fluorescence and minimal red (dead) signals (Fig. 2E–F). On 3D scaffolds, BMSCs aligned along the printed fibers, and the porous architecture increased surface area and local cell density, thereby promoting cell aggregation and cell–cell interactions. Notably, ECM scaffolds supported a greater number of viable cells than GelMA scaffolds, indicating superior biocompatibility and proliferative capacity.

BMSC proliferation on different scaffolds was quantified using the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 2G). A time-dependent increase in optical density (OD) was observed in all groups. On days 3 and 5, both 3D scaffold groups showed significantly enhanced proliferation (P < 0.05) compared to the 2D control, with the ECM scaffold group consistently outperforming the GelMA scaffold group. These findings highlight the superior capacity of hierarchical porous ECM scaffolds to support BMSC adhesion, expansion, and proliferation.

Characterization, yield, and internalization of exosomes

Exosomes isolated from all groups exhibited the characteristic cup-shaped morphology under TEM (Fig. 3B). Western blot confirmed the presence of exosomal markers CD9, CD81, and TSG101, with the absence of calnexin (Fig. 3C). NTA analysis showed that particle size distributions were within the expected range (Fig. 3D). Next, we compared the yield and purity of the exosome preparations. Regarding yield, quantitative analysis revealed that 3D culture significantly enhanced exosome secretion efficiency per cell, with the E-Exo group showing the highest production rate (Fig. S4). To assess purity and ensure comparability across groups, we then calculated the particle-to-protein ratio. This analysis showed no significant difference among the three groups (P > 0.05), indicating a comparable level of purity for all preparations (Fig. S5). Therefore, to isolate and specifically evaluate the intrinsic bioactivity of the vesicles, all subsequent functional experiments were conducted using exosome dosages strictly normalized by particle number. This design ensures that observed functional differences are attributable to the per-vesicle quality, rather than variations in yield or purity. To assess cellular uptake, red fluorescence-labeled exosomes were incubated with BMSCs, chondrocytes, and BMDMs. After 24 h, strong perinuclear fluorescence was observed in all three cell types (Fig. 3E), demonstrating efficient internalization of exosomes and indicating their capacity to mediate downstream biological effects.

E-Exo enhances chondrogenesis via miR-503-5p–mediated regulation of Smad7/TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling

To explore the molecular basis underlying the superior activity of E-Exo, we profiled the miRNA cargo of exosomes derived from different 3D scaffolds. Sequencing revealed distinct expression patterns between G-Exo and E-Exo, with strong intra-group consistency (Fig. 9A). Venn analysis identified 568 shared miRNAs, along with 41 unique to G-Exo and 180 unique to E-Exo (Fig. 9B). Differential expression analysis showed 271 miRNAs upregulated and 54 downregulated in E-Exo compared with G-Exo (Fig. 9C). Hierarchical clustering highlighted miR-503-5p as the most strongly upregulated miRNA in E-Exo (Fig. 9D). Functional enrichment (GO and KEGG) indicated that the target genes of these differentially expressed miRNAs were predominantly associated with chondrogenesis and stem cell pluripotency (Fig. 9E and F). In vivo cartilage repair study

Macroscopic evaluation

Macroscopic evaluation of cartilage regeneration (Fig. 11D) showed that at both 6 and 12 weeks, the scaffold/E-Exo group exhibited tissue morphology and integration with adjacent native cartilage more closely resembling the sham group than the control, scaffold-only, or scaffold/G-Exo groups. Consistently, ICRS scores in the Scaffold/E-Exo group were higher (P < 0.05) than those in the control and scaffold groups at both time points. Compared to the Scaffold/G-Exo group, scores were higher at 6 weeks (P < 0.05), while no significant difference (P > 0.05) was observed at 12 weeks (Fig. 11E and F). These findings indicate that E-Exo markedly enhances macroscopic cartilage regeneration when combined with scaffolds.

Imaging evaluation

Micro-CT analysis was performed to assess the quality of cartilage regeneration. Morphological reconstruction (Fig. 11G) showed that the Scaffold/E-Exo group exhibited markedly enhanced tissue regeneration than the Scaffold/G-Exo, Scaffold-only, and control groups at both 6 and 12 weeks. Cartilage-like tissue formation was already evident in the Scaffold/E-Exo group at week 6, and defects were nearly filled by week 12. Quantitative assessment further revealed higher BMD and BV/TV (P < 0.05) in the Scaffold/E-Exo group compared with all other groups (Fig. 11H and I), underscoring its superior capacity to restore subchondral bone structure and support functional cartilage repair.

Biochemical analysis

Biochemical analysis of regenerated cartilage (Fig. 12E and F; Fig. 13E and F) showed progressive increase in total collagen and GAG content over time. The Scaffold/E-Exo group consistently exhibited higher levels (P < 0.05) of both components than the control, Scaffold-only, and Scaffold/G-Exo groups, indicating superior matrix synthesis. However, even at 12 weeks, collagen and GAG contents remained below those of native cartilage, suggesting that while Scaffold/E-Exo treatment markedly accelerates extracellular matrix maturation, complete biomechanical restoration requires longer remodeling.

Discussion

Articular cartilage injury remains a major challenge in orthopedics due to its limited self-repair capacity and the lack of effective surgical and pharmacological interventions. In recent years, MSC-derived exosomes have emerged as a promising cell-free therapeutic alternative to MSC transplantation, as they can deliver bioactive molecules that regulate proliferation, migration, differentiation, and immune response. Despite these advantages, conventional exosomes remain suboptimal, largely due to the dependence of their composition and function on the microenvironment in which they are produced [4]. This underscores the need for strategies that can enhance and tailor exosome function to meet the requirements of effective cartilage regeneration.

Our findings highlight the pivotal role of the culture microenvironment in determining the biological activity of MSC-derived exosomes. Compared with conventional 2D culture, 3D scaffolds provided a more physiologically relevant niche that enhanced BMSC proliferation, adhesion, and exosome secretion, with ECM scaffolds proving superior to GelMA. Functional assays confirmed that exosomes from 3D cultures, particularly E-Exo, exhibited stronger effects on MSC recruitment, macrophage polarization, and chondrocyte metabolic regulation than 2D-Exo or G-Exo. By promoting M2 polarization and restoring chondrocyte homeostasis under inflammatory conditions, E-Exo actively remodeled the local microenvironment and created a regenerative milieu conducive to cartilage repair.

Beyond structural cues, scaffold composition critically influences MSC behavior and exosome bioactivity [28]. GelMA scaffolds provide tunable mechanics and adhesive motifs that support cell attachment and viability but lack the complex biochemical signals required for lineage-specific differentiation [19]. By contrast, cartilage ECM scaffolds retain cartilage-specific components such as COL2, GAGs, hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulfate, and endogenous growth factors like TGF-β. These biochemical cues not only promote chondrogenic differentiation [19,29], and maintain the chondrocyte phenotype, but also activate the TGF-β/Smad pathway [29] and facilitate local biofeedback loops. However, we acknowledge that biophysical properties constitute another critical factor regulating MSC behavior and exosome functionality. Our mechanical testing indicated that the stiffness was comparable between the two scaffolds, whereas GelMA scaffold exhibited larger pores, which typically favor mass transport. Despite these structural conditions, the superior performance of E-Exo strongly underscores the dominant role of the biochemical composition of the cartilage ECM. Therefore, while we attribute the overall effect to the holistic cartilage ECM microenvironment, the biochemical signaling likely acts as the primary driver. Through this integrated microenvironmental regulation, ECM scaffolds endow secreted exosomes with superior molecular profiles and regenerative activity, explaining why E-Exo consistently outperformed G-Exo in promoting cartilage repair.

MiRNAs are conserved, small non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by targeting complementary sequences [30]. To elucidate the molecular basis underlying the differential reparative capacities of G-Exo and E-Exo, we performed high-throughput miRNA sequencing. Comparative analysis revealed the top 10 up-regulated miRNAs in E-Exo, including miR-503-5p, miR-144-5p, miR-486, miR-20a-5p, miR-451-5p, miR-425-5p, miR-144-3p, miR-142-3p, miR-92a-3p, and miR-19a-3p. In parallel, the 10 most down-regulated miRNAs comprised miR-145-5p, miR-29a-3p, miR-129-5p, miR-324-5p, miR-211-5p, miR-672-5p, miR-350, miR-154-5p, miR-337-3p, and miR-147. Many of these miRNAs may be involved in various regulatory processes in cartilage regeneration, including cell proliferation [[31], [32], [33]], migration [31,33,34], chondrogenic differentiation [35,36], chondrocyte apoptosis [37], and immunomodulation [38]. Table S4 describes the functions of some of these miRNAs. Through GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, we found that the target genes of differentially expressed miRNAs were primarily concentrated in biological processes and signaling pathways related to cartilage formation and stem cell pluripotency. These results further explain our in vivo and in vitro findings. Collectively, the data suggest that the superior bioactivity of E-Exo is driven by an enhanced molecular cargo shaped by an ECM-based 3D microenvironment. While a full elucidation of all active components is beyond the scope of this study, our data allowed us to identify and validate one of the key mechanistic drivers, as detailed below.

At the mechanistic level, the enrichment of miR-503-5p in E-Exo was found to play a key role in promoting the chondrogenic differentiation of BMSCs. The TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling pathway is critically involved in cartilage differentiation [29], while Smad7, an inhibitory Smad, suppresses this pathway by blocking Smad2/3 phosphorylation through competitive binding to TGF-β receptors [26]. Consistent with previous studies, miR-503-5p downregulates Smad7 expression in Tca8113 cells [27]. In this study, we identified Smad7 as a direct downstream target of miR-503-5p using target gene prediction and dual-luciferase assays. Inhibition of Smad7 relieved its blockade on TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling, thereby enabling Smad2/3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, which subsequently activated key chondrogenic genes, including SOX9, COL2, and ACAN. Functionally, both miR-503-5p overexpression and E-Exo treatment significantly enhanced chondrogenic marker expression, whereas inhibition of miR-503-5p attenuated this effect. Together, these results establish the miR-503-5p/Smad7/TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling axis as a major and functionally critical pathway through which E-Exo promotes chondrogenic differentiation. While we concede that other miRNAs or proteins within the complex cargo of E-Exo may also contribute to the overall therapeutic effect, our gain- and loss-of-function experiments confirm the functional necessity of miR-503-5p in this process.

The role of miR-503-5p and its downstream effector, the TGF-β pathway, warrants a critical discussion on safety. An increasing number of research suggests that the functional output of exosomal miRNAs is context-dependent and may be dictated by the recipient cell [39]. Therefore, while some studies have characterized miR-503-5p as an oncogene in certain cancer models or linked it to vascular smooth muscle differentiation in other systems, we argue that these risks are profoundly mitigated in the unique context of our system. The articular joint is a unique, avascular, and hypoxic microenvironment that is fundamentally non-permissive for tumorigenesis and aberrant vascularization. This resistance is actively maintained by endogenous anti-angiogenic factors within the cartilage matrix, such as chondromodulin-I (ChM-I) and thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1) [40,41]. This restrictive milieu likely suppresses any potential off-target capacity of miR-503-5p, confining its regulatory function to chondrogenesis. Furthermore, considering the “Holistic Effect” of the exosomal cargo, E-Exo's pro-chondrogenic activity is likely synergistically enhanced by other co-transported molecules that reinforce this tissue-specific outcome. This is likely further supported by our therapeutic strategy, where intermittent local injections result in transient, rather than sustained, pathway activation, thereby keeping the signaling within a therapeutic window.

Importantly, our in vivo data provide direct empirical evidence supporting the safety of our E-Exo treatment. Comprehensive histological analysis of major organs revealed no systemic toxicity or tumor formation (Fig. S16). Locally, our immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that the regenerated cartilage remained completely avascular (Fig. S15), and careful examination via micro-CT and histology showed no signs of osteophyte formation, a known side effect of excessive TGF-β activation. This convergence of evidence from systemic and local assessments strongly supports the favorable safety profile of this approach in our preclinical model.

For clinical translation, the strategy used to enhance exosome functionality is of critical importance. Conventional approaches such as chemical stimulation, [9,42], hypoxic preconditioning [43], and mechanical stimulation (e.g., shear force, magnetic fields) [44,45] have been investigated, but they often raise concerns regarding safety, specificity, scalability, or cost. In contrast, our method leverages a DCM scaffold derived from porcine tissue, which is inexpensive, reproducible, and safe. By programming exosomes through structural and biochemical microenvironmental cues rather than exogenous agents, this approach aligns with GMP standards and enhances translational feasibility [46]. It is worth noting that we utilized a combinatorial strategy (DCM scaffold implantation + E-Exo injection) rather than E-Exo alone. While our in vitro assays confirmed the independent bioactivity of E-Exo, the critical-sized defects in our in vivo model require structural support to bridge the lesion, making the DCM scaffold indispensable. Although we acknowledge that the absence of a scaffold-free control limits the precise quantification of the relative contributions of the scaffold versus the exosomes, this design reflects the clinical requirement for structural templates in repairing large osteochondral defects. While this study focused on optimizing exosome bioactivity via microenvironmental modulation, it is valuable to benchmark our E-Exo strategy against current clinical treatments. Intra-articular HA injections primarily provide symptomatic relief through lubrication, while microfracture stimulates endogenous repair, which often yields fibrocartilage rather than hyaline cartilage. In contrast, E-Exo represents a biologically targeted strategy aimed at modulating the regenerative microenvironment through defined molecular signaling pathways. We acknowledge that rigorous comparisons with these established standards will be essential to definitively establish therapeutic superiority in future translational studies.

Based on these findings, we propose a mechanistic model for E-Exo-mediated cartilage repair (Fig. 14). After implantation, the DCM scaffold provides mechanical support and serves as a structural matrix to facilitate new tissue formation. Following intra-articular injection, E-Exo first mobilizes and recruits endogenous stem/progenitor cells (ESPCs) within their native ecological niche to the site of injury [47]. Second, E-Exo promotes ESPCs proliferation and differentiation. Meanwhile, E-Exo directs macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype to suppress inflammation and to protect chondrocytes by restoring metabolic homeostasis and reducing apoptosis. It also suppresses matrix degradation by downregulating enzymes such as MMP-13 and ADAMTS-4, thereby preserving ECM integrity. Over time, scaffold degradation coupled with deposition of newly synthesized ECM results in the formation of hyaline-like cartilage, which undergoes further maturation and remodeling under physiological loading. Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our findings are based on neonatal rat BMSCs, which differ significantly from the MSCs of adult patients in their regenerative potential. Therefore, validation using clinically relevant cell sources is a critical next step for translation. Second, although the rat femoral trochlear defect model is a standard approach for preliminary evaluation, rodents possess a notably higher intrinsic healing capacity than humans. Therefore, the validation in large animal models (e.g., sheep or minipigs) is essential to assess efficacy within a load-bearing environment comparable to human physiology. Third, a significant limitation of this study is the absence of direct biomechanical testing of the regenerated cartilage. While our histological and biochemical data, particularly the elevated GAG content, strongly suggest functional improvement, future studies, especially those in large-animal models, must include rigorous mechanical assessment (e.g., compression testing) to definitively evaluate the load-bearing capacity of the repaired tissue. Fourth, our in vivo study did not include a Scaffold/2D-Exo group, which, while omitted for ethical and strategic reasons, would have provided definitive in vivo confirmation of the benefits of 3D conditioning. Fifth, while our study identified a key mechanistic pathway, a deeper validation is required. Our investigation lacked certain definitive experiments to fully solidify the proposed signaling axis. Specifically, we did not perform the gold-standard Smad7 overexpression rescue experiment in vitro, nor were in vivo rescue experiments using miR-503-5p antagomirs conducted. Furthermore, our mechanistic investigation focused exclusively on miRNAs, omitting a proteomics analysis. The protein cargo of E-Exo, which may include directly incorporated matrix components or reflect changes in cellular secretion, undoubtedly contributes to the overall therapeutic effect. A complete understanding of E-Exo's function will require future multi-omics studies to elucidate the synergistic roles of both protein and RNA cargo. Sixth, we did not perform detailed pharmacokinetic or biodistribution analyses of the injected exosomes. While the weekly injection protocol yielded positive regenerative outcomes, future studies utilizing advanced tracking technologies (e.g., in vivo fluorescence imaging) are needed to precisely quantify the retention time and biodistribution of E-Exo within the articular cavity. Finally, several major translational hurdles remain, primarily the challenges in scaling up GMP-compliant exosome production and the need for comprehensive long-term safety studies in large animals. While our 12-week study showed no major safety signals, such long-term validation is an essential prerequisite to rule out biotoxicity or potential tumorigenicity before any clinical consideration. Addressing these issues will be essential to accelerate the clinical application of E-Exo in cartilage repair.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the integrated physicochemical microenvironment—encompassing spatial architecture, biochemical composition, and biophysical cues—critically determines the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-derived exosomes. Among the tested conditions, E-Exo derived from a 3D hierarchical porous ECM scaffold exhibited superior capacity to promote cell proliferation, migration, chondrogenic differentiation, chondrocyte protection, immunomodulation, and in vivo cartilage regeneration. Mechanistically, we identified a key axis through which E-Exo promote BMSC chondrogenesis: they are enriched in miR-503-5p, which specifically inhibits Smad7, thereby enhancing the TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling pathway. Collectively, these results establish a biomaterials-based strategy for programming exosome function and provide a mechanistically validated, clinically translatable approach for “cell-free” cartilage regeneration therapy.
