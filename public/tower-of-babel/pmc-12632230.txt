Altered oral microbiomes in patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness
Authors: Mingwei Xu, Zhuolin He, Jiajia Zhou, Jiajia Zhao, Xuejiao Tian, Qisheng Cheng, Yongxing Lin, Hailiang Xin, Chenye Mou, Qiang Xue, Benyan Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe host microbiome is increasingly recognized as a key modulator of brain function and disease progression, yet the role of the oral microbiome in patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness remains underexplored.MethodsThis study characterized oral microbiota differences among pDoC patients (n = 89) in the vegetative state (VS), the minimally conscious state (MCS), and emerging from the MCS (EMCS), with a particular focus on the impact of antibiotic use. We used 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing to profile oral microbiota in patients with different levels of consciousness.Resultsβ-diversity was significantly reduced in the VS group compared to the EMCS group. Differential abundance analysis identified five taxa (i.e., species Streptococcus danieliae, species Corynebacterium durum, family Lachnospiraceae, species Phocaeicola abscessus, and species Campylobacter showae) that exhibited significant differences between VS and EMCS, suggesting they were potentially involved in regulating oral microbial dysbiosis and brain-microbiome interactions. Antibiotic treatment induced pronounced microbial shifts in the VS group, while no such effect was observed in the MCS or EMCS groups. Prognostic models built using differential and dominant microbiota panels demonstrated strong predictive performance, achieving areas under the curve of 0.820 and 0.920, respectively.ConclusionsThese findings highlight oral microbiome alterations in pDoC and their potential relevance to disease progression, emphasizing the importance of microbiome-informed clinical strategies.Keywords: Disorders of consciousness, oral microbiome, antibiotics, prognosis, 16S rRNA sequencing

Introduction

Prolonged disorders of consciousness (pDoC) refer to a condition in which patients remain in a state of severely impaired consciousness for more than 28 days following various brain injuries such as traumatic brain injury (TBI), stroke, or hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy [1,2]. Based on the Coma Recovery Scale–Revised (CRS–R) [3], pDoC is primarily categorized into two states: the vegetative state (VS), characterized by wakefulness without any signs of awareness, and the minimally conscious state (MCS), in which patients exhibit minimal but discernible signs of conscious awareness [2,4,5]. Furthermore, patients who recover functional object use or communication abilities from VS or MCS are termed EMCS [5,6]. Unlike brain–dead patients, those in VS, MCS, or EMCS still maintain some degree of brain function and are not dependent on mechanical ventilation for life support, often receiving enteral nutrition [7]. While significant research has focused on the central nervous system of pDoC patients, the role of the peripheral physiological environment, particularly the microbiome, remains poorly explored despite its potential influence on brain function and recovery.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of the gut microbiome in modulating brain health through the gut–brain axis, a bidirectional communication system that links the gut and the brain [8–12]. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota has been shown to affect brain function and associated with various neurological disorders and neuropsychiatric conditions, especially those involving cognitive impairment, neurodegenerative diseases, mood disorders, and severe conditions such as schizophrenia (SZ) [13–15]. In our previous research, using 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) high–throughput sequencing, we identified significant differences in the gut microbiome of patients with pDoC which may influence brain function [10]. However, the relationship between the oral microbiome and pDoC remains unexplored.

In addition to the gut microbiome, the oral microbiome is increasingly recognized for its role in both oral and systemic health [16]. Hosting over 700 bacterial taxa, it is the second most diverse microbiota in the human body, predominantly forming biofilms (dental plaque) within the anaerobic niche of the subgingival region [17]. Emerging evidence suggests that oral microbiota can migrate from the oral cavity and establish colonies at distant sites in the body, including the brain [18–25]. For example, infection with serotype k Streptococcus mutans, a key etiological agent of dental caries, has been shown to significantly exacerbate cerebral hemorrhage in mice [22]. Porphyromonas gingivalis, the keystone pathogen in chronic periodontitis, was considered a significant risk factor for the development of plaques, dementia, and Alzheimer's disease (AD) [26–29]. Moreover, dysbiosis of the oral fungal microbiota, particularly genera such as Malassezia, has been shown to correlate with peripheral pro–inflammatory cytokines involving in the development of SZ [30].

Besides, oral microbiology may also have a potential relevance in the context of pDoC. Due to their condition, pDoC patients exhibit limited oral movement, often rely on nasogastric tubes for nutrition, and receive consistent oral care, which collectively contribute to a relatively stable oral environment over time [31,32]. Additionally, the loss of self–care abilities in pDoC patients, such as chewing, swallowing, and salivation, further disrupts the oral microbiome, potentially leading to microbial imbalances. Swallowing deficits in pDoC individuals had been reported closely linked to their level of consciousness [33]. Given the bidirectional interplay between the oral microbiome, its metabolites, and the brain, it is important to explore how alterations in the oral microbiome may affect brain function and consciousness recovery in pDoC patients. To bridge this gap, we analyzed oral microbiome composition in pDoC patients across different levels of consciousness using 16S rRNA sequencing. Given that patients with TBI often receive intermittent antibiotic treatment to prevent infections, we further compared oral microbiome differences between antibiotic treated and non–treated patients. Lastly, we identified differentially abundant microbial taxa associated with prognostic outcomes, highlighting potential microbial signatures relevant to disease progression and recovery in pDoC.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of each patient. The study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. NCT04530968) and the Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Mingzhou Brain Rehabilitation Hospital.

Subjects

This study included 89 pDoC patients hospitalized at Hangzhou Mingzhou Brain Rehabilitation Hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of VS or MCS or EMCS according to the CRS–R scale; (2) aged 20 to 80 years; (3) at least 1 month post–injury; (4) stable vital signs for more than 1 month prior to enrollment; (5) receiving a standardized enteral nutritional formula provided by the hospital. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) being in an unstable vital condition, as indicated by signs of spontaneous recovery or deterioration within a one–week period; (2) using probiotics, prebiotics, or synbiotics within one month prior to sample collection; (3) having gastrointestinal or oral diseases, chronic wasting conditions, severe malnutrition, infections, or anemia; (4) having psychiatric disorders (e.g. schizophrenia), neurodegenerative diseases, or other serious systemic or primary diseases. The diagnosis of VS, MCS, or EMCS was made based on five assessments conducted within 10 days by experts using the CRS–R [3]. In short, VS patients can open their eyes and maintain sleep–wake cycles but are unaware of themselves and their surroundings. MCS patients show reproducible signs of awareness and experience fluctuations in consciousness, while EMCS patients demonstrate the recovery of functional object use or communication from pDoC. The Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended (GOS–E) was used to assess the prognosis of these patients [34]. All patients had been on a standardized hospital diet for an extended period and received enteral nutrition provided by the hospital. The nursing staff remained consistent throughout the study to minimize variability. Saliva samples were collected following a standardized protocol under unstimulated conditions between 7:00 a.m. and 8:00 a.m. before the morning enteral feeding and prior any oral hygiene procedures. Enteral nutrition was withheld for 10 hours prior to sample collection. At the time of sample collection, centrally acting drugs, neuromuscular blockers, and sedatives were discontinued for a minimum of 24 hours [6].

A portion of the patients with pDoC received brief prophylactic antibiotic treatment to prevent aspiration pneumonia. Some continued the treatment for a short duration to avoid reinfection, even after the prior infection had been suppressed. Among the 89 recruited patients, 63 individuals received antibiotic intervention (Beta–lactam) within a month prior to sample collection, while the other 26 had no antibiotic treatment during the same period. Patients who did not receive antibiotics were classified into three groups: VS without antibiotics (VS_noABx) (n = 8, average age = 61.4 ± 8.8, 5 men and 3 women); MCS without antibiotics (MCS_noABx) (n = 9, average age = 55.9 ± 17.3, 3 men and 6 women); and EMCS without antibiotics (EMCS_noABx) (n = 9, average age = 54.6 ± 15.0, 6 men and 3 women). Patients who had a history of antibiotic were classified as: VS with antibiotics (VS_ABx) (n = 13, average age = 63.1 ± 9.3, 8 men and 5 women); MCS with antibiotics (MCS_ABx) (n = 37, average age = 57.7 ± 13.8, 29 men and 8 women); and EMCS with antibiotics (EMCS_ABx) (n = 13, average age = 51.8 ± 13.9, 9 men and 4 women). The patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Sample collection and extraction of genome DNA

Microbial samples were collected with disposable cotton swabs from the upper and lower gingival surfaces of the patients. The samples were stored in cryotubes and immediately frozen at -80℃ until DNA extraction. DNA was extracted from the oral samples using the sodium dodecyl sulfate method. The purity and concentration of the DNA were monitored by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the DNA samples were diluted to 1ng/μL with sterile water.

Amplicon generation, polymerase chain reaction, and sequencing

Using the diluted bacterial genomic DNA as a template, primers for the 16S rRNA V4 region (5’-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA−3’ and 5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT−3’) with barcodes were used for PCR amplification with Phusion® High–Fidelity PCR Master Mix and GC Buffer (New England Biolabs). Equal amounts of 1 × loading buffer (containing SYBR Green) were combined with the PCR products and subjected to electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. PCR products were mixed in equal concentrations. Bright bands between 400−450 bp were selected and purified using the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Finally, sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq® DNA PCR–Free Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with added index barcodes. The library quality was assessed using a Qubit® 2.0 fluorometer (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to obtain 250bp paired–end sequences. After removing the barcode and primer sequences, the read sequences of each sample were merged using the flash software to produce the raw tagged data.

Data processing

Sequence analysis was conducted using Uparse software [35]. All reads were deposited and grouped into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a sequence identity of 97%, and the taxonomic affiliation of the OTUs was determined with quantitative insights into microbial ecology (QIIME, version 1.8.0) against the Greengenes database, version 13.8 [36]. The following downstream data analyzes were conducted in R software (version 4.4.0). To analyze the complexity of species diversity within a sample, alpha diversity was evaluated using four indices: Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and abundance–based coverage estimator (ACE). The calculations for these indices were performed on the samples using QIIME. To test the overall differences in the microbial community composition across groups, cross sample distance measurements were compared. Four metrics for β-diversity analysis were calculated between samples using QIIME software: Weighted and Unweighted UniFrac distances, Bray–Curtis distances, and Binary Jaccard. The community differences were visualized using the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) method [37]. Cluster analysis was carried out on the samples to build a sample clustering tree to better observe the similarities and differences between groups. The Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) method was employed to identify taxa that showed both statistical significance and biological relevance. A linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score >3.7 and P < 0.05 was used to ensure that a relatively complete set of top microbial taxa were displayed for visualization purposes. Partial least squares latent structure discriminant analysis (PLS–DA) was performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (www.metaboanalyst.ca) to observe the oral microbiota structure across different groups based on the OTUs from the sequencing data of each sample. Variable importance in projection (VIP) reflects the importance of the variables that have the most significant contribution to the discrimination. Microbiota taxa with a VIP score greater than 2 were identified as the most influential factors in distinguishing EMCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or as median and quartiles for non–normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for two groups, and the one–way ANOVA test or Kruskal–Wallis test for more than two groups were used to determine statistical significance. Pearson’s Chi–square test was performed for comparison of categorical variables. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method with FDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were constructed to explore the prognostic potential of oral microbiota in pDoC. Univariate model was based on the sole differential microbial taxa with highest VIP score comparing good and poor outcome groups, and the multivariate model was based on dominant microbial taxa (representative genara with relative abundance > 1%) in both groups. For the multivariate logistic regression model, predictor variables were further selected using stepwise selection based on Stepwise Akaike Information Criterion (StepAIC) to optimize the model. No additional regularization techniques (e.g. LASSO) were applied, as the number of predictors after StepAIC selection was small relative to the sample size, and the risk of overfitting was further assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness–of–fit test. The predictive performance of the models was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) reported to assess discrimination ability. Statistical analyzes were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and R software (version 4.4.2).

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 89 patients with pDoC were enrolled, including 21 in VS, 46 in MCS, and 22 in EMCS. The demographic summary of the study cohort is shown in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the three groups in terms of age, sex, etiology, or duration of disease, either within the antibiotic or non–antibiotic innervation cohort. Additionally, we recorded and compared the main biochemical indicators of the patients on the same day of sample collection. No significant differences were found in biochemical markers (including white blood cell count, lymphocyte count, C–reactive protein levels, etc.) among the three groups, though creatinine levels showed near–significant variation, both within the antibiotic and the non–antibiotic intervention groups.

Alterations in the oral microbiome of pDoC patients without antibiotic treatment

To investigate the differences in oral microbiome composition among pDoC patients with varying levels of consciousness, we first compared the oral microbiota profiles between EMCS and VS patients using 16S rRNA sequencing. The Venn diagram (Figure 1A) illustrates the unique and shared OTUs between the EMCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups. The EMCS_noABx group exhibited 2,423 unique OTUs, while the VS_noABx group had 642 unique OTUs, with 1,214 OTUs shared between the two groups. Comparative analysis at the phylum level revealed variations in microbial composition between the two groups (Figure 1B). While alpha diversity indices did not show significant differences (Figure 1C), beta diversity analysis revealed a significantly greater weighted UniFrac distance in the EMCS_noABx group (P = 0.019; Figure 1D), indicating a higher level of microbial community complexity. PCA effectively distinguished and visualized the differences between groups (Figure 1E), while hierarchical clustering further confirmed intra–group similarities and inter–group distinctions (Figure 1F). These findings highlight that oral microbiome composition varies significantly with consciousness level, with greater microbial complexity observed in the EMCS_noABx group.

In the comparative analysis of microbial communities between the MCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups, we identified 1,479 and 356 unique OTUs, respectively, with 1,207 OTUs shared between the two groups, indicating differences in microbial community composition (Figure S1A). However, no significant differences were observed in alpha or beta diversity indices (Figure S1B, C). PCA revealed a degree of separation between the MCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups (Figure S1D), while cluster analysis further demonstrated intra–group similarities and inter–group differences (Figure S1E). These results suggest that while the MCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups exhibit some differences in microbial composition, the alterations are less pronounced compared to those observed between the EMCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups.

Taxonomic differences in the oral microbiome of pDoC patients without antibiotic 290 treatment

Subsequently, we analyzed the relative abundances of different taxonomic units at the family, genus, and species levels in patients with varying levels of consciousness who did not receive antibiotic treatment. As there was a clear difference in microbial diversity between the EMCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups, while the MCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups exhibited greater similarity, our analysis primarily focused on the taxonomic differences between the EMCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups. Using PLS–DA, we identified the top 18 microbial groups with VIP scores > 2, as visualized in a heatmap (Figure 2A). Among these, five taxa exhibited significant variation (Figure 2B). Specifically, higher abundances of species Streptococcus danieliae (P = 0.001) and species Corynebacterium durum (P = 0.003) were observed in the EMCS_noABx group, whereas the family Lachnospiraceae (P = 0.002), species Phocaeicola abscessus (P = 0.002), and species Campylobacter showae (P = 0.002) showed higher abundance in the VS_noABx group (Figure 2B).

Finally, we conducted LEfSe analysis to confirm the distinguishing microbiota composition between the two groups and to identify potential discriminative biomarkers. Notably, the family Neisseriaceae (including the genus Neisseria), the genus Prevotella, the family Fusobacteriaceae (including the genus Fusobacterium), the phylum Campylobacterota (including the class Campylobacteria, order Campylobacterales, and family Campylobacteraceae), and the phylum Synergistota (including the class Synergistia, order Synergistales, and family Synergistaceae) were enriched in the VS_noABx group (Figure 2C).

In contrast, the genus Veillonella, species Streptococcus danieliae, the family Leptotrichiaceae (including the genus Leptotrichia), the order Micrococcales (including the family Micrococcaceae), and the phylum Cyanobacteria (including the class Cyanobacteria, order Chloroplast, and the family unidentified Chloroplast) were enriched in the EMCS_noABx group (Figure 2C). All of these taxa showed associations with the patients’ consciousness state. By integrating the phylogenetic tree analysis with the microbial community data, we elucidated the evolutionary relationships of these key microbiota across multiple taxonomic levels (Figure 2D). Furthermore, the combination of the family Neisseriaceae and the genus Veillonella displayed the highest LDA score in distinguishing EMCS_noABx from VS_noABx. These findings suggest that specific oral microbial signatures are correlated with the consciousness state and may serve as potential biomarkers for distinguishing different levels of pDoC.

Differential composition of the oral microbiome in pDoC patients with and without 324 antibiotic treatment

A subset of pDoC patients received long–term antibiotic treatment, primarily due to tracheostomy or aspiration pneumonia, as a preventive measure against infections [38]. To evaluate the impact of antibiotics on the oral microbiome, we compared the microbial composition between antibiotic–treated and non–antibiotic–treated groups within each consciousness category (VS, MCS, and EMCS). We first examined the differences in oral microbial composition between the VS_noABx and VS_ABx groups. The Venn diagram (Figure 3A) illustrates the shared and unique OTUs within the VS group. The VS_noABx group harbored 454 unique OTUs, while the VS_ABx group exhibited 2,351 unique OTUs, with 1,411 OTUs shared between the two groups. At the phylum level, the microbiota composition differed markedly between VS_noABx and VS_ABx (Figure 3B). Notably, the Simpson diversity index was significantly higher in the VS_noABx group compared to the VS_ABx group (Figure 3C), indicating decreased microbial community evenness and increased dominance of specific taxa in the antibiotic–treated group. Furthermore, β-diversity analysis revealed significant differences in species composition heterogeneity, as evidenced by elevated Binary Jaccard, unweighted UniFrac, and Bray–Curtis in the VS_ABx group (Figure 3D). These findings collectively support that antibiotic exposure not only diminished within–sample diversity but also induced substantial structural reorganization of the oral microbiome, characterized by increased diversity between samples and loss of community stability. PCA and hierarchical clustering analysis further corroborated these findings, demonstrating clear differences between the two groups while showing relative intra–group similarity (Figures 3E, F).

We further refined the taxonomic comparison and identified several microbial taxa that exhibited significant differences between the groups across multiple taxonomic levels. Specifically, the family Prevotellaceae (P = 0.003) and its related genera, Prevotella (P = 0.009) and Prevotella_7 (P = 0.002), were significantly enriched in the VS_noABx group (Figure 4A). Additionally, genus F0058 (P = 0.003) showed a higher abundance in the VS_noABx group (Figure 4B). Conversely, genus Desulfovibrio (P = 0.015), genus Stenotrophomonas (P = 0.002), order Coriobacteriales (P = 0.006), family Atopobiaceae (P = 0.015), order Oscillospirales (P = 0.009), family Ruminococcaceae (P = 0.003) and its related genera Ruminococcus (P = 0.002), and genus Subdoligranulum (P = 0.013) were significantly enriched in the VS_ABx group (Figures 4C–F), suggesting a profound shift in microbial community composition following antibiotic treatment.

When assessing the impact of antibiotic treatment within the EMCS (Figure S2) and MCS (Figure S3) groups, no significant differences were observed in either alpha diversity or beta diversity indices. Although PCA (Figure S2C, Figure S3C) provided a visualization of sample clustering between antibiotic–treated and non–antibiotic–treated subgroups within each consciousness group, the separation was not statistically significant. These findings indicate that the oral microbiome of VS patients is more susceptible to antibiotic–induced alterations compared to that of MCS and EMCS patients, suggesting a greater microbiome sensitivity to external perturbations in individuals with more severe consciousness impairment. We further evaluated the interactive effects of duration after injury and antibiotic treatment on oral microbiome diversity in pDoC, adjusting for common clinical covariates. In robust regression models, none of the four α-diversity indices showed significant associations with either duration or antibiotic use (Table S1, Figure S4A). In contrast, β-diversity analysis using PERMANOVA, which is well suited for dissimilarity–based indices, revealed notable patterns.

After adjusting for level of consciousness and other covariates, Bray–curtis distances remained significantly associated with both antibiotic use (R² = 0.025, q = 0.008) and duration (R² = 0.023, q = 0.012) (Table S2, Figure S4B,C). Weighted UniFrac distances also showed a significant association with Duration (R² = 0.041, q = 0.012), which collectively indicates that the oral microbiota community underwent structural adjustments over time after injury, rather than a replacement of species. In contrast, no significant interaction between antibiotic use and duration of injury was observed for any index (highest R² = 0.014, q = 0.209), suggesting that the effects of these two factors on β-diversity are largely independent in this cohort. These results may indicate that antibiotics tend to exert an acute or relatively short–term effect on community structure, whereas duration of injury could capture longer–term ecological shift such as recovery dynamics and host immune changes in pDoC patients.

Prognostic value of the oral microbiome in pDoC patients with antibiotic intervention

To investigate the potential relationship between the oral microbiome and long–term recovery of consciousness, we conducted a one–year follow–up study on patients with VS and MCS. Based on their GOS–E scores, patients were categorized into a good prognosis group (GOS–E > 2) and a poor prognosis group (GOS–E ≤ 2). In the antibiotic–treated cohort, 32 patients completed the follow–up, of whom 18 had a good prognosis and 14 had a poor prognosis. Table 2 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients. As shown, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of sex, age, duration of disease, or CRS–R total scores. However, a significant difference was observed in the distribution of etiology between the two groups (P = 0.002). Additionally, no statistically significant differences were found in key biochemical indicators between the two groups, excluding a near–significant variation found in the level of lymphocytes. Due to excessive loss of follow–up in the non–antibiotic–treated cohort, these patients were excluded from further analysis.

We next compared the composition of the oral microbiota between the good and poor prognosis groups. A total of 835 OTUs were shared, while 134 OTUs were uniquely identified in the good prognosis group, and 745 OTUs were unique to the poor prognosis group (Figure 5A). Further taxonomic comparisons at the phylum (Figure 5B) and genus levels (Figure 5C) revealed differences in the relative abundances of key microbial taxa between the two groups. PLS–DA was performed to visualize the microbial composition differences between prognosis groups in a reduced–dimensional space, which revealed a clear separation between groups (Figure 5D). To identify key microbial taxa contributing to the observed separation, we computed VIP scores (Figure S5A). A total of 50 key OTUs meeting this threshold were identified, and a heatmap was generated to visualize the distinct microbial composition differences between the two groups (Figure S5B). Notably, most of the high VIP–value taxa with significant enrichment were predominantly found in the poor prognosis group, including the family Pasteurellaceae, the genera Acinetobacter, Actinobacillus, and Corynebacterium, as well as the species Aggregatibacter segnis. Intriguingly, among the top 30 significant OTUs, only the family Enterobacteriaceae was significantly enriched in the good prognosis group. These findings suggest that the poor prognosis group harbors a distinct set of high–abundance, potentially pathogenic microbial taxa, possibly linked to disease progression or hindered recovery.

To further explore the prognostic potential of oral microbiota in pDoC, we developed logistic regression models based on differential microbial taxa and dominant taxa, respectively. A predictive model using the family Pasteurellaceae was constructed due to its strong discriminatory ability between prognosis groups (VIP = 3.493, P < 0.001). This model demonstrated good prognostic performance (Figure 5E, AUC = 0.816, 95% CI: 0.669–0.945). Additionally, the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness–of–fit test (X² = 0.452, P = 0.501) indicated an adequate model fit. Furthermore, a comprehensive model was developed using representative genera with relative abundance > 1% in both groups, incorporating Streptococcus, Pseudomonas, Neisseria, Aggregatibacter, Corynebacterium, Fusobacterium, Campylobacter, Actinomyces, Veillonella, Porphyromonas, Prevotella_7, and Others (Table S3). Stepwise selection via StepAIC was employed for optimal feature selection. This multivariate microbial model achieved enhanced predictive performance (Figure 5F, AUC = 0.921, 95% CI: 0.826−0.987) and a satisfactory Hosmer–Lemeshow test result (X² = 2.813, P = 0.946), indicating good model fit. The improved predictive accuracy of the multivariate model suggests that multiple microbial taxa may collectively be associated with consciousness recovery. Collectively, these findings indicate that oral microbial composition differs significantly between different prognosis groups in the antibiotic–treated cohort and that specific microbial signatures may serve as potential biomarkers for predicting long–term recovery in pDoC.

Discussion

In this study, we provide a comprehensive characterization of the oral microbiome in pDoC patients across different levels of consciousness (VS, MCS, and EMCS) and examine microbial differences between antibiotic–treated and non–antibiotic–treated subgroups. Additionally, we identify distinct microbial signatures that can differentiate between good and poor prognosis. All patients received enteral nutrition via nasogastric feeding due to impaired consciousness, which significantly limited oral activity. Besides, standardized oral care protocols ensured consistency in hygiene, minimizing confounding factors related to dietary variation and microbial fluctuations. Our findings show associations between oral microbiota alterations and the severity of consciousness impairment, while specific taxa show potential as biomarkers for disease severity and prognosis. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the oral microbiome in pDoC patients, offering a novel perspective on the clinical evaluation and management of pDoC.

A key finding of this study is the variation in microbial diversity among EMCS, MCS, and VS patients, particularly in those who did not receive antibiotic treatment. The EMCS_noABx group exhibited relatively higher oral microbial diversity compared to the VS_noABx group, suggesting that greater consciousness impairment may be associated with a reduction in microbial diversity. In contrast, no significant differences in microbial diversity were observed between the MCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups. This may be attributed to the clinical similarities between MCS and VS patients or could be influenced by the relatively smaller sample size in these cohorts. Compared to both the MCS_noABx and VS_noABx groups, the EMCS_noABx group demonstrated significantly better consciousness stateand long–term recovery, reinforcing the potential association between microbial diversity and neurological status. This finding aligns with previous evidence indicating that decreased microbial diversity can increase susceptibility to infections and systemic complications, potentially exacerbating neuroinflammation and impeding neurological recovery [39,40].

Taxonomic analysis revealed significant differences in microbial composition among patients with different levels of consciousness, suggesting that specific taxa may be associated with disease severity. Notably, the Gram–positive species Corynebacterium durum and Streptococcus danieliae were significantly enriched in the EMCS_noABx group. While some studies have linked Corynebacterium to the progression of AD [41,42], Corynebacterium durum and Streptococcus danieliae are predominantly recognized as commensal residents of the healthy oropharynx, where they contribute to maintaining oral homeostasis. Research indicates that certain Streptococcus and Corynebacterium species promote oral health by producing hydrogen peroxide and bacteriocins, which inhibit the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria, thereby supporting microbial balance [43]. Moreover, Corynebacterium durum is considered a core member of the healthy oral microbiota, with its presence being associated with oral health stability. Therefore, the enrichment of these taxa in the EMCS_noABx group may reflect a more stable and resilient microbial ecosystem, aligning with better neurological function in these patients [44].

In contrast, the VS_noABx group exhibited enrichment of bacterial taxa commonly associated with microbial dysbiosis and poor health outcomes. Three key taxa, species Campylobacter showae, species Phocaeicola abscessus, and the family Lachnospiraceae, were predominantly enriched in the VS_noABx group, suggesting a potential correlation with disease severity. These taxa have been previously linked to multiple neurological diseases, reinforcing their possible role in brain function impairment [11,45,46]. Specifically, Campylobacter species, particularly Campylobacter jejuni, have been implicated in the pathogenesis of axonal Guillain–Barré syndrome, which can cause cranial nerve damage and, in severe cases, altered consciousness [47,48]. Furthermore, the genus Phocaeicola and its related species have been associated with intra cranial aneurysms, Huntington's disease, and immunodeficiency virus–associated neurocognitive disorder, likely mediated through the gut brain axis [49–51]. In addition, variations in the abundance of Lachnospiraceae have been linked to AD, Parkinson’s disease, and multiple sclerosis, supporting its potential role in neural dysfunction [52–54]. Beyond these taxa, LEfSe analysis further confirmed a higher abundance of the phylum Campylobacterota, including the class Campylobacteria, order Campylobacterales, and family Campylobacteraceae, reinforcing their potential role in neuroinflammation and immune–mediated neural damage. Additionally, other differentially abundant taxa, such as Fusobacteriaceae and Veillonella, have been implicated in hepatic encephalopathy [55,56] and AD [57,58], indicating a broader association between these bacteria and cognitive or consciousness impairment. Meanwhile, species Neisseria, though typically commensal, include Neisseria meningitidis, a known pathogen of bacterial meningitis that can disrupt the meningeal barrier and contribute to neurological dysfunction [59].

Beyond their associations with neurodegenerative and neuroimmune diseases, these microbiotas may also interact with metabolic and neurological pathways, providing further mechanistic insights into their potential role in consciousness impairment [12,60,61]. For instance, the Lachnospiraceae family is known for its anaerobic fermentation and ability to produce short–chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as butyrate, which are critical for gut health and systemic metabolism [62]. Similarly, Phocaeicola species possess glycoside hydrolase activity, which can influence SCFA production and neurotransmitter synthesis, including gamma aminobutyric acid [50,63]. Furthermore, Campylobacter species may modulate glutamate metabolism and N-Methyl–D–Aspartate receptor activity [64], which are crucial for cognitive function. Importantly, our previous research on the gut microbiome in pDoC patients has consistently shown that certain taxa produce SCFAs, including acetic, propionic, and butyric acids, which play essential roles in immune regulation and neural signaling [10]. Although we did not measure SCFA concentrations directly in oral samples, the presence of SCFA–producing taxa in the VS_noABx group suggests a plausible link between oral dysbiosis and neuroinflammatory cascades or neurotransmitter imbalances.

When evaluating the impact of antibiotic use, our findings indicate that only the VS group exhibited a pronounced shift in microbial composition between antibiotic–treated and non–antibiotic–treated subgroups. This may be attributed to the severely reduced oral activity (e.g. chewing, swallowing) and lower salivary flow in VS patients, which could lead to prolonged antibiotic retention in the oral cavity, exacerbating microbial dysbiosis [65,66]. In contrast, patients with higher levels of consciousness (MCS or EMCS) likely swallow more frequently, facilitating greater oral microbial turnover, which may mitigate the local impact of antibiotics. The observed reduction in α-diversity alongside an increase in β-diversity in the VS group following antibiotic treatment suggests substantial ecological shifts within the oral microbiome. The loss of α-diversity indicates a decline in microbial richness and evenness, likely due to the suppression of antibiotic–sensitive commensal bacteria, resulting in a more homogenized microbiome at the individual level. Meanwhile, the increase in β-diversity suggests greater inter–individual variation, reflecting differential antibiotic effects across patients, selective expansion of antibiotic–resistant or opportunistic taxa, and compensatory microbial shifts. Notably, the Prevotellaceae family and its related genus Prevotella, which were preferentially enriched in the non–antibiotic treatment group, have been implicated in both beneficial and detrimental roles. Some studies suggest that Prevotella contributes to SCFA production [67] and mucosal immunity [68], exerting anti–inflammatory and gut–brain axis regulatory effects [69–71]. In contrast, the antibiotic treatment group showed a preferential increase in taxa such as Desulfovibrio and Stenotrophomonas, which have been implicated in neurotoxicity and systemic infections, potentially exacerbating host vulnerability [72,73]. Beyond consciousness level and antibiotics, our β-diversity analyzes identified duration after injury as an independent correlate of community structure (Bray–Curtis and unweighted unifrac) and there was no significant interaction between antibiotics and duration. This pattern may indicate a gradual, lineage–informed compositional drift rather than a simple loss of richness, consistent with duration of injury acting as a slow, cumulative driver of ecological change. Collectively, our results may support a bidirectional framework linking consciousness level, oral activity, and the oral microbiome. From a top–down perspective, brain injury and consciousness decline could reduce autonomic function and oral motor behaviors (e.g. chewing, swallowing), leading to decreased salivary secretion, altered pH and oxygen tension, and plaque maturation, which may favor anaerobe–leaning, dysbiosis associated taxa. Under such conditions, antibiotic exposure appeared to markedly alter α diversity and increase β-diversity only in the VS cohort, suggesting that more severely reduced oral activity might increase susceptibility to perturbations. Conversely, from a bottom–up perspective, enrichment of pathobiont–leaning communities (e.g. Campylobacterota, Fusobacteriaceae, Phocaeicola) could plausibly contribute to neural recovery barriers through virulence factors and inflammatory or metabolic mediators. In the antibiotic–treated cohort, taxa such as Pasteurellaceae, Acinetobacter, Actinobacillus, and Corynebacterium were more abundant in the poor–outcome group, implying that microbial community states might be associated with long–term recovery potential. Moreover, duration after injury may act as a slow variable in this oral–brain loop, in which prolonged dysbiosis and gradual community shifts could, in theory, reinforce both top–down and bottom–up feedbacks, with possible implications for recovery trajectories.

Our observations also align with some findings in gut microbiome studies and our previous study, where certain subpopulations (often those with greater impairment of consciousness) display more pronounced disruptions [10,74,75]. The parallel between gut and oral microbiota shifts underscores the need for careful antibiotic stewardship in pDoC patients, balancing infection control with microbiome preservation. Future studies should explore early targeted oral care interventions aimed at maintaining beneficial taxa while preventing opportunistic overgrowth, as well as strategies to restore neuroprotective and immunomodulatory microbiotas and their metabolites that may support consciousness recovery. Previous study has also highlighted the widespread presence of swallowing disorders in pDoC patients and suggested that the effectiveness of the oral phase of swallowing serving as an important marker of consciousness [33]. Furthermore, considering the variations in oral microbiota across different levels of consciousness impairment and the potential influence of oral activity on microbial abundance, we hypothesize that swallowing stimulation therapy to increase swallowing frequency may help restore oral microbiome balance and mitigate the adverse effects of antibiotics. This approach may potentially help improve consciousness recovery in patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness.

Another key highlight of our study is the identification of potential microbial biomarkers for predicting showing long–term prognosis in pDoC patients. Both univariate and multivariate logistic regression models demonstrated strong predictive performance, indicating that specific oral taxa could serve as reliable prognostic markers for long–term consciousness recovery. Notably, a model incorporating dominant genera, including Streptococcus, Pseudomonas, Neisseria, Aggregatibacter, Corynebacterium, Fusobacterium, Campylobacter, Actinomyces, Veillonella, Porphyromonas, Prevotella_7 and Others, achieved an AUC of 0.921 in predicting outcomes for VS and MCS patients receiving antibiotic treatment. Given the limited accuracy of current clinical scales and the practical constraints of electrophysiology and neuroimaging tools [76,77], this model presents a noninvasive, accessible, and clinically valuable approach for prognosis assessment and informed decision–making in pDoC management.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while we recruited patients with enteral nutrition and standardized oral care protocols and controlled for antibiotic exposure, other potential confounders, such as nutritional status and underlying etiology, may also influence the oral microbiome and warrant further investigation. Second, the lack of dynamic physiological measurements, including salivary flow, swallowing frequency, and immune markers, limits mechanistic insights into how antibiotic retention or local inflammation may be associated with delayed consciousness recovery. Third, the sample size in certain subgroups, particularly in the subgroup analysis involving antibiotic use, remains modest, which may affect the statistical power of the results. Lastly, while SCFAs, cytokines, and metabolic pathways have been extensively studied in gut microbiome research, similar mechanisms in the oral cavity remain underexplored. To address current limitations, future studies should aim to expand the cohort size, incorporate multicenter approaches, and adopt longitudinal designs to validate these initial findings and enhance their broader applicability. Additionally, to gain deeper mechanistic insights, metagenomic sequencing could be employed, which offers higher taxonomic resolution and the ability to predict functional pathways, building upon these initial explorations using 16S rRNA sequencing. Furthermore, integrating metabolomics, transcriptomics, and immune profiling could clarify whether oral dysbiosis directly or indirectly influences neuronal repair and plasticity in pDoC.

Conclusion

In summary, our study reveals that oral microbial composition varies with consciousness states in pDoC patients and is influenced by antibiotic treatment, highlighting a complex bidirectional interaction between the microbiome, metabolism, immunity, and brain function. Specific microbial taxa were associated with both consciousness levels and antibiotic exposure, suggesting their potential involvement in disease progression and therapeutic response. Furthermore, we identified a panel of oral microbial biomarkers with strong predictive performance, offering a promising tool for prognostic assessment in pDoC patients.
