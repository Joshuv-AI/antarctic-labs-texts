Successful treatment of multicentric reticulohistiocytosis with JAK inhibitor: a case series
Authors: Stéphane Hilliquin, Nicolas Dupin, Nathalie Franck, Sara Laurent-Roussel, Frédérique Larousserie, Jérôme Avouac, Corinne Miceli-Richard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMulticentric reticulohistiocytosis (MRH) is a rare non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis characterised by erosive arthritis and papulonodular skin lesions, frequently associated with autoimmune features. Therapeutic strategies remain empirical despite the potentially severe and disabling course. We report three cases of MRH with heterogeneous systemic involvement, all showing sustained, multidomain responses to Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors.All patients developed inflammatory arthritis, ranging from arthralgia to destructive arthropathy, and early cutaneous papules predominantly affecting the face and trunk. Systemic manifestations included digital microangiopathy with a scleroderma-like capillaroscopic pattern in one patient and interstitial lung disease with lymphocytic alveolitis in another. Two patients fulfilled criteria for Sjögren’s syndrome, and all were positive for anti-Sjögren’s-syndrome-related antigen A/Ro52kD antibodies, highlighting the autoimmune overlap of MRH.Diagnosis was confirmed histologically by CD68-positive histiocytic and multinucleated giant cell infiltration. Conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs and tumour necrosis factor inhibitors were ineffective or poorly tolerated, whereas tofacitinib and upadacitinib induced rapid and durable improvement across articular, cutaneous, vascular and pulmonary domains. These observations support a role for JAK–STAT signalling in MRH and suggest JAK inhibition as a rational therapeutic option in refractory multisystem disease.Keywords: Rare Diseases, Treatment, Arthritis

Introduction

Multicentric reticulohistiocytosis (MRH) is a rare non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis classically characterised by combined cutaneous and articular involvement.1 Arthritis frequently affects the hands, wrists, shoulders, knees, hips and ankles and is often aggressive, leading to destructive changes in approximately half of patients, with possible flexion deformities and, in severe cases, mutilating ‘opera glass hand’ deformities.2 A distinctive feature is preferential involvement of the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints, with radiographs showing sharply marginated erosions and juxta-articular bone resorption.

Cutaneous manifestations typically consist of erythematous to flesh-coloured papulonodular lesions (1–10 mm), predominantly involving sun-exposed areas such as the face, forearms and hands.3 Less common features include photodistributed erythema, periungual telangiectasias, xanthelasma-like lesions and nail abnormalities (atrophy, ridging, brittleness or hyperpigmentation).

The pathogenesis of MRH remains incompletely understood but involves inflammatory infiltration of macrophage-derived histiocytes.4 Advanced lesions show multinucleated giant cells with abundant eosinophilic, ground glass cytoplasm and no necrosis. Histological examination, usually based on skin biopsy, is essential for diagnosis. Histiocytes are typically PAS-positive, express acid phosphatase, non-specific esterase and lysozyme, and show immunoreactivity mainly for CD107, CD68 and CD1c.

Given the rarity of MRH (approximately 300–400 reported cases), available evidence is largely derived from case reports, limiting assessment of treatment efficacy. Standard first-line therapy often combines methotrexate with tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, while bisphosphonates may benefit predominantly osseous disease.5 More recently, isolated reports have suggested efficacy of Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi).6

This series further expands the clinical spectrum of MRH and supports a potential role for JAKi in refractory disease.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective case series of three patients diagnosed with MRH and followed at a tertiary referral centre between 2019 and 2024. Clinical, biological, imaging and histopathological data were collected from medical records.

Treatment response was assessed based on clinical evolution of skin and joint involvement, laboratory parameters (C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), creatine kinase (CK)) and imaging findings, including pulmonary function tests and chest computed tomography (CT) when available.

Remission was defined as the disappearance of arthralgias and synovitis associated with stability or improvement of radiographic findings.

All patients were informed about the publication project and provided written non-opposition for the use of anonymised data, in accordance with ethical standards for scientific publication.

A 64-year-old woman presented with recent-onset papular lesions on the left malar region and forehead, associated with periungual chilblain-like changes, without ulceration or Raynaud’s phenomenon (figure 1A). She reported hand pain mainly affecting distal DIP and proximal interphalangeal joints, without swelling, nocturnal pain or morning stiffness, and had associated sicca symptoms. Hand radiographs revealed marginal erosions of bilateral DIP joints (2–5) and the left second proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints (figure 2A), while ultrasound showed no synovitis. Nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrated microangiopathy suggestive of connective tissue disease (figure 1C). Laboratory testing showed no systemic inflammation (CRP- <1 mg/L) but elevated creatine kinase levels, antinuclear antibodies (ANA) positivity (1:160) with anti-Sjögren’s-syndrome-related antigen A (anti-SSA/Ro52) antibodies, and low-grade sialadenitis on minor salivary gland biopsy (Chisholm and Mason grade 2). Fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18-FDG PET) imaging showed diffuse muscular and musculotendinous hypermetabolism, predominantly involving the cervical and paravertebral muscles, upper limbs and pelvic girdle, initially suggesting myositis, with no articular uptake and no findings. suggestive of neoplasia (figure 2B).

Skin biopsies from the forehead and submalar region confirmed superficial and mid-dermal histiocytic infiltrates forming nodular clusters, with large multinucleated histiocytes displaying abundant eosinophilic, periodic acid-Schiff staining-positive cytoplasm. Immunohistochemistry showed CD68 and occasional cyclin D1 expression, with negative CD1a and S100 staining (figure 2C, table 2). Initial treatment with methotrexate and golimumab led to partial cutaneous improvement but persistent periungual lesions and disabling joint pain, with radiographic progression. Switching to upadacitinib, combined with methotrexate, induced complete clinical remission within 6 months, including resolution of skin, joint and capillaroscopic abnormalities. At 18 months, imaging showed no further erosive progression. Remission was defined by the disappearance of arthralgias and synovitis, together with stability of radiographic findings.

The second patient, a 62-year-old woman diagnosed with MRH at age 38, presented with distal symmetrical polyarthritis and papular lesions of the hands and face. Laboratory testing revealed ANA positivity (1:640, homogeneous and speckled patterns), anti-SSA/Ro52 antibodies and type III polyclonal cryoglobulinaemia. She also reported sicca symptoms and uncomplicated Raynaud’s phenomenon. MRH was confirmed by skin biopsy showing histiocytic infiltration. Treatment with infliximab and adalimumab, both combined with methotrexate, failed because of secondary loss of efficacy. Long-term azathioprine therapy (17 years) provided partial control but eventually became insufficient. As a fourth-line therapy, tofacitinib was initiated, leading to excellent and sustained clinical efficacy. After 3 years, no major relapse occurred, with only mild, transient cutaneous recurrences and no inflammatory synovitis.

The third case involved a 45-year-old man diagnosed with MRH based on characteristic cutaneous papules on the chest, shoulders and face (figure 1B), followed by inflammatory involvement of small and large joints. Pulmonary assessment revealed interstitial lung disease (ILD) with an organising pattern and ground glass opacities, without respiratory symptoms (figure 1D). Bronchoalveolar lavage showed lymphocytic alveolitis, while pulmonary function tests were normal except for moderately reduced DLCO (73%). Immunological evaluation demonstrated high-titre ANA (1:5120) with anti-SSA/Ro52 specificity. Accessory salivary gland biopsy showed dense inflammatory infiltration (Chisholm stage III), supporting associated Sjögren’s disease. Synovial and skin biopsies confirmed MRH, demonstrating multinucleated histiocytic infiltrates with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm (figure 2D) and CD163 positivity without S100 or CD1a expression (table 2).

Methotrexate was discontinued because of digestive intolerance. Given persistent oligoarticular symptoms, upadacitinib was introduced as second-line therapy, resulting in complete resolution of cutaneous lesions, disappearance of joint manifestations including arthritis and regression of pulmonary ground glass opacities on CT (figure 1D). After 2 years, treatment is ongoing, with only mild residual skin lesions and no articular relapse. Pulmonary function tests remained stable at 2-year follow-up (DLCO 72% vs 73%, FVC 97% vs 97%). The organising interstitial pneumonia completely resolved.

Discussion

We report a series of MRH cases illustrating the broad spectrum of clinical manifestations associated with this condition and supporting the efficacy of JAKi across diverse presentations. JAKi proved effective not only for typical cutaneous and articular symptoms but also for less common manifestations, including microangiopathy and pulmonary involvement.

As consistently reported in the literature,1 cutaneous and articular involvement were the most frequent features. Skin lesions, typically pinkish papules affecting the face and chest, were the most constant but sometimes overlooked clinical sign.

Articular manifestations were the predominant and most disabling feature in all three cases and frequently guided therapeutic decisions. Two patients (patients 2 and 3) exhibited bilateral, symmetrical polyarthritis involving distal hand and wrist joints as well as larger joints, particularly the knees. The third patient (patient 1) reported inflammatory polyarthralgia without clinical synovitis, despite severe radiographic joint destruction. In all cases, articular involvement was refractory to conventional therapies, including conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (cDMARDs) and TNF inhibitors. Destructive arthropathy mainly affected the hands, with predominantly proximal and marginal erosions involving proximal and DIP joints, limited metacarpophalangeal involvement, absence of periosteal reaction or osteophytes, and limited joint space narrowing. DIP joint involvement—unusual in rheumatoid arthritis—remains a helpful distinguishing feature of MRH.

Our cases also highlight rare MRH manifestations, particularly scleroderma-like digital microangiopathy. Capillaroscopy revealed megacapillaries consistent with a scleroderma pattern. Such abnormalities are exceptionally rare in MRH; only one case associated with Raynaud’s phenomenon has been reported.7 In contrast, our first case showed similar findings without Raynaud’s syndrome, with complete resolution under upadacitinib.

Another atypical feature was interstitial lung disease (ILD), rarely described in MRH.8 9 Although histiocytic and macrophagic lung infiltration has been suggested, no fibrotic progression occurred in our case; instead, ground glass opacities improved under tofacitinib. These findings suggest that MRH may promote latent autoimmune or autoinflammatory processes driven by histiocytic infiltration and downstream inflammatory cascades.

Immunological testing may reveal autoantibodies, most commonly anti-SSA/Ro52kDa.10,12 Autoimmune comorbidities occur in approximately 15% of cases,2 including Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic sclerosis, systemic lupus erythematosus13 and dermatomyositis.14 No specific serological marker has been identified; however, a non-specific inflammatory syndrome with elevated ESR and CRP is observed in about half of patients. Lipid metabolism abnormalities are also reported, although their significance remains unclear.15 MRH is associated with malignancy in approximately 25% of cases,5 most often solid tumours, though haematological malignancies have also been described. Malignancy screening strategies remain heterogeneous. In our cohort, only one patient underwent positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET-CT), suggesting that systematic PET-CT assessment could be discussed during the initial evaluation of patients with MRH.

This case series supports the potential efficacy of JAKi in MRH, with improvement across cutaneous, articular and rarer manifestations such as ILD and microangiopathy. Although the level of evidence is inherently limited, the extreme rarity of MRH makes randomised trials unrealistic, underscoring the value of well-documented case-based data. Prior reports have similarly suggested benefit with tofacitinib and upadacitinib.6

A proposed mechanism involves modulation of the monocyte–macrophage lineage central to MRH pathogenesis. JAK signalling regulates differentiation and activation of monocyte-derived cells, consistent with the dermal infiltration by activated histiocytes and multinucleated giant cells characteristic of MRH. The JAK family mediates intracellular signalling downstream of cytokines such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and interleukin 6, with JAK2 playing a key role in myelopoiesis via Granulocyte–Macrophage Colony-Stimulating Factor (GM-CSF) signalling.16 JAK–STAT activation in macrophages through innate immune pathways further amplifies cytokine production.17 JAKi reduce macrophage differentiation and inflammatory activity. In vitro, tofacitinib decreases macrophage production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, likely through interactions with activated memory T cells.18

In rheumatoid arthritis, macrophages are major contributors to synovial inflammation. Both TNF and JAK inhibitors reduce macrophage infiltration, although JAKi may preferentially target IFN-activated subsets such as secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1+), also known as osteopontin macrophages19 and downregulate IFN-responsive chemokines involved in macrophage recruitment.20 The regression of scleroderma-like microangiopathy observed in one patient further illustrates the systemic effects of JAKi. Although the mechanism remains uncertain, direct histiocytic infiltration of small vessels is a plausible hypothesis. Tofacitinib may also exert effects in systemic sclerosis by modulating macrophage and fibroblast function through mitochondrial and oxidative phosphorylation pathways, with less direct endothelial involvement.21

In conclusion, we report a series of MRH cases illustrating the broad spectrum of clinical manifestations and supporting the efficacy of JAKi across both typical and atypical presentations, including cutaneous, articular, vascular and pulmonary involvement.
