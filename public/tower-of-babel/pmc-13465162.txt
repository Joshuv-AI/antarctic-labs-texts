Predictive value of frailty indices for postoperative outcomes of metastatic spine tumor: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hai-bo Xie, Ling-shan Fan, Qi Zhang, Xiao-yu Zhang, Si Wu, Xiao-qing Zhang, Kang-jian Li, Ai-feng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surgical management of spinal metastases aims to palliate symptoms but poses significant perioperative risks. Traditional tools like survival scores and comorbidity indices inadequately capture the multidimensional frailty in cancer patients, prompting interest in frailty indices for risk stratification. This meta-analysis evaluates the predictive value of frailty indices for postoperative outcomes in spinal metastasis surgery. Adhering to PRISMA guidelines, PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and other databases were systematically searched until May 2025. Observational clinical studies reporting frailty indices and postoperative outcomes (complications, LOS, nonroutine discharge, and survival rate) in spinal metastasis surgery were included. Study quality was assessed via Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) were calculated using fixed-effect model. A total of 12 studies involving 17,446 patients were included. The predictive value of several frailty indices, such as the 5-item/ 11-item modified frailty index (mFI-5/mFI-11), Metastatic Spinal Tumor Frailty Index (MSTFI), and Johns Hopkins Adjusted Clinical Groups (JHACG), were assessed. Some of the frailty indices predicted adverse outcomes: prolonged LOS (mFI-5 OR = 1.67, p = 0.014; JHACG OR = 2.65, p < 0.001), nonroutine discharge (MSTFI OR = 1.59, JHACG OR = 1.79; all p < 0.001), and complications (mFI-11 OR = 2.94, p = 0.003; MSTFI OR = 1.42, p < 0.001; JHACG OR = 1.54, p < 0.001). Survival prediction was inconsistent; only MSTFI correlated with 30-day mortality in one study (p < 0.05). Synthesized evidence from observational studies suggests that frailty indices were potential prognostic factors to predict post-operative morbidity, LOS, and discharge complexity. However, survival prognostication remains limited by tumor biology variability and methodological heterogeneity. Future efforts should integrate frailty assessments with tumor-specific factors to enhance prognostic precision and guide personalized perioperative optimization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06978-y.

Introduction

The surgical management of spinal metastases represents a critical yet complex aspect of oncologic care, where the primary goal is palliative—to alleviate pain, preserve neurological function, and improve quality of life [1, 2]. However, these procedures carry significant risks of adverse postoperative outcomes, including prolonged hospitalization, nonroutine discharge, complications, and mortality. Historically, survival prognostication tools such as the Tokuhashi and Tomita scores [3, 4], alongside performance status metrics like the Karnofsky performance status (KPS), have guided surgical decision-making. More recently, comorbidity indices such as the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) have been employed to assess perioperative risk [5, 6]. Despite their utility, these traditional tools often fail to fully capture the multidimensional physiological decline inherent in cancer patients, particularly those with metastatic disease. This gap has spurred interest in frailty indices as novel predictors of postoperative outcomes, offering a more holistic assessment of a patient’s physiological reserve and vulnerability to stressors.

Frailty, defined as an age-related decline in physiological resilience, has emerged as a critical determinant of surgical outcomes across disciplines [7, 8]. In spinal oncology, frailty reflects not only the cumulative burden of comorbidities but also the systemic effects of cancer, including sarcopenia, malnutrition, and impaired functional status. Several frailty metrics—such as the 5-item/ 11-item modified frailty index (mFI-5/mFI-11) [9, 10] and the Metastatic Spinal Tumor Frailty Index (MSTFI) [11]—have been specifically evaluated in this population. The mFI-5, derived from the Canadian Study of Health and Aging Frailty Index, aggregates five comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, chronic lung disease) to quantify frailty [12]. In contrast, the MSTFI, developed using nationwide databases, incorporates nine perioperative variables, including anemia, coagulopathy, and emergent surgical status, to predict complications and mortality [13]. Studies comparing these indices to the CCI consistently demonstrate their superior predictive capacity [14, 15]. For instance, Hersh et al. [14] found that both mFI-5 and MSTFI outperformed CCI in forecasting prolonged length of stay (LOS), nonroutine discharge, and complications in a cohort of 322 patients, with MSTFI showing particularly strong associations (OR [odds ratio] 1.63 per point for LOS). Similarly, Elsamadicy et al. [15], in a national database analysis of 1613 patients, reported that MSTFI was the only index significantly predictive of unplanned readmissions, while mFI-5 and CCI showed sporadic utility. Massaad et al. [16] explored ML algorithms to enhance the performance of MSTFI, though results emphasized the need for larger datasets and clinically relevant variables. Moreover, the integration of frailty assessments with existing prognostic tools—such as the H2-FAILS score for 30-day mortality—could yield composite models with enhanced discriminative power [17].

In summary, frailty indices represent a paradigm shift in risk stratification for spinal metastasis surgery, addressing the limitations of traditional comorbidity-based tools. Despite growing evidence, existing studies predominantly rely on retrospective designs, administrative databases, or single-center cohorts, introducing potential biases. What’s more, the field remains fragmented, with inconsistent definitions, variable validation, and unresolved questions about optimal implementation.

This meta-analysis seeks to synthesize existing evidence on the predictive value of frailty indices—particularly mFI-5/mFI-11 and MSTFI—for postoperative outcomes in spinal metastasis surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design and eligibility criteria

This meta-analysis adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [18].

Studies were included in accordance with the following criteria: (1) Studies evaluated the predictive value of frailty indices (e.g., MSTFI, Hospital Frailty Risk Score [HFRS], mFI-5, mFI-11) in patients undergoing surgery for spinal metastases. (2) Studies reported postoperative outcomes, including overall survival, mortality rates (at different time points), post-operative complications, LOS, nonroutine discharge, or cost. (3) Studies designed as cohort or case-control study. (4) Studies published in English as full-text articles.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) Studies involved primary spinal tumors. (2) Studies designed as case reports, case series, or literature/ systematic reviews. (3) Studies lacking related data about the impact of frailty indices on post-operative outcomes.

Data sources and search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, CENTRAL), Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases from inception to May 2025. Keywords included combinations of: “frailty index”, “frailty indices”, “modified frailty index”, “Hospital Frailty Risk Score”, “metastatic spinal tumor frailty index”, “MSTFI”, “mFI-5”, “spinal metastases”, “metastatic spinal tumors”, “spine surgery”, “postoperative complications”, “mortality”, “length of stay”, and “outcomes”. Manual searches of references from included studies and relevant reviews were performed to identify additional articles.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers extracted data using a standardized form, including: (1) Study characteristics (author, year, country, design, sample size). (2) Patients characteristics (age, sex, BMI [body mass index], primary tumors, location of metastasis, number of involved segments, performance status, medical history). (3) Treatment information (operation approaches and procedures, adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy). (4) Frailty indices assessed (e.g., MSTFI, HFRS, mFI-5, and mFI-11) and their scoring criteria. (5) Postoperative outcomes (follow-up period, complications, survival rate, LOS, non-routine discharge). (6) Prediction value of frailty indexes on post-operative survival.

Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies, evaluating selection, comparability, and outcome assessment. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed only for outcomes where comparable quantitative data were available. Studies that did not report a specific outcome or reported it in an incompatible format were excluded from the quantitative synthesis for that outcome but contributed to the qualitative review. Pooled effect estimates were calculated using OR as the effect size. Outcomes were stratified by frailty index type (e.g., MSTFI, mFI-5, mFI-11, and JHACG) and postoperative endpoints (complications, LOS and nonroutine discharge). Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, with I² > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Random-effect model was selected in case of significant heterogeneity. Since all pooling procedures involved no more than two eligible studies, sensitivity analyses and publication bias detection were thus not conducted.

All the aforementioned data analyses were conducted using R language version 4.3.1 (Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for differences.

Results

Study search and screening process

The flowchart depicting the literature search and screening process is presented in Fig. 1. Our systematic search yielded 290 records, supplemented by two additional records identified through other sources. After deduplication using EndNote software (removing 103 duplicates), 189 unique records underwent title/abstract screening. Of these, 168 studies were excluded as irrelevant to our eligibility criteria. The remaining 21 full-text articles were assessed for inclusion, leading to the exclusion of nine studies for the following reasons: four included primary spinal tumors, three lacked related data, and two were literature/systematic reviews. Twelve studies [10, 11, 13–15, 17, 19–24] were ultimately included in the meta-analysis.

Baseline characteristics of the included studies

A total of 12 studies investigating frailty indices in patients undergoing surgery for spinal metastases were included (Table 1). The sample size of individual studies ranged from 41 to 4583 participants, yielding a pooled cohort of 17,446 patients—including 10,538 males (60.75%) and 6,808 females (39.25%). Most studies (n = 9) originated from the United States [11, 13–15, 17, 20, 22–24], with one each derived from Japan [10], Canada [19], and New Zealand [21]. The methodological designs consisted of 10 retrospective cohort studies [11, 13–15, 17, 19–21, 23, 24], one prospective cohort study [10], and one case-control study [22]. Across the investigations, the mean patient age ranged from 58.0 to 67.3 years, and reported BMI values spanned 21.0 to 28.1 kg/m². Details regarding the top−3 primary tumors and location of metastasis are comprehensively listed in Table 1. Among five studies [10, 19, 22–24] reporting vertebral segment involvement, single-segment metastases accounted for 23.38% (1,102/4,713) of cases, while multi-segment involvement constituted 76.62% (3,611/4,713). Preoperative performance status was assessed using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scale in three studies and the KPS in one study.

The treatment characteristics of the included patients are detailed in Table 2. Considerable heterogeneity existed in the surgical and adjuvant treatment strategies among the included patient cohorts. Regarding surgical approaches, data were available in six studies [10, 11, 13, 14, 19, 22], showing that 1550 (15.9%), 7069 (72.6%) and 1112 (11.4%) patients were operated with anterior, posterior, and combined approaches respectively. The utilization and timing of adjuvant therapies varied substantially. Pre-operative radiotherapy was administered in cohorts from 4 studies [10, 14, 19, 21], while post-operative radiotherapy was reported in 3 studies [14, 19, 21]. LOS, recorded in five studies [11, 13–15, 19], exhibited a mean duration ranging from 7.6 to 11.2 days. Frailty indices evaluated included MSTFI (6 studies), mFI-5 (4 studies), mFI-11 (3 studies), HFRS (1 study), H₂-FAILS (1 study), and JHACG (1 study). A notable methodological heterogeneity was observed in the application of frailty indices. The MSTFI was primarily applied using predefined score cutoffs (e.g., mild, moderate, severe frailty), the JHACG was employed as a binary (frail vs. non-frail) classifier, while other indices like the mFI-5 and mFI-11 were often treated as continuous or ordinal scales. This variability in operationalization complicates direct head-to-head comparisons of their predictive performance. Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 12 observational studies encompassing 17,446 patients with spinal metastases to evaluate the predictive utility of frailty indices for postoperative outcomes. Our results demonstrate an association between frailty indices—notably the mFI-5, mFI-11, MSTFI, and JHACG—and adverse post-operative outcomes (LOS, nonroutine discharge, and complication risk), while revealing critical limitations in their prognostic value for survival endpoints.

Across all assessed frailty tools, higher burden significantly predicted prolonged hospitalizations, nonroutine discharges, and postoperative complications. The dose-dependent relationship was particularly striking with MSTFI: each point increase corresponded to 42%-75% greater odds of extended LOS, nonroutine discharge, or complication risk (OR = 1.75, 1.59 and 1.42, respectively; p < 0.05), while severe frailty quintupled complication risk compared to non-frail patients (OR = 5.18, p < 0.001). Such associations outperformed traditional metrics like the CCI, supporting studies that attribute this superiority to capture of multidimensional vulnerability for frailty indices—integrating malnutrition, functional decline, and systemic manifestations of malignancy beyond mere comorbidity counts [14, 15]. Spinal metastases surgery inherently disrupts mechanical stability, neurologic function, and hematopoietic niches, demanding substantial physiological reserves [25–27]. Frail patients—commonly exhibiting sarcopenia, immunosenescence, and impaired tissue repair—have difficulty in meeting these demands, explaining observed outcomes [28–30]. Importantly, the incorporation of emergent surgery status and coagulopathy (variables absent in conventional tools) in MSTFI likely enhances its discriminative capacity for acute care needs.

Contrasting these operational insights, frailty indices showed inconsistent utility in forecasting survival outcomes. MSTFI predicted 30-day mortality only in Bourassa-Moreau et al. [19], while lacking significance in others. Similarly, mFI-5/mFI-11 [21, 24] and JHACG [22] failed to predict overall survival in multiple studies. The inconsistent utility of frailty indices for predicting survival, in contrast to their more significant association with perioperative morbidity, LOS and nonroutine discharge, can be explained by the distinct clinical domains these outcomes represent. Frailty indices are primarily designed to measure a patients’ baseline physiological reserve and vulnerability to acute physiological stress. In the context of surgery, this reserve is directly tested by the systemic insult of anesthesia, tissue injury, blood loss, and metabolic demand. Therefore, it is conceptually coherent that a depleted reserve (higher frailty score) consistently predicts a higher likelihood of acute post-operative decompensation, manifesting as complications, prolonged hospitalization, or inability to return directly home. Conversely, long-term survival in metastatic spinal disease is predominantly governed by the biology of the underlying malignancy. Factors such as primary tumor histology, molecular subtype, mutational burden, systemic disease burden (e.g., visceral metastases), and sensitivity to adjuvant therapies are the principal determinants of overall survival [31–33]. These factors are largely independent of a patients’ pre-operative physiological reserve. Our synthesis suggests that frailty indices complement—but do not replace—oncology-specific risk prognostication. For instance, H₂-FAILS successfully predicted 30-day mortality by combining functional dependence and inflammatory markers with cardiac failure, creating an acute-risk overlay to existing tools. Similarly, the focus of MSTFI on anemia and emergent surgery status targets perioperative stability rather than long-term biology [13, 34, 35]. Integrating frailty metrics with molecularly-informed prognostic scores could thus create comprehensive “acute/chronic risk” stratification systems. For example, pairing MSTFI (operative risk) with a modern tool like the New England Spinal Metastasis Score (NESMS) could guide decisions around surgical radicality or preoperative optimization pathways. Despite compelling advantages, barriers to implementation persist. MSTFI, while predictive, requires nine variables not routinely collected in all health systems. Conversely, mFI-5 relies solely on electronic health record comorbidities but sacrifices discriminatory precision [17].

While this meta-analysis confirms the value of frailty indices in predicting postoperative morbidity, their potential extends beyond risk stratification to directly inform surgical candidacy. In the context of spinal metastases—where treatment goals are predominantly palliative—the decision to operate must balance potential benefits (pain relief, neurological preservation) against procedural risks and resource utilization. The consistent association between frailty indices and adverse outcomes such as prolonged hospitalization, nonroutine discharge, and complications suggests that these tools could be integrated into a structured, multidisciplinary decision-making framework. For instance, patients identified as “severely frail” by indices like the MSTFI (which showed a five-fold increase in complication risk) might be considered for less invasive alternatives (e.g., stereotactic body radiotherapy or percutaneous stabilization) or undergo targeted prehabilitation to optimize physiological reserve before surgery is contemplated. Furthermore, the predictive utility for LOS and discharge disposition underscores the cost-effectiveness implications of frailty assessment. Prolonged hospital stays and nonroutine discharges to skilled nursing or rehabilitation facilities represent significant healthcare expenditures. By identifying high-risk patients preoperatively, frailty assessment could guide resource allocation, support realistic goal-setting with patients and families, and potentially reduce avoidable healthcare costs associated with postoperative complications and extended care. Future health economic studies are needed to quantify the cost-benefit ratio of implementing routine frailty screening in this population.

This review has inherent limitations. First, the robustness of our quantitative synthesis is constrained by the limited number of studies (≤ 2) available for each specific frailty index-outcome pairing. Estimates derived from such a small number of studies are less stable and more susceptible to the influence of individual study characteristics or biases. Therefore, the pooled odds ratios for these subgroups should be interpreted with considerable caution and are not definitive. Second, significant inconsistency in the definition and categorization of frailty impedes synthesis and comparison. As noted, studies utilized different operationalizations: score cutoffs for MSTFI, binary categorization for JHACG, and ordinal/continuous scales for mFI-5/mFI-11. This lack of standardization limited the generalizability of pooled estimates and highlighting an urgent need for consensus on measurement and reporting standards in this field. Third, outcomes like cost, readmissions, and quality-of-life measures were underreported. Based on the synthesized evidence, future research should prioritize several interconnected directions to overcome current limitations: firstly, developing integrated prognostic models that combine frailty indices with tumor biology parameters, such as circulating tumor DNA and molecular subtypes, to address the observed inconsistencies in survival prediction and capture tumor heterogeneity; secondly, establishing dynamic frailty assessment protocols that track physiological changes across preoperative timepoints, enabling quantification of the impact from neoadjuvant therapies or prehabilitation on functional reserve and surgical outcomes. Finally, the conclusions of this review are primarily based on retrospective cohort studies, which constitute moderate-quality observational evidence. The inherent risks of bias in such designs—such as selection bias, unmeasured confounding, and reliance on administrative coding—mean that the reported associations, while robust across studies, should be validated in prospective, ideally multicenter, settings to establish higher-level evidence.

Conclusions

Synthesized evidence from observational studies suggests that frailty indices were potential prognostic factors to predict postoperative morbidity, LOS, and discharge complexity in patients undergoing spinal metastasis surgery. These findings suggest that frailty assessments capture a dimension of perioperative risk that is relevant in this vulnerable population. While frailty indices show promise as tools for perioperative risk awareness and stratification, their role as independent guides for overarching surgical decision-making remains to be fully established. Future prospective studies that integrate multidimensional frailty assessments with robust oncologic and surgical variables are needed to determine how best to incorporate these tools into comprehensive, patient-centered decision-making frameworks.
