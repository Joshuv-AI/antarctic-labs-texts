Mpox in a Neonate: A Case Report of a 14-day-old Baby in Nigeria
Authors: Ebenovbe Idemudia, Ayebo Evawere Sadoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

More data is needed to establish the frequency of transmission from asymptomatic carriers and from mother to child.Initially confined to 10 West and Central African countries, mpox assumed a global dimension in 2022. These symptoms may mimic other childhood skin infections like chicken pox and impetigo; thus, a high index of suspicion is needed among health care workers to establish a diagnosis.Case Presentation:This case report discusses the presentation, clinical course, and outcome of a 14-day-old neonate, Baby C, who presented with a fever and rash and became a confirmed case after PCR testing.Conclusion:This case report underscores the need for heightened clinical vigilance in recognizing mpox in neonates, where the rarity of the disease, absence of clear exposure history, and overlap with common neonatal skin conditions may delay diagnosis. It will also add to the limited literature on neonatal mpox, which is a rare presentation to date.

Introduction:

Mpox (HMPX), formerly referred to as Monkeypox, was first described in monkeys in the year 1958. [1] In 1970, the first Human mpox (HMPX) was reported in a rural community in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) in a 9-month-old child. [2]

HMPX has shown changes in its demographic features over the years. Initially confined to 10 West and Central African countries, the bulk of cases reported from the Democratic Republic of the Congo were in children less than 15 years of age.[3] However, outbreaks became a global concern in 2022, with spread to over 120 countries of the world as of 2024,[4] with an increased prevalence among male adults who had sex with men.[5]

Since the reemergence of mpox in 2017, Nigeria has recorded over a thousand confirmed cases of the disease. Data from Nigeria have consistently shown that the disease predominantly affects young adults aged 21–40 years, in keeping with long-established epidemiological patterns.[6] However, analysis of the 2024 Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) situation reports indicates a notable upsurge in reported cases among children aged 0–10 years,[7] even though young adults remain the most affected age group overall. This emerging trend underscores the need for increased vigilance in the paediatric population, particularly in vulnerable subgroups such as neonates, where clinical suspicion may be low and diagnosis easily missed.

Neonatal mpox, though rare, has been previously identified in a review of probable and confirmed cases.[8] In another case report, a 16-day old HIV exposed neonate had confirmed mpox following a rash noticed on the fourth day of life and with a similar rash in both parents who were also immunocompromised. While transplacental transmission seemed most plausible, post-natal transmission could not be ruled out.[9]

The identifiable risk factors for HMPX transmission include contact with infected wildlife or their carcasses, direct respiratory and mucosal contact with infected persons, sexual contact and contact with fomites.[10] While some data is available on vertical transmission from mother to child,[11-14] more data is needed to establish the existence and frequency of transmission from asymptomatic carriers to neonates.

MPox typically presents with a fever, rash, and lymphadenopathy across all age groups.[15] The signs and symptoms of mpox usually begin within a week but can start anywhere between 1–21 days after exposure. Symptoms typically last 2–4 weeks. The rash often begins on the face and spreads over the body, extending to the palms of the hands and soles of the feet. As it progresses, the rash develops into blisters that may be itchy or painful. As the rash heals, it dries up, crusts, and falls off.[16] The rash is typically painful, firm or rubbery, well-circumscribed, deep-seated, and often develops umbilication, which resembles a dot on the top of the lesion.[17]

It is worth noting that these symptoms may mimic other childhood skin infections, such as chickenpox and impetigo, when they present; thus, a high index of suspicion is needed among healthcare workers to establish a diagnosis in the paediatric age group. Whereas. Chickenpox and HMPV are both viral rashes with similar incubation periods. Varicella rash will commonly affect the face and back, with a flimsy membrane. On the other hand, impetigo, which is caused by bacteria, is often itchy, mainly on exposed areas, and is rarely associated with lymphadenopathy.

In the index case under review, the initial diagnosis was that of a bacterial skin rash. Further review classified the neonate as a probable case of mpox, and a laboratory test confirmed the diagnosis.

Case Presentation

Baby C is a 14-day-old male neonate who presented to a private specialist facility in Edo state with a 5-day history of fever and rash.

The fever was continuous with minimal transient relief when given antipyretics. The fever persisted till presentation.

The rash was first noticed on the face and abdomen. They were discreet and appeared vesicular but did not exude fluid when they opened before crusting. They rapidly spread to involve all other parts of the body, including the palms and soles within 48 hours of onset.

The child was also noticed to be irritable and to cry, mainly when the mother carried him. Review of the other systems showed no remarkable involvement.

The mother, who is a 28-year-old primiparous woman, booked her pregnancy at 12 weeks' gestational age and had uneventful antenatal care visits at both public and private health facilities. Booking parameters were normal, and viral screens, which were carried out, were unremarkable. However, the mother had a fever the day prior to delivery, which resolved the next day spontaneously, but there was no rash. She was not vaccinated against the smallpox virus.

Baby C was born at term, through spontaneous vaginal delivery. There were no immediate perinatal complications. The baby weighed 3.3kg at presentation.

On examination, Baby C. had a fever (38.6°C) and a generalized vesiculopapular rash. The rash was characterized by lesions in different stages of development, the earliest ones, which were on the face and periumbilical regions, already showed signs of crusting. This is as shown in Figures 1a and 1b below. The palms and the soles were not spared, as shown in Figures 2a and 2b below. The rash was firm and rubbery, with many appearing umbilicated, as seen in Figure 3 below. The lesions with open blisters had no active discharge. There were no palpably enlarged lymph nodes, and examination of other systems was unremarkable.

Since birth, the only external contact outside of immediate family was a visit from his paternal aunt and her toddler. The visit was for 3 days. None of them had a similar rash before coming or throughout the length of their stay. There were no known cases of HMPX in the family nor the immediate surroundings to the best of the mother's knowledge.

Samples were collected, and the swabs were sent for testing at the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) National Central Laboratory in Gaduwa, Abuja. A real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test from a swab of skin lesions confirmed the presence of monkeypox virus (Mpox) DNA. It also excluded the presence of the Varicella Zoster virus. The HIV 1 and HIV-2 screening test (HIVST) done for the mother was negative.

In the management of the baby, inpatient supportive care was provided, including the use of antipyretics for the fever and wound care for the open rash. Baby continued breastfeeding and was fed on demand to ensure adequate nutrition and hydration. Oral broad-spectrum antibiotics were given to prevent secondary bacterial infection of open sores.

The neonate was isolated to prevent further transmission. He, however, could not be separated from the mother, who was the sole caregiver.

Throughout the length of admission, the baby was monitored for complications, including secondary bacterial skin infections and encephalitis.

After 2 days of supportive care, Baby C’s fever subsided, and by the 3rd day of admission, which was the 8th day since the rash appeared, there were no signs of new rash. By the 6th day of admission, which was the 11th day of the illness, most of the existing rash showed signs of resolution. Figure 5 below shows the rash on day 11.

Due to financial constraints, the parents requested discharge after 7 days on admission and appealed for a waiver of their medical bills, which was granted. A follow-up visit, 3 days after discharge, showed that the rash had continued to heal- areas which previously had rash now had hypopigmented macules. No other complications were observed at the time of this report.

Discussion

HMPX is a zoonotic infection caused by the Monkeypox virus, which has assumed a global dimension since 2022. Nigeria has, in recent times, shown an increasing rise in infections among the paediatric age group of 0-10 years.[7] Very few reports describe the incidence and clinical course of neonatal mpox infection in Nigeria. This may be attributed to the fact that mpox is rare in neonates, and its initial presentation may resemble that of more common neonatal infections. Onyeaghala et al, however, described the clinical course of mpox in a 16-day-old HIV-exposed neonate.[9]

This case report describes the presentation and clinical outcome of a 14-day-old neonate who had clinical and laboratory confirmation of mpox infection.

He had the typical features of fever and rash, but no palpably enlarged lymph nodes and no systemic complications. The rash was umbilicated, firm, rubbery, and involved the soles and palms. This, however, did not prevent the initial misdiagnosis of impetigo, which is a commonly encountered skin condition in the paediatric population in this part of the world. This implies that clinicians running paediatric clinics should have a high index of suspicion for any child presenting with a vesiculopustular or vesiculopapular rash. They can be labelled as probable cases and excluded or confirmed after further clinical examination and testing.

Apart from mimicking other paediatric skin lesions, the absence of an obvious or identifiable route of transmission at the time of presentation may have contributed to the challenge of diagnosing mpox. In this case, maternal fever without a rash prior to delivery and minimal postnatal exposure complicated clinical suspicion.

This highlights the possibility of postnatal transmission from an asymptomatic carrier like the paternal aunt, or from infected fomites at any point between the health facility where he was born, and the home environment. However, given the mother’s history of fever 24 hours before delivery, the possibility of vertical transmission cannot be excluded.

Financial constraints in resource-poor settings are another factor that should be considered in the efforts to limit the spread of mpox. Patients may present late into the appearance of the rash and may refuse inpatient isolation at health facilities because of a lack of funds. As in the index case, presentation was on the 5th day of rash appearance and inpatient care could be obtained only for the rash appearance, and only for 7 days with a waiver.

The unavailability of mpox vaccines at the time of this report means that all contacts of the index case were put under a 21-day surveillance as recommended by the World Health Organization. None has been reported to have any signs suggestive of mpox infection as of the time of this report.

Conclusion

Mpox in neonates is exceedingly rare, and this case highlights its presentation and clinical course. It brings to the fore and underscores the importance of considering other paediatric skin lesions in the differential diagnosis of fever and rash in neonates, even in low-exposure settings.

The case also identifies the potential for vertical transmission, although definitive evidence is lacking, and calls for increased vigilance and reporting of similar cases. Early diagnosis and supportive care are key to effectively managing such cases.
