Drawdowns of diffusions
Paavo Salminen, Pierre Vallois

Abstract
In this paper we give excursion theoretical proofs of Lehoczky’s formula (in an extended form allowing a lower bound for the underlying diffusion) for the joint distribution of the first drawdown time and the maximum before this time, and of Malyutin’s formula for the joint distribution of the first hitting time and the maximum drawdown before this time. It is remarkable – but there is a clean explanation – that the excursion theoretical approach which we developed first for Lehoczky’s formula provides also a proof for Malyutin’s formula. Moreover, we discuss some generalizations and analyze the pure jump process describing the maximum before the first drawdown time when the size of the drawdown is varying.



Keywords:
Brownian motion, Poisson point process, additive functional, local time, pure jump process.

MSC Classification:
60J60, 60J65, 60G40.

1
Introduction

To calculate probability distributions of functionals of stochastic processes is an essential/central/important issue/topic per se but also, of course, many applications of stochastic processes and stochastic models call for explicit formulas for such distributions. This theme is developed also in this paper. We focus here on one-dimensional diffusions and functionals associated with the running maximum and the position of the underlying diffusion. In particular, we analyze various aspects and distributions of drawdowns from the running maximum. To be more precise, let
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote a one-dimensional diffusion defined on an interval. Then the running maximum process and the drawdown process are defined via

M_{t}:=\max_{0\leq s\leq t}X_{s}\ \ {\rm{and}}\ \ DD_{t}:=M_{t}-X_{t},

respectively. We introduce also the stopping time when the process first drops below the maximum by
\delta>0
units as

\theta_{\delta}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,M_{t}-X_{t}>\delta\};

(1.1)

also called as the first drawdown time of size
\delta
. Another object of our interest is the maximum drawdown up to time
t>0
given by

D^{-}_{t}:=\max_{0\leq s\leq t}(M_{s}-X_{s}).

(1.2)

Lehoczky derived in
[
15
]
an expression for the joint Laplace transform of the variables of
\theta_{\delta}
and
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
when
X
is generated via an Itô SDE. The distribution for Brownian motion with drift was characterized earlier by Taylor in
[
31
]
. Taylor’s proof is based on particular properties of Brownian motion; especially, on the spatial homogeneity. Lehoczky’s approach uses a passage to the limit in a discrete setting. Fitzsimmons in
[
10
]
addresses Lehoczky’s formula via excursion theory. The first main theme of our paper is to present a proof of Lehoczky’s formula via excursion theory. Our proof uses slightly different “angle of attack” than Fitzsimmons’ approach in
[
10
]
. Some misprints in
[
10
]
are also corrected.

Another main issue in the paper is to study the maximum drawdown up to a hitting time, that is, the random variable
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
where
H_{\eta}
stands for the first hitting time of the state
\eta
, i.e.,

H_{\eta}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,X_{t}=\eta\}.

(1.3)

We determine the joint distribution of
H_{\eta}
and
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
.
The distribution of
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
for Brownian motion with drift was calculated by Brockwell in
[
4
]
via a passage to the limit in a discrete setting. Malyutin in
[
18
]
treated more general processes satisfying regularity conditions on some key distributions of the underlying process. He then deduced the joint distribution of
H_{\eta}
and
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
when passing to the limit in a discrete setting. We demonstrate that the joint distribution can be otained via the excursion theoretical approach developed here to prove Lehoczky’s formula. For this the observation connecting the distributions of
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
and
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
(see (
3.8
)) is crucial. It is seen that the approach yields a very appealing formula characterizing the distribution where also the function appearing in Lehoczky’s formula is present.

In addition to the papers refered above there are many others serving both theoretical and applied interests. For alternative proofs for Taylor’s theorem we refer to Williams
[

[

The application indicated in Taylor’s and Lehoczky’s papers concerns a selling strategy on a stock market where the asset should be sold when the price has fallen below the previous maximum by a given fixed amount. Much of the more recent litterature on drawdowns (and drawups) finds its applications in financial mathematics; in particular, analyzing the properties of the options based on drawdowns. We refer, e.g., to

Concerning the maximum drawdown before a hitting time the potential applications discussed in Brockwell’s and Malyutin’s papers are in queuing theory and in mathematical biology when modeling cell growth. Later also this functional is much studied in the framework of financial mathematics, see, e.g.,
[

In addition to the topics listed above we discuss the process

. It is seen that this process is Markov and many characteristics of it are presented. In
[

the process
(D^{-}_{H_{\eta}})_{\eta\geq 0}
is similarly analyzed. As indicated above, the one-dimensional marginals of these processes are closely related. Hence, a natural task is to compare the probabilistic structures of the processes more closely. In particular, we deduce the Markov generator of
(D^{-}_{H_{\eta}})_{\eta\geq 0}
from the generator of
(M_{\theta_{\delta}})_{\delta\geq 0}

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section some preliminary facts, needed later in the paper, on linear diffusions are presented.
Two classes of diffusions are introduced for which the results are derived. In particular, geometric Brownian motion when the parameters are such that the process does not tend to 0 is included. Taylor’s and Lehoczky’s formulas are given in Section 3. Lehoczky’s formula in

2
Preliminaries

Let
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a regular one-dimensional diffusion in the sense of Itô and McKean
[
13
]
, see also
[
3
]
. In particular,
X
is a strong Markov process with continuous sample paths taking values on an interval
I\subseteq{\bf R}
.
We let
{\bf P}_{x}
and
{\bf E}_{x}
denote the probability measure and
the expectation operator associated with
X
when
X_{0}=x
. The standard notation
{\cal F}_{t}
is used for the

\sigma
-algebra generated by
X
up
to time
t\geq 0,
and we set
{\cal F}:={\cal F}_{\infty}.
It is also assumed that
X
is not killed inside
I
.

Then it is known from the theory of one-dimensional diffusions that
for
\alpha>0

{\bf E}_{x}(\exp(-\alpha\,H_{y}))=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle{\frac{\psi_{\alpha}(x)}{\psi_{\alpha}(y)}}},&x\leq y,\\
{\displaystyle{\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}(x)}{\varphi_{\alpha}(y)}}},&x\geq y,\\
\end{cases}

(2.1)

where
H_{y}
is the first hitting time of
y
(see (
1.3
)) and
\varphi_{\alpha}
(
\psi_{\alpha}
) are a decreasing (increasing) solution of the generalized differential equation

\frac{d}{dm}\frac{d}{dS}u=\alpha u,

(2.2)

associated with
X
. The notation
m
and
S
are used for the speed measure and the scale function, respectively, of
X.

Imposing appropriate boundary conditions (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
)
determine
\psi_{\alpha}
and
\varphi_{\alpha}
uniquely up to multiplicative constants. The Wronskian
w_{\alpha}
is defined as

\displaystyle w_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\psi^{+}_{\alpha}(x)\varphi_{\alpha}(x)-\psi_{\alpha}(x)\varphi^{+}_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69054pt=\psi^{-}_{\alpha}(x)\varphi_{\alpha}(x)-\psi_{\alpha}(x)\varphi^{-}_{\alpha}(x),

(2.3)

where the superscripts
+
and
-
denote the right and left
derivatives with respect to the scale function. Notice that
w_{\alpha}
does not depend on
x
.
It is well-known (see
[
13
]
p. 150) that

g_{\alpha}(x,y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}w^{-1}_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha}(x)\varphi_{\alpha}(y),\quad x\leq y,\\
w^{-1}_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha}(y)\varphi_{\alpha}(x),\quad x\geq y,\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

serves as a resolvent density (also called the Green function) of
X,
i.e., for any Borel subset
A
of
I

G_{\alpha}(x,A):={\bf E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\zeta}{\rm e}^{-\alpha t}{\bf 1}_{A}(X_{t})\,dt\right)=\int_{A}g_{\alpha}(x,y)\,m(dy),

(2.5)

where
\zeta
denotes the lifetime of the diffusion.

For simplicity, it is assumed throughout the paper that the scale function
S
is continuously differentiable and the speed measure
m
has a continuous derivative with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Under these assumptions,
\varphi_{\alpha}
and
\psi_{\alpha}
are continuously differentiable. However, at many places in this paper, it is practical to consider derivatives with respect to the scale function. Hence, we keep the notations for the derivatives introduced above; e.g.,

\psi^{+}_{\alpha}(x)=\psi^{-}_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{d\psi_{\alpha}(x)}{dx}\frac{dx}{dS(x)}=\frac{1}{S^{\prime}(x)}\,\psi^{{}^{\prime}}_{\alpha}(x).

Let
l
and
r
denote the left hand and the right hand, respectively, end point of
I
. To fix idea, we let, throughout the whole paper,
l\geq-\infty
and
r=+\infty
. Moreover, we concentrate ourselves to two specific classes of underlying diffusions:

Class 1.
X
is recurrent with a)
S(+\infty)=+\infty,S(l)=-\infty
or b)

S(+\infty)=+\infty
,
S(l)>-\infty
,
l>-\infty
and reflecting.

Class 2.
X
is transient with a)
S(+\infty)<\infty
,
S(l)=-\infty
, or b)
S(+\infty)<\infty
,
S(l)>-\infty
,
l>-\infty
and reflecting.

In particular, standard Brownian motion belongs to Class 1a and Brownian motion with drift
\mu>0
to Class 2a. Notice, e.g., that killed BM on
(-\infty,0)
(killed when hitting 0) is excluded. For a geometric Brownian motion
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with

X_{t}:=x\exp\left(\left(\mu-\sigma^{2}/2\right)t+\sigma W_{t}\right),

where
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard BM,
x>0
, and
\mu,\sigma\in{\bf R}
, it follows by checking the scale function (cf.
[
3
]
p. 136) that
X
is in Class 1a in case
\mu=\sigma^{2}/2
, and in Class 2a if
\mu>\sigma^{2}/2
.

3
Main theorems

3.1
Drawdown time

In this section we recall Lehoczky’s formula characterizing the joint distribution of the first drawdown time
{\theta_{\delta}}
and the maximum
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
at this moment. Assuming
\theta_{\delta}<\infty
– we show that this holds for diffusions in class 1 – then
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
is bigger than the initial value of
X
but also that
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
is bigger than
\delta+l
which is important to remember in case
l>-\infty
. Below (
3.1
) is Lehoczky’s formula. Taylor’s formula for standard Brownian motion is taken up in Example
3.9
and displayed in (
3.15
). In Theorem
3.1
Lehoczky’s formula is extended to include the possibility that
l>-\infty
. As explained in the introduction, Lehoczky’s theorem is proved in Section
4
using excursion theory.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
be in class 1 or 2.
Then the joint Laplace transform of
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
and
\theta_{\delta}
is given for
x\in I
,
\delta>0
,
\alpha>0
and
\beta\geq 0
by

{\bf E}_{x}\left(\exp(-\alpha\theta_{\delta}-\beta M_{\theta_{\delta}})\right)=\frac{\psi_{\alpha}(x)}{\psi_{\alpha}(x\vee(\delta+l))}\int_{x\vee(\delta+l)}^{\infty}c_{\alpha}(y;\delta)\qquad\qquad\qquad

(3.1)

\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\displaystyle\exp\left(-\beta y-\int_{x\vee(\delta+l)}^{y}b_{\alpha}(z;\delta)\,dS(z)\right)dS(y)

where
\vee
is the usual maximum operator,

b_{\alpha}(y;\delta):=\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)\psi^{-}_{\alpha}(y)-\varphi^{-}_{\alpha}(y)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)}{\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)\psi_{\alpha}(y)-\varphi_{\alpha}(y)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)}

(3.2)

and

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}(y;\delta)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}(y)\psi^{-}_{\alpha}(y)-\varphi^{-}_{\alpha}(y)\psi_{\alpha}(y)}{\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)\psi_{\alpha}(y)-\varphi_{\alpha}(y)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{\alpha}}{\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)\psi_{\alpha}(y)-\varphi_{\alpha}(y)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)}.

(3.3)

Moreover, for
y\geq x\vee(\delta+l)

{\bf P}_{x}(M_{\theta_{\delta}}>y)=\exp\left(-\int_{x\vee(\delta+l)}^{y}\frac{dS(z)}{S(z)-S(z-\delta)}\right).

(3.4)

Remark 3.2
.

1.

In (
3.1
) the convention
\exp(-\infty)=0
is used in case
\theta_{\delta}=+\infty
. Clearly, if
\theta_{\delta}<+\infty
then also
M_{\theta_{\delta}}<+\infty
.

2.
For a diffusion in class 2 the formula (
3.1
) is valid also for
\alpha=0
with
\psi_{0}\equiv 1
and
\varphi_{0}(x)=S(+\infty)-S(x)
. If the diffusion is in class 1 approriate functions
\psi_{0}
and
\varphi_{0}
do not exist. To find the Laplace transform of
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
one could try to passage to limit as
\alpha\to 0
. However, in the proof in Section
4
we do not exploit this approach but instead work directly with
\alpha=0
.

3.
From (
3.1
) it immediately follows that for the bounded and measurable function
f
we have

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{x}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha\theta_{\delta}}\,f(M_{\theta_{\delta}})\right)=\int_{x}^{\infty}f(y)\exp\left(-\int_{x}^{y}b_{\alpha}(z;\delta)dS(z)\right)\,c_{\alpha}(y;\delta)dS(y).

We conclude this subsection with a result stating the finiteness of
{\theta_{\delta}}
in Class 1. We demonstrate via examples that it is possible that
X
converges to
+\infty
so fast that
\theta_{\delta}=+\infty
with positive probability for all values on
\delta
, and that there are diffusions in Class 2 such that a.s.
\theta_{\delta}<+\infty
for all values on
\delta
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
X
be in Class 1. Then
\theta_{\delta}
is finite for all
\delta>0
and so is
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
.

Proof.

Consider the distribution of
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
as given in (
3.4
). For the integral on the right hand side we have since
S
is non-decreasing

\displaystyle\int_{x\vee(\delta+l)}^{y}\frac{S^{\prime}(z)dz}{S(z)-S(z-\delta)}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{x\vee(\delta+l)}^{y}\frac{S^{\prime}(z)dz}{S(z)-S(x-\delta)}

\displaystyle\geq\hbox{\rm ln\,}\left(\frac{S(y)-S(x-\delta)}{S({x\vee(\delta+l)})-S(x-\delta)}\right).

Since, by the assumption,
\lim_{y\to\infty}S(y)=+\infty
it follows that

{\bf P}_{0}(M_{\theta_{\delta}}=+\infty)=0,

which implies that
\theta_{\delta}
is finite for all
\delta>0
, as claimed.
∎

Example 3.4
.

Assume
l=0
and

S(z):=1-\exp\big(-\hbox{\rm e}^{z}\big),\quad z\geq 0.

Then
X
is in Class 2 (case b)) and

{\bf P}_{x}(\theta_{\delta}<+\infty)=\exp\Big(-\int_{\hbox{\rm e}^{x\vee\delta}}^{+\infty}\frac{du}{\hbox{\rm e}^{(1-\rho)u}-1}\Big)<1

(3.5)

where
\rho:=\hbox{\rm e}^{-\delta}
. Indeed,

S(z)-S(z-\delta)=\exp\big(-\hbox{\rm e}^{z}\big)\Big(\exp\big((1-\rho)\hbox{\rm e}^{z}\big)-1\Big)

and

{\bf P}_{x}(\theta_{\delta}<+\infty)=\exp\Big(-\int_{x\vee\delta}^{\infty}\frac{\hbox{\rm e}^{z}dz}{\exp\big((1-\rho)\hbox{\rm e}^{z}\big)-1}\Big)

Setting
u=\hbox{\rm e}^{z}
yields (
3.5
).

Example 3.5
.

Assume
l=0
and
\displaystyle S(z):=1-\hbox{\rm e}^{-z},\,z\geq 0
. Reflecting Brownian motion with drift 1 is a particular example satisfying these assumptions.
Clearly,
X
is in Class 2 (case b). It straightforward to show (and left to the reader) that
{\bf P}_{x}(\theta_{\delta}<+\infty)=1
.

3.2
Maximum drawdown

Recall from (
1.2
) the definition of
D^{-}_{t}
. Here we study this functional up to the first hitting time
{H_{\eta}}
, where
\eta
is assumed to be bigger than the initial value of
X
. The distribution of

D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
is given in (
3.6
) below. Brockwell derived in
[
4
]
this distribution for Brownian motion with non-negative drift. Malyutin in
[
18
]
gives a formula characterizing the joint distribution of
{H_{\eta}}
and
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
corresponding to (
3.10
) below, but the rôle of
l
is not transparent in Malyutin’s work. The formula in
[
18
]
is made explicit for Brownian motion with drift, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes and Bessel processes. We remark also that in
[
28
]
, see Proposition 2.3, the distribution of
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
is derived using stochastic calculus.

Theorem 3.6
.

Assume that the underlying diffusion is in class 1 or 2. Then
{H_{\eta}}
is finite
{\bf P}_{x}
-a.s. for
x<\eta
, and the
distribution of
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
is given for
x<\eta
and
0\leq y\leq\eta-l
by

{\bf P}_{x}(D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}<y)=\exp\left(-\int_{x\vee(y+l)}^{\eta}\frac{S(dz)}{S(z)-S(z-y)}\right).

(3.6)

A striking observation is the similarity of the right hand sides of the formulas (
3.6
) and (
3.4
). Indeed, there is a close connection between
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
and
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
which can be deduced without any knowledge of the explicit forms of the distributions. To see this, note that

\theta_{\delta}=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,D^{-}_{t}>\delta\},

(3.7)

and then a.s.

\{D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}\leq\delta\}=\{\theta_{\delta}\geq H_{\eta}\}=\{M_{\theta_{\delta}}\geq\eta\},

(3.8)

where the first equality follows from (
3.7
) and the second one from the monotonicity of
t\mapsto M_{t}
. We may thus deduce from Lehoczky’s formula (
3.4
) the distribution function of
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
, and vice versa.

Next we characterize the joint distribution of
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
and
H_{\eta}
. The proof is presented in Section 5 and follows from excursion theoretical calculations needed also for the proof of Lehozcky’s formula.

Theorem 3.7
.

The joint distribution of
D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}
and
H_{\eta}
is determined for
\eta>x\vee(y+l)
by

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt{\bf E}_{x}\left(\exp(-\alpha H_{\eta});\,D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}<y\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi_{\alpha}(x)}{\psi_{\alpha}(x\vee(y+l))}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\exp\left(-\int_{x\vee(y+l)}^{\eta}b_{\alpha}(z,y)S(dz)\right),

(3.9)

where
\alpha>0
and
b_{\alpha}
is as in (
3.2
).

Remark 3.8
.

An alternative proof of (
3.9
) can be done via conditioning. To explain this briefly, let
T
be an exponentially distributed random variable with mean
1/\alpha
. Assume that
T
is independent of
X
. Recall that
r=+\infty
and consider for
l<x<\eta

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt{\bf E}_{x}\left(\exp(-\alpha H_{\eta});\,D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}<y\right)

\displaystyle={\bf P}_{x}\left(H_{\eta}<T,\,D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}<y\right)

\displaystyle={\bf P}_{x}\left(D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}<y\,|\,H_{\eta}<T\right){\bf P}_{x}\left(H_{\eta}<T\right).

The conditional distribution above can be found by applying the theory of
h
-transforms (and (
3.6
)). This yields

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{x}\left(\exp(-\alpha H_{\eta});\,D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}<y\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\frac{\psi_{\alpha}(x)}{\psi_{\alpha}(\eta)}\exp\left(-\int_{x\vee(y+l)}^{\eta}c_{\alpha}(z,y)\frac{\psi_{\alpha}(z-y)}{\psi_{\alpha}(z)}S(dz)\right),

(3.10)

and it can be shown that (
3.10
) and (
3.9
) are equivalent.

3.3
Additional remarks

It is possible to develop the above concepts and results to various directions. Here we indicate some of these generalizations.

1. More general drawdown time depending on the maximum.

Let
\phi
be a continuous positive function consider the stopping time

\displaystyle\theta_{\phi}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,M_{t}-X_{t}=\phi(M_{t})\}.

Note that if the function
\phi
is constant and equal to
\delta
, then
\theta_{\phi}
coincides with the stopping time
\theta_{\delta}
defined by (
1.1
).
The joint distribution of
M_{\theta_{\phi}}
and
\theta_{\phi}
is characterized in
[
15
]
.
Recall (in case
l=-\infty
) therefrom

{\bf P}_{x}(M_{\theta_{\phi}}>y)=\exp\left(-\int_{x}^{y}\frac{dS(z)}{S(z)-S(z-\phi(z))}\right).

It would be easy to adapt our proof of Theorem
3.1
to determine the distribution of the pair
\big(M_{\theta_{\phi}},\theta_{\phi}\big)
.
The stopping time
\theta_{\phi}
appears also in studies on Skorokhod embedding, see Azéma and Yor
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
.

2. Maximum drawdown for killed diffusions.

Let
g
be a piecewise continuous non-negative (but non-vanishing) function and define an additive functional associated with a diffusion
X
via

A_{g}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}g(X_{s})\,ds,

(3.11)

where
g
is bounded and piecewise continuous. Let
T
be an exponentially distributed random variable with mean
1
and independent of
X
. We assume that
X
in class 1 or 2 and introduce for
t>0

\widehat{X}_{t}:=\begin{cases}X_{t},&A_{g}(t)<T,\\
\partial,&A_{g}(t)\geq T,\\
\end{cases}

(3.12)

where
\partial
is the so called cemetary state isolated from the state space of
X
. As is well known,
\widehat{X}=(\widehat{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a diffusion and it is said that
\widehat{X}
is obtained from
X
by killing via the additive functional defined in (
3.11
). Let
\varphi_{\alpha,g}
and
\psi_{\alpha,g}
be a decreasing and an increasing, respectively, solution of the generalized differential equation

\frac{d}{dm}\frac{d}{dS}u=(\alpha+g)u

(3.13)

describing the resolvent density of
\widehat{X}
similarly as explained in (
2.1
), (
2.1
), (
2.3
), and (
2.4
) for the (unkilled) diffusion
X.

Then letting
\widehat{\bf E}
denote the expectation operator associated with
\widehat{X}
it can be proved

\displaystyle\widehat{\bf E}_{x}\left(\exp(-\alpha H_{\eta});\,D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}<u\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt={\bf E}_{x}\left(\exp(-\alpha H_{\eta}-A_{g}(H_{\eta}));\,D^{-}_{H_{\eta}}<u\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\frac{\psi_{\alpha,g}(x)}{\psi_{\alpha,g}(\eta)}\exp\left(-\int_{x\vee(u+l)}^{\eta}c_{\alpha,g}(z,u)\frac{\psi_{\alpha,g}(z-u)}{\psi_{\alpha,g}(z)}S(dz)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\exp\left(-\int_{x\vee(u+l)}^{\eta}b_{\alpha,g}(z,u)S(dz)\right),

(3.14)

where
c_{\alpha,g}
and
b_{\alpha,g}
are constructed as
c_{\alpha}
and
b_{\alpha}
in (
3.3
) and (
3.2
), respectively, but with the functions
\varphi_{\alpha,g}
and
\psi_{\alpha,g}
.

Example 3.9
.

In our first example
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion. The joint distribution of

4
Proofs of Theorems
3.1
and
3.7

This section is organized in 4 subsections. In the first one we present the notation and the basic facts from the classical excursion theory for excursions from a point. The second one concerns the theory for excursions below the maxima of the underlying diffusion. A crucial element hereby is the master formula (
4.9
) due to Fitzsimmons
[
9
]
, see also
[
23
]
. These two subsections can be seen as background material to make the paper more readable. The main ingredients of the proof are given in Subsections
4.3
and
4.4
. In particular, the functions
b_{\alpha}
and
c_{\alpha}
, cf. (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), respectively, are expressed in terms of the excursion law, cf.
[
10
]
. Finally, the proofs of Theorems
3.1
and

3.7
are collected from the presented facts at the end of Subsection
4.4
.

4.1
Excursions around a fixed point

Let
(L^{y}_{t}\,:\,t\geq 0,y\in I)
denote a jointly continuous version of local time for
X
normalized to be the occupation density relative to
m
. Then it holds

{\bf E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha t}dL^{y}_{t}\right)=g_{\alpha}(x,y)

(4.1)

where
g_{\alpha}
is the Green function as given in (
2.4
).
Fixing a level
y\in I
, let
(T^{y}_{s}\,:\,s\geq 0)
be the right continuous inverse of
(L^{y}_{t}\,:\,t\geq 0)
. Define the excursions
around
y
via

\kappa^{y}_{t}(s):=\begin{cases}X_{T^{y}_{t-}+s},&0\leq s<T^{y}_{t}-T^{y}_{t-},\\
y,&s\geq T^{y}_{t}-T^{y}_{t-}.\end{cases}

(4.2)

Then,
\kappa^{y}_{t}
belongs to the space

U^{y}:=\big\{e\in C^{y}:\exists\ \zeta(e)<\infty\ {\text{such that}}\ e(t)\not=y\ {\text{for\ all}}\ 0<t<\zeta(e)

{\text{and}}\ e(t)=y\ {\text{for\ all}}\ t\geq\zeta(e)\big\},

where
C^{y}
denotes the class of continuous functions defined on
[0,+\infty)
such that
e(0)=y
. Clearly,
\zeta(e)=\inf\{t>0;e(t)=y\}
for
e\in U^{y}
. Let
{\cal U}^{y}
denote the smallest
\sigma
-algebra in
U^{y}
making all coordinate mappings

t\mapsto e(t)
measurable. Then
(U^{y},{\cal U}^{y})
is called an excursion space.
Notice that if
T^{y}_{t}-T^{y}_{t-}=0
then
\kappa^{y}_{t}(s)=y
for all
s\geq 0
, and this is also taken to be an element in
U^{y}
called
e^{y}
. The Itô excursion law
n^{y}
, which is a
\sigma
-finite non-negative measure defined on
(U^{y},{\cal U}^{y})
, is determined by the identity

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{x}\Big(\sum_{t>0}Z_{T^{y}_{t-}}F(\kappa^{y}_{t})\Big)

\displaystyle={\bf E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}Z_{T^{y}_{t}}dt\right)n^{y}(F)

\displaystyle={\bf E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}Z_{t}dL^{y}_{t}\right)n^{y}(F),

(4.3)

where
F
is a non-negative measurable function defined on
U^{y}
such that
F(e^{y})=0
, and
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a non-negative and progressively measurable process.
For (
4.3
), see
[
25
]
(1.10) Proposition p. 475 and (2.6) Proposition p. 483 where the latter reference is for Brownian motion but can be extended to our case. We refer also to Maisonneuve
[
17
]
(4.1) Theorem p. 401 where general strong Markov processes are considered.

To give an explicit description of
n^{y}
, let
(Q^{y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the Markovian semigroup for the diffusion
{X^{y}}
obtained from
X
by killing at the hitting time
H_{y}
, that is, for
x\not=y
and
A
a Borel set in
(l,r)\setminus\{y\}

Q^{y}_{t}(x,A):={\bf P}_{x}\left(X^{y}_{t}\in A\right)={\bf P}_{x}\left(X_{t}\in A,\,t<H_{y}\right).

It is well known that
{X^{y}}
has a transition density
q^{y}
, say, with respect to the speed measure. Hence it holds

{\bf P}_{x}\left(X^{y}_{t}\in A\right)=\int_{A}q^{y}(t;x,z)\,m(dz).

Recall that
q^{y}(t;x,y)=0
and that
q^{y}(t;\cdot,\cdot)
is a symmetric function of
x
and
z,
i.e.,
q^{y}(t;x,z)=q^{y}(t;z,x)
. Finally, let
f^{H}_{xy}
denote the density of the
{\bf P}_{x}
-distribution of
H_{y}
. Now we are ready to characterize the finite dimensional distributions of the Itô excursion law
n^{y}
for excursions below
y
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
x_{i}<y,i=1,2,\dots,n,
and
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<...<t_{n}
it holds

\displaystyle n^{y}\left(e(t_{1})\in dx_{1},e(t_{2})\in dx_{2},...,e(t_{n})\in dx_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\eta_{t_{1}}(dx_{1})Q^{y}_{t_{2}-t_{1}}(x_{1},dx_{2})\cdot...\cdot Q^{y}_{t_{n}-t_{n-1}}(x_{n-1},dx_{n}),

(4.4)

where
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
constitutes an entrance law for
(Q^{y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, i.e., it satisfies

\int_{(l,r)\setminus y}\eta_{t}(dx)Q^{y}_{s}(x,A)=\eta_{t+s}(A),

and is given by

\eta_{t}(dx)=f^{H}_{xy}(t)m(dx).

(4.5)

Proof.

The result is extracted from
[
26
]
No’s 48-49 pp. 416-420, see also
[
29
]
. The only thing to clarify is the explicit formula (
4.5
) for the entrance law. However, in ibid., we have the Laplace transform

\int_{0}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha t}\eta_{t}(A)\,dt=\frac{G_{\alpha}(y,A)}{g_{\alpha}(y,y)}

with
G_{\alpha}
as given in (
2.5
). Applying here the formulas (
2.4
) and (
2.1
) yields
(
4.5
).
∎

In the next proposition we give a useful way to determine the excursion law via a limiting procedure (cf.
[
22
]
p. 437). The construction is done for excursions below
y
, a similar formula is valid for excursions above
y
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
f_{i},i=1,2,\dots,n,
It holds for
x_{i}<y,i=1,2,\dots,n,

\displaystyle n^{y}\left(e(t_{1})\in dx_{1},e(t_{2})\in dx_{2},...,e(t_{n})\in dx_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}\frac{Q^{y}_{t_{1}}(y-\epsilon,dx_{1})}{S(y)-S(y-\epsilon)}\,Q^{y}_{t_{2}-t_{1}}(x_{1},dx_{2})\cdot...\cdot Q^{y}_{t_{n}-t_{n-1}}(x_{n-1},dx_{n}).

(4.6)

We need, in fact, an integrated form of the formula (
4.6
) valid for functionals of excursions. For this aim, let

\displaystyle C^{*}:=\big\{f:[0,\infty)\mapsto{\bf R},\ {\text{continuous, and}}\ \exists\ \zeta(f)<\infty

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\ {\text{such that}}\ f(t)=f(\zeta(f))\ {\text{for\ all}}\ t\geq\zeta(f)\big\}.

In particular, notice that
U^{y}\subset C^{*}
for all
y\in I
.
The next proposition can be proved from Proposition
4.2
via monotone class-arguments.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
F
be a measurable, bounded and non-negative functional defined in
C^{*}
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{U^{y}_{-}}F(e)n^{y}(de)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}\frac{1}{S(y)-S(y-\epsilon)}\,{{\bf E}_{y-\epsilon}\left(F(X^{y})\right)},

where
{U^{y}_{-}}\subset U^{y}
denotes the excursions below
y
.

4.2
Excursions below the maxima

We proceed to study the excursion process below the maxima (cf. also Fitzsimmons
[
9
]
and Pitman and Yor
[
23
]
). Assume that
X_{0}=x
and define for
y>x
the first passage time over the level
y
via

H_{y+}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,X_{t}>y\}.

(4.7)

The excursions below the maxima are defined as follows. For
y>x
assume first that
H_{y+}-H_{y}>0
then we put

\xi^{y}(t):=\begin{cases}X_{H_{y}+t},&\quad 0\leq t\leq H_{y+}-H_{y}\\
y,&\quad t>H_{y+}-H_{y}.\end{cases}

(4.8)

In case
H_{y+}-H_{y}=0
we define
\xi^{y}(t):=y
for all
t\geq 0
. Notice that
\xi^{y}\in U_{-}^{y}
. The process
\Xi_{x}=((y,\xi^{y})_{y\geq x})
is a Poisson point process taking values in
[x,r)\times U_{-}^{y}
. For a definition of a point process, see, e.g. section XII 1 in
[
25
]
. For Poisson point processes
\Xi_{x}
we have the master formula (see
[
9
]
)

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt{\bf E}_{x}\Big(\sum_{x\leq y}Z_{H_{y}}F(\xi^{y}){\bf 1}_{\{H_{y}<+\infty\}}\Big)

(4.9)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt={\bf E}_{x}\Big(\int_{x}^{\infty}Z_{H_{y}}n^{y}_{-}(F){\bf 1}_{\{H_{y}<+\infty\}}dS(y)\Big),

where

F
is a measurable non-negative functional defined on
U_{-}^{y}
such that
F(e^{y})=0
for constant function
e^{y}
,
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a non-negative and progressively measurable process
and
n^{y}_{-}
is the restriction of
n^{y}
on the set of excursions below
y
. From (
4.9
) it is seen that the intensity measure of
\Xi_{x}
is given by
(dy,de)\mapsto dS(y)\,n^{y}_{-}(de)
for
y\geq x
and
e\in U_{-}^{y}
.

To specialize the master formula (
4.9
) to our particular case involving
\theta_{\delta}
and
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
we recall (cf. (
3.7
)) that a.s. for all
y
and
\delta

\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}}\}=\{H_{y}\leq\theta_{\delta}\}.

(4.10)

Moreover,

\{H_{y}\leq\theta_{\delta}\}=\{H_{y}<\theta_{\delta}\}.

(4.11)

To see (
4.11
) notice that if
H_{y}=\theta_{\delta}
then also
X_{H_{y}}=X_{\theta_{\delta}}
and
M_{H_{y}}=M_{\theta_{\delta}}
which leads to a contradiction. Letting
Z
be a non-negative and progressively measurable process then, since
\theta_{\delta}
is a stopping time, also the process defined by

t\mapsto Z^{\prime}_{t}:=Z_{t}\,{\bf 1}_{\{t<\theta_{\delta}\}}1_{\{M_{t}>\delta+l\}}

is non-negative and progressively measurable. Since

Z^{\prime}_{H_{y}}=Z_{H_{y}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}}\}}1_{\{y>\delta+l\}}

we obtain from (
4.9
) when applied for
Z^{\prime}_{H_{y}}
the identity

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt{\bf E}_{x}\Big(\sum_{{x\vee(\delta+l)}<y}Z_{H_{y}}F(\xi^{y}){\bf 1}_{\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}},H_{y}<+\infty\}}\Big)

(4.12)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt={\bf E}_{x}\Big(\int_{x\vee(\delta+l)}^{\infty}Z_{H_{y}}n^{y}_{-}(F){\bf 1}_{\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}},H_{y}<+\infty\}}dS(y)\Big),

which is the key to the proofs of Theorem
3.1
and
3.7
.

4.3
Two crucial formulas

In the next lemma, the functions
b_{\alpha}
and
c_{\alpha}
, cf. (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) are expressed in terms of the excursion law
n^{y}
, cf. Fitzsimmons
[
10
]
. These expressions are used in the next section.

Lemma 4.4
.

It holds for
\delta>0
and
y>\delta+l

\displaystyle n^{y}_{-}\left(1-\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha\zeta(e)}\,{\bf 1}_{\{H_{y-\delta}(e)=\infty\}}\right)=\begin{cases}b_{\alpha}(y;\delta)&\alpha>0,\\
(S(y)-S(y-\delta))^{-1},&\alpha=0,\\
\end{cases}

(4.13)

and

\displaystyle n^{y}_{-}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y-\delta}(e)}\,;\,H_{y-\delta}(e)<\infty\right)=\begin{cases}c_{\alpha}(y;\delta),&\alpha>0,\\
(S(y)-S(y-\delta))^{-1},&\alpha=0,\\
\end{cases}

(4.14)

where
H_{y-\delta}(e)
denotes the first hitting time of
{y-\delta}
for a generic excursion
e
.

Proof.

For
0<\varepsilon<\delta
recall the formulas, see
[
13
]
p. 29, where the formulas for Brownian motion are discussed, see also Darling and Siegert
[
6
]
,

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{y-\varepsilon}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y-\delta}}\,;\,H_{y-\delta}<H_{y}\right)=\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\varepsilon)\psi_{\alpha}(y)-\varphi_{\alpha}(y)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\varepsilon)}{\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)\psi_{\alpha}(y)-\varphi_{\alpha}(y)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)}

(4.15)

and

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt{\bf E}_{y-\varepsilon}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y}}\,;\,H_{y}<H_{y-\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\varepsilon)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)-\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\varepsilon)}{\varphi_{\alpha}(y)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)-\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)\psi_{\alpha}(y)},

(4.16)

where
y-\varepsilon>l
.
We prove first (
4.14
). This is a straightforward application of Proposition
4.3

\displaystyle n^{y}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y-\delta}(e)}\,;\,H_{y-\delta}(e)<\infty\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{S(y)-S(y-\varepsilon)}{\bf E}_{y-\varepsilon}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y-\delta}}\,;\,H_{y-\delta}<H_{y}\right).

(4.17)

The fact that the functions
\varphi_{\alpha}
and
\psi_{\alpha}
have the scale derivatives yields the claimed formula. The claim (
4.13
) is proved using the same approach. For this, consider first

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{y-\varepsilon}\left(1-\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{H_{y-\delta}>H_{y}\}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=1-\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\varepsilon)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)-\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\varepsilon)}{\varphi_{\alpha}(y)\psi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)-\varphi_{\alpha}(y-\delta)\psi_{\alpha}(y)},

and applying again Proposition
4.3
gives the formula (
4.13
); we skip the details. The formulas (
4.14
) and (
4.14
) in case
\alpha=0
are obtained by applying the well known fact that, e.g.,

{\bf P}_{y-\varepsilon}\left(H_{y-\delta}<H_{y}\right)=\frac{S(y)-S(y-\varepsilon)}{S(y)-S(y-\delta)}.

∎

4.4
Completion of the proof

We begin with an identity which allows us to use the formalism of the excursion theory.

Lemma 4.5
.

The following identity holds for all excursions
\xi^{y},\,y>l,
below the maxima

{\bf 1}_{\{M_{\theta_{\delta}}<z\}}=\sum_{x\vee(\delta+l)<y<z}{\bf 1}_{\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}},H_{y-\delta}(\xi^{y})<\infty\}}.

(4.18)

Proof.

We can take
z>{x\vee(\delta+l)}
. Assume that
M_{\theta_{\delta}}=y^{*}
. Then, if
y^{*}\geq z
both sides of (
4.18
) equal 0. For
y^{*}<z
the indicator on the right hand side is 0 for all
y>y^{*}
. Also for
y<y^{*}
the indicator is 0 since for such
y
the excursion
\xi^{y}
does not hit
y-\delta
. For
y=y^{*}
the indicator is 1, and (
4.18
) holds also for
y^{*}<z
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

For
\alpha\geq 0

{\bf E}_{x}\Big(\,\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha\theta_{\delta}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{M_{\theta_{\delta}}<z\}}\Big)=\int_{x\vee(\delta+l)}^{z}\lambda(y;x)n^{y}_{-}\big(\ \hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y-\delta}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{H_{y-\delta}<\infty\}}\big)dS(y)

(4.19)

where for
y>{x\vee(\delta+l)}

\lambda(y;x):={\bf E}_{x}\Big(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}}\}}\Big).

(4.20)

Proof.

Using (
4.18
)

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha\theta_{\delta}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{M_{\theta_{\delta}}<z\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\vee(\delta+l)<y<z}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha\theta_{\delta}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}},H_{y-\delta}(\xi^{y})<\infty\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\vee(\delta+l)<y<z}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha\theta_{\delta}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{H_{y}\leq{\theta_{\delta}},H_{y-\delta}(\xi^{y})<\infty\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\vee(\delta+l)<y<z}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{H_{y}\leq{\theta_{\delta}}\}}\,\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y-\delta}(\xi^{y})}\,{\bf 1}_{\{H_{y-\delta}(\xi^{y})<\infty\}},

(4.21)

where in the second step we have applied (
4.10
) and for the third step notice that

{\theta_{\delta}}=H_{y}+H_{y-\delta}(\xi^{y}),

(4.22)

when
M_{\theta_{\delta}}=y
. Hence we conclude

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha\theta_{\delta}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{M_{\theta_{\delta}}<z\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\vee(\delta+l)<y<z}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}},H_{y}<\infty\}}\,\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y-\delta}(\xi^{y})}\,{\bf 1}_{\{H_{y-\delta}(\xi^{y})<\infty\}},

and can then apply directly (
4.12
) to prove the claim.
∎

We proceed now with the calculation of the function

as defined in (

).

Lemma 4.7
.

For
y>x\vee(\delta+l)

\lambda(y;x)=\frac{\psi_{\alpha}(x)}{\psi_{\alpha}(x\vee(\delta+l))}\exp\left(-\int_{x\vee(\delta+l)}^{y}b_{\alpha}(z;\delta)\,dS(z)\right),

(4.23)

where

is given in (

Proof.

Let
y>x\vee(\delta+l)
and recall that

. Note first

{\bf 1}_{\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}}\}}=\prod_{x\vee(\delta+l)<z<y}{\bf 1}_{\{H_{z-\delta}(\xi^{z})=\infty\}},

and

H_{y}=H_{x\vee(\delta+l)}+\sum_{x\vee(\delta+l)<z<y}\left(H_{z+}-H_{z}\right).

Then

\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}}\}}=e^{-\alpha H_{x\vee(\delta+l)}}\prod_{x\vee(\delta+l)<z<y}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha\left(H_{z+}-H_{z}\right)}{\bf 1}_{\{H_{z-\delta}(\xi^{z})=\infty\}}.

(4.24)

Recall the following elementary fact: given a sequence
a_{i},i=1,2,\cdots,
such that
a_{i}\in[0,1]
for all
i
then with
a_{0}:=1

1-\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}a_{i}\right)(1-a_{k}).

(4.25)

We apply (
4.25
) for the countable product in (
4.24
) to obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274ptA:=e^{-\alpha H_{x\vee(\delta+l)}}-\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha H_{y}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{y\leq M_{\theta_{\delta}}\}}

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt=e^{-\alpha H_{x\vee(\delta+l)}}\sum_{x\vee(\delta+l)<z<y}\left(\prod_{x\vee(\delta+l)<u<z}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha\left(H_{u+}-H_{u}\right)}{\bf 1}_{\{H_{z-\delta}(\xi^{z})=\infty\}}\right)

(4.26)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\times\left(1-\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha\left(H_{z+}-H_{z}\right)}{\bf 1}_{\{H_{z-\delta}(\xi^{z})=\infty\}}\right).

Using (
4.24
) for the product in (

5

(M_{\theta_{\delta}})_{\delta\geq 0}

5.1
Basic properties

Let the underlying diffusions
X
be such that
\theta_{\delta}
is finite a.s. for all values on
\theta
. Then, by the contnuity of the paths of
X
, both
\theta_{\delta}
and
M_{\theta_{\delta}}
tend to
\infty
as
\delta\to\infty
. Recall that
r=+\infty
, and to fix ideas we take
l=-\infty
. It is assumed also that
X(0)=0
which implies that
M_{\theta_{0}}=0
. Clearly,
M_{\theta}
is a non-decreasing, right continuous, pure jump process. On every compact subinterval of
(0,\infty)

M_{\theta}
undertakes finitely many jumps and is constant between the jumps. On any interval
[0,\varepsilon)
with
\varepsilon>0
there are countably infinitely many jumps accumulating at 0 and
M_{\theta_{\delta}}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
.

We study first the Markov property of
(M_{\theta_{\delta}})_{\delta\geq 0}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

For a Borel-measurable
f:{\bf R}_{+}\times{\bf R}_{+}\mapsto{\bf R}_{+}
and
\delta>\rho\geq 0

{\bf E}_{0}\left(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}},{\theta_{\delta}})\,|\,{\cal F}_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)=Q_{\rho,\delta}\left(M_{\theta_{\rho}},{\theta_{\rho}};f\right),

(5.1)

where

\displaystyle Q_{\rho,\delta}\left(y,s;f\right)

\displaystyle={\bf E}_{y-\rho}\left(f(y,s+H_{y-\delta})\,;\,H_{y-\delta}<H_{y}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+{\bf E}_{y-\rho}\left(F_{\delta}(y,H_{y})\,;\,H_{y}<H_{y-\delta}\right)

and for
a>0

\displaystyle F_{\delta}(y,{a}):={\bf E}_{y}\left(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}},a+\theta_{\delta})\right).

In particular, for a Borel-measurable
f:{\bf R}_{\mapsto}{\bf R}_{+}

{\bf E}_{0}\left(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}})\,|\,{\cal F}_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)=Q_{\rho,\delta}\left(M_{\theta_{\rho}};f\right),

(5.2)

where

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046ptQ_{\rho,\delta}\left(y;f\right)

\displaystyle:=f(y){\bf P}_{y-\rho}\left(H_{y-\delta}<H_{y}\right)+{\bf E}_{y}\left(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}})\right){\bf P}_{y-\rho}\left(H_{y}<H_{y-\delta}\right),

(5.3)

and, hence,
(M_{\theta_{\delta}})_{\delta\geq 0}
is a Markov process with respect to its own filtration
{\cal F}^{M}_{\theta}=({\cal F}^{M}_{\theta_{\tau}})_{\tau\geq 0}:=(\sigma\{M_{\theta_{u}};u\leq\tau\})_{\tau\geq 0}
.

Proof.

For notational simplicity, we prove (
5.2
) and leave (
5.1
) to the reader.
For
\delta>\rho\geq 0
introduce

\displaystyle U:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,;\,X_{t+\theta_{\rho}}\leq M_{\theta_{\rho}}-\delta\}\ {\text{and}}\ V:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,;\,X_{t+\theta_{\rho}}=M_{\theta_{\rho}}\}.

In case
U<V
, we have
M_{\theta_{\delta}}=M_{\theta_{\rho}}
and

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{0}\left(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}})\,{\bf 1}_{\{U<V\}}\,|\,{\cal F}_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)=f(M_{\theta_{\rho}})g\left(X_{\theta_{\rho}},M_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)=f(M_{\theta_{\rho}})g\left(M_{\theta_{\rho}}-\rho,M_{\theta_{\rho}}\right),

where

\displaystyle g(x,y)={\bf P}_{x}\left(H_{y-\delta}<H_{y}\right)=\frac{S(y)-S(x)}{S(y)-S(y-\delta)}

(5.4)

If
U>V
then
\theta_{\delta}=\theta_{\rho}+\inf\{t\geq 0\,;\,M_{t+\theta_{\rho}}-X_{t+\theta_{\rho}}>\delta\}
and evoking the strong Markov property at
\theta_{\rho}
yields

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{0}\left(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}})\,{\bf 1}_{\{U>V\}}\,|\,{\cal F}_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)={\bf E}_{X_{\theta_{\rho}}}\left(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}})\right)\left(1-g\left(X_{\theta_{\rho}},M_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)\right).

This completes the proof of (
5.2
). The Markov property of
(M_{\theta_{\delta}})_{\delta\geq 0}

follows from (
5.2
) by applying the tower property of conditional expectations:

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{0}\left(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}})\,|\,{\cal F}^{M}_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)

\displaystyle={\bf E}_{0}\left({\bf E}_{0}\left(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}})\,|\,{\cal F}_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)\,|\,{\cal F}^{M}_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)=Q_{\rho,\delta}\left(M_{\theta_{\rho}};f\right),

and, similarly,

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{0}\left(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}})\,|\,M_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)

\displaystyle=Q_{\rho,\delta}\left(M_{\theta_{\rho}};f\right).

∎

Corollary 5.2
.

For
\delta>\rho\geq 0
, and if
M_{\theta_{\rho}}>v
then

\displaystyle{\bf P}_{0}\left(M_{\theta_{\delta}}>v\,|\,M_{\theta_{\rho}}\right)=1,

(5.5)

and if

\displaystyle{\bf P}_{0}\left(M_{\theta_{\delta}}>v\,|\,M_{\theta_{\rho}}=y\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\frac{S(y-\rho)-S(y-\delta)}{S(y)-S(y-\delta)}\exp\left(-\int_{y}^{v}\frac{dS(z)}{S(z)-S(z-\delta)}\right).

(5.6)

Proof.

The claim in (

is nondecreasing. For (

\displaystyle=f(y)+\left({\bf E}_{y}\big(f(M_{\theta_{\delta}})\right)-f(y)\big)\frac{S(y-\rho)-S(y-\delta)}{S(y)-S(y-\delta)}.

(5.7)

Substituting

where

. Formula (

Remark 5.8
.

Results presented above for the process
(M_{\theta_{\theta}})_{\theta\geq 0}
can be seen in the general framework of piecewise constant real-valued strong Markov processes. Indeed, let
Y=(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be such a process which at time
s\geq 0
is located at
x\in{\bf R}
. Define

(5.20)

and notice that the first time the process leaves the state

after time

is then
s+{\cal{T}}_{x,s}

\displaystyle\phi_{s}(t):=\phi_{x,s}(t)

\displaystyle:={\bf P}({\cal{T}}_{x,s}\geq t)

Then using the strong Markov property it is seen that
for all

\displaystyle\phi_{s}(t+u)

\displaystyle=\phi_{s}(t)\phi_{t+s}(u)=\phi_{s}(u)\phi_{u+s}(t).

Consequently, if for all
s\geq 0

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{s}(0+):=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi_{s}(\varepsilon)-1}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\phi_{s}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\phi^{\prime}_{r+s}(0+)dr\right).

(5.22)

To see that the formulas (
5.21
) and (

) are indeed valid in our particular case let
Y_{t}:=M_{\theta_{t}}
. Introduce

{\cal{T}}_{y,\rho}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:M_{\theta_{t+\rho}}>y\}

where

. Then with
T^{+}_{\rho}
as defined in (
5.11
) we have

{\cal{T}}_{y,\rho}=T^{+}_{\rho}-\rho,

and

\phi_{\rho}(t)={\bf P}_{0}(T^{+}_{\rho}\geq\rho+t|M_{\theta_{\rho}}=y).

From (
5.13
)

\phi_{\rho}(t)=\frac{S(y)-S(y-\rho)}{S(y)-S(y-t-\rho)},

(5.23)

and it is straightforward to check that (
5.21
) holds for the expression on the right hand side of (
5.23
). If

exists we have

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{\rho+s}(0+)=-\frac{S^{\prime}(y-\rho-s)}{S(y)-S(y-\rho+s)},

and it is easily seen that

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{S^{\prime}(y-\rho-r)}{S(y)-S(y-r-\rho)}dr\right)=\frac{S(y)-S(y-\rho)}{S(y)-S(y-t-\rho)}
