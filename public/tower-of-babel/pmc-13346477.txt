The relationship between normative beliefs about aggression, perceived online anonymity, and cyber aggression among university students: the mediating role of moral disengagement
Authors: Zhenjun Jiao, Luqiu Liu, Shuguo Yang, Di Hu, Yunhao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven how quickly information technology is developing and how diverse online worlds are becoming, research on the cyber behavior and psychological mechanisms of university students has become an important topic in educational and cyber psychology. Unlike previous studies, this research innovatively integrates normative beliefs about aggression and perceived online anonymity into a unified analytical framework, and introduces moral disengagement as a key mediating variable to explain the underlying psychological mechanism. Grounded in the General Aggression Model and Social Cognitive Theory, this cross-sectional study collected questionnaire data from 738 university students to thoroughly examine the relationships among these variables. The results indicate that normative beliefs about aggression and perceived online anonymity are positively associated with cyber aggression, and moral disengagement exerts a partial mediating effect. This study extends our understanding of cyber aggression through an empirical focus on the distinctive features present among university students, investigating the dual pathways of normative beliefs about aggression and perceived online anonymity, and systematically revealing the key mediating mechanism of moral disengagement. These results offer a fresh theoretical framework for comprehending how cyber aggression develops, as well as empirical evidence and practical guidance for promoting cyber ethics education and behavioral interventions in higher education.Keywords: Cyber aggression, Normative beliefs about aggression, Perceived online anonymity, Moral disengagement, University studentsSubject terms: Psychology; Psychology; Science, technology and society

Introduction

The deep integration of the internet and digital technologies has established cyberspace as a central domain for university students to engage in learning, socializing, and entertainment. However, these technological conveniences also introduce new social risks, including cyber aggression (CA). CA refers to intentional acts of harm carried out through digital devices while deliberately avoiding face-to-face confrontation1. As a major group of internet users, university students are in a critical period of identity exploration and values formation. Their social activities heavily rely on online environments, which may also make them more likely to exhibit CA2. It is worth noting that most existing studies focus on environmental factors, such as family, school, and societal influences3,4, while ignoring the internal psychological processes underlying CA among university students. In this context, gaining a deeper understanding of the intrinsic cognitive mechanisms related to such behavior is of significant importance.

From the perspective of internal cognitive factors, there is a close linkage between an individual’s acceptance of aggression and their related conduct. This internal standard, known as normative beliefs about aggression (NBA), reflects the extent to which a person considers aggressive behavior acceptable under specific circumstances. This factor could be pivotal in the conversion of aggressive thoughts into tangible actions5.

Concurrently, the unique nature of the online environment subtly interacts with individual decision-making. When individuals perceive their identity as hidden and their actions as difficult to trace, this sense of perceived online anonymity (POA) tends to coincide with reduced anticipation of consequences. It also shows connections to behaviors typically restrained in offline settings. In the context of CA, POA frequently appears in situations where university students engage in malicious comments, privacy violations, or cyberbullying. These conditions make it easier for students to exhibit CA when social oversight is lacking6.

Beyond these cognitive and environmental factors, individuals may also employ specific psychological mechanisms to reconcile their actions with moral standards. Within the complex interplay of cognition and behavior, people can use various mental strategies to reinterpret their own actions in response to moral self-condemnation. This mechanism, referred to as moral disengagement (MD), captures the cognitive process through which individuals align their harmful behaviors with their moral framework. It has been linked to reduced inner moral conflict and increased likelihood of engaging in aggression7.

Therefore, uncovering the underlying mechanisms connecting NBA, POA, MD, and CA will not only enhance theoretical models of online aggression but also give a scientific foundation for encouraging cyber ethics education and behavioral interventions in universities. The purpose of this work is to close this research gap by systematically examining the relationships among NBA, POA, and CA among university students, while testing the mediating role of MD. These efforts carry significant theoretical and practical value.

Theoretical foundation framework and hypotheses

Theoretical foundation

This study employs the General Aggression Model (GAM) as its core theoretical framework to investigate the formation mechanisms of CA among university students. According to GAM, aggressive behavior stems from the interaction between personal and situational factors. These factors influence an individual’s cognition, affect, and arousal, ultimately leading to either aggressive or non-aggressive outcomes in social interactions8. In recent years, researchers have widely applied this model to explain various forms of online aggression, including cyberbullying and hostile behavior in digital environments, demonstrating its strong applicability and explanatory power in cyber contexts9–11.

Within this framework, NBA and POA are regarded as key factors closely associated with CA. NBA represents an individual’s cognitive assessment of how socially sanctioned aggressive conduct is within their peer group. When individuals perceive that their group condones aggression, these individuals are more likely to engage in aggressive behavior7. POA describes the self-perception of having one’s identity obscured in online settings. This perception reduces the pressure of social monitoring and diminishes personal accountability, thereby creating conditions that facilitate aggressive behavior4.

Furthermore, Social Cognitive Theory (SCT)12 posits that human conduct is influenced not only by personal factors but also by observational learning and imitation. Within this theoretical perspective, MD plays a critical role. MD describes a psychological process through which individuals cognitively restructure and rationalize aggressive behaviors by reconstructing their perception of the actions through justification, responsibility reassignment, and consequence misrepresentation13. These cognitive mechanisms are more readily engaged under conditions of robust NBA or substantial POA. This allows them to rationalize aggressive actions, thereby reducing the inhibitory effect of internal moral standards on behavior7,14. From a social cognitive standpoint, MD alters an individual’s cognitive appraisal of behavioral outcomes, making them more inclined to choose aggressive rather than prosocial responses.

In summary, through a theoretical integration of GAM and SCT, this research creates a model to investigate the fundamental processes by which NBA and POA are associated with online aggressive behavior, with particular emphasis on the role of MD. This integration not only deepens the theoretical understanding of how CA develops but also provides a robust foundation for future empirical research.

Hypotheses

Normative beliefs about aggression and cyber aggression

NBA refer to an individual’s cognitive judgment regarding the appropriateness or acceptability of aggressive behavior15,16. This construct comprises two dimensions: general beliefs, which reflect one’s overall view of aggression, and retaliatory beliefs, which specifically indicate the acceptance of aggression in provocative situations. NBA reflects the extent to which an individual condones aggressive acts and is closely linked to their likelihood of engaging in such behavior16.

In the context of online interactions, NBA shows a strong association with CA. CA encompasses any intentional behavior carried out through digital devices that aims to harm others and is perceived as unwanted by the target1. Common forms of CA include cyberbullying. Research indicates a positive linkage between NBA and the exhibition of aggressive behaviors17. Elevated NBA levels are associated with greater engagement in online aggression18. This association is especially pronounced in student samples: individuals with stronger NBA demonstrate higher frequencies of CA, encompassing cyberbullying behaviors7.

In summary, as a key cognitive factor related to moral reasoning and behavioral choices, NBA demonstrates a significant association with CA. Therefore, this research explores the mechanisms underlying the association between NBA and CA to enhance understanding of the causes of online aggression. We propose the following hypothesis:

H 1: NBA are positively associated with CA.

Perceived online anonymity and cyber aggression

POA denotes an individual’s perception that their identity remains unidentifiable or untraceable by others in online environments19. Previous research has indicated a positive association between POA and CA20. Within online settings, activation of aggression-associated triggering elements shows correspondence with heightened aggressive impulses in users. POA acts as a “facilitator” that amplifies these impulses and heightens the likelihood of engaging in CA21. These findings indicate that higher POA levels correspond to increased CA in the presence of aggression propensity factors, including stressful experiences or anxiety. This conclusion is further supported by empirical evidence. For instance, Guo et al.,6 implemented a cross-sectional research design with participation from 3069 university students and found that those with higher POA were more likely to demonstrate stronger CA under the influence of anxiety, compared to those with lower POA. Therefore, based on previous research, the current research examines the following theoretical proposition:

H 2: POA is positively associated with CA.

Normative beliefs about aggression, moral disengagement and cyber aggression

According to Bandura et al.,22 SCT, MD refers to a set of cognitive mechanisms through which individuals reinterpret their role in unethical conduct, thereby mitigating or denying responsibility for their actions. Bandura,13 proposed that individuals internalize moral standards to regulate social behavior, and that moral development evolves through dynamic interrelations among individual values, actions, affective states, and societal contexts. However, moral reasoning alone is not a consistent predictor of behavior; individuals may demonstrate inconsistencies between their moral principles and actual behaviors. Instead, they may selectively activate self-regulatory mechanisms to disengage morally in certain situations, leading to actions that deviate from ethical judgments23. Behaviors such as CA, which are typically considered immoral, may be reinforced through mechanisms of MD.

Research indicates that MD mediates the relationship between NBA and bullying behavior7. This observation is consistent with the GAM24, offering a theoretical perspective to explain how NBA influences individual aggressive behavior. According to GAM24, NBA serves as a key individual factor that associates indirectly with aggression through MD. Specifically, these beliefs activate a hostile interpretation bias, which encourages individuals to adopt moral disengagement mechanisms such as denying responsibility or minimizing consequences to justify their actions. This process weakens moral self-regulation and ultimately leads to aggressive behavior7. Individuals with stronger NBA often demonstrate elevated MD25. Therefore, NBA not only correlates directly with aggression but also indirectly reinforces aggressive behaviors, including bullying, through MD7. While this mechanism has been validated in traditional bullying contexts, the mediating role of MD between NBA and CA remains insufficiently examined empirically. Grounded in the preceding theoretical framework and empirical evidence, the current investigation advances the following predictive statement:

H 3: MD mediates the relationship between NBA and CA.

Perceived online anonymity, moral disengagement and cyber aggression

According to deindividuation theory26, when individuals perceive themselves to be in an anonymous state, these individuals show a greater tendency to depart from socially accepted moral standards and display MD. Existing research indicates a positive correlation between perceived anonymity and moral disengagement: individuals tend to experience greater moral disengagement when they feel completely anonymous14. In online environments, individuals with high perceived anonymity are particularly prone to a psychological state of deindividuation. This state is often associated with reduced self-regulation, diminished sense of responsibility, and the emergence of moral disengagement, which may further relate to the occurrence of CA6. Prior investigations have reliably demonstrated a marked positive connection between MD and conventional bullying behaviors27,28, and a similar mechanism may underlie its relationship with cyber aggression. The available theoretical and empirical support suggests that MD could function as an important mediating variable in the relationship between POA and CA. Specifically, higher levels of perceived anonymity accompany increased MD, which in turn indirectly facilitates aggressive behavior in online contexts. Therefore, a deeper investigation into the mediating mechanism of moral disengagement holds significant theoretical value for understanding the formation pathways of cyber aggression. Accordingly, the current investigation puts forward the following predictive proposition:

H 4: MD mediates the relationship between POA and CA.

The current study

Although existing research has explored the relationships between NBA, POA, and CA, the current literature still exhibits several notable limitations. First, few studies have systematically examined the group differences in CA based on demographic and behavioral variables such as gender, age, grade level, and daily internet use. These factors may lead to significant variations in CA across different subgroups, yet they have not been sufficiently analyzed within a unified framework.

Second, although some studies suggest that MD may be associated with CA, the mediating pathways through which it operates between NBA, POA, and this form of aggression lack empirical validation. Accordingly, the present investigation seeks to examine these underexplored areas by exploring the connections among these variables, with particular attention to the intermediary function of MD. Through this approach, we seek to elucidate the formation mechanisms of CA among university students. The research model is shown in Fig. 1.

Method

Sample and data collection

Data were gathered utilizing the online survey tool Questionnaire Star (www.Sojump.com). To enhance sample generalizability and minimize sampling bias, a random sampling approach was utilized to select participants from three higher education institutions in Shandong, China. The investigation was carried out between July and August, 2025. Prior to participation, we informed all participants about the research objectives. Participation was voluntary, and we explicitly guaranteed the confidentiality of personal information. Ethical clearance for this research was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Qingdao Huanghai University.

During data cleaning, we excluded invalid questionnaires that had more than 20% of items unanswered or exhibited patterned responses, such as selecting “strongly agree” or “strongly disagree” for over 80% of items, as such patterns may introduce bias29. After removing invalid responses based on these criteria, the final sample consisted of 738 participants. Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics. The sample included 234 male (31.71%) and 504 female (68.29%) participants. The sample exhibits a certain imbalance in gender ratio, which is primarily related to the disciplinary structure of the surveyed institution. These institutions’ disciplinary composition is dominated by humanities, social sciences, arts, and economics and management programs, while the scale of engineering departments is limited. This distribution of disciplines naturally results in a higher proportion of female students in the overall student population, thereby influencing the gender composition of the sample. The age distribution was relatively balanced, with the majority being over 22 years old (35.23%). Most participants were sophomores (32.25%), and the mean daily internet engagement duration fell predominantly within the 4–6 h range (21.41%).

Measurement instruments

Normative beliefs about aggression

NBA was evaluated using the established General Normative Beliefs about Aggression measure created by Huesmann and Guerra15. This measurement tool contains 8 items with both directly and inversely formulated questions, designed to measure individuals’ normative beliefs regarding aggression. Participants responded on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very wrong) to 4 (very right). Higher total scores indicate stronger normative beliefs approving aggression, suggesting a greater perceived acceptability of aggressive behavior. Prior research has established the reliability and validity of this subscale within the Chinese context7. The scale’s internal consistency was judged to be sufficient in this study, with an overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.918. The fit indices that Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) produced are as follows: χ²/df = 3.065, CFI = 0.997, NFI = 0.995, IFI = 0.997, RMSEA = 0.053.

Perceived online anonymity

The assessment of POA was conducted using the corresponding scale initially created by Jung et al.30 and later adapted for Chinese university students by31. This unidimensional scale contains 4 items rated using a 7-point Likert-type format with response options ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Elevated scores indicate a more pronounced perception of anonymity online and a greater tendency to conceal one’s real identity in digital environments. Previous studies have shown that the scale is valid and dependable in the Chinese environment6,32. The instrument demonstrated excellent internal reliability in this investigation, achieving a Cronbach’s α of 0.847 for the full scale. CFA results indicated: χ²/df = 6.169, CFI = 0.991, NFI = 0.990, IFI = 0.991, RMSEA = 0.084.

Cyber aggression

We measured CA using the Instrumental Aggression subscale of the Adolescent Online Aggressive Behavior Scale (AOABS) developed by Feng33. This subscale consists of 15 items across two dimensions: overt aggression and relational aggression. A 4-point Likert scale was used for responses, from 1 (never) to 4 (always), where higher scores reflect greater frequency of engagement in CA. This measure has shown appropriate reliability and validity indices in previous studies involving Chinese university students10. The instrument showed outstanding internal dependability, achieving a Cronbach’s α of 0.930. CFA yielded the following fit indices: χ²/df = 1.794, CFI = 0.986, NFI = 0.969, IFI = 0.986, RMSEA = 0.033.

Moral disengagement

We assessed MD using the Moral Disengagement Scale developed by Bandura et al.22 and revised by Wang and Yang34 for Chinese populations. This measure contains 26 items distributed among eight facets: ethical rationalization, mitigated phrasing, favorable comparison, accountability shifting, accountability distribution, outcome misrepresentation, depersonalization, and culpability assignment. Participants provided ratings using a 5-point Likert-type format with options extending from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate stronger tendencies toward moral disengagement among university students. Validation tests conducted in China have confirmed the scale’s validity and reliability35. In the present study, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability, showing a Cronbach’s α of 0.961. CFA results were as follows: χ²/df = 2.165, CFI = 0.968, NFI = 0.941, IFI = 0.968, RMSEA = 0.040.

Data analysis

Smart PLS 4.0 was used for the data analysis. The partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) methodology was chosen based on its demonstrated capabilities in managing non-normality in data, accommodating moderate sample sizes, and enhancing the variance explained in endogenous latent variables within complex model frameworks36. This research endeavors to examine the associations between NBA, POA, and CA among university students, as well as the mediating role of MD. The mediation analysis utilized a bootstrapping procedure involving 5000 resamples to derive confidence intervals for indirect effects, consequently strengthening the reliability of the results.

Mediation model testing

To examine the mediating role of MD in the relationships between NBA and CA, as well as between POA and CA, we conducted mediation analyses using a bootstrap approach. All analyses controlled for grade level and assessed the significance of direct and indirect effects, along with the sign and magnitude of effect products, to determine the type of mediation42. Table 14 summarizes the mediation analysis results. After controlling for grade level, NBA had a significant indirect effect on CA through MD, and its direct effect on CA also remained significant. These results indicate that MD partially mediates the relationship between NBA and CA42. Similarly, when controlling for grade level, POA had a significant indirect effect on CA through MD, and its direct effect on CA also remained significant. These results point to MD playing a partial mediating role in how POA relates to CA.

Discussion

This study examined the relationships between NBA, POA, and CA among university students, while investigating the mediating role of MD. We analyzed the proposed research model using PLS-SEM, and all research hypotheses were supported. Below we discuss the findings in detail regarding the initial research questions and hypotheses.

The results revealed a significant positive association between NBA and CA among university students. This finding aligns with previous research on traditional aggression by Quan et al.,43, further supporting the close relationship between NBA and aggressive behavior. In this study, individuals with stronger NBA tended to exhibit cognitive tendencies such as believing that online retaliation is justified when provoked. Such rationalization of aggressive behavior makes them more likely to interpret ambiguous online situations as intentional provocations, thereby increasing the possibility of aggressive responses7. Additionally, individuals with stronger NBA often perceive CA as an effective social tool and means of identity expression. They may believe that assertive behavior online helps gain peer recognition or that aggression is an important way to demonstrate personal capability. This cognitive pattern makes them more inclined to use aggressive behavior to establish social image and gain attention4.Long-term reliance on this approach to maintain self-worth may further increase the frequency and severity of CA. Therefore, future intervention strategies should focus on reconstructing university students’ normative cognitions about online behavior while implementing digital literacy education programs. These efforts should help establish more positive and constructive online interaction patterns, ultimately reducing CA at its cognitive source.

POA showed a significant positive association with CA among university students, which aligns with findings from Guo et al.,6 and supports Hypothesis 2. Individuals with higher levels of POA may develop cognitive tendencies such as believing that online identities cannot be traced. This expectation of reduced accountability makes them more likely to violate social norms during online interactions, thereby increasing the possibility of aggressive behavior4. Furthermore, the anonymous online environment reduces social monitoring pressure, leading individuals to use cyberspace as an outlet for negative emotions. When they perceive that online expressions carry no real-world consequences, they are more inclined to exhibit aggressive tendencies that are usually suppressed in daily life20. Sustained high levels of POA can reinforce repeated engagement in CA. This reinforcement may not only solidify stable patterns of aggressive behavior but also promote the development of differentiated online and offline behavioral standards. Ultimately, these processes can impair self-regulatory mechanisms and intensify the severity and developmental trajectory of CA6. Therefore, future interventions should focus on enhancing the traceability of online identities and implementing digital citizenship education to strengthen university students’ sense of online responsibility. These measures can help individuals maintain ethical self-discipline even in anonymous environments.

MD mediates the relationship between NBA and CA among university students, revealing the underlying psychological mechanisms of online aggressive behavior. When individuals hold stronger NBA, they are more likely to activate cognitive mechanisms of moral disengagement25, such as diffusing responsibility, minimizing consequences, or attributing blame to justify their aggressive actions. These moral-cognitive regulatory mechanisms further weaken internal moral restraints, making individuals more inclined to perceive CA as acceptable behavior44. Therefore, CA is not merely a result of environmental triggers but represents a psychological adaptation process driven by cognitive beliefs and mediated by MD. These findings suggest that higher education institutions should emphasize students’ moral cognitive development in educational practices. Specifically, in the context of online behavioral norms, cognitive restructuring training could help students identify and correct tendencies toward MD fostering autonomous moral restraint and self-regulatory awareness in digital environments7.

MD mediates the relationship between POA and CA among university students. Research by Wu et al.,14 indicates a significant positive correlation between POA and MD, suggesting that individuals with higher levels of POA tend to develop stronger tendencies toward MD. This cognitive mechanism may subsequently facilitate engagement in CA6. In this study, individuals with higher POA often demonstrated reduced anticipation of behavioral consequences, making them more likely to employ moral disengagement strategies. This psychological state facilitates the rationalization of aggressive behaviors via cognitive restructuring. In this process, individuals may diffuse responsibility for their actions or minimize the ensuing consequences, which lowers the psychological barriers to committing CA44. Furthermore, individuals with higher levels of MD often exhibit frequent CA due to impaired moral self-regulation mechanisms45. Those with pronounced moral disengagement may persistently engage in aggressive behaviors, manifesting as continuing harmful actions despite awareness of their negative effects or dismissing cyber aggression as harmless jokes. Therefore, future interventions targeting CA among university students should integrate moral cognitive education into digital literacy programs. Establishing reflective mechanisms for online behavior could also encourage students to regularly evaluate and reflect on their actions in digital environments.

Implications and limitations

Theoretical implications

Based on social cognitive theory and the general aggression model, this study developed and validated a framework examining the relationships between NBA, POA, MD, and CA among university students. The research offers several theoretical innovations.

First, this study expands the theoretical perspective on CA. Previous studies have primarily focused on environmental factors such as family, school, and social influences, paying less attention to internal psychological mechanisms. By incorporating NBA and POA into the research framework, this study systematically investigates their relationships with CA and the underlying mechanisms, providing a more nuanced theoretical explanation for the multi-factorial nature of CA.

Second, this study validates the applicability of the general aggression model, a theory originally developed for offline aggression, in online contexts. Despite the distinctive features of online environments, such as anonymity, asynchrony, and disinhibition, individual aggressive behavior still follows the fundamental cognition-affect-behavior framework. This suggests that the core psychological mechanisms of aggressive behavior remain stable and continuous across different media environments. The findings provide preliminary evidence for understanding the common mechanisms of aggression across contexts.

Future research could build on this foundation by incorporating more diverse influencing variables such as contextual features of online environments and individual difference factors to further refine the theoretical understanding of CA.

Practical implications

The study’s conclusions have obvious applications for online platform developers, academic institutions, and university students, providing empirically grounded guidance for preventing and intervening in CA.

For university students, the results highlight the importance of cultivating healthy online behavioral norms and strengthening digital responsibility. Students should recognize that the anonymous nature of online environments may reduce behavioral constraints and consciously resist the negative influence of NBA. For example, when engaging in online interactions, students could adopt a think before you post strategy, which involves three levels of self-reflection before publishing content: assessing the accuracy of information, considering the necessity of expression, and evaluating potential consequences. Additionally, students may benefit from participating in digital ethics workshops and digital citizenship courses to deepen their understanding of the psychological mechanisms and social harms of CA, thereby enhancing self-awareness of MD. In daily internet use, students should establish self-monitoring mechanisms, such as periodically reviewing their online communications and reflecting on whether they exhibit moral disengagement strategies like diffusion of responsibility or minimization of consequences.

For Universities and Educational Institutions, educational institutions should clearly communicate support messages such as seek help promptly if experiencing cyber violence and establish accessible support channels (e.g., departmental faculty, academic advisors, mental health centers). These measures can ensure that students receive timely and effective assistance and psychological support when they or others experience online harassment, reducing the likelihood of suffering in isolation or resorting to retaliatory aggression. Simultaneously, institutions should strengthen education on digital ethics and behavioral norms. We recommend incorporating a rational online behavior and moral cognition module into mental health education curricula. Using real-case studies and situational simulations, this module can help students identify tendencies toward MD in themselves and others, and establish basic online behavioral principles such as do not intentionally harm others. Students should understand that even in anonymous environments, they should refrain from engaging in unwarranted attacks. Furthermore, student affairs teams and psychological counselors should receive specialized training to enhance their ability to recognize and respond to risks related to CA. When students exhibit strong NBA (e.g., endorsing statements like CA can solve problems) or are frequently exposed to online conflicts, timely psychological guidance and cognitive interventions should be provided. These interventions can help students adopt constructive approaches to managing negative emotions and interpersonal conflicts.

For Online Platform Developers and Administrators, this study underscores the importance of integrating psychological perspectives into platform design and operation. Developers should avoid creating features that may reinforce deindividuation effects and instead focus on building interaction mechanisms that promote responsible online behavior. For example, platforms could incorporate a cooling-off function in anonymous communities, where the system delays sending emotionally charged content and prompts users to reconsider their messages. Implementing an early warning system for MD could help identify discourse patterns related to diffusion of responsibility or dehumanization using natural language processing techniques, triggering real-time intervention prompts. Additionally, developers should create transparent reporting and handling mechanisms, provide users with clear guidelines for online conduct, and optimize algorithms to reduce the dissemination of aggressive content. These measures can contribute to building a psychologically safer online environment.

Limitations and future research directions

This study provides valuable insights into the relationships between NBA, POA, and CA among university students, as well as the mediating role of MD. However, a number of limitations must be recognized, and these also indicate avenues for subsequent investigations.

Firstly, the research utilized a cross-sectional methodology, which determines that the findings are limited to correlations among variables and cannot establish causal relationships. Although this study treated moral disengagement as a mediating variable based on theoretical assumptions, cross-sectional data cannot rule out the possibility of reverse causality. Specifically, CA may, in turn, reinforce individuals’ tendency toward MD. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of associations among variables rather than validation of causal pathways. Subsequent studies might adopt longitudinal approaches to explore how variables relate over time, or adopt experimental designs to more rigorously test causal directions.

Second, the primary method of data collecting used in this study was questionnaire surveys, which may have limited a thorough knowledge of the real factors influencing participants’ behavior. Self-reports were used to measure all variables, which introduces the possibility of CMB and social desirability effects. While results from the Harman single-factor test did not reveal serious CMB, this technique nevertheless possesses certain constraints. Future research should incorporate behavioral data or multi-informant reports to strengthen the trustworthiness of the results.

Thirdly, the sample had an imbalanced gender ratio and was predominantly drawn from universities in Shandong, China. This restricts the findings’ applicability at the regional and gender levels. Although we verified through independent sample t-tests that there was no significant gender difference in NBA, caution is still needed when inferential this sample to the national scale or the male population. Additionally, cultural norms regarding aggression and anonymity may vary across different social contexts, potentially influencing the observed relationships. In collectivist cultures such as China, aggressive behaviors may be more strongly stigmatized, while perceptions of anonymity—whether in online or offline settings—could shape how individuals respond to the constructs examined in this study. Future research should therefore validate these findings in samples with a more balanced gender ratio, across diverse cultural contexts, and with explicit consideration of how cultural factors, including norms around aggression and anonymity, may moderate the proposed relationships.

Finally, this study examined MD as a single mediating variable and did not investigate other potential mediating mechanisms (such as empathy, impulsivity, social support, etc.) or their interactions. The occurrence of CA may result from the interplay of multiple psychological and social factors. Future research could construct complex models involving parallel or serial mediators to more comprehensively reveal its underlying psychological mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study investigated the formation mechanisms of CA among university students, with a specific focus on the pathways through which NBA and POA influence CA, as well as the mediating role of MD. The results indicate that both NBA and POA show significant positive associations with CA, and that they indirectly strengthen this relationship by increasing levels of MD. These findings reveal the mediating mechanism of MD between NBA, POA, and CA, deepening our understanding of the social-cognitive processes underlying CA. The study also provides an important theoretical foundation and practical intervention pathways for preventing and addressing CA in educational settings. To further evaluate the correlations between these variables, future research should employ longitudinal designs, as the cross-sectional approach restricts causal inference.
