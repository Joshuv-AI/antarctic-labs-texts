Discovery of dietary polyphenols targeting the autoinducer-2 quorum sensing system of pathogenic bacteria
Authors: Shengbo Wu, Peng Zhang, Manman Wang, Xue Xiao, Hongyu Jin, Hongrui Zhang, Danlei Chen, Xiaoli Liu, Juane Lu, Daoguang Tian, Hao Wu, Qinggele Caiyin, Fei Guo, Xiaolong Liu, Aidong Yang, Mingzhang Wen, Jianjun Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTargeting autoinducer-2 (AI-2) quorum sensing (QS) systems with dietary compounds represents a promising strategy to combat pathogens, yet mechanisms remain elusive. Here, we develop a machine learning-driven framework combining computational screening with multi-level experimental analysis to identify AI-2 quorum sensing interference molecules (QSIMs). A graph neural network (GNN)-based classifier (AI2IMpred) screens over 7000 phytochemicals and identifies dietary polyphenols as potent QSIMs. Using Salmonella Typhimurium LT2 as a model, we demonstrate that 17 polyphenols, including pterostilbene, 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol, directly target LuxS and LsrB, as confirmed by SPR assays, while molecular docking predicts potential interactions with TqsA and LsrR. Structural analysis revealed that para-substituted hydrocarbyl groups enhance LsrB binding. Furthermore, polyphenol-mediated regulatory effects have been validated via gene expression modulation, bacterial phenotypes, NCM460 cell adhesion/invasion, and cross-pathogen analysis (Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa). This work provides insights for the potential development of diet-based interventions using polyphenol compounds against drug-resistant pathogenic infections.Keywords: Food is medicine, dietary intervention, quorum sensing, polyphenols, antibiotic resistance, pathogenic bacteria

Introduction

Food intervention is a critical determinant of human health and disease development, 1-3 profoundly influencing the composition and function of the gut microbiome. 4-6 This connection underpins the “food is medicine” (FIM) initiative, 7 , 8 which seeks to incorporate dietary interventions into health systems by leveraging the medicinal properties of foods. 9 , 10 For example, fruits and vegetables enriches probiotics like Lachnospiraceae, Butyricicoccus spp., and Roseburia hominis, which contribute to cardiometabolic health through fiber fermentation and short-chain fatty acid production. 11 , 12 However, the specific bioactive components and mechanisms linking dietary diversity to microbiome dynamics remain poorly characterized, hindering clinical applications. A deeper understanding of these elements is essential to advancing food-based therapies and combating pathogenic bacteria.

The global overuse of antibiotics has accelerated the evolution of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) 13-15 in pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, 16 Pseudomonas aeruginosa, 17 Escherichia coli, 18 and Salmonella spp., 19 contributing to an estimated 1.27 million deaths in 2019 and projected economic losses of up to $3.4 trillion annually by 2030. 20 Salmonella Typhimurium, a major widespread enteric pathogen, 21-23 employs diverse resistance mechanisms, including efflux pumps, 24 biofilm formation, 25 and virulence factor expression, 26 largely governed by the autoinducer-2 (AI-2) QS systems. 27 , 28 AI-2 QS system involves the LuxS-catalyzed conversion of S-ribosyl-homocysteine (SRH) to AI-2, followed by TqsA-mediated exportation, LsrB-mediated recognition, LsrR-mediated regulation, and subsequent activation of the lsrKRACDBFG operon and downstream regulatory genes. 29 In response, interference with AI-2 QS has emerged as a promising anti-AMR strategy, driving the search for natural quorum sensing interference molecules (QSIMs), 30 , 31 especially for food-derived compounds. 32

Polyphenols, widely present in fruits, vegetables, and beverages, possess diverse bioactive properties, 33 including antimicrobial and anti-virulence effects. 4 , 34 Evidence suggests that polyphenols can attenuate biofilm formation, 35 inhibit bacterial motility, 36 and suppress the release of virulence factors, 37 indicating their potential for targeting on QS systems in drug-resistant pathogens. However, research progress remains constrained by a fragmented experimental landscape and a lack of systematic frameworks for both compound discovery and mechanistic validation. The absence of molecular-level characterization of common scaffold architectures further complicates the development of coherent structure–activity relationships. Current studies focus on narrow phenotypic outcomes rather than a comprehensive analysis of QS pathways, and the mechanisms, particularly whether polyphenols act on single or multiple QS targets, are still poorly understood. Moreover, existing investigations predominantly concentrate on individual pathogenic bacteria, with limited exploration of broader applicability across clinically prevalent pathogens.

In this study, we established a machine learning-driven systematic framework to identify phytochemicals that interfere with AI-2 QS across bacterial pathogens and elucidate their mechanisms (Supplementary Figure S1). Specifically, based on a carefully curated dataset of reported AI-2 QSIMs, we first trained a graph neural network (GNN)-based classifier (AI2IMpred) to identify potential QSIMs from more than 7000 plant-derived compounds, especially 318 dietary polyphenols. Taken S. Typhimurium LT2 as a model organism, we obtained dozens of polyphenol QSIMs by integrating in silico and in vitro screening approaches. Through molecular docking and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis, we identified multiple QS targets of these polyphenols and characterized the conserved scaffold responsible for LsrB binding. The effects of polyphenol QSIMs were further validated genetically, phenotypically, and cellularly for effects on QS-regulated phenotypes, such as motility, biofilm formation, virulence, and invasion. Furthermore, we elucidated the polyphenol-mediated regulatory mechanism targeting the AI-2 QS system and extended our findings to other resistant pathogens (E. coli, S. aureus, and P. aeruginosa) with varying degrees of AI-2 pathway completeness. Finally, we discuss the challenges and future perspectives of QS-targeting dietary intervention, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic strategy.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and cultures conditions

S. Typhimurium LT2, E. coli MG1655, S. aureus NAS_OP_071 and P. aeruginosa PAO1 strains were grown in lysogeny broth (LB) medium at 37 °C, in which 1.5% (w/v) agar was added when solid medium was required. For the experiments descripted below, overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 in LB medium to OD600 = 0.05 and grown at 37 °C. Antibiotics were added at the following concentrations: ampicillin (AMP), 30 μg/mL, kanamycin (Kana), 30 μg/mL, chloramphenicol (CM), 20 μg/mL.

The knockout of luxS and lsrB genes of S. Typhimurium LT2 were performed using the λ-Red homologous recombination system, which was referred to as strains LT2ΔluxS and LT2ΔlsrB throughout this study, respectively. Briefly, PCR products were cloned from plasmid pKD3 with 50 bp of upstream and downstream of corresponding genes and transferred into S. Typhimurium LT2 expressing Redγ-Redβ-EXO enzyme from plasmid pKD46. After transformant selection and PCR conformation, the resistance cassette was resolved with FLP recombinase from plasmid pCP20, which was then cured at 42 °C. The generated mutant was confirmed by sequencing. The overexpression of the luxS and lsrB genes of S. Typhimurium LT2 were performed using the recombinant plasmids. The recombinant plasmids of pSC101-J23119-luxS and pSC101-J23119-lsrB were introduced into S. Typhimurium LT2 strain, which were named LT2:luxS and LT2:lsrB, respectively. For more details, refer to Supplementary Table S1.

The polyphenols were dissolved in DMSO to prepare stock solutions at a concentration of 400 mM, which were stored at −20 °C. The CAS number, supplier, catalog number of polyphenols and antibiotics were listed in Table S2.

Development of the GNN-based AI-2 QSIM classifier

We used RDKit 38 construct molecular graphs and extract atomic features. Specifically, with the help from DeepChem, 39 we determined that the node feature vector includes one-hot encoding of the atom element, one-hot encoding of the degree of the atom in the molecule, number of directly-bonded neighbors (atoms), one-hot encoding of the total number of experiments following descripted H bound to the atom, one-hot encoding of the number of implicit H bound to the atom, and whether the atom is aromatic. Then, an indirect binary graph with attribute nodes was constructed for the corresponding input SMILES strings of the positive and negative samples. The measurement of the AI-2 QSIMs classifier (AI2IMpred) was based on the accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score, which are the combination of the true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN).

Precision=TPTP+FP,

Recall=TPTP+FN,

Accuracy=TP+TNTP+TN+FP+FN,

F1score=2*Precision*Recall/(Precision+Recall)=2TP2TP+FN+FP.

Furthermore, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was another important measurement of the performances on the AI2IMpred classifier. In general, the closer the AUC value is to 1, the better the performance of the classifier.

MIC and growth curve assay

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of polyphenols that against S. Typhimurium LT2, P. aeruginosa PAO1, E. coli MG1655 and S. aureus NAS_OP_071 strains were determined by double dilution method. Briefly, the treatment was performed in 96-well plates, and the polyphenols were doubling diluted across the columns serially (the concentration of the polyphenols were 160 mM, 80 mM, 40 mM, 20 mM, 10 mM, 5 mM, 2.5 mM, 1.25 mM, 0.625 mM, 0.3125 mM, 0.15625 mM, and 0.078 mM, ordinally). Each well contained 180 μL of bacteria culture (to a final CFU/mL of ca. 1 × 106), 20 μL of polyphenols dissolved in DMSO. LB broth with bacterial medium and DMSO as a blank control, and LB broth with bacterial medium and ligands dissolved in normal saline as negative control. The growth of bacteria was observed after 24 h of growth at 37 °C, then the spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Varioskan LUX) was used to determine OD600 of bacteria, and the minimum concentration of bacteria without growth was defined as the MIC.

The synergistic effects of antibiotics and polyphenols against S. Typhimurium LT2 were determined as the same method. The well contained 20 μL of doubling dilutions of antibiotics and 180 μL of bacteria culture with pre-added polyphenols. The S. Typhimurium LT2 was grown in 37 °C for 24 h, then the spectrophotometer was used to determine the MIC of antibiotics by OD600.

Plsr-eGFP luminescence assay

The plasmid pBAD24 was constructed by generating Plsr-eGFP fusion reporter cassette. The lsr operon promoter is the DNA fragment between lsrR gene and lsrA gene located at the −1 to −255 bp of the lsrA gene, which is repressed by LsrR protein and disinhibited by Pi-AI-2 molecule. We constructed pBAD24-Plsr-eGFP reporter plasmid in S. Typhimurium LT2 to detect the regulatory expression of lsr operon in S. Typhimurium LT2 under polyphenol treatment. The pBAD24-Plsr-eGFP plasmid was transmitted into S. Typhimurium LT2, LT2ΔluxS, LT2ΔlsrB, LT2:luxS, and LT2:lsrB strains to build fluorescent biosensor strains. The reporter strains after hours of incubation were washed with 10 mM PBS buffer to remove LB medium for three times, and then dissolved in an equal volume PBS buffer. The fluorescence of eGFP protein was measured by spectrophotometer, which excited wavelength and emitted wavelength were 488 nm and 507 nm, respectively. The strains without the reporter plasmid were regarded as blank control.

Biofilm formation assay

The effect of polyphenols on biofilm formation were calculated according to previous reports. 40 Briefly, an overnight bacterial suspension dilution in LB medium was transferred to a 96-well flat-bottomed plate (200 μL/well), and the sub-MIC of the polyphenols were pre-added, respectively. There is no AI-2 synthetic enzyme, but AI-2 can enhance the biofilm formation and virulence of P. aeruginosa. 41 P. aeruginosa grew in sterile supernatant from the 4-h S. Typhimurium LT2 culture, where AI-2 was extracellularly enriched. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, then were washed with phosphate-buffered saline buffer (PBS, 10 mM) for three times, and the biofilm was stained with 1% crystal violet for 30 min. The 96-well plates were washed with PBS to remove free crystal violet for three times, then solubilized with 200 μL of 95% ethanol. OD590 was determined as quantification of the biofilm mass by spectrophotometer.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulation was performed by the Discovery Studio tool. The structure of LuxS (5V2W, 2.3 Å), LsrB (1TJY, 1.3 Å), LsrR (4L4Z, 2.3 Å), and TqsA (7N6B, 2.66 Å) proteins in S. Typhimurium LT2 were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. The structures of SdiA (H9L4B0) and QseC (Q8ZLZ9) in S. Typhimurium LT2, LuxS (P45578), LsrB (P76142), LsrR (P76141), TqsA (P0AFS5), SdiA (P07026), and QseC (P40719) in E. coli MG1655, LuxS (P65330) in S. aureus NAS_OP_071, LasR (P25084), RhlR (P54292), QscR (G3XD77), and QseC (Q9HV31) in P. aeruginosa PAO1 were obtained from the UniProt database. All water molecules were removed from the protein.pdb file, and the polar hydrogens and the CHARMm fields were added. The amino acid residues of the LuxS (Glu57, Arg65, Asp77, Ile78, Ser79, Gln127, and Gly129), LsrB (Lys35, Asp116, Asp166, Gln167, Pro220, and Ala222), LsrR (Glu125, Ala126, Ser219, Ile220, Asp242, and Lys287) and TqsA (Tyr196, Lys200, Asn234, Pro237, Asn238, Glu280, and Met284) proteins were defined as flexible docking sites of polyphenols. The space spheres of LuxS (58, −0.08, 4.71, R = 9Å), LsrB (30.5, 29.26, 28.69, R = 10Å), LsrR (1.91, 6.20, −5.16, R = 12 Å) and TqsA (0.85, −0.92, 4.96, R = 12 Å) proteins were set as docking pockets, and the residues in the flexible docking site were considered as mobile and variable. The molecular structures of the polyphenols were downloaded from the PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database and were polar hydrogenated, minimized, and isomerized to generate coordinates of the polyphenols with structure optimization. 42 The protein‒ligand interactions were visualized using PyMOL 3.0 program. The specific substrates SRH R-THMF and D5X docking with corresponding proteins were regarded as the standard control, and polyphenols with similar interaction energy were thought to have competitive binding force.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

S. Typhimurium autoinducer 2 ABC transporter substrate-binding gene lsrB (STM4077) and S-ribosyl-homocysteine lyase gene luxS (STM2817) were cloned into pET-28a (+) plasmid and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3). LuxS and LsrB proteins were purified using High Affinity Ni-Charged Resin FF (GenScript) and kept at −80 °C.

LuxS and LsrB proteins were immobilized on the CM5 chips by amine coupling as the stationary phase, and the polyphenols were doubling diluted to the concertation of 1000 μM, 500 μM, 250 μM, 125 μM, 62.5 μM, 31.25 μM, 15.625 μM, and 7.8 μM into 1.05 × PBS (v/v, 5% DMSO) buffer as mobile phase flowing through the chip. The determination of the binding kinetics evaluation between polyphenols and proteins by fitting the experimental data to a kinetic model or steady state model on Biacore T200 (Cytiva, USA). The affinity constant of the kinetic model was calculated as the ratio of association (kd ) and dissociation (ka ) constants, which means KD = k d /k a . 43

Extracellular AI-2 detection experiment

The overnight activated Vibrio harveyi BB170 strain was diluted at a ratio of 1:5000 in AB medium. The overnight activated S. Typhimurium LT2 was diluted 1:100 in LB medium and grown for 4 h at 37 °C, after which the cell-free supernatants were collected. The cell-free supernatants and BB170 suspension were added at a ratio of 1:9 respectively, and the culture was conducted at 30 °C for 4 h. Then, chemiluminescence was measured by spectrophotometer. 44

RNA extraction and qPCR assay

Cultures grown overnight were diluted 1:100 in LB medium and grown for 4 h at 37 °C with shaking, and then collected bacteria sediment. The cells were maintained in 100 μL of lysozyme (20 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 30 min, then RNA was purified from three biological replicates with the RNAprep Pure Cell/Bacteria Kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH Co., LTD., Beijing, DP430) according to the manufacture's specifications. The cDNA used for qPCR was obtained by reverse transcription using SPARKscript II RT Plus Kit (with gDNA Eraser) (SparkJade, Shandong, AG0304).

The cDNA was diluted to a concentration of 20 ng/μL and used with 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Mix (SparkJade, Shandong, AH0101) on LightCycler 480 II (Roche), and the 16s rRNA was used as endogenous control. The relative transcriptional levels of the target genes were calculated using comparative Ct method, and log2foldchange value of |−ΔΔCt| ≥ 1.5 was considered as significant difference.

Swarming motility and swimming motility assay

The subculture of S. Typhimurium LT2 or E. coli MG1655 were diluted into LB medium at a ratio of 1:100, grew at 30 °C for 4 h (OD600 ~ 0.4). 5 μL of the culture were spotted atop and in the center of swarm agar plates (0.5% agar, w/v). The plates avoided penetrated and allowed culture to dry until the liquid was absorbed into agar, then grew at 30 °C for 10 h. The migration and swarm of LT2 or MG1655 were observed and measured.

Subculture 100 μL of S. Typhimurium LT2 or E. coli MG1655 into 10 mL of LB and grown at 30 °C with shaking to an OD600 of 0.4. Gently pipette 5 μL of the culture penetrated into the center of 0.3% (w/v) agar plate, and the inoculated plates were incubated in 30 °C. The characteristic chemotactic rings became evident and visible after 10 h of incubation. Photographed and recorded the diameter of the plates.

Cell cytotoxicity, adhesion, and invasion assay

Intestinal epithelial cells NCM460 were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 104​​ cells/well. NCM460 cells were cultured at 37 °C for 12 h, and then treated with polyphenols and further cultured for another 12 h. Remove the waste culture medium and add 1 mL of DMEM complete medium containing 10% CCK-8. Incubate for 1.5 h, and measure the OD450 to determine the cell cytotoxicity.

Overnight cultured S. Typhimurium LT2 was inoculated and cultured at 1:100 ratio for 4 h, then collected and washed with PBS for 3 times. NCM460 cells were inoculated in 12-well tissue culture plate with 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C for 12 h. Bacteria and polyphenols were added to the cell culture wells for 1 h according to MOI = 20 (bacteria numbers: cell numbers = 20:1), and then washed with sterile PBS for 3 times. For the adhesion ability assay, PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 was blown repeatedly, then incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, and the bacteria were collected and counted. For the invasion ability test, DMEM medium containing 100 μg/mL gentamicin was cultured for 50 min at 37 °C, washed with PBS for 3 times, then repeatedly blown with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, and bacteria were collected and counted.

Virulence factor expression of Pseudomonas aeruginosa assays

For the pyocyanin assay, P. aeruginosa PAO1 was cultured for 24 h in sterile supernatant from a 4-h LT2-inoculated PPB (pyocyanin production broth; 2% peptone, 1% K₂SO₄, 0.3% MgCl2) medium, and the blank PPB medium served as the control group. 3 mL of the P. aeruginosa PAO1 culture supernatant was extracted with 3 mL of chloroform, then the lower blue chloroform layer was then re-extracted with 0.2 M HCl, yielding a red layer. The pyocyanin concentration was calculated by multiplying the OD₅₂₀ value with 17.07 μg/mL.

For the elastase assay, P. aeruginosa PAO1 was cultured for 6 h in sterile supernatant from a 4-h LT2-inoculated PTSB (peptone tryptic soy broth; 5% peptone, 0.1% tryptic soy broth (Hopebio)) medium, and the blank PTSB medium served as the control group. 100 μL of the culture supernatant was added to 900 μL of elastin-Congo red (EGR) buffer (10 mg/mL ECR (Solarbio), 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), and 1 mM CaCl2). The mixture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking for 2 h, after which the unreacted substrate was removed by centrifugation. Finally, elastase production was determined by measuring the optical density (OD) at 495 nm.

Statistical analyses

All the experiments were repeated at least three times. The data were statistically analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10, t-test was performed to determine differences between two groups. p-values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Training and performance evaluation of the AI-2 QSIM classifier

We first constructed a general AI-2 QSIM classifier, named as AI2IMpred, using a curated dataset comprising 215 validated AI-2 QSIMs as positive samples and 469 non-AI-2 QSIMs as negative samples. Analysis of known AI-2 QSIMs revealed that natural compounds account for only 19.07% (41/215), highlighting the largely untapped potential of natural sources in AI-2 quorum sensing interference (Figure 1A). To elucidate the structural characteristics of these molecules, we systematically analyzed the physicochemical properties of the 215 validated AI-2 QSIMs. Based on their core scaffolds, these compounds were classified into five major chemotypes: furanones, cyclic ethers, hydrocarbons, phenols, and aromatics. Notably, most of these chemotypes contain oxygen-bearing functional groups capable of forming hydrogen bond coordination (Figure 1B), providing a structural basis for their interfering activity. For classifier construction, the simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES) strings of all training samples were converted into molecular graphs with topological and structural information using RDKit (www.rdkit.org). These graph representations were subsequently encoded via a graph neural network (GNN) algorithm to learn discriminative features of positive and negative samples (Figure 1C). The AI2IMpred was trained using fivefold cross-validation and demonstrated robust predictive performance. The model achieved an average area under the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.936 (Figure 1D) and an average precision‒recall curve (PRC) of 0.834 (Figure 1E), indicating high accuracy and reliability in distinguishing active AI-2 QSIMs from inactive compounds. AI2IMpred thus provides a powerful tool for subsequent large-scale virtual screening of phytochemical libraries.

Screening polyphenol QSIMs based on AI2IMpred and lsr biosensor

We applied AI2IMpred to screen natural compounds from the collective molecular activities of useful plants (CMAUP) database. Among the top 100 scoring molecules, polyphenols emerged as the predominant chemical class (53%, Figure 2A), highlighting their strong potential as AI-2 QSIMs. Subsequent evaluation of polyphenols from the Phenol-Explorer database using AI2IMpred predicted 263 compounds as true positives, indicating that the majority possess potential QS activity. Structural analysis revealed that flavonoids, lignans, phenolic acids, and stilbenes share conserved core scaffolds with common functional groups, while lignans and phenolic acids also exhibit unique derived structures (Figure 2B). Food source analysis showed that fruits, seeds, and vegetables predominantly contain flavonoids, phenolic acids, lignans, and stilbenes; other food categories display greater polyphenolic diversity, encompassing nearly all major structural types (Figure 2C). Collectively, these database screening results demonstrate that polyphenols represent a promising class of natural products with significant AI-2 QS activities.

Using S. Typhimurium LT2 as a model organism, we next determined the antibacterial properties and AI-2 QS expression of the polyphenols. With regarded to the former, 100 of 263 polyphenols were selected for the antibacterial assay based on AI2IMpred predictions, solubleness and accessibility. Among these, 38 polyphenols with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) less than 10 mM were identified to exhibit high antibacterial activity (Figure 2D). We then analyzed the growth curves to obtain the optimal sub-MICs against S. Typhimurium LT2 (Supplementary Figure S2; Supplementary Table S3). These sub-MICs were confirmed to have no significant impact on bacterial growth and were used for all subsequent experiments to ensure that the observed phenotypic changes were independent of growth inhibition. Considering the potential AI-2 QS activities of polyphenols predicted by AI2IMpred, we then determined their AI-2 expression effects. In S. Typhimurium, the complete AI-2 QS pathway involves LuxS lyase generating AI-2 ((2R, 4S)-2-methyl-2, 3, 3, 4-tetrahydroxytetrahydrofuran, R-THMF), and the transportation of AI-2 to the extracellular for accumulation. When the AI-2 concentration reaches to a threshold, it is recognized by LsrB and transported into bacteria by LsrACD. The AI-2 is phosphorylated by LsrK, and Pi-AI-2 releases the repression of the lsr promoter by LsrR, thus by activating the lsr operon expression (Figure 2E). We examined the regulatory effects of polyphenols on AI-2 QS expression by constructing a lsr promoter-eGFP reporter (lsr biosensor, Figure 2F), which reports changes in AI-2 levels through fluorescence readouts and subsequently measured their impact on lsr operon expression. All 38 polyphenols were found to interfere with lsr promoter expression. Specifically, 21 suppressed and 5 enhanced lsr operon activity, leading to the selection of these 26 compounds for subsequent receptors testing and multi-level experimental validations.

Receptor testing of polyphenol QSIMs within AI-2 pathway

Given the compound-specific phenotypic variations observed in S. Typhimurium (Figure 2), we hypothesized that polyphenols target distinct stages of the AI-2 pathway, i.e., synthesis (LuxS), transport (TqsA), detection (LsrB), or regulation (LsrR). To test this, we examined the binding interactions between polyphenol QSIMs and each of these four effectors. To investigate this hypothesis, we performed molecular docking, SPR, and in vitro verification assays to assess polyphenol binding to the four QS effectors.

Docking analysis first confirmed that the binding conformations of the four targets with their native substrates aligned well with reported crystal structures (Supplementary Figure S3A–D). The calculated binding energies for the native complexes were –60.85 kJ/mol (LuxS–SRH), –41.78 kJ/mol (LsrB–R-THMF), –54.11 kJ/mol (LsrR–D5X), and –29.86 kJ/mol (TqsA–R-THMF) (Figure 3A). Polyphenols that exhibited stronger binding than these native complexes were considered potential competitive AI-2 QSIMs across four targets (Figure 3A). To further validate these computational predictions experimentally, we focused on LuxS and LsrB using SPR assays. We then extended the docking analysis to all 263 screened polyphenols with four proteins, revealing a wide spectrum of binding energies and conformations (Supplementary Figure S3E). To further validate the molecular docking findings, we employed SPR assays to evaluate the binding affinities. The results revealed dissociation constant (KD) values ranging from 10−5 M to 10−9 M (Supplementary Figures S4 and S5). Specifically, tangeretin, pterostilbene, formononetin, and chrysin exhibited binding affinities to LuxS with KD values being 1.603 × 10−8, 2.051 × 10−7, 1.322 × 10−5, and 8.403 × 10−6 (M), respectively (Figure 3B). Similarly, 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, 3-methylcatechol, and 4-hydroxycumarin bound to LsrB with KD values being 3.226 × 10−7, 8.742 × 10−7, 9.945 × 10−7, and 2.988 × 10−7 (M), respectively (Figure 3C). These results experimentally confirmed that 8 polyphenols that specifically target LuxS and LsrB (Supplementary Table S4). Notably, the SPR-derived binding affinities of these polyphenols were substantially lower than their reported abundances in common dietary sources, with the latter exceeding the former by orders of magnitude (Figure 3D), indicating that they could be potential candidates for future dietary intervention studies.

Considering the practical targeting effect of polyphenols in the cultivation environment, we then tested the regulatory effects of 8 polyphenols on strains LT2, LT2:luxS (overexpressing the luxS gene), and LT2:lsrB (overexpressing the lsrB gene). The results indicated that the excessive promotion of lsr expression by pterostilbene in LT2:luxS, while 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol showed significant regulatory changes of lsr expression in LT2:lsrB (Figure 3E; Supplementary Figure S6). Taken together, our experimental results demonstrate that pterostilbene disrupts AI-2 QS through LuxS targeting, while 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol and 3-methylcatechol act via LsrB binding.

Characterizing the common scaffold of LsrB-bound polyphenols

As described above, we identified that 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol as potential polyphenol QSIMs that target LsrB (Figure 3). Given their structural similarity, we investigated the binding mode of this polyphenol scaffold within the LsrB binding pocket. Guided by the structural constraints and substrate accessibility profile of the LsrB active site (Figure 4A), we designed and synthesized a library of 55 simplified polyphenolic derivatives (Supplementary Figure S7A). These compounds featured different substituents at various positions on a phenolic core, enabling systematic exploration of conserved polyphenol motifs essential for LsrB binding. The 55 polyphenolic derivatives were further categorized into three categories: Class I (fully hydroxyl-substituted), Class II (substituted with hydroxyl and lower hydrocarbyl groups, e.g., methyl, ethyl, propyl), and Class III (bearing hydroxyl along with methoxy/ethoxy groups) (Figure 4B; Supplementary Figure S7A).

(C) The docking results of 55 derivative polyphenols (class I, II, III) with LsrB protein. (D) The hydrogen bonding results, (E) the visualization analysis of three classes of derivative polyphenols with the amino acid residues of the active pocket of LsrB, and (F) The disruption of the lsr operon promoter expression of derivative polyphenols in S. Molecular docking was then employed to evaluate the binding efficacy and modes of the three classes toward LsrB. Results showed that Class II derivatives exhibited stronger binding (−30.63 kJ/mol) than those in Class I (−28.04 kJ/mol) and Class III (−23.70 kJ/mol) (Figure 4C). Hydrogen bond analysis indicated that the interactions of Class II polyphenols were substitution-dependent. Meta-substituted derivatives formed hydrogen bonds with residues K35, D166, Q167, and D221. In contrast, para-substituted derivatives interacted with K35, Q167, N195, and D221 and exhibited higher docking energy (Figure 4D; Supplementary Table S5). Structural visualization of the LsrB-binding also confirmed that Class II derivatives formed markedly more hydrogen bonds with active residues compared to both Class I and Class III compounds (Figure 4E). Furthermore, the analysis of hydrophobic interactions revealed that Class III polyphenols exhibited the strongest hydrophobic character, followed by Class II and then Class I (Supplementary Figure S7B).

Furthermore, we also investigated the anti-QS effects of these polyphenolic derivatives on the AI-2 QS pathway. The results of the lsr operon expression indicated that all three classes of derivative polyphenols suppressed the expression of AI-2 QS system (Figure 4F). Overall, by extending the common scaffold based on 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol, we demonstrated that class II derivatives, featuring para-substitution with low chain hydrocarbyl groups, bind more strongly to LsrB and thereby disrupt the AI-2 QS system.

Validating polyphenol QSIMs by combining genetic, strain, and cellular assays

Using S. Typhimurium LT2 as the model pathogen, we identified that four polyphenols interfered with the AI-2 pathway and exhibited high protein affinities. The AI-2 QS regulates the motility, virulence, and biofilm formation of bacteria. However, the effectiveness of these phenotypes interfered by polyphenol QSIMs remained uncertain. To evaluate their efficacy, we conducted multi-level validations of the four polyphenol QSIMs, encompassing genetic, strain, and cellular levels.

At the genetic level, we investigated the regulation of QS-regulated gene expression by polyphenols, including those related to bacterial motility, virulence, and the AI-2 QS pathway. These results suggested that polyphenols target the expression of the AI-2 QS pathway and its downstream regulatory genes (Figure 5A). For instance, the expression of flhC and fliA, which regulate flagella synthesis and assembly, were markedly reduced upon polyphenol treatment. Similarly, fimD, encoding an outer membrane usher protein essential for pilus assembly and bacterial adhesion, and relA, which is responsible for synthesizing the stringent response alarmone (p)ppGpp (which regulates bacterial persistence and biofilm formation in the host), were also downregulated. Additionally, SPI-1 effector genes—sopB (encoding membrane perforin), sopE (encoding the type III secretion protein SopE2), sptP (encoding tyrosine phosphatase SptP), and invH (encoding invasion lipoprotein)—play critical roles in bacterial aggressiveness and pathogenicity by encoding and secreting effector proteins. Treatment with 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol significantly downregulated the expression of these SPI-1 genes, indicating that these polyphenol QSIMs substantially reduce the invasive capacity of S. Typhimurium LT2. Regarding the lsr operon-related genes, we assessed the expression levels of lsrK and lsrR. The expression of lsrK (encoding AI-2 phosphorylated kinase LsrK) and lsrR (encoding the lsr operon transcriptional repressor LsrR) remained largely unchanged, which indicated that polyphenols have no effects on the conversion process of AI-2 molecule. In summary, polyphenols acting as QSIMs, effectively reduce the expression of flagella-related genes, virulence factors, and lsr operon components, likely contributing to the reduction of motility and invasive ability of S. Typhimurium LT2.

At the strain level, we evaluated the efficacy of polyphenols by assessing swarming and swimming motility, biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance. Firstly, we examined bacterial motility on culture plates incubated with polyphenols. The results demonstrated that polyphenols significantly inhibited both the swarming motility (Figure 5B; Supplementary Figure S8A) and swimming motility (Figure 5C; Supplementary Figure S8B) of S. Typhimurium LT2, which were consistent with the downregulation of the flhC and fliA genes observed in the qPCR results (Figure 5A). Considering that biofilms act as protective barriers to resist harsh environmental stress, we investigated how polyphenol interfere with biofilm formation. Results revealed that all four polyphenol QSIMs reduced the biofilm formation of S. Typhimurium LT2 (Figure 5D). Researches have shown that the antibiotic resistance of bacteria is related to AI-2 QS, and we further examined the effects of potential polyphenols on antibiotic resistance in S. Typhimurium LT2. The results indicated that the sensitivity of S. Typhimurium LT2 to β-lactams, tetracyclines, chloramphenicol, and nitrofurans would be increased when these antibiotics were combined with polyphenols (Figure 5E). For example, nitrofurantoin, a nitrofuran antibiotic that is reduced to active intermediates to disrupt bacterial DNA, protein synthesis, and cell wall integrity, showed a twofold reduction in the MIC when combined with polyphenol QSIMs. However, no such synergistic effect was observed when polyphenol QSIMs were combined with sulfonamides, which inhibit dihydrofolic acid synthesis by inhibiting dihydropteroate synthase and arresting cell growth. In summary, polyphenol QSIMs significantly enhanced the antibacterial efficacy of antibiotics targeting peptidoglycan synthesis and protein synthesis but showed no synergistic effects with antibiotics that inhibit DNA synthesis.

At the cellular level, we evaluated the effects of polyphenols on the adhesion and invasion capabilities of S. Typhimurium LT2 toward the colon epithelial cell line NCM460. We firstly examined the cytotoxicity of polyphenols on NCM460 cells. The results showed that polyphenols at sub-MIC concentrations had no significant effect on the viability of the ncm460 cells (Figure 5F). The results demonstrated that polyphenol QSIMs significantly reduced the adhesion (Figure 5G) and invasion (Figure 5H) of S. Typhimurium LT2 to intestinal epithelial cells. These findings align with the downregulated expression of SPI-1-related genes (sopB, sopE, sptP, and invH) observed in Figure 5D. Notably, polyphenols reduced the adhesion ability of S. Typhimurium LT2 by no more than 33%, whereas the invasion ability was reduced by up to 50% in the presence of polyphenol QSIMs.

In summary, genetic, strain, and cellular level experiments confirmed that polyphenol QSIMs modulate motility, biofilm formation, and virulence, thereby reducing antibiotic resistance and impairing the adhesion and invasion capabilities of S. Typhimurium LT2. These results confirm our hypothesis that polyphenols interfere the QS of pathogenic bacteria by targeting distinct receptors at different stages of the AI-2 signaling pathway, consequently modulating associated phenotypes.

Deciphering polyphenol QSIMs mechanism in bacteria with varying AI-2 completeness

Considering the phenotypic effects of polyphenols on S. Typhimurium LT2 and the integrity of the lsr pathway, we propose a mechanistic model to elucidate the interactions between 17 polyphenol QSIMs and four AI-2 QS receptors (LuxS, TqsA, LsrB, and LsrR) (Figure 6A). Specifically, pterostilbene uniquely enhanced AI-2 QS activity through specific binding interactions with the LuxS protein. In contrast, 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol functioned as potent AI-2 QSIMs by competitively binding to the LsrB receptor, thereby disrupting AI-2 signal recognition and transduction. Molecular docking and SPR analysis further suggested that certain polyphenols may interfere with AI-2 levels and lsr operon expression by targeting different QS effector proteins (more details in Figure 3A–C; Supplementary Table S4). This mechanistic model comprehensively illustrates how polyphenols interact with critical components of the AI-2 QS pathway to modulate QS-regulated phenotypes. Given the potential for broad-spectrum application of polyphenol QSIMs in disrupting widespread AI-2 signaling, we further investigated their effects and the proposed mechanism on some other pathogens, such as E. coli MG1655, P. aeruginosa PAO1, and S. aureus NAS_OP_071, which exhibit distinct genetic profiles regarding the four components of AI-2 system (Figure 6B).

Specifically, we investigated the effects of polyphenol QSIMs on E. coli MG1655. The literature review and sequence alignment analysis revealed that E. coli and S. Typhimurium similarly possess a comprehensive AI-2 synthesis, transport, sensing and transduction system (Figure 6B). 45 , 46 Interestingly, both E. coli and S. Typhimurium, which possess intact AI-2 system, exhibited remarkably consistent responses to polyphenol-mediated biofilm interference across multiple experimental replicates (Figure 6C). The results demonstrated that pterostilbene significantly enhanced biofilm formation in E. coli, whereas 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol reduced biofilm formation (Figure 6C). We further examined the effects of polyphenols on the expression of motility- and chemotaxis-related genes in E. coli. Results showed that pterostilbene significantly upregulated the expression of motA (encoding the flagellar motility protein MotA), cheY (encoding the chemotaxis protein CheY), tsr (encoding the chemoreceptor Tsr), and fliC (encoding the flagellar filament structural protein FliC). In contrast, 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol had no significant effect on motA, cheY, or tsr expression but significantly downregulated fliC expression (Figure 6D). Consistent with these findings, pterostilbene enhanced swarming (Supplementary Figure S9A) and swarming (Supplementary Figure S9B) motility, while the other three polyphenols strongly inhibited motility. These results indicate that pterostilbene functions as a LuxS modifier, enhancing biofilm formation, AI-2-mediated chemotaxis, and motility in E. coli. In contrast, 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol act as AI-2 QSIMs, significantly suppressing these QS-regulated processes. Our findings demonstrate that polyphenols exert highly consistent regulatory effects on QS-mediated phenotypes in both E. coli and S. Typhimurium. This consistency may be attributed to the completeness and functional similarity of their AI-2 systems. Notably, structural analysis revealed a high degree of homology between LuxS (Supplementary Figure S9C) and LsrB (Supplementary Figure S9G), LsrR and TqsA proteins in E. coli and S. Typhimurium. Molecular docking results further demonstrated that the LuxS, LsrB, LsrR and TqsA proteins in both species exhibit similar binding modes and energy to their native substrates and polyphenols (Supplementary Table S6; Supplementary Figure S9C–P).

In addition to investigating the well-characterized AI-2 system, we conducted a comprehensive analysis to determine what deficiencies in different AI-2 components across bacterial strains affect the outcomes of polyphenol-mediated QS interferences. Specifically, although S. aureus possesses LuxS synthase, which lacks the lsr operon genes 45 (Figure 6B). Initially, we determined the MIC and growth curves of polyphenols in S. aureus (Supplementary Table S7; Supplementary Figure S10A). Our results revealed that pterostilbene significantly enhanced biofilm formation in S. aureus, whereas 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol exhibited inhibitory effects on biofilm formation (Figure 6E), which is consistent with the observations in S. Typhimurium and E. coli. In particular, the structures of LuxS protein were highly similar between S. aureus and S. Typhimurium (Supplementary Figure S10B, RMSD = 0.984 Å), and the comparable molecular docking results of LuxS with those of its original substrate and pterostilbene (Supplementary Table S6; Supplementary Figure S10C and D) were comparable. Furthermore, we observed that 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol exhibited similar inhibitory effects in S. aureus as in S. Typhimurium and E. coli, suggesting the potential existence of a receptor in S. aureus. Certainly, the observed discrepancy between the experimental results and expectations may be attributed to fundamental differences between gram-positive (S. aureus) and gram-negative (E. coli) bacteria, including variations in cell wall composition and QS regulatory mechanisms.

We further investigated the effects of polyphenols on Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1, a model Gram-negative pathogenic bacteria that lacks the integral AI-2 lsr operon pathway. We determined the MIC, sub-MIC, and growth curves of the polyphenols in P. aeruginosa (Supplementary Table S7; Supplementary Figure S10E). Our experimental results demonstrated that the sole administration of polyphenols exhibited limited efficacy in disrupting the biofilm formation of P. aeruginosa (Figure 6F; Supplementary Figure S10F). Interestingly, we also found that polyphenols alone did not significantly affect elastase production (Supplementary Figure S10G) and that pyocyanin production was independent of AI-2 presence (Supplementary Figure S10H). This suggests that, due to the absence of LuxS and LsrB proteins in P. aeruginosa, pterostilbene, 4-methylcatechol, pyrogallol, and 3-methylcatechol lack specific targets and thus exhibit no significant impact on biofilm formation in this strain. These experimental findings are consistent with our proposed mechanism of polyphenol QSIMs (Figure 6A). Notably, while exogenous AI-2 increased the production of virulence factors such as elastase (Figure 6G) and pyocyanin (Figure 6H), polyphenols effectively suppressed the production of these factors. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that exogenous AI-2 influences systems beyond the LuxS and LsrB targets, such as PctA, TlpQ and some other alternative QS-related systems, which could also be modulated by polyphenols. Certainly, further in-depth studies are required to elucidate these potential mechanisms.

To sum up, we proposed a universal mechanism by which distinct polyphenols interact with various AI-2 effector proteins to modulate QS-related phenotypes. The differential responses of pathogenic bacteria to polyphenols are likely due to variations in the completeness and conservation of AI-2 effector proteins. Therefore, it is potential to develop broad-spectrum polyphenol QSIMs with an understanding of the distribution of AI-2 effector proteins in various bacterial pathogens.

Discussion

Targeting QS represents a promising strategy for inhibiting pathogenic virulence, addressing antibiotic resistance, and treating associated diseases. In this study, we employed an integrated approach combining machine learning predictions, molecular docking calculations, SPR-aided experimental screening, and multi-level experimental validation to systematically identify and characterize polyphenol QSIMs. The results demonstrated that polyphenol QSIMs can target distinct stages of the autoinducer-2 (AI-2) QS system, which was validated by genetic, phenotypic, and cellular assays, as well as different pathogenic bacteria. Our findings underscore the importance of adopting a multi-target, multi-system perspective in QS interference, highlighting the inherent limitations of single-target approaches. This study also provides a foundation for developing dietary polyphenol QSIMs against drug-resistant bacterial infections, although comprehensive mechanistic elucidation regarding additional QS systems and resistance mechanisms remains challenging.

It is important to note that bacteria usually use more than one QS system of synthase, receptor and autoinducers to regulate downstream signal transduction components. 47 For instance, diverse QS autoinducers and receptors have been found in Salmonella spp. and E. coli. 45 In addition to the AI-2 QS system, the SdiA receptor can detect acyl-homoserine lactones (AHLs) from symbiotic bacteria, and QseB/C and QseEF can sense AI-3/epinephrine/norepinephrine molecules (Supplementary Figure S11A, B, G). Additionally, Salmonella and E. coli can utilize indole and diffusible signal factor (DSF)-type QS systems. 45 P. aeruginosa also employs hierarchical QS systems, including LuxR-type receptors (LasR, RhlR, and QscR) to sense AHLs, 48 and two-component system (TCS) kinase PmrB to sense AI-3 (Supplementary Figure S11A, C, D, E, H). 49 , 50 Similarly, S. aureus QS systems include the accessory gene regulator (Agr) system and LuxS system, which utilize autoinducing peptides (AIPs) and AI-2 as autoinducers, respectively. 27 Our docking results also showed that polyphenols bind with AHL-type receptors and AI-3 receptors (Supplementary Figure S11F and I). Interestingly, some reported studies have shown that catecholamine hormones could activate QseC in Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans 51 and (S)-equol derived from daidzein negatively regulated SdiA, 52 suggesting that polyphenol-mediated QS phenotypes may also be influenced by interactions with other QS effector proteins. Interestingly, we observed that several polyphenols known to exhibit anti-QS activity against P. aeruginosa via las and rhl systems, such as epigallocatechin gallate, 53 sesamin, 54 eugenol 55 and curcumin, 56 also demonstrated binding affinity to the AI-2 QS system, highlighting the regulatory complexity of polyphenols in P. aeruginosa. While this study focused primarily on polyphenol-mediated interference within the AI-2 QS system of several pathogenic bacteria and their underlying mechanisms, the effects of polyphenols on other QS systems and other bacteria remain underexplored. Further research is needed to comprehensively analyze the QS communication network (QSCN) and the broader impact of polyphenols for combating more drug-resistant pathogens.

Using S. Typhimurium, S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa as a model drug-resistant pathogens, this study demonstrated a direct link between polyphenols QSIMs and different phenotypes. The results showed that the QSIMs could interfere with biofilm formation, virulence, and motility while also exhibiting synergistic effects with antibiotics in S. Typhimurium. Studies have shown that AI-2 regulates the expression of the TEM gene through LsrR, and the TEM-type extended spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) are recognized as one of the most prevalent mechanisms of resistance to β-lactam antibiotics in Enterobacteriaceae. 57 Notably, beta-lactam antibiotics (ampicillin, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, and aztreonam) function by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis. As illustrated in Figure 5E, polyphenol QSIMs may regulate TEM gene expression, affect cell wall synthesis and membrane permeability, thereby reducing bacterial antibiotic resistance. However, polyphenol QSIMs showed no significant impact on resistance to aminoglycosides (e.g., streptomycin, gentamicin, kanamycin, amikacin, and apramycin), which primarily induce translational errors and disrupt bacterial protein synthesis. Unlike aminoglycosides, tetracycline and chloramphenicol exert bactericidal effects by inhibiting aminoacyl-tRNA binding and peptide chain elongation, respectively, targeting early- and mid-stage protein synthesis processes. The research also indicated that AI-2 influences tetracycline resistance in Streptococcus suis by regulating the tet(M) gene via Tn916 transposon, which suggested the potential role of polyphenols in regulating the effect of AI-2 QS on tetracycline resistance. 58 Specifically, our findings demonstrated that polyphenols may have the potential to attenuate drug resistance by interfering with biofilm formation and modulating the expression of resistance-associated genes. These findings highlight the promising role of polyphenols as dietary candidates for the complementary prevention and treatment of pathogen infections, warranting further investigation in complex biological systems.

A critical observation in our study is the divergent response of S. Typhimurium and E. coli to pterostilbene, which challenges a simplistic classification of the compound as merely an agonist or antagonist. Our data indicates that pterostilbene targets LuxS, the key synthase for AI-2. In S. Typhimurium, this interaction leads to a distinct compartmentalization defect: the intracellular AI-2 levels rise (Plsr-eGFP assay), yet the extracellular signaling activity plummets (BB170 assay, Supplementary Figure S12). This suggests that pterostilbene may interfere with the efficient export or stability of extracellular AI-2. Since the QS system relies on extracellular AI-2 accumulation to trigger virulence, this blockade effectively suppresses pathogenicity despite intracellular accumulation. Conversely, in E. coli, the same targeting of LuxS resulted in enhanced biofilm formation, likely due to species-specific structural variations in the LuxS active site or downstream transport systems (e.g., lsr operon differences). This highlights that targeting central metabolic enzymes such as LuxS can yield complex, species-dependent outcomes, necessitating precise evaluation rather than broad generalization for therapeutic applications.

In addition to exerting direct effects on pathogen bacteria, polyphenols also have comprehensive influences on host health through indirect interactions with beneficial intestinal symbiotic bacteria (probiotics). Increasingly, studies have shown that polyphenols can selectively stimulate the growth of probiotics while inhibiting pathogens, which is consistent with the anti-pathogen effects observed in this study. For instance, it has been demonstrated that polyphenols from grape and red wine suppress the growth of Clostridium and Shigella, while enhancing the abundance of probiotics including Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, Lactobacillus, Ruminococcus spp., and Akkermansia spp., thereby modulating gut microbial metabolism and reinforcing intestinal barrier protection. 59 Due to their potent antioxidant and free radical scavenging activities, polyphenols can reduce the inactivation of probiotics and may also form protective mesh structures combined with metal ions to protect probiotics. 60 , 61 Furthermore, enzymes produced by probiotics such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus can convert conjugated polyphenols into flavonoids and phenolic acids with hydrolyzed and highly active functional groups via deglycosylation, demethylation, and hydrolysis catalyzation, thereby increasing hydrogen supply and electron transfer capacity, and enhancing the antioxidant activity of polyphenols. 62 This bidirectional interaction between polyphenols and probiotics highlights the potential of polyphenols as prebiotic-like prebiotics, capable not only modulating the probiotic and metabolic functions of probiotics, but also promoting a favorable gut environment conducive to host health.

Nevertheless, there are several limitations to this study. Firstly, due to the tendency of the LsrR 63 and TqsA 64 proteins to form inclusion bodies and the protein unfolding and refolding processes may alter the binding affinity of polyphenols in vitro, so the interactions between polyphenols and TqsA/LsrR are currently supported only by molecular docking predictions. Experimental validation of the binding interactions employing SPR or mutation assays remains to be conducted to confirm the targets and fully elucidate the multi-target mechanism of the polyphenols. Secondly, our study relied primarily on in vitro bacterial models. The complex metabolic transformation of polyphenols by the host and gut microbiota in vivo, as well as their bioavailability, were not fully recapitulated. Although we evaluated the intracellular and extracellular AI-2 levels by the Plsr-eGFP 26 and the V. harveyi BB170 bioluminescence assay 44 in this study, these methods have not conducted absolute quantitative assessment of AI-2 molecules, 65 the development of accurate and precise methods for real-time quantification of AI-2 levels is critical to unraveling the regulatory mechanisms of QS systems. Finally, we have not performed animal experiments in vivo to validate whether dietary polyphenol intake can effectively modulate the gut microbiome composition or enhance colonization resistance against pathogens, which warrants further investigation.

The human gut harbors a highly diverse microbial ecosystem, and host‒microbiota interactions play a crucial role in maintaining the ecological balance of the microbiome. 66-68 Notably, interspecies AI-2 levels influence the abundance of major microbial phyla and contribute to gut dysbiosis, which is closely associated with various diseases and has emerged as a biomarker and therapeutic target for disease prevention and intervention. 69 , 70 Given the high sequence similarity and conserved binding sites of QS effector proteins, 71 we hypothesize that polyphenol-mediated modulation of microbiota phenotypes may be a widespread phenomenon within the human gut microbial ecosystem. However, whether dietary polyphenol intake can effectively reach sufficient concentrations in the gut to modulate QS systems in vivo, and whether such modulation can enhance colonization resistance against pathogens, remains to be experimentally determined. Future studies employing animal models—such as challenging polyphenol-fed mice with pathogenic bacteria (e.g., S. Typhimurium, E. coli, S. aureus, P. aeruginosa) and measuring colonization resistance interfered by polyphenols—would be essential to validate this hypothesis. Such investigations could provide critical insights into the feasibility of rationally designing dietary polyphenol compositions to modulate microbial community structure, 72 stability, 73 , 74 and function. 75
