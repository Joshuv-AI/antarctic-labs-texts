Incidentally detected pulmonary embolism during diagnostic endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA)
Authors: Sujay Halkur Shankar, Saurabh Mittal, Karan Madan, Pawan Tiwari, Vijay Hadda, Anant Mohan
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

A 42-year-old lady presented to our center with a current illness of two months duration. She complained of severe low back ache with no diurnal or postural variation. There were no symptoms suggestive of radiculopathy or spinal cord involvement. She was a lifelong non-smoker, with no prior comorbidities or history of tuberculosis. There was no spinal tenderness on examination. She was being managed conservatively with analgesics. An MRI of the spine done before presentation revealed a lytic lesion in the L1 vertebra. Subsequently, a whole body FDG PET-CT with the suspicion of malignancy was done which showed an FDG-avid large nodal mass at the left hilar region [Figure 1a] with a left lower lobe cavitating lesion [Figure 1b]. She was posted for an EBUS-TBNA for diagnosis of suspected metastatic lung carcinoma. EBUS demonstrated enlarged lymph nodes in the left paratracheal and left interlobar stations from which EBUS-TBNA was performed. Incidentally, a filling defect was noted in the pulmonary artery in the 11L position [Figure 1c].

(a) FDG-avid lesion in left hilar position on whole-body PET-CT, (b) Left lower lobe soft tissue lesion likely an infarct secondary to embolism, (c) EBUS view of pulmonary artery branch in left interlobar position showing echogenic material within the vessel suggestive of thrombus (Black arrow)

She was advised a CT pulmonary angiography for confirmation of thrombosis which showed evidence of filling defects in the segmental branches of the left lower lobe pulmonary artery. She was initiated on anticoagulation for the same. The patient was finally diagnosed with adenocarcinoma lung on EBUS-TBNA cytology.

Asymptomatic pulmonary embolism in lung cancer has a prevalence of as high as about 15%.[1] The diagnostic gold standard for the identification of pulmonary embolism is CT pulmonary angiography. Central mediastinal vasculature including the pulmonary trunk up to the segmental branches of the pulmonary artery can be visualized during EBUS where intraluminal contents (like thrombi) may be detected.[2]

Pulmonary embolism has been identified incidentally during EBUS in a variety of conditions including trauma. A recent study on patients with COVID-19 requiring VV-ECMO demonstrated its utility in diagnosing pulmonary embolism in non-transportable patients and it is one of the uncommon indications for performing EBUS-TBNA.[3–5] In one retrospective study in EBUS-TBNA performed for various indications (n = 548), incidental pulmonary embolism was detected in 4 cases (0.7%).[6] In a pilot study of 101 pulmonary embolisms diagnosed on CT angiography, EBUS was able to detect it (within 24 hours of CT angiography) in 96% of cases with no procedural complications and a procedure time between 3 and 5 min.[7] A prospective study was performed on 100 patients with suspected lung cancer who underwent protocolized EBUS-TBNA for diagnosis and concomitant pulmonary embolism screening. They found asymptomatic embolism in 2% of participants with a number needed to scan of 50.[8]

While extensive data exists on the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism during EBUS exists, recent interest in the field has grown due to a pre-clinical study performed in the pig model. The authors found that transbronchial needle injection (TBNI) of tPA led to significantly higher volume of pulmonary artery clot dissolution compared to intravenous low-dose tPA and TBNI of distilled water.[9] This study has opened up possible additional positive therapeutic implications to diagnosing pulmonary embolism via EBUS which would require further research prior to implementation.

Pulmonary embolism apart, EBUS has also been used to differentiate intravascular tumors from pulmonary embolism, transbronchial needle injections (TBNI), restaging of lung cancer by detecting vascular invasion, transvascular needle aspiration (TVNA), pericardial sampling, and thyroid sampling.[1011]

This case highlights one of the possible alternative utilities of EBUS in addition to the other uses detailed above. It is important for pulmonary physicians to be aware of the possible utility of EBUS beyond routine sampling.
