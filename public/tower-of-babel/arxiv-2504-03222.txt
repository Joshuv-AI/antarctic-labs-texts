Globally Stable Attitude Control along Neutrally Stable Trajectories
Yujendra Bharathi Mitikiri, Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract
It is quite often claimed, and correctly so, that linear methods cannot achieve global stability results for attitude control, and conversely that nonlinear control is essential in order to achieve (almost) globally stable tracking of general attitude trajectories. On account of this definitive result, and also because of the existence of powerful nonlinear control techniques, there has been relatively very little work analyzing the limits and performance of linear attitude control. This paper provides a characterization of the stability achievable for one class of linear attitude control problems, namely those leading to a constant quaternion difference, and shows how the strategy can be used to design an efficient attitude controller along a neutrally stable trajectory. In this paper, we analytically derive a critical error angle below which linearized dynamics lead to natural neutral stability for such a system, and above which the system is unstable. The dynamics are then used to derive a locally stable linear attitude controller and a globally stable nonlinear attitude controller whose performance is validated using simulations. The globally stable nonlinear controller is also shown to provide more efficient control compared to previously published work.

II
Notation and Terminology

The attitude of a rigid body will be represented by a unit-magnitude 4-component quaternion
\check{q}=[q_{0}\quad q_{1}\quad q_{2}\quad q_{3}]^{\text{T}}
, with a check accent above the symbol representing it. The first component
q_{0}
of
\check{q}
is also referred to as the scalar component
q_{\text{s}}
, while the three components
[q_{1}\quad q_{2}\quad q_{3}]^{\text{T}}
are also referred to as the vector part
\vec{q}_{\text{v}}
. The most important law of quaternion algebra is their multiplication rule
[
17
]
:

\displaystyle\check{p}\otimes\check{q}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}\\
\vec{p}_{\text{v}}\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}q_{0}\\
\vec{q}_{\text{v}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}q_{0}-\vec{p}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{q}_{\text{v}}\\
p_{0}\vec{q}_{\text{v}}+q_{0}\vec{p}_{\text{v}}+\vec{p}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{q}_{\text{v}}\end{bmatrix},

(1)

where
\vec{p}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{q}_{\text{v}}
(third term in the vector part of the product) denotes the usual vector product of two Euclidean vectors in three-dimensional Euclidean space. The skew-symmetric matrix corresponding to the vector cross product will be denoted as
[\vec{p}_{\text{v}}\times]
so that
\vec{p}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{q}_{\text{v}}
may also be expressed in matrix notation as
[\vec{p}_{\text{v}}\times]\vec{q}_{\text{v}}
. It follows from the above multiplication rule that the multiplicative identity is the quaternion with unit scalar part and zero vector part, and that the inverse of a unit-magnitude quaternion is given by negating its vector part
[
17
]
:

\displaystyle\check{q}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}q_{0}\\
\vec{q}_{\text{v}}\end{bmatrix}\Rightarrow\check{q}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}q_{0}\\
-\vec{q}_{\text{v}}\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

We will refer to unit-magnitude quaternions simply as unit quaternions for brevity.

It may be recalled that unit quaternions provide a minimal, singularity-free, and global description of rotations
[
18
]
, with composition of rotations achieved by multiplication of quaternions. The kinematics of an attitude quaternion may be expressed in terms of the body-frame angular velocity
\vec{\omega}
as
[
17
]

\displaystyle\dot{\check{q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\check{q}\otimes\vec{\omega}.

(3)

When Euclidean 3-vectors such as
\vec{\omega}
are used as quaternion operands as in (
3
), they are prefixed with a scalar component of zero.

User control over rigid-body rotation is ultimately exercised not through the angular velocity itself, but rather through torques (or moments of forces) which influence the angular acceleration and hence the angular velocity. The relationship between the angular velocity and the torque may be described by Euler’s equations in vector form as

\displaystyle\vec{n}

\displaystyle=\vec{\omega}\times J\vec{\omega}+J\dot{\vec{\omega}},

(4)

where
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the net external torque, and
J\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is the (second) moment of inertia of the rigid body in the body-fixed coordinate frame. Assuming a solid body, the moment of inertia is positive definite, and the above equation may be inverted to obtain a one-to-one correspondence between the angular acceleration and the applied external torque. Therefore, one may justifiably consider the angular acceleration itself as the user input instead of the torque.

The problem we are concerned with in this article is that of controlling the attitude
\check{q}
of a rigid body so as to track a reference attitude trajectory
\check{p}(t)
, whose kinematics are specified to be

\displaystyle\dot{\check{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\check{p}\otimes\vec{\nu},

(5)

where
\vec{\nu}
is the reference angular velocity. We will assume that the angular acceleration
\dot{\vec{\nu}}
is also given to us. We will sometimes use the half-angular-velocities,
\vec{v}
for
\vec{\nu}/2
and
\vec{w}
for
\vec{\omega}/2
, to avoid the factors of 2 and reduce notational clutter. In terms of
\vec{v}
and
\vec{w}
, we have

\displaystyle\dot{\check{p}}

\displaystyle=\check{p}\otimes\vec{v},\;\dot{\check{q}}=\check{q}\otimes\vec{w}.

(6)

III
Linear constant difference attitude dynamics

In this section, we will consider the linear stability of the constant difference attitude control problem. The problem is interesting on its own from a theoretical standpoint. From a practical perspective, the problem manifests, for example, in the presence of an unknown disturbance that is quasistatic (constant, or very slowly varying relative to time scales relevant to the problem) referred to the body-frame. In the presence of such a disturbance, we cannot ensure convergence of the attitude difference to zero. Instead, it is its derivative alone which can be driven to zero.

III-A

Nominal Dynamics

From a purely geometric perspective, the constant difference constraint implies that the angle between the two attitudes is also a constant. Let

\displaystyle\check{r}

\displaystyle=\check{q}-\check{p}

(7)

denote the constant difference between the desired attitude
\check{p}
and the body attitude
\check{q}
.
Define the unit error quaternion as

\displaystyle\check{e}

\displaystyle=\check{p}^{-1}\otimes\check{q}.

The scalar part of the error rotation may be expressed in terms of

and

\displaystyle e_{0}

\displaystyle=p_{0}q_{0}+\vec{p}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{q}_{\text{v}}=p_{0}(p_{0}+r_{0})+\vec{p}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}(\vec{p}_{\text{v}}+\vec{r}_{\text{v}})

\displaystyle=1+r_{0}p_{0}+\vec{r}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{p}_{\text{v}}.

(9)

Similarly,

and

\displaystyle e_{0}

\displaystyle=1-r_{0}q_{0}-\vec{r}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{q}_{\text{v}}.

By adding up (

) and (

\displaystyle e_{0}

\displaystyle=1-|\check{r}|^{2}/2.

(11)

The constant difference constraint (
7

The difference quaternion

\displaystyle\dot{\check{q}}=\dot{\check{p}}.

Using (

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{w}

\displaystyle\vec{v}

\displaystyle=e_{0}\vec{w}+\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{w}.

It may be noted that we could have as well used the constant difference constraint and the inverse of (

\displaystyle=\check{e}^{-1}\otimes\vec{v}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{v}=0,

(15)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Rightarrow}\vec{w}=e_{0}\vec{v}-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v}.

(16)

The relationship in (

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}(\vec{v}-\vec{w})\\

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(e_{0}-1)\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{w}\\

We have so far utilized the relation in (

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-e_{0})|\vec{w}|^{2}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}}{|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|^{2}}=\frac{|\vec{w}|^{2}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}}{1+e_{0}}.

(21)

IV
Attitude Control Design

IV-A

Nominal Trajectory Parameterization

Without loss of generality, a nominal constant quaternion difference trajectory for the desired and body attitudes
\check{p}
and
\check{q}
may be parameterized (modulo a constant rotation isomorphism) in terms of two scalar signals

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\check{e}

-s_{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}.

(37)

We can see from (

), (
36
) and (
37
) that
\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{v}=\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{w}=0
, which motivates us to define the orthonormal triad

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\hat{x}&\hat{y}&\hat{z}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}c_{\beta}&-c_{\alpha}s_{\beta}&-s_{\alpha}s_{\beta}\\
s_{\beta}&c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}&s_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\\
0&-s_{\alpha}&c_{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}c_{\beta}&-s_{\beta}\\
s_{\beta}&c_{\beta}\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
&c_{\alpha}&s_{\alpha}\\
&-s_{\alpha}&c_{\alpha}\end{bmatrix},

(38)

with derivatives

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{x}}

\displaystyle=\dot{\beta}(c_{\alpha}\hat{y}+s_{\alpha}\hat{z}),

(39)

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{y}}

\displaystyle=-\dot{\alpha}\hat{z}-\dot{\beta}c_{\alpha}\hat{x},

(40)

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{z}}

\displaystyle=-\dot{\beta}s_{\alpha}\hat{x}+\dot{\alpha}\hat{y},

(41)

\displaystyle\vec{\omega}

\displaystyle=-\dot{\alpha}\hat{x}-\dot{\beta}s_{\alpha}\hat{y}+\dot{\beta}c_{\alpha}\hat{z}.

(42)

From (

), the magnitudes of the nominal

are

\displaystyle\vec{v}^{\text{T}}\vec{v}

\displaystyle=\vec{w}^{\text{T}}\vec{w}=(\dot{\alpha}c_{\phi}^{2}\mp\dot{\beta}c_{\phi}s_{\phi}s_{\alpha})^{2}+(\dot{\beta}c_{\phi}^{2}s_{\alpha}\pm\dot{\alpha}c_{\phi}s_{\phi})^{2}

(43)

The desired (half-) angular acceleration

and

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{v}}

\displaystyle=(\ddot{\alpha}c_{\phi}^{2}-2\dot{\alpha}\dot{\beta}c_{\phi}s_{\phi}c_{\alpha}-\ddot{\beta}c_{\phi}s_{\phi}s_{\alpha}-\dot{\beta}^{2}c_{\phi}^{2}c_{\alpha}s_{\alpha})\hat{x}

\displaystyle\;+(\ddot{\alpha}c_{\phi}s_{\phi}+2\dot{\alpha}\dot{\beta}c_{\phi}^{2}c_{\alpha}+\ddot{\beta}c_{\phi}^{2}s_{\alpha}-\dot{\beta}^{2}c_{\phi}s_{\phi}c_{\alpha}s_{\alpha})\hat{y}

The body (half-) angular acceleration

IV-B

Feedback and Control of General Attitude Trajectories

For general desired attitude trajectories that do not satisfy the requirement in (
15
) for constant quaternion difference dynamics, we cannot directly use feedforward parameterization and control of the body attitude presented in the previous subsection. However, the conditions in (
13
) and (
16
) can still be used advantageously to design an efficient attitude controller along a neutrally stable trajectory as described in this subsection.

Since equations (
13
) and (
16
) together impose four scalar degrees of constraint on the three scalar coordinates of
w
, we could use feedback to define two independent control laws for
\dot{\vec{w}}

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\dot{\vec{w}}

\displaystyle=g(\vec{v},\dot{\vec{v}},\check{e},\vec{w})

\displaystyle=-\vec{w}^{\text{T}}\dot{\vec{e}}_{\text{v}}-k\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{w}

\displaystyle=e_{0}\vec{w}^{\text{T}}(\vec{v}-\vec{w})-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{v}\times\vec{w}-k\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{w},

(45)

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{w}}

\displaystyle=\vec{h}(\vec{v},\dot{\vec{v}},\check{e},\vec{w})

\displaystyle=\dot{e}_{0}\vec{v}+e_{0}\dot{\vec{v}}-\dot{\vec{e}}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v}-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\dot{\vec{v}}-L(\vec{w}-e_{0}\vec{v}+\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v})

\displaystyle=e_{0}\dot{\vec{v}}-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\dot{\vec{v}}-\vec{v}\,\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{w}-\vec{w}\,\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{v}+e_{0}\vec{v}\times\vec{w}+\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\vec{v}^{\text{T}}(\vec{v}+\vec{w})

\displaystyle\quad-L(\vec{w}-e_{0}\vec{v}+\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v}).

(46)

where
k\in\mathbb{R}
is a positive feedback gain and
L\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is a positive definite feedback matrix. We may design the overall control input as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{w}}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\vec{h}+u\frac{(g-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{h})\vec{e}_{\text{v}}}{\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}},

(47)

where
u
is a tunable weight. When
u=0
, we identically have
\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\dot{\vec{w}}^{\ast}=0
, and when
u=1
, we identically have
\dot{\vec{w}}^{\ast}=\vec{h}
.
One remaining flaw with the control law in (
47
) is that
g
(defined in (
45
)) could remain nonzero as
\check{e}
approaches identity and
\vec{e}_{\text{v}}
approaches zero, leading to unbounded control for
\dot{\vec{w}}
in (
47
). A simple remedy is to replace
\vec{w}
with
e_{0}\vec{v}-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v}
in
\dot{\vec{e}}_{\text{v}}
in
g
as follows

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=e_{0}\vec{w}^{\text{T}}((1-e_{0})\vec{v}+\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v})-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{v}\times\vec{w}-k\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{w},

(48)

yielding a bounded control law even when
\check{e}\rightarrow\check{1}
.

Rather than take the weighted average of two acceleration control laws as done above, a theoretically more fruitful approach yielding a strong stability result is to first weigh the constraints in (
13
) and (
16
), and then differentiate the combination to derive the acceleration control law as done below.

Let the target angular velocity be

\displaystyle\vec{w}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=e_{0}\vec{v}-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v}-u\frac{e_{0}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{v}}{|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|}

(49)

where
u
is a weighting factor between the constraints in (
13
) and (
16
). Note that (
13
) is only approximately satisfied for
|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|\neq 1
. Equation (
13
) could have been identically satisfied by using the combination

\displaystyle\vec{w}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=e_{0}\vec{v}-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v}-u\frac{e_{0}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{v}}{|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|^{2}}

(50)

with
|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|^{2}
replacing the
|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|
in the denominator of the third term on the right hand side of (
49
), but this new law has the even bigger problem of diverging when
|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|\rightarrow 0
upon differentiation, which is an important consideration for nonlinear control design.

Returning to (
49
), we see that

\displaystyle\lim_{u\rightarrow 0}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{w}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=e_{0}(1-|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|)\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{v},

\displaystyle\lim_{u\rightarrow 1}\vec{w}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=e_{0}\vec{v}-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v},

which satisfy (
13
) approximately and (
16
) exactly. The residual noncompliance of (
13
) is of the same order of magnitude as the residual noncompliance of (
15
) in the desired trajectory. The acceleration control law from (
49
) is therefore

\displaystyle\dot{w}

\displaystyle=\dot{e}_{0}\vec{v}+e_{0}\dot{\vec{v}}-\dot{\vec{e}}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v}-\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\dot{\vec{v}}-u\frac{\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}}{|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|}(\dot{e}_{0}\vec{v}+e_{0}\dot{\vec{v}})

\displaystyle\quad-u\frac{e_{0}(\dot{\vec{e}}_{\text{v}}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}+\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\dot{\vec{e}}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}})\vec{v}}{|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|}+u\frac{e_{0}(\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\dot{\vec{e}}_{\text{v}})\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{v}}{|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|^{3}}

\displaystyle\quad-L\left[\vec{w}-e_{0}\vec{v}+\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\times\vec{v}+u\frac{e_{0}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}\vec{e}_{\text{v}}^{\text{T}}\vec{v}}{|\vec{e}_{\text{v}}|}\right].

(51)

In the above control law,
\dot{e}_{0}
and
\dot{\vec{e}}_{\text{v}}
are given in terms of
e_{0}
,
\vec{e}_{\text{v}}
,
\vec{v}
, and
\vec{w}
through (
18
).

Calling the right hand side of (
49
) as
\vec{f}
, we have

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{w}}

\displaystyle=\dot{\vec{f}}-L(\vec{w}-\vec{f}).

Defining a positive definite candidate Lyapunov function

\displaystyle V_{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|\vec{w}-\vec{f}|^{2},

(52)

we obtain for its first derivative

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{L}

\displaystyle=-(\vec{w}-\vec{f})^{\text{T}}L(\vec{w}-\vec{f}),

(53)

which is a negative definite function, and for its second derivative

\displaystyle\ddot{V}_{L}=-(\vec{w}-\vec{f})^{\text{T}}L(\dot{\vec{w}}-\dot{\vec{f}}),

which is bounded and therefore implies asymptotic convergence of
\vec{w}
to
\vec{f}
using Barbalat’s Lemma. It should now be clear why we insisted on choosing the bounded control law of (
51
).

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we have looked at the limitations of linear attitude control for the special case of maintaining a constant quaternion difference. The resulting analysis shows that we can design a neutrally stable controller for initial error angles less than a certain value that can be derived analytically. The stability analysis can further be used to design a stable controller that achieves asymptotic convergence with lesser control effort than previous works. It would be interesting to see if it is possible to derive an optimal filtering gain
u
in (
51
) which would yield the best compromise between the rate of asymtotic error convergence and the control cost as future work.
