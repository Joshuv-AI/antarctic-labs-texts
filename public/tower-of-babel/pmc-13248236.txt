Reconfiguring institutional authority through Mood: a cross-cultural systemic functional analysis of Egyptian and American medical drama
Authors: Afnan Mohammed Ali Hassan, Nagwa Younis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study compares power dynamics in two culturally and generically distinct medical television series: the Egyptian comedy Balto (2023) and the American drama The Resident (2018). Drawing on Halliday’s Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL), particularly the Mood system, six scenes (three from each series) are analysed to show how declarative, interrogative, and imperative clauses construct hierarchy, authority, and resistance. The analysis reveals that in Balto, power is framed through humour and exaggeration, with interns positioned by softened or ironic speech, while senior doctors assert dominance in playful yet critical ways. In The Resident, authority is enforced through direct commands and evaluative questions that blur mentorship with intimidation. These contrasts demonstrate how genre and cultural context shape linguistic realizations of institutional power, highlighting Mood as a key resource for negotiating authority across divergent settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13010-026-00220-7.

Introduction

Medical dramas have long enchanted audiences by weaving together personal struggles and professional intensity, portraying stressful settings where characters face life-and-death decisions. Emerging on television in the mid-twentieth century, with early examples such as Dr. Kildare (1961) and gaining global popularity through ER (1994) and Grey’s Anatomy (2005), the genre has established itself as one of the most enduring forms of televised drama. These shows do more than entertain; as Sonego and Rocchi observe, they shape public understandings of healthcare, communication, and ethics (2) [17]. Goodman likewise argues that medical dramas function as cultural texts, offering a “psychosocial reading” that dramatizes emotional and ethical aspects of medical work (183) [5].

As a genre, medical dramas follow recognizable conventions that shape audience expectations. These conventions include high-stakes medical cases, ethical dilemmas, hierarchical workplace interactions, and the interplay of personal and professional conflicts. Viewers anticipate a combination of realistic medical procedures, emotionally charged patient–doctor encounters, and the development of character arcs within rigid institutional frameworks. While these narrative conventions remain consistent, the genre allows for variation in tone and style: comedic medical dramas often employ humour, sarcasm, and exaggerated interpersonal dynamics to critique bureaucracy, relieve tension, and humanize characters, whereas serious medical dramas emphasize realism, ethical tension, and moral complexity, foregrounding the emotional and professional burdens of healthcare work. These tonal differences also shape linguistic choices, particularly Mood distribution: in comedic contexts, declaratives may be softened, imperatives playful, and interrogatives humorous or ironic to negotiate authority in a lighter register, whereas in serious contexts, declaratives assert clear control, imperatives demand compliance, and interrogatives are often evaluative or challenging, reflecting rigid hierarchy and mentorship dynamics.

Medical dramas have increasingly shifted their narrative focus to the role of interns and residents, who occupy the lowest ranks in medical hierarchies. Their position allows the audience to see how authority is negotiated through mentorship, discipline, and conflict. In the American series The Resident (2018–), which belongs firmly to the medical drama tradition, interns and residents are shown in a serious, high-stakes environment where decisions often carry immediate consequences. In contrast, the Egyptian series Balto (2023) is a medical comedy. While it portrays similar hierarchies, interns (ṭabīb emtyaz) and residents (ṭabīb muqīm), its use of humor, exaggeration, and irony changes how power is displayed. In Balto, institutional authority is often mocked or subverted, reflecting local frustrations with bureaucracy and limited resources.

This difference in genre is central to the present study. By comparing a serious American drama with an Egyptian medical comedy, the paper highlights how grammatical Mood (declarative, interrogative, imperative) is used not only to establish hierarchy but also to dramatize or parody it. In doing so, the analysis contributes to the field of medical humanities by showing how linguistic choices reflect both cultural settings and genre conventions that shape public views of doctors, medical training, and institutional authority. The contrast between a drama and a comedy is therefore not a limitation but a strength, as it allows the study to explore how genre itself shapes the linguistic construction of authority in medical contexts.

Review of literature

Linguistic analysis of medical discourse

The role of language in constructing professional authority and interpersonal relations in medical contexts has been well-documented. For example, Roberts and Sarangi demonstrate how consultations use declarative Mood to reinforce doctors’ authority, while interrogatives facilitate negotiation with patients (112) [16]. Another study by Bellés-Fortuño [2] analyses medical lectures, finding that Mood distribution, especially declaratives, signals pedagogical control and professional stance, underscoring how SFL analysis of mood can reveal subtle forms of authority and expertise (155).

Fictional medical dramas have likewise been recognized as valuable sites for linguistic research. For instance, Mohammed identifies certainty markers in The Good Doctor that support the credibility of medical professionals (44–55) [13]. Law, in a corpus-driven multimodal analysis of House M.D., shows how linguistic variation builds character identity and authority (28) [11]. These examples confirm that medical television dialogue reflects institutional power, professional credibility, and interpersonal negotiation, making it suitable for applying Mood analysis (Roberts and Sarangi 112; Bellés-Fortuño 54; Mohammed 44–55; Law 28)[2, 11, 13, 14].

Mood system in the interpersonal metafunction

Within SFL, Mood is the core resource of the interpersonal metafunction, encoding speaker–addressee dynamics and authority structures. As Banks [1] explains, declaratives typically follow the Subject–Finite order and present statements of fact or judgment, while interrogatives and imperatives manipulate that order to request information or enact commands (25).

Ulya [18], in her study of Peter Pan, observes that declaratives are the most frequent Mood type, reflecting their role in giving information, followed by interrogatives, which require information from the addressee. Imperatives and exclamatives appear least often, indicating that characters do not primarily rely on commands or emotional outbursts to advance the interaction (56–57). This distribution highlights how the declarative Mood supports the construction of narrative progression and interpersonal balance by privileging information-giving over directive speech.

Similarly, Harlizanti [7] reports that declaratives shape the authoritative stance of BTS’s UNICEF speech, highlighting how formal speech reproduces institutional power (35). Also, Mandal [12] analyses Lady Macbeth’s soliloquy in Macbeth, showing how imperatives and declaratives project ambition and authority (16). While not grounded in SFL, Jannah’s Foucauldian analysis underscores how interpersonal power is linguistically constructed, complementing SFL-based approaches. In her examination of The Best of Me, she draws on Foucault’s theory to identify patterns of power and resistance within family structures. Although not strictly SFL-based, her study demonstrates the value of analysing interpersonal dynamics (Jannah 91) [10]. Together, these works support the view that declaratives, interrogatives, and imperatives are key linguistic tools for negotiating authority, resistance, and hierarchy in discourse (Ulya 56–57; Harlizanti 35; Mandal 16; Jannah 91) [7, 10, 12, 18].

Theoretical framework

This research draws upon Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL), focusing on Halliday’s interpersonal metafunction (2014) [6], which explores how language shapes social relationships through choices in mood and modality. Within this framework, mood analysis is instrumental in examining how power is asserted, challenged, or maintained, making it particularly relevant for understanding hierarchical interactions in medical dramas.

Mood system in SFL

Halliday and Matthiessen identify three primary types of mood in communication: declarative, interrogative, and imperative (134–140) [6]. Components of mood and residue

In Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL), every clause is composed of two main elements: the Mood (made up of the Subject and Finite) and the Residue (including the Predicator, Complement, and Adjuncts) (Halliday and Matthiessen 140) [6]. These components are essential for analysing how speakers position themselves and others in interaction. Circumstantial adjuncts (e.g., in the ER, at night) establish situational context, while mood adjuncts (e.g., certainly, probably) express the speaker’s stance or level of commitment. Understanding how Mood and Residue interact is particularly important in institutional settings like hospitals, where language not only communicates information but also enacts hierarchy, authority, and resistance. In the analysis of Balto and The Resident, this framework helps explain how doctors and interns use declaratives, interrogatives, and imperatives to assert control, challenge decisions, or negotiate professional identity.

Mood and power structures in medical settings

Language in institutional environments, particularly in medical settings, plays a central role in negotiating authority, managing uncertainty, and maintaining hierarchical order. Research in institutional discourse has shown that professional interactions are shaped by asymmetrical power relations that are enacted and reinforced through linguistic practices (Drew and Heritage 3; Sarangi and Roberts 12) [3, 14]. Within Halliday’s SFL, mood is a key resource for constructing interpersonal meaning, making it central to understanding how authority is expressed in medical contexts (Halliday and Matthiessen 134–136) [6].

In clinical settings, patterns of dominance and deference often become visible through the distribution and function of different mood types across professional ranks. Declaratives frequently encode statements of knowledge, decision-making, or indirect directives, reflecting the epistemic authority typically held by senior doctors (Heritage and Clayman 98) [8]. Interrogatives may function as information-seeking moves, but they can also signal uncertainty or requests for validation by lower-ranking staff such as interns or residents. Imperatives, especially when unmodified, tend to mark strong authority and are associated with the hierarchical command structures characteristic of medical discourse.

Situating the analysis of Balto and The Resident within this theoretical framework allows for a systematic examination of how medical professionals in Egyptian and American television dramas linguistically construct, reinforce, or challenge institutional power. By focusing on mood choices across ranks and cultural contexts, the analysis connects these series to broader discussions of authority, professionalism, and interpersonal negotiation in medical communication.

Methodology

This study employs a mixed-methods approach within Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL), combining quantitative analysis of Mood types with qualitative interpretations of how Mood choices construct power relations. To begin, six scenes were selected from two series: three from the Egyptian comedy Balto (2023) and three from the American drama The Resident (2018, Season 1). These particular scenes were chosen for their clear depiction of interactions between junior doctors and their superiors, where power dynamics, hierarchy, or mentorship are evident.

Next, the scenes were manually transcribed in their original languages: Arabic for Balto and English for The Resident. Non-verbal cues, such as tone, were noted only when they affected the interpretation of Mood. Cultural expressions, such as Egyptian politeness markers in Balto, were preserved due to their relevance to power dynamics. The unit of analysis was the clause, as SFL treats the clause as the basic unit for Mood analysis. For example, “Three times we thought we had a heart. Three times he was sent home” was split into two clauses. Each clause was coded based on its Mood type (declarative, interrogative, imperative), as per SFL’s distinction of interpersonal function, and the character roles (intern, resident, senior doctor) were assigned according to their spoken lines within each scene.

Because naturally occurring speech does not always follow neat grammatical prototypes, ambiguous or non-literal clauses were interpreted through pragmatic function, following Halliday and Matthiessen (134–36) [6]. Rhetorical questions were coded according to their actual communicative force; for example, when an interrogative operated as a reprimand rather than a genuine question. Mitigated or indirect directives were treated as imperatives when their pragmatic aim was to issue a command, even when softened through politeness markers or modal verbs. Elliptical clauses; such as “Men?” or “Followed all the rules” were coded based on their contextual interpersonal meaning. These steps ensured that even clauses that deviated from surface grammatical patterns were classified consistently and in line with SFL principles.

To maintain reliability and objectivity, coding was carried out by two trained SFL coders working independently. Each coder classified all clauses across the six scenes: Scene 1 (Balto, Ep. 1), Scene 2 (Balto, Ep. 7), Scene 3 (Balto, Ep. 10), Scene 4 (The Resident, S1E2), Scene 5 (The Resident, S1E1), and Scene 6 (The Resident, S1E2). After completing their individual coding, they met to compare classifications. Rather than treating differences as mistakes, both coders used them as opportunities to explore why a particular Mood choice might be ambiguous in emotionally charged or hierarchical medical settings, or why a clause boundary might reasonably be interpreted in more than one way. Through discussion, they arrived at shared readings that felt both fair and theoretically grounded. This approach follows long-standing practices in interpersonal metafunction analysis where having more than one perspective helps capture the shades of meaning in spoken dialogue.

Appendices A and B contain the full coded data. Appendix A includes, for each of the three Balto scenes, (1) a table presenting the transcript with clause-by-clause coding and (2) a table showing the Mood distribution for that scene. Appendix B mirrors this structure for The Resident, providing both the coded transcript and the scene-level Mood distribution. The Mood distributions for each scene, shown in Appendix A and B, illustrate how different characters use Mood to assert power, negotiate authority, or express subordination. These tables compare the frequency of declaratives, interrogatives, and imperatives, highlighting the power dynamics in both series. For example, in Balto, the intern relies mostly on declaratives and mitigated imperatives, while the senior doctor uses declaratives, rhetorical interrogatives, and direct imperatives to assert authority. For the quantitative analysis, the coded clauses were tallied by character role (intern, resident, senior doctor). These frequencies were then compared across the two series to analyse Mood distribution at different hierarchical levels. The detailed tables in Appendix A and B show the complete breakdown of each scene.

In parallel, the qualitative analysis examined how Mood choices reflected authority, resistance, and compliance. For example, in Balto, imperatives softened with “والنبي” (“By the Prophet”) indicated pleading rather than commanding. In The Resident, rhetorical questions like “Were you Affirmative Action at Harvard?” functioned as insults rather than inquiries. This interpretation focused on how Mood choices reflect cultural values, such as humour in Egyptian workplace hierarchies versus efficiency in American hospital discourse.

The qualitative analysis shows how the imbalance of power in this scene is reflected in the way each character structures their clauses. Atef’s declaratives are framed as personal truths rather than assertions of authority. For instance, when he says, “أنا مش من هنا يا دكتور” (“I’m not from here, doctor”) (BAL-7), the declarative states a fact but lacks any demand for recognition or institutional weight. This pattern shows Atef pleading for empathy, not negotiating as an equal. His interrogative (polite request), “ممكن تمضي لي وتختم لي على الورقه بتاعه التظلم؟” (“Can you please sign and stamp the complaint for me?”) (BAL-3), is typical of Egyptian politeness strategies, where a question softens what is essentially a request. Even his only imperative, “والنبي امضي لي على الورقه” (“By the Prophet, sign this paper for me”) (BAL-11), is mitigated by a religious appeal, turning the command into a plea. In short, Atef’s Mood choices reflect his lack of power: he relies on declaratives to state personal circumstances and on softened interrogatives or mitigated imperatives to negotiate authority he does not possess.

The Senior Doctor, in contrast, uses declaratives to assert institutional authority, e.g., “ده القانون وده أمر إداري” (“This is the law, this is an administrative order”) (BAL-8). These are not neutral statements of fact but declaratives functioning as institutional judgments. His supposed “imperative” in the same clause (“بقول لك ايه … مليش فيه”) (“Listen up! I’m telling you … I have nothing to do with it”) is in fact a rhetorical interrogative, since it does not command action but dismisses Atef’s request by ridiculing its legitimacy. Likewise, his use of repeated rhetorical interrogatives, “تظلم إيه. يا ابني أنا بسايسك” (“What complaint … my son, I was deceiving you …”) (BAL-4), are not genuine questions but power moves designed to expose Atef’s weakness and mock his persistence.

This disparity in their Mood choices highlights a mismatch in their interpersonal dynamics. Atef’s tone is deferential, reflecting his subordinate position, as he uses mitigated forms of requests and declaratives. Conversely, the Senior Doctor adopts an authoritative tone, using declaratives and rhetorical interrogatives that assert his institutional power while mocking Atef’s position. These Mood choices not only reflect their differing levels of authority but also the cultural context of hierarchy and politeness strategies in Egyptian medical settings. Thus, the quantitative data (Table 1) and the qualitative analysis together show how Mood is not only a grammatical choice but also a reflection of institutional hierarchy, cultural politeness strategies, and interpersonal stance. Scene 1 demonstrates that in Egyptian medical settings, superiors may combine institutional declaratives with humour and ridicule, while subordinates resort to mitigated requests, revealing the unequal negotiation of authority.

Qualitative analysis of their speech patterns further supports this interpretation. Atef’s use of interrogatives, such as “مين اللي قالك كده يا حاجة؟” / “Who told you that, ma’am?” (BAL-14) and “عيادة الأسنان هي اللي قالت لك عايزين 50 جنيه؟” / “The dental clinic told you they want 50 pounds?” (BAL-15), represents a shift from passive submission to active inquiry. Although these questions remain framed politely, they reflect a deeper engagement with the issue at hand, positioning Atef as someone trying to uncover institutional truth. However, his declaratives, such as “بس ده ما ينفعش يا دكتور سمير” / “But that’s not right, Dr. Sameer” (BAL-20), remain defensive, appealing more to ethical norms than asserting personal authority. In contrast, Sameer’s declaratives like “أهو نفع! هتعمل إيه برضو؟” / “Well, it worked! … What are you gonna do now?!” (BAL-21) function as cynical assertions of control, dismissing Atef’s moral reasoning and reinforcing his dominance.

In SFL, terms of address are an important part of the interpersonal metafunction, as they realize interpersonal meaning by signalling power, social distance, and relational stance between participants. Address forms indicate the relative ranking of power, the level of solidarity or familiarity, and the speaker’s attitude toward the addressee, making them a key resource for enacting authority and negotiating social relationships (Halliday and Matthiessen 134–36). In Balto, the power imbalance is evident in how characters use these forms. Atef consistently uses respectful titles, such as “دكتور سمير / Dr. Sameer” (BAL-20) and “مس ألفت / Miss Olfat” (BAL-18), which maintain a formal tone and index his recognition of hierarchical norms and institutional authority. These choices reflect his adherence to professional and hierarchical expectations. In contrast, Sameer employs dismissive address forms like “يا حبيبي” / “sweetheart” (BAL-24), stripping Atef of his professional dignity, and “يا بت ألفت” / “girl Olfat”, showing that he has no respect whatsoever for anyone in this medical setting—not for the doctor who holds a temporary superior position, nor for the nurse who manages his own work, and not even for the patients (BAL-24). This contrast in address forms highlights two competing models of authority: Atef’s formal, institutional authority versus Sameer’s informal, dominant control. The cultural dimension of these address forms is also crucial, as Egyptian politeness markers and familiar terms carry strong interpersonal and hierarchical connotations, which SFL interprets as part of the system of resources used to construct social relationships and negotiate power within specific cultural contexts.

The climactic exchange in this scene underscores the stark difference in authority between the two characters. Atef’s declarative, “لو دي هتخليك تشتغل فخد … اهي” / “If that’s what you need to work, then here—take it” (BAL-22), although grammatically a declarative, functions as a submission move. It signals Atef’s inability to assert his authority, offering money in an attempt to buy peace, which contrasts with Sameer’s sarcastic imperative, “يلا اطلع فوق يا حبيبي” / “Go on, get upstairs, sweetheart” (BAL-24). Sameer’s command further humiliates Atef, reinforcing his own sense of superiority. Finally, Atef’s parting line, “ما أنا هطلع فوق … عشان ده مكاني” / “I will go upstairs—because that’s where I belong” (BAL-25), delivered with a declarative mood, is a futile, almost childish attempt to assert dominance. It lacks any real authority in the moment and feels performative, yet it also subtly foreshadows his future rise and psychological shift. Despite losing the battle, Atef signals the coming transformation of his identity, a spark that will ignite in Scene 3 when he takes full control. This moment is pivotal as it captures a character in the middle of a transition from submission to leadership, communicated entirely through subtle linguistic choices.

In sum, this scene illustrates a crucial transitional moment for Atef, who begins to challenge Sameer’s authority through his use of interrogatives. However, his declaratives remain tied to moral appeals rather than institutional power. Sameer, conversely, uses declaratives and imperatives not just to communicate but to assert dominance and belittle Atef. The quantitative data confirms this power imbalance, but the qualitative analysis reveals how the same mood types can serve opposite functions depending on the tenor and interpersonal positioning of the speakers. Atef’s interrogatives and declaratives reflect his moral struggle for authority, while Sameer’s use of declaratives and imperatives reinforces his cynical control over the situation.

The qualitative analysis of this scene highlights the comedic nature of the exchange, where humour is achieved through exaggerated declaratives, mock medical authority, and ridicule. Atef begins with a declarative designed to fabricate authority: “الحقنة دي فيها مخدر الدبوكوفين” / “This injection contains the anesthetic Dubocovin” (BAL-26). Sameer’s hesitant response, “دد.دييروفيكين؟” / “D … D-Dirovikin?” (BAL-27), shows his confusion and loss of composure, exposing his lack of medical expertise. This shift from Sameer’s uncertain response to Atef’s confident declarative marks the beginning of the power dynamic reversal, where Atef gains control through confident and fabricated authority.

Atef immediately capitalizes on Sameer’s confusion with a series of declaratives that combine authority with insult. For example, “كنت فاكر إنك كدكتور المفروض تعرفه” / “I thought that as a doctor, you were supposed to know that” (BAL-28), a rhetorical attack rather than an informative statement. The declarative Mood here is weaponized to degrade Sameer, reinforcing Atef’s superior position. Similarly, “بس واضح إنك لا دكتور ولا نيلة” / “But clearly, you’re neither a doctor nor anything close” (BAL-28) and “المخدر ده بيدوه للحيوانات” / “That anesthetic is used on animals” (BAL-28) use exaggeration to belittle Sameer, comparing him to an animal and exposing his ignorance in front of colleagues. The declarative Mood in these instances is not informative but rather designed to humiliate Sameer, asserting Atef’s linguistic superiority.

In contrast, Sameer’s Mood choices highlight his defensiveness and desperation. His imperative plea, “متتجننش … متوديش نفسك في ستين داهية!” / “Don’t lose your mind … don’t drag yourself into deep trouble!” (BAL-31), is framed as a plea rather than a command, signalling his vulnerability and loss of control. The imperative Mood here, instead of asserting power, reflects Sameer’s weakness, marking a stark contrast to Atef’s commanding use of declaratives. His defensive interrogative, “انتوا واقفين تتفرجوا عليا يا حلاليف؟” / “You’re all just standing there watching me, you useless pigs?!” (BAL-31), fails to elicit support, further exposing his collapse and turning the interrogative into a source of ridicule. The informality and derogation in his tone only magnify his loss of authority.

Discussion

The quantitative results confirm that declaratives overwhelmingly dominate in both Balto and The Resident, but their pragmatic functions differ markedly across ranks and cultural contexts. Interns such as Atef (Balto) and Devon (The Resident) rely on declaratives framed as personal accounts or admissions of inexperience, while their superiors deploy declaratives as institutional judgments. This pattern aligns with earlier SFL-based studies of institutional discourse, which found declaratives to be the primary resource for enacting authority in hierarchical workplaces [4, 9]. However, the present analysis extends this by demonstrating that declaratives are not monolithic: they range from deferential statements of fact (Atef in Scene 1) to declaratives weaponized as ridicule (Atef in Scene 3) or moral accusations (Conrad in Scene 1). The resident in The Resident uses declaratives at a notably higher rate (68.7%) than the resident in Balto (46.2%), quantitatively reflecting the drama’s emphasis on unmitigated professional dominance.

Conclusion

While the analysis is based on a small set of six selected scenes, these examples were chosen for their clear depiction of hierarchical interactions and power negotiation. The findings show that, within these scenes, both series dramatize hierarchy through linguistic choices, though they do so in distinct ways that reflect their cultural and stylistic contexts. In Balto, Atef’s passivity and Sameer’s exploitation illustrate how humour and sarcasm function as indirect tools of resistance and authority. Declaratives and imperatives often serve as comic devices, ridiculing hierarchy without dismantling it. In The Resident, by contrast, power is negotiated through more direct declaratives, imperatives, and rhetorical questions that assert professional competence or moral authority. power is expressed through more direct declaratives, imperatives, and rhetorical questions, which assert professional competence or moral authority. Devon’s progression from a hesitant intern to an assertive professional reflects the series’ emphasis on individual responsibility and ethical defiance. These observations are drawn from the selected scenes and are intended as exploratory rather than definitive conclusions about the entire genres or all cultural practices. Within this context, the analysis suggests that Mood choices reveal interpersonal struggles and also reflect broader cultural logics of authority: relational politeness and ridicule in Egyptian comedy versus professionalism and moral confrontation in American drama. The study contributes to genre-based Mood analysis by demonstrating how the same linguistic resources—declaratives, interrogatives, and imperatives—can operate differently across cultural contexts, shaping distinct models of resistance, hierarchy, and power negotiation. The findings are context-bound to the selected scenes and may not generalize to all medical dramas or cultural settings. Future research could extend the analysis to additional scenes or series and explore audience responses to determine how these linguistic representations influence perceptions of professional authority and workplace dynamics.
