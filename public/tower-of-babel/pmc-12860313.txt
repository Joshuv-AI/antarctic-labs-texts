Recurrent distal ventriculoperitoneal shunt migration in an obese patient with high-grade glioma: A case report
Authors: Ahmed H. Bin Salem, Faisal T. Muzian, Hussam Y. Kutub
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: In neurosurgical practice, ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunting is the predominant intervention for hydrocephalus. While shunt-related complications such as obstruction, infection, and mechanical failure are well recognized, distal catheter migration remains an uncommon but clinically significant problem. Migration may occur internally or externally and is influenced by patient-specific and technical factors.Case Description: A 63-year-old woman with a newly diagnosed World Health Organization grade IV astrocytoma presented with acute obstructive hydrocephalus requiring emergent VP shunt insertion. Two weeks later, she developed a painless abdominal swelling; imaging revealed distal catheter migration into the anterior abdominal wall. Revision surgery was performed with repositioning of the catheter into the peritoneal cavity. Within 1 month, the patient presented again with abdominal fullness and recurrent catheter migration. A second revision with shunt extension and secure fixation was required, involving dense intra-abdominal adhesions. Obesity, repeated revisions, and increased intra-abdominal pressure were considered major contributing factors.Conclusion: This case highlights recurrent distal migration of a VP shunt in an obese patient with glioblastoma-related hydrocephalus. Although rare, distal catheter migration is an important cause of shunt malfunction. Awareness of predisposing factors such as obesity and adhesions, combined with careful intraoperative technique and secure distal catheter fixation, is crucial to reducing the likelihood of recurrence.

INTRODUCTION

Hydrocephalus is a clinical and neuroradiographic diagnosis characterized by an abnormal accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), which can occur in concurrence with, or in the absence of, changes to intracranial pressure.[5]

A ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt is a common surgical intervention used to manage cases of hydrocephalus caused by various etiologies, including tumors, post-traumatic head injury, and post-infectious conditions. Certain elements that can contribute to shunt survival include age at placement, etiology, type of hydrocephalus, and prior treatment before shunting.[9] However, despite its common use, the procedure is associated with several complications, including infection, obstruction, and mechanical failure.[10] It occurs in approximately one-fifth to four-fifths of all inserted cases with a high rate of revision.[8] Distal migration is a rare but fatal consequence, recognized as a complication that can occur in three ways: internally, when the catheter invades visceral structures; externally, when the catheter penetrates the body; or combined, involving both internal and external routes. It may occur at various intra-abdominal or extra-abdominal sites, including the thoracic cavity, gastrointestinal tract, or through the abdominal wall and skin.[4] Such migrations can result in shunt malfunction, infection, or abdominal complications. Nonmechanical complications may also arise, including CSF leaks, pseudocyst formation, and shunt tract infections. Rarely, these can lead to meningitis, peritonitis, or CSF infection.

In this report, we present a case of a patient who developed recurrent distal catheter migration of a VP shunt into the anterior abdominal wall and outline its clinical presentations, possible etiologies, and management strategies.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 63-year-old female with no significant chronic medical history presented to the emergency department after experiencing a brief loss of consciousness while in the bathroom. She had fallen and landed on her lower back. On arrival, she was alert, oriented, and moving all four limbs without difficulty. Her vital signs were stable.

A non-contrast computed tomography scan of the brain revealed a poorly defined midline lesion in the splenium of the corpus callosum, surrounded by vasogenic edema. Imaging of the lumbosacral spine identified a transverse fracture of the coccyx, along with sacrococcygeal subluxation. Further, evaluation with brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed a mass involving the body of the corpus callosum, extending bilaterally into the adjacent white matter. The lesion measured approximately 4.5 × 3 × 3 cm and showed a heterogeneous signal on T2-weighted and fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) MRI sequences, with irregular peripheral enhancement [Figure 1]. In addition, the mass caused a mild compressive effect on the lateral ventricles, though no obvious hydrocephalus was observed at that time. Smaller nodular foci were also noted in the rostrum and left posterior periventricular region.

Preoperative MRI. The red arrrow shows a 4.5 × 3 × 3 cm mass with bilateral extension, heterogeneous enhancement.

On day of the operation, a biopsy was performed through a right frontal burr hole. Intraoperative frozen section was consistent with glioma, and subsequent pathology confirmed a World Health Organization grade IV astrocytoma. Following the procedure, she developed a new weakness on the left side. She started physiotherapy and continued taking dexamethasone.

In the days that followed, the patient began reporting worsening headaches and intermittent dizziness. Repeating the MRI on day 11 postoperative, demonstrated interval progression of the tumor. Subsequently, on postoperative day 24, her level of consciousness declined, with her Glasgow coma scale (GCS) falling to 14. A head computed tomography (CT) revealed acute hydrocephalus [Figures 2a and b], likely due to obstructed CSF flow from tumor mass effect. A VP shunt was inserted emergently. Her postoperative GCS initially dropped to 6 but gradually improved to full orientation by the following 2 days. A post-surgical CT confirmed satisfactory shunt placement and no new hemorrhage.

Post-operative CT. The (a) sagittal and (b) axial views, the red arrows show enlarged lateral ventricles consistent with hydrocephalus.

First shunt revision

About 2 weeks later, the patient experienced soft, painless swelling in the abdomen. Aside from this, they remained asymptomatic, with no fever, vomiting, or headache. A CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis revealed mispositioning of the VP shunt, showing the distal end coiled in the left anterior abdominal wall along with a surrounding fluid collection [Figure 3]. The next day, we revised the shunt and reopened the previous abdominal incision, and with assistance from the general surgery team, the shunt tubing was repositioned after accessing the peritoneal cavity. The cranial wound was also opened to confirm that the ventricular catheter was positioned adequately at a depth of 6 cm. The procedure was completed without complications.

Abdominal CT The coronal view, the red arrow shows Malpositioned VP shunt with coiled distal end in the left anterior abdominal wall with surrounding fluid collection marked with the yellow arrow.

Second revision and shunt extension

In the coming month, the patient presented with abdominal fullness and imaging consistent with another episode of catheter migration [Figure 4]. Twenty-two days after the first shunt revision, she underwent a second revision. Dense intra-abdominal adhesions were present, requiring general surgery involvement. The existing peritoneal catheter was extended using a straight connector and sutured securely in place. Approximately 60 cm of tubing was inserted to ensure stability [Figure 5]. Clear CSF flow was confirmed before closure. The procedure was completed without incident, and the patient returned to the recovery room in stable condition.

DISCUSSION

Hydrocephalus is a well-known condition that has been treated by a VP shunt for decades, and it is associated with multiple complications. It reported that in the 1st year, the risk of shunt survival reached 40%, and by the 2nd year, the failure rate was up to 50%.[2,12] Of those, distal catheter migration is one of the less frequent but clinically significant mechanical complications of VP shunts, comprising 10% of distal shunt complications and occurring in approximately 1 in 1000 cases.[7] In adults, migration mostly occurs internally, while in pediatrics, it occurs externally. In our case, we observed a case of distal VP shunt migration with extrusion through the anterior abdominal wall. It has been reported in the literature, for example, Abode-Iyamah (2019) reported that individuals with a BMI of >30 kg/m2 and history of multiple revision are at risk of catheter migration.[1] Similarly, Chugh et al.[3] reported that in malnourished patients with tuberculous meningitis, the distal catheter extruded through the anterior abdominal wall. This suggests that compromised tissue integrity may facilitate shunt erosion, leading to its exit through the body wall.[3] Likewise, abdominal wall defects, including incisional and ventral hernias, have been identified as sites for distal catheter complications, suggesting that pre-existing weaknesses may allow migration or extrusion. Another proposed mechanism is illustrated by Scott et al. initially suggested the “windlass effect,” in which repetitive head and neck movements reel the peritoneal tube proximally until it coils around the valve.[11] In contrast, Nakahara et al. described a case where the distal catheter migrated outward into the abdominal wall, a process credited to elevated intra-abdominal pressure combined with the extensive subcutaneous dead space typical of obesity.[7] A leading theory is that increased intra-abdominal pressure led to the catheter extrusion, given that it is commonly seen in obese individuals. In addition, it has been suggested that the switch to catheters with a hydrophilic surface and reduced friction may contribute to catheter migration.[6] Notably, our patient was obese, a factor that likely amplified the same outward traction forces donating to the repeated distal catheter migration. In addition, extreme variants include upward passage through the thoracic cavity.[13] These reports underscore the sheer diversity of migration pathways and suggest that no single mechanism can explain every failure.

In our case, the repeated distal shunt malfunctions were the result of several contributing factors. The patient’s obesity likely played a significant role by increasing intra-abdominal pressure and expanding subcutaneous dead space, both of which can promote catheter displacement. In addition, the short interval between multiple revisions may have weakened the abdominal fascia, making it more susceptible to further migration with regular body movements.

CONCLUSION

Distal shunt malfunction remains a difficult complication in the management of tumor-related hydrocephalus, particularly in patients with multiple risk factors. In this case, despite appropriate surgical technique and multiple revisions, the patient experienced multiple episodes of distal catheter migration within 2 months. Contributing factors likely include obesity, adhesion, and repeated movement. These challenges reflect the role of patient anatomy and mechanical strain on the shunt system. Nonetheless, this case reflects the importance of a flexible, patient-centered approach that anticipates complications and adapts management strategies to the individual.
