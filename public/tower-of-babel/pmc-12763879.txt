Heatwaves and emergency department utilisation in Queensland: a 10-year retrospective study
Authors: Richard C Franklin, Amy E Peden, Jemma C King, Blesson Varghese, Nicole Mandalios, Luke Lawton, Michael T Meehan, Kenneth Koon-Ho Wong, Hannah M Mason
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, temperatures are increasing due to the effects of climate change. Extreme heat is a public health threat, increasing the burden on health systems during heatwaves, and the days following. Australia is susceptible to extreme heat and must plan accordingly to ensure health systems can respond as heatwave conditions increase in frequency, severity, and duration. This study investigates the impact of heatwaves on Emergency Department (ED) presentations in the state of Queensland, Australia, considering key demographic, clinical, and temporal factors.MethodsWarm-season (November-March) ED presentations (2010-19) obtained from Queensland Health at the postcode level were analysed in relation to heatwaves. Heatwaves were defined using the Excess Heat Factor, a metric designed by the Australian Bureau of Meteorology. Quasi-Poisson regression models produced incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to quantify differences in ED demand between heatwave and non-heatwave days. Subgroup analyses were conducted by heatwave severity, rurality, sex, age, primary diagnosis, triage category and financial year.ResultsThere was a 10.47% (IRRadj: 1.10; 95% CI: 1.10, 1.11) increase in ED presentations on heatwave days. The effect was greatest during extreme heatwaves (IRRadj: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.20, 1.26). There was a direct relationship between increasing impact and rurality, with those living in very remote areas most impacted, experiencing a 54.26% increase in ED presentations in comparison to non-heatwave days (IRRadj: 1.54; 95% CI: 1.50, 1.59). There was no clear age-related trend, and no significant variation by sex. Presentations increased on heatwave days for most primary diagnoses, with the most notable increase observed in cases classified under ‘endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic disorders’ (IRRadj: 1.27; 95% CI: 1.24, 1.29). The least urgent triage category (category 5) exhibited the most significant increase in ED incidence on heatwave days (IRRadj: 1.27; 95% CI: 1.25, 1.29). Temporal trends showed that the 2015-16 financial year (IRR: 1.13; 95% CI: 1.12, 1.15) demonstrated the greatest increase in ED incidence on heatwave days.ConclusionsEmergency departments must prepare for higher numbers of people presenting during heatwaves. The variability in impact across different demographic, clinical, and temporal factors underscores the complex relationship between heatwaves and health system demand. As climate change intensifies heatwaves, future research, including prospective studies, will be needed to provide essential evidence to inform public health planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13777-4.

Introduction

Temperatures are increasing globally due to the effects of climate change, and projections show that heatwaves are expected to occur with more frequency and greater intensity in the future [1, 2]. Heatwaves are considered the deadliest natural hazard both globally and in Australia, causing more deaths than all other hazards combined [1, 2]. The effects of extreme heat and heatwaves are significant and include intensification of forest/bush fires, loss of harvests and livestock, and detrimental effects to human health, including loss of life [2–5].

Australia is susceptible to heatwaves and periods of extreme heat [6]. A recent Royal Commission into National Natural Disaster Arrangements highlighted the increasing and intersecting impact of extreme heat with other disasters, such as bushfires [7]. Periods of extreme heat have been found to result in increased health system demand across several public health services, impacting hospital admissions [8–10], emergency department (ED) presentations [11–13] and ambulance callouts [14–16] and resulting in increased mortality [17, 18]. Extreme heat also has economic consequences [19], costing Australia an estimated $8.7 billion AUD in lost labour productivity annually [20], and driving up costs for healthcare [21]. Studies conducted in Adelaide, South Australia [22], and Perth, Western Australia [23], have found that heatwaves significantly increase emergency department presentations and healthcare costs, with projections indicating a growing burden under future climate scenarios. Given these wide ranging impacts, there is a need for health systems to prepare for a future characterised by increasingly frequent and severe heatwaves [24].

Queensland is an Australian state in the country’s northeast, with an average estimated resident population of 4.76 million over the 2010–2019 period [25]. The state features a mix of metropolitan, regional, rural and extremely remote populations that span both tropical and subtropical environments. It is known that extreme heat contributes to the highest proportion of injury hospitalisations caused by extreme weather events in Australia, and this is also true for the state of Queensland [26]. Previous work has explored the impact of extreme temperatures and heatwaves on mortality and health system utilisation, including ambulance callouts, ED presentations, hospital admissions, and deaths; however, the majority of studies explore Brisbane (the capital city) [27–30] to the detriment of non-metropolitan locations [30].

Extreme heat impacts health by causing stress on the human body, disrupting the body’s ability to thermoregulate [31]. During a heatwave, the body’s natural cooling systems, such as increased blood flow to the skin and sweating, can be overwhelmed, leading to dehydration, heat exhaustion, heat stroke, and the worsening of pre-existing conditions [31]. In Queensland, there are population groups that have been identified in previous research who are particularly vulnerable to the health consequences of heatwaves [17, 30, 32]. For example, young children [33], older adults [34], and those with chronic medical conditions [35]. As climate change intensifies, it is crucial to understand and mitigate the health impacts of heatwaves for those disproportionately impacted.

Emergency Departments are the primary entry point to hospital services, providing the first line of care for most entering the health system and, as such, are more likely to be impacted by the immediate effects of a heatwave. In Queensland, EDs have experienced record-breaking numbers of patients accessing treatment [36]. Heatwaves can add additional pressure to an already stressed service and have been shown to increase ED patient volume, acuity and length of stay [37]. The impact of heatwaves on ED services across the entire state of Queensland is currently unknown. To address this gap, this study aims to explore the impact of heatwaves on ED presentations in Queensland, considering key demographic, temporal, and clinical factors to promote data-informed recommendations for ED planning, resourcing and wider community-facing alerts.

Materials and methods

A retrospective population-based analysis was undertaken for ED presentations in Queensland for the period of 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2019. Analysis of the warm season months (November-March) was conducted to avoid the confounding effects of seasonality, as these warmer months exhibited the potential for heatwave conditions within the climate data (proportion of heatwave days >2.0%). This seasonal window also aligns with the Bureau of Meteorology’s national heatwave service, which since its launch in 2014 has defined the heatwave season as November to March [38]. Although in more recent years the service has been extended to begin in October, the November–March period was most relevant to the 2010–2019 study timeframe.

Ethical approval was obtained (Children’s Health Queensland HHS HREC: LNR/21/QCHQ/72461), and approval was granted under the Public Health Act (PHA 72461).

Data collection

Historical temperature data were obtained from the Queensland Department of Environment, Tourism, Science and Innovation’s SILO Climate Database [39], from which an Excess Heat Factor (EHF) value and heatwave severity category were calculated for each day across the study period by postcode (Australian Statistical Geography Standard [ASGS]: Volume 5 [40]). The EHF is a metric developed by the Bureau of Meteorology to define locally relevant heatwave periods [41]. The metric incorporates short-term and long-term temperature anomalies and categorises heatwaves by their severity [41, 42]. This takes into account the idea that people acclimatise (at least to some extent) to their local climate [43], with respect to its temperature variation across regions and throughout the year, but may not be prepared for a sudden rise in temperature above that of the recent past. For more detailed information regarding the methodology of the EHF, see Nairn and Fawcett (2015). As the EHF is a three-day metric, a single day was coded as a heatwave day if the EHF severity exceeded zero, along with the two days following it. All other days were coded as non-heatwave days (i.e. days with negative EHF severity values that did not come within two days after a heatwave), following the methodology of Franklin et al. [17].

Emergency department data were sourced from the Queensland Emergency Data Collection [44]. The data included presentations from public EDs and facilities that provide emergency care services [45]. Variables of interest included postcode of patient residence, presentation date, visit type (emergency presentation, planned return visit, pre-arranged admissions, and dead on arrival), episode end status, age group, biological sex, triage category (where 1 is most urgent, and 5 is least urgent), and principal diagnosis code (International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision [ICD-10]). Principal diagnosis codes were aggregated to ICD-10 chapter levels [46]. The ED data were linked to the EHF data by postcode of usual residence. Cases from postcodes in which climate data were missing were removed (4219, 4471, 4813, 4857; n = 967).

Census population data were sourced from the ABS Tablebuilder [47] from 2006, 2011, 2016, 2021 and, where possible, was linearly interpolated between years. There were 1837 cases that were removed due to a lack of population data for their postcode and year, and 117 cases were removed where population was recorded as zero (Supplementary File 1).

Data analysis

Data were exported from Microsoft Excel 2016 and imported into RStudio version 4.4.3 [48]. Population exposure days were used to measure person-time risk. We estimated the crude association between heatwaves and ED presentations using an incidence rate ratio (IRR). Incidence rates were calculated separately for heatwave and non-heatwave days by dividing the total number of ED presentations in each category by the corresponding person-time at risk. The crude IRR was then obtained by dividing the rate on heatwave days by the rate on non-heatwave days. 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated using large-sample methods for person-time data as described by Rothman et al. [49].

Given that the outcome was count data and aligning with common practices in heatwave research [50, 51], Poisson regression was used to model ED presentations. However, overdispersion was detected (dispersion statistic >1), violating Poisson assumptions [52]. Accordingly, Quasi-Poisson regression was used to accommodate the over dispersed count data [53]. Month, day of week, and public holiday demonstrated significant variations across strata in the association between heatwaves and ED presentations (Supplementary File 2) and were subsequently adjusted for in the Quasi-Poisson regression models. Stratified analyses were conducted to explore potential variation in effect by heatwave severity, rurality, trends over time, biological sex, age, primary diagnosis, triage category, and age. Results

During the months of November through March in Queensland from 2010 to 2019, there were 1,796,257 ED presentations on heatwave days and 4,401,354 ED presentations on non-heatwave days. The total number of heatwave postcode-days was 172,541, and the total number of non-heatwave postcode-days was 469,161. Descriptive statistics are provided in Table 1. Additional descriptive analyses examined population age structure by rurality, the relationship between age and triage urgency, and the relationship between rurality and triage urgency (Supplementary File 4). Very remote areas had a younger population profile, with a higher proportion of young children and fewer older adults. Older adults were over represented in higher-acuity triage categories, and overall acuity increased with remoteness, with remote regions showing a greater share of urgent presentations.

The crude IRR of ED presentations for heatwave in comparison to non-heatwave days was 1.08 (95% CI: 1.08–1.08). After adjusting for calendar month, public holidays, and weekday, and including an offset to account for differing population sizes, heatwave days were associated with a 10.47% higher ED presentation rate compared to non-heatwave days (IRRadj=1.10; 95% CI = 1.10–1.11). When compared to non-heatwave days, extreme heatwaves showed the greatest increase in ED demand (IRRadj=1.23; 95% CI = 1.20–1.26), followed by severe heatwaves (IRRadj=1.14; 95% CI = 1.13–1.15), and the smallest effect was exhibited during low-intensity heatwaves (IRRadj=1.09; 95% CI = 1.09–1.10). The number of heatwave days for each category of severity is reported in Supplementary File 5.

Demographic factors

The incidence of ED presentations on heatwave days in comparison to non-heatwave days increased directly with increasing rurality. For very remote postcodes, heatwave days were associated with a 54.26% higher ED presentation rate (IRRadj=1.54; 95% CI: 1.50–1.59), followed by an increase of 35.53% in remote postcodes (IRRadj=1.36; 95% CI: 1.33–1.38), an increase of 28.10% in outer regional postcodes (IRRadj=1.28; 95% CI: 1.27–1.29), an increase of 8.59% in inner regional postcodes (IRRadj=1.09; 95% CI: 1.08–1.09), and an increase of 6.31% in Major Cities (IRRadj=1.06; 95% CI: 1.06–1.07). See Fig. 1.

Clinical factors

There was an increase in the rate of ED presentations across most primary diagnoses during heatwave days (Fig. 3), with only ‘neoplasms’, ‘congenital disorders’ and ‘factors influencing health status and contact with health services’ showing non-statistically significant associations. Notably, the incidence of ‘endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diagnoses’ increased by a factor of 1.27 (95% CI: 1.24, 1.29), and ‘perinatal conditions’ increased by a factor of 1.17 (95% CI: 1.13, 1.22). No diagnostic categories showed a significant decrease during heatwaves.

Temporal factors

Stratified analyses by financial year showed that the association between heatwaves and ED presentations varied over time (Fig. 5). Heatwaves in 2015/2016 exhibited the greatest effect, with the adjusted incidence rate of ED presentations 1.13 times higher during heatwave days compared with non-heatwave days (IRRadj = 1.13; 95% CI: 1.12–1.15). More modest increases were observed in 2012/2013 (IRRadj = 1.04; 95% CI: 1.02–1.05), 2013/2014 (IRRadj = 1.05; 95% CI: 1.03–1.06), 2014/2015 (IRRadj = 1.06; 95% CI: 1.05–1.07) and 2017/2018 (IRRadj = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.01–1.04). Results for 2010/2011, 2011/2012, and 2016/2017 were not statistically significant. An inverse association was observed in 2018/2019 (IRRadj = 0.94; 95% CI: 0.93–0.95), indicating a lower rate of ED presentations on heatwave days versus non-heatwave days in that year. The number of heatwave days for each financial year are reported in Supplementary File 5.

Discussion

Heatwaves have a significant impact on ED presentations in Queensland, with a 10.47% increase on heatwave days in comparison to non-heatwave days, when adjusting for month, weekday, and public holidays. This increase was expected, as ED presentations have been found to increase in the capital city of Brisbane [54, 55], and in other Australian states such as Tasmania [56], Western Australia [57], South Australia [22], and New South Wales [58]. The increases in ED presentations found in this study are significant, specifically for extreme (increase of 22.79%) and severe heatwaves (increase of 14.01%) when contextualised to other regions in Australia, where more modest increases have been reported. For example, Tasmania saw a 5% increase during severe and extreme heatwaves (OR 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01, 1.09) [56], Western Australia also reported a 5% increase for similar conditions (RR 1.05, 95% CI: 1.04, 1.06) [57], and New South Wales observed a 3% increase during extreme heatwaves (IRR 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.05) [58]. However, it is noted that differences in ways that these studies applied the EHF and modelled exposure windows may contribute to the variation observed. The 10.47% increase found in the present study also appears to be high in comparison to international studies, where a 3.6% excess risk in ED visits was indicated for a heatwave in Houston, Texas [59], a 2% increase was found in Seattle during the 2021 heat dome [60], and from 2011 to 13 in the Pudong New Area, China, researchers reported non-significant or even negative associations [61]. These disparities may also reflect methodological differences across studies, including variations in study design, population characteristics, healthcare system structures, and statistical approaches.

Demographics and medical conditions

Mapping the impact of heatwaves across Queensland rurality showed that postcodes in remote and very remote areas were most impacted. This disparity likely reflects multiple factors, including inequitable access to primary health care services and poorer health outcomes for rural and remote patients [62, 63]. We know from other studies that tree cover, income levels, availability and access to cooling options, including air conditioning and rurality, also play a role [17, 30, 32]. When investigating heat stress in the USA, researchers found that heat stress-related ED visit rates in non-metropolitan areas were approximately 41% higher than in large central metropolitan areas (95% CI: 31%–52%) [64]. Other regions of the world such as South Korea [65] have also identified similar rural-urban disparities in the impact of heatwaves on mortality. Understanding distributions of increased ED presentations by rurality can inform planning and preparedness efforts when heatwaves are forecasted [66, 67].

Our analysis revealed that while all age groups experience increased ED presentations during heatwaves, the magnitude of this increase did not follow a monotonic pattern with age after adjusting for confounding factors. Existing literature suggests that as people age, they become more vulnerable to the impacts of heat [27, 68–70]. Infants and young children are also considered vulnerable due to their unique physiology and ongoing development [71]. In the present study, certain older age groups (such as 85+) did experience substantial increases, yet these effects were not uniform across all older cohorts. Some younger and middle-aged groups also showed elevated risks, aligning with studies that suggest occupational and recreational exposure may amplify vulnerability in these age brackets [72, 73]. For example, Varghese and colleagues identified elevated risks of work-related injuries and illnesses across three Australian cities during moderate to high-severity heatwaves, particularly among younger workers, apprentices, labour hire employees, and those in physically demanding or industrial roles [74]. These findings underscore the importance of considering a range of factors beyond chronological age when developing public health interventions to mitigate the effects of heatwaves with further work required to understand what is driving ED usage during heat waves by age group in Queensland.

Increases in ED presentations during heatwaves occurred across most principal diagnosis categories, and some categories, such as ‘endocrine, nutritional and metabolic’, and ‘perinatal’, experienced the greatest increases in ED presentations during heatwaves in comparison to non-heatwave conditions. However, these categories represented 0.9% and 0.2% of all ED cases across the study period, and so large relative increases may partly reflect small denominators. It should also be noted that these were the ICD-10 principal diagnoses noted at presentation. It is possible that the primary condition may change once they have been hospitalised, and further diagnostics are undertaken [75]. Opportunities to explore final diagnosis and alignment between primary diagnosis could provide additional insights. With more of the population experiencing a range of chronic conditions, unpacking the effects of multimorbidity is necessary [76].

Regarding the triage category, category 5 (less urgent e.g. minor illnesses or rashes) experienced the greatest increases in ED presentation during heatwaves, followed by category 2 (imminently life-threatening e.g. critical illness or serious chest pain), category 1 (immediately life-threatening e.g. critical illness or cardiac arrest), and category 3 (potentially life-threatening e.g. heavy bleeding from cuts or severe illness). The smallest effect was noted for category 4 (potentially serious e.g. migraine or sprained ankle) [77]. Contrary to this finding, Toloo et al. [69] found an exposure-response relationship for heatwave days ≥ 37 °C, with high acuity triage (category 1 to 2) experiencing the highest relative risk, followed by moderate (category 3) and low (category 4 to 5) acuity. Continued provision and access to non-urgent medical support via virtual care services or urgent care [78, 79], combined with targeted public messaging before and during heatwaves, may help reduce the number of less urgent cases presenting to emergency departments. However, it is noted that a different definition of heatwave was used for this study and the EHF was not yet used as a heatwave metric. To the author’s knowledge, this relationship between triage category and EHF has not previously been explored and requires further investigation.

Temporal trends in heatwave emergency department demand

There was variability in the effect of heatwaves on EDs across the study period. Notably, the biggest increases in ED incidence on heatwave days occurred in the 2015–2016 financial year. This result may be impacted by El Niño, which occurred during this period leading to below average rainfall, an early start to the bushfire season, and prolonged heatwaves in the Autumn of 2016 [80]. Contrary to expectations, the 2018-19 warm season, which has been recognised as a “summer of extremes” with heatwaves, bushfires, flooding, and drought, exhibited an inverse effect, where ED presentation incidence was higher on non-heatwave days [81]. These findings suggest that extreme weather doesn’t always lead to proportional health system impacts, pointing to complex interactions between environment, public health, and resilience. The later years of the study period were also marked by increased efforts to improved heatwave public health messaging in Queensland, which may have had an impact [2]. Further research is needed to untangle these complex relationships.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This was the first analysis of the impact of heatwaves on ED presentations across the entire state of Queensland, and included 10 years of public ED data. The results of this population-based study can be used to inform the public of their risk during future heatwave periods. It can also be used to inform health, workplace, education, and a range of other policies to protect the public and mitigate heatwave-related health impacts both within and external to the health system.

A limitation of this study is that EHF data were aggregated from 5-kilometre resolution to the postcode level. As the data were averaged to scale up to wider regions, variations and nuances present in the finer resolution have been smoothed out, leading to a potential loss of accuracy in depicting localised variations in heatwave patterns. The methodology assumes that the whole population in each postcode are exposed to the same level of exposure. Additionally, this study only captured presentations to public EDs, which may limit the generalisability of the findings due to the exclusion of private ED presentations. Further, the inclusion of confounders such as humidity and air pollution are important for future work but were outside of the scope of this study. Future studies should address these gaps to enhance accuracy. Lastly, ED impacts were explored exclusively in this study. It is recognised that other health services, such as general practices, are also impacted during periods of extreme heat [82], and these were not captured in this study.

While a number of areas for future research have been identified throughout the discussion, it would be remiss not to advocate for prospective studies that look at the impact of heatwaves on ED in real-time and to try to establish, with greater clarity, how heatwaves are impacting an individual’s health. Future research should also consider lag effects to capture delayed heat impacts, and spatial patterns of risk and potential effect modification by area-level factors so that heat health interventions can be more effectively tailored to the needs of specific communities. Heatwaves are occurring more often and lasting longer with greater severity under a changing climate [2]. It is likely that these changes will also lead to changes in human behaviour (such as those around sport [83]) and, thus, ED admission, necessitating ongoing research in this space along with the development, implementation, and evaluation of strategies to manage these impacts.

Conclusion

Heatwaves impact the ED with increased presentations, and as such, emergency services need to prepare for this increase with predictions of longer and more severe heatwaves. Demographic, clinical, and temporal variations in ED demand suggest that targeted preparedness and mitigation strategies are needed, particularly for those most vulnerable. Understanding who is most at risk, where and when demand surges occur, and what types of presentations increase can help guide tailored interventions and build a more resilient health system in the face of intensifying climate extremes. There is a need to continue to explore the impact of heatwaves on EDs, including prospective studies to provide timely evidence to inform public health planning, resource allocation, and policy development.
