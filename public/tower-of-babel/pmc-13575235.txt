Machine learning-driven optimization of high-velocity impact resistance for three-dimensional biphasic composites
Authors: Lehu Bu, Pengfei Wang, Sida Hao, Shan Li, Yangfan Wu, Deya Wang, Mao Liu, Tianzhi Luo, Songlin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder high-velocity loading conditions, composite materials inevitably exhibit wave propagation and localized temperature rise, yet their complex microstructural arrangement contributes to the characteristic stress localization and attenuation behaviors. To mitigate this challenge and optimize the impact resistance of composite materials, we developed a three-dimensional convolutional neural network (3D-CNN) with experimental and simulation methods to quantitatively correlate microstructural configurations with the dynamic response of biphasic composites. This methodology synergizes experimental data from split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) dynamic tests with finite element (FE) simulations, enabling a comprehensive characterization of the dynamic behavior of composites. The optimized microstructures improve dynamic stress uniformity from 21.54% to 97.45% and increase dynamic load capacity by approximately 22% to 57% compared with random designs at the same stiff-phase fractions. This machine learning-driven approach can improve dynamic stress equilibrium properties for soft and stiff composites. The thermal transport module enables integrated evaluation of impact resistance and heat-spreading performance, revealing multi-material architectures that sustain high dynamic mechanical performance while allowing effective thermal conductivity through phase connectivity and percolation pathways. This proposed 3D CNN framework facilitates rapid exploration of the design space and accurate prediction of thermomechanical properties, thereby enabling the inverse design of next-generation impact-resistant composites for aerospace and automotive applications.Subject terms: Mechanical properties, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Aerospace and transportation structures experience coupled thermomechanical conditions, requiring simultaneous high-velocity impact resistance and efficient heat dissipation. Composite materials with high specific strength and energy-absorption capacity have become indispensable in aerospace and transportation engineering, where high-velocity loading scenarios (e.g., collision, bird strikes, and debris impacts) require high impact resistance1–3. Under high-strain-rate conditions (>103 s−1), strain-rate hardening and stress-wave propagation trigger severe nonlinear deformation and localization4,5. This dynamic stress and strain localization phenomenon, which can lead to structural failure, underscores the critical importance of optimizing microstructural configuration and mitigating heterogeneous deformation patterns. To address these critical dynamic challenges, microstructure engineering emerges as a pathway, a concept inspired by evolutionary, optimized biological systems like nacre6, bone7, and tendon8, e.g., a structural strategy capable of combining the advantages of nacre-like and laminated architectures at varying impact velocities4. Soft components generally have high toughness and ductility, whereas stiff components offer strength and stiffness. These biological structures integrate soft and stiff constituents in tailored combinations, motivating the design of biphasic composites with enhanced mechanical properties9–11. Traditional biphasic composites that blend ductile phases with stiff reinforcement12–15 face complex manufacturing16 and a combinatorially large design space17, making it challenging to establish clear microstructure–property relationships via conventional methods. Furthermore, multiscale simulation methods18,19 enable the simulation of mechanical behaviors in composites across varying loading conditions. However, the computational cost remains prohibitive in complex cases, and existing numerical approaches do not address the inverse design challenges of composite materials.

To meet the exigencies of complex application environments, researchers are dedicated to the development of multifunctional composites endowed with superior properties. Ultrahigh-performance fibers demonstrate that exceptional tensile properties can coexist with high thermal and electrical conductivities, establishing a clear precedent for load-bearing conductors with intrinsic heat-transport capability20. Complementarily, processing-enabled metallic microstructures demonstrate that high strength and high conductivity can be achieved concurrently, highlighting the feasibility of structural components that also serve as effective heat transport pathways21. Meanwhile, measurements conducted at dynamic strain rates demonstrate a non-classical temperature dependence of strength22. Furthermore, hierarchical architectures can overcome conventional mechanical trade-offs inherent in superhard composites15. These findings collectively motivate the development of a unified design strategy that co-optimizes strength/energy absorption while addressing thermal management. Nowadays, artificial intelligence demonstrates potential in composite design through data-driven microstructure design strategies23–26. Significant progress has been made in optimizing the mechanical performance of composites, notably through the application of two-dimensional convolutional neural networks (2D CNNs) to enhance both strength and toughness27,28. Contemporary approaches employing convolutional LSTM architectures have successfully realized inverse structural design paradigms29,30, while generative adversarial neural networks (GAN) enable macroscopic property prediction through microstructure analysis and strain-stress field in binary-phase systems31. Beyond conventional two-dimensional (2D) composite materials, voxel-based methods enable three-dimensional microstructure characterization. Three-dimensional convolutional neural networks (3D CNNs) have shown utility in predicting anisotropic material properties of representative volume elements (RVEs) containing stochastic inclusions32–34, as well as in resolving strain-field distributions within high-contrast 3D composites35,36. Nevertheless, current mechanical evaluations predominantly focus on quasi-static loading regimes, with limited exploration of dynamic responses under high-velocity bird-strike or debris-impact conditions. The split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) methodology, though instrumental in decoupling strain rate and wave propagation effects under dynamic loading37,38, remains challenging when applied to soft-phase materials, where the coupling effect intensifies, and homogeneous stress/strain fields prove experimentally elusive39–41. Prior to stress equilibrium, inertia-induced wave propagation can generate pronounced end-to-end stress gradients and impact-end–biased deformation, violating the uniform-stress assumption in the conventional SHPB method42,43. Addressing these limitations through optimized microstructure design and dynamic stress equilibration strategies could substantially improve the dynamic reliability of soft-stiff composites and offer support for the fundamental assumption of the SHPB technique.

To overcome these limitations, we propose a machine learning (ML) framework to predict and optimize the dynamics of composites under high-velocity dynamic loading. A comprehensive workflow that uses a 3D CNN model to investigate the dynamic mechanical properties and perform the inverse design of three-dimensional architected structures composed of binary-phase materials. Through systematic integration of SHPB experiments and numerical simulations, we characterize the dynamic microstructural deformation and thermal transport of 3D printed composites with distributions of soft and stiff materials. This hybrid methodology accelerates the development of composites tailored for specific thermomechanical properties.

Results

Machine learning model

We present a machine-learning framework to optimize composites for targeted dynamic performance. The composite structure comprises alternating stiff (Vero White) and soft (Agilus 30 Black) material blocks arranged in three-dimensional space, as shown in Fig. 1a. To digitally characterize the 3D microstructure distribution, material phases are encoded into a binary numerical matrix (stiff: 1, soft: 0). Each matrix element corresponds to a geometric voxel, with individual material blocks comprising 27 voxels each (3 × 3 × 3). The composite structure features 125 material blocks (1.5 mm edge length) arranged in 7.5 × 7.5 × 7.5 mm3 cubic geometry. These blocks divide the composite into 3375 voxels (15 × 15 × 15), digitally encoded as binary inputs (0 or 1) for machine learning analysis of 3D microstructural patterns (Fig. 1a). Microstructural distributions were generated using the Knuth shuffle algorithm44, ensuring unbiased spatial arrangements.

The composite design space adjusts the stiff material’s volume fraction (ϕ) through different 3D microstructural configurations, as depicted in Fig. 1b. A stepwise gradient of 4% controls the incremental increase in ϕ across composite geometries. These randomized configurations are computationally processed via finite element analysis to generate strain-time profiles under dynamic loading conditions (Fig. 1c). Dynamic stress–strain curves are derived from the strain–time data using the two-wave analytical method45. Detailed simulation protocols for impact dynamics are formalized in the Materials and Methods section. Key dynamic mechanical parameters, including stress, strain, and strain energy density, are quantitatively extracted from these curves to establish an ML training model. The strain energy density, calculated through numerical integration of the stress-strain relationship under the curve, serves as a vital method for evaluating energy absorption performance. A 3D CNN architecture processes composite microstructural patterns to predict these dynamic properties with spatial resolution. Subsequent analysis of ML-predicted parameters systematically identifies optimal design spaces for each volume fraction (ϕ) class, enabling computational inverse design of composites with tailored impact-resistant characteristics.

Here, we propose a 3D CNN framework to predict dynamic mechanical properties by spatially analyzing composite phase arrangements. As illustrated in Fig. 1d, the architecture integrates two convolutional layers (Conv), two pooling layers, and one fully connected layer (FC). The 3D CNN model encodes individual blocks as 3 × 3 × 3 tensors, defining composite input as 15 × 15 × 15 arrays.

To accurately capture the spatial distribution of soft and stiff materials within the 3D composite array, a 3D convolution kernel is chosen to extract spatial distribution features through multiple convolutions between the soft and stiff phases, and the receptive field of filters was used as 3 × 3 × 3 in each convolutional layer. The primary convolutional layer (C1) implements 32 filters for spatial feature extraction, with interleaved pooling layers deployed between the subsequent convolutional layers to achieve computational efficiency while preserving critical feature information. During convolution, the 3D kernel scans the phase voxel input, applying the convolution operation to generate a feature map with a stride length of 1 and no padding. At each position, the dot product between the kernel’s values and the corresponding values of the input tensor is computed, then summed and added to a bias term. After each convolutional layer, pooling layers are employed to downsample the input feature maps’ spatial dimensions, reducing the dimensionality of the feature maps that flow into the following layers and resulting in the same number of feature maps but with reduced spatial resolution in P147. Batch normalization (BN) is applied after each pooling layer to accelerate the training process and enhance the generalization capability of the 3D CNN48. In subsequent layers, 64 filters are applied in C2 to increase the number of feature maps, with max pooling in P2 identical to P1.

In the final stage of the 3D CNN, the normalized feature maps from BN layers are flattened to reduce spatial dimensionality, producing a compressed 1D vector (C3). This vectorized representation is then fed into the fully connected layer (H) for predictive modeling of mechanical property relationships. The C3 layer transmits 512 feature maps through structural coupling to the fully connected layer H. This layer comprises multiple neurons that map the learned feature representations to the label space of the samples. The rectified linear unit (ReLU)49 is the activation function to prevent gradient vanishing in convolutional and fully connected layers. The last layer includes two single neurons without any activation function as the prediction output. The proposed architecture balances hierarchical spatial-feature extraction with computational efficiency, enabling prediction of microstructure-dependent dynamic responses from voxelized soft–stiff phase arrangements.

Heterogeneous multiphase architectures can be customized by adjusting stiffness gradients and the spatial arrangement of stiff and soft phases, enabling programmable mechanical properties. Then, 3D-printed microstructures were subjected to split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) testing under an impact velocity of 10 m/s. As shown in Fig. 2a, the composite microstructures feature different compositions and ratios of stiff and soft materials aligned parallel to the impact direction (negative Z-axis), while maintaining a uniform microstructure distribution along the X-axis. Notably, microstructure ‘III’ reaches the highest stress compared to the other configurations, in Fig. 2b, while microstructure ‘I’ shows the lowest peak stress at the same stiff-phase fraction. This finding is primarily attributed to the wave-induced stress nonuniformity under dynamic loading50,51, which differs from that under quasi-static loading. This suggests that symmetrical configurations are more effective for composite structures perpendicular to the impact direction than other design approaches.

Dynamics prediction

The 5 × 5 × 5 voxel-based composite system exhibits a combinatorial explosion in design permutations (N = 2125) even after symmetry-inclusive configuration filtering, as exhaustive computational enumeration proves infeasible beyond such high-order parametric optimization. To reduce the complexity, 26 equidistant stiff-phase volume fractions (0–100% in 4.0% arithmetic progression increments) were discretized to balance computational tractability with microstructural resolution. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, the stochastic combinatorial sampling methodology employs a parametric framework where intermediate stiff phases are each assigned 500 randomized microstructural configurations. Monolithic endpoints (0% and 100% stiff-phase) exhibit singular material combinations due to phase exclusivity constraints. All designs underwent explicit dynamic finite element method (FEM) simulations. The dataset comprising 12002 FEM simulation results was partitioned into training (80%), validation (10%) and testing (10%) subsets through random sampling. The logarithmic mean squared error (log-MSE)53 was employed as the loss function to quantify discrepancies between ground truth and model prediction of the training and testing subsets (Fig. 3b). The training and validation log-MSE curves exhibit exponential decay characteristics, achieving terminal values below 10% of initial magnitudes throughout progressive training epochs. A stepwise learning rate was implemented, triggering a 10× reduction factor at epoch 50, thereby accelerating convergence dynamics in optimization landscapes.

Exploring the design space facilitates the composites with specified properties. The design space exploration employs 10000 3D number arrays per 4–96% stiff phase volumetric gradation. A total of 240000 randomly generated configurations undergo automated property prediction via a pre-trained 3D CNN architecture to resolve dynamic mechanical responses. The predicted results under the identical stiff fraction are counted and analyzed, as shown in Fig. 3e, f, the highest and lowest values of strain energy density and stress constitute the upper and lower bounds, respectively. The strain energy density design space achieves the largest variability at a 52% stiff fraction, with extremal values spanning 6.454–10.730 MJm−3 under dynamic loading conditions. Here, the strain energy density is defined as the path-dependent integral of the stress-strain curve under nonlinear constitutive evolution. When the stiff fraction increases or decreases, the design space of strain energy density is gradually reduced. The stress has the most significant design space at a stiff fraction of 72%, with extremal stress values spanning 76.79–122.92 MPa under dynamic loading conditions. Therefore, the maximum design space for stress and strain energy density is not at the same fraction. The present results address two fundamental questions: what design principles can be revealed from this optimization method? And how can machine-learned operators mitigate the wave-induced stress inhomogeneity under high-velocity impact loading? The subsequent analysis and discussion address these challenges through neural network architectures.

The hyperelastic soft components deform substantially and contribute to energy absorption, whereas the stiff components help maintain structural integrity under dynamic loading. To investigate how soft-stiff block configurations influence strain energy density and stress in composite structures, the top configurations from Fig. 4a, b were subjected to FEM analysis under Z-axis dynamic compression. This methodology captures stress field distributions and deformation profiles, enabling quantitative assessment of mechanical performance. The simulation results demonstrate that composites with centralized stress concentrations in the midplane region exhibit elevated strain energy density and stress values. Conversely, edge-localized stress distributions correlate with reduced strain energy density and weaker stress magnitudes, suggesting a critical dependence of mechanical performance on spatial stress configuration.

Moreover, the Z-axis distribution of the connected soft or stiff blocks within composite architectures, especially when aligned parallel or perpendicular to the impact direction, strongly influences energy dissipation capacity and load transfer efficiency under dynamic loading conditions. When the stiff and soft phases are aligned parallel to the impact direction (Fig. 2c), the strain energy density and stress increase. The continuous stiff phase reinforces structural integrity, whereas soft-phase domains contribute to energy absorption and redistribute stress field. However, when connected soft-phase blocks are distributed perpendicular to the impact direction (Fig. 2a), the composite’s mechanical strength decreases. Consequently, the dynamic performance (e.g., stress and strain energy) of biphasic structures can be optimized by precisely controlling phase alignment relative to the impact axis.

Discussion

Enhanced dynamic stress uniformity

Stress wave propagation under high-velocity impact induces dynamic localization phenomena with distinct characteristics from those observed in quasi-static loading conditions. The dynamic stress/strain equilibrium constitutes the fundamental assumption of split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) experiments, thereby establishing the method’s validity criterion. Figure 5 presents two structural configurations (ϕ=80%) with measured stress profiles at specimen interfaces, correlated with SHPB-based simulations demonstrating dynamic deformation mechanisms. Figure 5a illustrates that the proximal front surface of the sample is constituted by the stiff phase, whereas the distal back surface comprises the soft phase. The front surface exhibits markedly higher stress compared to the back surface.

Figure 5b presents the FE-simulated deformation and von Mises stress-field evolution of the vertical-continuity configuration at four representative time points during dynamic loading. The stresses of the specimen exhibit a pronounced increase from (i) to (ii), but this configuration results in the front surface stress consistently rising at a higher rate than that at the back surface due to the heterogeneous distribution of soft and stiff phases within the specimen. During the transient loading and unloading stage, the stresses at the front and back surfaces fail to achieve equilibrium. These stress and strain heterogeneities induce asymmetric deformation patterns, thereby impeding stress equilibrium between opposing surfaces under dynamic loading conditions.

Figure 5c illustrates a topologically optimized specimen with an identical stiff fraction (ϕ=80%). Notably, the front and back surface stresses exhibit satisfactory dynamic equilibrium throughout both loading/unloading phases of the impact event. Figure 5d reveals a comparatively symmetric von Mises stress distribution during the initial loading stage (i) and the subsequent deformations (ii), (iii), and (iv) stages, demonstrating relatively uniform deformation under dynamic loading.

Under SHPB dynamic loading conditions, stress equilibration serves as a key determinant of material yield strength54–56. Figure 5e demonstrates the comparative stress uniformity analysis of three configurations with the same stiff fraction (ϕ=80%): Under quasi-static conditions, the materials exhibit the identical stresses of both sides, with a stress uniformity (Uk) of 100%. But the stress uniformity Uk is only 21.54% for the vertical continuity configuration (Fig. 5a) under dynamic loading. For structures designed by Symmetric-A (Fig. 5c), Uk is 96.96% under the same dynamic condition. Interestingly, the dynamic stress uniformity, Uk reaches a high level of 97.45% after the 3D CNN optimal random design. The 3D CNN-optimized topology exhibits improved stress distribution homogeneity, which is attributed to the synergistic arrangement of symmetric and random patterns along the direction of impact.

These findings confirm that optimizing microstructure arrangements significantly enhances the uniformity of dynamic stress. Crucially, sample thickness in SHPB emerges as the dominant factor, together with the loading wave’s rising edge, which governs the dynamic equilibration process55,56. A high incident wave loading rate induces pronounced initial dynamic non-equilibrium in the specimen, requiring pulse shaping techniques or conical projectors to modulate the loading rate for achieving the dynamic equilibrium. The inherent wave impedance reduction in soft materials, particularly cellular foams, aerogels, and biological tissues, results in increasingly significant stress discrepancies under dynamic loading conditions. In this study, the maximum compressive strain is 15% due to the initial condition of the SHPB test. Although higher impact velocities and longer wavelengths may theoretically enable greater strain, experimental observations reveal that excessive strain causes delamination and failure in soft/stiff composite, primarily attributable to weak interfacial bonding (Supplementary Fig. 5). Moreover, the inertia effect induces a velocity-dependent stress discrepancy amplification between the front and back surfaces, and dynamic stress uniformity measurements reveal a significant drop as the loading velocity increases50,57. Thus, microstructure optimization helps minimize the stress non-uniformity under dynamic loading, and a higher dynamic response is associated with improved stress uniformity.

Figure 5f demonstrates the implementation of a 3D CNN-driven topology optimization framework, which produces structures with quantifiable stress uniformity and improves dynamic load capacity by ~22–57% compared with random designs. This finding underscores the critical role of optimizing material-phase distribution in enhancing specimen dynamic stress under impact conditions, thereby supporting the effectiveness of the proposed 3D CNN-based optimization design method.

Thermomechanical synergistic optimization

These results indicate that this 3D CNN method not only facilitates the optimization of dynamic properties but also paves a pathway in designing the thermal or electrical properties through microstructure arrangement. A primary motivation for employing complex 3D architectures lies in their ability to enable simultaneous optimization of thermal management and impact performance capabilities that monolithic, single-material baselines inherently lack.

As depicted in Fig. 6a, the equivalent thermal conductivity (k¯) exhibits strong variation across the design space, and this variation persists even at constant composition, suggesting that thermal transport behavior is primarily governed by architecture rather than phase fraction alone. In agreement with this, Fig. 6b illustrates a wide range of equivalent thermal conductivity versus strain energy density (SED) for candidate microstructures at a 52% stiff fraction, where the highlighted design represents the maximum thermal conductivity achievable at identical composition. Notably, the microstructure corresponding to the ‘maximum’ point simultaneously delivers the highest dynamic mechanical strength among the candidates, indicating that the architecture maximizing heat transport is the same architecture that optimizes load-bearing performance. The high-k¯ architecture in Fig. 6c arranges the phases in a parallel-like configuration, forming percolating high-conductivity pathways that bridge the isothermal faces. The broad distribution of equivalent thermal conductivity at a fixed stiff-phase fraction further indicates that thermal transport is governed not only by phase composition but also by microstructural topology. In the maximum-k¯ architecture, the higher-conductivity phase forms continuous, approximately parallel pathways between the prescribed-temperature surfaces, thereby reducing the effective thermal resistance and facilitating through-thickness heat transport. In the thermal model, the stiff Vero White phase and soft Agilus30 Black phase were assigned thermal conductivities of 0.3 and 3 W m−1 K−1, respectively. This tenfold conductivity contrast makes the equivalent thermal conductivity particularly sensitive to the connectivity and orientation of the higher-conductivity Agilus30 Black phase. Consequently, the heat-flux field in Fig. 6d is concentrated along the high-conductivity network, and the temperature-gradient magnitude in Fig. 6e remains comparatively homogeneous, consistent with efficient and robust through-thickness thermal conduction.

Under transient dynamic loading, the adiabatic temperature rise58,59 precipitates localized shear failure in materials, where enhanced thermal conductivity serves to attenuate thermal-induced localization phenomena and consequently delay the dynamic failure onset of composites60,61. High thermal conductivity facilitates outward heat dissipation, thereby mitigating localized thermal-softening and contributing to improved load-bearing capacity under high-impact conditions. Importantly, this machine-learning architectural synergistically controls the dynamic mechanical properties and thermal conductivity by redirecting load paths and regulating deformation localization, thereby simultaneously enhancing dynamic energy absorption and thermal dissipation compared to homogeneous materials.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a 3D-CNN-based method for predicting and optimizing the dynamic mechanical response of biphasic composites under high-velocity transient loading conditions through a three-dimensional convolutional neural network (3D CNN). By establishing a direct correlation between microstructural arrangements and mechanical performance through integrated SHPB dynamic experimental and FEM numerical analyses, our machine learning framework successfully identifies optimized microstructures with enhanced dynamic energy absorption capabilities. Furthermore, it enhances stress equilibrium in the conventional SHPB dynamic testing protocols. This methodology accelerates exploration of the microstructure-property design space while providing reverse engineering insights for high-velocity dynamic-loading applications. Additionally, a thermal-transport module was incorporated to quantify the architecture-dependent effective thermal conductivity of the same voxelized biphasic designs, thereby enabling the synergistic optimization of dynamic mechanical and thermal properties. These findings demonstrate the potential of machine learning in advancing high-performance composite engineering, establishing an efficient paradigm to guide future developments in dynamic energy absorption and thermal transportation of composites.

Methods

3D printing biphasic composites

Biphasic composite specimens were fabricated via a multi-material 3D printer (Objet260 Connex, Stratasys Ltd.). The 3D-printed composites incorporated two commercial PolyJet photopolymer resins with proprietary formulations: Vero White, a white rigid opaque photopolymer, as the stiff phase, and Agilus30 Black, a black rubber-like photopolymer, as the soft phase. FullCure 705 support material was used during printing and removed after fabrication using a water jet. Specimens were fabricated using the default PolyJet printing settings for the Objet260 Connex system. The printed specimens were then cleaned and used for SHPB testing. Figure 7a demonstrates that the X-axis was defined as the printing direction of the strips, and the y-axis was oriented perpendicular to the X-axis, governing adjacent strip deposition. Finally, Z-axis controlled vertical stacking of successive layers to achieve composite structural integrity. X-Y axis symmetry constraints are implemented to align with the Z-axis impact direction, reducing design permutations from 2125 to 225 configurations. Figure 7b shows the composite structure comprised of 125 units of cubic blocks (1.5 mm edge length), with an integrated cubic geometry measuring 7.5 mm per side. Various composite architectures can be designed and fabricated through parametric control of stiff/soft phase ratios and topological configurations within the structure.

Split hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) methods

SHPB technique, a cornerstone high-strain-rate testing apparatus for characterizing dynamic mechanical properties of materials, utilizes a 1D bar system comprising a striker bar, incident bar, and transmission bar. The SHPB system (Fig. 7c) employs 14.5 mm diameter bars, with the striker bar accelerated to 10 m/s via controlled high-pressure gas release. This impact generates a compressive stress wave that propagates axially through the incident bar. The incident wave propagates along the bar and bifurcates at the sample interface into a transmitted wave and a reflected wave. Dynamic properties of 3D-printed samples were analyzed using synchronized high-speed camera (Photron FASTCAM SA5) to record the SHPB dynamic deformation processes.

Finite element configuration

Finite element simulations offer a streamlined alternative to resource-heavy experiments, rapidly producing sufficient data for machine learning model training. A comprehensive dataset for 3D composite designs was generated through finite element simulations implemented in Abaqus/Explicit 2024 (Dassault Systèmes SIMULIA Corp.). Two 1000-mm-long bars were meshed into 15000 linear hexahedral elements (C3D8), and the specimen with a 7.5 mm side length comprised 7500 elements. Surface-to-surface contact with a penalty friction algorithm defined the specimen-bar interaction. The bars were defined as elastic steel (E=210 GPa and ν=0.3). Two block materials were used: a stiff material (VeroWhite) and a soft material (Agilus30 Black). The stiff phase was modeled using a rate-dependent elastic–visco-plastic formulation in ABAQUS (keyword *PLASTIC, RATE = YES), where the yield stress is tabulated as a function of plastic strain and strain rate. Specifically, true stress–true strain curves from quasi-static tests (10−3 s−1) and high-rate tests (~103 s−1) on the pure stiff material (Supplementary Note 2) were converted to true stress versus plastic strain and entered into ABAQUS as a table, enabling interpolation between quasi-static and high-rate responses. Material properties included a density of 1200 kgm−3, elastic modulus E=2637 MPa, initial yield stress σy=90 MPa, a maximum plastic strain of 0.12 (defined as the upper bound of the tabulated plastic hardening data used in the constitutive input), and a failure strain of 0.10 (used as a calibrated ductile-damage initiation threshold to capture the onset of shear-localization–dominated degradation under dynamic compression, as evidenced in Supplementary Fig. 2c). Plastic hardening behavior was defined using tabulated true stress–plastic strain data digitized from high-rate test curves and implemented via interpolation in ABAQUS. The soft phase was modeled as a hyperelastic material using a second-order polynomial model fitted to our SHPB true stress–strain data at ~103 s−1, with a density of 1200 kgm−3. Full details of the constitutive models and parameters for both materials are provided in the Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. The dataset inputs comprise randomized material distribution configurations. The FE model replicated the incident wave loading directly to the incident bar’s front section. The incident wave amplitude was obtained by fitting the experimental data (Fig. 7d).

The time-varying transmitted and reflected strain waves were monitored at mid-span elements of the transmission and incident bars during each simulation process. Then, high strain-rate stress-strain curves were derived via the two-wave method (Fig. 7e), with quantified curve areas serving as strain energy density for 3D composite architectures. Automated Python scripting generated a finite element model, with subsequent dataset partitioning into training/validation/testing subsets at an 8:1:1 ratio.

Construction and training of 3D CNN model

While 2D CNNs extract a local receptive field on two spatial dimensions, 3D CNNs model has a 3-dimensional (3D) convolution kernel, adding a spatial-depth hyperparameter R to the 2-dimensional (2D) convolution kernel to extract the information across the third spatial dimension65,66. The 3D CNN architecture predicts dynamic mechanical responses in composites with heterogeneous material distributions across three-dimensional geometries. The 3D CNN framework is elaborated in the results section.

The 3D CNN framework was trained using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 20 for a maximum of 100 epochs. The initial learning rate was set to 0.01 and reduced by a factor of 10 to 0.001 at epoch 50. Early stopping was applied based on the validation log mean squared error (log -MSE) with a patience of 20 epochs. Training concluded at epoch 93, as no further improvement in validation log -MSE was observed. The model weights from epoch 81, corresponding to the lowest validation log-MSE, were retained for all subsequent evaluations. The network was implemented in Python 3.8 using TensorFlow 2.3.167,68 with the built-in Keras API, and trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2070 GPU.

Computational cost of FEM and 3D CNN model

Finite-element (FE) simulations for dynamic impact required ~10 min per microstructure on an AMD Ryzen 5 3600 CPU. As a result, exhaustively evaluating the full design space of 240,000 configurations would require ~40,000 h of computation. In contrast, our machine-learning–driven workflow, executed on an NVIDIA RTX 2070 GPU, combines dataset generation (12,002 FE simulations), CNN training (~10 min), and high-throughput inference (~0.924 s per configuration) to complete full design-space exploration in ~2062 h—only ~5.16% of the time demanded by brute-force FE screening. For thermal transport, the FE computation of effective conductivity requires ~20 s per microstructure on an AMD Ryzen 7 5700 G CPU, implying ~1333 h to exhaustively evaluate all 240,000 configurations. Together, these results highlight the substantial computational advantage of the proposed learning-accelerated pipeline for rapid multimodal property screening at scale.
