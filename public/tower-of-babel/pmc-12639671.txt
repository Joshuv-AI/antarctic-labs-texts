Preoperative sedation with intravenous S-ketamine versus midazolam in preschool children: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yu-Qi Liao, Jun Ma, Yi He, Yong-Wei Su, Ming-Zhe Xu, Meng-Qiu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective management of preoperative anxiety is paramount in pediatric anesthesia, as untreated preoperative anxiety often leads to adverse clinical outcomes—most notably difficult parent-child separation and challenges during anesthesia induction. Therefore, preoperative sedation is essential for alleviating preoperative anxiety in preschool-aged children.MethodsIn this prospective randomized controlled clinical trial, children aged 2–6 years were assigned to one of two study groups: Group S (S-ketamine, 0.5 mg/kg) and Group M (midazolam, 0.08 mg/kg). The drugs were intravenously administrated in a 1:1 ratio in the anesthesia holding area. The primary outcome was the sedative effect, evaluated via the Modified Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale (mYPAS). Second outcomes included the parent separation anxiety score, postoperative agitation, caregivers’ and anesthesia care providers’ satisfaction, and mask compliance.ResultsOne hundred and four children were enrolled. Both prophylactic drugs effectively controlled preoperative stress. Compared with Group M, Group S had significantly lower mYPAS scores approximately 5 min after intravenous premedication [median (interquartile range, IQR): 27.08 (22.92–33.33) versus 31.25 (27.08–33.33), P = 0.025]. A significant longer emergence time was found in Group S (P = 0.036). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of parent separation anxiety score, mask compliance, or postoperative agitation.ConclusionIntravenous premedication with a single dose of 0.5 mg/kg S-ketamine results in a deeper sedative state compared with 0.08 mg/kg midazolam, albeit with a slight prolongation of emergence time.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration number# ChiCTR2300069998); Date of Registration: 30/03/2023.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03454-8.Keywords: S-ketamine, Midazolam, Premedication, Children

Background

Preoperative anxiety poses significant risks, particularly in preschool-aged children [1]. Usually, it typically presents as crying, uncooperativeness, and fear during separation from caregivers [2]. It is important for anesthesiologists to recognize the clinical impact of preoperative anxiety, and the administration of sedatives prior to entering the operating room (OR) remains the most common approach to address this issue [3]. Thus, identifying an optimal premedication agent to mitigate children’s stress and facilitate smooth anesthesia induction is of crucial importance.

Midazolam is widely utilized in anesthesia practice due to its sedative properties and anterograde amnestic effect [2]. It has long been the most commonly used drug for premedication in pediatric patients [4]. When administered intravenously, it exhibits a rapid onset of action (2–3 min) [5]. However, a notable adverse effect is its potential to induce paradoxical excitation, which may increase agitation—an effect that is particularly prominent in preschool-aged children [6]. Despite its widespread use, few randomized clinical trials have evaluated the efficacy of intravenous midazolam for pediatric premedication [2, 7].

First introduced in 1970, ketamine is well recognized for its diverse pharmacological effects [8]. S-ketamine, the S-enantiomer of ketamine, shares similar pharmacological profiles, but exhibits a higher affinity for the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor. Clinically, its analgesic efficacy in anesthesia is twice that of racemic ketamine [9]. These unique peculiarities make it a promising adjuvant for anesthesia care providers to facilitate smooth parent-child separation [10]. A recently published dose-finding study demonstrated that the effective intranasal dose of S-ketamine for sedation is 0.7 mg/kg [11]. Similarly, Qiao et al. reported that intranasal S-ketamine achieves rapid sedation in preschool-aged children, although the dosage employed in their study was relatively high (2 mg/kg) [12]. However, there remains a lack of research data regarding the intravenous administration of S-ketamine.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial to compare the preoperative sedation effectiveness of intravenous S-ketamine and midazolam in preschool-aged children [13]. We hypothesized that S-ketamine would be more effective than midazolam in reducing preoperative anxiety.

Methods

Study design

A single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial was designed to evaluate the effects of intravenous S-ketamine versus midazolam on preoperative sedation in preschool-aged children undergoing elective surgery.

Study setting and participants

This trial was conducted at West China Hospital from June 2023 to October 2024. It received approval from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University on 15 March 2023 and was registered before participants were enrolled in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300069998). Written informed consent was obtained from each eligible child’s legally authorized representatives (LARs; defined as a legal guardian, parents, or other individual designated by law to make decisions on behalf of the participant) in the ward on the day before surgery. The study protocol has been published elsewhere [13].

The inclusion criteria were as follows: ① aged 2–6 years; ② scheduled for elective surgery under general anesthesia; ③ the physical status class of the American Society of Anesthesiologists(ASA) Physical Status Classification I or II; and ④ successful establishment of an intravenous line preoperatively. Participants who met one of the following criteria were excluded: ① allergy to any experimental drug; ② predicted difficult airway; ③ dysfunction of vital organs (hepatic or renal insufficiency, cardiac or pulmonary abnormalities, or neurological disorders); ④ the consumption of any sedatives or analgesics before surgery; and ⑤ refusal of participation by participant’s LARs.

Sample size calculation

The Modified Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale (mYPAS) [14] was selected as the primary endpoint for sample size calculation. This validated scale has been used in over 100 studies across multiple healthcare fields, including anesthesia, surgery, pediatrics, and dentistry [15, 16]. Previous studies have shown that the mean mYPAS score is 37 ± 17 when premedicated with midazolam [17]. The score was expected to be reduced by 12 points, which was considered a score reduction that was clinically relevant after being premedicated by S-ketamine [18]. With a statistical power of 0.9, an alpha coefficient of 0.05, and considering that the rate of loss to follow-up was approximately 20%, a total sample size of 110 participants (55 in each group) was determined in this study.

Randomization and allocation

Eligible children were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either intravenous S-ketamine (0.5 mg/kg; Group S) or intravenous midazolam (0.08 mg/kg; Group M) in the anesthesia holding area. It is a specially decorated room with cartoon wall art and a sofa for the family members to rest and wait. All the children were allowed to bring their favorite toy or electronic product with them and were accompanied by their LARs until entering the OR. Children were transferred to the OR about approximately 5–10 min after premedication administration. An independent research assistant not involved in patient management prepared opaque envelopes containing randomization codes generated by SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA), which corresponded to group assignment. Another study coordinator not involved in the follow-up screened potential participants from the anesthesia-surgery scheduling list on the day before surgery. The order of consent signing was used to assign participant numbers (1–110). Then, the subjects were consecutively enrolled and randomized strictly according to the allocation sequence to either Group S or Group M. When the children entered the anesthesia holding area, the anesthesia care provider opened the envelope with the matching participant number to confirm the group assignment. The attending anesthesiologists then prepared the drugs in syringes with blank labels; the syringes used were of the same type and volume, and so was the injection volume. A single independent blinded evaluator (not involved in patient management) estimated and collected perioperative data when the anesthesia care providers administered premedication drugs in the anesthesia holding area. Owing to the clinical schedule and safety considerations, the anesthesia care providers were unblinded. In addition, the LARs, the data evaluator and collector, statistician, and surgeon remained blinded throughout the study.

Study protocol

Preoperative fasting time of the children was in accordance with the latest published guidelines [19]. To ensure participants’ safety, the pulse rate and pulse oxygen saturation were monitored by a portable pulse oximeter after the participants transferred to the anesthesia holding area. Before the experimental drug was administered, the “time-out” process was confirmed by our medical team [20]. In the anesthesia holding area, a trained observer first assessed children’s baseline preoperative anxiety using the mYPAS (T0), prior to premedication. Approximately 5 min after premedication, the children’s preoperative anxiety was reassessed via the same scale (T1). The parent separation anxiety scale (PSAS) [21] was also implemented. The LARs were asked to rate their overall satisfaction with their child’s management via a numeric rating scale (NRS: 0 ~ 10, an NRS score > 7 was considered “satisfactory”).

The mask acceptance score (MAS) [21] was assessed before initiating standard noninvasive monitoring. Intravenous induction was achieved with propofol 2 mg/kg, fentanyl 2–3 µg/kg and cisatracurium besilate 0.2 mg/kg. Medical caregivers’ satisfaction and smoothness during the entire procedure before loss of consciousness were assessed via the NRS. Following induction, artificial airway devices were inserted for mechanical ventilation. After induction of anesthesia in the OR, attending anesthesiologists administered additional relevant nerve blocks, which were performed either with or without ultrasound guidance. The local anesthetic type was ropivacaine (Naropin®), with a relatively low concentration of 0.2%, which we deemed effective analgesia effect and muscle strength retained. The total doses were 2 mg/kg.

Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (2–3%) combined with remifentanil (0.1–0.2 µg/kg/min). During the surgery, the anesthetics were adjusted to maintain the vital signs fluctuation within 20% of the baseline level. Pressure-controlled ventilation mode was used to achieve the tidal volume at 6–8 ml/kg and respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain the end-tidal carbon dioxide between 30 and 40 mmHg. Intraoperative lung-protective ventilation strategy was implemented. Near the end of the surgery, ondansetron hydrochloride 0.15 mg/kg was administered for prophylaxis of postoperative nausea and vomiting. The timing of anesthetic discontinuation was when the surgical drapes were removed. Then, the children were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit shortly. The post-anesthesia care unit is staffed with dedicated anesthesiologists and nurses responsible for extubation. Ventilation was continued until there were signs of swallowing and respiratory movement, at which point neuromuscular blockade was reversed with atropine (0.02 mg/kg) and neostigmine (0.04 mg/kg). The artificial airway devices were removed when the neuromuscular blocker had been completely reversed, with the patient showing signs of adequate spontaneous breathing tidal volume greater than 5 ml/kg, eye opening and purposeful body movements. The emergence time was measured in minutes from the moment that discontinue the administration of all anesthetic drugs until removal of the artificial airway devices. Rescue analgesic administration such as intravenous fentanyl was documented. The incidence of emergence delirium, defined by pediatric anesthesia emergence delirium (PAED) [22] scale was also evaluated.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was carried out as an intention-to-treat analysis. The results are expressed as numbers (n) with frequencies (%) for qualitative data and mean values with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR, 25th −75th percentiles) for quantitative data. The comparison of categorical variables was performed by the Chi-square test or the Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. For continuous variables, comparisons were carried out with analysis of variance or a non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test. The significance level was 0.05 for each hypothesis. All the statistical tests were 2-tailed. The data were analyzed and imaged via GraphPad Prism 9.5.1.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 384 children were assessed for eligibility, and 274 children were excluded (Fig. 1). Among the 274 excluded children, 117 did not meet the inclusion criteria, 105 underwent day surgeries, 38 experienced difficulties in language communication because they were ethnic minorities (Yi nationality and Tibetan), and 14 declined to be included in the study. A total of 110 patients underwent randomization, with 55 in Group S and 55 in Group M. There were 2 cases in Group S and 1 case in Group M with unanticipated venous access malfunction, respectively. And 3 operations were canceled by the surgeon in Group M; thus, those 6 cases did not receive any allocated intervention and were not included in the analysis. No cases were lost to follow-up. In summary, a total of 104 cases (53 in Group S and 51 in Group M) were analyzed. As illustrated in Table 1, basic demographic information, perioperative parameters, and other baseline variables were not substantially different between Group S and Group M. No significant differences were also found in nerve block types (see Additional File 1: Table S1).

Primary outcomes

There were no statistically significant differences in the baseline mYPAS score between the two groups [Group S: median (interquartile range, IQR) 54.4 (45.2–65.3); Group M: 61.8 (43.5–69.8); P = 0.805; Table 1]. However, compared with Group M, Group S had significantly lower scores at approximately 5 min after intravenous administration of premedication [median (IQR): 27.08 (22.92–33.33) vs. 31.25 (27.08–33.33); P = 0.025], as shown in Table 2; Fig. 2. No severe drug-related adverse reactions, such as paradoxical excitatory, excessive salivation, or laryngospasm were observed during the premedication period.

Secondary outcomes

PSAS scores were not significantly different between the two groups. Additionally, there were also no significant differences observed in PAED, LARs satisfaction, or care provider satisfaction. However, Group S exhibited a significantly higher heart rate after premedication and a longer postoperative recovery time compared with Group M (Table 2). During the 24-hour postoperative follow-up, minor adverse events were observed in some patients. In Group S, two children experienced dizziness and drowsiness. One child complained of nausea and vomiting. These events contributed to adverse reaction incidences of 3.7% and 1.9%, respectively. Importantly, no S-ketamine-related neurological adverse effects (e.g., hallucinations or nightmares) were recorded.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that intravenous premedication with S-ketamine at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg achieves a deeper and more effective sedative state in preschool-aged children (2–6 years) than premedication with midazolam at 0.08 mg/kg. Notably, this superior sedative effect is accompanied by a slight prolongation of emergence time. These results address the key research question of our study, which is identifying a more effective intravenous premedication regimen for preschool children undergoing elective surgery, and provide preliminary evidence for the clinical application of S-ketamine in this population.

Many institutions have developed their own clinical pathways for sedative use in children within their hospitals. The choice of appropriate ancillary anesthetic drugs for premedication not only reduces the incidence of preoperative anxiety, but also improve the comfort of pediatric patients [23]. For example, the incidence of sevoflurane-related emergence agitation after surgery can be reduced [24]. Several studies have explored audiovisual distraction and other non-pharmacological strategies for alleviating perioperative anxiety in children, demonstrating their effectiveness [23, 25, 26]. In our hospital, established venous access in the ward by topical anesthesia of the compound lidocaine cream (EMLA®) 1 h before needle puncture. Other non-pharm interventions were implemented to attenuate fear of needle puncture such as playing music, carrying children’s favorite toy, parental accompaniment, comfort, and encouragement. All these measures resulted in the successful establishment of intravenous access in over 90% of the children before surgery. Anesthetists would prone to use the intravenous access to achieve the purpose of sedation.

Few studies have directly compared the premedication sedative effects of ketamine and midazolam. Our findings align with those of previous studies, which reported that intravenous midazolam (administered at varying doses before anesthesia induction) produces comparable effects on sedation, parent-child separation, and mask compliance [2]. In our trial, we administered a relatively high dose of midazolam (0.08 mg/kg) to achieve a rapid onset time, which was sufficient to reduce crying and facilitate smooth separation. Children were transferred to the OR shortly after premedication, meaning that premedication may have functioned as an extension of the induction process—potentially contributing to the low adverse event rate. By acting on γ-aminobutyric acid type A receptors, midazolam relieves preoperative anxiety, and positive rapport between medical staff and patients may have further contributed to the smooth induction of anesthesia [27]. We observed that the children who received midazolam remained cooperative and were more prone to be cheerful, while S-ketamine induced a more profound sedation state. This finding aligns with that of Bromfalk et al. [3] The transition from a state of intense anxiety to sudden relaxation inherently brings a sense of relief and comfort. In pediatric patients, this relaxed state is likely to manifest as euphoria, giggling, silly, “happy drunk” state, and cooperativeness.

Unlike that, when children’s caregivers saw their child turn to be a “dull-doll” after being premedicated with S-ketamine, they may sometimes experience confusion and nervousness. However, after our brief explanation, they were able to accept that the clinical manifestations of reduced blinking, fixed expressions, and no response to external calls are the specific characteristics of the effect of S-ketamine and can be restored with no long-term sequelae. Studies focusing on the premedication of S-ketamine were mainly intranasal [11, 12, 28]. It may have some disadvantages, such as onset time varies greatly among individuals, uncertain effect and difficult to control sedation depth. Therefore, it is difficult to achieve a satisfactory and appropriate depth of sedation before entering the operation room, either over-sedated or under-sedated. Similar to the design of this study, Sajedi et al. compared intravenous administration of 0.1 mg/kg midazolam, or intravenous ketamine 1 mg/kg, or a combination of half doses of both. They revealed that the combination group was superior [29]. In general, three-arm tests require a larger sample size. Typically, anesthesia care providers may be reluctant to use two distinct drugs for preoperative sedation. In the design of our trial, only two groups were compared, which can make the research sufficiently powered when the results are interpret [30]. In our study, when producing a sedative effect, S-ketamine was accompanied by an increase in heart rate—this increase, however, was considered to be within the acceptable range. These phenomena reflect the typical pharmacological profile of S-ketamine [31].

While our primary focus was on preoperative sedation, postoperative recovery parameters also merit attention. No obviously side effects were recorded after surgery. Parents reported their children who received S-ketamine for premedication even had a better and deeper sleep during the night. It is consisting with previous study that S-ketamine may significantly improve postoperative sleep quality [32]. Notably, children in Group S had a longer emergence time than those in Group M. This can be explained by its ability to alleviate postoperative pain [33]. This discrepancy highlights the need for future studies to explore the optimal dosage of S-ketamine, as a higher dosage is associated with both a faster onset of sedation and a prolonged emergence time, which was extended by approximately 8 min in the present trial. Even so, this difference did not impact the efficiency of the busy PACU. Both two drugs were shown to be safe, with no severe adverse events observed, especially laryngospasm. Given its significant effect in relieving preoperative anxiety, S-ketamine represents a favorable new option for pediatric preoperative premedication.

This trial has certain limitations. First, this study adopted a single-center study design, with a sample size of only 104 children. Additionally, to ensure data quality, we restricted the study population to a narrow age range—specifically, preschool-aged children. These factors (i.e., single-center design, small sample size, and narrow age range) will result in limitations in our research, including restricted external validity and insufficient representation of special pediatric populations (e.g., children with autism). Second, given that most of the surgeries were minor, intraoperative monitoring of anesthetic depth and neuromuscular blockade was not performed. In fact, the vast majority of anesthesiologists do not utilize these two monitoring modalities in daily clinical practice. This alignment with actual clinical circumstances also makes our research more grounded in real-world contexts, as it reflects the practical constraints and routine patterns of anesthesia management. Third, the family background, the guardians’ demographic information, such as the age and their educational level may have an influence on the results but were not assessed in this study. Fourth, the intravenous administration route of premedication may restrict the generalizability of the trial. For those children who lack an intravenous route before anesthesia, the use of a dexmedetomidine nasal spray may be a novel alternative for preoperative sedation [34].

Conclusion

In summary, premedication with intravenous administration of 0.5 mg/kg S-ketamine or 0.08 mg/kg midazolam can produce satisfactory effects on sedation status and parental separation in preschool children scheduled for elective surgeries while a single dose of 0.5 mg/kg S-ketamine provides a deeper sedative state, albeit with a slight prolongation of emergence time.
