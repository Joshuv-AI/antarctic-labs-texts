Prototype-Guided Robust Learning against Backdoor Attacks
Wei Guo, Maura Pintor, Ambra Demontis, Battista Biggio

Abstract
Backdoor attacks poison the training data to embed a backdoor in the model, causing it to behave normally on legitimate inputs but maliciously when specific trigger signals appear. Training a benign model from a dataset poisoned by backdoor attacks is challenging. Existing works rely on various assumptions and can only defend against backdoor attacks with specific trigger signals, high poisoning ratios, or when the defender possesses a large, untainted, validation dataset. In this paper, we propose a defense called Prototype-Guided Robust Learning (PGRL) , which overcomes all the aforementioned limitations, being robust against diverse backdoor attacks. Leveraging a tiny set of benign samples, PGRL generates prototype vectors to guide the training process. We compare our PGRL with 8 existing defenses, showing that it achieves superior robustness. We also demonstrate that PGRL generalizes well across various architectures, datasets, and advanced attacks. Finally, to evaluate our PGRL in the worst-case scenario, we perform an adaptive attack, where the attackers fully know the details of the defense.

We propose a novel robust learning method called Prototype-Guided Robust Learning (PGRL),
and assume the defender has a small amount of benign (validation) data (10 samples per class) to generate one representative vector for each class as a prototype.
As shown in Figure
1
, the PGRL mainly consists of two steps:
1)
label consistency verification
(LCV), exploiting the prototypes to predict pseudo-labels for all training samples, and training the model over label-consistent samples (whose original labels in the dataset match the predicted pseudo-labels);
2)
weighted cross entropy
(WCE), analyzing the feature distribution and assigning negative weights to those training samples whose features are far from the benign ones.
As shown in Table
1
, WCE is effective against attacks
without
cover samples but struggles when
cover samples are included
, particularly at low poisoning ratios. Conversely, LCV is effective
only when
cover samples are present and fails otherwise. Since LCV and WCE are complementary, PGRL combines both to provide a more general defense.

Our main contributions are summarized as
: 1) identifying limitations of 8 existing robust learning defenses and showing that they completely fail for at least one typology of state-of-the-art attacks, and proposing PGRL which instead can face all the typologies of proposed backdoor attacks; 2) demonstrating that PGRL generalizes effectively across diverse architectures, datasets, and advanced attack strategies; 3) proposing an adaptive attack to evaluate PGRL in the worst situation, where the attacker fully knows all details of PGRL.

Background on Backdoor Attacks

Focusing on the classification task, we denote the training dataset as
D_{tr}={(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})}_{i=1,...,n}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{X}
represents input features and
y_{i}\in[0,k-1]
represents corresponding labels belonging to
k
classes.
Let
M:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
denote the target model, mapping the input to a softmax vector, and the predicted label is
\arg\max(M(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))
.
We assume the architecture of
M
as:
M=l\circ s\circ f
, where
f:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
is the feature extractor (with ReLU at the end),
s:\mathbb{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
is the sphere projector (with L2 norm at the end), and
l:\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the classifier outputting the softmax vector. Compared with traditional models, the additional
s
aims to facilitate the calculation of cosine similarity by the dot product on the sphere space. Given an input
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{X}
, for simplicity, we will denote the corresponding element in
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{S}
as
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}=s\circ f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
.

The sample selection policies of backdoors
with/without
label corruption are different: for attacks without label corruption, the samples are taken from the target class
t
, i.e.,
D_{tr}^{\alpha}\subset D_{tr,t}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})|y_{i}=t\}
and for attacks with label corruption, they can be taken from the other classes as well, i.e.,
D_{tr}^{\alpha}\subset D_{tr}
.

Attacker’s goal.
The attacker aims to inject a backdoor into a model, so that at the test time the backdoored model performs normally on the benign inputs (without the trigger), but misclassifies samples as
t
once a trigger signal
\boldsymbol{v}
is present in them.

Attacker’s knowledge.
We assume the attacker has black-box access to the model and the training process. This means that the attacker has zero knowledge of the training information, e.g, model architectures, loss functions, and optimization algorithms. Moreover, the attacker cannot query the model during training time to improve attack efficiency. In Appendix
G
, we also consider an adaptive attacker with full knowledge (white-box) of the target system and the proposed defense.

Attacker’s capability.
In the robust learning (defenders as trainers
2
2

2

Therefore, the attacks (e.g.
[
26
]
) assuming the attacker as the trainer are out of scope.
), without controlling the training process, the attacker can randomly select a
\alpha
percentage (poisoning ratio) of samples from
D_{tr}
, and poison them by adding the trigger
\boldsymbol{v}
over them and modifying their labels to target class
t
, i.e.,
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\rightarrow(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\oplus\boldsymbol{v},t)
.
We denote the poisoned dataset as
D_{tr}^{\alpha}
.

3
Limitations of Exiting Robust Learning Defenses

FPF-based methods

[
18
,
20
,
21
,
19
]
rely on FPF assumption. We rethink this assumption and originally find that, as shown by Figure
4
: 1) it fails entirely for backdoor attacks with cover samples, and 2) it performs poorly in traditional attacks (without cover samples) under low poisoning ratios.
The underlying rationale is that poisoned and cover samples transfer conflicting supervisory signals
: poisoned samples
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\oplus\boldsymbol{v},t)
push the model to associate the trigger
\boldsymbol{v}
with the target label
t
, whereas cover samples
(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\oplus\boldsymbol{v},y_{j})
encourage it to ignore
\boldsymbol{v}
and predict the correct label
y_{j}
. Therefore, it is more difficult to learn the poisoning samples in the presence of cover samples. It is more difficult as well when the poisoning ratio is low.
As summarized in Table
1
, FPF-based methods fail against attacks with cover samples (e.g., AdapBlend) and low-poisoning-ratio attacks without cover samples (e.g., Pattern, Freq). More details on the learning priority can be discussed in Appendix
C
.

DBD

[
22
]
uses the contrastive learning (a self-supervised learning) to train models without labels, breaking the link between triggers and target labels in label-corruption backdoors. However,
Li et al. 2023
shows that contrastive learning is ineffective against attacks without label corruption. Therefore, as shown in Table
1
, DBD defends well against label-corruption attacks (Pattern, AdapBlend) but fails against the ones without label corruption (Freq).

EBD

[
23
]
assumes that trigger signals are less robust to data augmentations than benign features. This holds for the Pattern trigger, not for AdapBlend and Freq, which are augmentation-robust. Appendix
D
provides the robustness evaluations for all three attacks. Thus, as shown in Table
1
, EBD only works against less robust triggers (Pattern).

CT

[
24
]
mitigates backdoor threats by injecting mislabeled benign samples into the poisoned dataset, thereby hindering the ability of the model to learn from clean data. Since the mislabeled benign samples don’t affect the poisoned samples, this approach indirectly amplifies the signal of poisoned samples for easier detection. However, CT relies on access to a large set of benign samples and performs poorly when only limited benign data is available, as shown in Section
5
.

VaB

[
25
]
leverages both the FPF assumption and semi-supervised learning. However,
Shejwalkar et al. 2023
found that backdoors without label corruption can also violate semi-supervised learning. As a result, it inherits the limitations of FPF-based methods and also fails against the attacks without label corruption (Freq).

4
Our Proposed Method: PGRL

The overview of PGRL is shown in Figure
1
, while the corresponding pseudocode is shown in Algorithm
1
.
Our system consists of the two complementary methodologies described below, combined to obtain a defense effective against all state-of-the-art attacks.

Label Consistency Verification (LCV).
LCV starts from a network warmed-up on clean (validation) samples and then fine-tunes it on the label-consistent samples.
3
3

3

The label-consistent samples are those with the pseudo-labels (labels assigned considering the sample’s distance to the prototype in the feature space) same as the labels in the dataset.
When LCV is employed alone, the standard cross-entropy is used (
w_{i}
=1 in WCE loss).
LCV is effective against
backdoor attacks with cover samples
because the cover samples impede the learning of the model over poisoned samples, allowing the model to remain benign after initial training (Figure
5(b)
). This benign but not well-trained model will gradually (during the learning) reassign poisoned samples to their correct classes via pseudo-labeling. LCV leverages the fact that cover samples cause the model to learn benign samples first.
In contrast, under backdoor attacks
without cover samples
, the model fits poisoned samples early, making pseudo-label correction ineffective.

Weighted Cross Entropy (WCE).
WCE assigns to each training sample a weight that depends on its distance from the benign (validation) samples. When WCE is employed alone, it uses the whole batch in WCE loss (
|I_{t}|=|B|
).
The weight
w_{i}
is estimated based on the feature separability assumption—that poisoned samples are well-separated from benign ones in the feature space.
Guo et al. 2023a
demonstrated its effectiveness against backdoor attacks without cover samples (regardless of with/without the label corruption). However,
Qi et al. 2023b
later showed that AdapBlend (with cover samples) can bypass this assumption when the poisoning ratio is low (under higher poisoning ratios, backdoor detection becomes easier, making AdapBlend detectable). Like other methods relying on feature separability, WCE inherits this vulnerability. Combining it with LCV overcomes this problem.

4.1
Label Consistency Verification

LCV predicts a pseudo-label for each sample and checks whether it aligns with the label shown in the training dataset. If yes, the sample is further utilized to train the model.
The naïve approach to calculate the pseudo-label is to measure the cosine similarity between a sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
with
k
prototypes (one per class) in sphere space, assigning the class of the closest prototype as the pseudo-label, i.e.,
p_{i}=\arg\max(C\cdot\boldsymbol{s}_{i})
,
where
C=\{\boldsymbol{c}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{c}_{k}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d_{2}}
is the prototype matrix (with
\boldsymbol{c}_{j}
as the mean sphere vector of benign samples belonging to class
j
from
D_{\text{val}}
),
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
is the corresponding sphere vector of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, and
p_{i}
is the index of a prototype with maximum
\boldsymbol{c}_{p_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}_{i}
. The above-mentioned pseudo-labeling can be extended to process one batch
B
:
P=\arg\max(S\cdot C^{T})
, where
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{2}}
is the sphere matrix
including
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}
of all samples from
B
.

However, cosine similarity alone fails when all samples cluster near a single prototype and are assigned the same pseudolabel, which often occurs in the initial training process. This leads to the model learning the feature only from one class. To address this, we exploit the optimal transport algorithm
[
30
]
to combine the
cosine similarity
with
uniformity property
of the dataset, where each sample has roughly equal probabilities (
\approx 1/k
) of belonging to one of the
k
classes. The influence of optimal transport is discussed in Appendix
E
.

The optimal transport algorithm produces a probability matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times k}
, where each element
Q_{ij}
represents the probability of assigning pseudo-label
j
to the sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, and the probabilities for each sample across all classes sum to 1, i.e.,
\sum_{j=1}^{k}Q_{ij}=1
. The
Q
is calculated via:

Q^{*}=\arg\max_{Q\in\mathbb{Q}}\langle Q,S\cdot C^{T}\rangle+\epsilon\cdot H(Q)

(1)

where
\mathbb{Q}=\{Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times k}\mid Q^{T}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{n}=\frac{n}{k}\mathbf{1}_{k},\,Q\cdot\mathbf{1}_{k}=\mathbf{1}_{n}\}
ensures the uniformity property, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{ij}\approx\frac{n}{k}
(
\mathbf{1}_{n}
and
\mathbf{1}_{k}
are column vectors of ones with dimensions
n
and
k
),
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the Frobenius inner product (element-wise dot product),
and
H(Q)=-\sum_{ij}(Q_{ij}\cdot\log Q_{ij})
. The hyperparameter
\epsilon
balances the cosine similarity (first term) and uniformity regularization (second one), i.e., each sample is uniformly assigned to all classes. A larger
\epsilon
increases uniformity, diminishing the impact of cosine similarity.
Then, the pseudo-label
p_{i}
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is calculated as
p_{i}=\arg\max(Q^{*}_{i})
, where
Q^{*}_{i}
is the
i
-th row of
Q^{*}
.

Finally, label consistency is performed by inspecting the pseudo-label
p_{i}
and the label
y_{i}
taken from the dataset.
If
p_{i}=y_{i}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is considered as a label-consistent sample and its index is added into
I_{t}
; otherwise,
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is considered as a potential poisoning sample and its index is added to the set
I_{u}
of untrusted indices.

The
uniformity property
doesn’t restrict our method only working over balanced datasets, since 1) this property is used as a regularizer, heavily penalizing pseudo-label distributions that are far from uniformity, e.g., when predictions collapse to a single class; 2) for the extreme cases, when data distribution is super unbalanced, we can also update the uniform distribution assumption (
Q^{T}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{n}=\frac{n}{k}\mathbf{1}_{k}
) in
\mathbb{Q}
with the observed label distribution
[
30
]
.

Remark 1

A sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is trusted only when the prototype that
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is closest to in the sphere space belongs to the same class as its label
y_{i}
.

4.2
Weighted Cross Entropy

For the label-consistent samples, instead of treating them with equal confidence, the defender assigns a weight
w_{i}\in[-1,1]
to control their contribution to the training process. Specifically, the WCE loss is defined as follows:

\begin{split}&\sum_{i\in I_{t}}WCE(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i},w_{i})=\sum_{i\in I_{t}}w_{i}\cdot sgn_{i}\cdot CE(M(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),y_{i})\\
&\text{s.t.}~sgn_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $w_{i}<0$ and $\arg\max(M(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))\neq y_{i}$}\\
1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\end{split}

(2)

where
CE()
is the cross entropy loss.
If
w_{i}
is positive (negative), the model is encouraged to ‘learn’ (‘unlearn’) the corresponding sample. The term
sgn_{i}
ensures that the unlearning process stops once the model has already ‘forgotten’ the sample, i.e., when
\arg\max M(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\neq y_{i}
.
To avoid the statistical bias over batch data, the weight estimation analyzes the entire training dataset’s features to compute and update the weights
w_{i}
.

The weight
w_{i}
is estimated by analyzing the distance between
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and the benign (validation) samples of class
y_{i}
in
D_{val}
. Specifically, utilizing the
multivariate normal distribution
to calculate a probability
q_{i}=\max_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in F_{val,j=y_{i}}}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{f}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\mu},\Sigma)
,
where
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}
is the feature vector of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
,
F_{val,j=y_{i}}
is the set of corresponding benign (validation) features of class
y_{i}
from
D_{val}
, and
\Sigma
is an identity matrix whose number of columns and rows equals the dimension of
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}
. The value of
q_{i}
tends to be high if the distance is small. Moreover, since a high feature dimension (
d_{1}>1000
) can cause the curse of dimensionality,
4
4

4

The distance metrics fail to discriminate ‘near’ and ‘far’ distances
we reduce the dimensionality of
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}
and
F_{val,j=y_{i}}
by UMAP
[
31
]
to lower dimension before using them.

Then, the
q_{i}
is normalized to
w_{i}\in[-1,1]
by normalization with threshold
\tau
. Given probability sets
\boldsymbol{q}=\{q_{1},...,q_{N}\}
for all
N
samples in the whole dataset
D_{tr}^{\alpha}
, we normalize them into the range
[-1,1]
via

w_{i}=\begin{cases}1&q_{i}>\tau\\
2\cdot\frac{q_{i}-\min(\boldsymbol{q})}{\tau-\min(\boldsymbol{q})}-1&q_{i}\leq\tau.\end{cases}

(3)

Let
Pr(\boldsymbol{q}>\tau)
denote the probability that the elements in
\boldsymbol{q}
exceed this threshold. This normalization ensures that the top
Pr(\boldsymbol{q}>\tau)
percentage of weights are set to 1, while the remaining weights are scaled to lie within the range
[-1,1]
. The selection of
\tau
is described in Appendix
E
.
To ensure the stability, the weights are updated via momentum.

Why normalizing the weights to the range
[-1,1]
instead of
[0,1]
?
Compared to
[0,1]
normalization, scaling poisoned sample weights to
[-1,1]
facilitates more effective unlearning by the model, since the model will more actively unlearn the samples with negative weights than the zero ones.

Remark 2

Weight estimation assigns negative weights to the outlier training samples (as suspect poisoned samples), which are far from
D_{val}
in the feature space, in order to unlearn them.

5
Experiments

We first define 4 metrics to evaluate attack and defense performance:
Accuracy
(
ACC
), the fraction of benign samples correctly classified;
Attack Success Rate
(
ASR
), the fraction of triggered inputs misclassified as the target
t
;
True Positive Rate
(
TPR
), the fraction of poisoned samples correctly detected; and
False Positive Rate
(
FPR
), the fraction of benign samples falsely flagged. A successful backdoored model yields
ACC
similar to a clean model and with
ASR

>0.5
, whereas a robust defense maintains
ACC
but reduces
ASR
to
\approx 0
; effective detection demands
TPR

\approx 1
and
FPR

\approx 0
.

The feature extractor
f
is based on ResNet18
[
32
]
with feature dimension
d_{1}=2,048
. The sphere projector
s
consists of two fully connected (FC) layers with a ReLU activation in between and a normalization layer at the end, and the sphere space dimension is
d_{2}=128
. The classifier
l
comprises a single FC layer followed by a softmax activation. The experiments are performed over NVIDIA RTX A6000 with a memory size of 50 GB.
The experiments are carried out on CIFAR-10 unless otherwise specified; the experimental setup of the employed attacks is specified in the appendix
B
.

Defense Settings.

6
Related Work on Robust Learning Defenses

FPF-based methods.
Anti-Backdoor Learning (ABL)
[
18
]
first introduced the FPF assumption, enabling defenders to identify poisoned data by isolating samples with the lowest loss values after 10 training epochs.
Later, Progressive Isolation of Poisoned Data (PIPD)
[
19
]
leveraged activation maps to reduce the high false positive rate of ABL’s performance.
With a small benign dataset (10 samples per class), Adaptively Splitting dataset-based Defense (ASD)
[
20
]
distinguished clean hard samples (high loss) from poisoned data by a virtual model, which is trained on the entire dataset to identify poisoned samples based on the FPF assumption.

Zhang et al. 2023
introduced Causality-inspired Backdoor Defense (CBD), which employs an early-stop strategy to create an auxiliary model that primarily learns backdoor patterns, and then trains a benign model by minimizing mutual information with the auxiliary model.

Other methods.

Huang et al. 2022
proposed the Decoupling-based Backdoor Defense (DBD), which decouples the training process to mitigate backdoor attacks.
This approach employs the SimCLR contrasting learning framework
[
43
]
to train a benign feature extractor using only unlabeled training samples from the poisoned dataset.
The last layers of the model are then fine-tuned using labeled data.
Since the SimCLR method used in DBD is computationally intensive,
Chen et al. 2022
proposed a more efficient approach called Effective Backdoor defense (EBD).
This method leverages the
feature consistency towards transformation
assumption to differentiate between poisoned and benign samples, positing that benign samples exhibit more consistent (robust) features after data augmentations than poisoned ones.
Later,
Qi et al. 2023a
introduced a sophisticated approach called Confusing Training (CT), which assumes the defender has access to benign (validation) data.
By injecting randomly labeled benign samples into the poisoned dataset, the method seeks to disrupt the model’s learning of benign samples, increasing the likelihood of the model prioritizing poisoned samples.
Finally, Zhu et al.
[
25
]
introduced Victim and Beneficiary (VaB), which uses the FPF assumption to discard suspected poisoned samples, trains on the remaining data with AttentionMix (one method of semi-supervised learning), and then fine-tunes the model via semi-supervised suppression training.

7
Conclusion

We propose PGRL, a robust learning method that uses a small set of benign samples to generate prototypes, guiding the training process to produce a clean model even from a poisoned dataset. Compared with existing methods, PGRL shows superior robustness against diverse backdoor attacks—regardless of trigger types, poisoning rates, label corruption, or the use of cover samples. We also design an adaptive attack to challenge PGRL; however, it is a white-box attack (a super strong assumption to the attacker), whereas this work aims to defend against black-box attacks.

Appendix F
Implementation of Existing Works

ABL
:
In ABL, the defender first trains a model on the poisoned dataset for 10 epochs using SGD (learning rate 0.01, momentum 0.9, weight decay 1e-4). The model is then used to compute losses for all training samples, and the 5% with the lowest losses are isolated as poisoned. Then, this model is trained on the remaining 95% for 180 epochs, followed by 10 epochs of fine-tuning on the isolated samples with a learning rate of 5e-4 to unlearn (with negative cross entropy) them.

PIPD
: PIPD directly reuses the isolation results from ABL, and exploits the feature activation information to improve the detection performance. Then, with a improved isolation results, PIPD trains the model with same settings as ABL.

ASD
: The model is trained for 120 epochs using the Adam optimizer with lr 0.002. ASD progressively finds out benign samples from the poisoned dataset, selecting 5 samples every 10 epochs. The selection is based on the lowest loss values. Additionally, the virtual model is trained using Adam with lr 0.015 for 1 epoch.

CBD
: The auxiliary model is trained for 5 epochs using SGD with lr 0.1, momentum 0.9, and weight decay 1e-4. Using the same optimizer, CBD then trains a benign model for 100 epochs by minimizing mutual information between it with the auxiliary model.

DBD
: The feature extractor is trained with SimCLR for 1000 epochs using SGD (lr 0.4, momentum 0.9, weight decay 1e-4). Then, with the feature extractor frozen, the defender trains only the FC layer for 20 epochs. After that, the entire model is fine-tuned with MixMatch for 170 epochs using SGD (lr 1e-4, momentum 0.9, weight decay 1e-4).

EBD
: EBD first trains a backdoored model for 10 epochs without data augmentation using SGD (lr 0.01, momentum 0.9, weight decay 5e-4). This model is then used to analyze feature consistency and remove the top 0.05 of samples with the largest consistency changes, assumed to be poisoned. Next, the feature extractor is trained with supervised contrastive learning for 200 epochs using SGD (lr 0.05, momentum 0.9, weight decay 1e-4). Finally, the FC layers are fine-tuned for 10 epochs using SGD (lr 0.1, momentum 0.9, weight decay 0).

CT
: CT first trains a backdoored model on the poisoned dataset for 100 epochs using SGD (lr 0.01, momentum 0.9, weight decay 1e-4). Then, this model is fine-tuned on randomly mislabeled samples for 6000 batch iterations (approximately 4 epochs) using SGD (lr 0.001, momentum 0.7, weight decay 1e-4). Samples are identified as the poisoned samples when their labels of the original and fine-tuned models are consistent. Finally, a benign model is trained on the cleaned dataset for 100 epochs using SGD (lr 0.1, momentum 0.9, weight decay 1e-4).

VaB
: The model is first warmed up for 3 epochs using Adam (lr 0.001, weight decay 5e-4). This warmed-up model is then used to identify and isolate suspected poisoned samples. A benign model is subsequently trained on the remaining samples for 100 epochs using AttentionMix, followed by 100 epochs of semi-supervised suppression training. The optimization is based on SGD (lr 0.1, momentum 0.9, weight decay 5e-4).

Appendix G
Adaptive Attacks

We conduct an adaptive attack assuming that the attacker knows the PGRL algorithm, training data, and model architecture. Results indicate that the attack behaves more like an adversarial perturbation than a traditional backdoor.

By analyzing the proposed PGRL, the attacker observes following two potential weaknesses of its two components:

•

WK1
: LCV can be bypassed when poisoned and benign samples share the same label (i.e. attacks without label corruption);

•

WK2
: The weight estimation for the WCE loss based on feature separability can be circumvented if poisoned and benign samples are close to each other in the feature space.

Based on these weaknesses, we initially proposed a Freq attack (no label corruption targeting
WK1
) with cover samples (targeting
WK2
), as the cover samples can reduce feature separability
[
14
]
. However, non-label-corruption attacks are already inefficient, since the model tends to learn ground-truth features rather than the trigger signal
[
45
]
. The addition of cover samples further impairs the model’s ability to learn from poisoned data, compounding the inefficiency. As shown in Table
11
, the ASR drops sharply to 0.02 with cover samples, confirming the significant performance degradation.
