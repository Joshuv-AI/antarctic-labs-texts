Regularity properties of Macbeath–Hurwitz and related maps and surfaces
Gareth A. Jones

Abstract
The Macbeath–Hurwitz maps ℳ \mathcal{M} of type { 3 , 7 } \{3,7\} , obtained from the Hurwitz groups G = PSL 2 ​ ( q ) G={\rm PSL}_{2}(q) found by Macbeath, are fully regular by a result of Singerman, with automorphism group G × C 2 G\times{\rm C}_{2} or PGL 2 ​ ( q ) {\rm PGL}_{2}(q) . Hall’s criterion determines which of these two properties, called inner and outer regularity, ℳ \mathcal{M} has. Inner (but not outer) regular maps ℳ \mathcal{M} yield non-orientable regular maps ℳ / C 2 \mathcal{M}/{\rm C}_{2} of the same type with automorphism group G G . If q = p 3 q=p^{3} for a prime p ≡ ± 2 p\equiv\pm 2 or ± 3 \pm 3 mod ( 7 ) (7) the unique map ℳ \mathcal{M} is inner regular if and only if p ≡ 1 p\equiv 1 mod ( 4 ) (4) . If q = p q=p for a prime p ≡ ± 1 p\equiv\pm 1 mod ( 7 ) (7) there are three maps ℳ \mathcal{M} ; we use the density theorems of Frobenius and Chebotarev to show that in this case the sets of such primes p p for which 0 , 1 , 2 0,1,2 or 3 3 of them are inner regular have relative densities 1 / 8 1/8 , 3 / 8 3/8 , 3 / 8 3/8 and 1 / 8 1/8 respectively. Hall’s criterion and its consequences are extended to the analogous Macbeath maps of type { 3 , n } \{3,n\} obtained from PSL 2 ​ ( q ) {\rm PSL}_{2}(q) for all n ≥ 7 n\geq 7 ; theoretical predictions on their number and properties are supported by evidence from the map databases of Conder and Potočnik.

Introduction

Hurwitz
[
15
]
showed that if
\mathcal{S}
is a compact Riemann surface (equivalently, a complex projective algebraic curve) of genus
g\geq 2
then its automorphism group
G={\rm Aut}\,\mathcal{S}
has order
|G|\leq 84(g-1)
. The surfaces
\mathcal{S}
and groups

attaining this bound are called
Hurwitz surfaces
and
Hurwitz groups
. For a long time the only known examples were Klein’s quartic curve
[
21
]
of genus
3
with automorphism group
{\rm PSL}_{2}(7)
, and an example of genus
7
with automorphism group
{\rm PSL}_{2}(8)
discovered by Fricke
[
12
]
; this example was long forgotten, but was rediscovered in 1965 by Macbeath
[
25
]
. In 1961 he showed in
[
24
]
that any Hurwitz surface yields infinitely many more as its mod
(m)
homology covers for integers
m\geq 2
(the ‘Macbeath trick’). In 1969 Macbeath
[
26
]
extended the examples due to Klein and Fricke by proving the following:

Theorem 1.1

(Macbeath)

The group
G={\rm PSL}_{2}(q)
is a Hurwitz group if and only if

1.

q=7
, with a unique Hurwitz surface
\mathcal{S}
, or

2.

q=p

p\equiv\pm 1

mod

(7)

\mathcal{S}
for each such

, or

3.

or
\pm 3

mod

(7)

\mathcal{S}
for each such

Any Hurwitz surface

carries an orientably regular map

of type
\{3,7\}

The maps

{\rm Aut}\,\mathcal{M}\cong{\rm PSL}_{2}(q)\times{\rm C}_{2}\quad\hbox{or}\quad{\rm PGL}_{2}(q);

It is therefore of interest to know whether a given Macbeath–Hurwitz map

Let us define
\mathcal{M}
to be a

By Singerman’s result

Theorem 1.6

(Extended Regularity Criterion)

Let

Let

For instance, since

Sections

]

Applying the density theorems mentioned earlier requires knowledge of the Galois groups of certain polynomials; these are discussed in more detail in Section
14

Although the main objects of study in this paper, namely certain families of highly symmetric Riemann and Klein surfaces and their triangulations, are geometric in nature, the fact that their automorphism groups are most conveniently defined over finite fields means that the algebraic and number-theoretic properties of these fields and of some related polynomials play a major role in this work. In fact, one of the main attractions of this topic is the way in which it brings together ideas from different areas of mathematics. For example, the fact that the Riemann surfaces considered here are all uniformised by subgroups of finite index in triangle groups means that they and the maps they carry are all instances of Grothendieck’s dessins d’enfants (see
[
19

20
]

Triangle groups and regular maps

If
\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{n}<\frac{1}{2}
, let
T
be a triangle
ABC
in the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}
with internal angles
\pi/n
,
\pi/2
and
\pi/m
at
A
,
B
and
C
. Then the extended triangle group
\Delta=\Delta[2,m,n]
of type
(2,m,n)
is the group generated by reflections
a,b
and
c
of
\mathbb{H}
in the sides of
T
opposite
A
,
B
and
C
. It follows from the fact that
\mathbb{H}
is simply connected that
\Delta
has a presentation

\Delta=\langle a,b,c\mid a^{2}=b^{2}=c^{2}=(ab)^{m}=(bc)^{n}=(ca)^{2}=1\rangle.

The ordinary triangle group
\Delta^{+}=\Delta(2,m,n)
is the orientation-preserving subgroup of index
2
in
\Delta
consisting of the elements of even word-length in the generators
a
,
b
and
c
. It has a presentation

\Delta^{+}=\langle X,Y,Z\mid X^{m}=Y^{n}=Z^{2}=XYZ=1\rangle

where
X=ab
,
Y=bc
and
Z=(XY)^{-1}=ca
are rotations through
2\pi/m
,
2\pi/n
and
\pi
around
C
,
A
and
B
. The images of the side
AB
, under either of these two groups, form the edges of a map
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}(m,n)
of type
\{m,n\}
on
\mathbb{H}
, which is invariant under both groups: the vertices of
\mathcal{U}
(the images of
A
) have valency
n
, the edges are hyperbolic geodesics, and the faces (each with an image of
C
at its midpoint) are hyperbolic
m
-gons. The tessellation of
\mathbb{H}
by the images of
T
is the barycentric subdivision of
\mathcal{U}
.

If
K
is a torsion-free normal subgroup of
\Delta
then
\mathcal{U}/K
is a map
\mathcal{M}
of type
\{m,n\}
on the surface
\mathbb{H}/K
. This map
\mathcal{M}
is regular, in the sense that its automorphism group
\rm{Aut}\,\mathcal{M}
, isomorphic to
\Delta/K
, acts transitively on its vertex-edge-face flags. If
K\leq\Delta^{+}
then
\mathbb{H}/K
is a Riemann surface and
\mathcal{M}
is orientable, whereas if
K\not\leq\Delta^{+}
then
\mathbb{H}/K
is a Klein surface without boundary, and
\mathcal{M}
is non-orientable. If we merely assume that
K
is normal in
\Delta^{+}
, rather than
\Delta
, then
\mathcal{M}
is orientably regular, meaning that its orientation-preserving automorphism group
\rm{Aut}^{+}\mathcal{M}
, isomorphic to
\Delta^{+}/K
, acts transitively on its arcs (directed edges); then
\mathcal{M}
is (fully) regular if
K
is normal in
\Delta
, but if not then
K
is one of a pair of normal subgroups of
\Delta^{+}
, transposed by conjugation in
\Delta
and corresponding to a chiral (mirror-image) pair of orientably regular maps.

Conversely, if
\mathcal{M}
is any regular map
\mathcal{M}
of type
\{m,n\}
then
\rm{Aut}\,\mathcal{M}
is generated by three reflections
a^{\prime}
,
b^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime}
changing the
i
-dimensional component of a specific flag of
\mathcal{M}
for
i=0
,
1
and
2
, while preserving the other two components. These generators satisfy all the defining relations of
\Delta
, and their products have the same orders, so there is an epimorphism
\Delta\to\rm{Aut}\,\mathcal{M}
with a torsion-free kernel
K
, and
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathcal{U}/K
with
\rm{Aut}\,\mathcal{M}\cong\Delta/K
. Thus one can regard
\mathcal{U}
as the universal map of type
\{m,n\}
, in the sense that every regular map of this type is isomorphic to a quotient of it.
(In fact, this applies to
every
map of this type, regular or not, since every map is a quotient of a regular map of the same type, by some group of automorphisms; however, we will not need this full generality here.) The underlying surface
\mathbb{H}/K
of
\mathcal{M}
is compact if and only if
K
has finite index in
\Delta
, as we will assume throughout this paper.

There is an obvious analogue of the preceding paragraph for orientably regular maps, with the orientation-preserving automorphism group
\rm{Aut}^{+}\mathcal{M}
replacing
\rm{Aut}\,\mathcal{M}
, and
\Delta^{+}
replacing
\Delta
.

All of the statements in this section extend to the case where
\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{n}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, provided one replaces
\mathbb{H}
with the complex plane in the case of equality, or the Riemann sphere in the case of strict inequality. However, the resulting Riemann and Klein surfaces, together with the regular maps on them, are so well known and understood that we will avoid them here.
Similarly, one can extend these statements to arbitrary cocompact triangle groups
\Delta
, without the restriction that
(ca)^{2}=1
, provided one replaces maps with hypermaps, surface embeddings of hypergraphs. Here we will restrict our attention to maps, though extending some of our results to hypermaps would be an interesting project.

3
Hurwitz surfaces, groups and maps

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a compact Riemann surface of genus
g\geq 2
. Hurwitz’s upper bound
|G|\leq 84(g-1)
for
G={\rm Aut}\,\mathcal{S}
is attained if and only if
\mathcal{S}\cong\mathbb{H}/K
, where
\mathbb{H}
is the hyperbolic plane and
K
is a proper normal subgroup of finite index in the (orientation-preserving) triangle group

\Delta^{+}=\Delta(2,3,7)=\langle X,Y\mid X^{3}=Y^{7}=Z^{2}=XYZ=1\rangle,

in which case
G\cong\Delta/K
. Since
2
,
3
and
7
are distinct primes, such a subgroup
K
is automatically torsion-free, and is therefore a surface group of genus
g
.
It follows that a non-trivial finite group
G
is a Hurwitz group (that is, it attains the Hurwitz bound for some surface
\mathcal{S}
) if and only if it has generators
x
,
y
and
z
(the images of
X
,
Y
and
Z
) satisfying

x^{3}=y^{7}=z^{2}=xyz=1.

(See
[
4
,
6
]
for comprehensive surveys on Hurwitz groups.)

For any such group
G
there is a corresponding Hurwitz map
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{U}/K
of type
\{3,7\}
on
\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}(3,7)
. This map is orientably regular, with orientation-preserving automorphism group
\rm{Aut}^{+}\mathcal{M}\cong

G
, and is fully regular if and only if
K
is normal in the extended triangle group
\Delta
of the same type, which contains
\Delta^{+}
with index
2
, or equivalently
G
has an automorphism
\alpha
(induced by conjugation by
a
,
b
or
c
) inverting two of the generators
x
,
y
and
z
.
In this case
\mathcal{M}
has full automorphism group
{\rm Aut}\,\mathcal{M}\cong\Delta/K
, containing
{\rm Aut}^{+}\mathcal{M}
with index
2
, and the elements of
{\rm Aut}\,\mathcal{M}\setminus{\rm Aut}^{+}\mathcal{M}
induce anticonformal automorphisms of
\mathcal{S}
.

Every Hurwitz group is perfect, since
\Delta
is, so it covers a non-abelian finite simple group, which is itself a Hurwitz group.
Theorem
1.1
, due to Macbeath, gives an important infinite class of simple Hurwitz groups
G={\rm PSL}_{2}(q)
.

Singerman
[
29
]
has shown that if
q
is any prime power then each pair of generators of
{\rm PSL}_{2}(q)
are simultaneously inverted by some automorphism
\alpha
. It follows that the orientably regular Macbeath–Hurwitz maps
\mathcal{M}
corresponding to the surfaces
\mathcal{S}
in Theorem
1.1
are all fully regular. Moreover, since
{\rm Aut}\,{\rm PSL}_{2}(q)={\rm P\Gamma L}_{2}(q)
, with
q
an odd power of
p
in each case, it follows that the full automorphism group
{\rm Aut}\,\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{M}
must be either
G\times{\rm C}_{2}
or
{\rm PGL}_{2}(q)
, as the automorphism
\alpha
of
G
is respectively inner or outer (this is independent of which pair of generators
x
,
y
or
z
it inverts).

More generally, we will call
any
fully regular orientable map, such as
\mathcal{M}
,
inner
or
outer regular
in these two cases.
If
\mathcal{M}
is inner regular, it has a non-orientable quotient
{\mathcal{N}}=\mathcal{M}/{\rm C}_{2}
with
{\rm Aut}\,{\mathcal{N}}\cong G
, and
\mathcal{M}
is isomorphic to the canonical orientable double cover of
\mathcal{N}
. Conversely, every non-orientable regular map
\mathcal{N}
with automorphism group
G
has an orientable double cover
\mathcal{M}
with orientation-preserving automorphism group
G
and full automorphism group
G\times{\rm C}_{2}
, so that
\mathcal{M}
is inner regular. This gives a bijection between inner regular maps
\mathcal{M}
and non-orientable regular maps
\mathcal{N}
.

In this bijection, if
\mathcal{M}
has genus
g
and extended type
\{m,n\}_{l}
(meaning that it has type
\{m,n\}
and Petrie length
l
), then
{\mathcal{N}}
has genus
g+1
and extended type
\{m,n\}_{l^{\prime}}
, where
l^{\prime}={\rm lcm}\{l,2\}
. Given
\mathcal{M}
, this information about
\mathcal{N}
is often sufficient to identify it uniquely in the catalogues, as we will do without further comment in many of our examples. However, there are a few cases where further work is required, and here one can use a MAGMA computation by Conder
[
7
]
, which identifies the orientable double covers of all 9982 non-orientable regular maps of genus up to 1502. (Moreover, this list provides a check on all pairings which have been found ‘by hand’ as above.)

In particular, if the map
\mathcal{M}
of type
\{3,7\}
and genus
g
corresponding to a Hurwitz group
G
is inner regular, then its non-orientable quotient
\mathcal{N}
, having the same type and having genus
g^{\prime}=g+1
, has automorphism group
G
attaining the upper bound
84(g^{\prime}-2)
for the number of automorphisms of a non-orientable Klein surface or regular map of genus
g^{\prime}
, so that
G
is an
H^{*}
-group. Conversely, all
H^{*}
-groups, together with their associated non-orientable maps and Klein surfaces, arise in this way from inner regular Hurwitz groups, acting on their orientable double covers.

Warning
Not all Hurwitz maps are fully regular: for instance, all of those associated with the ‘small’ Ree groups

Wendy Hall

is inner regular if and only if

is a square in

of order

corresponding to

has trace

(see Theorem

). We will prove a stronger form of this result, namely Theorem

Example 1
If
q=7
then

4
Inner and outer regularity

In cases (2) and (3) of Theorem
1.1
the possible values of

are

t_{j}=t_{-j}:=\zeta^{j}+\zeta^{-j}\quad(j=1,2,3),

where
\zeta
is a primitive
7
th root of unity, contained in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
or
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
as

(7)
respectively. These traces

correspond to three Hurwitz maps

; these are isomorphic in case (3), where

induces automorphisms of

permuting the three conjugacy classes of elements of order

transitively, whereas they are mutually non-isomorphic in case (2), where no such automorphisms exist.

Simple algebra shows that the symmetric functions of the traces

are

t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3}=-1,\quad t_{1}t_{2}+t_{2}t_{3}+t_{3}t_{1}=-2\quad\hbox{and}\quad t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}=1,

so these traces are the roots in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
of the polynomial

f_{0}(t):=t^{3}+t^{2}-2t-1.

(1)

Let us now define
s_{j}:=3-t_{j}^{2}
, so that
\mathcal{M}_{j}
is inner or outer regular as

is or is not a square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Note that

s_{j}=3-t_{j}^{2}=3-(\zeta^{j}+\zeta^{-j})^{2}=1-(\zeta^{2j}+\zeta^{-2j})=1-t_{2j},

with subscripts taken mod
(7)
. The transformation

induces a permutation of the three traces

. It follows that the symmetric functions of the parameters

s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}=4,\quad s_{1}s_{2}+s_{2}s_{3}+s_{3}s_{1}=3\quad\hbox{and}\quad s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}=-1,

so these parameters are the roots in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
of the polynomial

f_{1}(s)=s^{3}-4s^{2}+3s+1.

(2)

(Equivalently, we could define

, with a minus sign to make this polynomial monic.)

Now let

, an epimorphism

taking the values

\chi(s_{1})\chi(s_{2})\chi(s_{3})=\chi(-1)=\pm 1\quad\hbox{as}\quad q\equiv\pm 1\;{\rm mod}~(4).

Thus an odd or even number of the maps

are inner regular as

or
-1
mod
(4)
. In case (3) these three maps are isomorphic, so they have the same regularity properties. We have therefore proved the following:

Proposition 4.1

(Theorem 2.10 of
[
14
]
)

In case (2), if
p\equiv 1

mod

(4)

In case (3) the unique map

is inner or outer regular as

The first part of this result is a reformulation of Theorem
1.4

5
Density theorems

This section will explain how the relative densities stated in Theorem
1.3
can be obtained from the density theorems of Frobenius and of Chebotarev (see
[
22
]
for a very readable account of the life and work of the latter, and the relationship between these two theorems).

A root
s_{j}
of
f_{1}(s)
is a square
s_{j}=a_{j}^{2}
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
if and only if
a_{j}
(together with
-a_{j}
) is a root of the polynomial

f_{2}(x):=f_{1}(x^{2})=x^{6}-4x^{4}+3x^{2}+1

(3)

in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, or equivalently,
f_{2}(x)
has a pair of linear factors
x\pm a_{j}
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}[x]
. The number
k_{p}
of inner regular maps
\mathcal{M}_{j}
for each
p
is therefore twice the number of linear factors of
f_{2}(x)
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}[x]
.

Example 5
If
p=13
the factorisation is

f_{2}(x)=(x-2)(x+2)(x^{2}-6)(x^{2}-7),

with a pair of linear factors
x\pm 2
corresponding to the root
s_{j}=4=(\pm 2)^{2}
of
f_{1}
and thus to the unique inner regular map
\mathcal{M}_{j}
, and two irreducible quadratic factors corresponding to the non-square roots

and
7
and thus to the two outer regular maps.

Any polynomial
f(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
, when reduced mod
(p)
for some prime
p
, has a factorisation as a product of irreducible polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
. If
f
is monic then the degrees of these factors form a partition
\pi
of its degree
d=\deg(f)
, which depends on
p
. If the discriminant of
f
is non-zero, then
f
has
d
simple roots, permuted faithfully by its Galois group as a subgroup of the symmetric group
{\rm S}_{d}
. Frobenius’s theorem
[
13
]
(proved, according to
[
22
]
, in 1880, though not published until 1896) states that the density of the set of primes giving a particular partition
\pi
is equal to the proportion of elements of the Galois group which have cycle-lengths (as permutations of the roots) giving the partition
\pi
.

Before proceeding, we need to explain more precisely what we mean here by density.
Let
\Pi
denote the set of all prime numbers, and let us define

\Pi^{\prime}=\{p\in\Pi\mid p\equiv\pm 1\,{\rm mod}~(7)\},

\Pi^{\prime\prime}=\{p\in\Pi\mid p\equiv\pm 2\;\hbox{or}\;\pm 3\,{\rm mod}~(7)\}.

Thus
\Pi
has a partition

\Pi=\{7\}\,\dot{\cup}\,\Pi^{\prime}\,\dot{\cup}\,\Pi^{\prime\prime}

corresponding to the three cases in Theorem
1.1
.
We will say that a subset
\Sigma\subseteq\Pi
has
natural density

d
(within
\Pi
) if

\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{|\Sigma\cap[0,x]|}{|\Pi\cap[0,x]|}=d.

For example, it follows from the quantified form of Dirichlet’s theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions that
\Pi^{\prime}
and
\Pi^{\prime\prime}
have natural densities
2/\varphi(7)=1/3
and
4/\varphi(7)=2/3
.
We will say that a subset
\Sigma\subseteq\Pi^{\prime}
has
relative density

d^{\prime}
(within
\Pi^{\prime}
) if

\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{|\Sigma\cap[0,x]|}{|\Pi^{\prime}\cap[0,x]|}=d^{\prime}.

Then
d^{\prime}=3d
if these densities exist.

Example 6
An irreducible cubic polynomial in
{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
has Galois group
{\rm A}_{3}
or
{\rm S}_{3}
as its discriminant is or is not a square. A general cubic

has discriminant
b^{2}c^{2}-4ac^{3}-4b^{3}d-27a^{2}d^{2}+18abcd
, so the polynomial
f_{1}(x)=x^{3}-4x^{2}+3x+1
in (

) has discriminant
49=7^{2}
; it therefore has Galois group
G={\rm A}_{3}\cong{\rm C}_{3}
, permuting the three complex (in fact real) roots regularly. One third of the elements of

(namely the identity) have cycle structure
1^{3}
, so the primes for which
f_{1}
splits into linear factors (i.e. has three roots in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
) have density
1/3
; these are, of course, the primes
p\equiv\pm 1
mod
(7)
forming
\Pi^{\prime}
, with roots

. The other two elements of

is irreducible have density

; these are the primes

forming
\Pi^{\prime\prime}
. (If

Chebotarev’s theorem
[
1

2

refines that of Frobenius by associating sets of primes, not with cycle-structures but with specific conjugacy classes in the Galois group, a finer distinction. (Of course, conjugate elements have the same cycle-structure, but the converse may fail.) It also considers arbitrary Galois extensions of algebraic number fields, not just extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
. Without going into the general theory (see
[
22
]
for this), it is sufficient here to explain how it works in our particular situation.

The splitting field of

An alternative approach

An alternative way of restricting attention to primes

is to consider the Galois group of
f_{2}
, not over
\mathbb{Q}
, but over
K_{1}
, where it has the factorisation

f_{2}(x)=(x^{2}-\sigma_{1})(x^{2}-\sigma_{2})(x^{2}-\sigma_{3}).

(4)

The motivation for this is that since the fields
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
appearing in case (2), with
p\equiv\pm 1
mod
(7)
, all contain the traces
t_{j}=\zeta^{j}+\zeta^{-j}
of the elements of order
7
in
{\rm PSL}_{2}(p)
, it is appropriate to regard
f_{2}
as a polynomial, not over
\mathbb{Q}
but over the field
K_{1}
generated by the traces
\tau_{j}=\zeta_{7}^{j}+\zeta_{7}^{-j}
in
\mathbb{C}
, or equivalently by the roots
\sigma_{j}
of

Now the triangle group
\Delta^{+}
is an arithmetic group, defined over

.
In the ring
{\mathcal{O}}={\mathbb{Z}}[2\cos 2\pi/7]
of integers of this field, rational primes

generates a product of three prime ideals

(

. (The prime

ramifies in

, so its quotient has nilpotent elements and is not a field; for instance

, so

[

by factoring out its principle congruence subgroups corresponding to these prime ideals

and

; see

As a polynomial in

In the group

or

,

Problem
Can one use this approach to be more specific about which primes are in each set

9
Macbeath maps of type
\{3,n\}

In the rest of this paper we will consider how much of this work extends to the groups, surfaces and maps where the latter have type
\{3,n\}
for some
n\geq 7
. We will therefore redefine

\Delta^{+}:=\Delta(2,3,n)=\langle X,Y,Z\mid X^{3}=Y^{n}=Z^{2}=XYZ=1\rangle

from now on. The genus
g
of such an orientably regular map
\mathcal{M}
, with
G=\rm{Aut}^{+}\mathcal{M}
, is given by

|G|=\frac{12n}{n-6}(g-1),

so when
G={\rm PSL}_{2}(q)
, as we will continue to assume, we have

g=1+\frac{n-6}{12n}\cdot\frac{q(q^{2}-1)}{\gcd(q,2)}

=1+\frac{(n-6)\cdot q(q^{2}-1)}{24n}\quad\hbox{or}\quad 1+\frac{(n-6)\cdot q(q^{2}-1)}{12n}

as
q
is odd or even.

Let us assume that
p
is coprime to

. Then
G
is a smooth quotient
\Delta^{+}/K
of
\Delta^{+}
if and only if
q=p^{d}
where

is the multiplicative order of
p
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}^{*}/\{\pm 1\}
. There are
\varphi(n)/2
conjugacy classes of elements

of order

in
G
, each of them giving, via a generating triple
x
,
y
,

, an orientably regular map
\mathcal{M}
of type
\{3,n\}
, which we call a Macbeath map. Two such maps are isomorphic if and only if the traces of their canonical generators

are conjugate under
{\rm Gal}\,\mathbb{F}_{q}\cong{\rm C}_{d}
, so we obtain
\varphi(n)/2d
isomorphism classes of maps, and also of Riemann surfaces
\mathcal{S}
. (We have
{\rm Aut}\,\mathcal{S}=G
since
\Delta^{+}=\Delta(2,3,n)
is finitely maximal, see
[
28
]
, so that it is the normaliser of
K
in
\rm{PSL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.) By Singerman’s result on inverting generating pairs for
G
, these maps are all fully regular, with full automorphism group
G\times{\rm C}_{2}
or
{\rm PGL}_{2}(q)
as the automorphism of
G
inverting two of the canonical generators

,
y
,
z
is inner or outer. In the next section we will prove Theorem

, that these two cases, inner and outer regularity, arise as

is or is not a square in

, where

.

Extension of Hall’s Criterion

Here we summarise Hall’s proof of Theorem
1.2
in
[
14
]
, omitting any statements (explicit or implicit) that
n=7
since these are never essential to the argument. This means that the proof in fact applies to Macbeath maps of type
\{3,n\}
for all
n\geq 7
, as claimed in Theorem
1.6
. In some places we have changed Hall’s notation to make it consistent with that in the rest of this paper.

Identifying each matrix in
{\rm SL}_{2}(q)
with its negative, as usual, let

z=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\hbox{and}\quad x=\begin{pmatrix}r&s\\
u&v\end{pmatrix}

be generators of
G={\rm PSL}_{2}(q)
of orders
2
and
3
, with
y=(zx)^{-1}
of order
n
. (Since all involutions are conjugate in
G
, we may assume that
z
has this form.) By Singerman’s result (Hall proves this by a direct construction) there is a (unique) involution
w\in{\rm PGL}_{2}(q)
which, acting by conjugation, inverts
z
and
x
, that is,
w
,
wz
and
wx
are involutions, or equivalently they have trace
0
. If we write
w=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}
the first and second trace conditions give
\alpha+\delta=-\beta+\gamma=0
, so that
w=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta&-\alpha\end{pmatrix}
. The corresponding map
\mathcal{M}
is inner regular if and only if
w\in{\rm PSL}_{2}(q)
, or equivalently
\det w=-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}
is a (necessarily non-zero) square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.

The third trace condition gives

\alpha r+\beta u+\beta s-\alpha v=0
, so that

\alpha^{2}(r-v)^{2}=\beta^{2}(s+u)^{2}.

(5)

(Squaring here introduces a second involution
w^{\prime}
, which inverts
z
but not
y
; however, everything which follows applies to
w
.)
Adding
\beta^{2}(r-v)^{2}
to each side in (
5
), and then expanding the squares on the right-hand side gives

(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})(r-v)^{2}=\beta^{2}(r^{2}+s^{2}+u^{2}+v^{2}-2)

(6)

since
rv-su=\det x=1
.

Macbeath
[
26
]
showed that if any pair
z
and
x
generate
{\rm PSL}_{2}(q)
, then the quadratic form

Q(\xi,\eta,\zeta)=\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}+\zeta^{2}+a\eta\zeta+b\zeta\xi+c\xi\eta,

where
a,b,c
are the traces of
z
,
x
and
zx
,
is nonsingular, which implies that the determinant

\det\begin{pmatrix}1&c/2&b/2\\
c/2&1&a/2\\
b/2&a/2&1\\
\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{4}(4+abc-a^{2}-b^{2}-c^{2})

(7)

is non-zero. In our case we have
a=0
,
b=r+v
and
c=u-s
, and from this it follows that
r^{2}+s^{2}+u^{2}+v^{2}\neq 2
. Thus the second factor on the right-hand side of (
6
) is non-zero.

Now
(r+v)^{2}={\rm tr}^{2}\,x=1
and
(u-s)^{2}={\rm tr}^{2}\,zx=t^{2}
, so adding and using
\det x=1
again gives

1+t^{2}=r^{2}+v^{2}+2rv+u^{2}+s^{2}-2su=r^{2}+s^{2}+u^{2}+v^{2}+2.

and hence

t^{2}-3=r^{2}+s^{2}+u^{2}+v^{2}-2,

with both sides non-zero, as shown above. Substituting this in the right-hand side of (
6
) gives

(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})(r-v)^{2}=\beta^{2}(t^{2}-3).

(8)

Provided both sides of (
8
) are non-zero (a possibility not considered in
[
14
]
), we see that
-(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})
is a square if and only if
3-t^{2}
is, as required.

What if both sides of (
8
) are zero? Clearly
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=-\det w\neq 0
, and we saw above that
t^{2}-3\neq 0
, so the only possibility is that
\beta=0
and
r=v
. Then
\det w=-\alpha^{2}
is a square if and only if
-1
is. If
r=v
then since
{\rm tr}\,x=1
we have
r=v=1/2
and hence
su=rv-\det y=-3/4
, while
-s+u={\rm tr}\,zx=t
, so
-s
and
u
are the roots of the quadratic polynomial
T^{2}-tT+3/4
, the discriminant
t^{2}-3
of which must be a square. Thus
3-t^{2}
is a square if and only if
-1
is, the same condition as for
\det w
to be a square.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
, and also, since the condition that
n=7
has not been used, that of Theorem
1.6
.

\square

Generalisation to other types

One could ask similar questions about the maps or hypermaps of other types besides those considered here. In this case the approach used in Section
8
meets the difficulty that, by a theorem of Takeuchi
[
31
]
, triangle groups of only finitely many types are arithmetic groups. However, the approach used in Sections
6
and
7
can be transferred from maps of type
\{3,n\}
to those of type
\{m,n\}
for any fixed
m>3
. The general principles are the same as for
m=3
, so here we will just outline a short illustrative example.

Hall’s Criterion has recently been generalised by Conder, Potočnik and Širáň in
[
9
]
to apply to orientably regular hypermaps
\mathcal{H}
of arbitrary hyperbolic type
(k,l,m)
with orientation-preserving automorphism group
{\rm PSL}_{2}(q)
: the necessary and sufficient condition for the inner regularity (stated equivalently for the existence of a non-orientable regular hypermap) is that

-D:=4+\omega_{\kappa}\omega_{\lambda}\omega_{\mu}-\omega_{\kappa}^{2}-\omega_{\lambda}^{2}-\omega_{\mu}^{2}

should be a square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, where
\omega_{\kappa}
,
\omega_{\lambda}
and
\omega_{\mu}
are the traces of the standard generators, rotations of
\mathcal{H}
through
2\pi/k
,
2\pi/l
and
2\pi/m
around a hypervertex, hyperedge and hyperface. (This is the factor on the right-hand side of (
7
), in different notation,) The Macbeath maps of type
\{3,n\}
can be regarded as hypermaps of type
(n,2,3)
, so that
\omega_{\lambda}=0
,
\omega_{\mu}=\pm 1
and
-D=3-\omega_{\kappa}^{2}=3-t^{2}
as in the extension of Hall’s Criterion.

As an example of this generalisation, let
m=4
, so that we consider maps
\mathcal{M}
of type
\{4,n\}
. The group
G={\rm PSL}_{2}(q)
has elements of this order if and only if
q\equiv\pm 1
mod
(8)
, as we will assume throughout this section. Elements of order
4
have trace
\pm\sqrt{2}
, so that
\mathcal{M}
, of type
\{m,n\}
and with
{\rm{Aut}^{+}\mathcal{M}}=G
, is inner regular if and only if
s:=2-t^{2}
is a square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, where
t
is the trace of the canonical generator
y
of order
n
. To have hyperbolic types we require
n\geq 5
, so for simplicity let us take
n=5
, so that
\mathcal{M}
has genus
1+|G|/40
. We obtain maps
\mathcal{M}
of type
\{4,5\}
with
{\rm Aut}^{+}\mathcal{M}\cong G
if and only if either
q=p\equiv\pm 1
or
\pm 9
mod
(40)
, with two maps, or
q=p^{2}
where
p\equiv\pm 7
or
\pm 17
mod
(40)
, with one map (more precisely, two isomorphic maps). The traces of elements of order
5
are the roots
t_{j}=(-1\pm\sqrt{5})/2
of
f_{0}(t)=t^{2}+t-1
, and the corresponding elements
s_{j}
are the roots
2-t_{j}^{2}=t_{j}+1=(1\pm\sqrt{5})/2
of
f_{1}(s)=s^{2}-s-1
. Since

Other generalisations

In addition to considering the problem raised at the end of Sections
7
and
8
, of characterising the primes in each set
\Sigma_{k}
, either for
n=7
or for arbitrary
n
, one can generalise the problem addressed in this paper in other ways.

Building on joint work on maps with Mačaj and Širáň in
[
17
]
, a similar approach has recently been used with the same authors in
[
18
]
to show that for each hyperbolic triple there exist infinitely many non-orientable regular hypermaps of that type. (This is significantly harder to prove than the corresponding result for orientable hypermaps, which is an easy consequence of the residual finiteness of triangle groups.) The hypermaps constructed have automorphism group
{\rm PSL}_{2}(p)
for sets of primes
p
of positive density, those where the inner regularity of an orientably regular hypermap yields a non-orientable hypermap of the same type and with the same group. This approach is both more general than that in the present paper, in that it applies to hypermaps of every hyperbolic type, but also less general in that it applies only in the case
q=p
and that it does not yield exact or relative densities. Thus the two papers are similar but complementary.

Alternatively, one could consider other families of Hurwitz groups. For example, Conder
[
3
]
has proved that all but finitely many alternating groups
{\rm A}_{n}
are Hurwitz groups. Many (perhaps most) of the associated Hurwitz maps are chiral, rather than fully regular, but it still makes sense to call the latter inner or outer regular as the full automorphism group is
{\rm A}_{n}\times{\rm C}_{2}
or the symmetric group
{\rm S}_{n}
(the only possibilities), and to consider the Klein surfaces appearing in the inner regular cases. For instance, Etayo and Martínez
[
11
]
have shown that, of the five smallest alternating groups which are Hurwitz groups, namely those with
n=15
,
21
,
22
,
28
and
29
, only
{\rm A}_{15}
and
{\rm A}_{28}
also arise as
H^{*}
-groups. The rapidly increasing size of the alternating groups, together with their lack of any simple representation over a finite field, makes this a much harder problem than that for the Macbeath-Hurwitz groups considered here.
