Barriers and facilitators of sports participation among rural-dwelling, Hispanic girls in southwestern United States: a qualitative study
Authors: Ashleigh Johnson, Jason Bocarro, Emily Kroshus, Shiloh Williams, Elva Arredondo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLess than one third of American children meet physical activity guidelines, with well-established disparities by gender. Sports are one of the best investments for promoting youth physical activity, but both rural and Hispanic girls participate in lower numbers and enter at a later age than their counterparts. There is a dearth of literature on sport participation factors among Hispanic girls living in rural areas, particularly among those living in border regions. This study aimed to examine sport participation experiences among rural, predominantly Hispanic girls from the perspectives of girls, parents, coaches, and program staff.MethodsData were collected in 2024 using semi-structured interviews with girls (n = 15), parents (n = 18), and coaches/staff (n = 4) in a predominantly rural, Hispanic county in Southern California, United States. The Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and the ecological-based Framework for Understanding Youth Sports Participation (FUYSP) guided analysis. The research team used an iteratively developed codebook to code, thematically analyze, and synthesize the data.ResultsSport participation factors were found at multiple levels of FUYSP influence, including the public policy/awareness (professional role models), community (access to sports programming, gender expectations, economic challenges, hot weather, shared value in supporting youth), organization (resources, high cost to families), interpersonal (perceived benefits of sports participation, quality coaches, lack of time/scheduling, parental support), and individual (attitudes and beliefs, personal growth, perceived competence, social connections) levels. SDT-related factors (e.g., competence, social connections) were incorporated into the individual level of FUYSP.ConclusionsFindings highlight the intersectionality of geographic, gender-based, and cultural factors associated with rural, Hispanic girls’ sports participation. Efforts to promote positive, sustained sports participation among this population should focus on understanding and addressing unique community needs while maintaining a community-wide approach that galvanizes existing resources and support. Increasing access to youth sports can provide additional opportunities for physical activity; address disparities in physical activity levels by gender, ethnicity, SES, and geography; and subsequently improve overall health among this population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25686-6.Keywords: Sports, Hispanic americans, Child, Female, Rural population, Qualitative research, Socioeconomic factors, Self-determination theory

Background

Despite the numerous physical and mental health benefits [1], less than 30% of elementary-aged youth in the United States (US) meet aerobic physical activity (PA) guidelines of ≥ 60 min/day of moderate-to-vigorous intensity PA [2]. There are well-established disparities in PA by gender, with lower PA levels consistently found among girls versus boys [2]. There is also some evidence of disparities in PA by urbanicity, with lower activity levels among rural versus urban and/or suburban youth [3–7], including within border regions (e.g., US-Mexico border) [8].

Sport is one of the best investments for promoting youth PA [9], and youth who participate in sport are 64% more likely to meet PA guidelines [10]. Sport participation provides additional psychological benefits including improvements in self-esteem and a reduced risk of depression [11]. Still, < 40% of youth aged 6–12 play sport regularly [12], and gender, geographic, and ethnic disparities persist. Girls regularly play sports at a lower rate than boys [12], and both rural and Hispanic girls participate in lower numbers and enter at a later age than their counterparts [2, 13, 14]. One study among 281 youth ages 8–12 living in rural Midwestern US communities showed that non-Hispanic White children were over five times more likely to participate in youth sports than their Hispanic peers [15].

Lack of competence, autonomy, and relatedness are commonly cited barriers to sports participation among youth more broadly [16–20], in line with the Self-Determination Theory (SDT) [21]. However, there is a dearth of literature on sport participation factors among Hispanic girls living in rural areas, particularly among those living in border regions. An important step in reducing gender, geographic, and ethnic disparities in sports participation and PA is to identify determinants that may increase this population’s early and sustained sport engagement.

Geographic differences in sport-based opportunities [22], and disparities in sport participation by gender, ethnicity, and urbanicity [23, 24], suggest that Hispanic girls living in rural areas – including coaches and caregivers – may face unique challenges around sport that should be identified and addressed in conjunction with well-established sport participation determinants [25]. Sport participation and PA levels dramatically decline by age among female youth in the US [14, 26]. Targeting younger girls leads to more enduring sport involvement [14, 20], and evidence suggests that the prepubescent period represents an age when preferences and motivation for PA and sport are established [27, 28]. However, most studies examining gender-specific sport barriers have been conducted among adolescent (versus elementary-aged) girls, making it difficult to support positive sport experiences in younger children (e.g., ages 8–10) [29]. Further, there remains a paucity of information on the unique determinants of sport participation and cultural needs among rural, Hispanic girls. Identifying and addressing sport participation barriers among this population can help to further inform and culturally tailor interventions designed to promote positive behavior change around PA and sport.

To address these gaps, we conducted an exploratory qualitative study among community members living in a rural, predominantly Hispanic county in Southern California, US. The purpose of this study was to examine sport participation experiences among rural girls from the perspectives of girls, parents, coaches, and out-of-school program staff, interpreted through the lens of the SDT and FUYSP.

Methods

We conducted a qualitative case study in Southern California from July - October 2024, informed by the SDT and FUYSP [21, 30], and grounded in the constructivist/interpretivist paradigm [31]. Individual, semi-structured interviews were conducted with youth, parents, coaches, and out-of-school program staff. The research team included two female public health researchers (AMJ, EK), a female health behavior researcher (EA), a male youth sports researcher (JB), and three female research assistants (DR, ASO, JCC). AMJ, ASO, DR, and JCC collected data. Data were analyzed by AMJ, ASO, and DR. JCC, ASO, and DR were trained in qualitative interviewing, codebook development, coding, and/or synthesis by AMJ. Interviewers and interviewees were culturally and ethnically congruent as much as possible. Team members had experience with sport culture as athletes and coaches, and there was a representative from the community involved with data collection and analysis.

Setting and recruitment

This study was conducted in California, US in a predominantly rural county by land area as defined by the US Department of Agriculture Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes [32, 33]. RUCA codes incorporate the Bureau of Census Urbanized Area and Urban Cluster definitions and work commuting information to characterize rural-urban status at the census tract and zip code levels [32, 33]. RUCA codes 4–10 are considered rural [34]. This county has a population of ~ 180,000. About 29% are < 18 years old, 85% identify as Hispanic/Latino, and 75% speak a language other than English at home [35]. The median household income was $60,243 in 2023 [35].

A purposeful sample of girls, parents, coaches, and program staff were recruited in-person, via email, and/or through social media and interviewed by phone or Zoom. In-person recruitment took place at two out-of-school youth organizations in the community, including a community recreation center, by members of the research team and through the social media platforms Facebook and X. Individuals who expressed interest in the study during the in-person recruitment events were then contacted via email. Interviews were conducted in English or Spanish, as preferred by participants. Interviews lasted 10–77 min, with most lasting 15–30 min. There were 15 girls (10 English-speaking; 5 Spanish-speaking), 18 parents (8 English-speaking; 10 Spanish-speaking), and 4 stakeholders (3 coaches, 1 out-of-school youth organization staff member – all English-speaking) that were interviewed and compensated with a $50 gift card. The initial sample estimates achieved data sufficiency, so sample size did not change after an early transcript review. To be eligible, girls needed to be aged 8–10 years, parents had to have a daughter aged 8–10 years, and coaches and staff needed to have experience developing or providing youth sport programs to girls ages 8–10. All participants also needed to live (youth, parents) or work (coaches, staff) in the county of interest.

This report conforms to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (Supplement 1) [36]. This study was approved by the San Diego State University Institutional Review Board (HS-2023-0186) and is part of a larger study that is registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06229457; Registered January 2024). Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their parents (for children under 16) and assent was obtained from participants under 16. Clinical trial number is not applicable.

Theoretical framework

The US Department of Health and Human Services (USDHHS) created an ecological framework to organize determinants of youth sports participation at multiple levels called the Framework for Understanding Youth Sport Participation (FUYSP, Fig. 1) [30]. This framework highlights determinants of youth sports participation at different levels including individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and public policy/awareness. The individual level focuses on four primary factors that affect youth participation, including awareness, knowledge, and beliefs; sport sampling; physical literacy; and personal growth. The interpersonal level considers factors related to adults (e.g., parents, coaches) that interact directly with youth sport participants, such as positive role modeling, knowledge and beliefs, and quality coaching skills. The third framework level – organization – includes organizational factors such as funding, coach training, transportation, and quality sports programming. The community level considers factors that may affect community sport activities, such as access to play spaces, beliefs, and resources. Finally, the public policy/awareness level considers both sport-related legislation and policy as well as awareness and other media (e.g., famous athletes promoting health messaging).

Data collection

Data were collected July–October 2024. We developed interview guides for each interview type (parent, child, coach, staff). Interview guides were developed according to study goals and adjusted as necessary throughout data collection as per standard qualitative methodology [37]. An overview of interview guide topics are presented in Supplement 2. Interview guide topics focused on the individual, interpersonal, organizational, and community levels of the USDHHS framework. Finalized interview guides were professionally translated into Spanish. Open-ended questions elicited perspectives on youth experiences in sports, reasons for participating or not, barriers and facilitators of participation, desired sport experience, experiences with rural youth sport programming, and determinants of implementing and sustaining new programming.

Interviews were digitally recorded, professionally transcribed verbatim in the language they were conducted, and spot-checked to ensure data integrity. Audio files were deleted after transcription. Quotes are identified by interview type (youth = Y, parent = P, coach = C, staff = S), language (English = E, Spanish = S), and participant number. Participant confidentiality was prioritized throughout the study.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted December 2024–April 2025. Data were coded in the language in which the interview was conducted. Interview data were uploaded to nVivo Version 14 (Lumivero, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) for coding and thematic analysis following procedures outlined by Braun and Clarke [38–41]. Steps to codebook development included (1) initial codes were derived from the study goals and informed by the SDT and FUYSP [21, 30], (2) codes were augmented by a reading of two transcripts, (3) codes were tested on three additional transcripts by two coders and the codebook was edited until an exhaustive but manageable code list was reached. Transcripts were open-coded, and coders were blind to each other’s coding. Differences were resolved by discussion until 100% agreement was reached. For synthesis, coded excerpts were systematically summarized into themes and subthemes with supporting quotes. SDT-derived codes were incorporated into the individual level of FUYSP.

Participants included 15 students aged 8–11 years (one child had a birthday between recruitment and the interview), 100% female, 100% speaking a language other than English at home, and 100% Hispanic (Table 1). There were 18 parents aged 26–48, 83% female, 89% speaking a language other than English at home, 100% Hispanic. There were 4 stakeholders (coaches, staff) aged 22–54, 100% female, 75% Hispanic. Results were mapped onto the FUYSP (Fig. 2). Themes and subthemes are organized according to the levels of influence: “public policy/awareness”, “community”, “organization”, “interpersonal”, and “individual” factors (Table 2).

Public policy/awareness level

Professional role models emerged as a facilitator to sports at the public policy/awareness level, which includes awareness and other media around sports and physical activity. As one girl (YE012) shared, “A gymnast that I would really like look up to is like Simone Biles”. This professional athlete was commonly identified as a role model whom the girls in this community recognized and admired.

Community level

Community-level participation factors included access to sports programming, perceived gender differences in sport, economic challenges, hot weather, and a shared value of supporting youth. Access to sports programming included subthemes of nearby sport opportunities/convenience, long driving distances, desire for more affordable opportunities, and desire for more opportunities. A facilitator of participation in sports was the availability of nearby sport opportunities and the convenience of participating. For some families, sports opportunities were close to the home (e.g., local recreation center), facilitating participation. For others, there were facilities nearby but physically gaining access was difficult. One mother (PE061) reported having to ‘fight for’ access to local fields:

It went as far as having to have a very strong discussion with our city council people. And then, one parent was on the city council, and they advocated for us, as well, to help us have free access to the parks that were not being used to its fullest.

Parents also reported elements of convenience (e.g., organized staff), as supportive of their children’s involvement in organized sports. Conversely, many parents reported that long driving distances were an inconvenience and major barrier to youth sports participation. Some families lived a long distance from the sports facilities, which made it difficult to drive their children to and from organized sport opportunities.

Overall, parents had a strong desire for more sport opportunities for girls, including more affordable options. Parents wanted improved sport accessibility by having more low-cost options and more variety in community sports programs, as the available sports were often limited to the ‘basic’ options (e.g., volleyball, basketball). Some parents also noted that the sports options were even more limited among girls versus boys within the community. They reported that additional sport options and programming could increase girls’ interest and participation in sports.

Gender expectations emerged as a key community-level theme, including subthemes of cultural norms and desire for all girls’ teams. Some participants shared perceptions around sports being perceived as a more masculine pastime, with one girl describing playing sports as ‘boyish’. One parent (PE061) stated that such perceptions could stem from the influence of Hispanic culture:

We’re a prominently Hispanic community, and I think we still have that. A lot of these children are first-generation American born, American raised. I think we carry a lot of our stubbornness, attitudes in our Hispanic culture of girls don’t do sports; girls don’t rough play.

Still, many girls and parents – who were predominantly female – acknowledged that these perceptions did not necessarily prevent girls’ participation in sports. Many mothers spoke about participating despite these cultural norms. As one mother (PE061) said, ‘There just isn’t a lot of support, sometimes, from parents. But I’ve met other parents like myself, who are girl parents, and it’s like “girl power”.’

One of the subthemes that arose within gender differences is the desire for all girls’ teams to increase sports participation. Parents stated that all-girl teams (versus co-ed) would be beneficial for their daughters because they would feel more confident. Further, they noted that most of the youth sport options in the community were either co-ed (i.e., both boys and girls) or all boys. Few all-girls sport opportunities were reported to exist within the community outside of dance and gymnastics.

Economic challenges were identified as another important community issue that shaped family priorities and capacity to support youth sports participation. Parents noted the broader socioeconomic pressures facing families in the community, where providing basic needs often required working long hours, leaving limited time and resources for their daughter’s extracurricular activities. As one parent (PE074) noted, ‘I think people focus a lot on…making money and providing for their families, ‘cause it’s kind of hard out here.’ This community-wide economic context created an environment where sports participation was often viewed as a luxury rather than a priority.

The hot weather was a commonly cited barrier to youth sports participation. In combination with a lack of indoor facilities, the hot weather in this region made it very difficult for girls to engage in youth sports, particularly during certain times of the year when temperatures could regularly reach 108 °F/42°C. One mother (PE006) shared, ‘We’re living in very hot weather. It’s really hard to be outside and playing for the kids right now.’

Organization level

Resources and high cost to families emerged as themes at the organization level. Resources included subthemes of funding, staff, and material sources and were commonly cited among coaches and program staff as factors associated with their ability to provide quality sports program. Funding, both lack and inconsistency of, were reported to affect program planning and quality. Staffing was another important consideration. Some programs had a lot of support through volunteers – which provided valuable contributions – but leadership acknowledged a difference in working with volunteers compared to staff. Even with employed staff, there was a need to be flexible and prepare for the possibility that staff may be unavailable. As one staff member (SE002) shared, ‘You never know what’s gonna happen that day. Maybe half your staff isn’t able to come in because they’re sick.’

Material resources were another commonly reported factor in program delivery. Staff acknowledged the quality programming that they were still able to provide considering their smaller budget compared to neighboring cities. There were notable challenges, however, with the physical spaces available for organized sports. Staff from one organization acknowledged the value in having access to a gymnasium, but since it was a shared space, other groups had priority. Further, the gymnasium was an older facility and inclement weather would affect classes. One of the coaches (CE006) stated: ‘And that gym, it’s a little old…like, if it’s raining, it will get flooded sometimes. Or you can see like, water dripping. So, we have to cancel classes.’

Beyond the broader economic challenges facing families in the community, the direct costs of organized sports participation – including registration fees, uniforms, and equipment - were commonly cited barriers to sports participation. This was particularly challenging when families had multiple children who were interested in playing sports. One parent (PE040) shared:

There’s things available, it’s just that it’s hard because there’s three [children], and sometimes we try ballet and we try other things, but it was too much when you have three, it was very – very expensive, so we were not able to enroll them.

Interpersonal level

Interpersonal level factors included perceived benefits of sports participation, parental support, quality coaches, and lack of time. Perceived benefits included subthemes of decreasing screen time, physical and mental health, life and social skills, and reducing risk behaviors. Parents had a strong understanding of benefits of youth sports participation, including supporting physical and mental health, decreasing screen time and risk behaviors, and improving life and social skills.

Parental support emerged as an important participation factor, with many parents reporting strong support for their daughter’s participation in sports. However, some parents observed other families who were unable to stay after dropping off their child for practice or games, which was attributed to competing priorities or lack of time. For others, parental support stemmed from their own experiences playing youth sports. One mother (PE059) shared, ‘And I grew up playing sports, so…I can relate. And I know that’s why it’s so important to me for them to stay active and play sports.’

Lack of time emerged as an important barrier to girls’ sports participation, particularly among parents that worked long hours or had multiple children involved in extracurricular activities. In some instances, parents would need to prioritize one child’s participation over the other, often deferring to the child who was involved in more ‘competitive’ sports (e.g., an older brother). Some parents also discussed strategies for addressing this barrier but still noted the challenge it presented. One parent (PE061) stated:

It’s challenging, when everybody has a different activity… my husband works away, so, it’s just myself. And when he’s here, it’s great. Just split kids, and everybody goes away, but it’s quite challenging to get them into different activities, different practice times, and being there for them all.

The theme of quality coaches included subthemes of impact of quality coaches and desire for more female coaches and role models. Parents placed high value in the quality coaches within their community that were knowledgeable, kind, dedicated, and organized. Parents said it was worth the cost when their child could work with a quality coach, particularly over multiple years, and it made it easier to enroll their child in sports.

When parents and youth were asked about what an ‘ideal’ sports program for girls would look like within their community, a common response was a desire for more female coaches in their community to serve as role models. As one parent (PE006) requested:

More female coaches that can coach them and… serve as an example for the girls, that show them…techniques, because it’s different when you have a guy coach…for them feel more comfortable, because, “Oh, this is a lady, she can teach me. I feel more comfortable”.

One girl expressed interest in having more female coaches within sports because she felt safer with female (versus male) coaches. She (YE027) said, ‘I like that the teacher would be girls…I feel more safer with girls.’

Individual level

Attitudes and beliefs, personal growth, perceived competence, and social connection emerged as themes at the individual level. Safety concerns and interest in sports were two subthemes that emerged within girls’ attitudes and beliefs. Some girls expressed concerns about getting hurt, occasionally due to previous injury experiences. However, concerns about safety were not necessarily a deterrent for playing sports but had a role in which sport they played. Girls’ interest in sports and whether they considered it fun was an important participation factor. For some, their interest was sport-dependent (i.e., enjoyed some sports but not others), while others either had an interest (or lacked interest) in sports in general. Interest/having fun was reported to influence girls’ decisions in switching sports, trying new sports, and/or ending participation altogether. In some cases, interest/fun was reportedly influenced by social connections, perceived competence, and potential for personal growth. One girl (YE014), when asked what she liked about playing sports, responded: ‘The challenging part of it and the fun part of it, and just like, staying active.’

Social connections emerged as a key individual-level theme, in line with the SDT (e.g., relatedness). Girls reported that social connections (or lack thereof) often determined their interest in sports. Some girls stopped participating in sports because their friends or siblings left the sport, and others reported that having friends was what they enjoyed most. One girl (YE001) stated, ‘Well, it’s really fun doing the sports because…I can play sports with my brother and I can play with my friends.’

Perceived competence, or an individual’s sense of proficiency, is another key construct within SDT. Competence emerged as a theme within the individual level, with some girls reporting that their inability to participate had caused them to switch sports or to stop playing entirely. One girl (YE016), when asked why she stopped playing soccer, responded: ‘I couldn’t really understand it.’

Discussion

This study examined sport participation experiences among rural, predominantly Hispanic girls from the perspectives of girls, parents, coaches, and program staff, interpreted through the lens of the SDT and FUYSP. Sport participation factors were found at multiple levels of influence, including the public policy/awareness (professional role models); community (programming access, gender differences, economic challenges, hot weather, value in supporting youth); organization (resources, high cost); interpersonal (perceived benefits, quality coaches, lack of time/scheduling, parental support); and individual (attitudes and beliefs, personal growth, perceived competence, social connections) levels.

Access to sports programming was described as an important participation factor. This included considerations for convenience (e.g., distance), participation costs, and program type (e.g., variety, target age and/or gender, competitiveness). Proximity to sports programs acted as a facilitator, particularly for those programs that made it easier for families to participate (e.g., providing information via a website). Conversely, long driving distances were a barrier to accessing sports programming and were often compounded with families’ reported lack of time and scheduling challenges. Long distances to programs have been reported in other rural communities [42], due in part to fewer programming options and sports facilities that are not conveniently located for everyone [17, 43]. Practitioners aiming to address these challenges should consider how to utilize existing facilities (e.g., schools) for sports, and scheduling programming at convenient times (e.g., after-school, weekends). Policy-level approaches may include transportation solutions [44], and encouraging shared use agreements between schools, local government, and community organizations. These have been shown to increase usage of facilities and PA levels [45].

Although families reported some availability of sport options, many desired additional sport opportunities, including more opportunities for girls and sports ‘beyond the basics’. Families reported that local sport opportunities were often limited to more traditional sports or were predominantly available for boys only or co-ed (i.e., boys and girls combined). A lack of sport diversity has been previously shown among girls of racial minority [46], and is suggested to contribute to lower PA levels among this population. A qualitative study based among rural Swedish youth showed similar results to the presented findings, with the limited range of sports in the area (versus lack of sports) being more problematic for participation [47]. A lack of diverse sport opportunities in rural areas likely hinders youths’ ability to engage in sport sampling (i.e., participating in multiple sports), which is recommended for sustained sports participation and a determinant within the FUYSP [30, 48]. However, challenges may arise for communities if there is not a consistent interest in all-girls sports teams, particularly in smaller towns, and may make it difficult to maintain these opportunities. Practitioners can informally introduce diverse sport options and non-traditional sports at low cost through physical education (PE) or afterschool programs. Such efforts can increase interest in sports while supporting sport sampling and engagement in unstructured PA [49].

Another community-level theme was gender expectations for youth sports participation. Gender expectations included sports being perceived as more of a male pastime, and thus girls should avoid sports that involved high levels of physical exertion. One parent attributed these views to prevailing attitudes within Hispanic culture, highlighting messages she received as a child about how girls should not play sports or ‘play rough’. Latina and Hispanic girls have commonly cited gender-related barriers to sports participation [50], although families – including some within the present study – report resisting these traditional gender-based expectations and supporting girls’ participation [50]. Some research suggests that Hispanic gender expectations contribute to the disparities in sports participation among this population [50]. Although not explicitly examined in the present study, there is also research to suggest that generational status and social class affect sports participation among Hispanic girls, with those from lower income households or immigrant families being less likely to play sports compared to those from higher income or native-born families [51]. Practitioners can encourage coach training that provides strategies for addressing community-level gender expectations, such as increasing confidence among Hispanic girls and highlighting female role models within the Hispanic community.

The high cost of organized sport participation and lack of time were identified as major participation barriers, operating at both community and organizational levels. At the community level, broader economic pressures meant families often need to focus on working to provide basic needs, with sports participation considered more of a luxury. At the organizational level, families – particularly those with multiple children – reported that the direct cost of enrollment fees, uniforms, and equipment presented significant financial challenges. These community economic pressures also meant parents were working longer hours to support their families, resulting in less free time to transport their daughter to sports opportunities – challenges that that have been shown in other studies [52, 53]. Although lack of time is a commonly reported barrier within youth sports [17, 54], this challenge may be compounded by the often rural-specific challenge of fewer programming options and sports facilities that are not conveniently located [17, 43].

Cost was a commonly reported barrier to participating [17, 54], and youth from families of lower socioeconomic families have been shown to have lower levels of sport participation [12, 55]. Communities can support low-cost sport options by identifying creative solutions that are tailored to community needs and resources, such as leveraging existing community assets (e.g., public parks, community centers), prioritizing volunteer-driven programs, implementing sport equipment exchange programs, and focusing on sports that can be done with minimal equipment (e.g., track). Practitioners can also support unstructured sport engagement (i.e., ‘pick-up’ games) or incorporate basic sport skill development into PE curriculum or after-school programs. This approach would also align with recommendations around developmentally-appropriate athlete development [56], in which children under the age of 12 should focus on developing basic movement skills by participating in unstructured activities.

At the organization level, resource availability was a major theme related to organizations’ ability to provide sport opportunities. For city-supported programs, challenges included both a limited budget and inconsistency of funding. This required leadership to be creative, flexible, and proactive with their purchases to capitalize on the funding they received. Material resources also played an important role in programming provision. Some organizations faced challenges due to the quality of existing facilities and sharing facilities with other organizations, which would result in sports being cancelled because the building flooded or it was being used by another organization. Despite these challenges, coaches and staff highlighted their ability to provide impactful sports programs for youth but acknowledged the greater impact they could have with consistent access to quality sports facilities. Approaches to providing low-cost sport programs could also be incorporated to address funding challenges among community programs. Practitioners and policymakers can also develop a local repository for sharing existing resources (curriculum, sports equipment) and cross-training coaches so they are knowledgeable across multiple sports.

In line with the FUYSP, quality coaches were an important participation facilitator. Coaches play a crucial role in youth sports and can have an impact on athletes’ development and motivation [57]. In the present study, parents greatly appreciated the time, social connections, and experience that coaches provided. Quality coaching has been defined, in part, as supportive strong coach-athlete relationships, shared decision-making, and subject-matter expertise [57]. However, there are many challenges with finding and retaining quality coaches, particularly in rural areas, as coaches are typically volunteers or staff with limited access to training resources [58], lower (if any) pay, and competing priorities [59]. Still, families in rural America have cited existing support networks as a valuable strength within these areas [60], and the social connections and safe space that local coaches can provide for rural athletes are invaluable.

At the interpersonal level, parents reported a desire for more female coaches and role models for their daughters. They placed value in their daughter having a female role model they could look up to within the sports world to learn from and help build their confidence. Notably, both parents and girls reported that they feel more comfortable with female (versus male) coaches. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of having females in sport-based leadership roles, both as coaches and role models. However, in addition to the challenges with retaining quality coaches within rural areas, females have reported challenges to pursuing coaching careers such as lack of female mentorship, gender discrimination, and career path opportunities [56, 61, 62]. Policymakers can promote rural girls’ participation in sport by supporting the recruitment, training, and retention of high quality, female coaches that represent the population they aim to serve.

In line with SDT, social connection (i.e., relatedness) was a major theme at the individual level and was often associated with girls’ motivation to participate in sports. Often, social connections with friends and teammates determined whether girls participated in sports and which sports girls most enjoyed. Conversely, some girls cited a lack of social connection (e.g., their sister or friend no longer participated) as the reason they dropped out of a particular sport. Social connections, enjoyment, and motivation are consistently shown to be primary factors of youth sports participation [18, 63, 64]. Practitioners should incorporate SDT-informed coaching, which can address these factors through strategies that foster positive relationships within the team and coach, encourage personal growth and enjoyment, and subsequently increase intrinsic motivation for sport participation and lifelong PA [65, 66].

Collectively, these findings can help inform the development and tailoring of youth sports programs for Hispanic girls living in rural areas. Existing youth sports programs targeting racial and ethnic minorities, such as Uptown Soccer [67], Soccer Without Borders [68], and Girls on the Run [69], address many of the sport participation factors identified in the present study. For example, some of these programs provide free or low-cost programming, girls-only programming, female coaches, positive interpersonal relationships, language support and/or address girls’ self-esteem, confidence, and social-emotional skills [67–69]. However, most of these programs are focused on urban areas and/or lack rigorous program evaluation. One exception is Girls on the Run, which cites rural presence and has a peer-reviewed evidence base. Although existing studies show evidence of Girls on the Run’s effectiveness in promoting factors such as competence, connection, self-efficacy, and physical activity among girls, these studies were not conducted among rural girls specifically [70–72], and additional research is needed to develop and examine the impact of youth sports programs specifically among Hispanic girls living in rural areas.

Limitations

Study limitations should be noted. This study focused on families with girls ages 8–10 in a rural, predominantly Hispanic community in Southern California. Thus, our findings may not generalize to other ages, boys, or other communities. For example, this work was conducted in a rural-border community that differs from a rural community located in central or northern California. Additionally, most of the caregivers identified as female, and male caregivers may have different perspectives. Future studies should recruit from a larger number of rural, Hispanic communities to identify the extent to which these findings are generalizable.

Conclusion

The presented findings emphasize the importance of considering sport participation factors among rural, Hispanic girls at multiple levels. Although existing sports programs and social connections support current sport participation among this population, there are opportunities to improve access to and diversity of programs, increase quality of programming and coach training within a resource-constrained environment, and address gender expectations through positive role modeling and social support. These findings also highlight the intersectionality of geography, gender, socioeconomic status (SES), and culture, and the need for tailored strategies that align with community needs. Efforts to promote positive, sustained sports participation among rural, Hispanic girls must maintain a focus on understanding and addressing community needs while maintaining a community-wide approach that galvanizes existing resources and support for this work. Increasing access to youth sport can provide additional opportunities for PA; address disparities in PA levels by gender, ethnicity, SES, and geography; and subsequently improve overall health among this population [73].
