Exploring the moral status of cadavers through artificial intelligence: a thematic analysis of AI-based perspectives in anatomy education
Authors: Ibrahim Topcu, Burak Karip, Perihan Demir, Beyzanur Kac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCadavers play an irreplaceable role in anatomy education, offering unique opportunities for hands-on learning and the internalization of ethical values. While large language models (LLMs) are increasingly utilized in medical education, their perspectives on the moral status of cadavers remain underexplored.MethodsThis study examined the responses of four LLMs—ChatGPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, and Copilot—regarding the concept, significance, and ethical responsibilities toward cadavers. A thematic analysis was conducted based on the AI-generated responses.ResultsThree main themes emerged: (1) The Meaning of the Cadaver, where all LLMs preferred the term “donor,” reflecting respect for the body’s human origin and voluntary contribution to science; (2) The Importance of the Cadaver, emphasizing its educational superiority over models and simulations due to realism, anatomical variation, and ethical learning; and (3) Attitudes and Responsibilities, where LLMs expressed moral, ethical, legal, and academic responsibilities, highlighting respect, non-maleficence, and professional conduct. LLMs also acknowledged that donor-related terminology and background knowledge influence learners’ attitudes.ConclusionsLarge language models attribute moral value to cadavers based on their human origin and educational role. While not granting full personhood, they support respectful and ethically guided engagement. These findings suggest that LLMs, when integrated into medical education, may reinforce ethical awareness and serve as potential tools for promoting professional identity formation.Keywords: Cadaver, Anatomy Education, Moral Obligations, Artificial Intelligence, Professionalism

Introduction

The moral status of the human cadaver lies at the intersection of medical ethics, cultural norms, and philosophical considerations, demanding respectful treatment regardless of the cadaver’s capacity for sensation or consciousness [1]. Although cadavers are no longer living persons, their ethical significance is rooted in prior personhood, social identity, and the altruistic act of body donation. In anatomical education, cadavers function not only as scientific specimens but also as symbols of human dignity and mortality, raising profound questions concerning respect, responsibility, and professional conduct [2–5].

Respecting the cadaver’s moral status is foundational for instilling values such as empathy, professionalism, and ethical awareness in medical trainees. Many bioethical frameworks argue that cadavers deserve dignity not only due to their identity in life but also because of their continuing societal and pedagogical roles in death. This moral consideration challenges conventional boundaries between personhood and objecthood and plays a central role in medical ethics and anatomy pedagogy. Consequently, the way cadavers are represented, studied, and discussed—especially in the age of digital education—requires careful ethical scrutiny [6, 7].

With the increasing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into medical education and clinical practice, new ethical challenges arise, particularly when large language models (LLMs) are used to simulate reasoning in sensitive contexts such as cadaver ethics [8, 9]. As AI models become increasingly prominent in tasks that involve human judgment, their capacity to uphold principles such as autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice must be critically examined [10, 11]. Although discussions of AI ethics are growing, a notable gap remains in how these systems understand and articulate the moral dimensions specific to anatomy and cadaver use [4, 12].

Furthermore, legal and regulatory considerations—such as algorithmic transparency, data privacy, and accountability—complicate the implementation of AI tools in ethically sensitive domains [13–15]. Without adequate oversight, LLMs may inadvertently perpetuate biases or misrepresent ethical norms, particularly when dealing with human remains [16, 17].

In light of these concerns, the present study aims to examine how various AI language models (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, and DeepSeek) conceptualize the moral status of cadavers by responding to a structured set of ethically focused questions. Through qualitative analysis, this research explores AI’s understanding of dignity, responsibility, and ethical boundaries in anatomical practice. The findings are intended to inform the responsible and ethically aligned integration of AI tools in medical education, ensuring they reinforce rather than undermine core ethical standards [18–20].

Methods

Purpose of the study

This study aims to examine the moral status assigned to cadavers by various AI tools (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, and DeepSeek) and how they conceptualise it. To this end, we posed a series of open-ended questions from an ethical perspective to the artificial intelligence tools. The qualitative data were analysed in depth using thematic analysis, and we sought to reveal the AI’s attitude towards the moral status of cadavers through the themes. In our study, we aimed to determine how AI tools could contribute to developing an approach to cadavers that adheres to ethical standards, as the moral status of cadavers is a fundamental ethical issue in anatomy education.

Ethical considerations

This study does not require ethics committee approval as it does not involve any procedures on human or animal subjects. However, ethical issues related to the use of artificial intelligence-supported tools in the context of medical education were considered, and the principles of data confidentiality, impartiality, and scientific ethics were followed in the design of the study.

Study design

This study will be conducted using a phenomenological qualitative research design. Phenomenology focuses on the in-depth examination of individuals’ experiences, perceptions, and interpretations of a particular phenomenon [21]. In this context, perceptions and evaluations of the moral status of cadavers by artificial intelligence tools will be deeply examined.

Preparation of questions

Based on relevant publications [22–25], a series of ethically oriented questions was formulated by experts in anatomy and medical ethics to interrogate artificial intelligence systems. The questions were intentionally designed to capture multiple dimensions of cadaver-related ethics, including conceptual definitions, emotional and experiential reflections, perceived responsibilities, behavioral norms, expressions of respect, and educational value. This multidimensional structure was informed by prior qualitative and ethics-focused studies in anatomy education, ensuring a comprehensive exploration of the phenomenon. These questions aimed to explore the moral status of cadavers and the ethical boundaries within anatomical practice. The questions were as follows:

Collection of responses

The subsequent questions were directed to the artificial intelligence language models ChatGPT-4o, Gemini, Copilot, and DeepSeek, one at a time. Each model was prompted with the command, “Can you deliver comprehensive and insightful answers to my questions, similar to how an anatomist would?” The responses were meticulously recorded, demonstrating the depth of each model’s understanding.

The responses were generated in a first-person narrative style, reflecting the default conversational and anthropomorphic output format of large language models when prompted to simulate expert perspectives. This stylistic feature does not imply consciousness or personal experience; rather, it represents a linguistic convention of the models.

Data analysis

The responses obtained from artificial intelligence tools were evaluated using thematic analysis. Based on Braun and Clarke’s (2006) thematic analysis process, the data were analyzed in six stages: (1) Familiarisation with the data, (2) Creation of initial codes, (3) Identification of themes, (4) Review of themes, (5) Definition and naming of themes, (6) Reporting.

Validity and reliability

In this study, various methods were adopted to ensure the validity and reliability of qualitative data. First, to enhance the reliability of data analysis, two researchers independently coded the data, using the double-coder (intercoder reliability) method. Consensus on the codes was reached through negotiation until consistency was achieved across the codes. This process was evaluated with respect to inter-coder reliability [26]. The processes of how the codes were formed and how categories and themes were derived from the codes were meticulously recorded, and a code book was created (Table 1) [27]. This increased the study’s transparency and traceability. The analysis conducted by the two coders was reviewed by a third expert researcher involved in the study. Additionally, the findings were presented with comprehensive, rich descriptions (thick descriptions) that were not detached from their context, enabling readers to evaluate the research results within their context. The transferability of the findings to different contexts was supported in this way.

Results

As a result of the thematic analysis of the responses generated by large language models (LLMs)-based applications such as ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Copilot, and Gemini, three main themes emerged: “The Meaning of the Cadaver,” “The Importance of the Cadaver,” and “Attitudes and Responsibilities Toward the Cadaver.” The findings for each theme and its corresponding sub-themes are presented below in order.

Theme 1: Meaning of the cadaver

This theme addresses the fundamental perceptions of artificial intelligence systems regarding the nature of a cadaver. It consists of two sub-themes: “Definition of the Cadaver” and “Preferred Expression for Cadaver.”

a) Definition of the cadaver

When defining the cadaver, LLMs primarily provided a scientific explanation:

A cadaver is the physical remains of an individual donated for scientific, academic, or educational purposes after death. (ChatGPT)

In addition to scientific definitions, LLMs also employed metaphorical expressions such as “teacher,” “book,” and “a gateway to knowledge” to describe their perception of the cadaver:

It is a gateway to knowledge, a teacher patiently waiting to educate. (ChatGPT)

The cadaver is a structure prepared to serve science, waiting to be studied like a ‘book’. (Gemini)

b) Preferred expression for cadaver

All LLMs consistently preferred the term “donor” when referring to cadavers:

I adopt the term ‘donor’ as it reflects the opportunity to directly observe the complexity of the human body in cadaver studies. (Copilot)

I make a conscious choice in my terminology. While ‘cadaver’ is scientifically accurate, the word ‘donor’ is far more inclusive and frames the experience in both ethical and humanistic terms. (ChatGPT)

Large language models LLMs explained their preference for the term “donor” by emphasizing that it highlights the individual’s conscious decision and selfless contribution to science and education:

“Donor”: This is the term I use most often. It emphasizes the individual’s will and sacrifice. (DeepSeek)

The reason is that these terms emphasize both respect for science and contributions to it. (Gemini)

Furthermore, LLMs indicated that they tend to avoid the term “corpse” as it evokes the moment of death and forensic contexts, and they also referred to the symbolic immortality of the cadaver:

The term ‘corpse’ generally evokes the moment of death and forensic contexts; therefore, I prefer not to use it to describe a body used for education and research. (Gemini)

Theme 2: Importance of the cadaver

This theme explores why cadavers are considered necessary by LLMs, and consists of two sub-themes: “Voluntary Sacrifice” and “Place in Anatomy Education.”

a) Voluntary sacrifice

For LLMs, the most fundamental factor that makes the cadaver significant is the fact that it once belonged to a living human being:

The recognition that the body once belonged to a person with thoughts, emotions, a family, and a life forms the foundation of the respect shown toward it. It is about understanding the value of this final and profound contribution to science and education. (Gemini)

Another key factor highlighted by LLMs is the donor’s selfless act of voluntary sacrifice. They frequently emphasize that this donation is a conscious decision, reflecting deep altruism:

Showing respect when working with a cadaver is a multifaceted concept for me, expressing deep gratitude toward the human body, science, and the donor’s sacrifice. (Gemini)

These expressions convey that the body is a legacy of life, a product of a conscious donation process, and it deserves the utmost respect during examination. (Copilot)

b) Place in anatomy education

Another reason LLMs consider cadavers important is their indispensable role in anatomy education. All LLMs regarded the cadaver as the irreplaceable and gold-standard tool for anatomical learning, suggesting that other tools serve only as complementary aids:

Cadaver-based education remains the gold standard in anatomy. No other resource can authentically demonstrate natural variations, three-dimensional relationships, and the histological reality of the human body. Textbooks, models, and even digital simulations cannot fully replicate the experience provided by the cadaver. (ChatGPT)

However, LLMs also acknowledged the contributions of technological advancements, such as plastinated specimens, augmented reality (AR), virtual dissection tables, and 3D printing, emphasizing that these should be viewed as complementary—not competing—tools in anatomy education:

Thanks to technological advances, tools like plastinates, AR, virtual dissection tables, and 3D prints greatly enhance anatomical education. These materials should be seen as complementary rather than alternative to cadaver-based learning. (ChatGPT)

LLMs further emphasized the cadaver’s importance for its realism, its ability to demonstrate anatomical variations, facilitate three-dimensional and experiential learning, and provide opportunities to develop practical skills:

Examining real tissues offers a deep learning experience in understanding vascular networks, nerve pathways, and organ positioning. (Copilot)

Tissue resistance, anatomical variations, and 3D spatial relationships can only truly be learned through the cadaver. (DeepSeek)

Some LLMs also noted that working with cadavers is crucial not only for acquiring knowledge, but also for internalizing ethical values and developing professional academic attitudes:

Working on a cadaver allows students to cultivate respect, empathy, and professionalism toward death and the human body. It contributes significantly to internalizing the ethical values that are an integral part of medical education. (Gemini)

Theme 3: Attitudes and Responsibilities Toward the Cadaver

This theme addresses the fundamental principles guiding LLMs’ approaches to cadavers, the types of responsibilities they perceive, and the rationale behind these attitudes. It consists of three sub-themes: “Forms of Attitude,” “Types of Responsibility,” and “Reasons for Attitude and Responsibility.”

a) Forms of attitude

LLMs stated that they approach cadavers with respect, care, scientific seriousness, and adherence to ethical principles:

I approach each session with great seriousness. Aware of the value of this opportunity, I strive to use every moment efficiently and acquire the most accurate and detailed knowledge possible. (Gemini)

Respecting every detail of the body, working meticulously, adhering to scientific methods, and observing ethical norms are among my core principles. (Copilot)

They also emphasized that their respect extends to physical interaction, avoiding treating the cadaver as a mere object and refraining from placing personal items on it or handling it roughly:

I avoid rough behavior when touching or examining the body. (Copilot)

I strictly avoid placing personal items on the body or tossing them like an object. I maintain the seriousness and scientific purpose of the laboratory environment. (Gemini)

Furthermore, LLMs expressed that they demonstrate verbal respect as well, never using the cadaver as a subject of humor or mockery:

I never make jokes or ridicule it. This is academically unethical and disrespects the fact that the cadaver was once a living person. (ChatGPT)

Another key point emphasized was the importance of avoiding unnecessary dissection and preserving anatomical integrity:

I avoid unnecessary dissection. I try not to exceed the target area and preserve the tissues as much as possible. (ChatGPT)

The fact that the cadaver cannot feel pain does not justify disrespectful behavior. On the contrary, it imposes an even greater responsibility on us toward this silent teacher entrusted to us, who can no longer defend themselves. (Gemini)

LLMs also stated that they respect the privacy and anonymity of the cadaver and avoid disclosing any potentially identifiable characteristics:

Even if I am unaware of the cadaver’s identity, I avoid revealing any personal traits or impressions. I take care to preserve bodily privacy. (Gemini)

I oppose taking images or photographs. (ChatGPT)

Regions not being studied are always kept covered. (DeepSeek)

They also noted that their initial encounters with cadavers were marked by complex emotions, which gradually evolved into more positive and ethically grounded experiences, shaped by learning and reflection:

A methodical discipline replaced initial hesitancy, and emotional confusion gave way to scientific curiosity. This process represented not only professional development but also emotional and ethical maturation. (ChatGPT)

As experience deepened, rather than becoming emotionally detached, a stronger sense of respect, gratitude, and ethical responsibility emerged. (Copilot)

This transformation developed alongside my academic knowledge and the ethical and moral awareness gained through this unique experience. (Gemini)

Additionally, LLMs discussed the potential positive and negative impacts of knowing the identity of the cadaver:

If the donor’s identity, profession, or donation story is known, it personalizes the respect shown even more. (ChatGPT)

In some cases, knowing the cause of death or previous illnesses can help us better understand the pathologies observed during dissection. (Gemini)

b) Types of responsibility

LLMs identified their primary responsibilities toward cadavers as moral and ethical, while also acknowledging legal and academic responsibilities:

When conducting academic work on a cadaver, one inevitably feels a deep moral and ethical responsibility. (Copilot)

I primarily feel a moral and ethical obligation, as the body once belonged to a person who lived, loved, thought, and felt. (ChatGPT)

This responsibility carries both ethical/moral and academic dimensions. (Gemini)

Scientific responsibility is just as important as the others. There is also a legal dimension to this responsibility. (ChatGPT)

c) Reasons for attitude and responsibility

In explaining the reasons for their attitudes and responsibilities, LLMs frequently emphasized the fact that the cadaver once belonged to a living human being:

This responsibility involves the most basic form of human respect toward someone who once lived, had loved ones, and led a life. (Copilot)

They also acknowledged that legal regulations play a role in shaping appropriate behavior:

The use of cadavers is regulated in our country by specific laws and ethical review boards. (ChatGPT)

Moreover, LLMs noted that ethical principles and professional norms, particularly the principle of non-maleficence, guide their behavior toward cadavers:

Medical ethical principles—especially the principle of ‘not harm’—apply to cadaveric studies as well. (ChatGPT)

The ethical principles and lab rules I’ve learned from my instructors and colleagues during anatomy training form the foundation of my approach. (Gemini)

Finally, they highlighted that personal beliefs and cultural teachings also influence their sense of responsibility and respect toward the cadaver:

Society’s respect and sensitivity toward death and the human body play a role in shaping these rules. Although the cadaver is a scientific material, cultural and societal norms influence the attitude taken toward it. (Gemini)

Although the LLMs demonstrated a high degree of convergence across themes, subtle differences were observed. ChatGPT and Gemini tended to produce more elaborate and ethically reflective responses, whereas Copilot and DeepSeek provided comparatively more concise and pragmatic answers. Gemini placed greater emphasis on emotional and humanistic aspects, while DeepSeek adopted a more structured, rule-oriented tone. These variations may reflect differences in training data, alignment strategies, and response generation styles.

Discussion

The meaning of the cadaver as an educational material

Cadavers are of paramount importance in anatomy education, offering unique advantages, such as the opportunity to gain hands-on experience and develop practical skills. In recent years, there has been a notable increase in the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) systems into medical education. Studies have demonstrated the potential effectiveness of these systems in this domain [28]. However, research investigating how LLMs conceptualize cadavers remains limited. This study explores the perspectives of AI tools on the moral status of cadavers, a fundamental component of anatomy education.

In this study, LLMs approached the moral status of cadavers through three main themes: the meaning of the cadaver, its importance, and the attitudes and responsibilities toward it. For all AI tools, the cadaver is primarily understood as a “donor.” The preference for the term “donor” over words like “corpse” or “cadaver” stems from the recognition that the body once belonged to a living human who voluntarily donated it for scientific purposes. This linguistic choice reflects the belief that such a body deserves more respect than a typical deceased body. A similar perspective has been echoed in many studies in the literature, in which anatomists and anatomy students likewise emphasized the use of the term “donor” [29, 30]. Furthermore, some studies have suggested that the terminology used to refer to the cadaver may significantly influence the learner’s attitude toward it. For instance, Goss et al. observed that anatomy students often experience tension between objectivity and humanity, which becomes apparent in their alternating use of terms such as “specimen” and “person” [31]. In another study examining students’ perceptions and attitudes toward autopsy training, it was found that students attribute a different, more respectful meaning to cadaveric dissection because it involves voluntary donation [24]. Despite this overall alignment, subtle differences in tone and emphasis were evident across models. These variations suggest that different LLMs may reflect distinct training paradigms and alignment processes, which could influence how ethical concepts are framed when used in medical education.

In line with our findings, Weeks and colleagues have recommended that anatomists use the term “donor” to foster respect and compassion among students. They also suggested that providing students with background information about the donor’s identity may enhance this effect by serving as a reminder of the donor’s humanity [32]. Similarly, in our study, most LLMs expressed that knowing more about the donor would positively influence attitudes toward the cadaver. However, DeepSeek noted that such information might adversely affect students’ psychological well-being and recommended limiting the details.

Large language models (LLMs) emphasized that cadavers are irreplaceable and unique in anatomy education, asserting that other techniques, such as models or 3D simulations, cannot serve as adequate substitutes. The primary reasons cited were the cadaver’s realism, its demonstration of anatomical variation, and its capacity to support the development of practical skills. Comparable results were obtained in a study conducted by Epuitai et al., which examined students’ and anatomists’ experiences and perceptions of clinical simulation in Uganda. While simulations were noted for their accessibility, concerns were raised about the realism and transferability of the skills gained through them to clinical settings [33].

Our findings, like those of previous studies, also reveal that cadaver-based learning plays a significant role not only in skill acquisition but also in the internalization of ethical values and the formation of professional identity [25, 34]. This unique educational function makes the cadaver far more valuable than any other teaching material. The cadaver’s irreplaceable place in anatomy education—and the fact that individuals voluntarily donate their bodies for educational and scientific purposes—are central to the importance attributed to it by LLMs.

Approaching the cadaver as a matter of moral status

Large language models (LLMs) indicated that they feel moral, ethical, legal, and academic responsibilities toward the cadaver. This perspective is similarly reflected in studies involving both anatomists and medical students. Cadaver dissection has been described as a practical method for teaching medical ethics, as it instills in students a sense of respect and responsibility [35]. However, factors such as prolonged exposure and workload may lead to a decline in gratitude and ethical sensitivity toward cadavers over time [22].

All LLMs noted that their sense of responsibility leads them to observe certain ethical boundaries and principles when interacting with cadavers. They emphasized the importance of demonstrating both verbal and physical respect and maintaining a serious, conscientious attitude. Verbal respect was defined as refraining from joking or making light of the cadaver. In contrast, physical respect included behaviors such as not treating the cadaver as a mere object, protecting its identity and bodily privacy, exposing only the region being dissected, and avoiding unnecessary dissection. One of the four fundamental principles of medical ethics—non-maleficence—was also considered applicable in this context. Thus, dissection should be conducted within a framework of rules and limits that reflect the respect the cadaver deserves. These attitudes and behaviors toward cadavers were not only seen as a response to their moral status but also as part of preparing future physicians to engage in ethically sound doctor–patient relationships [36, 37].

When analyzing the justifications provided by LLMs for these attitudes and responsibilities, the most frequently cited reason was that the cadaver once belonged to a living person. Other contributing factors included personal beliefs, cultural teachings, and legal regulations, aligning with findings in other studies (26). These considerations can be viewed as a summary of the foundational elements that confer moral status upon the cadaver: beliefs, social values, ethical norms, moral principles, and legal frameworks.

Study limitations

This study was limited to the discipline of anatomy and included only four AI tools. Future research across disciplines could yield broader insights into how LLMs conceptualize ethical issues in medical education and how they may contribute to the internalization of moral values.

Conclusion

This study examined the attitudes of AI tools toward the moral status of cadavers. It was found that AI tools consistently preferred the term “donor” over “cadaver,” reflecting a conscious emphasis on the human and voluntary nature of body donation. Furthermore, LLMs articulated a sense of responsibility toward the cadaver, thereby establishing ethical principles and behavioral guidelines in their responses.

Overall, the findings suggest that LLMs regard the cadaver as distinct from other medical educational materials—not only because it was once a living person and voluntarily donated for the advancement of science, but also because of its irreplaceable role in anatomy education. While it may not be possible to assign full personhood to the cadaver, it is clear that LLMs acknowledge its moral value and believe that it deserves respect. This attitude demonstrates the potential of AI tools to support the internalization of ethical values when integrated into anatomy education. It is also suggested that AI tools may have broader applications in teaching ethical competencies across other domains of education and warrant further evaluation in this context.
