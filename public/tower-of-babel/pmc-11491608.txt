Development of electronic health record based algorithms to identify individuals with diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Joseph H Breeyear, Sabrina L Mitchell, Cari L Nealon, Jacklyn N Hellwege, Brian Charest, Anjali Khakharia, Christopher W Halladay, Janine Yang, Gustavo A Garriga, Otis D Wilson, Til B Basnet, Adriana M Hung, Peter D Reaven, James B Meigs, Mary K Rhee, Yang Sun, Mary G Lynch, Lucia Sobrin, Milam A Brantley, Yan V Sun, Peter W Wilson, Sudha K Iyengar, Neal S Peachey, Lawrence S Phillips, Todd L Edwards, Ayush Giri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop, validate, and implement algorithms to identify diabetic retinopathy (DR) cases and controls from electronic health care records (EHRs).Materials and MethodsWe developed and validated electronic health record (EHR)-based algorithms to identify DR cases and individuals with type I or II diabetes without DR (controls) in 3 independent EHR systems: Vanderbilt University Medical Center Synthetic Derivative (VUMC), the VA Northeast Ohio Healthcare System (VANEOHS), and Massachusetts General Brigham (MGB). Cases were required to meet 1 of the following 3 criteria: (1) 2 or more dates with any DR ICD-9/10 code documented in the EHR, (2) at least one affirmative health-factor or EPIC code for DR along with an ICD9/10 code for DR on a different day, or (3) at least one ICD-9/10 code for any DR occurring within 24 hours of an ophthalmology examination. Criteria for controls included affirmative evidence for diabetes as well as an ophthalmology examination.ResultsThe algorithms, developed and evaluated in VUMC through manual chart review, resulted in a positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.93 for cases and negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.91 for controls. Implementation of algorithms yielded similar metrics in VANEOHS (PPV = 0.94; NPV = 0.86) and lower in MGB (PPV = 0.84; NPV = 0.76). In comparison, the algorithm for DR implemented in Phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) in VUMC yielded similar PPV (0.92) but substantially reduced NPV (0.48). Implementation of the algorithms to the Million Veteran Program identified over 62 000 DR cases with genetic data including 14 549 African Americans and 6209 Hispanics with DR.Conclusions/DiscussionWe demonstrate the robustness of the algorithms at 3 separate healthcare centers, with a minimum PPV of 0.84 and substantially improved NPV than existing automated methods. We strongly encourage independent validation and incorporation of features unique to each EHR to enhance algorithm performance for DR cases and controls.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), which can manifest with breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier and neovascularization in the retina, is a leading cause of visual impairment and preventable blindness among working-age adults, affecting an estimated 103 million individuals worldwide.1 As the global prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) rises, the global prevalence of DR will also rise, with estimates suggesting there will be more than 160 million individuals with DR by 2045.1

The prevalence of DR varies between individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes, but has been estimated to be between 37% and 94% for type 1 DM and 35% for type 2 DM.2 Nearly one-third of those with DR progress to vision-threatening DR over years of disease.2–4 Early-stage DR, termed non-proliferative DR, is characterized by blot hemorrhages, cotton wool spots, intraretinal microvascular abnormalities, retinal microaneurysms, and venous beading.5 Based on the extent of retinal findings, non-proliferative DR is classified as mild, moderate, or severe, while the more advanced proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) is defined by neovascularization of the retina or iris.5

DR is a complex disease with multiple genetic and environmental factors contributing to the pathophysiology. The strongest established risk factors for DR and for progression to vision-threatening DR are DM duration and poor glycemic control, while other reported risk factors include hypertension, diabetic neuropathy, diabetic nephropathy, and increased body mass index (BMI).4–6 Compared with populations of European descent, the prevalence of DR and vision-threatening DR is higher in African American and Hispanic populations.7–11 Conducting epidemiologic and genetic studies of DR based on manual reviews of retinal imaging and electronic health record (EHR) data, or prospective evaluation has been challenging, resulting in smaller sample sizes.12–17 Additionally, many prospective studies have been performed primarily in individuals of European ancestry and Asian ancestry.12–17

At present, only a small number of genetic loci have been associated with DR.14,16,18–23 These have been identified in studies with recruitment following face-to-face encounters, where cases are individuals with diabetes and DR, while controls are individuals with diabetes without DR. Large-scale biobanks provide important opportunities to pursue additional loci and to identify genetic and lifestyle-related risk factors for DR.24–28 To leverage these resources for understanding the etiology behind DR, an efficient and accurate means to identify individuals with diabetes and DR as well as individuals with diabetes without DR is required. We identified one EHR-based algorithm for diabetic retinopathy cases and controls through the eMERGE PheKB resource.29 Developed in the Marshfield Clinic Research Foundation (Positive Predictive Value [PPV]: 0.80, Negative Predictive Value [NPV]: 0.98), this EHR-based algorithm was validated with limited success at Vanderbilt University (PPV: 0.68, NPV: 1.00).29 The resource provided a detailed chart-review abstraction form, which discussed the potential need for natural language processing (NLP) depending on the EHR system.29 General applicability of this algorithm may be limited as (1) it only uses ICD-9 codes, (2) the PPV for DR cases in the second institution was less than desirable, and (3) reliance on NLP may limit the wide-spread usability of this algorithm across multiple EHR systems. Furthermore, details regarding validation such as the sample sizes used to generate these PPVs, the number of cases and controls identified, and characteristics of cases and controls from these algorithms are not provided. As an alternative, we recognize PheCodes (higher order ICD classifications used in Phenome-wide association studies [PheWAS]) are an easily implemented method of identifying a wide array of phenotypes in EHR systems and are useful in discovery of associations, novel hypothesis generation, and drug discovery30–33; however, the validity of the DR PheCode has not been evaluated in peer-reviewed literature to our knowledge.

We recognized the need for updated, validated, high-throughput, and adaptable EHR-based algorithms that use structured codes rather than NLP to identify DR cases and controls. We hypothesized that these relatively easy-to-implement algorithms for DR can promote usability across EHR systems, improve sample size and statistical power, and enable research groups to ask previously intractable research questions to increase our understanding of the underlying pathophysiology of this potentially severe DM complication. Here, we describe the development and transferability of EHR-based algorithms to identify individuals with DR (cases) and individuals with DM but without DR (controls) across 3 healthcare systems in the United States.

Methods

In this study, we utilized EHR data from 3 independent healthcare systems, the Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC Synthetic Derivative [SD]), the VA Northeast Ohio Healthcare System (VANEOHS) Eye Clinic, and the Massachusetts General Brigham (MGB) biobank. Briefly, the VUMC SD is a database containing de-identified clinical information derived from VUMC’s EHR systems (eSTAR and Epic) on >2.2 million individuals and includes diagnostic and procedure codes, demographics (age, sex, race/ethnicity), clinical notes, laboratory values, and medication information.28 The MGB system utilizes the Epic system, and in addition to using standard diagnostic and procedure codes, it also utilized Epic provider codes specific to MGB. The VANEOHS utilizes VA-sanctioned EHR systems and uses the VA Informatics and Computing Infrastructure (VINCI) to provide secure access to VA and health-related data to researchers.34 In addition to the standard OMOP model, VINCI system also houses VA-specific features such as Health Factor and stop codes (to identify visit type by specialty), which are unique to the VA and not applicable to VUMC SD and MGB. This study was conducted upon approval of Institutional Review Boards of VUMC, VANEOHS, MGB, and Million Veteran Program (MVP) central and local offices.

Algorithm development

Algorithm conceptualization and initial development was performed in the VUMC SD. The development process is summarized as 3 hierarchical steps: (1) Algorithm Design provides the basic framework; (2) Algorithm Construct entails defining criteria to construct a given algorithm; and (3) Operationalizing criteria, which entails finding EHR-specific features that fulfil criteria for the algorithm construct (Table S1; Figure 1). Algorithm development was an iterative process that required moving between steps 2 and 3 to generate multiple versions of algorithms and was conducted between September 2019 and October 2021 (Figure S1). For example, cases defined under Version 0 (V0) required that cases have at least one International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9/10 code for any form/severity of DR, while controls were required to have at least one ICD-9/10 code for DM and no codes from the DM exclusion list (Tables S1-S3) and at least one eye examination without codes for DR. In V1, we required cases to have at least 2 unique dates with an ICD-9/10 code for any form of DR, and controls to have at least 3 dates with an ICD-9/10 code for DM without any DR codes. This additional requirement for control was put in place to improve confidence that a given patient indeed had diabetes during chart review. In V2, we improved the algorithm for cases by adding an eligibility criterion allowing them to have a single ICD-9/10 code for any form of DR if accompanied within 24 hours of an ophthalmology examination. Along with the DR algorithm, we developed an algorithm for PDR based on restricting criteria in the “any DR” algorithm to only PDR ICD-9/10 codes. The V0 algorithm was originally implemented in September 2019, while additional versions including the V0 version were again implemented and extracted from an updated SD system in October 2021 (Figure S1).

Sampling of records and manual review

We first applied the base algorithms (V0) for cases and controls in the VUMC SD. Cases were assigned the highest grade of DR severity as determined by the algorithm: proliferative DR (PDR), severe non proliferative DR (SNPDR), mild/moderate non proliferative DR (MNPDR), and non-specific DR (NSDR). We provide a detailed data dictionary describing variables and codes specific diagnostic codes for each of these staging variables (Tables S1, S2a and b). A priori we planned to randomly sample 100 cases from each DR severity and 100 controls for a total of 500 unique records. These records were then to be randomized to 5 anonymized sets. Ten percent of each of these sets would then be resampled and distributed across other sets to achieve overlap between reviewers for assessment of inter-rater reliability. In its first implementation (September 2019) of the V0 algorithms in the VUMC SD, we identified 11 894 controls, 1595 PDR cases, 88 SNPDR cases, 1734 MNPDR cases, and 2804 NSDR cases as their highest DR stages. We followed our a priori sampling plan, with the exception of SNPDR for which only 88 algorithmically defined SNPDR cases were available, to yield 488 unique records with 48 overlapping between reviewers. We extracted all necessary data to be able to additionally apply or relax features of the algorithm beyond the base algorithm in this set of 488 unique records for curation and validation of additional versions of cases and controls.

Manual review was conducted by an ophthalmologist and 4 other trained reviewers blinded to algorithmic determination of case-control status. Reviewers looked for multiple criteria to classify cases and controls and recorded their determinations and reasons for determinations using a standardized RedCAP survey (Supplemental Material). The following are criteria where individuals would be classified as a true case: (1) ophthalmology notes with mention of some form of DR, (2) CPT evidence of treatment for DR, (3) CPT evidence of ophthalmology examination as well as multiple DR ICD codes, and (4) multiple ICD codes for DR, with the severity progressing over time. The following are criteria where individuals would be classified as a true control: (1) clinical notes with mention of DM from endocrinology, (2) any clinical notes with mention of DM along with mention of duration of diabetes, and (3) DM ICD codes with prescription of DM medications, along with requiring no evidence of DR.

Performance of the algorithms was primarily assessed by computing PPV for cases and NPV for controls. The V0 algorithm was assessed using the September 2019 data-pull first, followed by assessment of all 3 versions in the October 2021 data pull (Figure S1 and Table S11). If an algorithmically determined case or control could not be validated with manual review, they were classified as undetermined and were excluded from the numerator but included in the denominator for computing NPV. For the 48 overlapped records, if the reviewers disagreed, we used a conservative approach to include the manual review that did not bias our results towards a higher PPV or NPV. Three of the 48 overlapped records were classified as “undetermined” by both reviewers and were removed from the calculation of concordance rate. As we could not manually review retinal imaging data for individuals in the VUMC SD, we captured all EHRs (n = 152) from the 488 that included EHR notes from the Vanderbilt Eye Institute (VEI). These VEI notes indicate that an individual was assessed in the clinic with a retinal examination with an ophthalmologist reporting on their findings. Of the 48 overlapped EHRs, 21 had VEI notes. Finally, in the VUMC SD, we compared the performance of our algorithm with the widely used PheCode definition of DR, by evaluating test characteristics of DR cases and controls identified using the PheCode definition.35

Algorithm transferability: operationalizing criteria through EHR-specific implementation across institutions

VA Northeast Ohio Healthcare System

In the VANEOHS EHR, individuals with DM were first identified using a combination of diagnosis and procedure codes, clinic stop codes, and medications. Briefly, individuals with affirmative evidence of DM were identified based on presence of a diagnosis code at 1 or more face-to-face outpatient primary care physician visits or a DM diagnosis code at 2 or more outpatient visits of any kind, and at least one outpatient fill of a diabetes-indicated medication (Table S3). In addition to ICD9/10 codes, the VA also routinely utilizes Health Factor codes to annotate health records. To take advantage of this additional information source, the case-algorithm construct (V2) was operationalized in the VA to include Health Factor codes in addition ICD-9/10 diagnostic codes. The algorithm was subsequently applied in the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs biorepository, the Million Veteran Program.

At the VANEOHS, we pulled 50 algorithm-defined cases and 50 algorithm-defined controls for chart review. Charts were reviewed by a single optometrist that was blinded to the algorithm assignments. Twenty-one additional charts were pulled for further review to improve precision of the PDR algorithm since there were too few PDR cases in the first 50 records with any-DR. Of the 50 any-DR cases, we identified 105 visits for 24 DR patients with at least one fundus imaging stored for manual review. We investigated the performance of the algorithm against these fundus images manually reviewed and staged by an optometrist who provided information to assess accuracy for DR identification in general (yes/no) and accuracy by DR severity as well.36

Mass General Brigham

In the MGB, individuals with DM were first identified with a previously curated DM phenotype.37 To identify DR cases, we applied the DR case-algorithm construct (V2), whose criteria were operationalized to include an EPIC Provider code along with an ICD-9/10 code for any form of DR on unique days, to take advantage of features unique to MGB. We used the more stringent algorithm construct for controls in MGB (V1), which requires at least 3 unique days of diabetes codes to be included as controls, as applying only one ICD code for diabetes (V0) yielded a large proportion of records where diabetes status could not be validated with manual review.

In the MGB biobank, we pulled 50 algorithm-defined cases (V2) and 50 algorithm-defined controls (V1) for chart review. Charts were reviewed by a fellowship-trained retinal specialist that was blinded to the algorithm assignments.

Phenome-wide association study

We performed a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) of the (V2) DR algorithm in VUMC SD (nmax = 12 991) and the MVP (nmax = 165 322) leveraging the full catalog of ICD-9/10 codes.30 The ∼1800 PheCode traits were modeled with logistic regressions as a function of the (V2) algorithm designation, adjusted for age, sex, and the duration of DM, stratified by self-reported race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White [NHW], non-Hispanic Black [NHB], and Hispanic [HIS]). Interpretation of results was limited to phenotypes with 20 or more cases. The results were meta-analyzed both within and across populations. Bonferroni-corrected statistical significance threshold was set to P-value ≤ 2.75 × 10−5. Analyses were conducted in R with the PheWAS package.38

Results

Algorithm validation at Vanderbilt University Medical Center

We compared the performance of the 3 DR algorithm versions developed in the VUMC SD dataset (Table 1; Table S11). The initial algorithm (V0) from the September 2019 data-pull aimed at identifying possible cases and controls, resulted in a PPV and NPV of 0.814 and 0.89, respectively (Table S11). In reimplementing the algorithms to an updated data pull in October 2021, the V0 algorithm has similar PPV (0.857) and NPV (0.912). The second algorithm (V1), designed to increase algorithm precision, resulted in a substantially improved PPV (0.969) and provided minimal gains on NPV (0.932) compared to the V0 versions. The final algorithm for cases (V2) intended to maximize cases and maintain a high PPV by including ophthalmology examination data along with a single DR ICD code instead of 2, resulted in a similar PPV (0.927) as V1, while increasing case-numbers. Compared to the PheCode definition (PPV = 0.922, NPV = 0.480), our algorithm classified cases (V2) with a similar PPV, but a considerably higher NPV (V0 and V1). The same upward trend was seen for the PDR algorithm, with the PPV increasing from 0.802 to 0.914 (Table 1). In the subset of EHRs with VEI notes, we saw similar algorithm performance for PPV (0.938) and improved performance for NPV (1.0). The concordance of the 5 reviewers for DM, DR, and PDR designations was 96%, 91%, and 97%, respectively (Table S4). When restricted to EHRs with notes from the VEI, the concordance for DM, DR, and PDR designations was 100%, 95%, and 100% of the individuals, respectively (Table S4).

Sources of false positive and negative cases

There were instances where the algorithm failed to accurately identify cases and controls. The most common instance of false-positive cases (ie, satisfied the algorithm case definition but not manual review) came from the V0 algorithm, which classified 41 false positives (Table S11). In these instances, individuals with a single ICD code for DR either did not have a second mention of DR in the EHR or had an ophthalmology note stating no DR. We updated the case algorithm to require 2 or more days of ICD codes for DR. The V1 algorithm correctly classified most cases, with only 5 false positives (Table S11). Of these 5 individuals, 3 had multiple DR ICD codes; however, at the most recent ophthalmology examination, the ophthalmologist stated no DR. The V2 algorithm also correctly classified most cases, with 16 false positives (Table S11). These misclassifications were similar to those identified with V1; however, the gain in sample size outweighed the slight decrease in PPV (0.969-0.927). There were only 5, 3, and 3 instances of a false negative for V0, V1, and V2, respectively. In these instances, individuals were missing DR ICD codes, yet clinical notes stated DR on multiple occasions.

Algorithm transferability at a Northeast Ohio Healthcare System and Mass General Brigham

The algorithms performed well in VANEOHS, where the DR algorithm PPV was 0.940 and PDR algorithm PPV was 0.818 (Table 1 and Table S5). There was a slight drop in the NPV for the VANOEHS (NPV = 0.860), while the NPV for the PDR algorithm (NPV = 0.989) was comparable to the VUMC. Among the 50 algorithmically defined any-DR cases, we found fundus photography images associated with 105 visits for 24 patients. On a visit-level, 99 of 105 (PPV = 0.94) algorithmically defined cases were verified to have DR by manual review of imaging. Six DR-determinations could not be verified directly via imaging due to poor image quality. Of the 99 visits with adequate image quality, staging of DR (background/mild non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy, moderate non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy, severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy, and proliferative diabetic retinopathy) agreed for 86 images between our manual review and algorithm-determined diagnosis (Table S10). The algorithm tended to underestimate the staging of DR compared to manual review for the other remaining 13 images. Nine were identified as MNDPR by the algorithm, but imaging review indicated 4 had SNPDR and 5 had PDR. Four were identified as NSDR by the algorithm, but were identified as MNPDR upon image review. On a patient-level, 23 of the 24 patients (PPV = 0.96) were verified as DR cases through manual review of images, and 21 of 24 were verified for staging of DR. Application of the algorithm to MGB showed lower PPV (0.840) for V2 cases and lower NPV (0.760) for V1 controls compared to VUMC SD and VANEOHS.

Characterization of cases and controls identified by algorithms

We applied these algorithms to identify DR cases and DM controls in 3 separate EHR systems: 7952 DR cases (V2) and 18 310 controls (V0) in the VUMC SD (implemented in October 2021 with updated EHR data), 62 101 DR cases (V2) and 110 688 controls (V0) in the MVP, and 471 DR cases (V2) and 1137 controls (V1) in the MGB (Table 2). The 3 cohorts differed substantially in their sex composition with VUMC SD and MGB having approximately 50% females, whereas MVP consisted of predominantly males (>95%) (Table 2). Despite these major differences in cohort compositions, seminal observations for DR cases and controls were consistent with those in epidemiologic literature, where individuals with DR on average were more likely to have diabetic macular edema, neovascular glaucoma, diabetic neuropathy, and diabetic nephropathy in both the VUMC SD and MVP EHR systems. Average age at first DR diagnosis was lower at VUMC (59.2 years) than in the MVP (63.1 years) and in the MGB studies (67.9 years). However, in VUMC, the average age at DM diagnosis was lower for V2 cases (56.1 years) than for V0 (59.9) or V1 (58.4) controls. By design, individuals with diabetes without DR were more likely to be older than DR cases across all 3 EHR systems. Average duration of diabetes, captured as time since first diagnosis of diabetes in EHR or first mention of antihypertensive medication in EHR, whichever came first, was similar between VUMC and MVP cases, while controls in the MVP had a longer duration than in VUMC. At VUMC SD, controls (V1) were more likely to have diagnostic codes for type II diabetes, more likely to have hypertension than DR cases, and more likely to have ophthalmic visits in the last 5 years. Such phenomenon was not observed in the MVP EHR system. Cases and controls were similar on average with regard to BMI and had similar proportions of inherited retinal disease in VUMC SD and MVP. In VUMC, the average HbA1c (%) for V2 cases (8.14) was nearly a point higher than either V0 (7.04) or V1 (7.21) controls.

Phenome-wide association study

To systematically evaluate the relationship of algorithmically defined DR cases and controls with the phenome derived from EHR, we performed a Phenome-wide association study across study populations in the MVP and VUMC SD. The DR algorithm PheWAS identified a total of 959 unique PheCodes significantly associated with DR, with 99, 8, 8, and 7 significantly associated in the multi, NHW, NHB, and HIS populations, respectively (Table 3 and Tables S6-S9). In the multi-ancestry meta-analysis, a total of 784 PheCodes were associated with increased odds of DR while 151 were associated with decreased odds of DR. The most significantly associated PheCodes included Diabetic Retinopathy, Type 2 DM with Ophthalmic Manifestations, and Type 1 DM with Ophthalmic Manifestations, all with P-values below 1.00 × 10−305. In addition, the DR algorithm designation was associated with other phenotype groups related to downstream consequences of DM. Furthermore, many of these associations were stronger in non-Hispanic Blacks and Hispanics than in the non-Hispanic Whites (Table 3).

Effect estimate comparison for SNPs in the TCF7L2 locus

We compared effect-estimate sizes of SNPs with P-value < 5 × 10−6 in the TCF7L2 locus from a recent GWAS that utilized this EHR-based algorithm for case and control selection39 with estimates from the previously largest GWAS18 that was the combination of multiple smaller studies based on manual review (Figure S2). A total of 39 SNPs with P-value < 5 × 10−6 in the predominantly EHR-based GWAS (Nmax = 197 357) were also overlapping in the GWAS by Pollack et al (Nmax = 2464). All of the SNPs had consistent directions of association and overlapping confidence intervals with the same effect allele, and 3 were statistically significant in Pollack et al at a marginal P-value threshold of .05.

Discussion

In this study, we repurposed diagnostic codes and administrative data routinely collected in clinical practice to develop high-throughput algorithms that identify individuals with diabetes, with and without diabetic retinopathy, to be used for downstream research purposes. We report while the logic of the algorithm at the Algorithm Construct level (Figure 1) is transferrable across institutions, it is more beneficial to operationalize these criteria to elements that are specific to each institution or EHR system. We involved 3 separate healthcare systems to confirm the portability of transferable elements of the DR algorithms and identify site-specific elements that add to performance, along with validation of the algorithms with manually reviewed fundus imaging at one of the VA locations. While identification of individuals with DR typically relies on manual review or machine learning algorithms along with retinal imaging data, our algorithm based on ICD diagnostic codes allows for easy implementation and modification to capitalize on the unique features of EHR systems, thus requiring significantly fewer resources for identifying at-risk individuals. The DR algorithms tested accurately identified individuals with DR, with PPVs ranging from 0.840 to 0.940 and identified those with diabetes and without DR with NPVs ranging from 0.760 to 0.932. This is a vast improvement over existing algorithms implemented in high-throughput methods such as PheWAS (PPV = 0.922; NPV = 0.48) and represents a starting point for researchers looking to maximize use of EHR for DR research.

To date, most studies have used 1 of 3 methods to identify individuals with DR: (1) manual review of eye examination findings, (2) manual review of retinal imaging, or (3) machine learning algorithms to review retinal imaging data.14,18,40,41 While these methods are considered the gold standard for phenotyping, they are labor intensive and generally require retinal imaging data, which is not always available in research datasets. The largest DR genome-wide association study to date, including 19 372 cases and 19 615 controls, involved the manual review of retinal images for more than 38 987 individuals from multiple studies.18 As studies of DR are often under-powered, a method to easily identify individuals with DR with relatively minimal effort while maintaining high accuracy through EHRs offers an attractive opportunity to scale up research related to DR. We first show through manual review of records that the algorithms accurately identify individuals coded as DR cases and controls. Following the premise that DR diagnoses of patients seen by eye-specialists would be most reliable, we stratified cases and controls in the VUMC SD chart review validation by whether they visited the VEI. We showed patients who had VEI notes had higher, yet comparable, accuracy as cases and controls who were not seen by eye-specialists suggesting the high PPVs and NPVs were not entirely driven by one stratum. Then in the VA, we verified high PPV of our any-DR case-algorithm by manually reviewing 115 fundus images for 24 of the 50 any-DR cases for whom images were stored in the VA system. Further evaluation of DR-staging between algorithm and manual review showed high accuracy with close matches for 84 of 99 images with good quality.

We identified one additional EHR-based algorithm for diabetic retinopathy through the eMERGE PheKB resource.29 The algorithms presented in our study represents a substantial improvement on this past endeavor in the following ways: (1) current algorithm is updated for usage with ICD9 and ICD10 codes, (2) they do not rely on NLP based methods which expands its usability, and (3) we also provide substantial details about validation and characterize populations that are selected by these algorithms across three different EHR systems. While not stated explicitly in the PheKB resource, a common conclusion that could be drawn from the PheKB algorithm and the algorithms detailed in this paper is that although the logic of the Algorithm Construct may be transferrable across institutions, the elements that conform to the criteria need to be operationalized based on a given EHR system’s set up.

As described in other works developing EHR-algorithms, we note that embracing unique aspects of each EHR systems greatly improves validity of these algorithms.42,43 For example, in addition to the base ICD-9/10 diagnostic criteria that formed the backbone for the primary algorithm at VUMC, the VANEOHS and the MGB systems each had additional coding systems. Limiting our algorithm to ICD-9/10 codes and ignoring the Health Factor coding system in the VA would have led to more than 5000 individuals potentially misclassified as controls. In addition, we were able to capitalize on the richness of the VA EHR, which included detailed information on prescription, and the type of prescribing physician (primary care provider, specialist, inpatient, outpatient, etc) to gain affirmative evidence of diabetes status, features that were not accessible for the VUMC SD or MGB. Conversely, there were aspects of the base EHR-algorithm that did not work as intended in other EHR systems. For example, in the MGB, we found a sizable proportion of individuals were missing adequate EHR notes to validate ICD diagnoses. Since these undetermined individuals were conservatively counted against the PPV and NPV to avoid inflation of predictive values, the resulting PPV and NPV are noticeably lower than the VUMC and VANEOHS.

As was the case in our study, data accessibility across EHR systems varies greatly either due to heterogenous data collection systems, provider practices, or due to data availability constraints imposed across each EHR system. While data standardization models such as Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) allow for easy implementation in participating sites, EHR systems that have yet to implement these common data models can still implement diagnostic and procedure code-based EHR algorithm in addition to other site-specific features.44

Comparative analyses across versions of algorithms within and between institutions allowed us to test features of the algorithms, which have consequences on predictive properties, on the number and the composition of individuals identified. Requiring 2 or more unique days with a diagnostic code for DR substantially improved PPV, but it also reduced the number of cases identified. Relaxing the criteria to allow 1 or more ICD along with evidence of an eye-examination within 24 hours of the diagnostic code maintained previous gains in PPV from V1 and improved the number of cases (Table 1; Table S11). At VUMC, requiring at least 3 unique days of codes for diabetes for controls (compared to at least one), only marginally improved NPV and substantially reduced sample size. On the other hand, in MGB, requiring at least 3 unique days of diagnostic codes specifically for diabetes was necessary for the optimal performance of control selection.

In the VUMC SD, we observe a higher proportion of controls had T2D diagnostic codes than DR cases, a phenomenon not observed in the VA (Table 2). This is likely reflective of VUMC as a tertiary care facility, to which severe DR cases are referred for management and may not specifically note a diagnosis of diabetes even though it is implicit with DR. We note requiring at least 3 diabetes diagnostic codes for controls vs one, further changes characteristics of controls, as it prioritizes individuals who are older, have longer duration of diabetes, and are more likely to have other complications related to diabetes than controls with one ICD code. The VA’s role as a primary care facility for veterans is further evidenced in our selection of patients where both cases and controls are just as likely to have T2D diagnosis codes and just as likely to have had eye examinations in the past 5 years.

Although we show this approach reliably identifies cases and controls across multiple EHR systems, it is not without its limitations:

While it would have been ideal to validate our algorithms based on blinded review of retinal images as the gold standard, our algorithms were validated primarily based on EHR documentation. This level of access was not available in our de-identified system and, even if it were, it is not clear if all retinal images would necessarily be stored systematically. To address this limitation, we showed PPV and NPV for cases and controls were similar in a group of individuals for whom we had notes from the VEI compared to the PPV and NPV we obtained for the whole sample. Furthermore, we were able to validate the presence of DR, and also the severity of DR for a large proportion of DR cases whose images were stored at the VANOHS facility.

A cleaner approach would be to create functional definitions for incident diabetes, and only follow those individuals for DR. However, this approach severely limits sample size, which has been a key bottle neck for GWAS studies of DR in the past.

Cases on average had a younger age at first recorded diabetes date, on average had higher HbA1c levels and were more likely to have other diabetes-related complications than controls. These are phenomenon often observed in case-control studies of diabetic complications and is expected. To ensure controls with diabetes did not have DR but had the opportunity of being diagnosed with DR, we imposed requirements for controls including an ophthalmology/eye clinic visit and captured age at last visit as the index visit for controls, whereas ages of cases were indexed at first mention of DR diagnosis. Consequently, we observe controls on average are indexed at older ages than cases across all 3 EHR systems. This strategy greatly reduced misclassification of outcome, which may be highly desirable in the context of GWAS studies. Using a combination of variables such as age at diabetes diagnosis, duration of diabetes, age at index visit, and available glycemic measures such as HbA1c to adjust for differences may alleviate concerns during analysis. While matching on HbA1c across cases-control status could be a strategy to hone-in on retinopathy-specific signals, this approach would necessarily miss any signals in case-control studies that are mediated through lack of control for hyperglycemia and limit discovery in general. On the other hand, our approach of sampling cases of retinopathy and controls without regard to HbA1c status and other diabetic complications selects individuals with retinopathy who are representative of the population and allows discovery of signals (eg, genetic variants) that are retinopathy-specific and those potentially mediated through hyperglycemia; the latter can and should be assessed with further investigation as demonstrated recently.39 Further comparison of effect estimates for SNPs in the TCF7L2 region, a prominent diabetes locus, from GWAS generated from this algorithm39 to estimates generated by a GWAS of DR with a traditional manual-review approach18 showed directions of associations and magnitudes of associations were similar across both approaches. The algorithm-based approach having a greater than 70-fold advantage in sample size (Nmax = 197 357) than the previous GWAS (Nmax = 2464), which is substantially under-powered to detect these associations, as demonstrated by the wide confidence intervals (Figure S2).

Overall, by developing and evaluating algorithms to identify individuals with diabetes with and without DR, we show the potential of leveraging existing EHR systems to advance our understanding of the etiology of DR. We demonstrate these EHR-based algorithms, initially developed to include ICD codes, allow for modification based on each EHR system’s distinct reporting methods. These developed algorithms provide advanced starting points for researchers looking to implement DR algorithms in EHR systems rather than blind-automated application. We encourage researchers to investigate unique features in each EHR system to implement choices most appropriate to that system. As EHR systems increase in size, as well as the usage for research, a clear advantage to this approach is scalability as it offers an alternative to the manual review of large numbers of retinal images for research purposes while retaining validity.
