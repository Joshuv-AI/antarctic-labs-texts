Introduction

As early as 1952, Alan Turing, in his seminal paper
[
37
]
, pioneered the use of reaction–diffusion systems to model morphogenesis, the process responsible for the regeneration of tissue structures in hydra—an organism only a few millimeters long, composed of approximately 100,000 cells. Observations of morphogenesis in hydra suggested the involvement of two chemical substances (morphogens): a slowly diffusing (short-range) activator and a rapidly diffusing (long-range) inhibitor.

Turing was the first to demonstrate that while diffusion typically exerts a smoothing and homogenizing effect on individual chemicals, the interaction of two or more chemicals with differing diffusion rates could destabilize uniform steady states in reaction–diffusion systems. This instability leads to the emergence of nonhomogeneous patterns in the distribution of these reactants, a phenomenon now widely recognized as
diffusion-driven instability (DDI)
or
Turing instability
.

Building on Turing’s groundbreaking concept, Gierer and Meinhardt
[
14
]
proposed, in 1972, the activator–inhibitor system that has since become known as the Gierer–Meinhardt system. This model was specifically designed to capture the dynamics of hydra regeneration within a defined domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N},N\geq 1
and is given below:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle u_{t}-D_{u}\Delta u=-\lambda u+k_{u}\frac{u^{p}}{v^{q}}+\rho(x),&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,\infty)\,,\\[7.22743pt]
\displaystyle v_{t}-D_{v}\Delta v=-\mu v+k_{v}\frac{u^{m}}{v^{s}},&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,\infty),\\[7.22743pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial\eta}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial\eta}=0,&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty),\\[7.22743pt]
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\;v(x,0)=v_{0}(x),&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\eta
denotes the unit outer normal vector to
\partial\Omega
whilst
u
and
v
stand for the concentrations
of the activator and the inhibitor respectively. System (
1.1
) intends to provide a thorough explanation of symmetry breaking as well as of
de novo pattern formation
by virtue of the coupling of a local activation and a long-range inhibition process.
The two subpopulations move randomly with diffusion rates
D_{u}
and
D_{v}
and usually the inhibitor population
v
is much faster than the activator population
u.

The activator and inhibitor subpopulations die at rates
\lambda>0
and
\mu>0
, and proliferate at rates
k_{u}
and
k_{v}
, respectively. The inserted nonlinearities
\frac{u^{p}}{v^{q}},\frac{u^{m}}{v^{s}}
describe the fact that the activator promotes the differentiation process and it stimulates its own production, whereas the inhibitor acts a suppressant against the self-enhancing activator to prevent the unlimited growth. The power coefficients
p,q,m,s
in the proliferation terms model the strength of interactions between the two populations and satisfy the relations
p>1,q,m>0
and
s>-1,
cf.
[
17
,
18
]
. Furthermore, the term
\rho(x)\geq 0
is called
basic production term
for the activator subpopulation
u
and represents the amount of activator produced by cell in a unit time. It is assumed that
\rho\in C^{0,\gamma}(\overline{\Omega})
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Regarding the initial data, it is usually considered that
u_{0},v_{0}\in C^{2,\gamma}(\overline{\Omega}),\,u_{0}(x)>0,\;v_{0}(x)>0
in
\overline{\Omega}
and
\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial\eta}|_{\partial\Omega}=\frac{\partial v_{0}}{\partial\eta}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.

In the context of system (
1.1
), its dynamics can be effectively characterized by two key indices: the net self-activation index
\chi:=\frac{p-1}{m}
and the net cross-inhibition index
\delta:=\frac{q}{s+1}
.

The index
\chi
quantifies the interplay between the self-activation of the activator and the cross-activation of the inhibitor. A large value of
\chi
implies that the activator experiences significant net growth regardless of the influence of the inhibitor. Conversely, the index
\delta
measures the extent to which the inhibitor suppresses both the activator’s production and its own. When
\delta
is large, the inhibitor strongly suppresses the activator’s production, leading to a notable reduction in its activity.

From a biological and mathematical perspective, it is typically assumed that the parameters
p,q,m
, and
s
satisfy
\chi<\delta
which leads to the condition

(1.2)

\sigma:=\frac{mq}{(p-1)(s+1)}>1,

commonly referred to as the
Turing condition
. This condition ensures the emergence of Turing instability patterns, which play a crucial role in various natural and mathematical phenomena, as discussed in
[
17
,
18
,
33
]
. Under this setting, the existence of global solutions to (
1.1
) was obtained in
[
16
]
(see also
[
27
,
35
]
for some partial results on the exponents
p,q,m,s
). The
anti-Turing condition

\sigma\leq 1
is also relevant and appears in some recent mathematical studies
[
8
,
9
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. Nowadays, there is a vast literature around the Gierer-Meinhardt model and its steady-states in both bounded and unbounded domains, see
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
12
,
21
,
22
,
29
,
30
,
33
,
38
]
.

In an ecological framework, system (
1.1
) can be employed to model the coexistence of specialist and generalist species within a shared habitat—a phenomenon extensively documented in natural ecosystems, cf.
[
3
]
.
Several ecological mechanisms have been proposed to explain this coexistence, including habitat selection
[
31
]
, variations in dispersal rates
[
3
]
, and adaptive foraging behaviors in response to environmental fluctuations
[
39
]
. Among these, studies focusing on coexistence through differences in foraging strategies often consider systems with two distinct resources. For example, the authors in
[
39
]
investigated the dynamics of coexistence between generalist and specialist populations in an environment featuring dual resources. In
[
39
]
is highlighted the role of foraging strategies in shaping the interactions and stability of such populations.

In this context, the variable
u
represents the specialist subpopulation, which is characterized by a lower food consumption and higher motility measured again by the diffusion coefficient
D_{u}
and death rate equal to
\lambda.
On the other hand, the variable
v
corresponds to the generalist subpopulation, which requires a higher amount of food but has lower motility given by the diffusion coefficient
D_{v},
whilst it dies with rate equal to
\mu.

The enhanced motility exhibited by specialists enables them to search for and acquire food resources with greater efficiency compared to generalists. This advantage in foraging provides them with a significant competitive edge. This behavior can be mathematically represented through the reaction terms
\frac{u^{p}}{v^{q}},\frac{u^{m}}{v^{s}}
in (
1.1
), which encapsulate the dynamics of resource acquisition and utilization.
Despite this, both subpopulations depend on similar herbivorous food resources, creating a competitive yet interdependent dynamic within the ecosystem. This modeling framework facilitates the analysis of their interactions and provides insights into the ecological mechanisms that sustain their coexistence.

In the context of morphogenesis, the condition
D_{u}\ll D_{v},
which implies that inhibitors diffuse significantly faster than activators, is frequently emphasized. This disparity in mobility plays a critical role in pattern formation, as it satisfies the Turing conditions for instability in local systems like (
2.1
), ultimately driving morphogenetic processes. Conversely, in ecological settings, the condition
D_{u}\gg D_{v},

becomes more relevant, reflecting the greater mobility of specialists compared to generalists. However, recent studies (e.g.,
[
23
,
36
]
) suggest that while differing diffusion rates are crucial for Turing instability, other factors—such as reaction kinetics and specific parameter values—can also lead to pattern formation, even in systems where diffusion coefficients are comparable.

Classical formulations of the Gierer-Meinhardt model, as (
1.1
), often assume local interactions, which may not fully represent the spatially distributed nature of biological processes such as morphogen signals or tissue growth. Introducing non-local proliferation rates for the activator enriches the model by accounting for spatial averaging effects or long-range interactions, which are critical in phenomena like stem cell regulation or tumor growth dynamics. This extension aligns with experimental observations that highlight the significance of non-local feedback mechanisms in driving emergent patterns in various biological systems (
[
28
,
32
]
). By exploring these non-local effects, the model gains enhanced realism, offering novel insights into developmental processes and aiding in the design of bio-inspired systems. Resource consumption patterns within populations can exhibit variation based on the motility of individuals. Specifically, less motile individuals typically engage in local consumption, where interactions and resource consumption occur primarily within a limited range. On the other hand, more motile individuals are characteristic of non-local consumption, where their interactions and resource consumption extend over larger spatial areas
[
1
]
. This non-local behavior has significant implications for competing populations, as it introduces long-range effects into the competitive dynamics. The impact of non-local resource consumption on population interactions has been further explored in works such as
[
2
]
, highlighting the complexity of ecological interactions when spatial structure and motility are considered.

Motivated by the considerations outlined above, and under the assumption that the boundary effects of the interaction region have a minimal influence on the overall dynamics of the populations, we introduce the following non-local Gierer-Meinhardt system

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle u_{t}-D_{u}\Delta u+\lambda u=\frac{J*u^{p}}{v^{q}}+\rho(x)&\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty)\,,N\geq 1,\\[7.22743pt]
\displaystyle v_{t}-D_{v}\Delta v+\mu v=\frac{J*u^{m}}{v^{s}}&\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty),\\[7.22743pt]
u(x,t),v(x,t)\to 0&\mbox{ as }|x|\to\infty,\,\mbox{for any}\;t>0,\end{cases}

where
0<\rho\in C^{0,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
with
p>1,
and
q,m,s,\lambda,\mu>0
.
This model builds upon the foundational framework of Gierer-Meinhart systems, incorporating non-local interactions to better capture the spatially extended influence of cell/population dynamics. In doing so, we account for the broader biological/ecological context, where interactions between cells/species are not confined to their immediate vicinity but instead extend over larger spatial scales, reflecting the influence of long-range biological/ecological effects. This extension allows for a more realistic representation of cells/species interactions, particularly when considering the motility and dispersal capabilities of individuals within the population. The diffusion coefficients
D_{u},D_{v}
are assumed to be of comparable magnitude. For the sake of simplicity, throughout this work, they are further simplified by setting
D_{u}=D_{v}=1.

It is worth noting that non-local interactions have already been incorporated into the Fisher-KPP equation
[
24
]
and various cancer models
[
20
,
25
,
26
]
to provide a more accurate representation of the underlying biological phenomena.

Turning back to the model (
1.3
), the interaction terms
\frac{J*u^{p}}{u^{q}}
and
\frac{J*u^{m}}{u^{s}}
represent the non-local effects, where the growth of each population depends not only on local interactions but also on the presence of the competing cells/species at distant locations. The star symbol in (
1.3
) refers to the convolution operation in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, that is,

J*u^{p}(x)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-z)u^{p}(z)dz\quad\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

and
J*u^{m}(x)
is defined similarly.
This mechanism reflects the idea that more motile individuals, such as the specialists in the model, have an extended influence on the distribution and consumption of resources, affecting populations over a larger spatial domain, cf.
[
2
]
. In the context of morphogenesis, this non-local mechanism underscores the critical role of spatially distributed signal processes, where interactions extend beyond immediate neighbors, in driving the emergence of complex biological patterns and structures, cf.
[
18
]
.

Moreover, the environmental heterogeneity introduced by
\rho(x),
which represents spatially varying resource availability, further emphasizes the non-local nature of resource interactions.

2
Main results

In the current work, we focus on the study of the steady-state problem of (
1.3
), given by the following system and in any spatial dimensions
N\geq 1
,

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\Delta u+\lambda u=\frac{J*u^{p}}{v^{q}}+\rho(x)&\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\,,\\[7.22743pt]
\displaystyle-\Delta v+\mu v=\frac{J*u^{m}}{v^{s}}&\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\\[7.22743pt]
u(x),v(x)\to 0&\mbox{ as }|x|\to\infty,\end{cases}

where
\rho\in C^{0,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
is positive and the exponents
p>1,q,m,s>0
are assumed to satisfy the
anti-Turing condition

\sigma:=\frac{mq}{(p-1)(s+1)}\leq 1.

The convolution kernel
J\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\})
is positive and there exist
c>0
and
\theta\in(0,N)
such that

(2.2)

J(z)\leq c|z|^{\theta-N}\,,\quad|\nabla J(z)|\leq c|z|^{\theta-N-1}\quad\mbox{ for all }z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}.

We are looking for positive classical solutions of (
2.1
), that is, functions
u,v\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that:

•

u,v>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
;

•

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have

(2.3)

(J*u^{p})(x)<\infty\quad\mbox{ and }\quad(J*u^{m})(x)<\infty;

•

u,v
satisfy (
2.1
) pointwise.

The local version of (
2.1
), in which
J*u^{p}
and
J*u^{m}
are replaced by
u^{p}
and
u^{m}
respectively, was discussed in
[
8
]
where it is shown that anti-Turing condition
\sigma<1
is necessary for solutions to exhibit an exponential decay at infinity, see in particular
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
. Unlike the approach in
[
8
]
, which relies heavily on integral representation formulae for solutions of the Laplace and Schrödinger equation that hold in dimensions
N\geq 3
, in the current work we resort to various integral estimates, motivated by the presence of the non-local convolution terms in (
2.1
). This gives us the advantage to study the system (
2.1
) in all space dimensions
N\geq 1
.

To analyze the problem (
2.1
), we will initially focus on the scenario where
J\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Given that
u(x),v(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty,
it becomes straightforward to verify that condition (
2.3
) holds in this case.

We assume that the kernel
J
satisfies one of the following conditions:

(E)\;
(
J
has exponential decay):

There exists
M_{J}>0
such that for all
0<b<M_{J}
we have

(2.4)

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)e^{-b|y|}dy\leq C(J,N,b)e^{-b|x|}\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

or

(P)\;
(
J
has power decay):

For all
0<b<N
we have

(2.5)

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)|y|^{-b}dy\leq C(J,N,b)(1+|x|)^{-b}\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

As we shall see in Lemma
3.2
below, the kernel

J(y)=(1+|y|)^{-\alpha}e^{-\beta|y|}\quad\mbox{ with }\;\alpha\geq 0\,,\beta>0,

satisfies condition
(E)
with

, while the kernel

J(y)=(1+|y|)^{-\alpha}\quad\mbox{ with }\;\alpha>N,

satisfies condition

but not condition

above.

to denote that the quotient

is bounded in

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)e^{-b|y|}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(1+|x-y|)^{-\alpha}e^{-\beta|x-y|-b|y|}dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{-\beta|x-y|-b|y|}dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{-\beta|x-y|-b(|x|-|x-y|)}dy

\displaystyle=e^{-b|x|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{-(\beta-b)|x-y|}dy

\displaystyle=e^{-b|x|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{-(\beta-b)|z|}dz

\displaystyle=Ce^{-b|x|}.

(ii) Take
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and let us fix
\delta>0
such that
a+b<\delta<M_{J}
. We decompose

(3.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)(1+|y|)^{-a}e^{-b|y|}dy:=A_{1}+A_{2},

where

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{|y|>|x|}J(x-y)(1+|y|)^{-a}e^{-b|y|}dy,

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{|y|\leq|x|}J(x-y)(1+|y|)^{-a}e^{-b|y|}dy.

To estimate
A_{1}
we use the fact that
J\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(1+|x|)^{-a}e^{-b|x|}\int_{|y|>|x|}J(x-y)dy

\displaystyle\leq(1+|x|)^{-a}e^{-b|x|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(z)dz

\displaystyle=C(1+|x|)^{-a}e^{-b|x|}.

To estimate
A_{2}
we use the fact that
[0,\infty)\ni t\longmapsto(1+t)^{-a}e^{(\delta-b)t}
is increasing (since
\alpha+\beta<\delta
) and we have

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{|y|\leq|x|}J(x-y)(1+|y|)^{-a}e^{(\delta-b)|y|}e^{-\delta|y|}dy

\displaystyle\leq(1+|x|)^{-a}e^{(\delta-b)|x|}\int_{|y|\leq|x|}J(x-y)e^{-\delta|y|}dy

\displaystyle\leq(1+|x|)^{-a}e^{(\delta-b)|x|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)e^{-\delta|y|}dy.

Using the hypothesis (
2.4
) for
b=\delta
we find

(3.6)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+|x|)^{-a}e^{(\delta-b)|x|}e^{-\delta|x|}

\displaystyle=C(1+|x|)^{-a}e^{-b|x|}.

Using (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) into (
3.4
) we deduce the upper bound in the estimate (
3.2
). For the lower bound we further estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)(1+|y|)^{-a}e^{-b|y|}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(y)(1+|x-y|)^{-a}e^{-b|x-y|}dy

\displaystyle\geq\int_{|y|<1}J(y)(1+|x-y|)^{-a}e^{-b|x-y|}dy

\displaystyle\geq\int_{|y|<1}J(y)(1+|x|+|y|)^{-a}e^{-b(|x|+|y|)}dy

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-a}(1+|x|)^{-a}e^{-b|x|}\int_{|y|<1}J(y)e^{-b|y|}dy

\displaystyle=C(1+|x|)^{-a}e^{-b|x|}.

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

(i)

The kernel
J(y)=(1+|y|)^{-\alpha}
,
\alpha>N
, satisfies condition
(P)
but not condition
(E)
.

(ii)

Suppose the kernel
J
satisfies condition
(P)
and let
f\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be such that

f(x)\simeq(1+|x|)^{-a}\quad\mbox{ for some }0<a<N.

Then, there exist two constants
C_{0}>c_{0}>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have

(3.7)

c_{0}(1+|x|)^{-a}\leq\int\limits_{|y|<1}J(y)f(x-y)dy\leq(J*f)(x)\leq C_{0}(1+|x|)^{-a}.

In particular,

(3.8)

(J*f)(x)\simeq(1+|x|)^{-a}.

Proof.

(i) Assume
J(y)=(1+|y|)^{-\alpha}
,
\alpha>N
, and let
0<b<N
. We decompose

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)|y|^{-b}dy=\Big\{\int\limits_{|y|<\frac{|x|}{2}}+\int\limits_{\frac{|x|}{2}\leq|y|<2|x|}+\int\limits_{|y|\geq 2|x|}\Big\}J(x-y)|y|^{-b}dy:=B_{1}+B_{2}+B_{3}.

To estimate
B_{1}
we use

|y|<\frac{|x|}{2}\Longrightarrow|x-y|\geq|x|-|y|\geq\frac{|x|}{2}
.
Thus,

(3.9)

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{|y|<\frac{|x|}{2}}(1+|x-y|)^{-\alpha}|y|^{-b}dy

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+\frac{|x|}{2}\Big)^{-\alpha}\int\limits_{|y|<\frac{|x|}{2}}|y|^{-b}dy

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|)^{-\alpha}|x|^{N-b}

\displaystyle\leq C|x|^{-b}\;,\quad\mbox{ since }\alpha>N.

To estimate
B_{2}
we use

|y|<2|x|\Longrightarrow|x-y|\leq 3|x|
and then

(3.10)

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{|x-y|<3|x|}(1+|x-y|)^{-\alpha}\Big(\frac{|x|}{2}\Big)^{-b}dy

\displaystyle=C|x|^{-b}\int\limits_{0}^{3|x|}(1+t)^{-\alpha}t^{N-1}dt

\displaystyle\leq C|x|^{-b}\,,\quad\mbox{ since $\alpha>N$}.

Next, to estimate
B_{3}
we use

|y|\geq 2|x|\Longrightarrow|x-y|\geq|y|-|x|\geq\frac{|y|}{2}
and we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle B_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{|y|\geq 2|x|}\Big(1+\frac{|y|}{2}\Big)^{-\alpha}|y|^{-b}dy

\displaystyle\leq(2|x|)^{-b}\int\limits_{2|x|}^{\infty}\Big(1+\frac{t}{2}\Big)^{-\alpha}t^{N-1}dt

\displaystyle\leq C|x|^{-b}\,,\quad\mbox{ since $\alpha>N$}.

Combining (
3.9
), (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) we deduce (
2.5
).
However,
J
cannot satisfy condition
(E)
since for
|x|>1
we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)e^{-b|y|}dy

\displaystyle\geq\int\limits_{|y|<1}(1+2|x|)^{-\alpha}e^{-b|y|}dy

\displaystyle\geq(3|x|)^{-\alpha}\int\limits_{|y|<1}e^{-b|y|}dy\geq c|x|^{-\alpha}.

Thus, (
2.4
) cannot hold.

(ii) As in the proof of Lemma
3.2
we have

\displaystyle(J*f)(x)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(y)f(x-y)dy

\displaystyle\geq C\int_{|y|<1}J(y)(1+|x|+|y|)^{-a}dy

\displaystyle\geq C\int_{|y|<1}J(y)(2+|x|)^{-a}dy\geq C(1+|x|)^{-a}.

If
|x|\leq 1
, then the upper bound in (
3.7
) holds since

\displaystyle(J*f)(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)(1+|y|)^{-a}dy

\displaystyle\leq C\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)dy=C\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(z)dz

\displaystyle<C\leq 2^{a}C(1+|x|)^{-a}.

To check that the upper bound in (

) holds for

, we decompose

(3.12)

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(x-y)f(y)dy=\Big\{\int\limits_{|y|<|x|}+\int\limits_{|y|\geq|x|}\Big\}J(x-y)f(y)dy:=D_{1}+D_{2}.

To estimate
D_{1}
we let
0<a<b<N
and since
|x|>1
we have

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle\leq c\int\limits_{|y|<|x|}J(x-y)(1+|y|)^{-a}dy

\displaystyle=c\int\limits_{|y|<|x|}J(x-y)(1+|y|)^{-b}(1+|y|)^{b-a}dy

\displaystyle\leq c(1+|x|)^{b-a}\int\limits_{|y|<|x|}J(x-y)|y|^{-b}dy\quad\mbox{ since $b>a$}

\displaystyle\leq c(1+|x|)^{b-a}|x|^{-b}\quad\mbox{ by property $(P)$}

\displaystyle\leq c(1+|x|)^{-a}\,,\quad\mbox{ since }|x|>1.

To estimate
D_{2}
we also have

\displaystyle D_{2}

\displaystyle\leq c\int\limits_{|y|\geq|x|}J(x-y)(1+|y|)^{-a}dy

\displaystyle=c(1+|x|)^{-a}\int\limits_{|y|\geq|x|}J(x-y)dy

\displaystyle\leq c(1+|x|)^{-a}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(z)dz\leq c(1+|x|)^{-a}.

Using the last two estimates into (
3.12
) we deduce the upper bound for
J*f
in (
3.7
).

The problem (

This part is devoted to the study of the problem (

We first state the following comparison results, which are fairly standard. We include their proofs for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an open and bounded set,
s\geq 0
,
\mu>0
,
K\in C(\overline{\Omega})
be a positive function and
w_{1},w_{2}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
be such that

\begin{cases}-\Delta w_{1}+\mu w_{1}-K(x)w_{1}^{-s}\geq 0\geq-\Delta w_{2}+\mu w_{2}-K(x)w_{2}^{-s}\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\[5.69046pt]
\;\;\;w_{1},w_{2}>0\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\[5.69046pt]
\;\;\;\displaystyle w_{1}\geq w_{2}\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Then,
w_{1}\geq w_{2}
in
\overline{\Omega}
.

Proof.

Suppose by contradiction that the set
E=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:w_{1}(x)<w_{2}(x)\}
is nonempty. Then
w_{1}-w_{2}
has a minimum over
\overline{\Omega}
, say, at
x_{0}
, which is located in
E
. At this point, we have
\Delta(w_{1}-w_{2})(x_{0})\geq 0
. Using the conditions in the hypothesis we find

0>-\Delta(w_{1}-w_{2})(x_{0})+\mu(w_{1}-w_{2})(x_{0})\geq K(x_{0})(w_{1}^{-s}(x_{0})-w_{2}^{-s}(x_{0}))\geq 0,

which is a contradiction. Hence,
E=\emptyset
and thus
w_{1}\geq w_{2}
in
\overline{\Omega}
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
s\geq 0
,
\mu>0
,
K\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a positive function and
w_{1},w_{2}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be such that

\begin{cases}-\Delta w_{1}+\mu w_{1}-K(x)w_{1}^{-s}\geq 0\geq-\Delta w_{2}+\mu w_{2}-K(x)w_{2}^{-s}\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\\[5.69046pt]
\;\;\;w_{1},w_{2}>0\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\\[5.69046pt]
\;\;\;\displaystyle\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}(w_{1}(x)-w_{2}(x)\big)\geq 0.\end{cases}

Then,
w_{1}\geq w_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}(w_{1}(x)-w_{2}(x))\geq 0
, there exists
R>0
such that

w_{1}+\varepsilon\geq w_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{R}
.
On the other hand,
w_{1}+\varepsilon
and
w_{2}
satisfy the conditions of Lemma
3.5
in
\Omega=B_{R}
. Applying this result it follows that
w_{1}+\varepsilon\geq w_{2}
in
B_{R}
. Hence,
w_{1}+\varepsilon\geq w_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrarily taken, this entails
w_{1}\geq w_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
∎

Let
\phi(x)=(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and for all
a,b\geq 0
denote

(3.14)

\Psi_{a,b}(x)=\phi(x)^{-a}e^{-b\phi(x)}\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
a,b\geq 0
and

(3.15)

\lambda>\max\{2a+2(a+b)^{2},N(a+b)\}.

Then,

(3.16)

\frac{\lambda}{2}\Psi_{a,b}\leq-\Delta\Psi_{a,b}+\lambda\Psi_{a,b}\leq 2\lambda\Psi_{a,b}\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Proof.

By direct calculations we have

(3.17)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta\Psi_{a,b}+\lambda\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\lambda-b^{2}+\frac{b(N-2a-1)}{\phi}+\frac{b^{2}+a(N-a-2)}{\phi^{2}}+\frac{b(2a+1)}{\phi^{3}}+\frac{a(a+2)}{\phi^{4}}\Big\}\Psi_{a,b}.

Since
\phi\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle-\Delta\Psi_{a,b}+\lambda\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\{\lambda-b^{2}+\frac{b(N-2a-1)+b(2a+1)}{\phi}+\frac{b^{2}+a(N-a-2)+a(a+2)}{\phi^{2}}\Big\}\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\lambda-b^{2}+\frac{bN}{\phi}+\frac{b^{2}+aN}{\phi^{2}}\Big\}\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\{\lambda-b^{2}+bN+b^{2}+aN\Big\}\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\lambda+N(a+b)\Big\}\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda\Psi_{a,b}\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

This concludes the proof of the upper bound in (
3.16
). For the lower bound we note that the quantity in (
3.17
) is increasing with respect to
N\geq 1
. Thus:

\displaystyle-\Delta\Psi_{a,b}+\lambda\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle\geq\Big\{\lambda-b^{2}-\frac{2ab}{\phi}+\frac{b^{2}-a-a^{2}}{\phi^{2}}+\frac{b(2a+1)}{\phi^{3}}+\frac{a(a+2)}{\phi^{4}}\Big\}\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle\geq\Big\{\lambda-b^{2}-\frac{2ab}{\phi}-\frac{a+a^{2}}{\phi^{2}}\Big\}\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle\geq\Big\{\lambda-b^{2}-2ab-a-a^{2}\Big\}\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\lambda-(a+b)^{2}-a\Big\}\Psi_{a,b}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}\Psi_{a,b}\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
s\geq 0
,
\mu>0
and
K\in C^{0,\gamma}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a positive function such that
K(x)\simeq(1+|x|)^{-a}e^{-b|x|}
for some

.
Then, for all

(3.18)

\mu>\max\left\{\frac{2a}{1+s}+2\Big(\frac{a+b}{1+s}\Big)^{2},N\Big(\frac{a+b}{1+s}\Big)\right\},

the problem (
2.13
)
has a unique solution
w\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Moreover,

(3.19)

w(x)\simeq(1+|x|)^{-\frac{a}{1+s}}e^{-\frac{b}{1+s}|x|}.

Proof.

By our assumption on
K
, there exist constants
c_{2}>c_{1}>0
such that

c_{1}\Psi_{a,b}\leq K\leq c_{2}\Psi_{a,b}\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Define

D=\left(\frac{2c_{2}}{\mu}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+s}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad d=\left(\frac{c_{1}}{2\mu}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+s}}

and set

\overline{w}=D\Psi_{\frac{a}{1+s},\frac{b}{1+s}}\,,\quad\underline{w}=d\Psi_{\frac{a}{1+s},\frac{b}{1+s}}.

By the results in Lemma
3.7
we have

-\Delta\overline{w}+\mu\overline{w}\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\overline{w}=\frac{\mu}{2}D\Psi_{\frac{a}{1+s},\frac{b}{1+s}}=c_{2}\Psi_{a,b}\Big(D\Psi_{\frac{a}{1+s},\frac{b}{1+s}}\Big)^{-s}\geq K(x)\overline{w}^{-s}\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

This shows that
\overline{w}
is a super-solution of (
2.13
). Similarly,

-\Delta\underline{w}+\mu\underline{w}\leq 2\mu\underline{w}=2\mu d\Psi_{\frac{a}{1+s},\frac{b}{1+s}}=c_{1}\Psi_{a,b}\Big(d\Psi_{\frac{a}{1+s},\frac{b}{1+s}}\Big)^{-s}\leq K(x)\underline{w}^{-s}\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Hence,
\underline{w}
is a sub-solution of (
2.13
).
For all
n\geq 1
consider the problem

(3.20)

\begin{cases}-\Delta w+\mu w=K(x)w^{-s},w>0&\quad\mbox{ in }B_{n},\\
\;\;\;w(x)=\overline{w}&\quad\mbox{ on }\partial B_{n}.\end{cases}

Then
\underline{w}
and
\overline{w}
are respectively sub and supersolutions to (

[

), it follows that (
3.20
) has a solution

3.6

\underline{w}\leq w_{n+1}\leq w_{n}\leq\overline{w}

2.13
). The asymptotic behaviour (

) follows from

in
\mathbb{R}^{N}

.

) we have

\displaystyle ap-Aq-\theta

\displaystyle=ap-\frac{am-\theta}{s+1}q-\theta

\displaystyle=ap-\frac{amq}{s+1}-\theta\Big(1-\frac{q}{s+1}\Big)

\displaystyle=ap-a(p-1)\sigma-\theta\Big(1-\frac{q}{s+1}\Big)

\displaystyle=a+a(p-1)(1-\sigma)-\theta\Big(1-\frac{q}{s+1}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq a.

Thus, by the above estimates, we deduce

\frac{J*u^{p}}{v^{q}}+\rho(x)\simeq\Phi_{a}.

Also,
J*u^{m}\simeq\Phi_{am-\theta}
. From now on one follows line by line the proof of Theorem
2.2
. ∎

7
Discussion

In this paper we studied the positive steady-state solutions to a reaction-difusion system that originates from the model of Gierer and Meinhardt
[
14
]
in 1972. Our goal was to investigate the influence of the non-local terms
J*u^{p}
and
J*u^{m}
in this model, which are included to better illustrate the biological phenomena.

In the frame of the anti-Turing condition
0<\sigma\leq 1
we provided a general setting that contains a broad classes of kernels
J\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\})
such as

J(y)=(1+|y|)^{-\alpha}
,
J(y)=(1+|y|)^{-\alpha}e^{-\beta|y|}
and
J(y)=|y|^{-\alpha}
. Our findings reveal that the various integrability conditions imposed on
J
impact both the range of exponents
p,q,m,s
as well as the death rates
\lambda
and
\mu
for which a solution exists. This is more visible if we assume a low order of integrability of the kernel
J
as it was noted in Theorem
2.3
where
J\not\in L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for all
r\geq 1
. In all situations discussed in the present article, the range of death rates
\lambda
and
\mu
is given by the curve (
2.8
); this was also observed in the local case discussed in
[
8
]
. We pointed out that each situation discussed in Theorems
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
yields different values of the constants
C,c>0
in (
2.8
). Moreover, these variations can be compared with the findings in
[
8
]
, which provide an alternative perspective on how the non-local terms influence the existence of solutions of the non-local problem (
2.1
).

Appendix: Proof of Lemma
3.1

In this section, we prove Lemma
3.1
by providing all details of the estimate (
3.1
).

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
x\neq y
. For
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we decompose

\mathcal{T}f(\zeta)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J(z-\zeta)f(z)dz:=\mathcal{T}_{1}(\zeta)+\mathcal{T}_{2}(\zeta),

where

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}(\zeta)=\int\limits_{|z-x|<2|x-y|}J(z-\zeta)f(z)dz\\[28.90755pt]
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2}(\zeta)=\int\limits_{|z-x|\geq 2|x-y|}J(z-\zeta)f(z)dz\end{cases}\mbox{ for all }\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Using (
2.2
) and Hölder’s inequality we estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{|z-x|<2|x-y|}J(z-x)f(z)dz

\displaystyle\leq C\int\limits_{|z-x|<2|x-y|}|z-x|^{\theta-N}f(z)dz

\displaystyle\leq C\biggl(\int\limits_{|z-x|<2|x-y|}\big|z-x\big|^{(\theta-N)\frac{r}{r-1}}dz\biggl)^{\frac{r-1}{r}}\biggl(\int\limits_{|z-x|<2|x-y|}|f(y)|^{r}dy\biggl)^{\frac{1}{r}}

\displaystyle=C\biggl(\int\limits_{0}^{2|x-y|}t^{(\theta-N)\frac{r}{r-1}}t^{N-1}dt\biggl)^{\frac{r-1}{r}}\|f\|_{r}.

Since
(\theta-N)\frac{r}{r-1}+N>0
we deduce

(7.1)

\mathcal{T}_{1}(x)\leq C(N,\theta,r)\big|x-y\big|^{\theta-\frac{N}{r}}\|f\|_{r}.

We proceed similarly to deduce

(7.2)

\mathcal{T}_{1}(y)\leq C\big|x-y\big|^{\theta-\frac{N}{r}}\|f\|_{r}.

Next, we observe that

(7.3)

|\mathcal{T}_{2}(x)-\mathcal{T}_{2}(y)|\leq\int\limits_{|z-x|\geq 2|x-y|}\big|J(z-x)-J(z-y)\big|f(z)dz.

We apply the Mean Value Theorem to
g(\zeta)=J(z-\zeta)
over the segment
[x,y]\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Combining this fact with (
2.2
) we find

(7.4)

\displaystyle\big|J(z-x)-J(z-y)\big|

\displaystyle=|g(x)-g(y)|

\displaystyle=|\nabla g(\xi)||x-y|\quad\mbox{ for some }\xi\in[x,y]

\displaystyle=|\nabla J(z-\xi)||x-y|

\displaystyle\leq C|z-\xi|^{\theta-N-1}|x-y|.

From
|z-x|\geq 2|x-y|
and
\xi\in[x,y]
we find

|z-\xi|\geq|z-x|-|x-\xi|\geq|z-x|-|x-y|\geq\frac{|z-x|}{2}.

Using this last estimate in (
7.4
) and then in (
7.3
) we deduce

|\mathcal{T}_{2}(x)-\mathcal{T}_{2}(y)|\leq C|x-y|\int\limits_{|z-x|\geq 2|x-y|}\big|z-x\big|^{\theta-N-1}f(z)dz.

Next, by Hölder inequality we further estimate

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}_{2}(x)-\mathcal{T}_{2}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq C|x-y|\int\limits_{|z-x|\geq 2|x-y|}\big|z-x\big|^{\theta-N-1}f(z)dz

\displaystyle\leq C|x-y|\biggl(\int\limits_{|z-x|\geq 2|x-y|}\big|x-z\big|^{(\theta-N-1)\frac{r}{r-1}}dz\biggl)^{\frac{r-1}{r}}\|f\|_{r}

\displaystyle=C|x-y|\biggl(\int\limits_{2|x-y|}^{\infty}t^{(\theta-N-1)\frac{r}{r-1}}t^{N-1}dt\biggl)^{\frac{r-1}{r}}\|f\|_{r}.

Since
(\theta-N-1)\frac{r}{r-1}+N<0
we deduce

(7.5)

|\mathcal{T}_{2}(x)-\mathcal{T}_{2}(y)|\leq C(N,\theta,q)\big|x-y\big|^{\theta-\frac{N}{r}}\|f\|_{r}.

Finally, from (
7.1
), (
7.2
) and (
7.5
) we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}f(x)-\mathcal{T}f(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{T}_{1}(x)+\mathcal{T}_{1}(y)+|\mathcal{T}_{2}(x)-\mathcal{T}_{2}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq C(N,\theta,r)\big|x-y\big|^{\theta-\frac{N}{r}}\|f\|_{r},

which concludes the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
