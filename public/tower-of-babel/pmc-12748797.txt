Deep learning-based non-contrast MRI model for nasopharyngeal carcinoma diagnosis: an end-to-end gadolinium-free solution
Authors: Zhen Li, Yiqian Shi, Li Wang, Jiayu Lu, Xueli Liu, Jonghye Woo, Jingsong Ouyang, Jiyi Hu, Di Zhou, Wentao Gong, Xiaojing Yang, Haoyan Yu, Yongkang Wang, Feidi Liu, Yafei Dong, Meng Ye, Shang Shi, Xiao Chen, Mingyan Qiu, Georges El Fakhri, Lin Kong, Xicai Sun, Yefeng Zheng, Yuxuan Shi, Hongmeng Yu, Xiaofeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) diagnosis and routine follow-up for recurrence typically rely on contrast-enhanced MRI. This study introduces a deep learning model for diagnosing NPC using only non-contrast MRI, reducing the need for gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCA). This approach helps avoid safety concerns related to GBCA deposition, while also shortening scan times and reducing costs. In this study, we propose an innovative deep learning model for NPC diagnosis using only non-contrast MRI, reducing the need for gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCA). This approach not only mitigates potential safety concerns associated with residual GBCA deposition but also reduces scan time and examination costs. The study consisted of three phases. Firstly, a novel knowledge distilled modality fusion model is developed using a cohort of 854 cases and tested its performance on an internal set (257 cases, AUC = 0.95) and an independent external set (277 cases, AUC = 0.86). Secondly, the proposed method was compared with: (1) Non-contrast MRI without model improvement (Baseline 1) and (2) current virtual-contrast enhancement-based NPC diagnosis using three state-of-the-art methods (Baselines 2-4). The proposed model consistently outperformed Baselines 1 on both internal dataset (AUC: 0.95 vs. 0.93) and external test set (AUC: 0.86 vs. 0.82). Additionally, it surpassed Baselines 2-4, achieving performance gains of 6.7%, 69.8%, and 28.6% in AUC, over three state-of-the-art methods. Thirdly, the effectiveness of this model was evaluated through a fully crossed multi-reader, multi-case study involving 13 readers from 6 hospitals. The results showed that with AI assistance, readers could diagnose NPC using only non-contrast MRI, achieving results that were not inferior to contrast-enhanced imaging (AUC: 0.90 vs. 0.93, p<0.01). In conclusion, this study demonstrated the model’s potential as a safe, cost-effective, and GBCA-free option for NPC diagnosis in clinical practice.Subject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is an epithelial malignancy originating from the nasopharyngeal mucosa1,2. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been established as the preferred modality for NPC diagnosis due to superior soft tissue visualization3–5 and its capability of detecting more endoscopically occult NPC2,6,7. MRI demonstrates high diagnostic performance, with a reported pooled sensitivity of 98.1% (95% CI: 95.2 -99.3%) and specificity of 91.7% (95% CI: 88.3 -94.2%)8. Routine MRI protocols typically include non-contrast T1-weighted (T1), T2-weighted (T2) sequences, and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (T1c) sequence9–13, each providing crucial anatomical information about the nasopharyngeal region. Among these, T1c imaging with gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs) is particularly crucial, as it enhances the detection of tumor necrosis, hemorrhage, and cystic changes, which aids in distinguishing NPC from conditions such as adenoid hypertrophy and follicular hyperplasia8,14–16.

However, patients with chronic kidney disease are at risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis from linear GBCAs, prompting their withdrawal in several European countries since 201717. Macrocyclic GBCAs are considered safer, especially in individuals with mild-to-moderate renal impairment, but concerns persist regarding gadolinium retention. The American College of Radiology (ACR) acknowledges that all GBCAs—including macrocyclic agents—can result in residual gadolinium deposition in the brain (e.g., dentate nucleus, globus pallidus) and other tissues18, and this deposition can lead to uncertain long-term safety concerns. Thus, the ACR recommends cautious use, particularly in vulnerable groups such as children, pregnant patients, and those requiring repeated dosing. In addition, both ACR and the European Society of Urogenital Radiology support reducing GBCA use when non-contrast imaging provides adequate diagnostic value19. This is especially important for NPC patients who need lifelong MRI monitoring. Repeated use of contrast agents highlights the need for GBCA-sparing alternatives.

To reduce GBCA exposure, contrast-free virtual contrast enhancement (VCE) synthesis techniques have been developed, showing promise in synthesizing virtual contrast MRI from non-contrast MRI20–23 (Fig. 1, Method Two). While these methods offer potential for NPC diagnosis without GBCA risks, challenges remain. GBCA injection alters tissue appearance, complicating image synthesis24. Tumor diagnosis relies on subtle details, such as heterogeneous, irregular, or asymmetric enhancement and infiltrative margins, which comprise only a small fraction of pixels in the entire image set. These critical details can be lost during synthesis25. Thus, synthesized contrast images may lack reliability and fail to establish precise correlations with non-contrast images in the deep learning feature space.

Comparison of methods for NPC diagnosis without GBCA in inference stage.

All methods utilize non-contrast MRI for NPC diagnosis. Method One: Non-contrast images are directly used for NPC diagnosis without model modification and improvement. Method Two: Virtual contrast-enhanced synthesis methods26–28 synthesize T1c images in the first step, and the synthesized T1c are subsequently combined with non-contrast MRI for NPC diagnosis in the second step. Proposed Method: Contrast-enhanced images are utilized in teacher model training to direct the student network’s attention toward key features essential for NPC diagnosis, thereby establishing cross-modal relationships between non-contrast and contrast-enhanced MRI. The student model is employed during testing and clinical application, eliminating the need for contrast-enhanced MRI. Notably, for all three methods, T1c is not required during clinical deployment.

To resolve these issues, the study proceeded in three distinct stages. First, an end-to-end deep learning method was proposed for NPC diagnosis using non-contrast MRI, aiming to achieve diagnostic accuracy non-inferior to contrast-enhanced MRI models. Specifically, our knowledge distilled modality fusion model consists of three innovative blocks designed to effectively leverage information from contrast-enhanced MRI during training. These blocks include: (1) a Knowledge Distillation Block (KDB), which transfers diagnostic knowledge from a full-modality “teacher” model to the non-contrast “student” model; (2) a Cross-Feature Fusion Block (CFFB), which captures and integrates the intricate correlations between features from different imaging modalities; and (3) a Modality Contribution Weighting Block (MCWB), which dynamically learns the diagnostic importance of each modality to optimize the feature fusion process for the final classification. The model was developed using a cohort of 854 cases (427 NPC cases and 427 non-NPC cases). Its effectiveness was tested using an internal cohort (257 cases) and an independent external cohort (277 cases). Second, the proposed method was compared with: (1) Non-contrast MRI only without model modification (Baseline 1), to evaluate the improvements of the proposed model, and (2) current virtual-contrast enhancement-based diagnosis using three state-of-the-art methods (Baselines 2-4). Lastly, in the third stage, the model’s potential as a GBCA alternative solution in a clinical scenario was explored by evaluating its effectiveness in a fully-crossed multi-reader, multi-case (MRMC) study involving 13 readers from 6 hospitals.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 1111 individuals (mean age 53.8 ± 13.1 years; 61.9% male) were included in the internal study, and evaluated on an independent external test set: 277 cases (mean age 49.2 ± 15.6 years; 69.7% male). Details of patient characteristics, MRI scanner type and manufacturers, as well as data acquisition, are provided in Supplementary Information, Sections 4-6.

Discussion

This study introduces an end-to-end network for high-performance NPC diagnosis using only non-contrast MRI, offering a safer and more cost-effective alternative for clinical applications. The model achieved an accuracy of 0.90 [95% CI: 0.86-0.94], comparable to full-modality approaches (upper bound, 0.91 [95% CI: 0.87–0.95]) and outperforming traditional non-contrast MRI methods (Baseline 1, 0.84 [95% CI: 0.79–0.88]). The model’s success stems from leveraging contrast-enhanced MRI during training to guide feature learning, enabling the identification of diagnostically relevant features present in both contrast and non-contrast images. By sharing network training across both contrast and non-contrast images, the model establishes cross-modal feature associations, directing focuses to critical features difficult to detect in non-contrast MRI.

The proposed method outperformed existing two-step synthesis-based approaches26–28, which demonstrated inconsistent performance. This can be attributed to the fact that their generated MRIs prioritize overall image quality and generalization, rather than focusing specifically on tumor-related details. Consequently, subtle diagnostic features, such as small vessel supply, infiltration margins, and anatomical details around heterogeneous tumor regions, may be overlooked. In contrast, our method is specifically tailored to the nasopharyngeal area, allowing it to capture and emphasize essential features more effectively.

However, the model’s performance on external testing data shows a slight decline, likely due to discrepancies in image scanning parameters between the internal and external datasets. Specifically, the T2 sequence in the internal dataset utilized fat suppression, whereas the external test set did not, as detailed in Supplementary Information, Section 5. The data shift can potentially be mitigated with domain generalization technology, which is orthogonal to our contribution. Despite these challenges, the proposed method still outperforms Baseline 1, validating the effectiveness of our model. Additionally, an error analysis was conducted to identify the model’s limitations. The results showed that false positive and false negative cases were primarily due to benign hyperplasia being incorrectly identified as nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and early-stage NPC lesions, confined to the nasopharynx, being misclassified as benign hyperplasia (see Supplementary Information, Section 8).

A reader study was also involved in this study to evaluate whether physicians could achieve non-inferior diagnostic performance using only non-contrast images with AI assistance (T1+T2+AI), compared with the current state-of-the-art approach that combines contrast and non-contrast images (T1+T2+T1c). This evaluation was motivated by prior evidence showing that MRI offers high pooled sensitivity and specificity for detecting NPC, and may even aid lesion detection before endoscopic biopsy8. The T1+T2 setting is excluded as it does not reflect typical clinical practice, where radiologists do not diagnose based solely on non-contrast images; thus, comparing performance with T1 and T2 alone is not meaningful. The reader study provided evidence of non-inferiority for all readers across both settings. In subgroup analysis, ENT specialists generally outperformed general radiologists in diagnosing NPC, despite some general radiologists having more years of experience. This may be explained by the higher number of NPC cases encountered by ENT specialists, who see over 100 cases annually, compared to general radiologists who average 20 annually. However, ENT specialists did not show non-inferiority for sensitivity and AUC between the two settings, while all metrics for general radiologists did. This may primarily reflect limited statistical power due to smaller subgroup sample sizes, and could also be influenced by ENT specialists being more skeptical of AI-based diagnoses. By contrast, general radiologists, who may have less experience in diagnosing NPC, tend to rely more on AI results. Another reason is that AI’s accuracy is lower than that of ENT specialists but higher than that of general radiologists, making AI assistance particularly beneficial for the latter group. Taken together, these results suggest that the model could be especially beneficial for less experienced physicians.

Despite the generally safe profile of GBCAs, allergic reactions and gadolinium deposition can still occur. Responsible medical practice necessitates minimizing the administration of GBCAs through a rigorous risk versus reward assessment. In this study, a series of experiments demonstrated that, with assistance from our AI model, readers could achieve diagnostic performance that is non-inferior to that of using contrast-enhanced images. This approach has several potential clinical applications. (1) NPC patients typically require routine follow-up imaging throughout their lifetime5, resulting in repeated GBCA administration, potentially increasing the unknown risks associated with gadolinium accumulation. (2) Routine physical examinations typically involve non-contrast imaging, such as brain MRI. With the assistance of AI, physicians can achieve improved diagnostic accuracy. This approach holds promise for large-scale targeted screening of high-risk populations as well as opportunistic screening during routine check-ups. The gradual reduction in GBCA administration could lead to multiple clinical benefits, including improved patient safety by avoiding allergic reactions and residual gadolinium deposition, reduced scan times, and enhanced overall patient experience and throughput. Additionally, cost savings could be realized by eliminating the additional expenses related to contrast agents.

Despite the promising results, further research is needed to improve the applicability and generalizability of our approach. In the future, a large-scale, real-world evaluation and prospective validation is planned to assess its workflow design, adoption by radiologists and rhinologists, and long-term clinical outcomes. In addition, the current study utilized T1, T2, and T1c sequences. To further enhance diagnostic performance, additional imaging sequences, such as diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), are planned to be incorporated, as DWI has shown promise in detecting NPC29,30. Furthermore, integrating endoscopic and pathological report data as supplementary supervision is intended to enhance the model. By including features related to tumor shape, texture, size, and invasion depth, the model can capture more nuanced tumor characteristics, thereby improving both training and diagnostic accuracy. Previous work has also demonstrated the significant potential of combining endoscopic images and AI for NPC diagnosis31. A multi-modal approach is believed to offer a more comprehensive diagnostic framework, especially in clinical settings where contrast-enhanced imaging is not feasible. Second, the proposed model was trained on data from only two scanners of the same vendor at a single institution. This limited heterogeneity led to reduced performance when applied to the external test set. To improve generalizability, future work will incorporate multi-vendor, multi-site datasets and apply recently developed domain generalization and data harmonization techniques. Third, the current model is planned to be expanded beyond nasopharyngeal carcinoma to include other head and neck tumors, aiming to build a more versatile and comprehensive diagnostic tool. Fourth, NPC is often diagnosed at an advanced stage due to its asymptomatic nature in the early stages. Enhancing early detection is crucial. Therefore, future datasets will focus on including more T1-stage cases to improve model performance in this subset of patients. Overall, the proposed method represents a novel, gadolinium-free solution for NPC screening. It provides a viable alternative for routine follow-up and opportunistic screening during standard brain MRI examinations, with the potential to reduce contrast-agent-related risks and improve the cost-effectiveness of NPC management.

Methods

Data collection

For the training, validation, and internal testing datasets, cases were retrospectively collected from the internal center (Fudan University Eye & ENT Hospital) between January 2018 and December 2021. External testing datasets were obtained from Shanghai Proton and Heavy Ion Center. Data distribution and collection details are outlined in Fig. 5.

Data preprocessing

Rigid registration corrected misalignments from respiratory motion or inter-session movements among T1, T2, and T1c, ensuring precise alignment and detail continuity. Z-score normalization standardized image intensity to zero mean and unit variance. Images were resampled to 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 resolution and center-cropped or padded to 128 × 128 × 128 voxels for dataset uniformity and scanner compatibility.

Network architecture

Figure 6 provides an overview of the proposed knowledge distilled modality fusion network. Initially, a comprehensive 3D convolutional encoder and three 3D vision transformer (ViT) encoders32 were developed to extract features from all inputs. The 3D convolutional encoder is shared across both contrast-enhanced and non-contrast images, facilitating the learning of low-level common patterns and inter-modality correlations during parameter updates. Subsequently, three independent 3D ViT encoders are employed to capture modality-specific representations, which is particularly important given the substantial variability in tissue contrast across T1, T2, and T1c sequences. This design promotes robust feature learning that is tailored to each imaging modality. After encoding, three innovative blocks were introduced:

Illustration of the proposed knowledge distilled modality fusion network.

Both non-contrast and contrast images are included in the training, while only non-contrast images are included during testing. KDB Knowledge Distillation Block, CFFB Cross Feature Fusion Block, MCWB Modality Contribution Weighting Block.

(a) Knowledge Distillation Block (KDB): T1c provides essential features, such as blood supply, signal intensity, and tissue characteristics, crucial for diagnosing NPC. KDB is designed to address the absence of contrast information in non-contrast MRI sequences. By leveraging a full-modality teacher model (T1 + T2 + T1c) during training, KDB transfers critical diagnostic knowledge to single-modality student models (T1, T2, T1c). This enhances predictions, particularly for T1 and T2, where contrast information is missing, improving diagnostic accuracy without requiring contrast images during inference. Knowledge transfer is achieved by aligning student predictions Y^Si with teacher predictions Y^T using Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence loss:

Here, i indexes T1, T2, or T1c. KL divergence quantifies the information lost when Y^Si approximates Y^T.

A small constant ϵ = 10−5 is added to avoid division by zero and is empirically chosen to ensure numerical stability without affecting the output. A better approximation yields a smaller divergence, which improves the predictions of the student model.

(b) Cross-Feature Fusion Block (CFFB): CFFB captures long-range correlations between contrast and non-contrast features. It integrates features across three modality combinations using a binary mask M ∈ {0, 1}1×3, which randomly selects inputs for fusion. CFFB employs two branches: Fconv and Ftrans. The motivation to integrate both Fconv and Ftrans is driven by the complementary strengths of these two architectures. (1) Inter-Modality Transformer branch: It is specifically designed to learn and integrate correlated features across different modalities, generating Ftrans. Transformer is adept at modeling long-range dependencies and global correlations across features, a capability that is crucial for our task, as it enables the global capture of features from different MRI modalities (T1, T2, and T1c). Furthermore, in NPC patients, lymph node enlargement and metastasis—commonly associated with the disease—may extend beyond the nasopharyngeal region. This emphasizes the importance of global correlations in capturing distant relationships and accurately identifying the affected areas. (2) Convolution branch: It processes concatenated features to produce Fconv and enhances the inductive bias of the inter-modality Transformer33, improving its effectiveness in processing and representing complex features. The final fused representation combines these outputs via element-wise addition: F ″ = Ftrans ⊕ Fconv, and ⊕ denotes element-wise addition. This dual-branch structure ensures robust feature integration, especially in the absence of contrast-enhanced imaging data. An ablation study demonstrating the effectiveness of this design is provided in the Supplementary Information, Section 9.

(c) Modality Contribution Weighting Block (MCWB): MCWB integrates a modality-specific component to learn the contributions of each modality to the final predictions. This block quantifies and adjusts the contribution of T1, T2, and T1c in the fusion stage by a trainable modality-specific weighted average block. The design of this block is based on the fact that different modalities offer varying levels of diagnostic value when differentiating NPC signals from hyperplasia, necrosis, and radiation fibrosis. Each modality contributes differently, and thus requires distinct weighting. For instance, T1 demonstrates a lower signal in fibrosis, while T2 shows a higher signal in NPC. MCWB ensures that the model prioritizes the most diagnostically relevant features for NPC and further enhances performance. Model evaluation

To evaluate the model’s ability to effectively learn the relationship between contrast and non-contrast images and assess its overall performance, a series of comparative experiments were conducted. Two models, Baseline 1 and the full-modality model, were trained using only the basic architecture of the proposed method, excluding the specialized blocks designed to enhance learning (i.e., without the KDB, MCWB, and CFFB). Baseline 1 was trained exclusively on T1 and T2 inputs (Fig. 1, Method One). By comparing its performance with the performance of the proposed model, the improvements and effectiveness brought about by the proposed approach were evaluated. The full-modality model, trained and tested using both contrast-enhanced and non-contrast MRI, served as an upper bound for NPC diagnosis, offering a comprehensive benchmark for performance evaluation.

The method was further compared with VCE-based approaches. Since VCE only generates virtual contrast MRI, a diagnostic network was incorporated based on the generated contrast MRI for NPC diagnosis, resulting in a two-step synthesis method (Fig. 1, Method Two). For this comparison, three recent state-of-the-art methods were selected26–28, which represent the latest advancements in the field and offer a strong benchmark for evaluating the performance of our approach.

Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with its area under the curve (AUC) are used for evaluation (Detailed in Supplementary Information, Section 2). Ablation study was conducted to evaluate the impact of each model component by comparing the full model with versions excluding specific blocks.

Reader study

The aim of this fully-crossed, multi-reader multi-case reader study was to assess whether readers could achieve diagnostic performance non-inferior to that of contrast-enhanced MRI (the routine diagnostic method in clinical practice) when interpreting non-contrast MRI with AI assistance. All 257 cases from the internal test dataset were included in the reader study. A total of 13 readers participated in this study, including 5 from a leading national ENT specialty hospital and 8 readers from the radiology departments of general hospitals. Details of the readers can be found in Supplementary Information, Section 3. The study consisted of two reading sessions: (1) diagnosing using non-contrast MRI with AI-generated results as a reference (T1+T2+AI), and (2) diagnosing using both non-contrast and contrast-enhanced MRI (T1+T2+T1c). To minimize the influence of memory on diagnostic decisions, a 4-week washout period was implemented between sessions. All cases were anonymized and randomly ordered for each reading session.

Statistical analysis

For the standalone performance assessment, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were estimated using the Clopper-Pearson method34. DeLong’s method35 was used to compare AUC values and to calculate their corresponding 95% CIs. To account for both reader and case variability and to evaluate differences in diagnostic performance between the two reading settings (T1+T2+AI vs. T1+T2+T1c), the Dorfman-Berbaum-Metz (DBM) method36 was applied. A non-inferiority margin of -5% for AUC and -10% for both sensitivity and specificity was prespecified to assess whether the diagnostic performance of the T1+T2+AI setting was non-inferior to that of the T1+T2+T1c setting in the reader study. The reference standard for all assessments was biopsy for NPC cases and either biopsy or follow-up results for non-NPC cases. Readers and cases were modeled as random effects following a multivariate normal distribution. All evaluation metrics in the reader study were assessed for non-inferiority by verifying whether the lower limit of their two-sided 95% CIs exceeded the prespecified thresholds; corresponding p-values for the reader study were calculated using a one-sided test, with p < 0.025 considered statistically significant. Subgroup analyses were also conducted by stratifying readers according to hospital type and NPC cases according to T stage. Unless otherwise specified, all other statistical evaluations used two-sided tests, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.3) and RStudio (version 2025.05.1).
