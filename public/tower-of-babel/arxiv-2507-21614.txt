Metastability of 2D-material-on-metal-islands structures Preprint: AIP/123-QED
V.A. Ievleva, V. A. Prudkoglyad, L.A. Morgun, A. Yu. Kuntsevich

Abstract
The integration of 2D materials with artificially textured substrates offers exceptional opportunities for engineering novel functional devices. A straightforward technological route towards such devices is a mechanical dry or wet transfer of 2D layer or heterostructure onto prepared patterned elements with subsequent van der Waals bonding. Using hBN/graphene heterostructures transferred onto metallic island arrays as a model system, we reveal that thermal cycling between room and cryogenic temperatures leads to irreversible changes in electronic properties. This breakdown of reproducibility stems from the temperature-dependent redistribution of interfacial water or organic residues, which disrupts van der Waals bonding via a hydrophobic collapse mechanism. Our findings establish constraints for low-temperature applications of transferred 2D devices while providing insights into interfacial stability in van der Waals heterostructures.

While many 2D materials degrade rapidly under ambient conditions, certain compounds such as transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) sulfides, graphene, and hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) exhibit notable stability. Durability under variable environments is another critical factor for practical applications, yet remaining poorly understood for 2D materials and heterostructures. A common issue is the fragility of electrically sensitive VdWHs, that often does not let them survive more than one cryogenic measurement cycle.
In this work, we investigate the thermal cycling sustainability of graphene/hBN field effect structures transferred onto metallic electrodes. Through repeated cryogenic experiments, we demonstrate that metastability is a generic property of such systems. This finding not only imposes constraints on potential device applications, but also suggests that thermally induced phase transitions in 2D-on-textured-substrate systems could lead to novel physical states.

Preservance of almost the same resistivity values in the Nb/Pt sample after the second cooldown suggests that the electrical contacts to metal there were poor from the very beginning.

Loss of electrical contact between graphene and islands after the cooldown and heatup means that the van-der Waals bonds are violated. In the Nb/Pt sample a clean graphene, tensed between the islands and the substrate, lost its high mobility after the warm-up to room temperature. In the Re sample during the second cooldown the graphene lost both mobility and electrical connection to the islands. These observations suggest that a very thin water layer interposes between the graphene bottom surface and the substrate. Such layer would inevitably be presented at almost any surface at ambient conditions.

This idea, along with the first order nature of wetting-unwetting phase transition, allows to explain the observations.
Graphene itself is hydrophilic

Haigh et al. 2012
that allows to obtain extremely clean van der Waals heterostructures by mechanical assembly. A global minimum of free energy in a hydrophobic state at elevated temperature as shown by red curve in Fig.
4
. This diargam is a dependence of free energy on area covered with a surface water.

Yet at lower temperatures the majority of the surfaces have a tendency to become more hydrophilic, therefore, a local energy minimum, corresponding to wetting, becomes deeper. Significant mechanical tensions presented in graphene transferred onto a textured substrate (which are absent in graphene with top contacts) help to drive the system into a global wetting minimum of the system (violet curve Fig.
4

Thus, in a particular moment during the thermocycle the sample transits to a hydrophilic state, and the water (or organic contaminants) collected previously into bubbles spreads uniformly over the interface. We did observe resistivity jumps during the heatup though we can not unambigously relate these jumps to hydrophylic collapse.
The sample stays in the novel minimum during the second cooldown and further. Note that in the Re sample the bare graphene area also changed its properties. We believe that the transition was triggerd by contact areas. As one can see in Fig.

In order to confirm the above discussed model and prove the metastability of the Re sample state after the second warm-up, we decided drop it back to global hydrophobic free energy minimum by pressing with a hot PDMS drop (150℃ during 10 minutes). This action required detaching the sample with bonded contact wires from the measurement platform, its placement on the heating stage and return back again. To avoid sample damaging we did not clean it after the PDMS pressing. After this influence, we cooled the Re sample down to helium temperatures and measured its transport characteristics for the third time.

Our study experimentally uncovers a critical yet previously overlooked aspect of mechanically transferred 2D material structures: their metastability induced by interfacial water layers trapped between the substrate and the 2D flakes and emerging during thermal cycling.

Notably, this effect is absent in structures with evaporated top contacts or 1D edge contacts, highlighting the crucial role of mechanical strain in driving the transition.

Despite their fabrication simplicity, such transferred structures require careful optimization to ensure reproducible performance. One option is to use current annealing to stabilize the electrical response
Wang et al. 2010
. Alternatively, assembly in a controlled, anhydrous environment (though complete water elimination remains challenging) or the use of ultrathin metallic contacts may suppress strain-induced metastability. Another approach involves intentional preconditioning through thermal cycling to lock the system into a wetted state. These insights not only address a fundamental stability challenge but also provide guidelines for device engineering.

We also discovered a useful handle for experimentalists, i.e. degree of disorder, seen by electronic system: transport and optical phenomena could be studied before and after the thermal cycling and different results are expected. The metastable state should be responsive to laser annealing, offering additional control. Intriguingly, we found that mechanical perturbation via atomic force microscopy (AFM) at room temperature does not revert the system to its initial hydrophobic state, indicating a high energy barrier for the transition. These findings not only present new challenges for 2D electronics but also demand a critical re-examination of prior studies involving mechanically transferred 2D materials on textured substrates.

In summary, we provided repeated cryogenic transport measurements on graphene/hBN heterostructures transferred over metallic island arrays and contact electrodes. Initial cooldown experiments revealed strong van der Waals adhesion, as evidenced by gate voltage and magnetic field dependencies of the resistance. However, subsequent thermal cycling between room and cryogenic temperatures disrupts this interface bonding, leading to notably different transport properties characterized by enhanced uniformity and disorder. Hot pressing at elevated temperatures may restore van der Waals adhesion. We attribute this metastability to thermally activated redistribution of interfacial water layers. Beyond fundamental implications, this controllable disorder mechanism opens new routes for engineering 2D systems on structured surfaces, with potential applications ranging from reconfigurable electronics to strain-sensitive quantum devices.
