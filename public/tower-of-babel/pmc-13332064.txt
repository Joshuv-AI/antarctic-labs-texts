Comparison of scapular muscle activity between painful and asymptomatic shoulders in competitive swimmers
Authors: Pichaya Hengsomboon, Prasert Sakulsriprasert, Ninwisan Hengsomboon, Sivaporn Limpaninlachat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare serratus anterior (SA), upper trapezius (UT), middle trapezius (MT), and lower trapezius (LT) activity between painful versus asymptomatic shoulders within the pain group, and swimmers with versus without pain. This cross-sectional study evaluated 16 competitive swimmers with and without shoulder pain. Normalized electromyography (EMG) recorded SA, UT, MT, and LT activation during push-up plus (PUP) exercise was collected and analyzed. Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups. During the push-up, no significant differences were found between swimmers with shoulder pain and asymptomatic swimmers in UT, MT, LT, or SA activity (P > 0.05). In the hold task, UT, MT, and SA activities had no differences between groups or sides (P > 0.05). However, LT activity was significantly different in swimmers with shoulder pain (F = 4.649, P = 0.040). Dominant and non-dominant sides had no differences in any muscle across both tasks (P > 0.05). Scapular muscle activation patterns during PUP exercise were generally similar between swimmers with and without shoulder pain, as well as between painful and asymptomatic shoulders. The only notable difference was the LT activation during the hold task in swimmers with shoulder pain, suggesting a deficit in endurance-related control. For swimmers with shoulder pain, clinicians should prioritize LT assessment and endurance training within their routine practice.

Introduction

Swimming is a popular sport that offers a range of health benefits with comparatively low mechanical load and stress on joints1,2. However, competitive swimming requires consistently high training volumes and intensities, which can increase the risk of injury, especially for the shoulder girdle3,4. Competitive swimmers experience a notably high prevalence and incidence of shoulder pain and injury. Such injuries are most commonly attributed to repetitive strain on musculoskeletal tissues around the shoulder girdle5.

For scapular motion, the trapezius (upper, middle, and lower parts) and serratus anterior (SA) muscles function together as a force couple to produce upward rotation of the scapula, a movement crucial for optimal arm function. An imbalance between these muscles can disrupt this coordinated action, resulting in abnormal scapular motion6 and shoulder pain7,8. Alterations in scapular position and motion, referred to as scapular dyskinesis, have been associated with shoulder pain9, which is a common cause of shoulder injuries in competitive swimmers10. In pain-free swimmers, scapular dyskinesis was commonly observed during regular swimming practice. It often appeared early in the session and tended to increase progressively with the duration of training11.

The push-up plus (PUP) exercise is commonly used to elicit activation of the (SA) and trapezius muscles12. Although the PUP exercise is closed-chain, it is one of the most effective exercises for the SA, which is vital for preventing scapular winging. However, swimmers often suffer from shoulder pain due to a lack of endurance in the lower trapezius (LT). Therefore, the PUP exercise could be used as a surrogate for the assessment and training for the SA and the LT activation. The PUP is similar to the standard push-up (PU), but it has an additional movement of scapular protraction at full elbow extension. SA functions not only to stabilize the scapula but also to activate scapular protraction. While the upper trapezius (UT), middle trapezius (MT), and LT function as antagonists in a lengthened position, counterbalancing the activation of the SA and stabilizing the scapula12,13. Therefore, the PUP is an effective maneuver for examining the activation of these muscles as a force couple responsible for scapular upward rotation and stabilization at the terminal position.

Competitive swimmers with shoulder pain often exhibit poor scapular upward rotation and scapular stabilization, which can contribute to shoulder injuries such as impingement syndrome14. However, no study has yet investigated the comparison of these muscles between painful and asymptomatic shoulders in competitive swimmers with shoulder pain, nor between competitive swimmers with and without shoulder pain. This study offered a novel perspective on muscle activity in swimmers, highlighting distinct neuromuscular patterns that differentiate them from other overhead athletes. Therefore, this study aimed to compare muscle activity of the SA, UT, MT, LT, and ratio of UT/MT and UT/LT between painful and asymptomatic shoulders in competitive swimmers with shoulder pain, as well as between competitive swimmers with and without shoulder pain. We hypothesized that muscle activity of the SA, UT, MT, LT, and ratio of UT/MT and UT/LT would be different between painful versus asymptomatic shoulders within the pain group, and swimmers with versus without pain.

Methods

Participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted on a total of sixteen college swimmers (eight participants with shoulder pain and ages, gender and body mass index (BMI) matched pairs with the swimmer without shoulder pain). All participants (12 females and 4 males) were free of a history of surgery or fracture of the upper extremity and spine, diagnosis of spinal scoliosis, cardiovascular or serious neuromuscular problems, and implanted electronic devices. The criteria for shoulder pain in the study included mechanical pain or discomfort in the scapular and shoulder region, which occurred during swimming activities or shoulder movements. This pain began within 3 months and remained symptomatic on the day of the study. For the swimmers without shoulder pain group, they were no history of pain or discomfort in the shoulder or cervical region for at least three months before participating in the study. The participants’ characteristics are presented in Table 1. The study methodology was reviewed and approved by the Mahidol University Central Institutional Review Board (MU-CIRB) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, which has the COA number MU-CIRB 2025/077.0503. The data collection was done from January 2025 to March 2025 at the motion analysis laboratory, Faculty of Physical Therapy, Mahidol University, Salaya, Nakhon Pathom, Thailand. The written informed consent was obtained from all participants before study participation.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using a priori sample size estimation using G*Power 3.1.9.2, F-tests, ANOVA: Repeated measures, with a moderate effect size of 0.5, power of 0.8, and alpha of 0.05. It indicated that a minimum of 12 participants was required to detect a statistically significant difference. We recruited 16 participants to ensure the sufficiency of the data collection.

Procedures

This study used the Delsys Trigno® Avanti wireless sensor (Delsys Inc., Natick, Massachusetts, USA), which included the electromyography (EMG) sensor and inertial measurement unit (IMU) to collect the scapular muscle activity during PUP exercise. A total of eight sensors were used to acquire signals from the upper, middle, and lower trapezius and serratus anterior muscles on the left and right sides. The IMU signal from the sensor on the left and right middle trapezius was used for the detection of trunk motion, which determined the push-up and holding phases of the PUP. Before attaching the sensors, the researcher cleaned the participant’s skin in the area of sensor placement with alcohol wipes to reduce skin impedance and fixed the sensors on the skin with a double-sided adhesive interface. The areas of EMG sensor placement for the trapezius muscles were specific to their anatomical location, defined by anatomical landmarks, as outlined in the Surface Electromyography for the Non-Invasive Assessment of Muscles (SENIAM) guidelines. For the SA, the EMG sensor was placed on the mid-axillary line between ribs 6th and 8th15. All EMG placement locations were confirmed by performing muscle contraction again before being fixed to ensure that the EMG sensor is placed in the best area to get a clear signal for each muscle. For the UT, MT, and LT, the EMG sensors were placed on the midway between the spinous process of the seventh cervical vertebra (C7) and the posterior tip of the acromion process, the midway on a horizontal line between the root of the scapula and the third thoracic vertebra (T3), and at an oblique vertical angle, approximately two-thirds of the distance along a line between the spine of the scapula and the eighth thoracic vertebra (T8), respectively16.

After finishing the EMG sensor setup, participants were asked to perform the PUP task 3 times with at least 1-minute rest or until fatigue or discomfort had disappeared. Before the record began, they were asked to practice the PUP correctly. The PUP method of this study involved participants starting in a classic push-up position on the floor above a test surface, with 90° shoulder flexion and their weight supported by both hands. However, the lower part was supported on knees instead of both feet, aiming to reduce the load that may cause pain in the shoulder, especially in the shoulder pain group. Their hands were placed at a distance equal to the participant’s shoulder width. The index fingers of both hands were placed parallel to each other on the support surface. When the participant was ready, the researcher would say, “Start.” Then, the participant would move their body down to the floor, push up with full scapular protraction (the plus) at the end, and hold this position for 4 s. After that, they were allowed to get back to the starting position and rest. The EMG and IMU data were recorded before the participant started the PUP and ended the recording after they returned to the starting position. According to this procedure, all participants could perform completely without an increase in pain or discomfort in the upper limbs and cervical area.

Data processing and analysis

EMG and angular velocity data were exported in the C3D format by the Delsys File Utility software. All C3D data were processed by using the Visual3D (HAS-Motion Inc., Canada). Bilateral trapezius and serratus anterior EMG data were processed by removing the signal’s mean, high-pass filtered at 20 Hz to remove motion noise, full-wave rectified, and then low-pass filtered at 15 Hz Butterworth filter17. The PUP movement was separated into two phases for analysis, including push-up and holding phases. The push-up phase included the push-up motion until ending in the full PUP position. The hold phase was the holding period of the PUP position before the participant moved back to the starting position. These two phases of PUP motion of each side were determined by the angular velocity changing along the z-direction of the sensors that were placed on the middle trapezius muscles, which corresponded to the direction of thorax motion and synchronized timing of scapular protraction. The EMG data for at least 2 s during the holding phase were used for calculation. The averaged value of the normalized EMG of all muscles was used for analysis. All processed EMG data were normalized by the maximum observed signal17, which offers greater clinical feasibility and accuracy in populations with shoulder pain, where pain-induced inhibition may limit MVIC reliability18. This normalization method differs from the Maximum Voluntary Isometric Contraction (MVIC) approach.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used for demographic data, including sex, age, weight, height, body mass index, pain intensity measured by numerical rating scale (NRS), and side of shoulder pain (symptomatic swimmers only). The study variables included the averaged EMG data of the activity of UT, MT, LT, and SA muscles during push-up and plus (hold) position. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test data normality. All data were normally distributed, and the two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the effects of side (symptomatic and asymptomatic sides) and group (swimmers with and without shoulder pain). The statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the analyses.

Results

The characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. Sixteen swimmers were recruited: swimmers with shoulder pain (n = 8) and asymptomatic swimmers (n = 8). The two groups were comparable in age, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), with no significant differences observed (P > 0.05). The swimmers with shoulder pain reported a mean pain intensity of 3.25 ± 1.17 on the NRS with a pain duration of 1.66 months on average. Sex distribution was identical across groups. Among swimmers with pain, the affected side was more often the right shoulder (n = 5) than the left (n = 3). Other baseline characteristics were well matched between groups, regarding swimming experiences and types of swimming, indicating comparability for subsequent analyses.

Based on the results presented in Table 2, all the EMG activities were normalized by the maximum observed signal of the corresponding muscle. No significant group, side, or interaction effects were observed for most scapular muscles, and the ratio of UT/MT and UT/LT during either the push-up or hold conditions, except for the LT during the hold. For the push-up task, the mean muscle activities of the UT, MT, and LT, as well as the SA, showed comparable values between swimmers with shoulder pain and asymptomatic swimmers, with no statistically significant group effects (P > 0.05). Similarly, no significant differences were found between painful and non-painful sides, and no interaction effects were detected (P > 0.05). In the hold task, muscle activity of the UT, MT, SA, and the ratio of UT/MT and UT/LT also showed no significant differences across groups or sides (P > 0.05). However, a significant group effect was identified in the LT during the hold condition (F = 4.649, P = 0.040).

Discussion

The objectives of this study were to compare the muscle activity of the SA, UT, MT, LT, and the ratio of UT/MT, and UT/LT between painful and asymptomatic shoulders in competitive swimmers with shoulder pain, as well as between shoulders with and without pain among competitive swimmers. The demographic characteristics, including age, height, weight, BMI, swimming experiences, and types of swimming, were comparable between swimmers with and without shoulder pain. On average, swimmers experiencing shoulder pain reported a pain intensity of 3.25 out of 10 on the NRS, with a mean pain duration of 1.66 months (range: 0.25–3 months), indicating subacute, mild to moderate pain. All competitive swimmers in this study were collegiate-level athletes who regularly participated in training sessions and competitions.

The findings of this study indicate that scapular muscle activity was comparable between swimmers with shoulder pain and those without, as well as between painful and asymptomatic sides, during both the push-up and hold tasks. Specifically, no significant group, side, or interaction effects were observed for the UT, MT, LT, or SA in the push-up condition. Similarly, in the hold condition, muscle activities of the UT, MT, and SA showed no significant differences across groups or sides. The only exception was the LT, which demonstrated a significant group effect during the hold task (F = 4.649, P = 0.040), suggesting that swimmers with shoulder pain exhibited altered LT activation under this condition. In addition, the ratio of UT/MT, and UT/LT was investigated to represent the abnormal force coupling caused by trapezius muscle imbalance in the upward rotation of the scapula19,20. However, there were no significant differences across groups or sides. In contrast, previous studies19 have identified significant main effects for UT/MT and UT/LT ratios during side-lying external rotation and forward flexion, as well as prone horizontal abduction with external rotation. This discrepancy may stem from the specific actions and external loads used in those studies, which likely increased exercise intensity enough to highlight the abnormal force coupling associated with trapezius muscle imbalance.

Exercise selection and arm positioning significantly influence muscle activation patterns across various movement planes. Specifically, research indicates that the activity of the lower trapezius and deltoid muscles is markedly higher when the arm is positioned in the coronal plane compared to the scapular or sagittal planes. These findings underscore that precise shoulder positioning is critical for isolating target shoulder and scapular musculature during rehabilitation and strength training21.

The results of this study suggest that scapular muscle recruitment patterns during closed kinetic chain activities, such as push-up and hold tasks, are largely preserved among swimmers regardless of the presence of shoulder pain12,13. This might be due to the lower body was supported by the knees rather than the feet. This PUP position yielded lower muscle activation compared to open-chain exercises involving external loads22. Consequently, the reduced intensity of this position may have resulted in a floor effect. The comparable activation of the UT, MT, LT, and SA between painful and asymptomatic groups implies that shoulder pain may not always manifest through gross alterations in scapular muscle activity. This finding contrasts with the commonly proposed mechanism of scapular dyskinesis as a primary driver of pain in overhead athletes, raising the possibility that compensatory neuromuscular strategies or the multifactorial nature of shoulder pain could obscure expected imbalances23–25.

The exception observed in LT activity during the hold task may be clinically meaningful, as the LT is critical for scapular posterior tilt and upward rotation, functions that stabilize the scapulothoracic joint under sustained load12. Altered LT activation in swimmers with pain may therefore reflect a subtle deficit in endurance-based control rather than overall strength or recruitment26,27. This nuance highlights the importance of evaluating not only dynamic but also sustained tasks when investigating scapular muscle function in swimmers. Consequently, rehabilitation programs might benefit from targeting endurance and control of specific muscles like the LT during static or load-holding conditions, while also addressing broader biomechanical and pain mechanisms.

The present findings demonstrate that no significant differences in muscle activity were observed between the dominant and non-dominant shoulders, regardless of whether participants were swimmers with shoulder pain or asymptomatic swimmers. Specifically, the absence of main effects for group or side, and interaction during both the push-up and hold tasks, suggests that shoulder muscle activation patterns remain relatively symmetrical and consistent across both conditions. One possible explanation is that swimming, although highly repetitive, involves a cyclical bilateral movement pattern, which may promote balanced neuromuscular adaptation between the shoulders over time. This bilateral nature of the stroke cycle could counteract the development of substantial side-to-side discrepancies in muscle recruitment28,29.

Clinically, these results underscore the importance of evaluating sustained tasks in addition to dynamic movements when assessing scapular function and suggest that rehabilitation programs for swimmers with shoulder pain may benefit from emphasizing endurance and control training of the LT. The findings indicated that the primary neuromuscular deficit in swimmers with shoulder pain was pertaining to altered LT activation during sustained holds. Clinicians and trainers should aim to improve the LT muscle capacity to maintain scapular stability during the prolonged loading phases of the swimming stroke. Since the hold condition of the PUP exercise was the only phase showing significant differences, rehabilitation protocols should align with this finding. Therefore, the integration of the PUP exercises that emphasize the end-range protraction hold is suggested.

Limitations of the study, first, the examination of the upper trapezius (UT), middle trapezius (MT), lower trapezius (LT), and serratus anterior (SA) muscles was conducted only during the PUP exercise. Future research could extend this investigation by assessing the activation of these muscles underwater during actual swimming movements, thereby providing findings that more closely reflect real sporting conditions. In addition, the evaluation of rotator cuff muscles, specifically the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis, could be incorporated to achieve a more comprehensive understanding, as these muscles serve as an another force couple that plays a crucial role in coordinating glenohumeral and scapulothoracic motions. In addition, the exercise selection should reflect the specificity of training required for swimming. In conclusion, this study demonstrated that scapular muscle activation patterns during closed kinetic chain activities, PUP exercise, were generally comparable between competitive swimmers with and without shoulder pain, as well as between painful and asymptomatic shoulders. The only notable difference was observed in the LT muscle during the hold condition, where swimmers with shoulder pain exhibited altered activation, suggesting a potential deficit in endurance-related control rather than strength imbalance. Moreover, the absence of significant asymmetry between dominant and non-dominant shoulders highlights the influence of bilateral swimming strokes in promoting balanced muscle activity.
