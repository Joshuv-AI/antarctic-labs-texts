Spectral theory for Lévy and Lévy-Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroups on step 2 Carnot groups Thanks: 1 1 footnotemark: 1 † {\dagger} Research was supported in part by NSF Grant DMS-2246549.
Maria Gordina †, Rohan Sarkar †

Abstract
. We consider non-local perturbations Δ G ψ \Delta^{\psi}_{G} of sub-Laplacians on a step 2 2 Carnot group G G . The perturbations are by translation-invariant non-local operators acting along the vertical directions in G G . We use harmonic analysis on G G to obtain intertwining relationship between the semigroups generated by Δ G ψ \Delta^{\psi}_{G} and some strongly continuous contraction semigroups on Euclidean spaces with purely continuous spectrum, and as a result we identify the spectrum of Δ G ψ \Delta^{\psi}_{G} . Further we introduce the Lévy-Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) semigroup corresponding to Δ G ψ \Delta^{\psi}_{G} . We prove that these Markov semigroups are ergodic, though they are not normal operators on L 2 L^{2} space with respect to the invariant distribution 𝗉 ψ \mathsf{p}_{\psi} . The intertwining relationships allow us to show that all Lévy-OU generators on G G are isospectral, that is, they have the same eigenvalues with the same multiplicities. As a byproduct, we obtain a precise description of the eigenspaces, and also derive explicit formula for the co-eigenfunctions corresponding to some eigenvalues.

1.
Introduction

Spectral theory of Markov semigroups are of special interest as they are related to various functional inequalities such as Poincaré inequality, logarithmic Sobolev inequality, hypercontractivity. In particular, the spectral gap is often used to find the rate of convergence of a Markov semigroup to its invariant distribution (when it exists). One of our goals is to study the spectrum of a sub-Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
and its Lévy-type perturbations on a step
2
Carnot group
G\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, where
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is identified with the horizontal layer, see (
3.3
) below for details. When
m=0
, that is,
G
is a Euclidean space, the spectral theory of Lévy semigroups relies on the Fourier transform of such semigroups, see e.g.
[
2
]
. However, for a non-commutative locally compact Lie group, the Fourier transform is not scalar-valued, therefore making the spectral analysis more intricate. For the
3
-dimensional Heisenberg group, the spectrum of
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
was obtained in
[
11
]
using the Fourier-Wigner transform on the Heisenberg group. We also refer to
[
15
]
for the spectrum of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on Carnot groups of step
2
, where the authors proved that the spectrum equals
[0,\infty)
, and for Heisenberg groups, the spectrum is purely continuous. In
[
26
]
, the authors obtained spectrum of some second order differential operators on step
2
nilpotent Lie groups under some mild conditions on the group, and their methods rely on the unitary representations of such groups.

As the spectrum of the Laplace operator on Euclidean spaces is
[0,\infty)
,
[
15
, Theorem 1]
makes it natural to expect some connection between spectral properties of
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
and the Laplace operator on Euclidean space. We prove that the spectrum of the sub-Laplacian on any Carnot group of step
2
is purely continuous, and it is equal to
[0,\infty)
. The novelty of our approach is to rely on intertwining relationships with some strongly continuous contraction semigroups on Euclidean spaces, which not only strengthens the results in
[
15
]
, but also extends those to a larger class of non-local operators on
G
.

Two bounded linear operators
P,Q
on Banach spaces
\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2}
are said to be
intertwined
if there exists a bounded linear operator
\Lambda:\mathcal{X}_{2}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}_{1}
such that

\displaystyle P\Lambda=\Lambda Q.

When
\Lambda
is an invertible operator,
P,Q
have the same spectrum. Although
\Lambda
is not assumed to be injective in general, having an intertwining relationship often enables us to obtain some spectral information on
P
(resp.
Q
) if the same information on
Q
(resp.
P
) is known. The idea of using intertwining to study the spectral and ergodicity properties of non-self-adjoint integro-differential Markov operators have been used previously, and we refer to
[
28
,
25
,
29
]
for spectral theory of self-similar and related Markov semigroups using intertwining relationships with self-adjoint operators.

We now briefly describe the main idea that allows us to identifying the spectrum of
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
on
L^{2}(G)
, the space of all square-integrable functions on
G
with respect to the (bi-invariant) Haar measure. Denote by
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
the semigroup on
L^{2}(G)
whose infinitesimal generator is
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
, we first prove that there exists a closed subspace
\mathcal{L}\subset L^{2}(G)
such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\bigoplus_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{\beta},

where
\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\cong L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m+k})
,
m,k
are the dimension of the second layer of the Lie algebra of
G
and the
radical
respectively, as defined in Section
3.2
, and for each
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
there exists a strongly continuous contraction semigroup
Q^{\beta}
and a unitary operator
\operatorname{M}_{\beta}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m+k})\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{\beta}
such that

(1)

\mathbb{Q}_{t}\operatorname{M}_{\beta}=\operatorname{M}_{\beta}Q^{\beta}_{t}
on
\mathcal{L}_{\beta}
;

(2)

\sigma(Q^{\beta}_{t})=\sigma_{c}(Q^{\beta}_{t})=[0,1]
for all
t>0
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

The novelty of this approach lies in characterizing the subspaces
\mathcal{L}_{\beta}
in (
1.1
) by using the generalized Fourier transform (GFT) on Carnot groups. We refer to Proposition
3.12
and Section
4
for details about the construction of Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{L}_{\beta}
and the semigroups
Q^{\beta}_{t}
. In particular, for
H
-type groups and non-isotropic Heisenberg groups,
Q^{\beta}_{t}
corresponds to a rescaled Poisson semigroup on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, where
m
is the dimension of the center of the Lie algebra.

The decomposition technique in (
1
) and (
2
) extends further to non-local perturbations of the sub-Laplacian. Namely, we introduce Lévy-type perturbations of
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
along the vertical directions in
G
, denoted by
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
, where
\psi
is the Lévy-Khintchine exponent of a Lévy process. Such operators are generators of Lévy processes on
G
, and we show in Theorem
5.7
that
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
characterizes all Lévy processes on
G
whose
horizontal projection
is the Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Using the decomposition in (
1.1
) and intertwining relationships, we describe the spectrum of
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
in Theorem
5.9
, in particular, that the spectrum is purely continuous for all such perturbations. Our results also indicate that the spectrum of
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
depends on perturbations, and it is given explicitly in terms of the Lévy-Khintchine exponent
\psi
in Theorem
5.9
.

For a general treatment of Lévy processes on Lie groups we refer to
[
21
]
, and finding a core for the infinitesimal generator of such a process is a non-trivial question in general. In Theorem
5.4
, we show that compactly supported smooth functions on
G
, denoted by
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
, is a core for
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
, therefore generating a Feller semigroup, and a standard approximation argument using mollifiers proves that
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
is a core for
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
in
L^{2}(G)
. We also refer to
[
3
]
for the analysis of horizontal heat kernel on a (filiform)
n
-step nilpotent Lie group, and to
[
16
]
for the analysis of Lévy processes on
3
-step nilpotent Lie groups. Both of these references use Kirillov’s representation theory and the generalized Fourier transform relevant to the current article.

In addition to the operators mentioned above, we introduce the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) operators associated with
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
, that is, we define

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\psi}=\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}+\mathbb{D},

where
\mathbb{D}
is the generator of the dilation semigroup on
G
, that is,
\delta_{e^{-t}}=e^{t\mathbb{D}}
for all
t\geqslant 0
.
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
can be realized as the generator of the OU process on
G
driven by the Lévy process with generator
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
.
In Theorem
6.1
we prove that the closure of
(\mathbb{L}_{\psi},C^{\infty}_{c}(G))
generates a Feller semigroup
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
on
G
and it is ergodic with respect to a smooth invariant density
\mathsf{p}_{\psi}
. In particular, if we denote by
\mathbb{L}
the operator
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
when
\psi\equiv 0
, then
\mathbb{L}
is the generator of the OU semigroup corresponding to the horizontal heat semigroup on
G
. This operator can also be realized as the generator of
Mehler’s semigroup
in the sense of
[
22
, Equation (10)]
. Unlike in the Euclidean case, the OU semigroup on
G
is not a self-adjoint operator. In fact, it is not normal on
L^{2}
with respect to the invariant distribution, as we show in Corollary
6.7
. Using an argument involving the scale invariance property of the sub-Laplacian, see
[
22
, Lemma 5, Equation (17)]
, Lust-Piquard proved that the spectrum of
\mathbb{L}
on such an
L^{2}
space consists of eigenvalues and equals
-{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, a property also satisfied by OU operators on Euclidean spaces, see
[
24
]
.

Due to the presence of non-local perturbations in
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
, one cannot rely on scale invariance for
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
. In fact, the scale invariance does not even hold for second order local perturbations, as the scaling index for the horizontal and vertical variables in
G
are different in general. As a result, the approach in
[
22
]
is not applicable to study the spectrum of
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
. Instead we rely on intertwining relationships in Theorem
6.9
to prove isospectrality, that is, that all operators
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
for
\psi
with the Lévy measure satisfying the exponential moment condition (
6.28
) have the same eigenvalues with the same algebraic and geometric multiplicities. The intertwining operators are Fourier multiplier operators on
G
, which can also be viewed as Markov operators on
G
. In particular, if
G
is given a Riemannian structure, then our result implies that the OU operator corresponding to the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
G
has discrete point spectrum and equals
-{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Besides the intertwining relationship, another main ingredient in the proof of isospectrality is showing that the generalized eigenfunctions of
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
are polynomials on
G
. This involves proving regularity estimates for the semigroup
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
, and we refer to Section
6.5
for details. We note that the phenomenon of isospectrality is somewhat surprising as the spectrum of
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
does not depend on
\psi
at all, while the spectrum of
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
in (
1.2
) indeed depends on
\psi
. We refer to
[
33
]
for a similar observation in a very different context of Lévy-OU semigroups on Euclidean spaces.

Such resemblance also reflects deep connections between the OU operators on
G
and those on Euclidean spaces. In this vein, we prove the existence of Markov operators
\Pi:L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\widetilde{\mu})\longrightarrow L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
and
\Lambda_{\psi}:L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu)\longrightarrow L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}\Pi

\displaystyle=\Pi\widetilde{P}_{t}\text{ on }L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\widetilde{\mu})

\displaystyle P_{t}\Lambda_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\psi}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}\text{ on }L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})

where
\widetilde{P}
(resp.
P
) is a self-adjoint diffusion OU semigroup on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(resp.
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
), and
\widetilde{\mu}
,
\mu
are the unique invariant distributions of
\widetilde{P}
and
P
respectively. We refer to Theorem
6.9
and Proposition
6.6
for details. With the help of the above intertwining relations, we obtain a very different behavior of the spectrum for
p=1
, in which case the spectrum equals the unit disc
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leqslant 1\}
, see Theorem
6.31
for details. This result again conforms with the OU operators on Euclidean spaces as observed in
[
24
]
. Using (
2
), we provide explicit formula for the eigenfunctions of the adjoint of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
for even eigenvalues. To the best of our knowledge, such a formula has not appeared in the literature until now.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: in Section
3
we introduce the notation including the basics of Carnot groups. In Section
3.2
we briefly review the representation theory for Carnot groups of step
2
, which is crucial for defining the generalized Fourier transform. In Section
3.3
we prove properties of GFT on Carnot groups of step

, extending previous results on Heisenberg groups in
[

]
. These areessential to study the spectral properties of the sub-Laplacian and its perturbations. In Section
4

G
using intertwining relationships.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
Carnot groups

In this paper we concentrate on a particular class of metric spaces, namely, homogeneous Carnot groups of step
2
equipped with a left-invariant homogeneous metric. The assumption about homogeneity of the group can be made without loss of generality by
[
7
, Proposition 2.2.17, Proposition 2.2.18]
.

Recall that
G
is a Carnot group of step
r
if
G
is a connected and simply connected Lie group whose Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is
stratified
, that is, it can be written as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r},

where

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left[V_{1},V_{i-1}\right]=V_{i},\hskip 7.22743pt2\leqslant i\leqslant r,

\displaystyle[V_{1},V_{r}]=\left\{0\right\}.

To exclude trivial cases we assume that the dimension of
\mathfrak{g}
is at least
3
. In addition we will use a stratification such that the center of
\mathfrak{g}
is contained in
V_{r}
. In particular, Carnot groups are nilpotent. We will concentrate on the case of
r=2
.

Notation 3.1
.

By
\mathcal{H}:=V_{1}
we denote the space of
horizontal
vectors that generate the rest of the Lie algebra with
\mathcal{V}:=V_{2}=[\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}],\ldots,V_{r}=\mathcal{H}^{(r)}
.

For
r=2
, we therefore have the decomposition

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{V},

where for any
Z=(Z_{\mathcal{H}},Z_{\mathcal{V}})
,
Z^{\prime}=(Z^{\prime}_{\mathcal{H}},Z^{\prime}_{\mathcal{V}})\in\mathfrak{g}
with
Z_{\mathcal{H}},Z^{\prime}_{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathcal{H}
and
Z_{\mathcal{V}},Z^{\prime}_{\mathcal{V}}\in\mathcal{V}
, we have

\displaystyle[Z,Z^{\prime}]=(0,[Z_{\mathcal{H}},Z^{\prime}_{\mathcal{H}}])

As usual, we let

\displaystyle\exp

\displaystyle:\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow G,

\displaystyle\log

\displaystyle:G\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}

denote the exponential and logarithmic maps, which are global diffeomorphisms for connected nilpotent groups, see for example
[
9
, Theorem 1.2.1]
. For any
Z\in\mathfrak{g}
, the left-invariant vector field corresponding to
Z
is defined as

\displaystyle Zf(g)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}f(g\star\exp(tZ)).

Choosing a basis
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n+m}\}
of
\mathfrak{g}
with
\{Z_{i}:1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}\subset\mathcal{H}
and
\{Z_{i}:n+1\leqslant i\leqslant n+m\}\in\mathcal{V}
, this allows us to identify
G
with a linear space underlying its Lie algebra,
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, equipped with a non-trivial group law.
This is done by using the Baker-Campbell-Dynkin-Hausdorff formula, expressing the group operation in terms of the Lie algebra. Namely, the group operation on
G
can be described as

(3.3)

\displaystyle(h_{1},v_{1})\star(h_{2},v_{2})=(h_{1}+h_{2},v_{1}+v_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\omega(h_{1},h_{2})),

\displaystyle(h_{1},v_{1}),(h_{2},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\cong G,

where
\omega:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a skew-symmetric bilinear map. In this identification, we represent any element
g\in G
as
g=(h,v)
, where
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and we call
h

horizontal
and
v

vertical
components of
g
. Moreover, a Haar measure on
G
can be chosen to be the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}
. In the exponential coordinates we just described, one can show that
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n+m}
can be expressed as first order differential operators as follows

(3.4)

\displaystyle Z_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial h_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{i}(h,\nabla_{v}),\ Z_{n+j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{j}},\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant n,1\leqslant j\leqslant m.

(G,\star)
can also be equipped with dilations
(\delta_{c})_{c\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subset\operatorname{Aut}(G)
such that for any
(h,v)\in G
,

\displaystyle\delta_{c}(h,v)=(ch,c^{2}v).

Due to the decomposition in (
3.2
),
G
can be equipped with a sub-Riemannian structure with respect to the horizontal vector fields
\mathcal{H}
and the metric
g
on
\mathcal{H}
is chosen in such a way that
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
are orthonormal in
\mathcal{H}
. Then for the Carnot group equipped with this sub-Riemannian structure, the central object of interest is the sub-Laplacian on
G
defined as

(3.5)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z^{2}_{i}.

Due to Hörmander’s theorem,
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
is a hypoelliptic diffusion operator on
G
. By
[
12
, p. 428]
it is known that
(\Delta_{\mathcal{H}},C^{\infty}_{c}(G))
is essentially self-adjoint in
L^{2}(G)
, and with abuse of notation we denote its self-adjoint extension in
L^{2}(G)
by
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
. The corresponding
horizontal heat semigroup
on
L^{2}(G)
is defined as
\mathbb{Q}_{t}:=e^{t\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}}
.

For step

Then
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\oplus\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\oplus\operatorname{rad}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}_{l}=\operatorname{Span}\{X_{1}(\lambda),\ldots,X_{d}(\lambda),R_{1}(\lambda),\ldots,R_{k}(\lambda),Z_{n+1},\ldots,Z_{n+m}\},

3
). As a result,
\mathfrak{h}_{l}
is a polarizing sub-algebra for
l
. The one-dimensional unitary irreducible representations on
\operatorname{H}_{l}=\exp(\mathfrak{h}_{l})
are given by

\displaystyle\rho_{l}(\exp(X(\lambda)+R(\lambda)+W))=\exp(\textnormal{i}l(X(\lambda)+R(\lambda)+W))=\exp(\textnormal{i}l(R(\lambda))+\textnormal{i}l(W)),

since
l(X(\lambda))=0
. Following the argument described in
[
27
, p. 31]
, the induced representation
\pi_{l}=\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{\mathbb{H}_{l},\rho_{l}}
is defined on
L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda})
and it is given by

\displaystyle\pi_{l}(\exp(X(\lambda),Y(\lambda),R(\lambda),W))\phi(Y^{\prime}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=e^{\textnormal{i}l(R(\lambda))}e^{\textnormal{i}l(W+[Y^{\prime}(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}Y(\lambda),X(\lambda)-Y^{\prime}(\lambda)+R(\lambda)])}\phi(Y(\lambda)+Y^{\prime}(\lambda)).

Writing
X(\lambda),Y(\lambda),R(\lambda),W,Y^{\prime}(\lambda)
using the basis expansion in (
3.10
),
\pi_{l}=\pi_{\lambda,\nu}
can be realized on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and it is given by

(3.11)

\displaystyle\pi_{\lambda,\nu}(g)\phi(\xi)=e^{\textnormal{i}\langle r(\lambda),\nu\rangle}e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v\rangle}e^{\textnormal{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}(\lambda)(y_{j}(\lambda)\xi_{j}+\frac{1}{2}x_{j}(\lambda)y_{j}(\lambda))}\phi(\xi+x(\lambda)),

where

(3.12)

\displaystyle g=(x_{1}(\lambda),y_{1}(\lambda),\ldots,x_{d}(\lambda),y_{d}(\lambda),r(\lambda),v),

\phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Then the GFT on
G
is defined as

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=\int_{\mathbb{G}}f(g)\pi_{\lambda,\nu}(g)dg.

For each
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu):L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\longrightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. The Plancherel formula in Theorem
A.3
reduces to the following.

Proposition 3.2

(Plancherel’s theorem)
.

For all
f\in L^{1}(G)\cap L^{2}(G)
we have

\displaystyle\int_{G}|f(g)|^{2}dg=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\|\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)\|^{2}_{\operatorname{HS}}\frac{\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)}{(2\pi)^{d+k+m}}d\lambda d\nu.

Notation 3.3

(Coordinates on
G
)
.

Below we use coordinates
g=(z,r,v)\in G
in (
3.12
), where
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2d},r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k},v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

We end this section with the following lemma describing the behavior of the GFT with respect to the dilation on
G
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any
f\in L^{2}(G)
and
c>0

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\delta_{c}f)(\lambda,\nu)=c^{-(n+2m)}d_{c}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(c^{-2}\lambda,c^{-1}\nu)d_{\frac{1}{c}},

where
(d_{c})_{c>0}
is the dilation group on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
defined by
d_{c}f(x)=f(cx)
for any function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof.

First we note that for any
c>0
and
(z,r,w)\in G

\displaystyle d_{c}\pi_{\lambda,\nu}(z,r,v)=\pi_{c^{2}\lambda,c\nu}(z,r,v)d_{c}

Now (
3.13
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\delta_{c}f)(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}f(cz,cr,c^{2}v)\pi_{\lambda,\nu}(z,r,v)dzdv

\displaystyle=c^{-(n+2m)}\int_{G}f(v)\pi_{\lambda,\nu}\left(\frac{z}{c},\frac{r}{c},\frac{v}{c^{2}}\right)dzdv

\displaystyle=c^{-(n+2m)}d_{c}\left(\int_{G}f(v)\pi_{c^{-2}\lambda,c^{-1}\nu}(z,r,v)dzdv\right)d_{\frac{1}{c}}

\displaystyle=c^{-(n+2m)}d_{c}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(c^{-2}\lambda,c^{-1}\nu)d_{\frac{1}{c}}.

∎

3.3.
Properties of the GFT on step 2 Carnot groups

Let
G
be a Carnot group of step
2
. Using coordinates in Notation
3.3
, the GFT of
f\in L^{1}(G)
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(z,r,v)\pi_{\lambda,\nu}(z,r,v)dzdrdv

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(z,r,v)e^{\textnormal{i}\langle r,\nu\rangle}e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v\rangle}\pi_{\lambda,\nu}(z,0,0)dzdrdv,

where the last identity follows from the factorization

\displaystyle\pi_{\lambda,\nu}(z,r,v)=\pi_{\lambda,\nu}(z,0,0)e^{\textnormal{i}\langle r,\nu\rangle}e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v\rangle}

for all
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. This motivates the definition of the
Weyl transform
of a function as given below.

Definition 3.5

(Weyl transform)
.

For each
\lambda\in\mathcal{Z}
, the
\lambda
-Weyl transform of
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
is defined by

\displaystyle W_{\lambda}(f):=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}f(z)\pi_{\lambda}(z)dz,

where
\pi_{\lambda}(z):=\pi_{\lambda,\nu}(z,0,0)
, and
\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
denotes the Schwartz class of functions on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.

Note that
\pi_{\lambda,\nu}(z,0,0)
does not depend on
\nu
, which justifies the notation.

Remark 3.6
.

This definition of the Weyl transform is slightly different from the one considered in
[
36
]
, and it is similar to the definition introduced in
[
35
]
in the setting of Heisenberg groups.

Let us also consider the Fourier transform with respect to the
radical variables
, that is, for any
f\in L^{1}(G)
we define

(3.14)

\displaystyle f^{\lambda,\nu}(z):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(z,r,v)e^{\textnormal{i}\langle r,\nu\rangle}e^{\textnormal{i}\langle v,\lambda\rangle}dvdr.

Then by definition of the GFT
\mathcal{F}_{G}
in (
3.13
), we have that for all
f\in L^{1}(G)

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=W_{\lambda}(f^{\lambda,\nu}).

Proposition 3.7
.

For any
\lambda\in\mathcal{Z}
and
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
,
W_{\lambda}(f)
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and for all
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})

(3.16)

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)}{(2\pi)^{d}}\|W_{\lambda}(f)\|^{2}_{\operatorname{HS}}=\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}.

In addition, (
3.16
) extends to all
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
.

Proof.

We provide the proof for completeness, even though it is similar to the case of the Heisenberg groups. For any
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
, we note that the kernel associated to
W_{\lambda}
is given by

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}(\xi,\zeta)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x+\textnormal{i}(\xi-\zeta))e^{\frac{\textnormal{i}}{2}\langle\eta(\lambda)x,(\xi+\zeta)\rangle}dx,

where
\eta(\lambda)=\operatorname{diag}(\eta_{1}(\lambda),\cdots,\eta_{d}(\lambda))
, see (
3.8
). Using a simple change of variable
(\xi-\zeta,\xi+\zeta)\longmapsto(\xi,\zeta)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}|K_{\lambda}(\xi,\zeta)|^{2}d\xi d\zeta=4^{-d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x+\textnormal{i}\xi)e^{\frac{\textnormal{i}}{2}\langle\eta(\lambda)x,\zeta\rangle}dx\right|^{2}d\xi d\zeta.

Finally, using the Plancherel theorem for the classical Fourier transform we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x+\textnormal{i}\xi)e^{\frac{\textnormal{i}}{2}\langle\eta(\lambda)x,\zeta\rangle}dx\right|^{2}d\zeta=\frac{(8\pi)^{d}}{\det(\eta(\lambda))}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(x+\textnormal{i}\xi)|^{2}dx.

Since
\det(\eta(\lambda))=\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)
, it follows that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}|K_{\lambda}(\xi,\zeta)|^{2}d\xi d\zeta=(2\pi)^{d}\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{-1}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})},

which proves the proposition.
∎

Notation 3.8

(Vertical convolutions)
.

For
f\in L^{1}(G)
and a finite measure
\mu
on
\left({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mathcal{B}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{m}\right)\right)
, we denote by

(3.17)

\displaystyle f{\ast}_{v}\mu(h,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(h,v-u)\mu(du).

Lemma 3.9
.

For any
f\in L^{1}(G)
and a finite measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, one has
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f{\ast}_{v}\mu)(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(\mu)(\lambda)\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f{\ast}_{v}\overline{\mu})(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(\mu)(-\lambda)\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu),

where
\overline{\mu}(B)=\mu(-B)
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
.

Proof.

Using the convolution formula for the classical Fourier transform we have
(f{\ast}_{v}\mu)^{\lambda,\nu}=\mathcal{F}(\mu)(\lambda)f^{\lambda,\nu}
for all
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. Therefore by (
3.15
) we obtain
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f{\ast}_{v}\mu)(\lambda,\nu)=W_{\lambda}((f{\ast}_{v}\mu)^{\lambda,\nu})=\mathcal{F}(\mu)(\lambda)\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)
for all
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. Since
\mathcal{F}(\overline{\mu})(\lambda)=\mathcal{F}(\mu)(-\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, the second identity follows similarly.
∎

3.3.1.
GFT of functions with symmetries

The aim of this section is to analyze the GFT of functions which are invariant under orthogonal transformations of the horizontal variables. Using coordinates in Notation
3.3
, we consider the following group action on
G
. Let
\mathbb{U}
denote the group of all
2n\times 2n
orthogonal matrices of the form

\displaystyle U=U_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{d}=\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&U_{d}\end{pmatrix},\quad U_{i}\text{ is a }2\times 2\text{ orthogonal matrix},

and let
\mathbb{U}
act on
G
and
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
as follows

\displaystyle U\cdot g

\displaystyle=(U_{1}(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,U_{d}(x_{d},y_{d}),r,v),\quad g=(x_{1},y_{1},\ldots,x_{d},y_{d},r,v),

\displaystyle U\cdot z

\displaystyle=(U_{1}(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,U_{d}(x_{d},y_{d})),\quad z=(x_{1},y_{1},\ldots,x_{d},y_{d}).

We note that this group action does not necessarily induce an automorphism on
G
. We write
L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)
(resp.
L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
) to denote the space of all
\mathbb{U}
-invariant functions in
L^{2}(G)
(resp.
L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
), that is,

(3.18)

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)

\displaystyle=\{f\in L^{2}(G):f(U\cdot g)=f(g)\text{ for all }g\in G\},

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})

\displaystyle=\{f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{d}):f(U\cdot z)=f(z)\text{ for all }z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\}.

We aim to obtain the spectral expansion of the Hilbert-Schmidt operator
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu):L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\longrightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for any
f\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)
. For that, we introduce the Laguerre transform of functions in
L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
. For
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
and
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, let us define

\displaystyle\varphi^{\lambda}_{\beta}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})=\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}L_{\beta_{j}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\eta_{j}(\lambda)|z_{j}|^{2}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{4}\eta_{j}(\lambda)|z_{j}|^{2}},

where
L_{k}(x)=e^{x}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}(x^{k}e^{-x})
are the Laguerre polynomials. Now, for any
f\in L^{1}_{\mathbb{U}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
, we define its
Laguerre transform
by

(3.19)

\displaystyle R^{\lambda}_{\beta}(f)=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}f(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\varphi^{\lambda}_{\beta}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})dz_{1}\cdots dz_{d}.

Let us also introduce the projection operators
\{E^{\lambda}_{\beta}:\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}\}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

(3.20)

\displaystyle E^{\lambda}_{\beta}f=\langle f,\Phi^{\lambda}_{\beta}\rangle\Phi^{\lambda}_{\beta},\quad\Phi^{\lambda}_{\beta}(x)=|\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)|^{\frac{1}{4}}\Phi_{\beta_{1}}(\eta_{1}(\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}x_{1})\cdots\Phi_{\beta_{d}}(\eta_{d}(\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}x_{d}),

where
\{\Phi_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
are orthonormal Hermite functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}(2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi})^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}(e^{-x^{2}}).

Proposition 3.10
.

For all
f\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)
and
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}R^{\lambda}_{\beta}(f^{\lambda,\nu})E^{\lambda}_{\beta}.

Remark 3.11
.

This proposition can be regarded as a version of
[
35
, Theorem 1.4.3]
which provides the spectral expansion of the GFT of radially symmetric functions defined on Heisenberg groups.

Proof.

We start with the observation that for each
\lambda\in\mathcal{Z}
,
\{\varphi^{\lambda}_{\beta}:\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}\}
form an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
. Observe that by (
3.15
) and the isometry relation for the Weyl transform in (
3.16
),
f^{\lambda,\nu}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
for any
f\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)
, and therefore

\displaystyle f^{\lambda,\nu}=\sum_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}\langle f^{\lambda,\nu},\varphi^{\lambda}_{\beta}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\varphi^{\lambda}_{\beta}.

Using the identity
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=W_{\lambda}(f^{\lambda,\nu})
, we note that for any
f\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=\sum_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}R^{\lambda}_{\beta}(f^{\lambda})W_{\lambda}(\phi^{\lambda}_{\beta}).

Finally, we use the identity

(3.21)

\displaystyle W_{\lambda}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{\beta})=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}}E^{\lambda}_{\beta},

which follows from the proof of
[
36
, Theorem 3.63]
, to conclude the proof.
∎

As a consequence of the previous proposition, we obtain an orthogonal decomposition of the subspace
L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)
. For
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
, we define the following subspace of Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

(3.22)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\beta}:=\{(\lambda,\nu)\longmapsto\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{-\frac{1}{2}}f(\lambda,\nu)E^{\lambda}_{\beta}:f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k})\}.

Proposition 3.12
.

For all
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
,
\mathscr{L}_{\beta}\subset L^{2}\left(\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k},\operatorname{HS}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})),\frac{\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)}{(2\pi)^{d+k+m}}d\lambda d\nu\right)
, where
\operatorname{HS}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
is the space of all Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Moreover, one has the following orthogonal decomposition

(3.23)

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)=\bigoplus_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}\mathcal{L}_{\beta},

where
\mathcal{L}_{\beta}=\mathcal{F}_{G}^{-1}(\mathscr{L}_{\beta})
.

Proof.

To check the orthogonality, for any
T\in\mathcal{L}_{\beta},S\in\mathcal{L}_{\beta^{\prime}}
with
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0},\beta\neq\beta^{\prime}
, writing
T(\lambda,\nu)=\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{-1/2}f(\lambda,\nu)E^{\lambda}_{\beta}
and
S(\lambda,\nu)=\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{-1/2}g(\lambda,\nu)E^{\lambda}_{\beta^{\prime}}
we have

\displaystyle\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\langle T(\lambda,\nu),S(\lambda,\nu)\rangle_{\operatorname{HS}}\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)d\lambda d\nu

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{\mathcal{Z}}f(\lambda,\nu)\overline{g(\lambda,\nu)}\langle E^{\lambda}_{\beta},E^{\lambda}_{\beta^{\prime}}\rangle_{\operatorname{HS}}d\lambda d\nu

\displaystyle=0.

On the other hand, by Proposition
3.10
for any
f\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}R^{\lambda}_{\beta}(f^{\lambda,\nu})E^{\lambda}_{\beta}.

Then by Proposition
3.2
it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{k+m}}\sum_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{\mathcal{Z}}|R^{\lambda}_{\beta}(f^{\lambda,\nu})|^{2}d\lambda d\nu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+k+m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\|\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)\|^{2}_{\operatorname{HS}}\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)d\lambda d\nu

\displaystyle=\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}.

Therefore, for each
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
,
(\lambda,\nu)\longmapsto\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{-\frac{1}{2}}R^{\lambda}_{\beta}(f^{\lambda,\nu})\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k})
. This shows that

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)\subseteq\bigoplus_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}.

To prove the reverse inclusion, let
T\in\bigoplus_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}\mathscr{L}_{\beta}
. We want to show that there exists
f\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)
such that
T(\lambda,\nu)=\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)
for all
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. Let us write

\displaystyle T(\lambda,\nu)=\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}g_{\beta}(\lambda,\nu)E^{\lambda}_{\beta},

and define

\displaystyle f_{\beta}(z,r,v)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{k+m+\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}g_{\beta}(\lambda,\nu)\varphi^{\lambda}_{\beta}(z)e^{-\textnormal{i}\langle r,\nu\rangle}e^{-\textnormal{i}\langle v,\lambda\rangle}d\nu d\lambda.

By (
3.21
) we have
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f_{\beta})(\lambda,\nu)=W_{\lambda}(f^{\lambda,\nu}_{\beta})=\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)^{-\frac{1}{2}}g_{\beta}(\lambda,\nu)E^{\lambda}_{\beta}
, which shows that
f_{\beta}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
. Therefore, taking
f=\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}f_{\beta}
, we conclude that
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=T(\lambda,\nu)
for all
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. Moreover,
f\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)
, which completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

4.
Spectrum of the horizontal heat semigroup on
G
by intertwining

We now turn to the sub-Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
in (
3.5
) and the horizontal heat semigroup
\mathbb{Q}_{t}=e^{t\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}}
. Since
(\Delta_{\mathcal{H}},C^{\infty}_{c}(G))
is essentially self-adjoint in
L^{2}(G)
,
(\mathbb{Q}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a self-adjoint Markov semigroup on
L^{2}(G)
. The main result of this section is stated below.

Theorem 4.1
.

For all
t\geqslant 0
,
\sigma(\mathbb{Q}_{t};L^{2}(G))=\sigma_{\rm c}(\mathbb{Q}_{t};L^{2}(G))=[0,1]
.

Proof of this theorem requires several intermediate steps, including the Fourier analysis of the sub-Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
. Since
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
does not depend on the choice of the orthonormal basis in
\mathcal{H}
by
[
17
, Theorem 3.6]
, we note that for any
\lambda\in\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(X^{2}_{i}(\lambda)+Y^{2}_{i}(\lambda))+\sum_{j=1}^{k}R^{2}_{j}(\lambda),

where
X_{i}(\lambda),Y_{i}(\lambda),R_{j}(\lambda)
are defined by (
3.10
). Using
[
31
, Lemma 2.4]
, the GFT of the operator
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
applied to
f\in\mathcal{S}(G)
, the Schwartz space of functions on
G
, and for
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, is given by

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f)(\lambda,\nu)=\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)\circ B_{\lambda,\nu},

where
B_{\lambda,\nu}=\Delta_{x}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}(\lambda)^{2}x^{2}_{j}-|\nu|^{2}
is the Schrödinger operator on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. As a result, for any
t\geqslant 0
and
f\in L^{2}(G)
, the GFT for the heat semigroup
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
is given by

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathbb{Q}_{t}f)(\lambda,\nu)=\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)\circ H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t},

where
H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t}:=e^{tB_{\lambda,\nu}}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. It is known that
H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator with the generalized Hermite functions as its eigenfunctions, that is, for any
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}

(4.3)

\displaystyle H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t}\Phi^{\lambda}_{\beta}=e^{-t\mathfrak{n}(\beta,\lambda,\nu)}\Phi^{\lambda}_{\beta},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}(\beta,\lambda,\nu)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)+|\nu|^{2},

where
\Phi^{\lambda}_{\beta}
is defined by (
3.20
), and
\eta(\lambda)
is defined by (
3.8
).

(H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
also satisfies the following scaling property which follows directly from the scaling property of the Laplacian on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and homogeneity of
\eta_{j}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For all
c>0
and
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
we have

\displaystyle H^{c^{2}\lambda,c\nu}_{t}=d_{c}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{tc^{2}}d_{\frac{1}{c}}.

Let
\mathsf{q}_{t}(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the transition kernel associated to horizontal heat semigroup
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
, that is, for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(g)=\int_{G}\mathsf{q}_{t}(g,h)f(h)dh,g,h\in G.

Since
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
is left translation invariant, one has
\mathsf{q}_{t}(g,h)=\mathsf{q}_{t}(g^{-1}\star h)
with
\mathsf{q}_{t}(g)=\mathsf{q}_{t}(0,g)
. The next proposition provides an explicit formula for
\mathsf{q}_{t}
, and the proof can be found in
[
36
, Theorem 4.23]
, and a similar result in
[

Proof of Theorem

.

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{\mathbb{U}}_{t}=\bigoplus_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}^{\infty}\mathbb{Q}^{\beta}_{t},

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{t}f=e^{-at}f.

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathbb{Q}_{t}f)(\lambda,\nu)=\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)\circ H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t}=e^{-at}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu).

5.
Spectrum of some Lévy semigroups on
G

5.1.
Lévy processes on Lie groups and Euclidean spaces

Definition 5.1
.

A stochastic process
X=(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
on
G
is called a
Lévy process
if

(1)

X_{t}
has stationary and independent left-increment, that is, for any
0\leqslant t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}
,
X_{t_{1}},X^{-1}_{t_{1}}\star X_{t_{2}},\ldots,X^{-1}_{t_{n-1}}\star X_{t_{n}}
are independent random variables, and

\displaystyle X^{-1}_{s}\star X_{t+s}\overset{d}{=}X_{t}\text{ for all }s,t>0.

(2)

t\mapsto X_{t}
is stochastically continuous, that is,
\lim_{h\to 0}X^{-1}_{h}\star X_{t+h}=X_{t}
in probability for any
t>0
.

From the definition, it is clear that the semigroup
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}
associated to
X
is invariant under left translation on
G
. In other words, a Lévy semigroup
(\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
on
C_{0}(G)
is a left-invariant Feller Markov semigroup, that is, for any
g\in G
and
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\tau_{g}\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}=\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}\tau_{g},

where
\tau_{g}f(h):=f(g\star h)
is the left translation operator on
C_{0}(G)
. For example, the horizontal heat semigroup on
G
is a Lévy semigroup. The following result is due to G.A. Hunt
[
19
]
which characterizes all Lévy semigroups on
C_{0}(G)
through their generators. We provide a slightly different version of Hunt’s original result which can be found in
[
21
]
.

Theorem 5.2

(Theorem 1.1 in
[
21
]
)
.

Let
L
be the generator of a left-invariant Feller semigroup of probability kernels on a Lie group
G
. Then its domain
\mathcal{D}(L)
contains
C^{2,l}_{0}(G)
, and for all
f\in C^{2,l}_{0}(G)
and
g\in G
,

(5.1)

\displaystyle Lf(g)=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}a_{jk}X^{l}_{j}X^{l}_{k}f(g)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}X^{l}_{i}f(g)

\displaystyle+\int_{G}\left[f(g\star h)-f(g)-\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}(h)X^{l}_{i}f(g)\right]\Pi(dh),

where
C^{2,l}_{0}(G)
denotes the space of all functions twice differentiable with respect to the left-invariant vector fields and with vanishing derivatives at infinity,
(a_{ij})
and
c_{i}
are constants with
\{a_{ij}\}
being nonnegative definite symmetric matrix,
(X^{l}_{1},\ldots,X^{l}_{d})
is a basis of left invariant vector fields in
\mathfrak{g}
, the Lie algebra of
G
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
are coordinate functions satisfying
x_{i}(e)=0
and
X_{i}x_{j}(e)=\delta_{ij}
,
e
being the identity element in
G
. Moreover,
\Pi
is a measure on
G
that satisfies

\displaystyle\Pi(\{e\})=0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{U}x^{2}_{i}(g)\Pi(dg)<\infty,\quad\Pi(U^{c})<\infty

for any open neighborhood
U
of
e
.

It is worth noting that the above representation of the generator of Lévy semigroups does not require any metric structure on
\mathfrak{g}
. Although the above theorem is a very powerful result, it does not provide information about the core of the generator. Moreover, it is in general difficult to understand the spectrum of an operator in the form (
5.1
) as the GFT of such operators are not tractable objects. In what follows, we will consider Lévy semigroups on
G
which are obtained by perturbing the sub-Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
along the directions of vertical variables. To this end, we recall that the generator of a Lévy semigroup on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a second order integro-differential operators with the following representation.

(5.2)

\displaystyle Af(v)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(\sigma\nabla^{2}f(v))-\langle b,\nabla f(v)\rangle

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(f(v+v^{\prime})-f(v)-\langle\nabla f(v),v^{\prime}\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\{|v^{\prime}|\leqslant 1\}}\right)\kappa(dv^{\prime}),

where
\sigma
is a nonnegative definite matrix,
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and
\kappa
is a Lévy measure on
\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
, that is,

\displaystyle\kappa(\{0\})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(1\wedge|v|^{2})\kappa(dv)<\infty.

A direct computation shows that
A
is a Fourier multiplier operator with symbol
\psi
, that is, for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(Af)(\lambda)=\psi(\lambda)\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda),

where

(5.3)

\displaystyle\psi(\lambda):=-\langle\sigma\lambda,\lambda\rangle+\textnormal{i}\langle b,\lambda\rangle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}(e^{\textnormal{i}\langle v,\lambda\rangle}-1-\textnormal{i}\langle v,\lambda\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\{|v|\leqslant 1\}})\kappa(dv).

\psi
is called the
Lévy-Khintchine exponent
associated to the semigroup generated by
A
.

Notation 5.3
.

We denote by
\mathcal{N}
the set of all Lévy-Khintchine exponents
\psi
that are of the form (
5.3
). We note that any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
can be uniquely identified with the triple
(\sigma,b,\kappa)
, and we often write
\psi=(\sigma,b,\kappa)
.

From now on, we denote the operator
A
in (
5.2
) by
A^{\psi}
to indicate the dependence on the Lévy-Khintchine exponent. It is known by
[
34
, Theorem 31.5]
that
(A^{\psi},C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))
generates a Feller Markov semigroup
(Q^{\psi}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
on
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and for all
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(Q^{\psi}_{t}f)(\lambda)=e^{t\psi(\lambda)}\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda).

(Q^{\psi}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is called the
Lévy semigroup
corresponding to the Lévy-Khintchine exponent
\psi
. When
\psi(\lambda)=-|\lambda|^{2}
,
(Q^{\psi}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is the classical heat semigroup on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. We denote the transition kernel of
Q^{\psi}_{t}
by
\mu^{\psi}_{t}
, that is, for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle e^{t\psi(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v\rangle}\mu^{\psi}_{t}(dv).

5.2.
Lévy perturbations of a sub-Laplacian

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
, we consider perturbations of
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
defined by

\displaystyle\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}:=\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}+A^{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}},

where
A^{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}}
denotes
A^{\psi}
in the vertical direction of
G
. Note that when
\psi(\lambda)=-|\lambda|^{2}
,
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
is the full Laplacian on
G
. By
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
, it follows that
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
coincides with the generator of a Lévy semigroup on
G
at least on the space of all twice differentiable functions with respect to the left-invariant vector fields. The following theorem describes a core for the generator and also gives an explicit formula for the semigroup in terms of the horizontal heat semigroup
(\mathbb{Q}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
, the closure of
(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G},C^{\infty}_{c}(G))
generates a Lévy semigroup
(\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
on
C_{0}(G)
such that for any

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}f(h,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(h,v-u)\mu^{\psi}_{t}(du).

The heat kernel for
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}
,
\mathsf{q}^{\psi}_{t}(g):=\mathsf{q}^{\psi}_{t}(0,g)
satisfies

(5.7)

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\mathsf{q}^{\psi}_{t}(z,r,v)e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\nu,r\rangle}e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v\rangle}drdv

\displaystyle=e^{-t|\nu|^{2}}e^{t\psi(\lambda)}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\eta_{j}(\lambda)}{2\sinh(\eta_{j}(\lambda)t)}\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\eta_{j}(\lambda)|z_{j}|^{2}}{2}\coth(\eta_{j}(\lambda)t)\right),

where
g=(z,r,v)
are the coordinates on
G
given by (
3.12
).

Remark 5.5
.

The Markov process
(X^{\psi}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
associated to the semigroup
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}
has an explicit pathwise representation. Let
B(t)=(B_{1}(t),\ldots,B_{n}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
be

(Y^{\psi}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
denote the Lévy process on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
associated to
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
, independent of
(B(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. Then for all

,

\displaystyle X^{\psi}(t)=\left(B(t),Y^{\psi}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\omega(B(t),dB(t))\right).

Remark 5.6
.

Since
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}=(\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a convolution semigroup on
G
, the Haar measure on
G
is invariant with respect to
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}
, and therefore
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}
extends to a strongly continuous Markov semigroup on
L^{2}(G)
. By a routine approximation argument using mollifiers, it can be shown that

is a core for the
L^{2}(G)
-generator of
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}
.

The above representation for
X^{\psi}
indicates that the distribution of the horizontal components of
X^{\psi}
does not depend on
\psi
. Alternatively, if
\Pi:C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\longrightarrow C_{0}(G)
denotes the

operator defined by

(5.8)

\displaystyle\Pi f(h,v)=f(h),\quad(h,v)\in G,

then for all
f\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
,

(5.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}\Pi f=\Pi Q_{t}f\text{ for any }t\geqslant 0,

where
(Q_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is the classical heat semigroup on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
In the next theorem, we provide a characterization for the class of the Markov semigroups
\{\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}:\psi\in\mathcal{N}\}
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
(\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be a Lévy semigroup on
C_{0}(G)
such that for all

and
t\geqslant 0

(5.10)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}\Pi f=\Pi Q_{t}f.

Then there exists a
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
such that
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}=\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

We need the following lemma to prove this theorem.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset C_{0}(G)
denote the class of functions such that

(5.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\left\{f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(G):\lim_{h,v\to\infty}|h|^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial h^{\beta_{1}}\partial v^{\beta_{2}}}=0,\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\leqslant 2\right\},

Then
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{H}})
and
\mathbb{Q}_{t}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathcal{C}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. As a consequence,
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
is a core for the operator
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Proof.

Writing the skew-symmetric bilinear map
\omega
in (
3.3
) as

\displaystyle\omega(h,h^{\prime})=(\langle A_{1}h,h^{\prime}\rangle,\ldots,\langle A_{m}h,h^{\prime}\rangle),

where
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
are real skew-symmetric matrices, we observe that the sub-Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
on
G
can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}=\Delta_{h}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}=1}^{m}\langle A_{l_{1}}h,A_{l_{2}}h\rangle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial v_{l_{1}}\partial v_{l_{2}}}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}\langle B_{l}h,\nabla_{h}\rangle\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{l}}.

Thus for any
f\in\mathcal{C}
,
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f\in C_{0}(G)
. We need to show that
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{H}})
. We recall that the Brownian motion on
G
can be described by the stochastic differential equations

\displaystyle dX_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=dB_{i}(t)\text{ for any }1\leqslant i\leqslant n,

\displaystyle(dY_{1}(t),\ldots,dY_{m}(t))

\displaystyle=\omega(X_{1}(t),\ldots,X_{n}(t),dB_{1}(t),\ldots,dB_{n}(t)).

Therefore by Itô’s formula for any

\displaystyle M_{f}(t)=f(X(t))-f(X(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f(X(s))ds

is a
\operatorname{P}_{\!g}
-martingale for every
g\in G
. As
M_{f}(0)=0
, we have
\operatorname{E}_{g}(M_{f}(t))=0
for all
t\geqslant 0
and
g\in G
, which shows that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(g)-f(g)}{t}=\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f(g)\text{ for any }g\in G.

Therefore,
f\in\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{H}})
for any
f\in\mathcal{C}
.
We also note that the heat kernel
\mathsf{q}_{t}
of
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
has the following space-time scaling property

\displaystyle\mathsf{q}_{t}(g)=t^{-\frac{n+2m}{2}}\mathsf{q}_{1}\circ\delta_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}}(g)\text{ for any }g\in G,

and therefore for all
f\in\mathcal{C}
one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(g)=\int_{G}f(g\star\delta_{\sqrt{t}}(\widetilde{g}^{-1}))\mathsf{q}_{1}(\widetilde{g})d\widetilde{g}.

Writing
g=(h,v),\widetilde{g}=(\widetilde{h},\widetilde{g})
with
h,\widetilde{h}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
v,\widetilde{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, the above expression becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(h,v)=\int_{G}f(h-\sqrt{t}\widetilde{h},v-t\widetilde{v}-\sqrt{t}\omega(h,\widetilde{h}))\mathsf{q}_{1}(\widetilde{h},\widetilde{v})d\widetilde{h}d\widetilde{v}.

Since both
f
and
\mathsf{q}_{t}
are smooth and
\mathsf{q}_{t}
has moments of all order, the above expression also implies that
\mathbb{Q}_{t}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathcal{C}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. By
[
34
, Lemma 31.6]
we see that
\mathcal{C}
is a core for
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
. Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
be such that
\varphi\equiv 1
on
[-1,1]
and vanishes outside
[-2,2]
. Consider
f_{n}(g)=f(g)\varphi(|g|^{2}/n^{2})
. Clearly, for all
\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\leqslant 2

\displaystyle|h|^{2}\frac{\partial f_{n}}{\partial h^{\beta_{1}}\partial v^{\beta_{2}}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}|h|^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial h^{\beta_{1}}\partial v^{\beta_{2}}}\text{ in }C_{0}(G).

As a result, for any
f\in\mathcal{C}
, both
f_{n}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f_{n}
converge to
f,\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f
respectively in
C_{0}(G)
. This proves that
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
is also a core for
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
, and completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.

First, we show that
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}
is indeed a Markov semigroup on
C_{0}(G)
. Since
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
is a Markov operator, (
5.6
) shows that
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}
is a Markov operator as well. To prove the semigroup property, for any
t,s>0
,
f\in\mathcal{S}(G)
and
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{s}f)(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=e^{t\psi(\lambda)}\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{s}f)(\lambda,\nu)H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{t\psi(\lambda)}e^{s\psi(\lambda)}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)H^{\lambda,\nu}_{s}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{(t+s)\psi(\lambda)}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t+s}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t+s}f)(\lambda,\nu).

By injectivity of GFT, we have
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t+s}f=\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{s}f
for all
f\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. Finally, using the density of
\mathcal{S}(G)
in
C_{0}(G)
, the semigroup property follows. Next, we prove that
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
is the generator of
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}
with
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
as its core. We note that for any
f\in\mathcal{C}
,
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}f\in C_{0}(G)
. Next, we proceed to show that
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{D}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G})
. For any
t\geqslant 0
,
we can write

(5.12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}f(h,v)-f(h,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left(\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(h,v-v^{\prime})-f(h,v-v^{\prime})\right)\mu^{\psi}_{t}(dv^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(h,v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi}_{t}(dv^{\prime})-f(h,v).

Since
\mu^{\psi}_{t}
is the transition kernel of a Lévy process with the Lévy-Khintchine exponent
\psi
, and
\mathcal{C}\subset C^{\infty}_{0}(G)
,
[
34
, Theorem 31.5]
implies that

\displaystyle t^{-1}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(h,v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi}_{t}(dv^{\prime})-f(h,v)\right)=A^{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}}f(h,v).

On the other hand, since
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{H}})
by Lemma
5.8
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{t}f-f}{t}=\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f\text{ \ in }\ C_{0}(G).

We also note that
\mu^{\psi}_{t}\to\delta_{0}
weakly as
t\searrow 0
. As a result, given any
(h,v)\in G
,
w^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\epsilon>0
, one can choose small enough
t
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{t}f-f}{t}-\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f\right\|_{\infty}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}\text{ and }\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f(h,v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi}_{t}(dv^{\prime})-\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f(h,v)\right|<\frac{\epsilon}{2},

which implies that for each
(h,v)\in G
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\searrow 0}t^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left(\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(h,v-v^{\prime})-f(h,v-v^{\prime})\right)\mu^{\psi}_{t}(dv^{\prime})=\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f(h,v).

Using
[
34
, Lemma 31.7]
, we have
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{D}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G})
. Now, for any
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\leqslant 2
, due to the smoothness of
f
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{h,v\to\infty}|h|^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial h^{\beta_{1}}\partial v^{\beta_{2}}}\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}f(h,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\lim_{h,v\to\infty}|h|^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial h^{\beta_{1}}\partial v^{\beta_{2}}}\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(h,v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi}_{t}(dv^{\prime}).

By Lemma
5.8
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{h,v\to\infty}|h|^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial h^{\beta_{1}}\partial v^{\beta_{2}}}\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(h,v-v^{\prime})=0

as well as

\displaystyle\sup_{h,v,v^{\prime}}|h|^{2}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial h^{\beta_{1}}\partial v^{\beta_{2}}}\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(h,v-v^{\prime})\right|<\infty.

Thus by the dominated convergence theorem, we conclude that
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathcal{C}
. Using the approximation approach as in the proof of Lemma
5.8
, one can show that
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
is also a core for
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
. Finally, for any
g\in G
, let
\tau_{g}
denote the left translation operator on
C_{0}(G)
. Since
\tau_{g}
commutes with
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
, (
5.6
) implies that for all
(h,v)\in G

\displaystyle\tau_{g}\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}f(h,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\tau_{g}\tau_{0,-v^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{t}f(h,v)\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv^{\prime}).

Since
\tau_{g}
commutes with
\tau_{0,-v^{\prime}}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
, the above equation implies that
\tau_{g}\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}=\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}\tau_{g}
. This proves that
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}
is translation-invariant and the proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.7
.

Since
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}
is a left invariant Markov semigroup on
C_{0}(G)
, by Theorem
5.2
the generator of
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}
, denoted by
\widetilde{\Delta}_{G}
, has the following representation.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{G}f(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n+m}a_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}f(g)+\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}c_{i}Z_{i}f(g)

\displaystyle+\int_{G}\left[f(g\star g^{\prime})-f(g)-\mathbbm{1}_{\{g^{\prime}\in U\}}\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}\operatorname{x}_{i}(g^{\prime})Z_{i}f(g)\right]\kappa(dg^{\prime}),

where
U
is a relatively compact open neighborhood of
0
in
G
,

\operatorname{x}_{1},\ldots,\operatorname{x}_{n+m}\in C^{\infty}(G)
are local coordinate functions with respect to
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n+m}
such that
\operatorname{x}_{i}(0)=0
,
Z_{i}(\operatorname{x}_{j})(0)=\delta_{ij}
,
a_{ij},c_{i}
being constants such that
(a_{ij})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n+m}
is a symmetric nonnegative definite matrix, and
\kappa
is a measure on
G
satisfying

\displaystyle\kappa(\{0\})=0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}\int_{U}\operatorname{x}_{i}(g)^{2}\kappa(dg)<\infty,\text{ and }\kappa(U^{c})<\infty.

Since
G
is a homogeneous Carnot group, one can take
\operatorname{x}_{i}(g)=g_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,n+m
. Recalling the identity
\widetilde{\Delta}_{G}\Pi=\Pi\Delta
, the above representation of
\widetilde{\Delta}_{G}
forces that
a_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Moreover, it also implies that
c_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. Let us now write

(5.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}f(g)=\int_{G}\left[f(g\star g^{\prime})-f(g)-\mathbbm{1}_{\{g^{\prime}\in U\}}\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}g^{\prime}_{i}Z_{i}f(g)\right]\kappa(dg^{\prime}).

Then one has
\mathbb{J}\Pi f=0
for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Now, writing
g=(h,v),h=(h^{\prime},v^{\prime})
, where
h,h^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},v,v^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}\Pi f(h,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left[f(h+h^{\prime})-f(h)-\mathbbm{1}_{\{(h^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U\}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h^{\prime}_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial h_{i}}f(h)\right]\kappa(dh^{\prime},dv^{\prime})=0.

We claim that the above equality implies that
\kappa
must be supported on
\{0\}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. To see why, taking the Fourier transform with respect to
v
one gets that for all
\zeta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathbb{J}\Pi f(h,v)e^{\textnormal{i}\langle h,\zeta\rangle}dh

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(f)(\zeta)\int_{G}\left[e^{\textnormal{i}\langle h^{\prime},\zeta\rangle}-1-\textnormal{i}\langle h^{\prime},\zeta\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\{(h^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U\}}\right]\kappa(dh^{\prime},dv^{\prime})

\displaystyle=0.

Since
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is arbitrary, the above equality implies that

(5.14)

\displaystyle\int_{G}\left[e^{\textnormal{i}\langle h^{\prime},\zeta\rangle}-1-\textnormal{i}\langle h^{\prime},\zeta\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\{(h^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U\}}\right]\kappa(dh^{\prime},dv^{\prime})=0\text{ for all $\zeta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$}.

Using the facts that

\displaystyle\lim_{|\zeta_{i}|\to\infty}\frac{e^{\textnormal{i}\langle h^{\prime},\zeta\rangle}-1-\textnormal{i}\langle h^{\prime},\zeta\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\{(h^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U\}}}{\zeta^{2}_{i}}=-\frac{1}{2}h^{\prime 2}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{(h^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U\}}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\textnormal{i}\langle h^{\prime},\zeta\rangle}-1-\textnormal{i}\langle h^{\prime},\zeta\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\{(h^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U\}}}{\zeta^{2}_{i}\frac{1}{2}h^{\prime 2}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{(h^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U\}}}=\operatorname{O}_{\zeta_{i}}(1),

(
5.14
) implies that
\int_{U}h^{\prime 2}_{i}\kappa(dh^{\prime},dv^{\prime})=0
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
. On the other hand, (
5.14
) also implies that

\displaystyle\int_{U^{c}}(1-\cos(\langle h^{\prime},\zeta\rangle))\kappa(dh^{\prime},dv^{\prime})=0\text{ for all }\zeta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Since
(1-\cos(\langle h^{\prime},\zeta\rangle))
is strictly positive almost surely, we conclude that
\kappa
is supported on
\{0\}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, that is,
\kappa=\delta_{0}\otimes\kappa_{1}
where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac measure at
0\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\kappa_{1}
is a measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. As a result, from (
5.13
) it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}f(h,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left[f(h,v+v^{\prime})-f(h,v)-\langle v^{\prime},\nabla_{v}f(h,v)\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{\{v^{\prime}\in U_{0}\}}\right]\kappa_{1}(dv^{\prime}),

where
U_{0}=\{v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:(0,v)\in U\}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{U_{0}}|v|^{2}\kappa_{1}(dv)=\int_{U}\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}g^{2}_{i}\kappa(dg)<\infty,\quad\kappa_{1}(U^{c}_{0})=\kappa(U^{c})<\infty,

that is,
\kappa_{1}
is a Lévy measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Therefore,
\widetilde{\Delta}_{G}=\Delta^{\psi}_{G}
where
\psi=(\sigma,b,\kappa_{1})
with
\sigma=(a_{ij})_{n+1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n+m}
,
b=(c_{n+1},\ldots,c_{n+m})
. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.3.
Spectrum of Lévy perturbations of a sub-Laplacian

Recall that by Remark
5.6

\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}
extends to a strongly continuous Markov semigroup on
L^{2}(G)
and with an abuse of notation, we denote its
L^{2}(G)
-generator also by
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
. In this section, we analyze how the spectrum of
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
depends on
\psi
. In the case of Euclidean spaces, if
A^{\psi}
denotes the generator of the Lévy semigroup on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with the Lévy-Khintchine exponent
\psi
, then by (
5.4
) it follows that
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
-spectrum of
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma(A^{\psi};L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))=\sigma_{\rm c}(A^{\psi};L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))=\operatorname{Ran}(\psi).

Likewise, in the next theorem, we prove that the spectrum of
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
is also pure continuous and depends on the
\operatorname{Ran}(\psi)
.

Theorem 5.9
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
,

(5.15)

\displaystyle S_{\psi}\subseteq\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))=\sigma_{\rm c}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))\subseteq(-\infty,0]+\operatorname{Ran}(\psi),

where

(5.16)

\displaystyle S_{\psi}=\{\psi(\lambda)-\mathfrak{n}(\beta,\lambda,\nu):\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0},\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\},

and
\mathfrak{n}(\beta,\lambda,\nu)
is defined in (
4.3
).

The inclusions
S_{\psi}\subseteq\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))\subseteq(-\infty,0]+\operatorname{Ran}(\psi)
follow from an argument closely related to the proof of Theorem
4.1
. Proving the equality
\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))=\sigma_{\rm c}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
requires more delicate arguments where the fact that Lévy-Khintchine exponents are negative-definite plays a crucial role.

Corollary 5.10
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
,
\operatorname{Im}(\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G)))=\operatorname{Ran}(\operatorname{Im}(\psi))
. If
\psi
is real-valued then
\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))=\sigma_{\rm c}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))=(-\infty,\psi(0)]
.

Remark 5.11
.

In particular, when
\psi(\lambda)=-|\lambda|^{2}
,
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
is the full Laplacian on
G
, denoted by
\Delta_{G}
, and Corollary
5.10
implies that
\sigma(\Delta_{G};L^{2}(G))=\sigma_{\rm c}(\Delta_{G};L^{2}(G))=(-\infty,0]
. This strengthens
[
15
, Theorem 1]
in which the authors prove that the spectrum of the full Laplacian on any nilpotent Lie group of step two is
[0,\infty)
. They also proved that the spectrum is purely continuous in the case of the Heisenberg group, see
[
15
, Theorem 2]
.

Proof of Theorem
5.9
.

With an abuse of notation, if
Q^{\psi}
denotes the semigroup generated by
A^{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}}
on
G
, then we note that for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}=\mathbb{Q}_{t}Q^{\psi}_{t}=Q^{\psi}_{t}\mathbb{Q}_{t}\text{ on }L^{2}(G).

As a result,
\sigma(\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t};L^{2}(G))\subseteq\sigma(\mathbb{Q}_{t};L^{2}(G))\sigma(Q^{\psi}_{t};L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))
for all
t\geqslant 0
. Since
Q^{\psi}_{t}
is a Fourier multiplier operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
with the multiplier
\lambda\mapsto e^{t\psi(\lambda)}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sigma(Q^{\psi}_{t};L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))=\sigma_{\rm c}(Q^{\psi}_{t};L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))=\{e^{t\psi(\lambda)}:\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}\}.

Therefore,
\sigma(\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t};L^{2}(G))\subseteq\{e^{-t\lambda_{1}+t\psi(\lambda_{2})}:\lambda_{1}\geqslant 0,\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
. By spectral mapping theorem, we have
\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G})\subseteq[0,\infty)+\operatorname{Ran}(\psi)
. In order to show the other inclusion, using similar argument as in the proof of Proposition
4.5
, one can show that
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}(L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G))\subseteq L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(G)
for any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
. Moreover, for any
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
and
t\geqslant 0
,
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}(\mathcal{L}_{\beta})\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\beta}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}\operatorname{M}_{\beta}=\operatorname{M}_{\beta}\widetilde{Q}^{\beta}_{t},

where
\operatorname{M}_{\beta}
is defined in (
4.6
) and
\widetilde{Q}^{\beta}_{t}
is a Fourier multiplier operator defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\widetilde{Q}^{\beta}_{t}f)=e^{-t\mathfrak{n}(\beta,\lambda,\nu)+t\psi(\lambda)}\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda),\quad f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}).

As a consequence, for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\{e^{-t\mathfrak{n}(\beta,\lambda,\nu)+t\psi(\lambda)}:\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k},\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}\}\subseteq\sigma(\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}),

which is also equivalent to the inclusion
S_{\psi}\subseteq\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
. It remains to show that
\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
is purely continuous. First, we prove that any
a\in\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
with
\operatorname{Re}(a)=0
, one must have
a\in\sigma_{\rm c}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
. It suffices to show that
a\notin\sigma_{\rm p}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
and
a\notin\sigma_{\rm r}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
. Let
f\in L^{2}(G)
be such that
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}f=af
and
f\neq 0
. Then, for all
t\geqslant 0
,
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}f=e^{at}f
. Taking the GFT of the last identity we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)\circ H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t}=e^{-t(\psi(\lambda)-a)}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)\text{ for all }\lambda\in\mathcal{Z},\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}.

Since
H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t}
is a self-adjoint operator with eigenvalues
e^{-t\mathfrak{n}(\beta,\lambda,\nu)},\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
, the above identity forces that
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)
vanishes outside the following set

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}\left\{(\lambda,\nu)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m+k}:-a+\psi(\lambda)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)+|\nu|^{2}\right\}

However, for any
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
, we note that for any
(\lambda,\nu)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m+k}\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)+|\nu|^{2}-\operatorname{Re}(\psi(\lambda))>0,

which implies that
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=0
for all
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, that is,
f=0
, which contradicts our assumption. Therefore,
a\notin\sigma_{\rm p}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
. Using a similar argument and the fact that
\sigma_{\rm r}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))\subseteq\sigma_{\rm p}(\widehat{\Delta}^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
, we conclude that
a\notin\sigma_{\rm r}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
. Hence,
a\in\sigma_{\rm c}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
. Next, for the other values in the spectrum with strictly negative real part, we split the rest of the proof into two cases:
k\neq 0
and
k=0
respectively.

Case 1:
k\neq 0
.
Note that this equivalent to
\mathrm{rad}_{\lambda}\neq\{0\}
for any
\lambda\in\mathcal{Z}
. Let
a\in\sigma_{\rm p}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
for some
a\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}(a)\leqslant 0
. Then, we must have
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}f=e^{at}f
for some nonzero
f\in L^{2}(G)
. Taking GFT of the last identity, a similar computation as before implies that
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=0
whenever

(5.17)

\displaystyle-a+\psi(\lambda)\neq\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)+|\nu|^{2}\text{ for any }\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}.

As in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, for each
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
and
s\geqslant 0
, let us denote

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\beta}(s)=\operatorname{Leb}\left\{(\lambda,\nu)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m+k}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)+|\nu|^{2}-\operatorname{Re}(\psi(\lambda))\leqslant s\right\}.

Then, writing
\mathfrak{m}_{\beta}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)-\operatorname{Re}(\psi(\lambda))
, we have

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\beta}(s)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}C_{k}(s-\mathfrak{m}_{\beta}(\lambda))^{k}_{+}d\lambda,

where
C_{k}
denotes the volume of the unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. This shows that
\vartheta_{\beta}(s)
is continuous in
s
and hence for any
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\operatorname{Re}(a)<0
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Leb}\left\{(\lambda,\nu)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m+k}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)+|\nu|^{2}-\operatorname{Re}(\psi(\lambda))=-\operatorname{Re}(a)\right\}=0.

So,
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=0
on a set of full Lebesgue measure, which implies that
f=0
. Hence,
\sigma_{\rm p}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))=\emptyset
. By a similar argument, one can show that
\sigma_{\rm r}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))=\emptyset
, which proves that
\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))=\sigma_{\rm c}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
.

Case 2:
k=0
.
Let
a\in\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
be such that
\operatorname{Re}(a)<0
and
a\in\sigma_{\rm p}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
. If
f\in L^{2}(G)
is such that
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}f=e^{at}f
, using the same argument as before, we get that
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda)
vanishes on the complement of the following set

(5.18)

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}\left\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)-\psi(\lambda)=-a\right\}.

As before, for any
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
and
s>0
let denote

\displaystyle\varsigma_{\beta}(s)=\operatorname{Leb}\left\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)-\operatorname{Re}(\psi(\lambda))=s\right\}.

First, we claim that for any
\lambda_{0}\in S^{m-1}
, where
S^{m-1}
denotes the
m
-dimensional sphere, the ray
\{r\lambda_{0}:r>0\}
contains at most one solution of the equation

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)-\operatorname{Re}(\psi(\lambda))=s.

Indeed, any solution on the ray satisfies

(5.19)

\displaystyle r\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda_{0})-\operatorname{Re}(\psi(r\lambda_{0}))=\delta r-\operatorname{Re}(\psi(r\lambda_{0}))=s,

where
\delta:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda_{0})>0
. Suppose that there at least two solutions
r_{1},r_{2}
to (
5.19
). Since
x\mapsto-\operatorname{Re}(\psi(x\lambda_{0}))
is a negative definite function, the above equation implies that for any
c_{1},c_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
c_{1}+c_{2}=0
, we should have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}(s-\delta(r_{i}-r_{j}))c_{i}c_{j}\leqslant 0,

see
[
6
, Proposition 7.5]
. However, choosing
c_{1}=1,c_{2}=-1
, we get
\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}(s-\delta(r_{i}-r_{j}))c_{i}c_{j}=2s>0
, which is a contradiction. This proves our claim. Now using Fubini’s theorem with respect to the spherical coordinates, we conclude that
\varsigma_{\beta}(s)=0
for any
s>0
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
. As a result, the set defined in (
5.18
) has zero Lebesgue measure, which further implies that
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda)
vanishes on a set of full Lebesgue measure. This contradicts the assumption that
a\in\sigma_{\rm p}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
. A similar argument and the inclusion
\sigma_{\rm r}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))\subseteq\sigma_{\rm p}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
will imply that
a\notin\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
. Therefore,
\sigma(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))=\sigma_{\rm c}(\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G};L^{2}(G))
, which completes the proof of theorem.
∎

Proof of Corollary
5.10
.

From the definition of
S_{\psi}
in (
5.16
), it is evident that
\operatorname{Im}(S_{\psi})=\operatorname{Ran}(\operatorname{Im}(\psi))
. Thus, the first statement follows directly from (
5.15
). When
\psi
is real valued,
\psi(\lambda)\leqslant 0
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. On the other hand, since
\eta_{j}
for any
j
is a nonnegative homogeneous continuous function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we conclude that for every
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\{\psi(\lambda)-\mathfrak{n}(\beta,\lambda,\nu):\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\}=(-\infty,\psi(0)),

which implies that
S_{\psi}=(-\infty,\psi(0))
. Finally, the second statement of the theorem follows after noting that
(-\infty,0]+\operatorname{Ran}(\psi)=(-\infty,\psi(0)]
whenever
\psi
is real-valued.
∎

6.
Lévy-Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup and intertwinings

6.1.
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup and its perturbations

For the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) operator is defined as the diffusion operator

\displaystyle L=\Delta-\langle x,\nabla\rangle.

Then
L
generates an ergodic Markov semigroup known as the OU semigroup on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and the unique invariant distribution is standard Gaussian. We can alternatively view
L
as a perturbation of the Laplacian
\Delta
by
-\langle x,\nabla\rangle
, which is also the generator of the one parameter dilation semigroup
(d_{e^{-t}})_{t\geqslant 0}
on
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Similarly the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) operator on
G
is defined as the second order differential operator

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}=\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}+\mathbb{D},

where
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
is the sub-Laplacian on
G
, and
\mathbb{D}
denotes the generator of the dilation semigroup
(\delta_{e^{-t}})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
on
C_{0}(G)
.
In Lemma
6.4
below it is shown that
(\mathbb{L},C^{\infty}_{c}(G))
generates a Feller Markov semigroup
(\mathbb{P}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
on
C_{0}(G)
, which we call the
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup
on
G
. Using self-similarity of the horizontal heat semigroup
(\mathbb{Q}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
generated by
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
, one can show that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}=\delta_{e^{-t}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}.

This identity also implies that
(\mathbb{P}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is ergodic with respect to the unique invariant distribution
\mathsf{q}_{1/2}
, where
\mathsf{q}_{t}
denotes the heat kernel of the horizontal heat semigroup at time
t
. We refer to
[
22
]
for a detailed spectral analysis of OU semigroups on any stratified group, and to
[
5
]
for self-adjoint OU semigroups on Lie groups. By
[
22
, Theorem 3]
, it is known that
\mathbb{P}
is non-self-adjoint on
L^{2}(G,\mathsf{q}_{1/2})
.

The goal of this section is to study a class of operators obtained by some perturbations of
\mathbb{L}
along the directions of vertical vector fields, where the perturbation operators are the same as in Section
5
. We begin with a description of Lévy-type perturbations of OU operators on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. For each
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}
is defined in (
5.3
), the Lévy-OU operator
L_{\psi}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is defined by

\displaystyle L_{\psi}=A^{\psi}-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{j}},

where
A^{\psi}
is the generator of Lévy semigroup defined in (
5.2
).
By
[
34
]
it follows that
L_{\psi}
is the generator of a strongly continuous Markov semigroup on
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
, which is known as the Lévy-OU semigroup on
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
. Let us denote

(6.2)

\displaystyle P^{\psi}_{t}=\text{ semigroup generated by $L_{\psi}$ on $C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})$}.

We note that
L_{\psi}
is a generalization of the diffusion OU operator on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Moreover, the Markov process associated to the semigroup
P^{\psi}
satisfies the stochastic differential equation

(6.3)

\displaystyle dX(t)=-2X(t)dt+dY^{\psi}(t),

where
Y^{\psi}(t)
is the Lévy process with generator
A^{\psi}
.

By
[
34
, Theorem 17.5]
, if the Lévy measure of
\psi
has finite logarithmic moment away from
1
, that is,
\psi=(\sigma,b,\kappa)
with

\displaystyle\int_{|v|>1}\log|v|\kappa(dv)<\infty,

P^{\psi}
is ergodic with the unique invariant distribution
\mu_{\psi}
such that

(6.4)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}e^{\textnormal{i}\lambda v}\mu_{\psi}(dv)=\exp\left({\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(e^{-2s}\lambda)ds}\right).

Let us denote

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\log}:=\left\{\psi=(\sigma,b,\kappa)\in\mathcal{N}:\int_{|v|>1}\log|v|\kappa(dv)<\infty\right\}.

For Carnot groups, we consider Lévy-type perturbations of
\mathbb{L}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\psi}=\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}+\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{L}+A^{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}},

where
A^{\psi}_{\mathcal{V}}
denotes
A^{\psi}
in the vertical direction of
G
. In Theorem
6.1
, we prove that
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
generates a Markov semigroup on
G
. Moreover, when
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
,
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
generates an ergodic Markov semigroup. Before stating the main result, let us introduce a key notation which will be required in the statement and throughout the rest of the paper. For any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we define

(6.6)

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(\lambda):=\int_{0}^{t}\psi(e^{2s}\lambda)ds=

\displaystyle-\frac{e^{4t}-1}{4}\langle\sigma\lambda,\lambda\rangle+\textnormal{i}\frac{e^{2t}-1}{2}\langle b,\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}(\exp(\textnormal{i}e^{2s}\langle\lambda,v\rangle)-1-\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,e^{2s}v\rangle)\kappa(dv).

Then for all
t\in[0,\infty)
,
\psi_{t},-\psi_{-t}\in\mathcal{N}
. We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 6.1
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
, we have the following.

(1)

The closure of
(\mathbb{L}_{\psi},C^{\infty}_{c}(G))

generates a Feller Markov semigroup
(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
on
C_{0}(G)
such that

(6.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f(h,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathbb{P}_{t}f(h,v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv^{\prime}),

where
\psi_{t}
is defined in (
6.6
), and
\mu^{\psi_{t}}
is such that

(6.8)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v\rangle}\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv)=e^{\psi_{t}(\lambda)}\text{ for all }\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.

(2)

\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
has a unique invariant density
\mathsf{p}_{\psi}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(G)
with respect to the bi-invariant Haar measure on
G
whenever
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
, where
\mathcal{N}_{\log}
is defined in (
6.5
). Moreover, using Notation
3.3
for the coordinates of
G
,

(6.9)

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(z,r,v)e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\nu,r\rangle}e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v\rangle}drdv

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{|\nu|^{2}}{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(-e^{-2s}\lambda)ds\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\eta_{j}(\lambda)}{2\sinh(\eta_{j}(\lambda)/2)}\exp\left(-\frac{\eta_{j}(\lambda)|z_{j}|^{2}}{2}\coth(\eta_{j}(\lambda)/2)\right).

In fact,
\mathsf{p}_{\psi}=\mathsf{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}{\ast}_{v}\overline{\mu}_{\psi}
, where
\mu_{\psi}
is defined in (
6.4
) and
\overline{\mu}_{\psi}(B)=\mu_{\psi}(-B)
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
, and
\mathsf{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}
is the horizontal heat kernel at
t=1/2
.
As a result,
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
can be extended to a strongly continuous contraction Markov semigroup on
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
for
p\geqslant 1
.

Remark 6.2
.

In addition to the above theorem, using a standard approximation method with mollifiers one can prove that
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
is a core for
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
-generator of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
for any
p\geqslant 1
.

In the next proposition, we derive an explicit expression of the GFT of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
. This will play an important role in the proof of the above proposition and also the subsequent results.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
be defined as in (
6.7
). Then, for any
f\in\mathcal{S}(G)
and
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,

(6.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f)(\lambda,\nu)=e^{(n+2m)t}e^{\psi_{t}(\lambda)}d_{e^{-t}}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(e^{2t}\lambda,e^{t}\nu)d_{e^{t}}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{e^{2t}-1}{2}}.

If
\widehat{A}
denotes the
L^{2}(G)
-adjoint of
A
, then

(6.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{\psi}_{t}f)(\lambda,\nu)=e^{\psi_{t}(-e^{-2t}\lambda)}d_{e^{t}}\mathcal{F}_{G}({f})(e^{-2t}\lambda,e^{-t}\nu)d_{e^{-t}}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}.

Proof.

We note that (
6.1
) and Lemma
3.4
imply that for any
t>0
and
f\in\mathcal{S}(G)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathbb{P}_{t}f)(\lambda,\nu)=e^{(n+2m)t}d_{e^{-t}}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(e^{2t}\lambda,e^{t}\nu)d_{e^{t}}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{e^{2t}-1}{2}}.

If
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
is defined according to (
6.7
), we note that
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f\in L^{1}(G)
for any
f\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. Therefore, using the representation
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}=\mathbb{P}_{t}f{\ast}_{v}\mu^{\psi_{t}}
, we conclude the proof of (
6.10
). Next, we observe that

\displaystyle{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{t}=e^{(n+2m)t}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}\delta_{e^{t}}.}

Now, for any
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{S}(G)
, we note that

\displaystyle\int_{G}{\mathbb{P}}^{\psi}_{t}f(h,v)\widetilde{f}(h,v)dhdv=\int_{G}\mathbb{P}_{t}f(h,v)\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\widetilde{f}(h,v+v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv^{\prime})\right)dhdv,

which shows that
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{\psi}_{t}\widetilde{f}=\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{t}(\widetilde{f}{\ast}_{v}\overline{\mu}^{\psi_{t}})
, where
\overline{\mu}^{\psi_{t}}(B)=\overline{\mu}^{\psi_{t}}(-B)
for all

. Consequently, applying Lemma
3.9
, for all
f\in\mathcal{S}(G)
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{\psi}_{t}f)(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{G}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{t}({f}{\ast}_{v}\overline{\mu}^{\psi_{t}}))(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{G}({f}{\ast}_{v}\overline{\mu}^{\psi_{t}})(e^{-2t}\lambda,e^{-t}\nu)d_{e^{-t}}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{G}({f})(\lambda e^{-2t},\nu e^{-t})\mathcal{F}(\mu^{\psi_{t}})(-e^{-2t}\lambda)d_{e^{-t}}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}

\displaystyle=e^{\psi_{t}(-e^{-2t}\lambda)}d_{e^{t}}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(e^{-2t}\lambda,e^{-t}\nu)d_{e^{-t}}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}.

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

In the next lemma, we provide core for the generator
\mathbb{L}
that is also invariant under the action of the semigroup
\mathbb{P}_{t}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset C_{0}(G)
be defined by

(6.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\left\{f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(G):\lim_{h,v\to\infty}|h|^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial h^{\beta_{1}}\partial v^{\beta_{2}}}=0,\ \lim_{h,v\to\infty}|v|\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}=0,\ \beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\leqslant 2\right\},

Then,
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{L})
and
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. Moreover,
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
is a core for the generator
\mathbb{L}
.

Proof.

From the definition of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, it is evident that
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\subset C_{0}(G)
. Let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Then, for any fixed
g\in G
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{t}f(g)-f(g)}{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}f(\delta_{e^{-t}}g)-f(g)}{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}f(\delta_{e^{-t}}g)-f(\delta_{e^{-t}}g)+f(\delta_{e^{-t}}g)-f(g)}{t}.

Since
(\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}f-f)/t\to\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f
uniformly and
(f(\delta_{e^{-t}}g)-f(g))/t\to-\mathbb{D}f(g)
point-wise as
t\searrow 0
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{t}f-f}{t}=\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}f-\mathbb{D}f=\mathbb{L}f

point-wise. This proves that
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{L})
.
Also, using a very similar idea as in the proof of Lemma
5.8
, one can show that
\mathbb{Q}_{t}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. Therefore, for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=\delta_{e^{-t}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\subseteq\delta_{e^{-t}}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}.

Using the same approximation technique as in the proof of Lemma
5.8
, it follows that
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
is a core for
\mathbb{L}
.
∎

C_{0}(G)
for all
n\geqslant 1
, the above approximation shows that
(\alpha I-\mathbb{L}_{\psi})(C^{\infty}_{c}(G))
is dense in
C_{0}(G)
as well. Thus,
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
is a core for
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
for any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
. This completes the proof of (
1
).

For the existence of the limiting distribution, we note that for any
f\in C_{0}(G)
and
(h,v)\in G
, we can use (
6.7
) combined with (
6.1
), and a simple change of variable yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f(h,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\delta_{e^{-t}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}f(h,v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv^{\prime})

(6.15)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left(\delta_{e^{-t}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}f(h,v-v^{\prime})-\delta_{e^{-t}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}f(h,v-v^{\prime})\right)\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}f(e^{-t}h,e^{-2t}v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}\circ d_{e^{2t}}(dv^{\prime}).

The strong continuity of the semigroup
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
implies that
\mathbb{Q}_{(1-e^{-2t})/2}f\to\mathbb{Q}_{1/2}f
in the uniform topology of
C_{0}(G)
as
t\to\infty
. As a result, the contraction property of the dilation group
\delta_{e^{-t}}
implies that for each
(h,v)\in G
, the first term in (
6.15
) converges to
0
as
t\to\infty
.
To conclude the proof, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 6.5
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
,
\mu^{\psi_{t}}\circ d_{e^{2t}}\to\mu_{\psi}
weakly as
t\to\infty
, where
\mu^{\psi_{t}}
and
\mu_{\psi}
are defined by (
6.8
) and (
6.4
) respectively.

Proof.

For any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
t>0
, we note that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v\rangle}\mu^{\psi_{t}}\circ d_{e^{2t}}(dv)=e^{\psi_{t}(e^{-2t}\lambda)}.

Now, for all
t\geqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(e^{-2t}\lambda)=\int_{0}^{t}\psi(e^{-2(t-s)}\lambda)ds=\int_{0}^{t}\psi(e^{-2s}\lambda)ds,

which shows that
e^{\psi_{t}(e^{-2t}\lambda)}\overset{t\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}e^{-\psi_{-\infty}(\lambda)}
point-wise as
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
, see
[
34
, Theorem 17.5]
for details. This proves the lemma.
∎

Note that for any fixed
(h,v)\in G
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}f(e^{-t}h,e^{-2t}v-v^{\prime})=\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}f(0,-v^{\prime})

uniformly with respect to
v^{\prime}
. This fact follows from the uniform continuity of the function
\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}f
. Thus, Lemma
6.5
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}f(e^{-t}h,e^{-2t}v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}\circ d_{e^{2t}}(dv^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}f(0,-v^{\prime})\mu_{\psi}(dv^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{G}f(h,v)\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}(h,-v^{\prime}-v)\mu_{\psi}(dv^{\prime})\right)dhdv

\displaystyle=\int_{G}f(h,v)\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}(h,v+v^{\prime})\mu_{\psi}(dv^{\prime})\right)dhdv,

where the last line follows as the function
v\mapsto\mathsf{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}(h,v)
is even.
This shows that for any
g\in G
, the kernel
\mathsf{p}^{\psi}_{t}
of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathsf{p}^{\psi}_{t}(g,\cdot)=\mathsf{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}{\ast}_{v}\overline{\mu}_{\psi}\text{ weakly},

where
\overline{\mu}_{\psi}(B)=\mu_{\psi}(-B)
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
.
Therefore,
\mathsf{p}_{\psi}=\mathsf{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}{\ast}_{v}\overline{\mu}_{\psi}
.
Finally, the proof of (
2
) is concluded after observing that

\displaystyle(\mathsf{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}{\ast}_{v}\overline{\mu}_{\psi})^{\lambda,\nu}(z)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{|\nu|^{2}}{2}}e^{-\psi_{-\infty}(-\lambda)}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\eta_{j}(\lambda)}{2\sinh(\eta_{j}(\lambda)/2)}\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\eta_{j}(\lambda)|z_{j}|^{2}}{2}\coth(\eta_{j}(\lambda)/2)\right),

where for any
f\in L^{1}(G)
,
f^{\lambda,\nu}
is defined by (
3.14
).
Since
\mathsf{q}_{\frac{1}{2}}
is smooth, the limiting distribution
\mathsf{p}_{\psi}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Haar measure on
G
, and the density, still denoted by
\mathsf{p}_{\psi}
, is also smooth.
∎

Since we will be considering the Markov semigroup
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
on the weighted space
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, we restrict ourselves to the subclass
\mathcal{N}_{\log}
throughout the rest of the article.

6.2.
Intertwining with OU semigroups on Euclidean spaces

In the next result, we show that the horizontal projections of the semigroups
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
coincide with the OU semigroup on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. To this end, let us introduce the operator
\Pi:L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\widetilde{\mu})\longrightarrow L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
defined by

(6.16)

\displaystyle\Pi f(h,v)=f(h),

where
\widetilde{\mu}(dh)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-|h|^{2}/2}dh
is the Gaussian measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Then, we have the following result.

Proposition 6.6
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
(see (
6.5
) for definition) and
p\geqslant 1
,
\Pi:L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\widetilde{\mu})\longrightarrow L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
is a isometry. Moreover, for any
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\widetilde{\mu})
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}\Pi f=\Pi\widetilde{P}_{t}f,

where
(\widetilde{P}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is the OU semigroup on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\mu)
generated by
\widetilde{L}=\Delta-\langle h,\nabla\rangle
.

Proof.

Let us define the projection map

(6.17)

\displaystyle\pi:G

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

\displaystyle(h,v)

\displaystyle\longmapsto h.

With this notation, one has
\Pi f=f\circ\pi
. Denoting the heat kernel associated to
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}
by
\mathsf{q}^{\psi}_{t}
, we claim that
\widetilde{\mu}_{t}=\mathsf{q}^{\psi}_{t}\circ\pi^{-1}
, where
\widetilde{\mu}_{t}
is the heat kernel associated with the Laplacian on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Indeed, putting
\lambda=0
in (
5.7
) one gets

(6.18)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{q}^{\psi}_{t}(h,v)dv=\widetilde{\mu}_{t}(h),

which proves our claim. Since
\widetilde{\mu}=\widetilde{\mu}_{1/2}
, we have
\widetilde{\mu}=\mathsf{p}_{\psi}\circ\pi^{-1}
. This shows that
\Pi:L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\widetilde{\mu})\longrightarrow L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
is a isometry. Since
\pi
also commutes with the dilations, that is,

\displaystyle\pi\delta_{c}=d_{c}\pi\text{ for all }c>0,

(
6.18
) implies that
\mathbb{P}_{t}\Pi=\Pi\widetilde{P}_{t}
on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\widetilde{\mu})
for all
t>0
. Finally, for any
f\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, (
6.7
) shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}\Pi f(h,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathbb{P}_{t}\Pi f(h,v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\Pi\widetilde{P}_{t}f(h,v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{P}_{t}f(h)=\Pi\widetilde{P}_{t}f(h,v).

The above equality extends to
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\widetilde{\mu})
due to the density of
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\widetilde{\mu})
, which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 6.7
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
and
t>0
,
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
is not a normal operator on
L^{2}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
.

Proof.

Denoting the
L^{2}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
-adjoint of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
by
\mathbb{P}^{\psi*}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi*}_{t}f=\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{\psi}_{t}(\mathsf{p}_{\psi}f)}{\mathsf{p}_{\psi}}\text{ for all }f\in C_{b}(G).

Now assume that
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
is a normal operator. Then by the Fuglede-Putnam-Rosenblum theorem (see
[
32
, Theorem 12.16, p. 315]
), Proposition
6.6
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi*}_{t}\Pi=\Pi\widetilde{P}_{t}\text{ on }L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\mu).

Therefore for all
f\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
one must have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{\psi}_{t}(\mathsf{p}_{\psi}\Pi f)=\mathsf{p}_{\psi}\Pi\widetilde{P}_{t}f.

Taking the derivative of the above equality at
t=0
implies that at least for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,

\displaystyle(\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}+\mathbb{D}+A^{\overline{\psi}}_{\mathcal{V}}+n+2m)(\mathsf{p}_{\psi}\Pi f)=\mathsf{p}_{\psi}\Pi\widetilde{L}f,

which clearly does not hold for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. This leads to a contradiction, which completes the proof.
∎

6.3.
Intertwining between OU semigroups

We recall the semigroups
\mathbb{P}
,
P^{\psi}
, and
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
defined by (
6.1
), (
6.2
), and Theorem
6.1
(
1
) respectively.
Consider the Markov kernels
\Lambda:C_{0}(G)\longrightarrow C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and
\Gamma_{\psi}:C_{0}(G)\longrightarrow C_{0}(G)
such that

(6.19)

\displaystyle\Lambda f(v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(h,v+v^{\prime})\mathsf{p}(h,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}dh,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\psi}f(h,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(h,v+v^{\prime})\mu_{\psi}(dv^{\prime}),

where
\mathsf{p}
is the invariant distribution of
\mathbb{P}
that also coincides with
\mathsf{q}_{1/2}
, the horizontal heat kernel on
G
at
t=1/2
, and
\mu_{\psi}
defined in (
6.4
) is the invariant distribution of
P^{\psi}
.
The next lemma shows that both
\Lambda
and
\Gamma_{\psi}
can be extended as bounded operators on weighted
L^{p}
-spaces.

Lemma 6.8
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\psi}\Lambda

\displaystyle=\mathsf{p}_{\psi},

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}\Gamma_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{p}_{\psi}.

As a result,
\Lambda
(resp.
\Gamma_{\psi}
) extends as a bounded operator between
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
and
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\psi})
(resp.
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
and
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p})
) for any
p\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

For any
f\in C_{b}(G)
by a change of variable and using Fubini’s theorem we note that

\displaystyle\mu_{\psi}\Lambda f

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{p}(h,v^{\prime}-v)f(h,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}dh\right)\mu_{\psi}(dv)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{p}(h,v^{\prime}-v)\mu_{\psi}(dv)\right)f(h,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}dh

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(h,v^{\prime})f(h,v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}dh,

where we used fact that
\mathsf{p}{\ast}_{v}\mu_{\psi}=\mathsf{p}_{\psi}
.
Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}\Gamma_{\psi}f

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(h,v+v^{\prime})\mu_{\psi}(dv^{\prime})\right)\mathsf{p}(h,v)dvdh

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{p}(h,v-v^{\prime})p_{\psi}(dv^{\prime})\right)f(h,v)dvdh

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(h,v)\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(h,v)dhdv.

This completes the proof of the first statement. Next, for any
f\in L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})\cap C_{b}(G)
with
p\geqslant 1
one has

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}(\Lambda f)^{p}(v)p_{\psi}(dv)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\Lambda(|f|^{p})(v)p_{\psi}(dv)dv

\displaystyle=\int_{G}|f(h,v)|^{p}\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(h,v)dhdv.

The above inequality extends to
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
by a density argument. The extension of
\Gamma_{\psi}
between the
L^{p}
-spaces can be proved similarly as above.
∎

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 6.9
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
and
p\geqslant 1
, the following intertwining relationships hold.

(6.20)

\displaystyle P^{\psi}_{t}\Lambda

\displaystyle=\Lambda\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}\text{ on }L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}),

(6.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}\Gamma_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\psi}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}\text{ on }L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}),

where
\mathbb{P}
is defined in (
6.1
), and
P^{\psi}
is the semigroup corresponding to the stochastic differential equation (
6.3
).

We now consider a different representation of the operators
\Lambda
and
\Gamma_{\psi}
as Fourier multiplier operators, which will be useful in the proof of the above theorem. We start by defining two operators
\mathcal{I}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\longrightarrow L^{2}(G)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}:L^{2}(G)\longrightarrow L^{2}(G)
such that for all
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}),\widetilde{f}\in L^{2}(G)
, and
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

(6.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathcal{I}f)(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda)H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\widetilde{f})(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\psi_{-\infty}(-\lambda))\mathcal{F}_{G}(\widetilde{f})(\lambda,\nu),

where
H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1}{2}}
is defined in (
4.3
).

Lemma 6.10
.

\mathcal{I}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\longrightarrow L^{2}(G)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}:L^{2}(G)\longrightarrow L^{2}(G)
are bounded and injective. Moreover, for any
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and
\widetilde{f}\in L^{2}(G)\cap C_{b}(G)
,

(6.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}f(h,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{p}(h,v-v^{\prime})f(v^{\prime})dv^{\prime},

(6.24)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\widetilde{f}(h,v)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\psi}\widetilde{f}(h,v),

where
\Gamma_{\psi}
is defined in (
6.19
).

Proof.

Since
H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1}{2}}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator,
\mathcal{I}
is well defined on its domain. Now, let us verify the boundedness of
\mathcal{I}
. Using the Plancherel theorem for
\mathcal{F}_{G}
, for all
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
we have,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{I}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+k+m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\|\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathcal{I}f)(\lambda,\nu)\|^{2}_{\operatorname{HS}}\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)d\lambda d\nu.

Now, for each
t>0
, and
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}
we have

\displaystyle H^{\lambda,\nu}_{t}\Phi^{\lambda}_{\beta}=e^{-t\mathfrak{n}(\beta,\lambda,\nu)}\Phi^{\lambda}_{\beta},

where
\{\Phi^{\lambda}_{\beta}:\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}\}
are defined in (
3.20
). As a result,

\displaystyle\|H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}_{\operatorname{HS}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}e^{-\mathfrak{n}(\beta,\lambda,\nu)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}_{0}}\exp\left(-|\nu|^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-|\nu|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{\beta_{j}=0}^{\infty}e^{-(2\beta_{j}+1)\eta_{j}(\lambda)}=e^{-|\nu|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{e^{-\eta_{j}(\lambda)}}{1-e^{-2\eta_{j}(\lambda)}}.

Since
\eta_{j}
are smooth and homogeneous of degree
1
, one has
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\eta_{j}(\lambda)=0
for each
j=1,\ldots,d
. Therefore, the function defined by

\displaystyle u(\lambda):=\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{e^{-\eta_{j}(\lambda)}}{1-e^{-2\eta_{j}(\lambda)}}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\eta_{j}(\lambda)e^{-\eta_{j}(\lambda)}}{1-e^{-2\eta_{j}(\lambda)}}

is bounded on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Consequently, for all
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{I}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+k+m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m+k}}\|\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathcal{I}f)(\lambda,\nu)\|^{2}_{\operatorname{HS}}\operatorname{Pf}(\lambda)d\lambda d\nu

(6.25)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{c}{(2\pi)^{m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}|\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda)|^{2}d\lambda=c\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})},

where

\displaystyle c=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+k}}\|u\|_{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}e^{-|\nu|^{2}}d\nu,

and (
6.25
) follows by the Plancherel theorem on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
. To show the injectivity, let
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
be such that
\mathcal{I}f=0
. Then, for all
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda,\nu)H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1}{2}}=0.

Since
H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1}{2}}\neq 0
for any
(\lambda,\nu)\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, it implies that
\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda,\nu)=0
, which shows that
f=0
a.e.
Injectivity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
follows by a similar argument and the boundedness follows directly from the Plancherel theorem for
\mathcal{F}_{G}
. Now, for any
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
, we note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathsf{p}{\ast}_{v}{f})(\lambda,\nu)=\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda)\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathsf{p})(\lambda,\nu)=\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda)H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1}{2}},

which shows that
\mathcal{I}f=\mathsf{p}{\ast}_{v}{f}
for any
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
. Using the density of
C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
, the proof of (
6.23
) follows. Proof of (
6.24
) follows by a similar argument.
∎

In the next lemma, we establish the link between the operators
\mathcal{I}
and
\Lambda
.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}:L^{2}(G)\longrightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
denote the adjoint of
\mathcal{I}
. Then

\displaystyle\Lambda=\left.\widehat{\mathcal{I}}\right|_{L^{2}(G)\cap C_{b}(G)}

Proof.

For any
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and
\widetilde{f}\in L^{2}(G)\cap C_{b}(G)
we note that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{I}f,\widetilde{f}\rangle_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{p}(h,v-v^{\prime})f(v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}\right)\widetilde{f}(h,v)dvdh

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{p}(h,v)\widetilde{f}(h,v+v^{\prime})dvdh\right)f(v^{\prime})dv^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\langle f,\Lambda\widetilde{f}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})},

where the second to the last equality is justified by Fubini’s theorem.
∎

Lemma 6.12
.

For any
t\in[-\infty,\infty)
, let
\psi_{t}
be defined by (
6.6
). Then for all
t\in[-\infty,\infty)
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
we have

\displaystyle-\psi_{-\infty}(e^{2t}\lambda)=-\psi_{-\infty}(\lambda)+\psi_{t}(\lambda).

The proof follows directly from the definition of
\psi_{t}
in (
6.6
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.9
.

For (
6.20
), we will prove the adjoint of the intertwining relationship, that is, for all

,

(6.26)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{\psi}_{t}\mathcal{I}f=\mathcal{I}\widehat{P}^{\psi}_{t}f,

where
\mathcal{I}
is defined in (

). Using the result of Proposition

, for any

. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathcal{I}\widehat{P}^{\psi}_{t}f)(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=e^{\psi_{t}(-e^{-2t}\lambda)}\mathcal{F}(f)(e^{-2t}\lambda)H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{1}{2}}

where the last identity follows from (

). Finally using Lemma
6.11
and taking the adjoint of the identity in (

), (
6.20
) follows. Next, to prove (
6.21
), we note that for any
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
, (
6.10
) with
\psi\equiv 0
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathbb{P}_{t}\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}f)(\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=e^{-(n+2m)t}d_{e^{-t}}\mathcal{F}_{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}f)(e^{2t}\lambda,e^{t}\nu)d_{e^{t}}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{e^{2t}-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=e^{-(n+2m)t}e^{-\psi_{-\infty}(e^{2t}\lambda)}d_{e^{-t}}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(e^{2t}\lambda,e^{t}\nu)d_{e^{t}}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{e^{2t}-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\psi_{-\infty}(\lambda)}e^{-(n+2m)t}e^{\psi_{t}(\lambda)}d_{e^{-t}}\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(e^{2t}\lambda,e^{t}\nu)d_{e^{t}}H^{\lambda,\nu}_{\frac{e^{2t}-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f)(\lambda,\nu),

where we repeatedly used Proposition
6.3
along with Lemma
6.12
. Thus by injectivity of GFT and the density of
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
in
L^{2}(G)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}f=\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f

for all
f\in L^{2}(G)
.
The proof is concluded by using (
6.24
) in Lemma
6.10
.
∎

6.4.
Compactness and spectrum of perturbed OU semigroup

In this section, we consider a further sub-class of the Lévy-Khintchine exponents
\mathcal{N}
that satisfies the finite exponential moment condition. Let us define

(6.28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\exp}=\left\{\psi=(\sigma,b,\kappa)\in\mathcal{N}:\int_{|v|>1}e^{a|v|}\kappa(dv)<\infty\text{ for all }a>0\right\}.

We note that
\mathcal{N}_{\exp}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\log}
, where
\mathcal{N}_{\log}
is defined by (
6.5
), and therefore according to Theorem
6.1
,
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
is ergodic with the unique invariant density
\mathsf{p}_{\psi}
whenever
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
. We need to restrict ourselves to
\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
to ensure that the space of polynomials in
G
is included in
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
for all
p\geqslant 1
.
If
(S_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
denotes the Lévy process associated to
\psi
, the above condition is also equivalent to
\mathbb{E}(e^{\xi|S_{t}|})<\infty
for all
\xi>0
and
t>0
, see
[
34
, Theorem 25.3]
. In the next proposition we prove compactness of the semigroup
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
.

Proposition 6.13
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
, where
\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
is defined by (
6.28
),
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator on
L^{2}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
.

Proof.

We adapt an idea very similar to the proof of
[
22
, Proposition 6(a)]
. Writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f(g,g^{\prime})=\int_{G}f(g^{\prime})\frac{\mathsf{p}^{\psi}_{t}(g,g^{\prime})}{\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(g^{\prime})}\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(g^{\prime})dg^{\prime},

it suffices to show that

(6.29)

\displaystyle I(t):=\int\limits_{G\times G}\mathsf{p}^{\psi}_{t}(g,g^{\prime})^{2}\frac{\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(g)}{\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(g^{\prime})}dgdg^{\prime}<\infty.

Now, for any
g=(h,v)
and
g^{\prime}=(h^{\prime},v^{\prime})
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}^{\psi}_{t}(g,g^{\prime})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{p}_{t}((h,v-\widetilde{v}),(h^{\prime},v^{\prime}))\mu^{\psi_{t}}(d\widetilde{v}),

where
\mathsf{p}_{t}
is the kernel associated with the semigroup
\mathbb{P}_{t}
. Let
\rho
denote the Carnot-Carathéodory distance on
G
. Then, due to
[
7
, Proposition 5.1.4]
, there exists a positive constant
c
such that

(6.30)

\displaystyle c^{-1}|g-g^{\prime}|_{G}\leqslant\rho(g,g^{\prime})\leqslant c|g-g^{\prime}|_{G},

where
|g|_{G}
is the homogeneous norm on
G
defined as
|(h,v)|_{G}=\sqrt{|h|^{2}+|v|_{1}}
, where
|\cdot|_{1}
denotes the
l^{1}
norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Therefore, using the triangle inequality one gets

\displaystyle\rho(g,g^{\prime})^{2}-c|\widetilde{v}|_{1}\leqslant\rho((h,v-\widetilde{v}),(h^{\prime},v^{\prime}))^{2}\leqslant\rho(g,g^{\prime})^{2}+c^{-1}|\widetilde{v}|_{1}.

Also, using the estimates of the heat kernel
\mathsf{q}_{t}
in the proof of
[
22
, Proposition 6(a)]
we obtain that for any
0<\epsilon<1
, there exists a constant
C_{\epsilon}
such that

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}^{\psi}_{t}(g,g^{\prime})^{2}\leqslant C_{\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{\rho(\delta_{(e^{2t}-1)^{-1/2}}g^{-1}\delta_{(1-e^{-2t})^{-1/2}}g^{\prime})^{2}}{1+\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\times\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\exp\left(\frac{c|\widetilde{v}|_{1}}{(1+\epsilon)(e^{2t}-1)}\right)\mu^{\psi_{t}}(d\widetilde{v}),

where
\mu^{\psi_{t}}
is defined in (
6.8
), and we also used the Jensen’s inequality with respect to the probability measure
\mu^{\psi_{t}}
. Since
\psi_{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
whenever
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
, the integral above on the right hand side is convergent for all
t>0
. This shows that for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}^{\psi}_{t}(g,g^{\prime})^{2}\leqslant C_{\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{\rho(\delta_{(e^{2t}-1)^{-1/2}}g^{-1}\delta_{(1-e^{-2t})^{-1/2}}g^{\prime})^{2}}{1+\epsilon}\right)\text{ for all }t>0

for some constant
C_{\epsilon}>0
. A very similar argument also leads to the following estimates:

(6.31)

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(g)

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{\rho(g)^{2}}{2+2\epsilon}\right)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}e^{\frac{c|\widetilde{v}|_{1}}{2+2\epsilon}}\mu_{\psi}(d\widetilde{v})

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(g^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geqslant K_{\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{\rho(g^{\prime})^{2}}{2-2\epsilon}\right)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}e^{-\frac{c^{-1}|\widetilde{v}|_{1}}{2-2\epsilon}}\mu_{\psi}(d\widetilde{v})

for some positive constants
C_{\epsilon},K_{\epsilon}
. As a result, using the same argument as in the proof of
[
22
, Proposition 6(a)]
, one can show that
I(t)<\infty
for all
t>0
, which proves the proposition.
∎

Corollary 6.14
.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
(see (
6.28
) for definition). Then, for all
1<p<\infty
,
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}:L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})\longrightarrow L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
is a compact operator for all
t>0
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
is compact on
L^{2}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
and bounded on
L^{\infty}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
and
L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
, by interpolation,
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
is compact on
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
for any
1<p<\infty
.
∎

We denote the set of all polynomials on
G
by
\mathcal{P}
and the set of homogeneous polynomials in
\mathcal{P}
with degree
n
by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the eigenspace of
\mathbb{D}
on
\mathcal{P}
associated to the eigenvalue
n
. We also define
\mathcal{B}_{n}
to be the space of all polynomials on
G
with degree at most
n
. Note that

\mathcal{B}_{n}=\mathcal{P}_{0}+\cdots+\mathcal{P}_{n}
for each
n\geqslant 1
. Thus,
\mathcal{P}=\cup_{n\geqslant 0}\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Also, both
\mathbb{Q}_{1/2}
and
\Gamma_{\psi}
(see (
6.19
) for definition) are invertible on
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, hence on
\mathcal{P}
whenever
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
. Moreover, from the estimate in (
6.31
) it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{G}e^{a|g|_{1}}\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(dg)<\infty

for all
a>0
, where
|g|_{1}
denotes the
l^{1}
-norm of
g
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}
. As a result, by
[
1
]
,
\mathcal{P}
is dense in
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
for all
p\geqslant 1
. In the next theorem, we show that for any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
, the spectrum of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
does not depend on
\psi
. This phenomenon is very different from what we have observed for the spectrum of
\mathbb{Q}^{\psi}_{t}
(equivalently,
\Delta^{\!\psi}_{G}
), which clearly depends on
\psi
, according to Corollary
5.10
.

Theorem 6.15
.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{N}
. Then, for all
t>0
we have the following:

(1)

For all
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
and
1<p<\infty
,
e^{-t{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\subseteq\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t};L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}))
.

(2)

If
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
, the spectrum of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
in
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
does not depend on
p\in(1,\infty)
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t};L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}))\setminus\{0\}=\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t};L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}))=e^{-t{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}.

(3)

For any
1<p<\infty
, the eigenspace of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
in
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
associated to
e^{-nt}
is given by
\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n}=(\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma_{\psi})^{-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
, where
\Gamma_{\psi}
is defined in (
6.19
).

For any closed operator
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq E\longrightarrow E
, where
E
is a Banach space, the algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue
\theta
of
A
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathtt{M}_{a}(\theta,A)=\dim\left(\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\ker(A-\theta I)^{n}\right),

and the geometric multiplicity is defined as
\mathtt{M}_{g}(\theta,A)=\dim(\ker(A-\theta I))
. We note that both algebraic and geometric multiplicities can be infinite. The next proposition shows that the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{L}
and
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
have the same multiplicities whenever
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
.

Theorem 6.16
.

Let
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
(resp.
\mathbb{L})
denote the generator of the semigroup
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}:L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})\longrightarrow L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
(resp.
\mathbb{P}_{t}:L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p})\longrightarrow L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p})
). Then for any
1<p<\infty
,
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\exp}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\mathtt{M}_{a}(-\beta,\mathbb{L}_{\psi})

\displaystyle=\mathtt{M}_{a}(-\beta,\mathbb{L})<\infty,

\displaystyle\mathtt{M}_{g}(-\beta,\mathbb{L}_{\psi})

\displaystyle=\mathtt{M}_{g}(-\beta,\mathbb{L})<\infty.

In particular, choosing
\psi(\lambda)=-\epsilon|\lambda|^{2}
for
\epsilon>0
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 6.17
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, let us denote

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\epsilon}=\mathbb{L}+\epsilon\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}.

Then,
\mathbb{L}_{\epsilon}
generates a perturbed OU semigroup on
G
, denoted by
\mathbb{P}^{\epsilon}
. Denoting its invariant density by
\mathsf{p}_{\epsilon}
, for all
1<p<\infty
we have

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathbb{L}_{\epsilon};L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\epsilon}))=\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathbb{L}_{\epsilon};L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\epsilon}))=-\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Also, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and for all
\epsilon>0
we have

\displaystyle\mathtt{M}_{a}(-\beta,\mathbb{L}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\mathtt{M}_{a}(-\beta,\mathbb{L})<\infty,

\displaystyle\mathtt{M}_{g}(-\beta,\mathbb{L}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\mathtt{M}_{g}(-\beta,\mathbb{L})<\infty.

In order to prove the above results, we need the following two lemmas.

Lemma 6.18
.

For all
t\geqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma_{\psi}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}=\delta_{e^{-t}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma_{\psi}\text{ on }\mathcal{P}.

Proof.

By
[
22
, Lemma 5(b)]
it is known that
\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}\cos^{\mathbb{L}}\theta=\delta_{\cos\theta}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}
. Writing
\cos\theta=e^{-t}
for
t\geqslant 0
and using the intertwining relationship (
6.21
) concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The next lemma is about the spectrum and eigenspaces of a general compact operator and its proof can be found in the proof of
[
22
, Theorem 7]
.

Lemma 6.19
.

Let
T:E\longrightarrow E
be a compact operator on a separable Banach space
E
. Let
\Theta
be a set of eigenvalues of
T
and let
E_{\theta},\theta\in\Theta
be eigensubspaces of
T
whose union is total in
E
, i.e.,
\operatorname{Span}\{\cup_{\theta\in\Theta}E_{\theta}\}
is dense in
E
. Then

(1)

The spectrum of
T
is
\Theta\cup\{0\}
.

(2)

E_{\theta}
is the whole eigenspace of
T
associated to
\theta\in\Theta
.

Proof of Theorem
6.15
.

From Proposition
6.6
we have
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}\Pi=\Pi\widetilde{P}_{t}
on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\mu)
for all
t\geqslant 0
and
p>1
. Since
\Pi
is injective and
\sigma_{\rm p}(\widetilde{P}_{t};L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\mu))=e^{-t{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, (
1
) follows immediately.

Next, we observe that
\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma_{\psi}:\mathcal{P}_{n}\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}_{n}
is bijective for each
n\geqslant 1
. Therefore, Lemma
6.18
implies that for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}(\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma_{\psi})^{-1}=(\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma_{\psi})^{-1}\delta_{e^{-t}}\text{ on }\mathcal{P}.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is an eigenspace of
\delta_{e^{-t}}
associated to
e^{-nt}
, the above intertwining relation implies that
\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n}=(\mathbb{Q}_{1/2}\Gamma_{\psi})^{-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
is an eigenspace of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
associated to
e^{-nt}
. Also, from the equality
(\mathbb{Q}_{1/2}\Gamma_{\psi})^{-1}(\mathcal{P})=\mathcal{P}
we get that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{Q}_{1/2}\Gamma_{\psi})^{-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})=\mathcal{P},

and therefore,
\operatorname{Span}\{\cup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n}\}
is dense in
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
. Also, by Proposition
6.13
, there exists
T_{\psi}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
is compact on
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
for all
t>T_{\psi}
. Thus by Lemma
6.19
for all
t>T_{\psi}
we have

(6.32)

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t};L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}))\setminus\{0\}=\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t};L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}))=\{e^{-nt}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\},

and
\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n}
is the eigenspace of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
e^{-nt}
. By the spectral mapping theorem for semigroups, e. g.
[
13
, p. 180, Equation (2.7)]
, it follows that (
6.32
) holds for all
t>0
.
It remains to show that for any
0<t<T_{\psi}
, the eigenspace of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
e^{-nt}
is still given by
\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n}
. Note that Lemma
6.18
implies that
\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n}
is included in the eigenspace of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
associated to the eigenvalue
e^{-nt}
for any
t>0
. For
t<T_{\psi}
, let
\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n,t}
denote the eigenspace of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
in
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
associated to
e^{-nt}
. Also, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
kt>T_{\psi}
. Using the semigroup property of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
, for any
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n,t}
we have

\displaystyle e^{-knt}f=\underbrace{\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}\cdots\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}}_{k\text{ times }}f=\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{kt}f.

Since
kt>T_{\psi}
, from the above argument it follows that
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n,t}\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n}
, which implies that
\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n,t}=\mathcal{E}^{\psi}_{n}
for all
0<t<T_{\psi}
. This proves (
3
), and the proof of theorem is complete. ∎

6.5.
Regularity properties of perturbed OU semigroups

In order to prove Proposition
6.16
, we will first prove that any generalized eigenfunction of
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
must be a polynomial. To achieve this, let us denote the
right-invariant
vector field corresponding to
X\in\mathfrak{g}
by
X^{r}
. Then, the following is known from
[
23
]
.

Lemma 6.20

(Lemma 3.2 in
[
23
]
)
.

Let
P_{t}
be the left-invariant horizontal heat semigroup on a nilpotent Lie group
G
. Then, for any
X\in\mathfrak{g}
and
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)

\displaystyle X^{r}P_{t}f=P_{t}X^{r}f.

For a multi-index
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{k}
, let denote
X^{r}_{\alpha}=X^{r}_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots X^{r}_{\alpha_{k}}
. We also denote the weighted Sobolev spaces with respect to the right-invariant vector fields by
W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
, that is,

\displaystyle W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})=\left\{f\in L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}):X^{r}_{\alpha}f\in L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})\text{ for each }\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{k}\right\},

where
X^{r}_{\alpha}f
is defined in the weak sense. We note that for all
1<p<\infty
and
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
, the set of compactly supported smooth functions
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
is dense in
W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
. Indeed, a simple truncation argument shows that the set of all
W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
-functions with compact support is dense. On the other hand, given any
f\in W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
with compact support, it follows from
[
8
, Proposition 3.3]
with
\chi=1
(see also
[
10
, Proposition 19]
) that
f
can be approximated by functions in
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
. The next two lemmas provide some regularity properties of the semigroup
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
.

Lemma 6.21
.

For all
t>0
,
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
maps
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
to
W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
continuously for all
1<p<\infty
and
k\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

First, we show that for all
t>0
,
1<q<\infty
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
,

(6.33)

\displaystyle|X^{r}_{i}\mathbb{Q}_{t}f|\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}\mathbb{Q}_{t}(|f|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}

for some positive constant
C
. Indeed, using the same technique from the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
, we obtain that

\displaystyle X^{r}_{i}\mathbb{Q}_{1}f=\mathbb{Q}_{1}X^{r}_{i}f=-\langle f,X^{r}_{i}\mathsf{q}_{1}\rangle,

where
\mathsf{q}_{1}
is the horizontal heat kernel at
t=1
and the last identity follows from integration by parts. Noting that
X^{r}_{i}\mathsf{q}_{1}=X^{r}_{i}(\log\mathsf{q}_{1})\mathsf{q}_{1}
, using Hölder’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle|X^{r}_{i}\mathbb{Q}_{1}f|\leqslant\mathbb{Q}_{1}(|f|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{G}|X^{r}_{i}(\log\mathsf{q}_{1})|^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}=C\mathbb{Q}_{1}(|f|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Since
c\delta_{c}X^{r}_{i}=X^{r}_{i}\delta_{c}
and
\delta_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}}\mathbb{Q}_{1}\delta_{\sqrt{t}}=\mathbb{Q}_{t}
for all
t,c>0
, the above inequality yields (
6.33
). Recall that
\mathbb{P}_{t}=\delta_{e^{-t}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}
, therefore (
6.33
) implies that for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
and
t>0
,

\displaystyle|X^{r}_{i}\mathbb{P}_{t}f|\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{e^{2t}-1}}\mathbb{P}_{t}(|f|^{p}|)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Now, for any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
we can use (
6.7
), and then for any
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
we have

\displaystyle|X^{r}_{i}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f|^{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\int_{G}X^{r}_{i}\mathbb{P}_{t}f(h,v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv^{\prime})\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{G}|X^{r}_{i}\mathbb{P}_{t}f(h,v-v^{\prime})|^{p}\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C^{p}}{(e^{2t}-1)^{\frac{p}{2}}}\int_{G}\mathbb{P}_{t}(|f|^{p})(h,v-v^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dv^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{p}}{(e^{2t}-1)^{\frac{p}{2}}}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}(|f|^{p}).

Integrating the above inequality with respect to the invariant distribution
\mathsf{p}_{\psi}
, we get

(6.34)

\displaystyle\|X^{r}_{i}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f\|_{L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})}\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{e^{2t}-1}}\|f\|_{L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})}.

Using the density of
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
in
W^{1,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
, we conclude that (
6.34
) holds for all
f\in W^{1,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
, which proves the statement of the lemma for
k=1
. For
k\geqslant 2
, the proof follows in the same way with the additional observation

\displaystyle X^{r}_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f=e^{-kt}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f

for all
f\in W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
with
|\alpha|=k
. The last identity holds for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
and extends to
W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
by density.
∎

Lemma 6.22
.

For any
f\in W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
,
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{k}
, and
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
, we have

\displaystyle\|X^{r}_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f\|_{L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})}\leqslant e^{-kt}\|X^{r}_{\alpha}f\|_{L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})}.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the above lemma for
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
. The rest will follow from the density of
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
in
W^{k,q}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
. Using Lemma
6.20
, for any
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
and
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{k}
we have

\displaystyle X^{r}_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}_{t}f=e^{-kt}\mathbb{P}_{t}X^{r}_{\alpha}f,

where we used the fact that
\mathbb{P}_{t}=\delta_{e^{-t}}\mathbb{Q}_{\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}}
and
X^{r}\delta_{e^{-t}}=e^{-t}\delta_{e^{-t}}X^{r}
for any
X\in\mathfrak{g}
. Now, for any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
and
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
we have

\displaystyle X^{r}_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f=X^{r}_{\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathbb{P}_{t}f(v,w-w^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dw^{\prime}).

Since
X^{r}_{\alpha}
commutes with the vertical vector fields and
f,\mathbb{P}_{t}f
are smooth, we have

\displaystyle X^{r}_{\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathbb{P}_{t}f(v,w-w^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dw^{\prime})

\displaystyle=e^{-kt}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}X^{r}_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}_{t}f(v,w-w^{\prime})\mu^{\psi_{t}}(dw^{\prime})

\displaystyle=e^{-kt}X^{r}_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f.

Finally, using the contraction property of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
on
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
we conclude the proof of the lemma.
∎

Next, we call
f
to be a generalized eigenfunction of
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\theta
if there exists
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{L}^{s}_{\psi})
and
(\mathbb{L}_{\psi}-\theta I)^{s}f=0
.

Lemma 6.23
.

Let
f
be a generalized eigenfunction of
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
-\beta
satisfying
(\mathbb{L}_{\psi}+\beta I)^{s}f=0
. Then
f
is a polynomial of degree at most
\beta
.

Proof.

We will prove the lemma using an argument similar to the proof of
[
24
, Proposition 3.1]
. For
s=1
, the lemma holds true due to Theorem
6.15
(
3
). Now, we proceed by induction. Suppose that the statement of the lemma holds for some
s\geqslant 1
. Let
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{L}_{\psi}^{s+1})\subset L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
be such that
(\mathbb{L}_{\psi}+\beta I)^{s+1}f=0
. Then, we must have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f=e^{-\beta t}f+e^{-\beta t}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{(\mathbb{L}_{\psi}+\beta I)^{j}f}{j!}.

Also, by induction hypothesis, for each
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
,
(\mathbb{L}_{\psi}+\beta I)^{j}f
is a polynomial of degree at most
\beta
. Since by Lemma
6.21

P_{t}f\in W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
, we deduce from the above equation that
f\in W^{k,p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
. Therefore, for any
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{k}
,
X^{r}_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}f=e^{-\beta t}X^{r}_{\alpha}f
for all
k\geqslant\beta+1
. Using Lemma
6.22
we obtain that for all
k\geqslant\beta+1
and
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{k}
,

\displaystyle e^{-\beta t}\|X^{r}_{\alpha}f\|_{L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})}\leqslant e^{-kt}\|X^{r}_{\alpha}f\|_{L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})},

which forces that
X^{r}_{\alpha}f=0
whenever
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{k}
and
k\geqslant\beta+1
. Therefore,
f
is a polynomial of degree at most
\beta
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.16
.

We note that Lemma
6.23
implies that the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue
\beta
of
\mathbb{L}_{\psi}
is finite. Moreover, Theorem
6.9
also implies that for all
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
s\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle(\mathbb{L}+\beta I)^{s}\Gamma_{\psi}=\Gamma_{\psi}(\mathbb{L}_{\psi}+\beta I)^{s}\text{ on }\mathcal{P}.

Since
\Gamma_{\psi}
is invertible on
\mathcal{P}
, we conclude that
\dim(\ker(\mathbb{L}+\beta I)^{s})=\dim(\ker(\mathbb{L}_{\psi}+\beta I)^{s})
for all

, which concludes the proof.
∎

6.6.
Co-eigenfunctions of perturbed OU semigroup

Note that we only used the intertwining with
\mathbb{P}
to determine the spectrum and the eigenspaces of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
. On the other hand, the intertwining with
P^{\psi}
provides some information about the co-eigenfunctions of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
for even eigenvalues. For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
(see (
6.5
) for definition) and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{m}_{0}
, let us define

(6.35)

\displaystyle J^{\psi}_{\beta}(h,v)=(-1)^{|\beta|}\frac{\partial_{v}^{\beta}\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(h,v)}{\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(h,v)},

where
\partial^{\beta}_{v}=\partial^{\beta_{1}}_{v_{1}}\cdots\partial^{\beta_{m}}_{v_{m}}
. For any
1<p<\infty
, let us denote the adjoint of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}:L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})\longrightarrow L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
by
\mathbb{P}^{\psi*}_{t}:L^{q}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})\longrightarrow L^{q}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
, where
q=\frac{p}{p-1}
. Then we have the following result.

Theorem 6.24
.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
. Then, for all
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{m}_{0}
and
1<p<\infty
,
J^{\psi}_{\beta}\in L^{q}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
. Moreover, for all
t\geqslant 0
,

(6.36)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi*}_{t}J^{\psi}_{\beta}=e^{-2|\beta|t}J^{\psi}_{\beta}\text{ on }L^{q}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}).

Corollary 6.25
.

For any
\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{m}_{0}
, let us define

\displaystyle J(h,v)=(-1)^{|\beta|}\frac{\partial^{\beta}_{v}\mathsf{p}(h,v)}{\mathsf{p}(h,v)}.

Then, for all
t\geqslant 0
and
1<p<\infty
,
\mathbb{P}^{\ast}_{t}J_{\beta}=e^{-2|\beta|t}J_{\beta}
on
L^{q}(G,\mathsf{p})
.

We need some auxiliary results to prove the above theorem. To this end, for any nonnegative definite matrix
\sigma
of order
m\times m
, let
P^{\sigma}
denote the OU semigroup on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
generated by

\displaystyle L_{\sigma}f(v)=\mathrm{tr}(\sigma\nabla^{2}f(v))-2\langle v,\nabla f(v)\rangle.

Proposition 6.26
.

If
\psi=(\sigma,b,\kappa)\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
with
\sigma
non-singular, for all
p\geqslant 1
there exists a Markov operator
\Lambda_{\psi}:L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})\longrightarrow L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\varrho})
with dense range satisfying

(6.37)

\displaystyle P^{(\varrho)}_{t}\Lambda_{\psi}=\Lambda_{\psi}\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}\text{ on }L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}),

where
P^{(\varrho)}=P^{\varrho I_{m\times m}}
,
\varrho
being the smallest eigenvalue of
\frac{\sigma}{2}
, and
\mu_{\varrho}
is the invariant distribution of
P^{(\varrho)}
that is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{\varrho}(dv)=(\varrho\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{|v|^{2}}{\varrho}}dv.

We need the following lemmas to prove the above proposition.

Lemma 6.27
.

Let
\Lambda
be defined as in (
6.19
). Then for any
p\geqslant 1
,
\Lambda:L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})\longrightarrow L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\psi})
has dense range.

Remark 6.28
.

We note that
\Lambda
is not injective. Choosing
f(x,y,r,v)=x
, we have
f\in L^{2}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
. Also, due the symmetry of
\mathsf{p}
with respect to
x
, we must have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}x\mathsf{p}(x,y,r,v+v^{\prime})dxdydrdv^{\prime}=0.

Proof.

Let us write
e_{\lambda}(v)=e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v\rangle}
. Clearly,
e_{\lambda}\in L^{2}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
for any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Also,

\displaystyle\Lambda e_{\lambda}(v)=e_{\lambda}(v)\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathsf{p}(z,r,v^{\prime})e^{-\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v^{\prime}\rangle}dv^{\prime}drdz,

which shows that
e_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Ran}(\Lambda)
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Therefore, the proof of the lemma follows after noting that
\operatorname{Span}\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\}
is a dense subspace in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\psi})
for all
p\geqslant 1
.
∎

The next lemma is a version of
[
33
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Lemma 6.29
.

Let
\psi=(\sigma,b,\kappa)\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
. Then, for all
t\geqslant 0
,

(6.38)

\displaystyle P^{\sigma}_{t}T_{b,\kappa}=T_{b,\kappa}P^{\psi}_{t}\text{ on }C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}),

where
T_{b,\kappa}f=f{\ast}h_{b,\kappa}
and for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(h_{b,\kappa})(\lambda)=\exp(-\widetilde{\psi}_{-\infty}(-\lambda))

with
\widetilde{\psi}=(0,b,\kappa)\in\mathcal{N}
and
\widetilde{\psi}_{-\infty}=\lim_{t\to-\infty}\widetilde{\psi}_{t}
, see (
6.6
).
Moreover,
T_{b,\kappa}
is a Markov operator with dense range, and has a unique continuous extension, also denoted by
T_{b,\kappa}:L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\psi})\longrightarrow L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\varrho})
for all
p\geqslant 1
. As a result, (
6.38
) holds on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\psi})
for all
p\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

From
[
34
, Theorem 17.1]
it is known that for any
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\widehat{P}^{\psi}_{t}f)(\lambda)=e^{\psi_{t}(e^{-2t}\lambda)}\mathcal{F}(f)(e^{-2t}\lambda),

where
\psi_{t}
is defined in (
6.6
). In particular, we also have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\widehat{P}^{\sigma}_{t}f)(\lambda)=\exp\left(\frac{e^{-4t}-1}{4}\langle\sigma\lambda,\lambda\rangle\right)\mathcal{F}(f)(e^{-2t}\lambda)

for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Also,
T_{b,\kappa}
is a Markov operator as
h_{b,\kappa}
is the probability measure associated with the Lévy-Khintchine exponent
\lambda\mapsto-\widetilde{\psi}_{-\infty}(-\lambda)
. As a result,
T_{b,\kappa}
can be extended uniquely as a bounded operator on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
for all
p\geqslant 1
. Let
\widehat{T}_{b,\kappa}
denote the
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
-adjoint of
T_{b,\kappa}
. Then, from the definition of

it follows that for all

and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\widehat{T}_{b,\kappa}f)(\lambda)=e^{-\widetilde{\psi}_{-\infty}(\lambda)}\mathcal{F}(f)(\lambda).

Now, a simple computation shows that for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
t>0
,

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(e^{-2t}\lambda)-\widetilde{\psi}_{-\infty}(e^{-2t}\lambda)=-\widetilde{\psi}_{-\infty}(\lambda)+\frac{e^{-4t}-1}{4}\langle\sigma\lambda,\lambda\rangle.

As a result, for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\widehat{P}^{\psi}_{t}\widehat{T}_{b,\kappa}f)(\lambda)=\mathcal{F}(\widehat{T}_{b,\kappa}\widehat{P}^{\sigma}_{t}f)(\lambda),

which is equivalent to
\widehat{P}^{\psi}_{t}\widehat{T}_{b,\kappa}f=\widehat{\Lambda}_{b,\kappa}\widehat{P}^{\sigma}_{t}f
for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
. Taking the
L^{2}
-adjoint of the last identity proves (
6.38
) on
C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
. Using the density of
C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
in
C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and the contraction property of the semigroups, we conclude that (
6.38
) holds. We further get that

\displaystyle p_{\varrho}T_{b,\kappa}f=p_{\varrho}P^{(\varrho)}_{t}T_{b,\kappa}=p_{\varrho}T_{b,\kappa}P^{\psi}_{t}f

for all
t\geqslant 0
and
f\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
. Since
T_{b,\kappa}
is Markov and
\mu_{\psi}
is the unique invariant distribution of
P^{\psi}
, the above equation forces that
\mu_{\varrho}T_{b,\kappa}=\mu_{\psi}
. As a result,
T_{b,\kappa}
extends as a bounded operator from
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\psi})
to
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\varrho})
for all
p\geqslant 1
.
Finally, we note that for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
T_{b,\kappa}e_{\lambda}=e^{-\psi_{-\infty}(-\lambda)}e_{\lambda}
where
e_{\lambda}(v)=e^{\textnormal{i}\langle\lambda,v\rangle}
. Since
\operatorname{Span}\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\}
is dense in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\psi})
for all

, we conclude that
T_{b,\kappa}:L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\psi})\longrightarrow L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu_{\varrho})
has dense range for all
p\in[1,\infty]
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Proposition
6.26
.

Since
\sigma
is invertible with positive eigenvalues

, from
[

Lemma 6.30

Moreover, for any

For
\sigma

\Lambda_{\psi}

\displaystyle\int_{G}|J^{\psi}_{\beta}(h,v)|^{q}\mathsf{p}_{\psi}(h,v)dhdv

6.24
.

is non-singular. Taking the adjoint of (

6.7.
L^{1}
-spectrum

As noted above, the
L^{p}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
-spectrum of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
is discrete for all
1<p<\infty
, where
\mathsf{p}_{\psi}
is the unique invariant density of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
defined by (
6.9
). The spectrum of
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}
is very different in
L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
. The following result is analogous to
[
24
, Theorem 5.1]
.

Theorem 6.31
.

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{N}_{\log}
and
t\geqslant 0
,
\sigma(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t};L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}))=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|\leqslant 1\}
. In fact,
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|<1\}\subseteq\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t};L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}))
.
Therefore,
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}
is non-compact on
L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
for every
t>0
.

Proof.

From Proposition
6.6
, we have for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}\Pi=\Pi\widetilde{P}_{t}\text{ on }L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\widetilde{\mu}).

Since
\Pi:L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\widetilde{\mu})\longrightarrow L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
is injective and
e^{-t{\mathbb{C}}_{>0}}\subseteq\sigma_{\rm p}(\widetilde{P}_{t};L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\widetilde{\mu}))
, see
[
24
, Theorem 5.1]
, we conclude that
e^{-t{\mathbb{C}}_{>0}}\subseteq\sigma_{\rm p}(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t};L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}))
for all
t>0
, where
{\mathbb{C}}_{\geqslant 0}
(resp.
{\mathbb{C}}_{>0}
) denotes the the set of all complex numbers with non-positive (resp. strictly negative) real part. As
\sigma(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t};L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}))
is a closed subset of
{\mathbb{C}}
and also contained in
e^{-t{\mathbb{C}}_{\geqslant 0}}\cup\{0\}
, we have

\sigma(\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t};L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi}))=e^{-t{\mathbb{C}}_{\geqslant 0}}\cup\{0\}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|\leqslant 1\}
. Also,
\mathbb{P}^{\psi}_{t}:L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})\longrightarrow L^{1}(G,\mathsf{p}_{\psi})
is not compact as the its spectrum is not a discrete subset of
{\mathbb{C}}
.
∎

Appendix A
Generalized Fourier transform on nilpotent Lie groups

In the setting of locally compact groups the generalized Fourier transform (GFT) relies on the unitary dual
\widehat{G}
of the group
G
, that is, the space of irreducible unitary representations of
G
. Recall that the GFT
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)
of a function
f\in L^{1}(G)
is defined as

(A.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\pi):=\int_{G}f(g)\pi(g)dg,\quad\pi=\left(\pi,H_{\pi}\right)\in\widehat{G}.

Note that
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\pi)
is a linear operator on the representation space
H_{\pi}
. For nilpotent groups the unitary dual
\widehat{G}
can be described using
Kirillov’s orbit method
, see
[
9
,
20
]
.
For the sake of completeness, we briefly describe it here. First, we recall the following theorem due to Kirillov, we refer to
[
9
, Section 2.2]
for details. One of the simplifications in the case of nilpotent groups is that one can realize these representations on the same Hilbert space which is described explicitly as an
L^{2}
-space on a Euclidean space.

Theorem A.1

(Kirillov)
.

Let
G
be any connected nilpotent Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Then, the following holds.

(1)

For any
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
there exists a sub-algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{l}
of maximal dimension such that
l([\mathfrak{h}_{l},\mathfrak{h}_{l}])=0
.
\mathfrak{h}_{l}
is called polarizing for
l
.

(2)

H_{l}=\exp(\mathfrak{h}_{l})
is a closed subgroup of
G
and
\rho_{l}(\exp(X))=\exp(\textnormal{i}l(X))
are one-dimensional representations of
H_{l}
.

(3)

The induced representation
\pi_{l}=\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{H_{l},\rho_{l}}
is an irreducible unitary representation of
G
.

(4)

If
\pi
is an irreducible unitary representation of
G
then
\pi
is unitarily equivalent to
\pi_{l}
for some
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
.

(5)

\pi_{l_{1}}
and
\pi_{l_{2}}
are unitarily equivalent if and only if
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
belong to the same co-adjoint orbit.

In this case, the Hilbert space
H_{\pi_{l}}
for the representation
\pi_{l}
can be realized as
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, where
d=n-\dim(\mathfrak{h}_{l})
. For any
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, its
radical
is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{rad}_{l}=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}:l([X,X^{\prime}])=0\text{ for all }X^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{g}\}.

By
[
9
, Theorem 1.3.3]
,
\mathfrak{h}_{l}
in Theorem
A.1
(
1
) is a polarizing sub-algebra if and only if

(1)

\operatorname{rad}_{l}\subset\mathfrak{h}_{l}
,

(2)

\dim(\mathfrak{h}_{l})=\frac{\dim(\operatorname{rad}_{l})+\dim(\mathfrak{g})}{2}
,

(3)

l([\mathfrak{h}_{l},\mathfrak{h}_{l}])=0
.

The generalized Fourier transform on
G
is formally defined as follows: for any
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
and
f\in L^{1}(G)
,

(A.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\pi_{l})=\int_{G}f(g)\pi_{l}(g)dg.

Then, by
[
14
, Theorem 7.44]
,
\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\pi_{l}):L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\longrightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. We now briefly describe a few preliminaries to state the Plancherel theorem for
\mathcal{F}_{G}
. The major technicalities are required to define
Plancherel measure
for GFT. Let
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
be a basis of
\mathfrak{g}
, and define
K_{l}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
such that
K_{l}(i,j)=l([X_{i},X_{j}])
for all
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
.

Definition A.2
.

Let
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. Then,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
is called a
jump index
for
l
if
\operatorname{rank}(K^{i}_{l})
is strictly larger than
\operatorname{rank}(K^{i-1}_{l})
, where
K^{i}_{l}
is the
i\times n
sub-matrix of
K_{l}
formed by the first
i
rows of
K_{l}
.

From the above definition, it is clear that for each jump index, the rank increases exactly by
1
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let
R_{i}=\max\{\operatorname{rank}(K^{i}_{l}):l\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\}
. Let us define

(A.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\ast}=\{l\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}:\operatorname{rank}(K^{i}_{l})=R_{i}\text{ for any }1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{U}^{\ast}
is a Zariski open subset of
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, and therefore
\mathcal{U}^{\ast}
has full Lebesgue measure in
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. Hence, it suffices to consider irreducible unitary representation
\pi_{l}
for
l\in\mathcal{U}^{\ast}
.
According to
[
9
, Theorem 3.1.6]
,
\mathcal{U}^{\ast}
is
G
-invariant and therefore it is an union of co-adjoint orbits in
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, and each
l\in\mathcal{U}^{\ast}
has the same set of jump indices. Denoting the common set of jump indices by
S
, we have a partition of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
into two disjoint sets,
S
and
T
respectively. Note that
S
has even number of elements as
K_{l}
is a skew-symmetric matrix. Let
\{X^{\ast}_{1},\ldots,X^{\ast}_{n}\}
denote the dual basis of
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
and let us write

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}_{T}=\operatorname{Span}\{X^{\ast}_{i}:i\in T\}.

Then by
[
9
, Theorem 3.1.9]
, every co-adjoint orbit in
\mathcal{U}^{\ast}
intersects
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}_{T}
at a unique point. As a result, by Theorem
A.1
(
5
), all irreducible representations on
G
are unitarily equivalent to
\pi_{l}
for some
l\in\mathcal{U}^{\ast}\cap\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}_{T}
. Let
B_{l}
denote the
|S|\times|S|
matrix such that
\widetilde{K}_{l}(i,j)=l([X_{i},X_{j}])
for all
i,j\in S
. Then, the following Plancherel theorem holds, see
[
9
, Theorem 4.3.10]
.

Theorem A.3

(Plancherel’s theorem)
.

For any
f\in L^{2}(G)\cap L^{1}(G)

\displaystyle\int_{G}|f(g)|^{2}dg=\int_{\mathcal{U}^{\ast}\cap\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}_{T}}\|\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\pi_{l})\|^{2}_{\operatorname{HS}}|\operatorname{Pf}(l)|dl,

where
\operatorname{Pf}(l)^{2}=\det(\widetilde{K}_{l})
is the Pfaffian, and the above integral is understood by identifying
\mathcal{U}^{\ast}\cap\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}_{T}
as a subset of the Euclidean space, and
dl
is the Lebesgue measure.
