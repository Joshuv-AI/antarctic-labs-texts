AgentSense: Virtual Sensor Data Generation Using LLM Agents in Simulated Home Environments CCS: Human-centered computing Ubiquitous and mobile computing CCS: Computing methodologies Artificial intelligence
Zikang Leng, Megha Thukral, Yaqi Liu, Hrudhai Rajasekhar, Shruthi K. Hiremath, Thomas Plötz

Abstract
. A major obstacle in developing robust and generalizable smart home-based Human Activity Recognition (HAR) systems is the lack of large-scale, diverse labeled datasets. Variability in home layouts, sensor configurations, and user behavior adds further complexity, as individuals follow varied routines and perform activities in distinct ways. Building HAR systems that generalize well requires training data that captures the diversity across users and environments. To address these challenges, we introduce AgentSense , a virtual data generation pipeline where diverse personas are generated by leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs). These personas are used to create daily routines, which are then decomposed into low-level action sequences. Subsequently, the actions are executed in a simulated home environment called ‘VirtualHome’ that we extended with virtual ambient sensors capable of recording the agents’ activities as they unfold. Overall, AgentSense enables the generation of rich, virtual sensor datasets that represent a wide range of users and home settings. Across five benchmark HAR datasets, we show that leveraging our virtual sensor data substantially improves performance, particularly when real data are limited. Notably, models trained on a combination of virtual data and just a few days of real data achieve performance comparable to those trained on the entire real datasets. These results demonstrate and prove the potential of virtual data to address one of the most pressing challenges in ambient sensing, which is the distinct lack of large-scale, annotated datasets—without requiring any manual data collection efforts.



1.
Introduction

Smart Home-based Human Activity Recognition (HAR) systems
(
Liciotti et al. 2020
;
Bouchabou et al. 2021c
)
, which track human movements and activities in home environments using ambient sensors, are instrumental in healthcare tracking, elderly care, and caregiving support
(
Alam et al. 2012
;
Qi et al. 2018
;
Chernova et al. 2024
)
.
The non-intrusive and privacy-preserving nature of ambient sensors enables seamless integration into real-world home environments, offering continuous support without disrupting routines.
However, building robust and reliable HAR models is inherently challenging.
The performance of these models relies on large amounts of high-quality and carefully curated datasets
(
Ghods and Cook 2019
;
Bouchabou et al. 2021b
;
Bouchabou et al. 2021d
;
Liciotti et al. 2020
;
Bouchabou et al. 2021a
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Singh et al. 2017
)
.
Yet, collecting such sensor data in real home environments is expensive, time-consuming, and requires user involvement, often limiting the scale of HAR data
(
Hiremath et al. 2022
;
Thukral et al. 2025
)
.
Moreover, unlike modalities like video or audio, sensor data cannot be easily examined and interpreted for annotation purposes, and often requires domain expertise, leading to increased annotation costs.
(
Kwon et al. 2020
)
.

These challenges in building generalizable and robust HAR models in home environments are further amplified by the inherent diversity of humans.
People have different personalities, routines, and activities. Their homes vary in layout, sensor deployments, and usage patterns
(
Cook et al. 2012
;
Thukral et al. 2025
)
.
Ideally, for each home, its users, and their unique routines, large amounts of sensor data should be collected and precisely annotated to train effective HAR models.
This raises practical constraints on building HAR models, as a lack of standardization hinders HAR model generalization and performance.

In this work, we propose a scalable solution,
AgentSense
, to the data scarcity and diversity challenges in human activity recognition (HAR) by harnessing the generative capability of large language models (LLMs) to create diverse human personas and simulate their daily routines within an activity simulator, VirtualHome—originally developed for video activity datasets by Puig
et al.

(
Puig et al. 2018
;
Puig et al. 2021
)
—which we extend to ambient sensor simulation, termed
X-VirtualHome
.
Our core hypothesis is that LLM-generated personas and routines can approximate real-world human behaviors, as observed in prior studies
(
Park et al. 2023
;
Yonekura et al. 2024
;
Aher et al. 2023
;
Horton 2023
;
Shanahan et al. 2023
)
, and that such synthetic diversity in low-level action data can be leveraged to significantly scale up sensor datasets.
This, in turn, can enable the development of more generalizable HAR models while reducing the dependence on costly real-world data collection.

In
AgentSense
, we employ multi-level prompting of LLMs to generate a diverse set of human-like personas.
Each persona reflects different demographic, behavioral, and lifestyle traits.
Based on these generated personas, we further prompt the LLM agents to create realistic and varied daily routines.
These routines are then decomposed into fine-grained, low-level actions that are further adapted to be directly executed in the VirtualHome simulator, enabling the simulation of human activities in a variety of home environments.
This virtually generated sensor data can then be used as a base dataset for initial pretraining of models (then fine-tuned with real-world, domain-specific data) to boost human activity recognition on real-world smart home datasets while reducing the need for extensive data collection.

Additionally, by simulating sensor data for human agents –LLM-generated personas– within virtual home environments, we can generate rich, diverse, and well-structured datasets at virtually no cost.
As we can steer the LLMs for the generation of diverse personas and, hence related activities and the corresponding home layouts and sensor placements in which these activities are performed, we can systematically scale and diversify the HAR training datasets.
Furthermore, we have much finer control over the annotation of the corresponding virtual sensor streams due to our LLM-based simulation process, allowing for high-quality annotations and thus, well-curated HAR training datasets.
Our data synthesis pipeline,
AgentSense
, presents a scalable method for large-scale data synthesis and enables the development of HAR systems that are both generalizable and adaptable across a wide range of real-world scenarios.

In particular, for this work, we generate
eighteen
unique personas and their corresponding routines, and simulate their behaviors across
twenty-two
distinct home layouts. This results in a dataset of
250 days
of virtual sensor data, which can be easily scaled further as needed, owing to the flexibility of our data generation pipeline. Through extensive experiments using real-world smart home sensor datasets, namely CASAS
(
Cook et al. 2012
)
and Orange4Home
(
Cumin et al. 2017
)
, we demonstrate that our simulated virtual sensor data not only enhances model performance but also significantly reduces the dependence on large-scale, manually collected datasets.
Across a range of real-world smart home scenarios, we achieve consistent performance improvements when using HAR models pretrained with virtual data.
Notably, by combining only a small portion of real-world data with virtual data, we obtain HAR results that closely follow models trained on full real datasets.
This highlights the practical value of our approach in building more efficient and scalable HAR systems.

To summarize, through our LLM-based agents in simulated home environments, we make the following contributions:

(1)

Introduce a novel data generation framework designed for Smart Homes:
We propose
AgentSense
, a virtual data generation pipeline that uses LLMs to generate diverse personas, their routines, activities, and fine-grained actions.
We extend the VirtualHome simulator with virtual ambient sensors to simulate the behaviors of these LLM-generated human agents.

(2)

Evaluate the effectiveness of generated virtual data in enhancing HAR tasks:
We conduct extensive experiments demonstrating the efficacy of virtual sensor data in enhancing human activity recognition (HAR) on benchmark smart home datasets, including CASAS and Orange4Home datasets.

2.
Related Work

Advancements in sensing technologies have propelled sensor-based Human Activity Recognition (HAR), particularly with nearables, for smart home applications like health monitoring and assisted living
(
Rafferty et al. 2017
;
Chan et al. 2008
;
Dang et al. 2020
;
Kulsoom et al. 2022
;
Chatting 2023
;
Morita et al. 2023
)
.
However, challenges such as the diversity of home layouts and resident behaviors, along with the high cost of labeled data, limit the scalability of traditional supervised HAR systems
(
Hiremath and Plötz 2020
;
Zhai et al. 2019
)
.
To address these issues, we propose a simulation-based framework for generating virtual sensor data across varied contexts and review related work on HAR development resources, the role of large language models (LLMs), and approaches to simulating real-world data.

2.1.
Large Language Models for ambient HAR

Recent breakthroughs in large-language models (LLMs) have assisted in developing an ambient HAR procedure.
Homes encode more information than just the triggers for recorded sensor events.
Other contextual information, such as the location of the activity, time of occurrence, duration, and activity sequences, constitutes additional relevant information that aids in the recognition process.
To incorporate such contextual information, textual descriptions of sensor event triggers (TDOST) were used to build HAR systems that were generalized to varying home layouts and activity patterns of residents
(
Thukral et al. 2025
)
.
TDOST converts raw sensor data from smart homes into natural language sentences, incorporating contextual information such as sensor type, location, and timing.
These sentences are encoded into embeddings using pretrained sentence encoders
(
Ni et al. 2021
)
, enabling HAR models to transfer across different home layouts without retraining.
TDOST includes several variants, like Basic, Temporal, LLM, and LLM+Temporal, which differ in the level of contextual detail captured.
A feature encoder and classifier adapt these embeddings for layout-agnostic activity recognition, utilizing available metadata like floorplans and sensor specifications.

Traditional methods represent newly observed activities in homes using attribute-activity matrices
(
Civitarese et al. 2024
)
, which can facilitate zero-shot HAR in homes.
A novel LLM-based ADL recognition system was proposed to provide zero-shot recognition capabilities in homes, using the capabilities of LLMs
(
Civitarese et al. 2024
)
.
They further show that such zero-shot performance can be improved with few-shot prompting.
A synergistic approach between an LLM and supervised classification methods for activity recognition showcased that LLM based activity recognition performed well when no initial labeled training data is available, and this performance improves when using a combination of LLMs and Supervised Classification Models for activity recognition
(
Takeda et al. 2024
)
.

Recent neuro-symbolic recognition methods integrate data-driven and context-aware approaches to incorporate and evaluate contextual information, often sourced from LLMs into the recognition process
(
Arrotta et al. 2024b
;
Arrotta et al. 2024a
)
.
The use of diverse semantic loss functions has demonstrated improvements over purely data-driven techniques by embedding contextual knowledge into the HAR model during training.
Furthermore, given that LLMs are contextual knowledge bases and provide an alternative to traditional ontology-based approaches, they were used to identify structural constructs that constitute complex human activities in homes
(
Hiremath and Ploetz 2024
)
.
Approaches focused on enhancing explainability in zero-shot recognition, as well as improving recognition performance through data-driven explainable AI (XAI) techniques, have been investigated
(
Fiori et al. 2024
;
Kianpisheh et al. 2024
)
.
Other, yet related works include the use of LLMs to interact with devices in the environment in naturalistic ways
(
King et al. 2024a
)
and help these devices respond thoughtfully to the resident’s requests
(
King et al. 2024b
)
.

In our work, we use LLMs to solve the reverse problem.
Instead of using these models for recognition procedures or to generate naturalistic explanations, we use them to generate personas, high-level routines, and low-level actions.
These are used as inputs to the smart home simulator (X-VirtualHome) that generates virtual data corresponding to the inputs received.
Thus, we leverage the contextual knowledge of LLMs to capture diversity in environments, different routine patterns based on demographics, and varying low-level action sequences for activities of interest.
Given the evidence that complex activities occur within homes
(
Hiremath and Ploetz 2024
;
Hiremath et al. 2022
)
and that complex HAR constitutes a combination of simple actions
(
Peng et al. 2018
)
, we use LLMs to generate the low-level action sequences, which are then used to produce virtual data for training robust HAR models.
To assess the effectiveness of virtual data in developing generalizable HAR models, we utilize the TDOST-Basic and TDOST-Temporal variants
(
Thukral et al. 2025
)
.
To provide a complete understanding of these variants used in this work, we will detail them in Section.
4
.

2.2.
Resources for Human Activity Recognition in Smart Homes

Given the importance of activity recognition for both clinical applications and long-term behavioral monitoring, there is growing interest in developing such systems for the space of smart homes. Research in this domain necessitates a comprehensive set of resources to support robust data collection protocols.
Central to this is the deployment of suitable infrastructure, including sensors and IoT devices, to capture a wide range of behavioral and contextual information.
Ethical considerations require informed consent from residents to uphold privacy and regulatory standards.
Following data acquisition, the generation of high-quality, context-sensitive annotations is critical for enabling downstream machine learning or behavioral analysis.
However, this process is often resource-intensive, typically requiring domain experts to provide sample-precise annotations
(
Hiremath and Plötz 2020
;
Intille et al. 2005
;
Hooper et al. 2012
;
Alemdar et al. 2013
;
Cook et al. 2003
)
.
Additionally, issues such as sensor noise, data incompleteness, and hardware malfunctions pose substantial challenges to data quality and, consequently, research validity.
The collection of longitudinal data introduces additional complexities, as it requires sustained participant involvement and adaptability to evolving household conditions over time.

Several existing initiatives—such as the CASAS repository, Orange4Home, the Marble dataset, and the Data Analytics for Health and Connected Care initiative
(
Cumin et al. 2017
;
Cook et al. 2012
;
Arrotta et al. 2021
;
Steenwinckel et al. 2022
)
have been instrumental in advancing data-driven research in smart homes.
These efforts have laid foundational groundwork by offering curated datasets for analysis.
However, their scope remains constrained, often encompassing a limited number of sensor-instrumented homes and relatively short data collection durations.
While these datasets serve as valuable baselines for exploring activity recognition and behavioral modeling, they fall short in capturing the extensive heterogeneity inherent to real-world home settings
(
Rashidi and Cook 2009
)
.
Additionally, due to high maintenance costs and the effort required for data annotation, most of these studies are not designed for large-scale or long-term analysis.

Homes exhibit significant variability owing to diverse home layouts, differing sensor configurations, changes to resident routines, evolving behavioral patterns, variations in household composition (number of residents, pets, guests), and interactions with household objects
(
Yonekura et al. 2024
;
Puig et al. 2018
)
.
This inherent diversity presents challenges for developing robust and generalizable models.
Previous studies employ both data-driven and knowledge-driven approaches tailored to specific homes and activities; however, they often rely on domain-specific expertise or necessitate active involvement from the resident
(
Hiremath et al. 2022
;
Rashidi and Cook 2009
)
.
Consequently, future research must focus on scaling data collection efforts to include a broader range of living spaces and dynamic contexts, thereby enhancing the ecological validity and applicability of smart home activity recognition systems.
More recently, studies have explored scalable analysis of household routines by leveraging Amazon Mechanical Turk to collect annotations on various activities, including their timestamps and durations, as they unfold over time
(
Patel and Chernova 2022
)
.
Additionally, while the primary motivation for HAR in homes is often centered around Ambient Assisted Living (AAL), assisting with daily activities can benefit a wide range of demographics and populations
(
Yonekura et al. 2024
)
.

This work presents a framework designed to capture and accommodate the inherent variability present in homes, including differences in object interaction patterns and the diverse daily routines of residents.
Recognizing that no two households are the same, the framework is designed to accommodate a considerable number of scenarios.
By enabling the simulation of multiple home layouts, sensor configurations, and resident profiles—including variations in activity patterns, lifestyle habits, and interaction dynamics the system facilitates the creation of heterogeneous datasets that closely mirror the complexity and diversity encountered in real-world settings.
This approach provides for virtual data that aids in building generalizable and robust activity recognition models for the smart home contexts.

2.3.
Simulating Real-World Data

The integration of large language models (LLMs) for generating human-like activities has been extensively explored, showcasing their ability to produce diverse and contextually appropriate behaviors.
Techniques such as LoRA fine-tuning have been used to better align LLM outputs with simulation environments, ensuring effective and relevant activity generation
(
Yonekura et al. 2024
)
.
Complementing this, multi-modal frameworks leveraging large pretrained video-language models (VLMs) have enabled the development of generalizable embodied agents capable of executing a wide range of tasks
(
Fan et al. 2022
;
Qian et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2025
)
.
These LLM-based models also facilitate more personalized user experiences by drawing on general knowledge to support meaningful interactions with the environment
(
Rey-Jouanchicot et al. 2024
)
.
As LLMs and VLMs evolve with enhanced conversational abilities, research has shown that their outputs can be evaluated for personality traits and shaped through instruction tuning, paving the way for more adaptive and realistic agent behaviors
(
Serapio-García et al. 2023
)
.

Building on this progress, prior work has investigated whether LLMs can use their world knowledge to execute tasks in interactive environments by converting high-level natural language goals into executable actions.
Although large pre-trained LLMs could decompose such tasks into mid-level plans without additional training, the generated outputs often required refinement.
To address this, new methods conditioned on demonstrations were proposed to align these plans with valid, executable actions, showing improved performance in simulators like VirtualHome
(
Huang et al. 2022
;
Puig et al. 2018
)
.
Natural language input is more intuitive, although generating fully executable scripts from these descriptions still demanded additional engineering effort
(
Aoyama et al. 2023
)
.
Further efforts introduced program sketches from demonstrations or activity descriptions, which were later expanded into environment-constrained programs
(
Liao et al. 2019
)
.
Once these action scripts are available, the goal becomes generating virtual sensor data using simulators.
Cross-modality transfer methods have been employed to synthesize target modality data—especially sensor data (IMU)—from high-resource domains, to alleviate the “small dataset" problem, with notable applications in generating IMU data from video
(
Ahuja et al. 2021
;
Rey et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Kwon et al. 2021b
)
, images
(
Yoon et al. 2022
)
, audio
(
Liang et al. 2022
)
, and text
(
Leng et al. 2023a
;
Leng et al. 2024a
)
.

In this work, we utilize LLMs to generate low-level action sequences through a three-step pipeline.
Starting with natural language-based persona generation, the method captures high-level daily routines in an intuitive format, which are then converted into low-level action scripts compatible with a simulator.
High-quality annotations for these activity instances are also derived from the LLM
(
Ding et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
.
Unlike prior approaches, our method eliminates the need for environment-specific or activity-specific fine-tuning.
These natural language descriptions are ultimately transformed into executable action scripts, enabling the generation of ambient virtual sensor data.
While we use the VirtualHome simulator for this work, platforms such as Habitat AI
(
Savva et al. 2019
)
also support embodied AI research by allowing agents to interact with photorealistic environments using both visual and language inputs.

3.
AgentSense: Generating Virtual Sensor Data in Simulated Homes via Language-Driven Agents

We develop a system for generating virtual ambient sensor data across diverse home environments and resident profiles using LLMs and the (extended) X-VirtualHome Simulator as illustrated in
Figure 1
.
The pipeline begins by generating a diverse set of personas, each of which is used to create high-level daily routines. These routines are then decomposed into fine-grained action sequences that can be executed in the VirtualHome simulator
(
Puig et al. 2018
)
. To enable ambient sensor simulation, we extended the VirtualHome environments by adding virtual motion, appliance door, and object sensors that record the character’s activities as they are enacted in the scene.
Throughout the paper, we refer to the VirtualHome simulator equipped with X-privacy preserving sensors – where
X\in\{\text{Motion},\text{Appliance Door},\text{Device Activation}\}
– as
X-VirtualHome
.
The resulting virtual sensor data generated can be used to train activity recognition classifiers from scratch or in combination with real-world datasets to enhance model performance.

In what follows, we provide details of our (i) multi-level prompting of LLMs (Section
3.1
), (ii) conversion of LLM outputs to
X-VirtualHome
executable actions (Section
3.2
), and (iii) implementation of ambient sensor integration in
X-VirtualHome
(Section
3.3
).

3.1.
Multi-Level Prompting of LLM Agents to Generate Daily Routines in Diverse Home Environments

We outline our three-stage LLM prompting pipeline, inspired by the chain-of-thought prompting paradigm
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, which has been shown to produce more effective results than single-step prompts. The process begins with the personality generation module, where the LLM is prompted to generate a diverse set of personality descriptions. Next, in the high-level routine generation module, the model adopts one of the generated personalities and produces a broad daily schedule based on that persona. Finally, the low-level action generation module breaks down the high-level schedule into fine-grained action sequences. After post-processing, these sequences are used as input for the X-VirtualHome simulator to realistically enact the individual’s day.

3.1.1.
Persona Generation

We begin by recognizing that individuals in the real world exhibit diverse lifestyles that give rise to varied routines and behavioral patterns. For instance, a 30-year-old software engineer working remotely may opt to prepare quick, nutritious meals between meetings, whereas a 65-year-old retiree might engage in more time-intensive cooking, favoring traditional dishes. Though both individuals perform the same activity—cooking—their approaches differ significantly. Capturing such variability is essential for developing Human Activity Recognition (HAR) models that generalize well across populations in the smart home setting. To this end, we require a broad set of personality descriptions that reflect real-world behavioral diversity.

To support this, it is important to construct datasets that reflect the breadth of real-world behavior. This requires generating a wide range of personality descriptions that encompass key factors such as age, occupation, health status, lifestyle, and habits. However, collecting this kind of rich, diverse data from real users can be time-consuming and difficult to scale.

To address this challenge, we leverage large language models (LLMs), which have demonstrated strong performance in generating realistic and varied text
(
Jin et al. 2024
;
Lawley and Maclellan 2024
)
. Inspired by recent work
(
Abbasiantaeb et al. 2024
;
Smrke et al. 2025
;
Serapio-García et al. 2023
)
, we configure the LLM as a personality generator through the use of a system message that defines the task. Including this message improves the model’s ability to follow instructions—without it, the generated personas often lack one or more of the required traits.

We prompt the model to generate personality profiles that incorporate lifestyle, habits, age, occupation, and health conditions. These factors shape how activities are performed and are therefore essential for building diverse and representative HAR datasets
(
Welenc and Hopper 2023
)
. The exact prompt is shown in
Appendix A
.

An example of the generated personality profile is
“Aaron, he is 52 years old, a retired firefighter, and has the following health situation: heart condition. After an illustrious career, he now enjoys gardening and fishing as relaxing pursuits. He stays socially active, participating in local charity events and keeping in touch with old colleagues. Aaron is a storyteller at heart, sharing tales from his firefighting days. His resilience and humor inspire those around him, despite his health challenges.”
As can be seen, the generated personality description encompasses the person’s age, job, health condition, and a description of their lifestyle and habits.

3.1.2.
High-Level Daily Routine Generation

In this subsection, we describe the process of generating high-level daily routines for each persona. This work focuses on a scenario where a single individual lives in the home. Therefore, we only generate schedules for one person, excluding interactions with family members, friends, pets, or others.

To generate a person’s daily routine, we prompt LLM with specific contextual information. The inputs provided to the LLM include:

(1)

Persona
.
What:
A generated personality description that includes attributes such as age, occupation, health status, and lifestyle.
Why:
These personal characteristics significantly influence an individual’s daily activities. By generating diverse personas, we enable the LLM to produce a wide range of daily schedules that reflect real-world variability.

(2)

Day of the Week
.
What:
The specific day for which the schedule is to be generated.
Why:
Daily routines often vary across the week—for instance, a person’s Monday might involve commuting to work, while Saturday may center around leisure or errands. Providing the day of the week helps the LLM produce more contextually appropriate and diverse schedules.

(3)

Environment
.
What:
A list of rooms available in the home environment where the person resides.
Why:
The VirtualHome simulator includes 50 different home environments, each with varying room configurations. To ensure the generated routines are physically plausible, it is important for the LLM to be aware of the specific environment. We also instruct the LLM to label the location (either “at home” or“outside”) for each activity. This allows us to distinguish between in-home and out-of-home activities. Since our focus is on smart home scenarios, only routines that occur within the home are ultimately used.

(4)

Example Schedules
.
What:
A set of example daily routines that we curated using the Homer dataset
(
Patel and Chernova 2022
)
.
Why:
Providing a few example schedules helps guide the LLM to produce routines in the desired format—a technique known as few-shot learning. We chose to reference the Homer dataset because it includes schedules created by real individuals. Additionally, we instruct the LLM to generate time slots that are not divisible by 5 minutes. Without this constraint, the model tends to default to overly neat intervals (e.g., always ending in 0 or 5), which does not reflect how people naturally structure their day.

3.1.3.
Decomposing High-Level Routines into Low-Level Actions

In this subsection, we describe the process in which the high level daily routine is broken down into a sequence of low level actions. To break down a high level routine activity, we prompt the LLM with the personality description, activity details, environment information, available actions, and example breakdowns with more details as follows:

(1)

Persona
.
What:
The personality profile generated in Section
3.1.1
, which includes attributes such as age, occupation, lifestyle, and health conditions.

Why:
These attributes influence how activities are realistically performed—e.g., an elderly individual may move more slowly or require additional steps. Including the persona allows the LLM to tailor low-level actions to match the individual’s physical and lifestyle constraints.

(2)

Activity
.
What:
The high-level activity to be decomposed into a detailed sequence of actions, including its designated start and end times.
Why:
High-level activities from the daily schedule must be transformed into specific, executable actions for use in the X-VirtualHome simulator. Since our simulation focuses on home scenarios, we only perform decompositions for activities marked as occurring within the home.

(3)

Environment
.
What:
The room in which the activity occurs, along with a comprehensive list of the objects available within that room.
Why:
The LLM agent requires detailed contextual awareness of its surroundings to generate plausible actions. We utilize a separate LLM to identify the most suitable room for each activity from a predefined list of rooms. Once selected, all objects within the chosen room are provided to the LLM, granting it a working memory of the immediate environment. This grounding prevents the generation of unrealistic actions involving objects not present in the current environment.

(4)

Actions
.
What:

A predefined set of actions, along with brief descriptions, that the X-VirtualHome simulator can execute.
Why:
To ensure compatibility with the simulator, the generated actions must adhere strictly to a predefined schema.
Thus, we supply the LLM with a list of 18 allowable actions, each structured around a verb-noun format and accompanied by usage instructions. An example of an action description that we provide to the LLM is
“
[walk] <object> (e.g.: walk to the bedroom should be [walk] <bedroom>)
”. This constrains the LLM’s output to valid, simulator-compatible actions.

Providing concrete decomposition examples guides the LLM toward accurately formatting and structuring its output. These examples facilitate understanding by clarifying how abstract activities map onto executable action sequences, enhancing the consistency and realism of generated routines.

3.2.
Converting LLM Output to Executable Actions in VirtualHome

To ensure that the routines generated by our LLM can be reliably executed within X-VirtualHome, we must first convert them into a format that matches the simulator’s supported actions and objects. Left unchecked, LLM output can include hallucinated or out
-
of
-
vocabulary commands, causing execution failures. The following steps both clean the LLM generated text and mathematically verify each action and object against the VirtualHome’s ontology, guaranteeing correctness and simulator compatibility.

(1)

Output Cleaning.

We remove all extraneous metadata (day labels, high
-
level activities, step counts), retaining only low
-
level actions annotated with location and timestamp (e.g.,

[walk] <doorjamb> (06:42–06:42) (bedroom)
; see
Table 2
).

(2)

Embedding the VirtualHome Vocabulary.

Let

\mathcal{A}=\{\text{all valid actions}\},\quad\mathcal{O}=\{\text{all valid objects}\}.

We compute embeddings for each
x\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{O}
using OpenAI’s
text-embedding-3-small
:

\mathbf{e}(x)\;=\;\mathrm{Embed}(x)\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d}.

These vectors are stored in two FAISS indices—one for actions, one for objects
(
Douze et al. 2024
)
.

(3)

Nearest
-
Neighbor Retrieval.

For each LLM
-
generated token
g
(action or object), we compute its embedding
\mathbf{e}(g)
and find

x^{*}\;=\;\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\cos\!\bigl(\mathbf{e}(g),\,\mathbf{e}(x)\bigr),

where

\cos(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\;\|\mathbf{v}\|},

and
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{A}
for actions or
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{O}_{r}\subset\mathcal{O}
for objects constrained to room
r
.

(4)

Thresholding and Replacement.

We only accept the nearest neighbor
x^{*}
if its cosine similarity with the generated token
g
satisfies

\cos\!\bigl(\mathbf{e}(g),\,\mathbf{e}(x^{*})\bigr)\;\geq\;\tau,

where
\tau_{\text{act}}=0.8
for actions and
\tau_{\text{obj}}=0.6
for objects. If the similarity falls below this threshold,
g
is flagged as invalid. In such cases, we ask the LLM to regenerate that line—using the surrounding action steps as context—up to a fixed number of attempts. If the line remains invalid after these retries, we discard it entirely.

(5)

Final Command Assembly.

Validated tokens replace the originals, yielding simulator
-
ready commands of the form

\texttt{[action*] <object*> (start\text{--}end) (room)}.

We orchestrate the embedding, indexing, and similarity
-
search workflow using LangChain
(
Chase and contributors 2023
)
, which allows for the integration of OpenAI embeddings and FAISS vector stores. This retrieval
-
augmented grounding step ensures that only valid actions and objects are executed in X-VirtualHome, eliminating hallucinations and preserving execution correctness.

3.3.
Implementing Virtual Ambient Sensors in X-VirtualHome Simulator

This section describes the integration of motion, door, and device activation sensors into the existing VirtualHome simulator
(
Puig et al. 2018
)
.
VirtualHome enables simulation of household activities and other complex environment interactions—e.g., picking up objects, switching on/off appliances, and opening doors—via a Python API.
Users define activities as instruction sequences, which are rendered in the simulator.
Multi-modal output data for activity sequence, such as timestamped actions, instance/semantic segmentation, corresponding activity videos, and depth data, can be generated.
However, ambient sensors for privacy-preserving applications are not supported.
To address this, we extended the Unity-based simulator to generate sensor data, in particular, motion sensor, device activation, and appliance door open/close, aligned with LLM-generated routines.
Motion sensors are added to track human movement throughout the home environment. Additionally, using the VirtualHome API’s
(
Puig et al. 2018
;
Puig et al. 2021
)
environmental graph that tracks object states, we simulate device activation and appliance door sensors.

3.3.1.
Incorporating Motion Sensors in X-VirtualHome:

We augment each home environment programmatically by placing virtual motion sensors based on room size. During simulation, character trajectories are tracked at a fixed time interval, and motion detection events are recorded whenever a character enters a sensor’s detection radius. The detailed process of adding motion sensors is as below:

(1)

FindAllRooms
: We identify all the rooms within a given home environment using the
FindAllRooms
function of VirtualHome API utilities
1
1

1

https://github.com/xavierpuigf/virtualhome
.
This function traverses the environment hierarchy starting from a specified transform (home environment) and collects all active game objects tagged as
TYPE_ROOM
.
This function selects only active rooms and dynamically handles varying home layouts for sensor placements.

(2)

DetermineSensorCount:

For each detected room, we calculate its floor area using the room’s bounding box dimensions (width
\times
depth). The number of sensors placed in a room depends on its area. We use three kinds of rooms in our simulation setup:

•

Small rooms (
\leq 30\,m^{2}
): One virtual motion sensor.

•

Medium rooms (
30\,m^{2}<\text{area}\leq 60\,m^{2}
): Two virtual motion sensors.

•

Large rooms (
>60\,m^{2}
): Three virtual motion sensors.

We note that most of the rooms in our environment fall under the small or medium categories.

(3)

CalculateSensorPositions:

Sensor positions are calculated based on the room’s bounding box.
Motion sensors are placed near the four corners, offset by
0.3
meters, to ensure maximum coverage of room motion area.
For the vertical placement position,
0.3
meters is offset from the room’s maximum height.
If only one sensor is needed, it is placed at the top-left corner.
However, if two sensors are required, they are placed at diagonally opposite corners to enhance coverage.
Finally, for three motion sensors, any three different corners of the room are selected.

(4)

CreateVirtualSensor:

For each calculated sensor position, we instantiate a new sensor
with a unique ID and attach it to the corresponding room object. A
Motion_sensor
component is added, initialized with the room name and a detection radius of
5.0
meters.
The sensor is registered with the
MotionSensorManager
class, which maintains a list of all active sensors.

(5)

TrackCharacterPosition
:
During simulation of an action script, the position of the character (human agent) is continuously tracked using Unity’s built-in
position
property.
Tracking occurs at a fixed rate, for instance every
\Delta t
seconds, where
\Delta t
can be adjusted based on simulation needs (e.g.,
\Delta t=0.2
s in our implementation)
The character’s position
\mathbf{p}(t)
at time
t
is retrieved, and motion detection is calculated based on proximity to sensor locations.
This real-time tracking enables both trajectory recording and sensor-based motion detection

(6)

MotionDetectionEvent:

The character’s position at fixed interval,
\mathbf{p}(t)
is evaluated against all active motion sensors in the home environment. A detection is triggered if:

\|\mathbf{p}(t)-\mathbf{s}_{i}\|\leq r

where
\mathbf{s}_{i}
is the fixed 3D position of sensor
i
and
r
is the detection radius (set to
r=5.0
meters in our implementation).
If a detection occurs, a
MotionDetectionEvent
is generated, recording the : {
frame number
,
character ID
,
sensor ID,

room name
, character’s 3D position
(x,y,z)
}.

All motion events are stored during the simulation and outputted for further ambient data analysis.

(7)

MotionTriggers
Using the motion events generated during simulation, we derive binary virtual motion sensor trigger values (
ON
/
OFF
) that closely mimic the behavior of real-world motion sensors. In real world, a motion sensor triggers an
ON
event when it detects movement following a period of inactivity, and an
OFF
event when movement ceases after a previously active state. To emulate this behavior in our simulation, we use the recorded 3D trajectory of the character. For each motion sensor, we examine the recorded motion events and identify motion segments of continuous movements.
We define a motion segment as a continuous interval
[t_{\text{on}},t_{\text{off}}]
during which the character satisfies both conditions—remaining within the sensor’s detection radius and maintaining active movement:

\|\mathbf{p}(t)-\mathbf{s}_{i}\|\leq r\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathbf{p}(t)-\mathbf{p}(t-\Delta t)\|>\epsilon

Here,
\epsilon
represents a movement threshold that distinguishes actual motion from minor positional jitter (
\epsilon
= 0.1 meters in our implementation ). The beginning of the segment (
t_{\text{on}}
) marks the virtual
ON
trigger, while the end (
t_{\text{off}}
) marks the virtual
OFF
trigger. These triggers collectively form the virtual motion sensor data in our virtual dataset.

Simulating Door and Device Activation Sensors

Environment Graph

:
The Environment Graph is a structured representation of a simulated environment in the existing VirtualHome API.
It represents different objects as nodes and their spatial or semantic relationships as edges. Formally, it can be defined as a graph
G=(V,E)
, where:

•

V
is the set of nodes, each corresponding to an object instance (e.g.,
chair
,
table
,
toothbrush
).

•

E
is the set of directed edges representing relations between different objects (e.g.,
on top of
,
next to
).
For instance, the object
cup
is "ON" the
kitchen counter
.
Each node includes attributes such as class– object type, room the object belongs to, object states, like
OPEN
or
ON
, and object properties (
CAN_OPEN
,
HAS_SWITCH
)

We leverage the changes in this data structure which records the state of the environment after an action has been rendered in the simulater.
Specifically, to simulate door and device activation sensors, we monitor state changes in relevant objects within the Environment Graph. Specifically:

•

A transition from “closed” to “open” in state of an object with the property
CAN_OPEN
(e.g., doors, cabinets) triggers a door sensor event.

•

A transition from “off” to “on” in objects with
HAS_SWITCH
property(e.g., microwave, appliances) triggers a device activation event.

4.
Experimental Settings

With
AgentSense
, our goal is to generate virtual sensor data that can be used to train HAR models for real-world smart home deployments. By simulating diverse personas and daily routines across varied virtual environments, our system produces scalable and richly annotated virtual sensor datasets—without requiring manual data collection. The central question we aim to answer is whether this virtual data can effectively benefit HAR models when evaluated on real-world data. This setup reflects practical scenarios where pretraining on large-scale synthetic data may reduce reliance on costly real-world collection.

To assess this, we design our experiments around existing, real-world smart home datasets. These datasets provide a well-established benchmark for evaluating HAR performance and enable us to conduct controlled, reproducible comparisons. Importantly, this allows us to demonstrate the relevance and impact of our virtual data generation approach without necessitating an actual hardware deployment.
In the following sections, we describe the datasets used and the classifier training setup.

4.1.
Datasets

4.1.1.
Real Datasets

We conduct our experiments on five publicly available datasets: Aruba, Milan, Kyoto7, and Cairo from the CASAS collection
(
Cook et al. 2012
)
, and Orange4Home
(
Cumin et al. 2017
)
from the Amiqual4Home smart home environment
(
Cumin et al. 2017
)
.
Of the CASAS datasets, Aruba has the largest number of data points and balanced sensor types (motion, door, temperature), with a floorplan similar to Milan, which shares the same sensor modalities.
Kyoto7, Cairo, and Orange are multi-story homes with distinct locations (e.g., pantry, laundry, garage) and fewer data points. Cairo and Kyoto7 each have two residents, introducing behavioral variability, while Orange has only one. Sensor modalities vary: Cairo includes motion and temperature; Kyoto7 adds item use, light switch, and device activation; Orange features over 18 sensor types, such as noise, voltage, and humidity.
Table 4
(in the
Appendix E
) summarizes the datasets details, including sensor types, number of residents, floorplans, and activities.

4.1.2.
Virtual Dataset

We generated virtual sensor data for eighteen distinct personas across twenty-two simulated home environments, resulting in a total of 250 days of virtual activity data. The dataset comprises 3,266 activity windows, each containing between 3 and 393 sensor triggers, with an average of 36 triggers per window. All environments are single-story homes occupied by a single resident.
During data generation, the LLM agent was given the freedom to produce open-ended activities without any restrictions on the activity space. However, to leverage the generated data for training models that can be evaluated on real-world datasets, it is necessary to map the LLM-generated outputs to the set of activity labels defined in those datasets.

To perform this mapping, we prompt the LLM with three pieces of information: the name of the high-level activity from the daily routine, the sequence of low-level actions that it was decomposed into, and the complete list of activity labels from the target real-world dataset. Based on this, the LLM returns the most appropriate activity label, which we then associate with the corresponding generated virtual sensor data.

Since we operate the simulator in a single-resident setting, we treat overlapping multi-resident activities in the real datasets as actions performed by a single resident. Moreover, because the LLM was not constrained to generate only activities present in the real datasets, some real-world activities may not appear in the virtual data. In such cases, we assign the label “Other” to those missing classes. The original prompt we used for label generation is provided in
Appendix D
and a complete list of the activity mappings used for each real dataset is provided in
Appendix F
.

4.2.
Classifier Training:

4.2.1.
Classifier

Since the virtual and real data originate from different home layouts and contain different sets of sensors, we adopt the TDOST-based HAR method proposed by
Thukral et al. 2025
as our classifier. This approach allows us to evaluate how effectively the generated virtual sensor data improves downstream model performance.

We adopt a pre-segmented, activity-based windowing strategy, selecting the first 100 sensor triggers from each activity window for analysis. Both real and virtual sensor triggers include contextual metadata, such as the sensor’s location and the time of activation. Using this information, each trigger is converted into a descriptive sentence. We use two sentence-conversion schemes: TDOST-Basic and TDOST-Temporal. TDOST-Basic encodes the sensor type and its location, while TDOST-Temporal additionally incorporates the timestamp of the trigger.
For example, TDOST-Basic would produce a sentence such as
Motion sensor in bedroom fired with value ON
, whereas TDOST-Temporal template would incorporate time info and produce
Motion sensor in bedroom fired with value ON at twelve hours six minutes PM
. These sentences are then embedded into vector representations using SentenceTransformer’s all-distilroberta-v1 model
(
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
)
.

The resulting sequence of sentence embeddings is passed to a bidirectional LSTM (Bi-LSTM) backbone, which captures long-range temporal dependencies in both forward and backward directions. The Bi-LSTM model uses 64 hidden units, following the architecture used by
Thukral et al. 2025
. Finally, the encoded features are passed through a linear classification layer that outputs the probability distribution over the activity classes.

4.2.2.
Training Settings

To demonstrate the efficacy of the generated virtual sensor data, we conduct two types of experiments (on a range of datasets):
Real
and
Real + Virtual
.
In the
Real
setting, the model is trained and evaluated in a fully supervised manner on each dataset independently, serving as the baseline.
In the
Real + Virtual
setting, we adopt a two-stage training procedure inspired by
Kwon et al. 2020
. The model is first pretrained on the virtual sensor data, followed by finetuning on real sensor data with all weights updated. Evaluation is conducted on the real test split.
We train the model for up to 30 epochs using the Adam optimizer and a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler. Hyperparameters are selected via grid search over learning rates ([1e-2, 1e-3, 1e-4, 5e-5]) and weight decay values ([0, 1e-4, 1e-5]). For pretraining and finetuning, we also search over the same set of learning rates.

Both experimental settings use three-fold stratified cross-validation. In each fold, one subset is held out for testing, while the remaining two are split into training and validation sets. All virtual data is used during the pretraining phase without additional partitioning. We report the mean and standard deviation of accuracy, macro F1, and weighted F1 across the three folds, consistent with evaluation protocols in prior work.

The goal of generating virtual data is to scale HAR datasets, which are typically expensive and difficult to collect.
This helps reduce dependence on large-scale real-world data collection.
To test whether virtual data can reduce the need for real data, we conduct several experiments.

5.1.
Supervised Pretraining on Virtual Sensor Data Followed by Finetuning on Real Datasets

To assess the effectiveness of our virtual sensor data, we conduct supervised pretraining on virtual data, followed by finetuning on real-world datasets, using two model variants: TDOST-Basic and TDOST-Temporal.
From
Table 3
, we note that models pretrained with virtual data, Real + Virtual (TDOST-Basic) and Real + Virtual (TDOST-Temporal), consistently outperform their counterparts trained exclusively on real data across benchmark HAR datasets including Aruba, Milan, Cairo, Kyoto7, and Orange4Home.

For TDOST-Basic, the average accuracy increases across datasets, with notable gains such as 69.01% to 75.61% on Cairo, and 82.40% to 85.21% on Orange4Home.
Similarly, TDOST-Temporal shows substantial improvements, especially on low-resource datasets, as such accuracy improves by 10% on Cairo and 5% on Milan, while performance on other datasets such as Kyoto7 and Orange4Home remains stable.
Macro F1 Score shows substantial gains using our Virtual+Real supervised pipeline.
For TDOST-Basic, the Macro F1 increases from 11% on Cairo and by approximately 20% on the Orange4Home dataset.
Similarly, TDOST-Temporal shows a sharp improvement from 68.57% to 77.36% for Aruba, 51% to 73% for Milan, though performance remains low on Orange4Home, likely due to limitations of the underlying method rather than the virtual data itself.
Weighted F1 scores follow a similar upward trend, with TDOST-Basic showing significant improvements across datasets. While TDOST-Temporal also improves overall, we observe a slight drop on Kyoto7 performance when pretrained with virtual datasets.

We observe that performance improvements are particularly significant for datasets such as Cairo and Orange4Home , which have lower performance when trained solely on real data.
TDOST-Temporal, in particular, benefits more from pretraining, showing larger relative gains in both Macro and Weighted F1 scores.
The consistency of improvements across all five datasets—including Orange4Home, which is a large-scale non-CASAS dataset with over 18 different types of sensors—further reinforces the generalizability of pretraining and robustness of our virtual sensor data across different sensor types, activity sets, and home environments.
To sum up, substantial improvements in downstream HAR performance validate our virtual data-generation approach as an effective way to reduce reliance on costly real-world data collection, especially for complex or low-resource environments.

However, for the Kyoto7 dataset, we observe a slight downward trend with increasing virtual data amounts.
Still, the performance never drops below the highest performance achieved by real-world data, which indicates that adding virtual always improves or maintains HAR performance.
We hypothesize that the downward trend with increasing virtual data in Kyoto7 may be due to a larger distribution gap between the virtual sensor data and the real-world data.
This gap could be causing the pretrained representations to misalign during fine-tuning and thus increasing the virtual data leads to harming the downstream performance slightly.
Overall, the results demonstrate that virtual data contributes positively and increasing /scaling the amounts of virtual data leads to significant performance improvements in most cases.

6.
Discussion

A key challenge in developing robust smart home-based HAR models is the lack of large-scale labeled datasets, further complicated by variations in home layouts, sensor setups, and individual behaviors.
To address this, we introduce
AgentSense
, a virtual data generation pipeline that uses LLMs to create diverse personas and routines, which are then executed in a simulated home instrumented with virtual sensors.
This enables the generation of rich, diverse datasets that improve HAR model performance, particularly in settings where data is scarce.
Models trained with our virtual data and minimal real data achieve performance on par with those trained on full datasets, demonstrating the potential of simulation-driven approaches to overcome data scarcity.

In the following section, we outline how insights gained from this work can inform future directions and detail the next steps in advancing our research.

6.1.
Bridging the Domain Gap Between Real and Virtual Homes

We generated virtual sensor datasets from 22 distinct environments within the VirtualHome simulator. While diverse, none of these virtual environments precisely replicate the home layouts found in the real-world HAR datasets used for evaluation. As illustrated in
Figure 6
, a comparison between a VirtualHome environment and the Milan dataset reveals noticeable differences—Milan, for instance, includes more rooms and unique spaces such as an aisle. These discrepancies in layout introduce a domain gap between the virtual and real data. Despite this, our results show that incorporating virtual data significantly improves HAR model performance, even in the presence of this domain mismatch.

One promising future direction is to leverage the control offered by simulation to replicate the specific layout of a target home when needed. This enables personalization of HAR models for specific homes at virtually no additional cost. By generating layout-matched virtual sensor data, the model can be better adapted to the unique movement patterns and spatial transitions of that home.

Consider, for example, a newly instrumented smart home with no prior sensor data. Traditionally, HAR model development in such settings requires a "cold phase"
(
Hiremath et al. 2022
)
—a period of passive observation during which user activities are manually annotated to create labeled training data. By extending
AgentSense
, this phase could be bypassed entirely. A simple video walkthrough of the home can be used to reconstruct a 3D model of the environment using tools like Meshroom
(
Griwodz et al. 2021
)
. This model can then be imported into the simulator to generate virtual sensor data that matches the actual home layout.
With this virtual data, an initial HAR model can be trained and deployed from day one, enabling activity recognition without the need for manual labeling or real-world data collection – thus supporting the transition to the maintenance procedure for HAR in homes
(
Hiremath and Plötz 2024
)
.

6.2.
Optimizing Sensor Placement Through Virtual Data Simulation for HAR

We generated virtual sensor data using motion sensors placed according to predefined rules, as detailed in Section
3.3.1
. However, the sensor placement can be fully customized within the virtual environment. This flexibility is particularly useful for identifying optimal sensor configurations when instrumenting a new home with ambient sensors.
Consider a scenario in which a new home is to be equipped with sensors to support recognition of a specific set of user-desired activities. Rather than deploying sensors directly and relying on trial-and-error in the real world, one can first simulate various sensor layouts in the virtual environment. By generating virtual sensor data under different configurations and evaluating the resulting HAR model performance, the optimal sensor layout can be identified—defined as the configuration that yields the highest activity recognition accuracy. This approach enables cost-effective, large-scale experimentation without the time and expense associated with real-world sensor deployment.

6.3.
Extending to Multi-modal Data Generation in Simulated Home Environments:

As described in Section
3.3
, the VirtualHome simulator supports the generation of multiple camera-based modalities, including RGB images, depth maps, semantic segmentation, and pose data. Our extended version enhances this capability by adding support for multiple ambient sensor data streams that are time-synchronized with these visual modalities.

Among these, pose and video data are especially valuable for sensor-based HAR, as they allow the integration of existing Pose2IMU and Video2IMU methods
(
Kwon et al. 2020
;
Xiao et al. 2021
;
Uhlenberg and Amft 2022
;
Xia and Sugiura 2022
;
Kwon et al. 2021a
;
Leng et al. 2023b
;
Leng et al. 2024b
;
Hwang et al. 2024
)
to generate synchronized virtual accelerometer and gyroscope signals. In future work, we plan to jointly simulate these sensor modalities, leveraging LLM-based activity generation to create richly annotated, multi-modal datasets. Such simulation of synchronized ambient and wearable sensor streams would enable more comprehensive activity analysis—combining the contextual awareness of ambient sensors with the fine-grained motion tracking offered by wearables, particularly for activities of daily living
(
Arrotta et al. 2021
)
.

6.4.
Future Work

Several avenues for future work directly connect to the work we presented in this paper.
In what follows, we outline some of these future avenues.

6.4.1.
Exploring Alternative Large Language Models for Virtual Data Generation:

In this work, we utilize GPT-4o-mini or various language generation tasks, including persona creation, high-level routine synthesis, and decomposition into low-level action sequences.
This model offers near-zero cost for generating the required sequences.
It can be substituted with other open-source LLMs such as DeepSeek
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
, Claude
(
Anthropic 2024
)
, Gemini
(
Team et al. 2023
)
, or Llama
(
Patil and Gudivada 2024
;
Naveed et al. 2023
;
Touvron et al. 2023
)
.
In future iterations, we plan to investigate how different LLMs influence the generated action sequences and how these variations affect the resulting virtual data in simulation environments.

While our multi-stage prompting procedure and activity annotations rely on LLM-generated outputs, one could argue that these may not fully capture the nuances of real-world, situated home environments.
In future work, we will include a formal evaluation of LLM outputs by comparing them against real resident inputs of annotation sourced via Amazon Mechanical Turk
(
Patel and Chernova 2022
)
, as successfully demonstrated in prior studies.

6.4.2.
Exploring Additional Pretraining Methods with Virtual Data

In this work, we utilized the state-of-the-art TDOST framework to assess the benefits of incorporating virtual data into the Human Activity Recognition (HAR) pipeline.
Low-level action sequences generated by LLMs were fed into the VirtualHome simulator, and the resulting sensor event triggers were encoded using the TDOST representation.
Evaluation was conducted by pre-training on virtual data, followed by fine-tuning with a combination of virtual and real data.
This setup was benchmarked against the standard TDOST pipeline, which has demonstrated strong performance in generalizable, layout-agnostic HAR—where home layouts and activity patterns differ between source and target homes.
To examine whether virtual data enhances generalization, we directly compared TDOST alone with TDOST pre-trained on virtual data.
The improved performance in the pre-trained variant highlights the utility of synthetic data for building robust HAR systems.
In future work, we plan to expand this analysis by incorporating traditional baselines, for example, the use of CASAS features
(
Cook et al. 2012
;
Alam et al. 2012
)
and exploring other self-supervised pre-training strategies
(
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Haresamudram et al. 2022
;
Haresamudram et al. 2021
;
Oord et al. 2018
)
.

6.4.3.
Enhancing Coverage and Diversity in HAR through LLM-Generated Activities:

A notable advantage of incorporating LLMs in the simulation pipeline is their ability to generate broad spectrum of plausible human activities, many of which align with real-world behaviors yet remain underrepresented in existing HAR datasets.
While such datasets are typically constrained by a fixed set of annotated labels, actual human routines are significantly more varied and context-dependent.
LLMs, trained on diverse and expansive textual corpora, can implicitly capture this behavioral richness and often produce activities that, though missing from benchmark datasets, mirror everyday human actions.
In our generation process, we observed such instances, where the LLM surfaced realistic sequences absent from the labeled datasets – highlighting limitations in traditional data collection pipelines, which are often bound by cost or activity taxonomies.

Conversely, we also noted that certain activities commonly found in benchmark datasets, such as ‘Watch_TV’ or ‘Enter_Home’, were occasionally overlooked in the LLM-generated routines.
This suggests areas where the open-ended simulation process could benefit from more targeted prompting
In future work, we aim to explicitly include such activities to ensure alignment with established datasets and achieve fuller coverage of both common (occurring in benchmarked datasets) and underrepresented behaviors.

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we presented
AgentSense
, a framework for generating virtual ambient sensor data for human activity recognition using LLMs in simulated home environments. By simulating diverse personas and their corresponding routines across a wide variety of home environments,
AgentSense
enables the scalable creation of richly annotated, virtual sensor datasets without manual data collection. We extended the VirtualHome simulator with virtual motion, door, and device sensors, enabling us to translate LLM-generated action sequences into ambient sensor data streams.

Through extensive experiments across five real-world smart home datasets, we demonstrated that virtual data generated by
AgentSense
significantly improves HAR performance—especially in low-resource settings. Models pretrained on virtual data and fine-tuned with just a small fraction of real-world data achieved performance comparable to those trained entirely on real datasets. These findings underscore the potential of virtual sensor data to mitigate the challenges of costly, time-consuming data collection and annotation in smart home deployments.
Looking ahead,
AgentSense
offers a novel direction for smart-home based HAR model development—pretraining models in simulated environments and deploying in real homes with minimal reliance on real-world data.
Future directions include optimizing sensor placement through simulation, adapting virtual environments to match specific homes via 3D scans, and extending the framework to multi-resident settings and multi-modality scenarios, including wearable devices. Collectively, these directions can enable more generalizable, adaptable, and cost-effective HAR systems for real-world deployment.
