The Zero Forcing Number of Twisted Hypercubes
Collier, Peter, Janssen, Jeannette

Abstract
Twisted hypercubes are graphs that generalize the structure of the hypercube by relaxing the symmetry constraint while maintaining degree-regularity and connectivity. We study the zero forcing number of twisted hypercubes. Zero forcing is a graph infection process in which a particular colour change rule is iteratively applied to the graph and an initial set of vertices. We use the alternative framing of forcing arc sets to construct a family of twisted hypercubes of dimension k ≥ 3 \geq 3 with zero forcing sets of size 2 k − 1 − 2 k − 3 + 1 2^{k-1}-2^{k-3}+1 , which is below the minimum zero forcing number of the hypercube.

Introduction

In the realm of combinatorics and computer science, the hypercube stands out as a compelling and versatile structure. Hypercubes extend the geometric notion of a cube into higher dimensions, providing a robust framework for exploring complex problems in multidimensional spaces. These structures offer significant insights across various fields, including transport network design
[
12
]
, quantum optimization
[
19
]
, and data organization
[
4
]
.

The twisted hypercube generalizes the standard hypercube structure by relaxing the symmetry constraint while maintaining the degree-regularity and connectivity of the graphs. With this additional level of freedom, we seek to determine whether the underlying structure of the network can be better tailored to a specific process in order to optimize its performance. We will look at one particular graph infection process called zero forcing.

Zero forcing, introduced in
[
3
]
, is a graph infection process where every vertex of a graph is coloured either white or blue. An initial set of blue vertices will propagate according to a colour change rule, defined in Section
1.1
, until no further changes are possible. If all vertices in the graph eventually turn blue, then the initial blue set is called a zero forcing set. The goal of studying zero forcing is to find the smallest possible zero forcing set, whose size is called the zero forcing number of the graph.

Zero forcing was initially introduced in
[
3
]
as an upper bound for the maximum nullity of matrices with certain patterns of non-zero entries. In
[
3
]
, the zero forcing number of various families of graphs was determined, such as supertriangles, hypercubes, the line graphs as well as graph products of some graph families.
Zero forcing has also found applications in discrete mathematics (see inverse eigenvalue problem
[
17
]
), applied mathematics (see PMU placement problem
[
7
]
), and control theory (see quantum control problem
[
8
]
). For more on zero forcing and its applications, see
[
15
]
. The zero forcing number of graphs is an interesting field of research in its own right. Recently, the zero forcing number for families of graphs, such as Generalized Johnson, Generalized Grassmann, and Hamming graphs
[
2
]
were found. Additionally, characterizations of graphs with certain degree-related zero forcing numbers were given in
[
18
]
. The zero forcing number of random graphs and random regular graphs is studied in
[
16
]
and
[
5
]
, respectively. In
[
13
]
a bound for the zero forcing number of claw-free cubic graphs is given, answering a problem posed in
[
11
]
.

A common theme in zero forcing research is finding minimal zero forcing sets by using the structure of the graphs, while determining minimality using the maximum nullity of the graph. Both finding zero forcing sets and determining maximum nullity are known to be NP-Hard problems
[
1
,
20
]
.
There are some combinatorial lower bounds on the zero forcing number, such as the minimum degree or the clique cover number. However, these are trivial in almost all graphs and can be arbitrarily far from the actual zero forcing number.

Recently, a new method of determining whether an initial set is a zero forcing set was developed in
[
9
]
, which makes constructing and verifying such sets much simpler. In this paper, we use this method to find an upper bound on the zero forcing number of a family of twisted hypercubes.

1.1
Preliminaries

Given a graph,
G=(V,E)

If a blue vertex,

It was noted in

The
n
-dimensional hypercube,

, is the graph with vertex set
\{0,1\}^{n}
, all bit-strings of length

The zero forcing number of the hypercube was determined in
[
3
]
to be
Z(Q_{n})=2^{n-1}
. The vertices of one copy of
Q_{n-1}
form a zero forcing set for

will be blue vertices, the neighbour in the other copy will be the only white neighbour and will, therefore, be forced. So, a zero forcing set with half of the vertices of

can always be constructed this way. The authors of
[
3
]
found a lower bound on the maximum nullity of the hypercube graph of

in order to show that this is the best possible.

Twisted hypercubes are a generalization of the standard hypercube graph, first introduced in
[
14
]
, that have been studied for their high connectivity and low diameter
[
21
]
. The unique 0-dimensional twisted hypercube is the graph that consists of a single vertex, represented by the empty string. An
n
-dimensional twisted hypercube is a graph formed by taking two

-dimensional twisted hypercubes, not necessarily the same, appending 0 to the end of all vertices in one copy, appending 1 to all vertices of the other copy, and forming any matching between the copies. An example is given in Figure
2

standard matching
, and any edges whose endpoints differ in more than one bit as

While the zero forcing number of the hypercube was determined to be

By brute force computation, we were able to find twisted hypercubes of dimension 4, 5, and 6 with zero forcing number 7, 13, and 25 respectively and establish by computation that no twisted hypercube of the same dimension has smaller zero forcing number. Using Theorem

2^{n-1}-2^{n-4}-2^{n-5}-2^{n-6}

v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{k}

(v_{i},v_{i+1})\notin\mathcal{F}\Rightarrow(v_{i-1},v_{i})\in\mathcal{F},\mbox{and }(v_{i+1},v_{i+2})\in\mathcal{F},

for all
1<i<k
.

So a chain twist path is a path without consecutive non-arcs. It can be thought of as a section of a chain twist. We note an important consequence of this definition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{F})
be a graph
G
and arc set
\mathcal{F}
. Let
P=v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{k}
be a path in
G
. If
v_{i}
,
2\leq i\leq k-1
, is an isolated vertex in
\mathcal{F}
, then
P
is not a chain twist path.

Proof.

Assume that
P
is a chain twist path, and
v_{i}
is isolated in
\mathcal{F}
for some
2\leq i\leq k-1
. Since
v_{i}
is isolated, there are no arcs into
v_{i}
, and so
v_{i-1}v_{i}\notin\mathcal{F}
. By Definition
3.1
, this implies that
v_{i}v_{i+1}\in\mathcal{F}
, but this contradicts
v_{i}
being isolated, completing the proof.
∎

So we cannot include isolated vertices of an arc set in the interior of any chain twist path, and by extension, any chain twist.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{F})
be a graph
G
and arc set
\mathcal{F}
. If
v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{k}v_{1}
is a chain twist, then
v_{i}
is not isolated in
\mathcal{F}
, for all
i
.

can be reduced to a chain twist. We will use this fact in the following results.

Theorem 3.4
.

[
9
]
Let
G
be a graph with arc set
\mathcal{F}
. If
G
contains a closed walk that satisfies the property (
1
), then
\mathcal{F}
contains a chain twist.

It was shown in
[
3
]
that the zero forcing number of a Cartesian product is bounded above by the zero forcing number and size of the factors. We will offer a new proof of this known result in the context of forcing arc sets as it demonstrates a technique used in further results.

Proposition 3.5
.

[
3
]
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with a forcing arc set
\mathcal{F}
, and
H=(U,E^{\prime})
a graph. Then
G\square H
has a forcing arc set of size
|\mathcal{F}||H|
.

Proof.

Consider
G\square H
, and the arc set
\mathcal{F}^{\square}
formed as follows

(v_{i},u_{r})(v_{j},u_{s})\in\mathcal{F}^{\square}\iff u_{r}=u_{s}\text{ and }v_{i}v_{j}\in\mathcal{F}.

So
\mathcal{F}^{\square}
consists of a copy of
\mathcal{F}
in each copy of
G
in
G\square H
. Now, for the sake of contradiction, assume that
\mathcal{F}^{\square}
has a chain twist
C
.

If
C
includes no edge of the form
(v,u_{r})(v,u_{s})
, then
C
is contained in a single copy of
G
. Therefore,
\mathcal{F}
contains a chain twist which is a contradiction.

So
C
must include edges of the form
(v_{j},u_{r})(v_{j},u_{s})
. We know, by the construction of
\mathcal{F}^{\square}
, that none of these edges are arcs. Then Definition
1.2
tells us that these edges must be preceded and followed by arcs, which exist only in the copies of
G
. Consider the vertices of
C
and form a new sequence
W=v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{k},v_{1}
that consists of the first element of each vertex in
C
, where some vertices will be listed consecutively. Note that the same vertex will never be listed three times consecutively, as this equates to traversing two edges from
H
in a row which contradicts
C
being a chain twist. By Definition
1.2
and the construction of
\mathcal{F}^{\square}
, whenever
\dots v_{i-1},v_{i},v_{i},v_{i+1}\dots
appears in
W
,
v_{i-1}v_{i}
and
v_{i}v_{i+1}
are both arcs in
\mathcal{F}
. This means that removing these repeated vertices will result in a new sequence that satisfies property (
1
). We now form the sequence
W^{\prime}
, which is
W
but whenever the same vertex is listed consecutively, we remove the second instance of that vertex. The sequence
W^{\prime}
is a closed walk in
G
. Since every edge in
W^{\prime}
corresponds to an edge in
C
,
W^{\prime}
is a closed chain twist walk in
G
. By Theorem
3.4
,
W^{\prime}
can be reduced to a chain twist, and so
\mathcal{F}
must contain a chain twist, contradicting our assumption.

So
(G\square H,\mathcal{F}^{\square})
cannot contain a chain twist, and
\mathcal{F}^{\square}
is a forcing arc set.
∎

So if we have a Cartesian product of graphs and a set of arcs repeated in copies of one of the factors, any chain twist can be projected onto the factor. This result can be applied immediately to
(\hat{Q}_{n},\mathcal{F}_{n})
to tell us information about any chain twists, should they exist.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\hat{Q}_{n}
,
n\geq 4
, be a minority cube with arc set
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. If
\mathcal{F}_{n}
has a chain twist,

, and
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
does not, then
C
contains the bridge arc.

Proof.

Let
C
be a chain twist in
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Assume that the bridge arc is not in

. If
C
contains no matching edges, then
C
is contained in one copy of
(\hat{Q}_{n-1},\mathcal{F}_{n-1})
. But this contradicts
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
not having a chain twist. So
C
must contain matching edges. All available matching edges are not arcs, so

is of the form
v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,x,y,\cdots,v_{k-1},v_{0}
, where
x
and
y
are twin vertices connected by an edge, and

cannot be a chain twist. Therefore

cannot be a twisted edge. Now we consider a new sequence

v_{i}^{\prime}
is the vertex

with the final bit in the binary expansion removed. This means that

as

has a repeated element in its sequence. Define a new sequence

where we remove the second instance of each repeated element. Then

is a closed walk in
\hat{Q}_{n-1}

,

is a closed chain twist walk in

. By Theorem

, this can be reduced to a chain twist. This gives us a chain twist in

contradicting our assumption.

Therefore,

must contain the bridge arc, as required.
∎

Knowing that any chain twist must not only cross over the matching, but must do so at the bridge arc, greatly limits the possible chain twists we must consider in our twisted hypercubes. The following lemma demonstrates the scope of this restriction.

Future Work

In this paper, we defined a new family of twisted hypercube called the minority cube and provided an upper bound on the zero forcing number of these graphs. No lower bound is given, but we conjecture that this is the best possible for this family of graphs.

Conjecture 4.1
.

The zero forcing number of the
n
-dimensional minority cube is
2^{n-1}+2^{n-3}-1
.

We were able to achieve a zero forcing set of this size by performing only one twist at each step of the construction. We conjecture that this is the lowest zero forcing number that can be achieved with a single twist.

Conjecture 4.2
.

Given a family of twisted hypercubes, where at each step in the construction only one twist is performed, the zero forcing number is at least that of the minority cube of the same dimension.

We do not know whether performing multiple twists at each step will improve the efficiency of the zero forcing process, so we leave this as an open question.

Question 4.3
.

Is there another family of twisted hypercubes with smaller zero forcing number than the minority cube?
