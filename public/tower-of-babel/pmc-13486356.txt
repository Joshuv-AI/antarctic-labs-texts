Isolated fracture of the anterior wall of the acetabulum: A rare fracture, even rarer in the skeletally immature patient. Report of two cases and literature review
Authors: Vincenzo Giordano, Anderson Freitas, Nicolle Barreto, Jhordana Esteves dos Santos, Carolina Braga dos Santos Azevedo, Cynthia Storino Pavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIsolated anterior wall acetabular fracture (AWAF) is a rare injury in adults and even rarer among the skeletally immature population.Case presentationWe present the cases of two skeletally immature patients (a 13-year-old boy [case #1] and a 10-year-old boy [case #2]) with displaced isolated AWAF, surgically treated using the Smith-Petersen approach with osteotomy of the anterior superior iliac spine.ResultsThe patients were closely followed up for an average of 18 months, presenting normal function and a concentric hip. Despite the good functional outcome, the latest hip radiographs of case #2 showed initial signs of a bony bar in the triradiate cartilage and the presence of avascular necrosis of the femoral head.ConclusionWe emphasize the importance of long-term follow-up for skeletally immature patients with this type of injury, given the high potential for complications.

Introduction

Isolated anterior wall acetabular fracture (AWAF) is a rare injury, accounting for less than 2% of all acetabular fractures, according to the historical series by Judet and Letournel [1]. Radiologically, the osteoarticular fragment has a trapezoidal shape, affecting the anterior portion of the anatomical roof of the acetabulum, with the fracture line starting at the anterior edge of the acetabulum, slightly below the anterior inferior iliac spine, crossing the articular surface and descending through the acetabular fossa until reaching the ischiopubic notch, slightly behind the anterior lunate articular surface, however not involving the pelvic brim [1], [2], [3].

The injury mechanism generally involves moderate to high-energy trauma to the ipsilateral lower limb with the hip in abduction and external rotation [1], [2], [3]. The degree of hip flexion or extension at the time of trauma determines whether the fragment is more inferior or more superior, respectively [3], [4], [5]. Association with anterior hip dislocation is frequently reported in the literature, requiring urgent treatment to concentrically reduce the joint [2], [3], [4], [5], [6].

Surgical treatment is indicated when fragment displacement exceeds 5 mm with consequent articular incongruity or when there is persistent hip instability leading to recurrent dislocation episodes [3], [6]. Associated femoral head impact fractures, severe comminution, and/or marginal impaction of the articular cartilage of the acetabular roof have proven to be poor prognostic factors [3]. Due to the rarity of the injury and the lack of standardization of treatment, different surgical approaches and fixation methods have been described, which greatly complicates decision-making in the face of such a rare injury. In addition, most studies address simultaneous fractures of the anterior column and the anterior wall of the acetabulum, always in adult patients, which creates a selection bias, especially regarding the choice of surgical approach [6], [7].

In the present study, we report two cases of isolated AWAF in skeletally immature patients, an even rarer injury that, to our knowledge, has been poorly reported in the literature to date.

The detached trapezoidal osteoarticular fragment affected between 25 and 50% of the intermediate portion of the anterior acetabular wall and was classified as type 2 M, according to the Leeds-Naples AWC system [3].

Due to the open fracture, intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis with a first-generation cephalosporin was initiated, and the patient underwent emergency irrigation and debridement. Definitive surgery was performed two days after the injury. Under endotracheal anaesthesia, the patient was positioned supine on a radiolucent table, with the C-arm located on the contralateral side. The Smith-Petersen approach with osteotomy of the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) was performed, protecting the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. After exposing the anterior margin of the acetabulum, it was observed that the trapezoidal osteoarticular fragment was rotated 180°, with the articular surface facing the surgeon, partially exposing the femoral head. The fragment was derotated and anatomically reduced and temporarily fixed with 2.0-mm Kirschner wires (K-wires) (Fig. 4). After C-arm fluoroscopic control to verify the positioning of the fragment and the K-wires, the fracture was fixed with a 4-hole one-third tubular plate, with two 3.5-mm cortical screws proximal and two distal to the fracture (Fig. 5). The wound was irrigated with saline solution, the ASIS osteotomy was reduced and fixed with one 3.5-mm cortical screw, and the incision was closed in layers. The procedure was completed without complications.Fig. 4The trapezoidal osteoarticular fragment was rotated 180°, with the articular surface facing the surgeon (arrows), partially exposing the femoral head (asterix). A 10-year-old male patient was admitted to the hospital on September 7, 2024, after suffering a car accident – a frontal collision, during which he was ejected from the vehicle. The patient was awake but confused and presented with lacerations to the head with active bleeding (initial Glasgow Coma Score of 9), with no complaints in the cervical spine, but reporting pain in the thoracic spine. Definitive surgery was performed 11 days after hospital admission mainly due to the neurological impairment. Both the facial fractures and the AWAF were fixed in the same procedure. For the right hip fracture, the patient was positioned in the supine position on a radiolucent table, with the C-arm positioned on the contralateral side. The Smith-Petersen approach with osteotomy of the ASIS was performed, protecting the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. After exposing the anterior margin of the acetabulum, the fragment was anatomically reduced and temporarily fixed with 1.6-mm K-wires. After fluoroscopic control with the C-arm, the fracture was fixed with a non-locked 2.7-mm quarter tubular plate with eight holes, with one 2.7-mm cortical screw proximal and two distal to the fracture. The wound was irrigated with saline solution, the ASIS osteotomy was reduced and fixed with a 3.5 mm cortical screw, and the incision was closed in layers (Fig. 11). Discussion

Isolated AWAF is the rarest acetabular fracture pattern in adults and even rarer in immature skeletons. To the best of our knowledge, only a few studies have reported isolated fractures of the anterior wall acetabular fractures to date. In the study by Heeg et al. [12], this lesion was found in two of the 29 patients treated for acetabular fracture in three hospitals between 1972 and 1995. In their study, the two patients, one 5 years old presenting with an isolated AWAF with small fragments after hip dislocation and the other 9 years old presenting with a stable linear AWAF without displacement, were treated non-surgically, with excellent clinical and radiographic results, and no complications. The patients' follow-up time was not mentioned. In a multicentre registry study using data from the German Pelvic Trauma Registry Initiative, von Heyden et al. [13] found only 15 (9.8%) children with acetabular fractures out of a total of 152 children among 7360 patients with pelvic fractures registered in 3 distinct periods (1991–93, 1998–2000, 2004–2008). Three of the 15 acetabular fractures were associated with a pelvic fracture, and 12 were isolated. Only one patient suffered an AWAF, however, no specific details were reported about the patient, treatment, or follow-up.

In our study, both patients were treated surgically using the Smith-Petersen approach with ASIS osteotomy due to fracture displacement greater than 5 mm, with excellent clinical and radiographic results after a mean follow-up of 18 months. We opted for the Smith-Petersen approach with osteotomy of the ASIS because, in our opinion, it best exposes the anterior column of the acetabulum. Furthermore, by medially retracting the ASIS bone fragment along with the sartorius muscle, the neurovascular structures of the femoral triangle are completely protected. Other authors have suggested this approach in fractures involving the anterior wall, especially when extensile exposure of the anterior column of the acetabulum to the pubic root is necessary [14], [15], [16], as was required in our patients for the placement of the distal screws of the plate, without risk of articular penetration by the implants. In a recent study, Saracco et al. [3] observed that of 68 patients with isolated AWAF (in 17 studies), in 58.2% of cases (39 patients), the ilioinguinal approach was used, while the Smith-Petersen approach was used in 19.4% of cases (13 patients), showing that it is a viable option providing access to the low anterior acetabulum column and anterior wall. These authors conclude that the choice of surgical approach should be based on the surgeon's experience [3]. In any case, the risk of premature closure of the ASIS physis must be taken into account, since these are patients with immature skeletons. However, as observed in displaced traumatic avulsions of the ASIS in immature skeletons, its correct reduction and fixation do not usually cause complications in adulthood [17].

The choice of surgical approach is, in fact, a crucial aspect, since several authors have reported that, among the elementary patterns of acetabular fracture, AWAF presents the least satisfactory results, mainly due to the surgical complexity and the difficulty in obtaining anatomical reduction [3], [18], [19], [20]. Specifically in the immature skeleton, complications such as post-traumatic acetabular dysplasia and degenerative osteoarthritis can occur [21]. Furthermore, traumatic injury to the triradiate cartilage can be observed, with or without damage to the cartilage of the femoral head, which increases the risk of premature physeal closure due to the formation of a medial bony bridge, significantly worsening the prognosis [12], [22], [23], [24]. The younger the patient, the greater the risk of premature physeal closure. Therefore, it is essential that patients with immature skeletons who suffer acetabular fractures be followed up into adulthood, since the time interval between injury to the triradiate cartilage and the development of acetabular dysplasia is 12.4 years on average [23].

In our patients, no post-traumatic sequelae with clinical repercussion have been observed to date; however, in case #2, the initial appearance of a bony bar in the triradiate cartilage was noted. This patient, a 10-year-old boy with multiple fractures, including an isolated AWAF, after a car accident, despite the presence of premature physeal closure and avascular necrosis of the femoral head, presents with full and painless range of motion in the injured hip and mild limping after 18 months of follow-up. In our opinion, this could be due to both the young age and the delay in performing the definitive surgery. The patient is still a child (4 to 12 years old) [25], which is known to increase the risk of premature physeal arrest due to the displaced AWAF [12], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]. In addition, the presence of multiple fractures, including in the face and thoracic spine, delayed the definitive treatment of the acetabular fracture, especially due to the significant anaesthetic challenge posed by these associated injuries. Furthermore, the patient suffered a traumatic hip dislocation, which increases the risk of late avascular necrosis of the femoral head [22], [27]. Several studies addressing this complication after femoral neck fractures in patients with immature skeletons report the type of fracture, the degree of initial displacement, and the patient's age at the time of injury as the main factors associated with the development of avascular necrosis (ANV) of the femoral head. The young age of our patient, the association with traumatic hip dislocation, and the delay in adequate and definitive treatment of the acetabular fracture due to other skeletal and non-skeletal injuries should be considered relevant factors for the presence of avascular necrosis of the femoral head [28], [29], [30]. The other patient (case #1), a 13-year-old boy with an isolated open acetabular fracture, presents with full and painless range of motion in the injured hip after 18 months of follow-up. This case is even more unusual because, in addition to the rarity of the fracture pattern, the occurrence of open acetabular fractures in the paediatric population is extremely rare. Although the management of open fractures conceptually does not change compared to the adult population [31], in this case it became another potentially complicating factor, fortunately not interfering with the outcome.

Conclusion

Isolated AWAF is an even rarer injury in the immature skeletal population compared to adults, generally resulting from high-energy trauma. The cases reported herein highlight the importance of prompt diagnosis and adequate preoperative planning. In our patients, the Smith-Petersen approach with ASIS osteotomy provided excellent exposure of the anterior acetabular wall, facilitating anatomical reduction and stable fracture fixation. One must keep in mind the need for long-term follow-up of these patients, since complications related to both the triradiate cartilage (hip dysplasia) and the femoral head (avascular necrosis) can occur.
