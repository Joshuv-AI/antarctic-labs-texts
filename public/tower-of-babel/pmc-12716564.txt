Burden of herpes zoster among patients with systemic lupus erythematosus in the USA
Authors: Nikita Stempniewicz, Andrea Steffens, Kimvi Kim, Christopher F Bell, Mary DuCharme, David Singer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo estimate the incidence of herpes zoster (HZ) and the incremental healthcare resource utilisation (HRU) and costs associated with HZ among adults with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included insured patients from 2015 to 2022. HZ incidence rates were estimated among patients with SLE, SLE without other immunocompromising conditions (ICC) (SLE only) and no ICC. Adjusted incidence rate ratios (aIRRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) comparing the SLE and SLE only cohorts versus the no ICC cohort were estimated using Poisson regression. All-cause HRU and costs were compared between patients with SLE and HZ (SLE+/HZ+) and propensity score matched patients with SLE without HZ (SLE+/HZ–).ResultsThe incidence rates of HZ in the SLE (n=60 430), SLE only (n=21 206) and no ICC (n=1 000 000) cohorts were 19.72, 14.03 and 5.64 per 1000 patient-years, respectively (aIRR 1.89, 95% CI 1.79 to 2.00, and aIRR 1.54, 95% CI 1.40 to 1.69). Mean all-cause per-patient costs were significantly higher in the SLE+/HZ+ versus SLE+/HZ– cohorts during the first 1 month (n=2126 and n=8504, respectively; cost difference $1349, 95% CI $722 to 1977) and 3 months (n=1968 and n=7872; $1558, 95% CI $108 to 3007).ConclusionsPatients with SLE had a significantly higher incidence of HZ compared with adults without ICC. Patients with SLE who developed HZ incurred significantly higher all-cause costs than those who did not. These data support considering HZ vaccination for patients with SLE.

Introduction

The most recent systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) prevalence estimates in the USA range from approximately 73 per 100 000 persons in 2018 (based on data from 2002 to 2009 extrapolated to the 2018 Census population)1 to 150–250 per 100 000 persons in 2016 (based on claims data),2 for a total of 204 000 and 4 44 000–503 000 people, respectively.1 2 SLE has been estimated to be associated with a substantial economic burden of $1.4–3.2 billion in the US in 2016.3

Patients with SLE are at increased risk of infections.4 These include herpes zoster (HZ), which results from reactivation of varicella zoster virus that remains latent in the spinal dorsal root ganglia and the ganglia of sensory cranial nerves after varicella infection.5 This could be due to underlying aberrations of the immune systems of patients with SLE and/or immunosuppressive treatments,4 including hydroxychloroquine, corticosteroids, azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide and rituximab.68 Overall, patients with SLE have a 2–7-fold increased risk of HZ compared with those without SLE,913 with incidence rates of 12–20 per 1000 person-years among US adults in 2001–2010,911 but more recent US estimates are lacking. For comparison, HZ incidences in the overall US population are approximately 3–5, 7 and 10–12 per 1000 person-years among adults aged 21–50, 51–60 and ≥61 years, respectively (in 2007–2018).14

HZ typically causes a painful, pruritic rash on the torso, and approximately 5–30% of patients develop postherpetic neuralgia (long-term pain at the rash site), with the risk of postherpetic neuralgia increasing with age and in patients with certain conditions including SLE, diabetes and asthma.1517 In the USA, recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) is recommended by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) for immunocompetent adults aged ≥50 years18 and immunocompromised adults aged ≥19 years.19 The latter includes patients with autoimmune/inflammatory conditions (eg, SLE) and such patients are recommended to receive RZV when their disease is stable (ie, not during flares) and before taking immunosuppressive medications.20

HZ has been associated with increased healthcare resource utilisation (HRU) and costs among immunocompetent21 and immunocompromised22 individuals as well as patients with various autoimmune diseases2326 and other chronic conditions.27 28 However, costs associated with HZ among patients with SLE are unknown. Hence, the aims of the current study were to estimate the incidence of HZ among adults with SLE compared with adults without immunocompromising conditions (ICC) and estimate the incremental all-cause HRU and costs associated with HZ among patients with SLE.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective cohort study of commercially insured and Medicare Advantage with Part D (ie, including medical and pharmacy coverage) health plan members used administrative claims from the Optum Research Database during October 2015 to March 2022 (study period). The Optum Research Database is a large US healthcare claims database with fully adjudicated medical and pharmacy claims that includes approximately 20% of the US population (online supplemental text S1). Medical claims and encounter data were collected from all available healthcare sites for most types of services.

Study design

First, the incidence rates of HZ among patients with SLE (‘SLE cohort’) and patients with SLE without other ICC (‘SLE only cohort’; to assess HZ risk among a subpopulation of adults with SLE, isolated from the potential confounding effects of other ICC) were estimated and compared with a ‘no ICC cohort’. The index date for the SLE cohort was the latter of 12 months after the start of continuous enrolment and the first SLE diagnosis during the study period, while that for the no ICC cohort was 12 months after the start of continuous enrolment (figure 1a). The 12-month baseline period was used to collect demographic and clinical characteristics and exclude patients with prior HZ diagnoses. Patients were followed from the index date until the earliest of HZ vaccination, incident HZ diagnosis, health plan disenrolment, end of the study period or death.

Study designs for the estimation of (a) HZ incidence and (b) HRU and costs. *Earliest claim with an SLE diagnosis during index assignment period. †Patients had to have ≥1 month of available follow-up and were followed for up to 12 months. CE, continuous enrolment; HRU, healthcare resource utilisation; HZ, herpes zoster; ICC, immunocompromising conditions; SLE+/HZ+, patients with SLE and herpes zoster; SLE+/HZ–, patients with SLE but without herpes zoster; SLE only, SLE without any other immunocompromising conditions.

Second, using independently identified cohorts, the incremental HRU and costs among patients with SLE and HZ (‘SLE+/HZ+ cohort’) were estimated and compared with those with SLE without HZ (‘SLE+/HZ– cohort’). For the SLE+/HZ+ cohort, the index date was the first HZ diagnosis after 12 months of continuous enrolment that was during the index assignment period (1 October 2016 to 28 February 2022) and on/after the first SLE diagnosis (figure 1b). Patients in the SLE+/HZ+ cohort were propensity score matched (1:4) to patients in the SLE+/HZ– cohort. The index dates of patients in the SLE+/HZ– cohort were assigned to a random date within the index assignment period that was after 12 months of continuous enrolment and on/after the first SLE diagnosis. Patients were followed for 1–12 months from the index date to the earliest of health plan disenrolment, 12 months following the index date, the end of the study period or death.

Inclusion criteria

To be included in any of the five above-mentioned cohorts, patients had to be aged ≥18 years in the index year; have ≥12 months of continuous enrolment before the index date; have no medical claims for HZ during baseline (see online supplemental table 1) for International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes); have no medical/pharmacy claim for HZ immunisation before the index date (using up to 5 years of pre-index data when available); and have known age, sex, insurance type and geographic region. Patients with an HZ diagnosis during the 12-month baseline were excluded to ensure that HZ cases identified during the follow-up period were new HZ cases.

In the incidence part of the study, patients in the SLE cohort additionally had to have ≥1 inpatient medical claim for SLE (online supplemental table 1) or ≥2 outpatient medical claims with a diagnosis for SLE in any position ≥30 days apart during the study period. For the SLE only cohort, patients had to have SLE but no evidence of any of the other ICC listed in online supplemental table 2. For the no ICC cohort, patients had to have no evidence of SLE or any of the ICC listed online supplemental table 2. Evidence of ICC in both cohorts was defined as detailed in online supplemental text S2. A random sample of 1 000 000 individuals without any ICC comprised the no ICC cohort.

In the HRU/cost part of the study, inclusion criteria for the SLE+/HZ+ and SLE+/HZ– cohorts were the same as for the SLE cohort, but patients also had to have ≥1 month of follow-up after the index date. Patients were assigned to the SLE+/HZ+ cohort if they had ≥1 medical claim with a diagnosis for HZ (except if the first HZ diagnosis included other nervous system involvement (ICD-10-CM B02.2)) on a day not associated with HZ vaccination during the index assignment period.

Variables

Demographics on the index date included age, sex, race and ethnicity (based on known and derived data from a combination of sources using a proprietary model) and insurance type. Clinical characteristics during baseline (12 months before index to 1 day before index) included Quan–Charlson Comorbidity Index to which SLE contributed 1 (as a ‘connective tissue disease’),29 Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality comorbidities,30 immunosuppressive therapies (online supplemental table 3), all-cause healthcare costs and SLE severity (mild, moderate or severe based on treatments and diagnoses (online supplemental table 4)).31 32 Level of immunosuppression based on SLE therapies (none, non-immunosuppressive, less immunosuppressive or highly immunosuppressive (online supplemental table 5)) was measured during the 3 or 6 months before index.

Outcomes

The incidence rates of HZ in the SLE, SLE only and no ICC cohorts were estimated and compared, overall and by age group (18–49, 50–64 and ≥65 years). The incidence rates of HZ among patients in the SLE and SLE only cohorts aged 18–49 years were also compared with those among people aged ≥50 years without ICC. In the SLE and SLE only cohorts, HZ incidence rates by baseline SLE disease severity and immunosuppression based on follow-up SLE therapies (online supplemental tables 4,5) were also reported.

All-cause HRU counts (ambulatory visits (physician office or hospital outpatient), emergency department (ED) visits and inpatient stays) and all-cause healthcare costs (total, medical (ambulatory, ED, inpatient and other) and pharmacy) were estimated and compared between the SLE+/HZ+ and SLE+/HZ– cohorts during the first 1, 3 and 12 months of follow-up. HZ-related HRU and medical costs in the SLE+/HZ+ cohort, based on claims with a diagnosis for HZ or an HZ-related complication in any position, are also reported. Although HZ-related pharmacy costs were included in the all-cause costs, they were not included in the HZ-related costs as pharmacy claims did not include diagnosis codes. All-cause healthcare costs were the actual amounts paid to the provider by the health plan plus the patient copay and/or coinsurance amounts. Costs were adjusted to 2022 using the medical care component of the Consumer Price Index.33

Statistical analyses

Numbers and percentages are provided for categorical variables and means and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Standardised differences were used to compare the baseline characteristics of the SLE and SLE only versus no ICC cohorts.34 35 Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4.

HZ incidence rates in the SLE, SLE only and no ICC cohorts—calculated as the number of HZ cases divided by the person-time at risk—are presented as HZ cases per 1000 person-years. We report point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), along with adjusted incidence rate ratios (aIRRs) and 95% CIs between the SLE and SLE only versus no ICC cohorts. aIRRs were estimated using Poisson regression to account for baseline differences (included parameters are detailed under the relevant results figure), with a log link and log time-to-event as offset. HZ incidence was also estimated by immunosuppression based on SLE therapies during follow-up on a rolling basis (ie, measured daily based on medication use in the previous 90 days), and person-time was stratified by level of immunosuppression.

Propensity score matching36 was used to control for possible confounding of the association between HRU/costs and the baseline characteristics of the SLE+/HZ+ versus SLE+/HZ– cohorts with ≥1, ≥3 and ≥12 months of follow-up. Propensity score model covariates were selected based on clinical, statistical and methodologic reasoning and included: index year, age, sex, race, ethnicity, insurance type, geographic region, baseline comorbidity burden, treatments, all-cause HRU and costs and disease severity. Patients in the SLE+/HZ+ cohort were matched 1:4 using nearest neighbour matching without replacement with patients in the SLE+/HZ– cohort with at least as much follow-up. Patients were considered matched if their propensity scores were within a calliper of ±0.015. Standardised differences ≤10% between the matched SLE+/HZ+ vs SLE+/HZ– cohorts were considered acceptable for balance.

HRU and cost measures are reported for the SLE+/HZ+and SLE+/HZ– cohorts during the 1-, 3- and 12-month periods post-index among matched sets with ≥1, ≥3 and ≥12 months of follow-up, respectively. HRU and cost measures at each time point among patients with ≥12 months of follow-up are also reported. For each HRU category, rate ratios were calculated by dividing the mean rate in the SLE+/HZ+ cohorts by the mean rate in the SLE+/HZ– cohorts. Ninety-five per cent CIs were calculated using Wald z-test using robust standard errors in an ordinary least squares regression, and significance was inferred when the 95% CI did not include 1. Cost differences were calculated as the absolute differences between the SLE+/HZ+ and SLE+/HZ– cohorts. Ninety-five per cent CIs were estimated as described above, and significance was inferred when the 95% CI for the cost difference did not include 0.

Results

HZ incidence cohorts

The SLE cohort included 60 430 patients, of whom 64.9% had other ICC. These included diffuse connective tissue disease (28.4%), inflammatory arthropathy (28.2%), immune system disorders (11.7%), solid malignancy (8.7%) and uncertain behaviour neoplasm (7.6%). After removing patients with SLE and other ICC, there were 21 206 in the SLE only cohort (online supplemental figure 1).

Compared with patients in the no ICC cohort, those in the SLE and SLE only cohorts were older (mean ages: 55.3 and 52.6 vs 46.4 years), more often female (89.4% and 88.7% vs 50.2%), more often had Medicare Advantage with Part D insurance (50.2% and 40.8% vs 21.5%), had higher mean Quan–Charlson Comorbidity Index scores (2.1 and 1.6 vs 0.3) and were more likely to have taken immunosuppressive therapies during baseline (69.0% and 57.6% vs 19.2%), mainly corticosteroids (62.8% and 52.5% vs 18.6%) (table 1). Most patients in the SLE and SLE only cohorts had moderate baseline disease severity (51.6% and 47.3%, respectively), followed by mild (27.8% and 37.2%) and then severe (20.6% and 15.5%). Mean±SD lengths of follow-up in the SLE, SLE only and no ICC cohorts were 23.6±19.6, 20.4±18.3 and 23.5±21.3 months, respectively.

HZ incidence

Crude HZ incidence rates were 19.7 per 1000 person-years in the SLE cohort and 14.0 per 1000 person-years in the SLE only cohort; these were 3.5- and 2.5-fold higher than in the no ICC cohort (5.6 per 1000 person-years) (figure 2). After multivariable adjustment to account for baseline differences between cohorts (including age, comorbidity score and immunosuppressive medications), the overall aIRRs were 1.89 (95% CI 1.79 to 2.00) and 1.54 (95% CI 1.40 to 1.69) for the SLE and SLE only cohorts versus the no ICC cohort (both p<0.001). aIRRs showed significantly higher incidence rates of HZ among patients in the SLE and SLE only cohorts in all age groups. HZ incidence rates increased with age, while aIRRs decreased with age. Patients in the SLE cohort aged 18–49 years had a significantly higher incidence rate of HZ than patients aged ≥50 years without ICC (aIRR 1.47, 95% CI 1.35 to 1.61; p<0.001), while this was not significant for the SLE only cohort aged 18–49 years (aIRR 1.15, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.34; p=0.09).

Crude HZ incidence rates were numerically higher in the SLE and SLE only cohorts compared with the no ICC cohort across strata defined by age (seven groupings), sex and race and ethnicity (online supplemental figure 2).

In the SLE and SLE only cohorts, crude HZ incidence rates increased with baseline SLE severity and level of immunosuppression based on follow-up SLE therapies (online supplemental figure 3). Crude HZ incidence rates in the SLE and SLE only cohorts among patients who had no evidence of SLE therapies (or only non-immunosuppressive SLE therapies) were numerically higher than in the no ICC cohort (12.6–19.1 vs 5.6 per 1000 person-years).

HRU and cost cohorts

Overall, 2126 and 8504 patients with ≥1 month of follow-up were included in the matched SLE+/HZ+ and SLE+/HZ– cohorts, respectively (online supplemental figure 4). Baseline characteristics are detailed in table 1, while those for matched patients with ≥3 months (n=1968 vs n=7872 (92.6% of the overall cohorts)) and ≥12 months (n=1380 vs n=5520 (64.9%)) of follow-up are in online supplemental table 6. Standardised differences were all <10%, showing acceptable balance between cohorts.

HRU

Patients in the SLE+/HZ+ cohorts had significantly more ambulatory visits, ED visits and inpatient stays during the 1- and 3-month periods after index and significantly more ambulatory and ED visits during the first 12 months after index than those in the SLE+/HZ– cohorts (figure 3). Results were similar when only patients with ≥12 months of follow-up were considered at each time point (online supplemental figure 5).

In the SLE+/HZ+ cohorts, HZ-related HRU mainly accrued in month 1, during which time patients had means±SD of 1.53±1.52 ambulatory visits, 0.20±0.60 ED visits and 0.06±0.25 inpatient stays (online supplemental table 7).

Costs

Mean all-cause per-patient healthcare costs during month 1 were significantly higher in the SLE+/HZ+ versus SLE+/HZ– cohort: $4383 versus $3033, and cost difference $1349 (95% CI $722 to $1977), driven by inpatient costs (cost difference $1135, 95% CI 568 to 1702) (figure 4). Results during the first 3 months were similar (cost difference $1558, 95% CI $108 to $3007), but during the first 12 months, the cost difference was not statistically significant ($1346, 95% CI –$2653 to $5345). When only patients with ≥12 months of follow-up were considered, overall costs and cost differences were slightly smaller and the cost difference during the first 3 months was no longer significant (online supplemental figure 6).

Discussion

In this retrospective claims-based study, the incidence rates of HZ were significantly higher among patients with SLE or SLE only versus adults without ICC after adjustment (aIRRs 1.89 and 1.54; both p<0.001). Among adults with SLE, all-cause HRU and costs were higher among those who developed HZ compared with those who did not, with statistically significant differences during the first month (cost difference $1349) and the first 3 months ($1558). These results support recommendations from the ACIP for RZV use in immunocompromised adults aged ≥19 years19 and from the American College of Rheumatology to administer RZV to patients with rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases (such as SLE) aged >18 years taking immunosuppressive medication.37

HZ incidence

The HZ incidence rate of 19.7 per 1000 person-years among patients with SLE is towards the upper end of estimates from older US studies (12–20 per 1000 person-years).91138 More importantly, the current results confirm that US adults with SLE are at increased risk of HZ compared with various adult populations without SLE, including SLE versus no ICC (19.7 vs 5.6 per 1000 person-years (current study)), SLE versus the general population (15.2 vs 4.8 per 1000 person-years)9 and SLE versus those without SLE, various other autoimmune or inflammatory conditions, or diabetes (19.9 vs 5.3 per 1000 person-years).10

The incidence rate of HZ increased with age, which is aligned with results in the general population14 39 and some,9 11 but not all,10 12 earlier SLE studies. Patients with SLE aged 18–49 years had nearly twice the unadjusted incidence rate of HZ compared with adults aged ≥50 years without ICC (aIRR 1.47), suggesting that patients with SLE are at higher risk of HZ at a younger age relative to older patients without ICC, who have a well-established age-related risk of HZ.14 39

The incidence rate of HZ increased with increasing SLE severity (based on treatments and diagnoses) and level of immunosuppression (based on follow-up SLE therapies). Multiple earlier SLE studies have also reported that HZ risk increases with various treatments, including corticosteroids,114044 cyclophosphamide,38 41 42 44 45 mycophenolate,11 41 44 azathioprine41 44 and biological agents.7 8 As 69.0% of the SLE cohort were prescribed immunosuppressive therapies during baseline, this implies that many patients with SLE may be at increased risk of HZ due to treatment. Other reported risk factors for HZ among patients with SLE include lupus nephritis/renal involvement38 41 43 45 46 and lymphopenia.40 43 45

Patients in the SLE only cohort had a lower incidence rate of HZ than those in the SLE cohort (14.0 vs 19.7 per 1000 person-years). This may be because patients in the SLE only cohort were, on average, younger and had milder SLE and fewer comorbidities. Many patients with broad, non-specific diagnoses such as ‘diffuse connective tissue disease’ and ‘inflammatory arthropathy’ were excluded from the SLE only cohort, but these may have been manifestations of SLE rather than different diseases. As the SLE cohort is likely more representative of patients with SLE in the USA, the HRU and cost analyses were performed among cohorts that did not exclude patients with other ICC.

HRU

During the first month after an HZ diagnosis, patients in the SLE+/HZ+ cohort had 1.4 times as many ambulatory visits, 2.4 times as many ED visits and 2.3 times as many inpatient stays as those in the SLE+/HZ– cohort. These relative differences diminished over time, highlighting the acute nature of HZ. Comparable findings have been reported in similar studies of patients with psoriasis,25 psoriatic arthritis,26 rheumatoid arthritis,23 ulcerative colitis24 and Crohn’s disease.24

Costs

To our knowledge, this is the first study to report on the incremental costs associated with HZ among patients with SLE. There were significant incremental all-cause costs of $1349 and $1558 in the first 1 and 3 months after HZ, respectively, with a non-significant excess of $1346 in the first year. This suggests that much of the cost associated with HZ occurs soon after diagnosis, which aligns with the acute nature of the disease and was also found in the above-mentioned similar studies.2326 However, costs among patients who develop chronic complications (eg, postherpetic neuralgia) are considerably higher than among those who do not.21 22

The incremental costs in the first month after HZ diagnosis among patients with SLE ($1349 (2022)) were similar to those in patients with psoriasis ($1390 (2021))25 and multiple sclerosis ($1317 (2022))47 and lower than among patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease ($2983 (2018));27 ulcerative colitis ($2189),24 rheumatoid arthritis ($3404)23 and Crohn’s disease ($3774)24 (all 2020); psoriatic arthritis ($2012 (2021));26 and HIV ($3075 (2022)).48 This underscores the significant costs associated with HZ among patients with various autoimmune and other chronic diseases.

HZ-related costs also mostly accrued in the first month after HZ diagnosis but did continue to increase in months 2–3 and 4–12, likely due to treatment for complications such as postherpetic neuralgia. Findings were similar in the above-mentioned studies that specifically reported HZ-related costs.25 26

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the novelty of the HRU and cost findings among patients with SLE, the recent HZ incidence data and the large sample size. However, the lack of detailed clinical measures could have led to residual confounding and misclassification. Also, HRU and cost estimations may have been subject to coding errors, omissions, duplications or missing data. As the data were obtained from the Optum Research Database, which includes patients with commercial and Medicare Advantage with Part D plans, the results may not be generalisable to patients with Medicaid or no insurance.

SLE medication use in the SLE cohorts was lower than expected. This could reflect the lookback period used to capture SLE medications, which was limited to 3 months before index (6 months for intravenous rituximab). In addition, this could potentially reflect SLE medications filled outside the insurance database (eg, medication samples, patients paying out of pocket or medications paid for by other insurers) or inclusion of patients with milder SLE who may not have received disease-specific treatment.

Although the use of ICD-10-CM diagnosis code B02 for HZ has a high positive predictive value (76%),49 some HZ cases may not have been identified, and some cases may not have been true HZ cases. As patients with HZ may experience a prodromal phase before diagnosis, some HZ-related HRU and costs before the index date could have been missed, potentially leading to an underestimation of HRU and costs in the SLE+/HZ+ cohort. Furthermore, all-cause healthcare costs were computed as the combined health plan and patient paid amounts, but payments from other payers were not incorporated, potentially underestimating costs in this study. Finally, HZ-related costs were likely underestimated as HZ-related pharmacy costs could not be included due to a lack of diagnosis codes on pharmacy claims.

Conclusions

Patients with SLE had a significantly higher incidence rate of HZ than adults without ICC. Further, all-cause HRU and costs were significantly higher among patients with SLE who developed HZ compared with those who did not. These results (online supplemental figure 7) highlight the impact of HZ among patients with SLE and underscore the importance of considering preventive approaches, such as vaccination, to avoid HZ in this population.
