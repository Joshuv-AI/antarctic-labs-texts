A Tale of Two Systems:Characterizing Architectural Complexity onMachine Learning-Enabled Systems
Renato Cordeiro Ferreira

Abstract
How can the complexity of ML-enabled systems be managed effectively? The goal of this research is to investigate how complexity affects ML-Enabled Systems (MLES). To address this question, this research aims to introduce a metrics-based architectural model to characterize the complexity of MLES. The goal is to support architectural decisions, providing a guideline for the inception and growth of these systems. This paper brings, side-by-side, the architecture representation of two systems that can be used as case studies for creating the metrics-based architectural model: the SPIRA and the Ocean Guard MLES.



1
Introduction

Complexity
has been a subject of discussion since the early
days of the Software Engineering field
[
3
]
.
In the book “Mythical Man Month”, Brooks introduces the concept of

essential
and
accidental
complexity for software
[
3
]
:
the
essential
exists intrinsically to satisfy the requirements
of the problem solved, whereas the
accidental
may originate
from any external factors that influence the solution chosen.
Under this definition, an ML-enabled system (MLES) has high essential
complexity: it requires extra components in its architecture to
support data processing and model handling
[
1
,
2
]
.
As a consequence, this also creates more opportunities for
introducing accidental complexity into the system.

While there are many recent surveys summarizing important gaps in the SE4AI
literature, only Giray
et al.
highlights
handling complexity
as an
open challenge for MLE
[
9
]
.

In an interview
study with 18 professional ML engineers
[
17
]
,
Shankar et al. paper describes how participants
“expressed an aversion
to complexity”
, implying that practitioners consider it an issue.

This proposal presents a plan to create a
metrics-oriented
architectural model to characterize the complexity of MLES
.
By using metrics to identify where complexity emerges in the software
architecture of MLES, this research aims to provide a method to avoid
pitfalls that make them fail to reach production.

2
Research Questions

To achieve the goal of this research, this PhD proposes
two main research questions. They follow the SMART principle
[
18
]
, i.e., they should be

Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-Bound
.

RQ1:

What are the measurable dimensions of complexity in
the architecture of MLES?

Section

2
aims to explore metrics related to complexity available in
the literature.
Software Metrics
have been thoroughly researched
by Software Engineering
[
5
]
.
However, MLES also have their data and model dimensions
[
16
]
, which affect the software architecture.
Finding metrics that measure the data- and model-related complexity

beyond code
is the biggest potential challenge for
answering
section

2
.

RQ2:

How can complexity metrics be operationalized over
the architecture of MLES?

Section

2
aims to define a process to collect the metrics found
in
section

2
.
Metrics may be related to different abstraction levels of a system:
from code snippets, to components, to services
[
5
]
.
Moreover, they can have varying levels of different quality attributes
[
12
]
,
such as validity, reliability, computability, intuitiveness,
ease of implementation, and independence of other metrics.
As a consequence, only some metrics found in
section

2
may
be practical or useful to collect. In particular, if two metrics
provide similar information, it will be preferable to use the best
according to the quality attributes.
Therefore, the challenge for answering
section

2
is twofold: creating a
dataset of representative MLES to collect the metrics, and then choosing which
subset of metrics to collect.

Measuring a metric may require different levels of access to the system
(e.g., having the codebase available for processing) or rely on different
representations of the system (e.g., creating a graph describing its data
flow). As a consequence, only some metrics found in
section

2
may be
practical to collect. In particular, if two metrics provide similar
information, it may be preferable to use the simpler.

3.1
Understanding Metrics for Complexity

To answer
section

2
, this research will start studying

the complexity of software systems
and
metrics
.
For that,
section

3.1
has been divided into two steps:
(1) to understand how practitioners address complexity in their systems; and
(2) to describe and categorize related metrics based on the literature.

Survey Research with Industry.

Survey research proposes selecting a representative sample from
a well-defined population, identifying characteristics from it
(via questionnaires or structured interviews), and then applying
data analysis techniques to generalize the results
[
4
]
.
This method helps to answer a question about a particular target
population, such as professional developers working on software
companies, without polling every member of the population.

A survey allows a practical way to map how practitioners address
complexity while creating MLES, as is the goal of
section

3.1
.
This approach is
inductive
rather than
deductive
, i.e.,
first it proposes to understand what practitioners consider while
designing their systems, then it tries to create a theory around it.
Since MLES have their data and model dimensions
[
16
]
, these characteristics will likely
reflect during the characterization of the complexity of MLES.

Ontology Design.

Making an ontology results in a well-defined categorization of the metrics
[
15
]
,
as it is the goal of
section

3.1
. The ontology will focus on describing
different
types
of metrics
[
5
]
,
as well as possible
quality attributes
associated with them
[
12
]
. Some properties will be
related to the
applicability
of the metrics, which will be explored
only on
section

3.2
. Therefore, the knowledge base will be fully complete
only by the end of the research.

An ontology can be created via
ontology design

[
15
]
. An ontology
is the structure behind a knowledge base, including
classes
,

properties
, and
restrictions

[
15
]
.
The ontology will focus on describing different
types
of
metrics
[
5
]
, as well as possible

quality attributes
associated with them
[
12
]
.

3.2
Designing a Metrics-Oriented Architectural Model

To answer
section

2
, this research will focus on selecting a subset of the
metrics from
section

3.1
to compose the
metrics-oriented architectural
model
. For that,
section

3.2
has been divided into two steps:
(1) to find how to collect the metrics on a known MLES; and
(2) to validate the metric collection process on industry MLES.

Exploratory Case Study.

Making an exploratory case study provides a framework to propose an answer
to
section

2

[
4
]
.
The exploratory case study will have the following study proposition:

“What is a process to operationalize the collection of complexity
metrics on an MLES?”
, as it is the goal of
section

3.2
.
To achieve that, it will rely on two
typical cases

[
4
]
:
the
SPIRA
MLES
[
7
,
13
]

and the
Ocean Guard
MLES
[
8
]
, which are currently
being developed by the author alongside his PhD research.

The familiarity with the codebase should allow the author to focus
on operationalizing the metric collection rather than learning how the
project works. The goal is twofold:
document
the process of metric collection
,
to achieve the study proposition; and
determine
the quality attributes of the metrics
,
to refine the ontology made on
section

3.1
.

By choosing which metrics are worth collecting, and documenting
the requirements to collect them, the result will be the initial version
of the
metrics-oriented architectural model
.

Confirmatory Case Study.

Making a confirmatory case study provides a framework to validate the proposed
answer to
section

2

[
4
]
.
The confirmatory case study will repeat the same study proposition:

“What is a process to operationalize the collection of complexity
metrics on an MLES?”
, as it is the goal of
section

3.2
.
To achieve that, this research can rely on databases of open source
repositories compiled by previous works from the literature, such as
the 262 open-source ML products mined by Nahar
et al.
from GitHub
[
14
]
.

Production MLES can have various architectures, with different combinations
of components. The diversity of codebases should allow the researchers to
validate the robustness of the metric collection process. Two types of
data will be revisited:
the process of metric collection
, to achieve
the study proposition; and
the quality attributes of the metrics
,
to refine the ontology made on
section

3.1
.

By reviewing which metrics are worth collecting and how to collect them,
the result will be a refined version of the

metrics-oriented architectural model
.

SPIRA

via speech analysis. It is being developed since 2020 as a

multidisciplinary project

under the University of São Paulo (USP), in Brazil. The project aims to create
a tool to assist physicians and nurses to find early signs of respiratory
insufficiency, thus helping physicians to identify which patients need
urgent treatment.

5
Challenges and Open Questions

This PhD started at University of São Paulo (USP) in 2020, amidst
the COVID-19 pandemic. The author was employed in a full-time job as

Principal ML Engineer
. This expertise led him to participate in
the
SPIRA
project, which led him to supervise many students to develop
the MLES described in
figure

2
.

This PhD started a new phase at the Jheronimus Academy of Data Science
(JADS) in 2023, after an opportunity of a double degree with the Netherlands.
The author became employed as a full-time
Scientific Programmer
for
the
MARIT-D
project. This opportunity led him to develop the

Ocean Guard
MLES described in
figure

3
.

The development of
SPIRA
and
Ocean Guard
is intertwined with this PhD research, and
both projects can be excellent case studies for
section

3.2
. Currently,

section

3.1
is being executed with the help of two master students, whose
research includes a broader interview-based qualitative study about MLOps.
Therefore, the research is converging to a point where it will be possible
to measure the first metrics of complexity about the systems, thus creating
the first version of the
metrics-oriented architectural model
.

Given this context, some open questions for the research are:

•

How to take advantage of the familiarity with both systems to
explore the dimensions of complexity of MLES?

•

Is it acceptable to choose metrics for the first version of the

metrics-oriented architectural model

if they can be measured in these systems?

•

How to mitigate experimental bias and other threats to validity
when creating the
metrics-oriented architectural model
,
considering the familiarity with the case studies?
