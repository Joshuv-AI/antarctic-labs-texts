Measuring vancomycin using paired pulse voltammetry with a wireless instantaneous neurotransmitter concentration sensing system for point-of-care patient treatment
Authors: Sheng-Ta Tsai, Mohamed M. El-Gohary, Maximiliano A. Hawkes, Amra Sakusic, Sangmun Hwang, Youngjong Kwak, Juan M. Rojas-Cabrera, Sara A. Vettleson-Trutza, Kristen M. Scheitler, Charles D. Blaha, John S. Kuo, Jin Woo Chang, Dong Pyo Jang, Yoonbae Oh, Kendall H. Lee, Hojin Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vancomycin is a glycopeptide antibiotic widely used for severe Gram-positive infections, particularly methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). However, its narrow therapeutic index necessitates precise monitoring to avoid nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, or hematologic toxicity. Current therapeutic drug monitoring relies on serum assays requiring several hours, delaying timely dose adjustments. Electrochemical approaches such as fast-scan cyclic voltammetry (FSCV) offer potential for real-time bedside monitoring. We used bare AS4 carbon fiber microelectrodes (CFMs) as the vancomycin sensor. The Wireless Instantaneous Neurotransmitter Concentration Sensing (WINCS) Harmoni system, a portable and clinically compatible platform, was used to perform FSCV measurements of vancomycin. Waveform parameters were optimized, and paired-pulse voltammetry (PPV) was applied to enhance detection selectivity in vitro across varying vancomycin concentrations. Two distinct oxidation peaks of vancomycin were observed. PPV analysis indicated that the + 1.44 V peak is predominantly pH-independent, while the + 0.69 V peak demonstrates pH-dependent characteristics. Furthermore, the optimized FSCV waveform yielded a detection limit of 0.36 μM with stable and reproducible signal performance. Paired-pulse FSCV using the WINCS Harmoni platform enables sensitive, real-time electrochemical detection of vancomycin. This approach may support future rapid point-of care monitoring and inform individualized dosing strategies in critical care settings.Graphical abstract Electronic supplementary materialThe online version of this article (10.1007/s13534-026-00580-1) contains supplementary material, which is available to authorized users.

Introduction

Optimal antibiotic use is essential in managing infectious diseases, particularly in an era of rising antibiotic resistance [1]. Among drug-resistant pathogens, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) remains a leading cause of severe infection and high mortality rates. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported an all-cause mortality rate for MRSA bacteremia of 30–40%, compared to 10–20% for methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) bacteremia [1–3]. Vancomycin, a glycopeptide antibiotic, is the first-line therapy for treating infections caused by MRSA [4]. However, its narrow therapeutic index requires optimizing treatment efficacy whilst mitigating acute toxicities. Critically ill patients are particularly vulnerable to vancomycin-associated toxicity due to fluid shifts, altered body composition, and polypharmacy especially with concurrent nephrotoxic agents [5]. These factors collectively affect the clearance and distribution of vancomycin, heightening the risk of adverse effects such as nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity, neutropenia, and in medically complicated patients, the development of long-term cognitive impairment [6].

Dosing of vancomycin exhibits substantial interpatient variability [7]. In recent years, the ratio of the 24-h area under the normalized curve to the minimum inhibitory concentration (AUC24/MIC) has been advocated as the preferred pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic parameter for assessing vancomycin’s therapeutic efficacy [8]. Consequently, the 2020 American consensus guidelines on therapeutic drug monitoring recommended maintaining an AUC24/MIC between 400 and 600 µg x h/mL to optimize therapeutic outcomes while minimizing toxicity [9]. Accurate quantification of vancomycin concentrations is therefore essential to ensure effective and safe clinical management.

Several analytical methods have been developed to quantify vancomycin in plasma, including bioassays, radioimmunoassays (RIA), fluorescence polarization immunoassays, enzyme-multiplied immunoassay technique, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS), among others [10]. However, most of these approaches lack the capability to measure vancomycin concentrations in near real-time. To address this limitation, recent studies have the use of electrochemical techniques to characterize the complex voltammetric “fingerprint” of vancomycin [11–13]. We have summarized previously reported electrochemical vancomycin detection strategies, including highlighting their respective detection methods and analytic limits of detection in Table 1. These approaches have largely focused on enhancing analytical sensitivity through electrode modification, nanomaterial integration, or aptamer functionalization. Electrochemical methods for detecting vancomycin

Abbreviation: N/A: not mentioned in the study; Ref: reference

In the present study, we adopted a complementary approach by employing FSCV and PPV to further characterize the electrochemical behavior of vancomycin. By examining the faradaic nature of the oxidation peaks and distinguishing diffusion-controlled from capacitive contributions, this strategy facilitates improved signal interpretation and analytical reliability without additional electrode modification (we used bare AS4 carbon fiber CFMs).

Thus, our work aims to provide an additional electrochemical perspective on the oxidation characteristics of vancomycin.

In electrochemical systems, hydroxyl groups undergo oxidation when an electric potential is applied to a working electrode, typically composed of carbon-based materials [22]. Vancomycin contains ten hydroxyl groups, six of which, along with one secondary amine, can undergo oxidation (Fig. 1) [16]. Unlike smaller drug molecules such as ethambutol, acyclovir, and meropenem, vancomycin features a complex molecular structure containing multiple primary amine groups. These amines are thought to interfere with electrochemical detection by deactivating nitroxyl radicals, such as nortropine N-oxyl (NNO), by irreversibly binding to the oxoammonium ion formed during oxidation on the electrode surface [23, 24]. To address this challenge, Ono et al. (2021) utilized 4-acetamido-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine 1-oxyl (A-TEMPO) as a redox mediator and demonstrated that cyclic voltammetry (CV) could reliability detect vancomycin [16].Fig. 1Chemical structure of vancomycin. Fast-scan cyclic voltammetry (FSCV) is a well-established electrochemical technique most commonly applied to study neurotransmitter dynamics in vivo [25]. Unlike CV, FSCV involves applying a rapidly changing voltage (400 V/s compared to 100 mV/s in conventional CV) to a carbon fiber microelectrode and measuring the resulting current to identify and quantify target analytes [26]. The faster scan rates used in FSCV enable a sub-second temporal resolution for real-time detection of analytes [26]. Our group previously developed the Wireless Instantaneous Neurotransmitter Concentration Sensing (WINCS) Harmoni system, a compact, sterilizable, low-noise platform capable of performing microsecond electrochemical measurements with FSCV in the brain [27]. Building upon this platform, we introduced paired-pulse voltammetry (PPV), a variant of FSCV that enhances analyte discrimination. This method minimizes confounding factors such as pH shifts, and simplifies electrochemical signal interpretation [28]. In the present proof-of-concept study, we integrate these methodologies to evaluate the feasibility of employing voltammetry for real-time vancomycin detection, demonstrating a translational potential of neurochemical sensing technology to therapeutic drug monitoring.

Methods

Electrode preparation

A carbon fiber (AS4, diameter 7 μm; Hexel, Dublin, CA) was inserted into a silica tube (20 μm ID, 90 μm OD, 10 μm coat with polyimide; Polymicro Technologies, Phoenix, AZ). The distal connection between the carbon fiber and silica tubing was covered with epoxy resin. The silica tubing was then seated onto a Nitinol super elastic extension wire (diameter 0.008″, length 300 mm; Component Supply, Sparta, TN) and secured with a silver-based conductive paste [29]. Polyimide tubing (diameter 0.010″, length 270 mm; Nordson Medical, Chattanooga, TN) was then placed halfway up to the electrode, leaving the distal carbon fiber and half of the silica’s length exposed. The juncture between the silica and polyamide tubing was secured with epoxy resin. The carbon fiber was then trimmed under a dissecting microscope to an approximate length of 100 μm [28]. The Ag/AgCl reference electrode was fabricated by electrochemically chloridizing a 31-gauge silver wire using a DC battery in normal saline solution [30]. Recording electrodes were pre-cycled in Tris buffer (15 mM Tris, 3.25 mM KCl, 140 mM NaCl, 1.2 mM CaCl2, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 1.2 mM MgCl2, and 2.0 mM Na2SO4, pH titrated to ~ 7.4; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) using a modified FSCV waveform (-0.4 V to + 1.5 V, scan rate of 400 V/s) applied at a frequency of 10 Hz.

Vancomycin preparation

Vancomycin hydrochloride (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States, molecular weight = 1485.73 g/mol) was prepared by dissolving 14.86 mg in 10 mL of Tris buffer to prepare a 1 mM stock solution. This stock solution was then diluted to prepare vancomycin solutions of 0.7, 1.5, 3.0, 10, 25, and 50 μM.

Flow cell apparatus

For in vitro experimentation, a flow injection system (Supplementary Fig. 1) was utilized and interfaced with the WINCS Harmoni system [31]. A CFM was positioned at the center of an acrylic chamber, which was connected to a flanged fluid line (BOLA, Germany) via a 6-port switching valve (Rheodyne MX Series II, IDEX Health & Science, United States). A syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, United States) was used to maintain a constant flow rate of 2 mL per minute. Once the FSCV signal was stable, 15-s boluses of vancomycin for each concentration tested were injected for detection by the CFM (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Pseudo color plot presentation

Pseudo FSCV color plots were created with WINCSWare. The X-axis represents time (seconds), while the Y-axis indicates the applied voltage, ranging from -0.4 to + 1.45 V. This voltage range allows users to identify the peaks of oxidation or reduction currents by adjusting the cursors. A brown background represents a current of 0 nA. Green and purple indicate positive currents (oxidation), whereas black and blue represent negative currents (reduction).

Results

Voltammetric vancomycin signals

We observed a distinct oxidation peak at + 1.44 V while a smaller oxidation peak was observed around + 0.69 V using FSCV. Each oxidation peak was accompanied by a corresponding reduction peak (Fig. 2). We illustrate the temporal relationship of the two oxidation and two corresponding reduction peaks in Fig. 2, using background-subtracted voltammograms captured at distinct time points.

To investigate the relationship between the two oxidation peaks, we altered the potential sweep range of –0.4 to + 1.5 V to a narrower range of –0.4 to + 1.0 V. Under this reduced potential range, the first oxidation peak was greatly diminished, and the second oxidation peak was below detection levels (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results suggest that the two peaks are interdependent, and that the second peak may represent a consecutive phase of the first oxidation, or possibly the re-oxidation of a product generated during the first oxidation [33].

Although the peak at approximately + 1.44 V is close to the vertex potential (+ 1.45 V), and therefore the possibility of switching-related contributions must be considered, the voltammograms display a smooth and continuous current profile across the switching point (Figs. 2C and 3D), without evidence of an abrupt transient characteristic of a switching artifact. Furthermore, the log(i)–log(v) slope is 0.475 (Fig. 3F), which is consistent with predominantly diffusion-controlled behavior (expected slope ≈ 0.5) rather than a capacitive-dominated response (expected slope ≈ 1) [34]. While minor capacitive contributions near the vertex cannot be entirely excluded, the overall scan-rate dependence supports the conclusion that the observed peak primarily arises from a diffusion-controlled electrochemical process.

In addition, we observed that the second oxidation peak at + 0.69 V exhibits pH-dependent behavior, which is discussed in the Discussion section. For these reasons, we selected the + 1.44 V peak as the primary oxidation peak of vancomycin for subsequent analysis.

FSCV optimization for vancomycin detection

To determine the optimal parameters for vancomycin detection, we tested various holding potentials (-0.4 V, -0.2 V, 0 V, and + 0.2 V), all using a fixed scan rate of 1200 V/s. The results demonstrated that a negative holding potential enhanced the adsorption of vancomycin to the CFM surface. The raw voltammogram from one representative electrode at different holding potentials is shown in Fig. 3A. For clarity, a corresponding pseudo color plot of each holding potential is shown in Fig. 3B. The peak anodic current occurring at + 1.44 V was selected for direct comparisons of holding potentials (Fig. 3C). The mean (± standard deviation, SD) normalized peak anodic currents (Ip.a.) for holding potentials (n = 15) of -0.4 V, -0.2 V, 0 V, and + 0.2 V were 1, 0.86 ± 0.06, 0.71 ± 0.05, and 0.61 ± 0.06, respectively. A vertex potential of + 1.45 V was selected to avoid water oxidation which occurs at + 1.5 V and to prevent electrode instability due to excess surface etching [26].

Next, we evaluated the effect scan rate has on vancomycin detection using the scan rates of 400 V/s, 800 V/s, and 1200 V/s while maintaining a holding potential of -0.4 V (n = 15). The background-subtracted voltammogram from a representative CFM is shown in Fig. 3D and corresponding color plots are shown in Fig. 3E. Among the tested scan rates, 1200 V/s yielded the highest peak current at + 1.44 V. After normalizing the currents to the value of 1200 V/s, the normalized values (mean ± SD) for the three scan rates (n = 15) were 1.0, 0.83 ± 0.35, and 0.59 ± 0.23, respectively (1200, 800, and 400 V/s, Supplementary Fig. 3B). The normalized current and scan rate were converted to logarithmic values and plotted as log(i) versus log(v) in Fig. 3F. The log(i) values (mean ± SD) at scan rates of 400, 800, and 1200 V/s were − 0.23 ± 0.1, − 0.09 ± 0.09, and 0, respectively. The fitted slope of 0.475 is consistent with predominant diffusion-controlled behavior [35]. We also calculated the limit of detection for vancomycin using these optimal parameters as 0.36 μM.

For the second oxidation peak at + 0.69 V, the same optimization protocol was implemented. Although a more negative holding potential yielded the highest current response, the log(i) values did not scale proportionally with log(v), indicating that additional factors may contribute to the observed signal (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Sensitivity test

We selected six concentrations of vancomycin for sensitivity testing that encompass the physiological range of vancomycin levels in human blood. The American Society of Health-System Pharmacists, the Infectious Diseases Society of America, and the Society of Infectious Diseases Pharmacists recommended therapeutic trough levels of 15–20 mg/L (10.35–13.8 μM) for severe infections (e.g., sepsis, pneumonia, meningitis, osteomyelitis, and endocarditis) and 10–15 mg/L (6.9–10.35 μM) for less severe infections [36]. Trough serum vancomycin concentrations, rather than peak levels, was also considered the most accurate and practical measure for monitoring therapeutic efficacy [36].

Six concentrations of vancomycin (0.7, 1.5, 3.0, 10, 25, and 50 μM; n = 15 for each concentration) were tested in the flow cell. The results showed a quasi-linear response, with an R2 value of 0.895 (Fig. 4B). A representative background-subtracted voltammogram is shown in Fig. 4A. Additional pseudo color plots for each concentration tested are shown in Fig. 4C.

Fast-scan paired pulse voltammetry (PPV)

As previously mentioned, we observed two distinct oxidation and reduction peaks for vancomycin. To further investigate this phenomenon, we employed paired-pulse voltammetry to minimize potential confounding factors in vancomycin measurement, such as pH fluctuations or electrode surface contamination. The sensitivity and specificity of voltammetric detection of analytes in a physiological medium is challenging due to the influence of pH fluctuations and other environmental changes at the CFM surface. To address this, we utilized PPV to increase analyte specificity. PPV is a variant of standard FSCV that differentiates complex analytes by applying two identical binary waveforms (primary pulse and secondary pulse in Fig. 5A) separated by a specific time interval (e.g., 2 ms) [28]. This approach helps mitigate confounding factors and simplifies analysis. For instance, the pH of the solution is unlikely to change within such a brief time window (2 ms), so subtracting the two waveforms (primary minus secondary pulse) effectively cancels out pH-related effects. Double oxidation bands (around + 1.44 V and + 0.69 V on the color plot) were seen in both primary and secondary pulses, whereas only a single band (peak around + 1.32 V) was evident in the primary minus secondary pulse (Fig. 5B). This peak (+ 1.32 V) was then used to recalculate the sensitivity of the CFMs, resulting in a similar quasi-linear curve (R2 value of 0.855) (Fig. 6B). Discussion

This study demonstrates that FSCV and PPV can detect vancomycin concentration changes in real time in vitro, and identifies optimal electrochemical parameters for measurement. These findings establish a technical basis for developing a point-of-care monitoring platform suitable for future clinical use.

Previous cyclic voltammetry (CV) studies have demonstrated that vancomycin exhibits pH-sensitive oxidation peaks, with characteristic peaks observed at approximately + 1.0 V in strongly acidic media (pH 1.0) and at approximately + 0.6 V near physiological pH (pH 7.0) [11]. In the present system, the second oxidation peak at + 0.69 V corresponds closely to the previously reported + 0.6 V peak and likely reflects pH-dependent behavior.

To further evaluate this, Supplementary Fig. 4 compares the sensitivity curves derived from the + 1.44 V and + 0.69 V peaks. Selection of the + 0.69 V peak resulted in reduced sensitivity, as indicated by a decrease in slope from 0.016 to 0.011 (31.3% reduction). The sensitivity was further diminished under PPV mode (from 0.011 to 0.004; 63.6% reduction). These results demonstrate that PPV enables clearer differentiation between pH-dependent and pH-independent oxidation peaks [37]. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, PPV mode is preferable to standard FSCV for differentiating between these two oxidation peaks.

Notably, no significant pH or capacitive contributions were observed for the + 1.44 V peak across all concentrations. The comparable normalized peak anodic currents obtained using PPV and FSCV modes indicate minimal influence from capacitive background currents. Because PPV suppresses non-faradaic charging currents, the similar responses between the two modes support the conclusion that the + 1.44 V peak primarily reflects a faradaic oxidation process.

The observed dependence of oxidation potential on both pH and scan rate suggests a multi-step oxidation mechanism. Feier et al. (2019) proposed that vancomycin oxidation involves the sequential transfer of two electrons and two protons, accompanied by water molecule participation [11]. This mechanism is consistent with the presence of both primary and secondary redox peaks observed in the present study.

Compared with conventional assays such as RIA, HPLC, or LC–MS, FSCV and its variant PPV provide sub-second temporal resolution and high analytical sensitivity [38, 39]. The oxidation peaks at + 1.44 V (FSCV) and + 1.32 V (PPV) may serve as voltammetric fingerprints for vancomycin, enabling rapid identification without complex sample preparation. These attributes indicate potential for future bedside implementation, including monitoring in cerebrospinal fluid or other biological media.

The current in vitro study employed TRIS buffer to replicate physiological condition; however, previous work has demonstrated comparable FSCV performance in human blood [40]. Real-time quantification of vancomycin concentration may improve clinical outcomes, as regular monitoring of serum levels has been linked to reduced mortality in sepsis [41]. Nevertheless, translating CFM measurements from TRIS buffer to human serum or whole blood presents several challenges. In biological matrices, signal reliability may be affected by plasma protein binding, biofouling, and electroactive interfering species.

Vancomycin exhibits moderate plasma protein binding (~ 30–60%), primarily to albumin [42]. Because only the unbound (free) fraction is electrochemically accessible, protein binding may influence the measured signal in biological samples and should be carefully considered in future translational applications. In addition, biofouling resulting from protein adsorption and cellular components may attenuate current responses or alter peak characteristics [25]. The use of FSCV with background subtraction and PPV to suppress non-faradaic currents may help mitigate capacitive contributions and improve signal discrimination in complex matrices [37].

Furthermore, electroactive species present in biological fluids may contribute to background currents. The identification of a pH-independent oxidation peak at + 1.44 V, distinct from the pH-sensitive peak at + 0.69 V, may provide an analytical advantage for differentiating vancomycin signals from matrix-related interference. Nonetheless, further studies in biological samples are necessary to systematically evaluate matrix effects and long-term electrode stability.

A principal limitation of FSCV is the requirement for background subtraction, which precludes direct measurement of absolute concentrations [43]. Although FSCV removes large capacitive currents through background subtraction, quantitative analysis is based on the faradaic peak current obtained after subtraction. In this study, calibration curves were generated in vitro using known vancomycin concentrations, and the resulting linear relationship between peak current and concentration was used for quantification. Because background subtraction eliminates non-faradaic contributions while preserving concentration-dependent faradaic current changes, absolute quantification remains feasible provided that waveform parameters and electrode conditions are carefully controlled.

For future biological applications, matrix-matched calibration or post-experiment calibration procedures will be necessary to account for potential matrix effects and electrode surface variability. Alternatively, integration with systems capable of direct absolute concentration measurements may further enhance quantification accuracy [44]. In the present work, we focused on characterizing the electrochemical signatures of vancomycin and developing optimized signal-processing approaches to improve measurement reliability.

Another limitation is electrode-to-electrode variability and signal stability over time. Variability among CFMs is a well-recognized characteristic of FSCV measurements and may result from differences in fiber geometry, surface microstructure, and electrode conditioning [45]. To mitigate this variability, normalization was performed for each CFM based on the largest current generated by the same electrode [26].

Regarding signal stability, the optimized FSCV waveform demonstrated stable and reproducible responses over approximately eight hours of continuous in vitro experimentation (without background change). Because background subtraction reduces slow capacitive drift, signal stability was maintained throughout the experimental timeframe. Nevertheless, longer-term monitoring in biological matrices may introduce additional variability due to biofouling and matrix effects. Further studies will be required to systematically evaluate long-term stability and inter-electrode reproducibility under clinically relevant conditions.

Future development will focus on hardware and algorithmic approaches to enable basal-level detection. The PPV method, which differentiates analytes based on adsorption properties, offers a pathway for achieving this objective [46]. Additional refinements—such as the use of disposable CFMs to mitigate biofouling [25], improvements in electrode reproducibility, and enhanced reference electrode stability [47]—will further support clinical translation.

Conclusion

FSCV and its PPV variant provide a sensitive and temporally precise method for detecting vancomycin. The identification of reproducible oxidation peaks suggests potential as a rapid, real-time monitoring strategy. Further work should address calibration for absolute quantification and evaluate feasibility in biological matrices to advance toward clinical point-of-care deployment.
