Diagnosing the Properties and Evolutionary Fates of Black Hole and Wolf-Rayet X-ray Binaries as Potential Gravitational Wave Sources for the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA Network
Zi-Yuan Wang, Ying Qin, Georges Meynet, Qing-Zhong Liu, Xin-Wen Shu, Ya-Wen Xue, Liang Yuan, Jun-Qian Li, Kun Jia, Han-Feng Song

Abstract
IC 10 X-1, NGC 300 X-1, and Cyg X-3 represent a unique class of X-ray binaries consisting of a stellar-mass black hole (BH) accreting material from a Wolf-Rayet (WR) star companion. These systems are particularly intriguing due to their short orbital periods (less than 1.5 d), making them promising progenitors of gravitational wave (GW) sources detectable by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) network. With a newly implemented prescription for accretion efficiency in the standard Bondi–Hoyle–Lyttleton (BHL) framework and a corrected treatment of dynamical tides, we present for the first time detailed binary evolution models to diagnose their properties at different evolutionary states and evaluate their fates as potential GW sources detectable by the LVK network. With additional constraints on the observed properties of IC 10 X-1 and NGC 300 X-1, we determine that the upper limit of the BH mass in these systems (IC 10 X-1: M BH ≲ 25 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm BH}\lesssim 25\,M_{\odot} , NGC 300 X-1: M BH ≲ 15 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm BH}\lesssim 15\,M_{\odot} ) is much lower than previously estimated. Both systems are expected to form binary black holes (BBHs) that will merge within a Hubble time, unless the BH in NGC 300 X-1 has a mass of 9 ​ M ⊙ 9\,M_{\odot} , the lower limit estimated in a previous study based on the continuum-fitting method employing a relativistic slim-disc model. For Cyg X-3, we find that the upper limit on the BH spin magnitude cannot exceed 0.6. Interestingly, the WR star in Cyg X-3 is likely to form a lower-mass gap BH, and the resulting binary BH system will merge within the Hubble time.

Wolf–Rayet (WR) stars are hot, luminous, and evolved massive stars characterized by strong emission lines of helium (He), carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O). These spectral features arise from their powerful, high-velocity stellar winds and significant mass-loss rates, typically on the order of
10^{-5}M_{\odot}

Examples include IC 10 X-1 and NGC 300 X-1 in nearby galaxies, as well as Cyg X-3 in the Milky Way (see their main properties in Table

The standard Bondi-Hoyle-Lyttleton (BHL) accretion model

For BH-WR systems, such as IC 10 X-1, NGC 300 X-1, and Cyg X-3, observed in short orbital periods, the corresponding orbital velocities can become significantly high due to the efficient tidal interactions that occur when the orbital period is less than 2.0 d

Methods

II.1
Main physics adopted in
MESA

We performed the detailed binary modeling using the release version
mesa-r15140
of the Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics (
MESA
) stellar evolution code
(
Paxton2011
;
Paxton2013
;
Paxton2015
;
Paxton2018
;
Paxton2019
;
Jermyn2023
)
. Our WR (He) star models were constructed following the methodology outlined in recent studies
(
Fragos2023
;
Hu2023
;
lv2023
;
Qin2024_gap
;
Qin2024_casebb
, i.e.,)
. In this work, we adopted the solar metallicity of
Z_{\odot}=
0.0142 as in
Asplund2009
.

We applied the mixing-length theory (MLT)
(
MLT1958
)
to model convection, using a parameter value of
\alpha_{\rm mlt}=1.93
. The boundaries of the convective zone were determined using the Ledoux criterion, with a step-overshooting parameter of
\alpha_{\rm p}=0.335\,H_{\rm p}
, calibrated based on the observed drop in rotation rates of massive main-sequence stars
(
Brott2011
)
, where
H_{\rm p}
denotes the pressure scale height at the Ledoux boundary. Semiconvection was incorporated into the He star models following
Langer1983
, with an efficiency parameter
\alpha_{\rm sc}=1.0
. We also took into account thermohaline mixing with a free parameter
\alpha_{\rm th}=1.0

(
Kippenhahn1980
)
. For nucleosynthesis calculations, we employed the network
approx21.net
.

We modeled rotational mixing and angular momentum transport as diffusive processes
(
Heger2000
)
, accounting for the combined effects of the Goldreich–Schubert–Fricke instability, Eddington–Sweet circulations, and both secular and dynamical shear mixing. Diffusive element mixing resulting from these processes was incorporated with an efficiency parameter of
f_{\rm c}=1/30
, as proposed by
Chaboyer1992
;
Heger2000
. To account for the sensitivity of the
\mu
-gradient to rotationally induced mixing, we mitigated its impact by reducing
f_{\mu}
to a value of 0.05, following the recommendations of
Heger2000
.

We adopted the mass-loss prescription outlined in
Hu2022
, aligning with the recently updated models of winds from He stars
(
Higgins2021
)
. Additionally, we accounted for rotationally enhanced mass loss following the methodology described in
Heger1998
and
Langer1998
:

\centering\dot{M}(\omega)=\dot{M}(0)\left(\frac{1}{1-\omega/\omega_{\rm crit}}\right)^{\xi},\@add@centering

(1)

where
\omega
represents the angular velocity, and
\omega_{\rm crit}
denotes the critical angular velocity at the stellar surface. The critical angular velocity is defined as
\omega_{\rm crit}^{2}=(1-L/L_{\rm Edd})GM/R^{3}
, where
L
,
M
, and
R
are the star’s total luminosity, mass, and radius, respectively. The parameter
L_{\rm Edd}
is the standard Eddington luminosity (defined using the scattering opacity of pure free electron scattering for completely ionised medium, i.e.
\kappa
= 0.2(1 + X)
\rm cm^{2}

\rm g^{-1}
,
\rm X
= 0 for hydrogen-free envelope of WR stars), and
G
is the gravitational constant. The exponent
\xi=0.43
is adopted
Langer1998
. Notably, we do not include the effects of gravity-darkening, as discussed by
Maeder2000
.

We applied the theory of dynamical tides to helium-rich stars with radiative envelopes, following the framework described by
Zahn1977
. The synchronization timescale was calculated using the prescriptions provided in
Zahn1977
,
Hut1981
, and
Hurley2002
, while the tidal torque coefficient
E_{2}
was adopted from the updated fitting formula presented in
Qin2018
. Given the previously inconsistent implementation of the synchronization timescale
(
Sciarini2024
)
, we refer readers of interest to the details of the new implementation discussed in
Qin2024_gap
.

In our binary modeling, we evolved He stars until their central carbon exhaustion. To calculate the baryonic remnant mass (
M_{\rm bar}
), we adopted the “
delayed
” SN prescription from
Fryer2012
. The gravitational mass (
M_{\rm rem}
) for BHs was computed using Eq. (14) in
Fryer2012
. Additionally, we accounted for the neutrino loss mechanism described by
Zevin2020
. We assumed a maximum NS mass of 2.5
M_{\odot}
, with any object more massive being classified as a BH. We assumed direct collapse for BH formation, and as a result, the newly formed BH is not expected to experience mass loss or a natal kick
(
Belczynski2008
)
.

II.2
Wind accretion in BH-WR binary systems

Wind accretion is a key process governing the evolution of massive X-ray binaries
Lamers1976
;
Friend1982
;
Karino2019
. The average accretion rate onto the compact object can be approximated using the Bondi-Hoyle-Lyttleton (BHL) accretion framework
(
Hoyle1939
;
Bondi1944
;
Bondi1952
)
, expressed as:

\centering\left\langle\dot{M}_{\rm 2A}\right\rangle=\eta_{\rm BHL}\dot{M}_{\rm 1W},\@add@centering

(2)

\centering\eta_{\rm BHL}=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}\left(\frac{GM_{2}}{v^{2}_{\rm w}}\right)^{2}\frac{\alpha_{\rm w}}{2a^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{(1+\omega^{-2})^{3/2}}\right),\@add@centering

(3)

\centering\omega=\frac{v_{\rm w}}{v_{\rm orb}},\@add@centering

(4)

\centering v^{2}_{\rm orb}=\frac{GM_{\rm b}}{a},\@add@centering

(5)

where
e
represents the orbital eccentricity,
a
the semimajor axis,
M_{\rm b}
the total mass of the binary system,
\dot{M}_{\rm 2A}
the accretion rate on to the accretor and
\dot{M}_{\rm 1W}
the wind mass-loss rate of the donor star. The parameter
\alpha_{\rm w}
was constrained to a value of 3/2 in models of wind accretion from a supergiant, developed to explain the barium star
\zeta
Capricorni
(
Boffin1988
)
. The wind velocity (
v_{w}
) is commonly assumed to be proportional to the surface escape velocity, i.e.,

\centering v^{2}_{\rm w}=2\beta_{\rm w}\frac{GM_{1}}{R_{1}},\@add@centering

(6)

where the wind velocity structure parameter,
\beta_{\rm w}
, which characterizes the acceleration profile of the stellar wind, is determined based on the spectral type of the donor star. It was pointed out in
Hurley2002
that the accretor cannot capture all the material lost by the mass-losing companion through stellar winds. As a result, the accretion rate is constrained by the following conditions:

\centering|\dot{M}_{\rm 2A}|\leq 0.8|\dot{M}_{\rm 1W}|.\@add@centering

(7)

Previous studies have shown that wind acceleration plays a crucial role in predicting BHL accretion rates
Lamers1976
;
Friend1982
;
Karino2019
. The standard BHL accretion, however, could become inaccurate when the wind velocity is comparable to or lower than the orbital velocity (
v_{\rm orb}
), resulting in nonphysical accretion efficiencies exceeding unity
Tejeda2025
. To address this, they derived an updated expression for the accretion efficiency in the case of circular orbits, given by:

\centering\eta_{\rm Tejeda24}={\left[\frac{GM_{2}}{a(v_{\rm orb}^{2}+v_{\rm w}^{2})}\right]^{2}}=\left(\frac{q}{1+\omega^{2}}\right)^{2},\@add@centering

(8)

where

\centering q=\frac{M_{2}}{M_{1}+M_{2}}.\@add@centering

(9)

As the wind velocity structure parameter
\beta_{\rm w}
in Eq.
6
for WR stars is poorly constrained, we thus apply a
\beta-
velocity law to calculate the wind velocity at a distance
r
from the stellar surface, following
Lamers1999
. For WR stars, we use the prescription outlined in
Bhattacharya2023
, given by:

\centering v_{\rm w}=v_{0}+(v_{\infty}-v_{0})(1-\frac{R_{1}}{r})^{\beta},\@add@centering

(10)

where
v_{\infty}
is the terminal velocity of WR stars (see
v_{\infty}
values for different systems presented in Table
1
),
R_{1}
is the radius of the WR star, and
\beta
is set to 1.0 for WR stars following
Grafener2005
;
Carpano2007
. Additionally, and
v_{0}
represents the wind velocity at the stellar surface, for which we adopt
v_{0}\sim 0.01v_{\infty}
, as suggested for WR stars by
Carpano2007
. We note that a similar approach to estimating the wind velocity for IC 10 X-1 was also adopted
(
Steiner2016
)
. In this work, we thus use terminal wind velocities (
v_{\infty}
) collected from the literature (see Table
1
) to determine the wind velocities of the three target sources.

The asymptotic behavior of the accretion efficiency (
\eta_{\rm Tejeda24}
) exhibits two distinct limiting regimes. In cases where the wind velocity far exceeds the orbital velocity (
\omega\gg 1
), the accretion efficiency scales as
q^{2}/\omega^{4}
.
Conversely, when the orbital velocity dominates the wind velocity (
\omega\ll 1
), the efficiency becomes independent of
\omega
and scales exclusively with
q^{2}
. The updated formula not only ensures physically consistent accretion efficiencies for any combination of
q
and
\omega
, but it is particularly critical in the regime where
\omega\ll 1
, where the orbital velocity surpasses the wind velocity and the standard BHL accretion model predicts nonphysical results. Additionally, the work
Tejeda2025
emphasized that the modified version of the accretion efficiency requires the ratio of the refill time to the orbital period to be less than 1.0 (see their Section 2.1). Our testing confirms that our calculations, for the parameter space considered in this study, satisfy this condition. For a more detailed discussion, we refer interested readers to
Tejeda2025
. Notably, the predictions of the modified BHL accretion efficiency are better aligned with the outcomes of 3D hydrodynamical simulations reported in
Saladino2019
, compared to the standard model.

For a BH as the accreting object, the corresponding accretion luminosity
L_{\rm X}
is given by the standard expression provided in
Shakura1973
:

\centering L_{\rm X}=\eta_{\rm bol}\epsilon\frac{GM_{2}}{r_{\rm ISCO}}\dot{M}_{\rm acc},\@add@centering

(11)

where
G
is the gravitational constant,
M_{2}
is the mass of the BH, and
r_{\rm ISCO}
is the innermost stable circular orbit
(
Bardeen1972
)
. The parameter
\epsilon
represents the conversion efficiency of gravitational binding energy to radiation associated with disk accretion onto a BH
(
Belczynski2008
;
Wong2014
, with
\epsilon
= 0.5 adopted as in)
. We also adopt a bolometric correction factor
\eta_{\rm bol}
= 0.8 for wind-fed BH systems, as suggested in
Miller2001
. The modified mass accretion rate is given by
\dot{M}_{\rm acc}=\eta_{\rm Tejeda24}\dot{M}_{\rm 1W}
.

We further conduct detailed binary modeling to compare the results for different accretion efficiencies within the standard BHL framework. We test a range of BH-WR binary systems with different initial orbital periods
P_{\rm orb,i}
= 0.13, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 d. For systems with shorter orbital periods, the corresponding orbital velocity
v_{\rm orb}
increases, resulting in a higher ratio of the wind velocity (
v_{\rm w}
) to the orbital velocity given the same initial component mass configuration. We first note that the BHL accretion efficiency is inversely proportional to
v_{\rm w}/v_{\rm orb}
(see Figure
1
). Additionally, a slight discrepancy is observed between the standard BHL accretion efficiency and the modified one, with the latter showing a bifurcated trend for
v_{\rm w}/v_{\rm orb}<1
. This behavior is consistent with the results presented in
Tejeda2025
(see their Figure 3). In Figure
2
, we show the evolution of the X-ray luminosity (
L_{\rm X}
), with a range of
\sim 10^{38}
–
\sim 10^{40}\,\rm erg\,s^{-1}
. Furthermore, a slight difference in
L_{\rm X}
is observed between the standard BHL accretion efficiency and the modified one.

Detailed evolution of wind-fed BH-WR X-ray binaries

III.1
Dynamical tides in WR-BH close binaries

Tidal interactions in close binary systems play a crucial role in the secular evolution of the orbit and the internal angular momentum of the stellar components. The efficiency of dynamical tides in WR-BH close binaries has been systematically tested by
Qin2018
, who found that tides become significant only when the initial orbital periods are shorter than 2.0 d.
Sciarini2024
highlighted inconsistencies in the implementation of dynamical tides, particularly when compared to the framework of
Zahn1977
. A more recent study by
Qin2024_gap
shows that the newly implemented dynamical tides are slightly weaker than previously thought, as illustrated in their Figure A.3 (Appendix A).

With the new implementation of dynamical tides, we first identify the regions of parameter space where tides can become significant. We explore the initial parameter space of WR-BH binaries, including the initial mass, the BH companion mass, and the initial orbital period. Following the approach of
Qin2018
, we use the properties of the He-rich star’s half-way through its central He burning phase to calculate the synchronization timescale,
T_{\rm sync}
. The ratio of the synchronization timescale
T_{\rm sync}
to the lifetime of the core He burning phase
T_{\rm He}
serves as a good estimator of whether tides play a significant role in the system. In Figure
3
, we show the ratio
T_{\rm sync}/T_{\rm He}
as a function of the initial orbital period and the mass of the He-rich star, for two different companion masses (10 and 30
M_{\odot}
). All models of the He-rich stars assume a metallicity of 0.3
Z_{\odot}
(the metallicity of IC 10 X-1). As noted by
Qin2018
, the dependence of
T_{\rm sync}/T_{\rm He}
on metallicity is very weak. We find that tides become important only when the initial orbital period is shorter than 1.0 d (
P_{i}

\lesssim
1.0 d, see the top left panel). This result corroborates earlier findings that the strength of dynamical tides with radiative damping is overestimated when the star is close to synchronization
(
Sciarini2024
)
. Additionally, the tides become slightly stronger as the mass of the BH companion increases (see the panels between the left and right columns). Therefore, it is expected that the resulting BH would have lower spin magnitudes compared to those found in
Qin2018
.

III.3
Disk formation

To determine whether an accretion disk can form around the BH, we adopt the methodology outlined in
Sen2021
, as described below:

\centering\frac{R_{\rm disk}}{R_{\rm ISCO}}>1,\@add@centering

(12)

where
R_{\rm disk}
and
R_{\rm\rm ISCO}
are the circularization radius of the accretion disk and the radius of the innermost stable orbit, respectively. The disk formation criterion can be further expressed as:

\centering\frac{R_{\rm disk}}{R_{\rm ISCO}}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{\eta_{\rm nf}^{2}}{(1+q)^{2}}\left(\frac{v_{\rm orb}}{c}\right)^{-2}\left(1+\frac{v^{2}_{w}}{v^{2}_{\rm orb}}\right)^{-4}\gamma^{-1}_{\rm\pm}>1,\@add@centering

(13)

where
\eta_{\rm nf}
denotes the efficiency of specific angular momentum accretion by the BH,
q
represents the mass ratio (
q=M_{\rm WR}/M_{\rm BH}
), and
\gamma_{\rm\pm}
accounts for the modification of the innermost stable circular orbit radius induced by the BH spin. We adopt the efficiency factor
\eta_{\rm nf}=1/3
derived from detailed hydrodynamical simulations
(
Livio1986
;
Ruffert1999
)
. As
\gamma_{\rm\pm}
varies from 1/6 for a maximally spinning BH with a prograde disk to 3/2 for a maximally spinning BH with a retrograde disk, assuming alignment between the disk and the BH’s angular momentum
(
Ileyk2017
)
. In this work, we adopt the lower limit of
\gamma_{\rm\pm}=1/6
, which corresponds to the minimum values of
R_{\rm disk}/R_{\rm\rm ISCO}
.

In Figure
6
, we show the ratio
R_{\rm disk}/R_{\rm\rm ISCO}
for two specific BH-WR binaries with different initial orbital periods. First, we observe that the ratio is higher for shorter orbits, as it strongly depends on the orbital velocity (see Eq.
13
). Second, this ratio decreases as the binary system evolves. Notably, this ratio remains above unity when the WR star reaches its central helium (
Y_{\rm c}
) depletion, suggesting that an accretion disk can always form under this condition. Using the same parameter space as in Figure
5
, we further highlight the region where an accretion disk can form at the end of the core helium burning phase. As shown in Figure

, an accretion disk can consistently form across the entire WR mass range when the initial orbital period is shorter than approximately 2.0 d.

III.4
Evolution of chemical abundance of WR stars in close binaries

Figure
8
illustrates the chemical abundance evolution of a He star in a close binary system. In massive He stars, the central helium abundance steadily decreases as it is consumed as nuclear fuel. Through the triple-
\alpha
process, helium nuclei fuse to form carbon, which subsequently undergoes
\alpha
-capture to produce oxygen. As a result, the central helium declines while the oxygen abundance rises throughout the core helium-burning phase. The carbon abundance increases initially but later diminishes as it is progressively converted into oxygen. Nitrogen remains scarce in our massive pure He-star models. This is because the absence of hydrogen prevents its replenishment via the CNO cycle. Additionally, any residual nitrogen is rapidly destroyed during helium burning through
\alpha
-capture reactions, ultimately producing oxygen and neon.

During the core helium-burning phase of massive He stars, the surface helium abundance decreases as stellar winds uncover deeper helium-depleted layers whose chemical composition has been changed by the combined effect of nuclear burning, rotational, and convective mixing. At the same time, for the same reasons, carbon and oxygen also become increasingly abundant at the surface. Figure
9
shows the diffusion coefficients for the He star at two evolutionary stages (
left panel:

Y_{\rm c}
= 0.83,
right panel:

Y_{\rm c}
= 0.52). It shows that Eddington–Sweet circulation dominates over other rotation-induced instabilities outside the stellar core and is therefore mainly responsible for the chemical mixing. We note in Figure
8
that during the initial phase (
Y_{\rm c}<0.5
), carbon and oxygen are already more abundant than nitrogen, indicating that some helium-burning products have been mixed to the surface. At the WR stage (
Y_{\rm c}\sim 0.5
), layers depleted in nitrogen are exposed, and the surface abundances of carbon and oxygen increase significantly. The star then enters the WC phase, characterized by a surface composition dominated by material processed exclusively through helium burning.

In Figure
10
, we present the relative number abundance of carbon to helium (C/He) for WR stars with different initial conditions (mass and orbital period) and different evolutionary stages. For WR stars in the middle of the core-helium burning phase (see the left panel), the upper limit of C/He is found to be
\sim\,0.2
. As WR stars are initially more massive, the corresponding stellar winds are stronger, which thus strips off their outer helium-rich layers and exposes their inner, heavily processed regions. The initial orbital period is also found to play a significant role in enhancing the ratio of C/He. This is because tides become stronger for WR stars in an initially shorter orbit, resulting in stronger winds due to rotationally enhanced mass-loss
(
Heger2000
;
Langer1998
)
. By the end of the core-helium burning, more massive WR stars are found to be carbon-rich in the outer layers. But this trend is not present for WR stars with an initial mass below
\sim

20\,M_{\odot}
, where the winds are relatively weaker (see the right panel).

V
IC 10 X-1, NGC 300 X-1, and Cyg X-3 as potential gravitational-wave sources

IC 10 X-1, NGC 300 X-1, and Cyg X-3 are likely immediate progenitors of binary black holes, representing outcomes of classical isolated massive binary evolution. With the initial properties of BH-WR systems at formation now well-constrained, we can advance to predicting their potential evolution and final outcomes using detailed binary modeling.

As previously mentioned, we adopt the “
delayed
” SN prescription
(
Fryer2012
)
to calculate the gravitational mass of the BH. For simplicity, we assume that WR stars evolving up to carbon depletion will soon directly collapse to form BHs without any significant loss of angular momentum. We present the mass and spin magnitude of the resulting BH for the three sources in Table
2
. The chirp mass,
M_{\rm chirp}
, is defined as:

\centering M_{\rm chirp}=\frac{(M_{\rm 1}M_{2})^{3/5}}{(M_{\rm 1}+M_{2})^{1/5}},\@add@centering

(14)

where
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are the masses of the two BHs, respectively. The effective inspiral spin (
\chi_{\rm eff}
), which can be directly constrained by the GW signal, is defined as:

\chi_{\rm eff}=\frac{M_{1}\chi_{1}+M_{2}\chi_{2}}{M_{1}+M_{2}},

(15)

where
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
are the corresponding dimensionless spin parameters of the two BHs, aligned with the orbital angular momentum. Following the formation of double BHs, the emission of gravitational waves gradually shrinks their orbits by removing orbital angular momentum, ultimately driving the merger of the two BHs. To calculate the merger time for these binary BHs, we use the expression provided by
Peters1964
:

T_{\rm merger}=\frac{5}{256}\frac{c^{5}a_{\rm f}^{4}}{G^{3}(M_{1}+M_{2})^{2}M_{\rm r}}T(e),

(16)

where
c
is the speed of light and
M_{r}
is the binary’s reduced mass,
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are the first-born and second-born BHs, respectively, and
a_{\rm f}
is the orbital separation between the two components. For simplicity, we assume that the BHs form in circular orbits, i.e.,
T(e)=1
.

In Figure
18
, we first present
T_{\rm merger}
for IC 10 X-1 (circles) as a function of
M_{\rm chirp}
and
\chi_{\rm eff}
.
T_{\rm merger}
is found to range from approximately
\sim 10^{9.2}
yr to
\sim 10^{10.1}
yr (log(
T_{\rm merger}
[Myr])
\in[\sim 3.20,\sim 4.06]
), which is shorter than Hubble time. With a heavier BH companion mass,
\chi_{\rm eff}
increases for two reasons. First, tidal interactions are slightly more efficient with a more massive companion object, as shown in Figure
3

11
), likely producing less massive BHs with higher spins due to weaker stellar winds. Consequently, the separation between the two BHs just after formation decreases, leading to a shorter
T_{\rm merger}
. Additionally,
M_{\rm chirp}
and
\chi_{\rm eff}
are highly sensitive to the assumed mass of the BH companion. Specifically,

\sim 10.7\,M_{\odot}
to

and

spans from
\sim 0.3
to
\sim 0.6

VI
Discussion

It is important to note that our constraints on the three sources are subject to some uncertainties. In particular, X-ray luminosity plays a crucial role in setting the upper limit on the current BH mass for IC 10 X-1 and NGC 300 X-1, as well as the BH spin magnitude for Cyg X-3. Earlier investigations of O-star donors suggested that, despite high accretion rates, the angular momentum of the accreted wind is generally insufficient to form an accretion disk around the BH
Illarionov1975
;
Hirai2021
;
Sen2021
;
Sen2024
. However, recent studies of close BH-WR binaries indicate that a significant fraction of WR stars can transfer enough wind mass onto the BH to generate X-ray luminosities up to approximately
\rm 10^{39}erg\,s^{-1}
. Moreover, X-ray luminosities exceeding
\rm 10^{37}erg\,s^{-1}
are typically associated with BH-WR systems where an accretion disk can form around the BH
Sen2025
. The X-ray spectrum of IC 10 X-1 has also been found to be strongly disk-dominated
(
Steiner2016
)
. Recent observations of NGC 300 X-1 are consistent with the presence of a wind–Roche lobe overflow accretion disk. In summary, from the observational perspective, the inferred X-ray luminosity is very high (see Table 1), which may suggest the presence of an accretion disk; otherwise, a substantially lower X-ray luminosity would be expected.

In another class of wind-fed HMXBs (Cygnus X-1, LMC X-1, and M33 X-7), the BHs are observed to have extremely high spins, with the donor star being of OB type. A recent study
Xing2025
explored the formation of these systems using the binary population synthesis framework
POSYDON

(
Fragos2023
)
, which builds on
MESA
-based stellar grids at solar metallicity. They concluded that reproducing the observed high BH spins requires assuming highly conservative accretion (i.e., most or all of the mass lost by the donor star is accreted by the companion, rather than being lost from the system), regardless of the specific accretion prescription.

Additionally, the accretion rate is directly influenced by the wind mass-loss rates of WR stars, making it a key source of uncertainty. To account for this, we adopt the standard “
Dutch
” scheme wind prescription
Nugis2000
, and apply a scaling factor of 2/3 to align with the WR wind models
Higgins2021
. However, we recognize that this prescription does not fully account for the complexities of WR winds
(
Sander2020
)
, including clumping, metallicity dependence, and anisotropies, etc. We did not explore the impact of changing the prescription for the WR winds.

The new prescription for accretion efficiency within the standard BHL framework, together with the corrected treatment of dynamical tides, yields only modest quantitative changes. Nevertheless, the resulting modeling framework represents a significant step forward, offering valuable opportunities for future applications.

VII
Conclusions

In this work, we utilize the binary modeling tool
MESA
, incorporating the newly proposed dynamical tides framework by
Sciarini2024
, to assess the tidal efficiency of BH-WR binaries in close orbits. Additionally, we explore the wind accretion process in these systems, implementing the modified efficiency prescription from
Tejeda2025
. Using the observed properties of IC 10 X-1, NGC 300 X-1, and Cyg X-3 collected from the literature, we perform binary evolution calculations to constrain the parameter space of BH–WR systems at formation, thereby enabling a systematic investigation of their evolutionary pathways and their potential as GW sources detectable by the LVK network.

With constraints from the observation, we first find that the BH companion mass of the WR star in IC 10 X-1 should not exceed
25\,M_{\odot}
. Notably, the mass of the BH plays a crucial role in determining the mass range of the WR stars. The accreted mass onto the BH is found to range approximately 2
\times 10^{-4}M_{\odot}
to 1.7
\times 10^{-2}M_{\odot}
, depending on the BH mass. For NGC 300 X-1, the upper limit of the BH mass is constrained to be around
15\,M_{\odot}
, which is significantly lower than the earlier estimates (See Table
1
). The accreted mass onto the BH is less than
0.02\,M_{\odot}
(comparable to IC 10 X-1), which is insufficient to significantly spin up the BH. As the only known BH-WR system in our galaxy, the BH mass in Cyg X-3 is well-constrained, but its spin magnitude remains uncertain. Our findings suggest that the spin magnitude of the BH (
\chi_{1}
) is unlikely to exceed
\sim 0.6
. For convenience, Table

As the initial parameter space of the BH-WR systems for IC 10 X-1, NGC 300 X-1, and Cyg X-3 has been constrained, we further explore their properties as GW sources. First, both IC 10 X-1 (log(
T_{\rm merger}
[Myr])

9\,M_{\odot}
, the system will not merge within the Hubble time. Interestingly, the WR star in Cyg X-3 is predicted to form a BH that falls within the so-called lower-mass gap. For IC 10 X-1, the chirp mass is expected to range from approximately 10.7
M_{\odot}

M_{\odot}
