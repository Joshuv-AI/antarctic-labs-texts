A cortical output channel for perceptual categorization
Authors: Nathan A. Schneider, Michael I. Malina, Ross S. Williamson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perceptual categorization transforms graded sensory inputs into discrete representations that support decision-making. While the auditory cortex is causally important for this process, a specific circuit location where continuous physical inputs are transformed into discrete decision variables remains unknown. Here, we address this by performing longitudinal two-photon imaging of layer 5 (L5) extratelencephalic (ET) neurons alongside L2/3 and L5 intratelencephalic (IT) populations in mice learning a categorization task. With learning, L5 ET neurons underwent pronounced tuning shifts and developed robust categorical responses, whereas L2/3 and L5 IT neurons did not. This transformation is not merely an intrinsic feature of the cell type but is dynamically controlled by task engagement, emerging only during active decision-making. Using a generalized linear model, we confirmed that categorical selectivity in L5 ET neurons reflects genuine sensory encoding rather than being a by-product of choice-driven activity. These findings identify L5 ET neurons as a cortical output channel to categorize stimuli into a decision variable to guide actions.

INTRODUCTION

Categorization is a fundamental aspect of perception, allowing organisms to compress a virtually infinite range of sensory inputs into a finite set of behaviorally meaningful groups (1–6). In audition, sounds with diverse acoustic features are mapped onto discrete perceptual labels that, in turn, guide decisions and actions (6–14). This capacity for auditory categorization is critical for survival, supporting predator avoidance, foraging, and communication (6, 15, 16), and underlies speech comprehension in humans (10). To accomplish categorization, the auditory cortex (ACtx) must integrate and transform diverse spectrotemporal inputs into task-relevant representations. Neurophysiological studies demonstrate that ACtx neurons can represent auditory categories (17–24) but the cell types and circuits involved in forming these representations and leveraging them to guide decisions remain poorly understood.

Complex behaviors emerge from coordinated interactions across distributed brain networks. Understanding auditory categorization therefore requires delineating how ACtx communicates with, and influences, other regions. ACtx is not homogeneous; it is organized into layers containing distinct excitatory cell types with characteristic projection patterns (25–27). Neurons that project to subcortical action selection and motor structures are likely to carry different information than those targeting associative cortical areas involved in higher-order function. Consequently, elucidating ACtx’s contribution to auditory-guided behavior necessitates studying its major projection classes as separate populations. Cortical output is dominated by two broad classes of excitatory projection neurons: intratelencephalic (IT) and extratelencephalic (ET). In ACtx, ET neurons are concentrated in layer 5b (L5b) and send dense projections to the midbrain, thalamus, striatum, and lateral amygdala, whereas IT neurons span L2/3-L6 and project widely to ipsi- and contralateral cortex as well as striatum (28–32). Although both IT and ET neurons are present in L5, the canonical cortical output layer, their largely nonoverlapping projection patterns suggest distinct roles during behavior. This projection rationale has strong anatomical and functional support across multiple sensory and motor cortices (26, 28, 33–37), underscoring the need for a cell type–specific dissection of ACtx output channels during categorization.

Multiple lines of evidence indicate that ACtx L5 neurons play causal roles in auditory-guided behaviors. L5 ET projections to the midbrain and thalamus can rapidly modulate sensory tuning and gain (33–36, 38–43), whereas IT projections to frontal and association cortices shape the representation of auditory features and choice-related signals (44, 45). Selective manipulation of corticostriatal projections, comprising both IT and ET neurons, disrupts task performance (37, 46, 47), and inhibiting either L5 IT or ET neurons during auditory learning slows task acquisition (48). Research outside of ACtx further suggests that projection class strongly constrains function during behavior (49–57). Despite extensive evidence for category-like responses in ACtx, powerful corticofugal influences on subcortical processing, and projection-specific roles within behavior, prior studies have rarely parsed the major L5 output channels during auditory categorization itself. In particular, the respective contributions of IT versus ET neurons to forming categorical representations, committing to a choice, and initiating action remain unresolved (33, 37, 47). These considerations motivate a cell type–specific test of whether and how ACtx L5 IT and ET neurons differentially route category information to decision and motor circuits.

To delineate the contributions of major cortical projection types to auditory category learning, we trained mice on a rate categorization task and performed longitudinal two-photon calcium imaging of L2/3, L5 IT, and L5 ET neurons throughout learning. Across learning, L5 ET neurons, but not L2/3 or L5 IT neurons, exhibited marked tuning shifts, developing strong selectivity for the trained perceptual categories. This categorical selectivity persisted after excluding neurons whose activity was better explained by choice-related variables, indicating genuine stimulus-based encoding. Moreover, on perceptually ambiguous boundary trials, ACtx population activity encoded choice, and the neural state immediately preceding sound onset could predict future choices. Together, these findings implicate the ACtx L5 ET projection system as a specialized cortical output channel that links sensory categorization to choice.

RESULTS

Mice readily learn a head-fixed auditory categorization task

To examine learning-related changes across distinct ACtx cell types, we trained mice on a cortically dependent categorization task previously established in our laboratory (48). Head-fixed mice learned to categorize the rates of sinusoidally amplitude-modulated (sAM) white noise as “slow” or “fast” by licking left or right (Fig. 1A). Mice reported choices within a brief window following sound onset: Correct responses resulted in a small water reward, while incorrect choices were punished with a 5-s timeout before the next trial (Fig. 1B). Category/spout mappings (left/right) were counterbalanced across mice to avoid systematic side biases. Mice were first trained on the easy stimuli (2, 2.8, 22.6, and 32 Hz) until they reached a proficiency threshold of 85% correct across 200 trials, which took mice, on average, 12 sessions (Fig. 1C and fig. S1A). After this initial training, we introduced all nine stimuli (2 to 32 Hz in 0.5 octave steps) and tracked performance across days, grouped into early, middle, and late training epochs (Fig. 1C). Behavioral accuracy on nonboundary trials increased from early to late learning, driven by an improvement in categorizing both easy and hard stimuli (Fig. 1D). Session-wise psychometric fits showed steeper slopes and reduced bias across learning (Fig. 1, E and F), whereas choice bias and lapse rates remained stable (fig. S1, B and C). Together, these results indicate that mice can readily learn this task and continue to improve their ability to categorize stimuli across multiple days of training.

Category learning reshapes L5 ET tuning during active task engagement

We used our categorization task to investigate how distinct ACtx neuronal populations change with category learning. We selectively expressed GCaMP8s in L2/3, L5 IT, and L5 ET neurons using combinatorial viral, tracing, and genetic tools (Fig. 2A). L2/3 neurons were labeled with an adeno-associated virus (AAV) containing a pan-neuronal synapsin promoter injected into ACtx. Labeling of L5 IT cells was accomplished via injection of a Cre-dependent AAV in Tlx3_PL56-Cre mice. L5 ET neurons were targeted through retrograde labeling from the inferior colliculus (IC), a major projection target of ACtx ET neurons. We then performed in vivo two-photon imaging of somatic calcium signals from these three populations in primary ACtx of awake, head-fixed mice (Fig. 2B). Raw calcium signals were deconvolved and then z-scored relative to average prestimulus activity to estimate relative spiking activity (58, 59) (fig. S2).

After the initial training period (once mice reached our proficiency threshold of 85% correct on easy trials; Fig. 1C), we recorded activity during behavioral engagement on each subsequent day of categorical learning (active; Fig. 2, C and D). On the same days and while imaging the same neurons, we also presented identical sounds after retracting the lick spouts (passive) and alternated the starting context (active versus passive) across days to control for changes in motivation and satiety (Fig. 2, C and D). As an additional control, we imaged untrained (naive) mice to assess baseline sAM tuning properties within the three distinct neural populations (Fig. 2D).

Across sessions, neurons exhibited heterogeneous evoked response profiles (onset, sustained, and offset; fig. S3, A to D), but we restricted subsequent analyses to neurons that were responsive within a 500-ms window immediately after sound onset, which captured the peak neural activity and decision-making period (Fig. 2E and fig. S3, E and F). The fraction of onset-responsive neurons varied across cell types and behavioral contexts (fig. S3G). We then generated tuning curves for each neuron by averaging activity within this onset window to assess how stimulus selectivity evolved with learning (Fig. 2E). Visualizing normalized tuning curves suggested a learning-dependent change in L5 ET neurons from early to late training: Responses became more broadly tuned to stimuli within the slow or fast categories (Fig. 2F and fig. S3H). Calculating the number of stimuli that elicited a response for each neuron showed that only L5 ET neurons displayed a significant increase in the fraction of neurons encoding four stimuli, the number of stimuli within a category (Fig. 2G). To confirm that this broader tuning was specific to categorical stimuli, we isolated neurons selectively responsive to all four stimuli within the slow or fast category and confirmed that L5 ET neurons developed robust category-specific tuning, whereas L2/3 and L5 IT neurons did not (Fig. 2H). Notably, this categorical organization emerged only during active task engagement and was absent during passive listening and in naive mice (fig. S3I). These results indicate that L5 ET neurons adjust their sensory tuning in a context-dependent manner to generalize sounds within learned categories during behavior.

Categorical coding emerges selectively in L5 ET neurons

Our tuning curve analyses indicated that L5 ET neurons increasingly assigned stimuli to a learned auditory category. To quantify this category preference and its evolution with learning, we computed a categorical selectivity index (CSI) for each neuron, where values near −1 or +1 indicate a strong preference for the slow or fast category, respectively, and values near 0 indicate no categorical bias (Fig. 3, A and B). Examining CSI distributions across learning revealed minimal change for L2/3 and L5 IT neurons, whereas L5 ET neurons developed a pronounced bimodal distribution late in learning (Fig. 3C and fig. S4A). During passive presentation of the same sounds, categorical selectivity was reduced for all cell types (Fig. 3D and fig. S4B), and untrained mice showed no evidence of category preference (fig. S4C). Thus, across learning, only the L5 ET population dynamically reshaped its responses to encode behaviorally relevant categories, and this organization emerged specifically during active task engagement.

For each imaging session, we quantified population-level categorical selectivity as the session-averaged absolute CSI (|CSI|). Categorical selectivity was highest for L5 ET neurons and increased from early to late learning (fig. S4D). A complementary analysis, fitting a two-component Gaussian mixture to the CSI distribution, also revealed increasing bimodality in L5 ET across learning (fig. S4E). Session-wise categorical selectivity in L5 ET correlated with behavioral performance (Fig. 3E and fig. S4, F and H). For comparison, L2/3 and L5 IT neurons also showed higher categorical selectivity during active versus passive conditions, but their |CSI| values did not change with learning and did not track behavioral performance. Motivated by evidence that similarly tuned neurons form local cortical microcircuits (60–62), we asked whether categorically selective L5 ET neurons were spatially clustered. L5 ET neurons strongly encoding the slow or fast categories (|CSI| > 0.7) were significantly closer to each other than expected by chance (fig. S4G). Together, only the L5 ET population developed categorical selectivity across learning.

Categorical selectivity within the L5 ET population could, in principle, arise via two mechanisms: (i) differential amplification and suppression of preexisting preferences, such that strongly tuned neurons become more responsive, while weakly tuned neurons drop out of the effective coding pool; or (ii) within-cell retuning, whereby individual neurons shift their stimulus preferences to align with the learned categories (fig. S5A). The absence of categorically selective neurons in naive mice (fig. S4C), together with learning-dependent changes in L5 ET tuning curves (Fig. 2, F to H, and fig. S3H), is consistent with the second mechanism. To test this directly, we longitudinally tracked the same L5 ET neurons across all days of training and quantified changes in their tuning and CSI (fig. S5B). Individual L5 ET neurons altered their stimulus preferences to encode the slow and fast categories (fig. S5C), and |CSI| increased across learning (fig. S5D). These effects were not observed during passive listening and were absent in longitudinally matched L2/3 and L5 IT neurons (fig. S5D). These results show that, unlike L2/3 and L5 IT neurons, individual L5 ET neurons dynamically change their activity across learning to reflect learned perceptual categories specifically during active task engagement.

Functional diversity of L5 ET neurons underlies discrimination and categorization

Although many L5 ET neurons develop category-selective responses across learning, mice first learn to discriminate the “easy” slow and fast exemplars (far from the category boundary) before full categorical learning begins (Fig. 1C). This implies the presence of neurons that reliably distinguish slow from fast early in learning. We asked whether the neurons contributing to this early discrimination later acquire categorical selectivity (adapting-population hypothesis) or whether a largely separate set of neurons becomes category selective as learning progresses, while early discriminators remain stable or lose stimulus encoding (separate-population hypothesis; Fig. 4A).

We again analyzed neurons matched longitudinally across learning to address this question (Fig. 4B). We identified single-neuron examples representing multiple longitudinal tuning phenotypes (Fig. 4C). Some neurons displayed discrimination early in learning which then became more categorical late in learning, thus displaying an “adapting” response type. Other neurons showed initially weak stimulus preferences that, over learning, developed strong categorical tuning, suggesting that some category-selective neurons do not generally begin as easy discriminators. We also found neurons that discriminated stimuli early but did not develop categorical selectivity. These neurons either maintained stable tuning or decreased their responses across learning. These single-neuron examples suggest that L5 ET neurons can develop categorical selectivity through both adaptation of responsive neurons and recruitment of separate neurons. We therefore set out to classify neurons based on their discrimination and categorization across learning to determine the proportion of each response type.

We performed single-neuron binary decoding of slow versus fast categories, computed separately using only the easy (far from the category boundary) or hard (near the category boundary) stimuli (fig. S6). We identified early discriminator neurons as those that decoded easy stimuli with high accuracy (≥0.75) in early sessions. These neurons were then labeled as “adapting,” “stable,” or “decreasing” neurons based on their categorical selectivity and decoding accuracy late in learning (see Materials and Methods and Fig. 4D). We also identified “categorical” neurons that were strongly categorical by late learning but began with weaker easy-stimulus decoding. In line with the adapting-population hypothesis, the adapting neurons showed stable decoding accuracies for easy stimuli during behavior, while hard-stimulus accuracies significantly increased across learning (Fig. 4E, top). At the same time, we observed support for the separate-population hypothesis with categorical neurons increasing decoding accuracy across learning while stable discriminators remained fixed and decreasing neurons dropped their decoding accuracy across learning. During passive listening, adapting, stable, and decreasing neurons still decoded easy stimuli above chance (Fig. 4E, bottom), suggesting that they encode stimulus identity robustly across behavioral contexts, whereas categorical neurons performed near chance without behavioral engagement, reinforcing the context dependence of categorical coding. Together, these results indicate that both proposed mechanisms contribute to the emergence of categorical selectivity and that the L5 ET population comprises at least four longitudinally defined subgroups, highlighting the diversity of behaviorally relevant responses in ACtx L5 ET neurons.

ACtx encodes both stimulus and choice

On correct, categorizable trials, the stimulus category (slow versus fast) is perfectly coupled to choice (left versus right lick), creating a potential confound between stimulus- and choice-related activity. Prior studies have reported robust choice encoding during auditory discrimination in ACtx and, more generally, widespread modulation by movement and internal state (11, 44, 63–74). To characterize choice (lick-related) signals in our data, we first examined L5 ET activity around spontaneous licking in trained mice during silence and observed modulation that preceded lick onset (fig. S7, A to C). To test whether this effect depends on learning, we imaged a separate cohort performing a “licking-only” paradigm without sounds and again observed lick-locked modulation in ACtx L5 ET neurons (fig. S7, D to F), consistent with prior reports (64, 71). These observations indicate that choice/movement signals are present in ACtx and must be explicitly examined when assessing categorical selectivity.

We next separated neurons according to whether their activity was better explained by stimulus-related or choice-related variables using a generalized linear model (GLM) (75–78). The design matrix included time-lagged predictors for stimulus identity and choice direction, with the model leveraging the temporal offset between sound onset and lick, the natural dissociation provided by incorrect trials, and the perceptually ambiguous boundary stimulus to disentangle sensory from decision contributions. We fit cross-validated models to z-scored deconvolved calcium activity (see Materials and Methods, Fig. 5A, and fig. S8, A and B). We then compared this full model fit to cross-validated partial models containing shuffled predictors for stimulus or choice and calculated the unique explained variance (ΔR2; Fig. 5B and fig. S8C). Any neurons that did not have a unique explained variance for stimulus or choice above 0.005 were considered to have a poor GLM fit and were excluded from analysis. We then calculated a selectivity index (SI) to determine the relative strength of stimulus and choice encoding for each neuron (Fig. 5C and fig. S8D). Neurons with SI values greater than 0.2 were labeled “stimulus neurons,” those with SI values less than −0.2 were “choice neurons,” and those with SI values between −0.2 and 0.2 were considered “mixed neurons.” These thresholds were selected to maximize the number of stimulus and choice neurons in each session while ensuring less mixing between the two encoding types. All three cell types contained stimulus, choice, and mixed neurons, and the proportions of these classes were largely stable across learning (fig. S8E). This emphasizes that ACtx populations multiplex sensory- and choice-related signals as mice perform the categorization task.

To investigate the types of responses within these functional groups, we performed unsupervised clustering of averaged stimulus and choice responses (Fig. 5D). Stimulus neurons exhibited heterogeneous response properties, including monotonic stimulus tuning, nonselective activity, and even categorical responses (Fig. 5E). Notably, the averaged stimulus responses were consistently higher than the averaged choice responses, reinforcing that these neurons encode stimulus properties stronger than choices. In contrast, clustering choice neurons revealed two clear groups corresponding to the two choice directions and averaged choice responses were equivalent or higher than the stimulus responses (Fig. 5F and fig. S9, A and B). Mixed neurons also fell neatly into two groups (fig. S9, C and D). Since some L5 ET stimulus neuron clusters look similar to our early discriminator neurons defined by single-neuron decoding, we investigated the relationship between these GLM-defined functional groups and our L5 ET–matched neurons (fig. S6D). Even within our longitudinally defined groups, we observed heterogeneity of stimulus, mixed, and choice neurons, emphasizing that these two metrics are complementary but distinct (see Discussion). Together, these analyses identify functionally defined subpopulations during behavior: choice neurons, which robustly encode the mouse’s left/right decision, and stimulus neurons, which carry diverse sensory-related signals.

Categorical selectivity develops primarily within L5 ET stimulus neurons, not choice neurons

We wondered whether the learning-related increase in categorical selectivity observed in L5 ET neurons reflects genuine sensory encoding or a by-product of choice-related activity. Having classified neurons on a per-session basis as either stimulus or choice neurons with the GLM, we recomputed CSI within each subgroup. Restricting the analysis to stimulus neurons revealed that L5 ET showed a pronounced increase in categorical selectivity across learning and session-averaged |CSI| closely tracked behavioral performance (Fig. 5, G to I). L5 ET choice neurons also showed a learning-dependent increase in categorical selectivity but notably did not correlate with behavior (Fig. 5, J to L). This suggests that behavioral improvements in categorization are linked to changes in stimulus neurons rather than choice neurons. As before, categorical selectivity was markedly reduced during passive listening for both stimulus and choice neurons, and neither L2/3 nor L5 IT neurons exhibited learning-related changes (Fig. 5, H and K, and fig. S10, A to F). These results indicate that the emergence of categorical coding in ACtx arises within the stimulus-driven subset of L5 ET neurons and is not solely a by-product of movement or choice signals.

We next asked how each group represents category identity and behavioral choice over time. On the basis of our clustering results, we predicted that choice neurons should primarily encode choice and reflect category only on correct trials, whereas stimulus neurons would contain more information about categories than choices. It is also possible that these GLM-classified neurons carry information about variables they were not selected for, which we can directly test by decoding both category and choice from each subgroup. We therefore trained a linear classifier to decode category or choice from population activity using all nonboundary trials (correct and incorrect; Fig. 6 and fig. S11). Stimulus neurons supported robust decoding of both variables, exhibiting staggered temporal profiles (Fig. 6A). L5 ET populations yielded the highest decoding accuracies and strongest correlations to performance relative to L2/3 and L5 IT populations (Fig. 6, B and C, and fig. S12A). L2/3 and L5 IT stimulus neurons were significantly better at decoding category over choice, whereas L5 ET stimulus neurons decoded both with comparable fidelity (Fig. 6D). These findings indicate that stimulus-driven populations jointly encode both category and choice, with L5 ET stimulus neurons particularly well positioned to transform sensory information into a decision signal (see Discussion).

Choice neurons robustly encoded behavioral choice: Choice decoder accuracy ramped before the first lick and peaked around the decision (Fig. 6E). By contrast, category decoding from choice neurons was weaker and temporally diffuse (Fig. 6E and fig. S11B). Although this category decoding increased across learning and correlated with behavioral performance (Fig. 6, F and G), this pattern is consistent with improving alignment between category and choice as training progresses rather than with the emergence of genuine category signals in choice neurons. Supporting this interpretation, choice-decoder accuracy did not correlate with performance (Fig. 6F and fig. S12B). Across all three cell types, choice neurons did not encode category identity nearly as strongly as choice (Fig. 6H), confirming that they primarily participate in decision planning/execution rather than representing stimulus categories.

ACtx encodes choice history under sensory ambiguity

The finding that L5 ET stimulus neurons decode upcoming choice and category (Fig. 6D) raises the question of whether this choice signal is independently encoded or simply reflects the perfect correlation between category and choice on categorizable trials. To directly test whether the choice signal in L5 ET stimulus neurons reflects independent encoding or correlation with category, we included an ambiguous boundary stimulus (8 Hz) that carried no categorical evidence and was pseudo-randomly paired with left or right reward, prompting mice to make different choices to the same sound. On these boundary trials, any left-right neural separation reflects choice-related activity rather than stimulus category. State-space visualizations with principal components analysis (PCA) revealed that L5 ET population trajectories diverged for left versus right choices despite identical sensory input, with divergence peaking slightly before the decision (Fig. 7A). To quantify this effect, we calculated the Euclidean distance between the average lick-aligned left and right choice trajectories and found reliable choice separation in all three cell types, with the strongest effect in L5 ET (Fig. 7, B and C). This separation was less evident in stimulus neurons compared to mixed- and choice-classified subsets (fig. S13, A to F), consistent with stimulus neurons primarily encoding category rather than the decision; the choice information they appear to carry on categorizable trials (Fig. 6D) is likely inherited from the tight category-choice correlation, rather than reflecting independent choice encoding. Thus, within L5 ET populations, mixed and choice neurons carry robust choice signals even when sensory evidence is held constant.

We next investigated how mice made decisions on these ambiguous trials. Prior work shows that recent actions can bias subsequent decisions toward repetition, especially in the presence of uncertain stimuli (Fig. 7D) (8, 48, 79–84). Consistent with this, mice were more likely to repeat the previous trial’s choice on boundary trials, leading to upcoming choice predictions above chance (Fig. 7E). This suggests that when sensory evidence is uninformative, an internal representation of the prior decision biases the next. Although choice history signals are well established in higher-order cortices (8, 79, 83, 85–88), they may also be present in ACtx. Supporting this possibility, stimulus-aligned decoding showed that L5 ET populations carried some choice information before sound onset (Fig. 6A and fig. S11, B and D). Because trial onset was unpredictable and stimuli were pseudo-randomized, anticipatory preparation should be minimal; therefore, prestimulus choice-predictive activity in ACtx likely reflects an internal representation of the previous decision and/or top-down input conveying choice history.

To test whether ACtx carries choice history information, we asked whether prestimulus population activity predicts choices on boundary trials. We first defined left- and right-choice neural subspaces by computing a low-dimensional population space (PCA) from activity immediately before lick onset on correct, categorizable trials and then taking the mean (centroid) for each choice (Fig. 7F). These centroids provide canonical choice-linked population states. For each boundary trial, we compared the prestimulus population activity to the two centroids, predicted the upcoming choice based on whichever centroid was closer, and compared this to the true future choice (Fig. 7G). This simple nearest-centroid classifier yielded significantly above-chance prediction of left versus right choices across all three cell types (Fig. 7H and fig. S13G), supporting the view that choice-related activity is present in ACtx before stimulus onset on ambiguous trials. These results suggest that a choice history–dependent bias in the initial cortical state can tilt the population toward one of the choice-linked states when sensory evidence is uninformative.

DISCUSSION

Auditory categorization allows stimuli with different acoustic properties to be perceptually grouped together to guide behavior. Prior studies have implicated ACtx as a hub for categorization (17–24), but these past studies have lacked the cell type specificity required to truly link structure to function. Theoretical work has suggested that distinct neuron classes within local circuits can differentially compute behavioral information, underscoring the need for cell type–specific approaches (89, 90). To address this gap in knowledge, we used in vivo two-photon calcium imaging to record the activity of distinct projection neuron types while mice learned an auditory categorization task. We found that L5 ET neurons developed categorical representations of the trained auditory stimuli (Fig. 3). Notably, this categorical encoding was context dependent: It was present during active task engagement but absent during passive listening in the same neurons on the same day. This indicates that behavioral context is required for categorical coding. Furthermore, the emergence of categorical selectivity was a learned effect that developed across training and was absent in untrained mice. By restricting our analysis to GLM-defined stimulus neurons, we confirmed that the development of categorical selectivity in L5 ET neurons was driven by stimulus-specific encoding rather than a confound of licking behavior (Fig. 5). In contrast, neither L2/3 nor L5 IT neurons displayed strong categorical selectivity or learning-dependent changes in categorical tuning. Together, our results demonstrate that ACtx L5 ET projection neurons play a critical and specific role in forming categorical representations during an auditory-guided behavior.

Studies have shown that stimulus discrimination can be shaped even without explicit reinforcement. Passive exposure to training stimuli before the start of behavior increases the rate of learning (91, 92). Repeated stimulus presentations over many days can also produce comparable levels of neural discriminability between trained and passively exposed mice (92). These examples of unsupervised learning demonstrate cortical plasticity following repeated passive exposure to stimuli. In our paradigm, we also passively expose mice to the trained stimuli on each day of behavior, meaning that our learning-dependent effects could be driven, at least partially, by unsupervised learning from repeated passive presentation. However, our within-session comparison of active and passive listening blocks in the same neurons argues against passive exposure being the primary driver: If our findings were primarily driven by repeated passive exposure, then we would expect categorical selectivity to be preserved during passive listening within the same session, which it is not (Fig. 3D and fig. S4B). Dedicated experiments comparing trained mice to passively exposed cohorts will be needed to fully quantify the contribution of unsupervised plasticity to the categorical code we describe.

Previous studies recording from ACtx L2/3 neurons during categorization behavior reported enhanced responses for stimuli near the category boundary (20, 93). In our data, by contrast, very few neurons of any subtype exhibited preferential tuning to modulation rates near the boundary (5.7 to 11.3 Hz; Fig. 2F). Prior work predominantly used pure tone stimuli, whereas we used sAM noise. Pure tone training can lead to frequency-specific map expansion that sharpens discrimination around behaviorally relevant frequencies (94–96), a mechanism that could plausibly enhance representations near task-relevant frequencies, including the boundary, in tone-based tasks. By contrast, amplitude-modulated (AM) rate lacks a reliable, large-scale periodotopic map in rodent ACtx, with representations that are generally distributed or field dependent (97), making boundary amplification less likely. Consistent with this interpretation, training the same animals on both paradigms revealed boundary enhancement for tone categorization but not for AM noise categorization (19). Together, these observations suggest that temporally modulated stimuli such as sAM noise recruit plasticity mechanisms distinct from tonotopic map expansion, yielding categorical responses without boundary enhancement.

In addition to categorical responses, we investigated how these cell types encoded choice. Responses related to behavioral choice have been observed in multiple primary sensory cortices within both superficial and deep layers (44, 63–66, 68–70, 78, 98–100). Consistent with this, we found robust choice encoding across all three cell types, with choice signals being most pronounced in L5 IT and L5 ET neurons (Fig. 6). To ensure that these choice signals were not simply a by-product of the correlation between stimulus category and choice (since mice directionally licked to report their decision), we examined neural activity at the category boundary where stimuli were perceptually ambiguous. Even when the same ambiguous sound was presented, ACtx activity differentiated left versus right choices, with L5 ET neurons showing the strongest representation of the upcoming choice (Fig. 7). That is, ACtx (especially L5 ET cells) encoded the mouse’s decision independently of auditory category identity. We also found that mice tend to repeat their prior choice when presented with an ambiguous stimulus. This behavioral bias suggests that previous choices influence future decisions, a phenomenon often referred to as choice history bias. Frontal and parietal association areas have been implicated in mediating these choice history biases (8, 79, 83, 86–88, 101). In particular, the posterior parietal cortex (PPC) has direct projections to ACtx and is known to influence auditory decisions and biases (8, 101, 102). In line with this, we found that choice history influenced baseline (prestimulus) activity in ACtx: The neural activity immediately before an ambiguous sound could predict the ensuing choice on that trial with above-chance accuracy. Although choice history signals may also arrive through subcortical pathways, areas such as PPC are well positioned to convey these signals to ACtx, biasing ACtx activity and thereby biasing decisions when sensory information alone is insufficient to confidently categorize the stimulus. Together, our findings show that ACtx maintains distinct representations of category and choice, likely acting as an integrative hub where feedforward sensory evidence converges with nonsensory decision-related signals.

Our results suggest that L5 ET neurons play a more prominent role in auditory categorization than either L2/3 or L5 IT neurons. Several lines of evidence help explain this distinction. First, L5 ET neurons are thick-tufted pyramidal cells with apical dendrites that extend to L1, making them morphologically well situated to integrate inputs across the cortical column (26, 31, 103–105). Circuit mapping has shown that they receive synaptic inputs from multiple cortical areas and direct thalamic innervation (106–110), allowing them to combine bottom-up auditory information with top-down contextual signals. Second, L5 ET neurons project subcortically to decision- and reinforcement-related structures, including the IC and striatum (111–121). Notably, IC neurons in bats exhibit categorical responses to vocalizations (122), and corticocollicular feedback is required for learning-related plasticity in rodents (118, 119). Thus, L5 ET outputs are positioned not only to convey category information but also to shape subcortical circuits during learning. Third, L5 ET neurons have intrinsic properties that enhance their output efficacy. They fire at higher rates than other excitatory neurons (28, 123–125), which could drive plasticity in subcortical targets. Together, these morphological, anatomical, and physiological features may help explain why L5 ET neurons, more than L2/3 or L5 IT, develop categorical representations and play a privileged role in shaping auditory-guided behavior.

Our cell type–specific results provide a candidate mechanistic basis for prior causal evidence linking cortical outputs to task performance (48). Silencing ACtx altered decision strategies and impaired performance across learning, and inhibition of either L5 IT or L5 ET neurons significantly slowed learning while sparing expert performance (48). The categorical code that emerged specifically in L5 ET populations offers a plausible mechanistic basis, where disruption of these neurons would impede the formation and subsequent dissemination of categorical representations to subcortical targets. In parallel, the choice encoding observed in L5 IT neurons could propagate across cortical areas, contributing to the integration of task context, rule information, and history-dependent feedback during associative learning. Thus, both projection pathways make distinct yet complementary contributions; perturbing either is sufficient to degrade learning, even if moment-to-moment performance in experts remains relatively preserved.

We observed substantial heterogeneity within each neuronal class, and the SI from our GLM approach highlights the continuum of responses to stimuli and choices (Fig. 5C and fig. S8D). In all three cell types, a subset of stimulus neurons responded preferentially to the sounds themselves (with various tuning preferences) and carried information about stimulus identity or category. Another subset of choice neurons did not necessarily respond to the sound itself but showed activity aligned to decisions (e.g., ramping up activity when the mouse was about to lick left versus right). A third group of mixed neurons displayed activity that was comparably explained by stimulus and choice predictors, emphasizing the inherent coupling of these two behavioral variables. While we defined these three functional subgroups with the express purpose of disambiguating category from choice, it is worth noting that these are not the only groups that could exist. Other studies have defined four or more functional classes within L2/3 or L5 ET neurons in the ACtx of behaving mice (64, 126). These papers identify neurons responding to licks, inhibited by sound, preferentially tuned to passive stimuli, or encoding reward prediction, to name a few examples. Our GLM approach focused only on enhanced responses near the sound and choice onset during active behavior and thus would not capture any of these dynamics. Therefore, while we labeled neurons as either stimulus, choice, or mixed (or deemed them nonresponsive), this is an incomplete picture of the complexity of ACtx responses within a behavioral task.

L5 ET stimulus neurons developed strong categorical selectivity across learning, reinforcing that the emergence of categorical tuning was a genuine sensory representation preferentially expressed in this cell type. The stimulus neurons in L2/3 and L5 IT, by contrast, often remained more tied to the physical features of sounds and did not show a clear grouping by category even after learning. We found that L5 ET stimulus neurons were able to decode both the category and the eventual choice with high fidelity from their activity. However, on boundary trials where category was held constant, stimulus neurons showed less choice-aligned activity than choice or mixed neurons (Fig. 7), suggesting that the choice information they carry on standard trials is largely inherited from the tight category-choice correlation rather than reflecting independent choice encoding. In contrast to stimulus neurons, choice neurons seemed to primarily reflect the decision process or motor plan itself, especially in L5 IT and ET (Fig. 6). Their categorical activity did not correlate with behavioral performance, suggesting that these neurons did not drive improved perceptual groupings.

Despite the clear differences we observed between stimulus and choice neurons in our task, the origin of these functional subgroups remains unclear. One plausible explanation is that they correspond to differences in anatomical subcircuits even within the same genetic cell type. For example, recent work in motor cortex demonstrated that L5 ET neurons with different projection targets have distinct roles in movement control (127). However, teasing apart these differences in ACtx is challenging because many L5 ET neurons send collateral projections to multiple downstream targets (28, 37). Another possibility is that stimulus and choice neurons differ in the inputs they receive. Notably, both stimulus and choice neurons in our data changed their activity between passive and active sessions, indicating that both groups are modulated by behavioral context rather than being purely feedforward. These differences could arise through top-down modulation from several different structures encoding task rules, expectations, or motor plans (8, 73, 128–132), and these regions might differentially innervate each functional subgroup. Thalamus and IC also carry nonauditory information and could contribute to the context modulation or differentiation of stimulus and choice neurons in ACtx (113, 115, 116, 133, 134). These potential differences in functional connectivity could explain how neurons within the same cell type develop distinct and specialized roles in behavior.

In addition to the functional groups defined by the GLM on a session-by-session basis, we also defined subgroups of L5 ET neurons based on their longitudinal encoding. Neurons that accurately discriminated easy stimuli in early learning either maintained their tuning (stable), became more categorical (adapting), or lost stimulus responses (decreasing). Furthermore, we identified a separate population of neurons that became categorical late in learning despite showing weak discrimination early in learning. We compared these subgroups to the GLM results and found diverse mapping between the two measures, but a few notable trends emerged. Stable neurons were most likely to be labeled as stimulus neurons, reinforcing their role in stimulus discrimination. In addition, decreasing and categorical neurons were more likely to be excluded from the GLM due to poor fitting, which can be explained by categorical neurons being weakly tuned early in learning and decreasing neurons losing responses late in learning. While these two methods of dividing neurons accomplish different goals, there is at least some alignment between the longitudinal classifications and GLM functional groups.

Most analyses in this paper used deconvolved calcium activity, and we used the same deconvolution parameters across all sessions, but calcium dynamics can differ across cell types. Although GCaMP8s is better at single–action potential detection than earlier GCaMP variants, this has only been validated with L2/3 neural activity (135, 136). Simultaneous electrophysiological and two-photon recordings from L5 neurons expressing GCaMP6s suggest that calcium activity does not reliably capture spiking activity below 5 Hz (137). To date, no study has investigated how well GCaMP8 variants capture L5 neural activity. As a result, we may be undersampling the true activity of L5 IT and ET neurons or primarily capturing bursting activity. While we used a GLM to account for choice/lick-related activity, other movements also influence ACtx activity (71–74). We did not explicitly model additional movement-related variables (e.g., facial or body movements), which could have provided better explanatory power. Accounting for these variables would likely not change our results, as uninstructed movements should not change systematically across learning or differ across our cell type groups. In addition, our task design imposed a very short timing gap between sound onset and the required behavioral response. As a result, mice often made their decision while the sound was still playing (fig. S3E), making it intrinsically difficult to temporally separate sensory responses from decision- or motor-related activity. We mitigated this using analyses to separate neurons based on whether their activity was better explained by stimulus or choice, but future studies implementing reversal learning or delayed-response behaviors could more cleanly dissociate these two variables. Our study focused on changes occurring from the point when the mice already understood the basic discrimination (i.e., after initial behavioral shaping on easy stimuli) through to expert performance on the fine categorization. We did not capture the earliest phase of learning when mice first learned to associate sounds with actions and rewards. Prior work has shown that animals can acquire knowledge of task contingencies quite rapidly (sometimes within a session), but the expression of that knowledge in behavior can be delayed or gradual (126, 138). It is possible that L2/3 or L5 IT neurons play a crucial role in this rapid learning that we did not capture here. They may also show learning-dependent changes in dimensions of activity we did not analyze. Last, the present study is descriptive rather than mechanistic; identifying which input pathways and synaptic mechanisms drive the L5 ET-specific tuning shift will require future work combining input-specific optogenetics, monosynaptic tracing, and synaptic physiology.

Auditory categorization facilitates the rapid transduction of sensory stimuli into discrete behavioral decisions. Our findings show that L5 ET neurons play a key role in this process by developing categorical selectivity across learning in a context-dependent manner. Moreover, ACtx contains choice information and exhibits prestimulus biases that predict upcoming choices on ambiguous trials. These features position ACtx to route behaviorally relevant signals to downstream targets during sensory-guided behavior and illustrate how primary sensory cortices are modulated by task engagement. Together, our results clarify the differential contributions of cortical projection neuron classes and underscore the importance of ACtx in perceptual categorization and choice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice

All procedures were approved by the University of Pittsburgh Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol no. 19065155) and followed the guidelines established by the National Institutes of Health for the care and use of laboratory animals. All mice were aged 6 to 10 weeks at the start of procedures, and both male and female mice were included. A total of 21 wild-type C57Bl/6J mice (The Jackson Laboratory) were used for the L2/3 and L5 ET groups, and 9 Tlx3-Cre mice [B6.FVB(Cg)-Tg(Tlx3-Cre)PL56Gsat/Mmucd, Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Centers (MMRRC)], which selectively express Cre recombinase in L5 IT cortical neurons, were used for the L5 IT group. All mice were housed under a reversed 12-hour light/12-hour dark periodic light cycle and all experiments were conducted during the dark cycle. Mice were acclimated to head fixation for 30 min per day for 3 to 5 consecutive days before the start of behavioral or imaging experiments.

Surgical procedures

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (5%) and placed in a stereotaxic plane (Kopf model 1900). A surgical plane of anesthesia was maintained throughout the procedure via continuous delivery of isoflurane (1 to 2%) in oxygen. Mice lay atop a homeothermic blanket system (FHC) that maintained core body temperature at ∼36.5°C. The surgical area was shaved and cleaned with iodine and isopropyl alcohol before being numbed with lidocaine (5 mg/ml). Following all surgical procedures, antibiotic ointment was applied to wound margins, and an analgesic was administered (carprofen, 5 mg/kg). Mice had ad libitum access to a carprofen-laced MediGel (Clear H2O) for 3 days postsurgery.

Virus-mediated gene delivery

For access to L2/3 and L5 IT populations, a small incision was made on the right side of the scalp, the temporalis muscle was retracted, and two burr holes were made above ACtx lateral to the temporal ridge and ∼1 and 2 mm rostral to the lambdoid suture. A glass pipette attached to a programmable injector (Nanoject III, Drummond) was lowered 500 μm below the cortical surface and used to deliver either a nonspecific GCaMP8s virus [pGP-AAV-syn-jGCaMP8s-WPRE, Addgene; titer, 2 × 1012 to 7 × 1012 genome copies (gc)/ml] in wild-type mice to label L2/3 neurons or a Cre-dependent GCaMP8s virus (pGP-AAV-syn-FLEX-jGCaMP8s-WPRE, Addgene; titer, 4 × 1012 to 5 × 1012 gc/ml) in Tlx3-Cre mice to label L5 IT neurons. A total of 250 nl was injected into each burr hole at a rate of 10 to 15 nl/min (total of 500 nl). The pipette was left in place for 5 to 10 min following each injection to allow for adequate viral diffusion.

L5 ET neurons were labeled by retrograde injection into the IC. An incision was made along the midline, the skull was leveled, and a burr hole was made over right IC at coordinates 5.0 mm posterior and 0.9 to 1.0 mm lateral to bregma. We injected 300 nl of a retrograde GCaMP virus (pGP-AAV-syn-jGCaMP8s-WPRE, Addgene, or AAV-hSyn1-GCaMP6s-P2A-nls-dTomato, Addgene; titer, 5 × 1012 to 9 × 1012 gc/ml) in wild-type mice at two depths (800 and 300 μm below the cortical surface) at a rate of 10 to 15 nl/min (total of 600 nl). The pipette was left in place for 5 to 10 min following each injection to allow for adequate viral diffusion.

Headplate and cranial window implant

Cranial windows were created by stacking three round-glass coverslips (3, 3, and 4 mm, #1 thickness, Warner Instruments), which were etched with piranha solution and then bonded with transparent, ultraviolet-cured adhesive (Norland Products, Warner Instruments). The skin and periosteum above the dorsal surface of the skull was removed. To ensure strong adhesion of the dental cement, the skull surface was cleaned, dried, and treated with etchant (C&B Metabond), followed by 70% ethanol. A custom headplate (iMaterialize) (139) was affixed to the skull using dental cement (C&B Metabond). A 3-mm circular craniotomy was made over right ACtx, and the cranial window was placed in the craniotomy. An airtight seal was achieved using Kwik-Sil (World Precision Instruments) around the window edges before cementing it in place. Vetbond (3M) was applied to secure the surrounding tissue to the dental cement. Implants were allowed to heal for at least 5 days before the start of experiments.

Histological processing and anatomy

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane before transcardial perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline. Brains were extracted and stored in 4% paraformaldehyde for 12 hours before transferring to cryoprotectant (30% sucrose) until sectioning. Sections (50 μm) were cut using a cryostat (CM1950, Leica), mounted on glass slides, and coverslipped (VECTASHIELD, Vector Laboratories). Images were obtained with an epifluorescence microscope (Thunder Imager Tissue, Leica) and analyzed using Fiji.

Acoustic stimulation

Stimuli were generated with a 24-bit digital-to-analog converter (National Instruments model PXI-4461) using custom scripts written in MATLAB (MathWorks) and LabVIEW (National Instruments). Acoustic stimuli were delivered via a free-field speaker (PUI Audio) facing the left ear and calibrated to accurately determine the output voltage required to achieve the desired sound level using a free-field prepolarized microphone (377C01, PCB Piezotronics). sAM broadband noise bursts (0 to 50 kHz, 1-s duration, 5-ms cosine on/off ramps) were presented at 70-dB sound pressure level with 100% modulation depth, spanning 2- to 32-Hz modulation frequency in 0.5 octave increments. For passive listening experiments, a 3-s intertrial interval (ITI) separated successive sound presentations. Each stimulus was delivered in a pseudo-random order within a block, with 15 to 20 blocks per session.

Mouse behavior

Before behavioral training, mice were water restricted for 2 to 3 days and provided with a daily water allotment equal to 4% of their ad libitum body weight. During training, approximately half of this water was earned through task performance, with remainder supplemented at the end of each session. Most mice stabilized at ∼80% of their ad libitum weight, and additional water was provided as needed to maintain this weight.

Behavioral training

Mice were trained as previously described (48). Initially, mice were trained to lick a single spout in response to either a 2- or 32-Hz sAM noise burst. The response window was 200 to 1500 ms after sound onset (Fig. 1B), and early licks were not punished. Once mice reliably licked within the response window on 10 consecutive trials, both spouts were introduced. Mice were then trained to discriminate the easy stimuli (2 and 2.8 Hz versus 22.6 and 32 Hz) by licking either the left or right spout. Choices were registered as the first lick within the response window. The pairing of left/right spouts with slow/fast stimuli was counterbalanced across mice. Correct responses were rewarded with a 2-μl water drop, while incorrect responses resulted in a 5-s timeout. The ITI was randomly drawn from an exponential distribution ranging from 6 to 9 s. If mice developed a spout bias, defined as selecting the same spout on ≥6 consecutive trials, then bias correction was manually implemented by presenting only the opposite-choice stimulus until mice responded correctly for three consecutive trials. Mice that failed to achieve >60% correct after 8 days of training were removed from the study.

Once mice reached a proficiency threshold of 85% correct across 200 trials, they advanced to the categorization task. In this stage, mice categorized nine sAM noise bursts (2 to 32 Hz in 0.5 octave steps) as either slow (2 to 5.7 Hz) or fast (11.3 to 32 Hz) by licking the corresponding spout. The task also included a category boundary stimulus (8 Hz), which was neither slow nor fast. This boundary stimulus was presented twice within each block: once paired to the left and once to the right. Mice performed the full categorization task for 10 to 18 days while neural activity was recorded. At the start of each day, mice completed 20 to 40 easy trials to confirm that performance remained above the 85% proficiency threshold and that no response biases had emerged. If necessary, then debiasing was performed until performance on easy trials returned to ≥threshold. Debiasing was never applied to hard or boundary trials. Mice that dropped below the proficiency threshold on over half of all categorical sessions were excluded from the study.

Licking-only task

Water-restricted mice were required to lick either the left or right spout to receive water. No auditory cues were presented, and only one spout was available per session. Each trial began after the mouse withheld licking for 3 s, which triggered delivery of a water droplet. This withholding period prevented continuous licking behavior and enabled the analysis of lick bout onsets. Once the mouse licked the spout or if 60 s elapsed without a lick, the next trial began. Each session consisted of 30 trials. Mice required minimal shaping to produce sporadic licking in the absence of external cues.

Two-photon imaging

Viruses were allowed to express for a minimum of 3 weeks before imaging. Wide-field fluorescence imaging (Bergamo, Thorlabs) was used to measure neural responses to four pure tones (4, 8, 16, and 32 kHz) to generate a tonotopic map of ACtx (139). All two-photon fields of view (FOVs) were centered on primary ACtx (A1) and the tonotopic gradient was equally sampled across groups. Two-photon excitation was provided by an Insight X3 laser tuned to 940 nm (Spectra-Physics). A 16×/0.8 numerical aperture water-immersion objective (Nikon) was used to acquire a 750 μm–by–750 μm, 512 pixel–by–512 pixel FOV at 30 Hz with a Bergamo II Galvo-Resonant 8-kHz scanning microscope (Thorlabs). Scanning software (Thorlabs) and stimulus generation hardware (National Instruments) were synchronized via digital pulse trains. The microscope was rotated 40° to 60° from the vertical axis to image the lateral cortex, while mice remained in an upright, head-fixed position. Mice were imaged in a dark, sound-attenuating chamber and monitored throughout experiments using infrared cameras (Genie Nano, Teledyne). L2/3 neurons were imaged 140 to 250 μm below the pial surface with a power (measured at the objective) of 20 to 55 mW. L5 IT neurons were imaged 340 to 460 μm below the pial surface at 30 to 130 mW. L5 ET neurons were imaged 400 to 550 μm below the pial surface at 70 to 150 mW.

For the licking only task, we recorded multiple FOVs from L5 ET mice (n = 7 FOVs across three mice). For sAM responses in untrained mice (naive), we collected multiple unique FOVs from each mouse (L2/3, n = 27 FOVs across eight mice; L5 IT, n = 16 FOVs across six mice; L5 ET, n = 19 FOVs across eight mice). With the exception of one mouse from each cell type, this constituted a separate experimental group from the mice used in the categorization task. For mice trained on the categorization task (L2/3, N = 3 mice; L5 IT, N = 4; L5 ET, N = 4), only one unique FOV was imaged per mouse, and the same neurons were tracked longitudinally for 10 to 18 days. Each imaging day began with either a passive listening block or active discrimination block, alternating across days. During passive listening blocks, the lick spouts were inaccessible to the mouse. To minimize photo bleaching, two-photon imaging during behavior was limited to a subset of trials (150 to 250 trials per day, lasting 30 to 45 min).

Data analysis

Behavioral quantification

Behavioral sessions were divided into early, middle, and late epochs by evenly distributing the total number of sessions for each mouse. Percent correct was calculated as the number of correct trials divided by the sum of correct and incorrect trials. Trials in which the mouse did not respond, as well as boundary trials were excluded from this calculation. Choice bias was quantified as the difference between the number of left and right choices by their sum.

Psychometric curves were fit using a cumulative Gaussian (140)y(x)=g+(1−g−l)×0.5×1+erf(x−μ2v2)where y(x) is the probability of a rightward lick at sAM rate x, erf() is the error function, g is the low-frequency lapse rate, l is the high-frequency lapse rate, μ represents bias, and v is a measure of discrimination sensitivity which reflects the steepness of the psychometric curve (i.e., slope). However, because estimates of v were susceptible to noise in the presence of lapsing, psychometric slope was instead computed as the maximum derivative of the fitted psychometric function. Bias (μ) quantifies the x-axis shift of the psychometric curve relative to the category boundary (in our case, 8 Hz). Lapse rates (g and l) represent “lapses” of performance in response to easy stimuli, with the overall lapse rate quantified as g+l.

Processing of two-photon imaging data

Raw calcium videos were processed using Suite2P to accomplish motion correction, region of interest (ROI) detection, neuropil subtraction, and spike deconvolution (58). All ROIs were visually validated, and any low-quality or nonsomatic ROIs were excluded. For each session, the deconvolved activity trace of each ROI was normalized by z-scoring to its trial-averaged baseline activity (15 frames, 0.5 s before stimulus onset) within that session. This allowed us to observe fluctuations in activity relative to baseline firing rates, while correcting for differences in average fluorescence across ROIs and sessions. Across multiple imaging days, ROIs were matched longitudinally using the open-source software ROICaT (https://github.com/RichieHakim/ROICaT), and matched cells were manually validated to confirm accuracy. While some ROIs dropped out from day to day, neurons were included in the analysis if they were successfully tracked on ≥75% of sessions. Only one L5 ET mouse contained no ROIs that met this criterion.

The published kinetics of GCaMP8s (135) are well matched to our analyses: In mouse cortical pyramidal neurons, GCaMP8s has a 50% rise time of less than 5 ms, well within our 33-ms imaging frame. The decay phase is slower (on the order of ∼200 ms), but this is explicitly accounted for by the deconvolution step in our pipeline, which approximates the underlying spike rate by inverting the indicator’s calcium response kernel.

Analysis of sound-evoked responses

For determining sound responsiveness, activity was smoothed with a five-frame moving average. Responses were measured within a 15-frame (500 ms) window following sound onset. A neuron was considered responsive if it met two criteria: (i) ≥ 40% of the smoothed, z-scored single-trial traces exceeded 0.75 SDs within the onset window; and (ii) the trial-averaged, smoothed, z-scored trace exceeded 0.5 SDs within the onset window. Except for analyses using matched neurons, only neurons responsive to at least one stimulus were included in analyses. Tuning curves were generated by averaging the activity evoked by each stimulus during the onset window. For determining significant modulation without sound (fig. S7), a one-sample t test was used to determine whether average activity within a 10-frame (333-ms) window preceding lick onset differed from a Gaussian distribution with mean of 0.

To quantify categorical selectivity, we computed a CSICSI=resp(fast)−resp(slow)resp(fast)+resp(slow)where resp is the average response across all trials within a given category. We used raw deconvolved activity (before z-scoring) for this analysis to mitigate confounds caused by negative values. The response window for CSI calculations was defined individually for each neuron as a three-frame (100-ms) window centered on its peak response time across all trials, irrespective of stimulus identity. This helped mitigate differences in onset times across neurons. For active sessions, only correct trials were included to minimize behavioral confounds (except for fig. S4A). Population-level categorical selectivity was quantified as the session-average |CSI| across all neurons. To assess the bimodality of CSI distributions, we fit a two-component Gaussian mixture model to the CSI values and measured the distance between the component means. For matched neurons that were not present in all sessions, CSI values on missing days were linearly interpolated to maintain longitudinal continuity (Fig. 4D and fig. S5D). For session-wise CSI analyses (e.g., average |CSI|), sessions were only included if they contained 10 or more responsive neurons (or for functional groups, 10 or more stimulus/choice/mixed neurons).

Spatial clustering

To test whether categorical L5 ET neurons were spatially clustered, we measured their pairwise Euclidean distances. Neurons were classified as strongly categorical if their CSI value was <−0.7 or >0.7. For a given category, if an FOV contained at least 10 strongly categorical neurons, we calculated the pairwise Euclidean distances among those neurons and averaged them. This value was then compared to the average pairwise distances of a random, sample-matched population within the same FOV, resampled with replacement 100 times to generate a null distribution.

Single-neuron decoding

We performed decoding using longitudinally matched individual neurons. A binary logistic regression classifier was used to decode slow versus fast categories on easy and hard trials. Decoders were trained on the average onset-window activity of each trial. For training, we extracted 10 trials of each type (slow/fast) and performed fivefold cross-validation. To ensure that all trials within a session were included in the decoding, we resampled trials with replacement 10 times. Only correct trials were used for decoding. Reported decoding accuracy for each neuron was defined as the mean accuracy of the withheld test sets across cross-validation folds and resamples. Early discriminators were identified as those with an average slow/fast decoding accuracy of ≥0.75 on easy trials during early active sessions. These neurons were further classified on the basis of the decoding accuracies and CSI values late in learning. Adapting and categorical neurons were identified on the basis of average CSI values in late active sessions (<−0.7 or >0.7). Early discriminator neurons that were not categorical were labeled stable if their easy trial decoding accuracy stayed above 0.75 in late learning or were otherwise labeled decreasing. For matrices depicting decoding accuracy across days in matched neurons (Fig. 5D and fig. S6), values for missing days were linearly interpolated for visualization only; interpolated values were not included in statistical analyses.

GLM to separate stimulus and choice

To dissociate the contributions of stimuli and choices to neural activity, we fit a GLM using the glmnet package in MATLAB (https://github.com/junyangq/glmnet-matlab). Predictor variables included stimulus identity (2 to 32 Hz) and choice direction (left/right). Each auditory stimulus contributed 15 predictors (135 total for nine stimuli) corresponding to the first 15 imaging frames after sound onset (0 to 0.5 s) to capture the majority of peak stimulus-aligned neural activity (fig. S3A). Choice variables (15 left and 15 right) corresponded to the nine frames preceding choice onset and six frames after choice onset (−0.3 to 0.2 s) to capture the majority of peak choice-aligned neural signals (fig. S8A). Time periods without predictors (i.e., no sound or choice) were excluded from the model. In addition to a full model containing all predictors, we also fit two partial models with shuffled predictors for either stimulus variables or choice variables to eliminate their relevance while preserving the number of coefficients. Each model was fit to the z-scored, deconvolved activity trace of a given neuron. Models were trained with L1 regularization, testing 100 values of the regularization parameter λ across 50 randomly sampled neurons in the session using fivefold cross-validation across trials to select the largest λ within 1 SE of the models tested. This λ was used for all subsequent model fits within the session. We then fit the models for each responsive neuron with fivefold cross-validation across trials and 20 repetitions of cross-validation train/test splits. Within each fold and repetition, the coefficient of determination (R2) was calculated for the full model and two shuffled models by predicting the activity on held-out trialsR2=1−RSSTSSwhere RSS is the residual sum of squares (the difference between observed and predicted values) and TSS is the total sum of squares (the difference between observed values and their mean). The coefficient of determination for the full model (Rfull2) was then compared to the stimulus-shuffled model (Rshuffledstim2) and choice-shuffled model (Rshuffledchoice2) to determine the unique explained variance (ΔR2) of stimulus and choiceΔRstim2=Rfull2−Rshuffledstim2ΔRchoice2=Rfull2−Rshuffledchoice2

We then averaged ΔRstim2 and ΔRchoice2 across all folds and repetitions. Neurons with both ΔRstim2 and ΔRchoice2 below 0.005 were excluded as having poor model fits. To determine the relative contributions of stimulus and choice in each neuron, we calculated an SI from the averaged unique explained variancesSI=ΔRstim2−ΔRchoice2ΔRstim2+ΔRchoice2where values near −1 indicated more choice selectivity, those near 1 indicated more stimulus selectivity, and those near 0 indicated mixed selectivity. Last, we set SI thresholds to partition neurons into stimulus (SI≥0.2), choice (SI≤−0.2), and mixed (−0.2<SI<0.2) functional groups.

Clustering

For each cell type, we performed hierarchical clustering on the responses of stimulus, choice, and mixed neurons. Within each cell type, neurons were concatenated across sessions for each subgroup. For each neuron, we calculated the mean responses for the nine stimuli and two choices using the same response windows as the coefficients of the GLM (stimuli, 0 to 0.5 s from sound onset; choice, −0.3 to 0.2 s from choice onset). To minimize clustering predictors, activity was binned across five frames (166 ms), creating three values for each stimulus/choice (33 predictors total). An additional value of averaged prestimulus baseline activity of each neuron was added for rescaling purposes but was not used for clustering. Responses were rescaled to a 0 to 1 range and clustered using Euclidean distance with Ward’s linkage methods (MATLAB linkage and cluster functions) to generate dendrograms. The number of clusters were manually curated on the basis of the silhouette value, dendrogram, distance matrix, and underlying neural responses, selecting the fewest clusters necessary to capture the heterogeneity of response types.

Population decoding

To quantify how neural populations encoded the stimulus category (slow/fast sAM rate) and behavioral choice (left/right) across time and learning, we applied an L2-regularized logistic regression decoder (scikit-learn LogisticRegression, default L2 penalty, max_iter = 1000) on a per-session, per-frame basis. Within each session, we separated neurons based on their GLM functional group (stimulus/mixed/choice) and trained separate classifiers for each subgroup. Groups with fewer than 10 neurons in that session were excluded. Two binary classifiers were trained for each session: one predicting stimulus category and the other predicting upcoming choice. Decoding was performed separately on data aligned to stimulus onset or lick onset. For each classifier, we randomly sampled 50 neurons with replacement and balanced the number of slow and fast (or left and right) trials via downsampling. Both correct and incorrect categorizable trials were included for decoding. Classifiers were trained using fivefold cross-validation. Neurons and trials were resampled 20 times, and the reported decoding accuracy for each session and frame was the mean test-set accuracy across all cross-validation folds and resamples. Chance performance was estimated by independently shuffling trial labels within each decoder resampling and repeating the cross-validation procedure.

Dimensionality reduction

To visualize time-varying population activity on boundary trials, we used PCA to reduce the dimensionality of our dataset. For our example session (Fig. 7A), we averaged activity separately for left-choice and right-choice boundary trials aligned to lick onset and performed PCA on these averaged traces from all neurons. We then projected the averaged activity and individual boundary trials onto the first three principal components (PCs), revealing separation between left- and right-choice trials. To quantify this effect across sessions, we randomly sampled 50 responsive neurons with replacement and balanced left and right boundary-choice trials by downsampling each class to its minimum count. Sessions were included only if they contained at least five boundary trials of each choice. When using functionally defined subgroups (stimulus/mixed/choice), the session needed at least 10 of these neurons to be included. We then averaged left- and right-choice boundary trials aligned to lick onset for each neuron, concatenated the two class-means, applied per-neuron z-scoring (scikit-learn StandardScaler), and fit PCA on the resulting standardized data. The first three PCs were extracted, and the Euclidean distance between the two mean trajectories was computed at each time point to obtain a time-resolved measure of trajectory separation. This procedure was repeated 20 times, resampling neurons with replacement and rebalancing trials on each iteration. The reported distances for each session were the averages across resamples.

To test whether prestimulus activity could predict upcoming choices on the perceptually ambiguous boundary stimulus, we used a centroid-based classification analysis. For each session, we averaged activity across four trial types aligned to lick onset: lick left/right boundary trials and correct slow/fast nonboundary trials. Data were concatenated, standardized, and fit with PCA as before. All trials from this session were then projected into this PCA space. Using the first three PCs, we computed left- and right-choice centroids by averaging the population activity of correct nonboundary trials 100 ms before lick onset (where choice trajectory distances were at their maximum; Fig. 7B). These centroids represent choice-linked population subspaces for that session. Then, for each boundary trial in PC space, we took the projected activity immediately before sound onset, calculated the Euclidean distance to each choice centroid, and predicted the upcoming choice based on which distance was smaller. To control for class imbalance in boundary trial counts, we randomly downsampled the boundary trials of each choice to the per-class minimum on 20 independent resamples. The per-session classification accuracy was the proportion of boundary trials whose predicted label matched the animal’s actual upcoming choice, averaged across the 20 resamples. To generate a null distribution for statistical comparison, we shuffled the true left/right labels of boundary trials and computed nearest-centroid accuracy on the shuffled data per resample, then averaged across the 20 resamples.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was conducted in GraphPad Prism (v10.5.0, GraphPad Software) and MATLAB (R2024a, MathWorks). Data are reported as means ± SEM, unless otherwise stated. Nonparametric statistical tests were used where data samples did not meet the assumptions of parametric statistical tests. In cases where the same data sample was used for multiple comparisons, all P values remained significant after correction (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).
