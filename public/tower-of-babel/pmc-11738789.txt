Case study: May human norovirus infection be associated with premature delivery?
Authors: Jiaying Cao, Yuetong Li, Feiyang Xue, Ziyang Sheng, Libo Liu, Yingying Zhang, Lele Wang, Liang Zeng, Yanmin Jiang, Dongying Fan, Fang Li, Jing An
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman norovirus (HuNoV) is the leading cause of acute gastroenteritis. The varying severity of chronic infection in patients with underlying immune deficiencies poses additional burdens on public health. However, the potential effects of HuNoV infection during pregnancy, a specific immune perturbed state, have been rarely reported. Recently, four cases of HuNoV-infected patients in the late stages of pregnancy were admitted to the Guangzhou Women and Children's Medical Center, and premature rupture of membranes as primary adverse outcome was observed in these cases. Samples of fetal accessory tissue were collected from two of these cases at delivery to explore the potential pathogenesis. Pathological analysis showed placental malperfusion in both maternal and fetal vascular, while a decrease in vessels was not observed in villi of placenta. There was obvious pathological change in the chorion of fetal membrane, accompanied by a tendency of Th-1 immune bias. Notably, aggregation of M2 macrophages was observed in the chorion of the fetal membrane, potentially recruited for tissue repair. Next-generation sequencing showed minimal changes in immune pathways within placenta tissue. A gene panel associated with immunosuppression was identified in the fetal membrane of HuNoV-infected women compared to those of normal parturient. Taken together, this study provides clues for the association between the HuNoV and premature delivery, which requires the attention of the clinicians.Keywords: Human norovirus (HuNoV), Adverse pregnancy outcome, Premature delivery, Premature rupture of membranes, Virus infection

Introduction

Human norovirus (HuNoV), is a positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus classified in the family Caliciviridae. It's a highly infectious human pathogen with a global distribution (Liu and Moore, 2020). HuNoV is the leading cause of acute gastroenteritis, transmitted through fecal shedding. About one in every five cases of acute gastroenteritis resulting in diarrhea and vomiting is caused by HuNoV (Ahmed et al., 2014). Moreover, over 95% of nonbacterial gastroenteritis outbreaks are linked to HuNoV (Hickman et al., 2014). Globally, HuNoV is responsible for an estimated 685 million cases of gastroenteritis each year (Cavicchio et al., 2022).

HuNoV can infect individuals of all age groups, with over one-third of cases occurring in children under 5 years old, leading to an estimated 200,000 deaths annually, primarily in developing countries (Patel et al., 2008; Lu et al., 2023). However, both low- and high-income countries are facing the problem of HuNoV infections. The understanding of HuNoV pathogenesis remains limited due to the constraints of commonly used high-efficiency in vitro culture or in vivo animal models. Additionally, there is a lack of vaccines and specific antiviral therapeutics against HuNoV (Zhang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2021).

Clinical outcomes of HuNoV infection vary from asymptomatic viral shedding in stool to severe, life-threatening gastroenteritis. In immunocompetent individuals, HuNoV infection is usually a self-limiting disease with a short duration (Lopman et al., 2004). However, in immunocompromised patients, HuNoV-related diarrhea is an emerging challenge characterized by a more-severe clinical presentation (Westhoff et al., 2009; Bok and Green, 2012). In this patient population, persistent chronic infections may occur, with leading to prolonged viral shedding and symptomatic gastrointestinal disease (Brown et al., 2017; van Beek et al., 2017). The inability of immunocompromised hosts to effectively clear the virus can lead to persistent diarrhea, malabsorption, and weight loss lasting months to years (Bok and Green, 2012; Green, 2014; Gackler et al., 2021).

Epidemiological observations indicate that maternal immunity is perturbed during normal human pregnancy (Apps et al., 2020). This places pregnant women at high risk of infections, and many of which can pose serious problems for a growing fetus (Khan, 2019). Recently, we administered four cases of HuNoV-infected patients during late pregnancy and observed a potential increase in adverse outcomes following HuNoV infection, which has been rarely documented. All patients exhibited symptoms of adverse pregnancy, with premature rupture of membranes (PROM) being the most common complication. Our findings in this study might provide additional evidence regarding the role of HuNoV in adverse pregnancy outcome.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Four pregnant women infected with HuNoV, aged 31–38 years, were included in this study. Three uninfected pregnant women, aged 29–35 years, who exhibited no symptoms of acute gastroenteritis, such as vomiting and diarrhea, tested negative for HuNoV at admission, were concurrently collected as control. The infected pregnant women were primarily multiparas, which influenced the selection of the uninfected control. Both the control and HuNoV-infected cases had no underlying disease. To assess the impact of HuNoV on pregnancy, pregnancy-related tissues were collected immediately after delivery, including placenta and fetal membrane. Additionally, amniotic fluid and umbilical cord blood were collected and stored after birth.

RT-qPCR

Anal swab samples were collected from pregnant women suspected of HuNoV infection using a cotton swab to obtain feces. The samples were then placed into sterile glass tubes containing 0.4 ​mL of sterile physiological saline solution. The samples were submitted for examination immediately. RT-qPCR was used to detect HuNoV nucleic acid in the anal swab samples. An RNA extraction kit (magnetic bead viral DNA/RNA extraction kit from Tiangen Biochemical Technology Limited Company) was used to extract RNA. HuNoV nucleic acid was detected by PCR-fluorescent probe method with the HuNoV nucleic acid detection kit (Hubei Lande Medical Technology Limited Company) on the ABI 7500 FAST Real-Time PCR System. Detections were performed according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Histopathology analysis

Human placenta and fetal membrane samples were fixed in 10% buffered formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The 8 ​μm sections were prepared following standard method. Histopathological evaluation was performed using routine hematoxylin and eosin staining. The slides were examined under a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry staining

The sections were subjected to immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. Briefly, the sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a series of descending graded alcohols to phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 ​min. Then the slides were incubated with 1% bovine serum albumin (in PBS) to block nonspecific antibody bindings. For antigen retrieval, slides were boiled in 10 ​mmol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0; ZLI-9064, Zhongshan Golden Bridge Bio Co. Ltd., China) three times for 10, 5, and 5 ​min, respectively, in a microwave oven (800 ​W). Subsequently, the specimens were incubated with anti-CD34 (Abcam, ab81289), anti-CD163 (Abcam, ab182422), or anti-CD86 (Cell Signaling Technology, 19589) at 4 ​°C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse or rabbit IgG. Finally, after developing color and counterstaining with hematoxylin, slides were examined under a light microscope.

Cytokines protein microarray

Cytokines in placenta, fetal membrane, amniotic fluid and umbilical cord blood were measured by Quantibody Human Cytokine Array 1 (RayBiotech Co., China). Briefly, antibody array was immobilized on a glass slide. The immobilized proteins were incubated with homogenated samples. Then a biotin-labeled detection antibody cocktail was added. After incubation with dye-conjugated streptavidin, the array was scanned using the Axon GenePix 4000B (Silicon Valley, CA) and data were collected using the GenePix Pro software. Levels of cytokines were displayed by signal intensity.

Transcriptome analysis

Next-generation RNA sequencing analysis of pregnancy-related tissues from HuNoV-infected and uninfected pregnant patients was conducted (Vision Medicals, China). In brief, RNA was extracted by Qiagen™ miniRNA prep Kits. RNA libraries were created by Nugen™ Ovation Ultralow System V2 Kits. Qubit™ 4.0 and Agilent™ 2100 were used to quantify and qualify the sample libraries. Finally, the libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NextSeq 550 (Illumina, Inc., USA). Gene expression levels and the transcription levels (FPKM value) were analyzed by HISAT2 software (v2.1.1) to screen differentially expressed genes between groups.

Results

Clinical presentations of pregnant cases with and without HuNoV infection

Case 1: A 33-year-old pregnant woman at 39 ​+ ​3 weeks of gestation with her fourth pregnancy was evaluated at the outpatient maternity ward for unprovoked diarrhea and vomiting. On the day of her visit, she experienced two episodes of loose stools and one episode of watery stools. Her daughter had experienced diarrhea two days prior. Upon examination, she was afebrile with an axillary temperature of 36.5 ​°C. The patient did not report any nausea or lower abdominal pain. Laboratory tests using fluorescent probes-based RT-qPCR results were positive for HuNoV in the anal swab sample. Seven days later, a healthy full-term male baby was delivered spontaneously. During delivery, the fetus experienced a compensable decline in cardiac rate (Fig. 1A).

Patient information of pregnant cases with HuNoV infection.

Case 2: A 38-year-old pregnant woman at 35 ​+ ​5 weeks of gestation in her second pregnancy was admitted due to loose-stool diarrhea and vomiting occurring once after food intake. The patient did not exhibit fever or any other symptoms of gastrointestinal infection. HuNoV was positively detected in the anal swab sample by qRT-PCR. The patient complained of decreased fetal movement. On the 13th day after discharge, she was readmitted to the hospital due to premature rupture of membranes (PROM), and then a healthy full-term male baby was spontaneously labored. Her symptoms, including vomiting and diarrhea, had disappeared by the time of delivery (Fig. 1B).

Case 3: A 38-year-old pregnant woman at 38 ​+ ​1 weeks of gestation in her second pregnancy underwent a caesarean section due to breech presentation. Four weeks prior, her firstborn child was diagnosed with HuNoV infection, after which the patient developed loose-stool diarrhea and was admitted to the hospital. Her laboratory result of anal swab for HuNoV was positive. Two days after the diagnosis of HuNoV infection, an ultrasonographic examination revealed a low perfusion index in fetal umbilical artery and middle cerebral artery. The newborn exhibited intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) (Fig. 1C).

Case 4: A 31-year-old pregnant woman at 39 ​+ ​2 weeks of gestation with her first pregnancy was hospitalized due to PROM. Three days prior to admission, the patient dined out and subsequently developed diarrhea. Upon hospital admission, the patient complained of diarrhea for one day with loose stools for the first two times and watery stool for four times, accompanied by mild abdominal pain. HuNoV was detected in the anal swab sample by qRT-PCR. The fetus was delivered by obstetric forceps due to fetal distress at 14 ​h after PROM. No obvious abnormality was observed in the newborn (Fig. 1D).

Control 1: A 29-year-old pregnant woman at 39 weeks of gestation with her second pregnancy was evaluated at the outpatient maternity ward for macrosomia. Anal swabs were tested negative for HuNoV by RT-qPCR upon admission. A healthy full-term male baby was delivered on the third day of admission, with no adverse events during delivery.

Control 2: A 34-year-old pregnant woman at 38 ​+ ​5 weeks of gestation with her second pregnancy was admitted due to threatened labor with a scarred uterus. Anal swabs were tested negative for HuNoV via RT-qPCR upon admission. A healthy full-term female baby was delivered on the same day of admission, with no adverse events during delivery.

Control 3: A 35-year-old pregnant woman at 39 ​+ ​2 weeks of her third pregnancy was admitted. Anal swabs tested negative for HuNoV via RT-PCR at admission. A healthy full-term female baby was delivered on the second day of admission, with no adverse events during delivery.

The histopathology of placenta and fetal membrane were disrupted in pregnant women with HuNoV infection

Adverse outcomes were observed in pregnant women with HuNoV infection, even two weeks after symptoms disappeared. Placenta and fetal membrane from case 1 and case 2 were collected immediately after the delivery. Both cases exhibited similar histopathological changes in placental tissue. Extensive vacuolation and focal necrosis were observed in decidua area. In the chorion area, the terminal villi, which are responsible for fetal perfusion, appeared slender and hypoplastic in HuNoV-infected placentas compared to uninfected ones. Maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM) was also observed, primarily characterized by increased syncytial nodes and extensive perivascular fibrin deposition. Remarkably, obvious fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM), as indicated by conspicuously avascular villi, was observed in case 2 (Fig. 2). However, no apparent morphological changes were noted in the amnion area. These findings may indicate that placental malperfusion observed in HuNoV-infected patients may persist even after maternal symptoms have resolved.

Given that PROM was identified as the primary adverse pregnancy outcome in HuNoV-infected patients in this study, we analyzed the histomorphology of the fetal membrane. Both cases showed similar pathological changes in the chorion area. Trophoblast cells in the fetal membrane exhibited significant degeneration characterized by vacuolation and cytoplasmic reduction, suggesting potential hypoxia in the fetal membrane tissue. Additionally, a substantial infiltration of leukocytes was observed in the chorion area of case 2 (Fig. 3). These findings indicate that the fetal membrane lesions may contribute to PROM in HuNoV-infected patients.

Next, CD34 antigen was detected to assess the structure of blood vessels in the placenta using IHC staining. No obvious changes were observed in the diameter and density of blood vessels, or the number of vessels per villi in placenta (Fig. 4A and B). However, the red blood cells in blood vessels were decreased (Fig. 4C). These findings indicate that the decreased placental perfusion in HuNoV-infected patients might be attributed to vascular dysfunction or abnormal maternal blood supply, rather than structural damage.

Immunorelated genes in fetal membrane after HuNoV-infection was down-regulated

To investigate the impact of HuNoV, we analyzed the transcriptome of the placenta, fetal membrane, amniotic fluid, and umbilical cord blood using next-generation sequencing. Principal component analysis displayed distinct clustering among different samples. The separation of samples from the fetal membrane indicated a potential lasting influence of HuNoV on this tissue (Fig. 5A). Differences in gene expression were observed in all tissues from HuNoV group; however, the most obvious changes occurred in the fetal membrane, with 535 up-regulated and 937 down-regulated (Fig. 5B). Further analysis of gene ontology (GO) terms revealed that the top 10 changes were related to various metabolic processes, regardless of whether the genes were up-regulated or down-regulated. In contrast, the changes of pro-inflammatory pathways and anti-viral infection pathways were not significant (Fig. 5C). Together with the above mentioned placental malperfusion, these findings suggest that the lesions in fetal membrane might be primarily result from inadequate blood flow rather than persistent viral damage.

Further, we analyzed the expression of immune-related genes based on the gene list provided by ImmPortDB database. The results showed that in uninfected women, high expression of immune-related genes was observed in the fetal membrane, followed by amniotic fluid (Fig. 5D). However, in patients with HuNoV infection, immune-related genes in the fetal membrane was down-regulated (Fig. 5D). Taken together, these results suggest an immunosuppressive state of the fetal membrane, even after the symptoms of HuNoV infection disappear.

Cytokine levels were changed in HuNoV-infected fetal accessory tissues

We subsequently measured inflammatory cytokines in various fetal accessory tissues using a cytometric bead array. In uninfected tissues, the cytokine profiles exhibited a similar pattern, with IL-8 (a neutrophil chemotactic factor) and IL-10 (an immunomodulatory cytokine) being the most abundant, particularly in the fetal membrane (Fig. 6A and B).

In HuNoV–infected patients, levels of IL-8, TNF-α and IL-1β were elevated in the fetal membrane, in contrast to the placenta (Fig. 6A and C). The elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in the fetal membrane may indicate a pro-inflammatory T helper type 1 (Th1)-biased immune response (Fig. 6A and C). In combining with no changes in pro-inflammatory genes observed through RNA-Seq, we inferred that the increased cytokines likely originate from maternal peripheral blood rather than being produced by the fetal membrane itself. These results suggest a potential role of immune response in HuNoV-related adverse pregnancy outcomes and PROM.

M2 macrophages increased in fetal membrane of HuNoV-infected pregnant women

As mentioned above, an immunosuppressive state was observed in the fetal membrane of pregnant women with HuNoV infection, as indicated by gene transcriptomic analysis. Here, we analyzed the infiltration of immune cells in fetal accessory tissue using a deconvolution algorithm combined with gene transcriptomic analysis. Among all kinds of the immune cells, the proportion of M2 macrophages was highest in the fetal membrane and placenta of the control group. In HuNoV–infected cases, the proportion of M2 macrophages in placenta decreased, while the proportion in the fetal membrane increased (Fig. 7). This change may be closely related to excessive tissue repair and the immunosuppressive state. Additionally, other immune cells in fetal membrane exhibited varying degrees of decline, including naive B cells, plasma cells, and activated NK cells, which was consistent with the panel changes in transcriptome.

Immune cells in pregnancy-related tissues. The relative proportion of immune cells in pregnancy-related tissues was analyzed using a deconvolution algorithm combined with gene transcriptomic.

To verify above changes, the infiltration of M2 macrophages in the placenta and fetal membrane was detected by IHC using anti-CD163 antibody. We observed minimal change in M2 macrophages in the placenta of women with HuNoV-infection (Fig. 8). In contrast, there was an increase of M2 macrophages in the fetal membrane, particularly in the amnion and chorion, where M2 macrophages were increased and aggregated (Fig. 8), suggesting continuous tissue repair. The changes of M1 macrophages in the placenta and fetal membrane were also analyzed by IHC using anti-CD86 antibody. No obvious infiltration of M1 macrophages was observed in both placenta and fetal membrane of women with HuNoV infection (Fig. 9), which is consistent with the findings from transcriptomic analysis. Together with the local infiltration of M2 macrophages, these results suggest that the fetal membrane was already under the repair state after infection.

Discussion

HuNoV acts as the primary cause of severe childhood diarrhea. Although the infection is typically self-limited, chronic diarrhea has been described in immunocompromised, malnourished and elderly patients (Schwartz et al., 2011; van Beek et al., 2017). During pregnancy, the maternal immune system undergoes functional adaptations, as though as physiological immunosuppression. This adaptation is crucial for balancing maternal immunity and fetal development, supporting both the pregnancy and the growth of the fetus (Leber et al., 2011). It was reported that maternal immune imbalance is believed to be closely related to abnormal pregnancy outcomes. Additionally, immune-mediated diseases, such as necrotizing enterocolitis, may be triggered in newborns (Lucero et al., 2021).

Previous studies have shown that viral infections during pregnancy, including Zika virus, hepatitis viruses, varicella zoster virus and influenza A virus, are closely associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as spontaneous abortion, fetal death and intrauterine growth retardation (Silasi et al., 2015; Pereira, 2018). However, few reports have revealed a potential relationship between HuNoV and adverse pregnancy outcomes (Ferreira et al., 2010; Thorne et al., 2018). In this study, we reported four cases of pregnant patients with HuNoV infection, in which premature delivery were observed even after the symptoms vanished. To investigate the underlying mechanisms of adverse pregnancy in HuNoV-infected patients, fetal accessory tissues were collected to examine inflammation. A tendency of Th1-biased immune response was observed only in the fetal membranes, not in other fetal accessory tissues. Transcriptome data did not show changes in immune pathways, while changes in metabolic processes dominated. It is suggested that the immune response bias in the fetal membrane closely associated with the maternal systemic response to viral infection rather than direct virus damage. This also indicates that HuNoV infection may have a lasting impact on the mother even symptoms resolve.

Generally, Th1 and Th2 activities are essential components of immunological responses during pregnancy. Although Th1 plays an important role in implantation, placentation regulation, infection defense, and delivery initiation, many studies have suggested that excessive Th1 activity can negatively affect pregnancy outcome (Wilczynski, 2005). Predominant Th1-type immunity initiates and intensifies the cascade of inflammatory cytokine production involved in spontaneous abortion, preterm delivery, and/or preeclampsia (Darmochwal-Kolarz et al., 1999; Kwak-Kim et al., 2003; Challis et al., 2009). Therefore, Th1 bias immune response in the fetal membranes might play a role in adverse pregnancy outcomes in HuNoV-infected patients, and might also contribute to the premature rupture observed in our study cases.

Increasing evidence indicates that immune cells, particularly macrophages, respond to various stimuli under physiological and pathological conditions (Wynn and Vannella, 2016; Yao et al., 2019). It is proved that the secretion of IL-1β and TNF from macrophages stimulates the epithelial-mesenchymal transition of amnion epithelial cells (Mogami et al., 2017; Kawamura et al., 2022). In this study, we found that the proportion of immune cells in the fetal membrane was changed, and M2 macrophages were dominant. In the process of tissue inflammation, M1 macrophages appear in the early stage and recruit numerous inflammatory cells to fight against pathogens, exerting a pro-inflammatory effect (Yunna et al., 2020). After the elimination of pathogens, M2 macrophages inhibit the recruitment of inflammatory cells, thereby exerting an anti-inflammatory effect (Yunna et al., 2020). M2 macrophages promote both angiogenesis and tissue repair, facilitating the tissue recovery (Shapouri-Moghaddam et al., 2018). Prolonged activation of M1 macrophages results in tissue damage, leading to inflammatory disease. Conversely, overactivation of M2 macrophages may lead to fibrosis due to excessive tissue repair and can promote tumor growth through immunosuppression, such as in tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) (Shapouri-Moghaddam et al., 2018). The increase of M2 macrophages in tissues might indicate recruitment for tissue repair.

In most cases of premature rupture of membranes, a precise mechanism remains unconfirmed. The interaction of multiple risk factors is likely the primary cause of preterm labor. However, a common factor in many of those cases is inflammation at the maternal-fetal interface. The characteristic of advancing gestation is an increase in inflammatory responsiveness and proinflammatory cytokines, which actively remodel the cervix, weaken the fetal membranes, and ultimately lead to their rupture and the activation of uterine contractions. Challis et al. suggested that inflammation is involved in the mechanisms of both term and preterm parturition by influencing functions such as cervical ripening, membrane rupture, and myometrial contractility (Challis et al., 2009). Studies have shown that viral infections during pregnancy, including HSV, HPV, and HIV may increase the risk of PROM (Ehsanipoor, 2011; Littauer et al., 2017; Condrat et al., 2021). An animal study suggests that inflammation is involved in the structural destruction of the placenta caused by influenza viruses (Littauer et al., 2017). Although fetuses in utero are not directly exposed to the influenza virus, they can be adversely affected by maternal infection (Littauer et al., 2017). In our study, we observed a tendency towards a pro-inflammatory Th1-biased immune response and infiltration of M2 macrophages in the fetal membrane tissues of women with HuNoV infection, which might play an important role in HuNoV-related adverse pregnancy outcomes and PROM.

Finally, we must point out that the primary limitation of this study is the small sample size. We identified four cases of HuNoV-infected patients in late pregnancy and obtained tissue samples from two of them. The primary susceptible population for HuNoV is children. Infections in adults, particularly pregnant women, are rarely reported. Additionally, the epidemic of the virus is seasonal. Therefore, it is difficult to expand the sample size in a short time. Another limitation of this study is the lack of viral load detection in stool samples and viral genotyping. Due to issues with sample storage, we were unable to further detect the viral load of HuNoV or perform genotyping at the time of hospitalization. The genotypes of HuNoV and viral load may be associated with pathogenesis. Further investigations is required to determine whether these two factors have different effects on pregnancy outcomes. Additionally, virological indicator tests on fetal accessory tissues were not conducted due to limited sample types and the absence of detection reagents. In the future, we will cooperate with clinical researchers to collect additional samples and investigate various concerns, including presence of viral components in placental tissue, immune response in local tissue, and the infiltration characteristics of inflammatory cells.

Conclusions

In this study, we reported disturbances in pregnancy progression among pregnant women infected with HuNoV, suggesting a potential association between HuNoV and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Transcriptome analysis revealed that maternal inflammatory factors might contribute to secondary abnormal immune condition in pregnancy tissues, particularly in the fetal membrane, which might serve as a mechanism for adverse pregnancy outcomes in HuNoV-infected patients. This research provides clinical evidence for the association between HuNoV infection and maternal health. Given that HuNoV is a common cause of diarrhea in children, more attention should be paid to the perinatal health of women in families with multiple children.
